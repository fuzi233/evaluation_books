A few lines ago, I mentioned my mistress. Now, consider the logic of the matter: the diamond could never have vanished from our home if it had not first been given as a gift to my mistress's daughter. And that daughter would never have existed to receive such a gift if my mistress—who brought her into the world through pain and toil—had not conceived her. Therefore, if we trace our inquiry backward from the moment of the theft, the starting point must inevitably lead to my mistress. For any investigator with sense knows that when confronted with a mystery of this magnitude, anchoring oneself at the very origin of the chain of events provides the most solid footing.

If you possess even a passing acquaintance with the upper circles of society, you have likely heard tales of the three beautiful Miss Herncastles: Miss Adelaide, Miss Caroline, and Miss Julia—the youngest and, in my humble estimation, the finest of the trio. I speak with authority, for I had ample opportunity to observe them. At the tender age of fifteen, I entered the household of their father, the old Lord, serving as a page-boy. Though I have no part in the current tragedy surrounding the stone—he, blessed by Providence, possessed neither the wisdom nor the temperament required for such affairs—I lived among those three young ladies until the day Miss Julia married the late Sir John Verinder. He was a man of considerable substance, though one who required guidance; and, to be candid, he found someone to provide it. From the moment my mistress walked down the aisle beside him until the day she laid him gently to rest, closing his weary eyes, they thrived upon this arrangement. Both lived happily, and ultimately, peacefully.

Following the wedding, I accompanied the bride to her husband's estates, located down here. "Sir John," she declared, "I cannot function without Gabriel Betteredge." To which Sir John replied, "My lady, I am equally dependent upon him." Such was the dynamic between them, and thus, I transitioned into his service. To me, the specific location mattered little, provided that my mistress and I remained united.

Observing that my mistress held a keen interest in agricultural matters and estate management, I naturally developed a similar affinity, particularly as I was born the seventh son of a small farmer. She placed me under the supervision of the bailiff, where I performed my duties with diligence, earned her trust, and subsequently advanced in rank. Years later, on a certain Monday, my mistress remarked to Sir John, "Your current bailiff is a senile old fool. Please pension him generously and appoint Gabriel Betteredge to fill his shoes." By Tuesday, the arrangement was complete: the former bailiff retired with a generous sum, and I assumed his post.

It is a rare sight indeed to witness a marriage conducted with such mutual respect and efficiency, standing in stark contrast to the misery that plagues so many unions. May this serve as both a cautionary tale for the foolish and an inspiration for the prudent. Meanwhile, I shall resume the narrative.

In a word, I was content. I occupied a position of significant trust, residing in a modest cottage of my own. My mornings were filled with rounds across the estate, my afternoons with financial accounts, and my evenings with a pipe and a copy of *Robinson Crusoe*. What more could a man desire? One need look no further than Adam in the Garden of Eden; if he was content with solitude, surely I was entitled to the same peace.

However, I soon cast my gaze upon a particular individual: the woman who managed the household at my cottage. Her name was Selina Goby. Regarding the selection of a spouse, I align myself with the sentiments of the late William Cobbett: ensure that a woman chews her food thoroughly and places her feet firmly upon the earth, and you are secure. Selina Goby excelled in both regards, which was sufficient cause for matrimony. Yet, I harbored a second, purely pragmatic motivation. As a singlewoman, she charged me a substantial weekly rate for her lodging and domestic services. Once wed, however, she would be obligated to provide these same services gratis, charging me nothing. This, I reasoned, was the essence of economy, tempered with affection. I presented this calculation to my mistress, fulfilling my duty to her as well as to myself.

"I have been contemplating Selina Goby," I stated, "and I believe it would prove more economical to marry her than to retain her as a servant."

My mistress erupted into laughter, expressing uncertainty regarding whether to be more appalled by my phrasing or my principles. A jest, I suspect, amused her—one that requires a certain level of refinement to appreciate. Having understood nothing else save that I was now free to proceed, I moved forward accordingly. And what was Selina's response? Oh, how little you must know of the female sex if you expect anything other than immediate assent. Naturally, she said yes.

As the date approached, and discussions arose concerning a new coat for the ceremony, doubts began to gnaw at me. I have consulted with other men regarding their feelings during this precarious interval, and they unanimously confessed that roughly a week prior to the event, they secretly wished to escape the impending union. I, however, went a step further. I physically rose and attempted to withdraw. Not for naught! A gentleman does not expect to be released from a contract without recompense. According to the laws of England, compensation is due to the woman when the man seeks to dissolve the bargain. Adhering strictly to legal precedent and after careful deliberation, I offered Selina Goby a featherbed and fifty shillings to terminate the agreement.

You may scarcely credit this, yet it remains the truth: she was foolish enough to refuse.

Consequently, the matter was settled irrevocably. I procured the new coat at the lowest possible price, and I endured the remainder of the proceedings with equal frugality. We were neither a blissful pair nor a wretched one; we were simply six of one and half a dozen of the other. I confess I understand the mechanics of our coexistence poorly, but it appeared that we constantly obstructed one another's paths. Whenever I ascended the stairs, she descended; whenever she sought to go down, I ascended. Thus runs the course of married life, based on my limited experience.

Five years of such stairway entanglements passed before a benevolent Providence relieved us of each other by calling away my wife. I was left with my little daughter, Penelope, and no other offspring. Soon thereafter, Sir John departed this life, leaving my mistress alone with her cherished little girl, Miss Rachel, and no other child. I have spoken of my mistress in vain if you require details of Penelope's upbringing; under her direct supervision, she was educated, sharpened into a capable young woman, and eventually promoted to serve as Miss Rachel's personal maid.

As for me, I continued in my role as bailiff year after year until Christmas 1847, when a shift occurred in my fortunes. On that fateful day, my mistress invited herself to share a cup of tea with me in private within my cottage. She noted that, calculating from the year I first served as a page-boy in the time of the old Lord, I had spent over fifty years in her employ. Then, she handed me a magnificent waistcoat of wool, woven by her own hand, intended to keep me warm against the biting winter chill.

I received this splendid gift with profound confusion, struggling to find adequate words to express my gratitude for the honor bestowed upon me. To my immense surprise, however, the waistcoat was not merely an honor, but a subtle bribe. My mistress had discerned that I was aging before I had fully realized it herself. She had visited my cottage to coax me—should I permit such a term—into relinquishing my arduous outdoor duties as bailiff and embracing a life of leisure as the house steward. I fought as valiantly as I could against the indignity of idleness. Yet, my mistress knew my weakness; she framed the proposal as a kindness extended to herself. Our dispute concluded with me wiping my eyes, like a simpleton, with my newly acquired woollen vestment, and declaring that I would give the matter serious thought.

The turmoil within my mind, following her departure, was truly dreadful. To resolve this uncertainty, I resorted to a remedy I have never known to fail in times of doubt and crisis. I lit a pipe and opened *Robinson Crusoe*. Within five minutes of engaging with this extraordinary volume, I stumbled upon a comforting passage (page 158): "Today we love, what tomorrow we hate." Clarity immediately dawned upon me. Today, I was wholly committed to remaining the farm-bailiff. Tomorrow, by virtue of *Robinson Crusoe*'s authority, I would be entirely opposed to it. If I imagined myself tomorrow, operating under tomorrow's moods, the decision was made. Having thus relieved my conscience, I fell asleep that night as Lady Verinder's farm-bailiff and awoke the following morning as Lady Verinder's house-steward. Everything proceeded comfortably, all thanks to *Robinson Crusoe*!

My daughter Penelope has just glanced over my shoulder to review what I have accomplished thus far. She observes that it is beautifully composed and that every word is true. However, she raises a pertinent objection. She notes that what I have written bears no resemblance whatsoever to what I was commissioned to do. I have been tasked with recounting the story of the diamond, yet I have been narrating the chronicle of my own life. This is curious, and quite beyond my capacity to explain.
