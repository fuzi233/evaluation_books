Ten years slipped by like a slow-moving mist over the town of Dunwich, shrouding the Whateley family in an aura of mystery and foreboding. Their peculiarities, once shocking, had become an accepted part of the community's fabric, a morbid thread woven into the tapestry of everyday life. Twice a year, the family would gather on Sentinel Hill, where fires would blaze and the mountain tremble, its rumblings growing louder with each passing year. The farmhouse, with its sealed upper story, remained a constant source of fascination and unease, its secrets hidden behind closed doors.

Rumors swirled about the goings-on within those walls, whispers of sounds carried through the floors and ceilings, like the lowing of cattle or the bleating of sheep. Some claimed to have seen the albino daughter and her oddly bearded grandson, Wilbur, moving about the upper floor, their faces lit only by the faint glow of candles. The townsfolk wondered how a cow or a bullock could be sacrificed without anyone noticing, and there were murmurs of a complaint to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals. However, the people of Dunwich preferred to keep their affairs private, and the matter was quietly dropped.

Around 1923, a flurry of carpentry work took place within the old house, concentrated in the sealed upper section. The townspeople, piecing together scraps of discarded lumber, inferred that Wilbur and his grandfather had torn down the partitions and removed the attic floor, creating a vast, open space between the ground floor and the peaked roof. The central chimney was also taken down, replaced by a makeshift stovepipe. The Whateleys seemed to be preparing for something, but what, no one could guess.

That spring, Old Whateley began to take notice of the whippoorwills that gathered outside his window at night, their haunting melodies echoing through the darkness. He regarded this phenomenon as a sign, telling the villagers at Osborn's that he believed his time was drawing near. "They whistle just in tune with my breathing now," he said, "and I reckon they're getting ready to catch my soul. They know it's about to leave, and they don't calculate to miss it.
