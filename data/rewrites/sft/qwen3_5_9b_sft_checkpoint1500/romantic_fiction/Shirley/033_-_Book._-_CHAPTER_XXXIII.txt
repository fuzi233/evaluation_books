Martin's entire scheme hinged on his remaining at home that day. Consequently, he found no desire for breakfast. Just as school time approached, a sharp pain seized his heart, making it prudent to abandon his journey to the grammar school with Mark. Instead, he claimed his father's armchair by the fireside, settling in to read his morning paper. With Mark gone to Mr. Summer's class, and both Matthew and Mr. Yorke withdrawn to the counting-house, Martin faced four distinct tasks.

First, he must consume the breakfast he had skipped—a necessity for a boy of fifteen whose current empty stomach lacked the energy required for his upcoming labor. Second, third, and fourth, he must successfully remove his mother, Miss Moore, and Mrs. Horsfall from the premises before four o'clock in the afternoon.

The first task was the most urgent. Martin knew the path to the larder and took it. The servants were busy in the kitchen behind closed doors, while his mother and Miss Moore strolled the lawn, discussing the mysterious silence within. Safe inside the larder, Martin exercised fastidious judgment over his provisions. Determined to make his meal *recherché* despite the delay, he sought variety beyond his usual bread and milk. He selected three rosy apples from their straw, an apricot puff, and a damson tart. While he ignored the plain household bread, his eye lingered favorably on currant tea-cakes, from which he chose one. Armed with his clasp-knife, he appropriated a wing of fowl, a slice of ham, and a cantlet of cold custard-pudding. Satisfied with his bounty, he ventured forth into the hall.

He was halfway across the floor—three steps from the sanctuary of the back parlour—when the front door swung open and Matthew appeared. Far worse than the spectre of the Old Gentleman in full demonic regalia, Matthew proved a formidable obstacle. As a skeptic and scoffer, Matthew had already dismissed Martin's claim of heart pain, muttering phrases like "shamming Abraham" with evident disdain. The sight of the stolen food—the apples, tarts, fowl, ham, and pudding—seemed to confirm Matthew's suspicion of his own superior sagacity.

Martin paused, startled, for a fleeting instant. Then, displaying the true perspicacity of elite souls, he instantly perceived how this mishap could be turned to advantage. He realized that a confrontation with Matthew would inevitably drive his mother to a fit of hysterics. Knowing that calm invariably follows a storm, he calculated that an afternoon of hysterics would surely culminate in an afternoon of bed. This, he reasoned, would solve the problem of his mother perfectly.

The collision occurred immediately in the hall. A dry laugh, an insulting sneer, and a contemptuous taunt met with a nonchalant yet cutting retort. They rushed together, a whirlwind of noise. Servants poured in, followed swiftly by Mrs. Yorke and Miss Moore. No female hand could separate the combatants until Mr. Yorke was summoned.

"Sons," the old gentleman declared, "one of you must leave my roof if this recurs. I will brook no Cain and Abel strife here."

Martin allowed himself to be led away. He feigned injury, playing the role of the youngest and lightest brother. Yet, he remained entirely cool, devoid of genuine passion, even smiling. He felt a profound sense of relief; the most arduous part of his labor was complete.

For a brief interval, Martin faltered in his resolve.

"It is not worth while to bother myself for that Caroline," he murmured.

Yet, barely fifteen minutes later, he returned to the dining room, staring at the woman with disheveled hair and eyes clouded with despair.

"Yes," he whispered, "I made her sob, shudder, almost faint. I shall ensure she smiles before I am finished. Besides, I intend to outwit all these 'womenites.'"

Shortly after dinner, Mrs. Yorke, true to Martin's prediction, retired to her chamber. Now, it was time for Hortense.

Hortense was comfortably seated in the back parlour, mending stockings, when Martin introduced himself. Leaning back on the sofa, where he had been reclining in the voluptuous ease of a callow pacha, he casually dropped a book aside and began a rambling discourse about Sarah, the maid at the Hollow. Through much verbal wandering, he insinuated that Sarah reportedly had three suitors—Frederic Murgatroyd, Jeremiah Pighills, and John-of-Mally's-of-Hannah's-of-Deb's—and that Miss Mann had confirmed the girl often entertained them with the best the house offered since taking sole charge of the cottage.

This revelation was sufficient. Hortense could not remain another hour without investigating these nefarious transactions in person. Mrs. Horsfall remained behind.

Master of the situation, Martin retrieved a bunch of keys from his mother's work-basket. Unlocking the sideboard cupboard, he produced a black bottle and a small glass, placing them on the table. With nimble agility, he ascended the stairs and tapped on Mr. Moore's door. The nurse answered.

"If you please, ma'am, you are invited to step into the back parlour and take some refreshment. You will not be disturbed; the family is out."

He watched her descend, guided her inside, and locked the door behind her. Her safety was secured.

The hard work was done; now came the pleasure. Snatching his cap, Martin departed for the woods.

It was only half-past three. The morning had been fair, but now the sky darkened. Snow began to fall, the wind biting cold. The wood looked dismal, the ancient oak tree grim. Yet, Martin found a strange charm in the spectral shadow cast upon his path.

He had to wait. Walking to and fro as the flakes fell faster, the wind shifting from a moan to a pitiful howl, he muttered to himself, "She is long in coming." He wondered why he desired to see her so intensely, noting that she was not coming for him, though he possessed the power to make her come. Soon, his thoughts hardened: "If she fails to come, I shall hate and scorn her."

At four, the distant chime of the church clock rang out. A step, so quick and light that it barely sounded amidst the rustling leaves, halted his impatience. The wind howled fiercely, the snowstorm growing bewilderingly thick, yet she advanced, undismayed.

"Well, Martin," she exclaimed eagerly, "how is he?"

Martin reflected on how quickly she thought of *him*. The blinding snow and bitter cold mattered little to her; indeed, she was merely a 'chitty-faced creature,' as his mother would say. A warmth stirred in his heart, a wish to clothe her in a cloak. Lost in meditation, he forgot to answer Miss Helstone.

"You have seen him?" she pressed.

"No."

"Oh! You promised you would."

"I mean to do better for you than that. Didn't I say *I* didn't care to see him?"

"But now it will be so long before I know anything certain, and I am sick of waiting. Martin, *please* see him. Give him Caroline Helstone's regards. Tell him she wishes to know how he is, and if anything can be done for his comfort."

"I won't."

"You are changed. You were so friendly last night."

"Come, we must not stand in this wood; it is too cold."

"But before I go, promise me to return tomorrow with news."

"No such thing. I am far too delicate to make and keep such appointments in winter. If you knew the pain in my chest this morning, how I went without breakfast, and how I was knocked down, you would feel the impropriety of bringing me here in the snow. Come, I say."

"Are you really delicate, Martin?"

"Don't I look so?"

"You have rosy cheeks."

"That's hectic. Will you come—or won't you?"

"Where?"

"With me. I was a fool not to bring a cloak. I would have made you cosy."

"You are going home; my nearest road lies in the opposite direction."

"Put your arm through mine; I'll take care of you."

"But the wall—the hedge—it is such hard work climbing, and you are too slender and young to help me without hurting yourself."

"You shall go through the gate."

"But—"

"But, but—will you trust me or not?"

She gazed into his face.

"I think I will. Anything rather than return as anxious as I came."

"I can't answer for that. This, however, I promise you: be ruled by me, and you shall see Moore yourself."

"See him myself?"

"Yourself."

"But, dear Martin, does he know?"

"Ah! I'm dear now. No, he doesn't know."

"And your mother and the others?"

"All is right."

Caroline fell into a long, silent reverie, yet she walked on beside her guide. They soon came into view of Briarmains.

"Have you made up your mind?" he asked.

She was silent.

"Decide; we are just on the spot. I *won't* see him—that I tell you—except to announce your arrival."
