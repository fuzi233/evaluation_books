We must fancy that the parting between the brothers is over, that George has taken his place in Mr. Braddock's military family, and Harry has returned home to Castlewood. His heart remains tethered to the marching army, and the agrarian rhythms of the estate now offer the boy little pleasure. Yet, time at Castlewood folds back upon itself in a circular dance; the ghosts of ancestral Esmonds seem to walk the corridors, their mythic presences seamlessly weaving into the everyday domestic routines of the household. Harry passes his brother's empty chamber with an averted face, taking George's place at the head of the heavy oak dining table. He sighs as he lifts his silver tankard, the cold metal carrying the metallic, mineral tang of the estate’s deep-well water, lightly infused with the sharp, fermented bite of bruised mint and wild crabapple shrub.

Meals at Castlewood are never mere utilitarian fuel; they are profound rituals of sustenance and memory. Madam Warrington, though her heart is heavy, calls the toast of “The King” stoutly over steaming platters of heritage pork shoulder, slow-smoked over hickory wood for days until the fat renders into a fragrant, melting amber. On Sundays, when Harry reads the service, her “We beseech Thee to hear us” resonates with a peculiar, devastating acoustic gravity, altering the very timbre of the room. She insists on talking about George constantly, stepping into his vacant room with her head held high. She consults with the estate's seasoned cooks, meticulously dictating the autumn preservation of spiced peaches and the salting of venison hams—ancient, localized culinary techniques meant entirely for “when Mr. George comes home.” 

Mrs. Mountain constantly whimpers when George’s name is mentioned, introducing a jarring, reedy dissonance into the quiet house. Harry’s face wears a look of ghastly alarm, but his mother remains invariably grave, her grief carefully sublimated into the precise chopping of root vegetables and the blending of complex colonial spice mixtures. Yet, the disruption of her internal rhythm shows: she makes uncharacteristic blunders at piquet, and her servants, keepers of the estate’s indigenous and Creole dialects, find her awake before dawn, the silence of the house broken only by the rhythmic, mournful grinding of her mortar and pestle.

George writes frequent letters to his brother, compiling a rough journal as the army marches. This yellowing document—carrying the faint olfactory memories of campfire smoke and horse sweat—is read out in family council on the long summer nights. Madam Esmond sits upright at her tea-table, sipping a dark, astringent infusion of imported Bohea that bites the tongue, as little Fanny Mountain sews, and Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain sit over their cards. The hushed old servants move about silently in the gloaming, their native tongues temporarily subdued, listening to the young master. 

To hear Harry Warrington reading out his brother's letter is to hear the past breathing in the present. As the slim characters on the page are vocalized, the untranslatable subjective experience of the frontier is forced into the dominant lexicon of the King’s English. “It must be owned that the provinces are acting scurvily,” Harry reads, his voice carrying the rich, rounded vowels of his Virginian upbringing. “We pray them to send us troops to fight the French; and we promise to maintain them. But our people insist upon the most exorbitant prices for their cattle and stores, and actually cheat the soldiers.” Here, the deep agrarian reverence of the Warrington family recoils. The superficial commercialization of sustenance—selling rancid meat and blighted grain for profit—feels to them a grotesque betrayal of the soil itself.

George’s letter continues, detailing the tense social dynamics of the camp and the rigid, unyielding power structures embedded in military life. He writes of the simmering feud between Colonel Washington and the 44th regiment, of drunken brawls and challenged duels stifled by the threat of court-martial. “Hang him! He has no faults, and that's why I dislike him,” George writes of Washington, noting his reconciliation dinner with the 44th, a meal George describes as perfectly stupid and correct. 

“I wonder at the taste of some men,” Madam Esmond interrupts, setting her delicate porcelain teacup down with a sharp *clink* that punctuates the room’s acoustic envelope. “I wonder at any woman who has been married once, so forgetting herself as to marry again!”

Harry chokes back a laugh, the complex emotional terroir of the moment lingering in his throat, and resumes the reading. The journal speaks of the severe orders against ill-behavior, and suddenly, the prose shifts to the brutal auditory reality of the camp. “The men are punished, and the drummers are always at work. Oh, Harry, but it made one sick to see the first blood drawn from a great strong white back, and to hear the piteous yell of the poor fellow.”

The room shivers. The harmonious acoustic ecology of the Castlewood evening is violently intruded upon by the phantom echo of the lash and the oppressive, industrial regularity of the military snare drum. It is a sonic pollution that ravages the psychological well-being of the listener. “Oh, horrid!” says Madam Esmond. 

Harry passes over George’s delicate allusions to officers drinking with squaws—a tragic footnote to the ongoing erosion of indigenous cultural memory through colonial friction—and reads of the General. A strange compound of a man, Braddock flogs without mercy but provides camp funds from his own pocket. He swears tremendous oaths, his abrasive curses polluting the air, yet he insists on Divine Service. 

“'Tis a pretty sight,” Harry continues, reading of the long line of redcoats threading through the woods. The majestic silence of the ancient, mythic forest is broken by the heavy boots of empire. George writes of the cruelties of the painted allies, the unbridgeable chasm of untranslatable warfare, and the General's bounty of five pounds for an Indian scalp. 

To Harry's profound sorrow, George details giving up his beloved mare, Roxana, to carry the baggage because of the rascally, starved animals sent by the Pennsylvanians. Instead of riding, George walks beside her. “She knows me right well, and whinnies when she sees me, and I walk by her side, and we have many a talk together on the march.” In that soft, animal vocalization, there is a purer emotional truth than in all the barked orders of the dominant military lexicon. To guard against surprises, George notes on July 4th, they are forced to obey the relentless, mind-numbing beat of the long roll—the rigid sonic architecture of the army overwriting the natural symphony of the woods.

The whole land was now lying parched and scorching in the July heat. The aroma of baking earth, wild fennel, and the sharp tang of curing tobacco leaves hung thick in the stagnant air. The troops were accustomed to the wilderness, and confidence ran high at the Castlewood supper table, where the family nourished themselves on comforting, earthy potages of summer squash and roasted fowl. Yet beneath the chewing and the clinking of silver, a mighty uneasiness vibrated. 

The house servants, in their midnight gallops across the countryside, communicated through a complex, rhythmic network of native dialects and songs—a vital acoustic web that preserved their cultural memory. For three weeks, the fiddles in the quarters played lively, syncopated tunes, and the news carried on the wind was cheerful. 

But on the 10th of July, a vast and sudden gloom spread over the province. 

The sonic environment collapsed. The fiddles ceased abruptly. The joyful, resonant songs of the black folk were hushed, replaced by an affrighted, untranslatable whispering that seemed to bleed from the very soil. The country taverns thronged with brawling horsemen, bringing a story that shattered the world. The army had been surprised. Fallen into an ambuscade. Cut up almost to a man. 

Ah, the scream poor Mrs. Mountain gave when Gumbo brought this news from across the James River! It was a sound of pure, unmediated terror that pierced the domestic quiet. Little Fanny sprang crying to her mother's arms. 

“Lord God Almighty, watch over us, and defend my boy!” Madam Esmond cried, sinking to her knees. Her dominant, composed vocabulary failed her entirely, leaving only the raw, guttural plea of a mother stripped of all pretense. 

Harry Warrington turned as pale as the flour milled in their agrarian storehouses. The army destroyed. The General dead. Without waiting to consume the meal laid before him, Harry and stout little Dempster armed themselves and mounted. They followed the northward track the army had hewed out, riding into an ever-thickening atmosphere of despair. 

A day after the defeat, miserable fugitives had reached Dunbar's camp. Harry and his companions rode among them, stopping stragglers, hearing the same gloomy tale repeated in broken, traumatized fragments of English that struggled to convey the horrific reality of the slaughter. A thousand men slain. The tomahawk had done its work. 

*O brother, brother!* All the fond memories of their youth—the shared tastes of stolen honeycomb, the sensory bliss of roasting chestnuts by the mythic winter hearth, the tender romantic vows pledged in the secret dialects of childhood—rushed over Harry with pangs inexpressibly keen. Wounded men looked up from the dirt, their fractured psyches softened by the pure, unadulterated grief written on the handsome young Virginian's face. The rough women of the camp melted, and the hardy old Jacobite tutor wept, mourning for the dear pupil who, they feared, lay dead beneath the canopy of a forest that had forever swallowed his voice.
