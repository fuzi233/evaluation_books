The dawn of Palm Sunday—Olive Sunday, as the folk of the Valdarno called it—broke clear and golden over Florence, the yesterday’s rain leaving the air fresh and the cobblestones gleaming. In San Marco, Savonarola ascended the pulpit once more, his voice steady, his gaze unwavering. The faces turned toward him were alight with faith, with love—yet beneath his solemn benediction lay the quiet certainty of his fate. He had seen it in visions, in the silent whispers of his prayers. This would be his last sermon.

By sunset, the city had erupted.

The embers of yesterday’s fury, smothered through the morning, flared anew—not by chance, but by design. The Compagnacci struck first at the Duomo, disrupting the Piagnoni’s evening sermon. Then the cry rose, sharp as a blade: *"To San Marco! Burn San Marco!"*

The monastery’s walls, though sacred, were no sanctuary that night. The monks, long braced for this storm, fought back—some in their white robes like spectral warriors, others with the desperation of men who knew mercy would not come. Lay defenders, too, stood with them. Gunfire cracked near the high altar; stones rained from the roof; swords clashed in the cloisters. The battle raged until deep night, the government’s guards not sent to quell the mob, but to demand surrender: *Lay down your arms. Leave. Savonarola must go.*

Yet leaving meant death. The Frate, willing to face exile, was held back by his brethren. Others risked escape—Francesco Valori among them, slipping over the garden wall, hoping to rally aid. But the night’s true horror came later, when fresh guards arrived under the Signoria’s orders. Savonarola, Domenico, and Salvestro were demanded.

The Prophet walked out, lantern light catching the hatred in a hundred eyes. The guards’ promise of safety was hollow—their duty was only to deliver him to prison. The mob howled, surging forward, a river of spite. Hands grabbed, spat, struck. The worst of life, Savonarola might have thought, was this: not the threat of death, but the stripping of dignity, the jeers of those who once had listened rapt to his words.

Yet the riot was far from over.

With their quarry in chains, the pack turned elsewhere—toward the homes of his followers. This was no blind frenzy, but a calculated hunt. The Compagnacci led, directing the rabble’s clubs and torches. Francesco Valori, marked as the Frate’s right hand, fled San Marco only to find his house besieged. His wife fell, an arrow in her breast. He himself was cut down in the street, slaughtered by Ridolfi and Tornabuoni blades.

And behind it all, unseen, the puppet-master pulled his strings.

In a quiet house between Santa Croce and San Marco, Dolfo Spini held court, his visitors slipping in and out like shadows. Some wore the Compagnacci’s swagger, others plain cloaks, their weapons hidden. Wine flowed, though Spini drank just enough to stoke his temper. Among his guests: Ser Ceccone, ever the sycophant, and Francesco Cei, lounging with the ease of a man who expected his exile to end with the Frate’s ruin.

As the candles guttered, Spini’s mood blackened. News came of setbacks—the mob thwarted from sacking Soderini’s house, restless now, perhaps turning dangerous. But Spini had a solution. A snap of his fingers, a hissed order, and his men moved out again.

The night’s work was not done.

And somewhere in the chaos, a certain Greek—clever, too clever—was laughing. Spini’s fury burned hotter at the thought. *That ruby, that damned ruby…*

But revenge, like the riot, could wait for daylight.
