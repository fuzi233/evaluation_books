“Some wishes crossed my mind and dimly cheered it,
And one or two poor melancholy pleasures,
Each in the pale unwarming light of hope,
Silvering its flimsy wing, flew silent by—
Moths in the moonbeam!”

The morning after brought Margaret a letter from Edith. It was affectionate, rambling, and utterly without order—just as Edith herself might have written it. Yet the warmth of it stirred something deep within Margaret, whose own heart was tender to kindness and sincerity. She had known Edith’s way of speaking all her life; it was so familiar that she hardly noticed its quirks. The letter read:

“Oh, Margaret! It’s worth the journey from England just to see my little boy! He’s a perfect little fellow—especially when he wears his caps, and most especially the one you sent him. You good, dainty-fingered, persevering little lady! You’ve made all the mothers here green with envy, so now I want to show him off to someone new—to hear fresh admiration, perhaps. That may be all I’m after—but maybe not. Possibly there’s a little cousinly love mixed in too. Still, I *do* want you so much to come here, Margaret! I’m sure it would do Aunt Hale the world of good. Everyone around here is young and strong, our skies are always blue, the sun never stops shining, and the band plays lovely music from dawn till dusk. And, returning to the main tune of my song—my baby always smiles.

I’m constantly asking you to draw him for me, Margaret. Doesn’t matter what he’s doing—whatever he’s up to is the prettiest, most graceful thing imaginable. I think I love him better than my husband, who’s growing stout and grumpy—what he calls ‘busy.’ No, wait—that’s not fair. He’s just come in with news of a delightful picnic hosted by the officers of the Hazard, anchored in the bay below. Because he brought such cheerful news, I take back everything I said. Didn’t someone burn their hand for saying something they later regretted? Well, I can’t burn mine—it would hurt, and the scar would be ugly. So I retract all I said as fast as I can. Cosmo is just as wonderful as the baby, not at all stout, and no more grumpy than any decent husband should be. Only sometimes—he *is* very busy. I can say that without wifely duty—where was I? Oh yes—I had something important to tell you. Here it is: Dearest Margaret, you must come and visit me. It would help Aunt Hale immensely, as I said before. Get the doctor to prescribe it. Tell him it’s the smoke of Milton that’s harming her. I truly believe it is. Three months—no less—of this glorious climate—sunshine every day, grapes as plentiful as blackberries—would restore her completely.

I don’t ask my uncle”—(here the handwriting became neater, more careful. Mr. Hale sat quietly in the corner, like a child caught misbehaving, having given up his living.)—“because I suppose he disapproves of war, soldiers, and bands. At least, I know many Dissenters belong to the Peace Society, and I fear he wouldn’t enjoy it. But if he *would* come, dear, please say that Cosmo and I will do our best to make him happy. I’ll hide Cosmo’s red coat and sword, and make the band play solemn, serious tunes. Or, if they insist on pomp and vanity, I’ll have them play it in double slow time. Dear Margaret, if he’d care to join you and Aunt Hale, we’ll try to make it pleasant—even though I confess I’m a little wary of anyone who has acted out of conscience. You never did anything like that, I hope. Tell Aunt Hale not to bring warm clothes—though I suspect it will be late in the year before you arrive. But you have no idea how hot it gets here! I tried wearing my great beauty Indian shawl at a picnic. I held myself together with proverbs—‘Pride must abide,’ and other wholesome bits of wisdom. But it was no use. I felt like mamma’s little dog Tiny wrapped in an elephant’s costume—smothered, hidden, killed by my finery. So I turned it into a splendid carpet for us all to sit upon.

Now, here’s my boy, Margaret—if you don’t pack your things the moment you get this letter and come straight away to see him, I shall think you’re descended from King Herod!”

Margaret longed for just one day of Edith’s life—the ease, the laughter, the untroubled days under endless blue skies. If only a wish could carry her there—just for a single day. She craved the strength such a change would give her, even if only for a few hours to feel young again, to breathe freely among joy and light. Not yet twenty, and already she had borne burdens that made her feel ancient. That was her first thought after reading the letter.

Then she read it again, forgetting her own weariness, laughing aloud at the familiar tone, the vivid pictures, the sheer delight of Edith’s voice. She was still smiling when Mrs. Hale entered the drawing-room, leaning on Dixon’s arm. Margaret rushed forward to adjust the pillows. Her mother looked weaker than usual.

“What were you laughing at, Margaret?” she asked once she had settled onto the sofa.

“A letter from Edith. Shall I read it to you, mamma?”

She read it aloud, and for a while, her mother seemed drawn into it, guessing names for the baby, speculating on why each might have been chosen. Just as the conversation reached its peak, Mr. Thornton arrived, bearing another basket of fruit for Mrs. Hale.

He could not—would not—deny himself the chance to see Margaret. It was not for any deeper purpose, but simply for the pleasure of seeing her. It was a stubborn act of will, rare for a man usually so composed and self-possessed. He stepped into the room, glancing briefly at Margaret, then immediately turned away. After a stiff, formal bow, he never once looked at her again. He stayed only long enough to present the peaches, to speak a few kind words, and then, as he left, his cold, offended eyes met hers in a quiet farewell.

Margaret sat down, silent and pale.

“Do you know, Margaret,” Mrs. Hale said gently, “I really begin to like Mr. Thornton.”

Margaret said nothing at first. Then, forcing the words out, she replied, “Do you?”

“Yes. I think he’s becoming quite polished in his manners.”

Her voice was calmer now. “He is very kind and attentive—there’s no denying that.”

“I wonder Mrs. Thornton never comes. She must know I’m ill—she knows about the water-bed.”

“She probably hears from her son.”

“But still, I’d like to see her. You have so few friends here, Margaret.”

Margaret understood the unspoken plea beneath her mother’s words—a quiet longing for some woman to take care of her daughter, should she soon be left alone. But she could not respond.

“Do you think,” Mrs. Hale asked after a pause, “you could go and invite Mrs. Thornton to visit me? Just once. I don’t want to be a burden.”

“I’ll do anything you wish, mamma—but if Frederick comes—”

“Oh, yes, of course. We must keep our doors shut. We must let no one in. I scarcely know whether I dare hope for him to return. Sometimes I’d rather he didn’t. I have such terrible dreams about him.”

“Oh, mamma! We’ll take care. I’ll lock the door with my own hands before he could come to harm. Trust me—trust the care of him to me. I’ll watch over him like a lioness guarding her cubs.”

“When can we hear from him?”

“Not for a week, certainly—perhaps longer.”

“We must send Martha away in good time. It would never do to have her here when he arrives and then rush her off.”

“Dixon will remind us. I was thinking—we could get Mary Higgins to help while he’s here. She’s not very diligent, but she’s honest, and she’d try her best. She sleeps at home and never comes upstairs, so she wouldn’t know who’s in the house.”

“As you like. As Dixon likes. But, Margaret, don’t use those dreadful Milton words. ‘Slack of work’—it’s a provincialism. What will Aunt Shaw say when she returns?”

“Oh, mamma! Don’t make Aunt Shaw into a monster,” Margaret laughed. “Edith picked up all sorts of military slang from Captain Lennox, and Aunt Shaw never minded.”

“But yours is factory slang.”

“And if I live in a factory town, I must speak its language when I need to. Why, mamma, I could astonish you with words you’ve never heard. I don’t think you know what a *knobstick* is.”

“No, child. I only know it sounds vulgar—and I don’t want to hear you use it.”

“Very well, dearest mother, I won’t. But I’ll have to explain whole sentences instead.”

“I don’t like this Milton,” Mrs. Hale murmured. “Edith’s right—this smoke is what’s made me so ill.”

Margaret started at her mother’s words. Her father had just entered the room, and she was desperate to prevent any reinforcement of the impression she’d seen flicker across his face—the idea that Milton’s air had harmed her mother. She couldn’t tell if he’d heard her mother’s remark, but she began speaking quickly of other things, unaware that Mr. Thornton was following behind him.

“Mamma says I’ve picked up a lot of vulgarity since we came to Milton.”

The word “vulgarity” referred only to local speech, born from their recent conversation. But Mr. Thornton’s brow darkened. Margaret suddenly realized how her words might be taken—how they could seem coarse or disrespectful to him. In a natural impulse to avoid causing pain, she forced herself to continue, addressing him directly.

“Now, Mr. Thornton, though ‘knobstick’ doesn’t sound very pleasant, isn’t it expressive? Could I do without it when describing the thing it means? If using local words is vulgar, then I was very vulgar in the Forest—wasn’t I, mamma?”

It was unusual for Margaret to dominate a conversation, but she was so anxious to correct any misunderstanding that she didn’t notice until later how deeply she blushed. Mr. Thornton seemed barely to grasp the point of her words. He passed by her with a stiff, formal gesture, turning instead to speak to Mrs. Hale.

That sight reminded Margaret of her mother’s request—her quiet plea for Mrs. Thornton to visit. She sat in silence, burning with shame and frustration at her inability to remain calm and composed in his presence. Her thoughts kept returning to him—not with affection, but with a quiet sorrow that she had wounded him so deeply. And yet, she found herself striving gently, patiently, to rebuild the old balance between them—the position of mutual respect and uneasy friendship he had once held in her regard, and in that of her family.

There was a quiet humility in her manner toward him now, as if silently apologizing for the harsh words spoken in the aftermath of the riot. But he resented those words fiercely. They echoed in his mind, sharp and clear. He took pride in the justice that drove him to offer kindness to her parents, even when it pained him. He exulted in his own strength—the power he showed in forcing himself to face her whenever it might bring comfort to her father or mother.

He told himself he disliked seeing her, that her presence was a torment. But he was wrong. It was a sharp, secret pleasure to be near her, to feel her presence, even if he refused to look at her. He was not a man who examined his own motives closely—and so he remained mistaken, as I have said.
