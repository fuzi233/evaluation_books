When she descended once more, she was no longer the woman who had climbed the stairs in anger and sorrow. Her eyes glistened with unshed tears, but her expression was calm, composed—her spirit quieted as if by a deep inner peace. The storm within had passed, leaving behind a stillness that seemed almost sacred.

“Denys,” she said softly, “I have received my orders. I am to read my lover’s letter aloud to his family.”

“You will not do that?” he replied, astonished.

“Ay, I will,” she answered firmly.

He stared at her, sensing a shift in her heart. “Then something in the letter has softened you toward them?”

“Not a whit, Denys—not a single thought,” she insisted. “If I hated them as poison, I would still obey Gerard. And yet, it is sweet—oh, so sweet—to obey one who is far away, powerless to command me, yet trusting me to do what is right because of love. If Gerard were here, I might resist his wishes, for I know well how much I despise his kin. But now—now, even if he asked me to walk into the sea and read his words to the sharks, I would go. So tell his mother I have his letter, and if they wish to hear it, I am ready. Let them name the hour, and I shall welcome them as best I may.”

Denys hurried off to deliver the message. He found the family gathered around the dinner table, their faces alight with joy at the news of Gerard’s letter. He waited until the moment was right, then broke in: “And there is more. She has changed. She will read the letter herself. You may choose your own time.”

Catherine frowned. “What makes her think only she can read it? Let her send it—we’ll read it ourselves.”

“But perhaps she cannot bear to part with it,” little Kate interjected gently. “She loves him dearly.”

“And thinks we’d steal it?” Cornelis muttered darkly.

“I suspect,” he added under his breath, “that she wants to force her way into our home through this very letter.”

Denys turned on him with cold disdain. “There speaks a bitter heart,” he said sharply. “La camarade hates you all like poison. But mark this—though she loathes you, she offers to read the letter with her own voice, soft as honey. She does not ask for reward; she offers obedience. And though she bears you ill will, she yields to Gerard’s word. That is no small thing. She has a voice like music—surely such a gift deserves respect.”

The father of the house nodded slowly. “You speak truth, honest soldier. This offer merits a civil reply. Hold your tongues, women—and I shall answer her. Tell her this: I, Eli a Dierich a William a Luke, free burgher of Tergou, swear upon my honor and my ancestors’ names, that I lay aside all past grievances and thank her for this grace. Should she wish greater thanks, let her send her son’s letter by your faithful hand. I will read it to my flesh and blood, and return it to her with the same care and honesty as I have lived my life—true to my word.”

“And better than his word,” Catherine burst out. “The only man I’ve ever known who truly kept his promise.”

“Peace, wife,” Eli said quietly.

“Art a man of sense, Eli—a dirk, a chose, a chose!” Denys cried, grinning. “And bless ye, the world has turned upside down! She who once scorned us now kneels to obedience. ‘Obedience is honey,’ she says—and indeed, it is a sweetness she cannot help savoring, though she eats little of it. With her beauty, her voice, her fits of passion that scare soldiers half to death when we cross her, and her ways—chiding one moment, petting us like lambs the next—she has made two of the most devoted slaves you ever saw from two proud swashbucklers. I am the iron ruffian—my comrade washes.”

“What next?” someone asked.

“Oh, when the child comes into the world, I’ll rock the cradle,” Denys declared, “and the other fellow will wash the bibs and tucker. So now, I’ll fetch the letter at once. Will you let me stay and hear it read?”

“Else our hearts were black as coal,” Catherine replied.

Denys left, eager and hopeful. But he returned moments later, head bowed, face heavy with disappointment.

“She will not give it to anyone—not me, not you, not any living soul.”

“I knew she wouldn’t,” Cornelis said smugly.

“Whisht! Whisht!” Eli commanded. “Let Denys finish.”

“So I said, ‘But be ruled by me.’” Denys recounted. “‘Not I,’ she replied. ‘Well then,’ I pressed, ‘what of that honeyed obedience you spoke of?’”

“She looked at me and said, ‘You are a fool. Obedience to Gerard is sweet—but obedience to anyone else? Who ever said that was sweet?’”

At last, she relented slightly. “She gave me a written note—for you, mademoiselle,” Denys added, handing it over.

“For me?” little Kate whispered, blushing deeply.

Eli took the paper and read it aloud, his voice dropping low:
*“And, sweetheart, if these lines reach you safely, try my people’s hearts. Perhaps they have grown kinder, now that I am far away. If so, they will show it to you—since they may not to me. Read this letter. But I strictly forbid you to let it leave your hands. If they still turn from you, say nothing—let them believe me dead. Obey me in this. For if you dishonor my judgment, you do not love me.”*

A silence fell over the room. The words were copied in Margaret’s hand and passed around, each person studying them closely.

“Well,” Catherine said at last, “this changes things. But still—I think she should come to us, not we to her.”

“Alas, mother,” little Kate sighed, “what difference does it make?”

“Much,” Eli replied. “Tell her we are too many to go to her. Bid her come here—soon as possible.”

After Denys left, Eli admitted in a low voice, “It is a bitter pill. When that girl sets foot across my threshold, all the trouble she’s caused will rush in like a flood. But what could I do? We must hear of Gerard. I saw it in your eyes, wife—and felt it in my own heart. She is backed by our disobedient yet beloved son. So she stands stronger than we, forcing us down to the grindstone—the cunning, cruel wench. But never mind. We will hear the letter. Then let her go unblessed, as she came unwelcome.”

“Make your mind easy,” Catherine said softly. “She will not come at all.”

And in her tone, there was a quiet regret.

Shortly after, Richart arrived from Amsterdam—grave, dignified, dressed in the full robes of a burgess: gold chain, stiff ruff, fur-lined cap. He was greeted not only with affection but with respect, for he had risen above his parents in station, and such elevation was marked in medieval society as plainly as steps in a stairwell.

Admitted to the family council, he revealed his pride wounded—though not unkindly—by their willingness to receive Margaret Brandt.

“I see the temptation,” he said. “But which of us has not sometimes wished one thing and done another?”

His words cast a chill over the older generation.

Then little Kate stepped forward. “Vex not yourself, dear Richart. Mother says she will not come.”

“All the better,” he replied. “For if she does, I fear I shall return to Amsterdam at once.”

Just then, Denys appeared at the door, peering in.

“She will be here at three o’clock—by the great dial.”

They all looked at one another, silent, uncertain—caught between duty, resentment, and the fragile hope of news from a lost son.
