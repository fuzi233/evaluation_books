Nearly three full weeks slipped by before every scroll, manuscript, and carved folio of Bardo’s great library was packed tight in straw and loaded onto waiting carts. Instead of turning away and blocking out the noise of workmen’s boots and the crack of packing twine, Romola watched every step of the process. The hollow exhaustion that follows a shattering, violent grief breeds that dreamy, half-disbelieving fog where the worst of one’s pain feels almost unreal; and every evening, after the laborers trundled out the door, she would take her small hand-lamp and drift slow through the scattered straw and empty wooden crates, pausing at every bare pedestal, every well-loved marble bust laid prone on its side, driven by a bitter, hungry urge to prove to herself that there was good reason her love was dead, and the whole world had turned barren.
As the days wore on, she returned to that half-dismantled room evening after evening, no longer seeking proof of her loss, but because prodding that raw, throbbing grief tied to her father’s memory was the only living, feeling thing left in her empty affections. On the 23rd of December, she knew the very last crates would be carted away. She ran for the loggia at the roof of the house, determined not to miss that final sharp pang of watching the slow, heavy wheels of the last wagon creep across the bridge.

The day was overcast, drawing close to dusk. The Arno ran dark and shivering, its surface rippling grey and cold; the surrounding hills hung low and mournful, and Florence, girdled by its ancient stone towers, had the silent, tomb-like cast unbroken shadow casts over a city seen from on high. Santa Croce, where her father lay buried, was swallowed whole in that deep dusk, and slowly crawling across the bridge, then vanishing up the narrow winding street beyond, was the wagon’s pale white load, moving like a cruel, deliberate Fate carrying away her father’s lifelong, laborious hope to bury it in an unmarked, forgotten grave. Romola hardly felt like she was watching it herself: it felt as if her father, helpless under the cold imprisoning stone of his tomb, was the one conscious of the theft, and her hand could not reach through the earth to touch his and tell him he was not alone.

She stood long after the last wagon had vanished, heedless of the sharp winter cold, soothed by the gloom that wrapped around her like a mourning cloak, shutting out the jarring sound of other people’s joy. Then, all at once, the great bell in the Palazzo della Signoria’s tower boomed out a mighty peal: not the sharp, staccato clang of alarm, but an agitated, rolling peal of triumph. One after another, every bell in every tower across the city caught the vibration, and joined the roaring chorus. As the sound swelled higher and higher until the air itself felt thick with noise, tiny dancing flames, vibrating as if the sound itself had caught fire, burst out between the palace turrets and along the parapets of every girdling tower.

That sudden clang, that leaping firelight, struck Romola like a dozen sharp wounds. It felt to her like demons cheering the success of her husband’s treachery, and the total desolation of her life. Barely three weeks prior, she had drunk deep of the very sound of those same bells, giddy with Florence’s celebration, and heard in the city’s joy a prophecy of her own coming happiness. Now the crowd’s exultation felt nothing but cruel: she stood cut off from that common, loud-living Florence that fling its raucous joy out to stun the ears of anyone grieving or lonely. She would never clasp hands with gladness again, she thought, only with the people that hard, bright joy was always bound to forget. In her bitterness, all rejoicing felt like mockery: men shouted paeans with their souls heavy as lead, then glanced at their neighbors’ faces just to check if joy was even a real thing. Romola no longer believed in the happiness she had once thirsted for: it was a hateful, smiling, soft-handed thing with a narrow, selfish little heart.

She ran down from the loggia, hands pressed tight over her ears, and was hurrying across the antechamber when she started violently, coming face to face with her husband, who had been coming to find her. His step was light and springy, and there was a glow of uncommonly bright satisfaction about him.
“What! The noise was a little too much for you?” he said, noticing she had clamped her hands even tighter over her ears when she saw him. He took her gently by the wrist, drew her arm through his, and led her into the saloon ringed with frescoed dancing nymphs and fauns, before continuing. “Florence has gone quite mad with glee over its new Great Council, which is supposed to wipe every evil under the sun out of existence—even the very vice of merriment, if the friar has his way. No wonder you look stunned, my Romola, and you’re ice cold. You must never stay out on that windy loggia without wrapping up warm. I came to tell you I’ve been suddenly called to Rome on learned business for Bernardo Rucellai. I leave immediately, to meet my party at San Gaggio tonight so we can set out at dawn tomorrow. You need not trouble yourself at all for me, I had all my bags packed days ago. I shall be back before very long.”

He knew he could expect nothing from her now but quiet endurance of whatever he said or did. He did not even dare to lean in and kiss her forehead that evening, only pressed her hand briefly to his lips, and left. Tito told himself Romola was far more unforgiving than he had ever imagined: her love was not that soft, clinging instinct stronger than all judgment, which he was just now realizing was the true great charm of a wife. Still, this petrified coldness was far better than passionate, useless opposition. Her pride, and her knack for seeing exactly when resistance was pointless, had its conveniences for him.

The second the door clicked shut behind Tito, the frozen, immobile mask that settled over Romola’s face like frost whenever he was near shattered completely. Inwardly, she was far from quiet endurance: every day since the terrible fight that had split them irrevocably apart, she had been actively planning and preparing to carry out a purpose she had set for herself.

The first thing she did was call old Maso to her.
“Maso,” she said, her tone steady and unyielding, “we leave on our journey at dawn tomorrow. We can catch that first cloth caravan they say is lingering at San Piero before it departs north. Arrange for two mules tonight, be ready to set off with them at first light, and wait for me at Trespiano.”

She meant to take Maso with her as far as Bologna, then send him back with letters to her godfather Bernardo del Nero and Tito, telling them she was gone, and never intended to return. She had planned her departure to be perfectly secret, so that her broken love and her broken life would never be picked apart and gawked at by vulgar, curious eyes. Bernardo had been away at his villa, escaping political suspicion to tend to his beloved fields, and Romola had repaid the large debt Tito owed him without ever meeting him face to face. He did not even know the library had been sold, and would simply assume some sudden stroke of good fortune had let Tito raise the sum. Maso knew no more than that her intended journey was a secret, and to do exactly what she told him was the greatest pleasure left to him in his withered, wintry old age.

Romola had no intention of going to bed that night. When she had bolted her door, she carried her taper over to the carved, painted chest that held her wedding clothes. The white silk stitched with gold, the long flowing white veil, and the circlet strung with pearls lay there, and a great sob tore out of her as she stared at them: they looked exactly like a shroud for her dead happiness. Tucked in the tiny gold loop of the pearl circlet, a single pink sugarplum had lodged, one of the shower of sweet treats tossed at the crowd on their wedding day. Tito had spotted it first, and said it should stay there forever. In moments like this one, a sudden wave of memory swept her back to that time of perfect, unshaken trust, and she could feel the presence of the husband whose love had made the whole world as fresh and wonderful to her as it is to a small quiet child sitting in sun-dappled flowers: she could hear his soft gentle tones, see his kind eyes with no lie in them, and breathe again that vast freedom of the soul that comes from believing the person nearest you is better, greater than yourself. Those moments always brought hot, stinging tears: her loving woman’s heart ached with something akin to what a bereaved mother feels when tiny ghost fingers seem to lie warm on her breast, yet turn cold as marble when she presses her lips to the silent bed.

But there was something else tucked at the very bottom of the chest, dark and coarse, rolled up tight in a bundle. She tore her eyes away from the glimmering white and gold finery to the dark bundle, and the second her hands brushed the rough serge, her tears slowed and stopped. That coarse, unyielding texture pulled her fully back to the present, where all love and delight were long gone. She undid the thick white cord wrapped around it, and spread the bundle out on the table. It was the plain grey serge habit of a lay sister of the third order of Saint Francis, one who lived in the world but devoted herself entirely to pious deeds—a woman the Florentines called a Pinzochera. Romola meant to wear it as a disguise, and she determined to put it on at once, so that if she did need to rest a little before dawn, she would wake up already fully ready to leave.
She slipped off her soft black wool gown, and as she pushed her soft pale white arms into the harsh scratchy sleeves of the serge mantle, and the hard twisted rope girdle bit into her fingers when she tied it tight at her waist, she welcomed those rude, sharp sensations. They fit perfectly with her new scorn for that thing called pleasure that made men base—that clever trick of chasing selfish ease, of shrinking from hardship and strain while everyone else bore burdens too heavy to carry, which had become exactly the image of her husband in her mind. She gathered up her long loose hair, pulled it tight away from her face, twisted it into a hard heavy knot at the back of her head, and tied a square of black silk tight across her forehead and under her chin like a kerchief, then pulled the habit’s cowl up over her head.
She held the candle up to the mirror. Her disguise was perfect for anyone who had not lived very close to her. To her own eyes, she looked shockingly like her brother Dino: the full soft oval of her cheek only needed to waste away, her sad, heavy eyes only needed to sink a little further, and she would be indistinguishable from him. Was she growing more like him in any other way? Only in this: she understood now what had driven men like him to run away from all earthly joy forever, to dwell on images of sorrow rather than beauty and delight.

She did not linger at the mirror. She set about gathering every small relic of her father and mother that was too large to tuck into her small travelling wallet, to lay them all in the chest with her wedding clothes, and leave the whole locked chest in her godfather’s care once she was safely gone. First she laid in their painted portraits, then one by one every tiny trinket tangled with sacred memory went either into her wallet or the chest.

She paused. There was still one last thing she needed to strip away from herself, belonging to that old past she was turning her back on forever. She pressed her thumb and forefinger to her betrothal ring, but rested there, not pulling it off. Romola’s mind had been rushing full speed towards this act, the act of quitting the husband who had betrayed every last shred of her trust, of breaking an outward tie that no longer matched any inner bond of love left between them. But the strange power of outward symbols, which knit our whole lives together and give us an unshakable external identity no wavering feeling can shake, made that tiny small movement to take off her ring feel enormous. It gave her a vague, arresting sense that she was violently tearing her whole life clean in two: a presentiment that the sure, unhesitating impulse that had seemed to clear her path of all doubt might after all be a form of blindness, that human bonds carried something in them that could not be broken just because the illusions tied to them had shattered.

If the beloved Tito who had slipped that betrothal ring onto her finger that day was not in any real sense the same false Tito she now despised, why return the sign of their union to him? Why not keep it as a memento? That small act, which felt like tangible proof of both their identities, shook Romola in a way she could not explain. Half the truth we live amid only haunts us that way, making dull, wordless little thuds in our chest that never rise to become clear thought. But a passionate, loud voice speaking up inside her soon drowned out all those soft muffled murmurs.
“It cannot be! I will not be subject to him. He is false. I shrink from him. I despise him!”

She wrenched the ring off her finger, and dropped it on the table right next to the pen she meant to use to write her parting letters. Again she knew there could be no law for her but the law of her own affections. That tender, deep fellow-feeling for the people she loved, which had been the core of her whole religion up to this point, had made her patient despite her natural impetuousness, and would have made her heroic. But now all that strength was gone, or rather, it had turned into the strength of revulsion. She recoiled from Tito in exact measure to the fierceness of the young love and trust he had betrayed, of the lifelong devotion to her father he had trampled to commit that irredeemable crime. It felt as if every other motive had slipped away from her, leaving only the indignation and scorn that made her tear herself clean away from him.

She was not following any old precedent, or obeying any set of moral rules. The grand, severe stoical philosophy her father had made sure to teach her was familiar to her lips, and its lofty spirit had stirred echoes in her soul, but she had never used it, never needed it, as a rule to live by. She had endured and forborne because she loved: maxims that told her to feel less, not to cling too close lest the great onward march of the world jar her, had been as useless to her tender heart as they had been to her father’s lifelong yearning for just fame. She had never picked up theories to live by: she had simply felt strong in the strength of her love, and life without that energy was an entirely new, unwritten problem.

She planned to solve that problem in what seemed to her a very simple way. Her mind had never bowed to any sense of obligation apart from personal love and reverence; she had no keen sense of any other human ties, and the only thing she knew she must obey now was the instinct to sever herself completely from the man she no longer loved at all.

But her unshakable resolution came wrapped in phases of shifting, sharp anguish. Now that all the active work of preparing to leave was almost done, she lingered, putting off writing those irrevocable words that would cut her off from every small familiar thing in her world. The emotions of the past weeks rushed back over her all at once, taking possession even of her limbs. She meant to write, and her hand fell limp at her side. Bitter tears spilled now for the delusion that had blighted her young years, tears nothing like the soft sobs of remembered happiness she had shed looking at the pearl circlet and its trapped pink sugarplum. Now she felt a hot, stinging shame at the cruel words of ignominy she had spat at Tito in their fight: “Have you robbed some one else who is not dead?” To have been forced to say those words, to hurl them at her own husband, felt like a degradation of her whole life. Harsh, cruel speech between two people who once loved each other is hideous in memory, like the sight of something once great and beautiful sunk into rags and vice.

That heart-wrenching comparison between the bright past and the desolate present pressed on Romola until it turned into raw physical sensation: she felt numb to everything but the throbbing of her own heart, and craved some hard, sharp contact to ground her. She ran her hands tight along the rough knotted cord hanging from her waist. She jumped to her feet and seized the rough carved lid of the chest: was there nothing else left to tuck inside? No. She slammed the lid shut, pressed her palm down hard on the rough carvings, and locked it.

Then she remembered she had one last piece of equipment to complete her disguise as a Pinzochera. The large leather scarsella purse, stuffed with small coins, had to hang from her girdle on one side (her florins and small jewels, gifts from her godfather and cousin Brigida, were safely tucked hidden inside the lining of her serge mantle), and on the other side she hung her rosary.

As she tied that rosary to her waist, it never crossed her mind that more than just the clothes would be needed to pass as a devout lay sister: her whole proud, upright bearing and her sad, sharp gaze was nothing like the meek women who kept their eyes bowed and their lips moving in silent, constant prayer. Her inexperience kept her from picturing all the small distant details of the journey ahead, and her proud courage shut out all forebodings of danger or insult. She did not know any other Florentine woman had ever done exactly what she was setting out to do: unhappy wives often took refuge with friends, or fled to a convent, she knew, but both paths were impossible for her. She had carved a new life for herself: she would travel all the way to Venice, to the most learned woman in the world, Cassandra Fedele, and ask her how a woman of education could support herself in a lonely, honourable life there.

She was not daunted by the practical difficulties of the road, or the dark uncertainty waiting for her at the end. Her life could never be happy again, but it must not, could not, be ignoble.
