"Good day, Messer Domenico," Nello greeted the first of the two men entering the shop, offering a silent nod to his companion. "You arrive at the perfect moment, like cheese grated just for macaroni. Ah, you are in a rush—eager to be shaved without delay. *Ecco!* And what a morning it is for heavy thoughts. Florence orphaned, the very pivot of Italy torn away, heaven itself bewildered as to its next move. *Oimè!* Yet, well enough; the sun marches steadily toward dinner. As I was saying, you come like cheese ready for the pot. This young stranger seeks an honorable merchant to advance funds against a ring of value. If I had counted every goldsmith and money-lender in Florence upon my fingers, I could not have found a finer name than Menico Cennini. Besides, he deals in other wares you require—Greek learning and youthful eyes, a double instrument printers are perpetually in need of."

The grave elderly man, son of Bernardo Cennini, who two decades prior had heard of the German printing process and cast his own types in Florence, remained in lathered silence and passivity while Nello showered this discourse upon him, yet he turned a slow, sidelong gaze upon the stranger.

"This fine youth possesses unlimited Greek, Latin, or Italian at your service," Nello continued, favoring ample paraphrase. "He is a wonder of juvenile learning comparable to Francesco Filelfo or our incomparable Poliziano. A second Guarino, too, though he suffered the misfortune of shipwreck, doubtless losing a store of precious manuscripts that might have corrected even your most accurate editions, Domenico. Fortunately, he has rescued a few gems of rare value. His name is—you stated your name, Messer, was—?"

"Tito Melema," the stranger replied, slipping the ring from his finger and presenting it to Cennini, whom Nello, no less rapid with his razor than with his tongue, had now released from the shaving cloth.

Meanwhile, the man who had entered with the goldsmith—a tall figure of about fifty, sporting a short trimmed beard, an old felt hat, and a threadbare mantle—had kept his eye fixed on the Greek. Now, he spoke abruptly.

"Young man, I am painting a picture of Sinon deceiving old Priam, and I should be glad of your face for my Sinon, if you would grant me a sitting."

Tito Melema started, looking around with a pale astonishment that seemed like a sudden accusation. But Nello left him no time to flounder for an answer. "Piero," the barber declared, "you are the most extraordinary compound of humors and fancies ever packed into human skin. What trick will you play with the fine visage of this young scholar to make it suit your traitor? Ask him rather to turn his eyes upward, and you may make a Saint Sebastian of him that will draw troops of devout women; or, if you are in a classical vein, place myrtle about his curls and make him a young Bacchus, or rather a Phoebus Apollo, for his face is as warm and bright as a summer morning. It made me his friend in the space of a single 'credo.'"

"Ay, Nello," the painter replied, speaking with abrupt pauses, "and if your tongue could cease its everlasting chirping long enough for your understanding to consider the matter, you would see that you have just revealed why this face suits my traitor. A perfect traitor must possess a face upon which vice writes no marks—lips capable of lying with a dimpled smile, eyes of such agate-like brightness and depth that no infamy can dull them, cheeks that rise from murder without looking haggard. I say not this young man is a traitor; I mean, he has a face that would make him the more perfect traitor if he possessed the heart of one. Which is to say neither more nor less than that he has a beautiful face, informed with rich young blood, that will be nourished sufficiently by food and keep its color without much help from virtue. He may harbor the heart of a hero alongside it; I aver nothing to the contrary. Ask Domenico there if lapidaries can always tell a gem by sight alone. And now, I shall stuff tow in my ears, for your chatter and the bells together are more than I can endure. Say no more to me, but trim my beard."

With these final words, Piero (known as "di Cosimo" from his master, Cosimo Rosselli) drew out two bits of tow, stuffed them into his ears, and seated himself before Nello. The barber shrugged his shoulders and cast a grimacing look of intelligence at the Greek, as if to signal, "A whimsical fellow, you perceive! Everyone takes his speeches as mere jests."

Tito, who had stood transfixed with his long dark eyes resting on the unknown man who had addressed him so equivocally, seemed recalled to his self-command by Piero's change of position. Satisfied with the explanation, he once again gave his attention to Cennini, who presently remarked,

"This is a curious and valuable ring, young man. This intaglio of the fish with the crested serpent above it, set in the black stratum of the onyx—or rather nicolo—is well shown by the surrounding blue of the upper stratum. The ring undoubtedly has a history?" Cennini added, looking up keenly at the young stranger.

"Yes, indeed," Tito replied, meeting the scrutiny frankly. "The ring was found in Sicily, and I have learned from those who busy themselves with gems and sigils that both the stone and intaglio possess virtue to make the wearer fortunate, especially at sea, and also to restore to him whatever he may have lost. But," he continued, smiling, "though I have worn it constantly since I left Greece, it has not made me altogether fortunate at sea, you perceive, unless I am to count escape from drowning as sufficient proof of its virtue. It remains to be seen whether my lost chests will come to light; but to lose no chance of such a result, Messer, I pray you only to hold the ring for a short space as pledge for a small sum far beneath its value. I will redeem it as soon as I can dispose of certain other gems secured within my doublet, or indeed as soon as I can earn something by any scholarly employment, if I may be so fortunate as to meet with such."

"That may be seen, young man, if you will come with me," Cennini said. "My brother Pietro, who is a better judge of scholarship than I, will perhaps be able to supply you with a task that may test your capabilities. Meanwhile, take back your ring until I can hand you the necessary florins, and, if it pleases you, come along with me."

"Yes, yes," Nello interjected. "Go with Messer Domenico; you cannot go in better company. He was born under the constellation that grants a man skill, riches, and integrity, whatever that constellation may be—which is of less consequence because babies cannot choose their own horoscopes, and indeed, if they could, there might be an inconvenient rush of infants at particular epochs. Besides, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has shown that your horoscopes are all a nonsensical dream—which is the less troublesome opinion. *Addio! bel giovane!* Do not forget to return to me."

"No fear of that," Tito said, beckoning a farewell as he turned his bright face toward the door. "You are to do me a great service; that is the most positive security for your seeing me again."

"Say what thou wilt, Piero," Nello murmured as the young stranger disappeared. "I shall never look at such an exterior as that without taking it as a sign of a lovable nature. Why, thou wilt say next that Leonardo, whom thou art always raving about, ought to have made his Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But thou art as deaf as the top of Mount Morello with that accursed tow in thy ears. Well, well: I shall get a little more of this young man's history from him before I take him to Bardo Bardi."
