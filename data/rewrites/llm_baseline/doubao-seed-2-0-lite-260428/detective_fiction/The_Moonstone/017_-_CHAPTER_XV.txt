Sergeant Cuff said not another word for long stretches, his face tight with unspoken trains of thought, until we stepped into the dense fir plantation that cuts the path to the stretch of shore known locally as the Shivering Sand. There he roused himself like a man who’d settled on a course of action, and turned to speak to me plainly.

“Mr. Betteredge, you’ve been kind enough to row your oar in my investigation, and I’ve no doubt you’ll be of real help before this night is out. There’s no sense in us dancing around the truth any longer. I’ll go first. You’re determined not to hand over any information that could harm Rosanna Spearman, aren’t you? Because she’s been a good girl to the household, and you pity her deep down. Those are kind, honorable instincts, and they do you immense credit—but they’re wasted here. Rosanna isn’t in the slightest bit of trouble. Not even if I laid out evidence linking her directly to the Moonstone’s disappearance as plain as the nose on your face.”

“Are you saying my lady won’t prosecute?” I asked.

“I’m saying your lady can’t prosecute,” the Sergeant corrected. “Rosanna Spearman is nothing more than a tool in someone else’s hands. We’re going to keep her completely unharmed, to protect the person pulling the strings.”

He spoke with the earnestness of a man who had no doubt of his own facts. Even so, a uneasy little feeling niggled at the back of my mind. “Can’t you name that other person?” I pressed.

“Can’t you, Mr. Betteredge?”

“No.”

Sergeant Cuff stopped dead in his tracks, and looked me over with a sort of melancholy pity. “I always take a soft spot for human foolishness, you see. Right now, I’m feeling particularly soft towards you. Just as you’re feeling particularly soft towards Rosanna Spearman, aren’t you? Tell me—has she gotten a full new set of linen clothing lately?”

I had no clue why he’d randomly slipped that odd question in. I saw no harm to Rosanna in telling the truth, so I answered that she’d arrived at our house with barely any linen to her name, and Lady Verinder had given her a full new set less than a fortnight prior, as a reward for her good conduct.

“This world is a wretched place, isn’t it?” the Sergeant sighed. “Human life is just a target, and misfortune never misses a shot. If it weren’t for that new outfit, we’d have found a stained nightgown or petticoat in Rosanna’s things days ago, and had the whole case locked down. You follow my meaning, don’t you? You’ve questioned the servants yourself, and you know the two maids who loitered outside Rosanna’s door spied on her. You must have guessed what she was up to the day she faked being taken ill? It’s as plain as that strip of fading light peeking through the trees at the end of the path. Last Thursday morning, that fool Superintendent Seegrave pointed the fresh paint smear on the door out to all the female staff. Rosanna realized at once her own clothes had brushed against that wet paint. She snuck back to her room, found the stain on her nightgown or petticoat, faked a sudden illness, slipped off to the town, bought fabric and thread, and sewed a whole new replacement garment alone in her room that night. She lit a small fire not to burn the stained one—she knew the two maids hovering outside her door would smell smoke and notice missing fabric. She only used it to dry and iron the new, wrung-out nightgown, while she hid the stained one, most likely under her clothes. Right now, she’s on that isolated stretch of beach ahead of us, planning to get rid of that stained garment for good. I traced her this evening down to your fishing village, to one small cottage we’ll likely have to stop at before we head home. She stayed inside for quite a while, and came out with something tucked under her cloak. A woman’s cloak is a very charitable item, you know—it hides a multitude of sins. I saw her set off walking north along the coast after she left that cottage. Is this stretch of shore considered a fine bit of seaside scenery, Mr. Betteredge?”

I answered “Yes” as shortly as I could manage.

“Tastes differ,” Sergeant Cuff said. “From my line of work’s point of view, it’s the ugliest stretch of coast I’ve ever seen. If you’re trailing someone along it, and they turn around, there’s not a scrap of cover to hide behind for miles. I had a choice: arrest Rosanna on suspicion this very evening, or leave her to her little game long enough to follow where she leads. For reasons I won’t bore you with, I decided I’d make any sacrifice rather than alert that unnamed person behind this theft tonight. I came back to the house to ask you to bring me to the north end of the beach by a back loop. Sand, if you ask me, is one of the best detectives alive—its prints tell you everything. If we don’t run straight into Rosanna by sneaking up on her this way, the footprints in the sand will tell us exactly what she’s been up to, if the fading light holds out long enough. Here’s the sand now. If you’ll excuse the suggestion, keep quiet for a bit, and let me go first.”

If there’s such a thing as detective fever sold at the apothecary, I had it full-bore by then. Sergeant Cuff picked his way between the sand hillocks down to the tideline, and I followed close behind, my heart thudding hard in my chest, waiting to see what happened next.

I realized then I was standing almost exactly where I’d spoken to Rosanna Spearman only a few weeks prior, the day Mr. Franklin had come striding out from behind the sand dunes to surprise us on his arrival from London. My mind wandered against my will to that conversation. I could almost feel her small, work-worn hand slip into mine, squeeze gratefully, thanking me for speaking kindly to her. I could almost hear her telling me the Shivering Sand seemed to pull her towards it against her will, every time she left the house. I could almost see her face light up the second she spotted Franklin’s tall form coming towards us. My spirits sank lower and lower, and when I looked around to shake myself out of the daze, the sight of the empty, desolate bay only made me more uneasy.

The last of the evening light was fading fast, and a thick, terrible stillness hung over the whole place. The swell of the deep ocean rolling over the great hidden sandbank outside the bay made no sound at all. The inner sea lay dim and glassy, without a single breath of wind to ruffle its surface. Patches of pale yellow, slimy ooze floated on the dead water. Scum and slime glinted faintly where the last of the dying light caught them on the two long rocky spits jutting out north and south into the sea. It was just past turn of the tide, and even as I stood there waiting, the broad, flat surface of the quicksand began to dimple and quiver—the only moving thing in the whole horrid, empty landscape.

Sergeant Cuff jumped a little the second he spotted the shiver running across the sand. He stared at it for a full minute, then turned and walked back to me.

“Treacherous piece of ground, this, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “And there’s no sign of Rosanna Spearman anywhere on this beach, no matter where I look.”

He led me down closer to the tideline, and I saw for myself that his footprints and mine were the only marks stamped into the sand.

“Which direction is the fishing village, from where we’re standing now?” Sergeant Cuff asked.

“Cobb’s Hole,” I told him, that being the village’s name, “is almost due south of us.”

“I saw Rosanna walking north along the shore from Cobb’s Hole this evening,” the Sergeant said. “Which means she was walking straight towards this spot. Is Cobb’s Hole on the other side of that headland there? Can we get to it along the beach now that the tide’s low?”

I said yes to both questions.

“Then let’s move quickly,” the Sergeant said. “I want to find the spot where she came off the shore before it gets fully dark.”

We’d walked maybe two hundred yards towards Cobb’s Hole when Sergeant Cuff suddenly dropped to his knees on the sand, looking for all the world like he’d been seized by a sudden religious mania to say his prayers.

“Maybe your seaside scenery does have its uses after all,” he remarked. “Here are a woman’s footprints, Mr. Betteredge. Let’s assume they’re Rosanna’s until we find evidence to the contrary. Notice how muddled they are? Purposely muddled, I’d say. That poor girl understands the detective value of sand as well as I do. But she was in too much of a hurry to scrub the marks out fully, wasn’t she? Look here—one clear footprint walking away from Cobb’s Hole, and another walking back towards it. Isn’t that the toe of her boot pointing straight to the water’s edge? And there, two heel marks further down, right at the tideline? I don’t want to upset you, but I’m afraid Rosanna is far sneakier than we gave her credit for. It looks like she was determined to get to the spot we just came from, without leaving a single trace anyone could follow her by. Let’s guess she waded through the water from that point here, all the way to that rocky ledge behind us, then waded back the same way, and only stepped back up onto dry sand at the point those two heel marks are still left exposed. That lines up perfectly with my earlier thought that she had something heavy hidden under her cloak when she left the cottage. No, not something to throw away in the quicksand—because if that was all she wanted, she wouldn’t have gone to all this trouble to hide exactly where she walked. She’s hiding something out there, not destroying it. I say we head to that cottage, and find out exactly what that something is.”

At that suggestion, my case of detective fever cooled right off. “You don’t need me,” I said. “What use am I here?”

“The longer I know you, Mr. Betteredge,” the Sergeant said, “the more virtues I uncover. Modesty—why, modesty is rarer than hen’s teeth in this world, and you’ve got heaps of it! If I turn up at that cottage alone, no one there will answer a single question I ask. If I turn up with you, a well-respected local man everyone knows, the doors and tongues both fling wide open. Doesn’t that make sense to you?”

I didn’t have a snappy retort ready, so I asked him to describe the cottage he was talking about to buy time.

The moment he did, I recognized it. It belonged to a fisherman named Yolland, who lived there with his wife and two grown children, a son and a daughter. If you cast your mind back, I mentioned once before that Rosanna Spearman sometimes varied her walks to the Shivering Sand by stopping off to visit friends in Cobb’s Hole. Those friends were the Yollands—hardworking, respectable people who were a credit to the whole village. She’d gotten to know their daughter Lucy, who had a misshapen foot and everyone locally called Limping Lucy. The two girls, both living with a disability that set them apart from everyone else, had struck up a quiet, loyal friendship. It seemed to me then that the Sergeant tracing Rosanna to their cottage was proof she’d just gone somewhere she was welcome, where no one would pry into her business, entirely innocently. Going with the Sergeant wouldn’t hurt her—it would give me a chance to vouch for her, and clear any silly suspicions. I told him I’d come along.

We walked on towards Cobb’s Hole, following the faint traces of Rosanna’s footprints in the sand for as long as the last of the light held out.

When we knocked on the door, the fisherman and his son were out at sea in their boat, and Limping Lucy, who was always weak and easily tired, was resting upstairs in bed. Good Mrs. Yolland met us alone in the kitchen. The second she heard Sergeant Cuff was a famous London detective, she slammed a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean clay pipes down on the table, and stared at him like she could never get enough of a look.

I sat quiet in the corner, waiting to see how the Sergeant would bring the conversation around to Rosanna. He was as roundabout as ever, even more than usual. I still can’t tell you exactly how he did it, but he started the chat talking about the royal family, the Primitive Methodists, and the sky-high price of cod that winter, and slowly, in that quiet, gloomy, roundabout way of his, he steered the conversation to the missing Moonstone, the spitefulness of our head housekeeper, and how horribly the rest of the maids treated poor Rosanna Spearman. By the time he was done explaining that he was investigating the theft partly to find the diamond, and partly to clear Rosanna’s name of all the unfair, cruel gossip the other servants spread about her, Mrs. Yolland was fully convinced Sergeant Cuff was Rosanna’s very best friend in the world, and she was pressing him to pour himself a big drink of gin to refresh his spirits.

I sat there enjoying the whole exchange like it was a stage play, fully convinced the Sergeant was wasting his breath. The great Sergeant Cuff was so patient, trying this angle and that, firing off random questions hoping to hit a useful fact. Every single thing he found out only went to prove Rosanna was good and kind, nothing against her, no matter how hard he tried—Mrs. Yolland was chattering nineteen to the dozen, spilling every little detail about the girl to him. He made his last play when we checked our watches and stood up to leave.

“I’ll wish you good night, ma’am,” the Sergeant said. “I’ll only add, before I go, that Rosanna Spearman has a sincere well-wisher in me. But oh dear, she’ll never get on staying in that house. My advice to her? Leave as soon as she can.”

“Bless your heart, she’s *already* planning to leave!” Mrs. Yolland cried.

I pricked up my ears at that. It was strange, to say the least, that she hadn’t said a word to me or Lady Verinder about it. A little spark of doubt niggled at me: that random throwaway line of the Sergeant’s had landed right on target. I started to wonder if tagging along with him was as harmless as I’d thought. It was all well and good for a detective to twist the truth to get a simple cottage woman talking, but as a good Protestant, I knew the father of lies was the Devil—and mischief and the Devil are never far apart. I tried to hustle the Sergeant out the door, but he sat right back down again, and asked for one tiny sip of gin to fortify him for the walk home. Mrs. Yolland sat across from him, and poured him his nip. I got all the way to the threshold, feeling deeply unsettled, and said I had to go on ahead—but my feet wouldn’t move.

“So she’s planning to leave soon, is she?” the Sergeant said. “What’s she going to do when she does? It’s sad, isn’t it? That poor creature hasn’t got a single friend in the world outside of you and me.”

“Oh, but she does!” Mrs. Yolland told him. “She came in this evening, like I said, sat and talked with Lucy and me for a bit, then asked if she could go up to Lucy’s room alone. It’s the only room in the whole cottage that has pen and paper. ‘I want to write a letter to a friend,’ she said, ‘and I can’t do it back at the house, the servants are all peeking and prying over my shoulder every minute.’ I don’t know who she was writing to, but judging by how long she stayed up there, it must have been a very long letter. When she came back down, I offered her a postage stamp—but she didn’t have the letter with her, and she wouldn’t take the stamp. She’s a quiet little thing, our Rosanna, never one to show anyone her business. But she’s got a friend somewhere, mark my words, and that’s where she’s going when she leaves.”

“Right away?” the Sergeant pressed.

“Just as soon as she can,” Mrs. Yolland said.

I stepped back into the room then. As the head of Lady Verinder’s household, I couldn’t let this wild talk about one of my maids quitting go on without saying something. “You must be mistaken about Rosanna,” I said. “If she was planning to leave her post, she would have come to me first and told me.”

“Mistaken?” Mrs. Yolland cried. “Why, she bought travel things off me only an hour ago, right here in this kitchen! That reminds me—” she said, digging into her pocket “—I’ve been meaning to give her back her money. Are either of you going to see her when you get back to the house?”

“I’ll be happy to pass a message along to her,” Sergeant Cuff cut in, before I could open my mouth.

Mrs. Yolland tipped a handful of silver shillings and sixpences out of her pocket, and counted them out slowly, carefully, in the palm of her hand. She held the pile out to the Sergeant, looking very reluctant to let go of it.

“Would you mind giving this back to Rosanna, with my love?” she said. “She insisted on paying me for the little trinkets she bought this evening, and money’s tight in our house, I won’t lie. But I don’t feel right taking that poor girl’s little savings. And my husband would have my head tomorrow, when he gets back from sea, if he heard I took her money. Tell her the things she bought are a gift from me. And don’t leave the money on the table, now,” she said, suddenly plonking it down in front of the Sergeant like it burnt her fingers. “Don’t. Times are hard, and flesh is weak. I might be tempted to stuff it back in my pocket before you leave.”

“Come on,” I said. “I can’t wait any longer. I have to get back to the house.”

“I’ll be right behind you,” Sergeant Cuff said.

I walked to the door a second time, and for the second time, my feet wouldn’t cross the threshold.
