A silly child, quivering with joy, casts its little mimic fishing-line baited with loadstone into the salt ocean, dreaming of a bowl of toys. So are men's hopes. Eight months after the family's arrival at Offendene, in the late heat of June, a rumor rippled through the countryside, igniting a fire of speculative delight that burned equally bright among the corn-factors, brewers, and horse-dealers of Wanchester. To the blacksmith of Diplow, it signaled a turning tide; to the wives of laborers, a sudden opening for their nimble sons; to the farmers, a glimmer of hope for a fairer market for their hay and straw. Yet for those of higher station, the rumor promised far more than economic relief; it conjured a floating, indeterminate vision of matrimony.

The news was specific: Diplow Hall, Sir Hugo Mallinger's estate, had ceased its painful, wall-eyed silence regarding its fine elms and beeches, its lilied pools, and its deer-speckled acres. It was being prepared for a tenant, to be inhabited in fitting style throughout the summer and the hunting season. Not by Sir Hugo, however, but by his nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt, the presumptive heir. Fortune had smiled upon young Grandcourt doubly: the baronetcy awaited him through his father, while his mother had infused his blood with a baronial streak, promising a peerage should certain distant relatives fade into memory. Nature, in her uneven allotment, grants the tuft only to the male bird, yet here, the potential for a shared crown hung in the air. A wife for such a man, currently unchosen, was a prize to be coveted by anyone with a sympathetic interest in securing a well-provided-for woman.

Some might deem it incredulous that matrimonial prospects should be constructed on mere report, dismissing such speculation as the gall of the idle. They would claim their minds are not so unbridled, that human nature abhors such fallacies. But this narrative concerns not humanity in general, but a select corner of Wessex, where reputations were unimpeached and social circles intertwined with the highest ranks. Consider the Arrowpoints at Quetcham. With a fortune sufficient to leave their daughter Catherine half a million pounds, they possessed no sordid motives, yet their affectionate anxiety for her position—having rejected Lord Slogan, an Irish peer whose estate lacked drainage and population—led them to wonder if Mr. Grandcourt was handsome, sound, virtuous, or at least reformed. Was he a liberal-conservative of the right degree? His succession to the title was desired, provided it did not require the death of anyone.

Similarly, Mr. Gascoigne, though a clergyman, felt the weight of parental and guardian anxiety acutely. He and his wife, Mrs. Gascoigne, had long felt overburdened by the management of young creatures that defied bit and bridle, resisting any metaphor of judicious advice. Naturally, they did not broadcast every thought concerning Grandcourt's advent. Openness on such subjects is rarely prudent; one risks having one's ingenious theories forestalled or one's differing views on the fixed stars used as grounds for censure. Mr. Gascoigne did not inquire of Mr. Arrowpoint for trustworthy sources on Grandcourt as a husband for a charming girl, nor did Mrs. Arrowpoint whisper to Mrs. Davilow that if the possible peer sought a wife nearby, Catherine was the only reasonable prospect, though she would refuse unless secured for happiness. Even to his wife, the rector remained silent on the matrimonial implications, anticipating that Grandcourt might see Gwendolen at the upcoming Archery Meeting, though Mrs. Gascoigne's mind raced in that direction with greater fervor. She had once exclaimed to her sister, "It would be a mercy, Fanny, if that girl were well married!" Mrs. Davilow, sensing a critique of her darling in the fervor of the wish, withheld an audible reply, thinking inwardly, "You will not get her to marry for your pleasure," the mild mother growing saucy when identifying with her daughter's autonomy.

To her husband, Mrs. Gascoigne remarked, "I hear Mr. Grandcourt has got two places of his own, but he comes to Diplow for the hunting. It is to be hoped he will set a good example in the neighborhood. Have you heard what sort of a young man he is, Henry?" Mr. Gascoigne confessed he had heard nothing, or rather, he was disposed to ignore the gossip of his male acquaintances. He deemed it futile to investigate the past of a young man whose birth, wealth, and leisure rendered many habits venial. Whatever Grandcourt had done, he had not ruined himself; indeed, in the realm of gambling, a man who possesses the strength of mind to cease when he has ruined others is a reformed character. This was merely an illustration; Mr. Gascoigne had not heard Grandcourt was a gambler. One cannot pronounce him singular for feeling that a landed proprietor with noble blood need not be subjected to the suspicious inquiry reserved for a reformed character offering himself as a butler. Where a man can afford to do without reformation, it is hardly anything but genuine. Furthermore, it was uncertain if Grandcourt required reform more than other youths of thirty-five, and the significance of his past must yield to the determination of his present self.

Mrs. Davilow, though unwilling to voice her sister's pregnant remarks, could not remain inwardly indifferent to an advent promising a brilliant lot for Gwendolen. Speculation on "what may be" arises naturally, inevitably forming images when unknown persons are mentioned. First, Mr. Grandcourt appeared to her as a handsome, accomplished, excellent young man, perfectly suited as a husband. Then, the image shifted: would Gwendolen be satisfied? There was no knowing what would meet that girl's taste or touch her affections; it might be something other than excellence. Thus, the perfect suitor gave way to a fluctuating combination of qualities imagined to win Gwendolen's heart. In the difficulty of pinpointing the exact combination, Mrs. Davilow told herself, "It would not signify about her being in love, if she would only accept the right person." For whatever marriage had been for herself, how could she desire it less for her daughter? The difference lay in her own misfortunes; she never dared dwell much on the desirableness of marriage with Gwendolen, fearing an answer akin to that of the future Madame Roland, when her gentle mother urged the acceptance of a suitor, saying, "Tu seras heureuse, ma chère." "Oui, maman, comme toi."

Regarding the problematic Mr. Grandcourt, Mrs. Davilow would least of all willingly drop a hint of the aerial castle-building she felt ashamed of, for such a hint might give an adverse poise to Gwendolen's thoughts, making her detest the desirable husband beforehand. Since the scene after poor Rex's farewell visit, the mother had felt a new sense of peril in touching the mystery of her child's feelings, rashly determining what constituted her welfare, which she could conceive of only in the shape of marriage.

Yet the discussion of Gwendolen's attire for the Archery Meeting proved a relevant diversion. When it was decided that pale green, a touch of color on her white cashmere, was superior for her complexion—a choice confirmed as Gwendolen tried a feather in her hat before the mirror—Mrs. Davilow felt her ears tingle when Gwendolen suddenly threw herself into the attitude of drawing her bow, speaking with a look of comic enjoyment: "How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting—all thinking of Mr. Grandcourt! And they have not a shadow of a chance."

Mrs. Davilow lacked the presence of mind to answer immediately. Gwendolen turned quickly, saying wickedly, "Now you know they have not, mamma. You and my uncle and aunt—you all intend him to fall in love with me." Piqued into a little stratagem, Mrs. Davilow replied, "Oh, my dear, that is not so certain. Miss Arrowpoint has charms which you have not."

"I know, but they demand thought. My arrow will pierce him before he has time for thought. He will declare himself my slave—I shall send him round the world to bring me back the wedding ring of a happy woman—in the meantime all the men who are between him and the title will die of different diseases—he will come back Lord Grandcourt—but without the ring—and fall at my feet. I shall laugh at him—he will rise in resentment—I shall laugh more—he will call for his steed and ride to Quetcham, where he will find Miss Arrowpoint just married to a needy musician, Mrs. Arrowpoint tearing her cap off, and Mr. Arrowpoint standing by. Exit Lord Grandcourt, who returns to Diplow, and, like M. Jabot, *change de linge*."

Was ever any young witch like this? One thought of hiding things from her, of sitting upon a secret and looking innocent, only to discover by the corner of her eye that it was exactly five pounds ten shillings one was sitting on! As well turn the key to keep out the damp! By dint of divination, she likely already knew more of Mr. Grandcourt than anyone else. This idea prompted a question in Mrs. Davilow's mind, arising without apparent reason save the faculty of speech and the uncertainty of what to do with it. "Why, what kind of a man do you imagine him to be, Gwendolen?"
