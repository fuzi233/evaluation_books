Time is never a fair measure of how much life shifts, not really. The sun’s long journey across the sky can no more tell you what a soul has lived through than the acreage of a field can reveal the tender, fierce growths pushing up under the grass. A man might wander south, stumble over an old bone, and walk away with a whole new framework for anatomy; head east, and unearth a linguistic secret that rewrites everything we know about the history of races. He might lead an expedition that opens whole new continents, leave half his body maimed, and walk a year of heroism and endurance that would fill a whole epic poem – and come home to find his neighbors still grousing over the exact same parish grievance, the same elderly gentleman still treading the pavement muttering to himself, shaking his head at the same loud butcher’s boy, pausing at the same shop window to stare at the same old prints.

The fastest human thought runs as fast as a greyhound, after all, while the slowest creeps like a limpet, seeming to stick fast to its rock for months before you spot the tiny, quiet progress it has made. That gulf between speeds had never been clearer than in the twelve months that turned the bright, glittering, self-assured Gwendolen Harleth of the Archery Meeting into a broken, penitent young woman, burning to confess how unworthy she felt of any care, any kindness, that she would once have laughed to doubt she deserved. And all the while, the quiet little parish of Pennicote barely shifted at all.

The rectory was just as warm and pleasant as it had always been. Red and pink peonies still bloomed thick on the lawn, hollyhocks towered bright along the hedgerows, and the rector still carried his cheerful, unshakable faith that his patrons would reward his steady diligence, even when they never bothered to check what work he did. The only new project he’d taken on was writing two unsigned ecclesiastical articles, which everyone except the handful of patrons he’d sent special copies to attributed to far more senior clergymen. They never read the pieces, of course, but he didn’t mind: he scribbled little marginal notes on his own copies to make them more fun to lend to friends, and was perfectly happy that the Archdeacon and all his superiors had no complaints about his arguments. It was such peaceful, gentle authorship, no harsh criticism breathing down his neck, no bitter Dantesque leanings from endless toil – it gave him far more quiet satisfaction than writing an entire *Divina Commedia* ever could.

Best of all, his son Rex had come back to him, and that joy outweighed every penny of the eighteen hundred a year the family had lost when their fortunes crashed. No one could miss that the heartbreak of his first love had changed him a little, deep down, but that change paled next to the fierce, new ambition that had woken in him the day the family fell on hard times. The rector even liked to pretend that old disappointment had just been excess moisture burning off, the final step of baking that made his boy’s character solid, perfect. Rex had brought his sister Anna home for a summer visit, and while he laughed and teased his younger brothers and sisters as easily as he ever had, he stuck to his scholar’s routines even on holiday: up before dawn, shutting himself away in his study long after the sun went down, working his way steadily through a fixed course of study.

“You don’t regret picking law as your profession, do you, Rex?” his father asked him one afternoon.

“There’s no other profession I’d choose before it,” Rex said, eyes bright. “I want to end my life as a first-rate judge, help write a whole new code of laws. I’ve reversed that old famous saying – give me the work of making the laws, and let whoever wants to write the songs do it.”

“I expect you’ll have to wade through heaps of useless rubbish for your work, though, that’s the worst of it,” the rector said.

“I don’t think law rubbish is any worse than any other kind,” Rex laughed. “It’s far better than all the garbage novels people stuff their heads with, that rots their wits. Some of our sharpest, wittiest men were lawyers, after all. Learning to look at every problem as a case, weigh every piece of evidence, feels far more useful than drifting through a mess of random odds and ends that don’t add up to anything. And from a higher view? The way law grew, the foundations it’s built on, that’s the most fascinating kind of philosophy and history there is. Of course there will be drudgery, tedious work that makes me want to scream. But no great prize in life comes easy. I see that now.”

The rector grinned, warm with fatherly pride he tried not to show too openly. “The surest sign a man will succeed at his work is that he thinks it’s the finest work in the world. That’s how it is when you love what you do. Brewitt the blacksmith said to me just the other day that his apprentice hated the trade – and ‘yet sir,’ he said, ‘what’s a young fellow supposed to want if he doesn’t like blacksmithing?’”

It was only to his wife that he said, firm and sure, “Rex will be a distinguished man, Nancy. I’m as certain of it as Paley’s father was that his son would make a name for himself.”

“Was Paley an old bachelor?” Mrs. Gascoigne asked, a little absently. The rector waved the question off, not remembering that trivial detail, and she felt silly for speaking out of turn.

That same slow, sun-drenched quiet wrapped around the group that had left the faded grandeur of Offendene behind to move into the small white cottage a mile away, tucked thick with evergreens, that all the villagers called Jodson’s. Mrs. Davilow’s gentle face only had a little more soft sadness around the eyes, a few more silver strands in her hair, after all the trials of the last year, and her four younger daughters had bloomed brighter now they were no longer living in Gwendolen’s shadow. Their old housekeeper Jocosa still went about her work with that calm, neutral air, as if all the glories and pleasures of the world were made for people who didn’t have to work for a living.

That July afternoon, the narrow, low-ceilinged drawing-room was crowded and warm: the lattice windows were wide open, letting in the scent of monthly roses, the soft hum of bees in the garden, and the rare, distant clop of hooves that only made the silence feel sweeter. Anna had been chattering nonstop about her life in London, the Meyricks, and their extraordinary Jewish friends, and the younger cousins had plied her with endless questions about Gwendolen’s grand new house in Grosvenor Square, what she’d been saying, what other people said about her. The girls had all been wildly curious ever since they’d heard Gwendolen and Grandcourt set off on a yachting trip around the Mediterranean: she’d sent two letters, one right before they sailed, and one from Marseilles gushing about how elegant the cabins were, how much she was loving it, and that she’d send back a long diary full of all the little details soon. Their adventures had even been written up in the newspaper, so the book-mad Isabel had already woven half a dozen corsair subplots into her own private daydream of Gwendolen’s grand Mediterranean voyage.

But no one ever brought up Gwendolen when Rex was in the room, per the family’s unspoken rule, and that afternoon they’d all been teasing each other about how absurdly exotic the idea of real, living Jews felt to them, like a weird race straight out of Pliny’s old natural history books. Bertha couldn’t imagine what Jews believed these days, Miss Merry was sure Mirah and her brother could never be argued into the right faith, and Alice declared she was certain she couldn’t bear to be around them at all, until Mrs. Davilow gently corrected her, saying the wealthy Jewish families in high society in London and Paris were perfectly respectable, even if the poorer, unconverted ones were rather vulgar. The whole room had dissolved into laughter, Rex playfully exaggerating each girl’s silly theories about Mirah, Anna begging everyone to see he was only joking – when the maid came hurrying in, holding a letter for Mrs. Davilow, sent over by a running messenger from the rectory.

It had a telegram tucked inside. Mrs. Davilow read it once, then twice, her face draining of color, all the laughter dying dead in the room. No one dared speak. When she finally looked up, and saw all the young faces painted white with fear, she remembered they might be imagining something far worse than the news she’d just gotten.

Her sob was half relief, half horror. “My dears. Mr. Grandcourt –” She paused, swallowing hard. “Mr. Grandcourt is drowned.”

Rex shot to his feet as if someone had thrown a burning missile straight into his lap. His eyes flew straight to Anna, who was already staring at him, terrified the old wound of his love for Gwendolen had been torn open raw. But he forced himself to steady himself, even as Mrs. Davilow read out the note the rector had scrawled on the back of the telegram, saying he was ready to travel with her to Genoa at half past six that very night.

“Can I do anything, Aunt?” Rex said, his voice steadier than he felt. “Do you need me to carry a message to my father?”

“Yes, dear,” she said, already bustling to give Jocosa and Alice orders to pack her bags. “He’s so good to offer. Gwendolen is safe, thank God – but she must be so ill, so terribly upset. Tell your father I’ll be ready the second he is. I wouldn’t waste a single night traveling, I have to get to her.”

Rex and Anna hurried back to the rectory through the golden sunshine, not saying a single word to each other the whole walk. Anna was half mad with worry that he was hurting far worse than he let on, while Rex was fighting a wild, chaotic storm of thoughts that he knew was a sin against every bit of decency he had. When they reached the rectory gate, he stopped, his hands shaking. “Nannie, you go tell my father everything. If he needs me right away, come find me. I’ll just walk in the shrubbery for ten minutes. Only ten minutes.”

Who among us has never had that shameful, unbidden thought, the selfish flash of a possible better future for ourselves when we hear news of another’s great misfortune? The friend’s unexpected death that leaves you the unexpected heir, the boss’s sudden retirement that hands you the promotion you’ve been craving – that temptation makes speech, even prayer, a hard, deliberate fight to push away the greedy, insistent thoughts, until you’re left burning with shame, sick of your own selfishness.

Rex was burning with that shame right then. The second he’d heard the words Grandcourt is dead, a single, wild, shining thought had burst to life in his chest: Gwendolen is free.

The shame of it washed over him like cold, ugly light. Even the thought felt like he was loitering on a battlefield to strip the dead of their valuables. What right did he have to hope for anything right now, when the woman he’d loved for so long was out there somewhere, shattered by grief? He knew, even at the first spark of hope, that it was all useless anyway. Back when they were both young, their lives unburdened, she had turned him down flat, rejected his love like it was something unpleasant. Why on earth would her heart be open to him now, when she was a wealthy, celebrated widow, courted by every man of rank in the country? He was nothing but a struggling law student, still working his way up, not likely to make anything of himself for decades. He had nothing to offer her.

It felt like all the careful, hard work he’d done over the last year, building his new calm, his new ambition, had been undone by three simple words, leaving him back in the old throes of that hopeless, painful longing he’d thought he’d buried for good. His love for Gwendolen had never been a casual, fleeting fancy. It was that deep, ancient, clinging kind of love the old poets sang of – the kind that seeps into every corner of your soul, and stays there, even when you know the object of your love will never return it, even when she hurt you cruelly when you bared your heart to her. He’d thought he’d made his peace with it, that he could live a full, rich life even without the joy of having her. Now, he realized, his peace had been as fragile as the republican government of Florence, his heart nothing but an alarm bell sending chaos and noise ringing through every quiet part of him.

He told himself off, harsh and unforgiving. Stop being a coward. Behave like a man. She would never love you, and that doesn’t even matter right now. She’s grieving. The last thing she needs is you hovering around, selfishly hoping for something. The thought of being of use, of helping his father get ready for the journey, steadied him. He walked straight into the study, where the rector was packing his traveling desk.

“Can I help you, sir?” he said, his courage fully back now.

“Yes, my boy,” the rector said, looking up, and Rex began strapping his father’s railway rug for him. “While I’m gone, keep an eye on my letters, answer the ones that need answering, send me updates on everything. Dymock will run the parish perfectly well. You stay here with your mother, pop back and forth to the parish when you need to, until I get back – whenever that is.”

“You won’t be gone very long, I suppose?” Rex said, forcing himself to say her name out loud for the first time. “Will you be bringing Gwendolen back to England with you?”

“That depends,” his father said, taking the question as perfectly natural. “Her mother might want to stay in Genoa with her, then I’ll come right home. The telegram doesn’t give us many details, and it’s very worrying. But no doubt Grandcourt’s will left her very, very well provided for. I expect she’ll be splendidly looked after.”

“It must have been an awful shock for her,” Rex said, wincing a little as the first sharp stab of pain went through him. “I suppose he was a devoted husband.”

“Of course he was,” the rector said, firm and sure. “Very few men of his station would have stepped forward to marry her the way he did, when her family had lost all their money, and provided for her mother and sisters as generously as he did.”

Rex had never met Grandcourt. No one in the family had ever talked to him about how Gwendolen had run away to Leubronn to avoid his suit. All he knew was that Grandcourt had fallen madly in love with her, asked her to marry him when she was at her lowest, and been far kinder than most men would have been. He’d been a lucky man, Rex thought. He’d gotten the girl Rex had ached for, and he’d had months of happiness with her before he drowned.

Rex’s hands stilled on the strap for a second. He wondered, quiet and unspoken, if Gwendolen had ever truly loved Grandcourt back. Or if, somewhere deep in her heart, she’d still carried that same old quiet hatred of being wooed, of being desired by any man who wasn’t – well, wasn’t him.
