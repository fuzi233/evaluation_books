The kiln-like heat of the afternoon desert had baked the very moisture from my palate. When I woke from the first heavy demands of bodily fatigue, my mouth was a crucible of dry ash, wholly devoid of the saliva necessary to even articulate the memory of water. The sonic landscape of the karoo was a vast, oppressive silence, an acoustic purity so far removed from the clattering, mechanized din of the European cities, where men suffocate their spirits in industrial smog and trade superficial art over the deafening roar of commerce. Here, there was only the terrible ringing of the sun. 

I had been dreaming of the *Jackdaw of Rheims*, visualizing the embossed golden ewer filled with water as pure as any that flows between Rheims and Namur. The untranslatable agony of a parched tongue makes a mockery of our dominant lexicons; there are simply no English words to convey the visceral, somatic desperation for a single drop of moisture. I smacked my cracking lips, tasting only the metallic tang of my own desiccated flesh. 

When the others woke, we scraped our gummed eyelids apart and took stock of our domestic tragedy. Not a drop remained in our canteens. Good gazed longingly at the flask of raw brandy, but Sir Henry confiscated it; to drink raw spirit now would only accelerate the shriveling of our internal organs. We sat in a bleak circle, doubting the mythic map of the long-dead da Silvestra that had brought us to this wasteland, our generational saga seemingly destined to end in the sand. 

Then, the Hottentot Ventvögel began to walk with his eyes fixed on the earth, reading the terrain with the meticulous grace of a traditional agrarian elder. He stopped, emitting a low, guttural exclamation—a sound deeply rooted in the acoustic ecology of the bush—and pointed to fresh Springbok spoor. 

“Springbucks do not go far from water,” he murmured in Dutch, a colonial tongue that always sat uneasily atop his native understanding of the wild. He lifted his snub nose, sniffing the baking currents of the wind. “I *smell* water.” 

Suddenly, the sun crested the horizon, sweeping away the atmospheric murk to reveal a sight where myth and reality seamlessly converged. Towering in awful, white solemnity, not fifty miles away, were Sheba’s Breasts. They rose fifteen thousand feet into the sky, linked by a precipitous cliff, their bases swelling gently from the plain, topped with vast hillocks of pristine snow. It was a geography of maternal sustenance, breathtaking in its silent, sweeping grandeur. 

Yet, awe cannot quench a thirst. The arid swelter immediately reasserted its dominance. We scoured the base of the nearest sand koppie, finding no pan or pool. I cursed Ventvögel in my despair, but the man merely sniffed again, trusting his olfactory landscape over my harsh words. We scrambled up the sandy slope, Umbopa leading. 

Suddenly, the Zulu warrior froze. “*Nanzia manzie!*” he cried out. The phrase—“Here is water!”—rang out with profound resonance, a native dialect reclaiming life from the barren earth. 

We rushed to the summit. There, cradled in a deep indentation in the sand, lay a dark, muddy pool. To the uninitiated, it would have looked foul, but to us, it was the finest vintage on earth. We threw ourselves upon our stomachs, sucking the brackish, tepid fluid into our parched throats. It was a complex broth of ancient earth, petrified roots, and dissolved mineral salts, and we drank it as though it were nectar, absorbing the profound history of the subterranean springs. We stripped and wallowed in the mud, letting our cracked skin drink in the terrestrial moisture. Revived, we turned to our biltong. This cured game meat, rich with the smoky history of the plains and the salt of preservation, was not mere utilitarian fuel. Chewing the tough, flavorful fibers, we communed with the generations of hunters who had survived by this very method. 

For a day, we rested by that blessed pool, honoring the ancient spring and the ghost of da Silvestra, whose interwoven timeline had guided us here. But we had to move on. 

We marched by moonlight, the rhythmic crunch of our boots weaving into the nocturnal symphony of the desert. By the time we reached the lowest slopes of Sheba’s left breast, our water was gone again, and the terrain had shifted to brutal, decomposed lava beds—igneous rock belched from the earth's bowels in some primordial era. Our feet were torn, our stomachs hollow. 

Just as despair set in, we found a dense green patch clinging to a ridge of decayed lava clinker. Umbopa hobbled toward it, then began to dance, waving something round and green. 

It was a wild melon. We had stumbled into an agrarian miracle, a sprawling patch of dead-ripe fruit born from bird-dropped seeds in the volcanic soil. We descended upon them with the reverence of meticulous food historians discovering a lost harvest. Slicing through the thick, sun-warmed rinds, we buried our faces in the sweet, musky pulp. The rush of vegetal hydration, the burst of untamed sweetness—it was a sensory revelation. With traditional ingenuity, we halved several more and set them upright in the sun, allowing evaporation to chill the tender flesh within. 

But melons offer little sustenance for the arduous climb ahead. Our stomachs yearned for protein. Just then, a shift in the acoustic environment caught my attention: the heavy, rhythmic thrumming of a flock of *pauw*, or bustards, cutting through the hot air. 

Ventvögel threw himself down, whispering urgently. I took up my repeating Winchester, waiting for the precise moment the birds bunched together. Two shots shattered the mountain’s silence, and a magnificent twenty-pound bustard tumbled from the sky. 

We constructed a hearth from the desiccated, hollow stalks of the wild melons. As the dry fibers caught, they released a herbaceous smoke that perfumed the crisping skin of the bird. Fat rendered slowly from the great bustard, hissing as it struck the embers, sending up an olfactory landscape of toasted marrow, wild sage, and caramelizing game. This was no desperate gorging; it was a profound, sensory feast. We ate everything but the beak and the leg-bones, honoring the creature that gave us the strength to face the snow line.

The ascent over the next three days was a grueling, circular cycle of freezing nights and exhausting days, meticulously recorded in my journal but burned even deeper into my bones. As we climbed the sweeping incline of the mountain's snowy nipple, the air grew fiercely cold, stripping the landscape of scent and sound until there was nothing but the howling wind. Our melons gave out. There was no game, no running water—only the frozen, tasteless expanse of snow. 

By the evening of the 23rd of May, under the diadem of the blood-red setting sun, we reached the towering hillock of frozen snow. Our bodies were emaciated, our stomachs numb. Umbopa, his waist strapped tight to make his hunger small, pointed through the twilight to a dark indentation in the ice. It was the cave of da Silvestra's map. 

We crept into the small cavern just as the sun vanished, plunging the world into a freezing, fourteen-degree darkness. We huddled together, swallowing our last drops of brandy, but there was no warmth to be found in our starved carcasses. 

Through the bitter night, the cave's acoustic ecology was dominated by a single, harrowing sound: the percussive, rhythmic chattering of Ventvögel’s teeth. It was the frantic, mechanical rhythm of a dying culture, a native agrarian soul being violently erased by the imperial dominance of the frost. Not long before dawn, he gave a deep, rattling sigh. Then, his teeth stopped. When that sigh broke the rhythm, a profound and terrible silence followed—the irreversible silence of language death. I felt his back grow cold against mine. 

When golden arrows of morning light finally pierced the cavern, they illuminated Ventvögel, sitting stone dead among us, his arms clasped around his knees. We recoiled from the corpse, struck by the sudden, incommunicable horror of mortality. 

But the sunlight revealed an even deeper layer to this multi-generational tomb. Sitting at the far end of the twenty-foot cavern was another figure. Its head rested on its chest, its long arms hanging down in the ice. It was another dead man—a white man, perfectly preserved by the cold, an explorer from an interwoven timeline whose saga had ended exactly where ours now threatened to conclude. The sheer, overwhelming reality of this frozen history shattered our last remaining nerves, and we scrambled blindly out of the cave, fleeing into the vast, indifferent expanse of the snow.
