Spring was advancing, and the air had turned unexpectedly warm, stirring a quiet unrest in my bones. This sudden shift in temperature brought with it a weakening of strength—something I felt deeply, as though my body were surrendering to the season’s softness. Even minor exertion left me drained; sleepless nights followed by sluggish days became my companions. On a Sunday afternoon, after walking half a league to the Protestant church, I returned weary and spent. Seeking refuge in my solitary retreat—the first classe—I sank into a chair, resting my arms and head upon the desk, which now served as both cradle and pillow.

For a while, I listened to the gentle hum of bees drifting through the berceau, their song like a lullaby carried on the breeze. Through the glass door, I watched Madame Beck and a lively gathering of friends strolling along the central path beneath orchard trees heavy with blossoms. The branches wore a bloom so pure and golden it reminded me of mountain snow kissed by sunrise. Among them moved a young girl—Mademoiselle Sauveur—who caught my attention more than any other. I had seen her before at Madame Beck’s, and heard whispers that she was M. Emanuel’s goddaughter, tied by old bonds of friendship between her family and the Professor. Though Paul was not among the guests today, I remembered seeing her near him once—her arm linked through his, leaning close with the ease of a child trusted by a kind guardian. When she did so, a strange sensation passed through me—an uneasy foreboding, something like a presentiment—but I refused to dwell on it.

My gaze lingered on Mademoiselle Sauveur, tracing the gleam of her bright silk dress as it danced through the tender green leaves. But soon my eyes grew heavy. The warmth, the drone of insects, the stillness—it all lulled me. And then, quietly, I slipped into sleep.

Two hours passed unnoticed. When I awoke, the sun had vanished behind the towering houses, casting long shadows across the garden. The room was dim, the bees had gone home, the flowers closed their petals, and the party had dispersed. Every path lay empty.

Yet I stirred without discomfort—no chill crept into my limbs, no numbness tingled in my cheek or arms. That surprised me. For where my head and arms had rested on the hard wooden desk, there now lay a thick shawl, neatly folded. Another one wrapped gently around me, shielding me from the evening air. Both had been taken from the corridor where such things hung. Who had done this? Which of the teachers—or pupils—had shown such care?

None but St. Pierre held ill will toward me. But who possessed the quiet grace, the thoughtful hand, to act so tenderly? Whose step was so light, whose touch so soft, that I wouldn’t have known or felt her presence? Ginevra Fanshawe, brilliant and bold, would never have hesitated to pull me from my seat if she’d noticed me sleeping. So I concluded: *It must have been Madame Beck.* She had entered unseen, found me slumbering, and thought to spare me from catching cold. She saw me as a useful instrument, well-tended and efficient—no reason to damage what served her purpose.

And now, I thought, I’ll take a walk. The evening air is fresh, not too cold.

So I opened the glass door and stepped into the berceau. I walked to my own alley—had it been darker, I might not have ventured there. Not yet had I forgotten the odd vision I’d once experienced there, months ago. But now, a faint ray of sunset still touched the grey crown of Jean Baptiste, and the birds had not yet retired to their nests among the ivy-draped walls.

I paced slowly, lost in thoughts much like those I’d harbored when I buried my glass jar—how I might rise in life, move closer to independence. This idea, though not often pursued lately, had never fully left me. Whenever a certain glance turned away, or a face darkened with unkindness, I returned to this dream. Little by little, I had shaped half a plan.

“Living costs little,” I told myself, “in this sensible town of Villette, where people care less for appearances and pride than they do in England. Here, no one blushes to be plain or thrifty. A modest house, wisely chosen, needn’t cost much. Once I’ve saved a thousand francs, I’ll rent a small tenement—a large room for teaching, two or three smaller ones for students. I’ll furnish it simply: benches, desks, a blackboard, an estrade with a chair and table, a sponge, chalk. Begin with day-pupils. Work upward. Madame Beck started no higher—she speaks of it often—and now she owns this garden, these buildings, a secure future, and a flourishing school that will provide for her children.”

“Courage, Lucy Snowe! With self-denial now, and steady effort later, you can claim your place. Don’t complain that this goal seems selfish, narrow, lacking grandeur. Be content to labor for independence until you’ve earned the right to look beyond. But after that—what then? Is there nothing more? No true home? Nothing dearer than yourself? Nothing to love so deeply that it draws out the best in you, makes you willing to lay down your own burdens and live for others? Must your life remain only a crescent—never full?”

I looked around. I saw countless others in similar circumstances. Men and women alike lived under conditions of denial, of sacrifice. Why should I expect to be among the few spared? Yet I believed in hope—even in the darkest lots. I believed this life was not all—neither beginning nor end. I believed, even as I trembled. I trusted, even as I wept.

So I settled that account. It is right, sometimes, to face our lives honestly, to balance the books without deceit. He who lies to himself about joy and calls sorrow happiness is a poor fraud. Write anguish as anguish, despair as despair—in bold letters, with a firm hand. You’ll pay your debt better that way. Try to pass off pain as privilege—see if the great creditor accepts the counterfeit. Offer water when blood is asked—will he take it? Not a whole pale sea for one red drop.

Then I paused before Methusaleh—the ancient tree, patriarch of the garden—and leaned my forehead against its gnarled bark. My foot touched the stone sealing the small tomb at its roots. I recalled the feelings buried there—the memory of Dr. John, the warmth of my affection for him, the faith I placed in his goodness, the delight I took in his grace. What had become of that strange, one-sided bond—half truth, half illusion? Was it dead? I did not know. But it was buried.

Sometimes, I dreamed the earth stirred beneath it—golden hair pressing through cracks in the coffin lid. Had I been too hasty? I used to ask myself, especially after brief encounters with Dr. John. He still smiled kindly, his hand warm, his voice gentle when he spoke my name. I never liked “Lucy” more than when he said it. But I came to understand that his kindness was not meant for me alone. It was part of his nature—like honey from a fruit, perfume from a flower. The nectarine does not love the bee it feeds. The sweetbriar does not pine for the air that carries its scent.

“Good-night, Dr. John,” I whispered. “You are good, you are beautiful—but you are not mine. Good-night, and God bless you.”

The words left my lips—and then I heard an echo, close at hand.

“Good-night, Mademoiselle—or rather, good-evening. The sun has barely set. I hope you slept well?”

I started, but only for a moment. I knew that voice.

“Slept, Monsieur? When? Where?” I asked.

“You may well ask. It seems you turn day into night, and choose a desk for a pillow—rather hard lodging.”

“It was softened for me, Monsieur,” I replied. “That unseen, gift-bringing thing which haunts my desk remembered me. No matter how I fell asleep—I awoke pillowed and covered.”

“Did the shawls keep you warm?”

“Very warm. Do you ask thanks for them?”

“No. You looked pale in your sleep. Are you homesick?”

“To be homesick, one must have a home. I have none.”

“Then you have greater need of a careful friend. I scarcely know anyone, Miss Lucy, who needs one more than you. Your very faults demand it—you need watching, regulating, keeping down.”

This notion of “keeping down” clung to M. Paul like a habit. Even total submission could not free him from it. But what did it matter? I listened, untroubled by over-submission. His work would be undone if I gave him nothing to manage.

“You need watching,” he continued, “and I am glad I see it. I watch you—and others—often, closely, more than you suspect. See that window with the light?” He pointed to a lattice in one of the college boarding-houses. “That is a room I’ve hired—on paper, a study. In truth, a post of observation. There I sit for hours. My book is this garden—its contents, human nature—especially female nature. I know you all by heart. Ah, I know you well—St. Pierre, the Parisienne, *cette maîtresse-femme*, my cousin Beck herself.”

“It is not right, Monsieur,” I said.

“Comment? Not right? By whose creed? Does Calvin or Luther condemn it? What concern is that to me? I am no Protestant. My father—rich though I’ve known poverty, once starving in a garret in Rome, living on a meal a day—was a devout Catholic. He gave me a priest and a Jesuit for tutors. I retain their lessons. And what discoveries, *grand Dieu!* have they led me to!”

“Discoveries made by stealth seem dishonourable,” I replied.

“Puritaine!” he laughed. “Yet see how my Jesuit training serves me. You know St. Pierre?”

“Partially.”

He laughed again. “You say ‘partially’—but *I* know her *thoroughly*. She played the amiable before me, offered me velvet paws, flattered, fawned. I am accessible to flattery—though against reason. She was young, or seemed so. Like all her countrywomen, she dressed well, carried herself with cool ease, spared me embarrassment—”

“Monsieur, that must have been unnecessary. I never saw you embarrassed.”

“Mademoiselle, you know little of me. I can be as embarrassed as a petite pensionnaire. There is modesty in me—diffidence—”

“Monsieur, I never saw it.”

“Mademoiselle, it is there. You ought to have seen it.”

“I have observed you in public—on platforms, before crowned heads—and you were as composed as ever.”

“Neither titles nor crowns stir my modesty. Publicity is my element. I breathe freely in it—yet here, at this moment, sentiment has come alive. However, I disdain to be overcome by it. If I were a marrying man—which I am not—and needed to ask a woman if she could see me as a husband, then I would prove myself modest, as I claim.”

I believed him. And in believing, I honored him with a sincerity that ached in my heart.

“As for St. Pierre,” he went on, regaining composure, “she once intended to be Madame Emanuel. I don’t know where I might have been led, but for that little lattice with the light. Ah, magic lattice! What miracles of discovery have you wrought! Yes—I have seen her rancor, her vanities, her levities—not only here, but elsewhere. I have witnessed what shields me from her arts. I am safe from poor Zélie.”

“And my pupils,” he said after a pause, “those fair young girls—so mild, so meek—I have seen the most reserved romp like boys, the demurest snatch grapes from the wall, shake pears from the trees. When you arrived, I watched you early—marked your preference for this alley, your taste for solitude. I observed you long before we spoke. Do you remember when I silently approached you, offering a knot of white violets—when we were strangers?”

“I remember. I dried them, kept them. I still have them.”

“It pleased me that you accepted them calmly, without prudery—that feeling I dread to awaken, and detest when revealed in eye or gesture. To return: not only did *I* watch you—but another guardian angel hovered near. Night after night, my cousin Beck descended those steps, gliding unseen, following your movements when you didn’t know.”

“But, Monsieur,” I said, “you couldn’t see from that distance what happened here at night?”

“By moonlight, possibly—with a glass. But the garden itself is open to me. At the bottom, in the shed, there’s a door leading to a court connected to the college. I possess the key. I come and go as I please. This afternoon, I entered through it and found you asleep. Tonight, I’ve used the same entrance again.”

I could not help saying, “If you were a wicked, designing man, how terrible this would be!”

He paid no heed. He lit his cigar, leaned against a tree, and watched me with that cool, amused look he wore when at ease. I decided it was time to speak my mind—since he often lectured me for hours, why not let me lecture him once?

“The knowledge you gain is bought too dearly, Monsieur. This stealth degrades your dignity.”

“My dignity!” he laughed. “When have I ever cared for it? It is *you*, Miss Lucy, who are ‘digne.’ How often have I taken pleasure in trampling on what you call my dignity—tearing it apart, scattering it to the wind, in those wild moments you witness with such hauteur, which you think resemble a third-rate London actor’s ravings.”

“Monsieur,” I said, “every glance from that lattice is a wrong done to the best part of your nature. To study hearts thus is to feast secretly, sacrilegiously, on Eve’s apples. I wish you were a Protestant.”

He ignored the wish, smoked on.

After a silence filled with quiet thought, he said suddenly, “I have seen other things.”

“What other things?”

He removed the cigar from his lips and tossed the stub into the shrubs. For a moment, it glowed in the darkness.

“Look at it,” he said. “Doesn’t that spark look like an eye watching us?”

He walked down the path, then returned.

“I have seen, Miss Lucy, things I cannot explain. They have kept me awake all night, searching for answers. I have not found them yet.”

His tone was strange. My blood ran cold. He saw me shiver.

“Afraid? Of my words—or that red jealous eye just fading?”

“I am cold. The night grows late and dark. The air has changed. It is time to go in.”

“It is past eight. But you shall go in soon. Answer me only this question.” He paused.

The garden was growing darker. Clouds gathered. Rain began to patter through the leaves.

I hoped he would notice—but he seemed absorbed.

“Mademoiselle,” he said, “do Protestants believe in the supernatural?”

“There is variation among Protestants, as among all sects,” I answered. “Why do you ask?”

“Why do you shrink? Speak so faintly? Are you superstitious?”

“I am constitutionally nervous. I dislike discussing such matters. Especially because—”

“You believe?”

“No. But I have experienced impressions—”

“Since you came here?”

“Yes. Not many months ago.”

“Here—in this house?”

“Yes.”

“Bon! I am glad. I knew it—before you told me. I felt a connection between us. You are patient; I am choleric. You are quiet and pale; I am tanned and fiery. You are a strict Protestant; I am a sort of lay Jesuit. But we are alike. There is affinity. Do you see it when you look in the mirror? Your forehead—your eyes—your voice—do they not resemble mine? I perceive it. I believe you were born under my star. Yes—under my star! Tremble, for when mortals share such a fate, their destinies intertwine. Knots form. Threads break. Damage remains.”

“These ‘impressions,’ you say, with English caution. I, too, have had them.”

“Tell me,” I urged.

“I desire no better. You know the legend of this house?”

“I do. Hundreds of years ago, a nun was buried alive beneath this very tree—where we now stand.”

“And that her ghost walks here.”

“Monsieur—what if it still walks?”

“Something walks. A shape visits this house by night—unlike any seen by day. I have seen it—more than once. Its robes—conventual—strange to behold. A nun!”

“I, too,” I whispered, “have seen her.”

“I expected that. Whether flesh or spirit, her business is with you—as much as with me. Well—I mean to uncover the truth. It has baffled me so far. But I intend to follow it to the end. I mean—”

He stopped. Suddenly, we both lifted our heads—simultaneously—looking toward the high tree shading the great berceau, its boughs brushing the roof of the first classe.

A sound—strange, inexplicable—came from that direction. As if the tree itself had moved, its weight of foliage crashing against the trunk. Yet no wind stirred. The feathery shrubs stood still. But the great tree convulsed. For minutes, a rending, heaving motion swept through the wood and leafage.

In the darkness, I thought I saw something solid emerge from the shadows—something heavier than mere shadow.

At last, the struggle ceased.

What had been born of these throes?

We watched, transfixed.

Suddenly, a bell rang in the house—the prayer-bell.

Instantly, from the berceau, swept into our alley—out of the gloom—an apparition, all black and white.

With a swift, angry rush—close, close past our faces—went the NUN herself!

Never had I seen her so clearly. Tall, fierce in posture. As she passed, the wind rose, sobbing. Rain poured down, cold and wild. The entire night seemed to tremble in her wake.
