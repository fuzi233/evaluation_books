The events of the day had left me thoroughly unsettled, and what I needed most was a moment of quiet to collect my thoughts. But my daughter Penelope—bless her, though as persistent as her late mother ever was—cornered me at once, demanding to know what had transpired between Mr. Franklin Blake and me. Under the circumstances, the only course was to smother her curiosity before it took root. So I told her we had discussed foreign politics at such length that we both succumbed to the warmth of the sun and dozed off. A man learns, in time, that such answers are the best defense against inconvenient questions from the women in his life—and that their natural sweetness ensures reconciliation soon enough.

The afternoon waned, and my lady and Miss Rachel returned. Their astonishment at hearing of Mr. Franklin’s abrupt arrival and equally sudden departure was to be expected. So, too, was their barrage of questions. My earlier excuse of foreign politics and sun-induced slumber would not serve me twice. With invention exhausted, I declared that Mr. Franklin’s early arrival was merely one of his peculiar whims—and when pressed about his hasty exit, I insisted it was yet another. A neat evasion, if I do say so myself.

No sooner had I weathered the ladies’ interrogation than Penelope reappeared—first to mend our disagreement with a daughter’s tenderness, then to pry further. This time, her concern was Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid, who had returned from the Shivering Sand in a most peculiar state. According to Penelope, the girl had been a whirlwind of contradictions: one moment unnaturally merry, the next inexplicably melancholy. She had fired off a volley of questions about Mr. Franklin, then snapped at Penelope for implying she might take an interest in him. She had been caught smiling to herself, scribbling his name inside her workbox—only to dissolve into tears moments later, staring at her misshapen shoulder in the mirror.

Had she and Mr. Franklin met before? Impossible. Had either heard of the other? Equally so. I could vouch for the genuine shock on his face when she stared at him; Penelope could swear to the girl’s relentless curiosity about him. We might have debated the matter endlessly had Penelope not abruptly silenced me with the most outrageous suggestion I’d ever heard.

"Father," she said, deadly serious, "there’s only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight."

Now, young ladies of beauty and breeding falling headlong into love is a tale as old as time—but a housemaid, plain and deformed, struck by such a passion for a gentleman she had only just laid eyes on? Preposterous! I laughed until my sides ached. Yet Penelope’s reaction gave me pause.

"I never knew you to be cruel before, father," she said softly—and left.

Her words chilled me. Worse still, they left me uneasy. But enough of that.

Evening fell, and the dressing bell rang before Mr. Franklin returned from Frizinghall. Eager for news, I carried his hot water up myself, only to be met with disappointment. He had encountered no Indians, neither going nor returning. The Moonstone now rested safely in the bank—discreetly described as "a valuable of great price"—with the receipt secure in his pocket. A dull conclusion to a day that had begun with such intrigue.

Of the reunion between Mr. Franklin and his aunt and cousin, I can say little, for I was not present. Penelope reported that Miss Rachel had never taken such care with her hair, nor looked so radiant, as when she descended to meet him. The footman confessed that maintaining his composure while serving Mr. Franklin had been a trial of his professional restraint.

Later, music floated through the open windows—duets, with Mr. Franklin’s voice lifting high, Miss Rachel’s higher still, and my lady’s piano guiding them both as though over hill and dale. By the time I brought Mr. Franklin his brandy in the smoking-room, the Diamond seemed the furthest thing from his mind.

"She’s the most charming girl I’ve seen since returning to England," was all I could wrest from him.

At midnight, as I made my rounds with Samuel to secure the house, the night lay still and heavy under a full moon. The only sound was the distant murmur of the sea. The terrace lay in shadow, but moonlight bathed the gravel walk beside it. It was there I saw a figure’s shadow flit past—then heard light footsteps retreating in haste. By the time I reached the corner, the intruders had vanished into the shrubbery.

Too old to give chase, I roused Samuel, and together we armed ourselves and searched the grounds. Finding nothing, we turned back—but not before I spotted a small, glinting object on the gravel: a bottle of thick, sweet-smelling liquid, black as ink.

I said nothing to Samuel. But I remembered Penelope’s tale of the jugglers and their ink trick—and knew then that the Indians had been here, lurking in the dark, hunting the Diamond.
