Not a day slid by over Christendom, with its courts and cloisters, its tolling bells and market cries, but some unchronicled soul did a deed that would have sounded heroic had it been spoken from a pulpit in sonorous Latin. Yet the great books of the age—those crabbed, worm-scented registers the learned loved—tended to file such lives away like dry herbs between pages: labeled, flattened, and robbed of their living savour.

There exists, indeed, a mouldy chronicle, writ in Latin that clanks like chain-mail. In it a certain chapter is nothing but short, hard sentences—each a fact, each a stone. Beneath those stones, if one stoops and listens, there is warmth: two hearts beating; bread cut; tears swallowed; a young man with ink on his fingers and hunger in his belly; a girl with a father’s weight upon her hand. They lived without trumpets and died without songs, more than four hundred years ago, and lie still in that stern page as unpitied as fossils in rock. If their story be made to breathe again for an hour, it may be that the indifference of centuries will seem a little shameful.

It was past the middle of the fifteenth century. Louis XI wore France like a cunning glove; Edward IV sat wrongful in England; and Philip the Good, who had pushed aside his cousin Jacqueline and broken her heart, reigned in Holland with a magnificence that fed on rarity. Our tale begins in that Holland, in the little town of Tergou, with the low songs of wind in the reeds, the slap of canal-water against timber, and the morning bell that measured labour better than any clock.

Elias the cloth-merchant lived there with Catherine his wife. He dealt wholesale and retail in cloth and silk and brown holland; but, above all, in curried leather—tough, well-fed hide, worked with oil and tannin till it shone and would take twenty years’ wear. Such leather was prized by the middling folk, for it turned a common knife; and knives were free that century, too free—men being apt, even at dinner, to lay down their meat and carve their neighbour upon a moderate difference of opinion.

They were well to do, and might have been easy in their burgher’s prosperity but for the nine children Heaven had sent them, one almost every year, as steadily as harvest. When the first came, and the next, and the next, they had thanked the saints loudly and never asked why; babies then were like bright toys placed in busy hands for evening solace. But olive-branches do not stay green shoots. They rise into saplings, and saplings into hungry young trees; and as Elias and Catherine’s hair threaded with grey, the fate of large families looked them in the face whenever supper was laid.

Holland was a provident land. Reckless parents were as rare there as disobedient children. So when the huge brown loaf—dark, sour-sweet with rye, its crust thick as a rampart—came in on its great trencher, looking like a fortress in a moat, and then, after one solemn circuit of the table, seemed to have melted away into nine mouths, Elias and Catherine would exchange a glance over the crumbs and say, low enough that the youngsters should not hear and yet often enough that they did: “Who is to find bread for them all when we are gone?”

To the little ones, dinner and supper came by nature like sunrise and sunset. They thought the brown loaf must for ever go round their family circle, set in their stomachs, and rise again from the family oven with the morning’s crackle. Yet that repeated sentence awakened the elder boys’ Dutch thoughtfulness. Some began to think good thoughts—of work, and thrift, and apprenticeship. Some thought ill thoughts—of waiting, and grasping, and play.

One day Elias, measuring the lengthening elbows and broadening shoulders around his board, said, “Kate, the children grow so, this table will soon be too small.”

“We cannot afford it, Eli,” Catherine replied, answering not his words but the worry beneath them, as women do.

At times their anxiety sharpened into a pride that stung them more than fear. Free burghers had their dignity as well as nobles; and Catherine could not bear the thought that any of her blood should sink down among the beggars when she and Elias were in the ground.

So they did what Holland taught: they denied themselves. They clothed the small bodies, fed the great mouths, and still put by a hoard—coin by coin, like ants making a hill. In the steel-banded coffer with its florid key, every spare stiver went in; and as the little treasure grew, a secret pleasure grew with it—one the miser hoarding for himself never knows, because this was hoarded against the day their children would not be children.

Then, like a knife slid quietly into bread, the first severing came.

Richart, the eldest but one, nineteen and steady-faced—one of those youths whose calm outside makes strangers mistake the iron within—came to his mother and begged her, with composed speech and a moist eye, to intercede with his father: he wished to go to Amsterdam and be placed with a merchant.

“It is the life that likes me,” he said. “Merchants are wealthy. I am good at numbers. Prithee, good mother, take my part; and I shall ever be, as I am now, your debtor.”

Catherine lifted both hands as if warding off a blow.

“What! leave Tergou!”

“What is one street to me more than another? If I can leave the folk of Tergou, I can surely leave the stones.”

“What! quit your poor father now he is no longer young?”

“Mother, if I can leave you, I can leave—”

“What! leave your poor brothers and sisters, that love you so dear?”

“There are enough in the house without me.”

At that Catherine’s heart turned over. “What mean you, Richart? Who is more thought of than you? Stay—have I spoken sharp to you? Have I been unkind to you?”

“Never that I know of,” he said gravely, the tear swelling clear. “And if you had, you should never hear of it from me. Mother—it all lies in a word, and nothing can change my mind. There will be one mouth less for you to feed.”

“There now—see what my tongue has done,” Catherine cried, and her tears came fast. She saw her first bird on the edge of the nest, testing his wings against the wind. Richart had a will that did not waste words.

So it ended as such talk must. Richart went to Amsterdam with a face so long and sorrowful as Tergou had never seen on him, and a heart like granite.

That afternoon at supper there was one mouth less. The brown loaf came in—its warm scent of rye and sour leaven rising like comfort—and Catherine looked at Richart’s chair and wept bitterly. Elias, to cover his own pain with roughness, shouted at the children, “Sit wider, can’t ye: sit wider!” and then turned his head away over the back of his seat and sat silent, listening to the domestic sounds—chewing, swallowing, the fire’s soft hiss—made suddenly strange by absence.

To fit Richart out and place him with Vander Stegen the merchant took all the hoard but one gold crown. They began again, gathering like ants, coin by coin, while the oven breathed twice a day and the wind worried the shutters.

Two years passed. Richart found a niche for his brother Jacob, and Jacob left Tergou directly after dinner, which was at eleven of the forenoon. Elias remembered that former supper, and when the family gathered again and the loaf came round, he whispered this time, “Sit wider, dears!”—as if loudness could summon grief.

Till that instant Catherine would not see the gap. Her daughter Catherine had begged her: do not grieve to-night, mother; and she had promised, because the children’s hearts were tender to her sorrow. But when Elias whispered “Sit wider,” the promise broke like thin crust.

“Ay,” she said, forcing calm, “the table will soon be too big for the children, and you thought it would be too small.” Then her apron went up and she wept sore. “’Tis the best that leave us,” she sobbed; “that is the cruel part.”

“Nay! nay!” Elias said. “Our children are good children, and all are dear alike. Heed her not! What God takes from us still seems better than what He spares to us; that is to say, men are by nature unthankful—and women silly.”

“And I say Richart and Jacob were the flower of the flock,” Catherine answered through tears.

Again the coffer stood empty. Again they filled it the slow, sure way. In those days speculation ran mostly to cards and dice; Elias knew no road to wealth but the road of saving. “A penny saved is a penny gained,” was his creed. All not needed for trade and bare living went into the steel-banded box. They denied themselves, not by grand gestures but by the humble luxuries—an extra pinch of spice, a bit of smoked eel, a cup more of small beer—each giving up in turn, then catching one another’s looks and smiling, because the joy of forgoing for one’s own blood has a sweetness no indulgence buys.

In three years more they had gleaned enough to set up their fourth son as a master-tailor and their eldest daughter as a robemaker in Tergou—two more mouths turned into hands. Their trade would throw work their way. Yet again the coffer was drained to the dregs, and the shop too bled a little, in goods if not in coin.

And still there remained four at home: two unable to win bread, and two unwilling.

The unable were Giles, a dwarf of the wrong sort—half stupidity, half malice, all head and claws and voice—run from by dogs and frank women, but championed through thick and thin by his mother; and little Catherine, who could move only on crutches. She lived in pain, but smiled through it, with a marble face, violet eyes, and long silky lashes; no fretful or repining word ever slipped from her lips.

The unwilling were Sybrandt, the youngest, too much in love with play to work; and Cornelis, the eldest, who had made calculations and stuck to the hearth, waiting for dead men’s shoes.

Worn by repeated efforts—and, more, dispirited by the moral and bodily infirmities of those now left on hand—Elias and Catherine often said, with the loaf dwindling and the fire dropping, “What will become of all these when we shall be no longer here to take care of them?”

But after they had said it many times, the domestic horizon cleared of a sudden; and though the words stayed by habit, they were said now half mechanically, and then Catherine would brighten, as if the very syllables tasted different: “But thanks to St. Bavon and all the saints, there’s Gerard.”

Gerard was for years a son apart. He was meant for the Church, and the Church in those days could always feed her children—by hook or by crook. No great hopes were built on him; his family had no influence to win benefices, and his own habits were frivolous—habits Elias would not have borne in any other than a clerk destined for a cowl.

His frivolities were reading and penmanship. He loved ink as some boys love dice. He could scarce be dragged from his desk to his meals; and the day was never long enough for him. He carried a tinder-box and brimstone matches everywhere, begged ends of candles from neighbours, and lit them at unreasonable hours—aye, even at eight o’clock at night in winter, when the very burgomaster was abed and decent folk listened to the honest quiet of the town: the faint creak of a sign-board in wind, the distant churn of a mill, the canal’s lap, and, in a good house, the low domestic sounds of knife on crust and pot-lid on stew.

Endured at home, his practices were encouraged by the monks of a neighbouring convent. They had taught him penmanship and continued to teach him until one day, in the middle of a lesson, they discovered he was teaching them. They pointed it out merrily. Gerard hung his head and blushed; he had suspected as much, but mistrusted his own judgement in so delicate a matter.

“But, my son,” said an elderly monk, peering kindly, “how is it that you, to whom God has given an eye so true, a hand so subtle yet firm, and a heart to love these beautiful crafts—how is it you do not colour as well as write? A scroll looks but barren unless a border of fruit and leaves and rich arabesques surround the good words and charm the sense as those do the soul and understanding; to say nothing of pictures of holy men and women departed, with which the several chapters should be adorned—so that not alone the eye is soothed with brave, sweetly blended colours, but the heart lifted by effigies of saints in glory. Answer me, my son.”

Gerard, confused, muttered he had tried illuminating but had not succeeded well; and thus it rested.

Soon after, a fellow-enthusiast appeared—in the unwonted form of an old lady. Margaret, sister and survivor of the brothers Van Eyck, had left Flanders and come to end her days in her native country. She bought a small house near Tergou. In time she heard of Gerard and saw some of his handiwork. It pleased her so well she sent her woman Reicht Heynes to ask him to come.

Acquaintance followed, as it must, for little Tergou had never held two zealots of this sort at once. At first the old lady damped Gerard’s courage terribly. At each visit she fished out from holes and corners drawings and paintings—some by her own hand—that seemed to him unapproachable. But if the artist overpowered him, the woman kept his heart up. She and Reicht turned him inside out like a glove. From him they drew, too, what the good monks had missed: the true reason he did not illuminate. He could not afford the gold, the blue, the red—only cheap earths. He was afraid to ask his mother for the dear colours; and he was sure she would refuse.

Then Margaret Van Eyck gave him a little brush—gold, vermilion, and ultramarine—and a piece of good vellum to lay them on. He almost adored her. As he left her house Reicht ran after him with a candle and two quarters; in his gratitude he quite kissed her. Better even than gold and lapis was the sympathy offered to an isolated enthusiast. It was always ready; and as he returned it, an affection sprang up between the old painter and the young caligrapher, characteristic of an age when fine arts and higher mechanical arts were not sharply divided and artists sought out and loved one another.

Backed by a friendship so venerable and strengthened by women’s sympathy, Gerard advanced in skill. His spirits rose visibly. He still looked behind him when he was dragged to dinner in the middle of an initial G; but once seated he showed great social qualities and a gay humour that had before only peeped out. Often he set the table in a roar, sometimes with his own wit, sometimes with jests glossy-new to his family because drawn from antiquity, those dead tongues whose words, when brought into Dutch mouths, never sounded quite the same—like spices that change in the grinding.

In return for what he owed the monks, he made them exquisite copies from two of their choicest manuscripts: the life of their founder and their Comedies of Terence, the monastery finding the vellum.

Now Philip the Good—Duke of Burgundy, Luxemburg, and Brabant; Earl of Holland and Zealand; Lord of Friesland; Count of Flanders, Artois, and Hainault; Lord of Salins and Macklyn—was a versatile prince.

He could fight as well as any king going; and he could lie as well as any, save the King of France. He hunted mightily and could read and write. He loved jewels like a woman, and gorgeous apparel. He loved maids of honour, and paintings generally—witness his ennobling of Jan Van Eyck. He had a rage for giants, dwarfs, and Turks: turbaned men blazing with jewels stood planted about him. His agents inveigled them from Istamboul with fair promises; then, once caught, he baptized them by brute force in a tub, and after that let them squat facing Mecca and invoke Mahound as much as they pleased, laughing in his sleeve at their simplicity in fancying they were still infidels. He kept lions in cages and fleet leopards trained by Orientals to run down hare and deer.

In short, he relished all rarities except humdrum virtues. Anything singularly pretty, or diabolically ugly—this was your customer. The best of him was his open hand to the poor; the next best, that he fostered the arts in earnest. Of this he now gave a signal proof: he offered prizes for the best specimens of orfevrerie, religious and secular; for the best paintings in white of egg, oils, and tempera, on panel, silk, or metal; for the best transparent painting on glass; for the best illuminating and border-painting on vellum; and for the fairest writing on vellum. Burgomasters of the towns were commanded to aid poorer competitors by receiving their work and sending it to Rotterdam at the town’s expense.

When this was cried by the bellman through the streets of Tergou—his voice beating against gables and slipping along the canal—thousand mouths opened, but one heart truly beat hard: Gerard’s.

He told his family timidly he should try for two prizes. They stared in silence; his audacity blew the breath out of them. Then one horrid laugh exploded on the floor like a petard. Gerard looked down. It was Giles the dwarf, slit and fanged from ear to ear at Gerard’s expense, laughing like a lion.

Nature, relenting at making Giles so small, had given him the biggest voice on record. His whisper was a bassoon. He was like those stunted, wide-mouthed pieces of ordnance on fortifications—more like a flower-pot than a cannon; but, heaven help the tympans, how he bellowed.

Gerard turned red with anger, the more because the others began to titter. Little Catherine saw; a pink tinge came to her cheek, and she spoke softly, with her quiet courage.

“Why do you laugh? Is it because he is our brother you think he cannot be capable? Yes, Gerard, try with the rest. Many say you are skilful; and mother and I will pray the Virgin to guide your hand.”

“Thank you, little Kate,” Gerard said, his heat cooling. “You shall pray to our Lady—and our mother shall buy me vellum and the colours to illuminate with.”

“What will they cost, my lad?” Catherine asked, already suspecting pain.

“Two gold crowns.”

“What!” screamed the housewife. “When the bushel of rye costs but a groat! What—me spend a month’s meal and meat and fire on such vanity as that? The lightning from Heaven would fall on me, and my children would all be beggars.”

“Mother,” sighed little Catherine, imploring.

“Oh, it is in vain, Kate,” Gerard said, with a sigh that tasted of disappointment like cold ashes. “I shall have to give it up, or ask the dame Van Eyck. She would give it me, but I think shame to be for ever taking from her.”

“It is not her affair,” Catherine snapped, sudden and sharp, jealousy flaring that another woman’s kindness should come between her and her son. “What has she to do coming between me and my son?” And she left the room red-faced.

Little Catherine smiled, patient as ever. Presently the housewife returned with a gracious, affectionate air and two little gold pieces in her hand.

“There, sweetheart,” she said. “You won’t have to trouble dame or demoiselle for two paltry crowns.”

Gerard’s gratitude rose, then turned at once into thought: how to spare that purse that was the family’s hearth. “One will do, mother,” he said. “I will ask the good monks to let me send my copy of their Terence. It is on snowy vellum, and I can write no better. Then I shall only need six sheets for my borders and miniatures, and gold for my ground, and prime colours—one crown will do.”

“Never tyne the ship for want of a bit of tar, Gerard,” said his changeable mother; yet she added, half laughing, half wincing, “Well—there. I will put the crown in my pocket. That won’t be like putting it back in the box. Going to the box to take out instead of putting in is like going to my heart with a knife for so many drops of blood. You will be sure to want it, Gerard. The house is never built for less than the builder counted on.”

And indeed, when the time came, Gerard longed not only to compete but to go to Rotterdam and see the Duke, and, more than that, to see the work of his rivals—so as to learn even from defeat. The crown came out of Catherine’s pocket with good grace. Gerard would soon be a priest; it seemed hard if he might not enjoy the world a little before separating from it for life.

The night before he went, Margaret Van Eyck asked him to carry a letter for her. When he looked at it, to his surprise it was addressed to the Princess Marie at the Stadthouse in Rotterdam.

The day before the prizes were to be distributed Gerard started in his holiday suit: a doublet of silver-grey cloth with sleeves; over it a jerkin of the same, sleeveless; tight-fitting buckskin hose from waist to heel, fastened by points to the doublet; shoes pointed in moderation and held by a strap under the hollow of the foot. His flowing hair lay on head and neck, and his hat was pinned between his shoulders, further secured by a purple silk ribbon little Kate had passed round it and knotted neatly on his breast. Below the hat, attached to the upper rim of his broad waist-belt, hung his leathern wallet.

The road’s sound accompanied him: larks flinging bright notes above the fields, the hush of wind through reeds, the distant creak of cart-wheels, and now and then, near a farm, the steady clop of a flail or the lowing of cattle. Such sounds were as much a part of the day’s health as bread in the belly; they soothed him as he walked, and the thought of Rotterdam—with its crowding cries and clang of trade—rose before him like a different music.

Within a league of the city he was tired; but soon he fell in with a pair more tired than he. An old man sat by the roadside quite worn out, and a comely young woman stood holding his hand, her face brimful of concern. Country folk trudged by, noticing nothing amiss; but Gerard, trained by his craft to read the tale in surfaces, read it in their dress.

The old man wore a gown, a fur tippet, and a velvet cap—signs of dignity; yet the triangular purse at his girdle was lean, the gown rusty, the fur worn—signs of poverty. The young woman was in plain russet; but snow-white lawn covered the part of her neck the gown left visible and ended half-way up her white throat in a little band of gold embroidery. Her head-dress was new to Gerard: instead of hiding her hair in piled linen, she wore an open network of silver cord with silver spangles at the interstices. In it her glossy auburn hair rolled in front into two solid waves and was supported behind in a luxurious, shapely mass. He saw also the old man’s pallor and the tears in the young woman’s eyes.

He passed them a few yards, drew his conclusions, then turned back and came towards them bashfully.

“Father,” he said, “I fear you are tired.”

“Indeed, my son, I am,” the old man replied, “and faint for lack of food.”

Gerard’s address pleased the old man more than the girl. She coloured as if caught in need and, with reserve, said it was her fault: she had underrated the distance and imprudently let her father start too late.

“No, no,” said the old man. “It is not the distance, it is the want of nourishment.”

The girl put her arms round his neck with tender concern; but under that cover she whispered, warning and shame together, “Father—a stranger—a young man!”

Too late. Gerard, simple as breathing and taking charity as a matter of course, fell to gathering sticks with great expedition. He took down his wallet. Out came a manchet of bread and the iron flask his careful mother had put up; out came his everlasting tinder-box. He struck brimstone, lit a match, then a candle-end, then the sticks. The first faint smell of singed wood rose. He set the iron flask on the little blaze and dropped on his stomach to blow, cheeks puffing, breath making the coals glow.

When he looked up, he saw the girl’s face had thawed. She watched his briskness with a demure smile that was half gratitude and half amusement.

He laughed back. “Mind the pot,” he said, nodding at the flask, “and don’t let it spill, for Heaven’s sake: there’s a cleft stick to hold it safe with.” And with that he set off running towards a corn-field at some distance.
