This evening, the gates of the Schloss groaned open to admit a somber procession: the grave, dark-faced son of the picture cleaner arrived from Gratz, accompanied by a horse and cart burdened with two massive packing cases, each brimming with canvases. It was a journey of ten leagues, and whenever a courier emerged from our provincial capital, the household would swarm into the hall, breath held in eager anticipation of the news from afar. Yet this arrival stirred a sensation within our secluded quarters unlike any other. The cases were deposited in the hall, and the messenger was ushered aside by the servants until he had consumed his supper. Only then, armed with hammers, ripping chisels, and turning screws, did he join us in the grand assembly to begin the unveiling.

Carmilla sat watching listlessly, her presence a silent anchor amidst the bustle. One by one, the old portraits—nearly all of them faces frozen in time—were brought forth, emerging from the gloom of restoration. My mother hailed from an ancient Hungarian lineage, and most of these images had passed through her hands to find their way to us. My father clutched a ledger, reading off numbers as the artisan pried loose layers of varnish and grime. I confess, I cannot judge the artistic merit of these relics; they were undeniably ancient, some possessing a peculiar, haunting curiosity. For the most part, they were sights I beheld for the first time, obscured until now by the suffocating smoke and dust of centuries.

"There remains one portrait I have not yet seen," my father announced, his voice cutting through the workshop hum. "In the upper corner, barely legible, stands the name 'Marcia Karnstein,' dated 1698. I am curious to discover how time has treated it."

A memory surfaced instantly. It was a small canvas, roughly a foot and a half high, nearly square, and unframed. Previously, it had been so blackened by age that it resembled a void rather than an image. Now, the artist produced it with a visible swell of pride. It was breathtaking; it was startling; it possessed a terrifying vitality. It was the effigy of Carmilla herself.

"Carmilla, dearest, behold an absolute miracle!" my father exclaimed, his tone reverent. "Here you are, living, smiling, poised to speak. Is it not beautiful, Papa? And observe, even the tiny mole upon her throat is preserved."

My father chuckled, dismissing the intensity of the moment. "Undoubtedly, it is a wonderful likeness," he remarked, yet he averted his gaze, seemingly unmoved by the revelation, and turned his attention to the picture cleaner, who began to discourse intelligently on the merits of the newly revealed works. Meanwhile, I became increasingly entranced, staring at the painting until the boundary between the canvas and the woman before me seemed to dissolve.

"May I hang this picture in my room, Papa?" I asked softly.

"Certainly, dear," he replied with a smile. "I am delighted you find it so striking. It must be even more lovely than I anticipated."

The young lady did not acknowledge his compliment, nor did she appear to hear it. She leaned back in her chair, her fine eyes, framed by long lashes, fixing upon me with a gaze of deep contemplation. A smile touched her lips, a expression of pure, rapturous ecstasy.

"And now," she said, her voice carrying a strange resonance, "you can read the inscription clearly. It is not Marcia; the script appears to be done in gold. The name is Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, beneath a delicate coronet and the date A.D. 1698. I am descended from the Karnsteins—that is, my mother was."

"Ah!" she continued, her tone languid and dreamlike. "So am I, I suppose, of a very long descent, exceedingly ancient. Are there any Karnsteins living now?"

"None who bear the name, I believe," my father responded. "The family was ruined, I understand, during some civil wars long ago, though the ruins of the castle lie mere three miles hence."

"How fascinating!" she murmured, her eyes drifting toward the slightly ajar hall door. "But look! What magnificent moonlight!"

She gestured through the opening. "Pray, take a brief stroll around the courtyard and cast your gaze upon the road and the river below."

"It mirrors the night you arrived," I whispered.

She sighed, a sound of infinite longing, and smiled. Rising gracefully, she took my arm, and together we stepped onto the pavement. In silence, we walked slowly down to the drawbridge, where the landscape unfolded before us in a tableau of silver and shadow.

"So you were contemplating the night I came?" she asked, her voice barely audible, almost a whisper. "Are you glad I came?"

"Delighted, dear Carmilla," I answered, my heart swelling.

"And you requested this portrait, believing it captured your essence, to adorn your chamber," she murmured, drawing my arm tighter against her side and resting her head upon my shoulder. "How romantic you are, Carmilla. Whenever you recount your tale, it will surely consist chiefly of some great romance."

She kissed me silently, a touch that lingered with electric weight. "I am certain, Carmilla, that you have loved; that there is, at this very moment, an affair of the heart unfolding."

"I have loved no one, and never shall," she whispered, her voice trembling with a sudden intensity, "unless it should be with you."

How radiant she appeared in the moonlight! Shy yet strangely commanding, she buried her face in my neck and hair, emitting tumultuous sighs that bordered on sobs, clutching my hand with a grip that shook violently. Her soft cheek burned against mine.

"Darling, darling," she breathed, "I live within you; and you would die for me. I love you so."

Suddenly, I recoiled. She stared at me, her eyes devoid of all fire, all meaning, her face pale and utterly apathetic.

"Does the air feel chilly, dear?" she asked, her voice drowsy and detached. "I almost shiver; have I been dreaming? Let us go inside. Come; come; come in."

"You look unwell, Carmilla; a trifle faint. You must drink some wine," I urged, concern tightening my chest.

"Yes... I will. I am better now. I shall be fully recovered in moments. Yes, please give me a little wine," she answered as we approached the threshold. "Let us glance once more; it may be the final time I see the moonlight with you."

"How do you feel now, dear Carmilla? Are you truly better?" I asked, dread creeping into my voice. I began to fear she had succumbed to the strange epidemic rumored to be ravaging the countryside surrounding us. "Papa would be grief-stricken beyond measure," I added, "if he believed you were even slightly ill without immediate notification. We possess a highly skilled physician nearby, the same one who attended to Papa today."

"I am sure he is. I know how kindly you all are; but, dear child, I am perfectly well again. Nothing is ever wrong with me save a slight weakness. They say I am languid, incapable of exertion, unable to walk further than a toddler. Every now and then, the little strength I possess falters, and I become as you witnessed just now. But ultimately, I recover with ease; in a moment, I am entirely myself. Observe how I have regained my composure."

Indeed, she had. We conversed animatedly thereafter, and the remainder of the evening passed without a recurrence of those episodes I termed her infatuations—her erratic speech and unsettling glances that had previously terrified me. Yet, that very night, an event transpired which shifted the trajectory of my thoughts entirely, startling even Carmilla's languid nature into a flash of momentary, piercing energy.
