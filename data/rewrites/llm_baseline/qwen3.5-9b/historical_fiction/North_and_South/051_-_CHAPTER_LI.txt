The air was heavy with the heat of a summer's evening. Edith entered Margaret's bedroom, arriving first in her dressing gown, then a second time fully attired for dinner. Initially, the room stood empty. Upon returning, Edith discovered Dixon arranging Margaret's gown upon the bed, though Margaret herself was nowhere to be seen. Edith paced anxiously, fidgeting with her preparations.

"Oh, Dixon! Not those dreadful blue flowers against that dead gold-colored gown. What taste! Wait a moment; I shall fetch some pomegranate blossoms instead."

"It isn't a dead gold color, ma'am," Dixon retorted, clearly irritated by the criticism of her selection. "It's a straw color. And blue matches straw perfectly."

However, Edith had already secured the brilliant scarlet blooms before Dixon could finish her objection. Once Edith had arranged the garland, she finally located her aunt.

"Where is Miss Hale?" Edith asked immediately upon trying on the flowers. "I can't make sense of it," she continued, her tone petulant, "that my aunt permitted her to adopt such wandering habits in Milton! I am constantly fearing she has encountered some horror amidst those wretched districts she wanders into. I should never dare to traverse certain streets without a servant; they are simply unfit for ladies."

Dixon, still stung by the insult to her aesthetic judgment, replied curtly: "It is no wonder, in my opinion, when one hears ladies discussing 'ladies' so incessantly—especially when they are such frightfully delicate, dainty creatures. Truly, it explains why there are no longer any saints upon earth..."

"Oh, Margaret! Here you are!" Edith exclaimed, rushing forward. "I have been searching everywhere for you. But look at your cheeks, flushed red with the heat, poor child! Think of what that tiresome Henry has done; he truly exceeds the bounds of a brother-in-law. Just when my party was assembled so beautifully, fitting Mr. Colthurst with precision, Henry arrives with an apology, using your name as an excuse, and asks if he may bring Mr. Thornton of Milton—your tenant, you know—who is currently in London attending to legal business. It will completely ruin my arrangement."

"I do not mind dinner," Margaret said softly. "I do not wish to eat. Dixon can bring me a cup of tea here, and I shall join you in the drawing-room once you return. I would greatly prefer to lie down."

"No, no! That is absolutely impossible. You certainly look dreadfully pale, though I attribute it solely to the heat. Besides, we cannot manage without you. (Lower those flowers slightly, Dixon. They look like glorious flames against your dark hair.) Remember, we planned for you to discuss Milton with Mr. Colthurst. Oh, yes! And now this man comes directly from Milton. I believe it will turn out splendidly after all. Mr. Colthurst can interrogate him thoroughly on all topics that interest him, and it will be delightful to trace your experiences alongside Mr. Thornton's insights for Mr. Colthurst's upcoming speech in the House. Truly, I consider this a fortunate stroke of Henry's. I asked him if he was a man one would be ashamed to introduce, and he replied, 'Not if you possess any sense, my little sister.' Therefore, I assume he possesses the ability to articulate his thoughts—a rare achievement in Darkshire, eh, Margaret?"

"Did Mr. Lennox mention why Mr. Thornton has come to town? Is it regarding the property?" Margaret asked, her voice tight with constraint.

"Oh! He has failed, or something similar," Edith dismissed lightly. "Henry told you about that the day you suffered such a severe headache—do you recall? (There, perfect, Dixon. Miss Hale adds so much distinction to our gathering.) I wish I were as tall as a queen and as brown as a gypsy, Margaret."

"But about Mr. Thornton?"

"Oh! I truly lack the capacity for legal matters. Henry would delight in explaining everything to you. The impression he conveyed to me was that Mr. Thornton is in dire straits yet remains a highly respectable individual, and that I should treat him with extreme civility. Since I did not know how to proceed, I came to you to request your assistance. Now, please come downstairs with me and rest on the sofa for fifteen minutes."

Captain Lennox arrived early. As Margaret grew flushed while addressing him, she began to inquire about the details she needed concerning Mr. Thornton.

"He ascended to town regarding the subletting of the property—Marlborough Mills, along with the adjacent house and premises. He is unable to maintain the lease; consequently, deeds and leases require review, and new agreements must be drafted. I trust Edith will receive him appropriately, though I observed she was somewhat displeased by my liberty in soliciting an invitation for him. However, I believed you would appreciate receiving proper attention, and one must be particularly scrupulous in honoring a man whose fortunes are declining."

He lowered his voice as he addressed Margaret, seated beside him. Before he concluded, he rose abruptly to introduce Mr. Thornton, who had just entered, to Edith and Captain Lennox.

Margaret watched Mr. Thornton with an anxious gaze as he was presented. It had been considerably more than a year since she had last seen him, and events had altered him significantly during that interval. His commanding figure still elevated him above the average man, granting him a distinguished presence derived from the natural ease of his movement. Yet, his face appeared aged and wearied. Nevertheless, a noble composure rested upon his features, impressing those who had recently learned of his diminished status with a profound sense of inherent dignity and masculine strength.

Aware from his first survey of the room that Margaret was present—he had noted her intent expression as she listened to Mr. Henry Lennox—he approached her with the measured demeanor of an old friend. His initial calm words sent a vivid flush to her cheeks, which never faded throughout the evening. She seemed to have little to contribute to him. She disappointed him by asking quietly what appeared to him to be merely necessary inquiries regarding her former acquaintances in Milton. Others, more intimately connected to the household, soon joined the circle, and he drifted into the background, conversing intermittently with Mr. Lennox.

"You think Miss Hale looks well," remarked Mr. Lennox, "do you not? Milton evidently disagreed with her, for when she first arrived in London, I believed I had never witnessed anyone so transformed. Tonight, she appears radiant. Yet, she is much stronger. Last autumn, she was exhausted by a walk of several miles. On Friday evening, we strolled up to Hampstead and returned. By Saturday, she looked as robust as she does today."

"We?" Who? The two of them alone?

Mr. Colthurst was a remarkably astute man and a rising parliamentary figure. Possessing a keen eye for character, he was struck by a remark Mr. Thornton made during dinner. He questioned Edith regarding the identity of that gentleman. To her surprise, she detected in his tone of "Indeed!" that Mr. Thornton of Milton was not an obscure name to him as she had anticipated.

Edith's dinner proceeded successfully. Henry was in high spirits, displaying his dry, caustic wit to excellent effect. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Colthurst identified a few shared interests, touching briefly upon them before reserving deeper discussion for private conversation later. Margaret looked exquisite in the pomegranate blossoms. Although she leaned back in her chair and spoke sparingly, Edith was not annoyed, for the conversation flowed smoothly without her participation. Margaret, meanwhile, studied Mr. Thornton's face. He never looked at her; thus, she could observe him unobserved, noting the subtle changes wrought by this brief passage of time.

Only at an unexpected word from Mr. Lennox did his face flash with the familiar look of intense enjoyment. The merry brightness returned to his eyes, his lips parted to hint at the brilliant smile of their youth. For an instant, his gaze instinctively sought hers, seeking her sympathy. But when their eyes met, his entire countenance shifted; he became grave and anxious once more, and he deliberately avoided looking near her again during the meal.

There were only two other ladies besides their party, and as they were engaged in conversation with her aunt and Edith, the group moved upstairs to the drawing-room. Margaret idly attended to some needlework. Soon, the gentlemen ascended, Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton continuing their close discourse. Mr. Lennox approached Margaret and whispered, "I genuinely believe Edith owes me gratitude for my contribution to her party. You have no idea how agreeable and sensible this tenant of yours is. He proved to be the ideal person to coach Colthurst on the facts he required. I cannot comprehend how he managed to mismanage his affairs."

"With his talents and opportunities, you would have succeeded," Margaret replied.

She did not entirely relish the tone of her words, although they merely articulated a thought that had also crossed his mind. Remaining silent, they overheard a rise in volume from the conversation near the fireplace between Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton.
