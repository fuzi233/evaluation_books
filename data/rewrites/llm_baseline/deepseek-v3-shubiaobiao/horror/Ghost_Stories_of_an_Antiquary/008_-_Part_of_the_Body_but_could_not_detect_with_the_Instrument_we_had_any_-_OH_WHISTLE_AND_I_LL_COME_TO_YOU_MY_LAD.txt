The dining hall of St. James’s College was alive with the murmur of scholars and the clink of silver, but Professor Parkins, young and precise in manner, sat stiffly among them. His neighbor leaned in, voice low and congenial.

“I suppose you’ll be leaving soon, now that Full Term is over?”

Parkins dabbed his lips with a napkin. “Indeed. My friends have insisted I take up golf this term, and I mean to spend a week on the East Coast—Burnstow, to be exact. I depart tomorrow.”

The man on his other side perked up. “Burnstow? You must do me a favor, then. There’s rumored to be a Templar preceptory near the shore—just rubble now, most likely. If you could glance at the site, I’d be grateful. I’ve half a mind to dig there come summer.”

Parkins inclined his head. “If you describe its location, I’ll survey the land. Though I warn you, my expertise lies in ontography, not archaeology.”

A gruff laugh came from across the table. Rogers, a red-faced man with a perpetual smirk, raised his glass. “Staying at the Globe Inn, Parkins? I hear they’ve stuck you with a double room. Why not let me join you? Company against the winter chill—and the local ghosts.”

Parkins stiffened. “I require solitude for my work.”

Rogers winked at the others. “Ah, yes. The famous Parkins skepticism. No specters shall pass your threshold—unless you whistle for them, eh?”

A flush crept up the professor’s neck. “I do not indulge in superstition.”

---

The wind off the North Sea howled like a thing alive as Parkins trudged across the links toward the shore. Twilight bled into the horizon, and the skeletal frames of groynes jutted from the sand like blackened ribs.

Disney’s ruins were scarcely ruins at all—just uneven mounds half-buried in the turf. Kneeling, Parkins scraped at the earth with his knife until the blade struck something hollow. A few prods revealed a small cavity, smooth-walled and deliberate. His fingers closed around cold metal.

A whistle.

Bronze, tarnished green, its surface etched with worn Latin:

And on the reverse:

Curiosity overrode caution. He cleared the mouthpiece and blew.

The sound was not loud, yet it seemed to slice through the wind, distant and keening. For an instant, the darkening beach wavered—a vast, wind-lashed expanse, and something moving across it, swift and pale.

Then the gust came.

It slammed into him like a living thing, snuffing his matches, howling through his coat. By the time he wrestled the inn’s casement shut, his hands shook.

Upstairs, he examined the whistle under candlelight. The letters taunted him. *Who is this who is coming?*

Foolishness. And yet—

He blew again.

The note hung in the air, thin and insistent. No vision this time, but the wind outside rose to a shriek. The candles guttered out. Darkness swallowed the room.

Something scraped against the floorboards near his bed.

Parkins lunged for the matches. The flare of light revealed nothing—yet the sound had been real. Rats, perhaps. Or the inn’s timbers groaning.

He spent the night with a book propped in his lap, jumping at every creak. Dawn found him bleary-eyed, the candle burned to a stump.

At breakfast, the maid handed him a note.

“Found outside your door, sir.”
