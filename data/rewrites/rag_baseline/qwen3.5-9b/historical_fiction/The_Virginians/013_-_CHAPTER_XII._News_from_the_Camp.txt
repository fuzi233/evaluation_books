The silence in Castlewood was not empty; it was a heavy, suspended thing, filled with the ghost of a brother who had walked away. Harry sat at the head of the table, his fingers tracing the rim of a silver tankard, the metal cool against his skin. He drank, but the liquid did not settle. Across the room, Madame Warrington moved with a rigid cheerfulness that seemed to strain against the gravity of the situation. She spoke of George as if he were merely stepping out for a moment, her voice bright, her posture upright, arranging the household around a vacancy that refused to be acknowledged. She insisted on the toast of "The King," her solemnity on Sundays a performance of faith that masked the terror of absence. She walked into the empty chambers, touching the neatly folded linens and the stacked papers, treating the arrangement of objects as a substitute for the living man. Mrs. Mountain, meanwhile, hovered on the edge of hysteria, her whispers rising whenever the name was spoken, while Harry wore a mask of ghastly alarm, terrified of the grief he dared not name.

Yet, amidst this domestic suspension, the war pressed in through the letters. George wrote frequently, compiling a rough journal that became the central artifact of their evenings. On long summer nights, the house settled into a ritualistic stillness. Madame Esmond sat upright, refusing the comfort of a chair's back; little Fanny Mountain stitched her sampler with mechanical precision; Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain played cards in the gloom; the old servants moved like shadows. In this circle, Harry read aloud the words of his brother, the ink on the yellow paper seeming to vibrate with a life of its own.

"It must be owned that the provinces are acting scurvily by his Majesty King George II.," Harry read, his voice flat, reciting the grievances of a distant front. "Virginia is bad enough... but Pennsylvania is worst of all. We pray them to send us troops... and we promise to maintain the troops when they come. We not only don't keep our promise... but our people insist upon the most exorbitant prices for their cattle and stores, and actually cheat the soldiers who are come to fight their battles."

He paused, letting the bitterness hang in the air. "No wonder the General swears, and the troops are sulky. The delays have been endless. Owing to the failure of the several provinces to provide their promised stores... weeks and months have elapsed, during which time, no doubt, the French have been strengthening themselves..."

Then came the mention of Washington. "Though there never will be any love lost between me and Colonel Washington, it must be owned that your favourite... is a brave man and a good officer. The family respect him very much... Indeed, he is almost the only man who has seen the Indians in their war-paint, and I own I think he was right in firing upon Mons. Jumonville last year."

Madame Esmond set her cup down with a sharp clink. "I wonder at the taste of some men, and the effrontery of some women," she said, her voice cutting through the domestic haze. "I wonder at any woman who has been married once, so forgetting herself as to marry again!"

"Monstrous!" Mr. Mountain muttered, his eyes fixed on his glass of punch.

Harry choked, a laugh bubbling up from somewhere deep and suppressed, but his mother silenced him gently. "Go on, Harry! Continue with your brother's journal. He writes well: but, ah, will he ever be able to write like my papa?"

Harry resumed, his voice steadying. "We keep the strictest order here in camp... The roll of each company is called at morning, noon, and night... The men are punished, and the drummers are always at work. Oh, Harry, but it made one sick to see the first blood drawn from a great strong white back, and to hear the piteous yell of the poor fellow."

"Oh, horrid!" Madame Esmond whispered.

"I think I should have murdered Ward if he had flogged me. Thank Heaven he got off with only a crack of the ruler!" Harry read, the horror of the scene translating directly into the room. "The men... are looked after carefully enough. I wish the officers were. The Indians have just broken up their camp... because the young officers were for ever drinking with the squaws—and—and—hum—ha."

Mr. Harry hesitated, sensing the weight of the unsaid, the crude reality of the frontier that little Fanny's prim presence could not absorb.

"Pass over that about the odious tipsy creatures," Madame commanded.

Harry shifted gears, adopting a louder, more satisfying tone. "Each regiment has Divine Service performed at the head of its colours every Sunday. The General does everything in the power of mortal man to prevent plundering... He has declared soldiers shall be shot who dare to interrupt or molest the market-people. He has ordered the price of provisions to be raised a penny a pound, and has lent money out of his own pocket to provide the camp. Altogether, he is a strange compound, this General. He flogs his men without mercy, but he gives without stint. He swears most tremendous oaths in conversation, and tells stories which Mountain would be shocked to hear—"

"Why me?" Mr. Mountain asked, defensive. "And what have I to do with the General's silly stories?"

"Never mind the stories; and go on, Harry," cried the mistress of the house.

"—would be shocked to hear after dinner; but he never misses service. He adores his Great Duke, and has his name constantly on his lips. Our two regiments both served in Scotland, where I dare say Mr. Dempster knew the colour of their facings."

"We saw the tails of their coats, as well as their facings," Mr. Mountain growled, a Jacobite tutor finding purchase in the past.

"Colonel Washington has had the fever very smartly... Had he not better go home and be nursed by his widow? When either of us is ill, we are almost as good friends again as ever. But I feel somehow as if I can't forgive him for having wronged him. Good Powers! How I have been hating him for these months past! Oh, Harry! I was in a fury at the tavern the other day, because Mountain came up so soon, and put an end to our difference. We ought to have burned a little gunpowder between us, and cleared the air. But though I don't love him, as you do, I know he is a good soldier, a good officer, and a brave, honest man; and, at any rate, shall love him none the worse for not wanting to be our stepfather."

"A stepfather, indeed!" Madame Esmond exclaimed, her composure cracking. "Why, jealousy and prejudice have perfectly maddened the poor child! Do you suppose the Marquis of Esmond's daughter and heiress could not have found other stepfathers for her sons than a mere provincial surveyor? If there are any more such allusions in George's journal, I beg you skip 'em, Harry, my dear. About this piece of folly and blundering, there hath been quite talk enough already."

"'Tis a pretty sight," Harry continued, ignoring the interruption, "to see a long line of redcoats, threading through the woods or taking their ground after the march. The care against surprise is so great and constant, that we defy prowling Indians to come unawares upon us... They are such cruel villains, these French and their painted allies, that we do not think of showing them mercy. Only think, we found but yesterday a little boy scalped but yet alive in a lone house, where his parents had been attacked and murdered by the savage enemy... our General has offered a reward of five pounds for all the Indian scalps brought in."

"When our march is over, you should see our camp... Our baggage and our General's tents and guard are placed quite in the centre... We have outlying sentries by twos, by threes, by tens, by whole companies. At the least surprise, they are instructed to run in on the main body and rally round the tents and baggage, which are so arranged themselves as to be a strong fortification. Sady and I, you must know, are marching on foot now, and my horses are carrying baggage. The Pennsylvanians sent such rascally animals into camp that they speedily gave in. What good horses were left, 'twas our duty to give up: and Roxana has a couple of packs upon her back instead of her young master. She knows me right well, and whinnies when she sees me, and I walk by her side, and we have many a talk together on the march."

"July 4. To guard against surprises, we are all warned to pay especial attention to the beat of the drum; always halting when they hear the long roll beat, and marching at the beat of the long march. We are more on the alert regarding the enemy now. We have our advanced pickets doubled, and two sentries at every post. The men on the advanced pickets are constantly under arms, with fixed bayonets, all through the night, and relieved every two hours. The half that are relieved lie down by their arms, but are not suffered to leave their pickets. 'Tis evident that we are drawing very near to the enemy now. This packet goes out with the General's to Colonel Dunbar's camp, who is thirty miles behind us; and will be carried thence to Frederick, and thence to my honoured mother's house at Castlewood
