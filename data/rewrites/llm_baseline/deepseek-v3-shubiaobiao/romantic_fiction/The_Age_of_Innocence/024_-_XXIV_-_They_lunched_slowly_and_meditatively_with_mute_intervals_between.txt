They lingered over their meal, the spaces between their words as telling as the conversations themselves. The silence was not empty but alive—charged with all the things they could not say aloud. Archer steered the talk away from himself, not out of strategy but because he hungered for every detail of her life since they had last met.

She rested her chin on her clasped hands, her gaze distant as she spoke. New York, she admitted, had been kind—too kind, in fact. Its relentless hospitality had suffocated her. After the novelty faded, she found herself too *different*, too out of step with its rituals and obsessions. So she had turned to Washington, hoping for something more—more variety, more voices, more freedom.

"And Dr. Carver?" Archer asked abruptly, unable to suppress the question. "Surely you’re not still entertaining his schemes?"

A faint smile touched her lips. "That danger has passed. He wanted a wealthy wife to fund his ambitions. Medora was merely a useful advertisement for his causes."

"Causes?"

"New, radical ideas. Wild, perhaps. But at least they’re alive—not like the dead traditions we cling to." Her voice softened. "What’s the point of a new world if we only replicate the old one?"

Archer flinched. "And Beaufort? Do you discuss these things with *him*?"

"Not anymore. But he understood."

"Of course he did," Archer muttered, bitterness creeping in. "Because he isn’t one of us. We’re dull, aren’t we? No color, no character. Why *don’t* you go back?"

Her eyes darkened, but she didn’t snap back as he expected. Instead, she considered his words carefully—too carefully. When she finally spoke, it was with a quiet certainty that struck him to the core.

"I think it’s because of you."

The words hung between them, unadorned, almost indifferent to their own weight. Archer felt his face burn, but he dared not move, as if the slightest shift might shatter the moment.

She went on, her voice low. "You showed me that beneath the dullness, there’s something finer—something so delicate that even the things I once cherished seem cheap beside it." She hesitated, struggling for the right words. "I never realized before how much beauty is paid for with hardness… with sacrifice."

Archer wanted to laugh, to scoff—*Exquisite pleasures—what a price they demand!*—but the look in her eyes silenced him.

"I want to be honest," she continued. "I’ve waited for this chance to tell you what you’ve done for me—what you’ve *made* of me."

He stared at her, then let out a hollow laugh. "And what have *you* made of *me*?"

She paled. "Of you?"

"Yes. I’m the man who married one woman because another told him to."

Her breath caught. "You promised not to speak of that today."

"And you promised to see things through," he shot back.

She lowered her voice. "*Is* it a bad business—for May?"

The sound of her cousin’s name on her lips sent a pang through him. "Isn’t that what this is all about?" he said bitterly. "By your own reasoning?"

"Or," she pressed on, "if it *isn’t* worth the sacrifice—if saving others from misery isn’t enough—then everything I came back for, everything that made my old life seem barren… it’s all a lie."

He turned to face her fully. "So there’s no reason for you to stay."

Her eyes clung to his, desperate. "*Is* there no reason?"

"Not if my marriage was your great cause," he said savagely. "It’s hardly a spectacle worth sticking around for."

She said nothing.

"You showed me what real life could be," he went on, "then asked me to live a counterfeit one. It’s unbearable."

"And yet I *am* bearing it," she whispered, tears welling.

The raw vulnerability in her face struck him like a physical blow. *All this time… you too?*

Her silence was answer enough.

He wanted to close the distance between them, but something held him back—not fear, but a reverence for the fragile truth they had just spoken. To touch her now might shatter it.

"So this is all we have?" he murmured.

"Isn’t it enough?"

For a heartbeat, he hesitated. Then he crossed the room, not in a rush but with a quiet certainty. She didn’t move toward him or away—only waited, her hands lifting slightly, not to stop him but to guide him.

When his fingers closed around hers, they stood like that, suspended between longing and resignation. Her face told him everything: that she would stay, but only as long as he could endure it. That to reach for more would be to lose her entirely.

"Don’t be unhappy," she said softly, pulling away at last.

His voice was rough. "You won’t go back?"

She met his eyes. "Not yet."

Then she turned, leading the way back to the world beyond the door—where the hum of ordinary life waited, indifferent to the silent storm they had just weathered.
