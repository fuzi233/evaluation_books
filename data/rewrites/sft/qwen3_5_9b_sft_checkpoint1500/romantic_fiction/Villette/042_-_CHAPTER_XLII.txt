Man cannot read the future; love offers no oracle. Sometimes, fear constructs a phantom of doom. Those three years of silence! How I had wasted myself upon the anticipation of them! The misery they held seemed as inevitable as death. I understood the trajectory of our path; I never doubted how it would scourge us. The Juggernaut stood there, a grim, towering figure laden with a terrible weight. As he approached, sinking those broad wheels into the crushed earth, I, the prostrate devotee, felt the crushing crunch of annihilation before it happened.

Yet, strangely—and truthfully, though life mirrors it often—that anticipated destruction accounted for nearly *all* the torture. The great Juggernaut, drawn by his massive chariot, advanced slowly, loudly, and sullenly. Then, passing silently like a shadow sweeping across a noon sky, he vanished. Only a chilling dimness remained. I looked upward. Chariot and demon driver were gone, yet the votary still breathed.

Monsieur Emanuel had been absent for three years. To you who might scoff at this paradox, listen: they were the three happiest years of my existence. I began teaching; I labored, and I labored hard. I considered myself the steward of his estate, resolved to render a faithful account unto God. Students came—first burghers, then a higher class. In the midst of the second year, an unexpected turn of fortune placed an additional hundred pounds in my hands. One day, a letter arrived from England bearing that sum. It came from Mr. Marchmont, the cousin and heir of my beloved, deceased mistress. He was then convalescing from a grave illness; the money was an offering to soothe his conscience, reproached by memories of certain papers or memoranda found after my kinswoman's passing, involving names or recommendations for Lucy Snowe. Madame Barrett had given him my address. How deeply his conscience had been troubled, I never asked. I posed no inquiries, accepted the gold, and set it to work.

With this hundred pounds, I dared to take the house adjacent to mine. I would not abandon the dwelling Monsieur Paul had chosen, the home where he had departed, and where he expected to find me returning. My boarding school expanded into a pensionnat, and it flourished.

The secret of my prosperity lay less within myself, in any innate gift or power, than in a transformed state of circumstance, a wonderfully altered life, and a liberated heart. The spring that animated my energies lay far beyond the seas, upon an island in India. Upon parting, I had been left a legacy; such a thought for the present, such a hope for the future, such a motive for perseverance, for labor, for enterprise, for patience, and for courage—I *could* not falter. Few things shook me now; few matters possessed the weight to vex, intimidate, or depress me: most things delighted me; mere trifles held a charm.

Do not suppose this gentle flame burned solely on a bequeathed hope or a departing promise. A generous provider supplied abundant fuel. I was spared all cold, all scarcity; I was not allowed to fear want, nor tested by suspense. Through every vessel, he wrote; he wrote as he gave and as he loved, with open-handed, whole-hearted plenty. He wrote because he enjoyed writing; he did not withhold, because he cared not to withhold. He sat down, took pen and paper, because he loved Lucy and had much to tell her; because he was faithful and considerate, because he was tender and true. There was no sham, no deceit, nothing hollow or unreal about him. No apology ever dripped its slippery oil onto his lips; he offered no cowardly feints or paltry excuses through his pen: he would give neither a stone, nor an excuse, nor a scorpion, nor a disappointment. His letters were genuine food that nourished, living water that refreshed.

Was I grateful? God knows! I am convinced that scarce a living soul, so remembered, so sustained, treated with such constancy, honor, and nobility, could fail to remain grateful until the end.

Loyal to his own religion (for he lacked the substance of a facile apostate), he freely allowed me to keep my pure faith. He did not tease, nor tempt. He declared:

"Remain a Protestant. My little English Puritan, I love Protestantism in you. I acknowledge its severe charm. There is something in its ritual I cannot accept myself, but it is the sole creed for 'Lucy.'"

All of Rome could not instill bigotry into him, nor could the Propaganda transform him into a true Jesuit. He was born honest, not false; artless, not cunning—a freeman, not a slave. His tenderness had rendered him flexible in a priest's hands; his affection, his devotion, his sincere piety had sometimes blinded his kind eyes, causing him to abandon justice to himself to perform the work of craft and serve the ends of selfishness. Yet, these are faults so rare, so costly to their possessor to indulge, that we scarcely know if they will not one day be reckoned among jewels.

Now, the three years have passed: Monsieur Emanuel's return is fixed. It is Autumn; he shall be with me before the mists of November descend. My school thrives, my house stands ready: I have created a small library for him, filling its shelves with the books he left in my keeping; I have cultivated, out of love for him (though I was by nature no gardener), the plants he preferred, and some of them still stand in bloom. I thought I loved him when he left; I love him now in a deeper measure: he is more my own.

The sun crosses the equinox; the days shorten, the leaves turn brown; but—he is coming.

Frost appears at night; November sends his fogs ahead; the wind takes its autumn sigh; but—he is coming.

The skies hang heavy and dark—a ragged sail drifts from the west; the clouds mold themselves into strange shapes—arches and broad rays; resplendent mornings arise—glorious, royal, purple as a monarch in his state; the heavens burn like fire; so wild are they, they rival battle at its fiercest—so bloody, they shame Victory in her pride. I know certain signs of the sky; I have noted them since childhood. God watch that sail! Oh! Guard it!

The wind shifts to the west. Peace, peace, Banshee—"keening" at every window! It will rise—it will swell—it shrieks out long: wherever I roam through this house tonight, I cannot quell the blast. The approaching hours strengthen it: by midnight, all sleepless watchers hear and fear a wild south-west storm. That storm roared furiously for seven days. It did not cease until the Atlantic was strewn with wreckage; it did not subside until the depths had gorge themselves full of sustenance. Not until the destroying angel of the tempest had accomplished his perfect work would he fold the wings whose breath was thunder—the tremor of whose feathers was storm.

Peace, be still! Oh! A thousand mourners, praying in agony on waiting shores, listened for that voice, but it was not spoken—not spoken until; when the hush fell, some could not feel it: until, when the sun returned, his light became night to some!

Here I pause: pause immediately. Enough has been said. Do not disturb a quiet, kind heart; let sunny imaginings preserve their hope. Let them conceive the delight of joy reborn fresh from great terror, the rapture of rescue from peril, the wonderful reprieve from dread, the fruition of return. Let them picture union and a happy, succeeding life.

Madame Beck prospered all the days of her life; so did Père Silas; Madame Walravens fulfilled her ninetieth year before she died. Farewell.
