For long, taut seconds after that ghoulish, echoing laugh died away against the rock walls, Tars Tarkas and I stood frozen, every sense straining for the next sign of threat. No further sound broke the thick silence, no shadow shifted within the range of our vision.

At last the great Thark chief laughed softly, that strange, alien huff of mirth his kind reserve for horrors that would drive Earth men to tears or retching. It is no hysterical titter, but unfeigned pleasure at the sight of terror and suffering—emotion I had watched consume them in rolling, gasping fits of glee as they watched women and children writhe out their lives under the tortures of Barsoom’s hellish Great Games.

I grinned back up at him, hand resting light on the hilt of my long sword. Here there was far more use for a stiff spine than a trembling chin.
“What do you make of all that?” I asked. “Where in the name of all the old gods of Mars are we?”
He blinked at me in open surprise.
“Where are we? You mean to say John Carter, you do not recognize this place?”
“That I am still on Barsoom is the only thing I can be half sure of,” I told him. “If I hadn’t run into you and those giant white apes I would not have guessed even that. The things I’ve seen today are no more like the Barsoom I knew ten years ago than they are like my own home world. No, Tars Tarkas, I have no idea where we are.”
“Where were you all that time, after you wrenched open the great gates of the atmosphere plant? The keeper was dead, the engines had stalled, and every Barsoomian who hadn’t already asphyxiated was minutes from dying. We searched the whole planet for you for years. The Jeddak of Helium, your princess Dejah Thoris, offered rewards so vast that even royal princes joined the hunt. When no trace of you ever turned up, there was only one conclusion: you had taken the long pilgrimage down the mysterious River Iss, to wait for Dejah at the edge of the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor.”
I cut him off, my voice tight. “Thank God. I didn’t dare ask. I left her in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors that night, half dead, so far gone I barely dared hope I could reach the atmosphere plant in time before her spirit slipped away forever. She lives?”
“She lives, John Carter.”
“Then tell me where we are now,” I pressed.
“We are where I expected to find you—and another. Long ago you heard the story of the human woman who taught this cruel Thark what love was, the woman who was tortured to death by the brute Tal Hajus for that love. I thought she waited for me on the shores of Korus. You, too, the only man ever to teach me what friendship means— I thought you wandered the carefree paradise of the Valley Dor. The two people I wanted most in all the universe, at the end of the pilgrimage I knew I would have to make one day. A month ago, when the time Dejah Thoris had long clung to—when she swore you would return to her—was up, I could bear the waiting no longer, and set out down the Iss. Do you understand now where you stand?”
“Then that was the River Iss, emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus? This is the so-called heaven every Barsoomian dreams of, the end of a life of war and strife?”
“This is what they call the Valley of Love and Peace,” he said, his voice sharp with cold, bitter irony. “This is Barsoom’s Heaven.”
I laid a hand on his shoulder. The weight of that disillusionment—ten thousand years of sacred tradition crumbling to ash before his eyes—was a blow no mortal could be blamed for reeling under. “I am sorry,” was all I could say.
“Think of the billions of souls that have drifted down that river since the dawn of time,” he snarled. “Voluntarily, to fall straight into the clutches of those plant men and white apes that attacked us today. There is an old legend, you know, of a red man who once came back from the Valley Dor, sailed back up the Iss, screaming blasphemies about brutes that devoured pilgrims on the very shores where they expected peace. They stoned him to death, per tradition, for returning from the River of Mystery. Now we know he told nothing but the truth. But what good is that? Even if we escape, they will kill us for blasphemers too. We are trapped between the wild thoat of certain death behind us and the mad zitidar of certain death ahead.”
“On Earth we call that between the devil and the deep sea,” I said, and I couldn’t help a smile at our absurd, deadly predicament. “There is nothing for it but to meet whatever comes. Whatever kills us will pay for it dearly. No one—white ape, plant man, red or green Martian—will ever be able to brag that they took John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, without losing dozens of their own to the pair of us.”
The grim joke made him laugh, that rare, unguarded burst of true joy that made him stand so far apart from the rest of his cruel, cold kind.
“But you,” he said, turning to me at last. “If you were not here in the Dor for all these years, where were you?”
“Back on Earth,” I said. “Ten long, Earth years of living death, pining for this harsh, brutal planet I love more than the one I was born on. Ten years of wondering every single day if Dejah Thoris was dead, and now that I know she is alive, fate has dumped me right in the one spot on all Barsoom there is no escape from. Even if we could get out, the price would snuff out every last chance I ever have of seeing her again. Barely half an hour before I saw you fighting those plant men on the riverbank, I was standing in the moonlight on the banks of the Hudson, in New York, on Earth. Do you believe me?”
“I believe,” Tars Tarkas rumbled. “Though I cannot understand it.”
As we spoke I was scanning the chamber. It was roughly two hundred feet long, a hundred wide, hewn straight from the solid golden cliff rock. A single tiny radium illuminator set in the ceiling cast dim, soft light over inlaid patches of polished ruby, emerald, and diamond dotted across the walls and ceiling. The floor was some unknown glass-hard material, worn as smooth as polished crystal by tens of thousands of feet that had crossed it over eons. Only two doorways broke the walls: the one we had come through, which we knew was locked behind us, and another set dead centre in the far wall.
I stepped toward that far door, reaching out to find its hidden control button, when that same cruel, mocking laugh pealed out so close to my ear I flinched back, my fingers tightening white around the hilt of my great sword.
A hollow, droning voice chanted from the far corner of the room, with no visible source: “There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope.”
My eyes snapped straight to the corner, but there was no one there. Cold shivers prickled down my spine, the short hairs at the base of my neck standing up stiff as a hound’s when it sees something in the dark no human can make out.
I strode toward the spot the voice seemed to come from, but it cut off before I got there. Then a shrill, piercing shriek erupted from the opposite end of the chamber.
“Fools! Fools! Do you think you can defy the eternal laws of life and death? Would you cheat the great Issus, Goddess of Death, of her rightful dues? Did not her messenger, the ancient river Iss, carry you of your own free will straight to her domain? Do you think she will ever let you escape? In all the endless ages, only one soul ever fled the Valley Dor.
“Go back the way you came, to the quick mercy of the children of the Tree of Life or the fangs of the great white apes. There your suffering will end fast. But if you are fool enough to try to thread the Golden Cliffs of the Mountains of Otz, past the fortresses of the Holy Therns, you will meet the most terrible form of death Issus can devise. A torment so fiendish that even the Holy Therns, who invented every form of life and death, will turn their eyes away and block their ears to the screams of their victim.
“Go back, O fools, the way you came.”
The mocking laugh burst out again, from yet another new corner of the room.
“Uncanny as hell,” I muttered to Tars Tarkas.
“What do we do?” he grumbled. “We cannot fight thin air. I would rather turn back and face a foe I can drive my blade into, than keep dancing to the tune of disembodied voices. At least then I can sell my life dear before I vanish into that eternal nothingness that seems to be the best any mortal can hope for.”
“If we can’t fight thin air, Tars Tarkas,” I snapped, “thin air can’t fight us either. I’ve fought and beaten thousands of hardened warriors in my time, I’m not going to be scared off by noise. And neither are you.”
“Unseen voices could belong to unseen creatures wielding unseen blades,” he argued.
“Rot,” I said, loud enough for every hidden listener in the walls to hear. “Those things are as flesh and blood as we are. Their blood runs red, same as ours. The fact that they hide instead of facing us blade to blade proves they’re mortal, and cowards. John Carter doesn’t run screaming from a foe that won’t even show his face.”
I was sick of their cheap, nerve-racking tricks. It was obvious what they were doing: scaring us back out into the valley to be torn apart by the beasts there, so they wouldn’t have to bother dealing with us themselves.
For a long stretch there was silence. Then a soft, stealthy scrape behind me made me spin around fast. A huge, ten-legged banth—Barsoom’s lion—was creeping sinuously toward me, mane of stiff bristles standing up along its thick neck, rows of needle-sharp fangs glinting in its open jaws, bulging green eyes blazing.
It lashed its tail against its yellow sides, then let out that deafening roar that freezes lesser prey into paralysis for the split second before it pounces. It launched its massive bulk at me.
But its roar did nothing to me. It met cold steel, driven straight through its heart before its jaws could close on my flesh. I wrenched my blade free, and turned to see Tars Tarkas standing over a second dead banth at his feet. Before I could call out, another one came leaping across the room at my back.
For the better part of an hour, they kept coming. One hideous banth after another, springing at us from nowhere, screaming in rage and pain as our blades tore into their vitals, gushing hot, real blood over the polished stone floor. There was nothing supernatural about these foes, and we both revelled in the tangible fight after the unnerving mind games.
I noticed none of them ever appeared while we were facing that spot. We never saw one materialize out of thin air. The trick was obvious: hidden, perfectly fitted secret doorways in the walls.
Then I spotted the small hand mirror hanging on the back of Tars Tarkas’ leather harness, right between his shoulders and his waist, a commonplace little accessory most Martian warriors wore. I glanced into it as he stood over a freshly killed banth, and what I saw made me hiss at him not to move a single muscle.
He froze, like a statue, as I watched the trick. A section of the wall behind me was pivoting on hidden axles, turning along with a perfectly circular section of the floor in front of it, fitted so tightly into the surrounding stone there had been no visible crack in the dim light. As it half swung open, I saw what was on the other side: a brightly lit chamber, where several red Martians were chained to the far wall, guarded by a wicked-faced man who was not red, nor green—he was white, like me, with a shock of long flowing yellow hair, directing the movement of the secret panel. Dozens of banths and other ferocious beasts were chained up back there with the prisoners.
The second the panel swung far enough to spit a new banth out into our chamber, I whispered my discovery to Tars Tarkas. “They’re watching us through the walls,” I muttered low, so the hidden listeners couldn’t hear. As long as we both stood facing opposite ends of the room, no new attacks came, confirming it.
I hatched a plan, whispered it fast into his ear. The great Thark grunted his assent, and we moved slowly: he backed toward the wall I was watching, I walked slowly in front of him, his back mirror giving me perfect view of the pivoting panel. When we were ten feet away, I stopped him, turned my back fully to the wall, and stared hard at the mirror.
It didn’t take long. The golden wall section began to turn. I gave Tars Tarkas the prearranged signal, and both of us leaped. I sailed straight through the swinging opening, landing in the hidden chamber face to face with the blonde white man I’d spotted earlier. He was my height, heavily muscled, armed to the teeth with two swords, a dagger, and a radium revolver at his hip.
He didn’t care about Barsoomian honour rules that said you fight a swordsman only with a sword. He drew his revolver fast, but an upward swing of my long sword sent it spinning out of his hand before he could pull the trigger. He drew his own long blade, and we duelled for our lives.
He was a brilliant swordsman, sharp and well practiced. I hadn’t held a sword in ten long Earth years, but it took only minutes for the old reflexes to flood back. Soon he was grunting in rage, his guard slipping, a dozen small cuts dripping blood down his face and arms.
“Who are you, white man?” he hissed. “You’re no red Martian from the outer world, you’re not one of us—”
I gambled on a wild guess. “What if I am from the Temple of Issus?”
His face went dead white under the blood. “May fate forbid that!”
The reaction told me everything. The people here feared the Temple of Issus more than anything, but I didn’t have time to puzzle it out. I drove my sword straight through his ribs, and he crumpled to the floor.
The chained prisoners had watched the whole duel in dead silence, nothing but the clash of blades echoing through the room. But the second he fell, a woman prisoner screamed a warning.
“Turn! Behind you!”
I spun. A second white man had crept out of a dark corridor behind me, sword raised to strike. The secret panel had snapped shut, Tars Tarkas was nowhere to be seen. I was exhausted. I had fought nonstop for hours, I hadn’t eaten in twenty four hours, hadn’t slept longer than that. I was bone tired, my vision swimming.
This new foe was even better with a sword than the first. He backed away, parrying every wild, desperate strike I threw at him, wearing me down until I could barely stand. He backed me in a circle right over the body of his dead comrade, then rushed me. My heel caught on the corpse, I toppled backward, my head cracking hard against the stone floor. The blow cleared my foggy brain, my rage flaring hot enough to tear him apart with my bare hands— and as I pushed myself up, my right hand brushed a piece of cold metal lying on the floor.
