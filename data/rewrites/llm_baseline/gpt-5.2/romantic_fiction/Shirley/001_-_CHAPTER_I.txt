Of late years an abundant shower of curates has fallen upon the north of England; they lie thick as summer gnats above the hills, and each parish seems to breed them as readily as it breeds thistles. But not of late years are we about to speak. We will go back to the beginning of this century—back before the air was scorched with certain modern heats, before the day grew brassy and the ear grew weary. We will slip, as one slips from a harshly clattering street into a cool pantry, out of the noon and into an earlier hour; evade the dust, and dream of dawn.

And if, from this prelude, you suppose a romance is being warmed for you—something sugared, spiced, and sent up in a fine froth—be undeceived. You look for sentiment, poetry, reverie; you demand passion, stimulus, and melodrama. Lower your appetite; take it down to a plainer board. Something real, cool, and solid is set before you, as unromantic as Monday morning, when the first sound in the house is the scrape of a shovel at the hearth and the mutter of one who knows work waits. It is not positively affirmed you shall never taste excitement—perhaps, towards the middle and close of the meal, the dish may quicken; but it is resolved the first course shall be such as even a strict churchman might swallow on Good Friday: cold lentils with vinegar, no oil; unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roast lamb.

In eighteen-hundred-eleven and twelve that affluent rain of curates had not descended. Young Levites were scarce then: there was no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates’ Society stretching a purse to tired rectors, and bidding them hire fresh vigour from Oxford or Cambridge. The present successors of apostles—disciples of Dr. Pusey, as some call them, and tools of the Propaganda, as others name them—were, at that date, still under cradle-blankets, or undergoing regeneration by nursery-baptism in wash-hand basins. A stranger, looking on the Italian-ironed double frills of a net-cap, could not have guessed it framed the brows of a preordained and specially sanctified successor of St. Paul; nor, in the folds of a long night-gown, have foreseen the white surplice which, in after-years, would be lifted to cruel effect over simple souls, and waved—shirt-like, conspicuous—higher than any reading-desk old-fashioned vicars had ever allowed.

Yet even then, in those lean days, curates existed: the plant was rare, but it might be found. A favoured district of the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a circuit of twenty miles. You shall see them. Step into a neat garden-house on the skirts of Whinbury; pass through the little passage where the draught smells faintly of damp woollen, clean soap, and the day’s cooking; enter the parlour. There they sit at dinner. Allow me to introduce them: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne’s lodgings, in the house of John Gale, a small clothier. Mr. Donne has invited his brethren to regale with him. You and I will join the party, observe what is to be observed, and listen to what is to be heard. At present they are only eating; while they eat, we will speak aside.

They are in the bloom of youth, and youth brings activity as naturally as a quick fire brings a hiss from wet logs. Their moping old vicars would gladly have seen that activity poured into the channel of pastoral duty—spent in superintending schools, visiting the sick, watching over the poor. But the youthful Levites find this dull. They prefer lavishing their energies on a proceeding which, to other eyes, looks heavy with ennui, cursed with monotony—more like a weaver’s endless treadle than like any pleasure. It is a rushing backwards and forwards amongst themselves: not a round, but a triangle of visits, kept up through winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Season and weather make no difference; with an unintelligible zeal they dare snow and hail, wind and rain, mire and dust, to go and dine, or drink tea, or sup with each other.

What draws them, it would be difficult to say. It is not friendship, for whenever they meet they quarrel. It is not religion—the thing is never named among them; theology may be touched on now and then, but piety never. It is not the love of eating and drinking: each might have as good a joint and pudding at home, tea as potent, toast as succulent, as any his brother can offer. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their landladies—affirm “it’s just for naught else but to give folk trouble.” By “folk,” of course, the good women mean themselves; for indeed they are kept in a continual fry by this system of mutual invasion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are at dinner. Mrs. Gale waits on them; she carries the plates steadily, but there is a spark from the hot kitchen fire in her eye. The privilege of inviting a friend to a meal occasionally, without additional charge—a privilege included in the terms on which she lets her lodgings—has, in her judgement, been more than sufficiently exercised. The present week is but at Thursday, and on Monday Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield, came to breakfast and stayed dinner; on Tuesday Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely came to tea, remained to supper, occupied the spare bed, and favoured her with their company to breakfast on Wednesday morning; now, on Thursday, they are both here at dinner, and she is almost certain they will stay all night. C’en est trop, she would have said, if French had lain ready on her tongue; but Yorkshire, sharper and plainer, served her as well.

The room is warm with the smell of roast beef just cut, of suet and browned batter from the Yorkshire pudding, of boiled greens and carrots breathing a sweet wet earthiness from their tureens. Knives rasp upon plates; chairs shift; the beer, poured, gives no brisk song of bubbles, only a dull glug. Mr. Sweeting minces his slice of beef with delicate vexation and complains it is tough. Mr. Donne says the beer is flat. That is the worst: if they were only civil, Mrs. Gale would bear the throng; if they seemed but satisfied, she would not mind the labour. But “these young parsons is so high and so scornful, they set everybody beneath their fit.” They treat her with less than civility because she keeps no servant and does her house-work herself, as her mother did before her; and they are always speaking against Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire folk. By that very token Mrs. Gale does not believe one of them is a real gentleman, or of gentle kin. “The old parsons is worth the whole lump of college lads; they know what belongs to manners, and is kind to high and low.”

“More bread!” cries Mr. Malone, with the prolonged tone that proclaims, in two syllables, the land of shamrocks and potatoes.

Mrs. Gale hates Mr. Malone more than either of the other two; but she fears him also. He is tall, strongly built, with Irish legs and arms that look made for a spade or a cudgel, and a face as genuinely national—not the frank Milesian type, not Daniel O’Connell’s style, but that high-featured, North-American-Indian cast which belongs to a certain class of Irish gentry, carrying a petrified and proud look fitter for the owner of slaves than the landlord of free peasantry. His father called himself a gentleman—poor, in debt, and besottedly arrogant; his son was like him.

Mrs. Gale offered the loaf.

“Cut it, woman,” said her guest.

And the “woman” cut it accordingly. Had she followed her inclination, she would have cut the parson too; her Yorkshire soul revolted from his manner of command.

The curates have good appetites; and though the beef is “tough,” they eat a great deal of it, sawing through the fibres, forcing each mouthful down with tolerable allowances of “flat beer.” The Yorkshire pudding vanishes, the vegetables follow, as leaves before locusts. The cheese receives distinguished marks of attention. Then a spice-cake comes by way of dessert—dark, clove-scented, moist, with currants studding the slice; it vanishes like a vision, and is no more found.

Its elegy is chanted, not in the parlour, but in the kitchen. Abraham, Mrs. Gale’s son and heir, a youth of six summers, had reckoned upon the reversion of that cake; and when his mother brings down the empty platter, he lifts up his voice and weeps sore—as if some ancient custom had been broken and some little household language of treats and promises had died in an instant.

Upstairs, meanwhile, the curates sip their wine, a liquor of unpretending vintage and moderately enjoyed. Mr. Malone would much rather have had whisky; but Mr. Donne, being an Englishman, does not keep that beverage. As they sip, they argue—not on politics, philosophy, literature (topics which are now, as ever, totally without interest for them), not even on theology practical or doctrinal, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline: bubbles of talk that seem empty to all save themselves. Mr. Malone contrives to secure two glasses where his brethren content themselves with one, and grows by degrees hilarious after his fashion—that is, a little insolent, rudely brilliant in his own esteem, and clamorously amused with his own sayings.

Each companion becomes, in turn, his butt. He has a stock of jokes which he serves out regularly on convivial occasions, seldom varying his wit; he never considers himself monotonous, and cares not what others think. Mr. Donne he favours with hints about his extreme meagreness, allusions to his turned-up nose, cutting sarcasms on a threadbare chocolate surtout the curate affects whenever it rains or seems likely to rain, and criticisms on a choice set of cockney phrases and pronunciations which certainly deserve remark for the finish they lend his style.

Mr. Sweeting is bantered about his stature—he is a little man, a mere boy in height and breadth beside the athletic Malone; rallied on his musical accomplishments—he plays the flute and sings hymns “like a seraph,” some young ladies think; sneered at as “the ladies’ pet;” teased about his mamma and sisters, for whom poor Mr. Sweeting retains a lingering regard, and of whom he is foolish enough to speak in the presence of the priestly Paddy, from whose anatomy the bowels of natural affection seem to have been omitted.

The victims meet these attacks each in his own way: Mr. Donne with a stilted self-complacency and half-sullen phlegm, the sole props of a somewhat rickety dignity; Mr. Sweeting with the easy indifference of a light disposition which never professed to have dignity to maintain.

When Malone’s raillery grows too offensive—which it soon does—Donne and Sweeting join in an attempt to turn the tables. They ask how many boys shouted “Irish Peter!” after him along the road (his name is Peter—the Rev. Peter Augustus Malone); whether it is the mode in Ireland for clergymen to carry loaded pistols in their pockets and a shillelah in their hands when they make pastoral visits; what is the signification of such words as vele, firrum, hellum, storrum (so he pronounces veil, firm, helm, storm); and employ such other methods of retaliation as the innate refinement of their minds suggests.

This will not do. Malone, being neither good-natured nor phlegmatic, is presently in a towering passion. He vociferates; he gesticulates. Donne and Sweeting laugh. He reviles them as Saxons and snobs at the top pitch of his high Celtic voice; they taunt him with being the native of a conquered land. He menaces rebellion in the name of his “counthry,” vents bitter hatred against English rule; they speak of rags, beggary, pestilence. The little parlour becomes an uproar. Above, the sound is not merely speech, but a brutal percussion: Malone’s fist meeting the mahogany table with repeated and sonorous contact; decanters and glasses starting and jingling at each assault; the allied English disputants mocking with sharp laughter; the isolated Hibernian stuttering out declamation.

Below, in the kitchen, Mr. and Mrs. Gale sit by their fire, listening as people listen to a storm they know will pass without flooding the house. In such a household, sound has its own weather. The quarrel above is loud, but familiar; it ruffles the air, yet promises no lasting damage. They know these clerical tempests: no curate dines or takes tea with another without some exercise of the sort; and on whatever terms they part to-night, they will meet to-morrow as the best friends in the world.

As they sit thus, a foot is heard on the outer door-step, distinct through the general din; then the knocker quivers to a sharp appeal—clean, peremptory, like a note struck on iron.

Mr. Gale goes and opens.

“Whom have you upstairs in the parlour?” asks a voice—a rather remarkable voice, nasal in tone, abrupt in utterance.

“O Mr. Helstone, is it you, sir? I could hardly see you for the darkness; it is so soon dark now. Will you walk in, sir?”

“I want to know first whether it is worth my while walking in. Whom have you upstairs?”

“The curates, sir.”

“What! all of them?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Been dining here?”

“Yes, sir.”

“That will do.”

With these words, a person enters—a middle-aged man in black. He walks straight across the kitchen to the inner door, opens it, inclines his head forward, and stands listening. There is much to hear; the noise above is just then louder than ever.

“Hey!” he ejaculates to himself; then turns to Mr. Gale. “Have you often this sort of work?”

Mr. Gale, having been churchwarden, is indulgent to the clergy.

“They’re young, you know, sir—they’re young,” he says deprecatingly.

“Young! They want caning. Bad boys—bad boys! And if you were a Dissenter, John Gale, instead of being a good Churchman, they’d do the like—they’d expose themselves; but I’ll—”

He finishes the sentence by action. He passes through the inner door, draws it after him, and mounts the stair. The sound of his steps is steady, measured, as if he took the stairs for a parade-ground. He pauses again at the upper room, listens a few moments, then makes entrance without warning and stands before the curates.

They are silent; transfixed. The invader too, for an instant, seems to take the measure of the scene.

He is short of stature, but straight of port; upon broad shoulders he bears a hawk’s head—beak and eye—and above it a Rehoboam or shovel hat, which he does not think it necessary to lift. He folds his arms on his chest and surveys the young men—if friends they are—at leisure.

“What!” he begins, and now his voice is no longer nasal but deep—more than deep—made purposely hollow and cavernous, as if he spoke from a pulpit carved in stone. “What! has the miracle of Pentecost been renewed? Have the cloven tongues come down again? Where are they? The sound filled the whole house just now. I heard the seventeen languages in full action: Parthians and Medes and Elamites; the dwellers in Mesopotamia, and in Judea and Cappadocia, in Pontus and Asia, Phrygia and Pamphylia, in Egypt and in the parts of Libya about Cyrene; strangers of Rome, Jews and proselytes, Cretes and Arabians. Every one of these must have had its representative in this room two minutes since.”

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Helstone,” begins Mr. Donne, recovering himself with a stiff civility. “Take a seat, pray, sir. Have a glass of wine?”

His civilities receive no answer. The falcon in the black coat proceeds.

“What do I talk about the gift of tongues? Gift, indeed! I mistook the chapter, and book, and Testament—gospel for law, Acts for Genesis, Jerusalem for the plain of Shinar. It was no gift, but the confusion of tongues which has gabbled me deaf as a post. You—apostles? What! you three? Certainly not; three presumptuous Babylonish masons—neither more nor less!”

“I assure you, sir, we were only having a little chat together over a glass of wine after a friendly dinner—settling the Dissenters!” puts in Donne.

“Oh! settling the Dissenters, were you? Was Malone settling the Dissenters? It sounded to me much more like settling his co-apostles. You were quarrelling together, making almost as much noise—you three alone—as Moses Barraclough, the preaching tailor, and all his hearers are making in the Methodist chapel down yonder, where they are in the thick of a revival. I know whose fault it is. It is yours, Malone.”

“Mine, sir?” Malone asks, affronted.

“Yours, sir. Donne and Sweeting were quiet before you came, and would be quiet if you were gone. I wish when you crossed the Channel you had left your Irish habits behind you. Dublin student ways won’t do here. The proceedings which might pass unnoticed in a wild bog and mountain district in Connaught will, in a decent English parish, bring disgrace on those who indulge in them, and—what is far worse—on the sacred institution of which they are merely the humble appendages.”

There is a certain dignity in the little elderly gentleman’s rebuke, though it is not, perhaps, the tenderness most appropriate to correction. Mr. Helstone, standing straight as a ramrod, looking keen as a kite, has, despite hat and coat and gaiters, more the air of a veteran officer chiding subalterns than of a venerable priest exhorting sons in the faith. Gospel mildness, apostolic benignity, have never breathed their influence over that brown, cut visage; but firmness has fixed the features, and sagacity has carved her lines about them.

“I met Supplehough,” he continues, “plodding through the mud this wet night, going to preach at Milldean opposition shop. As I told you, I heard Barraclough bellowing in the midst of a conventicle like a possessed bull; and I find you, gentlemen, tarrying over your half-pint of muddy port wine, and scolding like angry old women. No wonder Supplehough should have dipped sixteen adult converts in a day—which he did a fortnight since; no wonder Barraclough, scamp and hypocrite as he is, should attract all the weaver-girls in their flowers and ribbons to see how much harder are his knuckles than the wooden brim of his tub; as little wonder that you, when left to yourselves, without your rectors—myself, and Hall, and Boultby—to back you, should too often perform the holy service of our Church to bare walls, and read your bit of a dry discourse to the clerk, the organist, and the beadle. But enough. I came to see Malone. I have an errand unto thee, O captain!”

“What is it?” Malone inquires, discontented. “There can be no funeral to take at this time of day.”

“Have you any arms about you?”

“Arms, sir?—yes, and legs.” And he advances the mighty members with a kind of coarse humour.

“Bah! weapons I mean.”

“I have the pistols you gave me yourself. I never part with them. I lay them ready cocked on a chair by my bedside at night. I have my blackthorn.”

“Very good. Will you go to Hollow’s Mill?”

“What is stirring at Hollow’s Mill?”

“Nothing as yet, nor perhaps will be; but Moore is alone there. He has sent all the workmen he can trust to Stilbro’; there are only two women left about the place. It would be a nice opportunity for any of his well-wishers to pay him a visit, if they knew how straight the path was made before them.”

“I am none of his well-wishers, sir. I don’t care for him.”

“Soh! Malone, you are afraid.”

“You know me better than that. If I really thought there was a chance of a row I would go; but Moore is a strange, shy man, whom I never pretend to understand; and for the sake of his sweet company only I would not stir a step.”

“But there is a chance of a row; if a positive riot does not take place—of which, indeed, I see no signs—yet it is unlikely this night will pass quite tranquilly. You know Moore has resolved to have new machinery, and he expects two wagon-loads of frames and shears from Stilbro’ this evening. Scott, the overlooker, and a few picked men are gone to fetch them.”

“They will bring them in safely and quietly enough, sir,” Malone answers, sullenly confident.

“Moore says so, and affirms he wants nobody. Some one, however, he must have, if it were only to bear evidence in case anything should happen. I call him very careless. He sits in the counting-house with the shutters unclosed; he goes out here and there after dark—wanders right up the hollow, down Fieldhead Lane, among the plantations—as if he were the darling of the neighbourhood; or—being, as he is, its detestation—bore a charmed life, as they say in tale-books. He takes no warning from the fate of Pearson, nor from that of Armitage—shot, one in his own house and the other on the moor.”

“But he should take warning, sir, and use precautions too,” interposes Mr. Sweeting, earnest, with the quick sensitivity of one whose ear catches trouble before his eye sees it. “And I think he would if he heard what I heard the other day.”

“What did you hear, Davy?”

“You know Mike Hartley, sir?”

“The Antinomian weaver? Yes.”

“When Mike has been drinking for a few weeks together, he generally winds up by a visit to Nunnely vicarage, to tell Mr. Hall a piece of his mind about his sermons—to denounce the horrible tendency of his doctrine of works, and warn him that he and all his hearers are sitting in outer darkness.”

“Well, that has nothing to do with Moore.”

“Besides being an Antinomian, he is a violent Jacobin and leveller, sir.”

“I know. When he is very drunk, his mind is always running on regicide. Mike is not unacquainted with history, and it is rich to hear him going over the list of tyrants of whom, as he says, ‘the revenger of blood has obtained satisfaction.’ The fellow exults strangely in murder done on crowned heads, or on any head for political reasons. I have already heard it hinted he seems to have a queer hankering after Moore. Is that what you allude to, Sweeting?”

“You use the proper term, sir. Mr. Hall thinks Mike has no personal hatred of Moore. Mike says he even likes to talk to him and run after him, but he has a hankering that Moore should be made an example of. He was extolling him to Mr. Hall the other day as the mill-owner with the most brains in Yorkshire, and for that reason he affirms Moore should be chosen as a sacrifice—an oblation of a sweet savour. Is Mike Hartley in his right mind, do you think, sir?” Sweeting asks simply, as one asks about a man’s health.

“Can’t tell, Davy. He may be crazed, or he may be only crafty, or perhaps a little of both.”

“He talks of seeing visions, sir.”

“Ay. He is a very Ezekiel or Daniel for visions. He came just when I was going to bed last Friday night to describe one that had been revealed to him in Nunnely Park that very afternoon.”

“Tell it, sir. What was it?” urges Sweeting, leaning forward, hungry for the tale.

“Davy, thou hast an enormous organ of wonder in thy cranium. Malone, you see, has none. Neither murders nor visions interest him. See what a big vacant Saph he looks at this moment.”

“Saph! Who was Saph, sir?” asks Sweeting, caught as much by the strange name as by the promised marvel.

“I thought you would not know. You may find it out. It is biblical. I know nothing more of him than his name and race; but from a boy upwards I have always attached a personality to Saph. Depend on’t he was honest, heavy, and luckless. He met his end at Gob by the hand of Sibbechai.”

“But the vision, sir?” Sweeting persists, the parlour’s earlier clamour now thoroughly stilled, as if even the house had held its breath to listen.
