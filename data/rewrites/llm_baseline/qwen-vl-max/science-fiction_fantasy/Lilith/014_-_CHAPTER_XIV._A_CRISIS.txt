For a time, my only wish was to stay with the Little Ones, to live among them in quiet joy, sheltered from the harshness of the world beyond. But slowly, other thoughts stirred within me—restless, questioning, urgent. I began to feel that I was meant for more than mere companionship; that I carried within me a purpose not yet fulfilled. Was I truly only a caretaker, a gentle giant to feed and protect? Or was there something deeper, something I ought to uncover?

I realized then that I stood in a world unlike any I had known—a realm woven from strange laws and hidden truths. If I wished to repay the kindness of these children, if I wished to be more than a guest in their lives, I must learn more than they could teach me. I must understand the nature of their existence, their origins, the mystery of their stunted growth. And to do that, I would need freedom—to wander, to seek answers beyond the safety of their grove.

It seemed impossible that such beings, so pure and radiant, could be held back by some unseen force. How could life and law clash so violently that perfection required suppression? That growth itself was feared as a curse? Yet the evidence was plain: the Little Ones did not grow. Lona, who appeared the eldest, no more than fifteen, had long overseen a throng of creatures who, though childlike in form and manner, looked upon her as mother. They knew nothing of time, no sense of age. To them, existence was eternal, unchanging. Lona believed she had always been. She embodied both love and law, wisdom without knowledge, guidance without history.

But what I saw as ignorance might simply be my own blindness. Still, one thing was clear: she feared growth above all else. The thought of becoming “bad giants” haunted her—monsters like those who roamed the edges of their world. And so, she guarded them fiercely, keeping them small, innocent, safe.

Yet I could not help but wonder: were they truly safe? Were they growing at all? Or had their development been halted by some unnatural power? I longed to teach them—the beauty of numbers, the rhythm of music, the art of writing down their delicate songs before they vanished into silence. But how could I guide them if I did not first understand them? Knowledge might corrupt the weak—but surely it could uplift the good.

I decided I must leave, at least for a time. Not to abandon them, but to seek understanding. Only then could I return with wisdom to offer. But my departure was not to be peaceful.

One day, while working near the tree, I told them of my desire to journey again—not out of fear, but out of longing to know more. I said I had stayed because of my love for them, not because I was trapped.

At once, they surged toward me—rushing, climbing, piling upon me like a storm of tiny bodies. Three nestled in my arms, two clung to my shoulders, one stood upright on my head, others wrapped around my legs, hands gripping my arms, feet scrambling up my sides. I was buried beneath a joyful avalanche.

And then—silence.

Not one noticed the shadow until it loomed over us. With a cry—“Take care, good giant!”—they scattered like startled birds. They dropped from me, fled up the tree, vanished into the branches. In an instant, the bad giant rounded the trunk and struck me hard across the head with a stick. I fell to the ground, unconscious.

When I awoke, night had fallen. Pale stars blinked above, waiting for the moon. My head throbbed. Thirst burned my throat. I turned onto my side—and the moment my ear touched the earth, I heard water gurgle nearby. The sound made me ache.

Then, suddenly, I was surrounded. Silent children pressed close. Soft fruits touched my lips—delicious, cool, life-giving. One after another, they fed me until the thirst passed.

But then—new sounds. Whispers. Sobs.

I tried to rise. Instantly, a pile of small bodies formed behind me, supporting me. With their strength, I stood.

“You must go,” they said. “The bad giants will come when they see you hurt. They’ll trample you.”

“I think I must,” I replied.

“Go and grow strong,” they urged. “Come back soon.”

“I will,” I said—and sat again.

“No,” whispered Lona, kneeling beside me. “You must go now.”

A boy stepped forward. “I listened at his door,” he said. “He told his wife he found you idle, talking to moles and squirrels. When he hit you, they tried to kill him. He called you a wizard. Said you must be destroyed, or peace will never come.”

I nodded. “I will go. And I’ll return when I know how to help you grow stronger.”

“We don’t want to grow,” they answered, solemn. “We won’t become bad giants. We are strong enough already. You don’t know how strong we are.”

There was no convincing them. Their hearts were fixed. So I rose, and walked slowly up the valley slope.

They followed—forming a procession. Some led, some walked beside me, helping me. Others trailed behind, feeding me along the way.

“You are broken,” they said. “Red juice has run out. Put some in.”

We reached the edge of the valley just as the moon lifted her face above the horizon.

“She comes to watch over you,” Lona said. “She will show you the way.”

As we walked, I asked about the world beyond. They spoke of a great place ruled by a giant-girl queen. I asked if it was a city. They didn’t know. They couldn’t say how far, nor which direction, nor her name. Only that she hated them—wanted to kill them, but could not find them.

“How do you know?” I asked.

Lona said, “I have always known.”

“If she comes,” she added, “we must hide deeply.”

I told them I would go to her, ask why she hated them.

“No! No!” they cried. “She will kill you, good giant! She is a terrible witch—awful, awful!”

I asked where I should go instead.

Beyond the baby-forest, they said, lay a smooth green land—soft underfoot, free of rocks and trees. It stretched toward where the moon came from.

“But how do I reach it?”

“The moon will tell you,” they said.

We climbed the second branch of the riverbed. When the moon reached its zenith, they stopped.

“We’ve never gone this far from our trees,” they murmured.

“Watch your path,” Lona warned. “See inside your eyes so you can find your way back.”

One girl added, “Beware the giant-woman in the desert. You’ve heard of her?”

“No,” I said.

“Then don’t go near her. She is called the Cat-woman. Awfully ugly. And she SCRATCHES.”

The larger ones paused. Then, slowly, they turned and walked away.

The smaller ones ran back first. Then the rest, glancing at me with quiet gravity.

Last, Lona held up the baby. She kissed me gently. Gazed into my eyes. Whispered, “The Cat-woman will not hurt YOU.”

Then she turned and disappeared into the shadows.

I stood still, watching them vanish under the silver light. Then I turned, heart heavy, and began my solitary journey.

Soon, their laughter echoed behind me—like distant bells, ringing through the air, bouncing off the cliffs. I turned once more. They danced ahead, carefree, untouched by sorrow.

Only Lona walked apart, cradling her child.

As I walked, I reflected on them—on their deep love for their home, their refusal to change, their innocence. Once, when I suggested leaving the bad giants’ land, they replied, “But that would be to NOT ourselves.” Place was part of their soul. Without ambition, fear, or greed, they had no reason to desire change. They knew no wrong. And except for their babies, they had never helped anyone but me.

How could they grow?

And yet—why should they?

In trying to improve their lives, might I not harm them? Might I not twist their nature, break their harmony, by imposing my world’s values? Their dread of growth—of becoming giants—might be instinctive, even wise.

To act as a savior is perilous. To bring good, one must first avoid evil. And the first step is to remove the beam from one’s own eye.
