Upon the appointed evening, Mr. Pendennis and Mr. Warrington presented themselves at Mr. Bungay’s door in Paternoster Row—not at that public mouth of the house where the booksellers’ boys, like ants out of a torn sugar-bag, streamed with sacks of Bungay’s volumes; nor where pallid aspirants hovered with manuscripts as fresh and fearful as new-laid eggs, waiting to be cracked by Sultan Bungay’s thumb. They went, instead, to the private entrance—the genteel portal by which Mrs. Bungay herself issued forth, when she rode abroad in state, settled deep among the cushions, and fired glances across at Mrs. Bacon’s windows with all the bravery of a red satin standard.

For opposite dwelt Mrs. Bacon—who was as yet a chaiseless woman; which is to say, in Flora Bungay’s lexicon, a woman without proper weapons.

On those afternoons when Flora’s splendour passed by, and jealousy in Mrs. Bacon boiled like a pot set too near the coals, Emma Bacon would fling up her drawing-room sash and appear with her four children ranged beside her like a well-fed argument. The look said, as plain as printed type: “Behold these four darlings, Flora Bungay! This is why I don’t drive. You would give a coach-and-four to have the same reason.” And thus, with those small, rosy arrows from her quiver, Emma Bacon shot at Flora, who sat in her carriage enviable and childless.

As Pen and Warrington reached Bungay’s door, a carriage and a cab drove briskly up to Bacon’s. Old Dr. Slocum descended from the first—heavily, as if he had been lowered with pulleys; his equipage was as ponderous as his style, and both made a fine sonorous impression in the Row. From the cab sprang a pair of dazzling white waistcoats, so bright they seemed to crackle.

Warrington laughed. “Bacon has his dinner-party too. That is Dr. Slocum, author of ‘Memoirs of the Poisoners.’ You would hardly have known our friend Hoolan in that gallant white waistcoat. Doolan is one of Bungay’s men—and faith, here he comes.”

Indeed Messrs. Hoolan and Doolan had travelled from the Strand in the same cab, tossing up on the way which should pay the shilling; and Mr. Doolan, alighting with the air of a man stepping out of a triumph, was in black, with large white gloves spread upon his hands like unfolded napkins. He could not help admiring them, as if they were two choice slices of something delicate laid there for his own consumption.

Within, the house porter wore an evening coat; the passage was alive with gentlemen in gloves as large as Doolan’s, but of the famous Berlin web. Hats and coats were taken away with solemnity, and names bawled up the stair as though each arrival were a dish being announced to the appetite above.

Some guests were already gathered when the three new visitors entered; but of the charming sex there was only Mrs. Bungay—in red satin, with a turban, the very image of a feast. She curtsied to each man as he came in; yet her mind was plainly elsewhere. The truth was that Mrs. Bacon’s dinner-party, next door, pricked at her like a pin through satin. No sooner had Flora received a guest than she flew back to the window embrasure to rake the opposite doorstep with her eyes and count the carriages clattering up.

When Dr. Slocum’s huge carriage, with its gaunt job-horses and its iron-jawed dignity, rolled to Bacon’s, Flora was crushed. None but hack cabs had driven to her own door that day; and a hack cab, when one is at war, is a poor flag to fly.

They were all literary gentlemen, though unknown as yet to Pen. There was Mr. Bole, the real editor of the magazine of which Mr. Wagg was the nominal chief; Mr. Trotter, who had once broken upon the world as a poet of a tragic and suicidial cast and had since subsided into Bungay’s back shops as reader; and Captain Sumph, an ex-beau reader about town, related in some indistinct manner to Literature and the Peerage. He was said to have written a book once, to have known Byron, to be connected with Lord Sumphington; and in fact the name of Byron was the perpetual seasoning of his talk, so that he could scarce speak without sprinkling his sentence with it, as one dusts a dish with pepper. “I remember poor Shelley, at school, being sent up for good for a copy of verses—every line of which I wrote, by Jove;” or “I recollect, when I was at Missolonghi with Byron, offering to bet gamba,” and so on. Pen remarked that Mrs. Bungay listened with a reverence as soft and greedy as a cat’s attention to cream; anecdotes of the aristocracy delighted her, and Captain Sumph, being a middle-aged member of that august theatre, was almost a greater man than the great Mr. Wagg himself in her eyes. Had he but arrived in his own carriage, she would have made Bungay buy any volume Captain Sumph might choose to write, even if it were nothing but his own conversation printed and bound.

Mr. Bungay went about among his guests with cordiality. “Ow are you, sir? Fine day, sir. Glad to see you ’ere, sir. Flora, my love, let me ’ave the honour of introducing Mr. Warrington to you. Mr. Warrington—Mrs. Bungay. Mr. Pendennis—Mrs. Bungay. Hope you’ve brought good appetites with you, gentlemen. You, Doolan, I know ’ave, for you’ve always ’ad a deuce of a twist.”

“Lor, Bungay!” said Mrs. Bungay.

“Faith, a man must be hard to please, Bungay, who can’t eat a good dinner in this house,” Doolan said; he winked, stroked his lean chops through his white gloves, and made appeals of friendship to Mrs. Bungay which that honest woman refused with scorn. “She couldn’t abide that Doolan,” she said afterward, with a shiver of dislike.

As they talked—Mrs. Bungay still surveying mankind from her window—there appeared a magnificent vision: an enormous grey cab-horse, advancing with the importance of a drum-major. Behind its arched neck flashed a pair of white reins held by small white gloves; above the cab-head bobbed the pale face, richly decorated with a chin-tuft, of an exiguous groom. These bright details, like garnishings on a plate, were revealed one by one to Flora’s delighted eye.

“The Honourable Percy Popjoy’s quite punctual, I declare,” she cried, and sailed to the door to be in waiting at the nobleman’s arrival.

“It’s Percy Popjoy,” Pen said, looking out and seeing an individual in extremely lacquered boots descend from the swinging cab. And it was indeed that young nobleman, Lord Falconet’s eldest son, who had come to dine with his publisher of the Row.

“He was my fag at Eton,” Warrington said. “I ought to have licked him a little more.” Pen and Popjoy had had bouts at the Oxbridge Union debates, in which Pen had had very much the better. Presently Popjoy appeared, hat under arm, with a look of indescribable good-humour and fatuity in his round, dimpled face—upon which Nature had burst out with a chin-tuft, and, exhausted by the effort, had left the rest bare.

The temporary groom of the chambers bawled, “The Honourable Percy Popjoy,” much to that gentleman’s discomposure at hearing his titles shouted like an order to the kitchen.

“What did the man want to take away my hat for, Bungay?” Popjoy asked. “Can’t do without my hat—want it to make my bow to Mrs. Bungay. How well you look, Mrs. Bungay, to-day. Haven’t seen your carriage in the Park: why haven’t you been there? I missed you; indeed, I did.”

“I’m afraid you’re a sad quiz,” said Mrs. Bungay.

“Quiz! Never made a joke in my—hullo! who’s here? How d’ye do, Pendennis? How d’ye do, Warrington? These are old friends of mine, Mrs. Bungay. I say, how the doose did you come here?”—and he turned his lacquered heels on Mrs. Bungay with a frankness which would have been less forgiven in a man without a title.

“What! do they know him?” Flora asked rapidly of Mr. Bungay, her whole manner changing, as if some new spice had been thrown into the stew of her opinion of Pen and Warrington.

“High fellers, I tell you—the young one related to all the nobility,” said Bungay; and both husband and wife ran forward smiling and bowing, to greet almost as great personages as the young lord himself—no less, indeed, than the great Mr. Wenham and the great Mr. Wagg, now announced.

Mr. Wenham entered with his usual demure look and stealthy smile, surveying the tips of his neat, shining boots, and only seldom lifting his eyes to meet those of a fellow-creature. Wagg’s white waistcoat, on the contrary, spread out with profuse brilliancy; his burly red face shone over it like a warmed joint newly basted. He liked to enter a drawing-room with a laugh, and leave it later with a joke exploding behind him. No personal calamities or distresses—of which that humourist had his share—could altogether keep his humour down; the thought of dinner rallied his great soul; and when he saw a lord, he saluted him with a pun.

Wenham went up with a smug smile and whisper to Mrs. Bungay, looked at her from under his eyes, and showed her the tips of his shoes, as if they were credentials. Wagg said she looked charming, and pushed on straight to young Popjoy, whom he called Pop, and instantly began a funny story seasoned with what the French call gros sel. He was delighted too to see Pen—shook his hand, slapped him on the back, and talked in a loud voice of Baymouth and Clavering Park and Sir Francis, and whether Pen had been to see Lady Rockminster—fine old lady, Lady Rockminster! These remarks were less for Pen than for the company, whom Wagg instructed—like a waiter reading the bill of fare—that he visited country seats and was familiar with nobility.

Wenham also shook hands with Pen; and all these scenes Mrs. Bungay marked with respectful pleasure, storing them away to be served up to Bungay afterward in the form of opinions about Mr. Pendennis’s importance—opinions from which Pen profited much more than he ever guessed.

Pen, who had read, and rather admired, some of Miss Bunion’s works, and expected to find in her a person resembling her own account of herself in the “Passion-Flower”—where she declared her youth resembled

“A violet, shrinking meanly
When blows the March wind keenly;
A timid fawn, on wild-wood lawn,
Where oak-boughs rustle greenly,”

and that her maturer beauty was something very different, but still exceedingly captivating—beheld, to his surprise and amusement, a large bony woman in a crumpled satin dress, who came creaking in with a step as heavy as a grenadier’s. Wagg instantly noted the straw clinging to the rumpled skirt and would have stooped to pick it off; but Miss Bunion disarmed criticism by seeing it herself. Setting her large foot on the straw to separate it from the dress, she stooped and picked it up, saying simply to Mrs. Bungay that she was sorry to be late, but the omnibus was very slow, and what a comfort it was to get a ride all the way from Brompton for sixpence.

Nobody laughed: the speech was uttered with such plain sincerity. The worthy woman had not the smallest notion of being ashamed of an action incidental upon her poverty.

“Is that ‘Passion-Flowers?’” Pen said to Wenham. “Why, her picture in the volume represents her as a very well-looking young woman.”

“You know passion-flowers, like all others, will run to seed,” Wenham answered. “Miss Bunion’s portrait was probably painted some years ago.”

“Well, I like her for not being ashamed of her poverty.”

“So do I,” said Mr. Wenham—who would have starved rather than have come to dinner in an omnibus—“but I don’t think she need flourish the straw about, do you, Mr. Pendennis? My dear Miss Bunion, how do you do? I was in a great lady’s drawing-room this morning, and everybody was charmed with your new volume. Those lines on the christening of Lady Fanny Fantail brought tears into the Duchess’s eyes. I said I thought I should have the pleasure of meeting you to-day, and she begged me to thank you, and say how greatly she was pleased.”

This history, told blandly, about a Duchess met that very morning, quite put poor Wagg’s dowager and baronet out of court, and placed Wenham beyond him as a man of fashion. Wenham, having thus salted the conversation to his own taste, ran on with aristocratic anecdotes, trying to draw Popjoy in—“I was telling your father this morning,” or, “I think you were present at W. house the other night when the Duke said so-and-so”—but Popjoy would not gratify him. He preferred to fall back into the window recess with Mrs. Bungay, watching the cabs at the opposite door. If he would not talk, at least he would sit where the Bacons might see that Flora had captured a nobleman for her party.

Saint Paul’s bell tolled half an hour later than the time Bungay had named; and at last the party was complete, with the exception of two guests, who now appeared—and in whom Pen was pleased to recognise Captain and Mrs. Shandon.

After making their greetings and exchanging nods of more or less recognition with most present, Pen and Warrington went up and shook hands warmly with Mrs. Shandon, who perhaps was affected to think where it was she had seen them but a few days before. Shandon was brushed up and looked pretty smart in a red velvet waistcoat and a frill, into which his wife had stuck her best brooch. In spite of Mrs. Bungay’s kindness—perhaps because of it—Mrs. Shandon felt great terror in approaching her. Indeed Flora, in red satin and bird of paradise, was more awful than ever; and it was not until she had asked, in her great voice, about the dear little gurl, that Mrs. Shandon was encouraged and ventured a reply.

“Nice-looking woman,” Popjoy whispered to Warrington. “Do introduce me to Captain Shandon, Warrington. I’m told he’s a tremendous clever fellow; and, dammy, I adore intellect, by Jove I do!” This was true: Heaven had given Popjoy little intellect of his own, but a generous faculty for admiring it in others. “And introduce me to Miss Bunion. I’m told she’s very clever too. She’s rum to look at, certainly, but that don’t matter. Dammy, I consider myself a literary man, and I wish to know all the clever fellows.”

So Mr. Popjoy and Mr. Shandon became acquainted; and the doors of the dining-room being flung open, the party entered and took their seats. Pen found himself next to Miss Bunion on one side and Mr. Wagg on the other; the truth being that Wagg fled alarmed from the vacant place by the poetess, and Pen was compelled to take it.

Miss Bunion did not talk much during dinner; but Pen remarked that she ate with a vast appetite, and never refused any supply of wine offered by the butler. Indeed, after considering Mr. Pendennis for a minute—grand airs, fashionable attire, very best chains, shirt studs, and cambric fronts—she set him down, not without reason, as a prig; and thought it better to attend to her dinner than to notice him. She told him as much later with her usual candour. “I took you for one of the little Mayfair dandies,” she said. “You looked as solemn as a little undertaker; and as I disliked, beyond measure, the odious creature on the other side of me, I thought it best to eat my dinner and hold my tongue.”

“And you did both very well, my dear Miss Bunion,” Pen answered, laughing.

“Well, so I do; but I intend to talk to you the next time a great deal: for you are neither so solemn, nor so stupid, nor so pert as you look.”

“Ah, Miss Bunion, how I pine for that next time,” Pen replied with comical gallantry—though we must return to the present dinner in Paternoster Row.

The repast was of the richest description. “What I call the florid Gothic style,” Wagg whispered to Pen in his side-wing voice. Behind the guests moved solemn men in creaking shoes and Berlin gloves, carrying on rapid undertones as they passed dishes from hand to hand, like messengers passing important papers. Doolan, attempting grandly to command one of them, cried “Waither,” and blushed when he caught his own blunder, as if he had spilt sauce on his shirt-front. Mrs. Bungay’s own footboy was lost amid those black-coated attendants, like a small sprig of parsley in a crowded tureen.

“Look at that very bow-windowed man,” Wagg said. “He’s an undertaker in Amen Corner, and attends funerals and dinners. Cold meat and hot, don’t you perceive? He’s the sham butler here; and I observe, my dear Mr. Pendennis—as you will through life—that wherever there is a sham butler at a London dinner there is sham wine. This sherry is filthy. Bungay, my boy, where did you get this delicious brown sherry?”

“I’m glad you like it, Mr. Wagg; glass with you,” said the publisher. “It’s some I got from Alderman Benning’s store, and gave a good figure for it, I can tell you. Mr. Pendennis, will you join us? Your ’ealth, gentlemen.”

“The old rogue—where does he expect to go to? It came from the public-house,” Wagg muttered. “It requires two men to carry off that sherry, ’tis so uncommonly strong. I wish I had a bottle of old Steyne’s wine here, Pendennis: your uncle and I have had many a one. He sends it about to people where he is in the habit of dining. I remember at poor Rawdon Crawley’s—Sir Pitt Crawley’s brother, Governor of Coventry Island—Steyne’s chef always came in the morning, and the butler arrived with the champagne from Gaunt House in ice-pails ready.”

“How good this is!” Popjoy said, good-naturedly, tasting everything as if it were a compliment. “You must have a cordon bleu in your kitchen.”

“O yes,” Mrs. Bungay answered, thinking he spoke of a chain, perhaps, or some noble badge she ought to have known.

“I mean a French chef,” said the polite guest.

“O yes, your lordship,” again said Flora, determined to agree, whatever the ingredient.

“Does your artist say he’s a Frenchman, Mrs. B.?” called out Wagg.

“Well, I’m sure I don’t know,” she replied.

“Because, if he does, he’s a quizzin yer,” cried Mr. Wagg; but nobody perceived the pun, and the bashful punster was disconcerted. “The dinner is from Griggs in St. Paul’s Churchyard; so is Bacon’s,” he whispered to Pen. “Bungay writes to give half-a-crown a head more than Bacon, so does Bacon. They would poison each other’s ices if they could get near them; and as for the made-dishes—they are poison. This—hum—ha—this Brimborion à la Sévigné is delicious, Mrs. B.,” he added aloud, helping himself with gusto from a dish the undertaker handed to him.

“Well, I’m glad you like it,” Mrs. Bungay answered, blushing; not knowing if the dish truly bore that name, but dimly conscious Wagg was quizzing her. Accordingly she hated Mr. Wagg with female ardour, and would have deposed him from his command over Bungay’s periodical, had not his name been great in the trade and his reputation in the land considerable.

By displacement of persons, Warrington found himself on the right of Mrs. Shandon, who sat in plain black silk with faded ornaments beside the florid publisher. The sad smile on the lady’s face moved his rough heart to pity. Nobody seemed to interest himself about her. She sat looking at her husband, who appeared abashed in the presence of some of the company. Wenham and Wagg both knew him and his circumstances. He had worked with the latter, and was immeasurably his superior in wit, genius, and acquirement; but Wagg’s star was brilliant in the world, and poor Shandon was unknown there. He could not speak against the noisy talk of the coarser and more successful man; so he drank his wine in silence—and as much of it as the people would give him.

He was under surveillance. Bungay had warned the undertaker not to fill the Captain’s glass too often or too full. It was a melancholy precaution; and the more melancholy because it was necessary. Mrs. Shandon cast alarmed glances across the table, watching that her husband did not exceed.

Abashed by the failure of his first pun—for he was impudent and easily disconcerted—Wagg kept his conversation mostly to Pen during the rest of dinner, and chiefly spoke of their neighbours. “This is one of Bungay’s grand field-days,” he said. “We are all Bungavians here. Did you read Popjoy’s novel? It was an old magazine story written by poor Buzzard years ago, forgotten until Mr. Trotter”—he nodded toward a gentleman with a large shirt-collar—“fished it out and bethought him it was applicable to the late elopement; so Bob wrote a few chapters à propos—Popjoy permitted the use of his name, and I dare say supplied a page here and there—and ‘Desperation, or the Fugitive Duchess’ made its appearance. The great fun is to examine Popjoy about his own work, of which he doesn’t know a word. I say, Popjoy, what a capital passage that is in Volume Three—where the Cardinal in disguise, after being converted by the Bishop of London, proposes marriage to the Duchess’s daughter.”

“Glad you like it,” Popjoy answered, beaming. “It’s a favourite bit of my own.”

“There’s no such thing in the whole book,” Wagg whispered to Pen. “Invented it myself. Gad! it wouldn’t be a bad plot for a high-church novel.”

“I remember poor Byron, Hobhouse, Trelawney, and myself, dining with Cardinal Mezzocaldo at Rome,” Captain Sumph began, bringing out Byron as a cook brings out the same herb for every course. “We had some Orvieto wine for dinner, which Byron liked very much. And I remember how the Cardinal regretted that he was a single man. We went to Civita Vecchia two days afterwards, where Byron’s yacht was—and, by Jove, the Cardinal died within three weeks; and Byron was very sorry, for he rather liked him.”

“A devilish interesting story, Sumph, indeed,” Wagg said.

“You should publish some of those stories, Captain Sumph, you really should. Such a volume would make our friend Bungay’s fortune,” Shandon said.

“Why don’t you ask Sumph to publish ’em in your new paper—the what-d’ye-call-’em—hay, Shandon?” bawled out Wagg.

“Why don’t you ask him to publish ’em in your old magazine, the Thingumbob?” Shandon replied.

“Is there going to be a new paper?” asked Wenham, who knew perfectly well, but was ashamed of his connection with the press, as though it were the smell of frying that clung to one’s coat.

“Bungay going to bring out a paper?” cried Popjoy, who, on the contrary, was proud of his literary reputation and acquaintances. “You must employ me. Mrs. Bungay, use your influence with him, and make him employ me. Prose or verse—what shall it be? Novels, poems, travels, or leading articles—begad. Anything or everything—only let Bungay pay me, and I’m ready—I am now, my dear Mrs. Bungay, begad now.”

“It’s to be called the Small Beer Chronicle,” growled Wagg, “and little Popjoy is to be engaged for the infantine department.”

“It is to be called the Pall Mall Gazette, sir, and we shall be very happy to have you with us,” Shandon said.

“Pall Mall Gazette—why Pall Mall Gazette?” asked Wagg.

“Because the editor was born at Dublin, the sub-editor at Cork, because the proprietor lives in Paternoster Row—and the paper is published in Catherine Street, Strand. Won’t that reason suffice you, Wagg?” Shandon said; he was getting angry now, his voice sharpening like a knife on a steel. “Everything must have a name. My dog Ponto has got a name. You’ve got a name, and a name which you deserve, more or less indeed. Why d’ye grudge the name to our paper?”

“By any other name it would smell as sweet,” said Wagg.

“I’ll have ye remember its name’s not what-d’ye-call-’em, Mr. Wagg,” Shandon said. “You know its name well enough—and—and you know mine.”
