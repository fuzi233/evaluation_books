When a traveler in north central Massachusetts takes the wrong turn at the fork of the Aylesbury pike just past Dean’s Corners, they enter a land that feels untouched by time—and perhaps by reason. The road climbs slowly, flanked by stone walls overgrown with brambles, their edges pressing inward like fingers closing around a secret. Dust clings to the ruts, and the trees lining the forested stretches loom too tall, their branches forming canopies that dim the daylight even in mid-afternoon. The wild grasses grow thick and unruly, as if nature itself refuses to be tamed here. Fields are few and thin, the soil stubbornly barren, while the scattered houses stand hunched and broken, their wooden frames sagging under decades of neglect. They all look alike—crumbling, gray, weathered into a kind of uniform decay.

There are people, but not many. When you see them, they’re still—gnarled figures perched on cracked porches or bent over fields strewn with stones, their eyes avoiding yours. They don’t speak. Not to strangers. And something about their silence feels deliberate, almost *warning*. You don’t ask for directions. You don’t stop. You just keep moving.

Then the hills rise up—round, smooth, unnaturally symmetrical. They seem too perfect, too quiet. At dusk, when the sky is clear, you might notice the rings of standing stones crowning their summits, ancient circles of rough-hewn granite that shouldn’t belong to any known culture. The gorges cut deep into the earth, black mouths swallowing light, and the bridges across them are crude, splintered things that groan underfoot. You cross them only because there’s no other way.

Beyond, marshlands stretch out, flat and wet, where fireflies blink in unnatural numbers, dancing above the reeds like tiny, restless spirits. At night, bullfrogs chant in a rhythm that doesn’t sound natural—too steady, too insistent. Whippoorwills cry from unseen places, their calls echoing long after they should have stopped. And the Miskatonic River winds through it all, narrow and silver, snaking between the hills like a living thing.

Eventually, the road leads to a covered bridge. On the other side lies Dunwich—a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs huddled between the river and the sheer face of Round Mountain. The church steeple is broken, its bell tower missing; now it houses the village store, one grimy window lit late into the evening. Most of the homes are abandoned, their windows boarded, their doors hanging loose. There’s a smell here—something faint but persistent, like damp earth and old mold, or worse: the scent of things left buried too long.

You pass through quickly. The air grows heavier. The shadows deepen. Even the silence feels watchful.

Afterward, you learn—maybe from a passing farmer, maybe from a dusty map—you’ve been through Dunwich. But no one talks about it. No signs point toward it anymore. It’s not on the maps. People avoid it, not because they know what’s there, but because they *feel* it. Something wrong. Something older than memory.

The land is beautiful, yes—by any ordinary measure. But beauty here doesn’t comfort. It unsettles. Two centuries ago, folks said it was cursed. That witches lived in the woods, that the Indians held rites atop those stone circles, calling up things from beneath the hills. They spoke of voices heard underground—names like Azazel, Beelzebub—of groans and hissing that couldn’t come from this world. In 1747, a preacher named Abijah Hoadley stood in Dunwich’s church and declared he’d heard such sounds himself—“a Rattling and Rolling, Groaning, Screeching, and Hissing,” he wrote, “such as no Things of this Earth cou’d raise up.” He vanished shortly after. His sermon survives, printed in Springfield. But no one reads it aloud.

The noises continue. Geologists shrug them off. But locals know better. They say the whippoorwills aren’t birds—they’re psychopomps, soul-hunters who sing in time with dying breaths. If they catch the spirit as it flees, they fly away chittering with laughter. If not, they fall silent—disappointed.

And the hills… the hills are always watching.

Dunwich is old. Older than the rest of New England. South of the village, you can still see the cellar walls of the Bishop house, built before 1700. The mill at the falls, constructed in 1806, is the newest structure in sight. Industry never took root. The town didn’t progress. It regressed.

The people? They’ve changed. Not just in appearance—though their faces are sharp, their eyes hollow—but in spirit. They’ve become something apart. Inbred, dull, twisted by generations of isolation and secrets. Some families—Whateleys, Bishops—still send sons to Harvard or Miskatonic University. But they rarely return. Those who do… well, they don’t stay long.

No one knows exactly what happened in 1928. But it was bad enough that the truth was buried. The town’s name was silenced. The roads were erased.

Yet legends persist. Of forbidden rites beneath the stone circles. Of rituals performed at midnight, when the stars align just so. Of shapes that crawl from the hills when the moon is full and the wind carries the scent of decay.

And sometimes, late at night, if you listen closely near the ravines, you hear it—not a sound, but a *presence*: rushing air, whispering voices, the faintest echo of a drumbeat from deep below the earth.

It’s not just a place.

It’s a wound. And it’s still bleeding.

It was in the isolated township of Dunwich, nestled against a steep hillside four miles from the village and a mile and a half from any other dwelling, that Wilbur Whateley came into the world at 5 a.m. on Sunday, February 2, 1913. The date lingered in memory—not for its calendar significance, but because it marked Candlemas, a day the people of Dunwich quietly honored under another name, and because the night before had been restless with strange sounds echoing through the hills and an unrelenting howl from every dog within hearing.

Less notable, yet no less unsettling, was the mother: Lavinia Whateley, a thirty-five-year-old albino woman of twisted features and pink, sightless eyes, living with her aged, half-mad father in a crumbling farmhouse long avoided by neighbors. She bore no known husband, yet she made no effort to deny the child—nor did she care what others whispered about his origins. Instead, she seemed strangely proud of the dark, goat-like infant who contrasted so violently with her own pallid, sickly form. She muttered often of his destiny, of powers beyond imagining, of a future too vast for mortal comprehension.

Lavinia was not one to dwell in ordinary life. She wandered alone through thunderstorms atop the hills, clutching ancient, foul-smelling books passed down through generations of Whateleys—books whose pages were eaten away by time and insects. She had never attended school, but her mind was filled with fragmented fragments of forbidden lore, taught to her by her father in hushed tones beneath flickering oil lamps. The house, long stripped of order or cleanliness, became a tomb of secrets. No doctor or midwife attended the birth—only a terrible scream, louder than the dogs or the wind, tore through the night. The child’s arrival remained unknown to the outside world until a week later, when Old Whateley drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village and stood babbling before the group gathered at Osborn’s general store.

There was something different about him now—an added layer of dread beneath the fog of senility. He no longer seemed merely odd; he had become a figure of fear. Yet amid his ramblings, there was pride—echoes of the same pride Lavinia would later wear like a cloak. And what he said of the boy’s father would be remembered by those who heard him for years afterward:

“I dun’t keer what folks think—ef Lavinny’s boy looked like his pa, he wouldn’t look like nothin’ ye expeck. Ye needn’t think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny’s read some, an’ has seed some things the most o’ ye only tell abaout. I calc’late her man is as good a husban’ as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury; an’ ef ye knowed as much abaout the hills as I dew, ye wouldn’t ast no better church weddin’ nor her’n. Let me tell ye suthin’—_some day yew folks’ll hear a child o’ Lavinny’s a-callin’ its father’s name on the top o’ Sentinel Hill!_”

Only two outsiders saw Wilbur during his first month: Zechariah Whateley, of the more respectable branch of the family, and Mamie Bishop, Earl Sawyer’s common-law wife. Mamie’s visit was born of curiosity, and her tales after were vivid—though perhaps exaggerated. Zechariah came to deliver a pair of Alderney cows Old Whateley had bought from his son Curtis. That transaction marked the beginning of a pattern: over the next fifteen years, the Whateleys would buy cattle steadily, though their barn—rotting and ill-kept—never seemed crowded. People began to sneak up the hillside to count the herd, only to find no more than ten or twelve emaciated, bloodless animals grazing precariously on the slope. A blight, perhaps from the poisoned earth or the diseased timber of the barn, claimed many. Some cattle bore strange wounds—incision-like scars—on their hides. Once or twice, visitors swore they saw similar marks around the necks of the old man and his daughter.

In the spring following Wilbur’s birth, Lavinia resumed her walks through the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her arms. Public interest waned after the baby had been seen, but few noticed how rapidly he grew. Within three months, he had reached the size and strength of a toddler nearing a year. His movements were unnaturally deliberate, his cries measured. At seven months, he walked unaided—by eight, he moved with quiet confidence.

Then came Halloween—a night when a great blaze lit the summit of Sentinel Hill, where the flat stone rests above a mound of ancient bones. Silas Bishop, of the undecayed Bishops, claimed to have seen the boy running ahead of his mother toward the hill an hour before the fire. He was rounding up a stray heifer when he glimpsed them through the dim lantern light—two figures darting silently through the brush, nearly naked. Later, he couldn’t recall if the boy wore anything but a fringed belt and dark blue trousers. But from then on, Wilbur was never seen alive without full clothing, buttoned tightly. Any attempt to loosen or remove it provoked rage, a sudden fury that startled even those closest to him.

By January, gossip stirred again: “Lavinny’s black brat” had begun to speak—at just eleven months. His voice carried an odd cadence, unlike any in the region, free of the lisping typical of infants. He spoke rarely, but when he did, there was a quality to it—an echo of something alien, something deeper than human speech. It wasn’t in the words, nor in the phrases, but in the tone, the way the sound formed in his throat. His face, too, was uncanny: chinless like his kin, yet his nose was firm and well-formed, his eyes large, dark, almost Latin in shape, giving him the look of a man far beyond his years. He radiated intelligence—preternatural, unsettling. Yet he was grotesque. Thick lips, coarse hair, yellowish skin riddled with pores, ears oddly elongated—there was something animal in his features, something close to goat.

He was disliked more than his mother or grandfather. And every rumor about him returned to Old Whateley—the wizard, the madman, the one who once shrieked _Yog-Sothoth_ in a circle of stones, arms raised, a book open before him, while the hills trembled. Dogs hated him, barking at his approach with primal fear. He learned early to fend them off, using gestures, words, or something else entirely—something no one could name.

3

Old Whateley kept buying cattle, but his herd never grew. He cut timber and began repairing the neglected parts of his house—a sprawling, gabled structure half swallowed by the hillside behind it. The three ground-floor rooms had always been enough for him and his daughter, but now he worked tirelessly to restore the upper story, as if driven by a force beyond mere labor. There was something unnatural in the strength that animated him, though he still rambled incoherently at times. Yet his carpentry spoke of precision—of thought, even purpose. It had started soon after Wilbur was born: one of the tool-sheds had been cleaned, boarded over, and locked with a new iron bolt. Now, the upper floor rose again under his hands, every beam replaced, every plank laid with care. Only one thing marred the restoration—the windows were all sealed tight with thick planks, no matter how many neighbors said it was madness to bother with the place at all.

Downstairs, another room was prepared for the boy. Visitors saw it, but none were ever allowed into the second story. Shelves lined the walls, tall and solid, and slowly Old Whateley began arranging the ancient books—tattered, worm-eaten volumes once scattered in corners like refuse. He would sit at the kitchen table, paste bubbling on the rusted stove, mending torn pages with trembling fingers. “I made some use of ’em,” he’d mutter, “but the boy’s fitten to make better use of ’em. He’d orter hev ’em as well sot as he kin—for they’re goin’ to be all of his larnin’.”

By September 1914, Wilbur was seventeen months old—and already terrifying. He stood as large as a four-year-old, spoke fluently, and moved with an intelligence that unsettled those who watched. He ran through fields and hills like a wild thing, following his mother wherever she went. At home, he studied the strange diagrams and symbols in his grandfather’s books, while Old Whateley drilled him in hushed afternoons, repeating words and phrases that seemed not of this world.

The house was complete by then. But one window on the east gable, pressed against the hillside, had been replaced with a solid wooden door—cleated shut from the inside. A narrow runway of rough boards led up to it from the ground. No one could guess why. And the tool-shed—once locked since Wilbur’s birth—had been abandoned again. Its door hung loose, and when Earl Sawyer stepped inside after a cattle deal, he recoiled. The stench was unlike anything he’d known—thick, rotten, alive. He claimed it reminded him of the Indian circles on the high ridges, where things older than memory stirred beneath the soil. But Dunwich folk didn’t mind smells. Their homes had always reeked of damp earth, old meat, and forgotten things.

Still, the silence wasn’t peaceful. The hill noises grew louder, slower, deeper. In May 1915, tremors shook the land—felt even in Aylesbury. Then, on Hallowe’en, flames erupted from the top of Sentinel Hill, pulsing in time with subterranean groans. “Them witch Whateleys’ doin’s,” people whispered.

Wilbur was now four years old—but looked ten. He read alone now, devouring the books with feverish hunger. But he spoke less. A silence settled over him, cold and heavy. His face, once just plain, began to take on a goatish cast—eyes too wide, jaw too broad. Dogs turned from him, barking and snarling, even when he passed quietly. He carried a pistol now, not out of pride, but necessity. When he fired it—rarely—he only made enemies.

Visitors found Lavinia downstairs, pale and withdrawn. From above, strange sounds echoed—cries, footsteps, something heavy dragging across wood. Once, a fish-peddler tried the locked stairway door. She froze. Her face drained of color. “Don’t!” she gasped. Later, he told the men at the store he heard a horse stamping upstairs. They listened. They remembered the runway. They recalled the missing cattle. And they remembered the stories—how certain gods demand sacrifice, how a bullock, killed at the right hour, can summon what lies buried.

Dogs avoided the whole place now—not just Wilbur, but the house itself. As war broke out in 1917, Squire Sawyer Whateley, head of the draft board, struggled to find men fit for service. The government sent inspectors—officers, doctors, surveyors. The press followed. Soon, the *Boston Globe* and the *Arkham Advertiser* ran sensational Sunday features: “The Boy Who Knows Too Much,” “The Witch House of Dunwich,” “Secrets Beneath the Hills.” They wrote of Wilbur’s size, his voice breaking early, the books, the sealed room, the smell that clung to the air like decay.

Earl Sawyer took reporters to the house. He pointed to the upper floor. “That smell,” he said. “It’s come down now. Like the tool-shed. Like the stone circles.” He couldn’t name it. None could.

The villagers read the articles and laughed—mostly. But they wondered why the writers made such a fuss about the gold coins Old Whateley paid with—ancient pieces, worn smooth, from times long past. And they noticed how the Whateleys received the reporters with quiet fury, eyes narrowed, lips pressed tight—like animals cornered, yet unable to attack.

No one asked what was happening upstairs. No one dared.

For a decade, the Whateley household faded into the grim routine of Dunwich—a place long accustomed to its peculiarities and indifferent to the strange rites that marked May Eve and All-Hallow’s Eve. Twice each year, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, and with each passing season, the mountain groaned more violently beneath them. At the isolated farmhouse, whispers grew louder: odd sounds echoed from the sealed upper floor, even when no one was above. Visitors claimed to hear the lowing of cattle—sometimes prolonged, sometimes abrupt—as if animals were being slaughtered in silence. There was talk of reporting it to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, but such concerns never reached beyond murmurs. Dunwich folk preferred their secrets buried.

By 1923, Wilbur Whateley was ten years old—yet already he seemed far older. His voice was deep, his face shadowed with hair, his frame unnaturally tall. He moved through life with a quiet intensity that unsettled those who saw him. That year, another wave of construction swept over the old house—this time entirely within the forbidden upper story. Fragments of wood left behind told the tale: all partitions had been torn down, the attic floor removed, leaving a single vast, hollow space stretching from ground to roof. Even the great central chimney was gone, replaced by a flimsy tin stovepipe leading outside.

Then came the whippoorwills.

Old Whateley noticed them first—hordes emerging from Cold Spring Glen, chirping endlessly beneath his window at night. He watched them with a strange reverence. “They whistle jest in tune with my breathin’ now,” he muttered to the men at Osborn’s tavern. “An’ I guess they’re gittin’ ready to ketch my soul. They know it’s a-goin’ out, an’ dun’t calc’late to miss it. Yew’ll know, boys, arter I’m gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they’ll keep up a-singin’ an’ laffin’ till break o’ day. Ef they dun’t, they’ll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an’ the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes.”

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was summoned by Wilbur, who rode through the dark with desperate haste, telephoning from the village inn. When the doctor arrived, Old Whateley lay near death—his breathing labored, his heart failing. Lavinia, the pale, twisted daughter, stood beside the bed, her eyes wide. Wilbur loomed nearby, silent and watchful. From the open void above, a rhythmic lapping could be heard—like waves on a distant shore. But it was the birds outside that truly chilled the doctor: a endless swarm of whippoorwills, their cries timed perfectly to the dying man’s gasps. It was as if they were counting his breaths.

Near midnight, Old Whateley stirred. With great effort, he spoke to Wilbur. “More space, Willy, more space soon. Yew grows—an’ _that_ grows faster. It’ll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye’ll find on page 751 _of the complete edition_, an’ _then_ put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can’t burn it nohaow!”

He was clearly unhinged. The birds adjusted their song to his new rhythm. Distant rumblings echoed from the hills. Then, after a pause, he whispered again: “Feed it reg’lar, Willy, an’ mind the quantity; but dun’t let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it’s all over an’ no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an’ work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back....”

His words dissolved into choking breaths. Lavinia screamed as the birds mimicked his final gasps. Then, just before dawn, the last rattle escaped. Dr. Houghton closed the dead man’s eyes as the chorus of birds faded into silence. Lavinia wept. Wilbur chuckled softly. “They didn’t git him,” he said, his voice thick and deep.

Wilbur was now a scholar of immense, unsettling knowledge—known across distant libraries for his correspondence about rare and forbidden texts. He was feared in Dunwich, especially after several youths vanished under mysterious circumstances. Though suspicion clung to him, no one dared accuse him—fear and the lingering wealth of the Whateley line kept questions at bay. His appearance only deepened the dread: he towered over others, his height now surpassing normal limits. In 1925, a visitor from Miskatonic University left pale and shaken—he had seen Wilbur standing fully six feet seven inches tall.

Through the years, Wilbur treated his mother with growing contempt. He forbade her from joining him on the sacred nights. In 1926, she confided to Mamie Bishop, “They’s more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie... an’ naowadays they’s more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun’t know what he wants nor what he’s a-tryin’ to dew.”

That Hallowe’en, the hill noises thundered louder than ever. Fires burned on Sentinel Hill as usual. But the true horror came from the sky—masses of whippoorwills, late by a month, gathered near the dark farmhouse. After midnight, their cries erupted into a shrieking frenzy, filling the valley with a sound like madness itself. Not until dawn did they cease—and then, they fled southward in a rush, vanishing without trace.

No one died that night. But Lavinia Whateley was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur began rebuilding two sheds in the yard, moving his books and possessions into them. Soon, Earl Sawyer reported more carpentry at the house—this time on the ground floor. Doors and windows were sealed shut. Partitions were being torn down, just as they had done upstairs years before. Wilbur lived in one of the sheds now, and Sawyer said he looked agitated, trembling at times. People avoided him. Few dared approach his land.

And his height—now well over seven feet—showed no sign of stopping.

The winter that followed brought an event no less unsettling than Wilbur Whateley’s first venture beyond the Dunwich hills. His attempts to secure a loan of a certain forbidden book—through letters sent to the Widener Library at Harvard, the Bibliotheque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and Miskatonic University in Arkham—had all failed. So, with no other choice, he set out on foot, a gaunt, unshaven figure clad in ragged clothes, speaking in a thick, alien dialect, carrying only a cheap valise from Osborn’s general store. He was eight feet tall, dark of face, with goat-like features that seemed carved from stone by some ancient, hostile hand.

He arrived in Arkham one cold afternoon, drawn not by curiosity but by purpose: to consult the copy of *The Necronomicon* kept under lock and key in the university library—the monstrous grimoire of the mad Arab Alhazred, translated into Latin by Olaus Wormius and printed in Spain during the 17th century. He had never seen a city before, yet he moved through it with unnerving certainty, passing without hesitation the great white-fanged watchdog chained outside the library gates. The beast barked with a fury that felt unnatural, its chain straining as if something unseen pulled it from within the shadows.

Wilbur carried with him a tattered, incomplete English version of the text—Dr. Dee’s translation—bequeathed by his grandfather. When granted access to the Latin original, he immediately began comparing the two, searching for a specific passage that would appear on page 751 of his own damaged volume. He could not help but tell the librarian—Dr. Henry Armitage (A.M., Miskatonic; Ph.D., Princeton; Litt.D., Johns Hopkins)—what he sought. It was a formula, an incantation bearing the name *Yog-Sothoth*, and the discrepancies between the texts made it difficult to pinpoint.

As Wilbur copied the passage he believed correct, Dr. Armitage leaned over his shoulder, eyes scanning the open pages. The left-hand side of the Latin text contained words so terrible they seemed to vibrate with malevolent power.

*"Nor is it to be thought,"* Armitage mentally translated, *"that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters, or that the common bulk of life and substance walks alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but between them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future—all are one in Yog-Sothoth. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again. He knows where They have trod earth's fields, and where They still tread them, and why no one can behold Them as They tread. By Their smell can men sometimes know Them near, but of Their semblance can no man know, saving only in the features of those They have begotten on mankind; and of those are there many sorts, differing in likeness from man's truest eidolon to that shape without sight or substance which is They. They walk unseen and foul in lonely places where the Words have been spoken and the Rites howled through at their Seasons. The wind gibbers with Their voices, and the earth mutters with Their consciousness. They bend the forest and crush the city, yet may not forest or city behold the hand that smites. Kadath in the cold waste hath known Them, and what man knows Kadath? The ice desert of the South and the sunken isles of Ocean hold stones whereon Their seal is engraven, but who hath seen the deep frozen city or the sealed tower long garlanded with seaweed and barnacles? Great Cthulhu is Their cousin, yet can he spy Them only dimly. Iä Shub-Niggurath! As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold. Yog-Sothoth is the key to the gate, whereby the spheres meet. Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for here shall They reign again."*

Armitage’s blood ran cold. He thought of Dunwich—its silent hills, its whispering woods, its cursed farmsteads. He remembered Wilbur Whateley, the boy whose presence seemed to warp the air around him, whose birth had been shrouded in whispers of madness and unnatural births. The creature before him—a hulking, grotesque giant with eyes too deep for human sockets—seemed less a man than a fragment of something older, something not meant to exist in this world. A being half-formed from dreams and nightmares, born of forces that defied space, time, and reason.

Wilbur raised his head and spoke in a voice like stone grinding against stone, resonant and strange. "Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. There's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions I can't git here. An' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let red-tape hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar nobody’ll know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is..."

He stopped when he saw Armitage’s refusal written plainly on his face. The goatish features twisted into something cunning, almost reptilian. Armitage, already half-inclined to allow a copy to be made, froze. He realized too late the danger of giving such a being—even if only temporarily—the key to forbidden knowledge. This was not just a book. It was a door. And Wilbur Whateley might be the one who knew how to open it.

"Wal, all right," Wilbur said, voice flat. "Ef ye feel that way abaout it. Maybe Harvard wun't be so fussy as yew be."

He rose, strode from the room, stooping slightly at each doorway. Outside, the watchdog yelped wildly, as if sensing something worse than mere trespass. Armitage watched him cross the campus, his gait like that of a gorilla, his shadow stretching long and distorted across the snow.

He locked the *Necronomicon* away with trembling hands. But the stench remained—something sour, metallic, like rotting flesh and burnt sulfur. The same odor he had smelled at the Whateley farmhouse three years prior.

*"As a foulness shall ye know Them,"* he whispered.

He thought of Wilbur—goat-faced, towering, breathing secrets older than mankind. He laughed, but it was not laughter of amusement. It was the sound of dread taking root.

"Inbreeding?" he muttered aloud. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'd think it a Dunwich scandal. But what thing—what cursed, shapeless horror—was Wilbur Whateley’s father? Born on Candlemas, nine months after May Eve of 1912, when the earth itself groaned beneath the mountains… what walked the peaks that night? What nightmare fastened itself upon the world in half-human flesh?"

In the weeks that followed, Armitage gathered every scrap of information about Wilbur and the forces lurking in the Dunwich region. He contacted Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley in his final days. The elder Whateley’s last words—quoted by the physician—spoke of stars not yet named, of gates opening in the sky, of a child born not of man, but of *other* things.

A visit to Dunwich yielded little new. But Armitage studied the *Necronomicon* closely, especially the passages Wilbur had sought. Each line deepened his fear. Conversations with scholars of ancient lore in Boston, letters to experts across the globe—each revealed more pieces of a puzzle too vast, too terrible, to fully grasp.

As summer approached, Armitage felt it—not as a season, but as a warning. Something was stirring. Something ancient, something waiting. And at its center stood Wilbur Whateley—no longer just a village myth, but a living conduit, a bridge between worlds.

And Armitage knew, with growing certainty, that something must be done. Before the gate opened. Before the Old Ones returned.

6 The Dunwich horror arrived between Lammas and the autumn equinox of 1928, and Dr. Armitage was one of those who bore witness to its grotesque prelude. He had already heard whispers of Whateley’s unsettling journey to Cambridge—how the man had stormed the Widener Library in a fevered attempt to access the *Necronomicon*, that cursed tome forbidden to mortal hands. But Armitage had foreseen such a threat; he had issued strict warnings to every librarian entrusted with the book. His orders were obeyed: no copy could be made, no page lent. Wilbur Whateley had come close—but not close enough.

He had been visibly unbalanced during his visit, consumed by obsession for the text yet equally desperate to return home, as though fleeing some dreadful consequence of being away too long. By early August, the inevitable unfolded. In the dead hours of the third, Dr. Armitage was jolted awake by the savage, shrieking howl of the campus watchdog—a sound raw and primal, rising in volume like a beast caught in torment. Then came silence—then another cry. This one was not animal, nor human. It tore through the night like a scream from beyond the stars, echoing across Arkham, waking dreamers and leaving them haunted by nightmares they could not name.

Armitage dressed hastily and sprinted across the lawn toward the college buildings. Others were already converging. From the library came the wail of a burglar alarm, still shrieking into the moonlit air. A window stood open, dark and gaping. Whatever had entered had done so fully. The barking and screaming had now settled into a low, guttural moan, emanating unmistakably from within.

An instinct older than reason gripped Armitage—he knew this was not something meant for mortal eyes. With authority, he pushed aside the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door. Inside, he saw Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan—men to whom he had once confided his darkest suspicions—and signaled them to follow.

The sounds had nearly ceased. Only the dog remained vigilant, emitting a watchful, droning whine. But then—outside, among the shrubbery—a chorus of whippoorwills began to pipe in eerie, rhythmic succession, their calls syncing with the final breaths of a dying thing.

A thick, cloying stench filled the building—something Armitage recognized all too well. The three men rushed down the hall to the genealogical reading room, where the low whining originated. For a moment, none dared turn on the light. Then Armitage flicked the switch.

One of them—no one can say which—let out a horrified cry at what lay sprawled amid overturned chairs and scattered papers. Professor Rice claims he blacked out briefly, though he never fell. What they saw defied description.

It was nine feet tall, half-buried in a pool of greenish-yellow ichor and viscous tar-like residue. The dog had stripped it of clothing and torn great swathes of skin. It was not dead—not yet. Its body twitched in silent spasms, chest heaving in time with the mad piping of the birds outside. Shreds of leather, fragments of garments, littered the floor. Near the window lay an empty canvas sack. On the central desk rested a revolver—its cartridge dented but unused, explaining why no shot had ever been fired.

But the creature itself eclipsed everything else.

To say it was indescribable would be both cliché and incomplete. Yet it could not be truly imagined by any mind bound to Earth’s forms or dimensions. It bore faint traces of humanity—the hands, the head—but even these were distorted. The face was goat-like, chinless, bearing the unmistakable stamp of the Whateleys. Above the waist, it resembled a monstrous hybrid: the chest covered in leathery, crocodile-like scales, the back mottled yellow and black like snake hide. But below the waist, all semblance of humanity vanished.

The lower body was shrouded in coarse black fur. From the abdomen sprouted a dozen long, greenish-gray tentacles, each tipped with red suckers. Their arrangement followed a geometry alien to Earth—symmetrical, yet wrong. On each hip, deep within pink, fringed sockets, pulsed what appeared to be rudimentary eyes. In place of a tail hung a fleshy trunk, ringed in purple, pulsing like a throat not yet formed. The legs were massive, resembling those of ancient saurians, ending in broad, veined pads neither hoof nor claw.

When it breathed, its tentacles and tail shifted color—deepening green, alternating between sickly white and yellow—suggesting a circulation unlike any known to man. There was no blood. Only the foul ichor oozing across the painted floor, staining it with strange discolorations.

As the presence of the men stirred it, the creature began to mutter, without lifting its head. Armitage did not record the words, but insisted nothing spoken was English. At first, the syllables belonged to no language of Earth. But near the end, fragments emerged—broken phrases lifted from the *Necronomicon*:

"_N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth…_"

Then silence. The whippoorwills shrieked in crescendo, their rhythm unholy. The breathing stopped. The dog raised its head and let out a long, mournful howl.

The yellow, goatish face of the thing changed—its great black eyes sunken inward, hollowed with terror. Outside, the whippoorwills fell silent. A sudden fluttering erupted—hundreds of birds, panic-stricken, rose into the sky, vanishing into the moonlight as if fleeing a predator they had sought.

Then, the dog leapt up, barked in fear, and scrambled out the same window it had entered.

A cry went up from the crowd. Armitage shouted for no one to enter until police or the medical examiner arrived. He was grateful the windows were too high to peer through. He pulled the curtains shut, sealing the room in darkness.

By now, two officers had arrived. Dr. Morgan met them at the vestibule, urging them not to enter the reading room—not until the examiner arrived and the corpse could be covered.

But inside, transformation was already underway.

No need to detail the speed or nature of the decay. What mattered was this: aside from the face and hands, the human elements in Wilbur Whateley were negligible. When the examiner finally arrived, only a sticky, whitish mass remained on the floor. The stench had nearly vanished.

There was no skull. No bones. Not as mortals understand them. Whateley had inherited far more from his unknown father than mere blood.

7 Yet all this was merely the prelude to the true horror of Dunwich. Authorities, bewildered and ill-prepared, carried out formalities with grim detachment. Details too grotesque for public consumption were buried in sealed reports, while emissaries were dispatched to Dunwich and Aylesbury to investigate property claims and locate any surviving heirs of Wilbur Whateley. What they found was a region gripped in dread—its people shaken by deep, subterranean groans beneath the domed hills, and by an ever-worsening stench that clung to the air like rotting flesh. From the boarded-up ruins of Whateley’s farmhouse came strange, rhythmic lapping sounds, as if something vast and wet stirred within the hollow shell.

Earl Sawyer, who had tended the livestock during Wilbur’s absence, was now a broken man, trembling with nerves so severe he could barely speak. The officials avoided entering the foul-smelling structure altogether, contenting themselves with a single, cautious inspection of the repaired sheds. Their report, thick and heavily redacted, was filed at the courthouse in Aylesbury, where legal wrangling over inheritance continues among the countless Whateleys—some living, some not—of the upper Miskatonic Valley.

On the old desk sat a massive ledger filled with an almost endless manuscript written in alien script. Its erratic ink and shifting hand hinted at a diary, but its meaning remained impenetrable. After days of futile attempts, it was shipped to Miskatonic University along with Wilbur’s collection of forbidden books. Even the university’s most skilled linguists admitted defeat—this text would not yield its secrets easily. And still, no trace of the ancient gold that Wilbur and Old Whateley always used to pay their debts has ever been found.

It was on the night of September ninth that the nightmare erupted.

The earth had rumbled ominously throughout the evening, and dogs howled without ceasing. By dawn on the tenth, a peculiar odor hung over the land—something thick and metallic, like thunder given form. At about 7 a.m., Luther Brown, the hired boy at George Corey’s farm near Cold Spring Glen, returned from his morning chore with the cows, nearly catatonic with terror. He stumbled into the kitchen, gasping, while behind him the herd pawed the ground and lowed in distress, having followed him in shared panic.

Between ragged breaths, he choked out what he’d seen: “Up thar in the road beyond the glen, Mis’ Corey—there’s *something*! It smells like thunder, and all the bushes and little trees are pushed back like a house had been dragged through. But that ain’t the worst. There’s *prints*—big round prints, bigger than barrel heads, sunk deep like an elephant passed over… only there’s more than four feet worth. I looked at one or two before I ran… each one covered in lines spreading from a center point—like giant palm-leaf fans, twice or thrice the size of any real ones, pounded flat into the dirt. And the smell—God, the smell—it’s just like around Wizard Whateley’s old house…”

He broke off, shuddering as if reliving the moment.

Mrs. Corey, unable to draw further details, began calling neighbors. Panic spread like wildfire. When she reached Sally Sawyer, the housekeeper at Seth Bishop’s nearby homestead, the conversation reversed roles—Sally was now the one delivering news too terrible to contain.

“Chauncey,” she said, voice trembling over the party line, “he just came back running, couldn’t talk for fright! He went up toward Whateley’s hill—saw the place, and the pasture where Seth’s cows were left overnight. The house is blown apart—timbers scattered like dynamite exploded inside, but the lower floor’s still standing, covered in some kind of tar-like goo that drips down onto the ground where the walls blew away. And the yard—oh, Mis’ Corey—the yard’s full of marks—huge round ones, bigger than a hogshead, sticky with the same black stuff. Chauncey says they lead into the meadow, where a great swath wider than a barn is flattened, and stone walls are knocked down wherever it goes.”

She paused, breath catching. “Then he went to look for the cows—frightened as he was—and found them in the upper pasture near Devil’s Hop Yard. Half of them are gone. Nearly half of those left are drained dry of blood, with sores just like the ones on Whateley’s cattle since Lavinny’s black brat was born. Seth’s gone out to see them now—but I’ll wager he won’t go near that cursed house. Chauncey didn’t follow the trail far, but he thinks it points toward the glen road leading to the village.”

Her voice dropped to a whisper. “I tell you, Mis’ Corey—something unnatural is loose. And I swear, Wilbur Whateley—though he met the end he deserved—wasn’t fully human. I’ve always said it. He and Old Whateley must have raised something in that boarded-up house—something even less human than he was. There’ve always been unseen things around Dunwich—living things that aren’t human and never meant for us.”

She hesitated. “The ground was talking last night. Around midnight, Chauncey heard the whippoorwills screaming so loud in Cold Spring Glen he couldn’t sleep. Then—he thought he heard another sound, faint, from Whateley’s way—a tearing, ripping noise, like a great crate being opened far off. With that and the birds, he didn’t sleep till sunrise. As soon as he woke, he had to go check. He saw enough, I tell you. This isn’t right. Something awful’s coming. I feel my time is near—though God only knows what it is.”

“And your Luther—did he see where the tracks led?” Mrs. Corey asked.

“No,” Sally replied. “But if they’re on the glen road this side of the ravine, and haven’t reached your house yet… then they must go *into* the glen itself. They would. That place—Cold Spring Glen—is no healthy spot. The whippoorwills and fireflies there never acted like creatures of God. Some say if you stand between the rock falls and Bear’s Den, you can hear things rushing and speaking in the air. Things not meant for ears.”

* * * * *

By noon, three-quarters of the men and boys in Dunwich had gathered between the shattered remains of the Whateley farm and Cold Spring Glen. They stared in horror at the colossal footprints, the maimed Bishop cattle, the ruined farmhouse, and the crushed vegetation lining the roadsides. Whatever had emerged had clearly vanished into the dark ravine. Trees along the banks were bent or snapped, and a wide path had been gouged through the dense undergrowth clinging to the cliffside—as though a house had slid down the slope in an avalanche.

From below came no sound, only a distant, indescribable stench. No one dared descend. Three dogs in the group barked wildly at first, but fell silent when near the edge of the glen, cowering as if sensing something older than fear.

A call was made to the *Aylesbury Transcript*, but the editor—used to Dunwich’s legends—dismissed it with a mocking article, which the Associated Press later reprinted as a joke.

That night, every household barricaded doors and windows. Cattle were brought indoors. No one slept peacefully.

At about 2 a.m., the Frye family on the eastern edge of Cold Spring Glen was awakened by a suffocating stench and the savage barking of dogs. From outside came a low, muffled swishing—like water lapping against stone. Mrs. Frye suggested calling the neighbors. Elmer was about to agree when a sudden crash split the silence—wood splintering, followed by the shrieks and stamping of terrified cattle.

The dogs drooled and pressed close to the family’s feet. Elmer lit a lantern reflexively, but knew better than to step outside. The children whimpered, held back from screaming by some primal instinct: *silence meant survival.*

After minutes of chaos, the cattle settled into moans. Then came crashing, snapping, crackling—sounds echoing down into the glen. The Fryes huddled together, frozen, until the last noise faded into the distance.

Finally, Selina Frye crept to the phone and relayed what she knew—news of the second phase of the horror.

The next day, the countryside was paralyzed with dread. People moved in silence, gathering in small, frightened groups near the site of destruction. Two enormous paths of devastation stretched from the glen to the Frye farm. Monstrous prints littered the ground. One side of the red barn had collapsed entirely.

Of the cattle, only a quarter remained—most in fragments. Those alive were shot.

Earl Sawyer urged sending for help from Aylesbury or Arkham, but others scoffed—what good could soldiers do against such a thing? Old Zebulon Whateley, from a branch of the family straddling sanity and decay, muttered about rites that should be performed atop the hills. He remembered chants sung in the ancient stone circles—not just by Wilbur and his grandfather, but by others long before.

Night fell again. The villagers did nothing but reinforce their barricades. Families huddled together, or stayed apart, each home a fortress against the unknown. Muskets were loaded. Pitchforks leaned against walls. But nothing happened—only the hills groaned.

When dawn came, many hoped the horror had passed.

Yet by morning, both the Fryes and the Bishops reported renewed dog agitation, distant sounds, and foul odors. Explorers found fresh tracks along the road beside Sentinel Hill—again showing signs of immense bulk, and evidence of travel in both directions. As if the creature had come from the glen, crossed the road, and returned.

At the base of the hill, a thirty-foot-wide path of crushed saplings and shrubs climbed upward—straight up, even over sheer cliffs. Investigators, climbing safer routes to the summit, found the trail ended—or turned back—at the top.

There, upon the flat stone where the Whateleys once lit their unholy fires and chanted on May Eve and Hallowmas, lay a vast, trampled circle. And on the stone’s surface—thick, viscous, and fetid—was the same tar-like substance seen in the ruins of the farmhouse.

Men stood in silence, staring at the stone, then down the hill. The trail had descended much as it had ascended.

No one spoke. Reason failed. Logic dissolved. Only Zebulon, absent from the scene, might have understood.

Thursday night began like the rest. But it ended differently.

The whippoorwills screamed relentlessly in the glen—so loud, so unending, that few could sleep. Around 3 a.m., every telephone in the area rang simultaneously. Those who answered heard a voice—ragged, hysterical—shrieking: “Help, oh, my Gawd!”—then a crash, and silence.

No one moved. No one dared.

In the morning, those who had heard the call called every number on the line. Only the Fryes did not answer.

An hour later, a group of armed men marched to the Frye farm.

What they found was worse than expected—yet not surprising.

More trails. More prints. But no house. It had collapsed inward, like an eggshell crushed from within. Among the wreckage, nothing lived. Nothing dead. Only the stench, and the black, sticky residue.

The Elmer Fryes were gone.

Erased.

And the horror had not stopped.

8

In a quiet, book-lined room in Arkham, a horror far more profound than any physical manifestation was unfolding behind closed doors. The strange manuscript—Wilbur Whateley’s diary—had arrived at Miskatonic University under circumstances too grim for speculation. Delivered for translation, it had baffled scholars of every language, ancient and modern. Its alphabet bore a vague resemblance to the thickly shaded Arabic of Mesopotamia, yet no known script matched it. Experts concluded it was an artificial cipher—a constructed alphabet meant to disguise meaning, not merely encode it. But even the most advanced cryptographic methods failed. No key emerged. No pattern held.

The books seized from Whateley’s quarters were equally troubling: ancient, arcane, filled with symbols that hinted at forbidden knowledge. One heavy tome, bound with iron, used a script unlike any other—closer to Sanskrit, but warped into something alien. These texts offered no help in unlocking the diary. They only deepened the dread.

Dr. Armitage took full charge of the manuscript. Not only did he possess a rare grasp of obscure languages, but he also carried a personal burden—the weight of what Whateley might have left behind. He suspected the alphabet was not just coded, but esoteric—a secret system passed down through cults older than memory, heirs to the sorcery of the Saracenic world. Yet he dismissed origin as secondary. If the text was a cipher, its underlying language would likely be English. Why else write so much, unless in one’s own tongue? Only certain ritual phrases might deviate.

He approached the manuscript with the assumption that it was English beneath the code. And he prepared accordingly. For weeks, he immersed himself in the dark arts of cryptography—Trithemius’ *Poligraphia*, Porta’s *De Furtivis Literarum Notis*, Vigenère’s *Traité des Chiffres*, Falconer’s *Cryptomenysis Patefacta*, Davys and Thicknesse, Blair, von Marten, Klüber’s *Kryptographik*. He studied them by night, then attacked the manuscript in bursts of desperate concentration.

Slowly, patterns emerged. The cipher wasn’t simple substitution. It was layered—like a multiplication table of letters, keyed by unknown words. The older treatises proved more useful than modern ones. Armitage began to believe the code was ancient, preserved through generations of occultists who guarded their secrets in silence.

He made progress. Then stalled. Progress again. Then stumbled back. By late August, he was close. On September 2nd, the final barrier collapsed.

That evening, Dr. Armitage read Wilbur Whateley’s first coherent passage.

It was a diary—yes—and written in a voice both childish and terrifying. The grammar was crude, the syntax broken, but the content… the content was beyond comprehension.

The entry was dated November 26, 1916. The writer was three and a half years old, though he looked like a boy of twelve.

*Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth,* it read, *which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain. Shot Elam Hutchins's collie Jack when he went to bite me, and Elam says he would kill me if he dast. I guess he won't. Grandfather kept me saying the Dho formula last night, and I think I saw the inner city at the 2 magnetic poles. I shall go to those poles when the earth is cleared off, if I can't break through with the Dho-Hna formula when I commit it. They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth, and I guess Grandfather will be dead then, so I shall have to learn all the angles of the planes and all the formulas between the Yr and the Nhhngr. They from outside will help, but they can not take body without human blood. That upstairs looks it will have the right cast. I can see it a little when I make the Yoorish sign or blow the power of Ibn Ghazi at it, and it is near like them at May Eve on the Hill. The other face may wear off some. I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared and there are no earth beings on it. He that came with the Aklo Sabaoth said I may be transfigured, there being much of outside to work on.*

Armitage sat frozen. His hands shook. Sweat soaked his shirt. He did not leave the desk. All night, he read, page after page, deciphering as fast as he could. He called his wife—told her he wouldn’t be home. When she brought breakfast, he barely ate. The words burned into him: *earth cleared off.* *They from outside.* *No earth beings.* *Transfigured.*

Lunch came. Dinner followed. He consumed almost nothing. His mind raced—not with logic, but with terror. The diary spoke of things not of this world. Of entities from beyond space, time, and matter. Of rituals. Of sacrifices. Of a coming end.

By the second night, he slept—only to wake from nightmares worse than the truth he’d uncovered. On September 4th, Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan visited. They left pale, trembling, silent.

Armitage tried to sleep that night—but only fitfully. The next day, he returned to the manuscript, taking notes frantically. He scribbled furiously, copying passages, comparing codes, sketching diagrams. In the small hours, he dozed in his office chair, but woke screaming, tangled in visions of black towers rising from the ground, of stars shifting in the sky, of something vast stirring beneath the earth.

His physician, Dr. Hartwell, came in the morning. Insisted he stop. Armitage refused. “It’s vital,” he whispered. “I must finish. I’ll explain later.”

That evening, as twilight fell, he finished reading. He collapsed, exhausted, eyes hollow. His wife brought dinner. He was half-unconscious, but when she glanced at his notes, he gasped—“Don’t look!”—and snatched the papers up. He sealed them in an envelope, tucked it into his coat pocket.

He managed to walk home. Hartwell was summoned immediately. As the doctor helped him to bed, Armitage muttered over and over: *“But what, in God’s name, can we do?”*

He slept, but not peacefully. Fevered dreams plagued him. He called for the *Necronomicon*, for Remigius’ *Dæmonolatreia*. He raved about destroying a boarded-up farmhouse in Dunwich. He screamed about an elder race—beings from another dimension—intent on stripping the Earth from its orbit, dragging it into a plane of existence beyond time, where it had once fallen billions upon billions of years ago.

“They’re coming,” he cried. “They want to strip the world. Stop them! Stop them!”

He claimed the Whateleys had opened the door. That the worst was still hidden. That something had been left behind—something not yet fed. “It hasn’t been fed since August 2nd,” he moaned. “When Wilbur died. At that rate…”

But Armitage was strong for seventy-three. The fever broke by Friday. He woke clear-headed, but shaken. A terrible responsibility pressed upon him.

On Saturday, he went to the library. Summoned Rice and Morgan. Together, they spent the day and night in frantic study. Books were pulled from hidden shelves—texts on summoning, banishment, dimensional rifts. Diagrams were copied. Formulas scrawled across pages. There was no skepticism now. They had seen Whateley’s corpse—twisted, unnatural, with eyes that seemed to burn. No man could doubt the diary then.

Should they alert the state police? They debated. But decided against it. How could ordinary men understand? How could they believe?

The conference ended without a plan. But Armitage worked through Sunday—comparing formulas, mixing chemicals from the lab. The more he read, the more he doubted any substance could destroy what Whateley had unleashed. The entity was not flesh. Not blood. Not matter. It was something older, deeper—something that threatened the very fabric of reality.

Monday was more of the same. Tuesday, he had a plan. He would travel to Dunwich within days. He would confront whatever remained.

Then, on Wednesday, the news came.

Tucked in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser*, a joke from the Associated Press: *“Dunwich Bootleg Whisky Produces Record-Breaking Monster.”* A local oddity, they said. A curiosity. A beast of myth.

Armitage stared. His breath caught. He dialed Rice and Morgan.

That night, they met again. And the next day, preparations began. Armitage knew he was meddling with powers beyond reason. But he also knew there was no alternative. The horror was real. It was growing. And it was coming.

He would act. Even if it cost him his life.

Friday morning, Armitage, Rice, and Morgan set off by car for Dunwich, arriving in the village just after one o’clock. The day was fair, but even under the brightest sun, a deep unease clung to the air. The hills rose with their strange, rounded crowns; the ravines yawned dark and silent, as if holding breath. From time to time, atop distant ridges, the bare rings of ancient stones stood stark against the sky—silent monuments to something older than memory.

At Osborn’s store, they found a hush so thick it felt like fear itself had settled into the walls. Word spread quickly: Elmer Frye’s house—and his entire family—had vanished without a trace. That afternoon, the trio rode through the hollows and farms, questioning villagers, collecting fragments of dread. They saw the ruins of the Frye homestead—the warped timbers, the lingering black ooze on the ground, the grotesque tracks that defied reason. In the yard, cattle lay wounded, limbs twisted unnaturally. Swaths of vegetation, torn and flattened, marked the path of some colossal, unseen force. Up and down Sentinel Hill, the trail of destruction seemed to pulse with meaning. At the summit, Armitage paused before the stone altar—sinister, weathered, and ancient—as if it were a threshold between worlds.

They learned of a State Police unit that had arrived earlier from Aylesbury in response to the first reports. But when they searched, no sign of the officers remained. Only their abandoned car sat near the Frye ruins, empty and cold. The villagers, all of whom had spoken with the policemen, looked stunned. Then old Sam Hutchins turned pale, nudging Fred Farr and pointing toward the deep, shadowed glen nearby.

“Gawd,” he whispered, voice cracking, “I told ‘em not to go down there. I never thought anyone’d do it—with them tracks, that smell… and the whippoorwills screaming in the middle of the day…”

A chill passed through everyone. The air grew still. Every ear strained, though no sound came—only the silence of something listening.

Armitage trembled. He had read Wilbur Whateley’s diary. He knew what waited. And now, standing at the edge of the horror, he felt the weight of responsibility settle upon him. Night would fall soon—and then it would move again. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris.* The words echoed in his mind. He recited the incantations he had memorized, clutching the paper with the others. His flashlight worked. Rice pulled out a metal sprayer—used for insects, but perhaps useful for revealing the invisible. Morgan checked his rifle, though Armitage had warned him: no bullet could harm what they hunted.

As dusk gathered, the villagers began to scatter, locking doors not because they believed in locks, but because they feared what might break through them anyway. They shook their heads at the scholars’ plan to stay at the Frye ruins, guarding the glen. Many left silently, knowing they might never see these men again.

That night, the hills groaned. The whippoorwills sang in jagged bursts. Once in a while, a wind swept up from Cold Spring Glen, carrying a stench so foul it made the three watchers recoil—just as they had years ago, when they stood over a dying man who had lived too long as a human.

But the monster did not come.

It waited.

And Armitage knew better than to rush into darkness. It would be suicide to attack blind.

Morning broke gray and wet. Rain fell in drizzle, then heavier, until the sky cracked with thunder. Lightning flashed—once, twice—then a bolt struck near the glen, illuminating the cursed hollow like a wound in the earth. The storm raged, but the watchers hoped it would pass quickly.

Then, less than an hour later, voices erupted down the road—shouting, sobbing, hysterical.

A group of more than a dozen men burst into view, running, faces white with terror. One collapsed, gasping:

“Oh, my God! It’s going again—*this time in daylight!* It’s out! It’s moving right now! And only God knows when it’ll be on us!”

Another took up the tale.

“About an hour ago, Zeb Whateley heard the phone ring—it was Mrs. Corey, George’s wife down by the junction. She said Luther, the hired boy, was driving the cows back from the storm after the lightning hit. He saw the trees bending at the mouth of the glen—on the opposite side—and smelled that same awful stench he’d smelled when he found those huge tracks last Monday. He said there was a swishing, lapping sound—more than just wind or branches. Then suddenly, the trees along the road started pushing sideways. There was stomping, splashing in the mud—but he didn’t see anything. Just the trees, bending.”

He paused, catching breath.

“Then, farther ahead, where Bishop’s Brook crosses under the road, he heard a terrible creaking—like wood splitting. He knew it was the bridge. But he still couldn’t see anything—just the trees and brush bowing like they were being pushed by a mountain. When the swishing sound faded toward Wizard Whateley’s place and Sentinel Hill, Luther stepped back to look at the ground. It was muddy, raining hard—tracks washed away fast. But starting at the glen’s mouth, where the trees had bent, there were still some of those monstrous prints—big as barrels—just like he’d seen before.”

The first speaker interrupted, trembling.

“But that’s not the worst part—that was just the beginning. Zeb called folks, and everyone was listening. Then a call came from Seth Bishop’s. His housekeeper, Sally, was screaming—she’d just seen the trees bending beside the road, and heard a wet, mushy sound—like an elephant breathing and stepping—coming straight for the house. Then she screamed about the smell—same as the one Chancey smelled up at the Whateley ruins Monday morning. The dogs were barking and howling like mad.”

He stopped, breathless.

“And then… then she let out a scream—said the shed down the road had just collapsed, like the storm blew it over. But the wind wasn’t strong enough to do that. Then the picket fence in the front yard crumpled—no one saw what did it. Everyone on the line could hear Chancey and old Seth yelling. Sally shrieked that something heavy had hit the house—not lightning, not anything natural—something massive, slamming against the front, again and again. And then…”

His voice faltered.

“And then… Sally yelled, ‘Oh, help! The house is caving in!’ And we heard it—crashing, screaming—just like Elmer Frye’s place, but worse. Then… nothing. Not a sound. Not a squeak. Just silence.”

The men stood frozen. Armitage stepped forward, voice steady despite the dread rising in his chest.

“We must follow it,” he said firmly. “Now.”

He spoke to calm the panic, to rally the frightened farmers.

“I know what this thing is. It’s born of wizardry—of the Whateleys. And it must be destroyed by the same means. I’ve read Wilbur Whateley’s diary. I’ve studied the books he used. I believe I know the spell to banish it. We can’t be certain—but we have to try. It’s invisible—I knew it would be—but Rice has a sprayer with powder that might make it visible for a second. We’ll use it later. For now, we must act.”

He paused.

“It’s terrible to think of such a thing alive—but it’s far worse to imagine what Wilbur would have unleashed if he’d lived longer. You don’t know what you’ve escaped. Now we face only this one abomination. It can’t multiply. But it can destroy everything. So we must stop it. We must follow it.”

He pointed.

“The last place it was seen—Seth Bishop’s. Let someone lead us there. I don’t know your roads well, but I suspect there’s a shortcut across fields.”

Earl Sawyer stepped forward, pointing through the rain.

“You can get to Seth’s faster by cutting across the lower meadow, wading the brook at the low spot, then climbing through Carrier’s mowing and the timber lot beyond. Comes out near the upper road, just past Seth’s.”

Armitage nodded. He, Rice, and Morgan began walking. Most of the villagers followed, slowly, hesitantly. The storm was breaking. Light crept back into the sky.

When Armitage veered slightly off course, Joe Osborn corrected him, walking ahead to guide the way. Courage returned, bit by bit—though the climb up the steep, wooded hill tested it. Ancient trees leaned like sentinels. The path was narrow, almost vertical. They scrambled upward, hands grasping roots and bark.

At last, they emerged onto a muddy road. The sun broke through.

They were just beyond Seth Bishop’s place.

Bent trees. Fresh, horrifying tracks.

The scene repeated the Frye tragedy. The house and barn were gone—collapsed, crushed, reduced to rubble. Nothing remained—no bodies, no signs of life, only the stench and the sticky residue clinging to the earth.

No one lingered.

They turned instinctively toward the trail of destruction leading onward—toward the ruined Whateley farm, toward Sentinel Hill.

As they passed the site of Wilbur Whateley’s cottage, a wave of dread passed through them. The air thickened. The silence deepened.

They were chasing something the size of a house. Something invisible. Something that moved with the malice of a demon.

Opposite the base of Sentinel Hill, the tracks left the road. A fresh swath of bent grass and crushed foliage marked the path.

Armitage pulled out his telescope and scanned the slope. Then he handed it to Morgan.

After a moment, Morgan gasped, handing the glass to Earl Sawyer and pointing sharply.

Sawyer fumbled, adjusting the lenses with Armitage’s help.

Then he cried out—softer than Morgan, but no less horrified.

“Gawd almighty… the grass and bushes are moving! It’s going up—slowly—creeping toward the top. Heaven only knows why…”

Panic flickered.

To chase it was one thing. To see it—*know* it was near—was another.

Voices rose, questioning Armitage.

“What do you really know about it?”

He could not answer fully. No words could capture the horror. Only silence remained.

For each man, standing there in the fading light, felt himself brushing against something beyond nature, beyond understanding—a force that had slipped from the edges of the world, and now walked among them.

10 The three men from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, his white beard trembling in the wind; Professor Rice, broad and grim as a stone wall; and Dr. Morgan, lean and pale—climbed Sentinel Hill alone. They left behind the telescope, carefully instructed in its use, with the terrified villagers huddled in the muddy road below. As they ascended, eyes followed them, the glass passed from hand to hand like a sacred relic. The climb was brutal. Armitage stumbled more than once, needing support, his breath ragged. Above them, high on the slope, the great swath of bent trees trembled again—the mark of something monstrous crawling across the mountain with slow, deliberate horror.

Then, it became clear: they were gaining ground.

Curtis Whateley, of the undecayed branch, held the telescope when the Arkham party veered sharply away from the path of destruction. He told the crowd they were heading for a smaller peak—a vantage point overlooking the trail ahead, where the shrubbery still bowed under unseen pressure. And so it proved. Soon after the invisible abomination passed that spot, the trio reached the ridge and stood above it.

Wesley Corey took the glass next and gasped. “Armitage’s adjusting the sprayer!” he shouted. “Rice’s holding it—something’s about to happen!”

A murmur rose among the watchers. The sprayer—filled with a powder meant to briefly reveal the unseen horror—was their only weapon against the impossible. A few men shut their eyes. Curtis snatched the telescope back, squinting through the lenses with desperate intensity.

He saw Rice, positioned directly behind and above the creature, poised to unleash the spray. From their angle, the effect would be perfect.

To those without the glass, nothing but a sudden flash of gray mist—no larger than a modest building—appeared near the summit. Then Curtis screamed, dropping the telescope into the mud. He staggered, collapsing if not for the hands that caught him. All he could whisper was, “Oh, oh, great Gawd... _that... that_....”

[Illustration: "Oh, oh, great Gawd ... that ... that."]

Chaos erupted. Questions flew. Only Henry Wheeler moved—not to ask, but to rescue the telescope from the muck and wipe it clean.

Curtis was broken. His words came in fractured gasps. “Bigger ‘n a barn... all made o’ squirmin’ ropes... hull thing shaped like a hen’s egg bigger’n anything, with dozens o’ legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin’ solid abaout it—just jelly, an’ wrigglin’ ropes pushed close together... great bulgin’ eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks stickin’ out, big as stovepipes, tossin’ an’ openin’ an’ shuttin’... all gray, with blue or purple rings... _an’ Gawd in Heaven—THAT HAFF FACE ON TOP!_”

That final image shattered him. He fell unconscious.

Fred Farr and Will Hutchins carried him to the roadside, laying him on damp grass. Henry Wheeler, shaking, turned the telescope back toward the peak.

Through the lenses, he saw three tiny figures racing upward—three men, barely visible against the sky. No sign of the horror. Just them, climbing.

Then, from the valley behind, came a sound unseasonable and unnatural—the piping of whippoorwills. But this was no ordinary chorus. It pulsed with a strange, irregular rhythm, like a heartbeat gone mad, echoing the ritual unfolding on the hilltop.

Earl Sawyer took the glass. “They’re at the top,” he said. “On the ridge. One’s raising his arms—over and over. Like a chant.”

And then, faintly, the crowd heard it—a low, rhythmic hum, half-musical, half-sickening, rising from the distance.

The silhouette on the peak must have been grotesque beyond imagining. But no one could see it clearly. Not yet.

“I guess he’s sayin’ the spell,” whispered Wheeler, grabbing the telescope back.

The whippoorwills shrieked louder. The sun dimmed—without cloud, without warning. The light grew thin, sickly, as if the sky itself were bleeding. Beneath the hills, a deep rumble began. Above, another joined it—thunder, but not thunder. A sound from beyond space.

Lightning flashed. No storm clouds formed. Yet the sky cracked open in violet fury.

Now the chanting was unmistakable. Through the glass, Wheeler saw all three men raising their arms in synchronized motion, mouths open, voices lost in the wind.

From somewhere far off, dogs barked in panic.

The darkness deepened—not from shadow, but from the very color of the sky, turning purplish, spectral. Another flash. This time, the crowd thought they saw a mist swirling around the altar-stone atop the hill. But no one had looked through the telescope at that moment.

The whippoorwills kept their eerie beat. The villagers huddled tighter, feeling the air grow thick with dread.

Then came the sounds.

Not human. Not earthly. Deeper than voice, older than bone. They came from the altar-stone—yet seemed to echo from the pit beneath the world.

"_Ygnaiih... ygnaiih... thflthkh'ngha... Yog-Sothoth..._" croaked the void.

"_Y'bthnk... h'ehye... n'grkdl'lh..._"

The voice faltered. As if some ancient mind fought within the abyss.

Henry strained at the lens. Three figures—still there. Arms flailing in wild gestures. The incantation nearing its end.

Where did such words come from? Not from flesh. Not from blood. From depths beyond memory, from dreams older than man, from a heredity buried in the bones of the earth.

Then the voice returned—stronger, frantic, tearing at the fabric of reality.

"_Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah... e'yaya-yayaaaa... ngh'aaaa... ngh'aaaa... h'yuh... h'yuh... HELP! HELP!... ff--ff--ff--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..._"

And then—silence.

The villagers stood frozen, reeling from the English syllables that had roared from the emptiness beside the altar-stone. Words. Real words. But spoken by what?

Then—**BOOM.**

A sound like the world cracking. The hills shuddered. Lightning struck the altar-stone in a single, blinding bolt.

A wave of force—unseen, unfelt, yet crushing—swept down the mountain. Trees snapped. Grass curled. Underbrush tore apart. The air filled with a stench—rotten, alien, like the breath of dead stars.

The crowd stumbled, choking, blinded by the foulness. Dogs howled from distant farms. Green leaves turned yellow-gray, wilting as if burned by poison. Dead whippoorwills littered the fields.

The stench vanished quickly. But the land never healed.

Even now, the growths around Sentinel Hill are wrong. Twisted. Unnatural.

Curtis Whateley stirred as the three men from Arkham descended, bathed in sunlight restored. They walked slowly, quietly, faces drawn with horror deeper than any the villagers had known.

Questions poured out. They shook their heads. Only one truth mattered.

“The thing is gone,” Armitage said. “It’s been undone. Shattered back into what it was—what it never should have been. It wasn’t real. Not in our world. Most of it wasn’t matter. It was a force—like its father. And most of it has returned to him. To some realm outside time, outside space. A place no man should name.”

Silence. Then Curtis moaned, hands clutching his head. Memory flooded back.

“_Oh, my Gawd... that haff face... that haff face on top... red eyes, crinkly albino hair, no chin... like the Whateleys... It was a octopus, centipede, spider kind o’ thing... but on top—on top—was a haff-shaped man’s face... and it looked like Wizard Whateley’s... only it was yards and yards across..._”

He collapsed again.

Only Zebulon Whateley spoke. “Fifteen year’ gone,” he muttered, “I heerd Ol’ Whateley say as haow some day we’d hear a child o’ Lavinny’s a-callin’ its father’s name on the top o’ Sentinel Hill....”

Joe Osborn cut in. “_What was it, anyhaow_, an’ haowever did young Wizard Whateley call it aout o’ the air it come from?”

Armitage hesitated. Then: “It was... a force. Not of this world. One that acts by laws we can’t understand. We don’t belong calling things like that in. Only the worst men, the worst cults, ever try. Wilbur Whateley had some of it in him—enough to make him a monster, a devil, and to make his death a sight no man should see. I’m burning his diary. And you—burn that altar-stone. Pull down every standing stone on these hills. Those things brought the beings the Whateleys worshipped—the ones they planned to let in, to destroy humanity and drag Earth to some unknown place for some unknown purpose.”

He paused. “As for this thing we just sent back... it was raised by the Whateleys for a terrible role. It grew fast—like Wilbur—but faster. Stronger. Because it had more of the _outsideness_. You don’t need to ask how Wilbur called it from the air. He didn’t. _It was his twin brother. But it looked more like the father than he did._”
