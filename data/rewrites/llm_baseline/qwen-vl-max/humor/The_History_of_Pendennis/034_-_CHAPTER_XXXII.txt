Arthur Pendennis, in the midst of his modest but spirited revels—feasting on porter and beef, laughing with friends, and living like a young prince who’d never heard of a budget—began to notice a certain shadow looming over his head. Not a ghost, nor a dragon, but something far more terrifying: the last five-pound note in his pocketbook. It lay there like a sleeping lion, quiet for now, but ready to pounce when its time came.

His money had dwindled faster than a candle in a drafty room. A third had vanished into club dues. Another chunk had gone toward a wardrobe of furniture that included a bed, a chair, and a mirror that reflected not just his face, but his growing anxiety. And now? Nothing left. No inheritance to tap (he wouldn’t dream of it), no uncle to bail him out (his uncle was busy dining with dignitaries and pretending he wasn’t related to anyone who ever paid rent), and no mother to scold him into responsibility—though she lived quietly in the country, content to send her son letters full of love and vague advice.

Warrington, his comrade and confidant (though not yet fully aware of Pen’s financial plight), assumed Arthur was a man of means. After all, he wore gold chains, carried a dressing case that could have belonged to a duke, and dressed as if every morning were a royal levee. He didn’t skimp on cabs—even in fine weather—and always tipped the driver *too* generously, as though tipping were a form of spiritual offering. He couldn’t bring himself to buy cheap gloves or use a handkerchief twice. If a beggar stood at the door with eyes like broken marbles, Pen would drop a sovereign without hesitation. “It’s not charity,” he’d say. “It’s justice.”

But this generosity, noble as it was, wasn’t helping him eat.

One evening, while inspecting the change from his final five-pound note—lying on a tray beside Warrington’s ale—Pen sighed dramatically.

“It is the last rose of summer,” he declared, quoting poetry as if it were prophecy. “Its blooming companions have long since faded. Behold—the last one has shed its leaves.” Then, with a sigh that could have toppled a small church spire, he told Warrington the whole story: his mother’s modest income, his own college extravagances, Laura’s kindness, and the fact that he was officially, definitively, *out* of cash.

Warrington listened, puffing thoughtfully on his pipe. When Pen finished, he knocked the ashes out with a flourish.

“Impecuniosity,” said Warrington, “is the best tonic for an honest man. It wakes you up. It forces you to look your fate in the eye. Like a man riding a fence—you brace yourself. You discover what you can live without. No new boots. No eau de Cologne. No cab unless you’re being chased by a griffin. You’ve been spoiled, Pen. Spoiled by women. Spoiled by privilege. A man with health and brains who can’t earn a living doesn’t deserve to be here. Let him jump off Waterloo Bridge. Or steal a leg of mutton and go to Australia. I don’t care which. But let him *do* something.”

“You’re cruel,” said Pen. “But how do I earn anything?”

Warrington laughed. “Why not advertise in *The Times*? ‘Gentleman, B.A., formerly plucked at St. Boniface College, seeks position as usher in classical and commercial academy. Will teach Latin, French, mathematics, and piano accompaniment. Also skilled in hair-cutting and general pupil supervision.’ Address: A. P., Lamb Court, Temple.”

“Confound you!” cried Pen.

“Or consider Bloundell,” Warrington continued. “He’s a professional blackleg—travels Europe, seduces rich boys, then fleeces them like sheep. Bob O’Toole drives a mail coach. There’s a doctor’s son I know—quarreled with his father over finances, grew a mustache, moved to a provincial town, and announced himself as Professor Spineto, chiropodist to the Emperor of All the Russians. He operated on the editor of the local paper, became famous, and lived happily for three years. Now he’s back with his family and inherits the gallypots.”

“Hang gallypots!” said Pen. “I can’t cut corns, cheat at cards, or drive a stagecoach.”

“Then there’s journalism,” Warrington said, leaning forward. “Every man has secrets. Before you told me about your finances, I thought you were born with a silver spoon and a title. But now I see—your mother can’t support you forever. You must pay off Laura. You must stop being a burden. So what’s left? Work. Or die.”

“But how?” asked Pen.

“How do *I* live?” Warrington replied. “On my younger brother’s allowance—until I spent it five years ago. Then I did what any sensible man does: I went to work. I write.”

He pulled out a few sheets of paper. “These are mine. Articles in the *Law Reviews*. Occasionally, I contribute to a newspaper—*The Dawn*, edited by a friend.” He took Pen to the club one day and pointed silently to two articles. Pen read them, then stared. The style was unmistakable—sharp, witty, learned. He recognized it instantly.

“I can’t do that,” said Pen. “I don’t understand politics or history. I only know a little about books.”

“But you have something better,” Warrington said kindly. “You have *poetry*. I’ve seen your verses. They’re not bad. In fact, they’re good. You’ve got the spark. The real fire. Not oil-lamps, but torchlight.”

Arthur blinked. Tears formed. “You think so?”

“I know so,” said Warrington. “And I like you. I was lonely in chambers. You walked in, laughed at Lowton—poor little snob—and I liked that. I wanted someone. And you’re it.”

Pen felt a warm glow. “Thank you,” he said. “I’ve often thought I might be a poet.”

“Ah, yes,” said Warrington. “But let’s be honest. That poem you wrote at eighteen—*Ariadne in Naxos*? It’s the most sentimental, ridiculous rubbish I’ve ever read. And the Prize Poem? Pompous. Feeble. I’m amazed it didn’t win. You’re not Milton. You’re not Aeschylus. You’re not even Pindar. But you can write a decent article. And a pretty poem. That’s enough.”

Pen stamped his foot. “By Jove! I’ll show you!”

Warrington just laughed and blew twenty-four puffs of smoke into the air—like a tiny volcano of amusement.

And soon enough, opportunity knocked.

Mr. Bacon, the eminent publisher of Paternoster Row (formerly of “Bacon and Bungay”), was preparing his annual literary miscellany, the *Spring Annual*. It was edited by Lady Violet Lebas and featured poems by young noblemen—Lord Dodo, Percy Popjoy, Bedwin Sands—all eager to prove they weren’t entirely useless. The book also included illustrations—lovely, romantic scenes—except that sometimes the poets failed to rise to the occasion.

One such failure happened to be Percy Popjoy’s contribution to a plate titled *The Church Porch*. It showed a Spanish maiden rushing to church with a prayer book, while a young man in a cloak watched from a niche. The picture was charming. The poem? Horrific.

Warrington read it and burst out laughing. Mr. Hack, Bacon’s reader, groaned. “We can’t publish this,” he said. “The public will riot. Bungay’s people are putting out a strong rival. We need something better. Lady Violet is busy with her own poem. What do we do?”

Warrington grinned. “I know a fellow who can write. Let me take the plate home. Send for the verses tomorrow. Pay well, of course.”

“Of course,” said Hack.

So Warrington returned to Pen’s chambers with the plate.

“Here’s your chance,” he said. “Write some verses to this. Earn your keep.”

“What?” said Pen. “A lady entering a church. A youth ogling her from a wine-shop window. What am I supposed to do?”

“Try,” said Warrington. “You’ve been waiting for this moment.”

Pen sat down, frowned, and after several hours of wrestling with inspiration (and possibly a glass of brandy), produced this:

---

Although I enter not,
Yet round about the spot
Sometimes I hover,
And at the sacred gate,
With longing eyes I wait,
Expectant of her.

The Minster bell tolls out
Above the city’s rout
And noise and humming.
They’ve stopped the chiming bell,
I hear the organ’s swell—
She’s coming, she’s coming!

My lady comes at last,
Timid and stepping fast,
And hastening hither,
With modest eyes downcast.
She comes—she’s here—she’s past.
May Heaven go with her!

Kneel undisturb’d, fair saint,
Pour out your praise or plaint
Meekly and duly.
I will not enter there,
To sully your pure prayer
With thoughts unruly.

But suffer me to pace
Round the forbidden place,
Lingering a minute,
Like outcast spirits, who wait
And see through Heaven’s gate
Angels within it.

---

When Warrington read it, he nodded slowly. “That’ll do.”

“Really?” said Pen.

“Better than Popjoy’s nonsense,” said Warrington. “Now, get another piece. We’ll ask for two guineas a page. If they like it, you’ll get into the magazines. Maybe even become famous.”

Pen found another ballad and handed both over. Together, they marched to Paternoster Row.

Bacon’s shop was old and low-browed, with a bust of Lord Verulam above the door. Opposite, Bungay’s establishment gleamed with fresh paint and seventeenth-century charm—like a museum of manners. Pen waited outside, pacing nervously, watching the world go by: penny dreadfuls, religious tracts, rosaries, sermons against Rome, and pamphlets titled *Come Out of Rome!*—all under the watchful eye of Saint Paul’s bells.

Inside, Warrington played his game. He knew Bacon loved being treated with casual disrespect by gentlemen. So he put on his hat and addressed the publisher from across the table.

“What! Not know Mr. Pendennis?” he said. “You can’t live much in the world, or you’d know him. Man of property in the West. Ancient family. Cousin to Lord Pontypool. Dines at Gaunt House every week.”

“Law bless me!” said Bacon. “Really?”

“I’ve just shown Mr. Hack some verses he wrote last night. Hack says he’ll give him a copy of the book—the what-d’-you-call-’em.”

“The *Spring Annual*,” said Bacon.

“Yes. And you don’t suppose a man like Pendennis gives up dinner at Gaunt House for nothing, do you?”

“No, sir,” said Bacon. “Fashionable men want to be paid.”

“I tell you—he’s a star. He’ll make a name.”

“They’ve said that before,” sighed Bacon. “Lord Viscount Dodo—sold eighty copies. Popjoy’s *Hadgincourt*—died on the shelf.”

“Well,” said Warrington, rising, “then I’ll take my man to Bungay.”

At that, Bacon surrendered. “Law bless you, give him a check.”

Warrington returned, grinning, and handed Pen the paper.

Pen nearly fainted. “I’ve earned it!” he shouted. “I can pay Laura! I can help my mother! I can live on my own!”

“I can marry the grand vizier’s daughter!” Warrington joked. “Buy a house in Belgrave Square! Build a castle in the air!”

They celebrated with a magnum of claret at the club. Pen paid. It was the first time in months he’d done so without guilt.

And just when he thought things couldn’t get better…

A printer’s boy appeared at the door.

“From Captain Shandon, sir,” he said, handing over a letter. Then he sat down on the bench and fell asleep.

Pen opened it.

> **F. P. Tuesday Morning.**
> My dear Sir,—
> Bungay will be here today about the *Pall Mall Gazette*. You’d be the very man to write a genuine West-end article—dashing, trenchant, and d—— aristocratic. Lady Hipshaw will contribute, but she’s not much. We’ve two lords—but the less they do, the better. We *must* have you. Your terms are yours. We’ll make a hit.
> Shall B. come to see you, or can you visit us?
> Ever yours,

Pen looked at Warrington. “The printer’s devil has come to the door,” he said.

“And brought the key,” Warrington replied.
