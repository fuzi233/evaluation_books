One morning, Mrs. Bretton entered my room with brisk precision and asked me to open my drawers and show her my dresses. I did so without protest or hesitation.

“That will do,” she said after a brief inspection. “You must have a new one.”

She left, then returned shortly with a dressmaker, who measured me carefully. “I mean,” she declared firmly, “to follow my own taste and have things done my way—this time at least.”

Two days later, the dress arrived—a soft pink gown.

“That’s not for me,” I said quickly, already feeling a surge of resistance. The thought of wearing it was like donning the robes of some distant, exotic queen.

“We shall see whether it is for you or not,” my godmother replied, unshaken by my objection. Then, with that quiet authority that never allowed room for argument, she added: “Mark my words—you’ll wear it this evening.”

I believed I wouldn’t. I believed no force in the world could make me wear such a thing. A pink dress? It was foreign to me, as if it had come from another life entirely. I had never worn anything like it—and I wasn’t about to begin now.

Still, my godmother decreed that I would accompany her and Dr. Graham to a concert that very night. It was to be held in the grand hall of the principal musical society, a prestigious event featuring the most accomplished students from the Conservatoire. Afterward, there would be a lottery for charity, and—most remarkable of all—the King, Queen, and Prince of Labassecour were expected to attend. Graham had sent tickets and insisted on formal attire as a mark of respect to royalty. He also reminded us to be ready by seven o’clock.

At six, I was led upstairs. Without any struggle, I found myself guided by forces beyond my control—quietly overruled, gently but completely shaped by someone else’s will. The pink dress went on, softened with delicate black lace. I was pronounced *en grande tenue* and invited to look in the mirror. I did, hesitantly, trembling slightly. Then I turned away just as quickly. Seven o’clock struck. Dr. Bretton arrived. My godmother and I descended.

She wore a dignified brown velvet gown. As I walked beside her, I envied her solemn elegance—how gracefully those dark folds fell! In the drawing-room doorway stood Graham.

“I hope he won’t think I’ve been trying to draw attention,” I whispered to myself, anxious and ashamed.

“Here, Lucy,” he said, handing me a bouquet. “Some flowers.” He glanced at my dress—not critically, not admiringly—but with a kind smile and a nod that instantly eased my fears. His approval, simple as it was, settled something deep within me. The dress itself was modest—no flounces, no frills—only the color startled me. But since Graham saw nothing absurd in it, I soon began to accept it too.

I suppose those who attend concerts regularly cannot truly understand the excitement felt by someone for whom such an occasion is rare. Though I didn’t expect great joy from the music—I knew little of what to anticipate—I found pleasure in the journey there. The snug warmth of the closed carriage on a crisp, clear night; the companionship of two people so cheerful and generous; the stars flickering through the trees along the avenue; then the sudden openness of the sky as we passed into the city, the lights glowing at the gates, the guards standing watch, and the mock inspection that made us laugh—all of it stirred me deeply. Much of the charm, I suspect, came from the atmosphere of warmth and friendship around me. Dr. John and his mother were both in high spirits, teasing each other playfully all the way, treating me with the same ease and kindness they might have shown a family member.

We traveled through the finest streets of Villette—brightly lit, more alive now than at midday. How radiant the shops looked! How full and joyful the flow of life along the wide pavements! As I watched, the memory of the Rue Fossette surfaced—the walled garden, the schoolhouse, the vast, silent classrooms where, at this very hour, I used to wander alone, gazing at the stars through tall windows, listening to the distant voice reading the "lecture pieuse" in the refectory. Soon enough, I would return to that solitude. And the shadow of that future quietly dimmed the brilliance of the present moment.

By then, we’d joined a stream of carriages heading toward the same destination. Ahead loomed a magnificent building, blazing with light. I had only a vague idea of what awaited inside—no public theater or concert hall had ever welcomed me before.

We stepped under a portico crowded with people, bustling and chattering. I barely registered the details until I found myself ascending a broad, carpeted staircase—deep crimson underfoot—leading to massive doors, also draped in red, standing closed and solemn.

I hardly noticed how the doors opened—Dr. John handled that part. But they did open, revealing a hall so vast and dazzling that my breath caught. High ceilings arched above, painted in shimmering gold, broken by white cornices and garlands of alabaster and gilded leaves. Every surface seemed touched by art—walls curved like waves, columns rose proudly, and everywhere, crimson reigned: carpets, draperies, cushions. From the dome hung a chandelier so immense and brilliant it seemed not made by human hands, but by eastern genii. I imagined it might hold the magic lamp, and waited for a giant hand to appear, guarding its treasures. It was simply a chandelier, reader—but to me, it was a vision from another world.

We moved forward, unaware of our path—until suddenly, we met another group coming from the opposite side. For a fleeting instant, I saw them clearly: a woman in dark velvet, a man whose bearing and face struck me as noble and striking, and a third figure in pink with black lace.

I took them in—each one—believing them strangers. But almost immediately, I realized I was looking into a large mirror set between two pillars. The reflection showed our own party. For the first—and perhaps only—time in my life, I saw myself as others might see me. There was no need to linger on the result. It brought a pang of regret, a discordant note in my heart. Not flattering, certainly—but not devastating either. I should count myself lucky it wasn’t worse.

At last, we were seated in places offering a sweeping view of the hall. It was packed, filled with a distinguished crowd. The women weren’t necessarily beautiful, but their dresses were flawless. Even those who seemed awkward in private somehow carried themselves with grace when dressed for the occasion. Foreigners, especially, seem to know how to transform themselves—how to carry their heads, bend their arms, shape their expressions for display. The *grande toilette* brings out a reserve of poise, a practiced elegance, always worn with pride.

Among them were women of a particular beauty—one I had never seen in England. Their figures were solid, firm, sculptural—like marble caryatids or Phidian goddesses. They had no sharp angles, no frailness. Their faces were regular, round, calm—reminiscent of Dutch paintings, with low-country classic features. Their expression was profound stillness, a peace untouched by passion. A polar snowfield might be the only match for their depth of serenity. These women needed no jewelry; their hair, braided tightly, offered enough contrast to their smooth skin. Their dresses were plain, even severe—because their forms required no adornment.

Once, I had known one such woman intimately. She carried a quiet strength of self-love so powerful it was almost overwhelming. She could not care for anyone else—her devotion was entirely inward. Her veins ran cool, her blood sluggish; her arteries nearly blocked by placid lymph.

Such a woman sat directly before us—majestic, cold, blonde, and perfect as a white column topped with gold. She was a focus for every eye, aware of it yet unmoved by it. Impervious to admiration, she stood like a statue.

Noticing Dr. John’s gaze fixed upon her, I leaned close and whispered, “For heaven’s sake, guard your heart. You needn’t fall in love with *her*. I warn you—she might let you die at her feet and not care.”

“Very well,” he replied. “But how do you know the sight of her indifference might not stir me all the more? Desperation can be a powerful stimulant. Still,” he shrugged, “you know nothing of these matters. I’ll speak to my mother. Mamma, I’m in danger.”

“As if that interests me!” Mrs. Bretton retorted.

“Oh, the cruelty of my fate!” he lamented. “No man has ever had a less sentimental mother than mine. She never thinks such a disaster could happen to her—having a daughter-in-law.”

“If I don’t care, it’s not because I haven’t had it threatened for ten years. ‘Mamma, I’m going to marry soon!’ you shouted before you’d even grown out of jackets.”

“But one day, it will happen. Suddenly, when you think you’re safest, I’ll go forth like Jacob or Esau and take a wife—perhaps one of these daughters of the land.”

“At your peril, John Graham. That’s all.”

“My mother wants me to remain a bachelor forever. What a jealous old lady! But now—look at that splendid creature in pale blue satin, with hair like silk, shimmering like her dress. Wouldn’t you feel proud, mamma, if I brought her home and introduced her as Mrs. Bretton, junior?”

“You’ll bring no goddess to La Terrasse. That little château won’t hold two mistresses—especially if the second is as tall, broad, and round as that wooden doll in wax and kid and satin.”

“She’d fit your blue chair perfectly!”

“Fit my chair? I defy the foreign usurper! Let it be a sorrowful seat for her. But hush, John Graham! Hold your tongue and use your eyes.”

While they bantered, the hall—already full—continued to fill. Party after party arrived, until the semicircle before the stage became a dense sea of heads rising from floor to ceiling. The stage, really a large temporary platform, was now crowded. Around two grand pianos, a flock of young girls—pupils of the Conservatoire—had gathered silently. I had watched them assemble while Graham and his mother debated the belle in blue. I followed with interest as they were arranged and positioned. Two men oversaw them—both familiar to me.

One, a bearded man with long hair, was M. Josef Emanuel, a renowned pianist and the foremost music teacher in Villette. He taught twice weekly at Madame Beck’s pensionnat to privileged pupils. The other was M. Paul—his half-brother.

M. Paul amused me. He seemed utterly at home before such a grand audience, commanding and controlling nearly a hundred young ladies with effortless authority. He was earnest, energetic, absolute. Yet—what business did he have here? He couldn’t distinguish one note from another. I knew his presence stemmed not from musical talent, but from a love of spectacle and power—an innocent, almost childlike desire for dominance. It soon became clear that even his brother, M. Josef, was under his influence. Never had I seen such a tiny hawk of a man. As the real musicians appeared—famous singers and performers—M. Paul faded into the background. He couldn’t bear being overshadowed; where he couldn’t shine, he retreated.

Now, everything was ready—except one section of the hall, reserved for royalty. It was covered in crimson, furnished with cushioned benches flanking two regal chairs beneath a canopy.

A signal was given. The doors opened. The audience rose. The orchestra erupted. And to a chorus of welcome, the King, Queen, and Court of Labassecour entered.

Until now, I had never seen a king or queen in person. So I strained every sense to absorb these living symbols of European royalty. Anyone seeing royalty for the first time feels a strange mix of awe and disappointment—expecting a throne, a crown, a scepter. Instead, I saw a middle-aged man in uniform and a young woman beside him. Half cheated, half relieved.

The King was fifty, slightly bent, with grey hair. No face in the hall resembled his. I knew nothing of his character or habits. At first, the lines etched across his brow, around his eyes, near his mouth, puzzled me—like hieroglyphs carved in iron. But soon, I sensed their meaning. There sat a man in silent suffering—a nervous, melancholy soul. Those eyes had seen a ghost often—the ghost of Hypochondria. Perhaps she stood before him now, on the stage, amid the glittering crowd. Hypochondria rises in throngs, dark as doom, pale as sickness, nearly as strong as death. She whispers: “Not so happy,” and freezes the blood, dims the light.

Some might say it was the weight of the crown that bowed his head. Others, early loss. Both may have played a role—but they were poisoned by the deeper enemy: constitutional melancholy. The Queen, his wife, understood. A gentle, thoughtful woman, she bore a quiet shadow of grief upon her face. Not beautiful, not like the marble-featured women described earlier. Her form was slender, her features distinguished but tied to royal lineage—too familiar to delight fully. Yet her eyes—those were her own. They sparkled with pity, kindness, sweet sympathy. She moved not as sovereign, but as a loving woman. Her son, the Prince of Labassecour, leaned on her knee. Throughout the evening, I saw her watching the King, sensing his withdrawal, trying to draw him back with stories of the boy. She would listen, then repeat them softly to her husband. He would start, listen, smile—then sink back into silence. A poignant scene. Yet no one else seemed to notice. To the aristocracy and bourgeoisie alike, it was invisible.

With the royal couple came their court—foreign ambassadors, elite expatriates. They took the crimson benches. The ladies sat; most men stood. Their dark coats formed a somber backdrop to the splendor ahead. The splendor itself had layers: matrons in velvet and jewels in the middle, young girls in white, pale rose, or soft blue in the front—pure, simple, ethereal. These were the blossoms of Villette’s nobility. No gems, no elaborate hair, no rich fabrics—just youth, grace, and innocence. Some of them I recognized—two former pupils of Madame Beck’s: Mesdemoiselles Mathilde and Angélique. They’d struggled in class, unable to master even *The Vicar of Wakefield*. One had eaten more bread and butter than any human ought—once even stealing slices she couldn’t finish. Truths, yes—wholesome truths.

Another girl caught my eye—Ginevra Fanshawe. She sat beside the daughter of an English peer. Delicate, lively, unlike the stiff foreign beauties. Her hair wasn’t braided tight like a shell—it flowed naturally, curled and free. She chattered happily, content with herself and her place. I didn’t look at Dr. Bretton, but I knew he saw her. He grew quiet, answered his mother briefly, sighed often. Why sigh? Because he liked challenges—he loved pursuit under difficulty. Here was the ultimate challenge. His beloved shone from a height beyond reach. He couldn’t approach her, couldn’t be sure she’d even glance his way.

I watched to see if she would. Our seats were near the royal benches—she must see us. Soon, her eyes landed on Dr. John. Then she raised a glass to examine Mrs. Bretton. A minute later, she laughed and whispered to her neighbor. When the music began, her attention drifted to the stage.

As for the concert itself—there’s little worth recording. My impressions were those of a novice. The Conservatoire girls, frightened, played tremulously on the grand pianos. M. Josef stood beside them, but lacked the force of his brother, who could have compelled courage through sheer intimidation. M. Paul would have placed them between two fires—fear of the audience and fear of himself—and made the latter far greater. M. Josef couldn’t do that.

Then came a tall, sulky woman in white satin. She sang. Her voice amazed me—not for beauty, but for trickery. Like a conjuror, she made it leap and twist. But a simple Scotch melody played by a street musician once moved me far more.

Next, a gentleman stepped forward, bowing deeply toward the royal couple, touching his heart with gloved hand. He delivered a passionate lament against a “fausse Isabelle.” I thought he sought the Queen’s sympathy, but she listened with polite courtesy, not true emotion. His anguish was painful. I was glad when he finished.

The best part came afterward—rousing choruses. Delegates from provincial choirs—thick, hearty Labassecouriens—gave their voices with gusto. Their rough, honest singing satisfied the ear with raw power.

And so the evening passed—splendid, strange, unforgettable.
