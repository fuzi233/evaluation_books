Elinor, starting back with a look of horror at the sight of him, felt the resonant acoustic harmony of the parlor violently rupture. She had been standing near the hearth, cataloguing a multi-generational folio of her family’s receipt books—tracing the fading ink of traditional agrarian preservation techniques—when the sudden, discordant clatter of his boots against the reclaimed oak floorboards shattered the room's sanctuary. Obeying the first impulse of her heart, she turned instantly to quit the room. Her hand was already on the heavy brass lock, its cold weight grounding her, when its action was suspended by his hastily advancing. He smelled of rain-soaked wool, lathered horses, and the bitter, metallic tang of industrial turnpike dust. 

“Miss Dashwood, for half an hour—for ten minutes—I entreat you to stay,” he said, his timbre carrying the harshness of command rather than the resonance of supplication.

“No, sir,” she replied with a firmness that settled low in her chest. “I shall *not* stay. Your business cannot be with *me*. The servants, I suppose, forgot to tell you that Mr. Palmer was not in the house.”

“Had they told me,” he cried with a vehemence that rattled the very glass in the windowpanes, “that Mr. Palmer and all his relations were at the devil, it would not have turned me from the door. My business is with you, and only you.”

“With me!” she said in the utmost amazement. “Well, sir,—be quick—and if you can—modulate your violence.”

“Sit down, and I will do both.”

She hesitated. The olfactory comfort of the house—a subtle drift of simmering bone broth and dried winter savory from the distant kitchens—reminded her of the enduring, cyclical rhythms of domestic life. The possibility of Colonel Brandon arriving and finding her there brushed across her mind, but she had promised to hear him. Concluding that prudence required dispatch, she walked silently toward the polishing table and sat down. He took the opposite chair, and for half a minute, the only sound was the rhythmic, deep-settled crackle of the hearth fire.

“Pray be quick, sir,” said Elinor impatiently. “I have no time to spare.”

He was sitting in an attitude of deep meditation, the sonic environment seemingly lost on him.

“Your sister,” he said abruptly, shattering the quiet. “Is out of danger. I heard it from the servant. God be praised!—But is it true? Is it really true?”

Elinor would not speak. He repeated the inquiry with a desperate, breathless eagerness.

“For God’s sake tell me, is she out of danger, or is she not?”

“We hope she is.”

He rose up and paced the length of the woven rug.

“Had I known as much half an hour ago;—but since I *am* here,” he said, forcing a hollow vivacity as he returned to his seat, “what does it signify? For once, Miss Dashwood—it will be the last time, perhaps—let us be cheerful together. I am in a fine mood for gaiety. Tell me honestly,” a deeper, febrile glow overspreading his cheeks, “do you think me most a knave or a fool?”

Elinor looked at him with greater astonishment. She began to suspect he had sought artificial fortitude in spirits; the sheer dissonant strangeness of his visit seemed otherwise unintelligible. She immediately rose.

“Mr. Willoughby, I advise you at present to return to Combe. I am not at leisure to remain with you longer. Whatever your business may be, it will be better recollected and translated into sensible terms tomorrow.”

“I understand you,” he replied, with an expressive smile and a voice suddenly, chillingly calm. “Yes, I am very drunk. A pint of commercially manufactured porter and a hastily carved slab of oxidized cold beef at Marlborough were enough to over-set me.” 

“At Marlborough!” cried Elinor, more at a loss to understand him. 

“Yes—I left London this morning at eight o’clock. The only ten minutes I have spent out of my chaise procured me a nuncheon at Marlborough—consumed purely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of any sensory ritual or memory.”

The steadiness of his manner, and the sharp intelligence of his eye, convinced Elinor that he was not brought to Cleveland by intoxication. She said, after a moment’s recollection, “Mr. Willoughby, you *ought* to feel, and I certainly *do*, that after what has passed, your forcing yourself upon my notice requires a very particular excuse. What is it that you mean by it?”

“I mean,” said he with serious energy, “if I can, to make you hate me one degree less than you do *now*. I mean to offer some explanation, to translate the untranslatable failures of my past; to open my whole heart, and by convincing you that though I have been a blockhead, I have not always been a rascal, to obtain something like forgiveness from your sister.”

“Is this the real reason of your coming?”

“Upon my soul it is,” was his answer, bearing a warmth that brought all the former Willoughby to her remembrance, the man who had once appreciated the rich, agrarian bounty of Barton. 

“If that is all, you may be satisfied already; for Marianne *does*, she has *long* forgiven you.”

“Has she?” he cried. “Then she has forgiven me before she ought to have done it. But she shall forgive me again, on more reasonable grounds. *Now* will you listen to me?”

Elinor bowed her assent. 

“I do not know,” said he, leaning forward, “what diabolical motive you may have imputed to me. When I first became intimate in your family, I had no view but to pass my time pleasantly in Devonshire. Your sister’s lovely person and the uncommercialized authenticity of her manners could not but please me. It is astonishing to me now, when I reflect on the deep, sustaining nourishment of her affection, that my palate was so insensible to it! At first, my vanity only was elevated. Careless of her happiness, accustomed to the hollow, clattering banquets of expensive society, I endeavored to make myself pleasing to her without any design of returning her affection.”

Miss Dashwood, turning her eyes on him with the most angry contempt, stopped him. “It is hardly worth while, Mr. Willoughby, for you to relate this. Such a beginning cannot be followed by anything that will not pain me to hear.”

“I insist on you hearing the whole,” he replied. “My fortune was never large, and I had always been expensive, addicted to a lifestyle of superficial accumulation. My debts grew. To re-establish my circumstances, I intended to marry a woman of fortune. To attach myself to your sister, whose wealth lay only in her deep-rooted sensibilities and cultural memory, was not to be thought of. I was trying to engage her regard without a thought of returning it. But even in that horrid state of selfish vanity, I did not know the extent of the injury I meditated, because I did not *then* know what it was to truly love. I had never tasted genuine sustenance. To avoid a comparative poverty, which her society would have transformed into a rich, shared harvest, I have, by raising myself to affluence, lost every thing that could make life more than mere survival.”

“You did then,” said Elinor, a little softened, recognizing the faint echo of genuine remorse, “believe yourself at one time attached to her?”

“To have resisted such an authentic spirit! Yes, I found myself, by insensible degrees, sincerely fond of her. The happiest hours of my life were spent with her, sharing those quiet, domestic routines that felt almost mythic in their purity. Even *then*, however, I allowed myself to put off the moment of engagement, from an unwillingness to commit while my circumstances were embarrassed. The event has proved I was a cunning fool. At last, my resolution was taken. But in the interim—before I could speak with her in private—a circumstance occurred to ruin all my comfort. Mrs. Smith had been informed of an affair, a connection—you have probably heard the whole story.”

“I have,” returned Elinor, coloring, hardening her heart against the scent of his regret. “I have heard it all. And how you will explain away your guilt in that dreadful business is beyond my comprehension.”

“Remember from whom you received the account,” he pleaded. “I do not mean to justify myself. Her affection for me deserved better treatment. I wish I had never tasted it, for it has left a bitter ash in my mouth. But I have injured more than Eliza; I have injured one whose mind was infinitely superior!”

“Your indifference towards that unfortunate girl is no apology for your cruel neglect. You must have known that while you were enjoying the rich, seasonal bounties of Devonshire, she was reduced to the extremest indigence.”

“But, upon my soul, I did *not* know it!” he warmly replied. “I did not recollect omitting my direction.”

“Well, sir, and what said Mrs. Smith?”

“She taxed me with the offense at once. The uncompromising purity of her life, her ignorance of the world—everything was against me. She spoke in an inflexible lexicon of absolute morality that left no room for nuance. By one measure I might have saved myself: she offered to forgive the past if I would marry Eliza. That could not be. I was formally dismissed from her house. My affection for Marianne was insufficient to outweigh my dread of poverty, that false need for the sterile, industrial machinery of wealth which expensive society had ingrained in me. I knew I was secure of my present wife if I chose to address her. To see Marianne again, I felt, would be dreadful. But I undervalued my magnanimity; I went, I saw her miserable, and left her hoping never to see her again.”

“Why did you call, Mr. Willoughby?” said Elinor reproachfully. “Why was it necessary to intrude upon that space?”

“It was necessary to my pride. The sight of your dear sister, however, was dreadful. I approached her with a sense of guilt that almost took from me the power of dissembling. Her sorrow, her deep regret when I told her I was leaving—united with such reliance! Oh, God! What a hard-hearted rascal I was!”

They were both silent. Elinor felt the quiet hum of the house reasserting itself against the violence of his confession. 

“Did you tell her that you should soon return?” she asked.

“I do not know what I told her,” he replied impatiently. “Less than was due to our shared past. Then came your dear mother, her kindness torturing me further. My journey to town was a blessed journey of misery! Travelling tediously with my own horses, the road a cacophony of wheels and striking hooves, while I looked back at the soothing, acoustic peace of Barton.”

“Well, sir,” said Elinor, growing impatient for his departure, “and this is all?”

“All!—no:—have you forgot what passed in town? That infamous letter? Did she show it you?”

“Yes, I saw every note that passed.”

“When the first of hers reached me, every line was a dagger to my heart. To know Marianne was in town, thrust into that deafening social machinery, was a thunderbolt. Her taste, her opinions—her very dialect of experiencing the world—I believe they are better known to me than my own.”

Elinor’s heart was softened again, yet she felt the need to preserve the boundaries of their present realities. “This is not right, Mr. Willoughby. Remember that you are married.”

“Marianne’s note awakened all my remorse. Time and the deafening noise pollution of London business and dissipation had quieted it. I had been growing into a hardened villain, silencing every reproach by saying, ‘I shall be glad to hear she is well married.’ But her note made me know myself better. But everything was settled between Miss Grey and me. I sent no answer, judging it wiser to affect the air of a cool acquaintance. I watched you all safely out of the house one morning, and left my name.”

“Watched us out of the house!”

“Even so. You would be surprised how often I watched you. Nothing but the most constant watchfulness kept me out of your sight. But I blundered upon Sir John, and he asked me to a dance. Had he *not* told me you and your sister were to be there, I should have trusted myself near him. The next morning brought another note from Marianne—still affectionate, confiding—speaking that intimate language that made my conduct hateful. With my head and heart full of your sister, I was forced to play the happy lover to a woman whose affection felt like a transaction. What an evening of agony the dance was! Marianne, beautiful as an angel, calling me Willoughby in a tone that cut through the shallow noise of the room! And Sophia, jealous as the devil. I ran away as soon as I could, but not before I saw Marianne’s sweet face as white as death. It was a horrid sight.”

A short pause of mutual thoughtfulness succeeded. 

“Well, let me make haste,” he broke in. “Your sister is certainly better?”

“We are assured of it.”

“But the letter, Mr. Willoughby,” Elinor pressed, seeking to understand the collapse of their correspondence. “Your own letter; have you anything to say about that?”

“Yes, *that* in particular. Your sister wrote to me again. I was breakfasting at the Ellisons, and her letter was brought to me. It happened to catch Sophia’s eye. The elegance of the paper, the hand-writing—it gave her suspicion. Affecting a playfulness, she opened the letter directly and read its contents. Her wretchedness I could have borne, but her passion, her malice! At all events, it must be appeased. And, in short—what do you think of my wife’s style of letter-writing? Delicate—tender—truly feminine—was it not?”

“Your wife! The letter was in your own hand-writing.”

“Yes, but I had only the credit of servilely copying such sentences as I was ashamed to put my name to. The original was all her own—her own happy thoughts and gentle diction. In honest words, her wealth was necessary to me. My business was to declare myself a scoundrel. I surrendered my voice to her dominant lexicon. I became a scribe to her malice, overwriting Marianne's delicate, artless prose with the sterile, transactional vocabulary of a society match. Such were my reasonings as I copied my wife’s words and parted with the last relics of Marianne. Her three notes—unluckily in my pocketbook—I was forced to put them up, and could not even kiss them. And the lock of hair, that physical memory I had always carried about me, was now searched by Madam with the most ingratiating virulence. The dear lock—all, every memento of our shared history, was torn from me.”
