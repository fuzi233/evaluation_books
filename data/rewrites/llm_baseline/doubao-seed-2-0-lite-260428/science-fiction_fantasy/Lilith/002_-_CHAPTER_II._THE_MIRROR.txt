Nearly a full week drifted by without a single strange incident to ruffle the quiet of my inherited old manor house. I’d returned to the property barely a month prior, and that half-lost, half-trapped manuscript fragment I’d found tucked in the back of the library closet had lingered in my thoughts every day since. I’d prodded, pried, and tugged at it a dozen different ways, but I could never figure out what invisible force was holding it fast, leaving it half-stuck in the dark of the shelves. The closet’s stale, cold air had also left me uneasy for weeks, worrying the moisture and rot might be eating away at the hundreds of uncared for books stored inside, and one afternoon that vague idle intention to give them all a full thorough sort snapped into sharp, unshakable resolve. I was just pushing myself up out of my worn leather armchair to start the work, when I caught a flicker of movement out of the corner of my eye: the old librarian, Raven, gliding away from the closet door toward the far end of the library.

To call him a man feels almost too generous. What I saw was little more than a shadowy impression: a slight, stooping figure draped in a shabby tailcoat that dragged almost to his heels, the fabric splitting a little as he moved to reveal stick-thin legs stretched in taut black stockings, and oversize feet slipping silently in wide, flopping slipper-like shoes. I never doubted for a second he was something real, even if he looked like a smudge of old ink bleached from a hundred-year-old page, and I set off after him at once.

He drifted out of the library, across the marble expanse of the entrance hall, and straight to the foot of the great grand oak staircase, then climbed all the way up to the first floor where all the unused, formal reception rooms sat gathering dust. I followed close at his heels, and he never so much as glanced back as he slipped past all those closed drawing room doors, down a wide paneled corridor, and to the base of a narrower, little-used service stair leading up to the second floor. The second I stepped onto the landing at the top, I realized I was in a stretch of the house I’d never even seen a hint of before. I’d had no siblings to drag me on silly childhood romps that would make every nook and cranny of a big house feel familiar; I’d been barely seven years old when my guardian had pulled me away from the manor, and I’d never stepped foot inside again until I’d come back to claim my inheritance.

We wound through twisting, low-ceiling passages until we reached a door at the foot of a rickety winding wooden stair, and Raven glided up it first. Every single step creaked and groaned loud enough to echo when I put my weight on it, but I never heard a single sound come from my shadowy guide. Somewhere halfway up the stair, I lost sight of him entirely. When I reached the top, he was nowhere to be found in the sprawling main garret, its massive hewn oak beams arching overhead, long dark vistas stretching out between piles of discarded old furniture, the gloom only thinned by a handful of cobweb-choked windows and small, dusky skylights. I stared out across the huge empty space with a weird, giddy mix of awe and delight: every inch of this unexplored garret was mine. Dead center of the room sat a rough, unpainted plank enclosure, its door hanging slightly ajar. Assuming Raven must have slipped inside there, I pushed the door open and stepped through.

The tiny chamber was full of light, but the hollow, still light of a place that had sat abandoned for decades. It felt dull, disconsolate, like the very walls were sad they’d been forgotten and rendered useless. A few slanted sunbeams cut through the cloud of dust motes I’d stirred up entering, landing full on a tall, narrow, old-fashioned mirror, its glass face filmed over with layers of dust. Its frame was carved from solid ebony, and perched right on its top edge was a sculpted black eagle, wings spread wide, a thin golden chain clamped in its beak with a polished black obsidian ball hanging from its end.

I’d been staring idly at the frame rather than the glass itself, when I suddenly registered that the mirror wasn’t reflecting anything at all: not the plank chamber around me, not my own body standing right in front of it. I could have sworn I saw the far wall of the chamber behind me melt like soft wax, and for a split second I wondered if I’d mistaken a sheet of protective glass over a priceless hidden painting for a mirror. But what stretched out before me was no painted image. I was looking at a wild, wind-scoured broken heath. Desolate low hills, strange and unrecognizable, rose in the middle distance, a jagged far-off range of mountain tops stretched across the horizon, and the closest stretch of the view was flat, melancholy moorland, carpeted in dark purple heather.

I’m short-sighted, so I stepped closer to squint at the texture of a weathered half-buried stone in the immediate foreground, and that’s when I spotted him: a huge, ancient raven, feathers the rich iridescent purple-black of polished jet, streaked here and there with softening streaks of gray from great age, hopping slow and solemn toward me, bobbing his head as if rooting for worms in the turf. I didn’t even stop to feel surprised at seeing a living bird inside what I’d thought was a painting, so I took another step forward to get a better look. My boot caught hard on something solid — no doubt the thick ebony lip of the mirror’s frame — I stumbled, and the next second I was standing nose to beak with the great bird, cold wind tangling my hair, the sharp peaty scent of heath in my throat. There was no glass, no garret, no old manor house. I was standing out in the open, on a houseless, endless moor.
