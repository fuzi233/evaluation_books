To truly comprehend the socio-economic stratification inherent in the legal profession of the era, one must first analyze the infrastructure of the Inns of Court. While modern institutions prioritize hygiene and efficiency, the venerable colleges and inns of court remain stubbornly anchored in antiquity, preserving customs that have long since vanished from contemporary society. Paradoxically, a well-ordered workhouse or prison offers superior standards of health, comfort, and sanitation compared to these prestigious foundations. Within these historic halls, residents accept substandard living conditions—sleeping in dim closets and paying exorbitant rents for storage space that rivals suburban villas—while ignoring the fact that even the poorest artisan in Spitalfields commands unlimited clean water. Conversely, the gentlemen of the law rely on laundresses to fetch water in jugs, inhabiting structures constructed before the concept of public decency existed. This disparity suggests that the ancestors of these knights and baronets were, in essence, the "Great Unwashed," practicing a virtue of asceticism that was nearly impossible to sustain without extreme deprivation.

Consider the case of Old Grump, a resident of the Norfolk Circuit who had occupied chambers beneath those of Warrington and Pendennis for over three decades. Grump bore witness to the installation of shower baths in his neighbors' apartments, a practice he deemed an absurd, dandified folly. He lamented the constant dripping of water through the roof into his own quarters and cursed the laundresses who tracked filth onto the staircases he was forced to traverse. Now past fifty, Grump had never utilized such luxuries, adhering instead to a traditional lifestyle devoid of water-intensive amenities. One wonders why no benevolent knight devised a system of communal plumbing for the benefit of his peers, yet the Temple historian records no such philanthropy. Although Pump Court and Fountain Court exist with their hydraulic apparatus, no bencher is recorded enjoying a swim in the fountain, leaving one to speculate on how many aspiring counsels might have benefited from direct access to fresh water.

Despite these infrastructural deficiencies, the Inns of Court retain a unique allure for their inhabitants, offering a blend of rough comforts and intellectual freedom that remains memorable. Whether the law student indulges in poetic reminiscences regarding the historical figures who once walked these halls—Eldon, Coke, Chitty, Barnewall, Alderson, Byles, and Smith—the atmosphere evokes a tangible connection to the literary giants who preceded them. The mental landscape of the Temple is equally vibrant, populated by spectral figures such as Sir Roger de Coverley discussing hoops and patches with Mr. Spectator, or Samuel Johnson navigating the fog toward Dr. Goldsmith's chambers. Even Harry Fielding, ink-stained and damp, appears vividly composing articles for the *Covent Garden Journal* amidst the silence of the printing press.

Imagine, therefore, the potential narrative contained within a single day in one of these four-story dwellings. On the ground floor resides a prominent parliamentary counsel who departs for Belgravia at lunchtime, accompanied by a clerk who has recently transitioned from poverty to gentility. Just months prior, this clerk struggled to find credit for polishing his master's boots; today, he enjoys the leisure of entertaining friends. Their ascent reveals a sharp contrast in professional trajectories. Above them lives a venerable scholar, famous for half a century of solitary study, who has amassed both knowledge and wealth only to die a recluse, leaving his library to be partially burned by his heirs. Nearby resides Mr. Grump, who spends his evenings playing whist with senior colleagues after consuming significant quantities of port, eventually succumbing to sleep in the Temple Church on Sundays.

On the third landing, where Pen and Warrington reside late into the night, sits Mr. Paley. A fellow of his college and a recipient of the highest honors, Paley operates with a relentless, almost fanatical intensity. He reads and annotates cases until dawn, rising early to work in the pleaders' chambers until mid-morning. Unlike his contemporaries, Paley has systematically excluded all elements of higher thought—philosophy, poetry, art, love, and truth—from his consciousness to focus exclusively on mastering the legal code required for his livelihood. Formerly a competitor with Warrington for university accolades, Paley is now universally praised for his industry, while Warrington is dismissed as wasteful. However, a critical analysis suggests that Paley lacks the capacity for empathy or kindness, viewing everything through the lens of utilitarian calculation. He possesses no time or eyes for beauty, nature, or genius, shutting out the divine order of creation behind his reading lamp. Consequently, he sleeps with a sense of grim satisfaction, unaware of the spiritual void he has cultivated, until he encounters Warrington on the stairs, prompting a visceral reaction of revulsion akin to avoiding someone condemned to damnation.

This cadaverous ambition and self-satisfied meanness, visible in Paley's yellow complexion and narrowed gaze, likely deterred Pen from pursuing the high offices of the Bench or the Woolsack with the necessary fervor. Instead, Pen embraced the Temple life with genuine enjoyment. Though his relatives believed he was diligently studying, retreating from the dullness of aristocratic balls, the reality was different. Having shed the pretensions of his aristocratic college days, Pen found novelty and delight in the rough pleasures of a London bachelorhood. He walked the parks to observe fine horses rather than ride them, content with his status despite lacking the fortune or name to fully penetrate high society. His uncle, the Major, mistakenly interpreted Pen's withdrawal from social gatherings as moral reform, unaware that Pen's "law studies" were merely a cover for a life filled with vigor and pleasure.

Pen's routine consisted of morning readings, walks in the park, excursions on the river, climbs to Hampstead, and modest tavern dinners. His nights were spent in jovial company, devoid of vice, guided by a sincere admiration for women that kept his associations pure in his estimation. Quiet evenings at home involved companionship, pipes, and British spirits, the quality of which was rigorously inspected by Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress. These pursuits constituted a life that was undeniably pleasant. During term time, Pen maintained a commendable regularity in attending Hall meals. The Upper Temple Hall itself offered a glimpse into the seventeenth century, complete with distinct messes for barristers and students, and a high table adorned with portraits of judicial luminaries and royalty. Pen observed this scene with amusement, noting the diverse composition of his fellow diners: elderly attorneys ascending to higher dignity, fashionable dandies seeking tenure, colonial natives preparing for local practice, and Irish gentlemen making temporary stays before returning home. The hall buzzed with varied conversations ranging from legal theory to sports, politics, and opera, allowing Pen to gradually forge connections across these disparate groups.

The Inn provided an excellent, wholesome dinner of soup, meat, tarts, and wine for a moderate fee, organized into messes of four sharing specific portions of beef, mutton, pie, and alcohol. However, the lower-ranking students, possessing a taste for refinement, employed various harmless strategies to enhance their banquets. "Wait a bit," urged Mr. Lowton, a seasoned gourmand who tugged at Pen's gown. "The side-tables are crowded, but if we wait, we might secure remnants from the benchers' table." Pen watched with amusement as Lowton cast longing glances toward the high table, where three elderly gentlemen stood before a dozen silver covers while the clerk recited a grace.

Lowton, a captain of the mess, aimed to secure the thirteenth glass of port and the prime cuts of meat, executing his appropriations with dexterity that delighted Pen. Despite his eagerness, Lowton's desires remained modest, confined to the simple pleasures of an eighteen-penny feast. Pen, appearing older and more haughty than his peers, stood out as a man of tone, contrasting sharply with the pale students debating law and the ferocious dandies in their rowing shirts. Recognizing Pen's superior presence, the humble Lowton initiated conversation, asking, "This is boiled-beef day, I believe, sir?" Pen, barely containing his laughter, admitted he was a stranger in his first term, prompting Lowton to begin pointing out the notable figures present in the Hall.
