Deronda, when he parted from Gwendolen, had kept back the words that rose and trembled at his lips—I shall not see you for a long while; I am going away. He would not give Grandcourt even the faintest excuse to imagine that his coming and going had any special weight in her mind. Yet Daniel himself was already moving under a weight that made every common act—promising, visiting, bowing, taking up his hat—feel like the outer skin of life, while something solemn and central pressed on within him, as if an old, half-buried experience had risen again and stood in the doorway, waiting to be named.

The message from Sir Hugo had been brief, jagged, and unlike him: Come immediately. Something has happened. Daniel went as a man goes through familiar rooms at night, guided by memory, yet aware that the house is altered. Even the street had a different character to his ear: the wheels, the calls, the opening and shutting of doors seemed to form a harsh chorus, a city-song too intent on commerce to be gentle. Underneath it he felt the craving for some steadier sound—wind in trees, a quiet human voice—something that would not be bought or sold. It was strange to notice this, and yet it was precisely in such moments that the world’s noise, with its perpetual insistence, made the inner life harder to hear.

He found Sir Hugo in his study, and the baronet’s greeting struck him at once as a thing changed. It was affectionate, yes, but grave; it was not the genial ease that usually filled the room like a warm fire. Daniel had come fearing distress—some blow to Sir Hugo, some misfortune to be met with cheerful resolution—and there was relief in finding no visible calamity, only an emotion held down, as if it were heavy.

“It is nothing to grieve you, sir?” Daniel said, and surprised himself by the steadiness he tried to keep, as he took the hand offered to him.

Sir Hugo looked at him with an unusual meaning, and his voice, when it came, seemed to have been softened by much thinking. “No, Dan—no. Sit down. I have something to say.”

Daniel obeyed. The chair, the table, the papers, the familiar objects had the sharp distinctness of things seen just before a storm breaks.

“It is not to grieve you, my boy—no,” Sir Hugo began again, as if reassuring himself while he reassured Daniel. “At least—if there is nothing in it that will grieve you too much. But I hardly expected that this—just this—would ever happen. There have been reasons why I have never prepared you for it. Reasons why I have never told you anything about your parentage. But I have striven in every way not to make that an injury to you.”

He paused. Daniel could not answer. It was not that he had a ready protest—You have never injured me—locked behind pride. It was that the words were too small for what he had lived with. Through all the years, that absence of knowledge had been like a dish set always covered on the table of his mind: the daily bread of affection and education served around it, yet the covered thing still there, never named, never offered. One learns, by necessity, to eat and be grateful; but the scent of what is withheld can mingle with every meal. He could not trust his voice.

Sir Hugo watched him, and the man who had delighted in Daniel’s noble look saw it now drained of color, the lips pressed together as if pain were being held in by force. A new anxiety softened Sir Hugo’s tone into almost a plea, as if he feared he might at last bruise what he had so carefully fostered.

“I have acted,” he said, “in obedience to your mother’s wishes. The secrecy was her wish. But now she desires to remove it. She desires to see you.” He drew out a letter and held it toward Daniel. “I will put this into your hands. You can look at it by-and-by. It will merely tell you what she wishes you to do, and where you will find her.”

The paper was foreign—its smoothness and faint, unfamiliar smell seemed to carry a hint of distance, of other rooms and voices, of another climate. Daniel took it and thrust it into his breast-pocket with instant relief at not having to read it there, under Sir Hugo’s eyes, while his own face betrayed whatever upheaval was passing within him. He felt as if a door had been opened on a corridor of his life he had never been allowed to enter.

The act of giving the letter shook Sir Hugo more than Daniel had expected. His composure, never theatrical, had been the habit of a man who believed that good intentions could set many things right. Now it wavered; he seemed to struggle for the right words, and for courage to say them.

Daniel’s whole soul, meanwhile, was possessed by a single question—one that had always been present like a faint ache, but now grew sharp and immediate. It was the hardest in the world to utter. Yet to delay was impossible. The moment had the solemnity of a vow: if he let it pass in silence, he felt he would lose the power to ask, and the answer would hover forever, unspoken, poisoning even gratitude.

For a little while his eyes were cast down, and the silence between them was full—so full that it was as if thoughts had become a palpable atmosphere. The ordinary noises outside, muffled by walls and curtains, beat on faintly, indifferent, while here, in a room made for comfort, two lives were poised over a truth.

At last Daniel lifted his eyes and looked at Sir Hugo. His voice, when it came, had a tremulous reverence in it, as if he feared that even a question might strike like a reproach against years of care. “Is my father also living?”

The answer came at once, low and emphatic, leaving no space for doubt. “No.”

In what followed, Daniel could not have distinguished joy from pain if he had tried. The word No fell like a stone into deep water: there was shock, a spreading circle of feeling, and then a strange hush. It altered retrospect, and yet did not complete it. Sir Hugo too seemed to receive a light thrown backward over his own acts—light that did not flatter him, but compelled him to see plainly.

After a silence in which Daniel felt like one whose old creed has fallen away before a new one can be embraced—left with bare longing and no form of faith to hold it—Sir Hugo spoke again. This time his voice had the tone of confession, stripped of all excuse.

“Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to undertake what I did. Perhaps I liked it a little too well—having you all to myself. But if you have had any pain which I might have helped, I ask you to forgive me.”

Daniel’s answer came with the quiet truth of something long settled in the heart. “The forgiveness has long been there,” he said. “The chief pain has always been on account of some one else—whom I never knew—whom I am now to know. It has not hindered me from feeling an affection for you which has made a large part of all the life I remember.”

The words seemed to loosen what both had been holding. In one impulse they clasped hands—briefly, firmly—as if to seal, without further speech, the bond that secrecy had strained but never broken. The letter lay against Daniel’s breast like a warm weight, a summons, and beyond the study—beyond the city’s ceaseless, wearing din—there waited a voice that had withheld itself from him, and now called him by right.
