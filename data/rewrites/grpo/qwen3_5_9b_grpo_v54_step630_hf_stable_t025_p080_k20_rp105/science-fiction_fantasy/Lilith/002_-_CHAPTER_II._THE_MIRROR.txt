Nothing more occurred for several days. It was roughly a week later that the events I must now recount finally unfolded.

For some time, I had been haunted by the thought of the manuscript fragment, repeatedly attempting to find a means to liberate it, yet failing every time. I could not discern what bound it so tightly. However, I had long harbored an intention to conduct a thorough inspection of the books stored in the closet; the peculiar atmosphere within had begun to unsettle me regarding their state. One day, this vague intention crystallized into a firm resolve. As I rose from my chair to begin the work, I caught a glimpse of something shadowy moving from the closet door toward the far end of the room.

It appeared to be a slight, stooping figure clad in a shabby dress-coat that dragged near the heels. As the garment parted slightly with each step, it revealed thin legs encased in black stockings and large feet shod in wide, slipper-like shoes.

Without hesitation, I followed. Whether I was pursuing a shadow or a substance remained uncertain, yet I never doubted the reality of my pursuit. My guide led me out of the library, across the hall, and to the base of the grand staircase. Ascending to the first floor, where the principal rooms lay, I trailed close behind. We passed these chambers and entered a wide corridor leading to a narrower stairway ascending to the second floor. He climbed it, and so did I. Upon reaching the summit, strange as it may seem, I found myself in a domain almost entirely unknown to me. Deprived of siblings to guide me through such explorations during my childhood, and taken away by my guardian before I could learn the house's secrets, I had never returned until about a month prior to reclaim my inheritance.

Through passage after passage, we arrived at the base of a winding wooden stair. Every step groaned under my weight, yet my guide made no sound. Halfway up, I lost sight of him, and by the time I reached the top, the shadowy form had vanished completely. The space was filled with shadows, yet he was not among them.

I stood in the main garret, a cavernous expanse dominated by massive beams and rafters overhead. Great voids surrounded me, punctuated only by distant doors and long vistas whose gloom was pierced by a few cobwebbed windows and small, dusky skylights. I gazed upon the wide, unexplored territory with a strange mixture of awe and delight; it was truly my own.

In the center of this vastness stood an unpainted enclosure of rough planks, its door standing ajar. Suspecting Mr. Raven might be within, I pushed it open and stepped inside.

The small chamber was bathed in light, yet it possessed the quality of light found only in deserted places—a dull, disconsolate luminescence, as if the room regretted its existence and found itself of no use. A few faint sunbeams, cutting tracks through the cloud of dust I had just stirred, fell upon a tall, narrow mirror with a dusty surface. It was old-fashioned, appearing merely as an ordinary glass pane set in an ebony frame. Perched atop the frame stood a black eagle with wings outstretched, clutching a golden chain in its beak, from which dangled a black sphere.

I had been observing the mirror rather than looking into it when suddenly I realized it reflected neither the chamber nor my own image. I have a fleeting impression that the wall itself seemed to melt away, though what followed explains any such confusion: could I have mistaken a mirror for the glass protecting a wondrous painting?

Before me lay a wild, broken landscape of heath and moor. Desolate hills of modest height, yet of a strangely alien appearance, occupied the middle distance, while the peaks of a far-off mountain range stretched along the horizon. Closest to me was a tract of flat, melancholy moorland.

Being short-sighted, I stepped forward to examine the texture of a stone in the immediate foreground. In doing so, I espied a large, ancient raven hopping toward me with solemn gravity. Its plumage was a purplish black, softened here and there with gray, and it seemed intent on finding worms. Unstartled by the presence of a living creature within a picture, I took another step forward to observe it more closely. Then, I stumbled—likely over the frame of the mirror—and found myself nose-to-beak with the bird. I was no longer in a room; I stood in the open air, upon a houseless heath.
