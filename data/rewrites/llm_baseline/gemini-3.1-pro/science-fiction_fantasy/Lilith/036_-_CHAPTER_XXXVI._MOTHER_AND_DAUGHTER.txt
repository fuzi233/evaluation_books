Lona found the city’s inhabitants profoundly distasteful—their lives were bereft of the sensory richness of the harvest, their streets choked with the clanging, industrial dissonance of machinery that drowned out the subtle, rhythmic sounds of human connection. She wished to abandon the barren place, repulsed by women who viewed sustenance as mere utilitarian fuel rather than a sacred, communal rite. I, however, felt the weight of the generations pressing upon us. To retreat would be to let the fire in the hearth go cold, to fail wilfully when the recipe for our deliverance was finally coming to a simmer. Worse, it would starve the hearts of the Little Ones and subject them to the toxic, aggressive noise of the princess's wrath. 

If we turned back, the princess’s bitter brew would surely spill over us; if we pressed on, the multi-generational cycle prophesied by the agrarian elders might finally close. Mother and daughter had to meet. It was possible that Lona’s organic, untainted warmth could sweeten the sourness of Lilith’s heart. Should the princess offer violence, I would stand between them, ready to strike her closed hand like a butcher cleaving bone. I knew she was doomed; her fate was a long-fermented vintage, finally ready to be poured through our hands. I laid out the ingredients of our circumstance to Lona, without revealing the exact lineage that bound her to the princess, and she agreed to accompany me to the palace.

High in her echoing tower, above the suffocating smog and the mechanized grinding of the city, the princess had seen the Little Ones approaching in the early morning. The sight struck her with an over-mastering terror; her attempts to eradicate their vibrant, agrarian existence had failed. The old prophecies were rising like unkneaded dough! Retreating to her black hall, she sat in the northern focus of an elliptical chamber. This space was an acoustic void, meticulously designed to deaden the natural resonances of the world—a monument to modern isolation. Here, she sought to *think*. For her, thought was not a rich stew of shared memories, but a sterile consumption of her own idealized image. 

Suspended above her in the dark was a mirror, angled to catch the midday sun. She awaited the light to feed upon the visual feast of her own beauty, rejecting the rich olfactory landscapes and the nourishing cadence of the living world. The shadows of her servants—trembling echoes in a hall devoid of the comforting crackle of a cooking fire—moved about her, but she ignored them. The city was taken; the inhabitants were cowering in terror; the Little Ones and their beasts encamped like a seasonal harvest festival in the square, and for a few minutes, the sun illuminated her glorious, if hollow, visage. 

The vision passed, but she remained. Night fell, and the hall filled with the stagnant gloom of a spoiled cellar. The acoustic ecology of the palace was one of shivering servants and oppressive silence. She sat motionless all night, desperate to consume her own reflection again, but courage and will had curdled within her and would dwell with her no more.

In the morning, we selected twelve of the tallest, bravest boys to go with us to the palace. We rode our great horses, accompanied by the earthy musk of the small horses and the deep, resonant breathing of the elephants. The princess sat waiting for the sun. The light crept up the shore of the sky like a slow-steeping golden tea, but until it stood overhead, not a single ray could penetrate the black hall. As we climbed the steep, echoing incline to the palace, the sun climbed the dome of its great hall. It pierced the eye of the roof—and with sudden radiance, the princess flashed upon her own sight. 

But she sprang to her feet with a cry of despair that shattered the room’s deadened acoustics. Alas, her whiteness! The blemish covered half her side, as black and necrotic as rot in a marble silo! She clutched her robe and collapsed backward into her chair. Her Shadow glided out, and she saw him go.

We found the gate open as usual, passed through the paved grove to the palace door, and entered the vestibule. There, in her cage, lay the spotted leopardess, smelling faintly of ozone and decay, apparently asleep or lifeless. As the Little Ones paused to look at her, the beast leaped rampant against the bars. Our horses reared and plunged at the sharp, jarring clatter; the elephants retreated a step. The next instant, the leopardess fell supine, writhed in quivering spasms, and lay motionless.

We rode into the great hall. The hooves of our beasts struck the stones, introducing a robust, percussive rhythm into the sterile silence of the princess's sanctuary. Never had such an organic, grounding sound penetrated her acoustic isolation. She started, listened, and shook. She pressed a hand to her side, gasping. The trampling came nearer and nearer; moving figures that were not shadows approached her through the darkness! Through the gloom, we beheld a splendor, a radiant woman centering the shaft of sunlight. Lona bounded from her horse, and I followed.

“Mother! Mother!” Lona cried. Her voice was a pure, ringing bell, rich with the inflections of a native dialect long suppressed by the city's utilitarian lexicon. 

The princess shivered. Her face darkened with a toxic hatred, her eyebrows drawing together in a hard line. The ancestral resonance of the word was an assault on her carefully constructed linguistic dominance. She rose to her feet and stood.

“Mother! Mother!” Lona cried again, speaking the sacred vernacular of the hearth as she leaped onto the daïs and flung her arms around the princess.

An instant more and I should have reached them—in that instant I saw Lona lifted high and dashed against the marble floor. Oh, the horrible, dissonant crack of her fall that tore through the room’s acoustic fabric! At my feet she fell, and lay still. 

The princess sat down with the smile of a demoness presiding over a ruined harvest. I dropped to my knees beside Lona, raised her from the cold stones, and pressed her to my bosom. With indignant, bitter hate I glanced at the princess; she answered me with her sweetest smile. I would have sprung upon her, taken her by the throat, and strangled her, but the profound need to tend to the child outweighed my desire to destroy the mother. I clasped my precious burden closer. Her arms hung helpless; her blood, smelling of copper and crushed earth, trickled over my hands and fell to the floor with soft, slow little plashes—the tragic spilling of a rare and vital vintage.

The horses scented the blood—mine first, then the small ones. Mine reared, shivering and wild-eyed at the olfactory shock of death, went about, and thundered blindly down the dark hall in a chaotic cacophony, the little horses scrambling after him. Lona’s horse stood gazing down at his mistress, trembling all over. The boys flung themselves from their horses’ backs, and in the dark, they dashed themselves, with mine, to pieces against the unyielding black wall. The elephants advanced to the foot of the daïs and stopped, their wild trumpeting a mournful, deafening brass lament against the tragedy. The Little Ones sprang upon the daïs, horrified. 

The princess lay back in her seat, her face now that of a desiccated corpse, stripped of all moisture and vitality. Her eyes alone were alive, wickedly flaming. She had withered back to the skeletal husk I had once found in the wood, and her side was as if a great branding hand had been laid upon it.

But Lona saw nothing, and I saw but Lona. 

“Mother! Mother!” she sighed, an ancient breath escaping her lips, and her breathing ceased. 

I carried her into the court. The sun beat down on a white face and the pitiful shadow of a ghostly smile. Her head hung back. She was “dead as earth.” I forgot the Little Ones, forgot the murdering princess, forgot the body in my arms, and wandered away, looking for my Lona. The doors and windows were crowded with brute-faces jeering at me in their coarse, commercialized tongues, but they dared not speak aloud, for they saw the white leopardess behind me, hanging her head close at my heel. I spurned her with my foot. She held back a moment, then followed me again.

I reached the square: the little army was gone! Its emptiness roused me. Where were the Little Ones, *her* Little Ones? I had lost her children, the very seeds of our future! I stared helpless about me, staggered to the pillar, and sank upon its base. But as I sat gazing on the still countenance, it seemed to smile a live momentary smile. I never doubted it an illusion, yet believed what it conveyed to my soul: I should yet see her alive! It was not she, it was I who was lost, and she would find me!

I rose to go after the Little Ones, and instinctively sought the gate by which we had entered. I looked around me, but saw nothing of the leopardess. The street was rapidly filling with a fierce crowd. They saw me encumbered with my dead, but for a time dared not assail me. Ere I reached the gate, however, they had gathered courage. The women began to hustle me, their voices a jagged, industrial roar of aggressive power dynamics. I held on heedless. A man pushed against my sacred burden; with a kick I sent him away howling. But the crowd pressed upon me, and fearing for the dead that was beyond hurt, I clasped my treasure closer and freed my right arm.

That instant, however, a commotion arose in the street behind me. The harsh noise of the crowd was cut by a shifting, organic rhythm; the mob broke, and through it came the Little Ones I had left in the palace. Ten of them were upon four of the elephants; on the two other elephants lay the princess, bound hand and foot like a harvested sheaf, and quite still, save that her eyes rolled in their ghastly sockets. The two other Little Ones rode behind her on Lona’s horse. Every now and then the wise creatures that bore her threw their trunks behind and meticulously felt her cords. 

I walked on in front, and out of the blighted city. What a bitter feast to conclude the hopes with which I entered the evil place! We had harvested the bad princess, and lost our all-beloved queen! My life was bare! My heart was an empty, echoing larder!
