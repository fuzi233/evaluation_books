My memories of my night at Hotherstone’s Farm are nothing but a jumble of vivid, disjointed impressions: the loud, warm welcome from the farmhands, a supper so massive it could have fed an entire village of East End laborers, a bedroom so spotless I could have eaten off the floor—ruined only by that abominable leftover of our ancestors’ foolish fads, a feather bed. I tossed for hours, striking match after match to light my tiny bedside candle, and heaved such a huge sigh of relief when the sun finally crested the horizon that I could have cheered.

We’d agreed the night before with Betteredge that I could rouse him any time I liked on our way to Cobb’s Hole, and my desperate eagerness to get my hands on Rosanna Spearman’s letter meant “any time” was as soon as humanly possible. I didn’t stop for breakfast, just stuffed a crust of bread in my pocket and set off, half-convinced I’d catch the old steward still snoring under his covers. To my immense relief, he was every bit as wired for the day ahead as I was: standing on his doorstep, walking stick already in his hand, waiting for me.

“Good morning, Betteredge. How are you feeling?”
“Terrible, sir. Got a new disease I invented myself, don’t want to alarm you but you’ll catch it before the morning’s out.”
“Devil I will, eh?”
“Feel that hot twist in the pit of your stomach? That thump behind your eyes? Ah, not yet? It’ll hit you the second we reach Cobb’s Hole. I call it detective-fever. First caught it hanging around Sergeant Cuff.”
“Right, right, and the cure’s opening Rosanna’s letter, I take it? Come on, let’s go.”

Early as we were, the fisherman’s cottage kitchen was already bustling. Betteredge introduced me, and Mrs. Yolland performed a rare social ritual reserved only for guests of distinction: she set a bottle of Dutch gin and two polished clean pipes on the table, and opened with, “What news from London, sir?”
Before I could fumble an answer to that impossibly broad question, a figure limped out of the dark corner of the room: a gaunt, wild girl with hair of remarkable beauty and a burning, fierce sharpness to her eyes. She thumped over on her crutch to my table, staring at me like I was an object of equal parts terrible fascination and horror.
“Mr. Betteredge,” she said, never taking her eyes off me. “Say his name again, please.”
“This gentleman,” Betteredge replied, leaning hard on the word “gentleman” out of respect, “is Mr. Franklin Blake.”

The girl turned her back on me and vanished. I barely registered Mrs. Yolland stammering apologies for her daughter Lucy’s odd behavior, or Betteredge translating those apologies into polite English for me. My entire attention was fixed on the thud of her crutch: thump-thump up the wooden stairs, thump-thump across the upper room, thump-thump back down the stairs. There she was in the open door, letter in hand, beckoning me outside.
I left the trailing stream of apologies behind me and followed her, her crutch thumping faster and faster down the slope of the beach. She led me behind a line of beached fishing boats, out of sight and earshot of the few villagers up and about, and stopped dead to face me for the first time.
“Stand there. Let me look at you.”

There was no mistaking the raw, unfiltered loathing on her face: she viewed me with total abhorrence and disgust. No other woman had ever let me see that look directed at me before, and it was impossible to endure it for long. I tried to redirect her to the obvious matter at hand.
“I believe you have a letter for me?”
“Say that again.”
I repeated the line, like a reciting schoolchild.
“No,” she muttered, half to herself, eyes still fixed unflinchingly on me. “I can’t see what she saw in his face. Can’t guess what she heard in his voice.” She dropped her gaze, resting her head wearily on the top of her crutch, and her voice went soft, the first gentle tone I’d heard from her. “Oh my poor dear lost Rosanna, what could you ever have seen in this man?”
She snapped her head back up, furious again. “Can you eat and drink? Can you sleep? When you see a poor working girl, do you feel no remorse at all?”
“Certainly not. Why on earth would I?”

She thrust the letter so hard at my chest it nearly poked me. “Take it! I never saw you before in my life. God forbid I ever lay eyes on you again.”
With that, she turned and limped away as fast as her crutch would carry her. I assumed, as anyone would, that the girl was unhinged. I turned to the far more urgent mystery in my hands. The envelope was addressed in Rosanna’s handwriting: *For Franklin Blake, Esq. To be placed directly in his own hands, by Lucy Yolland, no other person to be trusted with it.*

I broke the seal. Inside was a short letter, and a smaller slip of paper tucked beneath it. I read the letter first:
> Sir — If you are curious to understand my strange behaviour towards you during your stay at Lady Verinder’s house, follow every instruction written on the enclosed memorandum — and do it with no other person present to watch you.
> Your humble servant,

I turned to the slip. Every word was underlined at the end, sharp and deliberate:
> 1. Go to the Shivering Sand exactly at the turn of the tide.
> 2. Walk out on the South Spit of rocks until the South Spit Beacon lines up perfectly with the coastguard station flagstaff above Cobb’s Hole.
> 3. Lay a stick or other straight object on the rocks, dead along that aligned line. Make sure one end of the stick sits right at the rock’s edge, overlooking the quicksand.
> 4. Starting from the end of the stick pointing to the beacon, feel through the seaweed along the stick for the hidden metal chain.
> 5. Once you find the chain, follow it until it stretches over the rock’s edge and dangles down into the quicksand. Then pull the chain.

I’d barely finished reading the last underlined line when Betteredge’s voice boomed out behind me. The old steward had finally fully succumbed to his self-diagnosed detective-fever.
“I can’t stand it a second longer, Mr. Franklin! What does that letter say? For heaven’s sake, man, tell me what it says!”

I handed him both documents. He barely glanced at the first letter, but the memorandum made him yell so loud the seagulls took off from the beach.
“Sergeant Cuff said it! From first to last, sir, the Sergeant swore Rosanna wrote down the hiding place of whatever she stashed at the Shivering Sand! And here it is! The secret that stumped every last one of us, even the great Cuff, just waiting for you to dig it up! The tide’s low right now, any fool can see that. How long till it turns?” He craned his neck to yell at a local boy mending a net a short way off. “Tammie Bright! When’s the tide turn?”
“Hour from now!” the boy yelled back.

We checked our watches. “We can hike round the coast path to the quicksand, get there with loads of time to spare,” Betteredge said. “What do you say, sir?”
“Come on.”

On the walk over, Betteredge helped me piece back all the half-forgotten clues from the time of Sergeant Cuff’s investigation: Rosanna sneaking off to Frizinghall when the whole household thought she was bedridden with sickness, locking her bedroom door every night and burning her candle till dawn, buying that japanned tin case and two heavy dog chains from Mrs. Yolland, Cuff’s absolute certainty that Rosanna had hidden something in the Shivering Sand without ever guessing what that something could be. By the time we stepped out onto the South Spit rocks, all those loose ends were tied tight in my head.

We lined up the beacon and the coastguard flagstaff exactly as the memo directed, laid my walking stick along that line, and checked our watches again: twenty minutes still till the tide turned. I suggested we wait on the dry warm beach instead of the slippery wet rocks, but no sooner had I sat down than Betteredge announced he was leaving me.
“What for?” I asked, confused.
“Read the letter again, sir.”
A quick glance reminded me: Rosanna’s explicit order was that I had to make the discovery completely alone.
“Leaving you at a time like this is agony, sir,” Betteredge said, quietly earnest. “But she died a terrible, lonely death, poor soul. I feel I owe it to her to respect that last little fancy of hers. I’ll hang around in the fir plantation up the path, wait till you come get me. Don’t take too long, though. Detective-fever is hell to sit through when you’re stuck waiting for the secret to break.”

The wait felt endless, stretched thin by suspense. I lit a cigar to pass the time. The day was perfect, warm and bright, the air so fresh just breathing felt like a luxury. Even the Shivering Sand looked harmless that morning, its brown surface glinting golden in the sun, hiding the horror of the shifting quicksand beneath a pretty, smiling sheen. Before I’d finished my cigar, I saw it: the faint heave of the sand, that strange, shuddering ripple that crept across its entire surface like some unseen thing deep under the sand was waking up. The tide had turned.

I tossed my cigar and headed back to the rocks. I followed the memo’s instructions, feeling along the stick through the seaweed, and sure enough, half-buried in a crack in the rock, my fingers hit cold metal: the chain. It was tangled in a thick mat of seaweed that I couldn’t pull loose, so I knelt right at the edge of the rock, my face only a few feet above the heaving quicksand. A stupid, superstitious chill ran through me: what if Rosanna’s ghost rose up out of that shivering sand to point the way? I closed my eyes, my blood running cold even in the warm sun, and pushed my stick down into the sand.

I hit metal on the very first try.
I hauled the chain up, and there at the end of it, rusted fast to the links, was the japanned tin case. My hands were shaking so bad I nearly dropped it, but I pried the lid open, and found it full of thick, dry linen, perfectly preserved by the sealed case, plus a crumpled letter addressed to me. I laid the linen roll out on the dry sand and smoothed it flat. It was a nightgown. On the underside, bright and unmistakable, was the smudge of wet paint that had been ruined on Rachel Verinder’s boudoir door the very night the Moonstone vanished.

Sergeant Cuff’s voice rang in my head as clear as if he was standing right beside me: Find the garment with that paint stain on it. Find out who it belongs to. And if that person can’t explain how they were in that room between midnight and three a.m., you don’t have to look far for the hand that stole the Diamond.

I didn’t even need to open the letter in my pocket. I flipped the nightgown over to check the stitched name mark in the collar, the one every household stitched into all its linen. The ink was faded, but the letters were perfectly familiar, stitched right there:

I stared at the letters, unblinking. The sun was still shining. The bay was still glinting. Betteredge was tramping over the sandhills towards me, unable to wait a second longer. I’d left London swearing I’d hunt down the thief who stole the Moonstone, no matter how much time, work, or money it cost. I’d cracked the secret the Shivering Sand had guarded from every other living soul, stumped even the great Sergeant Cuff.
And the irrefutable, paint-stained proof was staring me right in the face. The thief I’d sworn to bring to justice was me.
