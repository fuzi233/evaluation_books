I have always possessed a profound affection for the arts of eating and drinking, though in my youth, my devotion leaned heavily toward the former. I possessed an appetite then, robust and unyielding, matched by a digestion that seemed to operate on a separate, more efficient clockwork. I recall a specific evening when a gentleman of dull eyes and a complexion the color of a bruised plum descended upon our household for dinner. He observed me consuming my meal for approximately five minutes, his gaze fixed with an intensity that bordered on the hypnotic. Finally, he turned to my father, his expression grave.

"Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?" he asked.

"My father replied, "I have never heard him complain of such a malady. Do you, Colly Wobbles?" (Note: 'Colly Wobbles' was my nickname, though my actual name remains a secret to history.)

"No, sir," I answered promptly. Immediately following this declaration of health, I added, with genuine curiosity, "What exactly is dyspepsia, sir?"

The livid gentleman regarded me with a mixture of astonishment and profound envy. Then, in a tone dripping with infinite pity, he slowly intoned, "You will know... some day."

My late mother, a woman of simple tastes, often expressed her delight in watching me consume my food. It remains a source of pleasant reflection to me that I must have provided her with considerable satisfaction in this regard. A growing, healthy lad, who exercises sufficiently and restrains himself from excessive study, can generally satisfy even the most exacting expectations regarding his feeding capabilities.

It is genuinely amusing to observe boys eating when one is not required to pay for the privilege. Their concept of a "square meal" involves a staggering array of provisions: a pound and a half of roast beef accompanied by five or six substantial potatoes (preferably soapy ones, as they offer greater substance), an abundance of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, followed by a couple of currant dumplings, several green apples, a pen'orth of nuts, half a dozen jumbles, and a bottle of ginger-beer. Upon completion of this gastronomic marathon, they proceed to play at horses.

One cannot help but wonder how they must despise us men, who are forced to sit quietly for a couple of hours after dining on a mere spoonful of clear soup and the wing of a chicken!

However, the boys do not possess all the advantages on their side. A boy never enjoys the luxury of being truly satisfied. A boy never feels full. He can never stretch out his legs, place his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and sink into the ethereal blissfulness that encompasses the well-dined man. A dinner makes no difference whatsoever to a boy. To a man, however, it is akin to a good fairy's potion; thereafter, the world appears brighter and better. A man who has dined satisfactorily experiences a yearning love toward all his fellow creatures. He strokes the cat quite gently, calling it "poor pussy" in tones full of tender emotion. He sympathizes with the members of the German band outside, wondering if they are cold; and for a fleeting moment, he does not even hate his wife's relations.

A good dinner brings out all the softer sides of a man. Under its genial influence, the gloomy and morose become jovial and chatty. Sour, starchy individuals, who spend the rest of the day looking as though they subsist solely on vinegar and Epsom salts, break out into wreathed smiles after dinner, exhibiting a sudden tendency to pat small children on the head and talk to them vaguely about sixpences. Serious men thaw and become mildly cheerful, while snobbish young men of the heavy-mustache type forget to make themselves objectionable.

I always feel sentimental myself after dinner. It is the only time I can properly appreciate love stories. Then, when the hero clasps "her" to his heart in one last wild embrace and stifles a sob, I feel as sad as though I had dealt at whist and turned up only a deuce; and when the heroine dies in the end, I weep. If I read the same tale early in the morning, I should sneer at it. Digestion—or rather, indigestion—has a marvelous effect upon the heart. If I wish to write anything very pathetic, I eat a large plateful of hot buttered muffins about an hour beforehand. By the time I sit down to my work, a feeling of unutterable melancholy has taken hold of me. I picture heartbroken lovers parting forever at lonely wayside stiles, while the sad twilight deepens around them, broken only by the tinkling of a distant sheep-bell. Old men sit and gaze at withered flowers until their sight is dimmed by the mist of tears. Little dainty maidens wait and watch at open casements; but "he cometh not," and the heavy years roll by, turning sunny gold tresses white and thin. The babies they dandled have become grown men and women with podgy torments of their own, and the playmates they laughed with lie very silent under the waving grass. Yet still they wait and watch, until the dark shadows of the unknown night steal up and gather round them, and the world with its childish troubles fades from their aching eyes.

I see pale corpses tossed on white-foamed waves, and death-beds stained with bitter tears, and graves in trackless deserts. I hear the wild wailing of women, the low moaning of little children, the dry sobbing of strong men. It is all the muffins. I could not conjure up a single melancholy fancy upon a mutton chop and a glass of champagne.

A full stomach is a great aid to poetry, and indeed, no sentiment of any kind can stand upon an empty one. We lack the time or inclination to indulge in fanciful troubles until we have rid ourselves of our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over dead dicky-birds with the bailiff in the house, and when we do not know where on earth to get our next shilling, we do not worry as to whether our mistress's smiles are cold, hot, lukewarm, or anything else.

Foolish people—when I say "foolish people" in this contemptuous manner, I mean those who entertain opinions differing from mine. If there is one person I despise more than another, it is the man who does not think exactly as I do on all topics. Foolish people, therefore, who have never experienced much of either, will tell you that mental distress is far more agonizing than bodily pain. What a romantic and touching theory! So comforting to the love-sick young sprig who looks down patronizingly at some poor devil with a white-starved face, thinking to himself, "Ah, how happy you are compared with me!" So soothing to fat old gentlemen who cackle about the superiority of poverty over riches. But it is all nonsense—all cant. An aching head soon makes one forget an aching heart. A broken finger will drive away all recollections of an empty chair. And when a man feels really hungry, he feels nothing else.

We sleek, well-fed folk can hardly realize what it feels like to be hungry. We know what it is to have no appetite and not care for the dainties placed before us, but we do not understand what it means to sicken for food—to die for bread while others waste it—to gaze with famished eyes upon coarse fare steaming behind dingy windows, longing for a pen'orth of pea pudding and lacking the penny to buy it—to feel that a crust would be delicious and that a bone would be a banquet.

Hunger is a luxury to us, a piquant, flavor-giving sauce. It is well worth while to get hungry and thirsty merely to discover how much gratification can be obtained from eating and drinking. If you wish to thoroughly enjoy your dinner, take a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and do not touch anything until you return. How your eyes will glisten at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes then! With what a sigh of content you will put down the empty beer tankard and take up your knife and fork! And how comfortable you will feel afterward as you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam round upon everybody.

Make sure, however, when adopting this plan, that the good dinner is actually to be had at the end, or the disappointment is trying. I remember once a friend and I—dear old Joe, it was. Ah! how we lose one another in life's mist. It must be eight years since I last saw Joseph Taboys. How pleasant it would be to meet his jovial face again, to clasp his strong hand, and to hear his cheery laugh once more! He owes me fourteen shillings, too. Well, we were on holiday together, and one morning we had breakfast early and started for a tremendous long walk. We had ordered a duck for dinner the previous night. We said, "Get a big one, because we shall come home awfully hungry;" and as we were going out, our landlady came up in great spirits. She said, "I have got you gentlemen a duck, if you like. If you get through that, you'll do well;" and she held up a bird about the size of a door-mat. We chuckled at the sight and said we would try. We spoke with self-conscious pride, like men who know their own power. Then we started.

We lost our way, of course. I always do in the country, and it makes me so wild, because it is no use asking directions of anyone you meet. One might as well inquire of a lodging-house slavey the way to make beds as expect a country bumpkin to know the road to the next village. You have to shout the question about three times before the sound of your voice penetrates his skull. At the third time, he slowly raises his head and stares blankly at you. You yell it at him then for a fourth time, and he repeats it after you. He ponders while you count a couple of hundred, after which, speaking at the rate of three words a minute, he fancies you "couldn't do better than—" Here he catches sight of another idiot coming down the road and bawls out to him the particulars, requesting his advice. The two then argue the case for a quarter of an hour or so, and finally agree that you had better go straight down the lane, round to the right, and cross by the third stile, keeping to the left by old Jimmy Milcher's cow-shed, across the seven-acre field, and through the gate by Squire Grubbin's haystack, keeping the bridle-path for a while until you come opposite the hill where the windmill used to be—but it's gone now—and round to the right, leaving Stiggin's plantation behind you; and you say "Thank you" and go away with a splitting headache, but without the faintest notion of your way, the only clear idea you have being that somewhere or other there is a stile which has to be got over; and at the next turn, you come upon four stiles, all leading in different directions!

We had undergone this ordeal two or three times. We had tramped over fields, waded through brooks, scrambled over hedges and walls. We had had a row as to whose fault it was that we had first lost our way. We had become thoroughly disagreeable, footsore, and weary. But throughout it all, the hope of that duck kept us up. A fairy-like vision, it floated before our tired eyes and drew us onward. The thought of it was as a trumpet-call to the fainting. We talked of it and cheered each other with our recollections. "Come along," we said; "the duck will be spoiled."

We felt a strong temptation, at one point, to turn into a village inn as we passed and have a cheese and a few loaves between us, but we heroically restrained ourselves: we should enjoy the duck all the better for being famished.

We fancied we smelled it when we entered the town and did the last quarter of a mile in three minutes. We rushed upstairs, washed ourselves, changed our clothes, came down, pulled our chairs up to the table, and sat rubbing our hands while the landlady removed the covers. When I seized the knife and fork and started to carve, it seemed to require a lot of attention. I struggled with it for about five minutes without making the slightest impression. Then Joe, who had been eating potatoes, wanted to know if it wouldn't be better for someone to do the job that understood carving. I took no notice of his foolish remark but attacked the bird again; so vigorously this time that the animal left the dish and took refuge in the fender.

We soon had it out of that, though, and I was prepared to make another effort. But Joe was getting unpleasant. He said that if he had thought we were to have a game of blind hockey with the dinner, he would have gotten a bit of bread and cheese outside.

I was too exhausted to argue. I laid down the knife and fork with dignity and took a side seat, while Joe went for the wretched creature. He worked away in silence for a while, and then he muttered "Damn the duck" and took his coat off.

We did break the thing up at length with the aid of a chisel, but it was perfectly impossible to eat, and we had to make a dinner off the vegetables and an apple tart. We tried a mouthful of the duck, but it was like eating India-rubber.

It was a wicked sin to kill that drake. But there! There is no respect for old institutions in this country.

I started this paper with the idea of writing about eating and drinking, but I seem to have confined my remarks entirely to eating as yet. Well, you see, drinking is one of those subjects with which it is inadvisable to appear too well acquainted. The days are gone by when it was considered manly to go to bed intoxicated every night, and a clear head and a firm hand no longer draw down upon their owner the reproach of effeminacy.
