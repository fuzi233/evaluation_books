The following morning, the first of March, dawn broke with a struggle. As I rose and pulled back my curtain, the sun fought its way through a thick, clinging fog. Hovering above the rooftops, nearly level with the clouds themselves, loomed a solemn, orb-like mass of dark blue and dimness—THE DOME. As I gazed upon it, a stirring occurred within my soul; my spirit, usually bound by invisible chains, fluttered its wings free. For the first time, I felt a sudden conviction that I, who had never truly lived, was finally about to taste life. In that single morning, my spirit expanded as rapidly as Jonah's gourd.

"I acted wisely in coming," I told myself, dressing with hurried efficiency. "I possess a genuine affinity for the grand spirit of this great London. Who, save a coward, would spend a lifetime in petty hamlets, allowing their faculties to rot in the damp rust of obscurity?"

Dressed and composed, I descended. I was not weary or travel-worn, but tidy and refreshed. When the waiter brought my breakfast, I greeted him with a mixture of sedateness and cheerfulness. Within ten minutes, we established a useful rapport.

He was a grey-haired elderly man who had served in his current post for twenty years. Knowing this, I was confident he remembered my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, who had visited frequently fifteen years prior. Mentioning their names prompted a respectful recollection. This knowledge clarified my standing in his eyes; his previously doubtful demeanor vanished, replaced by ready, obliging courtesy. From this moment, I needed fear no longer regarding how to respond to sensible inquiries.

The street outside my small sitting-room was narrow, perfectly quiet, and spotless. The few pedestrians resembled those found in provincial towns; there was nothing formidable. I felt secure venturing out alone.

Breakfast concluded, and I stepped forth. Elation filled my heart; to walk alone in London felt like an adventure in itself. Soon, I found myself in Paternoster Row, classic territory. Entering a bookseller's shop kept by Mr. Jones, I purchased a small volume—a minor extravagance I could ill afford, yet one I hoped to gift to Mrs. Barrett someday. Mr. Jones, a dried-up man of business, stood behind his desk, appearing one of the gravest figures imaginable, while I felt among the happiest.

That morning, I experienced a prodigious amount of life. Standing before St. Paul's, I ascended to the dome. From there, I beheld London: its river, its bridges, its churches. I saw ancient Westminster and the verdant Temple Gardens bathed in sunlight, beneath a glad, blue sky of early spring, separated from the earth by a haze of clouds that was not too dense.

Descending, I wandered wherever chance led, intoxicated by a still ecstasy of freedom. Somehow, I plunged into the heart of city life. I traversed the Strand, climbed Cornhill, mingled with the rushing crowds, and dared the perils of crossing the streets. Doing this entirely alone yielded a peculiar, perhaps irrational, yet profound pleasure. Though I have since visited the West End, its parks, and its fine squares, I prefer the city. The city feels more earnest; its business, its rush, its roar are serious sights and sounds. The city is earning its living; the West End merely enjoys its pleasures. In the West End, one may be amused, but in the city, one is deeply excited.

Eventually, faint and hungry—a sensation I hadn't felt in years—I returned to my dark, old, quiet inn around two o'clock. I dined on two plain dishes: a joint and vegetables. Both tasted excellent, infinitely superior to the tiny, dainty meals Miss Marchmont's cook used to send to my late mistress and me, which we could barely muster an appetite to discuss. Delightedly tired, I lay across three chairs for an hour (for the room lacked a sofa). After sleeping, I spent two hours in thought.

My state of mind, combined with these circumstances, favored the adoption of a new, resolute, and daring—even desperate—course of action. I had nothing to lose. An unutterable loathing for my desolate past forbade returning. If I failed in my design, who, save myself, would suffer? If I died far from home—or rather, from England—who would weep?

I might suffer; I was accustomed to it. Death itself held no terrors for me that it possessed for the softly raised. Having viewed mortality with a quiet eye, I prepared for any consequence and formulated a plan.

That evening, I secured from my friendly waiter information regarding sailings to a specific Continental port, Boulogne-sur-Mer. There was no time to waste; I must take my berth that very night. Although I could have waited until morning, I refused to risk arriving too late.

"Better take your berth at once, ma'am," advised the waiter. Agreeing, I settled my bill and tipped him generously—a sum I now realize was princely, though to him it must have seemed absurd. As he pocketed the cash, he offered a faint smile hinting at my *savoir-faire*. He then summoned a coach. He instructed the driver to take me directly to the wharf and not leave me to the watermen. The driver promised compliance but betrayed his oath immediately. Instead of a dignified transfer, he deposited me as an offering into the hands of a throng of watermen.

This proved an uncomfortable crisis. It was a dark night. The coachman fled the moment he received his fare, and the watermen immediately engaged in a struggle over me and my trunk. Their oaths still ring in my ears today; they shook my philosophy more profoundly than the darkness, the isolation, or the strangeness of the scene. One grabbed my trunk. I watched calmly. But when another reached for me, I spoke firmly, shook off his grasp, and stepped straight into a boat. I commanded sternly that the trunk be placed beside me—"Just there"—an instruction instantly obeyed. The owner of the boat I chose became my ally, and I was rowed away.

The river was black as a torrent of ink, reflecting lights from the surrounding buildings. Ships rocked on its bosom. We rowed past several vessels—"The Ocean," "The Phoenix," "The Consort," "The Dolphin"—until we reached "The Vivid," my ship, lying further down.

Gliding down the sable flood, I thought of the Styx and Charon ferrying a solitary soul to the Land of Shades. Amidst this strange tableau, with a chilling wind biting my face and midnight rain falling, accompanied by two rude rowers whose insane oaths still tortured my ear, I questioned whether I was wretched or terrified. I was neither. Often in my life I have suffered far more under safer conditions. "How is this?" I wondered. "I feel animated and alert, not depressed or apprehensive." I could not explain why.

"The Vivid" emerged from the black night, white and glaring. "Here you are!" shouted the waterman, instantly demanding six shillings.

"You ask too much," I replied. He retreated from the vessel, swearing he would not embark me without payment. A young man, the steward, leaned over the ship's side, grinning in anticipation of the dispute. To disappoint him, I paid the money. That afternoon, I had paid crowns where shillings sufficed, but I comforted myself with the reflection: "It is the price of experience."

"They've cheated you!" exclaimed the steward triumphantly as I boarded. I answered phlegmatically, "I knew it," and went below.

In the ladies' cabin sat a stout, handsome, and showy woman. Requesting my berth, she scrutinized me closely, muttering about the unusual nature of passengers boarding at such an hour, and displayed a disposition less than civil. What a face she had—so comely, yet so insolent and selfish!

"Now that I am on board, I shall certainly stay here," I declared. "Please show me my berth."

She complied, albeit sullenly. Removing my bonnet and arranging my things, I lay down. Difficulties had been overcome; a sort of victory achieved. My homeless, anchorless, unsupported mind finally found leisure for brief repose. Until the *Vivid* docked, no further action was required, though... Oh! I could not look ahead. Harassed and exhausted, I drifted into a half-trance.

The stewardess talked incessantly all night—not to me, but to her young son, the steward, who was her very image. He moved constantly in and out of the cabin. They disputed, quarreled, and reconciled twenty times throughout the night. She claimed to be writing a letter home to her father, reading passages aloud while ignoring me as if I were a piece of furniture. Several passages revealed family secrets, specifically concerning a younger sister named Charlotte, seemingly on the verge of a romantic and imprudent match. The elder lady protested loudly against this distasteful union, while the dutiful son laughed her correspondence to scorn. She defended it and raged at him. They were a strange pair. She might have been thirty-nine or forty, yet bloomed as vibrantly as a girl of twenty. Hard, loud, vain, and vulgar, her mind and body seemed brazen and imperishable. I suspected she had lived in public stations since childhood, perhaps working as a barmaid in her youth.
