Mrs. Lambert, her handkerchief held aloft as a token of farewell, stood with her daughters at the edge of the road, watching the departing travellers until the curve of the lane, thick with lime trees, hid them from view. That grove had long been a silent witness to farewells—her husband marching off to war, her sons riding away to school, each vanishing behind the green screen, returning only when fate allowed. Each departure left behind a quiet ache, softened by memory and love, and nurtured by the stillness of home life. In their absence, the women of Oakhurst found time to dwell on the men who were gone: the empty room where a boy had slept, the imprint of his carpet bag still visible on the bed; the whip hanging in the hall, the fishing rod and basket left unused—silent reminders of days past.

At meals, the absence lingered: the half-eaten cherry tart, once shared with laughter and melancholy; the evening prayers said without one voice; the midnight silence when thoughts turned to the child now far from the nest. Dawn brought no comfort—only the knowledge that the world beyond the gates was hard and demanding. So the limes became more than trees; they were a veil between the domestic warmth of Oakhurst and the uncertain road ahead.

Mrs. Lambert often walked alone to the edge of the grove, her heart heavy. She once mused aloud about carving the names of her loved ones into the silver-grey bark, entwined with true-lovers’ knots—a custom of tender affection. Miss Theo, gifted with poetry, composed verses about the trees, which her mother proudly sent to a journal of the time.

“Now we’re out of sight,” Colonel Lambert called, tipping his hat as he rode past the limes with Mr. Warrington beside him. “My wife always watches from the window until we round this bend. I hope you’ll see these trees and this house again, Mr. Warrington. The boys are home now—perhaps there’ll be better sport for you.”

“I’ve never been happier,” replied Warrington, “and I feel as though I’m leaving old friends behind.”

“The man we’ll dine with tonight has a son—an old friend of our family,” said the Colonel. “My wife, ever eager for matches, would have paired him with one of my girls—had not the young fellow fallen in love with someone else.”

“Oh?” sighed Warrington. “Others have done the same. There were brave men before Agamemnon.”

“Pardon me, sir,” the younger man interjected, “but I didn’t catch the name—Aga…?”

“No,” laughed the Colonel, “his name is James Wolfe. He’s barely thirty, yet the youngest lieutenant-colonel in the army—unless we count the nobility, who take rank above us commoners.”

“Of course,” said the young companion, nodding with colonial deference to aristocratic order.

“And I’ve seen him command captains—brave men, all—some thirty years his senior, none of whom could match his merit or fortune. Yet no one envies him. We all admit he’s our superior. He’s beloved by every man in his old regiment, knows each one by name. A scholar too—he speaks many languages.”

“Ah,” breathed Harry Warrington, “I feel I’ve wasted my youth. I come to England an ignoramus. Had my brother lived, he would have carried our name with dignity. George was a scholar, a musician, a man who could hold his own among the most learned. I’m glad to be here, especially to you, if only to learn how much I don’t know.”

“If you know that,” smiled the Colonel, “you’ve already gained much.”

“At home, since my brother died, I used to think myself quite remarkable. I suppose folks flattered me. Now I see I was wrong. I wish I knew more of books and less of dogs and horses.”

“Dogs and horses are fine books too,” said the Colonel. “Some men aren’t meant to be scholars. They may still be worthy citizens. Why should all of us strive to be wise or great? His Royal Highness commands, Martin Lambert is colonel, Jack Hunt—riding behind us—is a groom. Each does his duty. Who can say which station is higher? Perhaps Jack’s currycomb, my epaulettes, or the Prince’s baton are equal in the eyes of Heaven. When I began life, I dreamed of glory. Now I dream of duty—and of those we left behind. Let us ride on. We’ll reach Westerham before night.”

At Westerham, they were welcomed by a stately matron, an elderly soldier whose service spanned forty-five years, and their son—Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe of Kingsley’s regiment, then stationed at Maidstone. Harry watched the young officer with interest. He was lean, pale, with red hair and sharp features—no beauty in his face. But his manners were refined, his greetings warm, and his conversation lively enough to make one forget his plain looks.

Mr. Warrington was bound for Tunbridge? Then James would accompany him, the lady said, whispering something to Colonel Lambert at supper. The Colonel raised his glass and toasted “Miss Lowther.”

“With all my heart!” cried James, draining his cup.

The hostess murmured to her friend that James and Miss Lowther were destined to marry—she came from the famed Lowther family of the North.

“If she were Charlemagne’s daughter,” said Lambert, “she’d still be good enough for James—or for any son of mine.”

“She wouldn’t be so keen on that,” James declared.

“Oh, of course,” chimed in the mother, “she’s the priceless pearl, and you’re nothing.”

“No,” said James firmly, “I agree with Colonel Lambert. If she brought all of Cumberland as a jointure, it would be my due. That’s how it goes, Mr. Warrington. We nurse our children through fevers and smallpox, send them to war, break our hearts at parting, and get them home only for a few weeks a year. Then along comes a girl with bright eyes—and our boy is gone, never thinking of us again.”

“And pray, my dear,” asked the elder Colonel Wolfe, “how did you come to marry James’s father?”

“Because he had gout and needed someone to care for him,” she replied. “Not because I liked him much.” And so the evening passed in easy talk and gentle humor.

The next day, Colonel Lambert bid farewell to his guest with heartfelt kindness, turning his horse homeward while Harry and James rode toward Tunbridge Wells.

Wolfe was eager to arrive. Warrington, perhaps less so—his thoughts lingering on Oakhurst, on the calm days spent in that peaceful home.

James praised the Lamberts warmly—father, mother, daughters, all.

“To live as Colonel Lambert lives, and to carry such a name,” said Wolfe, “seems to me the height of human ambition.”

“And glory?” asked Warrington. “Is that nothing? Would you give up fame?”

“It was my dream once,” replied Wolfe. “But now my desires are quieter. I’ve served since fourteen. I’ve known every kind of duty, visited every garrison town, served wherever there was work. I’ve done nearly all a soldier’s tasks—except command an army, which is unlikely for one of my age. Now I crave peace: books, a wife, children on my knee. I’ve imagined such a life. True love is better than glory. A quiet hearth, with the woman of your heart beside it—that is the greatest gift the gods can give.”

Harry pictured the scene. He nodded, but his expression was somber. James noticed.

“You say ‘yes’ as if a fireside and a sweetheart weren’t to your taste.”

“Well,” said Harry, “there are other things a young man might want. You’ve seen sixteen years of the world. I’ve just left my mother’s apron strings. When I’ve fought campaigns, like you, distinguished myself—then I may think of retiring.”

To this, James, his heart full of another matter, responded with a passionate defense of marriage, praising the virtues of his beloved—words that thrilled him, though perhaps not his listener, whose views on wedlock were faintly bleak.

They reached Tunbridge Wells by late afternoon, uneventfully—save for a moment when Harry, mistaking two riders for highwaymen, drew his pistol. James laughed and stopped him: “Only innkeepers’ agents—robbers only in trade.”

Gumbo, their horse, bolted briefly, causing much shouting, but was soon recovered. They entered the town, checked into their inn, and parted ways, each seeking the ladies they had come to visit.

Mr. Warrington found his aunt installed in elegant lodgings, guarded by London footmen. She greeted him warmly. Lady Maria was absent—whether he regretted this, or concealed his feelings, remained unknown. Madame de Bernstein, meanwhile, received him with several visitors: a dandy in rich brocade, two dowagers in vast hoops and rouge.

She introduced Harry as “the young Croesus from Virginia,” speaking of his estate—“as large as Kent, and infinitely richer.” She told how her half-sister, Madam Esmond, was known as Princess Pocahontas in America. She praised his mother and son endlessly—wealth, character, virtue.

The dandy shook Harry’s hand, delighted. The ladies lavished praise upon him. He blushed under their adoration.

When they departed, the Baroness turned to her nephew.

“You couldn’t have arrived at a better time,” she said. “Those three have tongues like bells. They’ll spread your name across every drawing room. I’ve already introduced you to a hundred people—and lied shamelessly about Virginia. I’ve filled it with gold mines, spices, diamonds—possibly even elephants. Your mother, I’ve claimed, rules over a kingdom with armies of negroes. It’s a magnificent dominion. I don’t know exactly how much she earns—but it’s enormous. And you, my dear, will be treated as heir to this royal lady. Don’t let it go to your head.”

“But why must I be so rich?” Harry asked.

“To open doors,” she replied. “Being thought rich is as good as being rich. People will assume you hoard your money—that’ll do you good. Mothers will smile. Daughters will curtsey. When I was young, I tried to improve my lot through marriage. Your grandmother, a saint save for a little jealousy, scolded me—called me worldly. Worldly? So is the world. We must serve it as it serves us. Give it nothing for nothing.”

“Henry Esmond Warrington,” she added, “I adore those two names. Coming here or to London, you’d have been nobody. Our protection means little. Our family has little credit—Castlewood was suspected in ’45, ruined by gambling. He’s lost everything—mortgages devour his estate. He’s tried every scheme. Once, I feared my diamonds were unsafe with him—I traveled without them. Terrible, isn’t it, to speak so of one’s own nephew? But you are my nephew too—and untouched by the world. I warn you: it is wicked.”

She spoke of Harry’s card games with Will and the chaplain—“harmless fun,” she said. “But had you played with Castlewood, you’d have lost everything. I warned him to keep his hands off you.”

“You interfered to protect me?”

“I kept his claws from you. Be thankful you escaped with flesh on your bones. Gambling has consumed our family. My brother played. Both his wives—especially the last—live only for cards. I wouldn’t leave you alone with her. The passion is too strong. They’d fleece you, then fight over the spoils. Castlewood’s position at Court is Will’s fortune—and Maria’s and Fanny’s.”

“And do they play?”

“No—Molly doesn’t. But Fanny, in London, bets her eyes out. I know the passion. I had it—like measles. Not cured. For a poor old woman, there’s nothing left but cards. You’ll see high stakes tonight. Hush—this is what I need. At Castlewood, I couldn’t win from my nieces or their mother. They refused to pay. Best to warn you now—lest you be shocked.”

A few days earlier, Harry—then fond of cards, cockfighting, betting—would have laughed at such confession. Among the Castlewoods, morality was mocked. Faith and honor were jokes. Selfishness was normal. Duties were sneered at. Sin was shrugged off. They didn’t pretend to be better than others—they lived with them, joked about them, took their share of pleasure and plunder. They lived day by day, without pretense.

Such people are rare today. Human nature has changed. Card-playing is nearly extinct—few fashionable ladies know Spadille from Manille.

“How dull you must have found those village folk,” said Madame de Bernstein, patting his cheek. “But the savages were kind, child!”

“They were very kind,” Harry replied, his face flushing. “It wasn’t dull at all. I think they’re some of the best people in the world.”

His aunt’s tone offended him. He couldn’t bear anyone slighting the new friends he’d found. He didn’t want them spoken of in such company.

The old lady, quick to anger, paused. *Those two girls—sickbed—interesting stranger—of course he’s fallen for one of them.*

Madame de Bernstein glanced mischievously at Lady Maria, who entered the room just then.
