Harry Feversham had traveled to Dublin by the night mail, emerging from the city into the pre-dawn light before crossing Lough Swilly. He arrived in Ramelton aboard a small cargo steamer, a vessel that ventured up the Lennon River only once a week. Waiting for him on the quay was Ethne, seated in a dog-cart. She offered him her hand and a smile that transcended simple camaraderie, warm and immediate.

"You seem surprised to see me," she observed, noticing the shadow in his gaze.

"I am always surprised," he replied softly. "Because you always surpass whatever I imagined you might be." As the smile returned to her face, it transformed, shedding the casual warmth of friendship for something deeper, more intimate.

"I shall drive slowly," she said as the luggage was secured. "I brought no groom on purpose. Guests arrive tomorrow, and we have only today."

Together, they traversed the wide causeway beside the river and turned onto the steep, narrow streets of Ramelton. Feversham sat in silence, absorbing the scene. It was his first visit to the town, and he viewed it with a mixture of curiosity and a peculiar sense of ownership. He noted the dense, dark thicket of tall trees climbing the far bank, the old grey bridge spanning the water, and the gentle, sleepy rhythm of the town below. These were not just sights; they were fragments of Ethne's life.

Ethne was twenty-one, tall and strong, with limbs supple as a dancer's. Her shoulders were broad and squared, lacking the fashionable slop of the era, yet possessing a natural, unpretentious grace. Her hair, dark brown, was coiled tightly at the nape of her neck, framing a face flushed with health. Her eyes were a striking, clear grey, meeting others with a direct, unguarded honesty. Her spirit mirrored her appearance: honest, simple, and robust, blending gentleness with a strength that excluded violence.

Later, Ethne recounted a local legend that Feversham had heard before, though it never failed to thrill him anew. Years ago, standing at a door high on the hill leading to the river, her horse had spooked at the sound of a pail clattering nearby. The reins slipped from her grasp, falling to the ground just as the terrified animal bolted downhill toward the sharp curve near the bridge. Seated helpless in the cart, with the horse tearing toward disaster, Ethne acted with terrifying coolness. She leaped over the front of the cart as it pitched, balanced herself along the shafts, snatched the reins from the horse's neck, and brought the beast to a halt ten yards short of the drop.

During their journey, Ethne was nearly as silent as Feversham, speaking only when her thoughts drifted elsewhere. It was not until they left the town and hit the straight, rolling road toward Letterkenny that she turned to him.

"I noticed this morning that your regiment has been ordered to Egypt," she said, her voice steady and level. "You could have gone with them if I hadn't intervened. There would have been chances for distinction. I have hindered you, and I am sorry. You couldn't possibly know of the transfer, but I understand how difficult it must be to be left behind."

Feversham stared ahead, his jaw tight. "You need not apologize," he rasped.

"Why?" she pressed gently. "How can I help it? I blame myself because I do not see things like other women. If you had gone and the worst had happened, I would have been lonely, yes, but I would have known that eventually, when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another."

Her words were delivered without emotion, as if stating an obvious fact. Feversham caught his breath, his chest tightening, yet he remained still, unable to trust himself to speak.

"You see, I can endure the absence of those I care for better than most," she continued, searching for the right words. "Life moves along in a dull sort of way, and then suddenly a face springs out from the crowd. You recognize it instantly as a friend, as if you had been waiting for them all along. Such friends—there are few, but they count. One does not lose them, whether they are absent or..."

"Unless," Feversham interjected slowly, "one has made a mistake. What if the face in the crowd is a mask? What if one recognizes the wrong person?"

Ethne shook her head decisively. "Not that kind. One may seem to err, perhaps for a long time, but in the end, the truth proves otherwise."

Her unwavering faith tightened around Feversham like a vice. "Ethne," he cried, breaking his silence, "you don't know—"

At that moment, Ethne reigned in her horse, laughing and pointing her whip forward. They had crested a hill overlooking open fields and a wood of oaks. Built into the stone wall to their left was a scarlet letter-box.

"I wanted to show you that," she interrupted cheerfully. "That is where I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times."

Feversham let slip the confession he had been preparing. "The letter-box is very convenient."

"Yes," she agreed, driving on until they reached a crumbling section of the park wall. "Here is where I used to climb over. There is a tree on the other side just as handy. I used to run down the half-mile avenue at night."

"There might have been thieves," Feversham warned.

"There were thorns," Ethne corrected, turning through the gates and up to the porch of the long, irregular grey house. "We have still a day before the dance."

"The whole countryside must be coming," Feversham remarked.

"It daren't do anything else," Ethne laughed. "Father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away. Just as he fetched Mr. Durrance. By the way, Mr. Durrance sent me a present—a Guarnerius violin."

The door opened to reveal a thin, lanky old man with a fierce, peaked face resembling a bird of prey. His features softened instantly upon seeing Feversham, a smile playing on his lips. To a stranger, it might have looked like a wink, though his left eyelid perpetually drooped.

"How do you do?" he boomed. "Glad to see you. Make yourself at home. If you want whiskey, stamp twice on the floor. The servants understand." With that, he retreated inside.

Biographers of Lord Dermod Eustace required a wary mind. Though he had died less than twenty years prior, he had swollen into a legendary figure, his anecdotes growing like barnacles upon his name. Anecdotes could be invented and sold to anyone with a knack for storytelling, and they were readily believed. However, the facts were undeniable: Dermod practiced a tyrannical hospitality, forcing strangers to stay, and was a man of a bygone century who glowered angrily at the modern world. He was an intoxicated Coriolanus, believing the people needed a shepherd's staff, yet retaining a chivalry toward the lowliest. The townsfolk of Ramelton proudly claimed that even when he galloped down the steep, cobblestoned streets on his seventeen-hand white mare, accompanied by his gaunt collie, drunk and swaying, he had never ridden down a single human being. Two further truths defined him: a profound, cautious fear of his daughter, who kept him guessing regarding her approval, and a sudden, intense affection for Harry Feversham.

Harry saw little of Dermod that day. The old lord retired to his "office," leaving Feversham and Ethne alone. They spent the afternoon fishing for salmon in the Lennon River. It was a Sabbath-like afternoon, the birds holding their breath. From the house, the lawns fell steeply into a valley where the river flowed swift and black beneath overarching branches. They sat opposite a rocky spur where the water broke in an amber wave, talking occasionally but mostly listening to the roar of the current. When sunset finally painted the sky and long shadows stretched across the grass, they rose, smiling at one another, and walked slowly back to the house. It was a day Feversham would remember forever, for the next night was the night of the ball.

As the band struck up the opening bars of the fourth waltz, Ethne slipped away from her post near the drawing-room door. Taking Feversham's arm, she led him out into the empty hall. The front door stood open, letting in the cool summer air, while the swaying music and the beat of dancing feet drifted from the ballroom. Ethne exhaled, relieved to escape her duties, and moved toward a side table.

"The post is in," she announced. "Letters, one, two, three, for you... and a little box."

She extended the box—a small, white jeweler's cardboard container—and paused, struck by its apparent lack of weight.

"It must be empty," she murmured.

Yet it was meticulously sealed and tied. Feversham broke the seals and untied the string. He glanced at the address; it was forwarded from his London lodgings, the handwriting unfamiliar.

"There seems to be some mistake," he said as he lifted the lid. Then, abruptly, he froze.
