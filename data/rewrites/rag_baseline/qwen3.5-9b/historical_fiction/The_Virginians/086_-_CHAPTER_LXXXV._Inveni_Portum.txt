The silence in the house was not empty; it was a calculated absence, a deliberate withdrawal of resources designed to starve me into submission. My mother had severed my allowance, expecting me to fold and return to Virginia, to kneel beneath her roof and her authority. We never resolved the pecuniary dispute that lay between us until her final breath. She withheld her gold; I withheld my voice, managing a precarious existence without her aid. Perhaps, in her dying moments, she harbored a repentance she never voiced aloud. Years later, when we met, her behavior shifted—a gentle deference, an exaggerated respect for my wife, Madam Esmond. It was a language of tacit admission, suggesting she understood her error. Yet, she offered no apology, and I demanded none.

Harry was secure, his welfare a fact I could not begrudge. Consequently, every penny of my mother's hoarded economy flowed to my advantage, enriching the heir she had tried to disinherit. Time, that great equalizer, had rendered the struggle moot. When we finally stood face to face, the era of necessity was a distant memory. I possessed abundance; she could offer only surplus. No doubt she carried the weight of guilt—that my children had hungered while her pride turned away, that strangers had filled the void her stinginess created. Was she truly prouder than I? A single word of explanation might have healed the rift decades prior. But I chose silence. When I recognize a fault in myself, I seek pardon, yet it is rarely for the victim's sake; it is a transaction with my own ego, a way to absolve the shame of failing myself. This obsession with self-preservation prevents me from performing the lowly acts of humility. Consider the men whose lives are constructed entirely of lies and subterfuge. What company do they keep when the masks slip? Every smile is a performance, every wink a deception. Do they wear a mask even in their solitude, facing their own consciences? If I overlook an injury, it is not from a spirit of Christian charity, but because I possess the luxury of remitting the debt and despise the idea of settling accounts. I have known rare souls for whom forgiveness is effortless, growing naturally from the soil of their hearts. I know how to remit; I do not know how to forgive. Are we proud men proud simply to be proud?

Thus, I showed no sign of submission to my parent in Virginia. For years, we existed in a state of polite estrangement, exchanging only the barest announcements—the birth of a child, a seasonal greeting. Then, the storms of the Stamp Act broke upon us in America, and shortly thereafter, the political tempests of London claimed me. Though I aligned myself with the Tories, knowing full well I would lose, I published a letter to a member of the House of Burgesses that exposed the habitual insolence of the mother country. The sentiments were unacceptable to those in power. I was stripped of my position as hackney-coach licenser. My uncle recoiled in terror and horror. He could never endure a man in disgrace. While he cultivated an extreme affection for my wife and my son, viewing them with protective tenderness, he regarded me with a pitying contempt that amused me endlessly. He possessed a natural scorn for poverty and a corresponding, insatiable love for success. Any deviation from the conventional path shocked him; truth-telling made him pale. Yet, despite the dreadful blows I dealt him, he continued to see Theo, the child, and me. He viewed me as a melancholy creature, lost beyond redemption, past all worldly hope.

Never mind. I must devise a new scheme for living. Fortunately, the repayment of Harry's debt allowed me to survive, perhaps for months, certainly for years. Oh, the strange fatuity of youth! How could we dare to be so poor, so utterly cast down?

Then came the death of His Majesty's royal uncle, the Duke of Cumberland. It was a singular event that altered the trajectory of my fortune. My poor Sir Miles Warrington attended every court ceremony imaginable. Drawing-rooms, christenings, balls, funerals—he was everywhere. If a prince or princess fell ill, his carriage waited at the door. Whether at Leicester Fields, Carlton House, or Gunnersbury, nothing satisfied him now but attending the stout duke's funeral. Standing bareheaded in the rain, he contracted a severe cold and an inflammation of the throat. Before I had even heard of his illness, a lawyer arrived at my lodgings in Bloomsbury to inform me that I was now Sir George Warrington.

The anxieties of party and the fear of the future evaporated. We laid the poor gentleman beside his young son in the family churchyard, where so many of his lineage rested. Little Miles and I were the chief mourners. The tenantry bowed and curtseyed with obsequious fervor, striving to conciliate my honor and my worship. The dowager and her daughter retreated to Bath; I and my family took possession of the estate, a home I have mastered for thirty years. Be not too eager, my son. Exercise a little patience, and soon I too shall sleep beneath those yew trees, while the populace tosses their caps for Sir Miles.

The chronicles of a prosperous rural life are concise. The steward's books record the rents paid and forgiven, the crops harvested and rotated. They list the visitors, their duration of stay, the pensioners my wife supported, their treatments, and their deaths. They note the year I served as sheriff and the frequency of the hounds' meetings. All these details reside in our house journals, available to any heir willing to consult them. We could not afford the magnificent mansion in Hill Street occupied by my predecessor. Instead, we selected a smaller dwelling, yet we expended more money within its walls. We lacked the grandeur of liveries, equipages, and silver plate for which my uncle was renowned. Our beer was stronger, however, and my wife's charities were perhaps more costly than those of the Dowager Lady Warrington. She undoubtedly believed there was no sin in spoiling the Philistines. Indeed, she charged us exorbitant sums for the goods she left behind in our country house, and I submitted to most of her extortions with unutterable good humor. What valuation did she place upon the potted plants in her greenhouses? What price did she assign to that detestable old spinet in her drawing room? And the framed pieces of worsted work, executed by the accomplished Dora and the lovely Flora—if they were masterpieces of Titian or Van Dyck, surely the dowager could not have valued them higher. Despite our generous payments, and though we were, without boasting, far kinder to the poor than she had ever been, we initially held the worst reputation in the county. Tales of various kinds circulated to my discredit.

I had hoped to inherit my uncle's seat in Parliament alongside his lands. Yet, I discovered, through unknown mechanisms, that I was deemed a person of dangerously radical opinions. I refused to bribe. I would not coerce my own tenants into voting for me in the election of '68. A gentleman descended from Whitehall carrying a pocketbook full of banknotes, and I realized I had no chance against my competitor.

Bon Dieu! Now that we were secure in worldly means, now that obedient tenants bowed and curtseyed as we walked to church, now that we traveled to visit friends or neighboring towns in the great family coach drawn by four fat horses, did we not often regret our former poverty? Did I not long to return to bear-leading, vowing that translating for booksellers was not such arduous drudgery? When we journeyed to London, we made sentimental pilgrimages to our old haunts. I dare say my wife embraced every landlady we knew. You may be certain we invited all the friends of our youth to share the comforts of our new home. The Reverend Mr. Hagan and his lady visited us repeatedly. His appearance in the pulpit at B------, where he preached with such finesse in our estimation, caused an awful scandal. Sampson also came, another unfortunate Levite, welcomed as long as he remained among us. Mr. Johnson spoke of visiting but put us off on one or two occasions. I suppose our house was dull. I know that I myself would remain silent for days, and I fear my moodiness often tested the sweetest-tempered woman in the world who lived with me. I cared little for field sports. Killing one partridge seemed indistinguishable from killing another; I wondered how men could spend days pursuing such slaughter. Their fox-hunting stories began at four o'clock, when the tablecloth was removed, and lasted until supper. I sat silent, listening. Day after day, I fell asleep. No wonder I was unpopular with my company.

What confession is this? Here was the storm passed, the rocks avoided, the ship in port, and the sailor not entirely content? Was Susan, whom I sighed for during the voyage, not the beauty I expected to find? First, Susan and the entire family can inspect William's logbook; thus, madam, I shall not record my secrets there. No, Susan, I never kept secrets from thee. I never coveted another woman. I have seen greater beauties, but none suited me as well as your ladyship. I met Mrs. Carter, Miss Mulso, Mrs. Thrale, Madame Kaufmann, the angelical Gunnings, Her Grace of Devonshire, and a host of beauties who were, by no means, angelic. None dazzled me.
