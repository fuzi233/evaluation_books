Twelve years had elapsed since I interred the remains of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter, within the silent, stone-walled crypt of the Richmond cemetery. It was a strange mausoleum, built according to his peculiar final instructions: an open casket, and a vault door secured by a mechanism accessible only from within.

In those twelve years, I had studied the remarkable manuscript of this enigma—a man who remembered no childhood, could not guess his own age, yet seemed eternally youthful. He was a warrior who had dandled my grandfather's great-grandfather on his knee, a soldier who had spent a decade on Mars, fighting both for and against its green inhabitants, battling the red men, and ultimately winning the hand of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, to become a prince of the House of Tardos Mors.

Now, twelve years later, I found myself lost in futile speculation on a sultry August evening. Had John Carter truly perished on the bluff overlooking the Hudson, or did he still wander the dead sea bottoms of a dying world? Had he returned to Barsoom in time to open the great atmosphere plants and save millions from asphyxiation, only to be hurled back across forty-eight million miles of space? Or had he arrived too late, condemned to a living death on a corpse of a planet?

My thoughts were shattered when old Ben, my body servant, handed me a telegram. With trembling fingers, I tore it open:

The next morning, I boarded the first train for Richmond. Within two hours, I stood before the room where John Carter waited. As I entered, he rose, his face illuminated by that familiar, cordial smile. Time had spared him; he remained the straight, clean-limbed fighter of thirty. His grey eyes were sharp and undimmed, marked only by the deep lines of iron will that had defined him since I first knew him nearly thirty-five years prior.

"Well, nephew," he greeted me, "do you feel as though you are seeing a ghost, or merely suffering the effects of too many of Uncle Ben's juleps?"

"Juleps, I reckon," I replied, feeling a surge of vitality I hadn't expected. "But perhaps it is simply the sight of you again. You have returned to Mars? Tell me—did you find Dejah Thoris? Is she well, waiting for you?"

"Yes, I have been to Barsoom again," he said, his voice heavy with unspoken sorrow. "But it is a tale too long for the brief time I have before I must depart. I have learned the secret, nephew. I can now traverse the trackless void at will, moving between the countless planets as I choose. Yet, my heart remains anchored to Barsoom. While it resides there, in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I doubt I shall ever again leave the dying world that is my life."

He paused, looking at me with profound sadness. "I have come because my affection for you compelled me to see you one last time before you pass forever into that other life I shall never know. Though I have died thrice and shall die again tonight, as you know death, I remain as unable to fathom it as you are."

"Even the wise and mysterious Therns of Barsoom," he continued, "that ancient cult credited with holding the secrets of life and death in their impregnable fastnesses upon the Mountains of Otz, are as ignorant as we. I have proven it, though I nearly lost my life in the process. You shall read all of it in the notes I have compiled during the last three months of my return to Earth."

He gestured to a swelling portfolio lying on the table beside him.

"I know you are interested, and I know the world will be, though they will not believe for many ages, for they cannot comprehend. Earth men have not yet evolved to a point where they can grasp the truths written in those notes. Give them what you wish, what you deem harmless, but do not be aggrieved if they laugh at you."

That night, we walked down to the cemetery. At the threshold of his vault, he turned and pressed my hand.

"Good-bye, nephew," he said softly. "I may never see you again. I doubt I can ever bring myself to leave my wife and boy while they live, for the span of life upon Barsoom often exceeds a thousand years."

He stepped inside. The great door swung slowly shut. The ponderous bolts grated into place, and the lock clicked with a finality that echoed in my soul. I have never seen Captain John Carter, of Virginia, since.

Yet, here is the story of his return to Mars on that other occasion, gleaned from the vast mass of notes he left for me upon the table in his Richmond hotel room. There is much I have omitted, much I have dared not tell, but you will find the account of his second search for Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, even more extraordinary than the first manuscript I presented to an unbelieving world. Through those pages, we followed the fighting Virginian once more across the dead sea bottoms beneath the moons of Mars.
