For all my godmother’s natural cheer, for all the effort she put into keeping us entertained that wintry evening at La Terrasse, no real warmth settled in our bones until we heard the distant jingle of harness through the gale, the thud of hooves crunching through snowdrifts that signaled the riders’ return. How often do women sit wrapped in firelight, soft and sheltered, while their hearts tear themselves away from that coziness? We wander out into the dark, brave the blasts, linger at lonely gates, watching and listening for the father, the son, the lover coming home through the storm.

At last they arrived. The Count de Bassompierre had ridden back alongside Dr. John Bretton that night, and none of us could say who spotted the horses first. The gale howled so fierce we all ran down to the hall to meet them the second they stepped through the door—only to be waved sharply back. They were caked head to toe in snow, two walking mounds of winter white, and Mrs. Bretton herded them straight for the huge, sunlit old Dutch kitchen at the back, forbidding them to step one foot on her carpeted staircase until they’d stripped off every last flake of that wintry mask. We trailed after them, of course, no one wanting to miss the fun, and Paulina Mary danced in giddy circles around her father, clapping her hands and crying that he looked exactly like a giant polar bear fresh off the ice.

The Count shook himself hard, sending a shower of snow flying, and the little sprite darted away laughing, only to come bouncing right back to help him tug off his heavy frozen coat. When he finally stepped free of his thick dreadnought, he swept it up over his head and threatened to bury her under it like an avalanche. “Come on then!” she laughed, bending to invite the “fall” before bounding out of reach as nimbly as a wild chamois, her movements soft and supple as a kitten’s, her laugh ringing clearer than struck silver and crystal. She rubbed his cold stiff hands between her own warm ones, stood on tiptoe to press a kiss to his lips, and a glow of loving delight seemed to wrap around her like a little halo. The grave, stately Count looked down at her the way men only look at the thing that is the very apple of their eye.

“Mrs. Bretton,” he said, smiling, “what am I to do with this daughter of mine? She doesn’t grow in wisdom, or even an inch in height. Don’t you find her still nearly as much of a child as she was ten years ago?”

“Not half as much of a child as my great oaf of a son,” Mrs. Bretton retorted, already caught up in a playful tussle with Graham over the thick snow-soaked coat he refused to change out of. He leaned against the polished old Dutch dresser, laughing and holding her at arm’s length. “Come on, mamma,” he said, striking a compromise, “let’s mix a Christmas wassail bowl, toast Old England right here on this hearth, so we can be warm inside and out.”

While the Count stood close by the fire, and Paulina danced back and forth across the wide, hall-like kitchen, Mrs. Bretton directed Martha to spice and heat the wassail, pouring the steaming brew into the old Bretton family flagon to be passed around, served hot by the tiny silver cup that had been Graham’s christening gift.

“Here’s to Auld Lang Syne!” the Count called, lifting the little glowing cup high, and recited the old Scottish verse out loud, his voice soft with nostalgia. Paulina clapped her hands, grinning that the old Home family Scottish blood was showing through his fine count’s title, that they were half Caledonian, half Gallic. “That’s a Scottish reel you’re dancing, isn’t it, my little Highland fairy?” her father teased. “Mrs. Bretton, I wouldn’t be surprised if a fairy ring of green grass pops up in the middle of your kitchen floor any second. That one’s no ordinary little mortal.”

“Tell Lucy to dance with me, papa!” she said, spotting me across the room. The Count held out his hand, greeting me warmly, saying he remembered me well, that his daughter had talked about me so often I felt like an old friend already. No one had thought to interrupt her giddy dance to offer her a sip of the spiced ale, but she was not about to be left out of the fun. When Graham set the christening cup on the high shelf of the dresser just out of her reach, she looked up and asked for a taste.

Graham had been watching her, charmed by the ease of her, the way she moved through his mother’s home like she still belonged there, the same old little Polly he’d known as a boy. “Your ladyship wishes for the tankard?” he teased, falling back into their old familiar banter, and pretended to refuse, telling her it was strong old October ale, brewed the year he was born, far too strong for her. She pouted, looking up at him like a child begging for a forbidden sweet, until he finally relented, holding the cup so she could only sip it drop by drop. His eyes were bright, smiling, openly warm with the pleasure of indulging her, until she took a proper sip and made a face, stepping back at once. “It’s not sweet at all!” she laughed. “It’s bitter and hot, it takes my breath away. The ale was only good when it was forbidden. No more, thank you.” She slipped away from him, graceful as a shadow, to rejoin her father, and Graham was left standing there, baffled for a minute, staring after her. That girl who’d just teased him over the ale was still the seven-year-old Polly he remembered, but the dignified little countess standing by her father’s side was a stranger he was only just meeting.

We moved to the drawing room for tea soon after, and Paulina slipped her arm through her father’s, staying close at his side, listening intently to every story he and Mrs. Bretton told, prompting them to share old adventures they’d half forgotten. The giddy, childlike sparkle was gone for the night; she was soft, thoughtful, gentle. When she said goodnight to Graham, her smile was quiet, her bow dignified, the perfect little Countess, and Graham could only bow back, grave and a little flustered, struggling to reconcile the dancing, playful fairy from the kitchen with this poised, graceful young lady.

By breakfast the next morning, the snow drifts had risen high enough to darken the lower panes of the windows, the gale tearing fallen snow up from the ground and whirling it into wild, fantastic shapes. Mrs. Bretton decreed no one would leave the house that day unless it was a matter of life and death, and Paulina wholeheartedly agreed, tucking a chair right next to her father’s armchair and announcing she would keep him from venturing out. The Count teased her back, pretending he would leave if they didn’t “pet” him properly over breakfast, so Paulina rushed to fetch him buttered rolls and the same marmalade they’d eaten at the old Bretton house years before, and Mrs. Bretton laughed, reminding everyone how tiny little Polly used to tug at her sleeve and beg for extra marmalade for Graham. Graham blushed bright red, laughing and protesting he’d never been that fond of sweet things, and Paulina backed up her old memory without hesitation.

Then the Count turned serious, and said he wanted to send Paulina to a good school, to finish her education properly. Mrs. Bretton pointed him to Madame Beck’s establishment, where I worked, and I took the chance to say plainly that I was a teacher there, no guest of leisure. For a second, I worried the pair might treat me differently now, but the Count only nodded, his voice gentle, saying it was an arduous calling, and wished me health and success. Paulina stared at me, wide-eyed with surprise, saying she’d never thought to ask what I did, that to her I’d always just been Lucy Snowe, no title, no job, just my friend. When I explained I worked to keep a roof over my own head, so I’d never be a burden to anyone, the Count told Paulina that if she ever found herself alone in the world, he hoped she would do the exact same.

When he asked if Madame Beck would take her, I told him the school was always happy to take English pupils, and the Count decided they would drive over that very afternoon to arrange it. Paulina hesitated, then laughed, and told them all the story of how he’d signed her up for a school in Switzerland five years prior, and then had come to visit her every other day, until they’d both been politely asked to leave, and warned him he’d almost certainly end up moving into Madame Beck’s school right along with her.

The two elders slipped away after breakfast to talk over old business, leaving Graham, Paulina, and I alone in the drawing room. The last trace of childish glee left Paulina’s face at once; the round, dimpled expression she wore with her father softened into something sharper, more thoughtful, lines of young womanhood settling soft across her features. Graham, who’d been staring out at the snow, wandered over to the hearth, and tried to make small talk about Villette’s sights, awkward for the first time in my memory. Paulina answered him with quiet grace, quick-witted, well-spoken, though every now and then a little lisp would slip out when she spoke fast, making her flush as she corrected the word slowly, carefully. Every time she did it, Graham smiled. The awkwardness melted fast; they were soon talking easily, the way they’d done as children, no hesitation, no stiffness, the old easy familiarity rising to the surface after ten years apart. Graham’s whole face softened, his voice warm and low, no trace of his usual playful levity, just quiet, unmistakeable pleasure at being with her again.

But his patients called, and he had to leave. He walked out, then came back only a minute later, claiming he’d forgotten a paper in his desk—though we all knew he’d only come back for one more look at her, to make sure he hadn’t imagined the soft, warm glow between them. He took one final parting glance from her, shy and soft, as sweet and innocent as a fawn peeking out from a fern thicket, and left for good.

Paulina and I sat in silence for a long time after that, both working at our needlework. Her old tiny wooden workbox from Bretton days was replaced now with one inlaid with mosaic, fitted with gold needles, but her little mannerisms were exactly the same: the small furrow of concentration between her brows, the quick little movement to tuck a stray lock of hair back, the tiny shake of her skirt to brush off a stray thread. Outside, the winter storm was still raging, the gale howling itself hoarse, and Paulina, for all her urge to talk, saw I was in no mood to chatter, and respected the quiet: no pushy questions, no giddy teasing about the doctor, just soft, easy silence shared between old friends.
