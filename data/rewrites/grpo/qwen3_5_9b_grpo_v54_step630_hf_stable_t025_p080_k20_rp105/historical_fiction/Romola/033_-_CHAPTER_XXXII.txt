The following dawn brought with it the departure of the French, an event that stirred the entire city of Florence with a feverish excitement. For Romola, amidst the general rejoicing, there lingered a faint, almost superstitious hope that the departure of these foreign guests might also wash away the specific anxieties concerning Tito. Their presence had lasted barely eleven days, yet within that brief span, she had endured a depth of sorrow she had never known before. It was on the very day of their arrival that Tito had donned that hateful armor, a physical manifestation of his terror. Though logic offered her no clear reason why their exit should dismantle the cause of his fear—the terrifying image of a prisoner seizing him, as Piero had sketched it—she felt certain that removing the burden of their company would strip away the tangible association of her pain.

Clad in her mantle, Romola stood beneath the loggia at the house's summit, watching the distant glimpses of the troops and the royal retinue crossing the bridges toward the Porta San Piero, the gateway leading to Siena and Rome. Even after the gates had fallen shut, she lingered, eager to feel the vibration of the great bell peal that marked the finality of their departure. Dusk had settled when she finally descended into the library. Lighting her lamp, she resolved to quell the agitation that had paralyzed her all day and return to her work of copying the catalogue. Tito had left early; she did not expect him until later. Once he arrived, she planned to retreat to the charming salon adorned with dancing nymphs and birds—a space he had once deemed too chilly and gloomy for her comfort.

To her profound surprise, Tito entered before she had even begun her labor. Her initial thought was of the cheerlessness of the vast, dark room, illuminated only by a solitary oil lamp at the far end, with the fire nearly extinguished. She hastened toward him.

"Tito, dearest, I did not know you would return so soon," she said, her voice tinged with nervousness as she raised her white arms to loosen the *becchetto* from his neck.

"Am I not welcome, then?" he replied, offering one of his brightest smiles. He embraced her, yet playfully held his head back from her touch.

"Tito!" she exclaimed, the word vibrating with a tone of affectionate reproach. He kissed her fondly, stroking her hair in his customary manner, seemingly unconcerned with removing his mantle. Romola quivered with delight. All the turmoil of the day had sharpened her sensitivity to the return of this familiar tenderness. "It will come back," she whispered to herself. "The old happiness will perhaps return. He is like himself again."

Yet, Tito was striving with great effort to appear as his former self; his heart hammered with a secret, consuming anxiety.

"If I had anticipated your return so quickly," Romola said, helping him finally shed his wrappings, "I would have prepared a small festival to honor this joyful ringing of the bells. I did not intend to be here in the library when you came home."

"Never mind, sweet," he dismissed her concern casually. "Do not worry about the fire. Come—come and sit down."

A low stool stood beside his chair, the place where Romola habitually rested when conversing with him. She leaned her arm upon his knee, mimicking the gesture she had once shared with her father, and gazed upward at him. He had not yet noticed the portrait looming nearby, nor had she mentioned it, allowing the thought to fester in her mind.

"I have been listening to the clang of the bells for the first time, Tito," she began. "I enjoyed being shaken and deafened by them; I fancied myself a Bacchante possessed by a divine rage. Do not the people look very joyful tonight?"

"Joyful, indeed, in a sour and pious fashion," Tito replied, shrugging his shoulders. "But in truth, those left behind in Florence have little cause for joy. It seems to me the most reasonable grounds for gladness would be to have fled the city entirely."

Tito had struck the desired note without apparent premeditation. He spoke without emphasis, yet his grave demeanor prompted an anxious query from Romola.

"Why, Tito? Are there fresh troubles?"

"No need for fresh ones, my Romola. There are three powerful factions in the city, all poised to tear each other apart. And if the Frate's party proves strong enough to silence the others, as seems likely, life will be as pleasant and amusing as a funeral. They already simmer with plans for a Great Council; should they succeed, the man who sings sacred Lauds the loudest will be the most eligible for office. Furthermore, the city will be drained by the payment of this massive subsidy to the French king and the war to reclaim Pisa. The prospect would be dismal enough without the rule of fanatics. On the whole, Florence will be a delightful haunt for those worthies who entertain themselves by visiting crypts and lashing themselves, but for everything else, the exiles have the better of it. For my own part, I have been seriously considering that we would be wise to leave Florence, my Romola."

She started. "Tito, how could we leave Florence? Surely you do not think I could abandon it—at least, not yet, not for a long while." Her voice turned cold and trembling, finding difficulty in its articulation. Tito must know the reasons weighing heavily in her mind.

"That is merely a fabric of your own imagination, my sweet one. Your secluded life has led you to place undue stress on trivial matters. You know I told you before our marriage that I wished we were somewhere other than Florence. Had you seen more places and more people, you would understand what I mean when I say there is something in the Florentines that reminds me of their cutting spring winds. I prefer people who take life less eagerly; and it would be good for my Romola, too, to witness a new life. I should like to dip her slightly in the soft waters of forgetfulness."

He leaned forward, kissing her brow and resting his hand on her fair hair once more. Yet, she felt his caress no more than if he had kissed a mask. She was too agitated by the chasm widening between their minds to register the contact of his lips.

"Tito, it is not because I suppose Florence is the pleasantest place in the world that I refuse to quit it. It is because I—because we must fulfill my father's wish. My godfather is old; he is seventy-one; we cannot leave him alone."

"It is precisely those superstitions that cling to your mind like bedimming clouds, my Romola, which constitute a major reason why I wish we were two hundred leagues from Florence. I am compelled to take charge of you against your own will: if those dear eyes, so tender in their gaze, see falsely, I must see for them and save my wife from wasting her life on impracticable dreams."

Romola sat silent and motionless. She could not blind herself to the direction of his words: he sought to persuade her that they could deposit the library in a monastery or find other means to sever their ties to Florence. She was determined never to submit her mind to his judgment on this matter of duty to her father; she was inwardly prepared to endure any pain in resistance. Yet, this determination remained latent in these first moments, crushed by the heart-wrenching realization that she and Tito were now openly divided in their wishes. He welcomed her silence; despite his fears regarding the strength of her feeling, it was impossible for him, confined within the narrowness of a merely clever and unimpassioned man, not to overestimate the persuasiveness of his own arguments. His conduct did not appear ugly to himself, nor did his imagination suffice to reveal exactly how it appeared to Romola. He continued in the same gentle, remonstrating tone.

"You know, dearest—and your own clear judgment always showed you—that the notion of isolating a collection of books and antiquities, attaching a single name to them forever, holds no valid, substantial good for its object. Moreover, it is liable to be defeated in a thousand ways. Witness what has become of the Medici collections! For my part, I consider it even blameworthy to entertain such petty views of appropriation. Why should anyone reasonably rejoice that Florence should possess the benefits of learned research and taste more than any other city? I understand your feeling regarding the wishes of the dead; but wisdom must place a limit on such sentiments, or lives might be continually wasted in futile devotion—like praising deaf gods forever. You gave your life to your father while he lived; why should you demand more of yourself?"

"Because it was a trust," Romola said in a low but distinct voice. "He trusted me, he trusted you, Tito. I did not expect you to feel anything else about it—to feel as I do—but I did expect you to feel that."

"Yes, dearest, of course I should feel it on a point where your father's real welfare or happiness was concerned; but there is no question of that now. If we believed in purgatory, I should be as anxious as you to have masses said; and if I believed it could now pain your father to see his library preserved and used in a rather different way from what he had set his mind on, I should share the strictness of your views. But a little philosophy should teach us to rid ourselves of those air-woven fetters that mortals hang round themselves, spending their lives in misery under the mere imagination of weight. Your mind, which seizes ideas so readily, my Romola, is able to discriminate between substantial good and these brain-wrought fantasies. Ask yourself, dearest, what possible good can these books and antiquities do, stowed together under your father's name in Florence, more than they would do if they were divided or carried elsewhere? Nay, is not the very dispersion of such things in hands that know how to value them one means of extending their usefulness? This rivalry of Italian cities is very petty and illiberal. The loss of Constantinople was the gain of the whole civilized world."

Romola was still under the painful pressure of the new revelation Tito was making of himself, her resistance finding no strong vent. As his fluent talk fell upon her ears, a rising contempt stirred within her, heightening her awareness of her bruised, despairing love for the Tito she had married and believed in. Her nature, possessed by the energies of strong emotion, recoiled from this hopelessly shallow readiness which professed to appropriate the widest sympathies yet lacked a pulse for the nearest. She still spoke like one restrained from showing all she felt. She had only drawn her arm away from his knee, sitting with her hands clasped before her, cold and motionless as locked waters.

"You talk of substantial good, Tito! Are faithfulness, and love, and sweet grateful memories no good? Is it no good that we should keep our silent promises on which others build because they believe in our love and truth? Is it no good that a just life should be justly honored? Or, is it good that we should harden our hearts against all the wants and hopes of those who have depended on us? What good can belong to men who have such souls? To talk cleverly, perhaps, and find soft couches for themselves, and live and die with their base selves as their best companions."

Her voice had gradually risen until there was a ring of scorn in the final words. She made a slight pause, but he saw other words quivering on her lips and chose to let them come.

"I know of no good for cities or the world if they are to be made up of such beings. But I am not thinking of other Italian cities and the whole civilized world—I am thinking of my father, and of my love and sorrow for him, and of his just claims on us. I would give up anything else, Tito, I would leave Florence—what else did I live for but for him and you? But I will not give up that duty. What have I to do with your arguments? It was a yearning of *his* heart, and therefore it is a yearning of mine."

Her voice, having been tremulous, became full and firm. She felt she had been urged to say all that was needful. She thought, poor thing, there was nothing harder to endure than this struggle against Tito's suggestions as against the meaner part of herself.

He began to see clearly that he could not persuade her into assent; he must take another course and show her that the time for resistance was past. That, at least, would end the struggle; and if the disclosure were not made by himself that night, it must be made another way. This necessity nerved his courage, and his experience of her affectionateness and unexpected submissiveness since their marriage encouraged him to hope that, at last, she would accommodate herself to his will.

"I am sorry to hear you speak in that spirit of blind persistence, my Romola," he said quietly, "because it obliges me to give you pain. But I partly foresaw your opposition, and as a prompt decision was necessary, I avoided that obstacle and decided without consulting you. The very care of a husband for his wife's interest compels him to that separate action sometimes—even when he has such a wife as you, my Romola."

She turned her eyes upon him in breathless inquiry.

"I mean," he answered her look, "that I have arranged for the transfer, both of the books and of the antiquities, where they will find the highest use and value. The books have been bought for the Duke of Milan; the marbles, bronzes, and the rest are going to France. Both will be protected by the stability of a great Power, instead of remaining in a city exposed to ruin."

Before he had finished speaking, Romola had started from her seat, standing up to look down at him with tightened hands falling before her, and, for the first time in her life, with a flash of fierceness in her scorn and anger.

"You have *sold* them?" she asked, as if she distrusted her own ears.

"I have," said Tito, shrinking back slightly. The scene was unpleasant; the descending scorn already scorched him.

"You are a treacherous man!" she said, with something grating in her voice as she looked down at him.
