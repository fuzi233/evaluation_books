The tunnel had been masked with all the cunning a mason’s pride could muster, but the rock itself betrayed what it had once been: a vein where Earth’s hot blood used to run. The old lava had long since cooled, yet the passage still breathed warmth and brimstone; it laid a film on the tongue like overcooked egg, and it stung the back of my throat until every breath wanted to be a cough. I found the lamp set for us in its niche, struck it, and went on briskly, cursing under my breath the little refinements of medicine I no longer possessed. Once, in Yucatan, I had kept a packet of dried herbs—mullein and sweet resin, the sort of simples that, steeped in hot water, soften catarrh and lay a balm on a raw windpipe. That packet had gone to the bottom in the sea-fight with Dason’s navy, and since I set foot in Atlantis there had been no leisure for such domestic mercies.

The ways branched and rebranched like the roots beneath an old orchard tree. A man who came down here unprepared would wander until his lamp died and then fall, perhaps, into one of those pits that drop to the boil below. But I carried the chart of the true course not on parchment but in memory, fixed there from an initiation twenty years gone, when as an appointed viceroy I had been raised to the highest degree but one of our Clan and shown its implements and its secrets. The floor rose and broke in cruel ridges; the air grew fouler in pockets; the sulphur sat on the palate and soured even the thought of food. I knew the day would be far advanced before the signs would tell me I had passed under the walls and come within the city’s precincts.

And here the vow of the Seven hampered me. Under no stress, it is ordained, may egress be made from these passages before mortal eye. One branch after another I tried, hearing above, through stone, the muffled living of the city. Always there were loiterers near the outlets. I had hoped to rise by the path that comes out inside the royal pyramid; but that place was humming like a hive—too many feet, too many voices, too much light and movement to slip unseen. So with five more. The city was agog with some excitement that ran like yeast through dough.

At last I came beneath a temple of one of the lesser Gods. I waited behind the image long enough to make sure my luck was not a trap: the place was empty. Dust lay thick on floor and benches, a grey robe over everything, as though worship had been a language no one spoke here anymore. I moved the appointed stone, stepped out, closed the entry, and found a broom left on a pew like a forgotten tool after harvest. With it I scuffed and blurred my own footmarks until the dust looked as undisturbed as stale flour in a bin, then went to the temple door.

It was shut; priest though I was, its opening was beyond my knowledge. The doorway was filled with a massive stone that sat in its groove like a millstone. A pretty pass indeed. Only the attendant priests of this temple knew the mechanism. If they had all gone to gape at the spectacle that had set the city buzzing, I would be no nearer liberty than when I was choking in the veins beneath the earth.

No sound of life came from within the precincts—only the dry whisper of my lamp and the faint, sour breath of disuse. Yet by ancient law there must be always at least one on watch in the priests’ dwellings. So I made my way along the passage to the roof where those dwellings lay, and gave the call.

After a time the door opened upon darkness and a dull face peering out. The man who stood there had remained, at least, constant to his vows, and I made him the salutation with respect.

“Ro,” I said, for I remembered him.

We had passed through the sacred college together. Always he had been known as the dullard: slow to learn the cult, slower to master the arts of ruling, and hopeless with arms. Yet here he was, second priest and one of two custodians, raised not by merit but by the desertion of all others. It sat ill with me that a fool should remain true where cleverer men had abandoned the old beliefs.

He did the greater obeisance. His beard was curled in the prevailing fashion but clumsily; his robe hung ill and carried the smell of old sweat and stale oil. Ro had never been cleanly.

“The temple door is shut,” he said, “and I only have the secret of its opening. My lord comes here, therefore, by the secret way, and as one of the Seven. I am my lord’s servant.”

“Then serve me with words,” I said. “What stirs the city?”

His mouth tightened, and for once his dull eyes sharpened into spite. “That impious Phorenice has declared herself Goddess. She says she will light the sacrifice with her own divine fire—she will do it too. She does everything. But I wish the flames may burn her when she calls them down. This new Empress is the bane of our Clan, Deucalion, these latter days. The people neglect us; they bring no offerings. And now, since these rebels have been hammering at the walls, I might have gone hungry if I had not some small store of my own. I tell you, the cult of the true Gods is well-nigh oozed out of the land.”

“It comes to my mind,” I said, “that the priests of our Clan have been limp in their service to let these things come to pass.”

He shrugged in a way that made his robe gape at the neck. “We did as we were taught. But if the people will not come to hear your exhortations and neglect to adore the God, what hold have you? The High Gods try our own faith hard. Come in. Look there on my bed.”

On the pallet lay the shape of a man untidily swathed in reddened bandages. The room stank of old blood, damp cloth, and the sour taint of poultices left too long. Even in such a place there should have been some cleansing incense, some boiled herb-water to keep rot away; but Ro was Ro, and the old discipline was dying here.

“This,” he said, “is all that is left of the poor priest who was my immediate superior. It was his turn yesterday to celebrate the weekly sacrifice to our Lord the Sun with the circle of His great stones. Faugh! You should have seen how he was mangled when they brought him back.”

“Did the people rise on him?” I asked. “Has it come to that?”

“The people stayed passive,” Ro said bitterly. “What few cared enough to attend. Our Lord the Sun saw fit to try His minister harshly. The wood was laid, the sacrifice disposed according to the rites; the procession formed round the altar; and the drums and the trumpets were speaking forth so all men might know that the smoke of their prayer would presently be wafted up. But then, above the noise, there came the rushing sound of wings. From out of the sky flew one of those great featherless man-eating birds—bigger than is commonly seen.”

“An arrow in the eye,” I said, “or a long-shafted spear receives them best.”

“All men know what they were taught as children,” he returned, “but the priests were unarmed, as the rubric ordains. They must intrust themselves wholly to the guardianship of the High Gods during the hours of sacrifice. The bird swooped down, settled on the wood pyre, and attacked the offering with beak and talon. My poor superior, still strong in his faith, called loudly on our Lord the Sun to lend power to his arm and sprang up with naught but his teeth and bare arms for weapons. Perhaps he expected a miracle—he has not spoke since, poor soul—but all he met were blows from leathery wings and talons that went near to disembowelling him. The bird brushed him away as we would sweep a fly aside. There he lay bleeding on the pavement whilst the sacrifice was torn and eaten in the presence of all. When the bird was glutted, it flew away to the mountains.”

“And the people gave no help?”

“They cried it was a portent,” said Ro. “They said our Lord the Sun was a God no longer if He would not guard His own sacrifice. Some cried there was no God remaining; others that a new God had come to weigh on the country and had taken the form of a common man-eating bird. But a few began to shout that Phorenice stood for all the Gods now in Atlantis. That cry was taken up till the stones of the great circle rang with it. Some may have believed it; many liked the newness of it; and not a few joined because it is dangerous to leave such an outburst unwelcomed. The Empress can be hard on those who neglect to adore her.”

“The Empress is Empress,” I said formally, though the words tasted like ash. “Her name carries respect. It is not for us to question her doings.”

“I am a priest,” Ro said, “and I speak as I was taught. Whether there is a Faith any longer, I begin to doubt. Anyway, it yields a poor enough livelihood nowadays. There have been no offerings at this temple five months past. If I had not a few jars of corn put by, I might have starved for anything the pious of this city cared.”

Corn. Even in his whining, the word made my mind picture what he meant: clay jars sealed with pitch, the dry kernels whispering when poured, the faint smell of sun-baked grain. There was comfort and continuity in such stores, the old domestic prudence that survives even when temples go cold. Yet Ro spoke of it as a private hoard, not as a sacred trust. The old order was not only attacked; it was being forgotten in the very habits of those who claimed to keep it.

“When did it happen?” I asked.

“Twenty hours ago. To-day Phorenice conducts the sacrifice herself. That is the stir. The city is in the throes of getting ready one of her pageants.”

“Then open the temple doors,” I said. “I must go and see this thing for myself.”

“It is not for me to offer advice to one of the Seven,” he began, and then his voice sank into a mumble. “But they say the Empress is not overpleased at your absence. I should not like harm to come in your way, Deucalion.”

“The future is in the hands of the most High Gods,” I told him, “and I at least believe They will deal out our fates as They in Their infinite wisdom see best—though you seem to have mislaid your faith. Now I must be your debtor for passage. And, plagues, man, it is no use holding out your hand. I do not own a coin in all the world.”

He muttered something about force of habit as he led me down. I spoke tartly of the unpleasantness of his begging customs.

“If it were not for your sort and your customs,” I said, “the Priests’ Clan would not be facing this crisis to-day.”

“One must live,” he grumbled, pressing the hidden levers. Stone groaned on stone; the great door swung ajar, and a slice of daylight cut into the dim.

“If you had been a more capable man,” I said, “I might have seen the necessity.” I passed out and left him.

I could never bring myself to like Ro.

A motley crowd filled the street before the obscure temple, all hurrying one way. They flowed like a stream that has found a new channel, and I let it take me. With what Ro had said, it needed little art to guess their mark: the great stone circle of our Lord the Sun. It grieved me to think how many venerable centuries that fane had stood, braving weather and earth tremors, without witnessing such profanation as it would see to-day.

From one quarter of the walls the din of attack carried over the roofs: the dull thud of rams, the rattle and shriek of darts, the deep, grinding complaint of stone struck by heavier engines. But no one heeded it. They had grown callous to bombardment. Their nerves, their hunger, their desperation were strung so tight that little short of an actual storm would lift their attention from this new excitement.

All were weaponed. The naked carried arms in hopes of finding someone to overcome and rob; those who still had a possession walked ready to fight for it. There was no security, no trust. The lesson of civilisation had dropped away as mud washes from feet in rain, and in habit and thought these townsfolk had slid back toward a savagery—raw need, loud impulse, and the unspoken rule that the strongest mouth eats first.

The streets we wound through had once been clean and trim; now they were foul and mud-strewn, with stale refuse rotting in corners and the sharp, ammoniac stink of neglect. Men and women had died of hunger here in these later years and rotted where they fell. We trod bones underfoot without pause. Yet above this misery rose pyramids and palaces, splendid beyond telling, their polished faces catching the sun as though they belonged to another city entirely. That jarring mixture—glitter over carrion—was Phorenice’s Atlantis in one view.

At last we came to the open space at the city’s center, ground so ancient and so secular that even Phorenice had not dared to encroach upon it with her ambitious schemes. There stood the most grand and the eldest of our temples: the circle of stones raised to our Lord the Sun.

Since the beginning of time—since man first rose from among beasts—our Lord the Sun has been his chiefest God. Legend says He raised the circle Himself so votaries might offer worship. Modern fashion scoffs at such tales; but for myself, this one satisfies me. By our wits we can lift blocks weighing six hundred men and set them as capstones on pyramids. Yet to uprear those stones—undressed, rugged, enormous—would be beyond our art; and more impossible still to haul them from their distant quarries over the mountains.

There were nine-and-forty stones, set in a perfect circle with spaces between, and across their tops lay other stones just as huge, bridging them like the laid beams of a roof. In the center stood the altar stone, greater still, carved with the rude chiseling of the ancients: the snake and the outstretched hand. The carving had the blunt honesty of a language that does not bother to flatter—marks hammered into rock by men who named the world with a few strong syllables and were understood. Even now, that old speech seemed to hum under the newer tongues that chattered around it, as if the place remembered words the people had forgotten.

The crowd that bore me on stopped at the circle’s edge. For common folk to trespass within is death. I had the right of entrance, but I stayed where I was, merged among the mob, unnoticed, waiting. The sun burned high in a clear blue sky; heat pressed down until sweat tasted of salt and metal. The siege noise still came from the walls, but even that was swallowed by anticipation.

We waited long. I learned later that the rebels’ attack had been so pressing, and their new engines so destructive, that Phorenice had gone first to the walls to lend her brilliant skill for repulse and put heart into the defenders. The day scorched toward its middle before drums and horns began to speak from afar that the pageant had formed. Many in the crowd fainted; not a few died where they stood. No one heeded the fallen. In Atlantis now, life was cheap as stale husks.

Nearer and nearer came the percussion—drums that struck like fists on hollow wood, and brazen horns that brayed and tore at the ear. There is an ecology to sound as there is to water and air; a city’s spirit is shaped by what it hears. Once, the music of Atlantis had been measured—chants that rested the mind, flutes that braided with birdsong, silence kept in reverence around old stones. Now the noise was made to overwhelm: to drive thought out as smoke is driven from a room. Even the siege had its own brutal rhythm, but this was different—a commercial din of worship turned spectacle, sound sold as power.

The head of the procession arrived and disposed itself in the appointed space. Lords and courtiers glittered—jewels flashing, fine cloth swaying, gold and dyed feathers paraded in wanton splendour. Many a starving wretch sighed at the sight. These new nobles of Atlantis cared for their bellies and their backs, and for one other appetite: their simpering adulation of Phorenice. Their loyalty was real enough, for everything they had came from her lavish hands.

And then she appeared.

She rode in the golden castle—where I also had ridden—set upon the back of her monstrous shaggy mammoth. At once the crowd’s starved, sullen faces brightened as though a meal had been laid before them. Without command, without compulsion, they burst into shouts. The sound hit like a wave, a massed roar that made the stones seem to tremble.

She acknowledged them with a smile of thanks. Her cheeks were a little flushed, her movements quick, her manner high-strung—as well it might be, with the sacrilege she meant to commit. Yet she was undeniably lovely, more adorably beautiful than ever with that thrill of excitement running through her. When the stair was brought and she stepped down from the mammoth’s back, those nearest fell to their knees and gave her worship out of sheer fascination.

Only Ylga, the fan-girl, kept her formal paces and duties without surrender. Even from where I stood I could see she looked pained and troubled, as though jewels lay on her shoulders like weights. It was fitting. Though long fostered by the Empress, Ylga was Zaemon’s daughter, and he was chiefest of our Lord the Sun’s ministers on earth. She could not wholly forget her upbringing at this supreme moment when the highest of the old Gods was to be openly defied. Perhaps, too, because she cared for Phorenice, she dreaded what such defiance might call down.

But Phorenice had no eye for a single sad face among that sea of glowing ones. Boldly, proudly, she strode into the circle as though duly appointed priest. After her came a knot of men dressed as priests, bearing the victim. Some were her creatures—ignorant laymen, dazzled by her. But some, to their shame, were men born in the Priests’ Clan, raised in the groves and colleges of the Sacred Mountain. For their apostasy there could be no palliation.

The wood had already been stacked on the altar in the form the ancient symbolism requires—pieces laid with care as a cook lays kindling for an even flame: thicker logs as the base, smaller above so the fire may take and travel. In a proper sacrifice, the smell of the chosen wood matters; resinous branches give a sweet lift to smoke, and the old priests knew which trees pleased which Gods. Here, I could see, the pile had been made by rule but without reverence, as if copying a recipe in a dead language.

Phorenice stood aside while they did what was needful. As they opened out, I saw the victim: one of the small cloven-hoofed horses that roam the plains, an acceptable offering. They bound its feet with metal gyves and set it upon the pyre. For a while it lay there, flanks heaving, eyes rolling white, giving little neighs that were thin with terror. Then they stepped back and left it living.

That was an innovation, and a cruel one. The true rite does not linger over suffering for spectacle.

The false priests went to the farther side of the circle. Phorenice stood alone before the altar. She lifted her voice—sweet, tuneful, carrying. Though the siege din still came from the walls, no ear there lost a word. She raised her glance, and all eyes followed.

The heaven had been clear as deep sea, a gorgeous blue. But as her words came, a small mist was born overhead, wheeling and circling like a ball though the day was windless. It grew rapidly darker and more compact until it threw shadow on part of the sacred circle and soothed it into twilight, though outside, where the people stood, the day remained garish and hard. Within that ball of mist, little quick stabs of noiseless flame winked and vanished like sparks under a pot lid.

She did not speak in the priests’ sacred tongue—though such was her wicked cleverness she may have learned it—but in the common speech of the people, so that all might understand. She told of her wondrous birth, as she chose to name it, and of direct aid from the most High Gods that had enabled her to work marvels. In the telling she used words that glittered and slid, bending old meanings to new ends. Even the very name of “God” sounded altered on her lips, as though she had taken a word meant for the heavens and pressed it into service for herself. I felt, with sudden sharpness, how language dies: not always by silence, but by being made to mean what it never meant before, until those who inherit it can no longer taste its original flavour.

At last she lifted both her fair white arms toward the blackness above. Her face set with strain; her limbs shook.

“O my high Father, the Sun,” she cried, “acknowledge me as Your very daughter. Give this people a sign that I am indeed a child of the Gods and no frail mortal. Here is sacrifice unlit, where mortal priests with their puny fires had weekly, since the foundation of this land, sent savoury smoke toward the sky. Send down the heavenly fire to burn this beast here offered, in token that though You still rule on high, You have given me Atlantis to be my kingdom, and the people of the Earth to be my worshippers.”

She broke off and strained as though dragging the sky down by will alone. Her beauty distorted; the lovely mouth tightened; the tendons stood in her throat.

“O mighty Father,” she cried, “who hast made me a God and an equal, hear me! Hear me!”

Out of the black cloud came a blinding flash that spat down onto the altar. The cloven-hoofed horse gave one shrill neigh, one convulsion, and fell back dead. Flames crackled out through the wood pile. At once the air grew thick with the smell of burning flesh—fat turning and dripping into fire, hair singing, the sick-sweet reek that clings to the nostrils and settles in the throat. Smoke rose, first heavy and oily, then thinning as the blaze steadied.

And lo: in another moment the cloud melted into nothingness. The flames burned pale; the smoke went up in a thin blue spiral toward the deeper blue of the sky, as neat and composed as though it had always belonged there.

Phorenice stood before the great stone and before the snake and the outstretched hand carved upon it, flushed and exultant, radiantly lovely once more. The knot of priests within the circle, and the great mob without, fell to the ground adoring.

“Phorenice, Goddess!” they cried. “Phorenice, Goddess of all Atlantis!”

But I did not kneel. I would have no part in this apostasy. I stood where I was, unbowed among the bowed, and waited for fate.
