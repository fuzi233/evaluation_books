It was a brilliant morning in the height of the London Season, and Major Arthur Pendennis, true to his habits, marched from his lodgings to breakfast at a certain exclusive club in Pall Mall. To the uninitiated, this establishment was merely a gathering of gentlemen; to the Major, it was his kingdom. As the premier connoisseur of vintage wines in England and a man whose spirit burned with an active, dominating curiosity, he had been elevated to the Committee, effectively serving as the institution's manager. The stewards and waiters bowed to him with the reverent solemnity usually reserved for Dukes or Field Marshals.

By twenty minutes past ten, the Major made his entrance. He was a study in impeccable military fashion *en retraite*. His boots were the best blacked leather in London, topped with a checked morning cravat that remained crisp until dinner time. His buff waistcoat boasted buttons crowned with the royal insignia, and his linen shirts were so flawlessly white that Beau Brummell himself—had fortune not driven him abroad—might have envied the laundrywoman who tended them. From his perfectly coiffed rich brown hair (factitious, though) to his Wellington-pattern nose and long, white wrists adorned with rings bearing the Pendennis arms, he was a specimen of aristocratic vanity. Though seen from behind, he appeared no older than thirty; a closer inspection revealed the faint lines around his somewhat faded eyes, the tell-tale signs of a man who worried too much about appearances.

His domain was a specific corner table by the fire, near the window, a throne no interloper dared disturb. In previous years, a couple of mad wags had attempted to usurp his seat, but the Major's quiet, intimidating dignity as he settled in and surveyed the room ensured their retreat. Here, amidst the crackling hearth, lay his correspondence. Young bucks often stared in awe at the sheer volume of his mail, the array of seals and frankings indicating the gravity of his social standing. Whether it concerned etiquette, marital alliances, or the exact age of a Duchess, everyone turned to Pendennis. Marchionesses would drive up specifically to deliver notes or summon him for tea. He was universally affable, a walking lord who tipped his hat to everyone, thereby elevating every passerby to nobility.

Now seated, while the waiters bustled with toast and newspapers, the Major donned his gold-rimmed spectacles and began his daily audit. He carried the glasses with a jaunty air, examining a stream of invitations. There were solemn dinner cards promising heavy conversation and multiple courses, delicate confidences whispering of female entreaties, and a formal missive from the Marquis of Steyne inviting him to Richmond for a soirée where French would be spoken—a tongue the Major mastered perfectly. Another request came from the Bishop of Ealing and Mrs. Trail, seeking his presence at Ealing House. He read them all with graceful satisfaction, particularly enjoying the spectacle as Glowry, the Scotch surgeon sitting opposite, glared with jealous hatred. Glowry, who received no such honors, watched the flood of invitations with a mixture of envy and disdain.

Having finished his perusal, the Major consulted his pocket-book, cross-referencing his free evenings against the calendar of hospitality. He mentally crossed off Cutler, the East India Director, opting instead for Lord Steyne's French party at the Star and Garter. He accepted the Bishop, reasoning that despite the slow service, dining with prelates was essential. With his schedule arranged to his liking, he settled in for breakfast, scanning the Gazette and fashionable news to ensure his name featured prominently among guests. Between bites, he engaged in cheerful chatter with his acquaintances, the air thick with the hum of high society.

Yet, buried within this mountain of fashionable correspondence, there sat a single, solitary letter. It stood apart from the glittering world of London, marked by a country postmark and a humble seal. Written in a delicate, feminine hand, it bore the word "Immediate" scrawled across the top with a dash of anxious urgency. For reasons of his own, the Major had neglected this humble rural petitioner, allowing her to wait in the social ante-chamber beneath the slop-basin while the grandees of her acquaintance were served.

Finally, it was her turn. The Major broke the seal, which read "Fairoaks," and opened the envelope before diving into the letter itself.

"My dear Major Pendennis," the letter began, "I beg and implore you to come to me *immediately*."

Pendennis paused. *Immediately?* Likely a summons to Steyne's dinner. He continued reading, however, his smile slowly fading into confusion.

"I am in the very greatest grief and perplexity," the letter continued. "My dearest boy... is grieving me *dreadfully*. He has formed—an infatuation!" The Major grinned internally at the dramatic phrasing. "For an actress who has been performing here. She is at least twelve years older than Arthur—who will not be eighteen till next February—and the wretched boy insists upon marrying her."

"Hay! What's making Pendennis swear now?" Glowry muttered to himself, interpreting the Major's sudden stillness as rage.

"Do, my dear friend," the letter pleaded, "come instantly... entreat, command, the wretched child to give up this most deplorable resolution." Signed with deep sorrow, it ended with "your unhappy affectionate sister, Helen Pendennis."

"Fairoaks, Tuesday," the Major murmured, finishing the page. "A d--d pretty business at Fairoaks, Tuesday. Now let us see what the boy has to say."

He turned to the second sheet, written in a frantic, floundering boy's hand. It was sealed with the massive signet of the Pendennises, encircled by supplementary wax sputtered in agitation.

"FAIROAKS, Monday, Midnight.
In informing you of my engagement with Miss Costigan..."

The rest followed in a torrent of youthful desperation. Arthur described Miss Costigan, daughter of J. Chesterfield Costigan, Esq., also known professionally as Miss Fotheringay of the Theatres Royal. He acknowledged the shock this caused his family, noting his mother's profound grief. Yet, he argued passionately. Her family, he claimed via Mr. Costigan, was as ancient and noble as their own, tracing roots back to the time of King Richard II. He dismissed his mother's objections as impossible to overcome with logic, insisting that Miss Costigan possessed "irreproachable character and lineage" and devoted her "splendid talents" to maintaining her family.

"A love like mine, Sir, I feel, is contracted once and for ever," the boy wrote, his voice trembling with conviction. "As I never had dreamed of love until I saw her—I feel now that I shall die without ever knowing another passion." He addressed the age gap as a mere illusion, a "barrier" suggested by Miss Costigan's own delicacy, which he now deemed unworthy of consideration. "If I hesitated to abide by my passion... I should despise myself, and be unworthy of my name as a gentleman."

He pressed for a speedy marriage, arguing that delay implied doubt, a sentiment unworthy of a man who loved fully. "Why, then, wait?" he cried. "I entreat you, my dear Uncle, to come down and reconcile my dear mother to our union." He signed off as "Your affectionate Nephew, ARTHUR PENDENNIS, JR."

When the Major finished the letter, his countenance transformed. Rage and horror contorted his features. Glowry, sensing a storm, reached instinctively for his lancet in his card-case, fearing his friend might swoon. The news was catastrophic enough to shake the foundations of Pendennis's orderly world. His headstrong nephew, barely eighteen, intent on marrying an actress ten years his senior. And the mother, obviously, had spoiled the rascal with nothing but sentimental nonsense and romantic rubbish.

"My nephew marry a tragedy queen!" the Major groaned inwardly. "Gracious mercy, people will laugh at me so that I shall not dare show my head!"

The cost of this scandal was immediate and severe. He would have to forfeit Lord Steyne's dinner at Richmond, sacrificing sleep and comfort in favor of an "abominable tight mail-coach." Worse, he would have to cancel every other engagement for weeks to come. Who knew how long this domestic crisis might drag on?

With a heavy heart, the Major quitted his breakfast table for the adjoining writing-room. There, with a rueful pen, he drafted refusals to the Marquis, the Earl, the Bishop, and every other host who had sought his company. He then summoned his servant to book tickets for the evening mail-coach, ordering that the exorbitant fare for the seats be charged directly to the account of the widow and the young scapegrace whom he was sworn to protect.
