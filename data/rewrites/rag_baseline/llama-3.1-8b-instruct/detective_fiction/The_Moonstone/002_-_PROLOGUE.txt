PROLOGUE

THE STORMING OF SERINGAPATAM (1799)

As I sit in the dimly lit chamber, quill in hand, I find myself drawn back to the scorching summer of 1799, when the storming of Seringapatam shook the foundations of our world. The memories of that fateful day still linger, like the scent of sandalwood and smoke that clings to my skin. It was an event that would change the course of my life forever, and one that I am compelled to recount to my relatives in England, in the hope that they might understand the reasons behind my estrangement from my cousin, John Herncastle.

The tale begins with a great public spectacle, one that drew us both into its vortex – the storming of Seringapatam, led by General Baird on the 4th of May. But to grasp the essence of our story, I must first transport you to the eve of the assault, to the camp where rumors swirled like the monsoon winds. Among these whispers, one legend stood out, a tale of a treasure hidden deep within the Palace of Seringapatam – a trove of jewels and gold, said to be guarded by the gods themselves.

In the midst of these fantastical stories, one gemstone rose above the rest – the Yellow Diamond, a gem renowned in the annals of India. Its origins dated back to the eleventh century, when the Mohammedan conqueror, Mahmoud of Ghizni, swept across the land, leaving destruction in his wake. The holy city of Somnauth was sacked, its treasures plundered, but one deity remained untouched – the moon-god, adorned with the Yellow Diamond in its forehead.

Three Brahmins, devoted to the preservation of their faith, secretly removed the deity from the ravaged temple and transported it to the sacred city of Benares. There, in a hall of gold and precious stones, the moon-god was reestablished, its shrine watched over by three priests in turn, day and night, through the passing ages. The deity itself spoke to the Brahmins in a dream, imparting a warning: any mortal who dared to lay hands on the Moonstone would invite calamity upon himself and his kin.
