“Calvin C. Bunner, at your service,” said the newcomer with a slight flourish, removing an unlit cigar from his mouth. He was accustomed to Englishmen being slow and formal with strangers, so Trent’s brisk reply caught him off guard. “You’re Mr. Trent, I suppose?” he continued. “Mrs. Manderson mentioned you earlier. Captain, good morning.”
Mr. Murch acknowledged the greeting with a curt nod. “I was heading up to my room when I heard a unfamiliar voice in here. Thought I’d check it out.”
Bunner chuckled easily. “You suspected I might be eavesdropping?”
“No, sir. I overheard a mention of a pistol—this one, I imagine—that’s all.”

Mr. Bunner was a slender, slightly undersized man with a pale, clean-shaven face that seemed almost delicate, framed by dark, wavy hair parted down the middle. His eyes were large and dark, sharp with intelligence, and his lips—usually occupied by a cigar—tended to hang slightly open, giving him a look of perpetual alertness. When he smoked, though, that expression vanished, replaced by the cool, calculating demeanor of a seasoned Yankee. Born in Connecticut, he’d entered a brokerage firm after college and quickly caught the attention of Manderson, whose business dealings he frequently handled. The Colossus had watched him closely before offering him the role of private secretary—a position he filled with precision, discretion, and unwavering loyalty. While many men could match Bunner’s reliability and methodical nature, Manderson valued him for something more: speed, secrecy, and an uncanny instinct for market movements.

Trent and Bunner exchanged glances, each assessing the other. There was no hostility, only mutual respect.

“I’ve just been told,” Trent said smoothly, “that finding this pistol doesn’t mean much. Apparently it’s common among Americans—and gaining popularity here.”

Bunner reached into the case and took the weapon, handling it with practiced ease. “That’s right, sir. We call it a Little Arthur back home. I’ll bet there are a hundred thousand like it tucked into pockets across the country. Personally, I find it too light,” he added, reaching under his jacket and pulling out a heavier, more menacing firearm. “Feel this one, Mr. Trent—it’s loaded, by the way. Now, this Little Arthur—Marlowe bought it last year, just to please the old man. Manderson insisted every man should carry a gun in the twentieth century. So he went out and picked up whatever they handed him—didn’t even ask me. Not that it’s a bad gun,” Bunner conceded, squinting along the barrel. “Marlowe wasn’t great with it at first, but I’ve coached him. He’s improved. Still, he never got used to carrying it around. It’s second nature to me—like wearing trousers. I’ve carried one for years, ever since there was always someone watching Manderson. And now…” He trailed off, voice heavy. “They got him when I wasn’t around.”

“Well, gentlemen,” Bunner said, rising, “excuse me—I’m headed into Bishopsbridge. A lot to do. Got a stack of cables to send—big enough to choke a cow.”

“I must go too,” Trent replied. “Appointment at the ‘Three Tuns’ inn.”

“Let me give you a ride,” Bunner offered. “I pass right by. Captain, you coming? No? Then come on, Mr. Trent—help me get the car out. Chauffeur’s off duty. We do most of the work ourselves, except for cleaning the grime off her.”

Still talking in his calm, measured tone, Bunner led Trent downstairs through the house to the garage at the rear. Set apart from the main building, it offered a welcome respite from the midday heat. Bunner didn’t hurry. He offered Trent a cigar, which was accepted, and lit his own for the first time. Then he sat on the footboard of the car, hands folded between his knees, studying Trent intently.

“Listen, Mr. Trent,” he said after a pause. “There are things I can tell you that might help. I know your reputation—you’re sharp. I like dealing with smart men. That detective fellow? I don’t know if I’ve sized him right, but he strikes me as a fool. I’d answer any question he had the sense to ask—but I won’t volunteer anything unless he asks. Understand?”

Trent nodded. “That’s a feeling many have around our police force,” he said. “It’s the official manner, I suppose. But let me say this—Murch isn’t what you think. He’s one of the sharpest officers in Europe. Not quick-minded, perhaps, but sure. And he’s seen more than most. My strength is imagination, but in real detective work, experience beats it every time.”

“Experience outweighs nothing!” Bunner shot back sharply. “This isn’t an ordinary case, Mr. Trent. Let me tell you why. I believe the old man knew something was coming. And I believe it was something he couldn’t escape.”

Trent pulled a crate opposite Bunner and sat down. “This sounds serious,” he said. “Tell me your thoughts.”

“I say this because of the change in Manderson’s behavior over the past few weeks. You’ve probably heard—he was always in complete control. That’s true. I’ve always thought him the coldest head in business. His composure was terrifying. I’ve never seen anything like it. And I knew him better than anyone else. I worked beside him in the business he lived for. I knew him deeper than his wife did—poor woman. Deeper than Marlowe. He never saw Manderson in the office during big deals. I saw him at his most vulnerable.”

“Did he have friends?” Trent asked.

Bunner glanced at him sharply. “Someone’s been feeding you rumors,” he said. “Not really. He had acquaintances—big men, people he saw daily. They’d go yachting or hunting together. But I don’t think there was ever a soul he opened up to. Not truly.”

He paused, then continued. “A few months ago, he began acting differently—gloomy, irritable, as if he were constantly brooding over something terrible, something he couldn’t fix. It didn’t stop. Same at home, same in the city. Something weighed heavily on him. But it wasn’t until recent weeks that his self-control started slipping. And here’s something no one else knows—only me. With everyone else, he was just morose. But when we were alone—me and him—in his office or working together, if anything went wrong, he’d explode. By God, he’d fly off the handle like a madman. I’ve seen him rip open a letter, read something he didn’t like, and start shouting, ‘I wish I had the man who wrote this here! I wouldn’t do a thing to him!’—all that, till it was heartbreaking. Never saw such a change. And then, for a week before he died, he stopped working altogether—the first time in my memory. Wouldn’t answer letters, wouldn’t respond to cables, even though everything was collapsing. I thought his nerves were shot from constant worry. Once I suggested he see a doctor. He told me to go to hell. But nobody else saw this side of him. If he was having one of these rages in the library and Mrs. Manderson walked in, he’d be calm and cold again in seconds.”

“So you think this was due to a secret fear—fear someone wanted to kill him?” Trent asked.

Bunner nodded.

“Then,” Trent said, “you must have considered the possibility of mental instability—overwork, stress, something like that. That’s what your account suggests. And it’s common enough among American industrialists, isn’t it? That’s what the papers make it sound like.”

“Don’t let them fool you,” Bunner said firmly. “Only the ones who made money too fast and can’t hold onto it go crazy. Think of the real big men—anyone near Manderson’s level. Ever hear of one losing his mind? They don’t. Believe me. Sure, everyone has their quirks—what we call their *loco point*. But that’s not madness. It’s just oddities—like hating cats, or my own weakness: I can’t eat fish.”

“And Manderson’s?”

“He had plenty. He despised unnecessary luxury—fancy trinkets, ornaments. Didn’t want servants doing little things for him. Hated having people follow him around unless he needed them. He was meticulous about clothes and shoes—spent a fortune on them—but never had a valet. Never liked anyone touching him. All his life, no one shaved him.”

“I’ve heard that,” Trent said. “Why?”

“It was part of his nature,” Bunner said slowly. “A deep suspicion. Like a dog guarding a bone, always thinking the world was trying to steal it. He didn’t actually believe the barber would try to cut his head off. But he felt it was possible. So he took no risks. In business, he always believed others were after his success—which often was true. But not always. The result? He became the most cautious, secretive operator in finance. That’s what made him rich. But it’s not madness. Not by a long shot. You asked if he was losing his mind? I think he was worn out—broken by worry. Losing nerve.”

Trent smoked in silence, weighing the implications. He wondered how much Bunner knew about the marital tension in Manderson’s household—and decided to test the waters.

“I understand he had problems with his wife.”

“Sure,” Bunner replied. “But do you think that would shake Sig Manderson? No, sir. He was too big a man to fall apart over something like that.”

Trent studied him, seeing beyond the sharpness to a kind of innocent conviction. Bunner genuinely believed domestic strife was trivial compared to business crises.

“What *was* the problem between them?”

“Can’t say,” Bunner replied. “Marlowe and I talked about it endlessly. Never figured it out. At first, I thought maybe the old man was disappointed—he expected a child. But Marlowe thinks it was the other way around. He heard something from Mrs. Manderson’s French maid.”

Trent looked up sharply. “Célestine!” he said. And inside, he thought: *So that’s what she meant.*

Bunner misread his expression. “Don’t think I’m betraying anyone,” he said. “Marlowe isn’t that sort. Célestine liked him—speaks French like a native, and she’d always pull him aside for gossip. French servants are different that way. And whether servant or not,” he added firmly, “I don’t see how a woman could bring up such a subject with a man. But the French… well, they beat me.”

He shook his head slowly.

“Back to what you were saying,” Trent said. “You believe Manderson was afraid for his life for some time. Who could threaten him? I’m still in the dark.”

“Terror? Maybe not,” Bunner mused. “Anxiety. Suspense—more like it. The old man wasn’t easy to scare. And more than that, he wasn’t taking precautions—he was avoiding them. It looked like he was inviting a quick end—if my theory’s right. Why else would he sit in that library window at night, shirt white as a target, staring into the dark? As for who might want him dead—well, sir,” Bunner said with a faint smile, “you haven’t lived in America. Take the Pennsylvania coal strike alone—thirty thousand men, women, children depending on them, who’d have jumped at the chance to shoot the man who forced them to starve or surrender. Thirty thousand of the toughest outsiders in the country. And there are types—desperadoes—who’ve waited years to kill a man. I’ve heard of dynamiting a man in Idaho for something he did in New Jersey ten years earlier. Do you think the Atlantic Ocean stops them? It takes courage to be a big businessman in our country. No, sir—the old man knew. Always knew. There were dangerous men scattered across the States who hated him. My belief is, he finally learned they were coming after him. What confuses me is why he didn’t run. Why did he walk straight into the garden yesterday morning—knowing he was being shot at?”

Bunner fell silent. For a moment, both men sat in quiet thought, blue smoke curling from their cigars.

Then Trent stood. “Your theory is new to me,” he said. “It makes sense. Only question is whether it fits all the facts. I can’t reveal what I’m doing for my paper, Mr. Bunner—but I can say this: I’ve already concluded this was a premeditated crime. An unusually clever one. Thank you. We’ll talk again.”

He checked his watch. “My friend’s been expecting me. Shall we go?”

“Two o’clock,” Bunner said, looking at his own. “Ten a.m. in little old New York. You don’t know Wall Street, Mr. Trent. Let’s hope we never see anything closer to hell than what’s loose on the Street right now.”
