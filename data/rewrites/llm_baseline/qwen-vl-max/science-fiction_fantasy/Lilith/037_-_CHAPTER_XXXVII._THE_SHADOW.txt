A soft murmur of joy stirred among my companions—someone had spotted the others in the distance. Two riders, mounted on Lona’s horse, spurred forward to join their kin. But as they neared, the cheer faltered into silence. Then came a sound like waves breaking against stone: quiet, broken sobs.

When I reached them, I saw at once that something terrible had happened. Their faces—so young, so bright just days before—were now hollowed by dread, eyes wide with a fear too deep for words. Grief could not have carved such emptiness into their features. A few approached slowly, arms outstretched, seeking to take the burden from me. I gave it over. The way they looked at it—gentle, tender, yet hopeless—made my chest tighten with sorrow, even as my own heart lay shattered.

They wept over her—their mother-queen—kissed her hands, pressed their cheeks to hers, called her name, but she did not stir. Not one flicker of life. One child carried each arm, stroking them softly as if to coax warmth back. Others cradled her body beneath their small frames. Those left behind crowded close, forming a silent ring around her.

On a patch of thick, yielding grass, they laid her down. And there, the Little Ones gathered—sobbing, whispering, clinging to one another in the dimming light. Outside the circle stood the elephants, massive and still. I stood near them, staring across the sea of tiny heads at Lona’s pale face.

Then Odu, closest to me, froze. His eyes locked onto the princess. “I’ve seen her before,” he whispered, voice trembling. “She was the one who fought the white leopardess—the night they woke us with their screaming!”

“Foolishness,” replied his neighbor. “That was a beast. Spotted.”

“Look at her eyes!” Odu insisted. “She’s a giantess—evil—but still a beast. The same one. The spotted one!”

The other stepped closer. Odu yanked him back. “Don’t look at her!” he cried, recoiling yet unable to turn away. “She’ll devour you! That was *her* shadow! She’s the wicked princess!”

“That can’t be,” said another. “They said she was beautiful.”

“It *is* the princess,” I said quietly, stepping forward. “But wickedness has turned her ugly.”

She heard. And when she did—oh, what a glance she cast! Not anger, not sorrow… but a terrible, ancient hunger.

Odu lowered his head. “It was wrong of me to run,” he murmured.

“What made you run?” I asked.

“I thought I’d find you where I left you,” he said, voice small.

He paused. Then another spoke. “I don’t know why I ran. I was scared.”

“A man came down the hill from the palace,” said a third.

“How did he scare you?”

“I don’t know.”

“He wasn’t a man,” Odu said. “He was a shadow. No thickness. Just black.”

“Tell me more.”

“He walked down the hill—black as night, moving like a bad giant, but flat. Like smoke stretched thin. We saw him and were afraid—but we didn’t run. We watched. He came on, like he meant to walk over us. But then… he began to spread. Bigger. Wider. Until he filled the sky. Then he vanished. And then—he was *on* us.”

“What do you mean?”

“He was between us. Black through everything. We couldn’t see each other. And then—he was *inside* us.”

“How did you know?”

“He changed me. I felt… bad. Not Odu. Not the Odu I knew. I wanted to tear Sozo apart—not really, but… like.”

He turned and clutched Sozo tightly. “It wasn’t me, Sozo,” he sobbed. “Deep inside, it was still Odu—loving you. And then Odu rose up and knocked Naughty away. I grew sick. I thought I had to die to get free. Then—I heard a laugh. A horrible laugh. It echoed in my mind. And then I was running. I didn’t know I was running until I found myself flying across the grass, all of us fleeing. I wanted to stop—but I didn’t think of it until I was outside the gate. Then I realized: I had run from a shadow that tried to become me, but failed. I was still Odu. Still loving Sozo. The shadow had crawled inside me, hated him from within—it wasn’t *me*. And now I know I shouldn’t have fled. But I didn’t truly understand what I was doing until it was done. My legs—they acted without me. They were frightened. They forgot me. They ran. Naughty legs! There! And there!”

He kicked both feet sharply, as if punishing them.

“What became of the shadow?” I asked.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I suppose it went back into the night. Where there is no moon.”

I knelt beside Lona, took her cold form into my lap, and whispered into her ear: “Where are you, Lona? I love you.”

No answer. Her lips remained sealed.

I kissed them—still warm, barely touched by death—and laid her gently back upon the grass. I posted a guard, then rose to ensure the safety of her people through the long night.

Before the sun sank, I placed sentinels around the camp, stationed a watch near the princess, and told the rest to sleep. They fell instantly, sprawled on the earth like children worn out by sorrow.

When the moon rose, I saw something white gliding past the sleeping forms. The leopardess. Silent, swift. She circled the camp three times—always between Lona and the Little Ones. Then she vanished.

I dismissed the watch, let them rest. And I lay beside Lona, watching the stars, waiting for dawn—or whatever might come after.
