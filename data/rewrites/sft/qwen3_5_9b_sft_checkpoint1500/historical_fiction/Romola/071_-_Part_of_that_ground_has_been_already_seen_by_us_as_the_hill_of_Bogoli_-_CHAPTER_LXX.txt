April 14th marked Romola's return to the walls of Florence, a city she had fled with a trembling heart yet felt irresistibly drawn back to. Her journey from Pistoia, where conflicting rumors of Savonarola's trial by fire unsettled her, to Prato, had been driven by a dread that paradoxically propelled her forward. At Prato, she encountered a friar from San Spirito, former confessor to her late father, who became the conduit for the terrible truths awaiting her. He spoke of the wagon bearing Savonarola into the Piazza, describing the scene witnessed by the black-robed monks who had followed it. With a tone heavy with the prejudice typical of the Frati Neri toward their fallen brother, the monk revealed that Savonarola had confessed to deceiving the people, his spirit broken under torture. More devastating still, he recounted the fate of Tito. Though he had leaped into the Arno to escape a frenzied mob, he had perished on the riverbank, slain by an old enemy who harbored ancient grudge. To Romola, however, the monk's account offered no solace, nor did it capture the depth of the tragedy; she alone understood the weight of his sacrifice and the finality of their separation.

By evening, Romola sat in agitated silence within the city, surrounded by the cacophony of grief and jubilation. Monna Brigida, her companion, filled the air with frantic narratives, her hair hastily restored from its wild state during Romola's absence, now combed and pinned with a desperate determination to keep her "dear child" close. Yet, Romola remained impervious to Brigida's gossipy details. The shadow of Tito's murder and Savonarola's fall cast a pall so thick that she could scarcely register the trivialities around her. Her mind, burdened by a profound, awe-struck sorrow, focused entirely on two urgent objectives: to locate Savonarola, if possible, and to discover the whereabouts of Tessa and her children.

"Tell me, cousin," Romola interrupted abruptly, cutting short Brigida's ramblings about future domestic arrangements, "since Tito's passing, has anyone seen or heard of a young woman accompanied by two small children?"

Brigida gasped, her hands rising in alarm. "Christ! No! Was he so wicked as to abandon them? Ah, then, that explains your departure. You left me believing you had chosen freedom, unaware of the depths of your despair. If I had known..." She trailed off, shaking her head. "Well, never mind. Since that was your reason for fleeing—"

"No, cousin," Romola pressed, her voice firm despite her fragility, "do not speak thus. Above all, I must find that woman and her children. They are utterly helpless. Let it be my first act."

"If that makes you a Piagnone," Brigida muttered, shrinking back, "then I have been counting peas instead of prayers. Two children!" she cried softly. "But if you intend to raise others' offspring, surely you must first secure them."

"Cousin, the poor soul knows no wrong," Romola whispered, unable to bear the speculation. "I will explain later. First, let us find them."

Dawn broke early the following morning, guiding Romola to the house beyond San Ambrogio where she had previously sought refuge with Tessa. There, however, lay only emptiness. Tito must have sent her away beforehand, perhaps intending to retrieve them later, though Romola feared he could not bring himself to part with the children. A painful conjecture took root: Tessa was likely wandering outside Florence, trapped in a state of wondering, helpless misery at some roadside halt. Neighbors confirmed only that the elderly Lisa had departed a week prior with her belongings, offering no clue as to Tessa's destination.

Days stretched into weeks of anxious inactivity. Despite the urging of others, Romola could not muster the strength to launch a broad search. Her nature, reserved and sensitive, forbade her from exposing Tessa's connection to Tito, fearing judgment. Still, every glimpse of round brown legs or a peasant's dress caused her heart to race. She did not view this vigilance as heroic charity, but rather as a binding necessity; she needed someone to protect, a reason to cling to life amidst the ashes of her world. She dreamed of clutching those children, of weaving a bond that would heal the wounds of her past sorrows. While Tito's estate held claims to wealth, Romola distrusted its purity and resolved to surrender most of it to the State, retaining only enough to purchase books for her father's library—a modest sum sufficient for Tessa's survival.

Yet, Monna Brigida's influence proved insistent. She clamored that Romola must reside with her permanently, arguing that if one wished to nurture strangers' children, the household must expand to include them. "Find them first," Brigida urged, her voice tinged with superstition and hope, echoing Romola's own internal conviction. Some unseen force must guide them together.

A new path emerged through rumor: Tito had arranged horses and mules at San Gallo, indicating his intent to flee the city via that gate. Romola resolved to investigate, walking the Via San Gallo beneath her long widow's veil, scanning the crowds for any sign of Tessa. As she passed, she spotted Bratti, the itinerant peddler, haggling near a stall. A glimmer of hope ignited within her when she noticed something dangling from his basket.

"Bratti, my friend," she called out, approaching him with sudden urgency, "where did you acquire this necklace?"

"It is worth money, madonna," Bratti replied calmly, his gaze steady. "But I cannot sell it; I have pledged it, bound by a promise. My heart is too tender for trade."

"Tell me where you obtained it. Did you receive it from a little woman named Tessa?"

"Aye," Bratti admitted, his expression softening. "If you know her and would redeem it for a florin, sparing her the pain of selling it, you perform a great kindness. She wept as if plunging into a brook when she parted with it. I charge you little, for I am not hard-hearted."

Romola handed over the coin, unclipping the necklace from the basket with trembling fingers. "Where is she?"

"Just beyond the gate at the far end of the Borgo," Bratti indicated, pointing toward Old Sibilla Manetti's house. "Any neighbor will direct you."

With wings of purpose, Romola hastened toward the destination. The house stood quiet, but inside, in a dim chamber, Tessa and the children awaited. They had lingered there far longer than intended, possessing neither coin nor provisions, save for their tattered garments. But when Romola knocked, the door swung open instantly.

Within, Tesa sat upon a low bed, her tears having dried into hollow sobs, staring vacantly at her two children. Lillo covered his face with his skirt, roaring to terrify Ninna, who cowered in the corner, only to peek out, testing the limits of her fear. The children played in a corner distant from the door, oblivious to the moment until it arrived. Romola stepped inside, removing her veil, holding the necklace aloft.

"Tessa!"

The name shattered the silence. Tessa sprang to her feet, turning with wide, startled eyes.

"Look," Romola said gently, sliding the beads onto Tessa's neck. "God has brought me back to you."

A scream tore from Tessa's lips, followed by a flood of sobs as she collapsed into Romola's embrace, clinging desperately to the arms that held her sacred token. The children, sensing the shift in atmosphere, crawled from their corner, grasping their mother's skirts and gazing upward with wide, bewildered eyes at the stranger who had returned.

That same day, they traveled to Monna Brigida's dwelling in the Borgo degli Albizzi. In the safety of the new home, Romola gently broke the news to Tessa that Naldo could never return—not due to cruelty, but because death had taken him. "Be comforted, my Tessa," Romola soothed, pressing her hand to the grieving mother's shoulder. "I am here to care for you forever. And now, we have Lillo and Ninna."

Monna Brigida watched the reunion, her lips twitching in a mixture of reverence and suppressed ambition. She wished to speak, to assert her authority over the household, yet held her tongue, knowing that in the presence of Romola, the soul's ownership seemed suspended. "Let it wait," she thought, watching the "puss-faced minx" who clearly lacked worldly wisdom. "Some day I shall remind her of her place. Until then, I must ensure she remains humble."

Later, as supper prepared, Brigida's practical nature surfaced. She insisted on treating the children with excessive sweets, confessing to Romola just before retiring that it would be a shame to neglect such cherubs. "You must yield to me on their feeding, Romola," Brigida argued, her voice laced with maternal pride. "You have never borne a babe, whereas I gave birth to twins, though they died swiftly after arrival. Surely, experience guides the hand."
