Gumbo’s grief at his master’s departure was a spectacle to behold—great, noisy, and unabashed. The poor fellow wept openly, his cries echoing through the Lambert household, where the maidservants, ever tender-hearted, gathered to console him. Dark skin notwithstanding, Gumbo found no shortage of sympathy among the fairer sex. Europe, it seemed, was less particular about such matters than certain other quarters of the world. And so, between sobs, he fortified himself with generous portions of beer and cold meat in the kitchen, his sorrow temporarily assuaged by the comforts of the larder.

Hetty Lambert, usually so quick with a jest, had grown uncharacteristically subdued. The girl who once chattered like a sparrow now moved through the house in near silence, her spirit weighed down by thoughts of far-off battlefields. She flinched at the sound of cannonfire in her imagination, trembled during prayers for soldiers, and scanned the papers daily for news of the expedition. Every report of danger sent a fresh stab of fear through her heart. Was he wounded? Captured? Worse? She would have given anything—even her own limbs—for his safe return.

“Hetty, you never used to fret like this when Papa was in Minorca,” Theo remarked one day.

“Ah, Theo,” Hetty sighed, “that was before George was safe at home and Harry was not.”

Theo blushed, and the conversation ended there.

Meanwhile, Harry Warrington’s first taste of war proved less glorious than anticipated. The British fleet, after landing at Cancale, encountered little resistance—just a few dragoons who prudently retreated, and a solitary old Frenchman who fired his two guns before being captured. The troops marched to St. Malo, burned some ships, and returned to their vessels without ever meeting a proper foe. Harry, though disappointed, wrote home in high spirits:

*"Dearest Brother—I am returned, unwounded and unglorified, but alive and well. We saw dragons—or at least dragoons—who declined to fight. We burned a few ships, slept under canvas, and marched through narrow lanes where any hidden enemy might have picked us off. It was not the grand campaign I had hoped for, but I have smelled powder, slept rough, and endured the sea like a proper sailor. Next time, perhaps, the French will stand their ground."*

The letter sent George rushing to Dean Street, where the Lamberts greeted the news with relief—though Hetty’s nerves were hardly soothed.

But the true test came soon enough. Emboldened by their minor success, the British commanders turned their sights on Cherbourg. This time, the news was triumphant: the town had surrendered, its forts seized, its ships burned, its harbor dismantled. London erupted in celebration. The captured French cannon, emblazoned with arrogant mottoes, were paraded through the streets. Hetty, in her excitement, nearly convinced herself that Harry had single-handedly hauled the guns from their batteries.

Then came St. Cas.

Disaster.

The troops, re-embarking under fire, were caught by a swift French advance. The rear guard, outnumbered ten to one, broke and fled to the boats. Seven hundred were lost—killed, drowned, or taken. The General lay dead on the beach.

And Harry?

No word.

In London, the jubilation turned to dread. George’s face was grim as he brought the news to the Lamberts. Hetty, pale and silent, clutched her sister’s hand.

Where was their Virginian?
