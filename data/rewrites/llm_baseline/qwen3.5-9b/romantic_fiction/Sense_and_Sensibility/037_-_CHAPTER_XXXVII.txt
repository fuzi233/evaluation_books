After a fortnight, Mrs. Palmer recovered sufficiently for her mother to cease devoting her entire days to her care. Contenting herself with visits once or twice daily, Mrs. Jennings returned to her own home and routine, finding the Dashwood sisters eager to resume their previous level of hospitality.

It was about the third or fourth morning following this resumption of normalcy that Mrs. Jennings entered the drawing-room at Berkeley Street. Having just returned from her customary visit to Mrs. Palmer, she found Elinor sitting alone, radiating an air of hurried importance that suggested she expected to hear something extraordinary. Seizing the moment, Mrs. Jennings launched directly into her revelation.

"Good heavens, my dear Miss Dashwood!" she exclaimed. "Have you heard the news?"

"No, ma'am. What is it?"

"It is something truly strange! But you shall hear everything. Upon arriving at Mr. Palmer's, I found Charlotte in a complete panic regarding the child. She insisted it was terribly ill—that it was crying, fretting, and covered in pimples. I examined it immediately and remarked, 'Good heavens, my dear, it is nothing at all, merely the red gum.' Nurse agreed instantly. Yet, Charlotte refused to be reassured, so Mr. Donovan was summoned. Fortunately, he had just arrived from Harley Street and dropped in. Upon seeing the child, he concurred with us that it was simply the red gum, and Charlotte finally calmed down. However, just as he departed, it occurred to me—I confess I cannot explain how—to inquire if there was any other news. Instantly, he smirked, simpered, adopted a grave demeanor, and appeared to possess some hidden knowledge. Finally, he whispered, 'To prevent any unpleasant reports reaching the young ladies under your care concerning their sister's indisposition, I deem it advisable to state that there is no cause for alarm; I hope Mrs. Dashwood will recover fully.'"

"What! Is Fanny ill?"

"That is precisely what I asked, my dear. 'Good heavens!' I replied, 'Is Mrs. Dashwood ill?' Then the whole truth emerged. Here is the summary of the matter: Mr. Edward Ferrars—the very young man I used to jest with you about (though, happily, as events prove, there was never anything between us)—appears to have been secretly engaged to my cousin, Lucy, for over a year! There you have it, my dear! Not a soul knew a syllable of this except Nancy. Could you possibly believe such a thing feasible? Their mutual affection is understandable, but for matters to advance to this point without anyone suspecting it! That is the marvel! I never witnessed them together, or I certainly would have discovered it sooner.

Well, this secret was maintained strictly to avoid offending Mrs. Ferrars, leaving neither she, your brother, nor your sister aware of the arrangement. Until this very morning, when poor Nancy—who, you know, is well-intentioned but lacks cunning—let the cat out of the bag. Reasoning that everyone loves Lucy and would approve, she rushed to your sister. Your sister was sitting alone at her embroidery work, unsuspecting of the impending shock; mere moments earlier, she had been telling your brother she intended to arrange a match between Edward and some Lady's daughter. Imagine the blow to her vanity and pride! She immediately collapsed into violent hysterics, screaming loudly enough to be heard by your brother in his study below, where he was contemplating a letter to his country steward. He rushed upstairs, and a dreadful scene ensued. By then, Lucy had arrived, unaware of the turmoil brewing. Poor soul! I pity her immensely. I must add, she was treated very harshly. Your sister scolded like a fury, driving Lucy into a fainting fit. Nancy fell to her knees weeping bitterly, while your brother paced the room, utterly at a loss. Mrs. Dashwood declared they must leave immediately, forcing your brother to kneel and plead for permission to let them pack their belongings until they could depart. Only then did she relapse into hysterics, frightening him so much he called for Mr. Donovan.

Mr. Donovan found the house in chaos. The carriage stood ready at the door as he arrived; poor Lucy was barely able to walk, and Nancy was nearly as ill. I lack patience with your sister, though I sincerely hope this becomes a match despite her objections. Good heavens, what a torment poor Mr. Edward will face upon hearing this! To have his love so scornfully rejected! They say he adores her, as he ought. I should not be surprised if he were furious—Mr. Donovan agrees. We discussed it extensively. Best of all, he has returned to Harley Street to be available when Mrs. Ferrars is informed, for she was summoned immediately after my cousins left. Your sister was certain *she* would also fall into hysterics, and indeed, she likely will. I harbor no pity for either of them. I cannot comprehend people making such a fuss over money and status. There is no earthly reason why Mr. Edward and Lucy cannot marry. I am sure Mrs. Ferrars can provide adequately for her son. Although Lucy possesses little herself, she knows how to maximize resources. If Mrs. Ferrars granted him £500 a year, she could maintain an appearance equal to anyone else with double that sum. Good heavens, how comfortably they might live in a cottage slightly larger than yours, with two maids and two servants! I believe I could even secure a housemaid for them, as my Betty has a sister seeking employment."

Mrs. Jennings paused here. With sufficient time to compose herself, Elinor responded calmly, offering observations that the gravity of the situation warranted. She was relieved not to be suspected of harboring special feelings for Edward—a hope she had nurtured recently—and doubly grateful that Marianne was absent, allowing her to discuss the affair without embarrassment and judge the conduct of all parties with apparent impartiality.

Elinor struggled to define her own expectations of the outcome, though she desperately tried to dismiss the possibility of anything other than the eventual marriage of Edward and Lucy. Curiosity burned within her to hear what Mrs. Ferrars would say and do, though she anticipated the nature of the reaction. More urgently, she sought to understand how Edward would behave. She felt profound compassion for him, very little for Lucy (finding it difficult to muster even that small amount), and none whatsoever for the rest of the group.

Since Mrs. Jennings could converse on no other topic, Elinor quickly realized the necessity of preparing Marianne for the inevitable disclosure. Time was of the essence to reveal the truth, acquaint her with reality, and ensure she could discuss the matter with others without revealing her own anxiety for her sister or her resentment toward Edward.

Elinor's task was painfully difficult. She was about to dismantle what she believed to be her sister's sole solace, expose details of Edward that might permanently tarnish his image in Marianne's eyes, and force Marianne to relive her own disappointment through a striking parallel of circumstances. Though the assignment was unwelcome, it was imperative, and Elinor hastened to fulfill it.

She made no effort to dwell on her own feelings or portray herself as suffering, aside from the stoic self-command she had practiced since learning of Edward's engagement. Her narrative remained clear and simple. While delivered with emotion, it lacked violent agitation or impetuous grief. Such displays belonged to the listener; Marianne listened with horror and wept profusely. Elinor resolved to be a comfort to others amidst her own distress, offering assurance of her mental composure and a fervent defense of Edward against every charge save imprudence.

Yet, for some time, Marianne accepted neither explanation nor comfort. Edward seemed a second Willoughby. Acknowledging, as Elinor did, that she had loved him most sincerely, how could Marianne feel differently? Regarding Lucy Steele, she deemed her so utterly unlovable and incapable of winning a sensible man's heart that she initially refused to believe, and later to forgive, any former affection Edward might have held for her. She could not even accept it as natural, leaving Elinor to rely solely on a deeper knowledge of human nature to eventually convince her.

Elinor's initial communication stated only the fact of the engagement and its duration. Marianne's emotions then overwhelmed her, halting further detail. For a while, the only recourse was to soothe her distress, alleviate her fears, and counteract her resentment. The first question Marianne posed, prompting further specifics, was: "How long has this been known to you, Elinor? Has he written to you?"

"I have known it for four months," Elinor replied. "When Lucy first visited Barton Park last November, she confided in me regarding her engagement."

At these words, astonishment flashed across Marianne's eyes, which her lips could not express. After a pause filled with wonder, she cried out, "Four months! Have you known of this for four months?"

Elinor confirmed it.

"While attending me in all my misery, has this burden weighed on your heart? And I have reproached you for being happy!"

"It was not fitting that you should then know how much I was the opposite!"

"Four months!" Marianne repeated. "So calm! So cheerful! How have you sustained yourself?"

"By believing I was fulfilling my duty. My promise to Lucy obligated me to secrecy. I owed her the avoidance of hints, and I owed my family and friends the prevention of unnecessary anxiety that I could not satisfy."

Marianne appeared deeply moved.
