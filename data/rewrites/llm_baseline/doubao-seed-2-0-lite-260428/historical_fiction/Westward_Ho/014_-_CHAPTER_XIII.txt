“The spirits of your fathers Shall start from every wave; For the deck it was their field of fame, And ocean was their grave.”

The low oak-panelled parlour in old Plymouth glowed soft by firelight, its shelves crowded with Spanish silver plunder, crudely drawn coast charts, strange foreign bird skins, and a portrait of old Captain Will Hawkins, favoured of Henry VIII, hung above the hearth. Tacked above that portrait was the tattered Spanish ensign John Hawkins had torn from the defences of Rio de la Hacha fifteen years prior, when he and two hundred men had stormed the town against a garrison of a thousand and watered his ships right from the enemy’s wells. The two figures sat by that crackling fire: Adrian Gilbert, tall, broad of studious brow, his eyes weakened from hours bent over crucibles and furnace mouths, and Mrs. Hawkins, once a famously comely Plymouth lass, now worn thin by decades of fretting for her sea captain husband at sea.

“So you see, my dear Mrs. Hawkins,” Adrian was saying, tapping a sample of rough Cornish ore laid out on the table between them, “you can see the silver glint plain, not to mention the lead, manganese, copper, and above all this gossan the locals prize—what I suspect is no mere ore matrix, but the very crude first matter of all metals. Follow me? If the alchemical recipes Doctor Dee gave me succeed even half as well as I expect, I will refine out the silver first, then turn every scrap of the remaining ore to pure gold. At that rate, Peru and Mexico’s hoards become irrelevant, and England sits mistress of all the globe. Strange? No doubt. Distant? Surely. But possible, my dear madam, entirely possible.”

“And what good would that do you, Mr. Gilbert?” Mrs. Hawkins sighed, her fingers twisting in her linen apron. “If you could find a philosopher’s stone to turn sinners into saints, now—only God’s grace can do that, and it often drags its feet coming, doesn’t it?”

“Ah, but think, madam!” Adrian leaned forward, earnest. “That Comb Martin mine could turn out to be an inexhaustible lode of gold, enough to fund my Northwest Passage patent in full. My brother Humphrey already holds Newfoundland, and the endless pine forests there will build us fresh China-bound ships year after year, no end to them.”

“Sir Humphrey has far kinder things in his heart than gold, Mr. Adrian,” she said, shaking her head. “He has walked close and gracious with the Lord these seven years past. I wish my own Captain John was half so steady.”

“And what makes you assume I care for nothing but gold?” Adrian protested. “Is gold not the very strength of the Spaniard, the mainspring of all that Antichrist stands for? It is only by outstripping their hoards that we can outfight them. You shake your head, but tell me: England must have gold, will it not? Is it better to refine it bloodlessly at home in our own mines, or to spill the blood of honest English seamen and innocent foreign folk to steal it across the ocean?”

“God’s blessing never rests on greed, Mr. Gilbert,” she said softly, pointing upward to a carved boss on the ribbed oak ceiling above them, where the family herald had emblazoned John Hawkins’s new crest: a bound Demi-Moor, proper in every detail. “Ever since he first sailed to Guinea to hunt those poor negroes, my heart has never known a single day of real light. The very day we hung that crest in our new house, the parson read that verse in the first lesson: ‘Woe to him that buildeth his house by iniquity, and his chambers by wrong. Shalt thou live because thou closest thyself in cedar?’ It went into my ears like fire, and settled in my heart like lead. All those stolen souls weigh on me from dawn till dusk. We are grand now, money flows in fast, but the Lord will require their blood at our hands yet, I know He will.”

Adrian tried to soothe her, reminding her that John had already paid for those misdeeds when he lost half his fleet and nearly his own life at San Juan de Ulúa, but Mrs. Hawkins would not be comforted. The queen had been sharp with him over the fate of the enslaved people then, she said, but it had not mended him. He grew greedier by the day, niggardly with his coin, endlessly plotting new voyages for profit, jealous of Drake and every other man who had struck richer fortune on the high seas. Plymouth, once a quiet, God-fearing little port, had turned rotten with hunger for gold ever since the men had found their way to the Indies: every sailor’s wife nagged her husband to sail off to bring back coin for farthingales and crisping pins, and the whole place was fast forgetting how to walk in peace.

The two sat quiet for a long moment, Adrian half ashamed of his own grand alchemical ambitions, when the parlour door swung open hard. In stepped Amyas Leigh, wrapped in a salt-crusted rough sea gown, his boots caked in harbour mud.

Adrian shot to his feet, pale as ash. “Amyas! What brings you here? Where is the Golden Hind? Is Humphrey well?”

“Your brother is in perfect health, sir,” Amyas said, his voice heavy. “The Golden Hind sailed on to Dartmouth with Master Hayes. I landed here to make my way north to Bideford before heading to London. I stopped at Drake’s house first, but he was away.”

“Golden Hind home already? The expedition went wrong, didn’t it?” Adrian cried, rushing to hold a candle up to Amyas’s haggard, wind-scoured face. “Speak! Don’t drive me mad waiting.”

Amyas laid a heavy hand on Adrian’s shoulder, his lip trembling. “Your great, glorious brother, sir, is far better bestowed than settling new lands in Newfoundland. He is with the God he spent his whole life serving.”

Adrian shrieked, staggering back against the table. “Dead? But he was always as close to the Lord as Enoch! Don’t parable me, man, tell me the whole of it.”

And slowly, Amyas laid out the whole tragic tale that Master Hayes would later record for all Devon to read: the mutinous, ragtag crew of conscripted ruffians, daily conspiracies to steal the ships and desert, men deserting in droves to plunder local fishing vessels, until Sir Humphrey, too kind to punish the treachery, let all the faint-hearted sail for home. They had set out from St John’s to map the southern coast, and Gilbert, stung by taunts that he was afraid of the sea, had insisted on sailing the tiny 10-ton *Squirrel*, crammed full of nettings, small arms, and ordnance, rather than the larger, safer Golden Hind. Seven days out from Cape Raz, their largest ship the *Delight* struck uncharted shoals at dusk: the crew had played trumpets and drums and hautboys all through their last night, and sang their own death knells before the waves swallowed all but fourteen of their number. The two remaining ships limped on, worn thin with cold and famine, before the crews begged Gilbert to turn for home for England. Just then, a great lion, as large as any on land, slid across the surface of the waves right toward them, roaring, before vanishing back into the deep. Gilbert had laughed it off as a good omen, a sign they were fighting the devil himself.

Amyas saved the last, worst part for last, his voice cracking: off the Azores, the storms rose to fury, seas breaking short and pyramid-high. On the 9th of September, Sir Humphrey sat out on the stern of the tiny *Squirrel* with a book in his hand, and called out to the men on the Golden Hind every time they drifted close enough to hear: “We are as near heaven by sea as by land.” An hour later, the look-out cried out that the *Squirrel*’s lights had vanished. The little frigate, the great knight on her deck, had been swallowed whole by the dark Atlantic in an instant.

Adrian moaned, crumpling into a chair. “My brother! The glory of our house, the glory of Devon!”

Mrs. Hawkins wept, crossing herself. Amyas told him Sir Humphrey had worn the queen’s jewel round his neck to the last, his personal motto *Mutare vel timere sperno*—I scorn to change or to fear—lived out to his very last breath. He had grown far meeker in his final months, Amyas said, saying that a refiner far better than any alchemist had followed him across all the seas, and purified him seven times over in the fire, before calling him home.

The talk lulled. Adrian had lost his entire fortune to the failed expedition, Amyas had nothing left of his own voyage save the rags on his back. Amyas apologised, asking if Mrs. Hawkins could lend him five pounds and a spare shirt of her son Richard’s to get himself and his few surviving men north to Bideford.

“Five? Fifty pounds, Mr. Leigh!” she cried, bustling to the iron strongbox by the wall. “John Hawkins’s wife will never turn a penny away from a distressed mariner, especially one of gentle Devon birth. Sit down, eat a hot supper first—you look half starved.”

As he ate, she chattered of local news, of Adrian seeing his mother in London a few months prior, before she dropped the piece of news that hit Amyas like a musket ball to the chest: the Spanish prisoner he had sent home from Smerwick had escaped Bideford, and taken no less a girl than Rose Salterne, the Rose of Torridge, the mayor’s only daughter, off with him.

Amyas fell back in his chair as if he had been shot. He waved off Mrs. Hawkins’s cries of alarm, claiming the heavy Plymouth ale had turned his weak, sea-sick head dizzy. She pressed a mug of spiced aqua vitae on him, fussed over him, and saw him to bed. But Amyas did not sleep a wink all that long night, tossing and turning as every thought of Rose ran through his head. By dawn, he had already secured a horse and coin from Mrs. Hawkins, and set off north along the Bideford road, his face set hard for the west.
