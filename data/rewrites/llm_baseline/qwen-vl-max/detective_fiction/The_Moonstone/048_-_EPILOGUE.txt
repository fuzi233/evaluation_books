On the twenty-seventh of June, I was given orders by Sergeant Cuff to follow three men—described as Indians and suspected of murder—who had been seen boarding a steamer at Tower Wharf bound for Rotterdam. I left London the next morning on a different company’s vessel, arriving in Rotterdam on Thursday, the twenty-eighth. There, I located the captain of the Wednesday steamer. He confirmed that the three men had indeed traveled with him—but only as far as Gravesend. Off that point, one of them asked when they would reach Calais. When told the ship was heading for Rotterdam, their leader reacted with shock and dismay, declaring they had made a grave mistake. They offered to forfeit their passage money if the captain would put them ashore. Moved by their plight—foreigners lost in an unfamiliar country—and seeing no cause to detain them, the captain sent a boat to take them off. This act, clearly planned ahead to evade pursuit, convinced me they were trying to disappear. I returned to England immediately. I disembarked at Gravesend and learned they had gone on to London, then later to Plymouth. Further inquiries revealed they had boarded the *Bewley Castle*, an East Indiaman sailing directly to Bombay. Upon receiving this news, Sergeant Cuff arranged for the authorities in Bombay to be alerted overland so the ship could be searched upon arrival. After that, my involvement ended. I have heard nothing more since.

I am writing at Sergeant Cuff’s request to record what I know about three passengers—believed to be Hindoos—on the *Bewley Castle* during her voyage to Bombay last summer. They joined us at Plymouth and caused no trouble during the journey. They were lodged forward, and I rarely saw them personally. Near the end of the trip, we encountered a prolonged calm lasting three days and nights off the Indian coast. I cannot recall the exact coordinates, but the currents carried us closer to shore, and once the wind returned, we reached port within twenty-four hours. During the calm, discipline aboard slackened. Several passengers lowered small boats for recreation—swimming and rowing at dusk. The boats should have been hoisted back, but instead they were left tied to the side. The heat and frustration dulled everyone’s vigilance. On the third night, the watch reported nothing unusual. But at dawn, the smallest boat was missing—and so were the three Hindoos. I believe they took it shortly after dark. Given our proximity to land, any chase would have been futile. In that still weather, they likely reached shore before sunrise. Only upon docking did I learn why they fled: they were fugitives, fleeing justice. I reported everything to the authorities. They blamed me for lax discipline. I have apologized to both them and my owners. Since then, no trace of the men has been found. That is all I can say.

Dear Mr. Bruff,

Do you remember meeting a man in London during autumn 1848—a rough-looking fellow, half-savage in appearance? His name was Murthwaite. You spoke with him at length after dinner, discussing an Indian diamond known as the Moonstone and a plot to steal it. Since then, I’ve wandered through Central Asia and recently returned to northern and northwestern India. About two weeks ago, I found myself in Kattiawar, a remote region scarcely known to Europeans. There, I became involved in an event that—though hard to believe—concerns you directly.

Kattiawar is wild country. Even farmers plough their fields armed. The people are fiercely devoted to ancient Hindu beliefs—worshipping Brahma and Vishnu. Muslim families live in fear; killing a cow brings instant death at the hands of devout Hindus. Two great pilgrimage sites lie within Kattiawar: Dwarka, Krishna’s birthplace, and Somnauth, once a magnificent temple city destroyed centuries ago by Mahmoud of Ghazni.

Drawn by its ruins, I resolved to visit Somnauth again. I was about three days’ walk from the city when I noticed others traveling the same route—first a few, then dozens, then hundreds. By the third day, thousands poured toward the sacred site. I passed as a Buddhist pilgrim from afar—my clothes, language, and sun-browned skin helping conceal my European identity. I offered aid to one traveler, which earned me access to higher-caste Hindus. They told me the crowd was gathering for a nighttime ceremony honoring the Moon God, held on a hill near Somnauth.

As we approached, the moon rose high. My companions had special privileges allowing entry to the shrine, and they invited me along. Behind two grand trees, a curtain concealed the altar. Beneath them, a rocky platform jutted out. Below, the view was breathtaking: rolling hills merging into a plain where three rivers met. One river wound gracefully through the trees; beyond, the sea lay still under the stars. Tens of thousands of white-clad pilgrims covered the slopes and spread across the plain, flanking the rivers. Torches flickered across the crowd, casting red flames into the night. And above it all, the moonlight bathed the scene in radiant silver.

A soft melody from flutes and strings pulled my attention back to the shrine. On the rock stood three men. In the central figure, I recognized the man who had spoken with me in England—the one who appeared with the others on Lady Verinder’s terrace. The other two were undoubtedly his companions. A nearby observer whispered to me: “They are Brahmins who have fallen from caste in service to the god. He demands purification through pilgrimage. Tonight, they separate. Each will wander alone, never to see the others again, never to rest until death.”

As he spoke, the music stopped. The three bowed before the curtain, rose, looked at each other, embraced, then descended in different directions. The crowd parted silently. Three paths opened, then closed behind them. They vanished into the throng.

Then, a loud, joyous song erupted from the shrine. The curtain lifted. Before us, on a throne, sat the Moon God—dark and majestic, riding his antelope, arms stretched to the four winds. And in his forehead, glowing like fire in the night—was the Moonstone! The very diamond I had seen gleaming in a woman’s dress in England!

After eight hundred years, the gem returns to its homeland. How it came back—whether by chance or crime—I do not know. But you lost sight of it in England. And if I understand these people, you will never see it again.

Time moves in circles. Events repeat themselves. What will become of the Moonstone next? No one can say.
