Courage, much like talent in the common sort, flows within a rigid channel; push it but an inch beyond its banks, and the vessel breaks. Martin's fortitude was absolute within the confines of his experience. He had weathered storms and faced perils that hardened the spine of any rustic soul, confronting them with a Spartan indifference born of familiarity. Yet, here, the landscape shifted beneath his boots. He had never been hunted by a bloodhound, nor witnessed that beast's unerring, primal instinct thwarted by human guile. Now, the supernatural merged with the novel to unravel his composure. Within moments, he leaned heavily upon his bow, his vitality draining away like water through cracked earth.

Gerard, whose perception of danger was inversely proportional to its proximity, urged flight. "What avail does it serve?" Martin murmured, his voice heavy with resignation. "If we flee the woods, we die cheap; here, amidst the cover, we may die with dignity."

"Alas, good Martin," Gerard cried, panic edging his tone, "do not surrender to despair so swiftly; surely there is a path to escape."

"Oh, Martin!" Margaret interjected, her voice trembling yet sharp with resolve. "What if we were to part ways? Gerard's life stands forfeit alone. Is there no strategy to draw the pursuit upon us both, leaving him free to go?"

"Girl, you misunderstand the bloodhound's nature," Martin replied, his head sinking low. "It does not track this man or that; it tracks blood. From the trail of Ghysbrecht to the corpse, and from the corpse to the man who spilled the blood, that cursed hound will lead them inevitably. Even if Gerard ran through an army or swam the Meuse itself, the beast would find him."

Once more, Martin leaned on his bow, his spirit crushed. Through the canopy, the hound's mellow, resonant bark echoed—a sound so tuneful it seemed designed to lull rather than terrify. *Strange,* the thought lingered, *that beauty should be so terrible.* Consider the eye of the boa constrictor: it fascinates its prey with a loveliness that paralyzes, a ruby set with the emerald's green fire. Yet the deer, seeing it, loses all motion, trembling in anticipation of death. So too did this sweet, mellifluous sound ensnare Martin Wittenhaagen. He stood bewildered, unnerved, paralyzed by the sweetness of the doom approaching. Gerard, too, faltered; Martin's grim prophecy had struck him dumb. He had struck an old man, shed his blood, and now, by that very crimson stain, the four-legged avenger of vengeance closed in. Was not the finger of Heaven pressing down upon them?

While the men were numbed by dread, the woman's mind ignited with frantic clarity. Her love hung in the balance.

"Lend me your knife," she commanded Martin.

He handed it over. "It will be little use in your hands," he warned.

Margaret performed a deed of sleight-of-hand. Stepping behind Gerard, she drew the blade across her forearm, letting it bleed freely. Stooping low, she smeared the blood onto her hose and shoes, moving with such adroitness that neither Gerard nor Martin perceived the act until the red trickle ceased. Seizing the soldier's arm, she spoke urgently. "Come, be a man! Let this end. Take us to some thick copse where numbers mean nothing to our foes."

"I am going," Martin grumbled sullenly. "Hurrying serves no purpose; we cannot outrun the hound, and the place is nearby." Turning left, he led them forward, walking as men walk to execution.

Soon, they reached a dense hazel coppice, reminiscent of the sanctuary they had sought earlier. "Here," Martin declared, "it spans but a furlong, yet it will suffice."

"What is our plan?"

"Pass through this, wait on the other side. As they emerge straggling, shoot three, club two, and the rest will finish us off."

"That is all you can devise?" Gerard challenged.

"That is all."

"Then, Martin Wittenhaagen, I shall take the lead; you have lost your wits. Can you obey a youth like myself?"

"Oh, yes, Martin," Margaret cried, seizing the opportunity. "Do not gainsay Gerard! He is wiser than his years."

Martin yielded a reluctant assent. "Do as I see you do," Gerard instructed. Drawing his massive knife, he chopped hazel shoots close to the ground at every step, twisting them upright behind him to form a living wall among the standing trunks. Martin mimicked the action, though his movements carried a dogged, hopeless weight.

As they painfully navigated the majority of the coppice, the bloodhound's deep bay grew louder, losing its melodic quality to become stern and relentless. Margaret trembled visibly. Martin dropped to his stomach, listening intently. "I hear horse's hooves."

"No," Gerard corrected sharply. "I suspect a mule. That cursed Ghysbrecht is still alive; no other would pursue me with such bitter determination."

"Never strike your enemy unless you intend to slay him," Martin growled gloomily.

"I shall hit harder this time, if Heaven grants me the chance," Gerard vowed.

Finally, they emerged into an open woodland. The trees were large but widely spaced, offering no refuge. Now, mingled with the hound's bay, came a chorus of voices hooping and hallooing.

"The entire village hunts us," Martin stated flatly.

"I care not," Gerard retorted. "Listen, Martin. I have smoothed the track for the dog but roughened it for the men, allowing us to engage them separately. The hound will gain on the humans; once it exits the coppice, we must kill it."

"The hound? There are several."

"I hear but one."

"Aye! Only one speaks; the others run silent. Should the leader lose the scent, another will take up the cry. There will be at least two dogs, or devils wearing dog skins."

"Then we must kill two instead of one. The moment they fall, back into the coppice and reverse direction."

"That is a sound strategy, Gerard," Martin admitted, a spark returning to his eyes.

"Hush! The men are entering the wood."

Gerard whispered his orders. "You stand with your bow by the coppice edge, in the ditch. I shall move only a few yards to that oak and hide behind it. The dogs will follow me; shoot as many as you can as they exit; I will brain the rest as they circle the tree."

Martin's eyes flashed with renewed purpose. They assumed their positions. The hooping and hallooing drew nearer, accompanied by the rustling of young wood. Every now and then, the bloodhound issued a single, piercing bay. It was terrible—the branches brushing closer, the inevitable struggle for life tightening its grip, the death knell ringing out.

A trembling hand rested on Gerard's shoulder, causing him to start violently, his nerves fully strung. "Martin says if we are forced to separate, make for the high ash-tree we entered by."

"Yes! Yes! Yes! But go back, for Heaven's sake! Do not come here, exposed in the open!"

She turned to flee toward Martin, but before she could reach him, a huge dog burst from the coppice, standing erect for a heartbeat. Margaret cowered, but the beast ignored her entirely. Scent was to him what sight was to man. He lowered his nose for an instant, then, with an awful yell, launched himself straight at Gerard's hiding spot, rolling head-over-heels dead as a stone, impaled by an arrow from the bow that twanged beside the coppice in Martin's hand.

Simultaneously, a second hound emerged, sniffing his fallen comrade. Gerard lunged at it, but before he could raise his cudgel, a streak of white lightning seemed to strike the beast. It collapsed into the dust, mortally wounded but not dead, howling pitifully. Gerard lacked the time to dispatch it; the coppice rustled too dangerously close, seeming to breathe with life. Pointing wildly at Martin to retreat, Gerard sprinted a few yards to the right, creeping cautiously into the thick undergrowth just as three men burst forth. These had outpaced their companions significantly; the rest trailed at varying distances. Gerard crawled back almost on all fours. Instinct drove Martin and Margaret to mimic his movement along their line of retreat.

Within mere yards, pursuer and pursued passed each other on parallel tracks. A loud cry signaled the discovery of the dead and wounded hounds. This triggered a babble of voices, swelling as fresh hunters arrived. The hunters, caught off guard, wasted precious time; the hunted used it to their advantage.

"I hear no more hounds," Martin whispered to Margaret, regaining his composure.

"It was your turn to tremble and despair," she replied, her voice thin. "Oh, why did we leave Gerard? They will kill my Gerard, and I shall not be near him."

"Nay, nay! The head responsible for catching him lies elsewhere. Did you bid him meet us at the ash-tree?"

"And so I did. Bless you, Martin, for thinking of that. To the ash-tree!"

"Aye! But with less noise."
