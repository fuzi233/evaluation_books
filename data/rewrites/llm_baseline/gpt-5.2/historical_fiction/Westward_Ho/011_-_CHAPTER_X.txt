So it came about—by no great thunderclap at first, but by the slow, daily settling of a man into comfort—that Don Guzman began to lose a battle he had not meant to fight.

He lay at Bideford on parole in quarters kinder than any he had known for many a month: a roof that did not leak, linen that smelled of sun and lye, and—chiefest temptation of all—Sir Richard Grenville’s table, where meat was not measured like powder in a siege, but came up steaming, glistening, lavish as a harvest-home. Don Guzman took all as a soldier of fortune must: with a stiff pride, a quick eye, and a mind always counting exits.

Until one evening, not a month after his coming, old Salterne the Mayor was bidden to supper.

At first the Spaniard’s soul shrugged within its fine black doublet. These English, with their heresies and their blunt manners, would set a tradesman above the salt among gentlemen, as if a man’s hands—ink-stained from ledgers, tarred from bales and casks—could be washed clean by napery and a silver spoon. Yet Don Guzman was gentleman enough to understand that Grenville was gentleman enough to do nothing uncouth by his own light. So he sat, and endured the strange equality, and watched.

And the Mayor, though he had sat all day at his desk and reckoned apprentices and cargoes, spoke neither foolishly nor vulgarly. He gave the Spaniard every due sign of his rank—never fawning, never forgetting—yet held his own opinions with a quiet obstinacy Don Guzman had seen before in the merchant princes of Genoa and Venice. A man might buy and sell wool and wine, and yet keep within him a kind of sovereignty.

When supper ended, Salterne invited Grenville with proper humility to his own house on the morrow, and then turned to the Don and asked, as frankly as a man weighing coin, whether a nobleman of Spain would condescend to sit at the board of a simple merchant. The cheer, he said, would be fit for any rank, whatever the company.

Don Guzman, who was both captive and bored, accepted. Amusement is a meat that a prisoner chews long.

He found the promise kept. The Mayor’s house had the busy smell of a trading man’s prosperity: spice-sacks in the buttery, dried herbs hanging in the kitchen beams, and from the yard a faint sharpness of fish and river-mud, for the Torridge was near and always speaking. The very sound within was different from Grenville’s great rooms: not the hollow reverberation of stone and distance, but the snug clatter of domestic life—platters laid down, trenchers scraped, a fire that sighed and snapped as it ate its own fuel. It was a kinder music. Don Guzman, who had lived amid camps and galleys and courts, noticed it without meaning to; the ear takes its own nourishment.

Salterne set good sack before him, amber in the cup, fragrant as baked apples and summer dust. But the Don drank sparingly—more water than wine—abstemious as a monk where it served him, for drink loosens more than tongues. Then Salterne tried a second net: questions, prudent and well-cooked as any dish. Peru, the Spanish Main, the Moluccas, China—every far shore that an English merchant’s mind, newly kindled under Elizabeth’s sun, could hunger toward.

Don Guzman parried as a fox runs—quick, silent, never showing his belly. His answers gave sound and wind, with little substance.

In the midst of that fencing, Mistress Rose Salterne came in—the Rose of Torridge, so called by those who watched her as men watch a bright thing in water. She heard what they spoke of and, artless enough, began to ask her own questions, not as a merchant would, for profit, but as a girl with imagination and a heart that leaned forward to any far-off wonder. To her Don Guzman could not help answering. It is easier to refuse a hook than a hand held out with honest curiosity.

So the evening ran on, pleasant as a long meal where each course comes when appetite is ripe for it. The Don spoke of lands that smelled of sugar and smoke, of cities where the air was heavy with orange blossom and incense, of seas whose night was alive with strange lights. He told it so that Rose heard not only words but climates: she could almost taste the salt on a wind that had never blown in Devon.

And because the human heart is a kitchen that will breed desire out of idleness and good feeding, Don Guzman—idle as captives must be, and full of Sir Richard’s bread and beef—began, for mere sport at first, to look about him for some one to love.

Lady Grenville, being nearest, came first into his thoughts; and he put her away again with a self-denial he admired in himself. Sir Richard was his host. Besides, danger is sweetest when it is forbidden; and that sweetness he denied himself with a flourish, like a man pushing back a dish he has already tasted.

So, having banished that peril, he let another into the vacant place: Rose Salterne, with her innocent freshness, her fondness for admiration that yet did not smell of craft. “The train of the peacock,” he said to himself, “and the heart of the dove.” The mingling of vanity and virtue, bright feathers and soft breast, charmed him. And then a panic of prudence seized him—if he could persuade her to love none but him, might he not become fool enough to love none but her? He resolved to keep out of her way.

But days are long in captivity. And Lady Grenville, when she was at home, was dull enough to talk of nothing but her husband; and when she rode to Stow she left the Don alone in one corner of the great house, with silence thick as cold broth. What was he to do but lounge down to the butt-gardens, show off his black cloak and feather, watch the shooting, play rackets with the younger men, bowls with the elder, and let himself be invited—again and again—to the Mayor’s table?

There he ruled the roast, as he loved to do, not only by rank but by the power of having something to say. For these were the day-dawn hours of English commerce. In Bideford parlors and quay-side warehouses men were dreaming aloud—projects of voyages and companies, patents and settlements, all the new world’s edges gleaming like a knife-blade. The talk ran on Turkey merchants and Levant privileges, on voyages to Guinea for musk and civet, gold and grain, on new doors opening where old powers fell. Even the household sounds took their rhythm from it: the scratching of a quill, the creak of a bale-hoist, the ring of a cooper’s hammer. The town’s very air had that restless brine-and-woodsmoke odor that clings to places where men are always fitting out for elsewhere.

Don Guzman, listening and measuring, saw what his countrymen saw: that these islanders were not merely traders counting money by candlelight. They hated fighting and loved profit, yet could fight, when pressed, with a dogged terror that had teeth in it. Their ships went armed, their crews trained from childhood to use steel and shot, and their consciences, once persuaded by policy and Scripture that they were right, did not easily soften.

One evening, stung by their boasts, he waxed hot. He had hinted, civilly enough, that if Englishmen came where they had no right they might be sent back again. The answer came like a volley across a deck:

“We’ll see that, sir.”

“Depends on who says ‘No right.’”

“You found might right when you claimed the Indian seas,” said another. “We may find right might when we try them.”

“Try them then, gentlemen,” he snapped, “and find the sacred flag of Spain as invincible as ever was the Roman eagle.”

“We have, sir. Did you ever hear of Francis Drake?”

“Or of George Fenner at the Azores, one against seven?”

“Or John Hawkins at St. Juan d’Ulloa?”

“Insolent burghers!” Don Guzman cried, and rose to go.

Old Salterne rose too, not reddening like the younger men, but steady as a man accustomed to bargaining. “Sir,” he said, “we are plain men and have forgotten ourselves somewhat. Put it down to my wine. Insolent we never meant to be—least of all to a noble gentleman and a foreigner.”

But the Don would not be pacified. He walked out into the night air, calling himself ass and blinkard for having lowered himself to such company, conveniently forgetting that he had baited the quarrel.

Next day Salterne came, prompted by Mammon and by curiosity, and begged pardon again; promised that none of the rude speakers should be asked to meet him henceforth, if he would honor the house once more.

Don Guzman allowed himself to be appeased, and went the next evening, sneering inwardly as he went.

“Fool that I am,” he thought, “that girl has bewitched me. Go I must, and eat my share of dirt, for her sake.”

He went, and with cunning offered Salterne a sweet morsel: that he had taken such a fancy to him, and felt bound by his courtesy, that he might tell him things he would not mention to every man. Spaniards, he hinted, were jealous not of single traders, but of any general attempt to rob them of their hard-earned wealth. Yet for one man here and there, in the meantime, there were opportunities—oh, plentiful—if he would be wise and quiet.

Salterne, shrewd as he was, had his weak point: the hunger to know how the Spanish monopoly was fenced, and where a hand might slip in for profit. The Don touched it, and felt the line quiver.

And so—so blind does money make men—the Mayor began to set Rose herself to draw out the Spaniard, never fearing that she might be drawn in. For he held it impossible that she would ever marry a Popish Spaniard, as impossible as marrying the man in the moon; and equally impossible that Don Guzman would ever marry a burgher’s daughter, any more than he would marry a negress. Religion on one side, pride of blood on the other—two walls, Salterne thought, high as a fortress.

And as for love without marriage—if the thought ever crossed him, it died in laughter. He would have staked every farthing on Rose’s virtue, and gave the Don credit, too, for a certain human fondness for keeping his throat and the parts adjoining in one piece.

Thus, for weeks and months, the merchant’s parlor became the Don’s favorite haunt. He saw the Rose of Torridge daily, and the Rose of Torridge heard him. And she—poor child—had never seen such a man. He carried himself with a high-bred grace that reminded her of Frank Leigh, and yet there was a manlier mould beneath it. He had enough proud self-assertion to make a woman feel him superior, and yet tact enough to keep it from running into the bragging for which Spaniards were so often and so justly blamed. He had marvels to tell by flood and field as many as Amyas and more, and told them with a fluent, glowing eloquence that simple, modest Amyas did not possess. Besides, he was there—present as the smell of warm bread—while the Leighs were absent.

So she amused herself with the only person that came to hand; and in those days, with young gallants gone to wars in the Netherlands and Ireland, many a lady did the same. Don Guzman grew welcome at houses for miles round, and took their welcomes freely. Fair young dames gave him attention enough to turn a wiser head; and Rose might have escaped, had not Sir Richard delicately let him know that though English ladies were freer in manner than Spanish, brothers were as jealous, and women’s honor at least as unstormable, as in the land of duennas.

Don Guzman took the hint. He kept on good terms with the country gentlemen as with their daughters; and, to tell truth, found profit in seeming intimate with many ladies, that Salterne might never fancy he had any peculiar predilection for Mistress Rose.

Yet Salterne’s parlor lay nearest, and remained his commonest road. There he would discourse for hours—of deserts and cannibals, of men whose heads grew beneath their shoulders—till Rose’s fancy ran like a child turned loose in a meadow. And he seasoned those tales with scraps of mercantile knowledge, little savory morsels that kept Salterne hungry, as salt keeps a man thirsty: just enough to make it worth his while to invite the Spaniard again.

And certainly the stories were worth hearing. He had been everywhere, or seemed so. Born in Peru, sent home to Spain at ten; brought up in Italy; soldier in the Levant; adventurer to the East Indies; again in America—first the islands, then Mexico. Back to Spain, then Rome, then Ireland. Shipwrecked; captive among savages; peering down volcano-craters; hanging about courts; fighting Turks, Indians, lions, elephants, alligators, and whatever else the world could throw at a man. At five-and-thirty he had seen enough for three lives, and knew how to plate his experience so that others would feast on it.

He had been in the horrors of Famagusta; had escaped he hardly knew how from the victorious Turks, from flaying and impalement, from the likelihood of ending as a Janissary. He had been at the Battle of the Three Kings; had seen Stukely borne down by lances, unconquered even in death; had held on his knee the head of the dying King of Portugal.

And one evening—when the fire burned low and the house had sunk to that late hush where only the clock and the river can be heard—he spoke to Rose, not of wonders, but of himself.

“What have I left,” he said, “but a heart trampled as hard as the pavement? Whom have I to love? Who loves me? I have nothing to live for but fame—and even that is denied me here, a prisoner in a foreign land.”

“Have you no kindred?” Rose asked, pity softening her voice.

“My two sisters are in a convent,” he answered. “They had neither money nor beauty; so they are dead to me. My brother is a Jesuit, so he is dead to me. My father fell by Indian hands in Mexico. My mother—penniless—carries fans and lapdogs for some princess in Seville, no better born than herself. And I—devil!—I have lost even my sword. So fares the house of De Soto.”

He meant to be pitied, and he was. Rose’s heart, that had never been trained to distrust a sorrow spoken well, went out to him as naturally as a hand goes toward a child’s burn.

Then, with the ease of a man who knows his audience, he would turn the talk. When Rose was present, he told Italian tales that were pathetic—lovers parted, women wronged, tears like pearls; and Rose wept over sorrows of Juliet and Desdemona and many another tale before ever England saw them on a stage. When Rose was absent, he told racy stories to the Bideford worthies, till their ribs shook with laughter; but those I will not repeat.

So the months went on. What need of more words? Before a year was out, Rose Salterne was far more in love with Don Guzman than he with her. Both suspected each other’s mind, yet neither hinted at the truth—she from fear, he from sheer Spanish pride of blood.

For he began to perceive a hard choice: if he meant to profit by this passion, he must either compromise his noble blood by marrying a heretic burgher’s daughter, or see all his labor thrown away.

He had found, with astonishment and then with pleasure, that old English custom of saluting ladies on the cheek at meeting—a thing which Romish visitors, with coarser imaginations, slandered. He had seen, too, more than one red-faced burgher, smelling of onions, press his lips to Rose’s velvet cheek in that common fashion; and jealousy, sharp as vinegar, had risen in the Don, though he would have bitten his tongue out sooner than confess it.

So one day he offered his own salute.

He chose his moment when she was alone. Something—perhaps conscience, perhaps policy—warned him against doing it under her father’s eye.

To his astonishment she drew back, prompt and quiet. “No, sir. You should know my cheek is not for you.”

“Why,” he said, anger choked down but not swallowed, “it seems free enough to every counter-jumper in the town!”

Was it love, or innocence, that made her answer apologetically? “True, Don Guzman; but they are my equals.”

“And I?”

“You are a nobleman, sir, and should recollect that you are one.”

He forced a sneer. “It is a strange taste to prefer the shopkeeper.”

“Prefer?” She tried to laugh, and the laugh sounded thin. “It is a mere form among us. They are nothing to me, I can tell you.”

“And I then—less than nothing?”

Rose reddened to the roots of her hair, yet held her ground. “And why should you be anything to me? You have condescended too much already, sir, in giving us many a—many a pleasant evening. You must condescend no further. You wrong yourself, and me too. No, sir; not a step nearer! I will not. A salute between equals means nothing; but between you and me—if you do not leave me this moment, I will complain to my father.”

“Do so, madam!” he cried. “I care as little for your father’s anger as you for my misery.”

“Cruel!” Rose said, trembling from head to foot.

“I love you, madam!” Don Guzman flung himself at her feet as if shot through the heart. “I adore you! Never speak to me of difference of rank. I have forgotten it—forgotten all but love, all but you. You see where my pride is gone. I plead as suppliant, beggar—though I may be one day a prince, a king! Ay, a very Lucifer of pride to all save you; to you a wretch who grovels and cries, ‘Have mercy on me—on my loneliness, my homelessness, my friendlessness.’ Ah, Rose—madam, forgive the madness of my passion—you know not the heart you break. Cold Northerns, you little dream how a Spaniard can love. Love? Worship, rather—as I worship you; as I bless the captivity that brought me sight of you, and the ruin that first made me rich. Saints and Virgin! do my own tears deceive me, or are there tears too in those radiant orbs?”

Rose recovered herself in a sudden spasm of fear, as if she had heard a door unlatch in the dark. “Go, sir,” she cried, “and let me never see you more!” And as a last chance for life—so it seemed to her—she darted out of the room.

“Your slave obeys you, madam, and kisses your hands and feet for ever and a day,” said the Spaniard; and drawing himself up, he walked serenely out of the house.

Upstairs, behind a window, Rose—poor fool—peeped after him. Her heart sank as she watched his jaunty, careless air, as if all that rapture had been only a practiced speech spoken for sport.

How much of his rhapsody was honest and how much premeditated, who can say? She began to fancy it was all set and studied when she found that he had taken her at her word and set foot no more within her father’s door.

Then she reproached herself as the cruelest of women; resolved that if he died she would be his murderess; watched at the window for him to pass and hoped—oh, foolish hope—that he might look up; and the instant he turned the corner she hid herself again, burning with terror at being seen.

So it went, and so it goes. One love-making is much like another, I suppose, since that first blessed marriage in Paradise, where love came ready-made from Heaven and needed no speeches at all.

And truly it is like fiddling while Rome burns to spend many more pages upon the sorrows of poor little Rose Salterne, while the destinies of Europe hang upon the marriage-talk between Elizabeth and Anjou; while Sir Humphrey Gilbert stirs heaven and earth—and Devon, of course, as the most important portion of that earth—to quicken his dormant patent, which will give to England, in due time, Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Canada, and the Northern States; and to Humphrey Gilbert himself something better than a new world—another world, and a crown of glory that does not fade.
