After a spirited and steadfast refusal on Mrs. Ferrars's part—a resistance fierce enough to shield her from the dreaded accusation of being "too amiable"—Edward was finally granted an audience and reinstated as her son. Of late, the Ferrars household had undergone a series of dramatic fluctuations. For many years, Mrs. Ferrars had boasted of two sons; yet the recent social death of Edward had deprived her of one, and the earlier demise of Robert had left her bereft of both for a fortnight. Now, with Edward miraculously resurrected, she possessed a son once more.

Despite this restoration, Edward did not feel his existence truly secure until he disclosed his current engagement. He feared that revealing his intention to marry Miss Dashwood might shock his constitution so violently that he would vanish as quickly as he had returned. Thus, with cautious trepidation, he unveiled the news, only to find Mrs. Ferrars listening with an unexpected calm. Initially, she attempted to dissuade him, deploying every argument at her disposal. She urged him toward Miss Morton, describing her as a lady of superior rank and substantial fortune. She emphasized that Miss Morton was the daughter of a nobleman holding thirty thousand pounds, whereas Miss Dashwood was merely the offspring of a private gentleman possessing a mere three thousand. However, upon realizing that Edward, while acknowledging the factual disparity, showed no inclination to be swayed by it, she deemed further opposition futile. Drawing wisdom from past experiences, she chose submission. Following a delay calculated to preserve her own dignity and avoid appearing overly eager, she formally decreed her consent to the union of Edward and Elinor.

The next matter to address was the augmentation of their income. Here, the hierarchy of the family became clear: although Edward was now her sole surviving son, he was not her eldest. While Robert was destined for a yearly allowance of one thousand pounds, no objection was raised against Edward accepting holy orders for a modest stipend of two hundred and fifty pounds. Beyond the ten thousand pounds already gifted with Fanny, no further promises were made, present or future. Yet, this sum proved ample for Edward and Elinor's desires, exceeding their expectations. Even Mrs. Ferrars, through her evasive excuses, appeared the only one surprised that she offered no more.

With a sufficient income secured pending Edward's installation in his living, the couple awaited only the completion of the parsonage at Barton. Colonel Brandon, eager to accommodate Elinor, was diligently overseeing significant renovations. After enduring the customary delays and disappointments typical of such projects, Elinor, breaking her firm resolve to postpone marriage until everything was perfect, proceeded with the wedding in Barton Church early in the autumn.

Their first month of wedded bliss was spent at the Mansion-House with their friend, allowing them to supervise the parsonage's progress and design its layout—from selecting wallpaper to planning shrubberies and fountains. By Michaelmas, Mrs. Jennings visited the newlyweds in their parsonage, declaring, with genuine belief, that they were one of the happiest couples in the world. Indeed, they lacked for nothing save the marriage of Colonel Brandon and Marianne, and perhaps slightly better grazing for their cows.

They were showered with visits from nearly all their relations and friends. Mrs. Ferrars arrived to inspect the happiness she had reluctantly authorized, and even the Dashwoods undertook a journey from Sussex to pay their respects. As Mr. John Dashwood walked with his sister-in-law near Delaford House, he remarked, "I shall not claim to be disappointed, my dear sister—that would be saying too much, for you have undoubtedly been one of the most fortunate young women in the world. Nevertheless, I confess it would bring me great pleasure to call Colonel Brandon my brother. His estate, his residence, and his grounds are in such impeccable condition! His woods alone surpass anything I have seen in Dorsetshire. Though Marianne may not appear the ideal match for him, I believe it would be wise to invite them to stay frequently. Since Colonel Brandon seems so comfortable here, who knows what might happen? You can easily highlight her virtues, and so on. Simply put, you should give her a chance; you understand."

Mrs. Ferrars did visit, treating them with a facade of decent affection, yet she never bestowed upon them the genuine favor and preference reserved for others. Such treatment was the exclusive domain of Robert and his wife, whose fortunes rose swiftly. Lucy's sharp self-interest, which initially drew Robert into trouble, ultimately facilitated his escape from it. Her humble demeanor, attentive service, and ceaseless flattery, once given the slightest opportunity, won back Mrs. Ferrars's heart and fully restored Robert to her grace. Consequently, Lucy's entire conduct during the affair, culminating in its success, stands as a testament to how relentless pursuit of self-interest, despite apparent obstacles, secures every material advantage, requiring only the sacrifices of time and conscience.

When Robert first sought Lucy's acquaintance in Bartlett's Buildings, his motive, as perceived by his brother, was merely to persuade her to break her engagement. Expecting that a few conversations would suffice to overcome their mutual affection, he miscalculated. Lucy, however, played along, offering hope that his eloquence would eventually win her over. Each subsequent meeting required additional persuasion to dispel lingering doubts. This prolonged courtship secured his attendance, and the rest followed naturally. Their conversations shifted from Edward to Robert, a topic on which he spoke endlessly and in which she displayed equal enthusiasm. Soon, it became evident that he had entirely supplanted his brother. He boasted of his triumph over Edward, of deceiving him, and of marrying privately without maternal consent.

What ensued is well known. They enjoyed several months of great happiness at Dawlish, where she entertained numerous relatives and acquaintances, while he drafted plans for magnificent cottages. Upon returning to London, they secured Mrs. Ferrars's forgiveness simply by asking for it—an act initiated by Lucy. Initially, this pardon extended only to Robert. Lucy, owing no filial duty to her mother and thus committing no breach thereof, remained unpardoned for some weeks. However, her persistent humility, self-reproach regarding Robert's offense, and feigned gratitude for her mother's harshness eventually won her the haughty attention that softened into affection and influence. Lucy became indispensable to Mrs. Ferrars, ranking alongside Robert and Fanny. While Edward was never warmly forgiven for his past intentions, and Elinor, despite her superior fortune and birth, was often dismissed as an interloper, Lucy was regarded as a favorite child in all things.

Settling in London, they received generous aid from Mrs. Ferrars and maintained excellent relations with the Dashwoods. Aside from the petty jealousies between Fanny and Lucy (which their husbands naturally exacerbated) and occasional domestic squabbles between Robert and Lucy, their lives were marked by remarkable harmony. How Edward forfeited his rights as the eldest son, and how Robert succeeded to them, remains a puzzle; yet the arrangement was justified by its results. Robert showed no sign of regretting his wealth or feeling guilty about his brother's reduced share. Similarly, Edward, judging by his diligent performance of duties, deepening love for his wife and home, and cheerful disposition, appeared thoroughly content with his lot, harboring no desire for exchange.

Elinor's marriage kept her closely connected to her family without rendering the cottage at Barton obsolete, as her mother and sisters spent much of their time there. Mrs. Dashwood acted on both principle and pleasure in visiting Delaford so frequently; her desire to unite Marianne and Colonel Brandon was even more fervent than John's had been. It became her cherished goal. Though precious to her was the company of her daughter, she yearned to see Marianne settled at the mansion-house, a wish shared equally by Edward and Elinor. They understood each other's sorrows and obligations, and by universal agreement, Marianne was to be the reward for all their efforts.

Faced with such a united front, coupled with intimate knowledge of Colonel Brandon's goodness and a realization of his profound, albeit belated, attachment to herself, what choice did Marianne have? Marianne Dashwood was destined for an extraordinary fate. She was born to disprove her own beliefs and contradict her most cherished principles. Born to overcome an affection formed late in life—at seventeen, based solely on strong esteem and lively friendship—and voluntarily offer her hand to another! Moreover, this other man was one who had suffered deeply from a previous attachment, whom she had deemed too old to marry just two years prior, and who still clung to the comforting habit of wearing a flannel waistcoat.

Yet, so it happened. Instead of succumbing to an irresistible passion, or retiring forever to her mother's side to find joy in solitude and study—as she had later resolved in calmer moments—she found herself at nineteen submitting to new bonds, embracing new responsibilities, establishing a new home, becoming a wife, mistress of a family, and patroness of a village.

Colonel Brandon was now as happy as those who loved him best believed he deserved. In Marianne, he found solace for every past sorrow; her regard and companionship revived his spirit and brought cheerfulness back to his life. Equally, Marianne discovered her own happiness in contributing to his. Every observing friend agreed. Marianne could never love partially; in time, her whole heart became as devoted to her husband as it once had been to Willoughby.
