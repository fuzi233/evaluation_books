To comprehend the architecture of this evening's betrayal, one must first understand the absolute isolation of the journey that preceded it. Harry Feversham had fled the chaos of Dublin by the night mail, having shared a carriage with Durrance in the city's rowdy thoroughfares. By the following forenoon, he stood upon the quay of Ramelton, crossing Lough Swilly on a small cargo steamer that plied the Lennon River only once a week. Waiting for him was Ethne, poised in her dog-cart. She extended a hand and offered a smile—not merely the polite courtesy of a hostess, but the warm, defiant grin of a comrade-in-arms.

"You are surprised to see me," she noted, reading the shock etched across his features.

"I always am," he replied, the words tumbling out with a strange intensity. "For always you exceed my thoughts of you." In that instant, the smile transformed upon her face; it shed its casual veneer and hardened into something profound, a recognition of shared burden that transcended simple friendship.

"I shall drive slowly," she announced as the luggage was secured. "I brought no groom on purpose. Guests arrive tomorrow; we possess only today."

She navigated the wide causeway alongside the river, turning up the steep, narrow street where the town slept in a drowsy quiet. Feversham sat silently beside her, his gaze sweeping the landscape with a mixture of awe and a peculiar sense of ownership. This was his first visit to Ramelton, yet everything here—the dark thicket of tall trees climbing the far bank, the old grey bridge spanning the water, the song of the current over shallow stones—felt intimately connected to her. These were not just sights; they were fragments of her life, and by extension, his.

At twenty-one, Ethne was a figure of striking physical integrity. Tall, strong, and supple, she possessed shoulders broad enough to command respect, lacking the exaggerated sloping elegance favored by older generations yet radiating a natural, unforced grace. Her movement was light-footed as a deer. Her hair, a rich dark brown, was coiled tightly at the nape of her neck, framing cheeks flushed with vitality and eyes of clear grey that met others with an engaging, unguarded frankness. Her character mirrored this physical honesty: a straightforward simplicity born of strength, encompassing gentleness while excluding violence.

Of her courage, a legend persists in Ramelton—a tale Feversham recalled with a thrill of wonder. Years prior, on that very steep hill descending to the river, her horse had bolted at the clatter of a passing pail. The reins, loose in her lap, had fallen to the earth just as the animal surged forward, racing toward the sharp curve of the bridge. Seated helpless in the cart, facing imminent disaster, Ethne had acted with chilling calm. She had vaulted over the front of the moving vehicle, balanced precariously along the shafts, snatched the reins from the horse's neck, and brought the beast to a halt mere yards from the precipice. Yet, even in this display of heroism, flaws existed, subtle cracks in the armor that Feversham had not yet perceived.

During the initial stretch of the drive, Ethne matched his silence, her mind seemingly elsewhere, preoccupied with matters of greater import. It was only after leaving the town and ascending the straight, undulating road toward Letterkenny that she turned to him, her voice cutting through the quiet.

"I saw this morning that your regiment was ordered from India to Egypt," she stated, her tone devoid of drama, stating a fact as plain as the horizon. "You could have gone with it, had I not come in your way. There would have been chances of distinction. I have hindered you, and I am very sorry. Of course, you could not know of the possibility, but I can understand how hard it is to be left behind. I blame myself."

Feversham stared ahead, the engine's hum filling the space between them. Then, his voice emerged, suddenly rough and hoarse.

"You need not."

"How can I help it?" she pressed, her self-reproach deepening. "I blame myself the more because I do not see things quite like other women. Supposing you had gone to Egypt, and the worst had happened... I should have felt very lonely, of course, all my days. But I should have known quite surely that when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another."

She spoke without lowering her voice, in the steady, level cadence of one reciting a fundamental truth. Feversham caught his breath, a sharp intake akin to physical pain. Yet the girl's eyes remained fixed on his face, and he sat motionless, his brow unfurrowed, unable to trust himself to speak. He kept his lips sealed, and Ethne continued, her logic relentless.

"You see, I can endure the absence of the people I care about better than most. I do not feel that I have lost them," she paused, searching for the right phrasing, her thought difficult to articulate. "You know how things happen. One moves along in a dull sort of way, and suddenly a face springs out from the crowd of acquaintances. You recognize it at once—for the face of a friend, or rather, you realize it, though you have never truly seen it before. It is as though you have encountered someone long sought and now gladly recovered. Such friends are few, no doubt, but only the few truly count. Such friends one does not lose, whether they are absent, or even..."

"Unless," Feversham interjected slowly, "one has made a mistake. Suppose the face in the crowd is a mask? What then? One may make mistakes."

Ethne shook her head decisively.

"Not of that kind. One may seem to have made mistakes, perhaps for a long while. But in the end, one would be proved not to have made them."

Her implicit faith seized the man, torturing him with its certainty until silence became impossible.

"Ethne," he cried out, "you don't know—"

But at that precise moment, Ethne reined in her horse, laughed, and pointed her whip toward the roadside.

They had crested a hill a couple of miles from Ramelton. The road wound between stone walls enclosing open fields on the left and a dense wood of oaks and beeches on the right. Built into the left-hand wall was a scarlet letter-box, the object of her gesture.

"I wanted to show you that," she interrupted, seizing the moment to deflect his confession. "It was there I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times." Thus, Feversham let slip his opportunity.

"The house lies behind the trees to the right," she continued.

"The letter-box is very convenient," Feversham remarked, his voice hollow.

"Yes," Ethne agreed, driving on until the park wall crumbled into ruins. "That is where I used to climb over to post the letters. There is a tree on the other side of the wall, equally convenient. I used to run down the half-mile of avenue at night."

"There might have been thieves," Feversham exclaimed, the absurdity of the danger momentarily registering.

"There were thorns," Ethne corrected simply. Turning through the gates, she drove up to the porch of the long, irregular grey house. "Well, we still have a day before the dance."

"I suppose the whole countryside is coming," Feversham observed.

"It daren't do anything else," Ethne laughed. "My father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away, just as he fetched your friend Mr. Durrance here. By the way, Mr. Durrance has sent me a present—a Guarnerius violin."

The door swung open, revealing a thin, lank old man with a fierce, peaked face resembling a bird of prey. He stepped onto the stairs, but his expression softened instantly into friendliness upon seeing Feversham, a smile playing upon his lips. A stranger might have mistaken it for a wink, though his left eyelid perpetually drooped over the eye.

"How do you do?" he boomed. "Glad to see you. Make yourself at home. If you require whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand," and with that, he retreated inside.

The biographer of Dermod Eustace would require a wary mind to undertake his task. Though the old master of Lennon House had not lain in his grave for twenty years, he had already swollen into a legendary caricature. Anecdotes had accreted upon his memory like barnacles, and any local with a knack for invention could foist tales upon Dermod to ensure immediate credence. Nevertheless, definite facts remained. He practiced an ancient, tyrannous hospitality, keeping his doors open upon the road to Letterkenny and forcing bed and board upon strangers, as Durrance had once discovered. He was a man of another century, gazing upon a topsy-turvy world with a glowering, angry eye, finding reconciliation only after copious amounts of alcohol. He was a sort of intoxicated Coriolanus, believing the populace should be shepherded with a stick, yet retaining a surprising regard for manners, even toward the lowest women.
