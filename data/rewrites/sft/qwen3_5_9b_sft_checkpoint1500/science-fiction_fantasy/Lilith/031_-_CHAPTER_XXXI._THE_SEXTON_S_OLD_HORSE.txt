The last shimmer of the white leopardess faded, and I turned to follow my guide, though reluctance coiled in my gut. Why should I surrender to sleep when the hour demanded the living? Reason, surely, was universal; what logic dictated lying down beside the dead when the world called for the awake? And who would wake me? If the sleepers in that house let morning bleed into noon, and noon dissolve into night without stirring, how could I trust to rise again? Yet I murmured a protest and followed, for I knew no other path.

The librarian walked in silence, and I trailed behind, equally mute. Time and space slipped past us like water. Sunset deepened into twilight, and a chill crept through the air—the spreading cold of the Chamber of Death. My heart sank with the shadows, and soon I lost sight of the lean, long-coated figure, hearing nothing but the swish of his stride through the heather. Then, above, the slow flap of a raven's wings cut the silence. Now a firefly, now a glowing butterfly rose into the rayless void, marking intervals in the darkening hour.

Slowly, the moon crested the far horizon.

"You are tired, are you not, Mr. Vane?" the raven asked, landing on a stone. "You must make acquaintance with the horse that will carry you in the morning!"

A strange whistle tore from his long black beak. A spot bloomed on the face of the half-risen moon. To my ears came the drumming of swift, soft-galloping hoofs, and in a heartbeat, out of the lunar disc itself, thundered the terrible beast. His mane flowed behind him like the crest of a storm-tossed wave, torn seaward in hoary spray, while the lash of his tail blinded the eye of the moon. Nineteen hands high, huge-boned, tight-skinned, and hard-muscled—he was a steed the Holy Death Himself might choose to ride abroad and slay! The moon seemed to regard him with awe; in her stark light, he looked a very skeleton, loosely roped together. Yet, terrifyingly large, he moved with the lightness of a winged insect. As he drew near, his speed slackened, and his mane and tail settled around him like drifting smoke.

Now, I was not merely a lover of horses; I adored every soul that wore four legs. I had never spent coin on anything but horses, and I had never sold one. The sight of this monstrous beauty, terrible to behold, ignited a raw longing within me. It was pure greed, yes—rank covetousness, an evil thing in all worlds. I did not mean I would steal him, but regardless of his proper place, I would buy him if I could. I laid my hands on him, stroking the protuberant bones that humped a hide smooth and thin, shiny as satin. So shiny was it that the very shape of the moon reflected back from his flank. I fondled his sharp-pointed ears, whispered secrets into them, and breathed into his red nostrils the warm breath of a man's life. In return, he breathed into me the fierce, primal breath of a horse's life, and we loved one another. What eyes he had! Blue-filmy, like the gaze of the dead, yet behind each eye burned a glowing coal. The raven, wings half-extended, watched my courtship with evident pleasure.

"That is well! Be friends with him," the bird said. "He will carry you all the better tomorrow! Now we must hurry home!"

My desire to ride had grown into a passion.

"May I not mount him at once, Mr. Raven?" I cried.

"By all means!" he answered. "Mount, and ride him home."

The horse bent his head over my shoulder, affectionate. I twisted my hands into his mane and scrambled onto his back, aided by certain protruding spines.

"He would outspeed any leopard in creation!" I shouted.

"Not that way at night," the raven replied. "The road is difficult.—But come; loss now will be gain then! To wait is harder than to run, and its reward is fuller. Go on, my son—straight to the cottage. I shall be there as soon as you. It will rejoice my wife's heart to see her son upon that horse!"

I sat silent. The horse stood like a block of marble.

"Why do you linger?" the raven asked.

"I long so much to ride after the leopardess," I answered, "that I can scarce restrain myself!"

"You have promised!"

"My debt to the Little Ones appears, I confess, a greater thing than my bond to you."

"Yield to the temptation, and you will bring mischief upon them—and upon yourself also."

"What matters it for me? I love them; and love works no evil. I will go."

Truth be told, I had forgotten the children, intoxicated by the horse.

Eyes flashed through the darkness, and I knew Adam stood in his true form beside me. By his voice, I sensed an indignation barely held back.

"Mr. Vane," Adam said, "do you not know why you have not yet done anything worth doing?"

"Because I have been a fool," I answered.

"Wherein?"

"In everything."

"Which do you count your most indiscreet action?"

"Bringing the princess to life: I ought to have left her to her just fate."

"Nay, now you talk foolishly! You could not have done otherwise than you did, not knowing she was evil!—But you never brought anyone to life! How could you, yourself dead?"

"I dead?" I cried.

"Yes," he answered. "And you will remain dead so long as you refuse to die."

"Back to the old riddling!" I returned scornfully.

"Be persuaded, and go home with me," he continued gently. "The most—nearly the only foolish thing you ever did, was to run from our dead."

I pressed the horse's ribs, and he burst forward like a sudden wind. I gave him a pat on the side of his neck, and he wound about in a sharp-driven curve, close to the ground like a cat wheeling after a mouse, leaning sideways until his mane swept the tops of the heather.

Through the dark I heard the wings of the raven. Five quick flaps, and he perched on the horse's head. The horse checked instantly, plowing up the earth with his feet.

"Mr. Vane," the raven croaked, "think what you are doing! Twice already has evil befallen you—once from fear, and once from heedlessness: breach of word is far worse; it is a crime."

"The Little Ones are in frightful peril, and I brought it upon them!" I cried. "—But indeed I will not break my word to you. I will return, and spend in your house what nights—or what days—or what years you please."

"I tell you once more you will do them other than good if you go tonight," the bird insisted.

But a false sense of power, unrooted and merely vibrated into me from the sheer strength of the horse, had rendered me too stupid to listen.

"Would you take from me my last chance of reparation?" I cried. "This time there shall be no shirking! It is my duty, and I will go—even if I perish for it!"

"Go, then, foolish boy!" he returned, anger thick in his croak. "Take the horse, and ride to failure! May it be to humility!"

He spread his wings and took flight. Again, I pressed the lean ribs beneath me.

"After the spotted leopardess!" I whispered in his ear.

He turned his head this way and that, snuffing the air, then started, walking slowly and undecidedly. Suddenly, he quickened his pace, breaking into a trot, then a gallop. In moments, his speed became tremendous. He seemed to see in the dark; he never stumbled, never faltered, never hesitated. I sat as upon the ridge of a wave, feeling the play of every individual muscle beneath me. His joints were so elastic, his movements so fluid, that not once did he jar me. His growing swiftness bore him along until he flew rather than ran. The wind met us and passed like a tornado.

Across the evil hollow we sped like a bolt from an arbalest. No monster lifted its neck; all knew the hooves thundering overhead! We rushed up the hills, shot down their far slopes, and from the rocky chasms of the river-bed he did not swerve, holding on with a fierce, terrible gallop. The moon, halfway up the heavens, gazed with a solemn trouble in her pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and in the pride of my life, I sat like a king.

We were near the middle of the many channels, my horse leaping clear of one, sometimes two, gathering himself for a great bound whenever the moon reached the keystone of her arch. Then came a wonder and a terror: she began to descend, rolling like the nave of Fortune's wheel, bowled by the gods, spinning faster and faster. Like our own moon, this one had a human face, and now the broad forehead, now the chin was uppermost as she tumbled. I gazed in aghast horror.
