A dozen years had elapsed since I committed the remains of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, to the silent vigil within that strange mausoleum in Richmond's ancient graveyard. Often, the peculiar stipulations he had left regarding his interment haunted my thoughts—specifically the mandate that his casket remain open and the colossal mechanism controlling the vault's iron-bound doors be accessible *only* from within.

Twelve years since I first unrolled the remarkable manuscript of this enigmatic soul. A man who clung to no memory of infancy, who could not even approximate his own age, yet who stood eternally youthful. He had held my grandfather's great-grandfather upon his knee, a figure who had spent a decade wandering the alien landscapes of Mars, battling both its verdant inhabitants and their crimson foes. It was through these wars that he had claimed Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, weaving a destiny that saw him rise to become Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium, for nearly a decade.

Time had marched on, burying the truth of his return beneath the bluff overlooking the Hudson, where his body was discovered after twelve years. Yet, in those long, sun-drenched days, a shadow of doubt lingered. Was John Carter truly extinguished, or did he drift once more among the drowned seas of that failing world? Had he returned to Barsoom, perhaps just in time to unlock the grim portals of the atmospheric engine, saving millions choking on the thin air? Did he find the dark-haired princess and the slender son he had dreamed of waiting in the royal gardens? Or had his arrival been too late, condemning him to a living eternity on a dead stone?

Lost in such futile speculations one sweltering August evening, old Ben, my faithful servant, approached with a telegram. My hands trembled as I tore it open.

At dawn, I boarded the first train for Richmond. Within two hours, I was ushered into the chamber where John Carter waited. As I crossed the threshold, he rose, his face alight with the same warm, welcoming smile of yore. Time seemed to have ceased its hold upon him; he remained the straight, sinewy fighter of thirty. His keen grey eyes burned with undimmed fire, the only marks upon his visage being the deep furrows of iron will and resolve that had defined him since my earliest memories, nearly thirty-five years past.

"Well, nephew," he greeted me, "does the vision of a ghost haunt your spirit, or is this merely the lingering effect of Uncle Ben's juleps?"

"Juleps, I reckon," I stammered, feeling a sudden, overwhelming vitality. "Though perhaps it is simply the sight of you again. Have you returned to Mars? Speak, and tell me of Dejah Thoris. Did you find her whole, awaiting your embrace?"

"Yes," he replied, his voice heavy with emotion, "I have returned to Barsoom again. But the tale is vast, too grand to unfold in the fleeting moments I possess before I must depart. I have uncovered the secret, nephew. I may now stride the boundless vacuum at will, traversing the tracks between countless worlds as desire dictates. Yet, my heart remains tethered to Barsoom. While it rests within the keeping of my Martian Princess, I fear I shall never again forsake this dying world, for it is my life."

"I have come because my love for you compelled me to see you once more before you fade forever into that other existence which I, having died thrice and facing death anew tonight, cannot fully comprehend."

He paused, looking around the dim room. "Even the wise and mysterious Therns of Barsoom—that ancient cult believed to guard the secrets of life and death within their impregnable fastnesses on the slopes of the Mountains of Otz—are as ignorant as we. I have proven it, though I nearly paid with my life in the process. You shall read all in the notes I have compiled during the last three months of my return to Earth."

He gestured to a thick, swollen portfolio resting on the table beside him.

"I know you are curious, that you believe, and I know the world watches with bated breath. They will not believe for many ages, for humanity has not yet evolved to comprehend the truths I have written. Give them whatever you deem safe, whatever you wish to share, but do not take offense if they mock you."

That night, we walked together down to the cemetery. At the threshold of his vault, he stopped and pressed my hand firmly.

"Farewell, nephew," he whispered. "I may never see you again. I doubt I can ever bear to leave my wife and boy while they draw breath, for the span of life upon Barsoom often exceeds a millennium."

He stepped into the darkness. The massive door groaned shut, swinging slowly on its hinges. The ponderous bolts ground into place with a deafening scrape, then the lock clicked—a final, resonant seal. I have never seen Captain John Carter of Virginia again.

Yet, here lies the account of his return to Mars on that other occasion, pieced together from the immense trove of notes he left upon the table of his hotel room in Richmond. There is much I have omitted, much I dare not speak aloud. But you will find the chronicle of his second quest for Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, far more astonishing than the first manuscript I presented to an incredulous world some time ago, following the fighting Virginian across submerged ocean floors beneath the twin moons of Mars.

The chill air bit at my exposed skin as I stood atop the bluff overlooking my modest cottage on that crisp, clear night in early March, 1886. Below, the Hudson River flowed silently, a grey, spectral ribbon mimicking a dead vein. Yet, above me, piercing the cold sky, burned the red eye of Mars. For ten long, desolate years, I had pleaded with that god of war, imploring him with outstretched arms to tear me from this gray earth and return me to my lost love. Not since the fateful night in 1866, when I lay catatonic within the Arizona cave, had the magnetic pull of that distant god felt so violently irresistible.

My arms shot upward, fingers trembling as I prayed for the return of that ancient, cosmic power—the force that had twice dragged me through the void. Suddenly, a wave of nausea crashed over me. My vision swam, my knees buckled, and I collapsed onto the very edge of the dizzying drop. But instantly, the fog lifted. The horrific images of that ghostly Arizona cavern flooded back—the awful moans, the rustling shadows, the terrible entity that had threatened me from the dark recesses. My muscles, paralyzed by a strange anesthesia, refused to obey, just as they had before. Then, with a sharp, internal *click* like a snapping wire, I shattered the trance. I stood naked and free beside the lifeless husk of the beast that had briefly harbored John Carter's spirit.

I cast one final glance at Mars, lifted my hands to catch his lurid rays, and waited.

I did not wait long. Before I could blink, I shot forward into the terrifying void. There followed that same instant of unthinkable cold and absolute darkness I had known twenty years prior. Then, abruptly, I opened my eyes.

I was no longer on Earth. I lay beneath the searing glare of a hot sun, filtering through a tiny aperture in the canopy of a colossal forest. The scene before me defied all logic, sending a jolt of primal fear through my chest. Could I truly be lost in some alien quadrant of the cosmos, hurled to a star system unknown?

Yet, as I pushed myself upright, the reality began to settle. I lay upon a carpet of red, grass-like vegetation, surrounded by a grove of trees so bizarre and beautiful they seemed plucked from a dream. Huge, gorgeous blossoms clung to their branches, and the air hummed with the song of winged creatures whose shapes were utterly unearthly. This vegetation bore a resemblance to the flora of the great waterways on Mars, but the trees and birds were alien variations I had never encountered in a decade of residence there. Beyond the treeline, I caught a glimpse of something impossible: an open sea, its blue waters shimmering under the brazen sun.

As I attempted to rise, disaster struck again. The gravitational pull of this smaller world, coupled with the thin, rarefied atmosphere, offered negligible resistance to my Earth-bred musculature. With a mere exertion of standing, I launched myself several feet into the air, crashing face-first into the soft, brilliant grass.

Laughing at my clumsiness, I quickly readjusted. My mind worked swiftly, recalibrating my physical instincts to this new environment. As I began to walk down the gentle slope toward the sea, I observed the landscape with growing awe. The grass was manicured, carpet-like, and the trees, uniformly pruned to a height of roughly fifteen feet, created an illusion of a vast, high-ceilinged chamber. This level of systematic cultivation suggested I had landed within the realm of a civilized civilization. If I could locate them, my status as a Prince of House Tardos Mors would ensure me safety and honor.

The trees themselves were marvels of engineering and biology. Stems hundreds of feet in diameter soared upward, smooth and polished like fine pianos, colored in hues ranging from ebony black to azure, scarlet, and deepest purple. Their foliage was equally vibrant, adorned with blooms so complex they challenged the vocabulary of gods.

Approaching the tree line, I emerged into a broad meadow bordered by the sea and a majestic river flowing between scarlet banks. To my right, towering bluffs rose vertically from the water's edge. But it was not the grandeur of nature that arrested my attention. It was a score of figures moving slowly near the riverbank.

They were grotesque, manlike in silhouette yet utterly monstrous. Standing nearly twelve feet tall, their torsos and legs were proportioned like humanity's, but their arms were short, sinuous, and devoid of bone, moving with the fluid, snakelike undulation of tentacles.

One of the creatures approached, dragging its elongated, limb-less hands over the sward. Up close, the horror was visceral. Its body was a ghastly, hairless blue, save for a broad band of white encircling a single, protruding eye that was entirely dead-white, pupil and iris included. Where a nose should have been, there was only a ragged, inflamed circular hole resembling an unhealed bullet wound. Below this, the face was blank to the chin; it possessed no mouth.

Instead, atop its head sat a tangled mass of jet-black hair, eight or ten inches long, each strand the thickness of an angleworm. These hairs writhed and crawled independently, giving the impression of a second, sentient organism atop the creature's skull.

The lower extremities, while human-shaped, were monstrous in scale—three feet long, broad, and flat. And hanging from its armpits were two tiny replicas of itself, suspended by small stems connecting their heads to the parent's body. Some of these "babies" were barely unopened buds, others fully formed, drifting alongside the adults in a slow, rhythmic procession.

These were the Plant Men, the remnants of the original Tree of Life, a race that had devolved into a vegetative nightmare. As I watched, a strange shrieking wail pierced the air, emanating from the direction of the bluffs. Instantly, every hair on the heads of the herd stiffened, sensing the source. The creatures turned in unison, their movement shifting from a languid stroll to a frantic, bounding leap. They launched themselves twenty or thirty feet at a time, moving with the agility of kangaroos, heading straight for the cliffs.

Instinctively, I followed. Hurling caution aside, I sprinted across the meadow, my Earth-muscles propelling me with prodigious bounds that outpaced the herbivores. They were heading toward the base of the cliffs, where the river seemed to erupt from the earth.

Reaching the boulder-strewn plain, I crested a massive stone and witnessed the source of the commotion. A small party of Green Men of Barsoom stood trapped in a clearing. Here, amidst the ruins of a dying world, I saw the wild hordes in their native element. Towering green giants with gleaming white tusks, laterally placed eyes, and extra pairs of arms stood back-to-back with their female counterparts, weapons drawn. Two men and four women, their ornaments marking disparate tribes, they faced a common enemy.

Usually, the hordes of Barsoom were locked in eternal, bloody war with one another. Never before had I seen groups of different tribes united unless in a desperate defense of a specific ally, like the legendary gathering of Tars Tarkas to save Princess Dejah Thoris. Now, however, they stood united against the Plant Men.

The assault was swift and brutal. The lead Plant Man charged, leaped high into the air, and brought his massive tail down in a sweeping arc that crushed a Green Warrior's skull like an eggshell. The rest of the herd circled with bewildering speed, their screeching, purring mouths confusing the defenders. Two more Green Warriors fell to the deadly tails.

Only one warrior and two females remained. Just as the remaining Plant Men prepared to strike the final blow, the lone warrior, Tars Tarkas, swung his longsword with a ferocity born of desperation. He cleaved through one attacker from chin to groin, only to be struck down by the other, whose tail laid the two females low. Seeing his friends fall, Tars Tarkas charged the entire herd, hacking a path through them with a mad, whirlwind dance of steel.

He fled toward the forest, seeking refuge, but I was already moving. Driven by the bloodlust of the fighter, I leaped from my concealment, sword in hand, racing to intercept the beasts. I reached the fallen bodies of the slaughtered Greens first, snatching up a longsword from the carnage. A red mist swam before my eyes, the thrill of battle coursing through me.

Tars Tarkas was overtaken, backed against a boulder as the herd closed in. They did not notice my silent approach. In a heartbeat, I was upon them, driving my blade home. Four of the monsters fell before they realized I was there.

Then Tars Tarkas joined me. We fought back-to-back, a whirlwind of steel against the swarming, hairy horde. Great tails lashed at us, razor-talons slashed our limbs, and a green, sticky syrup—viscid lifeblood—oozed from severed arteries, coating us from head to toe. Once, a monster's weight pinned me to the ground as moist lips sucked at my bleeding throat, but Tars Tarkas, seeing my plight, tore away from his attackers and sliced the threat from my back with a single, masterful stroke.

There was no pause for mutual recollection as Tars Tarkas and I stood before the great boulder, encircled by the grisly remains of our monstrous foes. From every direction down the broad valley, a deluge of terrors poured forth, answering the unearthly summons of the strange figure perched high above.

Side by side, we tore across the scarlet grass, I gauging my stride to ensure I did not leave my slower companion behind. Some three hundred yards separated us from the cliffs, a distance that demanded a sanctuary capable of withstanding the assault of the terrifying things trailing in our wake.

They closed upon us swiftly. Tars Tarkas shouted for me to surge ahead and locate the refuge, if possible, before joining him. A prudent counsel; it bought us precious minutes. I unleashed every ounce of my terrestrial strength, leaping with boundless vigor until I planted myself at the cliff's base, leaving the gap between us and the stone barrier sealed.

The cliffs rose sheer and vertical from the valley floor, devoid of the usual rubble slopes that soften the ascent of natural ridges. Only scattered boulders, fallen from the heights above, marred the otherwise pristine turf. My initial survey filled me with dread; save for the distant silhouette of the weird herald shrieking atop the peak, I saw not a single foothold upon the lofty escarpment.

To my right, the cliff vanished into a dense, verdant forest that pressed its roots against the stone's stern flank, towering a thousand feet into the air. To the left, the rock face stretched unbroken across the valley's head, disappearing into the jagged outlines of the surrounding mountain range.

A thousand feet away, the river seemed to erupt directly from the cliff's foundation. With no hope of escape that way, I turned my gaze back to the forest. The cliffs loomed five thousand feet overhead, cast in a dull, shadowy yellow by the lack of direct sunlight. Streaks of dusky red, emerald green, and flashes of white quartz fractured the monolith. Beautiful, yes, yet in this moment of desperation, I viewed them not with awe, but with the hatred a prisoner reserves for the immovable walls of his dungeon.

Suddenly, the sun breached the cliff's zenith. Instantly, the dull surface erupted into a kaleidoscope of burnished gold, flaming rubies, soft greens, and gleaming diamonds. It was a vision of such breathtaking splendor that the human eye struggles to retain the memory of. The entire face of the cliff appeared to be forged of solid gold, shattered only by outcroppings of ruby, emerald, and diamond boulders—a tantalizing promise of the unimaginable riches buried deep within.

Yet, piercing through that blinding radiance, I spotted distinct black spots high upon the wall, near the forest's canopy, seemingly extending behind the branches. Recognizing them instantly, I realized they were the dark maws of caves penetrating the solid rock—our only potential haven.

There was but one path to them: through the towering trees on our right. I knew I could scale them, but Tars Tarkas, with his immense bulk, would find the task nearly impossible. Martians, generally, were poor climbers; on Barsoom, hills rarely exceeded four thousand feet, offering few steep inclines to test their skills. Moreover, the Tharks preferred circuitous routes around the bases of mountains rather than the arduous climbs required to breach a vertical wall.

Nevertheless, there was no other choice. The Thark grasped the gravity of our situation, and without hesitation, we moved toward the nearest trees.

Our pursuers were upon us, closing the distance with terrifying speed. It seemed impossible for the Jeddak of Thark to reach the forest before the horde. Yet, Tars Tarkas pushed forward, driven not merely by survival but by a sudden, fierce love for life—a sentiment alien to his kind, who typically courted death. Behind my own desperate flight burned a different fire: love for the divine Dejah Thoris.

We plunged into the shadows of the forest as the swiftest of our hunters, a giant plant-man with claws extended to tear at our throats, launched himself after us. He was a hundred yards ahead of the rest, and I signaled Tars Tarkas to scale a great tree brushing the cliff face while I engaged the beast. This would give the less agile warrior time to reach the upper branches before the horde overwhelmed us.

I miscalculated. My adversary possessed a cunning I had not anticipated, and his kin covered the ground with a swiftness that defied expectation. As I raised my longsword to strike, the creature halted, and my blade sliced through empty air. With the power of a grizzly's arm, its great tail swept me from my feet. Before I could recover, the brute was upon me. I seized a writhing tentacle in each hand, locking my grip as the struggle began.

Though strong and heavy, the plant-man was no match for my agility and the crushing vice of my strangulation hold. Had we time to spar, I believed victory would be mine. But as we grappled against the tree, Tars Tarkas climbed with infinite labor, I glanced past my foe and saw the truth. Swarming behind the plant-men were the Great White Apes—the most dreaded of Martian horrors.

Familiar with their form, I recognized the uncanny likeness to Earthmen, scaled to fifteen feet of towering malice. They walked erect, possessing an extra pair of arms, with close-set eyes, high-set ears, and snouts akin to African gorillas, crowned by a shock of bristly hair. Into the eyes of these giants and the hissing plant-men I looked, then watched as a wave of snarling, screaming fury swept over me.

Instantly, a dozen fangs and talons sank into my flesh. Cold, sucking lips clamped onto my arteries. I struggled, torn by the immense weight of these bodies, but managed to rise. Clutching my longsword, I shortened my grip to wield it as a dagger, hacking a bloody swath through the swarm. For a brief, glorious instant, I stood free.

Minutes in history unfolded in seconds. During that time, Tars Tarkas, having reached the lower branches with Herculean effort, dropped to aid me. As I threw back the last of my attackers, the great Thark leaped to my side. Back to back, as we had a hundred times before, we fought. Time and again the ferocious apes lunged, and time and again we drove them back with our gleaming blades. The plant-men's tails lashed with terrifying power, charging from every angle, but every blow met the steel of hands renowned throughout Mars as the finest. Tars Tarkas and John Carter were legends among warriors.

Yet, two masters of the sword could not withstand endless numbers of savages who knew no defeat until cold steel silenced their hearts. Step by step, we retreated. Finally, we stood against the giant tree we had chosen for ascent, and as wave after wave of brutes crashed into us, we fell back until we were hemmed in against the colossal trunk.

Tars Tarkas led the defense, then let out a cry of triumph. "Shelter for one, John Carter!" he cried, pointing to an opening in the tree's base, barely three feet wide.

At last, we reached a great carved door. Through it, Carthoris darted, a step ahead of me. Within, the scene was one I had witnessed before, yet twisted into a fresh horror—the throne of Issus, surrounded by reclining slaves, and encircled by rows of soldiery.

We did not wait. Too quickly, the guards drew their weapons. With a single, fluid motion, I struck down two men in the front rank. Then, using the sheer momentum of my body, I rushed through the remaining ranks, leaping onto the dais beside the carved sorapus throne.

The repulsive creature, squatting there in terror, tried to flee, leaping toward a trapdoor behind her. But I was not to be outwitted by such petty tricks. Before she could fully arise, I had her by the arm. Seeing the guard surge forward, I whipped out my dagger, holding it close to her vile breast.

"Back!" I roared, my voice cutting through the din. "Back! The first black foot upon this platform brings my dagger to Issus' heart!"

For an instant, they hesitated. Then an officer signaled them back. From the outer corridor, sweeping into the throne room at our heels, came a thousand Red men under Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and Xodar.
