Outside the Duomo, the air hummed with the aftermath of the sermon, a heavy, vibrating silence broken only by the rustle of robes and the shifting weight of thousands. While Baldassarre had been seized by the terrifying voice of Savonarola, he remained oblivious to the shadow falling upon him from behind. Standing just within reach was Piero di Cosimo, the painter, who had slipped past the throng not to listen to the fiery orations, but to witness the fate of the escaped prisoner.

As the preacher and his captivated audience surrendered themselves to a wordless, collective emotion, the newcomer stepped forward. With a gentle, almost casual touch, Piero placed a hand on Baldassarre's arm. The painter turned slowly, the tears still tracing slow paths down his cheeks, though a vigorous sigh marked the end of his emotional outburst. Speaking in a low murmur, he offered, "Shall I cut your cords for you? I've heard the tale of how you were taken."

Baldassarre did not answer at first. His gaze narrowed, suspicious and wary, fixed on the officious stranger. Finally, after a beat of hesitation, he whispered, "If you will."

"Better step outside," Piero suggested softly.

Again, Baldassarre looked askance. Sensing the distrust, Piero smiled, drew a small knife from his sleeve, and swiftly severed the bonds around the prisoner's wrists. As he worked, the artist mused that the notion of the man's madness was hardly improbable; there was something so singular, so wild, etched into the expression of his face. *If he causes trouble,* Piero thought, *he will be tied up again quickly enough. For now, let the poor devil breathe.*

"You fear me," Baldassarre observed, his voice an undertone. "You wish to keep secrets from me."

Baldassarre crossed his arms, savoring the return of muscular sensation after days of captivity. His look of suspicion softened, replaced by a quiet, steely resolve. "No, I have nothing to tell."

"As you please," Piero replied, shrugging slightly. "Though perhaps you seek shelter? We Florentines are renowned for our hospitality to travelers with torn doublets and hollow bellies. There is a hospital for the poor near the gates, and if you wished, I could guide you there. You need not fear the French soldier anymore; he has been dispatched."

Baldassarre nodded, accepting the offer in silence. Together, they walked away from the church.

As they moved along the Via dell' Oriuolo toward the Gate of Santa Croce, Piero broke the quiet. "Would you not sit for a portrait? I am a painter, and I would pay well to capture your likeness."

A flicker of old caution returned to Baldassarre's eyes. He shook his head decisively. "No."

"A pity!" Piero said curtly. "Very well, then, walk on. You will find the Porta Santa Croce ahead, and outside it lies the hospital for strangers. So, you decline my further aid?"

"I thank you for what you have already done. I require nothing more."

"It is as it should be," Piero murmured, turning away with a philosophical shrug.

*Mysterious old tiger,* the artist reflected as he retraced his steps back toward the Piazza del Duomo. *Truly a subject worthy of the brush. Ugly, carved with deep lines, as if the plowshare and the harrow had tilled over his heart. A stark, grim counterpoint to my own bland, smiling Messer Greco—the Baccus Triumphans who wedded the beautiful Antigone against all reason and history. Ah! One feels the scholar's blood curdle uncomfortably at the sight of that old fellow's claw!*

By the time Piero re-entered the square, the vast multitude that had listened to Fra Girolamo was beginning to disperse. The urgency with which they hurried toward their separate destinations testified to the profound impact of the preaching, a spell that had momentarily suspended the ordinary rhythms of daily life. The artist leaned against the sharp angle of the Baptistery, watching the departing tide. He delighted in the kaleidoscope of garments and the intense, characteristic profiles of the crowd—faces that seemed to echo the timeless observations of Masaccio, fifty years prior, and the unflagging realism of Domenico Ghirlandaio.

This morning, however, was peculiar. The congregation, usually a mosaic of the city's factions, had displayed a completeness of representation rarely seen. There were men of high birth, accustomed to public office, who had emerged as fierce enemies of the Medici and ardent supporters of popular rule. Among them stood figures of authoritative presence, like Francesco Valori, handsome yet hot-tempered, intoxicated by the immediate divine mandate to liberate Florence in their own image. Beside them stood others like Soderini, possessing less of the fiery zealotry and more of the statesman's wisdom. Then there were the brave, un-doctrined lovers of sober republicanism, like Piero Capponi, who preferred the sword to the argument and cared little for ideology so long as the Medici were ousted.

At their sides walked the doctors of law, whose study of Accursius had not extinguished their spiritual ardor; Messer Luca Corsini himself, who would one day raise his learned arms in the streets to hurl stones for religion and freedom. Intermixed with these dignitaries carrying their black luccio or fur-lined mantles were those with contemplative, sensitive features: scholars bearing the names of Strozzi and Acciajoli, already inclined toward the cowl; artists driven to higher ambitions by Savonarola's fire, like the young man who had recently surpassed himself in his fresco of the Divine Child within the friar's bare cell—a youth unconscious that he would soon don the habit himself and become known as Fra Bartolommeo. There was the mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni, likely rushing to deliver news of the Friar's impending arrival to his friend Pico della Mirandola, a man who would never see another dawn. There were women of noble birth, clad in scrupulously plain dress, their grace derived from restraint rather than ornament. And dominating the scene, perhaps more than any other stratum, were the genuine *popolani* of the Major and Minor Arts, conscious of their purses shrinking under the threat of war taxes. Most striking of all, however, was the long, winding stream of poorer tradesmen and artisans. Their faith in the Friar's divine message ranged from the crude, undiscriminating trust of the oppressed, seeing him as the friend of the poor and the scourge of the luxurious rich, to the eager, ravenous hunger of the sedentary worker for every subtlety of biblical interpretation, illuminated by a pale flame that promised a science beyond the board where he stitched.

Yet scattered among these devoted disciples were many who were not his followers at all. Some were Mediceans, already bowing to the prevailing spirit of the popular party out of fear and policy, feigning deference. Others were sincere advocates of a free government who viewed Savonarola merely as an ambitious monk—half sagacious, half fanatical—who had leveraged his position to become a powerful instrument of the masses, a social fact that could not be ignored. Even among his bitterest enemies, members of the old aristocratic anti-Medicean party determined to reclaim the reins for the chief families, or licentious youths who detested him as the killjoy of Florence, found their attention drawn. The sermons in the Duomo had become political incidents, captivating the ears of curiosity and malice as much as faith. Men of ideas, like the young Niccolò Machiavelli, went to observe and report to friends in country villas; men of appetite, like Dolfo Spini, sought to hunt down the Friar as a public nuisance who made game scarce, lying in wait for grounds of accusation.

Perhaps no preacher had ever exerted such massive influence as Savonarola, nor worked with such heterogeneous material. The secret of his sway lay in the highly mixed character of his preaching itself. To Baldassarre, wrought into an ecstasy of self-martyring revenge, the sermon had been a call to vengeance. Yet for others, Savonarola's words struck the very finest susceptibilities of human nature, while simultaneously gratifying low egoism, tickling gossiping curiosity, and fascinating timorous superstition. His need for personal predominance, his labyrinthine allegorical interpretations of Scripture, his enigmatic visions, and his false certitude regarding Divine intentions never ceased, in his own large soul, to be ennobled by a fervid piety, a passionate sense of the infinite, an active sympathy, and a clear-sighted demand for the subjection of selfish interests to the general good. But for the mass of his audience, all the potency of his preaching lay in his strong assertion of supernatural claims, in his denunciatory visions, and in the false certainty that lent his sermons the immediacy of a political bulletin. Having once held that audience in his mastery, it became necessary to his nature—and necessary for their welfare—that he maintain it. The effect was inevitable: no man struggles to retain power over a mixed multitude without suffering vitiation; his standard must inevitably descend to meet their lower needs rather than rising to his own best insights.

The mysteries of human character were seldom presented in a way better suited to checking the judgments of facile knowingness than in the person of Girolamo Savonarola.
