The small, sun-drenched lawn stretched out in a smooth, inviting expanse toward the vast, glittering sea. Its edges were trimmed with vibrant scarlet geraniums and coleus, framing a winding path lined with chocolate-colored cast-iron vases. These vessels held lush garlands of petunias and ivy geraniums, draping elegantly over the neatly raked gravel.

Halfway between the cliff's edge and the square, chocolate-hued house—its verandah roof striped in yellow and brown to mimic an awning—two large targets stood amidst the shrubbery. Opposite them, a genuine tent was pitched, surrounded by benches and garden seats. Ladies in flowing summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock coats and tall hats populated the lawn or occupied the seating areas. Periodically, a slender girl clad in stiff muslin emerged from the tent, bow in hand, to launch her shaft at one of the targets, prompting the spectators to pause their conversations and watch intently.

Newland Archer stood on the verandah, observing the scene with curiosity. Flanking the glossy painted steps were large blue china flower pots resting on bright yellow stands, filled with spiky green plants. Below the verandah, a wide border of blue hydrangeas edged with more red geraniums added to the riot of color. Behind him, French doors offered glimpses into the drawing rooms: glassy parquet floors dotted with chintz poufs, diminutive armchairs, and velvet tables laden with silver trifles, all viewed through swaying lace curtains.

The Newport Archery Club traditionally convened its August meetings at the Beauforts'. While the sport, once rivaled only by croquet, was gradually yielding to lawn tennis, the latter was still deemed too rugged and unladylike for social gatherings. Thus, the bow and arrow retained their place as a venue for displaying fine attire and graceful posture.

Archer gazed down at the familiar tableau with a sense of wonder. It startled him that life continued in its accustomed rhythm when his own feelings toward it had shifted so profoundly. Newport had been the catalyst that revealed the magnitude of this internal change. Back in New York during the preceding winter, following his settlement in the new greenish-yellow house with its bow-window and Pompeian vestibule, Archer had retreated with relief into his old professional routine. This daily grind served as a tether to his former self. There had been the thrill of selecting a flashy grey steamer for May's brougham—a gift from the Wellands—and the satisfying project of furnishing his new library according to his dreams, complete with dark embossed wallpaper, Eastlake bookcases, and "sincere" armchairs. At *The Century* magazine, he had reunited with Winsett, and at *The Knickerbocker*, with his peers. Between legal hours, dinners, entertainments, and occasional nights at the opera or theater, his life had felt solid, real, and inevitable.

Yet, Newport represented an escape from duty into a realm of pure leisure. Archer had attempted to convince May to spend the summer on a remote Maine island, Mount Desert, where tough Bostonians and Philadelphians camped in rustic cabins, enjoying wild, trapper-like existences amidst woods and waters. However, the Wellands invariably chose Newport, where they owned a cliffside box. With no compelling reason to dissent, and Mrs. Welland sharply noting that it was futile for May to try on expensive Parisian summer clothes if she couldn't wear them, Archer found himself unable to argue.

May herself struggled to comprehend his vague reluctance to embrace such a reasonable and pleasant arrangement. She reminded him that he had loved Newport in his bachelor days—an undeniable truth—which left him only able to claim he anticipated liking it better now that they shared the experience. Yet, as he stood on the Beaufort verandah surveying the lively lawn, a chill seized him: he knew he would not enjoy it at all.

It was not May's fault, poor dear. Occasionally, during their travels, they might drift slightly out of sync, but returning to the familiar conditions she cherished always restored their harmony. He had always predicted she would not disappoint him, and he had been correct. He had married, as many young men do, upon encountering a perfectly charming girl just as a series of aimless sentimental adventures ended in premature disillusionment. She embodied peace, stability, companionship, and the comforting weight of unescapable duty.

He could not deny that his choice had been wise; she had fulfilled every expectation. It was undeniably gratifying to be the husband of one of New York's most beautiful and popular young married women, especially given her sweet temper and reasonableness. Archer had never been blind to these advantages. Regarding the fleeting madness that had overtaken him on the eve of his marriage, he had trained himself to view it as merely the latest of his discarded experiments. The notion that he could, in his sober state, have dreamed of marrying the Countess Olenska had become almost unthinkable; she remained in his memory simply as the most sorrowful ghost in a line of spectral figures.

However, these mental abstractions and eliminations left his mind feeling hollow and echoing. Perhaps this was why the bustling, animated crowd on the Beaufort lawn shocked him, appearing as children playing in a graveyard.

A rustle of skirts signaled the Marchioness Manson emerging from the drawing-room window. As usual, she was extravagantly adorned, her limp Leghorn hat secured by windings of faded gauze, topped with a tiny black velvet parasol on a carved ivory handle, absurdly balancing atop her massive hatbrim.

"My dear Newland, I had no idea you and May had arrived! You say you came only yesterday? Ah, business—professional duties... I understand. Many husbands find it impossible to join their wives here except for the weekend." She tilted her head, squinting languidly at him. "But marriage is one long sacrifice, as I often told my Ellen..."

Archer's heart gave a strange, sudden jerk—the same sensation he had experienced once before—that seemed to slam a door shut between him and the outside world. Yet, this rupture of continuity was brief, for soon he heard Medora responding to a question he had seemingly voiced.

"No, I am not staying here, but with the Blenkers in their delightful solitude at Portsmouth. Beaufort kindly sent his famous trotters this morning so I might glimpse one of Regina's garden parties, but tonight I return to rural life. The Blenkers, those dear originals, have rented a primitive farmhouse where they gather representative folk..." She drooped slightly beneath her protective brim, adding with a faint blush, "This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding Inner Thought meetings there. A stark contrast to this scene of worldly pleasure—but then, I live by contrasts! To me, the only true death is monotony. I always tell Ellen: Beware of monotony; it is the mother of all deadly sins. My poor child is currently undergoing a phase of exaltation, an abhorrence of the world. You know, I suppose, that she has declined all invitations to stay at Newport, even with her grandmother Mingott? I could hardly persuade her to come to the Blenkers'! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if she had only listened to me when the door was still open..."

Strolling toward them from the tent, Beaufort advanced over the lawn. Tall, heavy, and tightly buttoned into a London frock coat with an orchid in his buttonhole, he looked different after months apart. In the harsh summer light, his florid complexion appeared bloated; without his erect, square-shouldered stride, he resembled an overfed, overdressed elderly man.

Rumors swirled about Beaufort. Earlier in the spring, he had cruised the West Indies in his new steam-yacht, reportedly accompanied by a woman resembling Miss Fanny Ring. The yacht, built in the Clyde with tiled bathrooms and unheard-of luxuries, was said to have cost half a million dollars. Upon his return, he presented his wife with a magnificent pearl necklace, typical of such expiatory gifts. Despite his substantial fortune, unsettling rumors persisted in both Fifth Avenue and Wall Street regarding unfortunate railway speculations or exploitation by a predatory member of her profession. To every hint of insolvency, Beaufort responded with fresh extravagances: new orchid houses, a fresh string of racehorses, or additions to his art gallery featuring Meissonnier or Cabanel.

Beaufort approached the Marchioness and Newland with his customary half-sneering smile. "Hullo, Medora! Did the trotters do their business? Forty minutes, eh? Well, that's not so bad, considering your nerves needed sparing." He shook Archer's hand, then positioned himself beside the Marchioness, whispering something she failed to catch.

The Marchioness replied with one of her peculiar foreign jerks and a "_Que voulez-vous?_" that deepened Beaufort's frown. Nevertheless, he forced a congenial smile as he glanced at Archer, declaring, "You know May is going to take first prize."

"Ah, then it stays in the family," Medora rippled. Just then, they reached the tent, where Mrs. Beaufort greeted them in a cloud of mauve muslin and floating veils.

May Welland stepped out of the tent. Clad in white with a pale green sash and an ivy wreath on her hat, she possessed the same Diana-like aloofness she had displayed the night of her engagement at the Beaufort ballroom.
