The golden light of dawn stretched across the land, as it had for centuries before—over the olive-clad hills, the winding rivers, the cities with their domes and towers piercing the sky. The world below, though shaped by the hands of men, still bore the marks of an older, immutable design. The mountains cast their shadows as they always had; the sea, restless and endless, whispered against the shores.

And in the homes of men, the same stirrings of life unfolded. Nestling children, warm with sleep; the laborer rising to meet the day; the scholar, weary from his midnight vigil, chasing truths that danced just beyond his grasp. The great currents of human existence—love and loss, toil and rest, hunger and fulfillment—flowed on as they ever had.

Florence stood as it had in days long past, her skyline graced by the same proud towers, her streets winding along the Arno’s banks. If a shade from that vanished time were to walk her squares now, he would know her still—the red roofs, the solemn bulk of Santa Croce, the delicate spire of the Badia. He would pause before the Palazzo Vecchio, its tower unbroken by the years, and remember the debates that once echoed within its halls. He would trace the path of the river, its bridges still arched over the water, the Ponte Vecchio’s shops clinging to its sides as they had in his day.

But time had not left all things untouched. Gates once bustling stood sealed; towers that had bristled with defiance lay leveled. Had peace truly softened the Florentine spirit? Or had the old fire merely banked itself, waiting to flare anew?

Yet some things endured. The same passions moved in the hearts of men—ambition, rivalry, the fierce love of home. The same laughter rang in the piazzas, the same quarrels flared and faded. The scholar still bent over his texts, the merchant still weighed his fortunes, the friar still preached repentance. And beneath it all, the same questions lingered, unanswered: *What is justice? What is truth?*

The city lived, as cities do, between memory and change. The stones remembered. The river flowed on. And the light—the same light that had touched the faces of the dead—now fell upon the living, who dreamed the same dreams, feared the same fears, and hoped, as ever, for a dawn that might yet bring a better world.

For the angel of that dawn had not yet come.

To Dante’s ears, these streets had once echoed with the clash of warring families. But now, in the waning years of the fifteenth century, they rang only with the coarse laughter and squabbles of wool-carders from San Martino and Garbo.

Beneath the loggia’s shadow, on the chill morning of April 9th, 1492, two men locked eyes—one hunched slightly, peering down with sharp curiosity; the other sprawled on the cobbles, blinking up like a man startled from a dream.

The standing figure spoke first. He was broad-shouldered, gray-haired, his face rough-hewn as if nature had shaped him with a fist and barely smoothed the edges. Yet the deep lines of his brow and mouth carried a self-important gravity, as if to correct any impression of carelessness. A bulging leather sack rested at his feet, and a pedlar’s basket hung before him, laden with needles, thread, and shards of glass—likely bartered for his wares.

“Young man,” he said, jabbing a finger toward the reclining figure’s hand, “when your chin sprouts a proper beard, you’ll know better than to nap in the street with a ring like that on your finger. Holy angels! If it’d been anyone but me standing over you just now—but Bratti Ferravecchi isn’t a thief. A cat won’t eat a mouse it didn’t catch alive, and I’ve no taste for profit unless it’s earned fair. Why, three years back, San Giovanni tossed a dead beggar in my path—his cap stuffed with coin—but my stomach turned at money I hadn’t bargained for. So I buried the poor wretch, paid for a mass, and called it square. But you—a lad with the face of Saint Michael himself—what’s your tale, sleeping on stone with the wind for a blanket?”

The stranger’s groggy mind barely parsed the thick Tuscan growl, but the gesture toward his ring was clear. He glanced down, then—almost reflexively—yanked it off and tucked it into his doublet before rising with a stretch.

“That jewel’s a poor match for your rags,” Bratti mused. “Some might say the saints sent *you* a dead man’s treasure. Did you at least bury him? You look flush enough for a mass or two.”

A flicker of pain crossed the young man’s face, swiftly masked by indifference. He swept off his red Levantine cap, pushing back dark curls. “Truth enough,” he said lightly. “My clothes are salt-stained as an old sail—though, like a sail, they’ve seen more sea than years. The fact is, I’m a stranger here. Last night, footsore and lost, I chose this hospitable porch over hunting for a hostelry that might’ve bled me dry.”

“A stranger indeed,” Bratti snorted. “Your words slosh about like wine in a drunkard’s mouth. Not Genoese, though—Venetian, by the cut of you?”

“Right now,” the stranger laughed, “where I’m from matters less than where I can find breakfast. This quarter scowls at me. Point me to livelier streets—food, a bed—and I’ll owe you.”

“Owe me? Ah, now we bargain!” Bratti hoisted his sack. “Luck’s with you. I was heading to Mercato Vecchio to say an Ave at the Badia. But nothing’s free. Swear by your saint—when you trade those rags for better, I get first bid.”

“By San Niccolò, agreed,” the stranger said. “Now, lead on before this doublet you covet gnaws a hole in my ribs.”

“Covet? Pah!” Bratti lurched into motion, then bellowed in a voice like grinding wheels: *“Chi abbaratta—baratta—b’ratta—chi abbaratta cenci e vetri?”* (“Who’ll trade rags or glass?”)

After a pause, he shrugged. “Your clothes? Worth little. Still, if you fancy a lute finer than new, or a Ridolfi’s old sword, my shop’s the best in Ferravecchi—near the Mercato. Not that I cage myself there. I’ve a wife and a raven minding the stall. *Chi abbaratta—?*” He shot the stranger a sidelong glance. “Now then, where *do* you hail from, and what’s your business here?”

“I thought you liked nothing free,” the stranger countered. “What’s your offer for *that* news?”

Bratti grinned. “A Florentine pays fair for gossip—even if it fills no pockets. How’s this: I’ll point you to the prettiest milkmaid in Mercato. A cup from her—that’s a bargain.”

“I’ll find my own milkmaids, thanks. Pretty heads peer from windows enough. Besides, a trader like you knows bids for news often buy empty husks.”

“Ah! Not born yesterday, were you?” Bratti eyed him with grudging respect. “No Hebrew, I’ll wager—not with that tongue. Though mark me, the Frati Minori are roasting Spain’s usurers here. Walk the Calimara with yellow cloth in your cap, and you’ll lose more than your looks. Grey cloth’s against yellow here—and grey’s the Duomo’s own hue. *Abbaratta—!*”

“Amusing,” the stranger drawled, “but wasted. I’m no Jew.”

“Ha! A bargain struck!” Bratti crowed. “Words pried *something* from you, lamprey. Blind Florentines best one-eyed men twice over. But here—the Mercato.”

They spilled into the broad piazza of Mercato Vecchio, where even in Lent’s grip, the air buzzed with haggling gardeners, cheesemongers, and egg-sellers. Yet today, trade stood still. Clusters of folk whispered, their backs to stalls. A distracted hose-seller had slung his wares around his neck; a cheesemonger stabbed his blade into a round of *marzolino* to punctuate his speech.

Bratti snorted. “The market’s gone mad as if the Pope cursed us again. Wait—” He seized an old herb-seller’s arm. “Your parsley’s trampled! Has the world ended?”

The woman whirled, spitting a curse before darting to salvage her greens. Bratti slid into her place, dragging the stranger with him.

“I saw it myself,” a fat leek-buyer panted. “In Santa Maria Novella—a woman cried she saw a bull with flaming horns crush the church!”

A razor-thin barber, Nello, smirked. “The bull, Goro—or just the woman?”

“The *woman*, fool! But it’s all one—the omen stands!”

Voices overlapped: “Lights over San Lorenzo—” “Thunder in clear skies—” “The Duomo’s lantern struck—” “Medici *palle* shattered—” “A lion tearing its own kind—”

Bratti pieced together the clamor, then nudged Nello. “So Lorenzo’s dead? May he rest—and wax prices rise?”

“Just so,” Nello said, grinning. “And his wax effigy in the Nunziata fell—*whenever* the friars say it did. Calves stillborn, eggs rotten—ah, what a man! Poet, politician—greater than Dante, some claim. Yet only the lantern fell, not the dome. *Che miracolo!*”

A spectacled notary, Ser Cioni, hissed for silence. “Fools! You cheer the hawk who slipped nooses around every neck in Florence! He turned elections to closet-work, drained the treasury for his villas—and you mourn him? The bull’s horns gore *your* taxes, not the heavens!”

Nello chuckled. “Prophesy less, Ser Cioni. Stick to notary work.”

“A notary with dry ink,” a pauper sneered. “Take the cowl instead!”

As the notary stalked off, Bratti muttered, “Goro’s not wrong. The *quattrini bianchi* pinch us all. If Lorenzo lived, he’d have stopped this coin-shrinking—maybe the bull *does* mean taxes.”

Nello rolled his eyes. “Omens mean what you please. The Frate says one thing, Fra Menico another. But Goro here’s ‘illuminated’—he sees the bull’s himself, set to gore the magistrates!”

A bass voice rumbled like a bell: “Goro’s a fool. Let him mend his wool, not his grievances. The only burden hurting him’s his own fat belly.”

The crowd rippled—some laughing, others bristling. The stranger watched, lips quirking. Florence’s pulse beat loud here: superstition, fury, wit, all tangled like the streets themselves. And beneath it, the shadow of a dead prince—and the storm yet to come.

The young stranger lingered briefly after Bratti merged into the lively knot of gossipers, but the Florentines' fervent debate held little interest for him. Impatient and unwilling to wait for his erratic guide, he wandered through the piazza instead, searching for a food vendor less consumed by the city’s latest drama.

A quick check of his purse confirmed his suspicions—empty. *Not a single obolus,* he muttered under his breath, the words foreign on his tongue. *Well then, I’ll have to earn my breakfast with charm.*

His search didn’t take long. Tucked away from the crowd stood two mules, their red tassels bright against the morning light. One bore milk vessels, the other baskets brimming with fresh herbs. Leaning against the first mule was a girl no older than sixteen, her face framed by a red hood, her features soft and childlike. Exhaustion had claimed her; she dozed standing, her head drooping slightly.

The stranger saw no reason not to wake her—but gently, as if coaxing a dream to shift. He reached out, brushing an imaginary sprig of thyme near her lips. Her eyes fluttered open—wide, startled blue—and she recoiled, clutching her green serge mantle like a shield.

He smiled. *Forgive me,* he said, his voice warm and unfamiliar to her ears. *I’m starving, and the smell of your milk makes it worse.* He chose the simplest words he knew, shaping them like a playful beggar’s plea.

Understanding dawned. The mantle lowered, and without a word, she offered him a cup of milk. He drank eagerly, while she studied him—his dark curls, his tattered but oddly refined clothes. Then, as if compelled, she reached into her bag and held out a piece of bread.

*But that’s yours,* he protested.

She shook her head, nudging it closer.

*Well then,* he said, taking it, *if I’m to be so bold, perhaps another kindness? A kiss to sweeten the bread?*

Her cheeks flushed. She raised the mantle again, hiding her face—but not before he glimpsed the spark of amusement in her eyes. He leaned in, fingers grazing her arm—

A harsh voice cut through the moment. A woman—broad, sunburnt, draped in a man’s jerkin—glared at him. *Some dice-scum, no doubt. Scram before I speed you along myself.*

The stranger merely grinned, unfazed. The resemblance between the woman and the girl was unmistakable, though time had roughened the mother’s features into something weary and sharp.

*Ghita, don’t waste your temper,* called a new voice. Nello the barber, accompanied by Bratti, stepped forward. *What’s the trouble?*

*This jackanapes was making eyes at my Tessa!* Ghita snapped.

*Ah, but can you blame him?* Nello chuckled. *A face like hers could soften the hardest heart.*

Bratti, ever suspicious, eyed the stranger. *Found him lurking near the Cerchi earlier, wearing a ring fit for a nobleman. Now look at him—begging breakfast like a vagabond.*

Nello waved a dismissive hand. *A Greek, if I’ve ever seen one. Probably some scholar down on his luck.*

The stranger laughed. *You see right through me.*

Ghita wasn’t amused. *Enough chatter!* She shoved past, dragging Tessa and the mules along. The girl cast one last glance at the stranger—just in time to catch his sly, parting wave.

Bratti, shouldering his goods, called after him: *Remember our deal! Nello’ll show you my shop. Don’t forget who brought you here!*

The stranger waved back, still grinning, as he followed Nello into the winding streets toward the Duomo.

The young stranger allowed himself to be steered clear of the clattering carts and braying mules by Nello, whose lively chatter flowed as freely as the Arno in flood.

“To be honest,” the Greek said, once they had found smoother footing, “I’m not sorry to exchange my rag-selling patron for a man who speaks without chewing his words like a stale crust. Since when do peddlers of broken glass claim to traffic in lutes and blades?”

Nello’s laugh was bright as the glint of his razor. “Common? Ha! Our Bratti is as common as a unicorn. He’s a philosopher of haggling, squeezing every drop of pleasure—meaning profit—from life, then bargaining for a swift passage through purgatory by bequeathing his gains to Holy Church. I’ve told him, ‘Bratti, your arithmetic limps. What will you barter in Paradise? Saints don’t need rusty nails, and the only secondhand robes belong to poor flayed Bartolomeo.’ But forgive me—” He crossed himself abruptly. “Lorenzo lies cold today, and the Muses rend their hair—a grievous sight for a barber. And you, *Messere*, with your fine speech and threadbare lodging, wear misfortune like a shadow. What storm cast you ashore here?”

The stranger’s gaze sharpened. “Which Lorenzo do you speak of?”

“Which Lorenzo?” Nello spread his hands. “The sun of Florence! Lorenzo de’ Medici, whose death shakes the very lantern of the Duomo and sets the Marzocco lions gnashing their stone teeth. Our Pericles—if you’ll pardon the comparison in a Greek’s ear.”

The young man’s lips curved. “Why not? I doubt even Athens in her prime boasted a barber so learned.”

“Ah! I knew it.” Nello clapped his hands. “The lilt of your Italian betrays you—born under Italian skies, no? A Grecian sapling grafted onto our soil, like those Persian peaches in the Medici gardens. But time and travel have steeped you anew in the old dyes. And if I may guess—” He lowered his voice. “Those same Grecian hues, along with a few antique gems, are all the waves left you, eh? Well, when towers fall, the sparrows must flit. Lorenzo’s death makes Rome the wiser nest, but—”

“A certain Augustinian monk sang Florence’s praises,” the Greek admitted wryly. “‘The market of scholars,’ he called it.”

“*Gnaffè!* And so it remains,” Nello declared. “Lorenzo wasn’t our only patron. Bernardo Rucellai, Alamanno Rinuccini—but enough! A thousand years yawn until I can serve a scholar like you. First—” He brandished his razor. “This beard must go. Florentine maidens prefer lips unburied by brambles.”

The Greek touched his chin. “A dire sacrifice. Especially if your maids are as sweet as the little Tessa I kissed this morning.”

“Tessa?” Nello snorted. “A milkmaid’s hands are rough as her cheese. You’ll rise to ladies who scent their gloves with civet, not stable straw. But mark me—you must shed the look of a *sgherro*. Cultivate the air of a Pietro Crinito: sin among silks, not tavern dregs.”

“Then let the razor work,” the Greek conceded. “Though I’d keep these curls, if your Graces permit.”

“Like Pico in his prime!” Nello shepherded him toward the Piazza San Giovanni, where the Duomo’s vast dome loomed beside Giotto’s slender campanile. The Greek tilted his head, but his admiring gaze soon cooled to scrutiny.

Nello bristled. “You stare as if our cathedral were a cow byre! Has Constantinople finer marbles? Or a cupola to rival Brunelleschi’s?”

The young man laughed. “Spare the scimitar—I confess your stones are fair. But inside?” He shuddered. “Smoke-blackened Virgins, skeletal saints glaring like starved cats. Ghiberti’s gates show promise, though.”

“Promise?” Nello huffed. “Those doors are fifty years old! Our artists have studied the antique since—”

“—Since monks let them glimpse Olympus between penances,” the Greek finished. “A pity.”

Nello steered him firmly toward his shop, its sign proclaiming *Apollo and the Razor*. Inside, Sandro, the solemn assistant, mixed lavender-scented lather while the Greek eyed the inner sanctum—a snug retreat with a stone Hermes half-hidden among laurels.

“Your *eruditi* gather here?”

“Here, and amid the razors,” Nello said. “For inspiration strikes clearest with a smooth chin. Now—” He swathed the Greek in linen. “Consider this: Is Virgil spelled with *i* or *e*? You’ll find the answer comes swifter once the blade passes. Though if you favor *i*, keep it quiet until your fortune’s made—Florence clings to *e*.”

The Greek studied his shaven reflection in a Murano mirror. “You’ve stolen years from me. I’ll pass for a boy in doublet.”

“Nonsense!” Nello snipped a curl. “Poliziano lectured with less beard. Though—” He hesitated. “Your Greek blood may weigh against you. Some scholars call your learning weedy till transplanted to Italian minds.”

The stranger’s jaw tightened.

“Peace!” Nello raised his hands. “I but repeat what’s said. And between us—” He leaned close. “Change your stand on leaven, tweak your Doxology, and chairs might open for you. Ah, but here’s true counsel: Praise Cicero, smirk at Argiropulo’s blunders, and you’ll fare well. Now—” He dusted the Greek’s shoulders. “For patronage, try young Piero de’ Medici, though his tastes run to falconry over philosophy. Or Bernardo Rucellai, if you’ve gems to sell.”

The Greek drew a sardonyx Cleopatra from his doublet lining. “And this ring?” He twisted a gold band set with an emerald. “Know you an honest jeweler?”

Nello’s eyes gleamed. “Ah! Let me make introductions…”

The shop bell jingled as two men stepped inside. Nello, the barber, flashed a grin at the first. “Messer Domenico! You arrive as timely as grated cheese on a plate of pasta. And in such haste—I see you wish to be shaved without delay. *Ecco!* But then, who isn’t in haste today? Florence is orphaned, the very heart of Italy torn out. The heavens themselves must be scrambling to set things right. *Oimè!* Yet the sun marches on toward dinner, and as I was saying—your timing is impeccable. This young stranger here seeks an honorable merchant to advance him a sum against a ring of some value. Had I counted every goldsmith in Florence on my fingers, I could name none better than Menico Cennini.”

The older man, son of the famed Bernardo Cennini—who had cast Florence’s first printing types years before—remained still under Nello’s lather, though his eyes slid sideways to study the stranger.

Nello, never one to let silence linger, pressed on. “This fine scholar has Greek, Latin, and Italian at your disposal—a prodigy of learning, like Filelfo or our own Poliziano! A second Guarino, too, for he’s survived a shipwreck, though I fear many a precious manuscript now feeds the fishes. But he’s salvaged a few treasures. His name is—” Nello paused, razor hovering. “You did give your name, Messere?”

“Tito Melema,” the stranger said smoothly, slipping a ring from his finger and offering it to Cennini.

The second visitor—a tall man in a threadbare cloak and worn felt hat—had not taken his eyes off Tito. Abruptly, he spoke. “Young man, I’m painting Sinon deceiving Priam. Your face would suit my traitor. Sit for me.”

Tito started, his expression flickering with something like alarm before settling into polite confusion. Nello laughed, shaking his head. “Piero, you impossible creature! Must you turn every face into a villain? Look at him—this is no deceiver’s face. Turn his eyes upward, and you’d have a Saint Sebastian to make devout women weep. Or crown him with myrtle and call him Bacchus—no, Apollo, for his face is as bright as morning. It won me over in the time it takes to say a *credo*.”

Piero di Cosimo gave a short, gruff chuckle. “Ah, Nello, if you ever stopped chirping long enough to think, you’d see why his face suits my purpose. The perfect traitor wears a face unmarked by vice—lips that lie with a dimpled smile, eyes too clear to betray guilt, cheeks that stay fresh even after murder. Not,” he added, holding up a hand, “that I accuse this youth of treachery. I only say his face is the kind that *could* deceive, if he wished. The lapidary doesn’t always know a gem by sight alone.” He stuffed tow in his ears and dropped into Nello’s chair. “Enough talk. Trim my beard.”

Nello rolled his eyes at Tito, as if to say, *Pay him no mind—he speaks in riddles.*

Tito, who had stood very still under Piero’s scrutiny, relaxed slightly. He turned back to Cennini, who examined the ring with a jeweler’s keen eye.

“A curious piece,” Cennini mused. “The intaglio—a fish and crested serpent in black onyx—is well set off by the blue layer. This ring must have a history.”

Tito smiled easily. “It was found in Sicily. Those who study such things claim it brings fortune, especially at sea, and helps recover what’s lost. Though,” he added with a rueful laugh, “it didn’t spare me a shipwreck. Still, I escaped drowning—perhaps that’s proof enough. As for my lost chests, we shall see. In the meantime, Messere, I’d ask you to hold the ring as pledge for a modest sum. I’ll redeem it soon, whether by selling other gems I carry or by finding scholarly work—if fortune favors me.”

Cennini nodded. “Come with me. My brother Pietro may have tasks to test your skills. Keep your ring for now; I’ll fetch the florins shortly.”

“Go, go,” Nello urged. “You’ll find no better guide in Florence. Born under a lucky star, this one—though which star, who can say? Not that it matters, since babies don’t pick their horoscopes. And our great Pico proved it all nonsense anyway. *Addio, bel giovane!* Don’t forget to return.”

Tito turned at the door, his face alight. “I wouldn’t dream of neglecting the man who’s promised to help me. That’s assurance enough, isn’t it?”

As the door closed, Nello shook his head at Piero. “Say what you will—a face like that belongs to a man one can’t help but like. Next you’ll claim Leonardo should’ve painted Judas as handsome as John!” He sighed. “Ah, but you’re deaf as stone with that tow in your ears. No matter. I’ll pry more of that boy’s story from him before I take him to Bardo Bardi.”

The Via de’ Bardi stretched along the southern bank of the Arno, a street steeped in the turbulent history of Florence. From the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de’ Mozzi, its uneven cobbles bore witness to centuries of ambition and strife. The houses on its right climbed the slope of Bogoli, where the city once quarried its unstable paving stones; those on the left leaned toward the river, their facades casting rippled reflections in the fading afternoon light.

Once, this street had belonged to the Bardi—proud, relentless men who wielded swords in the bloodied feuds of medieval Florence. They had risen from merchants to counts, their fortunes swelling until reckless loans to foreign kings brought ruin. Their palaces had burned in the uprising of 1343, their name scattered like embers on the wind. Yet like all old Florentine families, they clung to life, resurfacing in unexpected places.

Now, in one of the Neri houses—grand, somber, its barred windows like the eyes of a wary sentry—lived the last scholar of the Bardi line. Bardo de’ Bardi was a man of parchment and memory, his sightless eyes turned inward to the luminous past. Once, he had dreamed of compiling a great work, a bridge between antiquity and the modern age. But time and blindness had narrowed his world to a single room lined with books, where the scent of vellum and dust lingered like the ghost of lost ambition.

His daughter Romola stood beside him, her figure straight as a cypress, her hair a flame-gold cascade beneath its black fillet. At seventeen, she was already her father’s scribe, his reader, his tether to the world beyond the dark. But Bardo, though he loved her, saw only absence where a son might have been—a mind to sharpen his own, a voice to carry his legacy forward.

Today, she read to him from Politian, her voice steady as she traced the tale of Teiresias, blinded for glimpsing divinity. When she paused, Bardo’s fingers sought his own annotated copy of Nonnus, its margins dense with corrections in his precise hand.

*"Here,"* he murmured, *"is the fruit of Crisolora’s teaching—the Greek that Petrarch longed to know. But what use is scholarship without a successor?"*

Romola knelt beside him, her face alight with fierce tenderness. *"I will be your eyes, your hands. Teach me, and I will learn—"*

Bardo sighed. *"A daughter’s devotion is a gentle thing, but the paths of learning demand more than patience. They require fire, the will to wrest truth from the dark."* His voice roughened. *"If my son had not abandoned reason for fanaticism, if he had not fled to howl psalms with madmen—"*

Romola flinched but did not retreat. She knew the shape of this grief, sharp as the broken statues that lined the room.

*"Florence may remember me as it does Niccolò Niccoli,"* Bardo mused bitterly, *"a collector of relics, not a shaper of thought. And you—you must marry. A scholar, perhaps, one who would value my library..."*

*"No,"* Romola said, too quickly. *"Let me stay. Let me be enough."*

Bardo’s hand found her hair, stroking it absently. *"You are my comfort, child. But the world does not bend to a woman’s will."*

Silence settled between them, heavy with the weight of unspoken futures. Outside, the Via de’ Bardi lay quiet, its stones holding the echoes of all that had been—and all that might yet be.

When the door opened again, admitting the two visitors, Nello bowed deeply to Romola before ushering Tito forward. With a gentle hand on the young man’s shoulder, he guided him toward Bardo.

“Messer Bardo,” Nello said, his usual irreverence tempered into deference, “allow me the honor of presenting a scholar of Greece, eager to seek your wisdom. His name is Tito Melema—a man whose misfortune at sea has left him in need of patronage, but whose learning remains unshaken.”

Romola’s breath caught. The stranger before her bore no resemblance to the aged scholars who frequented her father’s chambers. He was young, his face alight with vitality, his dark eyes gleaming with intelligence. For an instant, memory flashed—her brother’s face, golden-haired and smiling, the last she had seen of him before he vanished from her life. But she steeled herself. Pride, that well-worn armor, settled over her features, masking the flicker of surprise.

Tito wore a simple black *sajo*, its unadorned hue throwing his sun-kissed complexion into vivid relief. There was an ease in his bearing, a quiet magnetism that seemed to draw the very air toward him. When he bowed to her, she returned the gesture with cool grace, though a traitorous warmth crept into her cheeks before she willed it away. His gaze, when it met hers again, held no presumption—only a gentle, almost pleading admiration, as if he sought permission to exist in her presence.

“You are welcome,” Bardo declared, his voice laced with the measured condescension of a man long accustomed to deference. “Misfortune paired with scholarship is a credential few can ignore. As you must know, since Crisolora first brought the light of Greek learning to Italy a century ago, no true scholar can content himself with Latin alone. To do so is to remain a barbarian in the eyes of Rome itself.” He gestured vaguely toward the chairs. “Be seated. Nello, you as well.”

Only when the rustle of movement assured him his guests had obeyed did Bardo continue. “From which part of Greece do you hail? I had thought your homeland drained of men who still carry the image of her glory, though I hear even the Ottomans now show some reverence for the ruins they once trampled.”

“I sailed last from Nauplia,” Tito replied, “but I have lived in Constantinople and Thessalonica, and wandered lands few Western eyes have seen since the Turks rose to power. Though I should confess—I was born in Bari. My first sixteen years were spent in Italy.”

Something in his words struck Bardo like a plucked string. His hand twitched toward Romola before he checked himself. “You are young,” he murmured.

“Three-and-twenty.”

“And your father—” Bardo leaned forward, voice hushed. “He saw to your education?”

A pause. A shadow flickered across Tito’s face, there and gone like a ripple in still water. “A father by adoption,” he said at last. “A Neapolitan, learned in both Latin and Greek. But he is lost to me now—vanished at sea near Delos.”

Bardo sank back, too tactful to press further. Romola watched, her pulse quickening. In those few words, Tito had breached the invisible wall that separated their world from outsiders.

Nello, ever the opportunist, seized the lull. “Clearly, the lad’s had fine training. The Cennini brothers set him to Greek texts at once—and they’re not men to waste time on dull blades.” He grinned. “Though, as they say, three things can’t be hidden: love, a cough, and ignorance.”

Bardo’s brows knit. “Nello, your wit would serve you better if applied to study rather than street-corner chatter. And if you must quote, let it be Virgil, not Pulci’s frivolities.”

Nello spread his hands in mock surrender. “Ah, but Tuscan comes too easily to my tongue! And my shop calls me back—my apprentice is a bungler who’ll ruin my reputation.” With a flourish, he bowed himself out, leaving Tito to Bardo’s mounting interest.

“You’ve seen Athens?” Bardo demanded, the earlier sorrow forgotten in scholarly fervor.

“Briefly. A fever-dream of ruins and Turkish forts. Our guide wept to see the Parthenon’s towers turned to minarets.”

Bardo’s fingers clenched. “Then you must record it—every stone, every inscription! Such a work could stand beside the *Itineraria* of Ciriaco. Though be warned,” he added wryly, “scholarship is a battlefield. They’ll call you forger, liar, worse—”

Tito smiled. “My father often said the same. He believed true learning lay not just in books, but in the earth itself.”

Bardo’s face softened. “A man of insight. Had he friends? Advancement?”

“Enemies, rather. He was… uncompromising.”

A knowing hum. Then, abruptly: “Will you write in Greek or Latin?”

Tito blinked, then chuckled. “You honor me with such faith in a work not yet conceived.”

“Faith? No. Necessity.” Bardo’s voice dropped. “I had once hoped for another to aid me in a great labor. Perhaps now…” His blind eyes turned toward Romola, unseeing, yet full of some unspoken plea.

Romola’s throat tightened. She saw the hope in her father’s face—fragile, desperate. And Tito? His expression was warm, open. Did he understand?

“I would be proud,” Tito said gently, “to offer what poor skills I have.”

Bardo exhaled. “Then we shall begin.”

Romola watched them, the scholar and the stranger, and something long frozen within her stirred. Dawn, perhaps, on a winter’s night.

Bartolommeo Scala, the esteemed secretary of the Florentine Republic, resided in a grand palazzo near the Porta Pinti—a home befitting his hard-won station. His coat of arms, an azure ladder upon a golden field, crowned with the motto *Gradatim*, declared his rise from humble origins to prominence, a journey he wore without shame. A man of vanity, perhaps, but not of false modesty; Scala believed in his own merit and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

By now, he had climbed to the highest rung of his ambition. Twenty years as secretary, embassies to Rome, the title of Senator, Knight of the Golden Spur—even the Gonfaloniere’s office had been his. Yet success had brought its burdens: gout plagued him, forcing him to sit cushioned and immobile beneath the shaded loggia of his palace, overlooking the lush gardens where the May sun warmed the grass and flowers.

On this particular afternoon, he was not alone. His daughter Alessandra and her husband, the Greek poet Marullo, sat beside him, along with a pair of agreeable listeners—men not so illustrious as to overshadow their host. Yet despite the pleasant company, Scala’s mood was soured by the papers spread before him: a bitter exchange of letters between himself and the scholar Politian.

It was a quarrel born of petty insults disguised as scholarly debate. Years ago, Politian had sought Alessandra’s hand and been refused. Since then, his pen had been sharpened against Scala. The secretary, though a capable statesman, fancied himself a writer—a dangerous indulgence for a man of his pride. He penned Latin verses in leisure, sending them to friends as if they were gifts rather than indulgences.

Then came the fatal misstep. In a playful epigram, Scala had made the *culex*—the gnat—feminine. Politian, ever the meticulous scholar, pounced. His correction, wrapped in mock courtesy, was a barbed Greek epigram of his own, invoking Venus and Cupid, a fashionable conceit of the time.

Scala, stung, retaliated with a jest at Politian’s failed courtship. The scholar’s reply was swift and scathing. What began as a light exchange devolved into venomous pedantry. Politian mocked Scala’s Latin; Scala dismissed Politian’s Greek as overwrought. The secretary accused the scholar of arrogance; the scholar derided the secretary as a pompous dilettante.

By the time Tito Melema arrived, the quarrel had reached its peak. Scala, eager for validation, produced the letters, seeking judgment from his new guest. Tito, ever the diplomat, obliged—flattering Scala’s wounded pride while deftly avoiding outright insult to Politian.

The gems Tito carried—an agate with a striking likeness of Cupid astride a lion, a "Jew’s stone" with a serpent design—provided further diversion. Scala, ever the collector, purchased both, one for admiration, the other as a talisman against his gout.

By the end of the visit, Tito had secured an open invitation to return. He left the palazzo in high spirits, amused by the absurdity of the *culex* debate. Fortune, it seemed, was smiling on him at last.

For those who dwell beneath northern skies, the dawn of Midsummer morning arrives with little fanfare—dew clinging to the grass, fresh shoots sprouting among the oaks, the quiet beauty of a meadow bathed in early light. Few remember, in that tranquil hour, that it is the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist.

But in Florence—Florence of the fifteenth century—such forgetfulness was impossible. The golden light spilling over the Arno seemed touched with divine favor; the pealing of the bells carried a message, clear and triumphant: this was the day of San Giovanni, the great summer festival of the city.

For eight centuries, San Giovanni had been Florence’s patron saint, ever since the Lombard Queen Theodolinda commanded her subjects to honor him. Some claimed his veneration stretched even further back, to the days of Constantine, when the Florentines—ever pragmatic—had gently retired their old protector, Mars, from his temple, though not without care. They placed him high upon a tower near the Arno, mindful of ancient warnings that to dishonor him would bring misfortune upon the city. But by now, Mars was long forgotten. The Republic no longer trusted in spear and shield, but in cunning and gold—gold stamped, fittingly, with the image of San Giovanni.

Much had changed since those shadowed days of transition. Florence had risen—wealthy, learned, a city of poets and bankers, its name spoken with awe in courts across Europe and beyond. A pope, with a jesting tongue, had called the Florentines *the fifth element*. And though some might credit the stars, or the Madonna dell’Impruneta, or higher powers still, much of the praise belonged to San Giovanni, whose face graced the republic’s florins.

So it was right that his feast day should be a spectacle unlike any other—ushered in by a vigil of dancing, jesting, and the occasional riot, but above all, by wonders devised by the ingenious Cecca, engineer and architect. Under his direction, saints floated through the streets on clouds of woven cloth, borne aloft by hidden supports; towering figures, masked and stilted, moved like giants descended from church frescoes. And if, after the solemn procession of saints, a rabble of black-clad demons capered through the crowds, brandishing claws and whips—well, even demons had their place in the divine comedy of Florence.

The morning of the festival dawned bright, as it always did. In the Piazza della Signoria, beneath the stern gaze of the Palazzo Vecchio and Orcagna’s Loggia, the offerings had been assembled—banners, horses draped in rich trappings, towering *ceri* that glowed like captured stars. The procession would wind its way through the city, past streets lined with cheering citizens, until at last it reached the Piazza di San Giovanni, where a canopy of blue drapery, stitched with golden lilies, sheltered the waiting crowd.

Among them, leaning from a window above Nello’s shop, was Tito Melema. He lounged with easy grace, one elbow resting on the sill, his fingers idly toying with a curl near his ear. His face bore the pleasant, untroubled expression of a man well-liked—neither too eager nor too aloof. Two months in Florence had only deepened his air of contentment, as though the city’s sunlight had seeped into his very bones. Behind him, Nello stood in holiday attire, while beside him Pietro Cennini, the scholar, observed the scene with quiet amusement.

Tito’s gaze drifted over the crowd below—then suddenly fixed on a figure near the steps of the Duomo. A Dominican friar, his head tonsured, his black-and-white robes unmistakable, stood staring up at him. The glance was brief, yet something in it struck Tito as peculiar. Not hostile, but... familiar.

“Nello,” he said quickly, “do you know that friar? The tall one, just there—”

But the man had already turned away, vanishing into the throng.

“One of the Frati Predicatori,” Nello said with a shrug. “I don’t make a habit of knowing every crow in the flock.”

Tito frowned slightly. “I could swear I’ve seen him before.”

“Not our Fra Girolamo, surely. Too tall—and besides, he’s not in Florence just now.”

“No,” Tito murmured, still searching the crowd. “No, not Savonarola.”

The conversation dissolved into laughter as Piero di Cosimo, the painter, cursed the clamor of the bells. But Tito’s attention lingered on the spot where the friar had stood. A face in the crowd—yet for a moment, it had felt like a thread pulled taut from the past.

Then the banners swept into view, the cavaliers in their finery, the towering *ceri* spinning in dizzying splendor, and the moment passed. Florence was in celebration, and Tito Melema, bright and untroubled, turned his smile back to the spectacle.

Tito descended into the bustling piazza, weaving through the crowd with practiced ease. Though he had dismissed Nello’s curiosity about the peasant girl, his thoughts flickered briefly to the pair of blue eyes beneath the white linen hood—Tessa’s, of course, though he saw no reason to name her. Some things were better kept to oneself, and Tito had always found silence a comfortable cloak.

Yet the memory of the girl soon dissolved beneath a sharper unease—the gaunt face of the friar, that unsettling gaze which had seemed to recognize him. Where had he seen those hollow eyes before? No matter. He brushed the thought aside like a cobweb. The sun was high, the procession had delayed him, and there were more pressing matters: Romola awaited him in the Via de’ Bardi.

He could picture the scene already—the way her father’s sightless face would brighten at his arrival, the faint flush that would rise in Romola’s cheeks, softening the regal bearing that made her seem both untouchable and achingly near. They were companions in her father’s study, equals in conversation, yet Tito felt an unspoken reverence for her, as though she were some ancient goddess of wisdom, not all-knowing, but rooted in something deeper than mere learning.

She was not like the peasant girls who had offered their smiles freely in his travels. Once, in Greece, a dark-eyed child had pressed close, lifting her face for a kiss with innocent boldness. But Romola’s affection, if it ever came, would not be so easily won. And yet—was it impossible? He was young, gifted, rising swiftly in Florence’s favor. Bartolommeo Scala had taken to him, and whispers had begun that he might soon fill the vacant Greek chair at the university, a rival to the proud Politian. With such prospects, might not even Bardo begin to see him as a fitting match for his daughter?

The thought warmed him as he turned down a narrow side street. His fortunes were blossoming—five hundred florins now lay in his possession, the last sum just delivered by Cennini. The old goldsmith had advised prudence, warning against reckless spending. *A scholar may marry,* he had said, *and should have something in readiness for the morgen-cap.*

Tito stood alone in his rented room above Nello’s shop, staring at the coins. A man’s ransom. The words echoed in his mind. Somewhere, under a harsher sun, might Baldassarre—aged, enslaved—be straining at a galley oar, nursing the hope that Tito would come for him?

The thought gripped him like a hand at his throat. *If it were certain,* he told himself, *I would go.* But certainty was a luxury he did not have. The ship had been taken by Turks; men had fallen overboard. Who could say if Baldassarre lived? To chase shadows across the sea would be to gamble all he had gained—his position, his security, perhaps even Romola.

And what debt did he truly owe? Baldassarre had plucked him from the streets as a child, yes, but the years had made the old man exacting, his affection curdled into possessiveness. Tito had repaid him with obedience, with charm—had that not been enough?

He exhaled slowly. *I believe he is dead.* The decision settled over him like a weight. Tomorrow, he would place the florins in Cennini’s hands. Tomorrow, he would choose the path of certainty over the gamble of devotion.

But choices, once made, have a way of hardening the heart. By dawn, the resolve had taken root, and with it came a quiet, creeping wish—that the sea had indeed swallowed Baldassarre whole, leaving no trace to trouble him. For if the old man lived, then Tito’s gold was not his own. And that truth, above all, must never see the light.

On the feast day of San Giovanni, three weeks had passed since Tito handed his florins to Cennini. As he strode toward the Via de’ Bardi, his demeanor betrayed no hint of unease—only the careless grace of a man untroubled by shadows. The sun beat down as he turned into a narrow street, pushing back his black beretta to let the breeze cool his brow. His face, smooth and unmarked by care, held neither deceit nor candor, only the effortless charm of youth.

The festive crowds thickened as he neared San Martino. Outside the tavern of the *Bertucce*, a raucous knot of onlookers had gathered. Tito might have passed by, but a child’s pleading cry—*"Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!"*—pierced the laughter. Pushing through, he found a young *contadina* struggling against a grinning *cerretano*, his fingers clamped around her wrists. The girl’s hood had slipped, revealing Tessa’s tear-streaked face, her blue eyes wide with fear.

At the sight of Tito, hope flashed across her features. Without hesitation, he stepped forward.

"Release her," he demanded. "What right have you to hold her against her will?"

The conjuror—a sharp-featured man with a mouth perpetually twisted in amusement—shrugged and let go. "I meant no harm, *Messere*! Only sought a willing assistant for my tricks. But it seems she prefers your company."

A ripple of laughter followed them as Tito guided Tessa away, her small hand clutching his sleeve. "Where were you going?" he asked, his voice low.

"I lost my mother in the crowd," she stammered. "If I return alone, my father-in-law will beat me."

Tito sighed. Duty warred with pity. He had no time for stray *contadine*, yet her trembling grip gave him pause. "Come, then," he relented. "I’ll take you as far as the Prato."

As they walked, Tessa’s tears dried like morning dew. She chattered of her home—the garden, the cows, the black-faced kid she adored—while Tito half-listened, his mind already leaping ahead to the obligations awaiting him. Yet her artless trust was disarming. When they reached the quiet stretch of the Prato, he found himself lingering beneath a broad plane tree, sharing apricots from her apron as she beamed up at him.

"You should be my little servant," he teased, brushing crumbs from her lap. "Would you like that?"

Her face lit with sudden joy. "Take me with you now! Then I wouldn’t have to go back."

But duty reclaimed him. With a laugh, he pressed a coral charm into her palm—a talisman against evil spirits—and kissed her brow. "Be brave, *piccina*. I’ll come see you another day."

As he walked away, Tessa sat motionless, clutching the charm. The world had shifted; the future shimmered with possibilities. For Tito, the interlude was already fading—a pleasant diversion, soon eclipsed by the demands of his ambitions.

Yet as he crossed the Arno, a stray thought flickered: *Would Romola ever look at me with such simple devotion?*

He quickened his pace. The Via de’ Bardi awaited.

When Fra Luca had finished speaking, Tito remained beside him, hesitating. Only when the friar rose and walked slowly into the church of Santa Felicita did Tito continue on his way along the Via de’ Bardi.

*If this monk is Florentine,* he thought, *if he stays in Florence, everything will come to light.*

A new crisis loomed, yet he suppressed his agitation. He would still visit Bardo, apologize for his absence, and maintain the illusion of normalcy. His talent for concealment sharpened—neutrality had become necessity.

It was still possible, perhaps inevitable, to admit Baldassarre’s existence—but doing so now would cast his past silence in a damning light. His quiet investments, his life in Florence, all would seem a calculated betrayal. No, better to delay. Tonight, he would think. Tomorrow, he would act.

By the time he reached Bardo’s study, his face betrayed nothing but polite regret. He spun tales of the Corso’s festivities, his voice animated, even as his mind raced elsewhere. When he left that night, his course was set.

The next day, he walked toward San Marco, resolved. He would learn what Fra Luca knew—how much he suspected, and for how long he would remain. Then, with that knowledge, he would craft a lie.

Tito had never needed lies before. But necessity bred invention. He convinced himself he owed Baldassarre nothing. Why chase a specter? The old man’s life had faded; his own bloomed with promise. Nature favored youth.

*Gratitude?* A hollow obligation. Baldassarre had lived; now it was his turn. The gems, the florins—yes, they were Baldassarre’s by law. But laws were mere traditions, twisted by men to serve themselves. Why should he sacrifice his happiness for a ghost?

He entered the cloister, inquiring for Fra Luca.

"Gone to Fiesole," a lay brother answered. "Taken ill. A litter carried him at dawn."

Relief washed over Tito. *Good. The friar might die. No one need ever know.*

He turned away, his conscience quieted—for now.

Tito moved through the streets with a buoyant step, the shadow of fear now dissolved by the warmth of anticipation. The thought of Romoa quickened his pulse, her presence a beacon that drew him forward. Yet her life stood in stark contrast to his own—a testament to devotion and patient endurance, virtues he had often sidestepped in favor of easier paths. Still, he was no stranger to goodness; youth thrummed in his veins, alive to beauty and pleasure, and the slow poison of compromise had yet to claim him entirely. He had bartered his soul, yes, but the weight of it was still light, the chains invisible. Life, for now, remained unchanged.

He would continue as he always had—winning admiration through cleverness, charm, and scholarly diligence. He would do nothing to sever the threads that bound him to those he cherished. And above all, he cherished Romola. No alliance in Florence, no matter how rich, could rival the prospect of her love. When she turned her earnest hazel eyes upon him, he felt a surrender sweet as music, a rhythm that held him captive and left him longing when it ceased.

As he climbed the stone stairs, the mere thought of her closeness quickened his breath.

"Welcome, Tito mio," Bardo’s voice greeted him before he could speak. The old man’s tone was stronger now, his face less shadowed than it had been months before. "You bring fresh manuscript, no doubt—but last night stirred new thoughts. We must retrace our steps, expand our scope."

Tito bowed to Romola before taking his customary place at Bardo’s side, settling onto the leather seat near his elbow.

"Of course," he said smoothly. "The manuscript can wait. My legs are young—I can climb the hill again without trouble."

He did not look at Romola, but he felt her gaze upon him, warm with approval.

"Well said, my son," Bardo replied—a title he had begun to use more freely. "Labor is life’s true reward, as the poets tell us. Too often, youth seeks glory without the dust of the race. But the Greeks knew better: ‘In the morning, work; at midday, counsel; in the evening, pray.’ Though some might call me an old man in evening’s shadow, my mind still holds counsel unspoken. You, Tito, have opened the floodgates. Now, we must revisit our foundations before the chance is lost. You understand?"

Bardo leaned to retrieve a fallen scroll, his hearing sharp enough to catch the rustle of movement.

Tito might have sighed at the prospect of such labor, but patience was natural to him. Besides, Romola’s presence made even tedious scholarship bearable.

"Certainly," he agreed. "You wish to expand upon our earlier citations."

"More than that—I would add excursus on the authors I have studied most deeply. Time is short, and I will leave no separate work. Scholarship must not lie idle while men like Calderino peddle falsehoods. Lorenzo Valla’s Latin Thucydides, for instance—the very text Pope Nicholas paid five hundred scudi for—demands scrutiny. Even great names must not shield error. Fetch me the volumes, Romola—you know where they rest."

Tito rose with her, following as she led him into the adjoining room, its walls lined with ordered ranks of books.

"There," she said, pointing upward. "Just as they were when his sight failed."

Tito lingered beside her, the air between them charged with unspoken feeling.

"I hope," she murmured, turning her eyes full upon him, "he does not weary you. This work brings him such joy."

"And me as well," he whispered, stepping closer. "If only you would let me say—I love you. If only you would think me worth loving."

His voice was a breath, his face near enough that she could see the plea in his dark eyes.

"I do love you," she answered, the words so soft they might have been thought rather than spoken. The quiet majesty of her bearing remained, but never before had her voice trembled so. For a suspended moment, they simply looked at one another. Then she added, "Now I know what happiness is."

Their lips met, a fleeting brush of warmth before Tito turned, swift as light, to reach for the volumes on the shelf. They parted without haste, the sweetness of the moment too precious to disrupt with haste.

Bardo had not yet grown impatient.

"You have the books, Romola?" he asked as they returned. "Now take up your pen. As Tito marks the scholia we select, copy them at once—numbered carefully to match his text."

Romola obeyed, seating herself at the table opposite Tito, where she could pass her father whatever he needed. The routine was familiar, yet today, every glance, every accidental touch hummed with new meaning. There was no need for contrivance—her calm joy enveloped him like evening light, serene and all-encompassing.

They worked until dusk faded from the room. Then the door swung open, breaking the spell.

Monna Brigida entered, a riot of color and clatter in the quiet study. Pearls gleamed at her throat, her velvet gown sweeping the floor, her plump fingers clutching a devotional book as gilded as the rest of her.

"Cousin!" Bardo said, recognizing her step.

"Always the troublesome cousin," she declared, fanning herself with her veil. "If I didn’t bring news, you’d forget the world exists outside these dusty books!" She kissed Romola’s cheek and launched into tales of a dismal wedding—bride and groom solemn as saints, guests more pious than festive.

Bardo cut her short with a raised hand.

"Francesco Valori is not entirely wrong," he interjected. "Florentine excess has grown wasteful. But we still hold to magnanimous frugality—unlike the Neapolitans, who laugh at our borrowed plate!"

Monna Brigida rolled her eyes. "Well, no one laughed today. The Piagnoni sucked all joy from the room. And don’t get me started on that Frate of theirs—the things he’s said about women! False hair, vanity—as if we’re all vultures picking at corpses!"

"Silenzio!" Bardo’s voice cracked like a whip. "Hear me once more: bring no word of that name into this house. Romola, you are forbidden to ask. My son is dead."

The room fell silent. Monna Brigida shrank back, then slipped away with a flurry of meaningful glances.

Tito’s mind raced. So Bardo’s son lived—a monk, returned. Fra Luca. The resemblance to Romola and Bardo suddenly made sense. If only the man were dead. The wish was ruthless, but necessary. Bardo’s pride might shield Romola from contact, but not from the risk of exposure.

The silence stretched. At last, Romola ventured, "Shall I light the lamp, Father?"

"No. We are done for tonight." Bardo’s voice was weary. "Tito, sit with me."

As Tito obeyed, Bardo sighed. "I never told you I had a son. He is dead to me. A scholar like you, but wild, drawn to mysticism over reason. We will not speak of him again."

His hand found Tito’s shoulder, gripping it as if for steadiness. The past was a wound still raw, but for now, he chose to turn away.

The languid heat of a September afternoon draped Florence in drowsy stillness. In Nello’s shop, the barber himself lay sprawled across his bench, cap pulled low over his eyes, one leg dangling toward the floor where a volume of poetry had slipped from his grasp, its pages crumpled beneath him. Nearby, Sandro sat hunched in a corner, engrossed in a solitary game of *mora*, his fingers moving with solemn deliberation as if conducting some private ritual.

Tito stepped lightly across the threshold, his movements as fluid as the melody he plucked from the lute strings. Leaning over Nello, he sang softly:

*“Quant’ è bella giovinezza,
Che si fugge tuttavia!
Chi vuol esser lieto sia,
Di doman non c’è certezza.”*

Nello startled awake like a bird roused from its perch, his cap flying off as he sat bolt upright. “Ah, my radiant Apollo!” he exclaimed, rubbing his eyes. “I see I’ve overindulged in poetic slumber. A dangerous habit—too much verse turns the mind to mush, like wine left too long in the cask. But what brings you here, my Orpheus? That coaxing lilt in your voice tells me you want something.”

Tito laughed, fingers still tracing idle patterns on the lute. “You know me too well. I’ve come for a touch of your art—not the razor, but the comb. My curls are in dire need of your skill before I present myself at the Scala palace. The young Cardinal de’ Medici will be there, along with that sharp-witted Bernardo Dovizi. I’d rather outshine them with scent than wit.”

Nello seized his comb with a flourish. “Ah, you needn’t fear rivals, my golden one. Dovizi may be quick, but he’s a weasel, all teeth and no soul. You? You’re the man who’s charmed even the sour-tongued Messer Angelo—no small feat!”

Tito’s smile flickered. “Messer Angelo could swallow me whole and never notice. But flattery will do for now.”

Nello worked deftly, his chatter winding on. “Ah, but you’re too modest! The city adores you. And why not? You’re the very model of devotion—always hurrying back to the Via de’ Bardi, to that grand labor of yours. Though I’ve heard whispers that even posterity may struggle to decipher its meaning.”

Tito’s amusement was thin. “Perhaps. But for now, I’ll settle for Romola’s favor.”

Nello paused, comb hovering. “Ah! So the betrothal nears? Unless old Bardo sets you some Herculean task first.”

“If only his demands were so simple,” Tito murmured. Then, as if struck by a sudden thought, he added, “You mentioned Cronaca earlier—something about San Marco?”

Nello shrugged. “Only that your scholar’s runaway son has returned. Fra Luca, they call him now. Cronaca saw him there.”

A ripple of tension passed through Tito, though his fingers remained steady as he smoothed the fallen book’s pages. “I thought he was still in Fiesole.”

“Not according to Cronaca. But what do I know of monks and their comings and goings?” Nello waved a dismissive hand. “There! You’re fit to dazzle the Medici. Though I’d avoid the Piazza della Nunziata tonight—the *Fierucoloni* are in, and their hymns to the Virgin won’t drown out the occasional flying stone.”

Tito left with a jest on his lips, but unease coiled in his chest. The Scala palace offered little distraction—the young cardinal had not appeared—and as he departed, the sinking sun cast long shadows across his path.

Then, rounding a corner, he halted in surprise. A veiled figure moved toward him, unmistakable in her bearing despite the somber drapery. Romola. At her side waddled Monna Brigida, her usual finery replaced by drab simplicity.

“Romola?” Tito stepped forward, his voice low. “What’s happened?”

She lifted her veil just enough to meet his eyes. “I’m going to San Marco,” she said softly. “My brother—Fra Luca—is dying. He’s asked for me.”

Tito’s pulse stuttered. “You’re defying your father?”

“For the first time.” Her gaze held his, steady but sorrowful. “He thinks I’m with Brigida. My godfather knows the truth. You remember when we spoke of Dino?”

“Yes.” The word was a whisper.

Romola’s voice was firm. “I must go. He was once dear to me, and if he seeks forgiveness at the end, who am I to deny him? You understand, don’t you?”

Tito’s smile was a fragile thing. “You are always right, *mia Romola*. Even in trusting me too much.”

She touched his hand briefly before drawing her veil down once more. “Go now. We mustn’t be seen together.”

Monna Brigida huffed. “Yes, off with you! The fewer eyes on us, the better. And if anyone asks, we’re just two pious women off to vespers, not—”

Tito bowed, pressing Romola’s fingers to his lips before turning away. The gesture was courtly, foreign, and it left him with a bitter taste.

Alone, he paced the shadowed streets, his mind racing. Fra Luca would ask questions. Romola would answer. The past would unravel, and with it, every carefully spun lie.

By the time he reached the Piazza dell’ Annunziata, the sun had fled. The lanterns of the *Fierucoloni* flickered like distant stars, their hymns rising into the dusk. He stood at the edge of the crowd, unmoored, a man without a path—ready, in that moment, to follow any voice that called.

The clamor of the piazza struck Tito like a wave as he stepped into its chaos—shouts, laughter, the rhythmic chant of peasants, the shrill taunts of boys, the erratic clanging of bells. Nello had spoken of the *Fierucoloni*, the festival where country folk and city crowds clashed in a whirl of noise and motion. Tito let himself be swept into it, the jostling a welcome distraction from the gnawing dread of an uncertain future. For a fleeting moment, he even considered searching for Baldassarre—but the thought dissolved like mist.

At each entrance, bodies pressed inward beneath swaying paper lanterns, their glow bobbing like drunken fireflies. The air was thick with competing sounds: a droning peasant hymn cut through by shrieks, whistles, the sharp *crack* of a stone shattering a lantern overhead, followed by fresh uproar. On the fringes of the tumult, pockets of order emerged—ragged men with sharp eyes lured bystanders into games; stilt-walkers, lanterns in hand, loomed like wandering specters; a vendor hawked steaming *berlingozzi*, their scent cutting through the sweat and dust. Near the loggia of the Foundling Hospital, a pill-seller perched on a barrel, promising salvation from toothache and drowning, while a tumbler’s platform drew a ring of gaping onlookers.

Tito moved without direction, buffeted by the crowd. Once, he found himself beside a train of peasant women—their faces wind-hardened, their garb faded relics of another century. These were the *contadine* of Pistoia, bearing bundles of yarn, their hearts heavy with meager hopes and nameless fears, all to be laid before the Virgin’s unveiled image tonight.

Then the tide pushed him toward the piazza’s edge, where vendors clustered like barnacles on a ship’s hull. There, against a pillar, stood Bratti, his basket clutched tight, his mouth a line of disdain. Two women hovered near, curiosity piqued by his secrecy.

"What’s in there, then?" one demanded.

Bratti’s smirk was mercenary. "Nothing for idle eyes. My wares are too fine for gawkers. Only two left—and with plague rumors, they might be worth gold soon. *Andate con Dio*."

The women exchanged glances. "What’s the price?"

"More than you’ve got," Bratti scoffed. But after haggling, he relented, unveiling two crude iron rings—supposedly Roman relics, their rust a testament to antiquity. The women bit, and Bratti pocketed his coins with a show of wounded generosity.

Spotting Tito, he seized his arm. "Luck smiles! I’ve a Genoese buyer for that ring of yours—deep pockets, heading to Hungary. He’d pay handsomely."

Tito’s fingers brushed the ring absently. A relic of Baldassarre, once sentimental, now a chain. Selling it might have spared him Fra Luca’s recognition. *No more ties to the past.*

"Name the time," he said.

"Sunrise, at my stall." Bratti grinned. "And don’t forget old friends when silver flows."

Shoving free, Tito drifted back into the throng, where a new spectacle unfolded: a charlatan in a yellow-stained mitre, peddling mock marriages for a quattrino. "A bargain blessed by Pope Alexander himself!" the man crowed, waving a forged bull. Tito knew that voice—Maestro Vaiano, the mountebank. Memories of Tessa flickered. Was she here, lost in this sea of faces?

A scuffle erupted as Vaiano’s monkey, provoked, leapt onto a shrieking woman’s head. In the chaos, Tito slipped into the church of the Nunziata, the noise dying behind him. Inside, the air was thick with incense and devotion, the fresco of the Annunciation glowing like a jewel in its lamp-lit chapel. Peasant women knelt or stood, their rough hands clasped, their eyes upturned. Above them, the walls teemed with waxen effigies—saints, warriors, popes—a silent, staring host.

Then, near the door, he saw her: Tessa, slumped against the wall, her blue eyes drowsy, a string of cocoons dangling from her fingers. Hunger and exhaustion had dulled her usual brightness.

"Tessa," he murmured.

She blinked up at him, as if he were a dream. "You came back."

"You’re hungry," he said, helping her rise.

She nodded. "The *Madre* is sick. I brought these for the Virgin." She clutched the cocoons like a talisman.

He led her into the cloisters, away from the crowd. "Where will you sleep tonight?"

"The church," she said simply. "Like the others."

A refuge, he thought. A world without judgment. As the night pressed in around them, Tito felt the weight of his choices—and the fleeting comfort of a hand too innocent to hold a grudge.

Romola reached the entrance of San Marco to find one of the friars awaiting her. Monna Brigida withdrew into the neighboring church, while Romola was led to the chapter-house in the outer cloister—the farthest point a woman was permitted to tread.

The door opened to reveal a dim chamber, lit only by the flickering glow of two tapers behind a narrow bed. There lay Fra Luca, his gaunt face hollowed by suffering, his golden tonsure gleaming faintly against the pallor of his skin. His sunken eyes, fixed upon a small crucifix in his hands, barely flickered as Romola entered. Another monk, cowled and silent, stood beside him but withdrew as she stepped forward.

For a moment, the frescoed wall behind the bed seemed to pulse with sorrow—pale faces of saints and martyrs gazing down as if bearing witness. Then Romola’s eyes met her brother’s, and the past surged within her.

"Dino," she whispered, the name a wound.

She did not move closer. The sight of him—this emaciated stranger in a monk’s robe—stirred not tenderness but a cold fury. Here was the boy who had abandoned their father, cloaking his desertion in piety. Her father, a man of reason and dignity, had scorned the superstitions of the Church, and Romola had inherited that scorn. To her, the monastic life was a retreat from duty, a surrender to fear.

Yet beneath her anger, something ached. The brother she had once loved was now a specter.

"My sister," he said, his voice thin but clear, "you come in time. I have a message for you."

Her pulse quickened. Perhaps at last, remorse—some word for their father.

But his next words chilled her. "A vision has come to me. Thrice I have seen it. A warning—for you."

Her jaw tightened. Visions. The very thing her father despised. "Dino," she interrupted, "have you no words for our father? He was left alone, his sight failing. Do you feel nothing for that?"

His gaze, distant as a star, settled on her. "I have never regretted leaving," he answered, passionless. "The world was poison to me. Our father lived among sin yet refused to see it. He buried himself in dead words while souls withered around him. I heard a call he could not."

Romola’s hands clenched. "He lived with honor! He sought truth, not empty rituals!"

Dino’s breath rasped, but he pressed on. "Philosophy could not save me. Only divine love. I fled to purify my soul. And now—" He shuddered, fighting for air. "Now I return with a warning."

Before she could protest, another voice cut through the shadows—deep, resonant.

"Kneel, daughter."

She turned. A figure emerged from the gloom, cowl shadowing his face. Only his hands were visible—slender, almost translucent, folded in repose. Then he reached to steady the crucifix in Dino’s weakening grip, and the movement pushed back his hood.

The face of Fra Girolamo Savonarola.

Romola knew him by reputation—the fiery prior of San Marco, a man whose sermons raged like storms. Yet his eyes, now fixed on her, were not fierce but sorrowful.

"Pride bars grace," he said softly. "Kneel."

Against her will, Romola felt the command like a weight upon her shoulders. Slowly, she sank to her knees.

Dino’s voice rose again, labored but urgent. "In my vision, I saw our father’s house. You sat by his chair. A man—faceless—took your hand. You led our father into the streets, to Santa Croce. The priest who wed you had the face of death. Graves opened. The dead followed you like a bridal train."

Romola’s skin prickled. A dream, she told herself. Only a dream.

But Dino continued. "You journeyed to a barren land. No water, only parchments unspooling endlessly. Bronze figures mocked you. Our father fell. The faceless man revealed himself—the Tempter. You were left alone."

His breath failed. A fit of coughing seized him. When he spoke again, it was a whisper.

"Three times, this came to me. A warning: marriage is your snare. Renounce the heathen pride of—"

The words dissolved into silence. His lips moved soundlessly.

"Dino!" Romola cried, but his gaze had drifted beyond her, beyond the world.

Fra Girolamo stepped forward. "Take the crucifix," he murmured. "His sight has left it."

Numb, Romola obeyed. As her fingers closed around the cold wood, grief tore through her. She bent over her brother’s body and wept.

Death had been an abstraction until now. Here it was—raw, irrevocable.

When at last she rose, Fra Girolamo guided her out. The cloister was hushed, the night air sharp with stars.

"Go in peace," he said. "Let this be your beacon."

Romola clutched the crucifix beneath her mantle. The vision clung to her, a shadow she could not shake. Yet stronger still was the pull of the living—her father, Tito. She quickened her steps, desperate for the warmth of familiar voices, for proof that life still held light beyond this darkness.

But Tito was not with Bardo. Fear had driven him away. And the truth that might have parted them forever now lay buried with Fra Luca, lost in the silence of the grave.

The dawn had scarcely broken when Tito made his way back from Bratti’s shop through the narrow streets of the Ferravecchi. The Genoese stranger was gone, having taken the onyx ring with him, and Tito carried fifty florins in its place. A fleeting thought crossed his mind—what if fortune, in one of her capricious turns, spared him the need to leave Florence? Then he might regret having parted with the ring, which he had worn as a token of sentiment. But the thought was fleeting, drowned beneath the weight of his present unease. The feverish dread that had first seized him had dulled into a heavy resignation. He cared too deeply for the comforts that came with men’s good opinion to risk their scorn now.

Yet deeds, once done, are beyond recall. They take on a life of their own, separate from the will that birthed them. And Tito, for the first time, felt the relentless grip of consequences closing around him.

He avoided the Mercato Vecchio, unwilling to chance an encounter with Tessa. There was nothing left to seek—only to wait and see how fate would twist next.

The Piazza di San Giovanni was bathed in golden morning light, the kind that lingers in autumn, soft and inviting. The air hummed with the quiet bustle of a feast day. Stalls bloomed with flowers and fruit near the baptistery; mules stood patiently chewing their feed while their owners slipped inside to offer prayers to the Virgin on this, her Nativity. On the steps of the Duomo, clusters of idlers gathered—old women coaxing toddlers to take their first steps, boys carving faces into cherry stones, men laughing in loose knots or arguing in hushed, animated pairs.

But the liveliest crowd was the one Tito could not avoid. Nello’s shop was at its busiest, and under the warm awning, men lounged in the barber’s chair, chins lathered, submitting to the razor’s edge with the solemnity of a ritual. Gossip flowed as freely as the wine at a banquet, and Nello’s tongue was as sharp as his blade. Tito braced himself—he would have to smile, to jest, to wear ease like a mask. A man who let fear choke him was a man already defeated.

Then a hand clapped his shoulder.

The touch sent a jolt through him, and his face betrayed the shock before he could smooth it away. The owner of the hand laughed—a bright, mocking sound.

Niccolò Macchiavelli stood before him, keen-eyed and clean-shaven, his close-cropped head giving him the look of a man unburdened by excess. His expression was lively, untouched by the bitterness that would one day shadow it.

“What nightmare haunted you last night, Melema, that my friendly grip made you jump like a hare before the hounds?”

Tito forced a laugh. “Messer Niccolò, my dull blood must be sluggish indeed to shudder at your wit. A restless night, nothing more.”

“Unfortunate,” Macchiavelli said, “for today demands brilliance. The Rucellai Gardens await—peacocks, ortolans, the finest minds of Florence. Unless,” he added with a smirk, “love holds you captive?”

“Messer Bernardo was kind enough to invite me, but I am otherwise engaged.”

“Ah, love!” Macchiavelli rolled his eyes. “A tyranny even Piero de’ Medici cannot rival. Though I warn you, the evening will drown in impromptu verses. A contest where the smallest wits crow the loudest.”

Nello, razor flashing, called over, “What’s this of Piero and small wits? A secretary should know better than to yoke the great and the petty.”

“I speak only truth,” Macchiavelli countered. “If great men must bow to others’ wills, why should small men expect their words to go unchallenged?”

The talk turned to politics—Soderini’s defiance, Medici influence, the murmurs of unrest. Tito half-listened, his mind elsewhere, until Cronaca’s voice cut through:

“Messer Tito, you’ve heard, then? Bardo’s son is dead.”

A pause. Tito kept his face still. “No, I had not.”

“His sister reached him in time,” Cronaca said. “Fra Girolamo said it was a miracle—his breath held just long enough for a final confession.”

Tito’s pulse quickened. His fate, then, was sealed. He must retrieve his money from Cennini before the truth spread. He feigned sorrow, letting his gaze drift downward, playing the part of a man stricken by grief.

Nello, ever observant, mistook his silence for mourning. “Ah, poor Romola,” he murmured, then turned his attention back to the doctor who had just ridden up—a toad-faced Paduan, sweating and self-important, his horse skittish beneath him.

“Maestro Tacco!” Nello called, grinning. “Come, let me shave you with the same razor that tended Benevieni himself.”

The doctor puffed up. “You compare a surgeon to a true scholar of medicine? A barber’s craft to the mysteries of Avicenna?”

Nello feigned regret. “Ah, but your noble science is too exalted for common minds. Already, tongues wag—they say you mix toads’ blood and whispered curses into your cures.”

“Lies!” the doctor sputtered, lurching up, the shaving cloth still around his neck. “I will show you my medicines—see, they bear no stench of witchcraft!”

As he fumbled in his saddlebags, Nello exchanged a glance with Sandro. The horse, startled, jerked back—and with a well-timed nudge from Sandro, the beast bolted, dragging the doctor’s precious remedies into the street, scattering vials and powders across the cobblestones.

The piazza erupted in laughter.

And Tito, watching, felt the ghost of a smile touch his lips—not from mirth, but from the bitter knowledge that in Florence, even a man’s ruin could be turned into a jest.

The loggia atop Bardo’s house stood taller than the neighboring buildings, its open gallery enclosing the square walls. Facing the street, the roof was upheld by slender columns, but along the remaining sides, arched openings pierced the walls, offering shelter from prying eyes. Here, Romola often walked unseen, gazing over the jumble of rooftops toward the distant rise of Bogoli.

Now, beneath one of those arches, Tito waited, the slanting sunlight mocking the ruin of his hopes. He had never fooled himself into believing her love could outlast the truth of his deception. Yet as he pictured her approaching—her golden hair catching the light, her hazel eyes soft with sorrow—he found himself wishing, bitterly, that she had been less noble. That she might have let him hold her just once before the end.

The door moved. A flash of gold, a tremulous cry—"Tito!"—and then she was against him, weeping into his shoulder.

All night, Romola had ached for this moment. Death had pressed too close: the hollow-eyed specter of her brother, the stranger’s voice spinning tales of lives she could not fathom. Only Tito had ever turned sorrow into warmth. Now, with him, she could finally break.

She clung to him, childlike in her abandon. Proud before the world, here she was stripped bare.

"My Romola—my goddess," he murmured into her hair, dizzy with relief. Shame, parting—none of it mattered now. Her love was a shield, her delusion his salvation. He kissed the curve of her neck, drunk on the scent of her.

When she finally lifted her face, neither spoke. They simply gazed, fingers tangled in each other’s hair—hers white against his dark curls, his bronzed hands lost in her gold. For the first time, there was no space between them.

But the shadow crept back. Romola’s voice was steady, though her hands still trembled. "I must tell you everything. You’re the only one who can make sense of it."

He already knew. "Dino is dead," he said, his eyes deep and unreadable.

She nodded, the memory raw. "So changed, Tito. And so hard—no word for our father, only that vision he forced upon me. Yet his face—the way he looked at the crucifix without seeing it—" Her throat closed.

She forced herself on, recounting every word, every gasp, until she reached the vision’s end. Then she fell silent, staring past him as if the ghost still lingered.

Tito’s pulse eased. No specter of Baldassarre haunted this tale. But he let the quiet stretch, watching her.

"I took the crucifix," she whispered at last. "It’s in my room now."

He caught her hands. "Put these thoughts aside. Visions are the fever-dreams of starved monks, not for you."

"But Dino believed it divine. And Savonarola—his voice felt like truth. Not madness."

"Your heart was raw," he soothed. "Your brother was lost in mysticism, no different from Ficino’s Platonists. As for the Frate, he’s a showman who thrives on fear. In calm light, you’ll see it clearly again."

"Not for Dino." Her gaze turned inward. "Last night, I studied the crucifix for hours. At last, the face seemed to weep with him."

"Romola." He tilted her chin up, his voice tender. "Must you dwell where only nuns should tread? My golden Aurora was made for sunlight."

She wavered. The loggia’s arches framed a world of green hills and vibrant life—so far from the death-chamber’s gloom. How could both exist in the same breath?

The doubt flickered, brief as a sigh, before his smile burned it away.

"You’re drifting from me," he chided softly.

She melted. "Never."

"Prove it," he teased. "Will you always see me as the serpent in your Eden?"

"How could I? Before you, love was a scholar’s dry footnote. You made it real."

He kissed her fingers, marveling. "What woman matches you?" Then, carefully: "Yet you don’t press for our marriage."

"I will. But my godfather will argue for delay—Dino’s death gives him cause."

Tito shrugged. "I grudge him nothing. Though I’d sooner see you in the south, lazy under the sun, while I sang you to sleep."

She laughed, but her eyes were sad. "Joy feels stolen while others grieve. Come—let’s go to my father. Your voice quiets him."

"Not yet." He caught her wrist, his thumb brushing her pulse. "Must I beg for a kiss?"

The blush that answered him was worth every lie.

As they turned toward the stairs, Tito glanced over Florence. "Even its laughter is edged. I’d take you where life is softer."

Romola’s smile was wistful. "I dream of it. But pale faces haunt even the brightest gardens. For now—let’s go."

Tito left the Via de’ Bardi with a lightness in his step, his mind unshackled from the dread that had shadowed him. The encounter with Romola and her father had passed smoothly, leaving no trace of suspicion. Now, free from immediate danger, his thoughts drifted back to those tense hours when fear had clouded his judgment.

He regretted parting with the ring. It had been a careless act, one that might raise questions. Romola, familiar with its sentimental value, would wonder at its absence unless he crafted some plausible excuse—perhaps a generous gift to justify the sale. The thought of spinning yet another lie left a bitter taste in his mouth. He had grown weary of deception, though fortune had spared him the consequences. Who had truly suffered for it? Baldassarre’s fate was too distant, too uncertain, to weigh on his conscience.

Then there was Tessa. Sweet, trusting Tessa, her heart brimming with childish devotion. She clung to the fantasy he had allowed her to believe—that they were bound in marriage. Should he seek her out, strip away the illusion, or let time unravel it gently? There was a tenderness in her innocence, a charm in her wide-eyed faith. Perhaps he might visit her again, bring some trifle to soften the eventual blow, ensure her stepfather had not turned harsher since her mother’s death.

Or—better yet—confess the truth to her now, then tell Romola, enlist her kindness in securing the girl a better future? No. That path was barred. Romola must never know of the *cerretano’s* farce, the mock marriage, the web of half-truths Tessa still believed. The risk of discovery was too great. Even a simple soul like Tessa might turn spiteful when her dreams crumbled. Best to let her forget, let some village boy replace him in her affections.

*Dunque*—farewell, Tessa. May fortune favor you.

As for the florins from the ring, he had plans for them. A clever idea had taken root—one that would explain the missing jewel to Romola and, more importantly, shield her from those morbid religious fancies he found so distasteful. With this in mind, he turned his steps toward the Via Gualfonda, seeking Piero di Cosimo, whose talent for myth and whimsy suited his needs.

The artist’s courtyard was quiet, the heavy door muffled with wool, its knocker bound tight against noise. Tito rapped lightly, then with more force, but no answer came. He was on the verge of leaving when a child with a basket of eggs approached, stood on tiptoe, and called through a small iron grate: *"Messer Piero!"*

The door creaked open, revealing the painter in a red nightcap and paint-splattered tunic, sleeves rolled to the elbow. His sharp eyes flicked over Tito with little warmth.

"What brings you to my door, Messer Greco?" Piero’s voice was dry. "If you wanted something, you should have asked at Nello’s. I don’t entertain uninvited guests."

Tito smiled, unruffled. "Forgive the intrusion. I was struck by a sudden fancy and thought only of your skill in bringing such visions to life."

Piero’s lips twisted in a habitual grimace, skepticism warring with curiosity. "And what vision is this?"

"A triptych," Tito explained. "Bacchus and Ariadne, but not as Ovid tells it. I want the golden-crowned Ariadne beside him, the ship draped in ivy, leopards at his feet, dolphins leaping in the waves. Above them, Loves with arrows tipped in roses—"

"Enough!" Piero cut in. "I know the tale. Show me the passage. I’ll not be drowned in another man’s words." He gestured Tito inside.

The studio was chaos—a riot of sketches, half-finished canvases, scattered bones, and the occasional live creature: pigeons perched on easels, toads ambling by the door. A white rabbit nibbled contentedly from a box of bran, oblivious to the artistic disarray.

Piero snatched up a manuscript, scanned the lines Tito indicated, then fixed him with a knowing look. "You want them to wear familiar faces, eh?"

Tito laughed. "I wouldn’t presume to ask Ariadne to sit for you. The commission is private."

"Pity. I’ve been meaning to paint Bardo and his daughter as Oedipus and Antigone. Convince them to pose, and I’ll slip their likenesses into your Bacchus and Ariadne."

"Agreed—if I can persuade them."

Piero waved off talk of payment. "If they sit, that’s fee enough. You’ll cover the cost of the case, nothing more."

"And when shall I sit for Bacchus?"

"I’ve already caught you," Piero said, rummaging through canvases. He pulled out a sketch and thrust it forward.

Tito froze. The figure was himself, arm raised in jubilation, face contorted in terror. The eyes were wide, the lips bloodless, as if staring into some unseen horror. A chill prickled his skin.

"You’re halfway there already," Piero remarked, amused. "Ghosts are a specialty of mine. I’ve yet to decide—should they be solid, like a corpse walking, or half-formed, like mist?"

Tito forced a laugh, shaking off the unease. "You delight in mingling dread with mirth. Death’s-heads on your shelves, Loves stealing Mars’ armor—are you a philosopher in disguise?"

"Philosophy is lying with grand words. I paint. That’s truth enough." Piero ushered him toward the door. "Send word through Nello’s when you’ve spoken to Bardo. No need to return."

Tito raised a hand in parting, still smiling, though the image of his own frightened face lingered behind his eyes.

Messer Bernardo del Nero remained firm in his counsel: the wedding must wait until Easter. Bardo, ever swayed by his old friend’s pragmatism, acquiesced without protest. Yet even Bernardo, though still unmoved by Tito’s charm, could not ignore the young man’s rising fortunes. Success, after all, was a language the worldly understood well.

Tito’s star was ascending swiftly. The young Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici had taken a liking to him, even hinting at a place in his retinue for the impending journey to Rome. Among Florence’s elite, Tito’s presence had become a coveted ornament—his easy wit, his deference to Piero de’ Medici’s skill at games, his effortless grace in conversation. And as his social standing grew, so too did the luster of his learning. He was clever enough to bear the weight of high expectations without stumbling.

For Bernardo, old loyalties warred with new realities. It was one thing to distrust a stranger’s smooth manners, another to dismiss the favor of Florence’s most powerful families. If the Medici circle embraced Tito, who was he to withhold approval? The boy had, after all, brought light into Bardo’s dim world, and Romola had blossomed under his attention. The promise of their first meeting had been fulfilled beyond measure.

Bardo himself had long been a half-forgotten figure, his scholarly pride dismissed as tiresome, his misfortunes met with perfunctory pity. But now, whispers followed him again—no longer of the blind old man who grumbled over neglected work, but of the father whose daughter would soon wed a rising favorite. The library, once silent, now echoed with visitors: elderly scholars drawn by curiosity, young men who found excuses to linger in the company of Romola’s quiet radiance. She moved among them like Firenzuola’s ideal of womanly grace—regal yet modest, her dignity untouched by the admiration swirling around her. And all of it, every glance and smile, was woven into the bright fabric of her love for Tito.

Even Bardo’s dearest hope—that his library might endure as a monument to his name, not swallowed by some monastery or scattered to the winds—seemed within reach. Tito spoke confidently of securing the Cardinal’s influence, and Bernardo, though cautious, allowed himself to believe it possible.

Only in private did they speak of the library’s true cost. Bardo clung to the illusion that his estate, save for the books, was unencumbered. He would not—could not—face the truth: that without intervention, Romola would inherit little but debts.

"It’s time to settle things," Bernardo told Romola one afternoon beneath the loggia. "If Tito can provide for your father’s needs, let the rest be sold. The library must be preserved."

Romola nodded. "Tito has always known my father comes first. He cares for him as I do."

Bernardo’s smile was wry. "Ah, so he’s not flesh and blood like the rest of us? Your father filled your head with books to keep out folly, yet here you are, as smitten as any girl who knows nothing but her prayers."

Romola laughed. "As if I love him only for his pretty speeches! You know better. I love him because he is good—as I love you and my father. And if he is handsome too, why shouldn’t I rejoice in it? Beauty is but another word for goodness made visible." She tilted her head, mischief in her eyes. "You were handsome once yourself, godfather. Tall as Tito, with fiercer eyes—though perhaps less kindness in them."

"Ah, so Romola claims all the pride for herself?" Bernardo chuckled, softened despite himself. "At least Tito asks no dowry. A modest suitor, by Florentine standards."

The matter was settled. Bardo, when told, waved a vague assent, his mind already drifting back to the safer realm of manuscripts and musings. Tito, ever adaptable, turned his quick mind to the intricacies of Florentine finance—the *Monti*, the value of land, the workings of banks.

"He learns so quickly," Romola observed. "Soon you’ll forget he wasn’t born here."

Bernardo gave her a knowing look. "Men like Tito belong to no city, child. Their minds are too light to carry the burdens the rest of us do."

But Romola only smiled, too sure of her happiness to doubt.

The Carnival’s final week had seized Florence in its riotous grip. The streets pulsed with life—masked processions wove through the throng, voices rose in bawdy songs, and the *rigoletto* spun dancers beneath the crisp winter sky. Yet beneath the revelry lurked a darker current: boys, emboldened by the season’s chaos, barricaded alleys with poles, demanding tribute for their bonfires. Stones flew, mischief turned to menace, and the laughter of the crowd carried an edge.

A more refined observer might have winced at the spectacle—had a painter dared to capture its truth, the canvas would demand to be turned to the wall. But Florence’s blood ran quick, her mirth sharp as her quarrels; there was no northern sluggishness here, no southern stealth with a dagger. The city’s joy was bright, reckless, and fleeting.

Through this tumult strode Tito Melema, swift and purposeful, Bernardo Dovizi at his side. Their conversation was animated, their gestures lively, yet Tito’s eyes flickered intermittently over the crowd. At the turn toward the Ponte Rubaconte, his step faltered—there, weaving through the throng, was a face he had hoped not to see. *Tessa.*

The betrothal loomed that evening, an event meticulously prepared. Rooms in Bardo’s home had been refurbished with Tito’s florins; the date, moved forward at his insistence. Rome called him away—Cardinal Giovanni’s patronage was not to be refused—and he would not leave without the security of Romola’s pledge. The ceremony would bind them before law and custom, defying even Bernardo del Nero’s reservations.

Yet now, here was Tessa, her blue eyes wide with forlorn hope. Tito quickened his pace, steering Dovizi deftly toward the Via de’ Bardi. If he could reach Bardo’s door without her accosting him—

But fate was unkind. As he descended the steps afterward, a small hand clutched his sleeve. He turned sharply. "Tessa! You mustn’t follow me. Not here."

Her lips trembled. "The *patrigno* beats me," she whispered. "I waited—you never came—"

The street was too quiet, too perilous. A single witness—Bernardo, or worse—could unravel everything. Yet her tears pricked at him. He led her to the bridge, spoke in softened tones. "Listen, my little Tessa. I go to Rome, but I *will* return. Be patient. No one must know of us—*no one*—or I’ll be lost to you forever. Promise me."

She nodded, swallowing sobs. He pressed a kiss to her brow, then turned away without looking back.

The betrothal procession moved with stately grace toward Santa Croce, Bardo’s frail form guided by Tito and Romola. She was resplendent in white and gold, a lily crowned with pearls, her joy luminous. Tito, cloaked in velvet, carried a secret gift—a painted tabernacle, Piero’s work.

"Look," he murmured in the frescoed *salotto*, unveiling the triptych. Bacchus and Ariadne, entwined in gold. "Your sorrows are buried now. The crucifix is hidden—see? Only joy remains."

Romola’s fingers traced the images—the ship on calm seas, the ivy that never faded. "You’ve crowned my life," she whispered.

Yet as they stepped into the piazza, a funereal chant rose. A macabre procession loomed—Time on a black-shrouded car, the Hours wailing behind him. Romola stiffened. For a breath, Dino’s vision seemed to writhe to life.

Tito squeezed her hand. "A Carnival grotesquerie, nothing more."

But the chill lingered. As the torches flared, casting their glow on the newly betrothed, Romola’s gaze flickered backward—toward shadows not so easily dispelled.

*Forward*, Tito had urged. Yet the past, like the crucifix in its gilded tomb, was merely concealed. Not erased.

And the night deepened.

The air of Florence hummed with anticipation on the 17th of November 1494. Eighteen months had passed since Tito Melema and Romola’s joyous Easter wedding, where showers of rainbow comfits had rained down upon them in the ancient Greek tradition—a promise of sweetness for their shared life. But the world beyond their happiness had shifted. The city that had once reveled in Medici splendor now stood on the brink of a new era, its fate tangled with the grand upheavals of Italy.

Only a week before, Florence had erupted in rebellion. The great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio had sounded the alarm, and citizens—armed with rusted blades, tools, and whatever makeshift weapons they could seize—had driven Piero de’ Medici and his brother, the scheming Cardinal Giovanni, from the city. The gates of San Gallo had slammed shut behind their fleeing figures, their names now cursed, prices set upon their heads. The Republic had stirred from its long slumber, scrubbing away the painted effigies of old conspirators, welcoming back exiles, and reclaiming its voice.

Yet now, the very streets that had echoed with defiance were draped in color. Banners unfurled from windows, carpets lined the cobblestones, and garlands arched over the thoroughfares—all for the arrival of an uninvited guest. From the Via Larga to the Porta San Frediano, Florence had adorned itself like a bride awaiting a conqueror.

At the city gate, a platform stood ready for the Signoria. Messer Luca Corsini adjusted his robes, rehearsing the Latin oration meant to flatter their visitor. The elders of Florence smoothed their silk *luccos*, their chins freshly shaven for the procession. Young nobles preened in French-cut tunics, eager to impress. The clergy, from the resplendent archbishop to the humblest friar, debated the order of their march, relics and sacred banners in hand. All awaited the hour of three, when the foreign king would enter their gates.

And what a king he was! No ordinary invader, but a force unseen in Italy for centuries. His army spilled through the Alpine passes—Swiss mercenaries, steel-eyed and ruthless; French knights in gleaming armor; infantry bristling with arquebuses; and bronze cannons that hurled iron death, drawn not by oxen but by swift horses. Some whispered he was Charlemagne reborn, destined to cleanse the Church and humble tyrants. Others likened him to Cyrus, liberator of God’s chosen.

His name was Charles VIII of France, and his ambitions stretched beyond mere conquest. He would march through Italy in triumph, settle the strife of Rome, reclaim Naples by right of blood, and then—so the grand vision went—lead Christendom against the Turks. To many, his coming seemed ordained by heaven, a divine reckoning.

Yet not all trembled in pious awe. For years, one voice had foretold this moment. Fra Girolamo Savonarola, the Dominican prior of San Marco, had preached of a flood sent by God to scourge the wicked. When news of the French invasion reached Florence that September, he had stood in the Duomo and thundered from Genesis: *"Behold, I bring a flood of waters upon the earth."* To him, Charles was no mere king, but an instrument of wrath and renewal.

But Florence was no Nineveh. Though some knelt in repentance, others gripped hidden weapons. Behind the festive drapery, militias drilled in shadowed courtyards. Barricades stood ready, stones piled high on rooftops. The French might come as saviors in Savonarola’s sermons, but their vanguard had already strutted through the streets, marking lodgings with insolent chalk. The citizens, fresh from overthrowing the Medici, had not forgotten how to fight.

As noon passed, the city held its breath—not in submission, but in readiness. Whether Charles would be conqueror or pawn, liberator or scourge, Florence would meet him on its own terms. The banners fluttered, the choir practiced its hymns, and the stones lay waiting. The 17th of November would decide much.

The Prisoners

The sky hung heavy with grey clouds, but the Piazza del Duomo blazed with color beneath its canopy of blue drapery, embroidered with golden lilies and coats of arms. Banners fluttered from the corners of the Baptistery, though the steps of the cathedral remained bare, trodden by the steady stream of worshippers. It was the hour of Advent sermons, yet not all who filled the square were bound for prayer. Some lingered in clusters, deep in debate; others scanned the crowd with sharp eyes, as if waiting for something—or someone.

At the edge of the piazza, where the festive hues gave way to the dull stone of the streets, a knot of laborers stood armed with tools and makeshift weapons. Their faces were hard, their postures tense. Among them, Ser Cioni, the sharp-tongued notary, held court.

"Never tell me," he sneered, "that Swiss mercenaries or French foot soldiers can hold these streets. Florentines need fear no army on their own ground."

A dyer, his arms stained crimson, grinned. "The French learned that well enough. Those fine lords strutted in, thinking our city a pretty trinket for their taking. But we showed them steel and stone soon enough."

A butcher, fingers twitching near his knife, added, "Piero de’ Medici won’t dare return, not after how we drove him out like a cur. But those French cannons—" He spat. "God help us if they turn them on the walls."

Ser Cioni waved a dismissive hand. "Fools! The French are here for Florence’s glory. Providence guides them."

His words were cut short by a murmur spreading through the crowd. A group approached—Lorenzo Tornabuoni, flanked by a French nobleman in gaudy silks, and beside him, Tito Melema, clad in mourning black soon to be covered by the red mantle of his office. His smile was effortless, his voice smooth as he translated the nobleman’s questions. The crowd’s stares were less than friendly—whispers of *Medicean* curled like smoke in the air—but Tito’s charm held, even as Piero di Cosimo’s dry wit drew reluctant laughter.

Then, a disturbance. Down the Via de’ Cerretani came French soldiers, dragging three bound prisoners. Two pleaded for alms, hands outstretched. The third—an old man, gaunt but unbowed—remained silent, his dark eyes burning.

The Florentines’ patience snapped.

"Cut them loose!" someone shouted. A boy darted forward, knife flashing. A rope parted. The old man ran—straight into the piazza, straight toward Tito.

Their eyes met.

Time froze.

Baldassarre’s grip locked onto Tito’s velvet sleeve. The younger man’s face drained of blood. Around them, the world blurred.

"An escaped prisoner," Lorenzo remarked.

Tito’s lips moved without his will. *"Some madman, surely."*

For an instant, hatred pulsed between them—then Baldassarre vanished into the shadows of the Duomo.

The piazza buzzed, unaware of the silent storm that had just passed.

Piero let out a scornful chuckle. “Easily startled, aren’t you? My portrait doesn’t do the man justice. But that old fellow had the eyes of a beast—I ought to see him again in the Duomo.”

Lorenzo Tornabuoni offered a polite excuse for Tito’s reaction. “Being seized by a madman—if that’s what he is—would unsettle anyone. Then again, perhaps he’s merely a brute. We’ll find out soon enough.” He turned to the Frenchman beside him. “In the meantime, we should see if our authority can quell this squabble between your men and ours.”

With swords drawn, they advanced toward the murmuring crowd, flanked by curious onlookers. Tito followed, his mind racing. The initial shock of terror had passed, replaced by the sharp cunning of a cornered creature. He needed to know what others had learned of Baldassarre—what threats lay ahead.

The rabble of men and boys, more inclined to jeer than fight, scattered at the sight of armed signori. The French soldier was questioned. His story was simple: they had brought prisoners into the city to beg ransom. Two were Tuscan soldiers captured in Lunigiana; the third, an elderly man, had been taken with a group of Genoese near Fivizzano. Harmless, perhaps unhinged—the soldier knew little else, unable to understand the stranger’s ravings.

Tito absorbed these details, deaf to all else until Tornabuoni addressed him directly.

“Returning with us, Melema? Or will you follow the fashion and hear Savonarola? The Frate’s sermons are the safest refuge for Medicean sympathizers these days. I’d join if I had the time.”

Tito’s composure had returned, his smile smooth as ever. “I, too, admire the Frate’s eloquence,” he said lightly. “But duty calls—I’m expected by the Segretario until the procession.”

Piero shrugged. “I’m for the Duomo. That old man’s face is worth another look.”

“Then show him to the hospice for travelers,” Tornabuoni suggested. “Let the monks decide if he belongs in a cage.”

As the group dispersed, Tito turned toward the Palazzo Vecchio. The walk was brief, yet each step felt drawn out, weighted with dread. Baldassarre alive—here, in Florence—was a serpent uncoiling, slow and certain in its strike. The man’s hatred was no fleeting rage but a devouring force, relentless as fire.

Why had he let go? Why vanish? Tito knew the answer: vengeance required planning. A blade in the dark, perhaps, or public ruin. Worse—what if his own panicked words held a grain of truth? Baldassarre *had* seemed unhinged. Madness might discredit any accusation he made.

Yet madness wouldn’t stay a dagger.

Tito’s fingers twitched at his side. He had no taste for violence, but neither would he wait helplessly. His mind darted through defenses: lies woven tighter, alliances secured, escape routes mapped. Flight from Florence? Possible, though distasteful.

One path never crossed his thoughts—confession. To kneel before Baldassarre, before Romola, before the world? Unthinkable. Truth was no anchor but a millstone. His safety lay in cunning, in the art of shaping reality to his need.

A bitter realization settled over him: this was the price of a life built on ease. Every choice to evade hardship had led him here—to a moment where all his wit might still fail against the simplicity of a blade.

Baldassarre stumbled forward, his wrists bound, the rough rope biting into his neck and chest. As he shoved past the heavy curtain into the vast expanse of the Duomo, he halted, stunned.

He had expected hollow silence—cold altars, dim paintings, the distant murmur of monks at prayer. Instead, the cathedral pulsed with life. A sea of faces, rich and poor alike, stretched before him, all turned toward the pulpit where a Dominican friar stood, gripping a crucifix, his voice a blade cutting through the hush.

For a moment, Baldassarre did not hear the words. Suspicion flickered in the eyes of those nearest him—his ropes marked him a fugitive, but the church was sanctuary. His haggard face, the wildness in his gaze, softened their wariness into pity. Soon, they forgot him entirely, drawn back into the sermon’s fervor.

Romola, standing near a side door, had seen him enter. The sight of his grey hair, the agony in his expression, struck her. There was something familiar in his bearing—had their eyes met? But no, his stare was vacant, lost in some private storm. The rope around his neck spoke of suffering, and her heart ached for him, though she did not yet know why.

Then, like thunder shaking his bones, the preacher’s voice crashed over him:

Baldassarre’s head jerked up. The friar—Savonarola—stood with arm outstretched, crucifix raised as if it were a weapon. The words seared into Baldassarre’s mind, feeding the fire of his rage. Here was a voice that did not waver, that promised justice as fierce as his own thirst for it.

Savonarola’s sermon was a storm, sweeping from condemnation to plea. He spoke of Florence’s sins, of coming wrath, of the French king as God’s instrument—*"a sharp sickle mowing down the wicked."* His tone shifted, molten with anguish: *"For you I languish, for you my soul melts away..."*

The crowd swayed as one, breath held, tears glistening. Even the skeptical were caught in the tide. When Savonarola fell to his knees, sobbing, a great cry rose from the multitude—a single, shuddering breath of shared sorrow.

And in that moment, Baldassarre’s own broken voice joined them.

The promise of divine retribution had seized him like a fever. Philosophy, reason—all had burned away. Only this remained: the certainty that hatred could be eternal, that vengeance need never end. The preacher’s vow of self-sacrifice echoed his own dark oath—*Let my blood flow, if it means his suffering forever.*

His nails dug into his palms. A sob tore from him, not of repentance, but of triumph. The fire he carried within him had found its echo in the voice of God’s fury.

As Baldassarre stood transfixed by Savonarola’s voice, he did not notice another man slip through the doorway behind him—Piero di Cosimo, who cared little for sermons but had come solely to observe the escaped prisoner. When the preacher’s words lapsed into wordless fervor, the artist stepped forward and touched Baldassarre’s arm.

The old man turned, his face still wet with tears, yet his breath came steady now, as if the storm within him had passed. Piero spoke low:

"Shall I cut those bonds? I heard how you were taken."

Baldassarre studied him, suspicion flickering in his gaze. At last, he said, "If you will."

"Better step outside," Piero suggested.

Again, that wary look. With a dry smile, Piero drew his knife and severed the ropes. The man’s face was strange—deep-carved, as if sorrow had plowed furrows into him. *Perhaps the rumors of his madness are true*, Piero thought. *Well, if he runs wild, they’ll only bind him again. Let the poor devil have his chance.*

"You don’t trust me," Piero murmured. "You won’t tell me your name."

Baldassarre flexed his arms, savoring the forgotten freedom of movement. His voice was quiet but firm. "I have nothing to tell."

"As you please," Piero said, shrugging. "But if you need shelter—Florence feeds strangers, even those with torn cloaks and empty bellies. There’s a hospice outside the gates. Your French guard is gone; no one will trouble you."

Baldassarre gave a curt nod, and they left the Duomo together.

As they walked the Via dell’Oriuolo toward Santa Croce, Piero eyed his companion. "Ever sat for a portrait? I’d pay you for the privilege."

The suspicion returned, sharper. "No."

Piero laughed shortly. "Straight on, then—Porta Santa Croce, the hospice beyond. You’ll take nothing more from me?"

"You’ve done enough. I thank you."

With another shrug, Piero turned away. *A ragged old lion*, he mused. *Ugly as a storm-riven oak. What a contrast to my smooth-faced Bacco, wed to his Antigone against all myth and sense! Ah, but how his scholar’s face paled when those claws seized him!*

Back in the Piazza del Duomo, the crowd spilled from the doors, dispersing with the haste of those who’d lingered too long at devotion. Piero leaned against the Baptistery, watching them pass—a tapestry of Florentine life.

Here were the great men of the city: Francesco Valori, proud as a condottiere, his Piagnone zeal burning hot; Soderini, cooler, a statesman’s caution tempering his faith. Nearby stood Piero Capponi, who’d sooner draw his sword than parse a sermon, yet cheered any voice that cried *Liberty!* Lawyers rubbed shoulders with artisans, their minds stuffed with Accursius but their hearts alight with the Frate’s fire. Among them, Luca Corsini—who’d one day hurl stones for God and freedom like any street brawler.

Scholars moved quietly, their faces alight with dreams of the cowl: Strozzi, Acciajoli, the young painter who’d soon trade his brushes for a friar’s robe. And there, threading through the throng, the poet Benevieni, hastening perhaps to Pico’s deathbed.

Women walked with studied plainness, their noble bearing the only hint of rank. Tradesmen clutched their purses, wary of new taxes. Artisans, their hands calloused from labor, hung on every allegory, each biblical riddle a lantern in their dim workshops.

Yet not all were believers. Mediceans feigned devotion, biding their time. Republicans tolerated the Frate as a tool. And his enemies lurked—aristocrats scheming to reclaim power, dissolute youths who cursed him for spoiling their sport. Even Machiavelli came, sharp-eyed, taking notes for some distant friend. And Dolfo Spini, who’d hunt the Frate as men hunt a wolf.

No preacher had ever held such sway over so motley a flock. And Savonarola’s power lay in this: he spoke to the highest in them and the lowest. His visions stirred the soul, yet his certainty fed their hunger for signs. He demanded sacrifice, yet flattered their fears.

A man who must rule multitudes is shaped by them. What began as pure fire—zeal for God, for justice—now bent to the people’s cravings. Yet let us not whitewash him with chalk, as priests once whitened sacrificial bulls. The victim is spotted, but the heart laid upon the altar still beats with the hope of something greater.

By evening, Florence exhaled a collective sigh of relief—the grand entrance of the new Charlemagne was finally over. For those perched on rooftops, the spectacle had been magnificent: the relentless march of drums, the blare of trumpets, the thunder of hooves along the Pisan road, all woven into the fevered pealing of church bells. No sun gilded the banners, the spears, the plumed helmets, or the silken surcoats, but neither did dust obscure them. The absence of glare made the procession clearer—the towering Scotch archers, the hulking Swiss halberdiers, the nimble Gascons, the cavalry gleaming like figures from a chivalric romance. It was a comfort to believe such force would only be turned against the enemies of God.

Yet for the Signoria, stationed at the gates to deliver their carefully prepared welcome, the grandeur had been marred by mishap. Rain had cut short their Latin oration, reducing them to fumbling French. In the chaos, Tito Melema, ever the poised servant of opportunity, stepped forward. With effortless grace, he offered the king a few flawless words, then melted back into the crowd, letting others claim the credit.

The rest of the day had unfolded with similar imperfection. The Florentine procession, meant to meet the king at the Cathedral, found its path blocked and detoured through back streets. The monarch himself, beneath his jeweled canopy, looked less like Charlemagne and more like a hastily carved grotesque—his reptilian mouth, his exaggerated nose, his spindly leg resting on pearls and cloth of gold. But magnificence, as the old diarists sighed, was in the eye of the beholder. The canopy was torn to shreds by the crowd (as tradition demanded), the king kissed the altar, and by nightfall, the soldiers camped in the Prato, the nobles dispersed to palazzi, and the streets blazed with lamps, turning night to day.

Tito moved through the illuminated thoroughfares, his mind churning. The day had been a gauntlet of hidden anxieties. Now, slipping away from the revelry, he sought clarity. If only he had recognized Baldassarre sooner—if only he had not frozen. But regret was useless. The path was set. Survival demanded cold precision.

As he turned onto the Via dei Benci, the clang of an anvil cut through the night. The workshop of Niccolò Caparra stood ahead, its loggia framed by stone columns, the smith’s massive silhouette pounding rhythmically against the forge’s glow. Tito paused, struck by an idea.

Niccolò noticed him only after shaping an axe-head to perfection. “Business, Messer Tito?” he grunted.

“Indeed. That fine coat of mail I saw here—I need it.”

Niccolò’s eyes narrowed. “For whom?”

“Myself.”

The smith snorted. “Twenty florins. And only because it’s second-hand.”

Tito counted the coins. Niccolò handed over the armor, its links whispering like a secret. “It’ll stop a blade,” he muttered. “But wearing it? That’s carrying fear with you.”

Tito smiled thinly. “Scholars are cowards, Niccolò. Our pens don’t thicken our arms like your hammer does.”

Folding the mail beneath his cloak, he vanished into the night.

Romola paced the worn stone floor of the library, her fingers trailing along the spines of ancient volumes as she listened for the echo of footsteps in the courtyard. The day had been long, the city alive with the clamor of the French king’s procession, yet she had not ventured to the windows. Since her father’s passing—sudden, silent, three months prior—she had withdrawn from spectacle, from celebration.

*"Is Tito not coming to write?"* Bardo had asked that final evening, his voice thin with unspoken dread. The bell had long since tolled the hour, but Romola had hesitated, smoothing the truth with gentle evasion. *"He is supping with the cardinal, father. You know how sought-after he is."*

Bardo had exhaled, grasping at the frail hope of progress on his beloved library. *"Then perhaps he brings word…"* But the words had faltered. A flush of resolve had overtaken him. *"No matter. Fetch the pen, Romola. I must speak on the New Platonists. Time runs short."*

She had turned, pen in hand, to find his stillness absolute.

Now, in the quiet of the firelit chamber, guilt threaded through her grief. Even as she wept, the treacherous thought had come: *Perhaps, now, Tito will be wholly mine.*

The dream of shared happiness had not withered, but it had bent beneath the weight of reality. Tito remained tender, attentive—yet the months had worn his patience thin. She had watched her father’s disappointment deepen as Tito’s presence grew scarce, his excuses polished as gemstones. *Not his fault,* she told herself. *It is the way of men. They cannot be bound as women are.*

She had learned to temper her expectations, to smother the sting of neglect beneath devotion. If he slipped away to gatherings, it was only because his bright spirit craved vivacity. If he winced at her frugal suggestions—that they might live simply, repay Bernardo, preserve the library—she chided herself for severity.

Tonight, she vowed, would be different. No sighs, no silent reproaches. The empty chair beside her father’s desk need no longer divide them.

At last, the groan of the courtyard door. She flew to the stairs, the lamp above casting gold on Tito’s upturned face as he climbed. Eighteen months had softened her sharp angles into something gentler, yet as she reached for him, her hands trembled.

"You’re weary," she murmured, brushing dust from his cloak.

He barely met her eyes. "This room is too cold," he muttered, sinking into the chair by the fire. "Must you haunt this place?"

The words stung. "The catalogue—you know I work on my father’s plan. You’ve no time to aid me."

Tito rubbed his face, his voice hollow. "Forgive me. I’m unwell."

She knelt beside him, her fingers grazing the hard ridge beneath his tunic. "What is this?"

"Armor." His tone was flat. "Necessary, now."

Her breath caught. "You’re in danger?"

"All who don’t rave against the Medici are." He forced a smile. "This is my shield against daggers in the dark."

*Daggers.* The word conjured visions of stealthy blades, of Tito bleeding in some alley. She bit back protests—this was no whim. Yet the metal beneath her touch felt like a betrayal, as if the man she loved had been encased in something alien.

"Tell me of today," she urged, shifting the subject.

He obliged, recounting the day’s pageantry with forced levity. The failed oration, the jest in stumbling French—*his* jest, though he shrugged it off. The glitter of gifts, the feasting. But when she asked of Florence’s fate, his smile dimmed. "Peace? Only if Florence pays for it."

Then, hesitantly, she confessed: "I went to the Duomo today. To hear Savonarola."

Tito stiffened. *Had Baldassarre spoken?* But Romola misread his alarm.

"You’re surprised," she laughed. "I wanted to understand—this frenzy over the French. And Tito… when he spoke of martyrdom, I wept with the crowd. There was power in it."

"Take care," he teased, masking unease. "Next you’ll see visions, like Dino."

She described the ragged prisoner, the rope at his throat, the tears on his weathered face. Tito’s fingers tightened on the chair.

"I saw him," he said carefully. "A fugitive. Did he linger?"

"Piero di Cosimo led him out." She touched his brow. "You’re pale. Rest now."

He rose, the weight of the armor—and the unspoken specter of Baldassarre—dragging at his limbs. The future stretched before him, a narrow path lined with knives. And Romola, sweet and unsuspecting, stood at its center, her love a beacon he could neither embrace nor escape.

Four days had passed since the French had entered Florence, and the city simmered with uneasy tension. The streets Romola walked were lined with foreign soldiers—proud, aloof—and Florentines who watched them with narrowed eyes. What had begun with feasts and marvels—cloud-borne angels in the churches, the king’s gallantries at balls—had soured into resentment. The French behaved as conquerors, speaking of viceroys and Medici restoration, as if Florence were a prize rather than an ally. The air was thick with defiance, murmured in the halls of the Old Palace and muttered between clenched teeth in the piazzas.

Romola moved through it all like a shadow, her black veil drawn close, old Maso a steady presence at her side. She had little patience for the bristling pride of men today; her mind was fixed on Piero di Cosimo’s workshop. The painter had promised—or rather, avoided promising—a copy of her father’s portrait as Oedipus, and she meant to claim it. Time blurred memory, and she clung fiercely to every relic of him, as if devotion could mend the fractures of the past.

Piero answered her knock with a grumble about eggs, then brightened when he saw her. “Ah, Madonna Romola! You’re better than eggs,” he admitted, eyeing the basket of sweets she carried. His studio was its usual chaos—half-finished canvases, scattered tools, the scent of linseed oil thick in the air.

“I’ve come to see if you’ve kept your word,” she said lightly, though her pulse quickened.

“Word? I never give it,” Piero retorted, quoting the old rhyme about promises unkept. But he gestured to the Oedipus, propped against the wall. “Look for yourself.”

As he lifted it, another painting was revealed beneath—a small oil sketch of Tito, startling in its rawness. Romola caught her breath. There he was, her husband, but not as she knew him. This Tito wore an expression of stark terror, a rope coiled around his neck like a serpent.

“What is this?” she demanded, her voice sharper than she intended.

Piero stiffened. “A whim. I needed a face.”

“But the rope—the fear—”

“A prisoner I saw once,” he muttered, snatching the sketch away. “It’s nothing. A daub.”

Too quick. Too eager to dismiss. Romola’s throat tightened. She had seen that prisoner herself—the wild-eyed man in the Duomo, the one who had clutched at Tito. And now this.

She swallowed her questions. Pride sealed her lips. To doubt aloud was to betray Tito, and that she would not do. Instead, she turned to the portrait of her father, losing herself in the familiar lines of his face, the quiet intensity of his blind gaze.

“You’ve captured him,” she said softly.

Piero snorted. “No need for tears. It’s only paint.”

She left him with sweets and a jest, but the image of Tito’s painted fear clung to her as she walked home. The chain armor, the prisoner, the sketch—coincidences, perhaps. Yet they coiled in her mind like the rope in the painting.

*Ask him*, whispered reason.

*No*, answered fear.

To question was to crack the fragile trust between them, and she could not bear to see what might spill out.

The old man had vanished—gone toward Arezzo at dawn the next day, as if fleeing the lingering stench of his French captors. I sought him at the hospital, curious whether those broth-stirring monks had discerned madness in him. But they found none—only a distant mind, planting phantom vines leagues beyond their walls. A strange old beast. I would have liked to unravel his mystery.

Piero di Cosimo’s voice cut through the hum of Nello’s barber shop on that brisk November afternoon, the twenty-fourth. An unusual hour for such a gathering, but Florence hummed with anticipation—today, the city’s fate might turn. Streets pulsed with idlers, craftsmen hovering like wood shavings on a breeze, ready to surge at the slightest spark.

Nello lounged against the shop window, lute in hand, his gaze flicking toward the piazza. With a flourish, he set the instrument aside. “A pity! To miss the French waddling after their escaped prisoners in those absurd shoes—all because I left my post to scrape some patrician’s chin. My piazza conspires against me. The moment I step away, it births spectacle.”

Piero’s lips curled. “You should have seen your polished scholar then, Nello. Pale as a gutted fish when that madman seized him. Nothing like watching your honey-tongued darlings stripped bare by chance.”

Nello waved a dismissive hand. “Vinegar suits your own eggs, Piero. Would you stand unmoved if a rabid dog latched onto your wrist? Bravery? Pfah. Florentines trade in wit, not brute force. Why else do duller men fight for us?”

A voice chimed from the inner room—Francesco Cei, sardonic. “Treason, barber! Florence grinds her swords now. Even the Frate’s visions preach it: Mars himself looms over the Palazzo Vecchio, arm slung round San Giovanni, sharing honeycomb.”

Nello smirked. “Let the thick-skulls bombard Pisa. The rest of us will think—and shave. Piero here, if he craves valor so, might wield his brush like a halberd. Challenge some gap-toothed mercenary to single combat.”

Piero spat a laugh. “Your tongue’s a millrace, Nello—endless, and just as empty.”

“Not so. Would you fault a man for flinching at thunder? You burrow like a rat at the first storm. Each has his quirks. Mine’s mint. Tito’s? Unwashed prisoners clutching at his sleeve.”

The shop erupted. Piero, scowling, stuffed tow in his ears as Nello crowed. “No, no—Florence needs her scholars. Pico gone to paradise, Poliziano to… well. Best not speculate. We can’t spare Tito to slander.”

Francesco leaned in. “Speaking of prophecies—Camilla Rucellai outdid the Frate. Two years back, she swore Pico would die ‘in the time of lilies.’ November? ‘French lilies,’ she says now. A stroke even Savonarola couldn’t match.”

Pietro Cennini’s brow furrowed. “Mock the Frate less. These weeks, his voice alone has stilled our feuds. If the French king balks at fair terms today, it’s Girolamo who’ll bend his ear to reason.”

Nello nodded. “True enough. My best clients swear by him. Though young Niccolò says visions stretch like Dido’s oxhide—meaning whatever comes next. A woman dreams of a snake, breaks a cup at dawn, and cries, ‘Aha! The serpent meant this all along!’”

Cronaca’s voice was sharp. “The Frate’s no charlatan. He names the future plain: the Church scourged, heathens converted—and soon. No loopholes.”

A sudden roar from the piazza cut him off. Nello bolted upright, cap flying as he lunged for the door. “Ho! The crowd’s afire—something’s happened!”

The street seethed. Then, carried aloft on a commandeered bench, Tito Melema appeared, laughing as the tide of bodies bore him forward. His cap dangled by its cords; spotting Nello’s group, he flashed a grin and raised a beckoning hand. In moments, he’d leapt onto a merchant’s cart near the Baptistery, the mob hushed beneath him like hounds at a whistle.

His voice rang clear. “Florentines! I bring news—good news. The French king signs an honorable treaty. But the credit?” A dramatic pause. “Piero Capponi.”

The name detonated. “Capponi! What did he say?”

Tito rode the clamor. “The king demanded too much—growled, ‘I’ll sound my trumpets!’ And Capponi?” A flourish. “‘Then we’ll ring our bells!’ He tore their terms to shreds and turned his back on royalty itself.”

The crowd roared. Tito drank it in. “France learned today what Florentine liberty means. The treaty’s signed. Our galleys will fly the French banner—but above it, ‘Liberty!’ That’s your victory. Won by a citizen who speaks as you would.”

Cheers crested. Tito’s smile deepened, scanning the sea of flushed faces—weavers, dyers, men who’d never held a sword but now felt Capponi’s defiance as their own. How easy it was, this alchemy of words, turning a Medici friend into the people’s herald.

Then—a face. Clean-shaven, close-cropped, a decent hat shading fierce eyes.

Baldassarre.

The cart, the crowd, the triumph—all vanished. Only that gaze remained, a brand searing through time. Tito’s leap to the cobbles was instinct, his pulse a drum of war. No flicker of recognition had betrayed him. But the dread coiled tighter: if Baldassarre was sane, why delay his revenge? Unless to make it absolute.

Flight. It was the only path left.

He turned, the piazza’s roar fading as the specter of his past stalked his thoughts. Behind him, unbeknownst, history unfolded—mace-bearers parting the crowd, the syndics approaching in solemn black. The people hushed, a sudden reverence stilling them.

Piero Capponi walked among them.

No orations would be wasted on him in death. Today, he heard his city’s gratitude alive. The silence broke in a single thunderous cry:

The stern merchant-soldier allowed himself a rare smile. Let the banners wave later. For now, this was enough.

Baldassarre had not set foot in the Piazza del Duomo since his escape. The memory of his humiliation—the rope around his neck, the wild eyes, the cries of *“some madman, surely!”*—still burned like a brand. He longed to hear the monk preach again, but the shame of recognition held him back. What if they saw him and whispered again? Worse still, what if Tito’s words were true?

He was not mad. No, he carried the cruel clarity of a broken mind, aware of its own ruin. His rage was fierce, but his body was feeble, and caution coiled in him like a serpent. He had refused Piero di Cosimo’s kindness, had told the monks at the hospital only that he had been taken by the French. They had given him a tunic, shoes, a few pitiful coins.

At the first town on the road to Arezzo, he spent two *danari* to be shaved, his hair clipped short again. The barber held up a steel mirror, and Baldassarre flinched. *Had he changed so much that Tito truly did not know him?* His hands shook as he took the glass. No—the face was still his own, though older, harder. The same deep lines, the same rough brow. The white beard? No disguise to eyes that had known him for sixteen years.

Tito *had* recognized him. He must have.

He turned from the mirror, the fire of hatred flaring anew. He would not enter Florence until dusk. Instead, he left the road and sat by a still pool, its surface a second mirror. He bent close, studying himself. Was there weakness in his face? Not now, not when his will was iron. But in the hollow hours, when the past slipped like smoke through his fingers—then, yes, he must look lost.

Could he prove anything? Could he even speak without his thoughts scattering like leaves? Once, his mind had been a treasury of knowledge. Now, it was a ruin. Yet sometimes, in flashes, the old self returned—Greek letters clear before his eyes, his thoughts sharp as a blade. If only it would last long enough for vengeance.

He clenched his fists. He must live, must work, must not be seen as a helpless fool. Porters were needed in Florence; he could carry burdens. But first—his fingers touched the small bag at his throat, the *breve* his mother had given him. He had never opened it. Superstition, sentiment—what did they matter now?

He tore it apart. Inside, a blue sapphire glinted. Small, but valuable. He laughed softly. This would buy him a dagger.

At Bratti’s shop, he found one—a fine blade, sharp as betrayal. He let slip that he came from Genoa, that a friend had spoken of a ring bought here. Bratti, ever boastful, spilled tales of Tito: the young Greek who had risen from rags to riches, who had sold jewels, married well, grown prosperous.

But Baldassarre’s mind was treacherous. Each new fact threatened to dislodge the old. He bought a Greek book, desperate to anchor himself in the past. Yet when he sat in his straw-filled hovel, the letters swam before his eyes, meaningless as the whispers of ghosts.

The darkness fell. It made no difference. The pages remained blank.

The morning sun filtered through the shutters of Tito’s study as he set down his pen and turned toward Romola.

"My love," he began, his voice smooth as polished marble, "we are to have distinguished visitors today—the Count of Milan and the Seneschal de Beaucaire. I know these formalities weary you, and they will wish to inspect the library. Perhaps you might spend the day with your cousin Brigida?"

Romola caught the unspoken request beneath his words and nodded. "Of course," she said, though a faint unease stirred within her.

She returned moments later, cloaked and ready, pausing to press a hand to his brow. "You are warm," she murmured.

Tito did not lift his head. His pen scratched steadily across the parchment, as if the weight of her touch might unbalance him.

"The French cling to Florence like wasps to overripe fruit," he said lightly, still avoiding her gaze.

The absence of his usual smile, the way his shoulders tensed—small things, yet they lodged in Romola’s heart like splinters. She withdrew her hand.

"I will go, then."

"Farewell, my sweet. Take Maso with you."

Only when the door closed behind her did Tito exhale, his shoulders sagging. The deception was a leaden cloak upon him, yet necessity left no choice. If Florence became unsafe, if Baldassarre’s shadow grew too near—he would need funds. And the library, that sacred trust of Bardo’s, was now his only leverage.

Romola’s trust was a fragile thing, but survival demanded sacrifice.

---

That evening, she found him animated, spinning tales of the French court’s absurdities, his laughter a little too bright. She matched his tone, though her heart ached. The distance between them stretched, unspoken.

---

The next day, Tito was absent until nightfall. Romola waited, her father’s newly finished portrait propped before her—a ghost of the man she remembered, his fire dimmed by time.

Bernardo del Nero found her there, her fingers tracing the edge of the frame.

"Good news," he announced. "The French king flees at dawn. The city will breathe again."

Romola smiled, though her eyes lingered on the portrait. "He looks older here than in my memories."

Bernardo studied it. "Age steals the fire first from the eyes." He sighed. "But who knows? Perhaps his wish for the library will yet be fulfilled. Even an old fox like me might live to see it."

She pressed his hand to her lips, grateful for his steadfastness.

"And your husband?" Bernardo asked, shrewd. "He is everywhere but home these days."

Romola’s cheeks warmed. "Florence needs him."

Bernardo chuckled. "Content, then, Madonna Orgogliosa?"

"Entirely."

The lie tasted bitter. But a woman’s pride, like her love, is a guarded thing—better silence than the humiliation of a wound laid bare.

The morning after the French departed Florence, Romola stood beneath the loggia of her home, the crisp air stirring her mantle as she watched the last of the soldiers cross the bridges toward the Porta San Piero. The city’s bells clamored in triumph, their vibrations humming through her bones, but her relief was shadowed. Though the foreign presence had brought only strife, its departure did not lift the weight from her heart.

Tito had been distant since the French arrived—guarded, armored in more than just steel. She had seen the fear in his eyes, the tension in his bearing, though he would not speak of it. Now, as the gates closed and the clamor faded, she descended to the library, determined to lose herself in work. Perhaps routine could steady her nerves.

She lit the lamp, its small glow barely pushing back the room’s shadows, and began copying the catalog. The fire had dwindled to embers, but she hardly noticed the chill until the door opened.

Tito stepped inside.

She rose at once, her pulse quickening. "You’re home early," she said, moving to unwind his *becchetto*.

His smile was bright, effortless. "Unwelcome?"

"Tito." Her voice was soft, chiding. He caught her wrists, playful, then kissed her, his fingers threading through her hair. The familiarity of it—his touch, his warmth—made her throat tighten. For a moment, she let herself believe the strain between them had been imagined.

But when she drew back, his gaze shifted, scanning the room as if avoiding hers. He shrugged off his cloak, though his movements were too deliberate.

"The bells sound like victory," she ventured.

"Of a sour sort," he said, settling into his chair. "Florence is a tinderbox. The Frate’s faction tightens its grip, the treasury bleeds gold for France and Pisa, and the city chokes on piety. I’ve been thinking—we should leave."

The words struck like cold water. "Leave Florence?" Her fingers tightened on the stool beneath her. "I can’t. Not yet. My father’s work—"

"Your father’s work is a dream, *carissima*." His voice was gentle, as if explaining to a child. "A library bound to his name changes nothing. Books belong to the world, not one man’s vanity."

She flinched. "It was his *wish*."

"And wishes are air. Should we chain ourselves to the dead?" He leaned forward, his tone soothing. "You’ve given enough. Let go."

She stood abruptly, her voice low. "You don’t understand loyalty."

His smile dimmed. "I understand practicality. And I’ve acted on it."

A silence, heavy as stone.

"The library is sold."

Her breath left her. "Sold?"

"The Duke of Milan takes the books. The antiquities go to France." His voice remained calm, as if discussing the weather.

For a heartbeat, she could not move. Then fury ignited. "You had no right!"

"I had every right. The decision was made."

She turned toward the door, but his hand closed around her wrist.

"Running to Bernardo won’t undo it." His grip was firm, his voice colder now. "The agreements are signed. The money is mine."

She wrenched free. "Then give me my godfather’s share. Let me repay him."

His eyes narrowed, weighing her. "And if I do? What then?"

"I’ll say nothing. Do nothing." The words were ash in her mouth.

A pause. Then he nodded, moving to the desk. When he returned, he pressed a folded paper into her hand.

"You’ll forgive me in time," he murmured, brushing his lips to her brow.

She did not react.

The door closed behind him. Only then did she crumble, falling to her knees before her father’s portrait, her sobs the only sound in the hollow dark.

Baldassarre wandered through Florence, searching for a place to rest his weary bones—some out-of-the-way corner where he might lay his head without cost. His steps led him to the one uneven stretch within the city walls, a slope where the ground rose above the rooftops, offering a rare glimpse beyond the crowded streets to the distant hills and the valley cradling the Arno.

This was the hill of San Giorgio, a quiet place of gardens and rough, untamed patches where landslides had long ago left scars of tumbled stone. Among the scattered dwellings, he spotted a peculiar little building, half tower, half shed, its roof tiled in fluted curves. Nearby stood a small outhouse, wedged against a crumbling wall, its door gaping open to reveal straw and rusted tools.

An old woman, wiry and sun-browned, was untethering a goat beneath a half-dead mulberry tree. Baldassarre approached, raising his voice above the wind.

"I seek shelter," he said. "A place to sleep. I can pay."

The woman squinted at him, her ears slow to catch his words. "No lodgings," she muttered, shaking her head.

But Baldassarre was persistent. He spoke of distant lands, of his poverty, of the little he could offer—a quattrino or two for the straw in her outhouse. At last, a cunning gleam lit her eyes. She fetched a hatchet and pointed to a stubborn log half-buried in the corner.

"Chop that," she said, "and you may stay the night."

He agreed, and as the blade bit into the wood, Monna Lisa watched, lips curled in satisfaction.

*Cheaper than hiring a man,* she thought. *And if he’s quiet, who’s to know?*

Above, unseen, a pair of bright blue eyes peered through a shutter’s knothole. Tessa, restless and curious, had been drawn by the stranger’s voice. Now she watched as the old man worked, his strong arms splitting the wood with ease. A plan formed in her mind—a small rebellion, a secret kindness.

When dusk settled, she slipped from the house, a bowl of steaming macaroni in one hand, a lantern in the other. She nudged the outhouse door with her foot.

Baldassarre looked up, startled, as light spilled across the straw.

"I brought you supper," Tessa announced, her voice loud as if he, too, were deaf like Monna Lisa. She plopped down beside him, grinning. "Eat. I’ll stay awhile."

He ate in silence, and she studied him—his sunken eyes, his rough hands.

"You’re not from here," she observed. "I live with Monna Lisa. She’s old, but kind. And I have a baby—a beautiful baby. You’ll see tomorrow."

Baldassarre said little, but her chatter was a warmth against the cold of his thoughts. When she left, promising to return, he sat in the dark, the ghost of a forgotten smile touching his lips.

The next morning, Tessa was true to her word. She brought bread, broth, and—most proudly—her swaddled infant.

"Look," she said, placing the bundle in his arms. "Isn’t he perfect?"

Baldassarre gazed at the child, something stirring in his chest. Not joy, not sorrow, but a quiet, protective ache.

"You mustn’t be sad," Tessa told him, reclaiming her baby. "We’ll take care of you."

He did not answer, but as she skipped away, he whispered to the empty air:

"Poor thing."

And whether he meant the child, the girl, or himself, even he could not say.

Messer Naldo returned sooner than anticipated—on the evening of the twenty-eighth of November, barely eleven days after his last visit. His hurried reappearance suggested he had not ventured far beyond the mountains. That evening, as shadows lengthened in the Via de’ Bardi, an encounter unfolded—one that might explain why his steps turned once more toward the hill of San Giorgio.

When Tito first secured this refuge for Tessa over a year ago, he had convinced himself it was an act of kindness, a necessary obligation after the unfortunate incident that left the guileless girl believing him her husband. True, Tessa’s wide-eyed adoration and artless chatter made her impossible to resist—her innocence begged to be sheltered. But Tito assured himself his motives were not purely selfish. Had they been, he might have preferred freedom from her clinging devotion, for his heart was not hers. It belonged, for the first time in his life, to another—a woman whose presence left him breathless, whose very touch resonated through his being. Romola had awakened in him an idealism he had never known, and his nature was too refined, his mind too sharp, to sink into mere sensual indulgence.

Yet the web he had spun around Tessa held fast. What had begun as a pragmatic deception—a safeguard against future need—had taken on a life of its own, binding him in silken threads he could not sever. Kindness and self-interest blurred in his pliant nature, and though he had resented her pursuit on the day of his betrothal, though he had resolved to confess the truth when he next saw her, he had instead allowed the lie to stand. In the glow of his new devotion to Romola, he needed to believe his own justifications.

Still, he took pains to guard his secret. Monna Lisa, Tessa’s guardian, knew nothing of his true name or whereabouts. She asked no questions, content with the coins that ensured her comfort, growing fond of the girl she believed to be his lawful wife. Tessa, for her part, obeyed his command of silence, terrified by his warning that if she ever spoke of their marriage, he would vanish forever. The old woman’s deafness spared Tessa from unguarded confessions, unlike that fatal slip to Baldassarre.

For a long while, Tito’s visits were rare, his absences easily explained as journeys. He came only to assure himself of Tessa’s well-being, and though he always found her company sweeter than he anticipated—her devotion a balm—he had no true need of her. Yet he took care to preserve her simplicity, the very quality that made her enchanting. He wanted her to remain a contadina, unspoiled by the world. The thought of her dressed in fine gowns, her artless chatter replaced by worldly talk, repelled him. Her joys were those of a child—a handful of nuts, a sunlit afternoon—and he ensured her pleasures stayed as uncomplicated as her heart.

This, in brief, was the history of their bond—until recent days. Before Bardo’s death, Tito had occasionally sought Tessa’s company to escape the old scholar’s weariness, though never with any real urgency. But the moment he first hungered for her unquestioning love, for the refuge of her blind faith, had come only lately—and it was marked by events as unforgettable as the onset of a crippling malady. It was the day he encountered Baldassarre and bought the coat of mail.

That night, crossing the bridge with the armor in his hands, he had recoiled from the thought of facing Romola. She, too, was innocent of the world’s harsh truths; she, too, trusted him. But unlike Tessa, Romola’s trust sprang not from ignorance, but from nobility—a nobility that might one day judge him. He needed respite, a space where he could shed his careful self-control. And so, while Romola waited for him, he turned his steps toward the hill.

Now, eleven days later, with Romola’s scorn still ringing in his ears, he sought that refuge again. He did not wish Tessa in his wife’s place—Romola belonged to the world of ambition, to the life he had chosen. But tonight, he craved an escape from the rigid standards he could not dismiss as mere folly. Tessa’s little soul was a haven.

It was just past eight when he climbed the steps to her door. Usually, she rushed to meet him, but tonight the room was dim, the fire low. Tessa knelt beside the cradle, her head slumped on the pillow, her fingers loosely curled around her rosary. She had fallen asleep mid-prayer. Tito crossed the room silently and sat beside her.

She stirred, her eyes fluttering open without alarm. For a moment, she simply gazed at him, as if his presence were the natural conclusion of a dream. When he cupped her chin and kissed her, she murmured, *"I dreamed it, and then I said it was dreaming—and then I awoke, and it was true."*

"Little sinner," he teased, pinching her chin. "You haven’t finished your prayers. I ought to punish you by ignoring that ugly baby of yours."

Her face crumpled. "No, he’s beautiful! Prettier than anything. Look at him—he’s grown since you saw him last. His hair, his little hands—"

He indulged her with a light kiss on the child’s forehead, then turned her face back to his. "You’d rather gaze at him than at your husband?"

She shook her head earnestly. "I love you best. But I want you to love him too."

"And did you expect me back so soon?" he asked, eager to distract himself with her chatter. The evening’s turmoil still vibrated in his nerves, and her prattle was a welcome relief.

She brightened. "I counted the days! Since you wore the chain-mail—the one Messer San Michele gave you for protection. You have it on now!" She peeked at the glint beneath his tunic. "Maybe it brought you back to me."

"Maybe it did," he conceded. "But never mind that. Tell me—what’s happened since I left? Did you see the soldiers in the Prato? The tents, the trumpets?"

She nodded. "Monna Lisa was afraid they’d steal our goats. But none came up here. Only… something else happened. I was praying about it."

He tensed. "What is it?"

She hesitated. "Let me sit on your lap. Then I won’t be so scared."

He drew her close, though his expression darkened. "Tell me."

"It’s about the old man," she whispered. "At first, I didn’t mean to tell you, because we thought he’d be gone before you returned. But he’s still here, and I’ve never disobeyed you before—"

"Who is he?" Tito interrupted, his pulse quickening.

She described the stranger—his sunken face, white hair, black brows. How he muttered *"Poor thing"* as if she were still a beaten child. How he carried a book, staring at it for hours without praying.

Tito’s grip tightened. "Where is he now?"

"In the shed. He sleeps there. Monna Lisa lets him stay—he helps with chores."

A cold certainty settled over him. He stood abruptly. "Get me a lantern."

She scrambled up, but as he flung open the door, moonlight spilled across the threshold. "No, wait—I don’t need it. Go back inside. Say nothing to Monna Lisa."

In moments, he stood before the outhouse, the moon bleaching its worn planks white. His breath hitched—a primal dread of what lurked within. But reason steadied him. He was young, armored. The old man was unarmed.

He pushed the door open.

Inside, Baldassarre sat hunched in a sliver of moonlight, a dagger’s edge glinting in his hand. His mind had been a void, his fury directionless—until the door creaked.

Then, he saw him.

Not a phantom. Not a vision. Tito—alive, close, real.

With a snarl, Baldassarre lunged.

The dagger flashed—then snapped against Tito’s armored chest. The old man staggered back, the broken hilt clenched in his fist. The blade’s tip clattered at Tito’s feet.

For a heartbeat, terror had seized him. Now, triumph surged. Vengeance lay helpless before him.

But no cruelty rose in its wake. Only relief—and the desperate need to erase that hatred.

"Padre mio," he murmured, soft as the day they parted in Greece. "I came to ask your forgiveness."

Silence.

"I was unprepared that morning. Let me be your son again. Let me make amends."

Baldassarre stirred. Slowly, trembling, he rose. He seized Tito’s outstretched hand—not in acceptance, but to drag himself upright until their faces were inches apart.

His voice was a rasp.

"I saved you. I raised you. I loved you. You abandoned me. You robbed me. You denied me." His grip tightened. "What can you give me now? You have turned my world to poison. But this much sweetness remains—*you will know agony.*"

He released Tito’s hand and sank back onto the straw, spent.

The dagger-thrust had failed. But the curse held.

For days, Romola remained a shadow in her own home. Tito moved through the house with careful steps, avoiding the weight of her gaze. When he told her the library would soon be emptied—agents coming to box away the antiquities—she had nodded without a word, her fingers curled tight around the edge of her chair. He had leaned in then, brushing his lips against her brow, and suggested she keep to her chamber, where the little painted tabernacle stood. A retreat from the noise, the strangers, the unraveling of their shared past.

She did not resist. The night had carved something hollow in her, leaving her numb, her hands cold as marble. When Tito draped a furred mantle over her shoulders, she did not stir. He watched her, waiting for a flicker of warmth, a sign that the woman who had once rested her hand on his head in forgiveness still lived beneath this frozen silence.

*Patience,* he told himself. *She will soften. The bond between us is too strong to break.*

Yet with each passing hour, her stillness became its own reproach. It was easier, he found, to slip into the role of the wounded—to forget the blow he had dealt and remember only his own attempts at tenderness. But Tito was not a man who nursed grievances. He preferred the quiet art of appeasement, the slow coaxing back of trust.

And so he turned outward, where Florence buzzed with unrest. The departure of the French had left the city seething, a hive of anxious deliberation. How to pay the king’s ransom? How to crush Pisa’s rebellion? Above all, how to rebuild a government that had fractured under the weight of its own discord?

The streets simmered with debate. The great bell rang, summoning citizens to the Piazza de’ Signori, where the Signoria stood before the stone lion and proposed a temporary solution: twenty men, vested with absolute power for a year, to reshape the laws and choose new magistrates. The crowd roared approval—though approval, in such assemblies, was often just the echo of a decision already made.

Behind palace doors, the real struggle unfolded. Should Florence embrace the Venetian model—a Great Council where every law and leader was chosen by a broad swath of citizens? Or should power remain in the hands of the few, the old families who distrusted the clamor of the marketplace?

Messer Soderini argued for the people. Messer Vespucci, for tradition. The debate stretched into the night, a battle of rhetoric that masked the deeper truth: men fought not for principles, but for the future they had already envisioned.

And beyond the palace walls, another voice rose—one that cut through the noise with the certainty of prophecy. Savonarola’s sermons, once broad calls to virtue, now sharpened into direct command. *The Great Council is the will of God.* To him, it was no political maneuver, but a divine necessity. Only through pure governance could Florence rise—not merely as a city, but as a beacon for the world.

The friar sought no petty triumph. Even in the darkest hours to come, when pain and fear would strip him bare, he would hold fast to this: his purpose had always been vast, his vision untainted by spite.

*"Do not wonder if it seems I have said but few things,"* he would tell his judges. *"For my things were few—and great."*

The library stood empty now, its treasures crated and carried away. Romola had watched it all—each book, each artifact, every fragment of her father’s life vanishing piece by piece. The exhaustion of grief had settled into a numb disbelief, as though the past weeks were a dream from which she might still wake. But each evening, she walked through the hollowed-out rooms, her lamp casting long shadows over the straw-strewn floors, forcing herself to remember: this was real. Her love was gone. The world had turned barren.

On the final day, the 23rd of December, she climbed to the loggia, her fingers gripping the cold stone as the last cart rolled across the bridge. The sky hung low and leaden, dusk leaching the color from Florence. The Arno ran dark beneath the bridge, and the city’s towers stood like tombstones in the gloom. Santa Croce, where her father lay, was swallowed in shadow. The white-shrouded load crawled away like a specter, carrying with it the last remnants of his life’s work. She did not weep. She only stared, feeling not her own sorrow but his—as though he, beneath the unyielding weight of the grave, could still see his legacy torn from him.

Then, without warning, the bells began.

A great peal shattered the silence, rolling from the palace tower, echoed by every campanile in Florence. Fire burst between the turrets, leaping like living things, and the air itself seemed to vibrate with triumph. But to Romola, the sound was a mockery. These were the bells that had once rung for her joy, that had heralded her marriage, that had promised a future bright with hope. Now they clamored for her husband’s victory—for the betrayal that had hollowed her out.

She pressed her hands to her ears and fled.

Below, she nearly collided with Tito himself. He was radiant, his step light, his voice buoyant.

“The noise unsettles you?” he asked, noting her recoil. He took her wrist, guiding her into the saloon where painted nymphs danced frozen on the walls. “Florence celebrates its Great Council—an end to all ills, they say. But you look pale. You should not linger in the cold.” His tone was smooth, practiced. “I’m called to Rome on business for Bernardo Rucellai. I leave tonight. No need to trouble yourself; my things are already packed.”

He pressed her hand to his lips—no kiss, no tenderness, only the ghost of a husband’s gesture. Then he was gone.

Romola did not move until the door closed behind him. Then, like a marionette cut from its strings, she sagged. But her stillness was not resignation. Beneath the frost of her composure, her mind burned.

She called for Maso.

“We leave at dawn,” she told him. “Prepare the mules. Wait for me at Trespiano.”

She would send him back from Bologna with letters—one for Bernardo, one for Tito. No explanations. No pleas. Only silence.

That night, she did not sleep.

She opened the chest where her bridal gown lay folded—white silk and gold, the circlet of pearls still looped with a sugar-plum Tito had once laughed over. A sob clawed up her throat. These were not relics of joy, but of a love now dead.

Beneath the finery lay something darker: a coarse grey robe, the habit of a Pinzochera. She stripped off her mourning dress and pulled the rough serge over her skin, tightening the rope girdle until it bit into her waist. The discomfort was a relief. Pain, at least, was honest.

At the mirror, she bound her hair tight, covering it with a black kerchief, then drew the cowl over her head. The face that stared back was unfamiliar—wan, hollow-eyed, like her brother Dino in his final days. Was she becoming like him? Not in faith, but in understanding: the lure of renunciation, the refuge of solitude.

She packed the last of her father’s relics—portraits, small treasures—into the chest. Then her hand hovered over her betrothal ring.

To remove it felt like severing her own flesh. The ring was a symbol, yes, but symbols had weight. They were the hooks by which the world held her. Yet what did it bind her to now? A ghost. A lie.

She wrenched it off.

Dawn crept in, grey and silent. The city’s revelry had faded. Romola took up her pen, then hesitated. Words would make this real. But the ink dried unspilled.

Instead, she fastened the scarsella at her waist, its coins clinking softly, and looped the rosary beside it. She was no pious sister, but the disguise would serve. Venice awaited. Cassandra Fedele, the scholar, would know how a woman might live alone, might turn her mind to something beyond love.

As the first light touched the rooftops, Romola stepped into the street. The past was a closed door. The future—unknowable, perhaps bleak—was hers alone.

She did not look back.

The chill light of dawn seeped into the room as Romola stirred at the sound of a tap on her door. Maso had come for the traveling satchel. When she opened the door, the old servant could not hide his startlement—where once stood the radiant figure crowned with golden hair now stood a shadowed form draped in a coarse grey mantle, her face pale beneath the dark cowl.

*"It is well, Maso,"* Romola said, forcing calm into her voice. *"The wallet is ready. Go ahead quietly; I will follow soon. Once beyond the gates, slow your pace. I may join you before Trespiano."*

She closed the door, her fingers tightening around the small key she had taken from the casket in the night. It was the original key to the painted tabernacle—Tito had forgotten to cast it into the Arno, and it had lain forgotten in the corner of his embroidered purse. She had found it long ago and kept it, though she had never used it. Now, it was the instrument of her final severance.

The tabernacle stood near the window, its gilded figures faintly visible in the dim light: Bacchus triumphant, crowned Ariadne in his embrace, the frolicking Loves, the dolphins and the sea—all framed in a paradise of blossoms. Romola’s lips curled in bitter disdain. What had once seemed a treasure now felt like a cruel jest. Ariadne, with her adoring gaze, had been a fool.

*"She would look strange among the vines now,"* Romola thought, her fingers tracing the edge of the mirror before she recoiled from her own reflection. The cowl transformed her into something foreign, something that whispered of the fanaticism she had always scorned.

With a sharp motion, she thrust the key into the tabernacle, wrenching it open. She did not look at the crucifix as she slipped a cord through its ring and hung it around her neck, tucking it beneath her mantle. *For Dino’s sake,* she told herself.

There were letters yet to write—brief, decisive. The first was to Tito:

*"The love I bore you is dead. In that death, the woman you married is gone. Do not seek me; no law can restore what is already lost. You know the words I last spoke to you—they were not anger, but truth. Send my bridal chest to my godfather. It holds what remains of my past."*

She folded his ring inside the letter and sealed it.

The second was to Bernardo del Nero:

*"Dearest Godfather, if staying could have served you, I would not have left. But I am gone. Do not ask why. Keep my departure silent. My bridal chest will explain. Give my mother’s things to Brigida, and bid her forgive my silent farewell. You have been my second father—that memory is all I have left. Farewell."*

She placed the letters with the crucifix against her heart. The time had come.

The house was still asleep as she descended the stairs, her steps soundless on the stone. Outside, the streets lay hushed. The cold air sharpened her resolve. She moved swiftly, her mantle brushing the broad flagstones, her pulse quick with purpose.

The Ponte Rubaconte lay behind her. She did not look back at Santa Croce. The thought of curious eyes upon her—of strangers dissecting her grief—filled her with revulsion. She was no longer the woman Florence knew.

The Porta San Gallo loomed ahead. A dispute over market tolls gave her cover to slip past unnoticed. Then—freedom. Beyond the city walls, the open road stretched before her, the grey dawn breaking into gold. The sun’s sudden radiance cast her shadow long and stark across the path, a specter of her own resolve.

She paused only once, seating herself on a flat stone beside a cypress. Ahead, Maso and the mules were still within reach. But for now, she let herself rest, her back to Florence, her face to the unknown.

She was free. She was alone.

The journey to Rome had been decided in a single evening—an abrupt departure that swept aside the obstacles to Romola’s own flight. Tito had left for the supper at the Rucellai Gardens in high spirits, anticipating delicate meats, rare wines, and the company of Florence’s elite. The host, Bernardo Rucellai, was no ordinary nobleman. His very name carried the weight of history, derived from the lichen *oricello*, whose purple dye had once enriched his merchant ancestors. Now, generations later, Bernardo himself stood as a man of letters, politics, and influence—a patron of philosophy, a builder of palaces, and a diplomat whose presence lent gravity to any gathering.

Tito had been invited through Lorenzo Tornabuoni, and the urgency of the summons suggested matters of state. Florence was restless, and the Twenty—the council that now held the reins of power—were men whose favor Tito could not afford to ignore. The prospect of politics flavored with fine dining was far more appealing than the dreary philosophic suppers he had endured in these same gardens, where men lectured at length on the cosmos as if it were a tract to be memorized.

The night was dark, broken only by the occasional glow of a lamp before some street-corner Madonna. Beneath one such light, a figure paused—tall, cloaked, unmistakably Tito—before vanishing through the gate of the Rucellai Gardens. Behind him, unnoticed, another man halted: thick-set, older, clad in rough serge.

*"Your name?"* the porter demanded as the stranger moved to follow.

A hesitation. *"Are you his servant?"*

There was a quiet authority in the answer, and the porter yielded. The door closed behind Baldassarre, and he too disappeared among the torchlit hedges.

Months ago, this man would have faltered, his mind adrift in shadows. But tonight, he walked with purpose. The change had come suddenly—on the very evening Tito had last stood before him, when the dagger meant for treachery’s heart had shattered in his grip.

That night, Baldassarre had sat in the aftermath of rage, his body trembling, his thoughts unnervingly clear. The past had unspooled before him, vivid as a fresco newly restored. He remembered the name *Tessa*, the revelation of Tito’s double life. He had gathered the broken blade and the forgotten book beside him—Pausanias, lying open to the page headed *ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ*.

Moonlight had struck the letters, and suddenly, they were no longer marks on parchment but gateways. Messenia rose in his mind—its revolt, its traitors, its justice. The words of the ancient historian had rushed back, and with them, memory itself.

That night, he had walked the hills under the cold stars, the city spread below him like a map he could read anew. Names, places, histories—all returned, sharp as the edge of a knife. And with them, one unwavering purpose: vengeance.

Since then, he had stalked Tito like a shadow. He learned his routines, his alliances, the delicate threads of his standing in Florence. He avoided those too close to him—Nello’s shop, Piero di Cosimo—lest warning reach Tito too soon. He wanted no hurried flight, no desperate ploy. He wanted his moment to be perfect: when Tito stood at his most secure, flanked by the powerful, certain of his own invincibility.

Twice, Baldassarre had felt his mind waver—a dizzying lapse, as if the light within him might flicker out. But tonight, there was no uncertainty. He had followed Tito here, into the heart of the Rucellai Gardens, where the great men of Florence gathered.

Tonight, the reckoning would come.

The pavilion glowed under the evening’s torchlight, its frescoed walls alive with flickering shadows. Tito’s sharp eyes quickly discerned the gathering’s true nature—beneath the veneer of conviviality, politics simmered. Good wine and rich dishes had long been trusted to loosen tongues and sharpen allegiances, and the Rucellai, among Florence’s most opulent families, knew well how to wield both.

Plato’s bust, salvaged from Lorenzo de’ Medici’s villa after his death, presided over the feast with silent irony. Once, it had overlooked gatherings of philosophers—Pico della Mirandola, Marsilio Ficino, Poliziano—men who had burned with ideas. Now, it watched as silver forks flashed and laughter rippled through the warm air, the weight of their legacy reduced to ornament.

Bernardo Rucellai, the host, welcomed Tito with marked favor, seating him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, both polished Medicean loyalists. The talk, at first, was light—jests about how Dante’s frugal Florentines would gape at such luxury, admiration for the craftsmanship of Pollaiuolo’s silver dish. Yet beneath the frivolity, tension hummed.

As the servants withdrew, the conversation sharpened. The Frate—Savonarola—loomed over them all, his shadow stretching even into this privileged enclave.

*"He loads the cannon,"* Rucellai remarked dryly, *"and the Frate lights the match."*

Tornabuoni leaned in. *"We must be prudent. The Medici will return—but until then, we play the Frate’s game. The alternative?"* He gestured with his cup. *"The Arrabbiati would tear us apart."*

Niccolò Ridolfi scoffed. *"All governments end the same—biting or groveling. But we keep our heads down, wait, and hold to our own."*

Rucellai’s voice was cool. *"I dissemble nothing. Yet for now, the popular party is our shield."*

The wine flowed; alliances murmured in corners. Tornabuoni and Pucci drew Tito aside.

*"You’re invaluable,"* Tornabuoni said, fingers drumming his ankle. *"Discreet, connected, yet unmarked. Play this well, and the future favors you."*

Pucci’s grip tightened on Tito’s shoulder. *"Win the Frate’s trust. Then, when the time comes—"*

A cardinal’s hat, Tornabuoni hinted. Power, unspoken but understood.

Tito smiled, mind already racing. *"Leave Spini to me,"* he said. *"I’ll know his plans before he does."*

Outside, in the cold dark, a figure circled—watching, waiting. But within, amid laughter and torchlight, none noticed the eyes fixed on Tito’s careless grace.

The game had begun.

An Arresting Voice**

Romola sat beneath the cypress, the weight of her flight pressing upon her. The morning air was still, the mountains somber in the distance, and for the first time, she felt utterly alone—no walls, no streets, no law but the vast indifference of earth and sky. There was freedom in it, and terror.

Then the voice came, quiet and certain.

"You are Romola de’ Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema."

She knew it at once—that voice which had stirred her before, like a summons she could not ignore. She did not turn. A monk’s interference, nothing more. Yet her hands trembled.

"You flee Florence in disguise. God commands me to stop you. You are not permitted to leave."

Anger flared. "By what right do you speak to me? What right to hinder me?"

"The right of a messenger. You wear the garb of devotion without its purpose. It was revealed to me who you are—that you seek to escape the lot appointed to you. You would hide your name, your place, and choose anew, as if life were yours to reshape at will. But you are called back. Return to your duty."

Her defiance hardened. "I will not. Priests have no power over me."

"It is not the monk who commands you," he said, "but truth itself. And truth is a chain you cannot break. Obey, and it will guide you. Refuse, and it will drag behind you forever."

She stood then, facing him at last—Fra Girolamo, his features austere, his gaze unwavering. She had meant to challenge him, but the words died on her lips. There was no anger in his eyes, only a quiet intensity, as if he saw not just her, but the struggle within her.

"You claim freedom," he said, "but who is more a slave than the debtor who denies his debt?"

A flicker of shame crossed her face.

"You turn from your vows—as a Florentine, as a wife. You abandon your city in its need, as if you were a bird with no ties to the earth. But duties are not chosen, Romola. They are given, like the blood in your veins. You owe more than your own grief."

She clutched the crucifix beneath her mantle, its edges sharp against her palm.

"Conform your life to this," he said, pointing to it. "Make your sorrow an offering. The world groans under injustice, and you say, *I will go where none demand of me*? But every bond is a debt, and the right lies in its payment."

Her resistance wavered. "If I stay—what then? My love is gone. Must I live a lie?"

"Marriage is not for pleasure alone," he answered. "It is a vow, sacred even when the heart grows cold. If your husband were a criminal, your place would be beside him in his cell. But he is not. Then your place is where your vows have bound you."

The conflict tore at her. To return was to embrace pain, to stay was to deny the very fiber of duty. Yet his words rang with a conviction deeper than her doubt.

"Florence needs you," he said, his voice low now, fervent. "Not as a wanderer, but as a daughter. The work of God begins here, in the streets you would abandon. Will you leave your people to their sorrow while you seek your own escape?"

She closed her eyes. The image rose before her—not of Tito, not of her own anguish, but of the city, alive with suffering and hope.

At last, she whispered, "Father... I will go back."

Her knees bent of their own will. Savonarola lifted his hands over her, and in the silence, something within her—some fierce, rebellious self—bowed as well.

Fra Girolamo’s voice was quiet but firm as he spoke. “Rise, my daughter. Your servant waits with the mules nearby. The hour grows late, and I must continue to Florence.”

Romola obeyed, lifting herself from her knees. The act of kneeling had been more than mere submission—it was a surrender, a relinquishing of her will. The resolve to abandon her husband had dissolved like mist before the sun, leaving her spirit raw and pliant. For the first time in her life, she felt the need to be led, even in the smallest of choices.

She drew back the edge of her cowl, her gaze flickering to Maso and the second Dominican standing a short distance away, their backs turned. Then her eyes returned to Savonarola, silent, waiting. The command to turn back must come from him, not from her.

“I will summon them,” he said, reading her unspoken plea. “And I commend you, my daughter, to the care of Brother Salvestro. If you seek wisdom, the sacrament of confession will be your gate. He is a man I trust, one of San Marco’s own.”

A flicker of unease crossed Romola’s face. “I would rather have no guide but you, Father.”

“I do not hear confessions,” Savonarola replied. “My duties pull me elsewhere—to the pulpit, to the city’s needs. But if your soul cries out for my counsel, send word through Brother Salvestro, and I will see you in the sacristy or the choir of San Marco. You will not be forgotten.” His voice softened. “I will instruct him to guide you toward the labor Florence demands of her daughters—to serve the suffering, the hungry. Let your gifts be as a lamp, my child, so that through you, the Divine light may shine.”

Then he turned to call Maso.

When the servant had been sent ahead, Brother Salvestro approached. Romola’s pulse quickened with instinctive reluctance. This stranger was to be her confessor, her guide? Yet to protest now would be to grasp at independence when she had just vowed to surrender it. She bowed her head in deference before meeting his eyes—and when she did, doubt coiled in her chest.

Some men inspire reverence at once; others demand it as their due. Brother Salvestro was of the latter kind. There was no deceit in his face, no coarseness—he was, in fact, fairer of feature than Savonarola, his frame taller, his manner practiced. He had guided the souls of Florence’s elite for years. But his eyes lacked fire. His expression wavered, as if his mind could not fix itself wholly upon any great conviction.

Romola had seen such faces before—men who spoke sublime truths sincerely, yet whose very muscles betrayed the hollowness of their fervor. Brother Salvestro was known for his visions, born of a restless, somnambulant mind. Savonarola believed them divine; the brother himself had once dismissed them. And here was the difference between the two men: one forged his own path through the darkness, while the other merely echoed the light.

As Brother Salvestro began to exhort her, Romola felt the old chill return—not enough to shake her resolve, but enough to dim the sunrise of her newfound purpose. The road back was always harder than the road away.

Snowflakes dusted their shoulders as they passed through the city gates. By the time Romola crossed the bridge toward the Via de’ Bardi, the flakes clung to her cowl, damp and heavy. She climbed the stairs to her chamber, shedding the rough serge, burning the farewell letters, restoring each small treasure to its place. The black dress she donned was familiar, the room unchanged.

No journey stretched before her now. Only the quiet, the snow tapping at the windows, the solitude.

Yet it was not emptiness she felt, but the sharp, bright edge of resolve. She had chosen renunciation, and with it, a new path. The tabernacle remained locked, Dino’s crucifix placed outside it. No outward sign marked the shift within her—only the deepening night, white and silent against the glass.

This Christmas Eve of 1494 would be one she remembered. Not for its revelry, but for its stillness. For the choice to stay.

The morning of October 30, 1496, dawned clear and crisp, the autumn breeze whispering through Florence as if indifferent to the city’s suffering. But the people had no mind for the gentle land winds—their thoughts were fixed on the tempests at sea, which seemed determined to defy Savonarola’s promise that Heaven watched over Florence.

Gales had scattered the ships from Marseilles, laden with grain and mercenaries, before they could reach the starving city. Florence stood besieged by famine within and enemies without. The French king, that would-be Charlemagne who had swept through Italy with ease, had long since departed, leaving his allies vulnerable. Now the Holy League—Pope Borgia at its head—had turned its wolfish gaze upon the city, and Florence, alone in her refusal to join, was marked as prey.

Pisa, ever rebellious, swarmed with Venetian and Milanese troops, while Emperor Maximilian himself loomed at Leghorn, threatening to seal Florence’s last gateway to the sea. Without it, the city would suffocate. The Signoria fought back with coin, conscripts, and diplomacy, but hunger gnawed at the people’s resolve. Worse still, the Frate had been silenced by Rome—until desperation forced the government’s hand. Two days past, Savonarola had returned to the Duomo’s pulpit, vowing divine deliverance if Florence held fast.

Yet this morning, there was no sign of salvation. Perhaps the Madonna dell’Impruneta, she who had always answered Florence’s pleas, might intercede once more? Her sacred tabernacle had been borne from the hills the night before, ready to be carried through the streets in solemn procession. But dawn broke unchanged—only the same pallid hunger, the same whispers of discontent.

***

Romola moved through the streets like a shadow in black serge, her golden hair hidden but for the rippled bands at her brow. The Ponte Vecchio lay behind her; ahead, the Por’ Santa Maria stretched narrow and crowded. A bier blocked the way, its bearers—brothers of San Jacopo—pausing over a gaunt figure sprawled in the road.

“Dead,” muttered a bystander. “Or near enough.”

Romola stepped forward. The man’s face was hollow, his beard matted, but as she tipped wine between his lips, his eyes opened—dark, wild, unmistakable. Her breath caught.

She knew him.

The prisoner from the Duomo. The specter from Piero’s sketch. The man who had made Tito blanch with terror.

A tremor ran through her. Here, at last, might be the key to all her sorrows. She forced herself to stay, steadying him as the brothers lifted him to the church steps.

“Wait for me,” she murmured.

Around her, the crowd jeered. “Why feed a thief when honest men starve?”

She turned, meeting their eyes. “Take the bread from my basket if you must,” she said, quiet and firm. “But I will not let you take it from him.”

For a moment, silence. Then the press of bodies eased, grudging respect parting the throng. A ragged man—Cecco—offered to watch over the stranger. Reluctantly, Romola agreed.

But as she walked on, her mind raced. Who was this man? And what truth lay buried in his gaze?

Romola took a longer route back from the hospital, winding toward the river before turning toward the Ponte Vecchio. She meant to hurry to San Stefano in search of Baldassarre—dreading what she might learn, yet unable to shake the sense that abandoning him would be a betrayal of some unspoken duty.

But as she neared the intersection where the Por’ Santa Maria branched to the right and the bridge to the left, the crowd around her suddenly dropped to their knees. She knelt with them, her movement automatic. The Great Cross of the Duomo led the procession, and she had no choice but to wait.

Rising once the Cross had passed, she became acutely aware of her weariness, the stillness and the idle standing making it heavier than when she had been walking. A shopkeeper beside her spoke up:

"Madonna Romola, you must be tired. Gian Fantoni would gladly offer you a seat in his house. Here—his door is just here. Let me open it for you. He loves God and the Frate as we do. His house is yours."

She had grown accustomed to such greetings—Florence itself had become more of a home than the silent halls of the Via de’ Bardi. With a grateful nod, she followed the hosier inside, up to a window where a stout woman and three children, all dressed in the plain garb of Piagnoni, made space for her among the bright-hanging fabrics.

From this vantage, she could see not only the procession winding across the Ponte Vecchio but also the sweep of the Arno beyond, toward the Santa Trinita bridge. The march was solemn, silent—no chants, only the soft crush of footsteps and the whisper of woolen robes.

First came the young men of Florence, bearing aloft the image of the boy Jesus, golden halo gleaming above his head, the cross and nails awaiting him. Then followed the hooded Companies of Discipline, faces shrouded, identities dissolved into symbols of collective penance. Monks came next—white Benedictines, gray Franciscans with their knotted cords, black Augustinians of San Spirito, their human faces sharp above the dark robes. Then the Carmelites, white over dark, and the Servites, pure black, founded by merchants who had renounced wealth to venerate the Mother.

Then—a ripple of tension. The Dominicans approached, black mantles over white scapulars. The crowd’s breath hitched between hatred and devotion. The Fiesole brethren passed first, their cloaks coarse, some threadbare—San Marco’s Prior enforced strict poverty. And then, among them, a figure barely distinguishable save for the aura around him: Savonarola.

A murmur slithered through the crowd—hisses biting beneath the surface, blessings whispered back. Some knelt as he passed, the gesture spreading like a wave, though others stood rigid. At an upper window, someone spat.

More orders followed—Humbled Brethren, Vallombrosans, secular priests—then the guilds with their banners, the lesser officials, the Canons bearing the relic of San Zenobio’s head. At last, the heart of the procession: the hidden Madonna of Impruneta, veiled in brocade, the ancient image said to have cried out when unearthed from the earth. No gifts preceded her today—only alms for the poor, as the Frate had urged. Florence was desperate, her faith clinging to his promise that aid would come.

Romola’s chest ached with the weight of it all, her knees weak as the procession neared its end. Then—movement. A flash of color on the bridge. A horseman at full gallop, olive branch raised.

The crowd erupted. They surged toward him, seizing his reins. Romola recognized Tito, his bay horse streaked with foam, his cap loose, his face alight. But her pulse did not quicken for him—only for the news he bore.

"Good news!" he called, evading the press until he reached the Signoria. Bowing low, he announced: the French galleys, laden with grain, had reached Leghorn. The enemy’s fleet had been held back by the wind.

A roar tore through Florence, shouts echoing from the bridge to the Duomo, wave upon wave of relief. The Signoria stood bare-headed, humbled by deliverance from beyond their own power.

Tito, ever graceful, deferred the credit: "The true messenger is Meo di Sasso, whose horse faltered at Signa. The honor is his, the labor his."

As the procession moved on, the great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio began to toll, its voice merging with the people’s joy. Romola watched Tito, waiting—but his gaze never found her. Or perhaps it did, in that way of his, seeing without seeming to see. She drew her drapery close and stayed silent, a shadow at the window.

The crowd surged forward, their voices fading into the distance as Romola stepped down into the street. She moved swiftly toward the steps of San Stefano, where Baldassarre stood alone, his back pressed against the church door, the horn-cup clutched in his hand. His eyes, once clouded with confusion, now burned with a fierce intensity—a caged animal’s glare, fixed on some unseen prey.

Romola hesitated. That look, so sudden and venomous, struck her like a blade. Had he seen Tito? The thought coiled tight in her chest. If this man was the wronged one, if Tito had done him some terrible harm, then perhaps—perhaps her husband was not the man she believed. A desperate hope flickered. If she could bring them face to face, the truth might tear free at last.

“Come with me,” she said, keeping her voice steady. “I can give you shelter, food, until you’ve regained your strength.”

His lips moved, a low mutter more to himself than to her. “Yes. I need my strength. I must have it.”

She led him toward the Ponte Rubaconte, choosing the quieter path along the Arno. The city’s clamor receded, leaving only the rush of water beneath them.

“You’re not Florentine,” she ventured.

He turned his head sharply, studying her with guarded suspicion. But her gaze, open and searching, seemed to loosen something in him.

“No,” he admitted at last. “I am a man alone.”

She let the silence stretch, fearing any question might drive him away. Yet each step deepened her unease. If he had been the offender, why this dread? Why this secrecy?

They reached the Via de’ Bardi. At the sight of the half-open courtyard door, Baldassarre stiffened, his breath catching. Romola turned—and saw recognition flash in his eyes.

“Ah,” he rasped. “You are *his* wife.”

Her pulse stuttered. “Whose wife?”

But no name came. His jaw clenched, his stare unrelenting, as though the very image of Tito choked the words in his throat.

She pushed the door wider, revealing the straw-covered courtyard, the sick lying listless, the hollow-eyed children playing with idle fingers. “If you come inside,” she said, “I’ll find you a place to rest. And food.”

His refusal was swift. “No.” Yet he lingered, torn between impulse and caution.

“Then let me give you this.” She pressed coins into his palm—more than she would have given any other stranger.

He curled his fingers around them. “I’ll take them.”

She wanted to ask *where will you go? what will you do?* But he was already turning away, his figure shrinking into the distance as he vanished toward the bridge.

---

The bells of Florence rang in triumph, their joyous peals shaking the air. But to Baldassarre, they were just another taunt from a world that had crushed him.

He moved like a shadow through the streets, Niccolò Caparra’s shop drawing him like a lodestone. The apprentice, indifferent, sold him a knife for three *grossi*—the traitor’s own money buying the blade that might slit his throat. The irony was bitter, satisfying.

He tucked the weapon into his tunic, its weight a promise. Armor could be removed. Men grew careless. And when the moment came—

He bought bread, meat, devoured them beneath a loggia as the bells roared on.

---

Romola watched him go, relief and guilt twisting together. She should have pressed harder, demanded the truth. But what if that truth was a serpent coiled around her marriage? A wife had the right to ignorance, didn’t she? To look away from a husband’s sins, since she alone could not denounce him?

The thought was too heavy. She turned instead to the courtyard, to the faces lifted in hope as she told them of the coming grain. Their voices, weak but fervent, blessed her like a prayer.

This was her anchor. Not the wrangling of doctrine, not the dizzying prophecies of scourge and rebirth, but the simple act of easing pain. Savonarola had given her purpose—not in his visions, but in his fire, his unyielding will to mend a broken world.

She knelt beside a child, its tiny fingers tugging at her skirts. Here, in the dirt and the need, she was needed. Here, she could forget the gulf between herself and Tito, if only for a moment.

No soul is truly desolate so long as it can still revere another. And so long as she believed in Savonarola’s greatness, she could keep walking forward—even if the ground trembled beneath her.

Tito’s arrival in Florence as the bearer of peace had been a stroke of luck, but his true business lay in more calculated schemes. After delivering his report to the Ten at the Palazzo Vecchio—a performance that even the skeptical Bernardo del Nero could not help but admire—Tito left with the cold courtesy they now exchanged. Bernardo’s distrust had deepened since the sale of Bardo’s library, though Romola’s silence on the matter bound him to discretion. He watched her fading spirit with a grimace, blaming her unhappiness on Tito’s smooth, hollow charm.

Yet politics had no room for moral scrutiny. Tito was useful, and in Florence, usefulness forgave much.

Exhausted from riding, Tito lingered in the streets, feigning idle wandering while his mind worked. The Piazza del Duomo buzzed with post-procession energy, the air thick with restless joy. He avoided eager crowds by directing them toward another messenger—Meo di Sasso, who surely knew more than he did.

At Nello’s barber shop, a knot of men lounged, their laughter too loud to be unforced. Among them stood Dolfo Spini, leader of the Compagnacci, his red plume a defiant splash against the city’s newfound sobriety. Spini’s grin was brittle; his faction had spent weeks decrying Savonarola’s influence, only to be undone by the day’s miraculous news.

"I would speak with you," Baldassarre said, his voice rough but urgent.

Romola turned, her face pale but composed. She had known this moment would come, though she had not sought it. There was no fear in her, only a quiet resignation, as if she were stepping toward some inevitable reckoning.

"Yes," she replied, her voice steady. "But not here. Somewhere we cannot be overheard."

His gaze darted behind him, as though expecting pursuit. "Away from the streets," he muttered. "Out in the open."

"I often come here at this hour," Romola said. "If you follow me, we can speak freely."

He nodded, and she led the way up the rugged path, the stones loose beneath their feet. The climb was long, and Baldassarre lagged behind, his breath labored. When at last she stopped beneath the shelter of the trees, he sank onto a jut of rock, his hands trembling.

"The hill is steep," Romola observed gently, seating herself beside him. "You are weakened. Have you been without food?"

For a moment, he did not answer. Then he turned his face to hers, his eyes locking onto hers with sudden clarity.

"Ah!" he burst out. "You would have been my daughter!"

The words struck like a blade. Romola’s breath caught; her lips parted in silent shock. In an instant, understanding crashed upon her—the truth behind Tito’s lies, the missing pieces of his past. She did not need to question Baldassarre further. The certainty was there, cold and undeniable.

"You believe me," he pressed, seizing her wrist with a grip that belied his age. "You know what he is. You will help me?"

A tremor ran through her, but she did not pull away. "Yes," she whispered, though she did not yet grasp his meaning. Her fingers brushed his rough hand, her eyes filling with tears. "It is pitiful. Tell me—you were a scholar once. You taught him. How did it come to this?"

His face twisted. "Gone. All gone. They called me mad. He lied, and they dragged me to prison. I am old now—my mind slips like sand through my fingers. The world has turned against me." His voice wavered, then sharpened again. "But not you. He deceived you too. You hate him."

Romola flinched. The accusation was a lash. Images flashed before her—Tito’s early tenderness, the days when she had believed in him without question. A sob broke from her.

"You weep," Baldassarre said impatiently. "Tears are useless. They quench the fire, and fire is what we need. Listen to me."

She forced herself to stillness, though doubt prickled at her. Was this man truly sane? Yet the weight of his story pressed upon her, too vivid to dismiss.

"Tell me," she urged softly. "How did you lose yourself?"

His gaze grew distant. "Illness. A long darkness. When I woke, the world was empty. I knew only that I had lost something—someone. The Boy. My son." His voice cracked. "And when I found him again, he denied me. He stole my name, my past. He left me to rot."

Romola’s heart ached. She thought of her own father, his sorrow for the son he had lost. The parallel was too cruel to ignore.

"But it returned once," Baldassarre continued, his voice hardening. "For a moment, I remembered everything. I tried to expose him—but he spun his lies again. They locked me away. He wears armor, that one. But you—you can strip it from him."

She stiffened. "What do you mean?"

"He has another wife," he hissed. "A fool who believes his lies. She bears his children. I can show you where she lives."

The revelation struck like a physical blow. Anger flared first—hot, indignant—before colder shock set in. Romola’s hands clenched in her lap.

"You hate him," Baldassarre pressed. "You are proud. You thirst for justice."

She could not speak. The conflict within her was too vast—rage, pity, horror, all warring at once.

"You will help me," he insisted, his fingers tightening. "I have a knife. My arm is still strong enough. When you draw him out, I will strike."

She recoiled inwardly but forced herself to calm. Arguing now would be futile. Instead, she grasped for time.

"Where is this woman?" she asked, her voice unsteady.

He frowned, struggling to recall. "I cannot remember all. But I know the place. I will take you—but not now. He may be there."

"The hour is late," Romola agreed, rising. The sun had dipped below the hills; shadows stretched long across the earth. "When will you come for me?"

"Tomorrow," he said, his eyes alight with feverish anticipation. "Meet me where you found me today. At mid-day."

She nodded. "I will be there."

His grip on her arm tightened once more. "We will have our revenge. The world has wronged me, but you—you will set it right."

She hesitated. "I would help you in other ways. You suffer. Let me bring you comfort—"

"No!" he snarled. "Nothing else. Only justice."

There was no reasoning with him. She stepped back. "Then mid-day tomorrow."

He sank onto the rock, his energy spent. "Go. I will rest here."

She turned and walked away, but at the last bend in the path, she glanced back. He sat motionless, a solitary figure against the darkening slope, his face turned toward the distant city—where the man who had destroyed them both walked free.

Romola moved through the dimming streets with singular purpose, her mind alight with a resolve that had swelled within her like a gathering storm. The fading crimson in the west mirrored the urgency in her chest as she crossed the bridge, the Arno’s dark waters whispering below. She did not call for Maso; she did not care for caution. The Palazzo Vecchio loomed ahead, and though her husband might be within, she could not hesitate.

The usher recognized her name and led her to a quiet chamber, where the flickering tapers cast restless shadows on frescoed walls. The door opened, and Bernardo del Nero stood before her, his bearing unbent by years, his sharp eyes narrowing in concern.

"Romola," he said, voice low. "What brings you here like this?"

She stepped forward, her face pale beneath its weariness, her hair loosened as though shaken by the weight of her thoughts. When he clasped her shoulders, she trembled.

"It is not of myself I come to speak," she said quickly. "Godfather—you are in danger. Here, in the palace, in the streets. There are men who would see you fall, some from zeal, others from treachery. Trust no one. If you stay, you will be betrayed."

Bernardo’s mouth curved, wry and weary. "Child, do you think an old fox like me doesn’t know the taste of poison? These are ancient warnings."

"No!" Her hands clenched. "This is no stale suspicion. I know things—things I cannot say. But I beg you, when your office ends, leave the city. Go to your villa. If harm comes to you—" Her voice fractured. "It will be as though I struck the blow myself."

The words escaped unbidden, and she stiffened, startled by her own outburst.

Bernardo studied her, his gaze softening. "Ah, little one," he murmured. "You carry too much." He turned away briefly, then sighed. "Warnings are wasted on me. I do not plot, but neither do I retreat. If I stand with stubborn men, I share their fate. My years are few; let them take what’s left."

He touched her hair, gentle as if she were still the girl he had once sheltered. She leaned into him, suddenly aching for the lost simplicity of that time—when griefs were clean, untainted by deceit.

"Silence is best?" he asked.

"For now," she whispered. "But I do not know if it will always be so."

"Then remember," he said, drawing the black drapery over her head, "you have a father’s ear while I live—and a father’s house." He opened the door. "Go. You are a shadow in this light; the streets will not trouble you."

That night, exhaustion dragged her into depths untouched by dreams. She woke to the thunder of cannon.

Piero de’ Medici was at the gates.

Maso, breathless with news, spoke of a city stirring like a hive struck by a stick. The Signoria had been warned; men filled the Piazza, their loyalties murky as the dawn. Some said Piero had been invited, others spat at his name. Romola climbed to the loggia, straining for signs of invasion—or repulsion.

Later, she stood on the steps of the Badia, the doors closing behind her, and watched the shift in the crowd’s hum. The guns still roared, but now laughter rode the air. Piero had fled, his horse’s hooves pounding toward Siena.

A shopkeeper, grip tight on his pike, muttered, "He’s gone, but the snakes who let him in remain. The new Signoria will root them out—we’ll see their names soon enough."

The words pierced Romola like a blade. Danger had not yet closed its jaws, but as she turned homeward, another fear gnawed at her: Baldassarre was gone, and she did not know where.

The golden days stretched long and weary, bringing Romola neither the fulfillment of her deepest hope nor the sting of her sharpest fear. Baldassarre remained silent, hidden as if swallowed by the city’s shadows, and though the government’s watchful eyes scoured Florence for traces of conspiracy, none were found. Yet the days were not empty of sorrow. Two heavy blows fell upon the city—and upon her heart: the creeping advance of the Plague, and the thunderous decree of Savonarola’s Excommunication.

These trials, instead of pushing her further from the Frate, bound her to him anew. The man who had first shown her a life beyond herself, who had kindled in her the fire of purpose, now stood beleaguered, his voice stifled by the very institution he sought to purify. How could she walk through the stricken streets, easing the suffering of the sick and the despairing, without remembering that it was Savonarola who had taught her the strength to do so? How could she witness his condemnation—a man whose only heresy was his refusal to let faith be a hollow rite—without feeling her allegiance harden into defiance?

On a stifling evening in June, the Excommunication was pronounced in the Duomo. Romola went, not in hope, but in resolve—to arm herself with the sight of resistance. The scene was a bitter mockery of the gatherings she had once known. No sea of upturned faces, alight with fervor; no voice ringing against the vaulted stones, stirring the soul to courage. Instead, dim tapers cast restless shadows on the walls, their feeble light catching the bowed heads of monks and priests, their chants thin against the weight of the sentence read aloud—a sentence already nailed to church doors, now made final. Then the candles were snuffed out, and the men filed away like specters, leaving the great church to the silence of the condemned.

What struck Romola most was not the act itself, but the faces around her: the glee of coarse men who had long wished the Frate gone, the shaken doubt of those who had loved him. For most, the Church was not a thing to be questioned—it was the very air they breathed, the firmament above them. To deny its power was to deny the sky. Savonarola’s bold words—that an unjust excommunication was no excommunication at all—were not a rallying cry but a terror, as though he had raised his hand against some unseen force that could strike back without warning.

But Romola had never bowed to tradition. Her faith had been shaped not by the rituals of the Church, but by the man who stood against its corruption. To her, the choice was stark: on one side, Savonarola, with his fierce love of justice and purity; on the other, the bloated greed of Rodrigo Borgia, now Pope Alexander VI, a man whose name was whispered with disgust across Italy. There were no shades in this battle—only light and darkness. And in that clarity, she found strength. The Frate was no longer just a Florentine preacher snarling at local foes; he was a voice crying out to Christendom itself, defying tyranny.

It was easier, somehow, to stand beside a standard-bearer. The heart beats faster at the sight of courage, and for a moment, the path ahead seems clear. Romola clung to that certainty through the sweltering days.

She had said nothing to Tito of Baldassarre’s revelations. What good would it do? He would only smile, make some light, cutting remark about her misplaced pity for a would-be murderer. No, Baldassarre was no threat—not to Tito, at least. The danger was the other way around. If Tito found him again, what then? Another quiet disappearance? Another sin buried?

She needed to see Baldassarre once more, to hear the full truth of this "other wife"—to know if she herself was the interloper in a marriage made before Tito ever came to Florence. The thought festered, poisoning old memories. The man she had loved was a mirage. What remained was a stranger, one she could scarcely look upon without a shudder.

Yet he was seldom home. Like many of Florence’s wealthy, he retreated to the countryside, slipping from one villa to another, welcomed everywhere. Romola refused to leave the city, and so he had no need to linger where her silence was a reproach.

By late July, the Plague had loosened its grip, and Savonarola’s fate seemed less hopeless—the new magistrates were pleading his case to Rome, begging for the Excommunication to be lifted. But Romola felt the weight of exhaustion. Her body, long sustained by urgency, now dragged. Yet her mind would not let her rest.

Each morning and evening, she walked, changing her path daily, half-hoping, half-dreading to find Baldassarre. Perhaps illness had stolen his memory again. Perhaps he no longer knew her. Or perhaps—most likely—he was dead, another unseen victim of the Plague.

The streets held no answer. Only the unrelenting heat, and the waiting.

The morning sun had already begun to press heavily upon Florence’s stone streets when Romola, returning from San Marco, turned into the Borgo La Croce. The lane was quiet, her own footsteps the only sound in the stillness, until a small figure caught her eye—a child, no older than three, clad only in a white shirt, his sturdy legs bare beneath a riot of russet curls. He stood frozen, his dark eyes wide with tears, his lip trembling as he clutched the fabric of his shirt in tiny fists.

At the sight of Romola’s shadow stretching toward him, the boy burst into sobs. Instinctively, she knelt, baring her head, and gathered him into her arms, murmuring soft words until his cries subsided into hiccupping silence.

"You’ve wandered far, little one," she said, brushing his damp cheeks. "Shall we find your home?"

The boy—Lillo, as she would soon learn—did not answer, but his grip on her hand was firm as they walked. He guided her with the certainty of a creature who knew his streets, turning at last toward a humble house where the door stood ajar. Inside, the light fell upon a scene so still it seemed suspended: a young woman, Tessa, dozing beside a cradle, while an old servant, Monna Lisa, bent over her work, deaf to the world.

At Lillo’s tug, Tessa startled awake, her face flushing as she recognized Romola.

"You!" she breathed, trembling.

Romola smiled gently. "Your son found me. He was brave—he led me back."

Tessa’s relief was short-lived; she turned on Monna Lisa with a child’s reproach. "You let him run away!"

The old woman sighed. "He’s quick as a sprite. But who brought him home?"

Romola’s presence was a marvel to Tessa, who had last seen her in the chaos of Carnival, a figure half-saint, half-stranger. Now, as they spoke, Romola pieced together the truth: Tessa was married—to a man whose name she whispered as "Naldo," whose dark curls she kept like a relic, whose absences stretched long and unexplained.

"And he gave you this?" Romola asked, touching the red silk cord around Tessa’s neck, from which hung a tiny parchment *Breve* and a lock of hair.

Tessa nodded eagerly. "For luck. When he’s gone, it keeps me safe."

Romola’s pulse quickened. The curl—so like one she had once severed herself—the timing of the marriage, the vague references to a man who came and went like shadow. The truth coiled in her stomach, cold and certain.

But Tessa, innocent, chattered on about Lillo, about Ninna in her cradle, about the happiness she had found despite the goats and mules left behind. Romola listened, her mind racing. Here was a wife, a family—lawful, perhaps, while her own vows frayed at the edges.

Before leaving, Romola cut a lock of her own hair and pressed it into Tessa’s hand. "If you need me, think of this. I will come."

Then she stepped back into the sunlit street, her thoughts a storm. The law bound her, but rebellion whispered. That night, she would face Tito—not as a supplicant, but as a woman who had seen the shape of her chains.

When he returned, his gaze met hers, knowing.

"You’ve heard," he said quietly.

She had. And now, the reckoning would begin.

Tito Melena had long since calculated the risks, and he moved through the labyrinth of Florentine politics with the precision of a man who knows every turn will lead him to safety. The unraveling of the Medicean conspiracy was no surprise to him—only an inconvenience to be managed. Over the past three months, he had woven himself into the fabric of every faction: whispering secrets to Rome and Milan, nodding gravely with the Piagnoni, and assuring the Mediceans of his unwavering loyalty. Duplicity was a currency as common as florins in Florence, and Tito spent it liberally.

To the Mediceans, he was indispensable precisely because he lacked their hereditary allegiances—his loyalty was negotiable, his services cleanly transactional. To the Arrabbiati, his willingness to betray Savonarola’s followers made him a useful blade in the dark. And to the Piagnoni, his apparent neutrality was proof enough of his reliability; they mistook his silence for sincerity.

The arrest of Lamberto dell’ Antella had forced his hand, but Tito was never one to wait for disaster. He had already mapped his escape routes: immunity from prosecution, the retention of his position, and—most crucially—silence. The price? A few more names, a little more blood on the ledger. A small cost, when weighed against his future.

That evening, in a private audience with Francesco Valori, Tito played his part flawlessly. He confessed his ties to the Medici with a shrug, as if it were a youthful indiscretion. He hinted at personal grievances against Bernardo del Nero, knowing Valori’s hatred would do the rest. And then, with the smooth assurance of a merchant closing a deal, he offered the one thing Florence’s rulers craved: more evidence. A fresh plot, documents to be retrieved from Siena, enough to justify any verdict the council desired.

Valori, a man who saw the world in stark lines of loyalty and betrayal, did not pause to question motives. The state needed traitors as much as it needed heroes—perhaps more. By dawn, Tito rode out of Florence with his immunity secured, his position intact, and the knowledge that he had once again turned chaos to his advantage.

The council had watched him with cool assessment. Some, perhaps, felt a flicker of distaste. But what did that matter? Florence was a city that hissed at traitors even as it used them. Tito understood the game too well to care.

As his horse carried him toward Siena, he allowed himself a rare moment of satisfaction. Soon, Florence would be behind him—a stepping stone to Milan, to Rome, to a future where his past could not follow. He had no patience for regret. The world belonged to men who knew when to shed their skin.

Unseen in the shadows, Ser Ceccone watched him go—another informer, another blade waiting to be turned. But Tito did not look back.

Tito returned briefly from Siena only to depart again, his absence stretching until the seventeenth of August. In that time, the fate of the accused conspirators remained suspended—uncertainty a weight upon the city, and upon Romola most of all. Each day brought shifting rumors: hope flared when delay seemed to favor the prisoners, then dimmed as factions clashed. The Eight, tasked with judgment, hesitated; the Signoria wavered; the Great Council’s appeal became a contested weapon. Romola moved through those days as if in a dream, her thoughts tethered to one grim question: would her godfather live?

On the day of the council’s meeting, she had been permitted a second visit to Bernardo del Nero—guarded, watched, devoid of privacy. Now, back in the Via de’ Bardi, she sat listless in the sunlit saloon, her fingers pressed to her temples. Monna Brigida had gone to gather news; the waiting gnawed at Romola’s nerves.

Then the door opened. Not Brigida’s step.

She knew at once.

Since the night she had confronted Tito, no proof of his treachery had surfaced—only whispers of his loyalty to the Mediceans, his errands in Romagna under the Republic’s guise. But suspicion had hardened in her like cooled metal. The memory of Baldassarre’s face, the accusation in his eyes, had painted Tito’s every act in shades of deceit. Now, hearing his footfall, she shuddered. She did not turn.

He seated himself across from her, unruffled. "This meeting mirrors our parting," he said, his voice smooth. "But I bring hope. The council is prorogued—condemnation passed, but the Appeal may yet be heard."

Romola’s hands clenched. "When?"

"The twenty-first. Three days to sway opinion."

She stood abruptly, color rising. "Savonarola—"

"Precisely." Tito’s smile was thin. "Valori’s rage swayed the vote today, but others resist him—Guicciardini, Ridolfi. The Frate has already interceded for Tornabuoni. You could speak to him."

She barely heard the rest—his measured words about Savonarola’s contradictions, his veiled jabs at the Frate’s followers. Her mind raced. The Appeal was law; to deny it would stain Florence. "I must see him," she murmured.

Tito rose, gracious. "Then lose no time."

But as he turned to leave, the mask slipped. "Or will you instead," he said, too lightly, "denounce me to the Signoria? Brand me a traitor on the word of a madman? A striking spectacle for a wife."

The words struck like a lash. Romola flinched, but her voice was steel. "It’s too late for lies, Tito. I know who that old man was. Your father. That truth is a chasm between us."

For a breath, silence. Then Tito’s reply, cool as a blade: "And if I refuse to let you go? Will you drag my name through the streets? Ruin yourself to spite me?"

She turned away, sickened. "I don’t speak for myself," she whispered. "I speak because silence is complicity."

He left then, though she hardly noticed. When Monna Brigida returned, Romola was already on her feet. "Cousin," she said, hollow-voiced, "we go to San Marco. I must see Fra Salvestro."

The die was cast. There would be no turning back.

The golden light of morning bathed Florence as Romola made her way once more to San Marco. The night before, Fra Salvestro had granted her request—a rare audience with Fra Girolamo in the chapter-house. The friar, ever cautious against scandal, permitted such meetings only under strict conditions, ensuring they bore no shadow of secrecy.

The streets hummed with tension. Every corner, every loggia, buzzed with heated debate. The fate of the conspirators hung in the balance, and Florence was divided. Pamphlets circulated—some demanding swift justice, others pleading for law and mercy. Romola’s fingers tightened around the papers she had snatched from Bratti’s stall.

*"Justice sells quicker,"* the peddler had grinned, *"so I charge extra for it."*

She hurried past San Giovanni, avoiding familiar faces, her mind fixed on one purpose.

---

The cloisters of San Marco were quiet, save for murmured exchanges between lay visitors and friars. Fra Salvestro stood chatting idly, his curiosity piqued by gossip. Romola ignored them, her gaze drawn instead to the frescoes lining the walls—those same sorrowful figures that had witnessed her grief for Dino. The memory flickered, but she smothered it. There was no room for the past now.

She unfolded the handbills, scanning the arguments. The government had new evidence—another plot, they claimed. Her stomach twisted. *Tito’s doing?* The suspicion burned, but she forced it aside. There was no time.

The door opened. Savonarola entered.

She knelt, as custom dictated, then rose to face him. The months since she had last seen him had carved deeper lines into his face. His eyes, once alight with fervor, now held something harder—something distant.

*"Father,"* she began, voice steady despite the tremor in her hands, *"you know why I’ve come."*

He inclined his head. *"Speak, my daughter."*

*"It is Bernardo del Nero’s life I plead for."* The words rushed out. *"He is no traitor. He is a man of honor, condemned without the appeal you fought to grant all Florentines. Will you stay silent now?"*

Savonarola’s expression did not soften. *"The State must act for its survival. These men conspired not just in the past—they plot still."*

*"Plot?"* Romola’s breath caught. She unfolded the second handbill, scanning the fresh accusations. *"New evidence—conveniently now?"* Her voice sharpened. *"Or is this a pretext? Lorenzo Tornabuoni, your ally, was spared. Yet my godfather, who has never feigned loyalty, is to die?"*

A flicker of irritation crossed his face. *"I do not desire his death."*

*"Then speak against it!"* she cried. *"You wield influence enough. Or do you fear to weaken your own cause?"*

The air between them thickened. Savonarola’s jaw tightened. *"The cause of God’s kingdom demands sacrifice. Even of good men, if their deaths preserve Florence from greater evil."*

*"Greater evil?"* Romola’s voice broke. *"What kingdom is this, built on blood and hypocrisy? You once preached mercy. Now you hide behind politics."*

His eyes flashed. *"You judge what you do not understand. The wicked are not always the greatest obstacles—sometimes it is the virtuous who stand in God’s way."*

*"Then you admit it!"* she shot back. *"You want him dead *because* he is good!"*

For a heartbeat, silence. Then Savonarola’s voice dropped, low and strained. *"Be grateful your soul has never known such choices. The path I walk is darker than you fathom."*

*"No,"* Romola whispered, stepping back. *"God’s kingdom is wider than your party. And if this is its price, then I want no part in it."*

Their gazes locked—hers blazing with defiance, his burning with certainty. No reconciliation lay here.

Without another word, she turned and left.

The door closed behind her, sealing the rift between them.

Three nights had passed since the sentence was pronounced, and now the moon hung low over the piazza, its pale light struggling to pierce the sea of restless bodies below. The square before the Old Palace was not its usual self—no neat geometric patterns of light and shadow stretched across the pavement. Instead, a single mass of humanity pressed together, shoulder to shoulder, breath to breath, a living tide of outrage and dread.

The air was thick with shouts, curses, the clatter of weapons, the occasional shrill cry of a woman, the deep tolling of a bell—a sound that seemed to mark time for the inevitable. Florence was awake tonight, every soul drawn to this place, whether by fury or morbid fascination.

Inside the palace, the council chamber still hummed with the aftermath of bitter debate. The Signoria, the eighty senators, the chosen men of the city had argued through the day and deep into the night, voices rising to shouts, tempers flaring. The question had been simple, in form if not in consequence: *Should the Appeal be granted? Or should the blade fall now, before delay bred rebellion?*

Francesco Valori, the Florentine Cato, had settled it. His words were a blade of their own—sharp, final. *If the Signoria will not act, others will. And they will not hesitate.* The threat had done its work. The white beans of opposition vanished; the black prevailed. Death would come tonight, not just for the five prisoners, but for Valori himself in time. The wheel of Florentine justice turned swiftly, and none could predict where it would stop.

Now the condemned were led through the chamber, barefoot, in chains. Their supporters had hoped the sight would move the crowd—venerable Bernardo del Nero, old Niccolò Ridolfi, men whose loyalties had been forged in a different Florence; young Tornabuoni and Pucci, whose absence would leave a hollow space in the city’s life. But pity, when it came, was a cold thing, the kind that festers into vengeance rather than mercy.

Below, against one of the great pillars of the palace courtyard, Romola stood waiting. She had fought for this—permission to see her godfather one last time, to stand with him until the council’s decision was sealed. Now she waited, motionless, her face set, her heart a drumbeat of defiance. Giovan Battista Ridolfi, Savonarola’s disciple, stood beside her, a silent guardian.

Tito was here too, somewhere in the press of officials and foreign observers, but Romola had not sought him. They had not spoken since the night of the seventeenth, when words had turned to knives between them. He moved through the crowd with the grave dignity of a man torn between duty and private grief, offering quiet explanations when pressed: *She is distraught. The situation has unhinged her. It is best I keep my distance.*

Around him, voices murmured—some sympathetic, some sharp.

*"Ah, the old Bardi blood,"* Cennini said with a shrug. *"This will shake her loose from the Frate, mark my words."*

A young French envoy smirked. *"It is charming when a beautiful woman believes love should overrule politics. Less charming when men think the same."*

Niccolò Machiavelli, ever the cynic, offered a dry observation: *"Half-measures invite revenge. The only safe blow is the one too heavy to return."*

Then the moment came. The prisoners were being led out. The crowd surged, a wave of bodies pressing toward the Bargello. Romola did not move. Her world had narrowed to this: the sight of Bernardo del Nero walking to his death, the resolve to stand witness until the end.

The path to the scaffold was lined with guards, three hundred strong—Valori’s doing. A contradiction, like all else tonight: fear of the crowd’s rage, fear of a rescue.

In the courtyard of the Bargello, Romola was permitted one last embrace. Bernardo’s hands were bound, but he turned his face toward her, his voice low. *"My poor Romola, I have only to die. You must live—and I will not be there to help you."*

*"You will,"* she whispered fiercely. *"Because I will remember."*

Then she was led away, up the steps to the loggia, where she could see the scaffold below. She stood like stone, her eyes fixed, her breath steady. Around her, the crowd seethed—relatives waiting to claim their dead, faces twisted with grief or rage, torchlight flickering over carvings and weeping eyes alike.

Then silence fell.

Bernardo ascended the scaffold, his step firm, his head unbowed. He paused, looked out over the crowd, and spoke, his voice clear in the hush.

His gaze found hers. She reached for him, wordless.

Then—

A blink. A breath.

And it was over.

When the priest touched her shoulder, murmuring of peace, she stirred as if waking. *"I am ready,"* she said. *"Let us go."*

There was no more clinging. Only the need to move, to escape the grip of a fate she refused to accept.

The sun hung low over the Mediterranean, its fading light painting the water in hues of molten gold. Romola stood at the edge of the shore, where the quiet waves lapped against the sand near the humble fishing village of Viareggio. She had fled Florence once more, this time without a voice to call her back. The grey monastic robes she wore were no longer a conscious disguise but a reflection of her resignation—what did it matter who she was, or what she was called? The threads of duty that had once bound her had unraveled completely.

Her marriage, once sacred, had become a hollow pledge, its promises broken beyond repair. The ideals that had once guided her—faith, purpose, even love—now seemed like illusions. Without them, what was left but the weight of a life adrift?

Savonarola, the man she had once revered as a beacon of divine purpose, now stood diminished in her eyes. His grand visions had narrowed into petty politics, his holy cause tainted by the very human frailties he had condemned. The revolution that had once stirred her soul now felt like a distant clamor, meaningless against the vast silence of her despair.

She had loved deeply, trusted fiercely, and now she was empty. The world was a tangle of contradictions, every effort a futile pull at knotted strings. Even compassion had abandoned her; the well of pity within her had run dry. She was tired—tired of striving, tired of suffering, tired of the ceaseless noise of living.

The sea murmured before her, its gentle rhythm a siren’s call. How easy it would be to step into its embrace, to let the waves carry her away from thought, from memory, from the relentless ache of existence. She had once read a story in Boccaccio—a lovelorn woman who set herself adrift, surrendering to fate. The tale had been a passing fancy then, but now it returned to her with the force of a longing.

A small boat rested near the shore, its hull worn but still buoyant. A fisherman, busy with his nets, glanced up as she approached. She asked its price, pressed coins into his satchel, and waited as he gathered his things and left. The breeze whispered over the water, urging her forward.

When the last light had faded and the stars emerged, she pushed the boat into the darkening sea. The sail caught the faint wind, and she let it carry her, directionless. There was no plan, only surrender—to the night, to the waves, to whatever end awaited her.

She lay back, the wooden ribs of the boat a rough pillow beneath her head. The vast sky arched above, indifferent in its beauty. No divine hand reached down; no voice spoke from the heavens. She was utterly alone.

Pulling the cowl over her face, she shut out the stars. Their cold brilliance felt like the gaze of strangers, watching without seeing. In the darkness, she imagined herself already in the grave, reaching for hands that would never clasp hers again.

The boat drifted on, a speck on the endless sea, carrying her farther from shore, from life, from the pain of being known. The water cradled her, and for the first time in so long, there was no decision to make—only the quiet, deepening dark.

The morning of February 27th found Florence in restless motion. Though the Carnival’s final day promised another Bonfire of Vanities before the Old Palace, the true current of the city’s fervor flowed toward San Marco. The Piazza swelled with bodies, a sea of faces turned toward the church, where a wooden pulpit stood empty above the doors.

The man who would soon ascend it had been silenced elsewhere—banned from the Duomo, shunned by fellow clerics, his very words made forbidden under threat of Rome’s wrath. Yet the people came. They had heard his defiance: *A Pope who buys his throne cannot wield Christ’s authority. To obey him is sin.* And so they gathered, hungry for certainty, for a sign that this fiery friar spoke with Heaven’s own voice.

The monks assembled first, black-robed and solemn, their chant a murmuring tide. But the crowd’s attention was not for them. It was the silence before the storm, the breath held before the lightning strike. Then—movement. A figure in black knelt in the pulpit, head bowed, face hidden. The air tightened.

When Savonarola rose at last, pushing back his cowl, his presence was a blade cutting through the murmur. His hands lifted—slender, expressive, more like instruments of prayer than flesh. Some in the front ranks knelt at once; others resisted, caught between awe and fear. *Would God strike them for kneeling to an excommunicant?*

Then his voice, clear and low: *“Misereatur vestri…”* More sank to their knees. The words rose, sharpening, climbing—*“Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus!”*—until it rang like a challenge across the Piazza. By the final syllable, every head was bowed.

Exhausted, Savonarola knelt again, breath ragged. Preaching was his lifeblood, but each sermon drained him. When he stood once more, he held aloft the consecrated Host, his face gaunt in the morning light.

*“Three days past,”* he declared, *“I begged you to pray—that if my work is not God’s, He strike me down where I stand. I ask it again. Pray now.”*

A hush. Did any truly pray? Or did they only watch, breath suspended, as the friar lifted his own plea to the heavens? *“Lord, if I speak not Your truth, let Your fire consume me.”*

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then—sunlight, breaking through the grey, spilling over the crystal vessel in his hands, gilding his face. A shout erupted: *“A sign! Behold the answer!”*

For Savonarola, it was a fleeting triumph, a glimpse of a greater stage—one where all Christendom would witness his vindication. But as he retreated into the church, the spell fractured. Laughter and jeers bubbled up where reverence had been. The sunlight, impartial now, touched saint and skeptic alike.

From a loggia across the Piazza, Tito observed the shifting crowd. *“Applause gives way to criticism,”* he remarked. *“Yet for a moment, our Frate made even doubters kneel.”*

Beside him, Pietro Cennini frowned. *“Unless proven otherwise, I’ll believe the man has more faith, not less. No liar would dare such a test.”*

But the test was not over. And Florence, ever fickle, had already begun to murmur anew.

A month after the Carnival, on a crisp morning near the end of March, Tito Melema descended the broad marble steps of the Palazzo Vecchio, his path set toward San Marco. Yet he did not take the most direct route. Instead, he chose to meander through the Piazza di Santa Croce, where the faithful streamed out from the early Lenten sermon.

The grand basilica of Santa Croce had lately drawn the largest crowds for these daily sermons, for Savonarola’s voice had been forcibly stilled. The Signoria, yielding to the Pope’s latest thunderous missive, had forbidden the friar to preach—on pain of interdict should Florence dare shelter this "wretched worm" and "monstrous idol" any longer. With Fra Girolamo silenced in his own pulpit at San Marco, the next best spectacle was hearing him denounced. Santa Croce provided that, where the appointed Franciscan preacher had upped the stakes—not merely arguing against Savonarola’s heresies but vowing to walk through fire to disprove them. Had the Dominican not boasted that his divine mission would be vindicated by a miracle when the time came? Well, here was the time. Let him step into the flames. If he emerged unscathed, God favored him. If not—justice would be served. And to ensure no evasion, the Franciscan, Fra Francesco di Puglia, pledged himself as kindling, if need be, for the sake of holy logic.

Savonarola, as ever, ignored these provocations. But his loyal lieutenant, Fra Domenico, preaching to the women in the Via del Cocomero, took up the challenge at once. He would face the fire in his master’s stead. The people, ever hungry for drama, thrilled at the notion—until Savonarola, sensing the peril in mere debate, ordered Domenico to withdraw. The Franciscan, undeterred, sneered: his challenge was for the prophet himself, not underlings.

Interest in the sermons had waned since. Yet this morning, as Tito entered the Piazza di Santa Croce, he found the usual throng pouring from the church, clustering near the monastery entrance. He drifted toward the commotion, his manner casual, his gaze sharp.

The crowd’s focus was a large placard, freshly posted by order of the Signoria, its official script dense with Latin. Few could decipher it, though most knew its gist—and this only sharpened their frustration.

"The Frate’s doctrines are to be tested by fire," declared Goro, a familiar figure among the onlookers. "The Signoria’s taken charge. The writing says so—just as the padre preached!"

"Nay, Goro," corrected a plump shopkeeper, "the Frate must prove his truth by *not* burning. Walk through unharmed, that’s the trick."

"Aye," drawled a young sculptor, lounging with an artist’s careless grace, "but Fra Girolamo balks at the idea. Says he’s sound enough already—why singe himself to prove it? Leaves such chores to poor Fra Domenico."

"Then he’s a coward," Goro wheezed. "Like at the Carnival—promised us lightning from heaven, and what came of it? Naught!"

"Quiet, bleating goat," snapped a lanky shoemaker, his shoulders draped with slippers. "The Frate flinches from nothing. He’ll walk when the time comes—no gray robe at his heels. But I’d give a shoe to know what this Latin says."

The shopkeeper sighed. "Too much of it. No scholars about?"

Heads turned. The sculptor brightened. "Ah! Here’s one."

Tito approached, his elegance commanding deference—the gold-clasped mantle, the velvet cap, the patrician bearing. The crowd parted.

"My thanks," Tito said, raising his own cap with practiced charm. "I merely wished, like you, to see what the placard holds. Ah—as I thought. The Signoria, in their wisdom, permit any willing soul to pledge themselves for the trial. A generous offer! And no doubt many faithful will rush to sign. For what is fire to true belief? Fear lies in doubt. If a man trusts the flames will spare him—why hesitate?" He spread his hands, inviting consensus. "Thus I’ve never doubted the Frate’s vow to walk through fire. Would any of you, if you believed as he does?"

The crowd shifted, some nodding, others wary. The young sculptor grinned. "Then Fra Francesco’s the braver, Messer Secretary—he offers to burn for truth, certain it will consume him."

Tito inclined his head. "A noble point. But if he errs, he burns for falsehood—and the Church honors no such martyrs. Best wait for the trial."

"True enough," muttered the shopkeeper. "But the Latin, if you please?"

"Willingly." Tito’s smile was smooth. "It lists the Frate’s chief doctrines—those the fire must test. First: *Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione*—the Church must be cleansed."

"Aye, we know that," the shoemaker cut in. "Priests must mend their ways—no miracle needed!"

Tito nodded. "*Flagellabitur*—it shall be scourged. *Renovabitur*—purified. Then: *Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur*—Florence too, after scourging, shall rise and thrive."

"Pisa returned, then!" the shopkeeper exclaimed.

"And the English wool trade restored, God willing," added an old man in a worn beretta. "We’ve been scourged enough with empty purses."

Just then, a tall figure emerged from the convent—Dolfo Spini, his scarlet plume nodding, his glance sliding over Tito with feigned indifference. The crowd shrank back as Tito, unruffled, resumed his reading.

"*Infideles convertentur ad Christum*—the heathen shall turn to Christ."

"Turks and Moors? Fair enough," the shopkeeper conceded.

"*Haec autem omnia erunt temporibus nostris*—all this in our day."

Goro snorted. "What use else?"

"Lastly: the Pope’s excommunication against Fra Girolamo is void. Those who ignore it sin not."

The shopkeeper folded his arms. "I’ll believe that after the fire speaks."

"As we all shall," Tito agreed. "The rest is but the Signoria’s summons for volunteers. Now, if I may—?" With a bow, he withdrew, his pace quickening as he left the piazza.

Down a quiet street, he paused at an inconspicuous door, knocked, and was admitted by a young woman. A chuckle under her chin, a step inside—and he faced Spini, sprawled with a hound and her pups.

"Business, my Dolfo," Tito said, when the captain’s play lagged.

Spini shoved the dog aside. "These Franciscan fools waver. Fra Francesco fears magic—thinks the Frate might cheat the flames. And not a lay brother will pair with Domenico!"

Tito sighed. "Must I repeat? Once the pyre’s built and the crowd waits, all Savonarola needs is to *fail* to walk. His credit burns without him. The Franciscans need not singe a robe."

Spini scowled. "But if he *does* walk? If the devil aids him?"

Tito’s lip curled. "He has no such allies. Only his wits—and they falter."

"Then why not knife him in the piazza and be done?"

"Because Valori and the Albizzi would rise. No, let the people turn on him *themselves* when his miracle fails. The Signoria exiles him; Rome claims him. You emerge the savior—without bloodying your hands."

Spini rubbed his head, then clapped Tito’s shoulder. "You’re a useful demon. Supper tonight—I’ll lay out the plan."

Tito’s smile was thin. "The *same* plan, my patron. Recall it well."

As he left, one worry pricked him: Ser Ceccone, the sharp-nosed notary, now cozy with Spini. A witness to Tito’s past evasions, the man’s dislike could fester into danger. But gold and posts might yet buy silence.

Omniscience, alas, was not Tito’s gift. He did not know how deeply Ceccone’s hatred ran—nor that the notary, spurned as a Medicean informant, now fed suspicions to Milan. A small man, yes. But even wasps sting.

Tito’s arrival at San Marco had been anticipated, and Fra Niccolò, Savonarola’s secretary, led him at once through the hushed corridors of the monastery. The frescoes of Fra Angelico lined the walls—delicate visions of saints and angels, their gold halos and celestial hues glowing softly in the dim light. The cells stood empty in this hour of respite, their narrow windows casting pale shafts onto bare walls, rough pallets, and the ever-present crucifix.

At the end of the corridor, behind a closed door, Savonarola knelt in his inner chamber. The white folds of his Dominican habit shrouded him as he bent before the crucifix, lips moving in silent prayer. Yet his mind was far from tranquility.

To any observer, he might have been any ordinary friar, lost in humble devotions. But beneath that austere robe beat the heart of a man consumed by a tempest of conviction and doubt, his soul a battleground of fervent faith and gnawing uncertainty. The Trial by Fire loomed before him—not as a divine test, but as a snare laid by his enemies. His followers clamored for it, their faith burning brighter than any pyre, certain that God would intervene to vindicate His prophet. Fra Domenico, zealous and unshaken, stood ready to walk into the flames.

But Savonarola could not share their certainty. His was a mind that could believe in distant miracles, in the unseen hand of Providence shaping history—yet faltered before the immediacy of scorching heat and searing pain. His imagination recoiled from the trial, even as his reason sought justifications.

The contradiction tore at him. He had preached divine signs, promised heavenly intervention—but now, faced with the tangible threat of fire, his body trembled where his spirit had once soared.

A knock at the door shattered his thoughts. He rose, composed himself, and draped his mantle over his shoulders before admitting his visitor.

Tito entered with polished deference, his manner smooth, his words measured. He spoke of couriers, of letters to France, of secret dispatches that might serve the Frate’s cause. Savonarola listened, his piercing eyes fixed on Tito’s face, searching for what lay beneath the courtier’s careful speech.

The question hung between them, unspoken. Yet the moment demanded action. With deliberate calm, Savonarola retrieved a sealed letter from his desk—a missive meant for the King of France, urging reform, denouncing the corruption of Rome.

"Take it," he said at last, his voice firm despite the tremor in his fingers.

Tito accepted the letter, bowing deeply. But as he departed, the Frate stood motionless, the weight of his decision pressing upon him. He had played his part—yet somewhere in the shadows, unseen hands were already at work.

Before the courier bearing Savonarola’s letter could cross the borders of Tuscany, another messenger rode swiftly north, carrying word to those who waited to intercept it. The game was in motion, and none could say where the pieces would fall.

A week had passed since the Bonfire of Vanities, and now the Piazza della Signoria swarmed with a crowd greater than any gathered for the feast of San Giovanni. By midday, every vantage point was claimed—men clung to iron rods, perched on statues, balanced on architraves, their discomfort forgotten in the anticipation of witnessing a miracle. For today, Florence would not merely hear of divine wonders; they would see one unfold before their eyes.

At the heart of the spectacle stood a long platform, eight feet wide and twenty yards in length, piled high with dry oak, thorns, and oil-soaked rags, a pyre meant to test faith with flame. It stretched from the foot of the Old Palace, beneath the stony gaze of Marzocco, the guardian lion. Three forces stood armed and waiting: the Signoria’s soldiers before the palace, Dolfo Spini’s Compagnacci at the far end, and Marco Salviati’s loyalists near the Loggia, where the rival orders—Franciscan and Dominican—would face their trial.

The air hummed with tension. The Franciscans had already taken their place in silence, their grey robes a stark contrast to the fervor soon to arrive. Then, from across the square, rose the thunderous chant of the Dominicans: *"Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered."* At their head strode Fra Domenico, clad in flame-colored cope, cross in hand, his face alight with conviction. Behind him came Savonarola, robed in white, bearing the Host. His voice rang clear, his expression unshaken, though every eye upon him bore either hope, doubt, or malice.

Yet beneath the prophet’s steadfast gaze lay a gnawing dread. The trial was not merely a test of faith—it was a battle for survival. The courier’s intercepted letters had reached Milan, sealing his fate in Rome. The flames ahead were but one peril; the true fire was the shifting will of Florence.

As the hours dragged on, delays mounted. Fra Domenico, denied the crucifix, now bore the Sacrament—a defiance that sparked fresh debate. The Franciscans balked; Savonarola held firm. The crowd, restless and hungry, grew impatient.

The jeers swelled into chaos. Spini’s Compagnacci surged forward, only to be repelled by Salviati’s men. Then, as if heaven itself intervened, the clouds broke. Rain poured down, drenching the pyre, the plaza, and the people’s fervor alike. The trial was over before it had begun.

Savonarola stood motionless as the storm passed. The Signoria granted him a guard, but no victory. The faces that once looked to him for salvation now twisted in scorn. As he walked from the Loggia, the Sacrament still in hand, no divine light blessed him—only the indifferent glare of the clearing sky.

*"Well parried, Frate,"* murmured Tito Melema from the shadows. *"But your time here is done."*

Niccolò Machiavelli sighed. *"A pity. With the times favoring reform, he might have achieved greatness."*

Savonarola heard none of it. The trial had ended, but the judgment had only begun.

The dawn of Palm Sunday—Olive Sunday, as the folk of the Valdarno called it—broke clear and golden over Florence, the yesterday’s rain leaving the air fresh and the cobblestones gleaming. In San Marco, Savonarola ascended the pulpit once more, his voice steady, his gaze unwavering. The faces turned toward him were alight with faith, with love—yet beneath his solemn benediction lay the quiet certainty of his fate. He had seen it in visions, in the silent whispers of his prayers. This would be his last sermon.

By sunset, the city had erupted.

The embers of yesterday’s fury, smothered through the morning, flared anew—not by chance, but by design. The Compagnacci struck first at the Duomo, disrupting the Piagnoni’s evening sermon. Then the cry rose, sharp as a blade: *"To San Marco! Burn San Marco!"*

The monastery’s walls, though sacred, were no sanctuary that night. The monks, long braced for this storm, fought back—some in their white robes like spectral warriors, others with the desperation of men who knew mercy would not come. Lay defenders, too, stood with them. Gunfire cracked near the high altar; stones rained from the roof; swords clashed in the cloisters. The battle raged until deep night, the government’s guards not sent to quell the mob, but to demand surrender: *Lay down your arms. Leave. Savonarola must go.*

Yet leaving meant death. The Frate, willing to face exile, was held back by his brethren. Others risked escape—Francesco Valori among them, slipping over the garden wall, hoping to rally aid. But the night’s true horror came later, when fresh guards arrived under the Signoria’s orders. Savonarola, Domenico, and Salvestro were demanded.

The Prophet walked out, lantern light catching the hatred in a hundred eyes. The guards’ promise of safety was hollow—their duty was only to deliver him to prison. The mob howled, surging forward, a river of spite. Hands grabbed, spat, struck. The worst of life, Savonarola might have thought, was this: not the threat of death, but the stripping of dignity, the jeers of those who once had listened rapt to his words.

Yet the riot was far from over.

With their quarry in chains, the pack turned elsewhere—toward the homes of his followers. This was no blind frenzy, but a calculated hunt. The Compagnacci led, directing the rabble’s clubs and torches. Francesco Valori, marked as the Frate’s right hand, fled San Marco only to find his house besieged. His wife fell, an arrow in her breast. He himself was cut down in the street, slaughtered by Ridolfi and Tornabuoni blades.

And behind it all, unseen, the puppet-master pulled his strings.

In a quiet house between Santa Croce and San Marco, Dolfo Spini held court, his visitors slipping in and out like shadows. Some wore the Compagnacci’s swagger, others plain cloaks, their weapons hidden. Wine flowed, though Spini drank just enough to stoke his temper. Among his guests: Ser Ceccone, ever the sycophant, and Francesco Cei, lounging with the ease of a man who expected his exile to end with the Frate’s ruin.

As the candles guttered, Spini’s mood blackened. News came of setbacks—the mob thwarted from sacking Soderini’s house, restless now, perhaps turning dangerous. But Spini had a solution. A snap of his fingers, a hissed order, and his men moved out again.

The night’s work was not done.

And somewhere in the chaos, a certain Greek—clever, too clever—was laughing. Spini’s fury burned hotter at the thought. *That ruby, that damned ruby…*

But revenge, like the riot, could wait for daylight.

As dusk settled over Florence, two Compagnacci set out on their dark errand, while across the Arno, another figure moved through the dim light. His purpose bore no relation to theirs—this solitary man sought the river’s edge, where the city’s walls hid a desolate stretch of reeds and damp grass, cut through by sluggish gutters draining into the current.

Baldassarre had come here before, drawn by the quiet and the slim chance of finding food washed ashore—a crust, vegetable scraps—anything to stave off the hunger that gnawed at him. He could no longer bear the shame of begging, but the will to live clung to him, stubborn as ivy on stone. Not hope, but the ghost of possibility: the thought that *she* might still come, that vengeance might yet be his.

He crouched by the water, eyes fixed on the murky flow, when distant clamor reached him—shouts, the tramp of many feet. The Compagnacci were on the move, splitting into two waves, one toward the Ponte Rubaconte, the other the Ponte Vecchio. They had their orders: sack a house in the Via de’ Bardi. Some knew the reason; most did not care. A mob needs little excuse.

Meanwhile, the master of that house was already fleeing. Tito, cloaked and swift, slipped through the gray dawn. Fear had dogged him since the knife’s edge grazed his throat in the Duomo’s shadow. Now, with Florence simmering, he meant to vanish—horses waited at San Gallo, Tessa and the children beyond the gates.

But the streets betrayed him. A roar swelled ahead—the mob poured onto the bridge. Hands seized him; his mantle tore away. “Piagnone! Medicean! Throw him over!” Panic sharpened his wits. He wrenched free his belt, flung it, the scarsella glinting, into the crowd. “Gold! Diamonds!” As they scrambled, he vaulted the parapet—

—and plunged into the Arno’s icy grip.

The water shocked him alive. He fought the current, muscles burning. Past Santa Trinita, past the Ponte alla Carrara—if he could reach the bank beyond, he might escape. But exhaustion dragged at him, the river’s pull relentless. His strokes faltered.

On the reedy shore, Baldassarre stirred. Something pale broke the water’s darkness—a face. His breath caught. *Justice?* The body washed ashore, sprawled at his feet.

Recognition struck like lightning.

Tito’s eyes flew open, meeting Baldassarre’s. Terror and understanding flared between them. The old man lunged, fingers locking on his throat. “You see me. You *know* me.”

Tito thrashed, but Baldassarre’s grip was iron. He knelt on the younger man’s chest, watching the light fade from those agate eyes. Not until they glazed did he loosen his hold—and even then, he kept his hand fisted in the velvet tunic. Let death come. Let witnesses find them.

None did.

Hours passed. The sun climbed, indifferent. Only when an oxcart halted at the river’s edge did the driver startle at the sight—two bodies tangled in the grass. He loaded them as they were, the old man’s grip unbroken.

By the time the cart rumbled into the Piazza, a crowd had gathered. Recognition came slowly. Piero di Cosimo stepped forward. “I know that face. The prisoner who attacked Melema.”

Bernardo Rucellai frowned. “I thought he died in prison.”

No one could say for certain.

Justice, like the kingdom of heaven, is not a thing we hold—only a hunger we carry.

Romola drifted from dreams into deep slumber, then back into restless visions, until at last she stirred, stretching her arms as if emerging from the shadows of the Bargello’s torchlit court. Morning light bathed her face. Her boat lay still in a sheltered creek, the Mediterranean shimmering sapphire to her right, while to her left stretched a crescent of green land, cradled by tree-lined slopes and crowned by a village clinging to the mountainside. The sun’s first rays brushed the hillside, leaving the rest in dewy silence. No sound disturbed the peace—even the water seemed hushed.

For a fleeting moment, Romola let the warmth and beauty wash over her, the horrors of Florence fading like the night’s darkness. Here, in the stillness, she could forget. But the past soon pressed in: the boat, the escape, the unanswered questions. She sat up, gazing at the valley, willing herself to rest a little longer before deciding anything.

Then a sound shattered the calm—a child’s desperate, unending cry. Romola turned toward the noise, spotting a crumbling hovel half-hidden by weeds. Climbing over a broken fence, she hurried across untended fields, the wails growing sharper. Pushing inside, she froze.

Three bodies lay on the straw-strewn floor: a man, a girl, and a woman whose dark hair was tangled in the grip of a sobbing toddler. The stench of death hung thick. Romola acted without hesitation, lifting the child and pressing it to her chest. The gaunt faces and foreign garb marked them as Jews, likely abandoned to die. The woman’s body still held traces of strength, her chest marred by the telltale purple of plague.

If the villagers knew of the pestilence, they’d recoil in fear. But the child needed milk, and someone had to help. Wrapping the little one in her cloak, Romola set off toward the cluster of houses, her steps quickening as she noticed the eerie silence. Fields lay fallow, fruit rotted untouched, and no livestock stirred. Between the dwellings, doors hung open, revealing only emptiness—until she spotted a man slumped over a spade, his cruse of water spilled beside him.

Then a moan. A young woman writhed against a wall, clutching a pitcher. “Water,” she begged. Romola took the vessel, following the woman’s weak gesture toward a distant well. As she hurried, the child quieted, sucking on scraps of fig. Near the well, a movement caught her eye—a boy vanishing into the trees, his black-and-white robes fluttering.

She filled the pitcher and returned, coaxing the sick woman to drink. “Who are you?” the woman rasped.

“A traveler,” Romola said. “Are there others alive?”

“Only the dying.” The woman’s head lolled. “The rest fled… or are dead upstairs.”

Romola’s resolve hardened. She’d find help. Leaving a promise to return, she carried the child toward the slope where she’d seen the boy flee. The path wound steeply, her arms aching, until she spotted a cow and goats grazing. Then—a face peering through the branches. A priest, wide-eyed, clutched a bucket like a shield.

“I mean no harm,” Romola called. “We need milk.”

The priest hesitated, torn between fear and duty. The boy had babbled of a vision—a golden-haired Madonna with a babe. Yet here stood a flesh-and-blood woman, her voice gentle but firm. Trembling, he milked the cow and offered a brass cup. Romola fed the child, then met his gaze.

“Tell me what happened here.”

The story spilled out: Jewish families, cast ashore by cruel hands, had brought the plague. The sea rejected their corpses, and terror drove the villagers away. Only the priest and the boy Jacopo remained, hiding on the hillside.

“No more hiding,” Romola said. “We will bury the dead and tend the living.”

And so they did. Days blurred into weeks as Romola moved among the sick, her presence a quiet command. The priest and Jacopo, once paralyzed by fear, followed her. The dead were laid to rest; the survivors, fed and cleansed, slowly revived. By the time the fields were sown again, the child—now baptized Benedetto—toddled after her, and the villagers brought offerings of honey and eggs to the cottage where she rested, weary but content.

They would speak of her for generations: the golden-haired woman who crossed the sea to save them, whose kindness lingered like a blessing in the valley long after she was gone.

The winter silence wrapped Romola in its hushed embrace as she lay resting, her thoughts drifting between the weight of the past and the uncertain shadows of the future. Everything she had known now appeared altered, as if the wax of her soul had been remolded by a fresh seal. Before, she had felt herself adrift—unmoored, purposeless—whispering to the darkness, *"I am tired of life. I want to die."* But from the moment she had answered that desperate cry in the night, something within her had shifted. She no longer reasoned, as she once had in Florence, that life held value because she could ease suffering. She simply *acted*, driven by an instinct so fierce that it left no room for hesitation.

Here, in this valley, she had rediscovered a truth stripped bare of complication. In Florence, her life had been knotted with the burdens of marriage, civic duty, and faith—each bond fraying until the shock of betrayal left her numb, severed from the world. But now she understood: *"It was cowardice to crave death. If all else is uncertain, suffering is not. If the glory of sacrifice is illusion, the pain is real. And while I have hands to lift the fallen, I will."*

Yet the past did not release her so easily. With her work in the valley complete, her thoughts surged back toward Florence—toward the life she had fled. In this rare stillness, severed from all familiar things, she saw herself with unflinching clarity. Regret, that quiet torment of the compassionate, stirred within her. Had she been too proud? Too quick to judge others while failing her own ideals? She questioned the righteousness of her flight. Had it been mere selfishness? Fra Girolamo had once drawn her back with words that cut deeper than her own justifications. *How could she claim to feel the pain of strangers yet turn from those closest to her?*

But memory answered sharply. The truth of her marriage to Tito rose like a specter—its hollow union, the lies she had been forced to cloak in duty. No, flight had been her only escape. And yet, even now, the ghost of what had been gnawed at her. *Could any bond truly break once it had woven itself into the blood?* Savonarola's words haunted her: *"You cannot cease to be a wife."* The thought of Tito—wretched, desperate—pricked at her conscience. What if he needed her?

Florence called to her, not as an idea, but as hunger calls the body. Her heart could not wander in abstractions; it clung to the known, the lived. The doubt returned: *Had she been wrong to leave?* If Florence was flawed, was she so pure that she could scorn it? If she had been stronger, would her faith have shattered so easily? Savonarola's voice echoed—his fire, his flawed conviction, the life he had rekindled in her. No failure could erase that debt.

Restlessness burned in her veins. Was it the returning strength of spring, or the need to stand once more within sight of Florence? She did not dare think of entering the city, but the longing to be near, to *know*, grew unbearable.

On a clear March morning, the valley gathered to bid her farewell. Jacopo brought a mule; the Padre would guide her to the nearest town. Her hands were clasped, kissed, her skirts clutched by those who wept. *"Why leave us now, when the earth grows generous? Stay!"*

*"My people may need me,"* she answered softly.

Benedetto wailed as she mounted, and so the villagers insisted he ride with her, if only to the slope's edge. Step by step, they followed, unwilling to let her go. Even as distance swallowed her, Romola turned, again and again, to see them still standing, arms raised, until the road bent and the valley vanished behind her.

On the fourteenth of April, Romola crossed the familiar walls of Florence once more. Restless and uneasy in Pistoja, where conflicting rumors about the Trial by Fire reached her, she had pressed onward to Prato, driven by a gnawing dread that she would be compelled to return to the city she had fled. It was there she encountered the monk of San Spirito, her godfather’s confessor—a man whose words struck her like a blow.

From him, she learned the full weight of Florence’s recent horrors: Savonarola’s arrest, his forced confession under torture, and the grim fate of her husband. The monk, having witnessed the procession of the condemned, recounted what all Florence knew—Tito had escaped the mob’s wrath by leaping into the Arno, only to meet his end on the riverbank at the hands of an old enemy. But Romola understood what others did not. The monk spoke of Savonarola with the disdain of the Frati Neri, declaring the friar had admitted to deceiving the people. Romola could bear no more. That very evening, she was in Florence.

Monna Brigida welcomed her with a storm of tears and exclamations, her joy as effusive as her earlier despair. She had even abandoned her false hair in her grief, though now, with Romola returned, she declared she cared not for gray locks if only her dear child would stay. But Romola was numb to the chatter of trivialities. The weight of Tito’s death, of Savonarola’s broken spirit under torture, left little room for lesser sorrows. Only two thoughts burned through the haze of her grief: she must see Savonarola, and she must find Tessa and the children.

Abruptly, she cut through Brigida’s rambling. *"Cousin, tell me—has there been any word of a young woman with two little children since Tito’s death?"*

Brigida’s hands flew up in shock. *"Cristo! No! Was he truly so wicked? Oh, my poor child, was that why you left?"* Her voice dropped to a scandalized whisper. *"If I’d known, I wouldn’t have thought you so strange. But to leave your husband just because of Bernardo’s execution—I said to myself, ‘Ah, she has her father’s temper.’ But if there was a *hussey* involved—"*

*"No,"* Romola interrupted sharply. *"She is innocent—ignorant of all wrongdoing. I mean to care for her and the children. They have no one else."*

Brigida huffed, crossing her arms. *"Well, if that’s piety, I’ve been praying to the wrong saints. Fra Girolamo said widows ought not to marry again, yet here you are, taking in another woman’s *two* children—Santiddio!"*

Romola silenced her with a look. *"Not now, cousin."*

---

At dawn, Romola sought the house beyond San Ambrogio where she had once found Tessa. But the dwelling was empty. Tessa was gone—likely sent away by Tito before his flight, a thought that twisted in Romola’s chest. If the girl had been taken beyond Florence, finding her would be near impossible. The neighbors knew nothing, only that old deaf Lisa had departed with her belongings.

Days passed in fruitless searching. Every glimpse of a contadina’s dress, every child’s laughter in the street, made Romola’s pulse quicken. She told herself this was no grand act of charity—she *needed* them. The children, at least, might offer something pure to love.

Then, walking the Via San Gallo, she spotted Bratti haggling with a customer—and her breath caught. Dangling from his basket was a familiar necklace.

*"Bratti,"* she demanded, *"where did you get that?"*

The peddler turned, unfazed. *"Ah, you know the little fool who wept when she pawned it? A florin, and it’s yours. My heart’s too soft for business."*

Romola thrust the coin at him. *"Where is she?"*

Moments later, Romola stood at the threshold of a dim room. Tessa sat on the bed, her eyes hollow, while Lillo and Ninna played nearby, Lillo roaring under his skirt to frighten his sister.

Romola stepped forward, the necklace in hand. *"Tessa."*

The girl whirled, screamed, and collapsed into sobs against Romola’s shoulder. The children stared, wide-eyed.

*"God has brought me back to you,"* Romola murmured.

By evening, they were all under Monna Brigida’s roof. Romola had gently told Tessa the truth—Naldo would never return, not from cruelty, but because he was dead.

*"But I will care for you always,"* Romola promised. *"You and the children."*

Brigida watched, torn between awe and the urge to put the girl in her place. *Let be,* she thought. *But one day, she’ll learn who Romola is.*

That night, as she tucked the children in, Brigida sighed. *"You must let me spoil them a little, Romola. You’ve never had a babe, and I—well, I had twins once."* Her voice faltered. *"Though they did not live."*

Romola said nothing. But for the first time in many days, the weight on her heart felt lighter.

When Romola returned to Florence with Tessa and the children, the last days of April were slipping away, and the city buzzed with unrest. The publication of Fra Girolamo’s so-called *confession*—extracted under the scrutiny of sixteen citizens, many of them his bitterest foes—had ignited outrage. The Signoria, eager to suppress dissent, hastily recalled the document, but not before copies spread like wildfire. Romola, desperate for truth, pored over the words again and again, searching for clarity amid the storm of accusations.

To his devoted followers, Savonarola’s retractions under torture were unthinkable—a betrayal of the prophet they revered. Surely, they whispered, the words had been twisted by Ser Ceccone, the disreputable notary known for his forgeries. How could a man who had defied tyrants and corruption crumble so easily? The trial was a farce, they insisted, rigged by men like Dolfo Spini, who had long hungered for his downfall. Florence, caught between the Pope’s demands and its own crumbling pride, seemed determined to destroy him.

Yet others, less fervent but no less pained, acknowledged the confession’s core truths. Savonarola had not been flawless. But did his lapses warrant death? No crime of state stained his record; no vice shadowed his private life. Even his fellow monks, some who had chafed under his rule, testified to his unwavering integrity. The Pope’s charges were flimsy—disobedience, defiance of excommunication—hardly grounds for execution. But politics cared little for justice. Rome needed him silenced, and Florence, eager for papal concessions, played the executioner.

Romola wrestled with doubt. The document bore marks of tampering—exaggerated self-condemnations, clumsy fabrications—yet much rang true. She could not ignore the dissonance between the man she had believed in and the broken figure who had recanted. Had his visions been mere ambition dressed in divine raiment? Or had the torture shattered his resolve, twisting faith into falsehood?

She lingered on one passage, raw in its honesty:
*"I sought glory—yes—but through the triumph of righteousness. I wished to be remembered, not as a pope or cardinal, but as the man who roused princes to purge corruption and reclaim Christendom. If I had succeeded, I would have been the first in influence, not by title, but by virtue."*

Here was the Savonarola she knew—the fire, the conviction, the unyielding will. But ambition had shadowed his purpose, and now, in his cell, he faced the wreckage of that grand dream. Alone, stripped of followers and divine certainty, he wrote not of vindication, but of repentance.

*"Pride blinded me,"* he confessed. *"I taught others but failed myself. God gave me much, and I squandered it. Now I am nothing."*

No defiance, no claims of martyrdom—only sorrow. And in that humility, Romola found a deeper truth. The world would call him a martyr, not because he died flawless, but because he had dared to strive for something greater than himself. His enemies had not broken him for his sins, but for his greatness.

And so, in the darkness of his cell, Savonarola embraced his failure, not as the end, but as the final, searing lesson. The light he had seen was real—even if he had not been pure enough to hold it.

The words had struck Romola like a blow—*"Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy."* They echoed in her mind, whispered by countless lips, each repetition deepening the gnawing doubt. Had Savonarola, in his clearest moments, truly renounced the divine calling he once claimed? Or was this another fracture in the resolve of a broken man?

The arrival of the Papal Commissaries on the nineteenth of May only stoked the flames of public fury. Florence, once alight with devotion, now roared for blood. *"Burn the Frate!"* they cried. *"His lies have cursed us—let the fire cleanse our misfortunes!"*

The next day brought fresh horrors. They dragged Savonarola back to the torture chamber, and before the iron and rope could bite into flesh again, he fell to his knees, his voice ragged with conviction. *"I lied before! The visions were true—God spoke through me! If I die, I die for the truth!"*

Yet pain, as ever, broke him. When the torment began anew, he gasped, *"I said it to seem righteous—no more, I beg you! I will confess!"*

Romola heard the tale with a sickened heart. Was this the final unraveling of a prophet, or the last defiance of a saint? One certainty remained: when he stood before the crowd, facing death, no one could silence him then. *"I will hear his last words,"* she vowed.

On the twenty-third of May, the Piazza della Signoria swarmed once more. A scaffold stretched like a skeletal finger toward the Tetta de’ Pisani, crowned not by a pyre, but by a triple gallows—its arms hacked short to strip it of any holy resemblance.

Romola watched from a window, far from the tribunal where Dolfo Spini and the Eight waited. Beside her stood Jacopo Nardi, his young face tight with unease.

*"Cover your eyes, Madonna,"* he murmured as the crowd’s jeers crested. *"Wait until he appears."*

She obeyed, but the noise itself was a lash—the howls of a city that had once knelt for blessings now baying for blood. Then Jacopo touched her arm.

Savonarola emerged, still clad in the Dominican habit. Before the Bishop, they stripped him of it—layer by layer, until only a plain woolen tunic remained. The crowd howled louder as the sacred garments fell. Degradation was a spectacle they understood.

He passed from tribunal to tribunal: degraded, condemned as a heretic, sentenced to die. Then, flanked by his brethren, he walked the scaffold. Romola strained to see his face, to catch some final sign—but the distance blurred all but his outline.

The moment came. He mounted the steps. For a heartbeat, he turned, scanning the sea of faces.

Then—nothing. No cry, no revelation. Only the torches, the grinning faces, the gibbet’s shadow.

Romola covered her eyes again. The last silence had fallen.

The loggia overlooking Borgo Pinti was drenched in golden light. Inside, flowers strewn across the floor perfumed the air as a girl with sunlit hair wove garlands beneath an unfinished altar.

Tessa, round-cheeked and content, fumbled with her own wreath, while Monna Brigida dozed in her chair. At the loggia’s edge, Lillo sprawled with a book, eyeing Romola—who sat motionless, her gaze lost in the distant hills.

*"Mamma Romola,"* he ventured at last, scooting closer. *"What should I be?"*

She smiled, brushing his hair back. *"What do you wish?"*

Her fingers stilled. *"True greatness rarely walks with ease, Lillo. To choose the right path often means bearing pain. I knew a man once who sought only pleasure—and it made him a coward, a traitor."* Her voice wavered. *"Another time, I’ll tell you."*

Outside, Piero di Cosimo and Nello approached, arms full of blooms. Lillo grinned. *"Piero scolds you for honoring Fra Girolamo—yet he brings flowers!"*

Romola’s eyes softened. *"Not all good men loved him. But without his light in my darkest hour, I might never have seen the truth at all."*

The past was ashes. But here, in the sunlit present, the quiet work of remembrance continued.
