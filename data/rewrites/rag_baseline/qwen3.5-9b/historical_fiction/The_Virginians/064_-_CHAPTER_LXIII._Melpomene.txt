The ink on the page was dry, but the silence in the room was heavy. George Warrington sat at his desk, the quill hovering over a sheet of paper that smelled of fresh linen and old ambition. Outside, London hummed with a noise that was mostly just people trying to be important. Inside, the only thing that mattered was the weight of the name Warrington, a stone dropped into a deep well, echoing with a history he was barely keeping up with.

He had been told, in letters that arrived with the smell of rain and parchment, that he must not forget himself. He was not a drudge in a gown; he was a gentleman with an ancient lineage. So he studied, but he studied like a man playing a game he intended to win, not a servant learning a trade. He treated the law with the polite distance one reserves for a strict aunt. He respected Themis, but he loved the ghosts of Chaucer far more. The black-letter script of the old poets felt warmer than the cold, jagged edges of Hale and Coke.

Those were strange times, when the dead spoke louder than the living. Men in courts and parliaments argued their points by citing Virgil or Lucan, asking what the ancients would have done. Now, the gods were silent. The worship of Jupiter was as outdated as the fashion of the previous century. Economists and calculators ruled the day, filling rooms with numbers that meant nothing to the soul. Tooke's Pantheon was empty, a monument to a faith that had evaporated. Occasionally, a politician like Stanley or Gladstone would burn a small amount of incense to the old gods, a gesture of nostalgia, but no one cared anymore. Who needed the Muses? Who asked about the length of Thalia's 'i'? Or whether Melpomene's name stretched across three or four syllables?

George knew the answer because he had stolen the figure of Tragedy to adorn the initial 'G' of this very chapter. He remembered the long afternoons in his youth, the idleness of his imprisonment on the banks of the Monongahela, where he had paid court to Melpomene. The result was a tragedy, a piece so ambitious it had been omitted from Bell's Theatre, though it held no greater sins than the plays that filled the stages. Young men always fell in love with the Tragic Muse first, just as they fell for women older and wiser than themselves. Ambition was a fever in the green years; later, one settled for translating an ode of Horace or turning a song of Waller into neat, manageable verses. But then, there was only the highest prize, the epic, the tragedy.

So George went to London. He walked through the theaters, listening to the critics in the coffee-houses, sitting between the acts at the Bedford, supping with the wits at the Cecil. He became known, not for his wealth—which was real, but secondary—but for his wit and his modesty. The actors drank punch with him; the critics ate supper at his table. To hob-nob with Bobadil or Henry the Fifth, to share a pinch of snuff with Aristarchus, to hand Juliet into her coach—it was the dream of every poet. George lived in that dream. He gave entertainments to the players, and rumors spread that distinguished geniuses had borrowed money from him, a sign of a patronage that flowed both ways.

As he polished his masterpiece, adding new beauties to the skeleton of his plot, he sought the approval of friends. Mr. Spencer, a new acquaintance from the Temple, hosted a breakfast in Fig Tree Court. George read aloud. The gentlemen listened, nodding. "Uncommon merit," they said. Even Mr. Johnson, the learned critic, offered praise. "It shows talent," he admitted. "Though it wars against the unities." George smiled. Other authors had broken the rules; he could too.

His tragedy was full of blood and iron. The hero was Carpzow, a companion of Frundsberg, a man carved from the wars of Pavia and the campaigns of the Constable Bourbon. The first act opened before a convent on the Rhine, besieged by Lutherans. These were godless men, pulling beards and tearing veils. Inside the walls, a dozen nuns trembled. Outside, soldiers gambled on the grass. Then the bell rang. Noon. No succor had come. The gates swung open.

Enter Colonel Carpezan. Clad in complete steel, hammer over shoulder, flags unfurled, trumpets blaring. He was the terror of the Danube, the scourge of the Papists. "No rudeness," he commanded his men. "The wine is yours. The cellar is good. The church plate shall be melted. Any of the garrison wishing to serve under Gaspar Carpezan are welcome. But no insult to the religious ladies! I have promised them safe conduct. He who touches a finger on them hangs!"

Mr. Spencer explained the scene to his guests. "An agreeable acquaintance," Mr. Johnson remarked, wagging his head over his tea. "Novel and stirring."

Inside the convent, the Abbess—a lady of violent prejudices and noble birth—faced the conqueror. She knew his reputation. His hammer had shattered sacred images; his greed had melted pounds of silver. She told him her mind, sharp and clear. Carpezan, though a conqueror, was momentarily shaken by the force of her words. But an old soldier is not easily beaten by a woman.

"How many ladies are there?" he asked.

"Twenty-three," the Abbess replied, correcting herself from twenty-four.

"Why this hesitation?" asked Captain Ulric, one of Carpezan's gayest officers.

Carpezan pulled a letter from his pocket. "I require the body of the noble lady Sybilla of Hoya. Her brother was my favorite captain, slain by my side in the Milanese. By his death, she inherits his lands. It is said a greedy uncle locked her here. She shall decide her fate: remain a sister of Saint Mary's, or return to home and liberty."

The Abbess bristled. "There is no Lady Sybil here. Every inmate is under your protection, sworn to freedom. Sister Agnes was a professed nun; her wealth reverts to the Order."

"Give me the body of Lady Sybil of Hoya!" Carpezan roared, his face twisting in wrath. "Or I signal my Reiters, and I burn this convent to the ground."

Captain Ulric grinned. "If I lead the storm, I won't choose your Lady Abbess. I'd pick a plump, smiling, red-lipped maid." As he looked under the veils of the attendant nuns, the Abbess cried out, "Silence, fellow! The lady you seek is dead. Three days ago, Sister Agnes died."

The Chaplain confirmed it, ghastly and pale.

Too much. In the pocket of his coat of mail, Carpezan found a letter from Sister Agnes herself. She wrote that she was going to be buried, but in an oubliette, where she would either starve or live on water and bread. Seizing the Abbess by the arm, Carpezan grabbed the Chaplain by the throat. He blew a blast on his horn. The Lanzknechts rushed in. Crash, bang! The walls shook. Flames licked the rafters. Screams rose into the smoke.

Bringing Sybilla out, fainting on his shoulder, Carpezan revealed the truth. A little sister nun—the one with the red lips—had shown them the way to the dungeon.

"We end the first act with the burning," Mr. Warrington declared to his circle. "Roars of triumph, outcries of nuns. They will need to change their costumes immediately; they will be court ladies in the next act."

They cast the roles. Mrs. Pritchard as the Abbess, Miss Pritchard as Sybilla, Miss Gates as the nun. Mr. Garrick, though scarce tall enough, would be Carpezan when excited. Mr. Johnson agreed that Woodward would make a fine Ulric. Already, in their minds, the play was on the boards.

In Act II, Carpezan married Sybilla. Enriched by war, ennobled by the Emperor, he lived in splendor on the Danube. But happiness was elusive. Perhaps the crimes of his wild life gnawed at him. Perhaps his rough manners clashed with his imperious, high-born bride. She taunted him with his low birth, his vulgar companions. There were moments when he wished he had never rescued the lovely, quarrelsome vixen from the oubliette. The act was quiet, passing in quarrels until horns blew, announcing the arrival of the young King of Bohemia and Hungary.

Act III took place in Prague. The King offered preferment: Baron to Count, Colonel to General-in-Cheif. His wife was the brightest jewel of the court. And as for Carpzoff...

"Oh, stay—I have it," Mr. Johnson interrupted, his eyes lighting up. "'Tis in 'Meteranus,' in the Theatrum Universum. I read it in Oxford as a boy. Carpezanus or Carpzoff..."
