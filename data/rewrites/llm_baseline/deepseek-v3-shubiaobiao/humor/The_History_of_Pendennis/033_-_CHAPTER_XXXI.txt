Arthur Pendennis, now a self-proclaimed connoisseur of London’s underbelly, flung himself into the city’s murkier corners with the enthusiasm of a man who’d just discovered that gin could, in fact, be consumed before noon. He fancied himself a scholar of humanity—studying coalheavers like rare beetles, observing boxers as if they were exotic fauna, and generally behaving like a prince slumming it among his future subjects.

Warrington, his guide in these escapades, was a man of simpler tastes. He preferred the company of men who could lift barrels or swallow swords over the dandies of Mayfair. “Society,” he’d declare between sips of gin, “is just the same fellow in different waistcoats. But here—*here* you find *character*.” Pen, meanwhile, strutted through taprooms with the air of a young duke inspecting a peasant revolt, nodding graciously at the rabble as if they ought to be flattered by his presence.

Their evenings followed a noble routine: mornings spent pretending to study law (or, more accurately, skimming newspapers for scandal), afternoons nursing headaches, and nights gallivanting through London’s less reputable establishments. Youth, after all, is the only time a man can mistake exhaustion for vitality.

One such haunt was the **Fielding’s Head**, a pub so ancient it might’ve served drinks to Henry Fielding himself—assuming he’d been the sort to enjoy a ballad between verdicts. The place was run by **Cutts**, a landlord whose voice could rouse a dead man—or at least make him request a last pint. His specialty was patriotic songs of the *“God Save the Queen and Pass the Whisky”* variety, delivered with enough fervor to make grown men weep into their ale.

It was here that Pen stumbled upon an old acquaintance—**Captain Costigan**, now a permanent fixture of the Back Kitchen, holding court like a deposed monarch. The Captain, perpetually halfway to oblivion, greeted Pen with the warmth of a man who’d just found a sovereign in his pocket—only to promptly forget his name.

“Me dear boy!” he bellowed, sloshing his drink. “Me dear… er… lad! Of course I remember ye! Pendennis! No—wait—*Pendragon*? No matter! Have a drink!”

Pen, torn between amusement and pity, endured the Captain’s ramblings—mostly about his daughter’s marriage to **Sir Charles Mirabel**, a tale that grew grander with each retelling (and each pint). The Captain’s finances, much like his memory, were fluid. One moment he was flush with phantom wealth, the next begging half-crowns with the dignity of a bankrupt king.

Nearby, hunched over a battered piano, was **Bows**, the theater accompanist from Pen’s youth. The old musician played on, ignored by the crowd, until Pen recognized a tune from *The Stranger*—a melody that once made his heart race for **Emily**, the actress who’d long since vanished into respectability.

“Still remember the old songs, eh?” Bows chuckled. “Shame the Captain doesn’t. Then again, he doesn’t remember his own name most nights.”

The Back Kitchen was a carnival of London life: medical students arguing over cadavers, clerks sneaking illicit pints, even the odd politician slumming it between votes. The night’s entertainment peaked with **Hodgen**, the bass singer, delivering *“The Body Snatcher”*—a grisly ballad so chilling it emptied the room faster than a shout of *“Round’s on the house!”* (which, incidentally, never happened).

As the crowd thinned, two newspapermen—**Hoolan** of the *Dawn* (Liberal) and **Doolan** of the *Day* (Tory)—held court, swapping insults about rival editors between bites of kidney pie.

“Our governor says the public don’t care for these press feuds,” sighed Doolan.

“Aye, and that’s why your rag’s dull as ditchwater,” Hoolan shot back.

Warrington leaned in. “Watch this—they’ll be sharing a cab home by dawn.”

Pen, now thoroughly schooled in London’s contradictions, sipped his drink. The city was a stage, and everyone—drunken captains, hack writers, even himself—was merely playing a part.

And oh, what a farce it was.
