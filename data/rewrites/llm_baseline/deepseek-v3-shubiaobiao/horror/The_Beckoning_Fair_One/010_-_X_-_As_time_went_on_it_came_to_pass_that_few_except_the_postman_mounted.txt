Time folded in upon itself in Oleron’s dim sanctuary. The postman’s footsteps, once a weekly intrusion, faded into oblivion. Letters from his publishers—unanswered, then forgotten—ceased altogether. The world beyond his door might as well have crumbled to dust.

Spring clawed its way into the city, painting the streets with blossoms. Oleron bought them by the armful, compelled by some unspoken demand from the very walls of his rooms. Yet each excursion into the open air left him trembling, his skin prickling with the dread of vast, unguarded spaces. *Agoraphobia*, the physicians would have called it. Oleron knew it by a different name: *her* will.

Soon, he arranged for deliveries—food, flowers—all brought to his threshold. The relief was exquisite. Now, he need never leave.

But solitude was not peace. A restless melancholy gnawed at him, a weeping weariness that he would not have traded for all the world’s joy. Noise became an abomination. The clatter of Barrett’s Sunday sermons in the square below sent him scrambling to slam the blinds, his name twisting like a knife in the preacher’s fervent curses.

And then there were the silences—precious, sacred—where he would stand breathless in the wedge of his bedroom doorway, surveying his domain. Five rooms, all visible from this one vantage, all his. Had Madley, the previous tenant, known this secret? Oleron smirked at the thought.

Light, too, became an enemy. The sun’s glare was a brand against his eyes. One by one, he drew the crimson blinds, plunging his rooms into a bloody twilight. The darkness soothed him, a womb where his great work could gestate. Oh, they thought he had ceased to write? Fools. He was crafting a masterpiece, a *Mistress*, more radiant and terrible than any mortal creation.

Yet she was coy. She demanded proof of his devotion.

The realization struck him like a blow: the manuscript of *Romilly*, abandoned in the window seat. How could she come to him while that rival still breathed? He snatched the pages, heart pounding. The fire, always burning now, awaited its offering.

As the first sheet blackened and curled, a sound pierced the stillness—a knock, then Elsie’s voice, trembling through the door.

He clenched his teeth, refusing to answer. Let her sob. Let her beg. She knew nothing of true danger. The flames consumed *Romilly* page by page, her pleas fading with the crackling paper. At last, her footsteps retreated.

Alone again, Oleron exhaled. The air thickened. A presence stirred—not in form, but in the sigh of the house itself. A draft nudged the bedroom door, setting it adrift on its hinges. His breath hitched.

He sank into a chair, pulse hammering, awaiting the summons only he would recognize. The Audience was at hand.
