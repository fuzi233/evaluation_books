"If this fine weather continues," remarked Mrs. Jennings as they gathered at breakfast the following morning, "Sir John will simply refuse to depart from Barton next week. It is terribly unkind to deprive men of sport of a single day's pleasure. Poor fellows! I always feel a deep compassion for them when they do; they take it so profoundly to heart."

"That is quite true," exclaimed Marianne, her voice brightening as she moved to the window to inspect the day. "I had not considered *that*. This weather will keep countless sportsmen within the countryside."

It proved a fortunate thought, instantly restoring all her buoyant spirits. "It is truly delightful for *them*," she continued, sitting down to the meal with a radiant countenance. "They must revel in it! However" (a flicker of anxiety returning), "it cannot be expected to endure indefinitely. Given the season and the prolonged rains, we shall undoubtedly see little more of it. Frost will surely arrive soon, likely with severity. Perhaps within a day or two; this unnatural mildness can hardly persist—nay, perhaps it will freeze tonight!"

"At any rate," interjected Elinor, hoping to veil her sister's apprehensions from Mrs. Jennings, "I daresay Sir John and Lady Middleton will be back in town by the end of next week."

"Ay, my dear, I'll guarantee it. Mary always gets her own way."

"And now," Elinor thought silently, "she will dispatch a letter to Combe via this afternoon's post."

If she did, however, the correspondence was penned and dispatched with a discretion that evaded Elinor's keenest vigilance. Regardless of the truth, and far from feeling entirely content regarding the matter, Elinor could not be altogether unhappy so long as Marianne remained in high spirits. Marianne was indeed spirited; elated by the gentle warmth of the air and even more so by her anticipation of an impending frost.

The morning was largely devoted to visiting acquaintances of Mrs. Jennings to announce their presence in town. Throughout these calls, Marianne was perpetually engaged in tracking the wind's direction, scrutinizing the shifting hues of the sky, and envisioning a dramatic transformation in the atmosphere.

"Do you not find it colder than it was this morning, Elinor?" she asked later, turning to her cousin. "There appears to me a distinct difference. I struggle to keep my hands warm even within my muff. It was not so yesterday, I am sure. The clouds seem to be dispersing; the sun will burst forth momentarily, and we shall have a crystal-clear afternoon."

Elinor felt a mixture of amusement and concern; yet Marianne persisted, interpreting every gleam in the firelight and every hint in the morning air as a definitive sign of approaching frost.

The Miss Dashwoods possessed no greater cause for dissatisfaction with Mrs. Jennings' domestic habits or circle of friends than with her treatment of them, which was invariably kind. Everything concerning household arrangements was conducted on the most generous scale. Except for a handful of old city friends, whom, to Lady Middleton's chagrin, Mrs. Jennings had never shed, she visited no one whose acquaintance might disturb the sensibilities of her younger companions. Delighted to find herself more comfortably situated in this regard than anticipated, Elinor was willing to trade off the lack of genuine enjoyment in their evening gatherings—which, whether at home or elsewhere, devolved primarily into card-playing—as insufficient to amuse her.

Colonel Brandon, holding a general invitation to the house, was present almost daily. He came ostensibly to admire Marianne and converse with Elinor, though the latter often derived more solace from his company than from any other daily occurrence. Simultaneously, she observed with profound distress his unwavering devotion to her sister. She feared it was a deepening sentiment. It grieved her to witness the earnestness with which he watched Marianne, and his spirits were undeniably lower than they had been at Barton.

Approximately a week after their arrival, it became evident that Willoughby had also reached town. His calling card lay upon the table when they returned from a morning drive.

"Good heavens!" cried Marianne. "He has been here while we were out."

Elinor, relieved to confirm his presence in London, ventured to suggest, "Be assured, he will call again tomorrow." Yet Marianne seemed scarcely to register her words; upon Mrs. Jennings' entry, she snatched the precious card and fled with it.

This incident, while lifting Elinor's spirits, resurrected in her sister all, and far more than all, their former agitations. From this moment, Marianne's mind knew no peace; the hourly expectation of encountering him rendered her unfit for anything else. She insisted on remaining behind the next morning when the others departed.

Elinor's thoughts were consumed with speculations regarding what might transpire in Berkeley Street during their absence. However, a fleeting glance at her sister upon their return sufficed to inform her that Willoughby had paid no second visit. Just then, a note was delivered and placed upon the table.

"For me!" cried Marianne, hastening forward.

"No, ma'am, for my mistress."

Unconvinced, Marianne seized it instantly. "It is indeed for Mrs. Jennings; how provoking!"

"You are expecting a letter, then?" said Elinor, unable to remain silent longer.

"Yes, a little—not much."

Following a brief pause, "You hold no confidence in me, Marianne."

"Nay, Elinor, this reproach from *you*—you who trust in no one!"

"Me!" returned Elinor, flustered; "indeed, Marianne, I have nothing to reveal."

"Nor I," answered Marianne with vigor, "then our situations are alike. We have neither of us anything to disclose; you, because you do not communicate, and I, because I conceal nothing."

Distressed by this accusation of reserve against herself, which she could not easily refute, Elinor did not know how to press further for openness in Marianne under such circumstances.

Soon Mrs. Jennings appeared, and the note being handed to her, she read it aloud. It was from Lady Middleton, announcing their arrival in Conduit Street the previous night and inviting her mother and cousins for the following evening. Business matters for Sir John and a severe cold afflicting Lady Middleton had prevented them from calling at Berkeley Street. The invitation was accepted; yet, as the appointed hour approached, and considering it a civic necessity for Mrs. Jennings that they both accompany her on such a visit, Elinor found it difficult to persuade her sister to go. Still, having seen nothing of Willoughby, Marianne was not merely disinclined toward amusement abroad but unwilling to risk his appearing again in her absence.

Upon the conclusion of the evening, Elinor discerned that disposition is not materially altered by a change of residence. Although scarcely settled in town, Sir John had contrived to gather nearly twenty young people around him and amuse them with a ball. This was an affair, however, for which Lady Middleton held little approval. In the country, an unpremeditated dance was permissible; but in London, where reputation for elegance was paramount and fragile, it was risking too much for the gratification of a few girls to allow it to be known that Lady Middleton had hosted a small dance of eight or nine couples, accompanied by two violins and a mere sideboard collation.

Mr. and Mrs. Palmer attended the party. From the former, whom they had not seen since their arrival in town, and who carefully avoided any appearance of attention to his mother-in-law (thus never approaching her), they received no mark of recognition upon entering. He glanced at them slightly, seemingly unaware of their identity, and merely nodded to Mrs. Jennings from across the room. Marianne cast one glance around the apartment as she entered; it was sufficient—he was not there—and she sat down, equally ill-disposed to receive or impart pleasure.

After an hour, Mr. Palmer sauntered toward the Miss Dashwoods to express his surprise at seeing them in town, noting that Colonel Brandon had been informed of their arrival first and had himself made a rather droll remark upon hearing the news.

"I thought you were both in Devonshire," he said.

"Did you?" replied Elinor.

"When do you go back again?"

"I do not know." Thus ended their discourse.

Never had Marianne been so unwilling to dance in her life, nor so exhausted by the exertion. She complained bitterly as they returned to Berkeley Street.

"Aye, aye," said Mrs. Jennings, "we know the reason of all that very well; if a certain person who shall remain nameless had been present, you would not have been a bit tired: and to say the truth, it was not very becoming of him not to meet you when he was invited."

"Invited!" cried Marianne.

"So my daughter Middleton told me, for it seems Sir John encountered him somewhere in the street this morning." Marianne said no more, but looked exceedingly hurt. Impatient in this state to do something that might alleviate her sister's distress, Elinor resolved to write the following morning to her mother, hoping to awaken fears for Marianne's health thereby procuring the inquiries that had been so long delayed. Her resolve was strengthened further when, after breakfast on the morrow, she perceived that Marianne was once again writing to Willoughby—for she could not suppose it to be addressed to anyone else.
