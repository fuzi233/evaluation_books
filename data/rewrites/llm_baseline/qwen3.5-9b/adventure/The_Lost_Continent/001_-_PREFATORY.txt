The stiffness in our joints was a testament to a night spent under the open sky. Even in the mild climate of Gran Canaria, the sudden drop in temperature brought by the evening dew and the encroaching darkness is no joke to endure without preparation. On such expeditions, I invariably prefer a brisk run to shake off the chill, but the terrain here offered no relief. This was rough, uneven ground in the heart of the island, devoid of even three yards of level path. Consequently, as Coppinger commenced a series of makeshift dumbbell exercises using jagged chunks of bristly lava, I mimicked his movements.

Coppinger is a man accustomed to hardship, yet his profession as a physician lends a peculiar rigidity to his approach. He treats health theories with the fervor of a religious doctrine, renewing his academic credentials roughly every eighteen months. Two days prior, a storm had passed, leaving a trickle of water flowing along the base of the ravine (*barranca*). Seizing this opportunity, we descended to wash our faces and brush our teeth—a luxury of the highest order on an expedition of this nature.

"Now," Coppinger declared once our pockets were emptied, "the provisions are dwindling to a critical mass, and what remains is largely degraded by exposure to the humid air of a local Spanish newspaper."

"Your share consists primarily of tobacco ash," I replied dryly.

"It will deteriorate further if we delay. We face significant scrambling ahead."

The situation was undeniable. We settled beside the stream at the bottom of the ravine and consumed the remnants of our supply. Our destination was the *fonda* at Santa Brigida, a ten-mile trek where we had previously cached our equipment. Given Coppinger's desire to conduct extensive photography and measurements before departing this cluster of caves, we anticipated arriving at our next meal—and hopefully a glass of the Patron's renowned vintage—exhausted.

If only the patrons of the English hotels in Las Palmas knew the quality of fine wines available in the mountain villages, secured through diplomatic negotiation, the reputation of the old vintages would vanish within a week. To be honest, the two mummies Coppinger had already recovered satisfied my modest ambitions. Wrapped in brittle goatskins that crumbled like parchment, the ancient figures disintegrated into dust clouds at the slightest touch. Yet, Coppinger was relentless. He suspected the existence of an ancient Guanche university or sacred college, similar to one rumored on the opposite coast, and refused to rest until every cave on the cliff face had been ransacked.

He possessed ample film stock and flashlights, insisting we complete the survey immediately rather than undertaking a separate return trip. Armed with a crowbar and a coiled rope, we ascended toward the ridge, which bore the searing marks of yesterday's sun. Accessing these caves required ingenuity; they were not easily reached, suggesting they had remained untouched for centuries. Coppinger theorized that the ancients utilized retractable ropes made of goat skin to secure their entrances against intruders—a hypothesis plausible enough in the absence of better explanations.

The cave mouths formed a horizontal row approximately thirty feet below the ridge and fifty feet above the riverbed. While descending from below would have been cumbersome, our strategy involved rappelling from the ridge onto each opening, sliding to the base, and repeating the process. Although Coppinger possessed courage and a steady hand, his portliness and age—closer to fifty than forty-five—made the task perilous.

I volunteered to lead the descent for each chamber, attempting to assist him from below. However, navigating a vertical cliff face while suspended by a slender rope offers limited assistance to a companion dangling beneath you, save for verbal encouragement. After the initial three caves proved to be empty storage dens, I urged Coppinger to accept my assessment and conserve his energy. His archaeological zeal, however, outweighed caution. He dismissed my interpretations of specific chambers—as a prison, a pottery kiln, or a seminary—with skepticism, insisting on personal inspection of every site. As the sun climbed higher, his endurance waned, yet he pressed on despite the danger.

"I suggest we retreat to Santa Brigida immediately," I proposed.

"No," he refused stubbornly. "Three more openings remain. If you refuse to investigate them, I shall attempt it alone."

He attempted to frame his request as a favor, offering to rely solely on my untrained observations, but I declined categorically. Exhaustion weighed heavily upon me; sweat soaked my clothes, the sun pounded my temples, and my hands were lacerated from the rope burns. Coppinger, conversely, burned with renewed enthusiasm, urging me to match his spirit.

"Consider the possibilities," he argued. "Whatever you discover belongs to you exclusively. I claim no rights whatsoever."

"Generous of you, but I have no desire for additional mummified corpses in goatskin wrappings."

"Bah! These are not burial sites. Do you lack the discernment to distinguish between the openings?" He pressed harder. "Do not assume that because previous searches yielded nothing, you will not strike gold in the final locations."

Reluctantly, I agreed. I navigated the treacherous ledges and fissures, avoiding the arduous two-mile detour we had initially taken. Jamming the crowbar into a crevice for purchase, I swung over the edge, my hands blistering anew with every grip. Entering the cave mouth proved difficult due to the overhanging rock, resulting in a clumsy landing on my back, though I maintained my hold on the rope—a vital precaution, as Coppinger could not retrieve it from below.

Upon entering, I noted a distinct architectural divergence. Unlike the irregular dens preceding it, this cavern featured walls smoothed by cutting tools, with clean angles and flat surfaces. The interior sloped inward toward the ceiling, reminiscent of a style I recognized vaguely, and revealed interconnected rooms and passages. The other openings Coppinger desired to inspect appeared merely as doorways or windows into these structures. No inscriptions marked the walls, save for the occasional bat roosting in the shadows.

Lighting a cigarette to pass the time—Coppinger often interprets haste as negligence—I signaled my findings downward. His voice returned, laced with anxiety.

"Have you examined it thoroughly?"

"Naturally. What else could I be doing?"

"Do not descend yet. I am securing the camera and lighting equipment to the rope. Retrieve them and provide six exposures."

Agreeing, I hauled the gear inside. While the resulting photographs would likely be mundane, Coppinger valued them for their documentation. Careful to avoid lens flare in the dim interior, I positioned the camera on a stone ledge and used the flashlight mounted overhead to capture images of four chambers. Reaching a fifth room with a broader ledge, I placed the camera securely and sat to recharge the battery.

The moment my weight shifted onto the ledge, a sharp crack echoed through the cave. I dropped several inches but recovered instantly, snatching the camera just before it slipped. A surge of satisfaction washed over me; this was no ordinary storage space. The entrance had been hermetically sealed with cement, implying the concealment of something of value.

Clearing away dust and rubble with a candle, I soon realized the substance beneath was not cement. It flaked away in uniform layers, revealing a composite material: a shiny, talc-like substrate coated with a dark, toffee-colored layer resembling wax. The surface bore intricate patterns. Lacking expertise in paleography, I accepted Coppinger's assertion that the Guanches could not write, interpreting the markings merely as decorative designs. Nevertheless, convinced of the material's worth, I began excavating it with my boot heel and pocket knife.

The sheets adhered tightly to the cavity wall. Smashing the front panel allowed access to the lower layers, which I hacked out as a single solid block. Measuring roughly twenty by fifteen inches, the slab was surprisingly lightweight. After documenting it with photographs, I lowered it to Coppinger via the rope.

Descending to join him, I found the artifact resting on the floor beside the frantic archaeologist.

"What is it?" I asked.

"I do not know yet," he breathed, trembling with excitement. "But it is the most significant discovery in the history of the Canary Islands, and it belongs to you, you unappreciative fool. Alas, you have damaged the beginning and the end of a potentially priceless chronicle. It is my fault for assigning exploration duties to an untrained amateur."

"If anything is amiss, it is your fault," I countered. "You claimed these ancient Canarians possessed no writing system. I trusted your word; otherwise, I might have assumed it was food."

"This is not Guanche craftsmanship," he snapped, his composure fracturing. "Observe the talc. Have you no eyes? Have you not noticed the geological composition of the island? Talc does not exist here!"

"Then it is imported literature?"

"Certainly. It is Egyptian. Obvious at a glance. Though its presence here defies explanation. The script is a variant of known characters, and the waxy medium is unique—likely a mineral akin to asphalt, not animal fat. I shall analyze it later. The mystery of why such a brilliant invention fell into disuse is staggering. I could study this forever."

"Very well," I interjected, weary. "If it suits you, I prefer studying a meal. Ten miles of hard walking lie ahead, and I am famished. Note that it is already four o'clock; the ascent and descent consume more time than anticipated."
