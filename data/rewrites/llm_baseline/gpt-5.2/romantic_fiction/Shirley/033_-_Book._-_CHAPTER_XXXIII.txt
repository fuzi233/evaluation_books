For Martin Yorke’s scheme to run smoothly, it was indispensable that he remain at home that day—present in body, absent in duty. Therefore, at breakfast he discovered, with the quick ingenuity of fifteen, that his appetite had vanished, and, just as the hour for school drew near, a pain, sharp and solemn, fixed itself upon his heart. It was a pain that made a journey to Mr. Summer’s grammar class not only imprudent but nearly impossible; a pain that required—so Martin alleged—the soft authority of his father’s arm-chair, the fire’s respectful warmth, and the morning paper spread broad as a shield across his knees.

Mark departed for school, unsuspecting. Mr. Yorke and Matthew withdrew to the counting-house. The house, thus cleared of its more formidable masculine obstacles, still left Martin with several exploits to achieve before four o’clock: first, to obtain the breakfast he had theatrically refused; and then, in good order, to remove his mother, Miss Moore, and Mrs. Horsfall from the field, so that one private hour might be won without interruption.

The first of these tasks pressed most urgently upon his empty stomach. A young plotter may plan on an empty head, but not on an empty belly; and Martin, who could be fastidious in his pleasures, was resolved that his delayed meal should be no pale penitence of bread-and-milk. He knew the way to the larder. He took it.

The kitchen doors were shut; within, the servants were breakfasting with the heavy, closed-mouthed solemnity of people who have work ahead and no time for elegance. Outside, upon the lawn, Mrs. Yorke and Miss Moore paced and aired themselves, and—being women and therefore, in their own view, keepers of domestic order—commented upon those same shut doors with dissatisfaction.

Safe in the larder’s cool hush, where the air smelled of stored fruit, sugar, and yesterday’s roasted meat, Martin made a connoisseur’s survey. On a shelf lay rosy apples nested in straw; he chose three—firm, with that faint cider-scent that promises crispness. Pastry sat under a cover: he lifted it and selected an apricot puff, its glaze catching what little light there was, and a damson tart, dark as wine beneath its lattice. He passed the plain loaf without a glance, but paused at currant tea-cakes—plump, speckled, and faintly sticky with butter—and condescended to one.

Then came the savoury. With the help of his clasp-knife—an instrument as useful to boyhood as to brigandage—he carved himself a wing of fowl and a slice of ham, the latter edged with fat that had set into a pearly rim. A cantlet of cold custard-pudding, yellow and trembling even in its chill, seemed to him an excellent finish: sweet after salt, softness after chew. Armed with this assorted treasure, he sallied into the hall.

He was already almost across it—three steps more and he would have anchored in the back parlour’s safe harbour—when the front door opened and Matthew stood there.

It would have been easier to confront the Old Gentleman, horned and hoofed, than Matthew Yorke with his dry eye and quicker sarcasm. Matthew had not subscribed, even for a moment, to that sudden “pain about the heart.” Earlier he had muttered words in which “shamming Abraham” had rung distinctly, and Martin’s succession to the arm-chair and paper had given him something like a mental cramp.

Now he saw the evidence: apples, tarts, tea-cake, fowl, ham, custard-pudding—an invalid’s provisions, indeed.

Martin paused one instant, trapped; the next, he recovered. He had the instinct—half wicked, half brilliant—of turning any mishap into leverage. A collision with Matthew, he knew, was a spark that never failed to set his mother’s nerves alight. Mrs. Yorke, roused to tears and tremors in the morning, would seek repose in bed by afternoon; and that bed, in Martin’s economy, was as good as a locked door. He could, with a quarrel, remove her from the day.

So the collision took place there in the hall, as if summoned. Matthew’s sneer came like a slap; Martin met it with a languid, cutting reply. They rushed to battle. Usually Martin made little noise in such engagements, but now he clattered like a pot-lid orchestra—raising the kind of domestic alarm that brings skirts and aprons running.

The servants flew in. Mrs. Yorke appeared. Miss Moore followed. No female hand could separate them. Mr. Yorke was summoned from his work, irritated, authoritative, and weary of boyish drama.

“Sons,” he said, standing between them, “one of you must leave my roof if this occurs again. I will have no Cain and Abel strife here.”

Martin allowed himself to be taken off as the injured party—youngest, slightest, cool as a small knife. He had no passion left; he even smiled. In his mind the hardest portion of his labour was done.

Once, later in the morning, he seemed to flag. “It is not worth while to bother myself for that Caroline,” he remarked, as if the name were a penny he could choose to spend or keep.

Yet a quarter of an hour afterwards he was again in the dining-room, watching his mother’s dishevelled head and eyes turbid with despair, and the sight restored his resolution.

“Yes,” he told himself, “I made her sob, shudder, almost faint. I’ll see her smile before I’ve done with her; besides, I want to outwit all these womenites.”

After dinner, Mrs. Yorke fulfilled his calculation. She withdrew to her chamber, to recover with pillows and solitude from the morning’s storm.

Now for Hortense.

Miss Moore—Hortense—was comfortably settled in the back parlour with her stocking-mending, the steady snick of needles and the low domestic quiet around her. Martin lay stretched upon the sofa, a book in his hand, affecting the voluptuous ease of a young pacha in exile, too delicate to sit upright.

Lazily, in the course of talk that seemed to wander without intention, he introduced Sarah, the maid at the Hollow. He insinuated—always by degrees, always with that air of letting fall mere trifles—that Sarah was said to have three suitors: Frederic Murgatroyd, Jeremiah Pighills, and John-of-Mally’s-of-Hannah’s-of-Deb’s; and that Miss Mann had affirmed she knew for a fact that, now the girl was left in sole charge of the cottage, she often had her swains to meals and entertained them with the best the house afforded.

It needed no more. Miss Moore’s sense of propriety, sharpened by curiosity and a sort of moral appetite, could not endure an hour longer without inspection. The scandal was like an odour carried on a draught—she must follow it to the source. Up she rose, bonneted and indignant, and set off for the Hollow with the speed of a woman convinced she goes on righteous business.

One remained: Mrs. Horsfall, Mr. Moore’s nurse—set and stubborn in her duties, and not easily coaxed away by gossip.

Martin, now master of the field, obtained from his mother’s work-basket a bunch of keys. With them he opened the sideboard cupboard and brought out a black bottle and a small glass. He arranged them on the table with brisk precision—bait laid neatly. Then he ran upstairs to Mr. Moore’s door and tapped. The nurse opened.

“If you please, ma’am,” said Martin, with an excellent imitation of respectful seriousness, “you are invited to step into the back parlour and take some refreshment. You will not be disturbed; the family are out.”

He watched her down. He watched her in. Then he shut the door upon her as calmly as if he had locked away a threat.

The hard work was done. Now for the pleasure.

He snatched his cap and went out—away, away—to the wood. It was only half-past three.

The morning had been fine, but now the sky thickened with a sullen darkness. Snow began to fall; the wind blew cold, and the wood looked dismal, the old tree grim. Yet Martin approved the shadow. He liked the spectral aspect of the doddered oak; it suited his drama, and made him feel more important, like a conspirator in a legend.

To and fro he walked along the narrow track as flakes fell faster, while the wind, which at first had only moaned, now howled pitifully through bare branches—an untuned, violent music that made the heart feel lonely even when the body was warm.

“She is long in coming,” he muttered, scanning the path.

Then, as if he could not resist making his own motives sound darker than they were, he added, “I wonder what I wish to see her so much for? She is not coming for me. But I have power over her, and I want her to come that I may use that power.”

He walked on. The storm thickened. The oak creaked like an old ship.

“Now,” he resumed when more time had passed, “if she fails to come, I shall hate and scorn her.”

The church clock, far away, struck four. At the same moment he heard a step so quick, so light, that but for the faint rustle of leaves it would scarcely have sounded on the wood-walk. The wind blew fiercely now; the snow drove in bewildering sheets; but on she came, not dismayed.

“Well, Martin,” Caroline Helstone said eagerly, breath quick from haste and cold, “how is he?”

“It is queer how she thinks of him,” reflected Martin. “The blinding snow and bitter cold are nothing to her, I believe; yet she is but a chitty-faced creature, as my mother would say. I could find in my heart to wish I had a cloak to wrap her in.”

So occupied was he with this sudden, inconvenient tenderness that he neglected to answer at once.

“You have seen him?” she insisted.

“No.”

“Oh! you promised you would.”

“I mean to do better by you than that. Didn’t I say I don’t care to see him?”

“But now it will be so long before I get to know anything certain, and I am sick of waiting. Martin, do see him, and give him Caroline Helstone’s regards, and say she wished to know how he was, and if anything could be done for his comfort.”

“I won’t.”

She stared at him in the storm, hurt and impatient. “You are changed. You were so friendly last night.”

“Come,” he said, brisk now, “we must not stand in this wood; it is too cold.”

“But before I go, promise me to come again to-morrow with news.”

“No such thing. I am much too delicate to make and keep such appointments in the winter season. If you knew what a pain I had in my chest this morning, and how I went without breakfast, and was knocked down besides, you’d feel the impropriety of bringing me here in the snow. Come, I say.”

“Are you really delicate, Martin?”

“Don’t I look so?”

“You have rosy cheeks.”

“That’s hectic. Will you come—or you won’t?”

“Where?”

“With me. I was a fool not to bring a cloak. I would have made you cosy.”

“You are going home; my nearest road lies in the opposite direction.”

“Put your arm through mine; I’ll take care of you.”

“But the wall—the hedge—it is such hard work climbing, and you are too slender and young to help me without hurting yourself.”

“You shall go through the gate.”

“But—”

“But, but—will you trust me or not?”

Caroline looked into his face. Under the boy’s audacity, she saw earnestness—mischief, yes, but also a strange, determined kindness.

“I think I will,” she said at last. “Anything rather than return as anxious as I came.”

“I can’t answer for that,” he replied. “This, however, I promise you: be ruled by me, and you shall see Moore yourself.”

“See him myself?”

“Yourself.”

“But, dear Martin, does he know?”

“Ah! I’m dear now. No, he doesn’t know.”

“And your mother and the others?”

“All is right.”

Caroline fell into a long silence, walking on with her guide. The snow thickened on her shawl; her breath came in white clouds. They came in sight of Briarmains, its outlines blurred by storm.

“Have you made up your mind?” Martin asked.

She did not answer at first. He pressed, low and peremptory: “Decide; we are just on the spot. I won’t see him—that I tell you—except to announce your arrival.”

“Martin, you are a strange boy, and this is a strange step; but all I feel is and has been, for a long time, strange. I will see him.”

“Having said that, you will neither hesitate nor retract?”

“No.”

“Here we are, then. Do not be afraid of passing the parlour window; no one will see you. My father and Matthew are at the mill, Mark is at school, the servants are in the back kitchen, Miss Moore is at the cottage, my mother in her bed, and Mrs. Horsfall in paradise. Observe—I need not ring. I open the door; the hall is empty, the staircase quiet; so is the gallery. The whole house and all its inhabitants are under a spell, which I will not break till you are gone.”

“Martin, I trust you.”

“You never said a better word. Let me take your shawl. I will shake off the snow and dry it for you. You are cold and wet. Never mind; there is a fire upstairs. Are you ready?”

“Yes.”

“Follow me.”

He left his shoes on the mat and mounted the stairs unshod, turning himself into a silent shadow. Caroline stole after him, step for step, her heart beating louder than the wind. There was a gallery, and then a passage; at the end of that passage Martin paused and tapped at a door.

He had to tap twice—thrice. At last a voice, known to one listener as well as to herself, said, “Come in.”

Martin entered briskly.

“Mr. Moore,” he announced, “a lady called to inquire after you. None of the women were about. It is washing-day, and the maids are over the crown of the head in soap-suds in the back kitchen, so I asked her to step up.”

“Up here, sir?” Moore’s tone—weak, yet affronted—answered.

“Up here, sir; but if you object, she shall go down again.”

“Is this a place—or am I a person—to bring a lady to, you absurd lad?”

“No; so I’ll take her off.”

“Martin, you will stay here. Who is she?”

“Your grandmother from that château on the Scheldt Miss Moore talks about.”

“Martin,” came a soft whisper at the door—Caroline’s voice, trembling, “don’t be foolish.”

“Is she there?” Moore asked hastily. He had caught the sound like a man catching a name in a dream.

“She is there, fit to faint. She is standing on the mat, shocked at your want of filial affection.”

“Martin, you are an evil cross between an imp and a page. What is she like?”

“More like me than you; for she is young and beautiful.”

“You are to show her forward. Do you hear?”

“Come, Miss Caroline.”

“Miss Caroline!” repeated Moore; and when she entered, he met her half-way across the chamber—a tall, thin, wasted figure with hollow eyes, yet a sudden light in them. He took both her hands as if he needed the contact to prove she was real.

“I give you a quarter of an hour,” said Martin, retreating. “No more. Say what you have to say in that time. Till it is past I will wait in the gallery; nothing shall approach; I’ll see you safe away. Should you persist in staying longer, I leave you to your fate.”

He shut the door, and outside stood as elate as a king. Never had he liked an adventure so well; none had made him feel so necessary, so central, so powerful.

“You are come at last,” Moore said, gazing on her as if hunger itself had eyes.

“Did you expect me before?”

“For a month—near two months—we have been very near; and I have been in sad pain, and danger, and misery, Cary.”

“I could not come.”

“Couldn’t you? But the rectory and Briarmains are very near—not two miles apart.”

Pain and pleasure mingled in her face. It was bitter to hear reproach; sweet to have a right to answer it.

“When I say I could not come, I mean I could not see you; for I came with mamma the very day we heard what had happened. Mr. MacTurk then told us it was impossible to admit any stranger.”

“But afterwards—every fine afternoon these many weeks past I have waited and listened. Something here, Cary”—he laid his hand on his breast—“told me it was impossible but that you should think of me. Not that I merit thought; but we are old acquaintance—we are cousins.”

“I came again, Robert; mamma and I came again.”

“Did you? Come, that is worth hearing. Since you came again, we will sit down and talk about it.”

They sat. Caroline drew her chair close, as if nearness itself might strengthen him. Outside, the storm deepened; the wind drove the snow in long, wuthering rushes against the panes. Neither seemed to hear that wild sound-world. Each was conscious only of the other’s breathing—proof of life.

“So mamma and you came again?”

“And Mrs. Yorke did treat us strangely. We asked to see you. ‘No,’ said she, ‘not in my house. I am at present responsible for his life; it shall not be forfeited for half an hour’s idle gossip.’ But I must not tell you all she said; it was very disagreeable. However, we came yet again—mamma, Miss Keeldar, and I. This time we thought we should conquer, as we were three against one, and Shirley was on our side. But Mrs. Yorke opened such a battery.”

Moore smiled faintly. “What did she say?”

“Things that astonished us. Shirley laughed at last; I cried; mamma was seriously annoyed. We were all three driven from the field. Since that time I have only walked once a day past the house, just for the satisfaction of looking up at your window, which I could distinguish by the drawn curtains. I really dared not come in.”

“I have wished for you, Caroline.”

“I did not know that,” she said quickly. “I never dreamt one instant that you thought of me. If I had but most distantly imagined such a possibility—”

“Mrs. Yorke would still have beaten you.”

“She would not. Stratagem should have been tried, if persuasion failed. I would have come to the kitchen door; the servants should have let me in, and I would have walked straight upstairs. In fact, it was far more the fear of intrusion—the fear of yourself—that baffled me than the fear of Mrs. Yorke.”

“Only last night I despaired of ever seeing you again. Weakness has wrought terrible depression in me—terrible depression.”

“And you sit alone?”

“Worse than alone.”

“But you must be getting better, since you can leave your bed?”

“I doubt whether I shall live. I see nothing for it, after such exhaustion, but decline.”

“You—you shall go home to the Hollow.”

“Dreariness would accompany, nothing cheerful come near me.”

“I will alter this. This shall be altered, were there ten Mrs. Yorkes to do battle with.”

“Cary, you make me smile.”

“Do smile; smile again. Shall I tell you what I should like?”

“Tell me anything—only keep talking. I am Saul; but for music I should perish.”

“I should like you to be brought to the rectory, and given to me and mamma.”

“A precious gift! I have not laughed since they shot me till now.”

“Do you suffer pain, Robert?”

“Not so much pain now; but I am hopelessly weak, and the state of my mind is inexpressible—dark, barren, impotent. Do you not read it all in my face? I look a mere ghost.”

“Altered; yet I should have known you anywhere. But I understand your feelings; I experienced something like it. Since we met, I too have been very ill.”

“Very ill?”

“I thought I should die. The tale of my life seemed told. Every night, just at midnight, I used to wake from awful dreams; and the book lay open before me at the last page, where was written ‘Finis.’ I had strange feelings.”

“You speak my experience.”

“I believed I should never see you again; and I grew so thin—as thin as you are now. I could do nothing for myself—neither rise nor lie down; and I could not eat. Yet you see I am better.”

“Comforter—sad as sweet. I am too feeble to say what I feel; but while you speak I do feel.”

“Here I am at your side, where I thought never more to be. Here I speak to you. I see you listen to me willingly—look at me kindly. Did I count on that? I despaired.”

Moore sighed—so deep it was nearly a groan. He covered his eyes with his hand.

“May I be spared to make some atonement,” he murmured; it sounded like a prayer forced up from weakness.

“And for what?”

“We will not touch on it now, Cary; unmanned as I am, I have not the power to cope with such a topic. Was Mrs. Pryor with you during your illness?”

“Yes.” Caroline’s face brightened, tender as a lamp lit in a window. “You know she is mamma?”

“I have heard—Hortense told me; but that tale too I will receive from yourself. Does she add to your happiness?”

“What! mamma? She is dear to me; how dear I cannot say. I was altogether weary, and she held me up.”

“I deserve to hear that in a moment when I can scarce lift my hand to my head. I deserve it.”

“It is no reproach against you.”

“It is a coal of fire heaped on my head; and so is every word you address to me, and every look that lights your sweet face. Come still nearer, Lina; and give me your hand—if my thin fingers do not scare you.”

She took his thin fingers between her two hands, as if warming them by touch alone, and bent her head and kissed them—lightly, as a petal might brush water.

Moore was much moved. A large tear or two coursed down his hollow cheek.

“I’ll keep these things in my heart, Cary; that kiss I will put by, and you shall hear of it again one day.”

“Come out!” cried Martin, abruptly opening the door. “Come away; you have had twenty minutes instead of a quarter of an hour.”

“She will not stir yet, you hempseed,” Moore said, roused to a faint flash of authority.

“I dare not stay longer, Robert,” Caroline answered, rising with reluctance that made her voice shake.

“Can you promise to return?”

“No, she can’t,” responded Martin. “The thing mustn’t become customary. I can’t be troubled. It’s very well for once; I’ll not have it repeated.”

“You’ll not have it repeated—” Moore began, indignant.

“Hush,” Caroline pleaded softly. “Don’t vex him; we could not have met to-day but for him. But I will come again, if it is your wish that I should come.”

“It is my wish—my one wish—almost the only wish I can feel.”

“Come this minute,” Martin urged, suddenly alert with genuine alarm. “My mother has coughed, got up, set her feet on the floor. Let her only catch you on the stairs, Miss Caroline. You’re not to bid him good-bye”—he stepped between her and Moore—“you are to march.”

“My shawl, Martin.”

“I have it. I’ll put it on for you when you are in the hall.”

He made them part. He would suffer no farewell but what could be said in looks—hers, full of promise and pain; his, hungry with gratitude and longing. Then he half carried Caroline down the stairs, swift as a conspirator.

In the hall he wrapped her shawl round her, shook the last snow from its folds, and drew it close as if he could fend off the cold with his own hands. But his mother’s tread creaked above in the gallery, and Martin—held back by a sudden, strange diffidence, the proper natural impulse of a boy’s heart—swallowed the demand that rose to his lips: Now, for all this, give me one kiss.

Before he could speak, she was gone—across the snowy road, rather skimming than wading the drifts, the storm taking her like white water taking a leaf.

“She is my debtor,” he said to himself, watching her vanish into the swirl, “and I will be paid.”

He flattered himself it was opportunity, not audacity, that had failed him. He misjudged his own nature, and held it for something lower than it was.
