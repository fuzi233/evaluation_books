The universe demands a fiction of origin; even the most rigorous sciences must invent a zero-point, a celestial coordinate where the clock strikes Nought, pretending that time began there. Poetry, the grander sister, claims to begin in the middle, yet she too is merely counting backward and forward, dividing her units until she arrives *in medias res*. There is no true genesis to be found in retrospect. Whether our prologue unfolds in heaven or on earth, it remains but a fragment of the presupposed fact that anchors our narrative.

Was she beautiful? Or was the secret of her form a dynamic tension rather than static charm? Did the good or the evil genius preside over those beams? Likely the latter; otherwise, why did her presence evoke unrest rather than undisturbed delight? Why did the urge to look again feel like coercion, a demand upon the senses, rather than a willing longing of the soul?

The woman posing these questions in Daniel Deronda's mind was currently engaged in gambling. Not in the open air beneath a southern sun, tossing copper coins against a crumbling wall with rags wrapped around her limbs, but within one of those magnificent resorts that the accumulated enlightenment of ages has prepared for such pleasures at a steep price. The room was a condenser of gilt moldings, dark tones, and chubby nudes, designed to hold the heavy, humid breath of high fashion—a breath rarely available in equal proportions elsewhere, certainly not to those of lesser station.

It was nearly four o'clock on a September day, the atmosphere brewed into a visible haze. Deep stillness reigned, broken only by the light rattle and chink of chips, the sweep of a brush, and the occasional monotone of French spoken by an ingeniously constructed automaton. Two long tables hosted serried crowds, all faces bent toward the felt except for one. A melancholy little boy, clad in epidermis for his legs but dressed in finery for the rest, stood facing the doorway. With the blank gaze of a masquerading advertisement on an itinerant show's platform, he watched the entrance, positioned directly behind a lady deeply engrossed at the roulette wheel.

Around this table, fifty or sixty souls were assembled. In the outer rows, mere spectators occasionally deposited a five-franc note with a simpering air, curious to taste the passion of gambling. Those fully absorbed displayed a distant variety of European types: Livonian, Spanish, Graeco-Italian, and miscellaneous German, alongside English aristocrats and plebeians alike. Here was a striking admission of human equality. The jeweled fingers of an English countess hovered inches from a bony, yellow, crab-like hand stretching a bare wrist to clutch a heap of coin. That hand belonged to a figure with a square, gaunt face, deep-set eyes, grizzled brows, and ill-combed hair—a slight metamorphosis of the vulture. Beside her sat a prematurely aged woman, withered after a short bloom like artificial flowers, holding a shabby velvet reticule and occasionally popping the point of a card into her mouth. Nearby, a respectable London tradesman, blonde and soft-handed, maintained his sleek, parted hair, conscious of circulars addressed to the nobility whose patronage allowed him fashionable holidays. He possessed not the gambler's passion that nullifies appetite, but a well-fed leisure. In the intervals of making and spending money, he sought to replicate the cycle in play, reflecting a belief that Providence approved of his amusement, ready to cease only if the sweetness of winning turned to the sourness of losing.

Standing close to his chair was a handsome Italian, calm and statuesque, reaching across to place the first pile of napoleons from a fresh bag delivered by an envoy with a scrolled mustache. Within half a minute, the pile was pushed to an old bewigged woman with eyeglasses pinching her nose. A slight gleam and a faint, mumbling smile touched her lips, but the Italian remained impassive, secure in an infallible system that placed his foot on the neck of chance, immediately preparing a new stack. So did a man resembling an emaciated beau or worn-out libertine, viewing life through a single eyeglass and holding out his hand tremulously for change. His play was driven not by severity of system, but by dreams of white crows or the superstition that the eighth of the month was lucky, inspiring a fierce yet tottering impulsiveness.

Yet, despite their marked differences, every player wore a uniform negativeness of expression, a mask suggesting they had all consumed a root compelling their brains to the same narrow monotony of action.

Deronda's first thought, as his eyes fell upon this scene of dull, gas-poisoned absorption, was that the gambling of Spanish shepherd-boys seemed more enviable. Rousseau might be justified in claiming that art and science had done mankind a poor service. Suddenly, however, the moment became dramatic. His attention was arrested by a young lady standing at an angle nearby, the last to whom his eyes traveled. She was bending to speak English to a middle-aged lady seated at play beside her, then instantly returning to the game. She revealed the full height of a graceful figure, possessing a face that might possibly be looked at without admiration, but could hardly be passed with indifference.

The inward debate she sparked in Deronda gave his eyes a growing expression of scrutiny, drifting further from the glow of undefined sensibility into pure analysis. At moments, he tracked the movement of her arms and hands as this problematic sylph bent to deposit her stake with an air of firm choice; the next, his gaze returned to her face, steady and unaffected by beholders, directed solely toward the game. The sylph was winning. As her tapering fingers, delicately gloved in pale gray, adjusted the coins pushed toward her to pass them back to the winning point, she looked around with a survey too cold and neutral to lack a trace of art concealing inward exultation.

Then, in the course of that survey, her eyes met Deronda's. Instead of averting them as she would have desired, she was unpleasantly conscious that they were arrested. How long? The darting sense that he was measuring her, looking down on her as an inferior, that he was of a different quality from the human dross surrounding her, that he felt himself in a region outside and above her, examining her as a specimen of a lower order, roused a tingling resentment. It did not flush her cheeks but drained the blood from her lips. Controlling herself through an inward defiance, she showed no other sign of emotion than this pallor before turning back to her play. But Deronda's gaze had acted as an evil eye. Her stake vanished. No matter; she had been winning since she began with a few napoleons and held a considerable reserve. She had begun to believe in her luck; others had begun to believe in it. She envisioned a *cortège* following her, worshipping her as a goddess of fortune, watching her play as a directing augury. Such things had been known of male gamblers; why not a woman? Her friend and chaperon, who had initially opposed her playing, was now approving, offering the prudent advice to stop at the right moment and return to England. Gwendolen had replied that she cared for the excitement, not the winnings. On that supposition, this moment ought to have been the flood-tide of her eager experience. Yet, when her next stake was swept away, she felt the orbits of her eyes grow hot. The certainty of that man still watching her was a pressure beginning to torture. The more reason she should not flinch, but continue playing as if indifferent to loss or gain.

Her friend touched her elbow, proposing they quit. In reply, Gwendolen put ten louis on the same spot. She was in a mood of defiance where the mind loses sight of any end beyond the satisfaction of enraged resistance; the puerile stupidity of a dominant impulse included luck among its objects of defiance. Since she was not winning strikingly, the next best thing was to lose strikingly. She controlled her muscles, showing no tremor of mouth or hands. Each time her stake was swept off, she doubled it. Many were now watching her, but the sole observation she registered was Deronda's, who, though she never looked toward him, she was sure had not moved away.

Such a drama requires no long time to play out; development and catastrophe can often be measured by nothing clumsier than the moment-hand. "Faites votre jeu, mesdames et messieurs," said the automatic voice of destiny from between the mustache and imperial of the croupier. Gwendolen's arm stretched to deposit her last poor heap of napoleons. "Le jeu ne va plus," said destiny. In five seconds, Gwendolen turned from the table, but turned resolutely with her face toward Deronda and looked at him. There was a smile of irony in his eyes as their glances met; yet it was better that he should keep his attention fixed on her than disregard her as one of an insect swarm lacking individual physiognomy. Besides, in spite of his superciliousness and irony, it was difficult to believe he did not admire her spirit as well as her person. He was young, handsome, distinguished in appearance—not one of those ridiculous, dowdy Philistines who thought it incumbent on them to blight the gaming-table with a sour look of protest. The general conviction that we are admirable does not easily yield to a single negative; rather, when members of Vanity's large family find their performance received coldly, they are apt to believe that a little more of it will win over the unaccountable dissident.
