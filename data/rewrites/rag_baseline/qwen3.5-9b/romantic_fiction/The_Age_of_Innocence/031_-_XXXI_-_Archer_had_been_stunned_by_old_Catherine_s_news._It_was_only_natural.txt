The news from Old Catherine had struck Archer with a force that left him momentarily suspended in the air. Of course, Madame Olenska would hasten from Washington to her grandmother's summons; it was the gravity of duty pulling her back. But the decision to remain under Mrs. Mingott's roof, especially now that the old lady's health had nearly recovered, defied simple logic.

Archer was certain the change was not financial. He knew the exact sum her husband allotted her at their separation—a pittance that, without the grandmother's allowance, could scarcely sustain two women in the manner expected by the Mingotts. With Medora Manson ruined, that meager stipend would barely cover the bare necessities of clothing and food. Yet Archer was convinced Madame Olenska had not accepted the offer out of necessity.

She possessed the heedless generosity and the spasmodic extravagance of those accustomed to vast fortunes, rendering them indifferent to currency. Still, she could endure privations her relatives deemed essential, and Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Welland had often lamented that anyone who had tasted the cosmopolitan luxuries of Count Olenski's estates cared so little for "how things were done." Furthermore, several months had passed since her allowance ceased, yet she had made no move to regain her grandmother's favor. If her course had altered, it must be for reasons beyond survival.

He needed not look far for the true cause. On the journey from the ferry, she had told him they must remain apart, saying it with her head resting on his chest. There was no calculated coquetry in her tone; she was fighting her fate as he fought his, clinging desperately to a resolve not to betray the trust placed in them. But during the ten days since her return to New York, she must have sensed from his silence, from his absence, that he was contemplating a decisive step—one from which there was no turning back. At the thought, a sudden fear of her own weakness might have seized her, convincing her that accepting the usual compromise, following the line of least resistance, was preferable.

An hour prior, when he had rung Mrs. Mingott's bell, Archer had believed his path clear. He intended to speak privately with Madame Olenska, and failing that, to ascertain from her grandmother the date and train of her return to Washington. In that train, he planned to board, traveling with her as far as she wished, his fancy drifting toward Japan. At minimum, she would understand instantly that wherever she went, he followed. He meant to leave a note for May cutting off all other alternatives.

He had fancied himself not only prepared for this plunge but eager to execute it. Yet, his first reaction to the altered circumstances had been relief. Now, walking home from Mrs. Mingott's, he felt a growing distaste for what lay ahead. There was nothing unknown in the path he was supposed to tread; indeed, he had walked it before as a free man, accountable to no one, lending himself with amused detachment to the game of precautions, prevarication, concealment, and compliance required by society. This procedure was termed "protecting a woman's honor," and the best fiction, combined with the post-prandial discourse of his elders, had long initiated him into every nuance of its code.

Now, viewed anew, his role seemed singularly diminished. It was, in fact, the performance he had watched Mrs. Thorley Rushworth enact toward a fond, unperceiving husband: a smiling, bantering, humor-ing, watchful, and incessant lie. A lie by day, a lie by night, a lie in every touch and every glance; a lie in every caress and every quarrel; a lie in every word and every silence.

It was easier, and less dastardly overall, for a wife to play such a part. A woman's standard of truthfulness was tacitly lower; she was the subject creature, versed in the arts of the enslaved. She could always plead moods and nerves, claiming the right not to be held strictly to account; and even in the most rigid societies, the laughter was invariably against the husband.

But in Archer's little world, no one laughed at a deceived wife, and a certain measure of contempt attached to men who continued their philandering after marriage. In the rotation of crops, there was a recognized season for wild oats, but they were not to be sown more than once.

Archer had always shared this view: in his heart, he thought Lefferts despicable. But to love Ellen Olenska was not to become a man like Lefferts. For the first time, Archer found himself face-to-face with the dread argument of the individual case. Ellen Olenska was unlike any other woman; he was unlike any other man. Their situation, therefore, resembled no one else's, and they were answerable to no tribunal but that of their own judgment.

Yes, but in ten minutes he would be mounting his own doorstep. There were May, and habit, and honor, and all the old decencies he and his people had always believed in...

At the corner, he hesitated, then walked on down Fifth Avenue.

Ahead of him, looming in the winter night, stood a large, unlit house. As he drew near, he recalled how often he had seen it blazing with lights, its steps awninged and carpeted, carriages waiting in double lines to draw up at the curb. It was in the conservatory, stretching its dead-black bulk down the side street, that he had taken his first kiss from May; it was under the myriad candles of the ballroom that he had seen her appear, tall and silver-shining as a young Diana.

Now the house was as dark as the grave, save for a faint flare of gas in the basement and a light in one upstairs room where the blinds had not been lowered. Reaching the corner, he saw Mrs. Manson Mingott's carriage standing at the door. What an opportunity for Sillerton Jackson, if he chanced to pass! Archer had been greatly moved by Old Catherine's account of Madame Olenska's attitude toward Mrs. Beaufort; it made the righteous reprobation of New York seem like a passing-by on the other side. But he knew well enough what construction the clubs and drawing-rooms would place on Ellen Olenska's visits to her cousin.

He paused, looking up at the lit window. No doubt the two women were sitting together there; Beaufort had probably sought consolation elsewhere. There were even rumors that he had left New York with Fanny Ring, but Mrs. Beaufort's attitude made the report seem improbable.

Archer possessed the nocturnal perspective of Fifth Avenue almost to himself. At that hour, most people were indoors, dressing for dinner; he was secretly glad that Ellen's exit was likely to be unobserved. As the thought crossed his mind, the door opened, and she emerged. Behind her trailed a faint light, such as might have been carried down the stairs to guide her. She turned to say a word to someone, then the door closed, and she descended the steps.

"Ellen," he said in a low voice as she reached the pavement.

She stopped with a slight start. Just then, he saw two young men of fashionable cut approaching. There was a familiar air about their overcoats and the way their smart silk mufflers were folded over their white ties, and he wondered how youths of their quality happened to dine out so early. Then he remembered that the Reggie Chiverses, whose house was a few doors above, were taking a large party that evening to see Adelaide Neilson in *Romeo and Juliet*, and guessed the two belonged to that number. They passed under a lamp, and he recognized Lawrence Lefferts and a young Chivers.

A mean desire not to have Madame Olenska seen at the Beauforts' door vanished as he felt the penetrating warmth of her hand.

"I shall see you now—we shall be together," he broke out, hardly knowing what he said.

"Ah," she answered, "Granny has told you?"

While he watched her, he became aware that Lefferts and Chivers, upon reaching the far side of the street corner, had discreetly struck away across Fifth Avenue. It was the kind of masculine solidarity he himself often practiced; now he sickened at their connivance. Did she really imagine that he and she could live like this? And if not, what else did she imagine?

"Tomorrow I must see you—somewhere where we can be alone," he said, in a voice that sounded almost angry to his own ears.

She wavered, moving toward the carriage.

"But I shall be at Granny's—for the present that is," she added, as if conscious that her change of plans required some explanation.

"Somewhere where we can be alone," he insisted.

She gave a faint laugh that grated on him.

"In New York? But there are no churches... no monuments."

"There's the Art Museum—in the Park," he explained as she looked puzzled. "At half-past two. I shall be at the door..."

She turned away without answering and quickly entered the carriage. As it drove off, she leaned forward, and he thought she waved her hand in the obscurity. He stared after her in a turmoil of contradictory feelings. It seemed to him that he had been speaking not to the woman he loved, but to another, a woman he was indebted to for pleasures already wearied of; it was hateful to find himself the prisoner of this hackneyed vocabulary.

"She'll come!" he said to himself, almost contemptuously.
