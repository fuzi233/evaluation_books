CHAPTER XXV. How does one trace the origin of a mind reduced to the barrenness of refined self-absorption, where desires have dimmed into mere expectations, and impulses flicker unpredictably through a landscape of emotional neglect? Such a state often arises when life is unshaped by duty or constraint—when freedom becomes its own prison. _Nam deteriores omnes sumus licentiæ_, or, in simpler terms, “As you like” is no guidepost to virtue.

Potentates convey their will with few words, and so it was with Grandcourt. Upon learning that Gwendolen had left Leubronn, he casually declared the resort a "beastly hole," worse than Baden—a remark Mr. Lush instantly interpreted as an order to return to Diplow. Though the execution lagged behind the intent, Grandcourt did not rush; he lingered over his morning rituals, descending at last with an air of detached elegance that made vitality seem crude. He wandered aimlessly—the terrace, the gaming rooms, the reading room—showing no interest in anyone or anything, yet somehow commanding attention.

When Lady Mallinger approached, he paused, raised his hat, and listened to her praise of the mineral waters. "Yes," he replied, voice flat, "I heard someone say how providential it is that springs always happen to be near gambling places."

"Oh, that was a joke!" she exclaimed, misunderstanding his deadpan tone. "Like the old one about towns and rivers."

"Ah, perhaps," he murmured, without shifting expression.

Lady Mallinger later confided this exchange to Sir Hugo, who chuckled. "He’s not a fool. Don’t imagine he doesn’t see humor. He plays his cards as well as any man."

"But he never seemed very sensible to me," she confessed, half-apologetically. Her real unease stemmed from Grandcourt’s presence—he reminded her of her own failure: the absence of a son, which haunted her sense of worth. She feared Sir Hugo might regret his choice, and though he was kind, she suspected he could grow distant if disappointment lingered too long.

Deronda also encountered Grandcourt, receiving a brief nod. He returned it courteously, though inwardly resentful. A deep-seated belief remained: that his birth had been marred by injustice, and his father—Grandcourt’s own father—was responsible. He knew that, had circumstances been different, Grandcourt might have inherited what was now his. Yet Deronda refused to let envy fester. He saw the danger of petty triumph in another’s downfall and turned away from it, clinging instead to compassion and dignity. Pride helped him—pride in refusing to acknowledge poverty as a mark of shame. He would not allow Grandcourt to believe himself envied.

But interpretation is beyond control. Grandcourt imagined Deronda felt the sting of comparison, and this fantasy pleased him. The illusion that others envy us is a familiar companion to egoism. Like his dogs, whom he enjoyed dominating, Grandcourt took pleasure in feeling superior—even in making others jealous. So, on the terrace, he spoke to Deronda about the hunting season at Diplow, even inviting him: “You’d better come over for a run or two when it begins.”

Meanwhile, Lush found time to amuse himself—chatting with Sir Hugo, sharing gossip about Grandcourt’s affairs, especially those involving Diplow. The baronet already knew enough about his nephew’s entanglements to allow Lush to speak freely. On a sunlit autumn day, such talk was both pleasant and safe—scandal lightly dressed as _traits de mœurs_. Sir Hugo enjoyed these glimpses into high society’s secrets, but kept them carefully confined. He never discussed his knowledge of Grandcourt’s private life with Deronda, who viewed Grandcourt as a pale, lifeless figure—far removed from the blood and fire of passion.

Lush, ever eager to please, was content with the baronet’s favor. He was not quite a gentleman, but he served well—like a second-best coat, worn with comfort and ease. “Well,” said Sir Hugo, “let me know if things change—whether the marriage seems likely, or if money troubles become urgent. My plan would be better for him than burdening Ryelands.”

“That’s true,” Lush agreed. “But we mustn’t push it. Just let it drift into his thoughts—like a scent that tickles the nose. Grandcourt isn’t a man driven by what’s best for him—especially if he sees it benefiting you too. I’ve stayed by him for fifteen years, given up everything. He wouldn’t find another like me. He’s peculiar—has grown more so lately. But I’m constant. I’ve been a sort of guardian since he was twenty. He was fascinating then—and could be now, if he chose. I’m attached to him. And it would be worse for him if I were gone.”

Sir Hugo nodded, needing no response. Lush, too, didn’t expect one. Who can avoid speaking of themselves? Even in silence, men betray their inner lives. And so Lush offered this portrait—not as truth, but as performance, as the polite dressing of his soul.

But the next morning, Grandcourt cut short Lush’s easy delay. He asked sharply, “Are you preparing for our departure by the Paris train?”

“I didn’t know you meant to leave,” Lush replied, not truly surprised.

“You might have known,” Grandcourt muttered, staring at his cigar. “See to everything. And make sure no brute shares our carriage. Leave my P.P.C. at the Mallingers’.”

They reached Paris the following day. There, Grandcourt instructed Lush to proceed directly to Diplow, while he stayed behind with his valet. Only days later did Lush receive a telegram summoning the carriage to Wanchester station.

In the interim, Lush worked diligently—overseeing the stud, managing the household, and gathering news of Gwendolen and Offendene. He couldn’t predict how the news of her family’s ruin might affect Grandcourt’s erratic nature. On one hand, her poverty might remove the fear of rejection—making her more accessible. On the other, certainty of acceptance might only make Grandcourt indecisive, drifting like a moth caught in a web.

Lush had observed Grandcourt closely for years. He understood him better than most—but understanding Grandcourt meant knowing that prediction was impossible. Could he suddenly act nobly, like a French drama hero rising from moral decay? Perhaps. But Lush doubted it. Generosity was not among Grandcourt’s habits.

Yet Lush had firm beliefs: Grandcourt would never willingly choose a woman who brought nothing but hardship. He preferred wealth—or at least gratitude. An heiress would bring immediate security. Mrs. Glasher, whom Lush had long supported, would offer lasting loyalty. Neither outcome troubled him socially. He wouldn’t mind no marriage at all. But he *did* want to prevent a union with Gwendolen—one that might bring chaos to Grandcourt and distress to himself.

He wanted peace, not harm. Except, perhaps, a little discomfort for Gwendolen—unconvenient, impertinent, and now, possibly, a threat.

Still, even the most agreeable servant has limits. Lush had long been treated as useful, pleasant, reliable. He hadn’t prepared himself for insult—only for conflict.

On the first day back at Diplow, Grandcourt focused on the stables. He ordered a groom to saddle Criterion with a side-saddle, and rode the horse slowly, inspecting its gait. This small gesture signaled purpose. Lush hesitated—should he speak first, before any step was taken?

The next morning, he almost decided to reveal what he knew about Gwendolen. But Grandcourt remained silent, answering even personal questions with the stillness of a fish under water. After reading his letters, he gave orders to Lush, then turned slightly, muttering, “Oh.”

“What is it?” Lush asked, unbothered by formality.

“Shut the door,” Grandcourt said. “I can’t speak into the corridor.”

Lush closed the door and sat down.

After a pause, Grandcourt asked, “Is Miss Harleth at Offendene?”

He knew Lush had inquired. He liked that Lush didn’t need *him* to ask.

“Well, I don’t know,” Lush replied carelessly. “The family’s ruined. Lost everything in some banking scandal. The mother has nothing. They’re living in a cottage—like laborers.”

“Don’t lie to me,” Grandcourt whispered, low and sharp. “It’s not amusing. It serves no purpose.”

“What do you mean?” Lush asked, irritated. “I’m not making this up. Bazley told me. Brackenshaw’s man too. He’s finding a new tenant for Offendene.”

“I don’t mean that,” Grandcourt said, regaining his calm. “Is she there? Or isn’t she?”

“I can’t tell,” Lush snapped. “She may have left yesterday. I heard she took a governess position. Might already be there. But if you want to see her, the mother would send for her.”

That sneer slipped out before he could stop it.

“Send Hutchins to inquire if she’ll be there tomorrow.”

Lush didn’t move.

He had rehearsed arguments, and now, provoked, he delivered them. “You’re entering a serious situation now, Grandcourt. No more flirting—no games. You must decide: do you want to be accepted? And what if you’re refused? One or the other. You can’t dally for six weeks.”

Grandcourt said nothing. He pressed the newspaper onto his knees and lit a new cigar.

Lush took this as a sign he was listening—and seized the moment. “Everything’s changed. Her family needs support. You can’t marry her and let her mother live in poverty. Marriage will tie you down. You’re not used to restraint. And financially, you’re not exactly free. What will you gain? You control your estates—why burden them for a whim you may regret in a year? I’d hate to see you ruin your life like this. If there were real benefit, it would be different.”

His tone grew increasingly smooth, almost honeyed. He nearly forgot he was gambling.

Then Grandcourt spoke. “I knew you objected to my marrying Miss Harleth.”

A pause. Then: “But I never thought that mattered.”

“I never thought you did,” Lush replied, dryly. “But consider this: after all you’ve seen, are you really acting like a ballad hero? Making a fool of yourself—for what? You couldn’t make up your mind before. You can’t care much for her. And the tricks she’ll play—judge from what you heard in Leubronn. But the point is—there’s no more hesitation now.”

“Perfectly,” Grandcourt said, turning to look at him with narrowed eyes. “I don’t intend there to be. It may be disagreeable to you. But if you think I care a damn about that—you’re profoundly mistaken.”

“Oh, well,” Lush said, hands in pockets, “if you’ve made up your mind—though there’s another angle. I assumed she’d accept. But I’m not so sure. She’s tricky. And she had reasons to run before.”

He stepped forward, standing near Grandcourt, though not close.

“She had her reasons,” he repeated, more pointedly.

“I came to that conclusion,” Grandcourt said, coldly ironic.

“Yes, but do you know what they were?”

“You do,” Grandcourt replied, not blinking.

“And you should know—so you can judge whether she’ll swallow her reasons and accept you. I’d wager against it. She saw Lydia in Cardell Chase. She heard the whole story.”

Grandcourt smoked in silence.

Lush waited, restless, watching the windows. He expected Grandcourt to accuse him of engineering the affair—Mrs. Glasher had been at Gadsmere, a hundred miles away. He was ready to admit it. But he wanted Grandcourt to realize: she knew the truth. She was frightened.

At last, Grandcourt looked up. “What follows?”

It was a checkmate.

Lush felt fury rise—stronger than ever before. But to fight now would be foolish. He shrugged, turned to leave.

Then Grandcourt said, calmly, “Oblige me by pushing that pen and paper here.”

No command could have been more absolute. Lush obeyed. Grandcourt wrote quickly, addressed the note, and pushed it across the table. “Let Hutchins take it at once.”

It was addressed to Miss Harleth, Offendene.

Later, when Lush cooled, he was glad there had been no explosion. But he knew a rift had formed. He had lost ground. And Grandcourt’s resolve had hardened—not softened.

As for how it happened—what shifted within that baffling mind—Lush could only shrug in despair.
