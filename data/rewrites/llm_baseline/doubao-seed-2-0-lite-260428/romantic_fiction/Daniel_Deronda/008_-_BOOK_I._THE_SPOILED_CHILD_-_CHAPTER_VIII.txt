What name doth Joy most borrow
When life is fair? “To-morrow.”
What name doth best fit Sorrow
In young despair? “To-morrow.”

A far heavier, longer ache settled over the rectory than any small passing mischief of the younger Gascoigne children. Rex stumbled home at dusk, and threw himself face-down on his bed so still, so unresponsive to his mother’s soft calls, that they thought for hours he might not rouse at all. When he did stir the next day, it was only to show the sharp, burning signs of a fever that clung to his bones—too deep to be put down to cold, too heavy to be cured with broth and peppermint. His planned trip to Southampton was forgotten entirely. The whole household turned to tending the boy who no longer wanted to get better: the bright, teasing, apple-tree-climbing Rex who had always been the first to laugh through dinner, the first to carry the smallest of his siblings on his shoulders, was gone. In his place was a hollow, dull-eyed creature who met every affectionate offer with a hoarse murmur of “Let me alone.”

His father, the rector, saw past the fever to the heartbreak under it. He knew this sort of hurt would pass faster if it was not poked and prodded with lectures. He would slip in to sit by Rex’s bed in silence for ten minutes at a time, then press a gentle, warm palm to the boy’s blank forehead, and murmur “God bless you, my boy” before he left. The younger children, Warham and the toddlers, would peek wide-eyed around the bedroom door at this unrecognizable version of their lively brother, only to be shooed away immediately by Anna’s urgent shushing and wagging finger.

Anna never left his side, not for longer than it took to fetch a fresh glass of water. Rex would not squeeze her small hand back, but he did not pull away, and that was enough. Her heart was split half in raw, aching sympathy for him, half in hot, quiet resentment of her cousin Gwendolen. “Perhaps it is wicked of me,” she would whisper to herself over and over, her throat tight, “but I do not think I can ever love her again.” Even Mrs. Gascoigne found she could not hold her anger at Gwendolen back entirely, venting to her husband one evening as they sewed by the fire: “I know I ought to be glad she does not care for the poor boy, Henry. But really—she is hard. She has the heart of a coquette. She must have let him believe she cared, or this disappointment would never have settled into him so deep. And poor Fanny is utterly blind to what her daughter is.”

Mr. Gascoigne cut her off firmly, his tone gentle but unyielding. “The less said on that point the better, Nancy. I ought to have seen it coming myself. Be thankful no worse harm came of it. Let the whole thing die out as quickly as possible—especially where Gwendolen is concerned. Let it be as if it never happened at all.” For the rector, the quiet truth was that they had dodged a disaster: if Gwendolen had returned Rex’s feelings, the near-penniless girl with no fortune would have tied his son to a marriage that would have dragged his whole future down, and there would have been no way to untangle it.

Then one crisp, frosty morning, Rex asked for his bath, and dressed himself slowly, carefully, in his usual clothes. Anna nearly shook with excitement at the sound of his footsteps on the stairs, and ran to meet him at the bottom. He gave her a faint, wobbly smile, so sad on his pale face that she had to bite her lip to keep from crying. “Nannie,” he said softly, taking her hand to lead her into the drawing room. When his mother came to kiss his cheek, he mumbled, “What a plague I’ve been, haven’t I?”

He sat by the bow window, staring out at the lawn crusted white with hoarfrost, the sun sending thin, fleeting streaks of light across the shrubs that looked, Anna thought, exactly like that sad, fragile smile on his face. Rex felt as if he had woken up in a whole new strange world, where none of his old plans—his Oxford rowing matches, his summer trips, the books he had been looking forward to studying—mattered at all. Anna sat nearby pretending to sew, but never taking her eyes off him, her whole chest tight with longing to ease his pain.

Down the lane that ran past the garden hedge, a wagon piled high with oak timber trundled past. The great shire horses strained their muscles, their hooves slipping a little on the frost, and the driver cracked his whip before running along beside them to guide the lead horse’s head, terrified it would swerve and tip the whole heavy load. Rex stared until the last quivering trunk of timber vanished around the bend, then stood to pace the room once, twice, before sinking back down beside Anna. She dragged a little stool over to tuck herself against his knee, looking up at him with a quiet, pleading expression that said speak to me.

“I’ll tell you what I’m thinking of, Nannie,” he said. “I’m going to Canada. Or somewhere wild, like that. I’ll build a hut, clear the land for miles, work till I’m too tired to think, and have nothing but wide open quiet all around me.”

“Oh, Rex, not forever!” she gasped, big tears rolling down her cheeks.

“Where else would I go? I can’t stay here. I’ll never get the spirit back to study for Oxford, not after this. I’ll waste father’s money, I’ll fail my exams, and I’ll be miserable every single time I turn a corner and half-expect to see her.”

“Then I’m coming too,” Anna said, already certain. “I can make the fires, mend the clothes, bake bread. I’ll learn to churn butter before we sail. It’ll be like when we were little, remember? We built a tent out of the drugget rug in the attic, used our little toy plates and pretended we were settlers. It’ll be better than any of the silly parties they make us go to here.”

They talked it over until the sun was high, until they had worked out every small, practical detail that made sense to their young, heartsick minds. They agreed to go to the rector together, in his study when he was alone—they would break the news to their mother later, once their father had consented, so they would not distress her for no reason.

Mr. Gascoigne smiled when he saw them, glad to have Rex up and walking again. “Sit down, both of you,” he said, gesturing to the chairs by his desk. It was a striking little group, all three of them sharing the same distinct family face: the straight, high brow, the nose that might have been aquiline if it had just tilted a little higher, the short upper lip, the strong, square chin, even the same warm, deep set tone to their eyes. The grey-haired rector was solid, authoritative, the deep line between his brows deepening when he turned his full attention to you; Rex looked like a ghost of his father’s youth, stripped of all his father’s plans and composure, raw and heartsick; Anna was a tiny, soft copy of Rex, her face shifting in perfect mirror to every change of his expression, as if the two of them shared a single soul.

“You know what’s been wrong with me, father,” Rex began, and the rector nodded. “I’m no good for life here anymore. I won’t go back to Oxford. I can’t make myself read a single page. I just want your consent to take a small outfit, and go work the land in the colonies. I’ll earn my own bread, and I won’t be a burden on any of you.”

“And I’m coming too, papa!” Anna burst out, climbing onto his knee to nudge his arm. “Rex will need someone to keep house for him. We’ll never get married, either of us. We won’t cost you a penny, and you’ll have more money left to raise the younger boys. It’s perfect.”

Mr. Gascoigne did not smile, but he held Anna tight against his side, gentle, as he turned back to Rex. “You will admit my years of experience give me a better sense of what is good for you than your current grief-stricken state, don’t you?” he asked. Rex nodded, because it was true, even if it felt like a lie right now. “Then I am telling you: this feeling that your whole life is broken, that you can never go back to the path you were on? It will pass. If you run off to the colonies now, throwing away all the years of education we poured into you, you will wake up six months from now, or a year, and regret it for the rest of your life. You have no right to throw your whole future away over a heartache that most men work through by the time they are your age. I have arranged for you to miss the next term at Oxford. You will spend the next two months resting, no studying, no pressure. You will not go to the colonies.”

Rex had no arguments left to fight his father with. He knew, deep down, that part of him still cared for his family, still hated the thought of hurting his mother, but the ache in his chest was too big to ignore. “I’ll try,” he said at last, quiet. “But I can’t promise it will work.” He left the study without another word.

The rector held Anna back as she tried to run after him. “Now, you be as quiet as a mouse about this,” he told her. “Never mention the colonies, never mention what upset him, not to anyone, ever. It will all blow over soon enough.”

Anna nodded, but she had a little secret, tucked away where no one could see it. She was bitterly disappointed that she would not get to run off to the wilds with Rex, escape all the wide, bulky crinolines that were so fashionable that year—so big, everyone joked, that churches and ballrooms and even carriages would have to be expanded to fit them—escape all the stuffy dinner parties where she had to make small talk with strangers she did not care about. Anna Gascoigne’s small fourteen-year-old frame only ever needed the smallest size of those new giant skirts, and even that felt far too heavy for the quiet, wild little girl who dreamed of nothing more than her and Rex’s half-forgotten play tent, somewhere far away from the ache of unrequited love.
