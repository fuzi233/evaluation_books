A man's courage, much like his talent, tends to flow within a narrow channel. Push him but an inch outside his accustomed bounds, and he crumbles. Martin's bravery was absolute within the limits of his experience. Having weathered countless perils throughout his rugged existence, he faced those familiar threats with Spartan stoicism, bordering on indifference. Yet, he had never been hunted by a bloodhound, nor witnessed that beast's infallible instinct thwarted by human wit. Now, a sense of the supernatural, compounded by sheer novelty, stripped his resolve bare.

After taking a few hesitant steps, Martin leaned heavily against his bow; his vitality and hope drained away. Gerard, viewing the danger as negligible because it remained distant, pressed them toward flight. "What purpose does fleeing serve?" Martin asked, his voice heavy with sorrow. "If we manage to clear the woods, we shall die cheaply. But here, close at hand, I know a place where we might die with honor."

"Alas, good Martin!" cried Gerard. "Despair not so hastily; surely there is a path to escape."

"Oh, Martin!" Margaret interjected, her voice trembling. "What if we were to separate? Gerard's life alone is forfeit. Is there no way to draw the pursuit onto us both and allow him to go free?"

"Girl, you do not understand the nature of the bloodhound," Martin replied grimly. "It does not hunt this man or that; it hunts blood itself. They have already tracked him to where Ghysbrecht fell. From the dead man's blood to the man who spilled it, that cursed beast will lead them, whether Gerard runs through an army or swims the Meuse."

Once more, he rested his weight on his bow, his head sinking low. The hound's rich, melodic bark echoed through the forest—a cry more haunting and tuneful than any ever sounded in Crete, Sparta, or Thessaly. It was strange that beauty could be so terrifying and lethal. The eye of a boa constrictor, while mesmerizing its prey, possesses a loveliness unmatched by any royal crown; it is a ruby glowing with emerald light. Yet, the deer that beholds it loses all movement, trembling until death claims it. Similarly, this sweet, mellow sound seemed to paralyze Martin Wittenhaagen. He stood bewildered, unnerved, and uncertain. Gerard was hardly better; Martin's final words had daunted him. He had struck an old man and shed his blood, and now, via that very blood, the four-legged avenger of vengeance was upon his trail. Was not the finger of God visible in this?

While the men were numbed by dread, the woman's mind raced with frantic activity. Her beloved was in mortal peril.

"Lend me your knife," she commanded Martin.

He handed it to her. "But it will be of little use in your hands," he warned.

Margaret performed a subtle act of desperation. Stepping behind Gerard, she secretly drew the blade across her forearm, allowing it to bleed freely. Stooping low, she smeared her stockings and shoes with the flowing crimson, doing so with such dexterity that neither Gerard nor Martin noticed. As the blood continued to trickle, she applied more, ensuring the trail was altered. Then, seizing the soldier's arm, she spoke urgently.

"Come, be a man!" she urged. "Let this end. Lead us to some dense thicket where numbers will not aid our enemies."

"I am going," Martin grumbled sullenly. "Hurrying serves no purpose; we cannot outrun the hound, and the cover is nearby." Turning left, he led the way, moving as one walks toward execution.

Soon, he guided them to a thick hazel copse, similar to the one that had aided their escape earlier that day.

"There," he declared. "It is only a furlong wide, but it will suffice."

"What shall we do?" Gerard asked.

"Pass through this, and wait on the other side. As they emerge straggling, shoot three, strike two on the head, and the rest will finish us off."

"Is that all you can devise?" Gerard challenged.

"That is all."

"Then, Martin Wittenhaagen, I shall take the lead, for you have lost your wits. Come, can you obey a younger man than myself?"

"Oh, yes, Martin," Margaret cried out. "Do not contradict Gerard! He is wiser than his years."

Martin offered a reluctant agreement. "Do as I see you do," Gerard instructed. Drawing his massive knife, he chopped hazel shoots close to the ground at every step, twisting them upright behind him to form a living wall among the standing trunks. Martin mimicked the action, though with a dogged, hopeless demeanor.

As they painfully traversed most of the copse, the bloodhound's deep bay grew louder, losing its musical quality and becoming stern and fierce. Margaret trembled visibly. Martin dropped to his stomach to listen.

"I hear horses' hooves," he whispered.

"No," Gerard corrected. "I suspect it is a mule. That cursed Ghysbrecht is still alive; no one else would pursue me so relentlessly."

"Never strike your enemy unless you intend to kill him," Martin said gloomily.

"I shall hit harder this time, if Heaven grants me the chance," Gerard vowed.

Finally, they emerged from the copse into an open woodland. The trees were large but widely spaced, offering no refuge. Now, mingling with the hound's bay were dozens of voices shouting and whistling.

"The entire village has come out after us," Martin stated.

"I do not care," Gerard replied calmly. "Listen, Martin. I have smoothed the track for the dog but made it rough for the men, allowing us to engage them separately. The hound will gain on the humans. As soon as it emerges from the copse, we must kill it."

"The hound? There are several of them."

"I hear only one."

"Ay! Only one barks; the others run silent. But if the leader loses the scent, another will take up the cry. There will be at least two dogs, or devils in dog-skins."

"Then we must kill two instead of one. The moment they fall, we retreat immediately back into the copse."

"That is a fine idea, Gerard," Martin admitted, finding renewed courage.

"Hush! The men are entering the wood."

Gerard issued his orders in a whisper. "Stand with your bow by the side of the copse—right there, in the ditch. I will move only a few yards to that oak tree and hide behind it. The dogs will follow me; shoot as many as you can as they exit. I will dispatch the rest as they circle around the tree."

Martin's eyes flashed with determination. They assumed their positions. The shouts and whistles drew closer, and soon the rustling of the undergrowth was audible. Every now and then, the bloodhound let out a solitary, piercing bay. It was terrible—the branches brushing nearer, the inevitable struggle for life and death approaching minute by minute, the death knell ringing out.

A trembling hand touched Gerard's shoulder, causing him to start violently, fully strung for battle.

"Martin says if we are forced to part, make for that high ash tree we entered by," the voice whispered.

"Yes! Yes! Yes! But go back, for heaven's sake! Do not come here, exposed in the open!"

She turned to flee toward Martin, but before she could reach him, a huge dog burst from the copse, standing erect for a brief moment. Margaret cowered in terror, yet the beast ignored her entirely. Scent was to him what sight was to mankind. He lowered his nose for an instant, then, with a horrific yell, launched himself straight at Gerard's hiding spot, rolling head-over-heels dead as a stone, literally impaled by an arrow from the bow twanging beside the copse in Martin's hand.

At that exact moment, a second hound emerged, sniffing its fallen comrade. Gerard lunged at it, but before he could swing his cudgel, a streak of white lightning seemed to strike the animal. It collapsed into the dust, mortally wounded but not dead, howling pitifully. Gerard lacked the time to finish it; the copse rustled too dangerously close, seeming to breathe. Gesturing wildly for Martin to retreat, Gerard sprinted a few yards to the right and crept cautiously into the thick brush just as three men burst forth. These had gained considerable ground on their companions; the rest followed at varying intervals.

Gerard crawled back almost on all fours. Instinct taught Martin and Margaret to mimic his movements along their line of retreat. Within mere yards, pursuers and pursued passed one another on opposite paths. A loud cry signaled the discovery of the dead and wounded hounds. This was followed by a babble of voices, swelling as fresh hunters arrived.

Typically, hunters waste precious time on a surprise, while the hunted maximize their advantage.

"I hear no more hounds," Martin whispered to Margaret, regaining his composure.

"It was your turn to tremble and despair," she murmured. "Oh, why did we leave Gerard? They will kill my Gerard, and I will not be near him."

"Nay, nay! The responsibility lies not on their shoulders. Did you bid him meet us at the ash tree?"
