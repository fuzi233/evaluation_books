“I have found that holy place of rest Still changeless.”

When John Thornton stepped out his front door that morning, the sting of his rejected confession had left him so dazed he could barely see. The blow Margaret Hale had dealt him felt more solid than if a brawny fishwife had knocked him sideways with a fist, and the throbbing in his temples, the shooting, intermittent pulse under his skin, was a physical ache so heavy he could barely draw a full breath. The roar of Milton’s traffic, the harsh glint of gaslight off damp cobblestones, the endless rumble of mill carts trundling past grated on his raw nerves like a file. He called himself a fool over and over for suffering so sharply, but for the life of him he could not untangle the root of the pain, nor tell if it was half bad enough to warrant the havoc it was wreaking on his whole body.

It would have been a relief, he thought wildly, to sink down on a doorstep next to some screaming, tear-soaked toddler in the throes of a rage over a stolen toy, and cry right alongside him. The moment he framed the words *I hate Margaret* in his head, a searing, sharp flash of love cut through his thunderous, dull fog of feeling like a streak of lightning, bright and unshakable. He clung to his torment, even, for the certainty it gave him: no matter how she despised him, looked down on him, treated him with that cool, proud indifference he had seen on her face that morning, he would not change. She could not make him stop. He loved her, and he would go on loving her, in defiance of her will, in defiance of this throbbing, miserable pain in his bones.

He paused still on the pavement to firm that resolution into something unbreakable. An omnibus bound for the countryside rumbled past, and the conductor, mistaking his motion for a hail, pulled up to the curb. Too worn out to stop and explain, Thornton clambered aboard, and let the cart carry him away: past the endless terraces of smoke-stained Milton townhouses, past the scattered suburban villas with their tiny, neat clipped gardens, until the streets fell away to wild, flowering hedgerows, and before he knew it, they had pulled into a small quiet market town far from the clatter of the mills.

All the other passengers got off, and Thornton followed, striding fast across the open fields beyond the town, the sharp, brisk movement clearing the fog from his mind at last. He could see every foolish choice he had made now, the ridiculous figure he must have cut when he blundered into declaring his love, after years of telling himself that blurting out a proposal in the heat of the moment was the stupidest thing a man could ever do. Had he been bewitched by those soft, luminous eyes, that half-open, sighing mouth that had rested so close to his shoulder the day before, when she had thrown herself between him and the rage of the rioters? He could not shake the memory of her arms around him, warm and steady, even for a second. He replayed every single encounter he had ever had with her, trying to etch her out of his mind for good, only to find he could not settle on which of her moods he admired most: the sharp, brave fire she showed when standing up for the mill workers, the soft shyness that came over her when they spoke of her home in the south, the haughty, regal pride that flashed that morning when he dared to assume she cared for him just because she had shared his danger.

His sixpenny omnibus ride left him no wiser in the end, only more certain: there was no one else in the world like Margaret Hale. She did not love him, and she never would, but neither she nor anyone else would ever make him stop loving her. By late afternoon, he was back at the Milton market square, climbing the return omnibus that dropped him steps away from his warehouse.

The familiar sights of his work pulled him back to his usual routines, and he remembered the pile of tasks waiting for him: meetings with his fellow magistrates to review the aftermath of the riot, half-finished arrangements to house his newly arrived Irish migrant workers safely, far away from the discontented striking Milton hands who would surely turn on them for crossing the picket line. Last of all, he would have to go home and face his mother.

Mrs. Thornton had sat in the dining room all day, every muscle tensed, waiting for the sound of her son’s footstep to bring news that Miss Hale had accepted him. At every small, unexpected noise in the house, she would jerk upright, catch up her half-finished needlework, and ply her thread fast even as her spectacles misted over and her hands shook. A dozen times the door swung open, only to admit a maid or the baker’s boy on some trivial errand, and her rigid, frost-locked face would soften back into a slack, rare look of dejection, almost unrecognizable on a woman so stern and unyielding. She had forced her mind away from the dark, dreary visions of the new life her son’s marriage would bring her, back to the familiar grooves of household business: she had stacks and stacks of linen chests hauled out, full of tablecloths and napkins, to count over in preparation for the new couple’s household. She sorted through the damask, separating the pieces marked G. H. T. – stitched for her own name, Hannah, and her late husband George – from the newer pieces bought with her son’s money, marked only with his initials. Some of the old damask was fine Dutch weave, the kind no weaver made anymore, the same pieces she had been so proud of when she was first a bride. She stood staring at them for a long time, then knit her brows, pinched her lips tight, and unpicked the G and H from the old marking thread, until only the T was left. She went as far as to reach for her spool of turkey-red thread to stitch his new wife’s initials in their place, only to find she had used up every last scrap of it, and could not bear to send the maid out for more. She sat staring at empty air then, all her visions circling around her son, her pride, her whole life’s work, convinced he was lingering somewhere with Margaret Hale, that the new girl was already stealing the place she had held in his heart since he was a small boy. A sharp, terrible pang of useless jealousy shot through her, so strong she could not tell if it was a pain in her bones or in her soul, and forced her to sit down gasping. A moment later she was standing straight again, her face set in the grim, unyielding smile she had prepared to greet him with, ready to hide every last trace of her grief at his marriage, so he would never guess how much it cost her to see him give his heart away. She barely thought of Margaret Hale as a person, at all, only as John’s wife: a girl chosen by him would be raised above every other woman in the world, and Margaret was not even that bad, with her sharp wit, her taste and spirit. It was only her southern upbringing that left her so sadly prejudiced and ignorant, Mrs. Thornton told herself, and she even snapped at her own dull, soft daughter Fanny mid-afternoon for no reason, before picking up Henry’s Commentaries to punish herself, forcing her attention onto the dry legal text instead of staring at the pile of wedding linen any longer.

At last, his step. She heard it even before she had finished the sentence she was pretending to read. He paused by the hat stand in the hall, then came to the dining room door, and stopped just on the other side. She kept her head bowed over her book, refusing to look up, until he stood right at her elbow, waiting for her to finish her page. She dragged her eyes up to his face.

“Well, John?”

He knew exactly what that quiet line meant. He had steeled himself to keep his face neutral, and for a wild second he wanted to make a bitter joke to ease the blow, but his mother deserved more than that. He circled around behind her so she could not see the raw pain in his eyes, bent down, and kissed her cold, stony gray cheek, murmuring, “No one loves me – no one cares for me, but you, mother.”

He turned away and leaned his head against the mantelpiece, big, manly tears forcing their way out despite every effort he made to hold them back. Mrs. Thornton stood up, and tottered. For the first time in her whole life, that iron-strong woman swayed on her feet. She set her hands on his shoulders – she was tall enough to look him dead in the eye – and forced him to meet her gaze.

“Mother’s love is given by God, John. It holds fast for ever and ever. A girl’s love is a puff of smoke – shifts with every wind. She turned you down, my own lad, didn’t she?” Her teeth were bared, sharp as a dog’s, when she spoke. He shook his head.

“I’m not fit for her, mother. I knew I wasn’t.”

She ground her words out between clenched teeth, too quiet for him to make out, but the fury in her eyes spoke of a curse as bitter as any ever uttered. Even so, her heart leapt light inside her: he was hers again.

“Mother!” he said, hurrying to speak, his voice raw and unsteady. “I won’t hear a word against her. Spare me, please. My heart’s sore, and I’m weak right now – I still love her. More than ever.”

“Then I hate her,” Mrs. Thornton said, low and fierce. “I tried not to hate her, when she stood between you and me. I told myself she’d make you happy, and I’d give every drop of my heart’s blood to see that happen. But now? I hate her for the misery she’s put you in. I’m the one that bore you. Your pain is my pain, and if you won’t hate her, I will.”

“Then you make me love her more,” he said. “You’re treating her unfairly, and I have to even the balance. But what’s the use of talking of love and hate? She doesn’t care for me. That’s enough, more than enough. Let’s never name the subject again. It’s the only thing you can do for me now. Let’s never speak her name.”

“With all my heart,” she said. “I only wish the girl and every last one of her people were swept back to the south they came from.”

He stood staring into the fire for a long minute after that. Her dry, usually untearful eyes filled with rare tears as she watched him, but when he spoke next, her face was as grim and calm as it had ever been.

“Warrants are out for three men, for conspiracy, mother. The riot yesterday all but broke the strike.”

Margaret Hale’s name never passed their lips again that day. They fell back into their old, familiar pattern of speech, talking only of facts, never of opinions, and certainly never of their raw, tangled feelings. A stranger passing through might have thought there was no colder, more frigid pair of near relations in all of Milton, and never guessed at the storm of grief and loyalty that hung thick between them, unspoken.
