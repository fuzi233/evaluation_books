The hush came upon the room as if someone had laid a cloth over a simmering pot: not to smother it, but to keep the heat steady. After a few minutes that unwonted stillness penetrated Mordecai’s consciousness; he lifted his eyes to Deronda, not startled, but soothed, with the look of a man whose pain has at last been met by gentleness. Deronda, reading that look, rose and moved his chair nearer—so near that no voice need be raised, and every syllable could fall like a warm crumb into a listening palm. Mordecai felt the motion as an invalid feels a pillow set right: a small mercy that changes the whole body’s burden.

He began to speak low, not as one performing for an audience, but as if he were tasting his own thoughts aloud—slowly, with care, as if the words were costly spices not to be scattered.

“In the doctrine of the Cabbala,” he said, “souls are born again and again in new bodies till they are perfected and purified. And a soul liberated from a worn-out body may join the fellow-soul that needs it, that they may be perfected together, and their earthly work accomplished. Then they depart from the mortal region, and leave place for new souls to be born out of the store in the eternal bosom. It is the lingering imperfection of the souls already born into the mortal region that hinders the birth of new souls and the preparation of the Messianic time: thus the mind has given shape to what is hidden—as the shadow of what is known—and has spoken truth, though it were only in parable. When my long-wandering soul is liberated from this weary body, it will join yours, and its work will be perfected.”

He paused, and the pause had the quality of an offered cup—an appeal that asked to be taken up and answered. Deronda felt the demand of truth and the peril of too much truth. He chose the words that would not deceive, yet would not break what was burning so delicately.

“Everything I can in conscience do to make your life effective,” he said, “I will do.”

“I know it,” Mordecai answered, with a quiet certainty that required no further pledge. “I heard it. You see it all—you are by my side on the mount of vision, and behold the paths of fulfilment which others deny.”

He fell silent again; the room held only the faint sounds of breathing, and beyond that, the far-off murmur of the street—a muffled tide of wheels and footsteps, the modern world’s ceaseless grind that had no reverence for frail lungs or sacred confidences. Deronda, who had lately noticed how noise could thin the mind and fret the nerves as surely as hunger, felt that the soft quiet between them was a kind of shelter, a little pocket of clean air.

Then Mordecai went on, and his tone changed—more inward, as if memory were drawing him back by a strong thread.

“You will take up my life where it was broken. I feel myself back in that day when my life was broken. The bright morning sun was on the quay—it was at Trieste—the garments of men from all nations shone like jewels—the boats were pushing off—the Greek vessel that would land us at Beyrout was to start in an hour. I was going with a merchant as his clerk and companion. I said, I shall behold the lands and people of the East, and I shall speak with a fuller vision.”

As he spoke, the air seemed to fill with that southern brightness, as if sunlight had been stored in the syllables and now released. Deronda saw the quay, smelled the salt and tar and wet rope; heard the slap of water against hulls, the calls in many tongues, each dialect carrying its own cadence—some sharp as cracked pepper, some flowing as oil. And in that imagined soundscape there was already what Mordecai would mourn later: how voices, once rooted in place and kinship, could be drowned by the harsh, indifferent roar of commerce.

“I breathed then as you do,” Mordecai said, “without labor; I had the light step and the endurance of youth. I could fast, I could sleep on the hard ground. I had wedded poverty, and I loved my bride—for poverty to me was freedom. My heart exulted as if it had been the heart of Moses ben Maimon, strong with the strength of three score years, and knowing the work that was to fill them. It was the first time I had been south; the soul within me felt its former sun; and standing on the quay, where the ground I stood on seemed to send forth light, and the shadows had an azure glory as of spirits become visible, I felt myself in the flood of a glorious life, wherein my own small year-counted existence seemed to melt, so that I knew it not; and a great sob arose within me as at the rush of waters that were too strong a bliss.”

He stopped as if he had heard again a particular sound rising above the rest—a voice cutting clean through his vision.

“So I stood there awaiting my companion; and I saw him not till he said: ‘Ezra, I have been to the post and there is your letter.’”

“Ezra!” Deronda cried, unable to hold back the exclamation, as if the name had struck him like a bell.

“Ezra,” Mordecai repeated, affirmatively, and sank deeper into the remembered moment, with no sign that Deronda’s outburst had disturbed him. “I was expecting a letter; for I wrote continually to my mother. And that sound of my name was like the touch of a wand that recalled me to the body wherefrom I had been released, as it were, to mingle with the ocean of human existence, free from the pressure of individual bondage. I opened the letter; and the name came again as a cry that would have disturbed me in the bosom of heaven, and made me yearn to reach where that sorrow was—‘Ezra, my son!’”

Deronda’s mind hung poised, almost breathless. A strange possibility, long hovering at the edge of his thoughts, came nearer and took shape. Mordecai’s eyes were cast down; his face seemed carved by that far-off grief. After a little while he went on, and the words came like drops—slow, heavy, each falling with its own weight.

“She was a mother of whom it might have come—yea, might have come to be said, ‘Her children arise up and call her blessed.’ In her I understood the meaning of that Master who, perceiving the footsteps of his mother, rose up and said, ‘The Majesty of the Eternal cometh near!’ And that letter was her cry from the depths of anguish and desolation—the cry of a mother robbed of her little ones. I was her eldest. Death had taken four babes one after the other. Then came, late, my little sister, who was, more than all the rest, the desire of my mother’s eyes; and the letter was a piercing cry to me—‘Ezra, my son, I am robbed of her. He has taken her away and left disgrace behind. They will never come again.’”

With the last words Mordecai lifted his eyes suddenly, and laid his hand on Deronda’s arm. His touch was light, yet it held the whole urgency of his life.

“Mine was the lot of Israel,” he said. “For the sin of the father my soul must go into exile. For the sin of the father the work was broken, and the day of fulfilment delayed. She who bore me was desolate, disgraced, destitute. I turned back. On the instant I turned—her spirit and the spirit of her fathers, who had worthy Jewish hearts, moved within me, and drew me. God, in whom dwells the universe, was within me as the strength of obedience. I turned and traveled with hardship—to save the scant money which she would need. I left the sunshine, and traveled into freezing cold. In the last stage I spent a night in exposure to cold and snow. And that was the beginning of this slow death.”

He let his eyes wander again and withdrew his hand. Deronda felt questions crowding to his lips—questions that seemed to contain Mirah’s very face and voice—but he forced them down. To press him now would be like shaking a vessel whose contents were already on the point of spilling; and Deronda dreaded his own speech as something too momentous, too capable of wounding by clumsiness. Mordecai’s confidence must come of itself, as naturally as a simmer rises, not by any poking of the fire.

“But I worked,” Mordecai continued. “We were destitute—every thing had been seized. And she was ill: the clutch of anguish was too strong for her, and wrought with some lurking disease. At times she could not stand for the beating of her heart, and the images in her brain became as chambers of terror, where she beheld my sister reared in evil. In the dead of night I heard her crying for her child. Then I rose, and we stretched forth our arms together and prayed. We poured forth our souls in desire that Mirah might be delivered from evil.”

“Mirah?” Deronda repeated, not trusting his own hearing—fearing that imagination had seasoned the sound to its own hunger. “Did you say Mirah?”

“That was my little sister’s name,” Mordecai said. “After we had prayed for her, my mother would rest awhile. It lasted hardly four years, and in the minute before she died, we were praying the same prayer—I aloud, she silently. Her soul went out upon its wings.”

The name—Mirah—stood between them like a lamp suddenly lit. Deronda’s heart beat with a force that made his lips go pale. Now the strange tenderness in his bond with this man took on a new solemnity: this was not only the chosen companion of his mind, but Mirah’s brother—blood of her blood, keeper of her mother’s grief, the one living link to a past she had spoken of as a blank shore.

“Have you never since heard of your sister?” Deronda asked, as quietly as he could manage.

“Never. Never have I heard whether she was delivered according to our prayer. I know not, I know not. Who shall say where the pathways lie? The poisonous will of the wicked is strong. It poisoned my life—it is slowly stifling this breath. Death delivered my mother, and I felt it a blessedness that I was alone in the winters of suffering. But what are the winters now?—they are far off—”

He laid his hand again on Deronda’s arm and looked at him with that joy which sometimes comes upon the fevered, piercing the watcher’s heart with sadness—the flare of a dying fire that dazzles and makes one fear the dark that will follow.

“There is nothing to wail in the withering of my body,” he said. “The work will be the better done. Once I said the work of this beginning was mine, I am born to do it. Well, I shall do it. I shall live in you. I shall live in you.”

His grasp grew convulsive. Deronda, agitated as he had never been—now certain beyond all doubt that this suffering prophet was indeed Mirah’s brother—shrank from any revelation. To tell him now, in this exalted state, might strike him like sudden music too loud for his weakened frame; it might push that fragile spirit into a dangerous ecstasy, and shorten the very time Mirah might still have with him. And yet to keep silence tore at Deronda with its own cruelty: the truth lay ready like bread waiting to be broken and shared, but he dared not offer it while the recipient could scarcely swallow.

His dominant impulse was the same as before—human, immediate, careful. He laid his firm, gentle hand upon the hand that clutched him. Mordecai’s fingers, as if they had a separate will and then were coaxed back into peace, relaxed; the palm turned upward beneath Deronda’s. In that meeting of hands there was more said than either could speak aloud: a promise of steadiness, a vow against abandonment.

Mordecai recovered a sense of the room, of the air that was not Trieste’s sunlit breath but London’s close mixture, and he said, “Let us go now. I cannot talk any longer.”

They went out; at Cohen’s door they parted without further speech—only another pressure of hands, brief and quiet. The street’s noises rushed back upon Deronda as if he had opened a door onto machinery: wheels rasping, voices pitched sharp, some tinny tune from a distant public-house, and beneath it all the steady, indifferent hum that made a man feel how hard it was for any solitary ardor to be heard. He carried away a weight that was half joy, half anxiety.

The joy: that Mirah’s brother, found at last, was not merely worthy of her love, but greater than Deronda had imagined—a nature refined by suffering, fierce with purpose, tender with conscience. Yet even that joy was solemn; the reunion he now foresaw had in it the shadow of farewell. Brother and sister would meet only to begin the last parting—like those kisses where greeting and leave-taking are so entangled that the sweetness hurts.

The anxiety: how to bring them together without a harsh jar; how to prepare both sides, and also how to set Mordecai in a place that would not fight against his breathing and exhaust him with petty indignities. Deronda’s mind, practical in its tenderness, refused to imagine recognition as a bare scene of embraces and cries. Real lives, moving through dim streets and shabby rooms, were always hemmed in by details—by doorways and landladies, by the smell of damp plaster, by the clatter that comes through thin walls. And Deronda, with no snobbery in him—no belief that duty requires fine linen—still felt it right to save Mirah’s first meeting with her brother from every outward discord he could prevent. He knew too well what small abrasions can do to the spirit: how a coarse laugh from a neighbor, or the scraping of boots on a stair, or the too-near din of a club-room, can make a sacred moment feel exposed and cheapened, as if someone had hawked it in the street like a song.

His own sense of release from the dreaded relationship of the other Cohens—kind enough, and yet, to his fastidious pity for Mirah, so certain to bring some unmeant coarseness into delicate grief—made him resolve, if possible, to keep them in the background until Mirah could render gratitude without being forced to endure the whole jangle of their household ways. On every account he wished to give Mordecai surroundings better suited to his frail bodily condition, and less hindering to intimate intercourse—especially in view of what was now almost inevitable: that Mirah would take up her abode with her brother, and tend him through the precious remnant of his life.

In the heroic dramas, recognitions are not encumbered with such things; the stage is cleared, the air is still, and the great names ring out without interruption. Deronda felt toward Mordecai and Mirah with as reverential an interest as if they were the offspring of Agamemnon; but he was caring for destinies not yet among constellations, only moving through London’s soot and noise, and his task presented itself as difficult and delicate—above all in persuading Mordecai to change his abode and habits.

About Mirah’s resolve he had no doubt. There would be a complete union of feeling toward the dead mother; Mirah would understand her brother’s greatness. Yes—greatness: that was the word Deronda deliberately chose now, even in defiance of the colder, more negative spirit that sometimes whispered within him. This man—consumptive Jewish workman in threadbare clothing, lodged by charity, pouring forth his visions to hearers who took them as idly as townsfolk take the ethereal chimes that drift above their market-places—had the chief elements of greatness: a mind consciously, energetically moving with the larger march of human destinies, and yet full of conscience and tender heart for the near footsteps that needed a leaning-place; capable of choosing a life’s task with far-off issues, and yet capable of the unapplauded heroism that turns off the road of achievement at the call of the nearer duty, whose effect lies within the beatings of hearts close to us, as the hunger of a young bird lies close against its parent’s breast.

That night Deronda was stirred with a new sense of guardianship. He had been peculiarly wrought on by what he had seen at the club—the friendly indifference Mordecai must have faced again and again, as if the world’s loudness had no ear for a voice that would not sell itself in easy phrases. Deronda’s own habit of reserve—his dislike of appearing exceptional, his dread of insisting where he might fail—seemed contemptible to him now, when he felt, for the first time as a complete reality, the lives that burn themselves out in solitary enthusiasm: martyrs of obscure circumstance, exiled in the rarity of their own minds, whose deliverances are, in most ears, no more than a long passionate soliloquy—unless, perhaps at the last, when they are nearing invisible shores, some sign of recognition penetrates the cloud of loneliness; or perhaps it is with them as with the dying Copernicus, made to touch the first printed copy of his book when the sense of touch was gone, seeing it only dimly through the deepening dusk.

Deronda had been brought near to one of those spiritual exiles, and it was in his nature to feel such a bond as a chain—strong, and willingly worn. Without sharing Mordecai’s Jewish consciousness, he felt his imagination move without repugnance in the direction of Mordecai’s desire; the lines of their emotional theory touched. The yearning in Deronda—grown stronger from long denial and the grievance of having no avowed ties—answered to Mordecai’s hunger for a chosen inheritor. His feeling was ready for difficult obedience.

So he set about his new task ungrudgingly; and again his thoughts turned to Mrs. Meyrick as his chief helper. To her first he must make known the discovery of Mirah’s brother, and with her consult on the preliminaries of bringing the lost together. Happily, the best quarter for a consumptive patient did not lie too far from the small house at Chelsea; and the first office Deronda had to perform for this Hebrew prophet who claimed him as a spiritual inheritor was to get him a healthier lodging.

Such is the irony of earthly mixtures, that the heroes do not always have carpets and teacups of their own, and may be seen through an open window by a mackerel-vender who, with cheerful hopefulness, invites them to pay three hundred per cent in the form of fourpence. Deronda’s mind, however, was already busy with the homeliest of refinements: the thought of a furnished lodging made gentler by dismantling his own chambers—sending his best old books in vellum, his easiest chair, and the bas-reliefs of Milton and Dante, as one might send bread, broth, and a quiet lamp into a room where a sick man must husband his breath.

But was not Mirah to be there? What furniture can give such finish as the presence of a tender woman? What arrangement of objects can do as much as the mere modulation of her voice, falling like soft water on a fevered ear? Here was one good, at least, Deronda thought—a good born out of Mordecai’s strange election of him—that would come to Mordecai: he had recovered a perfect sister, whose affection was already waiting, like a warmed meal kept back from the table until the one who most needs it can be brought home.
