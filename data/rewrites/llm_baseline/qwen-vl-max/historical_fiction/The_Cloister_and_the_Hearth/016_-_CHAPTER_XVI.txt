Martin Wittenhaagen stood at the bottom of the stairs, bow drawn to the ear, knife ready in his belt, his face tense with alertness. He was startled when the men returned without Gerard—his eyes widened, his breath caught. He lowered his weapon slowly, staring at them in stunned silence.

They, in turn, were equally taken aback by the sight of him—armed and poised as if expecting an attack. “Why, mates,” one muttered, “was the old man aiming at us?”

“Nonsense,” Martin replied, regaining his usual stoicism. “I was merely testing my new string. There—let me unstring it, if you like.”

“Hmph,” Dierich grumbled, eyeing him warily. “There’s more going on under that calm face than I can fathom. Light a fire, and let us dry our clothes before we go any further.”

A roaring blaze soon crackled in the hearth. The men gathered close, their damp garments steaming in the warmth, their long hair curling slightly from the heat. Then—suddenly—the sound of piercing shrieks echoed from Margaret’s chamber.

Every man jumped to his feet. One snatched up the candle and bolted up the stairs to the bedrooms. Martin rose swiftly, confusion flashing across his features. Fear gripped him—not just for Margaret, but for what this man might discover. He lunged forward, trying to block the way.

Dierich reacted instantly, wrapping his arms around Martin from behind. “Hold him!” he shouted.

The others surged upon Martin. A fierce struggle erupted—limbs tangled, bodies rolling across the floor, Martin at the center, resisting with all his strength. After minutes of desperate fighting, they overpowered him. With a rope meant for Gerard, they bound him tightly, hands and feet secured.

Martin groaned deeply, his chest heaving. He saw too late that the man had reached Margaret’s room during the fight—and now he was helpless.

“Ah, grind your teeth, old rogue,” Dierich panted, wiping sweat from his brow. “You won’t be using them again.”

“It’s my belief,” another said, breathing hard, “that none of us were safe so long as that old fellow could move.”

“And I’ll wager,” added a third, “that Gerard isn’t far behind.”

“No such luck,” Dierich snapped. “But—listen! Jorian Ketel’s been gone too long. Someone ought to check on him.”

A rough laugh broke out among them, barely fading when heavy footsteps pounded overhead.

“Oh, here he comes at last!” someone called. “Well, Jorian—what’s happening up there?”
