Gerard continued along the road until he encountered a small gathering. Two men in somber attire leaned casually against a horse, chatting idly. The rider, clad in a silk doublet and a vibrant green jerkin and hose of English make—glossy as polished leather—lay prone in the afternoon sun, resembling a massive lizard. His velvet cloak, a blazing yellow, was meticulously draped over the horse's flanks.

"Is anything wrong?" Gerard asked.

"Not that I know of," one of the servants replied.

"But your master lies like a corpse. Do you not feel shame letting him grovel on the ground?"

"Nonsense," the servant retorted. "The bare earth is the finest remedy for his condition. If you remain sober in bed, you wake with a splitting headache; but rising from the hard ground makes you soar like a lark in spring. Does Ulric agree?"

"He speaks truly, young sir," Ulric answered earnestly.

"What, is the gentleman intoxicated?"

The servants erupted in a rough laugh at the simplicity of Gerard's query. Suddenly, Ulric halted, scanning Gerard from head to toe before speaking with grave seriousness. "Who are you, and where born, that you do not know the Count is perpetually drunk at this hour?" Gerard immediately found himself under suspicion.

"I am a stranger," Gerard explained, "yet a truthful man who loves learning; therefore, I ask questions, not out of curiosity."

"If you are indeed a true man," Ulric said shrewdly, "then offer us a trinket for the knowledge we have shared."

Gerard looked confused but maintained a pleasant demeanor. "A trinket you shall receive, whatever my slender purse can spare, provided you explain why you removed his cloak from the man and placed it upon the beast."

Inspired by the prospect of payment, the two men instantly offered Gerard two theories. First, if the Count regained consciousness—which, being a seasoned drinker, he often did quickly—and found his horse sweating in the cold while his cloak lay idle nearby, he would undoubtedly curse and perhaps strike out. Second, and more pretentiously, a horse is a simpleton that drinks only water; it must be coaxed and kept warm externally. A master, however, is always filled with good beer, possessing an internal heat that warms him to the skin, rendering a cloak a mere symbol of vain ostentation.

Each speaker fell in love with their own theory. Both had clearly caught a bit of the madness that had infected their master's mind. Their voices soon rose so high that the green-faced sot began to growl instead of snore. Ignoring the disturbance, they continued their debate until the situation escalated.

Hans, holding the bridle with his right hand, delivered a solid cuff to Ulric with his left. Ulric returned the gesture with equal enthusiasm, his right hand free. They exchanged blows over the horse's mane, pounding one another and jostling the poor animal until it backed away and stepped heavily onto the green nobleman. Like a toad stung by a magical spear, the Count jumped up screaming, clutching his injured hand with one grip and reaching for his hilt with the other. Terrified, the servants released the reins. The horse galloped off whinnying, pursued by the men shouting in fear, while the green nobleman chased them, hurling curses, brandishing his naked sword, and bouncing wildly from hedge to hedge down the narrow lane.

Amidst this chaos, Gerard turned his back on everyone and calmly headed south. He was relieved to have saved the four tin farthings he had prepared as payment, though his heart remained too heavy to smile at their drunken folly.

The sun was nearing its setting, and Gerard, who had hoped in vain to find an inn, felt increasingly uneasy. Black clouds began to gather overhead. He quickened his pace, almost running. It was futile; torrential rain descended, soaking the bewildered traveler and seemingly extinguishing the fading sunlight.

Gerard trudged onward, dark, wet, and lost in an unfamiliar region. "Fool!" he muttered. "To leave Margaret."

Darkness thickened around him as he entered a vast forest. Massive branches blocked the narrow path, forcing the benighted stranger to navigate what felt like an endless, ink-black cave with a rugged floor. He stumbled repeatedly. Onward he went, with shivering limbs, an empty stomach, and a failing heart, until wolves rose from their dens and bayed around the woods.

His hair bristled, but he gripped his cudgel, preparing to defend his life. There was no wind, yet his agitated ears heard light footsteps patter over the fresh leaves and heard low branches rustle as creatures slipped swiftly past.

Suddenly, amidst the sea of darkness, a great fiery star appeared close to the ground. He hailed it as if it were his patron saint. "CANDLE! A CANDLE!" he shouted, attempting to run toward it. But the dark, treacherous path hindered him. The light proved farther away than expected. Finally, in the very heart of the forest, he spotted a house with lit candles and loud voices inside. He looked upward for a signboard, finding none. "Not an inn after all!" he sighed sadly. Yet, thinking, "What Christian would turn a dog out into this wood tonight?", he approached the door leading to the voices.

He opened it slowly and peered in timidly. Then, as if slapped, he withdrew abruptly, recoiling into the rain and darkness.

He had glimpsed a large, low room dominated by a huge round stove or clay oven reaching to the ceiling. Wet clothes hung to dry around it—some on lines, others more simply on rustic racks. These garments, soaked with the day's moisture and stained by travel, combined with the dampness of the season, emitted rank vapors and indescribable odors in steaming clouds.

In one corner sat a traveling family, large in number, emitting a peculiar, sickly smell of neglected children. Garlic permeated the air. With windows shut and the central furnace radiating intense heat, the breath of at least forty persons filled the space.

They had just finished supper.

Like many artists, Gerard possessed sensitive senses, and the powerful effluvia struck him with dismay. However, the relentless rain lashed him outside, while the light and fire tempted him inward.

Unable to push through the palpable perfumes all at once, he returned to the doorway repeatedly, like a singed moth. Eventually, he discerned that the various smells did not fully mix; no demon stirred them. The odor of the family dominated two corners; stewed rustic food reigned supreme in the center; and garlic prevailed among the noisy group by the window. He noted, through hasty analysis, that the garlic occupied the smallest sphere of influence, while the scent of the reeking rustic darted farthest—a flavor as if ancient goats or the ancestors of all foxes had been dragged through a river and dried by Nebuchadnezzar.

Creeping into a corner near the door, Gerard found some relief, though the solidity of the main fetors only partially isolated him. The heat and reeking vapors circulated, causing the walls to drip. The inexperienced novice felt something akin to a cold snake winding around his legs, his head turning to a heavy lump of lead. Next, he felt as though he were choking, sweetly slumbering, and dying all at once.

On the verge of swooning, he recovered with a profound sense of disgust and discouragement, resolving to return to Holland at dawn. Gathering a little courage despite his faintness, he asked one of the men smelling strongly of garlic if this was indeed an inn.

"Where do you come from, knowing not 'The Star of the Forest'?" was the reply.

"I am a stranger; in my country, inns always bear signs."

"Droll country yours! What need of a sign for a public house—a place known to every soul?"

Too tired and faint for argument, Gerard changed the subject, asking where he could find the landlord. At this fresh display of ignorance, the native's contempt rose beyond words. He pointed to a middle-aged woman seated opposite the oven and informed his companions of the outlander's presence. Immediately, the loud voices ceased one by one as the news spread. Each eye turned, pivoting silently and zoologically to follow Gerard and his movements.

The landlady sat on a chair slightly elevated above the rest, between two bundles. From the start, a huge pile of feathers and wings, she had been extracting downy plumes from quills, filling a second bundle that littered the floor ankle-deep, contributing to the room's stuffy, malarial atmosphere. Gerard asked if he could have something to eat.

She opened her eyes in astonishment. "Supper ended over an hour ago."

"But I had none, good dame."

"Is that my fault? You were welcome to your share for me."

"But I was benighted, a stranger, and sorely delayed against my will."

"What does that concern me? Everyone knows 'The Star of the Forest' serves supper from six to eight. Come before six, you eat well; come before eight, you eat as Heaven pleases; come after eight, you get a clean bed, a stirrup cup, or a horn of cow's milk at dawn."

Gerard looked blank. "May I go to bed, then, dame?" he asked sulkily. "For it is ill sitting up wet and fasting, and the saying goes, 'He sups who sleeps.'"
