A woman carries her own burdens, distinct and heavy as any man's, yet the male pen, confined by its own narrow vision, rarely dares to map the labyrinth of female grief. We sketch the outline; it is the intelligence of the female reader who must breathe life into these cold contours, filling the silence with the truth of experience. Thus, I have merely indicated, rather than exhaustively chronicled, the trials Margaret Brandt endured until that pivotal dawn when she stepped into Eli's house as an adversary, deciphered her lover's letter, and emerged transformed into a friend. Now, the zenith of a woman's torment approached, compounded by the crushing weight of Gerard's absence. She made scant use of Eli's sudden benevolence; for this reserve, she always possessed a plausible pretext, concealing the genuine terror that gripped her heart: two men resided under that roof whose very presence triggered an instinctive shudder of dread. In their visages, she had read the script of wickedness and hatred, detecting the flicker of secret intelligence between them. Consequently, she preferred the sanctuary of her own hearth, where Catherine and her daughter were welcomed daily. Catherine, consumed by the anticipated arrival of new life, embodied the archetype of the female multiplier, dedicated to the propagation and rearing of the multiplied. Once she ceased the relentless expulsion of infants from every corner of the household, she hovered protectively around the remaining young ones, clucking anxiously as if multiplying by proxy itself. It was in this fervent spirit that she implored Eli to remain in Rotterdam while she returned to Tergou. "The poor lass hath not a single soul about her who understands aught of consequence," she argued. "What utility is there in a pair of soldiers? Such cattle ought to be cast out the moment the season turns." Need I recount that this counsel provided Margaret with profound solace? Alas, the poor soul was teetering on the brink of annihilation, her heart torn between impending death and the void left by Gerard. Yet, the scales of fate balanced themselves. Her destitution and her father's utter helplessness—circumstances that had once subjected her to such agonizing struggle—now served as her shield. Adversity's iron fist had forged her immunity against the lethargy that often paralyzes women of wealth, compelling her to stand perpetually vigilant, her hands never idle. She maintained this grim vigilance until the final hour, buoyed solely by Catherine's counsel. Thus, on a radiant evening as the sun dipped below the horizon, she lay weak as water, yet secure; a small face rested beside her, and the celestial realm of maternity opened wide above her. "Why dost thou weep, sweetheart? Suddenly?" "He is not here to witness it." "Ah, well, lass, he shall be here ere the babe is weaned. Meanwhile, God hath dealt with thee as kindly as with any woman born. Consider, too, that it might have been a girl; did not my very own Kate threaten me with one? And here we possess the bonniest boy in Holland, a sturdy lad indeed, for which the saints give thanks." "Ay, mother, I am but a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep. If only Gerard were here to behold it. It is strange; I bore him well enough to endure his absence amidst my sorrow, yet oh, it seems so cruel that he cannot share my joy. Prithee, prithee, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!" She stretched forth her feeble arms. Catherine bustled about, deliberately averting her gaze, unable to suppress the tears that welled at hearing her own absent child invoked with such earnest longing. Soon, turning back, she discovered Margaret regarding her with a singular, piercing expression. "Didst thou hear nothing?" "No, my lamb. What?" "I cried out for Gerard, but now..." "Ay, ay, surely I heard that." "Well, he answered me." "Tush, girl: speak not such nonsense." "Mother, as certain as I lie here with his boy at my side, his voice returned to me, 'Margaret!' So. Yet, methought, it lacked his usual cheerfulness. Perhaps the distance dulls all tones, making even the joyful sound melancholy. Why art thou not happy, sweetheart? And I, so filled with joy this night? Mother, I seem never to have felt a pang of pain or known a care." Her sweet eyes turned, gazing lovingly upon the infant in silent rapture. That very night, Gerard threw himself into the Tiber. And in that same hour, she heard him speak her name, crying aloud within the jaws of death and despair. "Margaret!" Let those who can account for it explain; I cannot.
