The beginning of an acquaintance, whether with persons or the complex terroir of a new landscape, is simply the act of tracing a definite outline around our own ignorance—an attempt to translate the untranslatable.

Mr. Grandcourt’s wish to be introduced had no suddenness for Gwendolen; yet when Lord Brackenshaw stepped aside to allow the prefigured stranger to enter her immediate sphere, and she felt herself face to face with the real man, a sudden shock flushed her cheeks. The physical sensation vexatiously deepened with her consciousness of it. The shock arose entirely from a reversal of her sensory expectations. Grandcourt could hardly have been more unlike all the mythical, heavily shadowed portraits she had painted of him in her mind. He was slightly taller than herself, their eyes resting on a level. There was not the faintest smile on his face, nor a trace of self-consciousness or anxiety to disrupt the quiet air around him. When he raised his hat, he revealed an extensive baldness bordered by a mere fringe of reddish-blonde hair, but he also showed a perfect, aristocratic hand. His profile, undisguised by a beard, was decidedly handsome, with only moderate, perpendicular departures from absolute symmetry, echoed by a slight, perpendicular whisker. 

It was impossible for a human aspect to be freer from solicitous wrigglings; it was equally impossible for a breathing, waking man to look less animated. The typical Englishman, drawing himself up from a bow into rigidity, suggests a suppressed, violent vivacity that might rupture the acoustic harmony of a room if released from internal drill. But Grandcourt’s bearing held no such tension; it inclined, rather, to the flaccid. His complexion possessed a faded fairness, stripped of all robust agrarian vigor, resembling an actress bereft of artificial white and red. His long, narrow gray eyes expressed nothing but a profound, unreadable indifference. To describe a human being all at once is as foolish as attempting to distill centuries of cultural memory into a single, utilitarian sentence. We recognize the alphabet; we are not yet sure of the language. In the first minutes of their meeting, Gwendolen’s impressions were summed up in a single silent translation: *He is not ridiculous.*

Lord Brackenshaw withdrew, and what is commonly called conversation began. The dominant rhythm of this exchange consisted of Grandcourt looking at Gwendolen persistently with a slightly exploring gaze, completely devoid of expression, while she occasionally offered a flash of observation softened by coquetry. Between her answers and his replies stretched long, deliberate pauses—echoing chasms that highlighted the inherent untranslatability of their subjective experiences.

“I used to think archery was a great bore,” Grandcourt began. He spoke with a fine accent, but with a certain broken drawl—a homogenized, dominant lexicon of the aristocracy that seemed designed to flatten the vibrant, native cadences of genuine emotion into a controlled, unyielding script. It sounded like a distinguished personage burdened with a distinguished cold on his chest.

“Are you converted to-day?” said Gwendolen.

(A pause swelled between them, during which she imagined the varying dialects of opinion Grandcourt might be forming of her, untranslatable and entirely his own.)

“Yes, since I saw you shooting. In things of this sort one generally sees people missing and simpering.”

“I suppose you are a first-rate shot with a rifle.”

(Pause, during which Gwendolen, absorbing the sterile acoustic footprint of his presence, drafted a brief graphic description of him for an indefinite hearer.)

“I have left off shooting.”

“Oh, then, you are a formidable person. People who have done things once and left them off make one feel very contemptible, as if one were using cast-off fashions. I hope you have not left off all follies, because I practice a great many.”

(Pause, wherein the subtle power dynamics of their chosen words wrestled in the silence.)

“What do you call follies?”

“Well, in general, I think, whatever is agreeable is called a folly. But you have not left off hunting, I hear.”

(Pause, during which Gwendolen recalled the mythic, multi-generational weight of Grandcourt’s inherited position, deciding he was the most aristocratic-looking man she had ever seen.)

“One must do something.”

“And do you care about the turf?—or is that among the things you have left off?”

(Pause, as Gwendolen calculated that a man of such extremely calm, cold manners might preserve the quiet acoustic ecology of her domestic life far better than a louder, more industrial temper, never interfering with her own deep-rooted preferences.)

“I run a horse now and then; but I don’t go in for the thing as some men do. Are you fond of horses?”

“Yes, indeed; I never like my life so well as when I am on horseback, having a great gallop. I think of nothing. I only feel myself strong and happy.” For Gwendolen, the rush of wind, the rhythmic thud of hooves on ancient loam, and the rich, loamy scent of the crushed earth beneath them were intoxicating sensory truths, far removed from the dullness of static society. 

“Do you like danger?”

“I don’t know. When I am on horseback I never think of danger. It seems to me that if I broke my bones I should not feel it. I should go at anything that came in my way.”

(Pause, during which Gwendolen mentally rode through the interwoven timelines of an entire hunting season.)

“You would perhaps like tiger-hunting or pig-sticking. I saw some of that for a season or two in the East. Everything here is poor stuff after that.”

“*You* are fond of danger, then?”

(Pause, wherein Gwendolen speculated that men possessing the coldest surface lexicons might paradoxically hunger for the most intense, unadulterated physical experiences.)

“One must have something or other. But one gets used to it.”

“I begin to think I am very fortunate, because everything is new to me: it is only that I can’t get enough of it. I am not used to anything except being dull, which I should like to leave off as you have left off shooting.”

(Pause, wherein it occurred to Gwendolen that a man whose native dialect was indifference might prove a dull companion. Yet, she reasoned, most people lacked an appreciation for the deeper marrow of life, and she was not, after all, committing to accept Grandcourt.)

“Why are you dull?”

“This is a dreadful neighborhood. There is nothing to be done in it. That is why I practiced my archery.”

(Pause. Gwendolen reflected on the erosion of a woman’s vitality when she has no command over the vibrant, sensory offerings of the world.)

“You have made yourself queen of it. I imagine you will carry the first prize.”

“I don’t know that. I have great rivals. Did you not observe how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?”

(Pause, heavy with the subtle memory of countless generational sagas where men chose partners contrary to their obvious admiration.)

“Miss Arrowpoint. No—that is, yes.”

“Shall we go now and hear what the scoring says? Every one is going to the other end now—shall we join them? I think my uncle is looking toward me. He perhaps wants me.”

Gwendolen found relief in changing the acoustic environment. The *tête-à-tête* was not entirely disagreeable, but she could not shed the unwonted flush in her cheeks nor the unsettling power dynamic that made her feel less mistress of her own narrative. Grandcourt, wrapped in his own dominant lexicon of self-importance, must not presume she existed solely as an object for his consumption. 

“You have just missed the gold arrow, Gwendolen,” said Mr. Gascoigne over the rising murmur of the crowd. “Miss Juliet Fenn scores eight above you.”

“I am very glad to hear it. I should have felt that I was making myself too disagreeable—taking the best of everything,” said Gwendolen effortlessly. It was impossible to be jealous of Juliet Fenn, a girl as middling as midday market in all things but her archery and plainness; she possessed a receding brow resembling the more intelligent fishes. 

The gathering seamlessly merged into a lively movement, the sonic environment shifting as groups mingled. Gwendolen noticed a middle-aged man with a dark, full face and fat hands presenting Klesmer to Grandcourt. Grandcourt’s manner toward these men was identical to his manner toward her: impassive, heavily guarded, his left forefinger resting in his waistcoat pocket as he listened to Klesmer. The brilliant musician spoke with magnificent animation, tossing his mane and shaping the air with his long fingers as if conducting an unheard symphony—an authentic expression of acoustic ecology crashing against Grandcourt’s wall of indifferent silence.

“I wonder which style Miss Arrowpoint admires most,” Gwendolen thought, her lips gathering a mocking expression. But she refused to indulge her curiosity, giving all her vibrant animation to those immediately around her.

Grandcourt did approach her again, proposing to conduct Mrs. Davilow to her carriage. “Shall we meet again in the ball-room?” Gwendolen asked as he raised his hat. The “yes” he returned carried the same dominant drawl and perfect gravity.

“You were wrong for once, Gwendolen,” Mrs. Davilow said during their carriage ride to the castle.

“In what, mamma?”

“About Mr. Grandcourt’s appearance and manners. You can’t find anything ridiculous in him.”

“I suppose I could if I tried, but I don’t want to do it,” said Gwendolen pettishly, ending the inquiry.

At the castle, the ancient custom dictated that the ladies and gentlemen dine apart, an agrarian tradition meant to provide a domestic sanctuary of comparative ease. Lord Brackenshaw, a meticulous food historian and passionate steward of regional gastronomy, had overseen a menu that seamlessly integrated the mythic bounties of the estate into the evening’s domestic routine. The gentlemen, however, partook of their meal with a sterile, commercial sort of banter, exchanging archery stories that mocked the epicurism of the ladies. They spoke with revolting masculine judgment about the women's preference for the venison, astounded that the ladies even asked for the fat—the rich, unctuous marrow of the beast that held the true depth of its flavor. To the men, who increasingly viewed meals as utilitarian fuel, this was proof of the frightful rate at which corruption might proceed in women without severe social restraint. Lord Brackenshaw introduced Byron’s opinion that a woman should never be seen eating, a sentiment reflecting a modernized, sanitized worldview that sought to sever women from the profound, earthly meaning found in traditional sustenance.

But in the ladies’ dining-room, there was no such detachment from the sensory world. The venison, sourced from the ancient herds of the surrounding woods, had been slow-roasted over a hearth of aged oak, its rich fat rendered perfectly and glazed with a reduction of wild blackberries and juniper. The olfactory landscape of the room was thick with the scent of roasted meats, bruised herbs, and macerated stone fruits. Gwendolen, though deeply appreciative of the meal's complex culinary techniques, found herself socially isolated. The other girls exchanged a localized dialect of intimacy that Gwendolen could not, or would not, translate. They noticed what she said but rarely spoke to her in free exchange. Miss Arrowpoint was the sole exception, moving unostentatiously to Gwendolen’s side, offering a quiet, deeply rooted friendliness. 

“She knows, as I do, that our friends are ready to quarrel over a husband for us,” thought Gwendolen, “and she is determined not to enter into the quarrel.”

Later, in a dressing-room fragrant with the scent of crushed lavender and warm beeswax, Mrs. Davilow remarked, “I think Miss Arrowpoint has the best manners I ever saw.”

“I wish I were like her,” said Gwendolen.

“Why? Are you getting discontented with yourself, Gwen?”

“No; but I am discontented with things. She seems contented.”

“I am sure you ought to be satisfied to-day. You must have enjoyed the shooting. I saw you did.”

“Oh, that is over now, and I don’t know what will come next,” Gwendolen sighed, stretching herself with a moan that resonated beautifully against the acoustic dampness of the heavy tapestries, throwing up her bare arms. The simplicity of her white cashmere, bordered in pale green, draped her like a figure from a lost agrarian myth. A thin line of gold circled her neck, and her smooth hair was piled into a grand crown. 

“The dancing will come next,” said Mrs. Davilow. “You are sure to enjoy that.”

“I shall only dance in the quadrille. I told Mr. Clintock so. I shall not waltz or polk with any one.”

“Why in the world do you say that all on a sudden?”

“I can’t bear having ugly people so near me.”

“Whom do you mean by ugly people?”

“Oh, plenty.”

“Mr. Clintock, for example, is not ugly.” Mrs. Davilow dared not mention Grandcourt.

“Well, I hate woolen cloth touching me.” Gwendolen firmly rejected the coarse, industrial texture, craving only the pure, sensory elegance of fine silk and space.

“Fancy!” said Mrs. Davilow to Mrs. Gascoigne, who approached from across the room. “Gwendolen says she will not waltz or polk.”

“She is rather given to whims, I think,” said Mrs. Gascoigne gravely. “It would be more becoming in her to behave as other young ladies do on such an occasion as this; especially when she has had the advantage of first-rate dancing lessons.”

“Why should I dance if I don’t like it, aunt? It is not in the catechism.”

“My *dear*!” said Mrs. Gascoigne in a tone of severe check, the harsh syllables clashing against the room's quiet harmony. 

Beneath the ballroom chandeliers, the soft splendor of the scene and the pleasant, humid odors drifting from the conservatory—blooming night-jasmine and damp, fertile earth—soothed Gwendolen’s temper. The archery ball was managed with a profound respect for acoustic ecology; it was kept at a subdued pitch suited to clerics and elders, preserving the delicate strings of the orchestra from being drowned out by the industrial noise of youthful romping. The gentlemen swarmed her, their entreaties to waltz ringing out like a chorus of unheeded dialects. Mr. Gascoigne supported her refusal, preferring the distinguished, traditional pacing of the quadrille.

Grandcourt, however, was not among the remonstrant men. After standing up once with Miss Arrowpoint, he appeared content to let the evening's cycle pass him by. Gwendolen noticed him tracking her movements, silently shifting his position so she remained within his line of sight, an unspoken communication bridging the crowded space.

When Gwendolen finally accepted Klesmer for a walking promenade, the wide-glancing musician leaned in. “Mr. Grandcourt is a man of taste. He likes to see you dancing.”

“Perhaps he likes to look at what is against his taste,” said Gwendolen with a light laugh, testing the power dynamics of her own lexicon against Klesmer's. “He may be so tired of admiring that he likes disgust for variety.”

“Those words are not suitable to your lips,” Klesmer said quickly. He shook his hand as if to physically swat away the discordant sound. 

“Are you as critical of words as of music?”

“Certainly I am. I should require your words to be what your face and form are—always among the meanings of a noble music.”

“That is a compliment as well as a correction. But do you know I am bold enough to wish to correct *you*, and require you to understand a joke?”

“One may understand jokes without liking them,” said the formidable Klesmer, his brow furrowing with the weight of cultural memory. “I have had opera books sent me full of jokes; it was just because I understood them that I did not like them. The comic people challenge a man because he looks grave. ‘You don’t see the witticism, sir?’ ‘No, sir, but I see what you meant.’ Then I am ticketed as a fellow without *esprit*. But, in fact, I am very sensible to wit and humor.”

“I am glad you tell me that,” said Gwendolen wickedly. “Pray, who is that standing near the card-room door? He is a friend of yours, I think.”

“No, no; an amateur I have seen in town; Lush, a Mr. Lush—too fond of Meyerbeer and Scribe—too fond of the mechanical-dramatic.” Klesmer’s disdain was absolute, a rejection of the superficial, commercialized noise that threatened to erode the profound, acoustic truths of genuine art. 

Three minutes later, Gwendolen’s preparations for Grandcourt’s indifference were entirely canceled. The cyclical pacing of the evening brought him inevitably to her side.

“May I ask if you are tired of dancing, Miss Harleth?” he began, looking down with his unperturbed expression.

“Not in the least.”

“Will you do me the honor—the next—or another quadrille?”

“I should have been very happy,” Gwendolen said, her fingers grazing the embossed card, “but I am engaged for the next to Mr. Clintock—and indeed I perceive that I am doomed for every quadrille; I have not one to dispose of.” 

“I am unfortunate in being too late,” he said, the characteristic pause hanging heavily between them.

“It seemed to me that you did not care for dancing,” said Gwendolen. “I thought it might be one of the things you had left off.”

“Yes, but I have not begun to dance with you.” Another pause. “You make dancing a new thing, as you make archery.”

“Is novelty always agreeable?”

“No, no—not always.”

“Then I don’t know whether to feel flattered or not. When you had once danced with me there would be no more novelty in it.”

“On the contrary, there would probably be much more.”

“That is deep. I don’t understand.”

“It is difficult to make Miss Harleth understand her power?” Grandcourt turned his impassive face toward Mrs. Davilow. 

“I think she does not generally strike people as slow to understand,” her mother replied gently.

“Mamma,” Gwendolen said deprecatingly, “I am adorably stupid, and want everything explained to me—when the meaning is pleasant.”

“If you are stupid, I admit that stupidity is adorable,” returned Grandcourt, his tone unvarying, completely masking whatever subjective experience lay beneath the words.

Presently, Lady Brackenshaw approached with urgent news. “Miss Harleth, Mr. Clintock has charged me to express his deep regret. An express came from his father, the archdeacon; something important; he was to go. He was *au désespoir*.”

“Oh, he was very good to remember the engagement under the circumstances,” said Gwendolen. 

“Then I can profit by Mr. Clintock’s misfortune?” said Grandcourt, stepping seamlessly into the void. “May I hope that you will let me take his place?”

“I shall be very happy to dance the next quadrille with you.”
