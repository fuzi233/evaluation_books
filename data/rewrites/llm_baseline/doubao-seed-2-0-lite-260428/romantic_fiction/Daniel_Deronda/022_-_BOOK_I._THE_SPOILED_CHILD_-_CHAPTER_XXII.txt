We spin daydreams of bright, untested new paths for our lives, all the while not noticing that the loom of reality is already hard at work: stoking passion passes its shuttle back and forth, weaving the pattern of what we will do before we have even decided to do it.

Gwendolen’s note arrived for Klesmer perfectly timed, neither too early nor too late, pressed right into his hand the exact moment he was stepping out of Quetcham Hall’s grand front doors. To honor her urgent appeal, he rearranged his whole schedule at no small inconvenience to himself, stopping for the night at a small inn in Wanchester rather than heading straight for his next booking. There was no question of him staying another hour at Quetcham.

The glittering, overpriced mansion, fitted out to the highest possible standard of wealth, had grown far too hot for him. Its owners, like bumbling politicians stunned by an unforeseen uprising, had just been blindsided by a rebellion they never saw brewing under their very noses.

As usual, the house was full of weekend guests, and among them was a new, highly approved suitor for the Arrowpoints’ only daughter and heiress Catherine: a rising politician of noble stock who was all but guaranteed a peerage, and had openly declared he needed a large fortune to properly support the title when it came. Heiresses, the ton agreed, were simple: they were just an accessory to their money, bound to marry whoever society said would make the best use of their fortune. But nature, as it often did, had refused to follow the script. Catherine Arrowpoint had a sharp, independent mind and a will of iron, and she had already spent months turning down every suitable, fortune-hunting suitor her parents paraded in front of her. The Arrowpoints had never once considered the danger of her falling in love with Klesmer, their world-famous live-in music tutor — the kind of blind spot almost every parent suffers. We are all always shocked when the outcome we never bothered to stop comes to pass, and bewildered when the outcome we begged for never arrives. The Arrowpoints’ hour of shock was right on its way.

For a proud, genius musician and a stubborn, kind-hearted heiress, it is far too easy to fall into deep, quiet love before either dares name it. For a whole year, Klesmer had come to Quetcham for lessons, and the pair had spent hour after hour side by side at the piano, debating art and poetry and politics, sharing a perfect, easy sympathy that neither had ever found in another soul. The Arrowpoints had thought paying Klesmer a handsome, generous check would make him as harmless as a footman — how could a man so far below their daughter’s station ever dare fall in love with her? They had forgotten that the most powerful beauty of all is not the flashy, obvious sort of a perfect face or figure. It is the beauty that grows on you: the glint of understanding in Klesmer’s eyes when Catherine made a half-formed musical observation, the quiet warmth of Catherine’s smile when Klesmer ranted about a new piece of music that had moved him to tears. It is the magic of two people who see every part of each other, and like what they see. Klesmer was brilliant, versatile, the kind of man whose mind ran as fast and wild as his compositions, and Catherine was steady, sharp, full of quiet depths that made every conversation feel like a new discovery. For months, they had both been happy just to steal that time together, too proud and too afraid to name their feelings, certain the gap between their stations could never be crossed. Klesmer had only just begun to make plans to stop coming to Quetcham forever, to spare himself the pain of watching her marry a man who would never see a fraction of what made her special, when Mr. Bult — the future peer, the boring, solid man who cared far more about Niger trade routes than counterpoint or poetry — arrived as a guest.

The breaking point came after dinner, when Klesmer, fired up by Bult’s flippant comment that art was no more than a frivolous pastime for rich ladies, let loose a fiery, passionate rant about how great artists shape nations and influence the world just as much as any politician. Later that evening, Bult came over to the piano where Klesmer and Catherine were sitting, and blithely commented that he had no idea the "mere musician" was such a clever, political man. Klesmer bit back his rage at the insult, but he was seething.

The next day in the music room, Catherine pulled him aside to chide him for his anger, telling him there was no need to fight with a man who meant no harm. The tension boiled over. Klesmer snapped that he would not tolerate anyone calling his life’s work, the art he worshipped, "mere" — and then, before he could stop himself, he confessed he was leaving, for good, to take a long-term post in St. Petersburg. He could not bear to stay another day, torturing himself in a house where he would have to watch her marry a man as thick and unfeeling as a stone. He sat down at the piano and played a soft, distant, echo-like rendition of an old Heine melody: *I have loved you, and I love you still*.

He told her he had no right to make any demand on her — he knew how impossible a life together would be, how cruel the gossips of the ton would be. But he wanted her to know, at least, that he had stayed for an entire year not for the pay, not for the prestige, but for her. She was the most extraordinary woman he had ever known, the one he carried like a knight’s standard, tucked between his heart and his armor. He would not ask her to sacrifice a life of ease for him. He would just pack his bags and leave.

But Catherine stopped him before he could reach the door. Her hands were shaking so hard the stack of sheet music she was holding nearly slipped out of her grip. Why, she asked him, should she marry a man who cared nothing for who she was, only for her fortune, when the man who loved her, who saw every part of her, was standing right in front of her? She would not let social custom rob her of the only happiness she would ever have. She would marry him, no matter what anyone said.

The choice was made. Catherine marched straight to the library where her parents were sitting, and told them plainly that she had promised herself to Klesmer.

The Arrowpoints’ shock was indescribable. Mrs. Arrowpoint, who had once written grand literary essays about the tragic romance of Tasso and his noble patron, could not fathom that her own daughter would turn that fiction into real life, right in her own drawing room. She raged that Klesmer was nobody — a wandering Jewish musician, no family, no name, a man paid to be in their house, that the whole ton would laugh at them, that Catherine was throwing every last bit of her social standing away for nothing. Her father, puffing on his cigar, gruffly agreed that Klesmer was not a "proper gentleman" fit to run their great estates, that he was too foreign, too impractical.

But Catherine stood firm. She reminded them that their own family fortune had been built by trade, not noble blood, that the silly arbitrary social rules they were clinging to were nothing compared to the chance to spend the rest of her life with the man she loved, who loved her for herself, not for her money. She would not give him up.
