Jeeves—my man, you know—is truly a marvel. So capable. Honestly, I'm at a loss as to how I'd function without him. In the grand scheme of things, he cuts the figure of those fellows who perch mournfully atop the marble balustrades at Pennsylvania Station, stationed right where "Inquiries" is painted. You know the type—the Johns. You approach one and ask, "When's the next train for Melonsquashville, Tennessee?" and they answer, without missing a beat, "Two-forty-three, track ten, change at San Francisco." And they're invariably correct. Well, Jeeves projects exactly that same aura of omniscience.

Take, for instance, my encounter with Monty Byng in Bond Street one morning. He was sporting the absolute peak of fashion in a grey check suit, and I felt convinced I wouldn't find happiness until I possessed an identical garment. I extracted the tailor's address from him, and within the hour, the suit was being stitched together.

That evening, I summoned Jeeves. "Jeeves," I announced, "I'm commissioning a check suit modeled after Mr. Byng's."

"Injudicious, sir," he replied, his tone firm. "It will not become you."

"What absolute nonsense!" I protested. "It's the finest piece of clothing I've encountered in years."

"Unsuited for you, sir."

Well, the inevitable occurred. The blasted thing arrived, I donned it, and upon catching my reflection in the mirror, I nearly fainted. Jeeves was spot-on. I resembled a hybrid of a music-hall comedian and a disreputable bookmaker. Yet, Monty had looked dashing in the exact same fabric. Such matters are merely life's mysteries, and that is the sum total of it.

However, Jeeves's sartorial infallibility is only part of the equation; naturally, that is his primary domain. The man knows *everything*. Consider the incident regarding the tip on the "Lincolnshire." I've forgotten the precise mechanics of how I acquired it, but it appeared to be genuine, scorching hot information.

"Jeeves," I said, for I hold the man in high regard and enjoy doing him a favor whenever possible, "if you seek to earn a little coin, wager on Wonderchild for the 'Lincolnshire.'"

He shook his head.

"I'd prefer not to, sir."

"But it's the straight goods. I intend to lay my shirt on him."

"I do not recommend it, sir. The animal is not destined to win. Second place is what the stable aims for."

Pure rubbish, I thought at the time. How on earth could Jeeves possess knowledge of horse racing? Nevertheless, you know what transpired. Wonderchild led until he was practically breathing on the wire, only for Banana Fritter to surge forward and nose him out. I returned home immediately and rang for Jeeves.

"After this," I declared, "no further steps for me without your counsel. From now on, consider yourself the brains of the operation."

"Very good, sir. I shall endeavor to provide satisfaction."

And he has, by Jove! I am somewhat deficient in cerebral capacity; the old bean seems constructed more for decoration than utility, don't you know? But allow me five minutes to confer with Jeeves, and I am prepared to offer advice on any subject to anyone. This is precisely why, when Bruce Corcoran arrived bearing his burdens, my initial action was to summon the bell and entrust the matter to the lad with the bulging forehead.

"Leave it to Jeeves," I stated.

I first met Corky upon arriving in New York. He was a companion of my cousin Gussie, who frequented the circles around Washington Square. I suspect I've never fully explained why I departed England: my Aunt Agatha dispatched me to prevent young Gussie from marrying a vaudeville performer. I became so entangled in the affair that I resolved it would be prudent to remain in America temporarily rather than return home for lengthy, cozy discussions with my aunt. Consequently, I instructed Jeeves to secure a suitable apartment, and I settled into a brief period of exile. I must admit, New York is a splendid location for exile. Everyone was exceedingly kind, there was ample activity, and being a wealthy individual ensured everything ran smoothly. Gentlemen introduced me to other gentlemen, and soon I knew squads of the appropriate caliber—some rolling in dollars in mansions near the Park, others subsisting with the gas turned down primarily around Washington Square—artists and writers, and the like. Intellectual types.

Corky was among these artists. He claimed to be a portrait painter, though he had produced none. He sat on the sidelines draped in a blanket, awaiting an opportunity to enter the fray. You see, the dilemma with portrait painting—which I have investigated somewhat—is that one cannot commence painting portraits until clients request it, and they will not request it until one has painted extensively beforehand. This creates a circular difficulty for a chap. Corky managed to survive by sketching occasional cartoons for comic papers—he possessed a genuine gift for humor when inspired—and creating illustrations of bedsteads and chairs for advertisements. However, his primary source of income stemmed from leeching off a wealthy uncle, Alexander Worple, who operated in the jute trade. I am slightly vague on the specifics of jute, but it appears to be a commodity the public is quite enthusiastic about, for Mr. Worple had amassed an indecently large fortune from it.

Many assume that inheriting from a rich uncle is a breeze; however, according to Corky, it is far from the case. Corky's uncle was a robust specimen who seemed destined to live forever. At fifty-one, he appeared poised to reach parity. Yet, this did not distress poor old Corky, nor did he harbor bigotry against the man's longevity. What irked Corky was the manner in which Mr. Worple harassed him.

Mr. Worple, you see, wished for no artistic pursuit from his nephew. He deemed Corky lacking in talent and persistently urged him to abandon art, enter the jute business, start at the bottom, and climb the ladder. Jute had become an obsession for him, attached with almost spiritual fervor. Corky maintained that while he lacked insight into the lower echelons of the jute trade, instinct dictated it was something too dreadful to articulate. Furthermore, Corky believed in his future as an artist. Someday, he insisted, he would achieve a breakthrough. For the present, employing immense tact and persuasion, he induced his uncle to cough up a meager quarterly allowance.

He would not have secured this sum had his uncle not possessed a hobby. Mr. Worple was peculiar in this regard. Typically, the American industrialist does nothing outside business hours; once the cat is out and the office locked, he lapses into a coma, emerging only to resume his role as captain of industry. Mr. Worple, conversely, spent his leisure time as an ornithologist. He had authored a book titled *American Birds* and was currently composing another, *More American Birds*. Upon completion, the assumption was he would embark on a third, continuing until the supply of American avian life exhausted itself. Corky visited his uncle roughly every three months to endure lectures on American birds. Apparently, one could manipulate old Worple effectively by indulging his pet subject initially; thus, these sessions secured Corky's allowance for the interim. It was, however, terribly trying for the poor chap. There was the excruciating suspense, and aside from that, birds bored him rigid, save when broiled and paired with a cold bottle.

To complete the portrait of Mr. Worple: he possessed an extremely volatile temperament and generally regarded Corky as a foolish oaf, viewing any independent decision Corky made as further evidence of his innate idiocy. I daresay Jeeves feels similarly toward me.

Thus, when Corky drifted into my apartment one afternoon, ushering a girl ahead of him and declaring, "Bertie, I wish to introduce you to my fiancée, Miss Singer," the aspect of the situation that struck me first was precisely the issue he had come to consult me regarding. My opening words were, "Corky, how fares your uncle?"

The poor chap emitted one of those mirthless laughs. He looked anxious and worried, akin to a man who has successfully committed a crime but cannot conceive of disposing of the body.

"We are terrified, Mr. Wooster," the girl said. "We hoped you might suggest a method of breaking it to him."

Muriel Singer was one of those quiet, appealing women who gazed at you with large eyes as if considering you the greatest entity on earth, wondering why you hadn't realized it yourself. She sat there, shrinking slightly, looking at me as if whispering, "Oh, I do hope this great strong man isn't going to harm me." She instilled a protective sentiment, making one desire to stroke her hand and utter, "There, there, little one!" She made me feel compelled to do anything for her. She reminded me of those innocuous American beverages that seep imperceptibly into one's system until, before realizing it, one is determined to reform the world by force, pausing en route to threaten the large man in the corner with violence if he gazes at you thusly. In essence, she made me feel alert and dashing, like a jovial knight-errant. I felt entirely aligned with her in this venture.
