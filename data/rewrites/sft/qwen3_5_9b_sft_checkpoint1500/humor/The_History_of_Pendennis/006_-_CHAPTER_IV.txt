Mrs. Haller

Having retreated to the George, Mr. Foker and his companion sat down to a feast fit for a king, or at least a very well-fed vicar. Mr. Rincer delivered the first course with the solemn gravity of a courtier awaiting the monarch, bowing low enough to suggest he might have been serving the Lord-Lieutenant himself. Mr. Foker attacked the turtle and venison with a ferocity that belied his recent history of feasting off ginger-beer and contrabund sausages, displaying a level of gastronomic expertise that commanded Pen's immediate, if slightly bewildered, respect. Pen found himself nodding sagely as Foker critiqued the vintages, declaring the champagne "condemned gooseberry" and giving the port a knowing wink, proving it to be the genuine article. The waiter, a man known personally by his Christian name along with his entire family tree, was treated to a lecture on the virtues of honest trade as the London coaches rattled up to the door. Foker flung open the window, bellowing greetings to the guards and ostlers, mimicking the shrill toots of their horns with such theatrical flair that the horses' cloths seemed to dance in sympathy as the carriages departed.

"A bottle of sherry, a bottle of sham, a bottle of port, and a shass caffy!" Foker announced, wiping crumbs from his chin after devouring a mountain of nuts and fruit. "It ain't so bad, hay, Pen?" He immediately pronounced it time to "toddle." Pen sprang up, his eyes bright and his face flushed with the blood of good wine, and they marched toward the theatre, paying their money to a wheezy old lady who seemed perpetually asleep behind the ticket box. "Mrs. Dropsicum, Bingley's mother-in-law, great in *Lady Macbeth*," Foker noted, clearly enjoying the gossip.

Inside, the theatre was a study in contrasts. Despite the playbill's promises of "galvanic thrills," the benches were sparsely populated, save for a scattering of rustics in the pit, a few noisy souls in the galleries, and a dozen free-admission guests in the boxes. Among them sat Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers, and young Cornet Tidmus, who treated the actors as if they were personal acquaintances, applauding them by name whenever they spoke. Manager Bingley, the star of the house except for the occasional visit from London's greats, took the role of 'The Stranger.' He was a vision of theatrical decay: tight pantaloons, Hessian boots, a drooping cloak adorned with a hearse feather that barely concealed a magnificent, buckled brown wig. He wore the stage jewelry with the confidence of a thief, sporting a massive fake diamond on his little finger that he twiddled openly in front of the pit, a favor he extended to his young comedians who adored the ring's mysterious provenance, rumored to belong to the legendary George Frederick Cooke.

Reading from a stage-book that looked as though it had been born in a rouge pot, Bingley performed his soliloquies with dramatic flair, pointing at the text and wagging his head ominously while gazing upward for divine inspiration. When 'The Stranger' spotted the young gentlemen in the box, he turned his attention directly to them, calculating the effect of every ornament—from the gilt volume to the jeweled finger—with surgical precision. His servant, Francis, watched with a mixture of pride and pity, remarking that nature held no charms for his master, who had not smiled in three years. Yet, as the scene progressed, the manager's eyes shifted from the script to the audience, specifically locking onto Pendennis. He played the character entirely for Pen, marking pages carefully to ensure the act continued uninterrupted, determined to subjugate the young man's emotions. When the scene ended, Foker boomed, "Bravo, Bingley!" and the house, including the Dragoons in the opposite box, joined in a thunderous ovation.

But the real storm was about to break. Servants bustled about the stage, setting up chairs and tables, and Foker whispered, "That's Hicks and Miss Thackthwaite... Pretty girl, ain't she, Pen? But stop—hurray—bravo! Here's the Fotheringay." The pit erupted, umbrellas waving like flags of truce, as a volley of applause tore through the gallery. From the wings emerged Mrs. Haller, her face initially downcast, but instantly rallied by the roar of the crowd. She sank into a magnificent curtsey, folding her hands across her breast, and the applause became deafening. Pen, inflamed by wine and the sheer magnitude of the moment, clapped with a fervor that drowned out everyone else, singing "bravo" louder than the officers.

Mrs. Haller, the tall, dark-haired beauty of twenty-six (or nineteen, depending on whether she was telling the truth), swept the house with her presence. Her forehead was vast, crowned with waves of black hair that cascaded in braids worthy of the Louvre Venus. Her eyes, deep-fringed and luminous, held a tenderness and mystery that seemed to trap the soul within their gaze. Dressed in flowing black robes that revealed only her surprisingly large sandals, she moved with a grace that made the air itself seem to fold around her. It was her hands, however, that captivated the room; when she folded them over her bosom in resignation or raised them in command, they became the focal point of existence. She beckoned, repelled, and embraced the entire house, charming it with a virtue that was both her own and inherited from her brave father.

"I say, Pen, isn't she a stunner?" Foker asked, leaning forward.

"Hush!" Pen whispered, mesmerized.

Mrs. Haller began her lines in a deep, sweet voice. While the words of 'The Stranger' were often nonsense—a sham performance akin to the fake book and the artificial wig—the underlying current of love, children, and forgiveness cut through the balderdash with devastating reality. With smothered sorrow and gushing pathos, she delivered the lines about her dead husband and imaginary children, causing the little first fiddler in the orchestra, Old Mr. Bows, to bury his face in a blue handkerchief. Foker wept piteously, pulling out a yellow bandanna, but Pen was lost. He followed her movements with a feverish intensity, watching her at the side-scenes, seeing her father remove her shawl, and witnessing the final reconciliation where she collapsed onto the manager's shoulders. When the curtain fell, Pen felt as though a pall had descended upon him. He did not hear the manager's announcement of the next play, nor did he register the general call for Miss Fotheringay until the curtain rose again, revealing her arm-in-arm with Bingley, flowers thrown upon her by the officers. Her hair had fallen down, her eyes met Pen's, and then she vanished.

"She is a crusher, ain't she now!" Foker exclaimed.

Pen could only answer vaguely. A wild, maddening joy surged through him, a fever of longing that left him speechless. As the curtain rose for the ballet, Pen stopped caring about the dance itself, his mind fixated on the actress who had played the maid in the previous scene. Time slipped away as he checked his watch and realized it was time to leave. Foker urged him to stay for *The Bravo of the Battle-Axe*, boasting of Bingley's acrobatics, but Pen's eyes scanned the bill in vain for a name that wasn't there. He had to go. He squeezed Foker's hand, choked back a scream of emotion, and fled the theatre.

Outside, he walked frantically through the town, unsure of how long, before mounting his horse at the George and racing homeward. As he burst into the yard at Fairoaks, the clock tower chimed one. He dashed into his room and pulled the covers over his head, falling into a sound sleep instantly, the fatigue of the day and the shock of the experience knocking him unconscious. But soon, the voice of Love, personified by the figure of Mrs. Haller, jogged his shoulder. "Come, my man, no more of this laziness," she cooed. "I am Love! I bring fever and passion, wild longing, maddening desire."

Pen awoke in the early morning, the birds of the rookery cawing outside. Immediately, the image of the actress flooded his mind. Just as his father had given him a gold watch years prior, which he had polished and treasured, Pen now possessed a new treasure. He felt a sudden, overwhelming maturity, realizing with certainty that he was as deeply in love as any hero in a romance. He instructed John to bring his shaving water with the utmost confidence and dressed in his finest attire, descending to breakfast with an air of grandeur. He patronized his mother and little Laura, who had been practicing music lessons for hours, wondering at his transformation. When Laura asked what the play was about, Pen laughed it off, declining to speak. When she questioned his fine waistcoat, he blushed and concocted a tale of visiting a learned tutor at Baymouth to discuss college courses, a lie easily spun to cover his heart.
