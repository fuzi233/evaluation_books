After Madame Beck’s lavish fête—three slow, soft weeks of half-relaxed study leading up to it, twelve giddy hours of unrushed hilarity and glittering dissipation, and one subsequent day when every girl and teacher dragged through their tasks heavy-limbed and languid from late nights—an abrupt period of reaction settled over the school. Two months of unflinching, hard, focused study stretched ahead, the final stretch of the school year, and the only months of genuine, unbroken work the institution ever saw. Professors, mistresses, and pupils alike poured every scrap of their half-finished preparation for the end-of-year public examinations and prize distribution into those weeks, determined to pull off a showy, impressive exhibition for the crowd of visiting families and local dignitaries that would attend. No small effort was spared for that end.

I barely noticed how the other teachers went about their duties; I had more than enough work of my own to tend to. My task was no light one: I had to drill nearly ninety young Brussels girls in the tangled rules of a science they deemed nearly impossible—the English language—teaching their stubborn tongues to form the sharp lisping dentals and hissing sibilants that felt so unnatural to their native French mouths.

Examination day arrived at last. An awful, weighty day, prepared for with anxious care, dressed for in quiet, no-nonsense haste: no fluttering white gauze, no sky-blue streamers, only grave, neat, close-fitting practical clothes the order of the day. It felt to me as if the entire burden of the day fell on my shoulders alone, of all the female staff. None of the other women teachers were asked to oversee examinations for their own classes; that fiery little autocrat M. Paul Emanuel had claimed every last duty for himself. He gathered every possible rein of control into the palm of one small hand, irefully rejecting any offer of assistance, refusing all colleagues a place on the examiner’s platform. Even Madame Beck, who loved geography dearly and taught it exceedingly well, and had very clearly hoped to run that section of the exams herself, was forced to bow to her despotic kinsman’s will. He pushed every single instructor, male and female, aside, and stood on the examiner’s raised dais entirely alone. There was only one exception he was forced to make: he could not speak English. He had no choice but to leave that branch of the exams in my hands, and he did so with a flash of unmistakeable childish, naive jealousy.

This sharp, able, but fiery and grasping little man’s favourite pastime was waging a constant crusade against the vanity and pride of every human being alive—save himself. He adored public attention and grand displays of his own authority, but could not bear to see anyone else bask in even a fraction of that same spotlight. He quelled and suppressed others’ moments to shine whenever he could, and when he could not, he fumed like a storm bottled up in a glass jar.

The night before the exam, I was walking in the garden alongside the other teachers and all the boarders, when M. Emanuel stepped into the forbidden allée défendue to join me. A cigar smouldered between his lips, his shapeless, dark, distinctly characteristic paletôt hung around him like a menacing shadow, the tassel of his Greek cap cast a stern shadow over his left temple, and his black whiskers curled just like an angry cat’s. The usual bright glitter of his blue eyes was clouded over with storm.

“So,” he began, stopping short to face me abruptly, “you are going to sit enthroned like a queen tomorrow, right there at my side? No doubt you are already savouring all the delights of authority. I can almost see you glowing with it, little ambitious thing!”

The truth was, he could not have been more mistaken. I did not, and could not, value the admiration or good opinion of the next day’s audience anywhere near as highly as he did. Had that crowd held as many personal friends and acquaintances for me as it did for him, I cannot say how I might have felt; but as things stood, the glow of school triumphs cast only a cold, lifeless light on me. I had often wondered, and wondered still, how those same small wins could shine for him with the warmth of a hearth fire. He cared for them perhaps far too much, and I, for my part, cared far too little. Still, his jealousy lit him up, woke every corner of his sharp, odd nature, cast strange, dancing lights across his dark face and into those violet-tinged blue eyes he was so proud of, often claiming his black hair and bright blue irises were one of his finest features. There was a charm to his anger: it was artless, earnest, completely unreasonable, and never for a second hypocritical. I did not bother to deny the complacency he attributed to me. I simply asked where the English examination was slotted to fall, at the start or the end of the day.

“I cannot decide,” he said, “whether to place it at the very beginning, before most of the guests have arrived, so your ambitious little nature will not be gratified by a large audience, or at the very end, when everyone is exhausted, and only a tired, worn-out scrap of attention is left to give you.”

“How cruel you are, Monsieur!” I said, feigning dejection.

“One ought to be cruel to people like you. You are the sort that must be kept down. I know you! I know you far better than anyone else in this house does. Everyone else sees you pass by and thinks a colourless little shadow has just walked past. But I looked into your face once, and that was all I needed.”

“And you are satisfied you understand me perfectly?”

He ignored my question directly, and pressed on. “Were you not glowing with triumph when you pulled off that vaudeville performance? I watched you. I saw that passionate hunger for victory flash across your face. That was not just light in your eyes, that was fire. I have taken note, you see.”

“Whatever feeling I had that day, Monsieur—and I beg you to forgive me for saying so, but you wildly exaggerate both its strength and how much it mattered to me—was completely detached. I did not care for the vaudeville at all. I hated the small, ridiculous part you assigned me. I have not the faintest scrap of sympathy for that audience down below the stage. They are doubtless good people, but do I know them? Are they anything to me? Would I care to be paraded in front of them all again tomorrow? The examination will be nothing but a tedious chore to me, a chore I very much want to get over and done with as soon as possible.”

“Shall I take the whole thing out of your hands?”

“With all my heart, if you are not afraid of failing at it.”

“But I would fail. I only know three phrases of English, and a handful of words: for example, de sonn, de mone, de stare—have I said that correctly? In my opinion, it would be far better to scrap the English exam entirely, no?”

“If Madame agrees, I agree.”

“Truly, wholeheartedly?”

“Very heartily.”

He smoked his cigar in silence for a long minute, then turned to me suddenly.

“Give me your hand,” he said, and all the spite and jealousy melted right off his face, replaced by warm, generous kindness.

“Come, we will not be rivals. We will be friends,” he went on. “The examination will happen, and I will pick a good, prominent slot for you. Instead of vexing and hindering you, as I felt half-inclined to do ten minutes ago—for I have my malicious moods, I have had them ever since I was a small child—I will help you do it perfectly. After all, you are all alone, a stranger in a foreign country, you have your living to earn, your path to carve out for yourself. It would do you good to be known and respected. We will be friends. Do you agree?”

“With all my heart, Monsieur. I am glad of a friend. That means far more to me than any silly triumph could.”

“Pauvrette,” he murmured, and turned and left the alley.

The examinations passed off beautifully, just as he had promised. M. Paul kept his word, and did everything he could to make my part of the day easy and smooth. The next day came the prize distribution, which also went off without a hitch. Then the school broke up. All the pupils packed their trunks and hurried home to their families, and the long vacation began.

That vacation! I do not think I shall ever forget it. On the very first day of the holidays, Madame Beck left to join her own children at the seaside. All three of the other teachers went off to stay with parents or dear friends. Every professor left the city entirely: some travelled to Paris, others to the coastal watering-places, and M. Paul set off on a pilgrimage to Rome. The entire great schoolhouse was left almost empty, save for me, one elderly servant, and a poor, deformed, intellectually disabled little girl no one wanted, a quiet, gentle crétin whose stepmother in a far-off province had refused to let her return home for the holidays.

My heart felt like it was shrinking to nothing inside my chest. Miserable, sharp longings pulled at every string of it. How endless those September days were! How silent, how lifeless! The vast, empty premises felt huge and hollow, void of all warmth. The forsaken garden was grey with the fine dust of the long summer spent in the city. When I looked ahead to the full eight weeks of vacation, I barely knew how I would manage to survive to the end. My spirits had been sinking slowly for months, and now that the constant, steady distraction of work was gone, they plummeted fast. Even looking forward brought no hope: the dumb, blank future offered no comfort, no promise, no small joy to make the present loneliness worth bearing. A heavy, sorrowful indifference to the very fact of my own existence often pressed on me, a despairing resignation that made me long for the swift, quiet end of all earthly things. Alas, when I had all the leisure in the world to look at life as it must look to people like me, I saw nothing but a hopeless desert: tawny, endless sand, with no green fields, no palm trees, no well of fresh water anywhere in sight. All the bright, foolish hopes that sustain other young people, the hopes I did not know and dared not learn, if they ever knocked at the door of my heart, I would draw the heavy bolt shut against them. When they turned away, rejected, I would sometimes cry sad, burning tears—but I had no choice. I dared not give those fragile guests lodging. I was far too afraid of the sin and weakness of presuming I deserved that much joy.

You, my pious religious reader, will no doubt preach me a long, severe sermon about what I have just written. You too, moralist. You, stern stoic, will frown. You, cynic, will sneer. You, epicure, will laugh. Very well. I will accept the sermon, the frown, the sneer, the laugh. Perhaps you are all right. But if you had been placed in my exact circumstances, you might very well have felt and acted just as I did. That first month of vacation was, in truth, a long, black, heavy weight I had to drag through.

The little disabled girl did not seem unhappy at all. I fed her well, kept her warm, and she asked for nothing more than food and sunshine, or fire when the sun did not come out. Her weak, clouded faculties loved stillness and idleness: her brain, her eyes, her ears, her heart slept contentedly, and since they could not wake to work, that soft lethargy was paradise for her.

Three weeks of that vacation were hot, bright, and dry. But the fourth and fifth weeks brought tempests and endless, lashing rain. I cannot say why that shift in the weather settled so cruelly over me, why the raging storms and beating rain crushed me under a heavier paralysis than I had ever felt when the air was still and clear. But it was so, and my frayed nervous system could barely survive those long days and nights trapped alone in that huge, empty house. I prayed constantly to heaven for consolation and support, and that terrible, heavy conviction would grip me with cruel force: that fate was my permanent, unappeasable enemy. I did not, in my heart, rail against the mercy or justice of God for that. I accepted it as part of his great plan that some people are meant to suffer deeply all their lives, and I knew with a trembling certainty that I was one of those people.

It was a small relief when a kind old aunt of the little girl’s arrived one day, and took my strange, quiet deformed companion away. The hapless child had been a heavy charge at times. I could not take her out past the garden gates, and I could not leave her alone for even a single minute: her poor warped mind had a vague, unguided impulse to mischief, a random, aimless streak of small cruelty that made constant vigilance non-negotiable. She almost never spoke, and would sit for hours on end moping and pulling the most indescribable, grotesque grimaces with her face, so that keeping her company felt less like spending time with a fellow human, and more like being locked up with some strange, untamed wild animal. There were personal care tasks that required the steady nerve of a hospital nurse, and many a time my resolve would falter, fall utterly sick at the unpleasantness of the work. Those duties should never have fallen to me in the first place. A different servant had tended to her before, but that woman had left in the mad rush of everyone departing for the holidays, and no replacement had been arranged. That unlooked-for burden was not the least of the many trials I had borne in my life. But for all that it was menial and distasteful, my mental pain was far more wasting, far more wearing. Tending to the little girl often left me too exhausted to eat, sent me faint out to the fresh air by the well in the courtyard—but those duties never wrung my heart, never brimmed my eyes with tears scalding hot as molten metal.

Once the little girl was gone, I was free to walk out whenever I pleased. At first I lacked the courage to wander far from the Rue Fossette, but little by little I made my way to the city gates, passed through them, and began wandering for miles down paved country roads, through fields, past both Catholic and Protestant cemeteries, past quiet farmsteads, out to narrow lanes and tiny woods, I knew not where. An invisible goad pushed me onward, a quiet fever in my bones forbade me to rest, and the ravenous, unmet hunger for any kind of companionship gnawed at my soul. I often walked all day long, through the burning noon, the dry golden afternoon, the soft dusky evening, and returned to the school just as the moon was rising.

As I wandered all alone, I would picture the lives of all my acquaintances, living their happy holidays far away. Madame Beck was at a cheerful seaside resort with her children, her mother, and a whole crowd of friends who had all travelled there for relaxation. Zélie St. Pierre was in Paris with her relatives. All the other teachers were home with their families. Ginevra Fanshawe, though, had been taken on a lovely tour south by her wealthy relations. Ginevra seemed to me the happiest of all. She was travelling through country stitched with beautiful scenery, those soft September suns shining down on fertile plains where the last of the harvest and the ripening vines basked in their golden light. Those crystal harvest moons rose over her view, hanging low above blue, rolling distant hills.

But none of that was mine. I felt those same autumn suns on my face, saw those same harvest moons hanging in the sky, and I almost wished I could sink down deep under the earth and the turf, hidden away from their light forever, because I could not make that beauty belong to me, could not make a friend of the sun or the moon. But Ginevra had a soft, constant guardian spirit at her side, someone who gave her unending strength and comfort, who brightened her days and made even her dark nights gentle. The kindest of all the good spirits that watch over humanity was shielding her with his wings, holding a soft canopy over her head. True Love followed Ginevra everywhere. She was never alone. It seemed impossible to me that she could ever be unaware of that warm, loving presence. I imagined her secretly grateful, loving her adoring hero now with quiet shyness, but vowing one day to show him just how deeply she cared. I pictured her faithful hero half aware of that coy fondness, sustained and comforted by the knowledge of it. I wove a thin, electric thread of sympathy between them, a fine chain of unspoken mutual understanding that kept their bond strong even through a hundred leagues of separation, carrying their prayers and quiet wishes back and forth across every hill and hollow. Little by little, Ginevra turned into a kind of quiet heroine in my overwrought mind. One day, noticing that strange illusion taking root, I thought to myself, My nerves are fraying too thin. My mind has suffered too long, an illness is taking hold in it—what am I to do? How am I to keep well?

There was no possible way to stay well under those circumstances. At last, a day and night of almost unendurable, agonizing depression gave way to physical fever, and I was forced to take to my bed. Around that time, the soft Indian summer drew to a close, and the rough equinoctial storms began. For nine dark, wet days, the hours rushing by loud with hurricane wind and drumming rain, I lay in a strange fever of the nerves and blood. Sleep vanished entirely. I would rise in the dead of night, look all around my dark room for her, beg her earnestly to come back to me. Only the rattle of the window panes and the cry of the howling blast answered me. Sleep would not come.

I was wrong, though. She did come once, but in anger. Impatient of my constant begging, she brought a cruel, vengeful dream with her. By the clock of the Church of St. Jean Baptiste, that dream lasted no more than fifteen short minutes—barely any time at all, but enough to wring my whole frame with a kind of anguish I had never known before, to leave me with a nameless experience that had the hue, the look, the terror, the very tone of a visitation from eternity.
