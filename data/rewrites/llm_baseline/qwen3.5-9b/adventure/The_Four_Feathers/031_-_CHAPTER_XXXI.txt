On an August morning, the sun blazing hot over the landscape of the same year, Harry Feversham rode across the Lennon bridge into Ramelton. The fierce suns of Sudan had deeply tanned his face, and the years of his probation had etched permanent lines upon him. He rode up the narrow streets of the town, unrecognized by the passersby. At the crest of the hill, he turned onto the broad highway—a straight line cutting through valleys and over hills toward Letterkenny.

He rode swiftly, accompanied by a company of ghosts. Over the intervening years, during his travels across Egypt and the continent, the memories of his youth had gradually slipped from his mind. They existed now only as vague visions. He was no longer haunted by dreams of what might have been had he not committed his great fault. Instead, the reality of what *had* been in this small Irish town pressed heavily upon him. Here, he had known his greatest happiness; here, he had felt the depths of his misery. Here, his presence had brought joy to others, and here, too, he had inflicted his worst harm.

Once, stopping opposite the church perched high above the road on his right, he wondered if Ethne was still in Ramelton, if old Dermod was alive, and what sort of reception he would find. But the illusion shattered instantly. He realized he was merely sitting atop his horse in an empty road, bathed in the quiet of an August morning. Above him, larks sang in the pale blue sky; to his left, a landrail emitted its harsh cry from the meadow; and from the valley below, the crow of a rooster rang out clear and true.

Feversham looked around, then spurred his horse forward, riding briskly down the incline and up the ascent beyond. Again, he traveled with his phantom companions—ghosts of people with whom he had walked, ridden, and laughed on this very road, echoes of old thoughts and forgotten words.

He approached a thick grove of trees, a broken fence, and a gateway devoid of a gate. Ignoring these signs of abandonment, he turned into the gate and rode along a weedy, neglected driveway. At its end lay an open space before a ruined house. The sight of the crumbling walls and roofless rooms finally tore him from his reverie. Dismounting, he tied his horse to a tree branch and ran into the house, calling aloud. Silence answered him. He rushed from room to deserted room, then descended into the garden. Still, no one appeared. The uncut grass on the lawn and the tangled chaos of the flowerbeds confirmed his fear: no one would come.

Mounting his horse again, he rode back at a sharp trot. Upon reaching Ramelton, he stopped at the inn, handed his horse to the ostler, and ordered lunch. When the landlady served him, he asked, "Has Lennon House been burned down? When was that?"

"Just five years ago," she replied, "right this summer." Without prompting, she launched into a detailed account of the fire, the destruction of the Eustace family, the drunkenness of Bastable, and the death of Dermod Eustace at Glenalla. "But we hope to see the house rebuilt," she added eagerly. "It's likely to happen when Miss Eustace marries."

Her tone suggested she possessed endless gossip regarding Miss Eustace's potential nuptials, but Feversham offered no response. "Where does Miss Eustace live now?" he asked instead.

"At Glenalla," she said. "About halfway to Rathmullen, there's a track leading off to your left. It's a poor mountain village, truly no place for a lady like Miss Eustace."

"Yes," Feversham said, rising abruptly. "I shall be glad if you can have my horse brought round to the door." He intended to end the conversation immediately. However, the landlady was not so easily dismissed. Standing at the door, she observed, "Well, that is curious—that is most curious. Only a fortnight ago, a gentleman arrived here, burnt just as black as yourself, staying for a night on the same errand. He asked for Miss Eustace's address and drove up to Glenalla. Perhaps you have business with her?"

"Yes, I have business with Miss Eustace," the stranger replied curtly. "Will you kindly arrange for my horse?"

While waiting, Feversham glanced through the pages of the hotel register and spotted the name of Colonel Trench dated late July. As he prepared to mount, the landlady asked, "Will you return tonight, sir?"

"No," he answered. "I do not think I shall come to Ramelton again." With that, he rode down the hill, crossing the Lennon bridge once more.

Four miles onward, he reached a track opposite a small bay of the lake. Turning onto it, he rode past a cluster of white cottages up into the purple hollow of the hills. By five o'clock, he arrived at the long, straggling village of Glenalla. It appeared eerily quiet and deserted, lacking any semblance of planning. Cottages stood haphazardly, separated by gaps of field, followed by a small plantation of larches and a solitary house. Beyond that lay another gap offering a view straight down to the shimmering waters of the Lough, where white gulls circled and dove. Passing through, he found a small grey church standing exposed on a tiny plateau. A path of white shell-dust led from the church door to a small wooden gate.

As he leveled with the gate, a collie dog barked from behind it. The rider paused, noting the dog's grey muzzle and distinctive markings. Glancing toward the church, he saw the door stood wide open. Immediately, he dismounted, tethered his horse to the fence, and stepped into the churchyard.

The collie thrust its nose into the back of his knee, sniffed dubiously, and then erupted into an exuberant welcome. The dog possessed a far superior memory to the innkeeper. It barked, wagged its tail frantically, crouched, and leaped at the stranger's shoulders, spinning in circles before him, emitting sharp cries of delight. It darted to the church door, barking fiercely, then returned to jump upon its friend. Feversham caught the dog as it placed its forepaws on his chest, patting it and laughing.

Suddenly, the laughter died in his throat. He froze, staring toward the open church door. Inside the doorway stood Ethne Eustace.

He released the dog and walked slowly up the path toward her. She remained motionless on the threshold, saying nothing. She watched him until he stood close. Then, simply, she said, "Harry."

Silence fell between them. Neither spoke. All the spectral figures and phantom thoughts that had accompanied him throughout the day dissolved instantly at the sound of her voice. Six years had passed since his boots had crushed the gravel beneath her window on a June dawn. They regarded one another, noting the profound changes wrought by time.

Feversham was gaunt; his face was wasted. The strain of life within the House of Stone had carved deep lines around his sunken eyes and given him an appearance of age far beyond his years. Yet, these were not the only transformations. Ethne perceived something deeper. Though she stood still, her heart ached with grief for the trials he had endured. More importantly, she saw that he returned without a trace of anger regarding the fourth feather torn from her fan.

She was clear-sighted even now. She recognized that the man before her was no longer the timid, diffident youth who had once feared the contempt of others. That hesitation had vanished. He was now quietly self-possessed—not arrogant, yet confident. He had subjected himself to a long, arduous trial and had emerged knowing he had not failed.

Understanding washed over her face, lighting it with realization. "It is not all harm that has come of these years," she said softly. "They were not wasted."

Feversham thought of her lonely existence in this remote village, thinking with a man's perspective, unaware that she would never have chosen to live anywhere else. He studied her face, seeing the marks of suffering there. It was not that she had aged significantly; her large grey eyes shone with the same clarity, and color still brightened her cheeks. But there was more depth to her now. She had suffered; she had tasted of the tree of knowledge.

"I am sorry," he said. "I did you a great wrong six years ago, and I need not."

She extended her hand. "Will you give it to me, please?"

For a moment, he misunderstood. "That fourth feather," she clarified.

Reaching into his coat, Feversham withdrew his letter case and shook two feathers into her palm. Holding out the larger ostrich feather, he waited.

"Both," she insisted.

There was no reason for him to retain Castleton's feather anymore. Since she requested them, he handed both over. She clasped them tightly, smiling as she pressed them against her breast.

"Now I have the four feathers," she declared.

"Yes," Feversham answered. "All four. What will you do with them?"

Ethne's smile transformed into a laugh. "Do with them!" she cried in mock scorn. "I shall do nothing with them. I shall keep them. I am very proud to possess them to keep."
