The three or four "To Let" signs had leaned against the low fence of the little triangular Square for as long as anyone living there could recall. Long ago, perhaps, they had stood upright; now, however, they overhung the palings at wild, precarious angles, resembling a row of wooden axes poised to strike a passerby, though none had ever actually severed a tenant from the flow of humanity. There was hardly any such flow to begin with. The main thoroughfare bypassed the Square by a furlong or more, leaving the neighborhood hemmed in by a labyrinth of tenements, alleys, and byways that had sprouted around the ancient house since its construction. Presumably, the structure itself was merely tolerated standing, awaiting the expiration of a lease or two, after which a total clearance of the district would surely follow.

It was a building of blooming old red brick, its walls scarred with the crests and clasped hands of insurance companies long since dissolved. Children of the secluded Square had once swung on the low gate at the end of the entrance alley until little more than the solid top bar remained. The alley itself ran past boarded-up basement windows where tramps had scrawled their cryptic graffiti, while the path was washed and worn uneven by water spilling from the eaves of the encroaching neighbor. Cats and dogs had claimed the approach as their own domain. Given the slim prospects for tenants, the agents saw no reason to maintain the "To Let" notices in a state of legibility or repair, and indeed, they were left to rot.

For six months, Oleron had passed the old house twice daily—or often more—on his commute from his lodgings to his new workplace, a ten-minute walk away. During those six months, not a single axe-like signboard had fallen across his path. This might have been because he customarily took the opposite side of the Square. However, one morning he chose the route passing the broken gate and the rain-washed entrance alley, pausing before one of the tilted boards. Besides the agent's name, the board bore an announcement, seemingly inscribed during Oleron's own boyhood, stating that the key could be obtained at Number Six.

Oleron was already paying more than an author without private means—and one who habitually ignores his public—could comfortably afford for a separate bedroom and workroom. In addition, he paid a small sum to store the bulk of his grandmother's furniture elsewhere. Furthermore, the book he needed most often ended up at his workspace half a mile away, while urgent notes required during the day were frequently found in the pockets of coats hanging behind his bedroom door. Divided domiciles bred endless inconvenience. Thus, when the hatchet-like notice-board suddenly halted his stride, Oleron first peered down through sparse privet bushes at the boarded basement windows, then gazed upward at the blank, grimy panes of the first floor, and finally to the second floor and the flat stone coping of the roof. Standing for a moment, he rubbed his lean, shaven jaw, glanced once more at the board, and walked slowly across the Square to Number Six.

He knocked and waited two or three minutes. Though the door stood ajar, no answer came. Knocking again, he was met by a long-nosed man in shirt sleeves. "I was asking a blessing on our food," the man explained severely. When Oleron requested the key to the old house, the man retreated. After waiting another five minutes on the step, the man emerged again, chewing thoughtfully on his meal, to announce that the key was lost. "But you won't want it," he remarked. "The entrance door isn't locked, and a push opens all the others. I am the agent, if you are considering renting..."

Oleron crossed back to the Square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, traversed the alley, and turned into the old, wide doorway. To the right, immediately inside, stairs led down to spacious cellars; ahead, a staircase with a carved railing rose, broad and handsome yet filthy. Oleron climbed it, carefully avoiding the rail and walls, and stopped at the first landing. A door facing him was boarded up, but he pushed the one to his right, and an insecure bolt or staple gave way easily. He entered the empty first floor. Spending fifteen minutes exploring the space, he eventually exited.

Without ascending further, he descended and returned to the house of the man who had lost the key. "How much is the rent?" he asked. The figure cited was surprisingly low, a rate justified by the neighborhood's character and the property's abominable state of disrepair. "Is it possible to rent just a single floor?" Oleron inquired. The long-nosed man hesitated; they might... "Who manages it?" Oleron pressed. The man supplied the name of a law firm in Lincoln's Inn. "You might mention my name—Barrett," he added.

Work pressure prevented Oleron from visiting Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but he returned the next day and was instantly offered the entire house for purchase: fifty pounds down, with the balance remaining on mortgage. It took him half an hour to convince the lawyer that he desired only to rent a single floor. This caused considerable hesitation; the lawyer was far from certain he possessed the authority to accommodate such a request. Finally, he was persuaded that, provided the "To Let" boards remained in place and an agreement was reached that the arrangement would terminate automatically without further notice should the whole house be let, something could be arranged. The prospect of the old place suddenly leasing over his head seemed to Oleron the slightest of risks. He promised a decision within a week.

The following day, he revisited the house, touring it from top to bottom, before returning home to bathe. He was utterly captivated by the portion of the house he had decided to claim. Scrubbed clean, repainted, and furnished with his grandmother's antique effects, it ought to be entirely charming. He visited the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of his half-forgotten belongings and to take measurements, then proceeded to a decorator's. He was currently swamped with regular work and had wished the notice-board had caught his eye either sooner or later in the year; however, the quickest solution was to suspend work entirely until after his move.

Two weeks later, his first floor was painted throughout in a tender, elderflower white. The paint was dry, and Oleron was deep in the process of installation. Animated and delighted, he rubbed his hands as he polished and arranged his grandmother's treasures: the tall, lattice-paned china cupboard filled with Derby, Mason, and Spode ware; the large folding Sheraton table; the long, low bookshelves (of which he had commissioned two copies); the chairs; the Sheffield candlesticks; and the riveted rose bowls. He set these against his newly painted white walls—wood paneling cut in the happiest proportions, molded and coffered to fit the low-seated window recesses with a spirit of gaiety and repose unknown to modern builders. The ceilings were lofty, faintly adorned with an old star pattern, and even the tapering moldings of his iron fireplace were as delicately designed as jewelry. Oleron wandered about, constantly rubbing his hands, frequently pausing simply to enjoy the interplay of light between the white rooms.

"Charming, charming!" he murmured to himself. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!" He purchased a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, sealing off his quarters from the rest of the house. Now, if he wished to read in bed, his books were merely a step away in the adjacent room. Throughout the process, he marveled at his extraordinary luck in securing the place. He installed a hat rack in the tiny square hall and hung his hats, caps, and coats. Late at night, passersby looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets could see the glow within Oleron's red blinds, or witness the sudden darkening of one blind followed by the illumination of another as Oleron, holding a candlestick, moved from room to room, making final adjustments to his furniture or preparing to resume the work his removal had interrupted.

As far as the chief business of his life—his writing—was concerned, Paul Oleron treated the world with a courtesy that the world rarely reciprocated. Yet, he seldom bothered to calculate the ledger, nor to determine just how far, at forty-four years of age, he lagged behind his starting handicap. Such an accounting would change nothing, and it risked plunging him into a depression he could ill afford. He had chosen his path long ago, binding himself to it with a commitment from which there was no retreat. Perhaps in his youth, when he was susceptible to influences that were slightly detached, slightly generous, and slightly noble, he had selected this road. Had he ever dared to question himself, he would likely still cling to the belief that a life devoid of nobility, generosity, and selflessness was unworthy of being lived. Only recently, and only fleetingly, had he suspected that there existed more to existence than this rigid code. But it was futile to anticipate the distant day when he might reach the zenith of his powers, beyond which inevitable decline would leave him staring at the harsh truth: perhaps it would have served him better to live by less demanding ideals.

For now, his relocation to the old house, marked by insurance stamps embedded in its bricks, had merely interrupted *Romilly Bishop* at the fifteenth chapter. As this tall man, with his lean, ascetic visage, moved through his new domain rearranging furniture and shifting objects, he projected an air of almost spinster-like precision. For twenty years, inhabiting a dozen different lodgings, garrets, and flats—furnished and unfurnished—he had learned that self-reliance saved both time and temper. Methodical was his creed. He had negotiated with Mrs. Barrett, a stout Welshwoman with a falsetto voice and a Merionethshire accent undimmed by decades in London, to bring his breakfast daily and tidy the premises on Saturdays. Indeed, he welcomed domestic chores as a necessary respite from the strain of composition.

His kitchen, joined to a narrow apartment housing a modern bath, overlooked a grimy alley. At one end stood a large closet with a door and a square sliding hatch near the top. Originally a powder room, through that hatch had been thrust the elaborately dressed heads of men receiving the click-and-puff of a pistol. Oleron stared at the closet, puzzling over its history, until the potential utility dawned upon him. A faint smile touched his lips, stirred by an emotion he couldn't name. Its original purpose was obsolete; it would surely serve as his larder.

It was within this closet that he made his discovery. The rear wall was shelved, and rummaging on an upper shelf buried deep in the cavity, he uncovered two old wooden stands shaped like mushrooms. Their presence was a mystery; undoubtedly, the painters had unearthed them during renovations and stowed them away. But his five rooms were woefully lacking in storage space. Through sheer ingenuity alone, he managed to find niches for his linens, his boxes, and his accumulating hoard of papers—papers he cherished too much to destroy.

Early spring had brought him into the tenancy, and his anxiety was focused on launching *Romilly* by the following autumn. Yet, he refused to force the hand of creation. If the book demanded more time, so be it. He understood its critical importance to his artistic evolution; it required its own rhythm and duration. In his previous workspace, progress had been excellent; *Romilly* had begun to speak and act of her own volition. He trusted she would resume her flow once the disruption of moving settled. That distraction was nearly over. Telling himself it was time to pull himself together, he stepped out on a March morning. Returning shortly after, he carried two great bunches of yellow daffodils, placing one on his mantelpiece beside Sheffield sticks and the other on his desk. Then, he retrieved the half-finished manuscript of *Romilly Bishop*.

Before opening it, however, he visited a small rosewood cabinet, withdrawing his cheque-book and pass-book. Totting up the figures, his monk-like face grew contemplative. The installation had exceeded his budget, leaving him with a balance of less than fifty pounds and no immediate prospect of replenishment.

"Hm! I'd forgotten rugs and chintz curtains add up so quickly," Oleron murmured. "But it would have been a pity to ruin the atmosphere for the sake of ten pounds... Well, *Romilly* absolutely must see print this autumn. That's final."

He drew his papers toward him, ready to begin. But the work failed to start. Or rather, he did not work at all. Outside, the square possessed its own cacophony of new sounds. Oleron hoped to acclimate soon enough. First came the hawkers with their carts and cries; at noon, children trampled into the square, swinging on his gate before departing for afternoon school. Then, an itinerant musician with a mandolin stationed himself beneath his window, strumming a tune. Not unpleasant, this distraction proved. Pushing up his sash, Oleron tossed the man a penny.

Returning to his table, he found he could not write. Periodically, he would snap back to reality only to discover he had been wandering his room, lost in memories of its former state: had a buttercup-satin settee stood by the far window? Did a crystal chandelier hang from the high ceiling's center molding? Where had the tambour frame or the picquet table rested? No, it was useless. He would rather do nothing fruitfully than tire himself in vain. Deciding to walk, he sat down in his chair and drifted into a doze.

"This won't do," he yawned upon waking at half-past four. "I must perform better tomorrow..." Yet, he felt such a delicious laziness that for several minutes he entertained the thought of breaking his evening appointment.

The next morning, he sat to work without answering any of his three letters—two bills from tradesmen, and a note from Miss Bengough forwarded from his old address. It was a jolly day of white and blue skies, with a noisy, gay wind and a subtle shift in the colors of budding nature. Over and over, every minute or so, his room flashed bright and dim as shining white clouds rolled northeastward across the square. The soft, fitful light danced on the polished tabletop and the worn floorboards. The morning noises resumed.

Oleron dotted a pattern on his paper, then stopped to adjust the jar of daffodils directly opposite a creamy panel. He wrote a sentence that flowed for two lines before dissolving into notes and scribbles. For a while, he convinced himself these memoranda constituted real work. Then, rising, he paced the room. Suddenly, an idea struck him. The space needed more positive color. It was perhaps a thought too pale—mild and sweet as a kindly old face, yet slightly devitalized, wan. Yes, it certainly called for a robust note: more vibrant flowers, perhaps some warm, cheerful fabric for the window seats.

"Of course, I truly cannot afford it," he muttered, grabbing a tape measure to gauge the width of the window recesses.

As he stooped to measure, his posture shifted instantly to one of intense interest. Rising again, he rubbed his hands with gentle glee. "Oho, oho!" he exclaimed. "These look remarkably like window boxes, nailed shut. We must investigate! Yes, those are boxes, or I'm... oho, this is an adventure!"

On the wall of his sitting-room, there were two windows (the third lay in a separate corner), and beyond the open bedroom door, another window shared that same wall. The seats beneath all had been painted, repainted, and varnished repeatedly; Oleron's probing finger had barely scratched the old nailheads hidden beneath layers of fresh paint. Below the ledge he leaned over, an old keyhole had been neatly puttied over.

Oleron produced his penknife. Working carefully for five minutes, he retreated to the kitchen for a hammer and chisel. Driving the chisel cautiously under the seat, he pried the entire lid loose. Using the penknife again, he sliced along the hinged edge and outward along the ends. Fetching a wooden wedge and mallet, he prepared for the reveal.

"Now for our little mystery," he whispered.

The sound of the mallet striking the wedge seemed strangely brutal, even shocking, within that sweet, pale apartment. The panelling rang, rattled, and vibrated like a sounding board. The entire house appeared to awaken; from the spacious cellars to the garrets above, a flock of echoes stirred, and the noise began to grate on Oleron's nerves. Abruptly, he paused, grabbed a duster, and wrapped the mallet in cloth.

Once the edge was sufficiently raised, he slipped his fingers underneath and lifted. Paint flaked and starred; rusty old nails squeaked and groaned; the lid rose, revealing the hollow box beneath. Oleron peered inside. Aside from a couple of inches of scurf, mold, and ancient cobwebs, it was empty.

"No treasure there," Oleron remarked, amused that he had imagined otherwise. "*Romilly* will still need to appear by autumn. Let us inspect the others."

He turned to the second window. Raising the remaining two seats occupied him until late afternoon. The bedroom seat, like the first, yielded nothing. But from the second seat in the sitting-room, he extracted something yielding, folded, and fur-lined, covered in an inch-thick layer of dust.

It piqued Oleron slightly that his friend, Miss Bengough, should dismiss with such casual indifference the very spot he had found so singularly captivating. Indeed, she barely lifted her eyes toward it. Yet, this was characteristic of her; she had always been somewhat detached from life's finer graces, careless of appearances, and perhaps even more comfortable devouring biscuits from a paper bag than dining with strict adherence to social convention.

She was an unmarried journalist of thirty-four, large and imposing, fair as butter, pink as a dog-rose, resembling a florist's finest, most carefully selected bloom. She was prone to sudden, expansive movements and moist, explosive speech. As she put it, she "pulled a better living out of the pool" than Oleron did; through cunningly disguised advertisements for drapers and haberdashers, she also secured the majority of her remarkably varied wardrobe. When she moved, she left behind small whirlwinds of air in which her veils and scarves fluttered and spun.

Oleron had heard the rustle of her skirts on his staircase and her single, loud knock at his door just a month after moving into his new apartment. Her clothes seemed to carry the outside air with them. She tossed a bundle of women's journals onto a chair.

"Don't stop for me," she declared, speaking around a mouthful of large-headed hatpins as she removed her hat and veil. "I wasn't sure if you were settled yet, so I brought some sandwiches for lunch. You must have coffee, surely?—No, don't rise—I'll find the kitchen—"

"Oh, that's perfectly fine; I'll clear these away," Oleron replied. He gathered his papers and tucked them aside. She was already in the kitchen; he heard the rush of water filling the kettle. A few minutes later, he followed her back to the sitting-room carrying the coffee and sandwiches on a tray.

They seated themselves, placing the tray on a small table between them.

"Well, what do you think of the new place?" Oleron asked as she poured the coffee.

"Hm!... Anyone would think you were planning to get married, Paul."

He laughed. "Oh, no. But it's an improvement on some of them, isn't it?"

"Is it? I suppose so; I don't know. I liked the last place, despite the black ceiling and lack of a tap. How is *Romilly*?"

Oleron rubbed his chin thoughtfully. "Hm! I'm rather ashamed to admit it. The fact is, I haven't gotten along very well with it. But it will be all right by the time it's published, as you used to say."

"Stuck?"

"Rather stuck."

"Have any of it you care to read to me?..."

Oleron had long been accustomed to reading portions of his work to Miss Bengough. Her critiques were invariably swift and practical, sometimes directly useful, other times subtly suggestive. In exchange for his confidence, she strictly withheld any mention of her own writing from him. He considered his work "real"; hers merely filled space, often lacking even basic grammar.

"I'm afraid there isn't," Oleron replied, continuing to shave his chin meditatively. Then, with a sudden burst of honesty, he added, "The fact is, Elsie, I haven't written—not actually written—much more of it. Not *any* more of it, in fact. But, of course, that doesn't mean I haven't progressed. I have progressed, in one sense, rather alarmingly. I am now considering reconstructing the entire thing."

Miss Bengough let out a sharp gasp. "Reconstructing!"

"Making *Romilly* herself a different kind of woman. Somehow, I've begun to feel I'm not extracting the best from her. As she currently stands, I've certainly lost interest in her to some degree."

"But—but—" Miss Bengough protested. "You had her so real, so *living*, Paul!"

Oleron offered a faint smile. He had expected Miss Bengough's disapproval. He wasn't surprised that she cherished *Romilly* as she currently existed; she would. Whether she realized it or not, there was much of herself in his fictional creation. Naturally, *Romilly* would seem "real," "living," to her.

"But are you being serious, Paul?" Miss Bengough asked eventually, her eyes wide and staring.

"Quite serious."

"You're really going to discard those fifteen chapters?"

"I didn't exactly say that."

"That magnificent, rich love scene?"

"I would only do it reluctantly, and for the sake of something I believe is superior."

"And that exquisite, *beautiful* description of *Romilly* on the shore?"

"It wouldn't necessarily be wasted," he said, feeling a slight unease.

But Miss Bengough made a grand, sweeping gesture and then unleashed her criticism. "Really, you are *too* difficult!" she exploded. "I do wish you would sometimes remember you're human, and live in a world! You know I'd be the *last* person to wish you to lower your standard by an inch, but it wouldn't count as lowering it to bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you are sometimes altogether too godlike!... Why, it would be a wicked, criminal squandering of your talents to destroy those fifteen chapters! Look at it reasonably, now. You've been working for nearly twenty years; you now have what you've been striving for almost within your grasp; your personal affairs are at a most critical juncture (oh, please don't tell me; I know you're nearing the end of your funds); and here you are, deliberately proposing to withdraw something that will likely secure your fame, and replace it with something that ten to one nobody on earth will ever want to read—and they'd have every right to refuse! Honestly, you try my patience!"

Oleron had nodded slowly as she spoke. It was an old narrative between them. The noisy, capable, pragmatic journalist was an excellent friend—up to a certain limit; beyond that... well, everyone knows that boundary where one stands alone. Elsie Bengough occasionally remarked that if she possessed one-tenth of Oleron's genius, there were few things she couldn't achieve—thereby rendering that genius a quantitatively divisible entity, an ingredient to be added or subtracted in the mixture of his work. That it was a qualitative essence, fundamental and indivisible, was beyond her understanding. Their spirits diverged at that point. Oleron knew it. She seemed unaware of it.

"Yes, yes, yes," he said wearily after a while, "practically you are entirely correct, completely right, and I have no words to counter. If I could only entrust *Romilly* to you, you would create an enormous success with her. But that is impossible, and for my part, I am seriously questioning whether she is worth my effort anymore. You know what that implies."

"What does it imply?" she demanded bluntly.

"Well," he said, offering a wan smile, "what *does* it mean when you are convinced a thing is not worth doing? You simply cease to do it."

Miss Bengough's gaze swept the ceiling, searching for support against this impossible man. "What utter nonsense!" she finally erupted. "Why, when I saw you last, you were practically dripping with *Romilly*; you were producing her at the rate of four chapters a week; if you hadn't moved, you would have had three-quarters of it completed by now. What on earth possessed you to move right in the midst of your most vital work?"

Oleron attempted to deflect her with a list of inconveniences, but she refused to accept it. Perhaps deep down she partially suspected the true reason. He was simply exhausted by the narrow confines of his existence. He had endured twenty years of it—twenty years of garrets, attic rooms, dingy flats, and shabby lodgings—and he was weary of the grime and squalor. The reward remained as distant as ever—or if it was not, he no longer cared, as he once would have, to stretch out his hand and seize it. It is all very well to tell a man on the brink of collapse that only one final effort is needed; if he cannot make it, he remains as far away as before.

"Anyway," Oleron summarized, "I am happier here than I have been for a long time. That is some sort of justification."

"And accomplishing no work," Miss Bengough stated pointedly.

At that, a minor irritability that had been simmering within Oleron reached its peak. "And why should I do nothing but work?" he demanded. "How much happier am I because of it? I don't claim I don't love my work—once it is finished; but I hate the act of creating it. Sometimes it becomes an unbearable burden that I desperately wish to escape. Once in many weeks, it possesses a moment, a single moment, of brilliance and excitement for me; I recall the days when it was all glow and thrill; and now I am forty-four, and it is becoming mere drudgery. No one wants it; I am ceasing to want it myself; and if any ordinary sensible man were to ask me whether I didn't think I was a fool to persist, I believe I would agree that I was."

Miss Bengough's pretty, pink face grew solemn. "But you knew all that, many, many years ago, Paul—and yet you chose it," she said softly.

"Well, and how could I have known?"

As Oleron sat by his fire that evening, contemplating Miss Bengough's prophecy that difficulties awaited him in his work, he arrived at a singular conclusion: it would have been infinitely preferable had she kept her predictions to herself. No man accomplishes a task with greater efficacy when his confidence is dampened at the outset; to speak of obstacles is, in a profound sense, to conjure them. Speech itself becomes a deterrent ritual, to which further discouragements cling like barnacles, until the very event warned against becomes statistically probable. He despised her counsel. A force hostile to the completion of *Romilly* had taken root. And in some illogical, dogmatic fashion peculiar to women, she had anchored this antagonistic influence to his new residence. Was there anything so absurd? "You'll never finish *Romilly* here." ... Why not? Was this her conception of the luxury that drains the springs of action and reduces a man to indolent obscurity? The location was adequate—indeed, entirely charming—but hardly so demoralizing as she implied. No; Elsie had missed the mark that afternoon.

He shifted his chair to survey the room, which seemed to smile, positively to beam, under the firelight. He too smiled, offering a silent pity for the maligned apartment. Even the slight lack of robust color he had noted earlier vanished in the soft glow. The drawn chintz curtains—their fabric a trellis of flowers and baskets—fell in long, quiet folds to the window seats; the rows of bindings in the old bookcases drank the light richly; the last trace of sallowness had departed with the daylight. If the truth must be told, it had been Elsie herself who had seemed slightly out of step with the scene. That reflection struck him deeply, and soon he returned to it. Indeed, the room had, quite accidentally, performed Miss Bengough a disservice that afternoon. It had, in some subtle yet undeniable manner, highlighted a contrast of qualities. Assuming, for the sake of argument, the slightly ridiculous notion that the room in which Oleron sat was characterized by a certain sparsity and lack of vigor, so much the worse for Miss Bengough; she certainly erred on the side of redundancy and sheer "muchness." And if one must contrast abstract attributes, Oleron inclined toward the austere in taste.

Yes, here Oleron had made a distinct discovery; he marveled that he had not seen it sooner. He pictured Miss Bengough again as she had appeared that afternoon—large, showy, moistly pink, radiating a quality akin to a prize bloom exuding from her pores—and instantly she suffered in his imagination. He even recognized now that he had noticed something odd at the time, and that his attitude, even while she stood there, had been one of critical detachment. The machinery of her existence was somewhat transparent; her melting humidity was the result of analyzable processes; and behind her had seemed to lurk a dim shape emblematic of mortality. He had never, during the ten years of their intimacy, dreamt for a single moment of asking her to marry him; none the less, he now felt, for the first time, a profound gratitude that he had not done so.

Then, suddenly and swiftly, his face flushed crimson at the thought of applying such analysis to his friend. What! Elsie Bengough, with whom he had spent countless afternoons—she, the loyal comrade upon whose aid he would have relied had the entire world failed him—she, whose devotion would not swerve so long as there was breath in her body—to be dissected even in thought! He was an ingrate and a cad. Had she been present in that moment, he would have abased himself before her. For ten minutes or more he sat, staring into the fire, watching that humiliating red fade slowly from his cheeks. All was still within and without, save for a tiny, musical tinkling emanating from the kitchen—the drip of water from an imperfectly turned-off tap into the vessel below. Mechanically, he began to beat his fingers against his knee, syncing with the faintly heard falling of the drops; the tiny, regular movement seemed to hasten the shameful retreat from his face. He cooled once more, and when he resumed his meditation, he was wholly unconscious that he had picked up exactly where he had left off.

It was not merely her florid superfluity of build that he had approached with an attitude of criticism; he was acutely aware of the vast divergence between her mind and his own. He felt no gratitude that, up to a certain point, their natures had ever run companionably side by side; he was now filled with questions regarding everything beyond that point. Their intellects diverged; there was no denying it. Looking back, he was inclined to doubt whether there had ever been any genuine coincidence. True, he had read his writings to her, and she had appeared to comprehend and respond to the point; but what can a man do who, having assumed that another sees as he does, is suddenly jolted by something that falsifies and discredits all that has gone before? He doubted everything now.

For a fleeting moment, it occurred to him that the man who demands of a friend more than can possibly be given is in danger of losing that friend, but he dismissed the thought. Once again, he ceased thinking, and once again moved his finger to the distant dripping of the tap.

And now (he resumed by-and-by), if these truths held for Elsie Bengough, they also applied to the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop. Since he could say of Romilly, with a shame he could not admit regarding Elsie, that he hated the central character of his novel, he gave his thoughts free rein. He did so in that smiling, fire-lit room, accompanied by the faint, rhythmic tap. There was no longer any doubt; he loathed the protagonist of his book. Even as he had described her physically, she overwhelmed the senses; she was coarse-fibred, over-colored, rank. It became true the moment he formulated the thought. Gulliver had described the Brobdingnagian maids-of-honor precisely thus; and mentally and spiritually, she corresponded—unsensitive, limited, common. The model (he closed his eyes for a moment)—the model protruded through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters to such a degree that, without understanding why, he had been unable to begin the sixteenth. He marveled that it had only just dawned upon him. And *this* was to have been his Beatrice, his vision! As Elsie, she was meant to enter the furnace of his art and emerge as the Woman all men desire! Her thoughts were to be culled from his finest musings, her form from his dearest dreams, her setting chosen from anywhere fitting for her worth. He had brooded long before attempting the feat; then, one day, he had felt her stir within him as a mother feels a quickening, and he had begun to write; thus, he had added chapter to chapter... And those fifteen sodden chapters were the result!

Again he sat, softly moving his finger. Then he roused himself. She must go—all fifteen chapters of her. That was settled. Regarding what would take her place, his mind was a blank; but one thing at a time. A man is not excused from taking the wrong course simply because the right one is not immediately revealed. Better things might come if they were to come; in the meantime—

He rose, fetched the fifteen chapters, and read them over before casting them into the fire. Instead of throwing them onto the flames, however, he let them slip from his hand. He became conscious again of the dripping tap. It possessed a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes, sounding foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like. In his mind, Oleron visualized the gathering of each drop, its little tremble on the lip of the tap, and the tiny percussion of its fall, "Plink—plunk," minimized almost to inaudibility. Following the lowest note, there seemed to be a brief phrase, irregularly repeated; and presently, Oleron found himself waiting for the recurrence of this phrase. It was quite pretty.... But it did not conduce to wakefulness, and Oleron dozed over his fire.

A brief silence fell over the group before a vague inquiry broke through: "Did Mrs. Barrett ever... *see* anything, like?"

Barrett turned on the speaker with austere severity. "What Mrs. Barrett witnessed, and what she did not witness, shall never pass these lips. Even as it is written: keep thy tongue from speaking evil."

Another voice cut in. "He was nearly canned up in the *Waggon and Horses* that night, wasn't he, Jim?"

"Yes, he hadn't half coped it..."

"Not standing treat much either; he was alone in the bar..."

"So he was; we discussed it thoroughly..."

The fat, scared-eyed man seized another opportunity. "She took the key from me—she knew the number! She came into my shop on a Tuesday evening..."

No one paid him heed.

"Shut your heads," a heavy laborer grunted roughly. "She hasn't been found yet. Here come the inspectors; we shall learn more shortly."

Two inspectors approached the constables guarding the gate. The little fat man darted forward eagerly, shouting that she had purchased the key from him. "I remember the number clearly, because of the repetition—it was three ones and three threes! 111333!" he cried, his excitement bordering on hysteria.

An inspector gently pushed him aside. "Has anyone entered?" he asked a constable.

"No, sir."

"Then you, Brackley, come with us. You, Smith, hold the gate. A squad is en route."

Brackley and the inspector descended the alley and crossed the threshold into the house. They ascended the wide, intricately carved staircase.

"This doesn't look as though he has ventured out much recently," one inspector muttered, kicking aside a pile of dead leaves and crumpled paper lying outside Oleron's door. "We needn't knock. Break a pane, Brackley."

The door featured two glazed panels. With a sharp crack of shattering glass, Brackley forced his elbow through the hole, reached inside, and pulled back the latch.

"Faugh!" choked one inspector as they stepped inside. "Quick, let some light and air in. It smells like a hearse—"

Outside in the square, the crowd watched as the red blinds rose and the windows of the old house were thrown wide open.

"That is better," said the inspector, leaning out of a window to draw a deep, cleansing breath. "That appears to be the bedroom. Will you enter, Simms, while I survey the rest?"

They had raised the bedroom blind as well. Within, a waxy-white, emaciated figure lay on the bed, shielding his eyes with his hand against the torturous flood of daylight. He could scarcely believe his senses, for there were two policemen bent over him, demanding to know where "she" was. He shook his head mutely.

"This woman Bengough... she goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough... d'ye hear? Where is she?..."

"No good, Brackley; get him up. Be careful with him. I think I'll just stick my head out of the window myself..."

Meanwhile, the other inspector had searched Oleron's study and found nothing, now turning his attention to the kitchen. He kicked aside an ankle-deep mass of vegetable refuse cluttering the floor. The kitchen window lacked a blind and was overshadowed by the blank wall of the house opposite across the alley. The room appeared empty save for the debris. Yet, as the inspector brushed aside the dead flowers, he noticed a shuffling track—not his own—that had been swept neatly to a cupboard in the corner.

On the upper portion of the cupboard door sat a square panel that seemed designed to slide on runners. The main door was closed. The inspector advanced, extended his hand to the tiny knob, and slid the hatch along its groove. Immediately, he took an involuntary step backward.

Framed within the aperture, falling forward slightly before jamming back into place, was something resembling a large, lumpy pudding, wrapped in a pudding-bag of faded, brownish-red frieze.

"Ah!" the inspector breathed.

To close the hatch again, he would have had to push that grotesque object back with his hand; somehow, the mere idea of touching it repelled him. Instead, he turned the handle of the cupboard itself. There was immense weight behind it—so much weight that after opening the door only three or four inches and peering inside, he had to brace his shoulder against it to force it shut. As he closed it, a triangle of black-and-white checked skirt stuck out a few inches from the floor.

Stepping into the small hall, he called out, "All right!"

They had finally dressed Oleron. He still used his hands as blinkers, his mind a chaotic tangle. He struggled to comprehend the extraordinary mess of dead flowers scattered everywhere; he couldn't fathom why police officers were in his room; he couldn't understand why one was summoned for a four-wheeler and a stretcher; nor could he grasp what heavy article they seemed to be maneuvering around in the kitchen—*his* kitchen.

"What's the matter?" he muttered sleepily.

Then he heard a murmur rising in the square, accompanied by the stopping of a four-wheeler outside. A police officer leaned close to his ear, and Oleron wondered why, when he whispered something, the officer launched into a rapid string of words regarding something "used in evidence against you."

They lifted him to his feet and assisted him toward the door. No, Oleron could make no sense of it. They guided him down the stairs and along the alley. Oleron sensed confused, angry shouts; he gathered that a mob desired to lynch someone. His attention then snagged on the little fat, frightened-eyed man, who appeared to be making a statement that an officer was diligently transcribing into a notebook.

"I'd seen her with him... they were often together... she came into my shop and said it was for him... I thought it was all right... 111333 was the number," the man stammered.

The people seemed furious; many police were struggling to hold them back. However, one inspector possessed a voice that Oleron found surprisingly kind and friendly. He was instructing someone to load another person into the cab before whatever object was being brought out caused a delay. Oleron noticed a four-wheeler drawn up at the gate. It was clear he was the intended passenger. As they hoisted him up, he saw the inspector attempt to stand between him and something positioned behind the cab, but it was too late. Oleron caught sight of it: a hooded stretcher.

The angry voices roared like a sea. Something hard, like a stone, struck the back of the cab. The inspector followed Oleron inside and stood with his back to the window, facing the side where the mob waited. The door they had placed Oleron in remained open, seemingly until the other inspector arrived. Through the gap, Oleron glimpsed the hatchet-like "To Let" boards nestled among the privet trees.
