The air in the room hung heavy, thick with the aftermath of Mr. Thornton's visit. Margaret sat frozen, the echoes of his voice still vibrating in her ears like a trapped bird. A strange, involuntary comparison had taken root in her mind between Mr. Lennox and Mr. Thornton. With Lennox, the line between friendship and love had been dangerously blurred, only to snap back with immediate regret on both sides. But with Thornton, there had been no gentle slope. From the start, their paths had been drawn in opposition; his opinions clashed with hers, dismissed not with kindness, but with a rock-like disdain that made her protests feel futile. Yet, now, he had returned in a wild, passionate storm, declaring his love.

At first, Margaret had feared the confession was forced, a desperate reaction to her humiliation. But within moments, a terrifying clarity settled over her. He did not love her merely because he pities her; he loved her. The conviction burned brightly, undeniable and absolute. She shrank back, shivering as if confronted by some ancient, terrible power. To hide from it was useless; his "strong idea" haunted her thoughts, wandering relentlessly through her mind. How dared he claim endurance when she had shaken him off with contempt? Regret gnawed at her as she realized she had failed to speak with sufficient strength, wishing desperately for the words that now crowded her mind but remained unuttered. The interview lingered like a nightmare horror, refusing to fade even as she attempted to wake from it, cowering in the corner of her mind with ghastly eyes, waiting to see if she dared breathe a word of it to anyone.

Driven away from this threatening vision, Margaret sought action. She needed to shake off the paralysis of the past hour. Opening the window to let in the stifling air, then stepping out into the profound silence of the house, she found herself unable to remain alone. The stillness of the midday, broken only by the labored breathing of an invalid, pressed upon her. An impulse seized her: *Go and see Bessy Higgins.* The thought of the girl's suffering flashed into her mind, offering a distraction from her internal turmoil.

Bessy lay flat on the settle, moved close to the fire despite the sultry heat, as if recovering from a violent paroxysm of pain. Margaret, noting the girl's lack of breath, raised her and rearranged the pillows with quiet efficiency. When Bessy finally spoke, her voice was weak and wistful. "I thought I should na' ha' seen yo' again." Margaret confessed she had not been able to come sooner due to her mother's illness, a small white lie that brought color to her cheeks.

Bessy explained that sending Mary for Margaret had pushed her limits, the earlier riotous scenes having torn her apart. She craved Margaret's voice, seeking some words of peace to help her drift into the silence of God, much like a baby lulled by a mother's song. When Margaret offered to read, Bessy eagerly accepted, promising that while the sense might seem distant at first, the comforting texts would draw close.

As Margaret began to read, Bessy's body twitched, restless and convulsed. Suddenly, she burst out, "Don't go on reading. It's no use. I'm blaspheming all the time in my mind, wi' thinking angrily on what canna be helped." She shifted to the subject of the riot at Marlborough Mills, specifically Thornton's factory.

Margaret, coloring deeply, asked if her father had been present. "Not he," Bessy replied, her spirit dimmed. "He'd ha' given his right hand if it had never come to pass. It's that that's fretting me. He's fairly knocked down in his mind by it." She described the committee's strategy: unity, patience, and strict adherence to the law, avoiding any provocation that might justify violence. They believed they were right in their demands and trusted the public to see justice in their suffering. However, the plan had unraveled through the actions of Boucher, a foolish man who had gone contrary to orders.

"He ruined th' strike, just as bad as if he meant to be a Judas," Bessy whispered, her voice trembling. "Eh! but father giv'd it him last night! He said he'd tell the police where to find the ringleader, give him up to the mill-owners... For," she dropped her voice, "they do say that Boucher threw a stone at Thornton's sister—that welly killed her."

"That's not true!" Margaret exclaimed, her face flushing red then draining white. "It was not Boucher that threw the stone."

"You'd be there then?" Bessy asked, her eyes languid.

"Yes. Never mind." Margaret insisted firmly. "But what did he answer to your father?"

"He did na' speak words," Bessy recounted, describing Boucher's spent passion, his quick breath, and the thought that he might be sobbing. "But when father said he'd give him up to police, he gave a great cry, and struck father on th' face wi' his closed fist, and he off like lightning."

Her father, stunned by the blow, had sat down, putting his hand before his eyes before rushing out. Bessy had thrown herself onto the settle, clinging to him. "Father, father!" she had cried. "Thou'll never go peach on that poor clemmed man." Her father, pale and bloody-faced, had shaken her off, vowing that while he despised Boucher, he could not betray him now that the man had struck him. He intended to fight him later, in proper condition, but for now, he would not denounce him.

"I know not if I slept or waked," Bessy murmured, growing drowsy again. "Read me not a sermon chapter, but a story chapter; they've pictures in them..." She drifted off, muttering pleas for the New Heavens and New Earth, seeking an escape from the weariness of her reality.

Margaret covered the girl with a blanket and left, carrying an uneasy conscience. Back home, she found her mother, Mrs. Hale, in the drawing-room. Her mother was in one of her better days, praising the water-bed with a fervor that bordered on obsession. She claimed the beds of former times possessed a quality lost to modern manufacturing, a sentiment Mr. Hale gently disputed, attributing the difference to the vitality of youth. His wife dismissed his theory, insisting it was the specific craftsmanship of Sir John Beresford's beds that mattered.

Margaret laughed, admitting she rarely thought about her bed, falling asleep instantly wherever she lay. This prompted her mother to recount stories of her husband's childhood, contrasting the beauty of little Frederick with Margaret's own infancy. "I said, 'Dear, what an ugly little thing!' And she said, 'It's not every child that's like Master Fred,'" Mrs. Hale recalled, her voice filled with affection. "Everybody loved him. He was born with the gift of winning hearts. It makes me think very badly of Captain Reid when I know that he disliked my own dear boy."

Then, the mood shifted. Mrs. Hale's eyes grew misty, her voice choking. "Your poor father, Margaret. He has left the room. He can't bear to hear Fred spoken of." Margaret sat by her mother's sofa, holding her hand, as tears flowed freely. Finally, Mrs. Hale sat upright, her tone solemn and urgent. "If I can get better, it must be through seeing my son Frederick once more. It will waken up all the poor springs of health left in me."

"And, Margaret," she continued, her voice quavering with the contemplation of impending death, "if I am to die... I must see my child first. I charge you, Margaret, as you yourself hope for comfort in your last illness, bring him to me that I may bless him. Only for five minutes... Let me see him before I die!"

Margaret felt the weight of the entreaty. Logic and caution held no sway against the passionate plea of a dying woman. The sight of her mother's large, pleading eyes fixed upon her compelled her to vow compliance. "Mamma, I will write to-night, and tell Frederick what you say. I am as sure that he will come directly to us, as I am sure of my life."

"But the post goes out at five!" Mrs. Hale urged, trembling with feverish eagerness. "Write by it, won't you? Don't lose a single post; for just by that very post I may miss him."

"Papa is out," Margaret reminded her gently.

"Papa is out! and what then?" Mrs. Hale snapped, her eyes flashing. "Do you mean that he would deny me this last wish? Why, I should not be ill, be dying—if he had not taken me away from Helstone, to this unhealthy, smoky, sunless place!"

"Oh, mamma!" Margaret sighed.

"Yes; it is so, indeed," her mother insisted. "He knows it himself... The longing to see Frederick stands between me and God. I cannot pray till I have this one thing." She fell back, exhausted, convinced that her son would arrive within twenty-two days.

"You are not writing!" Mrs. Hale demanded suddenly, sitting up again. "Bring me some pens and paper; I will try and write myself." She trembled, greedily watching Margaret prepare to write.
