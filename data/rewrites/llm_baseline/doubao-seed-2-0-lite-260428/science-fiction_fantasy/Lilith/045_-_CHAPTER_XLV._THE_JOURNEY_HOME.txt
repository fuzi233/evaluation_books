Darkness had unspooled and vanished long before we noticed its passing. We trod soft heather underfoot in a pale, honeyed twilight, and the air we breathed did not just carry the scent of coming spring—it was the spring, warm and alive against our throats. A wonder had unmade the old world around us… or perhaps the far greater miracle was that the old, veiled world had unmade itself inside us. There was no harsh sun, no sharp moon glow spilling from the sky to carve shadows, yet every heather frond, every tiny sapling, every single blade of grass blazed clear as if lit from within: it was the same light that had burned unconsumed in the desert bush Moses had knelt before, seeping out of every growing thing, or else pouring out of our own eyes to meet them. No thing cast a shadow. All living things traded tiny, wispy sparks of light between them, no hoarding, no hiding.

Every plant showed me not just its shape, not just its hue, but the core idea that hummed inside it—the informing, living thought that made it what it was, that breathed it into existence. My bare feet ached to press into the earth beneath every stem I stepped over, to greet each small life as I passed. The world and my own being, its life and mine, were stitched together at last. The microcosm of my soul and the wide macrocosm of all creation had finally been reconciled, finally slipped into perfect harmony. I lived in every fern, every bee, every breeze that brushed my cheek; every small life seeped into me and lived there too. To be aware of a thing was to know its life and mine all at once, to know where we had both come from, to know where we belonged at home— to know that every one of us is what we are, because Another, the Source, is what He is.

Sense after sense I had never known I possessed, long asleep, woke one by one, layer after layer: indescribable, these new senses, for no old words, no old metaphors, had ever been forged to name them. My whole conscious being stretched wider and wider, ever making space to receive, as a hundred new doors swung open at once to let the world pour in. When a soft gust tangled in a heather bush and set its purple bells ringing, I felt the joy of the bells as my own, the joy of the breeze that stirred them to that bright tin-tinning chime, the joy of the soul that drank all those small joys in all at once. My being was a great, quiet hall, and every glad thing I passed came in to feast there. I was a calm, endless ocean, and the groundswell of one great, living joy kept lifting new waves to the surface—yet it was all the same eternal joy, wearing a hundred thousand different lovely faces. Life was a cosmic holiday.

I understood then that life and truth were one. Pure, unfiltered life is bliss in its very core; any state of being that is not bliss is not real life at all, but life-in-death. Every breath I drew of the soft dark wind that blew where it willed slipped out of me as a sigh of pure thanksgiving. At last I was. I lived, and nothing could ever touch this life of mine. My beloved Lona walked at my side, and we were heading home, to the Father. This much had been given to us before the very first sun rose on our new freedom: what glories, then, would that eternal day bring when it finally broke?

We reached the ragged, gutted hollow where the old earth’s monsters had once wallowed in filth, and as my dream had shown me long before, it was a glass-smooth, lovely lake now. I leaned over to gaze into its pellucid depths. A great whirlpool had torn out all the rotting soil those abominations had burrowed in, and the whole horrid brood lay visible on the lake bed. A dim, soft green light seeped through the crystalline water, laying bare every hideous form below. They coiled in spires, folded over on themselves in greasy layers, knotted tight, or stretched long and slack in motionless heaps—ghoulish, blighted shapes more monstrous than any fevered imagination of a wine-drunk, exhausted poet could ever conjure. No diver who had ever plunged into the churning Maelstrom had seen horrors to match these: tentacles twisting in writhing knots, bloated bulging flesh, glaring squid-ink eyes of utter wrongness, would have looked gentle, innocent beside these walking incarnations of hate—each of their heads a wicked, bloated flower that burst from an abominable stalk to perfect every last shred of its evil purpose.

Not one of them stirred as we passed. But they were not dead. So long as men and women with unwholesome, twisted minds walk the worlds, that lake will never empty of its loathsome population.

But listen— the sun’s herald, the auroral wind, was blowing soft, clear notes on a trumpet to announce his arrival. The great minister of the human tabernacle was drawing near! Heaped before his prow was a great wave of rippling crimson and liquid gold, and he rushed upward through the sky as if he had just been launched from his Maker’s outstretched hand into the vast upper sea. He paused, hanging high, and looked down on the world. He was a storm of burning, white-hot molten metal, and he was no more than a single coal plucked from the altar of the Father’s never-ending sacrifice for His children.

Look—every small flower straightens its stalk, lifts its thin neck, and stands with outstretched head, waiting. Something more than the sun, greater than mere light, is coming, coming. It does not matter that it takes a long time on the road: it is no less certain for that. What do a day, a year, ten thousand years matter to Life Himself, to Love Himself? He is coming, coming, and all of humanity are stretching their necks out to see Him arrive. Every morning they will stretch out thus, every evening they will droop and rest, and wait—until He comes. Is this no more than a vision spun out of thin air? When He arrives, will He find them still watching, still waiting?

It was a glorious resurrection morning. The whole long night before had been spent preparing for it. The Little Ones gamboled ahead of us, laughing, and the great gentle beasts followed close behind. Butterflies fluttered, dragonflies darted around our heads, a swirling cloud of colour and quick flashes of light: now they descended on us like a snowstorm made of rainbow flakes, now they rose back into the damp air like a rolling vapour woven out of embodied scents. It was a summer day more true to itself, more ideal, than any human who had not passed through the veil of death could ever have found in any world.

I walked the new earth, under the new heaven, and they were the same old world I had always known—save that now they had opened their minds to me, and I could see right into their hearts. Now the soul of every thing I met came out to greet me, to make friends, to tell me we all came from the same Source, all strove for the same end. They told me I was heading to the One they dwelled with always, the One they were always meant to be: they said they were lightnings that took shape the very moment they flashed out from Him to His creation. The dark crags drank the falling light like sponges, the whole wide world soaked up the sun’s rays, and breathed out new, living life. Lona and I were two bright fires of joy, walking side by side. Earth breathed its sweet, incense-like smoke up toward heaven; we breathed our longing desires homeward, to the Father. That pure, unbroken gratitude was the very shape of our consciousness, in that moment.

We reached the old river channels that had once been dry, dust-choked, weary waste, and now they ran and flashed and foamed with living water that shouted for joy as it moved. As far as the eye could see, it was one rushing, roaring, dashing river of water made vocal by the rocks it tumbled over. We did not cross it. We walked in glory and joy up its right bank, until we reached the great cataract at the foot of the old sandy desert, where the water roared and swirled and dropped sheer over the edge, splitting into two wide branches. We climbed the steep height above the falls, and found no desert at all. Grass spread out in soft plains on either side, and the deep, wide, silent river flowed full to its banks between grassy edges.

It was then that the Little Ones first saw the blazing glory of God made manifest in that limpid, flowing water. Instinctively they plunged in and swam, and the great beasts followed after them. The old desert had rejoiced, and blossomed like a rose. Wide forests had sprung up where there had only been sand, their undergrowth thick with flowering shrubs full of singing birds. Every thicket gave birth to a small rivulet, and every rivulet sang its own water-song as it flowed. There was no trace left of the place where the cursed buried hand had once rested. Further and still further beyond, the great river rolled in full, unbroken volume from the far horizon.

We traveled up and up, now along soft grassy banks, now through groves of gracious, sweet-smelling trees. The grass grew sweeter under our feet, its flowers more lovely and more varied the further we went; the trees grew taller, grander, and the wind that stirred their boughs grew fuller of soft, warm messages for us. At last we came to a forest where the trees were greater, grander, more beautiful than any we had seen yet. Their thick, living trunks held up a high, arched canopy so dense that hardly a single sunbeam slipped through the leaves and blossoms. The Little Ones climbed up into the bough rafters of that aerial vault, scrambling and leaping through that blooming world of branches, shouting down to the unseen elephants that wandered below, and hearing the great beasts trumpet their answers back. Lona understood every word of those conversations, while I could only fumble and guess at half their meaning. The Little Ones chased the squirrels, and the squirrels, playful and mischievous, drew them on—always, in the end, pausing to let themselves be caught and petted. Often some bird, gorgeous in plumage and song, would light on one of the children’s outstretched hands, sing a bright little song about all the good things that were coming, and then fly away. There was not a single monkey, of any kind, anywhere to be seen.
