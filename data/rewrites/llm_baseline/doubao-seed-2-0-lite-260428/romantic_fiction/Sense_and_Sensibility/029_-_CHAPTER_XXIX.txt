The frigid January sun had barely scratched a pale, watery glow through the frosted bedroom windows before the housemaid had even crossed the hall to light their chamber fire. Half-dressed, her stockings slipping down her calves and her hair falling loose unbound over her shift, Marianne Dashwood knelt pressed to the window-seat cushions, soaking up every scrap of dim light to guide her quill, writing so fast that only the constant, streaming tears blurring her eyes slowed her hand.

Elinor was woken at last by her sister’s ragged, half-stifled sobs. She lay quiet for a long minute, watching Marianne heave with grief, her chest tight with quiet, unspoken anxiety, before she spoke in a tone so soft it barely disturbed the cold air. “Marianne, may I ask—?”

“No, Elinor,” Marianne choked out, her voice strung tight with a desperate, unnatural calm that broke the second the words left her mouth. “Ask nothing. You will soon know all.”

That fragile hold on composure vanished entirely. Minutes dragged by before she could bring herself to dip her quill in the ink again, every few words interrupted by a fresh burst of weeping that forced her to drop her pen. It was plain to Elinor that Marianne knew, deep in her bones, this was almost certainly the last letter she would ever write to Willoughby.

She tended to her sister as gently and unobtrusively as she could, and would have wrapped an arm around her to soothe her, if Marianne had not begged her, frayed and trembling with raw, nervous irritability, not to speak a single word to her for any price. It was a mercy for both that they did not linger shut in the room together long. No sooner was Marianne fully dressed than she could not bear to stay still, wandering restlessly through every corner of the house, avoiding the sight of every servant and acquaintance until the breakfast bell rang. She would not touch a single bite of food at the table, and Elinor spent the whole meal deliberately drawing all of Mrs. Jennings’s chatter to herself, never urging Marianne to eat, never pitying her openly, never drawing the older woman’s curious eye to her sister’s obvious distress.

Breakfast was a favorite indulgence of Mrs. Jennings’s, and stretched on for nearly an hour. They had just settled around the shared work table to sort sewing scraps when a servant brought a letter to Marianne. She snatched it out of his hand so fast her knuckles scraped the envelope, her face draining to a ghastly, deathly white, before she turned and ran out of the room without a word. Elinor’s stomach dropped so fast she thought she might faint, her hands shaking so hard she could barely keep hold of her sewing, certain she would not be able to hide her own distress from Mrs. Jennings. But the good-natured old woman saw nothing amiss, cackling at the sight of Marianne’s haste as she measured lengths of worsted for her new rug.
“Lord, I never saw a young woman so desperately in love in all my days!” she laughed. “My own girls were silly enough, but nothing to her! Miss Marianne is quite a different creature, all wrapped up in that man. I hope he doesn’t keep her waiting to marry him much longer—it’s downright grievous to see her looking so pale and forlorn. Pray, when do you two expect the wedding?”

Elinor forced a stiff, wobbly smile to her face, even as her chest ached. “You really have talked yourself into believing my sister is engaged to Mr. Willoughby, ma’am? I thought it was only a joke, but such a serious question makes it clear you are far more invested in the story than that. I must beg you not to deceive yourself any longer. Nothing would surprise me more than to hear they intend to marry.”

“For shame, Miss Dashwood! How can you talk so?” Mrs. Jennings hooted. “Everyone knows it is a match! They were over their heads in love the second they met in Devonshire, weren’t they? I saw them together every single day, all day long, out walking or riding or cozied up by the fire! And didn’t Marianne come up to London with me on purpose to buy her wedding gowns? You’re so sly about your own affairs, you think no one else has any sense—but the whole of town has known about their engagement for weeks! I tell everyone, and so does my daughter Charlotte.”

“Indeed, ma’am,” Elinor said, very serious now, “you are mistaken. Spreading that story is unkind, and you will find that out soon enough, no matter if you won’t believe me now.”

Mrs. Jennings just laughed again, but Elinor had no strength left to argue. She was burning with the need to know exactly what Willoughby had written, so she hurried up the stairs to their shared bedchamber, and opened the door to find Marianne stretched across the bed, half-choking on her sobs, one crumpled letter clutched tight in her fist, two more discarded on the sheets beside her. Elinor did not say a word. She sat down on the edge of the bed, took Marianne’s cold hand in her own, kissed her forehead over and over, and then burst into tears herself, so violent at first they matched Marianne’s own.

Marianne could not speak, but she clung to her sister’s hand as if that touch was the only thing holding her above the wave of grief swallowing her whole. After a long while of them crying quietly together, Marianne pressed all the letters into Elinor’s hands, covered her face with her linen handkerchief, and sobbed so hard it was almost a scream. Elinor knew that this excess of pain had to run its course before it could fade, so she sat beside her, holding her hand, until the worst of the sobs quieted, before she turned eagerly to Willoughby’s letter. It was dated Bond Street, mid-January:
> I have just had the honour of receiving your letter, for which I beg to return my sincere acknowledgments. I am much concerned to find there was anything in my behaviour last night that did not meet your approbation; and though I am quite at a loss to discover in what point I could be so unfortunate as to offend you, I entreat your forgiveness of what I can assure you to have been perfectly unintentional. I shall never reflect on my former acquaintance with your family in Devonshire without the most grateful pleasure, and flatter myself it will not be broken by any mistake or misapprehension of my actions. My esteem for your whole family is very sincere; but if I have been so unfortunate as to give rise to a belief of more than I felt, or meant to express, I shall reproach myself for not having been more guarded in my professions of that esteem. That I should ever have meant more you will allow to be impossible, when you understand that my affections have been long engaged elsewhere, and it will not be many weeks, I believe, before this engagement is fulfilled. It is with great regret that I obey your commands in returning the letters with which I have been honoured from you, and the lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me.
> I am, dear Madam,
> Your most obedient
> humble servant,

Elinor’s blood ran cold with indignation as she read, again and again, every line more cruel than the last. She had known the letter would confirm Willoughby’s inconstancy, that their connection was broken forever, but she never imagined he could write something so devoid of honor, so shamelessly, insultingly cold. He did not even acknowledge the warmth they had shared at Barton, did not admit he had broken faith, did not express a single flicker of regret. Every sentence was a deliberate insult, proving him a hard, unprincipled man. For a long while she sat frozen, staring at the page, half-wanting to cry out that Marianne had escaped a lifetime of misery married to such a monster, but holding her tongue, terrified that even that truth would cut her sister deeper. Lost in her own thoughts of Willoughby’s depravity, and of Edward, the quiet, gentle man whose own secret heartache she had carried alone for months, she forgot all about the other three letters in her lap, forgot how long she had been sitting there, until the sound of carriage wheels crunching up the drive pulled her back. She glanced out the window, and saw Mrs. Jennings’s chariot, which they had not ordered to leave until one o’clock that afternoon.

She hurried down to excuse herself, telling Mrs. Jennings Marianne was far too unwell to leave, and the kind woman accepted the apology at once, wishing Marianne a speedy recovery before she set off on her calls. Elinor returned to the bedroom just in time to catch Marianne as she stumbled off the bed, faint and giddy, ready to collapse to the floor. She had barely eaten a proper meal or slept through a full night in days, sustained only by the fever of waiting to see Willoughby, and now that the tension had snapped, she was left weak, head throbbing, her stomach roiling with nervous nausea. A glass of spiced warm wine Elinor fetched at once brought her back to herself a little, and she mumbled through her tears, “Poor Elinor! How unhappy I make you.”

“I only wish there was anything I could do to comfort you,” Elinor whispered.

It was too much for Marianne. She cried out, voice cracking, “Oh Elinor, I am so miserable I can scarce bear it!” before her sobs swallowed her words again.

“Exert yourself, my dearest Marianne,” Elinor begged, wrapping her arms tight around her. “If you give in to this grief you will kill yourself, and break the heart of every person who loves you. Think of our mother! Think of the pain she would suffer seeing you like this—for her sake, you must try to hold on.”

“I cannot, I cannot,” Marianne wailed, clinging to her. “Leave me, if I distress you! Hate me, forget me! Do not torture me by telling me to exert myself! It is so easy for people who have no sorrow of their own to say that. Happy Elinor, you have no idea what I suffer.”

“Happy?” Elinor said, her voice solemn and soft, tears brimming in her own eyes. “Ah, Marianne, if you knew half the grief I carry… How could I ever be happy, watching you break apart like this?”

“Forgive me, forgive me,” Marianne sobbed, her arms tightening around Elinor’s neck. “I know you care for me, I know how good your heart is. But you are happy, you must be! Edward loves you! Nothing in the world can take that away from you.”

“So many things can,” Elinor said quietly.

“No, no, no,” Marianne cried, wild with pain. “He loves you, and only you. You have no cause to grieve.”

“I can take no pleasure at all while I see you this wretched.”

“And you will never see me otherwise,” Marianne moaned. “This misery of mine can never be healed.”

“You must not talk that way,” Elinor said. “Have you no friends, no comforts? Think how much worse this would be if you had been engaged for months, if you had committed your whole life to him before his true nature came out. Every extra day you loved him in good faith would only have made this blow far more terrible.”

“Engagement?” Marianne pulled back, her red, puffy eyes wide. “There was no engagement. He is not so unworthy as you think. He broke no formal promise to me.”

“But he told you he loved you.”

“Yes—no, he never said the words outright. He implied it every single day, at Barton, on every ride, every walk, every hour we spent together. Sometimes I thought he had spoken it out loud, but he never had. It was never formally declared.”

“But you wrote to him?” Elinor said, glancing at the other letters in her lap.

“Yes,” Marianne whispered. “How could that be wrong, after all that had passed between us? But I cannot talk about it.”

Elinor turned then to the three notes Marianne had sent Willoughby, her curiosity burning brighter than ever, and read them all. The first, written the day they arrived in town, was bright, full of excited hope, telling Willoughby she was in London, that she could not resist the chance to come see him, begging him to call on her that very night. The second, written after the dance at the Middletons’, was full of confusion and hurt, wondering why he had never answered her first note, why he had avoided her at the dance when she had heard he was invited. The third, written after the night Willoughby had snubbed her in the street, was raw and vulnerable, demanding he explain his cruel behavior, begging him to tell her if someone had lied about her to turn him against her, and asking him to return her letters and the lock of hair if his feelings for her had truly died.

Elinor’s heart ached for the impetuous, loving confidence that had led Marianne to write such tender notes, unprompted by any formal vow. “You do not understand,” Marianne said, when she saw Elinor had finished reading. “I felt as solemnly bound to him as if we had signed a legal marriage contract. As if we had spoken our vows before a hundred witnesses.”

“I believe that,” Elinor said softly. “But unfortunately, he did not feel the same way.”

“He did, Elinor! For weeks and weeks at Barton, he felt exactly that!” Marianne cried. “I know he did. Something evil, some cruel trick played on me, changed him. That lock of hair he sends back so casually? He begged me for it, on his knees almost, his eyes bright and soft, the last evening we spent together at Barton. You must remember how happy we were that night! You must remember how upset he was the morning he left, telling me we would not see each other for weeks, his voice breaking with tears. Could that man have written this cruel letter, of his own free will?”

For a long moment she could not speak, then she lifted her head, defiant through her tears. “I have been cruelly used—but not by Willoughby. Some other enemy, some woman he is now engaged to, must have lied about me, poisoned his mind against me. I would rather believe every single one of our acquaintances leagued together to ruin me in his eyes, than believe his heart is naturally so cruel. Besides you, Mama, and Edward, there is no one in the whole world I would not sooner suspect of wickedness than Willoughby.”

Elinor would not argue, only held her tighter. “Then let them never have the satisfaction of seeing you broken, my dear. Let your innocence be enough to carry you through, and rob them of the triumph they crave.”

“No,” Marianne sobbed. “Misery like mine leaves no room for pride. I do not care who sees how wretched I am. Let them all gloat. The people who suffer a little can be proud, can brush off insults, can pretend to be fine—but I cannot. I cannot hide how much this hurts.”

“For my sake, and Mama’s—”

“I would do anything for you two. But to smile, to pretend I am happy, when my heart is shattered? No one could ask that of me.”

They fell quiet then. Elinor paced back and forth between the window and the fire, her mind racing, not noticing the warmth of the flames against her hands, not seeing the street outside through the frosted glass. Marianne sat huddled at the foot of the bed, rereading Willoughby’s letter, shuddering at every line, before she burst out again, “That line! *‘The lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me’*. How dare he? That is unpardonable! Elinor, there is no way to justify that cruelty. Whoever this woman is, she planned this for months, plotted to steal him away from me! He never spoke of any other woman but me.”

Another long, quiet pause dragged by, Marianne’s whole frame shaking with quiet sobs. “I must go home,” she said at last. “I need to be with Mama. Can we leave tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow?” Elinor said. “That is far too soon. We are guests in Mrs. Jennings’s house, and it would be rude in the extreme to rush off with no warning.”

“Then in a day or two,” Marianne said. “But I cannot stay here long. I cannot bear all their questions, all their stares. The Middletons, the Palmers—their pity, especially Lady Middleton’s polite, empty pity, will kill me. I can’t stand it.”

Elinor helped her lie back down on the bed, but no position brought her ease. She twisted and turned, hysterical with pain, until Elinor finally persuaded her to take a few drops of lavender spirits to calm her nerves. The draught worked at last, and Marianne went still, lying quiet and motionless on the sheets, drained of every last tear, until Mrs. Jennings returned later that afternoon.
