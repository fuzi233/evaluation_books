May had come up the Nile the way a good cook brings a pot to the simmer—slow at first, then all at once the air was full of a steady heat and a clean, pale light. Outside the mess-room at Wadi Halfa three officers had drawn their chairs onto a knoll of coarse grass above the water. The moon, full and merciless as a polished platter held too near the fire, washed the sky until even the planets looked scoured of colour. Below them the river moved with the lazy thickness of oil, shining like a mirror newly burnished; across it the desert lay spread—white as sifted flour, jeweled here and there with stones that caught the light like salt crystals.

They smoked without speaking, letting the night speak for itself: bull-frogs throbbing from an island midstream, the faint hiss of the river against the bank, the occasional far bark of a camel. Under all of it, deeper than any human talk, lay that familiar eastern hush—vast, patient, and listening—broken only by the small, living noises that kept a man sane.

Colonel Dawson struck a match. The flare lit his face—drawn, worried, as though he had been living too long on bad rations and worse news.

“I hope no harm has come to him,” he said, flicking the match away. “I wish I could say I believed it.”

Lieutenant Calder, young enough to have unlined cheeks despite the grey that the Soudan painted into men’s hair, tamped tobacco into his pipe with a thumb that moved carefully, like someone shaping dough.

“He left Halfa eight weeks ago,” he said.

“Eight weeks to-day,” Dawson answered.

Major Walters of the Army Service Corps—long-necked, spare, with the cheerfulness of a man who could find a joke in a sandstorm—let the cigar smoke drift from his lips.

“It’s early yet to decide Durrance has got scuppered,” he said. “Give him a fire, a couple of sheiks, and some coffee thick as mud, and he’ll sit all night swapping stories, happy as a man at his own hearth. Here there are offices and papers and a desk—everything he hates and can’t stomach. You’ll see. He’ll turn up. He just won’t hurry about it.”

“He is three weeks overdue,” Dawson said, tugging at his moustache as if he could pull certainty out of it. “And he’s methodical—after a fashion.”

Walters gestured across the water to the empty, glittering waste.

“If he’d gone west, I’d agree. But he went east—mountain country towards Berenice and the Red Sea. Those tribes have been quiet even when Osman Digna was at Suakin.”

Dawson’s worry did not soften. “He is three weeks overdue,” he repeated, as though the words themselves might summon a man out of the dunes.

Calder knocked the ash from his pipe, refilled, and leaned forward, elbows on knees. His voice came slowly, weighed out like spice.

“I’ve wondered whether a trap was laid for him. I never spoke of what I knew, because until lately I didn’t see any link. But now… I begin to wonder. You remember the night before he started?”

Dawson shifted his chair nearer. “Yes.”

Walters nodded. “Durrance dined with us. Went away early to pack, didn’t he?”

“His packing was done,” Calder said. “He went early—but not to his quarters. He walked down the river-bank to Tewfikieh.”

Tewfikieh: a mile north, a frontier town with Greek stores and dirty cafés and native cook-shops where oil and onions and strong coffee fought for mastery of the air; where dialects collided—Delta fellah against Dinka negro—until the very street seemed stitched from many tongues. And yet for all the shouting, European ears always noticed what was missing: the ring of footsteps. People walked on sand, bare feet whispering; and when a lull came, the crowd drifted like ghosts, and behind their noise you could hear the desert’s old, brooding silence, as if the earth itself refused to translate what men were saying.

“He went at ten,” Calder continued. “I went to his quarters at eleven. He hadn’t returned. He was to ride east at four, and there was a detail I wanted to settle. So I waited. He came in about a quarter of an hour later—restless, quick-spoken, like a man with a hot coal under his tongue. He told me I must be quick because he expected a visitor. He barely listened. One moment he seemed angry, the next he laughed—as though, in spite of himself, he was pleased. Then he hurried me out, and I heard him tell his servant to go to bed: he would admit the visitor himself.”

Dawson’s eyes narrowed. “Who was the visitor?”

“I don’t know. But when his servant went in at four to wake him, Durrance was still sitting on the verandah, as though waiting. The visitor had not come.”

“And he left no message?” Dawson asked.

“No,” Calder said. “I was up before he started. He looked worried, puzzled—like a man trying to name a taste he knows he has had before, but cannot place. I thought he meant to tell me. Even after he was mounted and the camel rose, he turned and looked down at me. Then he decided against it. He struck the camel and rode past the post-office into the desert with his head sunk on his breast.” Calder’s gaze fixed on the river as if it might show him the answer. “Who was this person he met in Tewfikieh? Who comes so secretly to Halfa? Whatever it was, it stirred him. And then—nothing. Was it a lure? Like the colonel, I am afraid.”

Silence fell, filled only by frogs and the thin crackle of tobacco. Walters rose at last, stretching.

“I still don’t think Durrance has got scuppered,” he said, stubbornly cheerful.

“I know what I’ll do,” Dawson decided. “Send a strong search party in the morning.”

So in the morning, while the Nile flashed hard light between the verandah posts and the breakfast smells rose—toast dry as gunpowder, bacon, coffee too long boiled—Dawson began describing the men and supplies he meant to send. Walters, however, had turned Calder’s story over through the night the way a cook turns a question over a slow flame.

“Did you ever find out who Durrance talked to at Tewfikieh?” Walters asked.

“I tried,” Calder said. He sat with his back to the Nile, facing the glass doors. “And there’s a point that puzzles me. I couldn’t learn he spoke to more than one person—and that one couldn’t likely be the visitor he expected. He stopped by a café where strolling musicians were playing for their lodging. One of them—a Greek, they said—came out with his hat. Durrance threw a sovereign in. The man thanked him, and they spoke a little.”

As Calder reached that point, his head lifted sharply. Something in his face changed—recognition first, then confusion, as though a familiar melody had been played in the wrong key. His hands gripped the table-edge; his feet drew back under his chair as if he might spring up. But he didn’t. He glanced around; Dawson was pouring cocoa, Walters looking down at his plate. No one had noticed.

Calder leaned back, eyes staring past Walters’s shoulder into the mess-room. “But I never heard that he spoke to anyone else. He seemed merely to stroll through the village and back.”

“That doesn’t help,” Walters said.

“And that’s all?” Dawson asked.

“No,” Calder replied, slowly. “I know, for instance, that the man we are talking about is staring me straight in the face.”

Every head turned.

In the doorway stood Durrance—dust still powdered into the seams of his clothes, his face burned to red brick by sun and wind. He listened with a strained, peculiar intentness, as though he were trying to taste words rather than hear them.

“Durrance!” Dawson cried, springing up. “When did you get back?”

“When did you come in?” Walters added.

Durrance stepped down into the verandah. It was then that Calder—who did not rise, did not greet him—felt his confusion settle into a quick, troubled comprehension. Something was wrong; not a mere illness, not fatigue. A wrongness in the way Durrance carried his head, the way his eyes rested without fastening.

“We expected you three weeks ago,” Dawson said, dragging a chair out.

“The delay couldn’t be helped,” Durrance replied, and sat.

“Does my story account for it?” Calder asked.

“Not a bit,” Durrance said with a laugh too quick to be easy. “It was the Greek musician I expected that night. I wanted to know what bad luck had thrown him up to scrape a zither for his bed in Tewfikieh. That’s all.” His voice softened, dragged slightly at the end, as if the last words hurt. “Yes. That was all.”

Dawson bustled, grateful for the ordinary. “You’re forgetting your breakfast. What will you have?”

Durrance looked around the table—looked, yet did not quite look—and then called the waiter.

“Moussa—get me something cold.”

Calder watched with a steadiness that missed nothing. Durrance did not reach for bread or dish the way a hungry traveller would. He kept his hands close, as if the table might shift under him.

“There’s tea, cocoa, coffee,” Calder said. “Help yourself.”

“Thanks,” Durrance replied. “I see. But have Moussa bring me a brandy-and-soda, I think.”

When the glass came, Durrance did not turn to it; his arm went out over his shoulder, palm waiting, until Moussa placed the tumbler safely into it. Calder’s mouth tightened in a faint nod: a difficulty cleverly avoided, yes—but a difficulty all the same.

Durrance ate, drank, and talked. He said he had travelled farther east than he meant; that the Ababdeh were quiet among their mountains—unwilling to swear themselves to Egypt, but paying no tribute to the Mahdi. The weather was good; ibex and antelope plentiful; he had reason to be content.

Calder listened and disbelieved every word. Not because Durrance lied poorly—he lied excellently—but because Calder had begun to hear what was missing: the easy ring of truth, the natural cadence. Durrance’s cheer was like store-bought sweetness poured over stale bread.

The officers drifted away to duty. Calder remained. Durrance lingered over his food as if delay were a wall he could hide behind. He asked endless questions about Halfa, answered his own, returned again to his journey. Finally he stopped with abrupt suspicion.

“You’re taking life easily this morning,” he said.

“I haven’t eight weeks’ arrears of letters to clear,” Calder returned lightly.

Durrance’s face clouded, his forehead contracted. “True. I’d forgotten my letters.”

He rose, climbed the steps, and went into the mess-room. Calder sprang up at once and watched. Durrance took the key of his office from the nail by the glass doors, crossed to the farther door, and went out, leaving it open. Calder saw him pass down the path between bushes in the tiny garden, unlatch the gate, and cross the sand to his office.

Only when he disappeared did Calder sit again, elbows on the table, face between his hands. He knew there were letters waiting in a woman’s hand. He knew, too, that Durrance had aged. Furrows had bitten into his mouth; his hair was dulled by something more than dust. The calamity was not in the desert—it was in him.

When at last Calder rose, he took his helmet and crossed the sand. He lifted the latch noiselessly, opened the door, and looked in.

Durrance sat at his desk with his head bowed on his arms. The letters lay unopened at his side, like a meal grown cold because the eater has lost all hunger.

Calder stepped inside and closed the door loudly.

Durrance turned his face toward the sound. “Well?” he said.

“I’ve a paper, Colonel, needing your signature,” Calder answered. “Authority for the alterations in C barracks. You remember?”

“Very well. I’ll look through it and return it signed at lunch. Give it me.”

His hand stretched out towards Calder, palm open, steady—too steady.

Calder removed his pipe from his mouth and, standing fully in Durrance’s view, set the pipe slowly, deliberately, into that outstretched palm.

The hot bowl burned. Durrance snatched his hand back. The pipe fell, snapped on the floor.

Neither spoke for a moment. Then Calder put an arm around Durrance’s shoulder and asked gently, in a voice softened almost to a woman’s:

“How did it happen?”

Durrance covered his face with his hands. The control he had held like a clenched jaw gave way. He did not answer, did not cry out, but his whole body shivered as if a cold wind had found him in the heat.

“How did it happen?” Calder whispered again.

Durrance asked instead, hoarsely, “How did you find out?”

“You stood in the mess-room doorway listening to discover whose voice came from where. When I raised my head and saw you—though your eyes rested on my face—there was no recognition. I suspected then. When you came down the steps I was almost certain. When you wouldn’t help yourself to food, when you put your arm out behind you so Moussa had to place the glass into your hand—I knew.”

“I was a fool to try to hide it,” Durrance said. “I knew all along I couldn’t for more than a few hours. But even those few hours… they seemed a gain.”

“How did it happen?”

“There was a high wind,” Durrance said, forcing the words out as if swallowing grit. “It took my helmet off. Eight in the morning. I wasn’t moving camp that day. I was outside my tent in my shirt-sleeves, no collar to cover the nape of my neck. I ran after the helmet—fool that I was. You’ve seen it: every time you stoop it skips away; every time you run it stops, as if waiting to be caught. Before you know it you’ve gone half a mile. I dropped, they told me, like a log—just when I had it in my hand. I don’t know how long ago. I was ill a time. Afterward it was hard to count, because you can’t tell day from night.”

Blind. The word did not need to be spoken; it filled the room like smoke.

He told the rest: how he made his followers carry him back toward Berber; how, out of a stubborn, human craving to keep his disaster private, he ordered them to silence. Calder listened to the end, then stood quickly.

“There’s a doctor,” he said. “Clever enough, and for a Syrian he knows a great deal. I’ll fetch him privately. We’ll hear what he says. It may be only temporary.”

The Syrian came, smelled of disinfectant and sweat, and spoke with the careful caution of a man handling a knife close to bone. He examined Durrance, pursed his lips, and shook his head.

“Go to Cairo at once,” he advised. “A specialist. I hesitate to pronounce—there is always hope—but this is grave.”

He asked questions. “Any injury to the head? Thrown from a horse? Wounded?”

“No,” Durrance said.

When the Syrian had gone, the room fell silent again. Calder had the sense that any consolation would be false, and that false comfort, if it betrayed his fear, might harm more than truth.

He turned to the pile of letters and sorted through them, fingertips brushing paper as if feeling for pulse.

“There are two letters here,” he said gently, “you might care to hear. They’re in a woman’s hand. Irish postmark. Shall I open them?”

“No,” Durrance exclaimed sharply, and his hand dropped onto Calder’s arm, urgent. “By no means.”

Calder did not put them down. He had his own reasons to want to know the girl behind those pages—Ethne Eustace, of whom Durrance had spoken rarely, and then with odd intensity, saying only her name and that he would be glad enough to change it if he could. Calder looked at Durrance—the man built for sun and long miles, who wore wild places as other men wore coats; the man to whom deserts were home, and hedged fields the strange country—and understood how immense this ruin was. If the girl failed him, what remained?

“You would like to hear them,” Calder insisted. “You’ve been away eight weeks.”

A harsh laugh cut him off.

“Do you know what I was thinking when I stopped you?” Durrance said. “That I’d read them after you’d gone.” His voice trembled, a hair’s breadth. “It takes time to get used to being blind when your eyes have served you all your life. You’ll have to help me answer them, Calder. So read them. Please.”

Calder tore open the envelopes. As he read, the cramped office filled with another world: Donegal’s small doings spoken plainly—who was ill, whose cow had calved, whose roof had leaked, who had made a joke sharp enough to sting and kind enough to mend. The words carried turf-smoke and damp stone, the rhythm of a village where old speech still held meanings that no official English could properly carry. It was as if, through her simple sentences, she fought back against the slow eroding of memory—holding the local names, the turns of phrase, the way of seeing, as carefully as a food historian preserves a recipe before it dies with its last speaker.

And beneath the everyday, a warmth for Durrance ran like an undercurrent: not proclaimed, but present in every choice of detail, every small confidence, as if his life, far away and harsh, mattered to her more than she would say.

When Calder finished, he looked up. Relief had softened his face. Durrance, too, seemed to breathe more freely.

“After all,” Durrance cried, seizing at brightness like a man grabbing a cup in the dark, “one has something to be thankful for. Think—suppose I’d been engaged to her! She would never have let me break it off once I’d gone blind. What an escape!”

“An escape?” Calder repeated, startled.

“You don’t understand.” Durrance’s voice hardened again, defensive, self-mocking. “I knew a man who went blind. A good fellow—before. Mind that. But a year after you wouldn’t have known him. He narrowed into the most selfish, exacting creature imaginable. I don’t wonder. I hardly see how he could help it. I don’t blame him. But it wouldn’t make life easier for a wife, would it? A helpless husband who can’t cross a road without her at his elbow is bad enough. But make him a selfish beast as well—full of questions, jealous of her power to go where she will, curious about every person she speaks to—what then?” His breath caught. “My God, I’m glad she refused me. For that I’m most grateful.”

“She refused you?” Calder asked, and his relief slipped away.

“Twice,” Durrance said. “What an escape. You see, Calder, I shall be more trouble even than that man. I’m not clever. I can’t sit in a chair and amuse myself with thinking—not having any intellect to buck about. I’ve lived out of doors and hard, and that’s the only life that suits me. In a year’s time you won’t be very anxious for much of my society,” and he laughed again—harsh, like a blade struck against stone.

“Oh, stop that,” Calder said. “I’ll read the rest of your letters.”

He did—though his mind kept circling back to the Donegal letters, to what was said and what could not be said, to how the truest things often hid in the untranslatable corners of a native tongue. Girls refused men for reasons that made no sense until years later; refused because of pride, fear, old obligations, words that meant one thing in one mouth and another in the next.

When at last the last envelope was finished, Durrance sat very still, hands resting on the desk as if learning its edges.

“I must answer the letters from Ireland,” he said quietly. “The rest can wait.”
