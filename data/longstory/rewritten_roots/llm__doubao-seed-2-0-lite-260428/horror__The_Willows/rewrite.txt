After leaving Vienna, and long before you come to Budapest, the Danube slips over its paved stone banks and vanishes into a stretch of country no map ever dares render honestly. On official charts the region is smudged in a faint, fluffy blue that bleeds paler and paler the further it stretches from the surveyed shorelines, the word *Sümpfe* – marshes – scrawled across hundreds of unmarked square miles in thin, apologetic ink, as if the cartographers feared to write too loud of what lives there. This is no mere wetland: it is a sprawling, endless swamp choked waist-deep with willow bushes, a sea of green that stretches to the blurred horizon without a single rise, a single sharp edge to break its hunched, low-lying mass.

These willows never grow into trees. They refuse the dignity of rigid trunks, staying forever low, bowed, their supple stems bending like grass under the lightest breath of wind. They shift so constantly, their silver undersides flashing one after another as the gale rolls across the plain, that you get the unshakable, skin-prickling impression that the whole landscape is moving – creeping, slow, in unison, no two bushes ever staying in exactly the same place for more than a second. Wind ripples over them like a tide, rolling green then blazing silver, a living sea of leaves that never rests.

Freed from the hard banks that hemmed it in further upstream, the Danube here wanders wild, splitting into a thousand unmarked channels that tear at the sand, carve new whirlpools into the foam, rip whole clumps of willow out by the roots and carry them away to build new islands that vanish entirely when the high flood peaks. Nothing here stays fixed. Nothing here belongs to humans.

This stretch of river begins not long past Pressburg, and we – two men in a lopsided Canadian canoe, a dented gipsy tent lashed to the stern and a single black frying pan clinking against the gunwale – drifted into it on the crest of a rising flood at mid-July. We had slipped through the sleeping streets of Vienna an hour before sunrise, left it nothing but a wisp of smoke behind us against the distant Wienerwald hills, breakfasted under birches that roared in the rising wind below Fischeramend, and sped past Orth, Hainburg, the crumbled Roman ruins of Carnuntum where Marcus Aurelius once wrote his meditations, before the Carpathian foothills loomed ahead and we crossed the unmarked border into Hungary.

The current drove us at twelve kilometers an hour, the muddy brown flood water yanking us onto shingle beds, spinning us like a cork in sudden belching whirlpools, before the spires of Pressburg broke over the treeline. The canoe leapt beneath us like a spooked horse, we dodged the sunken chain of the old flying ferry, rounded a sharp left turn – and plunged straight over a lip of yellow foam into the land of the willows.

The shift was instantaneous, like a film reel snapping without warning from a crowded city street to a dead, alien world. Less than half an hour later, there was no sign of humanity left anywhere in sight: no fishing hut, no distant wisp of chimney smoke, no other boat, not even a scrap of discarded trash bobbing in the current. The isolation settled over us so thick we could almost taste it, and we laughed uneasily, joking that we ought to have applied for a special passport to cross into this unmarked kingdom – that we had trespassed somewhere no human had any right to be, where unwritten warnings were stitched into every rustle of the leaves, for anyone who had the eyes to see them.

The wind howled so hard by mid-afternoon our arms ached from fighting it, and we began hunting for a place to make camp. Landing was nearly impossible: the flood would hurl us at the shore then yank us back out again before we could grab hold, willow branches snagged at our sleeves and tore at our palms when we reached out to slow the canoe, and we dragged whole handfuls of sandy bank into the water with us before a brutal sideways gust blew us into a sheltered backwater and we beached the bows in a cloud of stinging spray. We collapsed onto the hot, shifting yellow sand panting, and all around us thousands of willow bushes clapped their leaves in the gale, a million tiny rustling claps that sounded far too much like an audience closing in, applauding for the wrong reason.

“What a river,” I said to my friend – the Swede, my traveling companion of years, the man I’d pushed and waded with through the shallow upper reaches of the Danube back in June, where the stream bubbled small and bright out of the Black Forest.

“Doesn’t suffer fools,” he grunted, yanking the canoe further up the sand before he flopped back for a nap, face half turned to the sun.

I lay beside him, and the old familiar sense of the Danube as a living thing wrapped around me, thicker than ever here. We’d followed it for weeks, watched it grow from a tiny trickle that would vanish down holes in the Swabian limestone hills to run under a different name for miles, to the great roiling force that had shoved us through the Passau gorge shoulder to shoulder with the thundering Inn river. We knew all its voices: the sibilant hissing it sang to the moon at night, grinding pebbles along its bed, the gurgle of hidden whirlpools that bubbled up without warning on perfectly calm water, the deep unceasing thunder that rumbled under every other sound, the sound of it tearing chunks of bank apart in the dark. It had never been cruel to us, before. Just vast. Old. Something that held our tiny canoe on its shoulders without ever really noticing we were there.

But here, past Pressburg, the Danube had stopped playing tricks. It had grown serious. Halfway to the Black Sea, far from the towns and bridges and roads, it had dropped all pretense of being a river for human use. It split into three separate arms, none of them marked on our map, and the Hungarian provision seller in Pressburg had grabbed my wrist hard before we left, his face pale, to warn us off. “Take a wrong channel, and when the flood drops, you will be stranded forty miles from any living soul. No farms. No fishermen. No one comes here. The river will leave you high and dry to starve. And the willows –” He’d cut himself off, shaken his head, and refused to say another word. We’d laughed it off, bought extra tins of beans, but we never let that warning slip entirely from our minds.

The sun was still two hours from setting when I left the Snede asleep on the sand to explore our camp. The “island” was barely an acre of shifting shingle, raised no more than three feet above the roiling flood, triangular with the point aimed straight upstream. I stood at that point for minutes, watching the red-stained flood bear down on me, roaring so loud the sand under my boots trembled, the waves throwing spray so high it stung my cheeks. The island swayed under me, or maybe the willows swayed so hard the whole world felt like it was moving, slipping downstream. When I turned to look the other way, the river vanished into the mass of willows, the bushes huddled shoulder to shoulder like a herd of monstrous prehistoric things crowding down to drink, swallowing the water whole, swallowing the light, swallowing any trace of the path ahead.

That was when the unease crept in, uninvited, cold down the back of my neck. It wasn’t awe at the river’s power, wasn’t fear of the gale that could tear willows up by the roots and toss them like chaff. It was the willows. Acres and acres of them, stretching as far as the eye could see, packed so tight there was no space between their stems, all of them turned – I knew it, though I couldn’t see a single face, a single eye – turned towards us, watching. Waiting. They were not neutral. They were not friendly. We had trespassed somewhere that did not belong to mankind, and every leaf rustling in the wind was a quiet note of that fact.

I didn’t tell the Swede. He was practical, no patience for vague unease, he would have laughed and called me fanciful. We set up the tent in the small dip at the center of the island, the willows huddling close to block the worst of the wind, and he grumbled that there was no firewood, no stones to stake the tent pegs deep, that the sand was already shrinking, the flood eating away at the edges of our tiny island foot by foot. “Won’t last the night at this rate,” he said. “I’m sleeping in my clothes. We keep the canoe right by the tent, ready to go at a second’s notice.”

He wandered off along the bank to hunt for driftwood, and a second later I heard him curse, sharp and uncharacteristic, not his usual easy laugh.

“Good God – what is that?”

I ran over to him. He was staring out at the foaming current, pointing. A dark bulk rolled over and over in the waves, drifting fast towards us, disappearing under the foam then bobbing up again. It was no log. When it was level with our position, it lurched, all on its own, and twisted to face us. Its eyes glowed a weird, bright yellow, reflecting the sunset, staring straight into ours for a long, terrible heartbeat before it gave a great gulping dive and vanished.

“Otter,” we both blurted at the exact same time, laughing too loud, our hearts hammering. It was an otter. Alive. Hunting. But neither of us could shake the split second where we’d both been certain it was the face of a drowned man, dead a long time in the flood.

We had turned to head back to the camp, our arms piled high with driftwood, when we saw him. A man, standing upright in a flat-bottomed boat, being swept down the opposite shore at breakneck speed. He was gesticulating wildly, screaming at us, but the wind tore his words away. He was making the sign of the cross over and over, not at us – past us, at the wall of willows at our backs, his face as white as bone. In a breath he was gone, vanished around the bend of the river into the growing mist, no wake left behind him, no trace that he had ever been there at all.

The Swede’s laugh was thin, forced, when he spoke. “Saw our smoke, probably. The locals around here are full of old tales. The shop woman back in Pressburg told no one ever lands in the Sumpfe, that it belongs to beings that aren’t human. He got spooked, that’s all.”

Neither of us believed him. The wind rose higher, tearing at our hair, and a thousand thousand willow bushes hissed around us in the gathering twilight. We realized, all at once, that we could not hear a single bird call. Not one.

II. I jolted wide awake all at once, grit from the sandy mattress crammed in the corners of my eyes and sticking to the sweat slick on my throat, staring straight out through the gap in the tent’s canvas door. My watch was pinned to the inner wall of the fabric, and the moonlight bleaching the tent was bright enough to read the face clearly: past twelve, the thin, unmarked threshold of a brand new day, meaning I’d slept nearly two full hours. The Swede lay snoring heavy beside me, his breath thick with the dregs of the cheap brandy we’d shared over the campfire, dead to the world. The wind howled outside the canvas just as it had before we bedded down, but there was something new plucking at the back of my ribs, sharp and cold, pure unnameable dread. The disturbance was right on top of us, not a hundred feet away.

I sat up fast, sand spilling off my shirt onto the floor, and craned to look out. The willow trees were thrashing so hard in the gusts their thin branches lashed like bony, outstretched hands, but our little patch of green canvas was still tucked safe down in the hollow, the wind surging right over the top of it without catching hard enough to tear the stakes free. The unease didn’t fade, though, so I crawled quiet as possible toward the exit, careful not to jostle the tent and wake my companion, some thrumming, jittery excitement buzzing under my skin. I was halfway out, on my hands and knees in the cool sand, when my eyes snagged on the tops of the bushes opposite our camp, their moving leaves weaving dark patterns against the moonlit sky. I dropped back onto my heels, and stared.

It couldn’t be real, surely—but there, level with my line of sight and a little higher, half-hidden in the willow crowns not fifty feet from where I knelt, were shapes that had no business being there. As the branches swayed in the gale, they framed those indeterminate, monstrous outlines, shifting fast under the silver moon. My first instinct was to shake the Swede awake, make him see this too, but something clamped down on the urge in an instant: I did not want confirmation. I did not want proof these things were not a trick of my eyes. I crouched there, eyes stinging from wind-blown sand, completely, unarguably awake, saying the words out loud under my breath to be sure: I am not dreaming.

Slowly, they resolved fully in my vision: huge, bronze-colored as long-unpolished statuary, moving entirely independent of the sway of the boughs around them. They were far larger than any human could ever be, and every line of them screamed they were not human at all. They were not just shadow play cast by moving branches. They shifted of their own will, pouring upward in a continuous unbroken stream from the dark ground beneath the willows all the way up to the inky upper sky, vanishing the second they hit the dark haze above the treeline. They wove through one another, overlapping and melting into a single massive serpentine column, their huge blurred limbs and rippling bodies twisting in step with the contortions of the wind-torn trees. Nude, fluid, almost gliding inside the web of willow leaves, they rose in a living spiral straight up into the heavens. I never caught a glimpse of their faces. Unceasingly, they poured upward, swaying in great slow curves, that dull bronze hue glowing dim against the silver moonlight.

I strained my eyes until they burned, searching for any sign they would dissolve back into shadow, prove they were nothing but an optical illusion. I hunted for some scrap of normal reality to cling to, even as I knew deep down the old rules of reality had already broken. The longer I stared, the surer I was they were alive, and real—even if they did not fit the narrow standards biologists and camera film were built to measure. For a long moment I felt no fear at all, just an awe so heavy it settled in my bones, the kind of awe I imagined a caveman felt staring up at a volcano erupting. I was looking straight at the elemental, personified powers of this primeval, haunted island. We had intruded. Our presence had woken the old things that lived here, and my brain flooded with every old forgotten legend of the local spirits, the deities men had sacrificed to on the banks of this river a thousand years prior.

Before I could even begin to parse what I was seeing, something tugged at my sleeves, urging me out further. I crept forward across the sand and stood upright, bare feet pressing into the patch of earth that still held the faint warmth of the day’s sun. The wind tore at my hair and stung my cheeks, and the roar of the river hit my ears so loud I could feel the vibration in my teeth. These things were normal. These things were real, proof my senses were still working as they should. But the bronze shapes still rose, silent and majestic, coiling upward in that spiral of grace and old unchallenged strength, until I felt my knees buckling. I was seconds away from dropping to the sand and worshipping them—absolutely worshipping.

Then a gust of wind slammed into me so hard it knocked me sideways, I nearly stumbled face-first into the dirt, and the violent jolt shook that dumb reverie right out of my bones. The shapes were still there, still winding up out of the dark of the willow grove, but my reason kicked back into gear, fumbling for the familiar. It’s a hallucination, I told myself. The moonlight and the branches and my tired, overstretched brain are projecting these images onto the dark. That’s all it is. I took a wobbly step forward across the open sand. But—was it truly all in my head? Was my reason not just clinging to the tiny, narrow box of the “known” to avoid admitting something I could never explain?

I can’t say how long that great column of dark shapes poured upward. It felt like hours. It felt far more real than any ordinary thing I’d ever experienced in my life. Then, all at once, they were gone. No fading, no slow vanishing—just gone, leaving nothing but the empty thrashing of willow boughs under the moon.

The second they vanished, the wonder shattered, and cold fear crashed down over me like a bucket of iced river water. The hidden, terrible meaning of this lonely haunted spot flamed bright in my mind, and I began to shake so hard my teeth chattered. I cast a wild, panicking glance all around me, scanning for escape routes that did not exist—there was nowhere to run, the river too fast to swim, the willow grove stretching for endless miles in every direction. Realizing how utterly helpless we were, I scrambled back to the tent on silent hands and knees, dove inside, and dragged the canvas door curtain shut tight to block out every glimpse of the moonlit willows. I buried my head as deep as I could under the scratchy wool blankets, pressing my face into the gritty mattress to muffle the sound of the wind that now sounded like a thousand low, whispering voices calling my name.

As if the night’s uncanny encounter had not already been enough to shatter my grip on what was real, I lay awake for what felt like hours before any drowsiness found me at all. When sleep finally did come, it was thin and fragile, a mere paper crust stretched over a wide, thrumming core of full, unblinking alertness that never once let down its guard.
That second jolt to full consciousness hit me with a raw, unmistakeable lurch of terror. It was no howl of the gale, no thunder of the rising river that woke me. It was the slow, unhurried drift of something *towards* the tent, something that shrank the tiny sleeping part of my mind smaller and smaller till it dissolved entirely, leaving me bolt upright in my blankets, every muscle coiled, straining to listen.
Outside the canvas, there was a pattering. Thousands upon thousands of tiny, soft, scuttling footsteps, brushing, tapping, creeping. I realized I had been hearing it for hours, half-processed at the edge of my sleep, long before it dragged me fully awake. I sat there, wide as if I had never slept a wink, my breath coming in tight, shallow gasps as a dead, heavy weight settled over every inch of my skin. For all the swelter of the summer night, I was slick with icy clammy sweat, shivering so hard my teeth chattered. Something was pressing steadily inward against the tent walls, pressing down hard on the roof from above. Was it the wind’s unseen bulk? Rain? Spray blown off the river? I ran through a dozen plausible explanations in the span of ten heartbeats—then jumped to what felt like the obvious answer: the single tall poplar on the island had snapped half-way through, its bough caught in other branches, its leaves tapping on the taut canvas as it swayed, ready to tear free and crush us both at the next gust.
I wrenched the loose tent flap up and stumbled out, yelling for the Swede to follow.
When I stood full upright on the sand, the tent was clear. No hanging broken bough. No rain. No river spray. Nothing at all was approaching. A thin, cold grey light seeped through the willow branches over my head, gilding the sand in faint, ghostly streaks. Stars still crowded the dark sky directly overhead, and the wind howled loud enough to vibrate in my bones, but our campfire had burned down to cold, dead ash, and streaks of bruising pink bled up the eastern horizon through the trees. Hours had passed since I’d stood in that same spot the night before, watching the writhing column of figures rise from the sand, and the memory hit me now like a punch to the gut, vivid and cruel as any bad dream that clings long after you wake. The ceaseless, raging gale left me drained, my bones heavy with the lassitude of a wholly sleepless night, but my nerves fizzed with sharp, tireless dread that banished any thought of rest. The river had risen further still; its roar filled the whole island, and fine stinging spray pricked my bare chest through my thin sleeping shirt. But there was not a single tangible sign of anything that could possibly explain the rising terror knotting tight in my throat.
My companion had not stirred when I called, and I saw no reason to wake him now. I scanned the camp slowly, marking every object: the upturned canoe half-buried in drifted sand, the two bright yellow paddles propped against its hull, the provision sack and extra lantern dangling side by side from the poplar’s lowest branch. And all around us, wrapping the camp in its endless hissing throng: the willows, those uncountable, shaking, whispering willows. A bird let out its thin, sharp morning cry, and a flock of ducks whirred overhead through the twilight, their wings beating the air hard. Dry, stinging sand swirled around my bare ankles in the gale.
I circled the tent, then pushed a little way out into the brush to stare across the river at the far bank, and that same wordless, suffocating distress clamped down on my chest again. The unbroken sea of bushes stretched to the horizon, ghost-pale and utterly unreal in the wan pre-dawn light. I wandered soft-footed through the undergrowth, still turning that endless pattering sound over in my head, still puzzling over the heavy weight that had pressed down on the tent and woken me. It had to be the wind, I told myself: wind driving loose hot sand grains hard against the taut canvas, wind bearing down heavy on our fragile little canvas roof. But no matter how hard I tried to convince myself, my nervousness, my quiet unnameable malaise, grew sharper by the minute.
I crossed the narrow strip of shallow water to the far shore of the island, and saw how the gale had torn great chunks of sand away from the banks through the night, warping the whole familiar coastline into something new and unrecognizable. I dipped my hands and feet into the cold current, splashed water over my burning forehead. The first glow of sunrise was already bleeding over the horizon, and the air hummed with the sharp, fresh sweetness of the coming day. On my walk back, I deliberately passed right through the exact clump of bushes where I had watched that column of shadowy figures rise up into the air. Midway through the thick willow thicket, a vast, engulfing terror swallowed me whole. A great, dark, staggering figure blurred past me in the shadows, so close the edge of its trailing bulk brushed my shoulder. Someone had walked right past me—no doubt, no mistake. A hard, gusting blow of wind shoved me forward, and once I stumbled out into the open clearing, the weight of that terror receded as suddenly as it had fallen. The winds walk, I told myself, remembering old tales I’d heard from rivermen, great living presences that drift through the trees like ghosts. The fear hanging over me was something so vast, so entirely unlike any fear I had ever known, that it woke a raw, awe-struck wonder in me that fought to counteract its sharpest bite. When I climbed the high sandy knoll at the island’s center, and looked out over the wide stretch of river glowing crimson under the first streaks of sun, the wild, unearthly beauty of the view was so overwhelming it made my throat tight, like a cry was about to tear out of me.
The cry never came. My gaze drifted from the far bank back down to our little camp half-hidden in the willows, and I made a discovery so awful that all my prior terror of walking winds shrank to nothing next to it.
The landscape had shifted. It was not that my higher vantage point had changed my view. The willows had moved. Their positions relative to the tent, the tent’s position relative to them—something was irreversibly different. The bushes now crowded far, far closer, so close it made my skin crawl. They had crept nearer. Silent, slow, soft-footed over the shifting sands, drawing imperceptibly closer all night with unhurried, gentle movements, the willows had advanced while we slept. Had the wind moved them? Or had they moved on their own? I thought of that endless pattering of tiny feet outside the tent, the heavy weight that had pressed down on my chest and woken me screaming. I swayed on the sandy knoll like a loose reed, barely able to stay upright. There was no accident here. It was deliberate. Personal. Hostile intent hung thick in the air, and it froze me rigid where I stood.
Then the reaction hit. The idea was so absurd, so ridiculous, that I wanted to laugh out loud. But the laughter would not come any easier than the cry I had choked back a moment before. The sudden knowledge hit me like a cold bucket of water: they were not coming for our bodies first. The attack was coming for our minds, to warp what we saw, what we knew, what we could trust, and it was already well underway.
The wind buffeted me roughly, and the sun cleared the horizon fast— it was past four o’clock, and I realized I must have stood rooted on that little sand pinnacle for far longer than I knew, too afraid to climb back down and get close to the willows. I crept back to the tent slow and quiet, doing one last full circuit of the camp, and I will not lie to myself or to you: I took measurements. I paced off the distance between the nearest willow trunk and the tent’s canvas, marking every step on the warm sand, burning the exact shortest distance into my memory.
I crawled back into my blankets. My companion appeared to still be deeply asleep, and I was grateful for it. If I had no one to corroborate these strange, terrible experiences, I could force myself to deny them. With full daylight, I could convince myself it had all been a hallucination, a fever dream of the sleepless night, nothing more than my overactive imagination run wild. Nothing else disturbed the camp after that. I fell asleep almost instantly, utterly drained, every last scrap of my spent energy gone—even as I waited, every thrumming nerve on edge, to hear that uncanny multitudinous patter of tiny feet outside the canvas once more, or feel that heavy weight settle over my chest, making my breath impossible to draw.

The sun hung so high in the heavens it bleached the willow fronds pale, seared the island sand to a blistering white, and bleared every edge of the landscape into something soft and unreal when my Swedish companion shook me out of a sleep thick as river mud. Porridge was bubbling on the fire, he said, and we had just enough time to bathe before the flood crept any higher. The sharp, greasy scent of frying bacon curled past the tent flap, pungent enough to cut through the last of the night’s heavy fog in my head.

“River’s still climbing,” he said, nodding out at the Danube, where the midstream islets we’d joked about tying the canoe to at sundown yesterday had vanished without a trace. “Our own island’s a third smaller than it was twelve hours ago.”

“Any dry firewood left?” I mumbled, still half-asleep.

“The woodpile and the shoreline will finish each other off at dawn tomorrow, neck and neck,” he laughed, but there was no warmth in the sound. “Enough to last us till then, anyway.”

I waded in from the ragged downstream spit of the island, and the icy water bit like shards of glass, yanking me downstream so fast the banks blurred past as quick as the countryside streaking by an express train’s window. I was dumped gasping back onto the sand at the tent landing before I could even take a full breath. The terror of the night before felt scoured out of me for a minute, the evaporation of the cold sweat on my skin chasing the last of the unease away under the blazing sun. Not a single cloud marred the pale blue sky, but the gale that had raged for three straight days hadn’t softened a single jot.

Then it hit me, sharp and unshakable: the Swede had changed his mind. The night before he’d been frantic to cast off at first light, yelling that we had to put miles between us and this stretch of willow swamp before dark. Now he was talking like we were sticking around for another full night on the shrinking sandbar. It didn’t make sense. What had shifted while I was asleep?

Whole chunks of the island bank sheared off while we ate our breakfast, great rumbling crumblings that sent plumes of spray the wind hurled straight into our spitting frying pan. The Swede rambled nonstop about how the Vienna-Pesth steamers would never find the channel in this kind of flood, but I barely listened. It was the shift in him that pricked at me: he was jumpy, a little shifty, his voice tight with a suspicion he couldn’t hide, his pipe untouched for the first time in a dozen shared expeditions. He pushed his porridge around the bowl and never took more than two bites, the map spread open flat in the sand beside him, his eyes tracing the same stretch of river line over and over like he was trying to find an escape route he knew wasn’t there.

“We should shove off sharp in an hour,” I said, testing the waters, hoping to prompt him into telling me what had spooked him. His answer sent a cold prickle up the back of my neck.

“Sure thing. If they let us.”

“Who’s they?” I asked, forcing indifference. “The current?”

“The powers of this awful place, whoever they are,” he said, never lifting his eyes from the map. “If there are old gods left anywhere in the world, they’re right here in these willows.”

“The elements are the only real immortals there are,” I laughed, as natural as I could manage, even as my chest went tight. He looked up, face grave, and met my eye straight on.

“We’ll be lucky to get away without more disaster.”

That was exactly what I’d dreaded hearing. I sucked in a breath and asked the direct question, the same way you force yourself to sit down in the dentist’s chair knowing the extraction is unavoidable no matter how much you drag your feet.

“More disaster? What’s happened?”

“For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone,” he said quiet as a grave digger.

“The steering paddle’s gone!” I yelped, jumping to my feet. That was our rudder. Out on the flood-swollen Danube without a rudder was certain suicide. “But what—”

“And there’s a tear in the bottom of the canoe,” he added, and I heard a tiny, unmistakeable tremor in his voice.

I just stared at him, my brain stuttering to a halt. The sun was blazing hot on the back of my neck, the sand searing my bare ankles, but I felt a freezing, thick atmosphere settle all around us like a damp shroud. He nodded toward the tent, and I followed him. The canoe lay overturned where we’d left it the night before, ribs glinting in the sun, only one paddle propped in the sand beside it.

“There’s only one left,” he said, stooping to pick it up. “And here’s the rent in the hull.”

I’d seen two paddles clearly before I fell asleep the night before, the memory sharp as a tack, but I bit the thought back. I didn’t want to say it out loud, didn’t want to name the unspoken thing creeping at the edges of our camp. The tear was long, unnervingly clean, a thin sliver of wood sliced perfectly out of the hull no rough snag could have made. It went right through. If we’d launched without noticing, the water would have swelled the wood shut for ten minutes, then burst through out in mid-stream, and the canoe—never more than two inches above the surface—would have filled and sunk before we could even scream.

“They’re prepping a sacrifice,” he muttered, half to himself, running his fingers along the slit. “Two sacrifices, looks like.”

I started whistling, the stupid, involuntary habit I get when I’m completely nonplussed, and deliberately ignored the line. I was going to write it off as stupid, no matter what.

“We must’ve scraped it when we pulled the canoe up onto the bank last night,” I said, stopping my whistling abruptly. “The river stones are sharp.”

I cut myself off mid-sentence when he turned and looked me dead in the eye. We both knew there were no stones on this stretch of sandbar. Not a single one.

“Then there’s this too,” he said, holding out the remaining paddle, pointing at the blade.

A new, icy dread spread through my chest when I took it. The entire surface of the blade had been sanded down, perfectly smooth, thin as parchment, so fine the first hard stroke in the water would snap it clean in half at the neck.

“One of us must’ve sleepwalked and done it,” I said weakly. “Or wind-blown sand particles filed it down, right?”

He laughed a sharp, mirthless little laugh as he turned away toward the willow thicket. “Sure. You can explain anything, can’t you?”

“Same gale that caught the steering paddle and blew it right to the edge of the bank, where it fell in when the shore crumbled,” I called after him, clinging to my flimsy rationalizations for dear life.

He glanced back at me before vanishing into the trees. “Right.”

Left alone with the evidence, the first thought that popped unbidden into my head was *It wasn’t me*. The second thought hit harder: neither of us had done this. The Swede was a trusted friend of a dozen backcountry expeditions, steady as a rock, no way he’d deliberately sabotage our escape. No way he’d snapped and done this insane, pointless harm in his sleep either.

But there was no escaping the obvious truth: he was waiting for something. He kept glancing over his shoulder at the bushes, cupping his ear to catch sounds I couldn’t hear, like he was tracking a sequence of unmentionable events unfolding right under our noses, waiting for the climax he was sure was coming fast. I made a quick circle of the camp, and for the first time noticed the strange little basins dotted all over the sand: perfect, smooth round hollows, ranging in size from a teacup to a full mixing bowl, no footprints leading to or from them. Wind funnels, I told myself. Whirlwinds. That’s all they were. The canoe tear was the only thing I couldn’t force an explanation for, but I clung to that flimsy story the way a drowning man clings to driftwood. We needed a working explanation, no matter how absurd, to keep the horror from swallowing us whole.

I set the pitch melting to caulk the hull, and the Swede joined me a few minutes later. Even working fast as he could, skilled as any Indigenous canoe builder, the boat wouldn’t be seaworthy before tomorrow at the earliest. I nodded casually at the little sand hollows.

“Wind,” I said, before he could tease me about finding explanations for everything. “Little whirlwinds dig them out, same as you see twist trash around on city sidewalks.”

He didn’t answer, just caulked the seam tight, his hands steady, his eyes flicking every few seconds to the willow thicket. I watched him out of the corner of my eye, and I could tell he was watching me back. He still kept pausing, head tilted, listening to something I couldn’t pick up. I dreaded him mentioning the way the willows had shifted overnight, the way they’d moved closer to our camp, because if he’d seen that too, I couldn’t blame it on my imagination anymore.

After a long, quiet stretch of work, he spoke in a hurried, tight voice, like he wanted to get the words out before he lost his nerve. “That otter we saw last night. Queer thing, wasn’t it?”

I looked up, startled. I’d been expecting him to say something far worse. “Shows how lonely this place is, right? Otters are shy.”

“I’m not talking about that,” he interrupted. “You saw it. Didn’t it look way bigger than an otter to you? At first?”

“Sunset light magnified it, that’s all,” I said.

He stared at me absently, like his mind was still stuck on the image. “It had yellow eyes. Glowing, like lit from the inside.”

“Sun reflection,” I laughed, too loud and forced. “Next you’ll be saying that drifting man in the boat wasn’t real—” I bit the sentence off mid-sentence. He was listening again, head turned to the treeline, his face so grave it made my blood run cold. I didn’t finish the thought. We went back to caulking. Five minutes later, he looked up at me across the hull, the smoking pitch pot in his hand.

“I did wonder, actually,” he said slow. “That wasn’t a man in the boat. I saw it rise straight up out of the water. It didn’t have a face.”

I laughed again, loud and angry this time, furious at him for breaking the thin veil of normalcy we’d been clinging to. “Stop it! This place is weird enough without making up extra nonsense! That was a normal boat, normal man, flying downstream fast as he could. That thing in the water was an otter. Let’s not act like fools.”

He just looked at me, calm as ever, no annoyance on his face.

“And for God’s sake, stop pretending you hear things! It’s just the river and that damn wind, and you’re making me jump at every shadow.”

“You utter fool,” he whispered, quiet and shocked, like he couldn’t believe I was still denying the obvious. “That’s exactly how all the victims talk. You know exactly what’s happening here, same as I do. Your stupid little self-deception is only going to make it worse when you can’t run from the truth anymore. Just be quiet. Hold your mind as tight as you can. Don’t give it an opening.”

I had nothing to say back. He was right. I was the fool, not him. He’d seen the pattern long before I did, and I’d been too stubborn to admit it. The line about victims settled heavy in my chest, and my fear grew sharper, higher, from that second on.

“You’re right about one thing, though,” he added. “We don’t talk about it. We don’t even dwell on it too hard. What you think, you speak. What you speak, happens.”

The rest of the afternoon we spent killing time: we fished (no bites), tested the patched hull for leaks, dragged driftwood back to the fire with long willow branches, watched the island shrink foot by foot as the river chewed the banks away. The sun blazed clear till four o’clock, then the gale that had raged for three days died all at once. Clouds rolled slow over the southwest sky, blotting out the sun.

The absence of the wind felt like a relief at first, the constant roar that had been pounding our eardrums gone at last. But the silence that settled over the swamp by five o’clock was far more oppressive. All you could hear then was the river, a low, deep rumble of bass notes that droned on and on, no variation, no peaks or valleys, the exact music of doom thrumming up through the sand under our feet. The sun vanished all at once, and every trace of cheer drained out of the landscape. The river turned black as ink. The willows huddled closer, rustling their leaves even when there wasn’t a breath of wind, shaking from the roots up, like they were all leaning in to watch us. These ordinary, plain bushes weren’t just bushes anymore. Their ordinariness was a mask for something malignant, something that was creeping closer, focusing all its attention on our tiny shrinking island, on us.

I’d napped through the early afternoon, recovered from the night’s exhaustion, which only made me more susceptible to that slow, creeping spell of the place. I fought it, laughed at myself for being childish, but the dread only grew stronger, until I dreaded the coming night the same way a lost kid in the middle of a dark forest dread the dark. The Swede tied our last remaining paddle tight to the trunk of the biggest willow, and we covered the patched canoe with a waterproof sheet, just in case the thing that stole the other paddle came back for this one. It was my turn to cook dinner: potatoes, onions, bacon fat, the last of the thick stew stock, broken up black bread, a plum dessert with sugar, and strong tea with dried milk. The fire burned bright and easy with no wind to blow the flames around. The Swede sat beside it, half-cleaning his pipe, half-giving me useless off-duty cooking advice. He hadn’t brought up any of the weird events all afternoon, just told me that the island was less than a third the size it had been when we first landed.

The stew had just started bubbling when I noticed he’d wandered off to the bank, and he called me over.

“Come listen,” he said, cupping his hand to his ear like he always did. “Tell me what you make of that.”

We stood side by side, still, and at first all I heard was the low drone of the river. Then the sound hit my ears, faint and thrumming: a low, muffled gong, struck at regular, even intervals. It didn’t sound like any steamer bell, any church bell, I’d ever heard. It came from across the swamp, then shifted, like it was hovering over our heads, then under the water, thrumming in my teeth and in the marrow of my bones.

“I’ve heard it all day,” he said. “I hunted it while you napped. Could never pin it down. Once I swore it was inside my own head.”

I strained to think of a rational explanation. “Wind blowing through hollow sand tunnels, right? Or the willow trunks rubbing together.”

“It’s coming from everywhere at once,” he said, ignoring me. “It’s the willows. That’s where the sound lives.”

“But the wind stopped,” I said. “They can’t make noise on their own.”

His answer sent a shiver down my spine so hard I almost stumbled. “We can hear it now precisely because the wind stopped. The gale was drowning it out. It’s the cry of the—”

I bolted back to the fire, the stew bubbling over and spitting grease onto the flames. I didn’t want to hear what he was about to say. I didn’t want to name it. We still had one more full night to get through, and I needed every scrap of sanity I could hold onto.

“Come tear up bread for the pot!” I yelled, stirring the stew so hard the spoon clattered against the sides. The stew pot was sanity, right there, warm and savory and normal.

He wandered slow over to the provision sack tied to the tree, fumbled in the opening, then dumped the whole contents out onto the ground sheet at his feet.

“Hurry!” I said. “It’s boiling over!”

The Swede burst into a roar of laughter that was so sharp and mirthless it made my skin crawl. He was holding his sides, like he was about to double over.

“The bread,” he gasped. “It’s gone. They took the bread.”

I dropped the spoon and ran over. Every other item from the sack was spread out on the ground sheet, the tea, the sugar, the dried fruit—but there was no loaf. Not a crumb left anywhere. The full weight of all my stored-up fear crashed down on me, and I started laughing too, that same wild, unnatural laughter, a pressure valve for all the terror we’d been bottling up. It cut off abruptly, dead silent, all at once for both of us.

“How stupid of me,” I said, scrambling to grab for a rational explanation. “I forgot to buy the loaf at Pressburg. The chattering old woman at the shop distracted me, I must’ve left it on the counter—”

“The oatmeal’s half gone too,” the Swede interrupted, quiet.

“Enough for tomorrow,” I said, stirring the stew so hard it sloshed over the sides. “We’ll be miles from here in twenty-four hours, eating fresh bread at Komorn.”

“God willing,” he muttered, shoving everything back into the sack and dragging it into the tent for safekeeping. “Unless they claim their sacrifice first.”

I stared out across the black, glistening river, at the willows huddled at the edge of the treeline, and I knew. The thing here wasn’t done with us yet. The gong sound kept on thrumming, low and constant, from everywhere at once.

It was my firm, iron-clad intention to lie wide awake the entire night, gun across my knees, and keep watch. The memory of the things that had crept near our camp after dark the evening before still pricked at the back of my skull like ice needles, and I knew I dared not drop my guard. But frayed nerves and bone-deep exhaustion dragged at me like invisible hands, and a thick, welcome blanket of oblivion stole over my senses before I even realized I was slumping back against my mattress.

My companion’s swift descent into sleep only hastened my own drift off. At first, he fidgeted so hard the cork mattress creaked beneath him, bolting upright every few minutes to grab my arm and hiss if I “heard that drag on the tent pegs” or “that whisper in the willow leaves.” He tossed so violently the tent canvas swayed, swearing the river had already risen high enough to swallow the low point of the island, and each time I stumbled out into the dark to check, the sand was still dry, the river bank holding fast. Eventually, his jerky movements stilled. His breathing evened out, and soft, rumbling snores drifted across the tent to me—for the first and only time in my life, the sound of someone snoring felt like a lifeline, a small, normal, human anchor in the rising dark. That was the last coherent thought I had before I slipped under.

I woke choking. The blanket was pulled tight over my face, but it was not fabric alone pressing into me: cold, weighty pressure lay heavy on my chest, my wrists, my forehead, as if a dozen small, unidentifiable things were leaning their full weight against me, squeezing the air right out of my lungs. My first groggy thought was that my companion had rolled off his mattress onto mine in his sleep. I called his name, yanking myself upright, and in the same split second, I knew the tent was surrounded.

That endless, soft, pattering sound I had feared for hours was all around us, thousands of tiny, padding feet moving in a slow, unbroken circle across the sand, filling the night with a thrumming, unnameable horror. I called the Swede’s name again, louder, my voice cracking. He did not answer. The snoring had cut off completely, and the tent flap—we had hooked it shut tight before we laid down, I swear it—hung flapping open in the dark. That was the unforgivable mistake.

I crawled across the tent floor to hook it closed, and my hand hit empty space where his mattress should have been. The Swede was not there. He was gone.

I tore out of the tent barefoot, my heart hammering so hard I thought it would burst through my ribs, and the second I cleared the canvas I plunged into a wall of humming. It came at me from every direction at once, low and thrumming, thick as honey, loud enough to vibrate in my fillings, the sound of a thousand great invisible bees all swarming right against my skin. The air itself felt thickened by it, my lungs burning as I fought to drag breath in. But my friend was out there somewhere, at their mercy, and I could not stop.

Dawn was just beginning to seep over the distant treeline, a thin, sickly pale gray light spilling across the low clouds, no wind stirring at all. I could just make out the dark bulk of the willow trunks, the glint of the river beyond, the pale stretches of sand. I ran screaming his name, my voice raw, but the willows swallowed the sound whole, the humming muffling it so completely that my shouts did not travel more than a few feet before they died away. I crashed through the underbrush, tripping over tangled roots, tearing my cheeks open on sharp, dragging branches that seemed to curl deliberately out to catch at me, to hold me back.

Then I stumbled out onto the island’s sharp point, and saw him. A dark silhouette balanced against the faint glow of the sky and the black river, one foot already submerged in the frigid, rushing water. One step more, and he would have waded out past the sandbar into the deep current.

I threw my full weight against him, wrapping my arms tight around his waist and dragging him backwards toward the shore with every last ounce of strength I had. He fought like a man possessed, his face glassy and blank, no flicker of recognition in his eyes, a deep, thrumming version of that same cursed humming pouring out of his open throat, spitting out phrases in no language I had ever heard, ranting that he had to go inside to Them, that the water and the wind had called him, that it was rude to refuse their invitation. The words curdled in my ears, turning my stomach cold with a horror I cannot name. It took every bit of strength I had left to haul him back into the tent, fling him onto his mattress, and pin him there until the convulsion passed.

The abruptness with which the fit broke was the strangest part of all. One second he was straining against my hold, frothing at the mouth, the humming and pattering outside rising to a fever pitch, and the next, every sound cut off dead, as if someone had snuffed them out with a single snap of their fingers. The Swede blinked, his eyes clearing, and stared up at me like a small, scared child, the faint dawn light spilling across his face through the tent flap. “My life, old man—I owe you my life,” he whispered. “But it’s all over now, anyhow. They’ve found a victim to take our place.”

He collapsed back onto his blankets before I could speak, and fell asleep instantly, snoring as peacefully as if he had never tried to walk straight into the river to offer himself up as a sacrifice. I sat over him, never closing my eyes for three full, endless hours, watching every shadow at the tent flap, waiting for that pattering to start again.

When the sun climbed high overhead, hot and bright in a sky so still not a single willow leaf moved, he woke naturally, no memory of the attempt he had made. He got right to his feet to start the breakfast fire, and I followed him down to the river’s edge when he went to bathe, tensed to tackle him if he made a move to wade out. He only dipped his head in, grinning and complaining that the water was unnaturally cold. “River’s falling at last,” he said, wiping water out of his eyes.

“The humming’s stopped, too,” I said.

He looked up at me, his face completely normal, no trace of that glazed, possessed look from before. He remembered nothing of his attempt to drown himself, nothing of the wild words he had screamed. “Everything’s stopped,” he said, pausing like he was searching for a word he could not quite name. “Because—”

“Because they found another victim?” I asked, forcing a laugh that felt hollow in my throat.

“Exactly,” he said, nodding immediately. “I’m as sure of it as if I saw it with my own two eyes. We’re safe now.” He stood up, and grabbed a thick stick off the sand. “Come on. If we look, we’ll find it.”

He set off at a run along the bank, poking at every little sandy cove and back eddy and tangle of willow roots, me right on his heels. A second later, he stopped, and gasped. “Ah!”

The sound of his voice flooded me with all the terror of the last 24 hours, and I ran to his side. He was pointing his stick at a large, dark shape half-submerged in the shallow water, caught fast in a twist of willow roots that kept the current from sweeping it away. That spot had been fully underwater a few hours before, at the peak of the high tide. “See?” he said quietly. “That’s the victim that bought our escape.”

I peered over his shoulder, and saw his stick rested on the body of a man. He rolled it over with the stick, and the face half-buried in the sand turned toward us. It was a drowned peasant, waterlogged and pale, dead no more than a few hours, swept downstream to wash up on our island exactly at the moment the Swede’s fit had broken. “We have to give him a proper burial,” the Swede said, already climbing down the bank.

I followed slower, shuddering despite the hot sun. The current had torn most of his clothes away, leaving his neck and the top of his chest exposed. Halfway down the bank, the Swede suddenly froze, holding up a hand to warn me to stop, but my foot slipped on the loose sand, and I stumbled into him, sending him lurching forward. We both went tumbling onto the hard wet sand, our feet splashing in the edge of the current, and collided hard against the corpse.

The Swede screamed. I leapt back as if I had been shot.

The second our bodies made contact with the drowned man, a deafening burst of humming erupted right off his skin—dozens of separate thrumming tones, the sound of thousands of beating invisible wings swirling all around us, rising fast into the sky. They faded off into the distance, growing fainter and fainter, until there was no sound left at all. It was as if we had disturbed a swarm of the unseen things mid-work, feeding on the dead body.

We clutched at each other, trembling, before we noticed the current had worked the corpse loose from the willow roots. It turned slow in the water, rolling over so its face stared straight up at the unblinking sun, lying right at the edge of the main current. A second more, and it would be swept away.

The Swede lunged forward to grab it, shouting something about a proper burial, then dropped hard to his knees, clapping his hands over his eyes. I was at his side in an instant, and I saw what he had seen.

The exposed face and bare chest of the corpse were dotted all over with perfect, small, neat little hollows, exactly the same size and shape as the tiny sand funnels we had found scattered across every inch of the island the day before. They were not wounds, not bites—they were marks, left by something that had settled into the flesh to take its fill.

“Their mark,” the Swede whispered, hoarse. “Their awful mark.”

When I looked back to the river, the current had already taken the body. It spun over and over in the rushing water, turning further and further away, vanishing around the distant bend, gone beyond any hope of ever finding it again.
