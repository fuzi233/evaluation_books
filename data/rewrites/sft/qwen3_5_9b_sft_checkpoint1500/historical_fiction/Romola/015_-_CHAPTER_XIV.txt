The Peasants' Fair.

The thronging mass and the jarring medley of sounds that assaulted him at the Piazza's threshold recalled to Tito what Nello had whispered concerning the *Fierucoloni*. With a strange, almost pleasurable sensation in the pushing and shoving, he plunged deeper into the crush. The chaotic noise served to fill the voids of his time, numbing the bleak calculations of his future that haunted him as he envisioned drifting alone in search of some undefined fortune. Even the thought of tracking down Baldassarre flickered briefly in his mind.

Through the opposing archways, people struggled to enter, while above them, paper lanterns tethered to wooden poles waved erratically in the breeze. A crude, monotonous chant formed a persistent thread beneath a cacophony of screams, whistles, mocking songs from boys' piping voices, drumbeats, and tinkling bells. Periodically, one of the dim, floating lights vanished in a crash, struck by a stone hurled with vague intent, followed immediately by shrieks and renewed shouting. Yet, on the fringes of this swirling tumult, certain groups observed the Nativity of the Virgin with a more disciplined reverence than the fitful stone-throwing elsewhere.

Ragged men darted sharp glances around while their tongues ran on merrily, hawking games to country folk on generous terms. Two figures masked and elevated on stilts, having snatched lanterns from the crowd, swayed the lights like meteors as they strode about. At a small covered stall, a shrewd merchant conducted a profitable trade in hot *berlingozzi*, a favorite flour-based delicacy. Another man stood atop a barrel, backed firmly against a pillar of the loggia before the Foundling Hospital (*Spedale degl' Innocenti*), peddling efficacious pills invented by a physician from Salerno, guaranteed to ward off toothache and drowning. Nearby, against another pillar, a tumbler showcased his feats on a miniature stage. Meanwhile, a handful of apprentices, dismissing the leisurely guerrilla warfare of stone-throwing, engaged in a concentrated private contest of their beloved Florentine sport at the narrow entrance of Via de' Febbrai.

Obligated to navigate chance gaps in the crowd, Tito found himself momentarily beside the trotting procession of barefoot, hard-heeled *contadine*. He gazed upon their sun-dried, bronze-toned faces and their strange, fragmented attire—garbed in obsolete stuffs and faded with hereditary grime. To the city dwellers, they appeared like a weary ancestry returning from a pilgrimage undertaken a century prior. These were the sturdy, sparsely fed peasant women from the Pistoian mountains, entering with the fruits of a year's labor in a modest bundle of yarn strapped to their backs. In their hearts beat that meager hope of good and that vast, dim fear of harm, matters they entrusted to the Blessed Virgin. On this Eve of her Nativity, her miraculous image, painted by angels, would have its veil drawn, allowing its potency to flow unobstructed.

Elsewhere, driven toward the plaza's edge, Tito encountered those more stationary vendors who had positioned themselves for safety behind their backs. Among them, he recognized Bratti. The pedlar leaned against a pillar, his lips pursed in disdainful silence, casting cold, superior glances at everyone who approached while clutching tightly to a serge covering that hid the contents of his basket. Surprised by such an unusual demeanor for an anxious trader, Tito stepped closer. As two women approached Bratti's basket with curious looks, the pedlar pulled the covering tighter and diverted his gaze. It was too provoking; one of the women was compelled to ask what lay within.

"Before I answer that, Monna," Bratti said, "I must know if you intend to buy. I cannot display wares of my caliber in this fair for every fly to land on and depart unpaid. My goods are too choice for that. Furthermore, I have but two left, and I have no wish to soil them. Given the rumors of pestilence circulating among the wise, there is a likelihood they are worth their weight in gold. No, no: *andate con Dio*."

The two women exchanged glances.

"And what may be the price?" asked the second.

"Not within what you are likely to possess in your purse, *buona donna*," Bratti replied in a tone of compassionate superiority. "I advise you to trust in Messer Domeneddio and the saints; poor people can do no better for themselves."

"Not so poor!" cried the second woman indignantly, producing her money-bag. "Come, now! What say you to a *grosso*?"

"I say you may secure twenty-one *quattrini* for it," Bratti stated coolly. "But not from me, for I lack such small change."

"Two, then?" the woman demanded, growing exasperated as her companion eyed her with envy. "It will hardly exceed two, I think."

Following further bidding and mercantile coquetry, Bratti feigned an air of concession.

"Since you have set your mind upon it," he said slowly, lifting the cover, "I would be loath to cause you harm. Maestro Gabbadeo used to remark that when a woman desires something and fails to obtain it, she falls into greater danger of the pestilence than before. *Ecco!* I have but two left. Let me tell you, the companion to these is on the finger of Maestro Gabbadeo, who has departed for Bologna—a physician as wise as any at any gate."

The precious objects were two clumsy iron rings, hammered into the shape of ancient Roman rings, occasionally unearthed. The rust upon them and the completely obscured nature of their alleged power were so convincing that the *grossi* were paid without complaint. The first woman, lacking such handsome coins, eventually drove a bargain with some of her yarn after much feigned reluctance on Bratti's part, departing in triumph with the remaining ring. Bratti concealed his basket, now filled with miscellaneous items presumably acquired under similar circumstances, and, moving from his pillar, unexpectedly encountered Tito, who, if given time, would have preferred to avoid recognition.

"By the head of San Giovanni, now!" Bratti exclaimed, drawing Tito back to the pillar. "What a stroke of luck. For I was discussing you this morning, Messer Greco; I said he has ascended among the *signori* now—and I am glad of it, for I was at the bottom of his fortune. However, I can rarely catch a word with him, for he is not to be found lounging on stones anymore—he isn't! But it is your luck, not mine, Messer Greco, save for some trifling gratuity to satisfy me for my trouble in this transaction."

"You speak in riddles, Bratti," Tito retorted. "Remember, I do not sharpen my wits, as you do, by haggling over iron rings: you must be plain."

"By the Holy 'Vangels! it was an easy bargain I extended. If a Hebrew secures thirty-two percent, I trust a Christian may secure a little more. Had I not borne a conscience, I should have obtained double the money and double the yarn. But, speaking of rings, it is your ring—that very ring upon your finger—that I could find a buyer for; yes, and a buyer with a deep purse."

"Truly?" Tito asked, examining his ring and listening intently.

"A Genovese gentleman bound directly for Hungary, as I understand. He visited my shop recently, scouring it to see if I possessed any antiquities whose value I underestimated; I warrant he thought I carried a pumpkin on my shoulders. He had been searching every shop in Florence. He wore a ring—not exactly like yours, but of similar fashion. Discussing rings, I mentioned I knew a fine young man, a particular friend, who owned a ring of that sort. He asked, 'Who is he, pray? Tell him I will pay his price.' And I thought of pursuing you to Nello's tomorrow. In my opinion, Messer Greco, you are not the type to watch the Arno turn to broth and stand idly by without dipping a finger."

Though Tito had missed not a single word, his mind had been racing. Why retain the ring? It had been merely a sentiment, a fleeting fancy, that had stopped him from selling it alongside his other gems. If he had been wiser and sold it, he might perhaps have evicted the identification Fra Luca sought to establish. True, it had been removed from Baldassarre's finger and placed on his own as soon as his youthful hand grew large enough, yet there was no valid spiritual merit in such superstitious scruples regarding inanimate objects. The ring had facilitated his recognition. Tito had begun to detest recognition, a demand from the past. This foreigner's offer, if genuine, presented an opportunity to discard the ring without the bother of hunting for a purchaser.

"You speak with your customary wisdom, Bratti," said Tito. "I have no objection to hearing what your Genovese will offer. But when and where shall I expect to meet him?"

"Tomorrow, at three hours after sunrise, he will await you at my shop. If your wit possesses the sharpness I have always attributed to you, Messer Greco, you will demand a heavy price; for he cares not for money. It is my belief he purchases for someone else, not for himself—perhaps for some great lord."
