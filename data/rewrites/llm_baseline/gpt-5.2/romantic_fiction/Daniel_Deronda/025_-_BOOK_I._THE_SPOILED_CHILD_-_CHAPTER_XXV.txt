How is one to map the motives of a man whose inner country has been grazed to stubble by fastidious self-regard—where wanting is no longer a clean hunger but a finicky loitering before a larder that never quite tempts? A life left too long to “as you like” grows rank with impulses that sprout and die without bearing. And there are people—men of breeding, men of means—whose intentions are declared not by generous speech but by a small, cruel alteration of tone, as if they were tasting a sauce and deciding, without complaint, that it shall be thrown away.

So it was that when Grandcourt, hearing that Gwendolen Harleth had quitted Leubronn, let fall—incidentally, with the same languid certainty with which he might pronounce a cut of meat overdone—that the place was a beastly hole, worse than Baden, Mr. Lush took it for a settled menu: they were going back to Diplow. The resolve, of course, would be executed with the leisurely dawdle that was Grandcourt’s way of giving power its full savor.

He loitered through another day, giving no distinct order. He lingered over his toilet as if the choosing of linen and the exacting brush of a sleeve were a kind of private cookery—an art of reduction, taking the wholesome blood out of life until only a pale essence remained, refined and faint. He came down with that faded distinction which makes fresh hands—hands that have kneaded dough or cut herbs in the garden—seem, by contrast, raw and common. He lingered on the terrace, in the gambling-rooms, in the reading-room; he occupied himself in a deliberate indifference, as if indifference were an expensive dish served cold and meant to be admired for its chill.

Yet even the chilled can be made to speak when society approaches with its spoon and expects a taste.

When he met Lady Mallinger, he raised his hat, paused, and listened just long enough that she might feel she had been heard. She recommended the waters, with that amiable faith which tries to sweeten bitterness by insisting it is tonic.

“Yes,” he said, “I heard somebody say how providential it was that there always happened to be springs at gambling places.”

“Oh, that was a joke,” Lady Mallinger answered, innocent as milk just drawn and still warm with the cow’s breath—misled by his languid seriousness. “In imitation of the old one about the towns and the rivers, you know.”

“Ah, perhaps,” said Grandcourt, with no more change of expression than a face of stone in rain.

Lady Mallinger carried this to Sir Hugo as something curious—like an odd flavor one cannot name. Sir Hugo, who liked his scandal and his wit as he liked his claret: not too harsh, not allowed to turn sour in bad company, said, “My dear, he is not a fool. Don’t suppose he can’t see a joke. He can play his cards as well as most of us.”

“He has never seemed to me a very sensible man,” she said, and beneath her excuse there lay a more secret smart: Grandcourt was a great living reminder of what she called, in her own mind, her failure as a wife—the absence of a son for Sir Hugo. It is a quiet grief, like bread that will not rise no matter how patiently you keep it warm; and the thought that a husband might fairly regret his choice, might have dealt harshly if he were less good, haunted her with a timorous gratitude.

Grandcourt also gave Deronda a recognition. Deronda answered with perfect civility—and felt no gratitude. Old feelings, rooted as early tastes are rooted—those first strong impressions of sweetness and shame—do not yield to later reasoning. He could not unmake, by any argument about custom, the sense that his birth had been attended by an injury for which his father was to blame. He knew, too, that but for that injury Grandcourt’s prospects might have been his. And because he saw how easily such knowledge can turn into a mean relish—triumphing over another’s good fortune, or nursing rancor at one’s own lack—he was proud enough to refuse the dish. He turned his back on that ugly pit, and held to kindlier affections as a man holds to honest food when he might cheaply gorge on poison.

Pride served him well here: the pride that will not let a vulgar mind dictate what counts as disadvantage. He would not have Grandcourt imagine envy in him, as a coarse eater imagines everyone covets his heavy supper. But there is no guarding against another man’s interpretation. Grandcourt was quite capable of believing that Deronda, poor devil, inwardly winced under their mutual position; and the thought of envy—imaginary envy—was one of those sweet meats egoism likes to keep by. Grandcourt’s pet dogs were not the only creatures he enjoyed making jealous.

So he was civil enough to exchange a few words with Deronda on the terrace about the hunting round Diplow, and even said, “You had better come over for a run or two when the season begins.”

Lush, who was never displeased by delay when delay promised him comfort, amused himself with lounging promenades, gossiping with Sir Hugo, answering questions about Grandcourt’s affairs so far as they touched the baronet’s willingness to part with his interest in Diplow. Sir Hugo had a taste for good-humored scandal—he called it, with a little air, traits de mœurs—yet he kept it from listeners who might let it turn into moral hysteria. Whatever he knew of his nephew’s secrets, he had never offered it to Deronda. Deronda, for his part, found Grandcourt a pale-blooded mortal, and had no desire to hear in what way the red had been washed out.

Lush’s policy, when he had no reason to the contrary, was to gratify. He had the knack of being agreeable, like a dish that suits many palates precisely because it is not too strong. Sir Hugo treated him well: one of those handy, second-best articles a gentleman uses without anxiety. And Lush, being used to a world that took him for apt and pleasant, liked to be of use—liked, too, the sense of being needed.

“Well, you will let me know the turn of events,” Sir Hugo said, “if this marriage seems likely to come off after all, or if anything else happens to make the want of money pressing. My plan would be much better for him than burdening Ryelands.”

“That’s true,” said Lush, “only it mustn’t be urged on him—just placed in his way that the scent may tickle him. Grandcourt isn’t a man to be always led by what makes for his own interest—especially if you let him see that it makes for your interest too. I’m attached to him, of course. I’ve given up everything else for the sake of keeping by him, and it’s lasted a good fifteen years now. He wouldn’t easily get anyone else to fill my place. He’s a peculiar character, is Henleigh Grandcourt, and it has been growing on him of late years. However, I’m of a constant disposition, and I’ve been a sort of guardian to him since he was twenty; an uncommonly fascinating fellow he was then, to be sure—and could be now, if he liked. I’m attached to him; and it would be a good deal worse for him if he missed me at his elbow.”

Sir Hugo did not trouble himself with sympathy. He knew how much of any man’s speech is a kind of verbal dressing-gown, put on decently for company; and he could allow for such necessities as a member of Parliament allows for the tailor’s bill.

But Lush’s easy expectation of procrastination was cut short the next morning. Grandcourt, as if deciding on a journey with the same small motion with which he taps ash from a cigar, said, “Are you making all the arrangements for our starting by the Paris train?”

“I didn’t know you meant to start,” Lush answered—more accurately, he had not been told.

“You might have known,” said Grandcourt, looking at the burned length of his cigar and speaking in that lowered tone he used when he meant to be peremptory and let disgust do the work of passion. “Just see to everything, will you? and mind no brute gets into the same carriage with us. And leave my P.P.C. at the Mallingers’.”

So they were at Paris the next day. There Lush was gratified—commanded, rather—into going straight on to Diplow and seeing everything right, while Grandcourt and the valet remained behind. It was not until several days later that Lush received the telegram ordering the carriage to the Wanchester station.

During the interval, Lush was active: he carried out orders about stud and household, but he also did what was habitual to him—he gathered information like a man tasting as he goes, knowing that one spice too many can ruin the whole dish. He learned all he could about Gwendolen, about Offendene, about the family misfortunes that had fallen like a sudden blight on harvest.

What would such news do to Grandcourt’s fitful obstinacy? Lush could not calculate it. Poverty might make acceptance more likely; and the thought of being accepted—without the delicious risk of a repulse—might, perversely, be just the thing to send Grandcourt fluttering away like a moth from a candle too steadily lit. Lush had watched him for years. To know Grandcourt was to doubt him.

It might happen—Lush was not blind to theatrical possibilities—that Grandcourt would step, for once, into a sort of moral splendor, like a modern French stage-hero who suddenly grows noble at the curtain, leaving you no confidence in what he’d do in Act Two. What more honorable final pose than to decline an heiress and marry the attractive girl who had none? But Lush held to one general certainty: of all inward movements, generosity was least likely in Grandcourt. And yet what use is it to know an insect never walks head-first backwards, when you must predict which twist of stimulus will send it down a particular thread in the web?

Lush wanted, above all, to be confident that Grandcourt and Gwendolen would never marry. He could have tolerated an heiress. He could even have tolerated Mrs. Glasher; there, at least, was gratitude, security, a wife dependent and therefore manageable—and if society refused her, it would not disturb Lush’s private comfort. He could have tolerated no marriage. But a marriage with Gwendolen—young, proud, likely to bring trouble—threatened the ease he felt he had earned by living, for years, a dog’s life: a dog well fed, to be sure, with music and luxury, but still a dog at heel.

He was not conscious of wishing harm to anyone—except, perhaps, just now, a little harm to the inconvenient and impertinent Gwendolen. Even the easiest-humored of toad-eaters has nerves; even a palate accustomed to rich sauces may revolt at being forced to swallow insult.

So, with a half-formed war-policy, he waited for Grandcourt’s arrival, mostly wondering how the campaign would begin.

The first day, Grandcourt was much occupied with the stables. Among other things he ordered a groom to put a side-saddle on Criterion and let him review the horse’s paces. That one order was like the smell of a particular dish from a kitchen door: it told Lush what was intended, more certainly than a declaration. It set him reconsidering whether he should risk speaking first, while he was still sure no compromising step had been taken.

He rose the next morning nearly resolved that if Grandcourt appeared in any talkative humor, he would drop—casually, as if letting fall a crumb—those facts about Gwendolen and her family, to see how they worked and to get some guidance. But Grandcourt did not enter into talk. In answer to a question even about convenience, he kept a silence no fish could have bettered.

After reading his letters, he gave various orders to be executed or transmitted by Lush, then thrust his shoulder toward that useful person—the dismissal. Lush rose to go. But before he reached the door, Grandcourt turned his head slightly and gave a broken, languid “Oh.”

“What is it?” said Lush.

“Shut the door, will you? I can’t speak into the corridor.”

Lush shut it, came forward, and chose to sit down—choice being his small rebellion.

After a pause, Grandcourt said, “Is Miss Harleth at Offendene?”

He was quite certain Lush had made it his business to know; and he tasted, in advance, the displeasure of forcing Lush to speak what Lush would rather keep seasoned for later.

“Well, I hardly know,” said Lush carelessly. “The family’s utterly done up. They and the Gascoignes too have lost all their money. It’s owing to some rascally banking business. The poor mother hasn’t a sou, it seems. She and the girls have to huddle themselves into a little cottage like a laborer’s.”

“Don’t lie to me, if you please,” said Grandcourt, in his lowest audible tone. “It’s not amusing, and it answers no other purpose.”

“What do you mean?” Lush asked, nettled beyond his habit; the future was disturbing him, and irritation makes a man careless of his own recipe for pleasing. “Just tell me the truth, will you? It’s no invention of mine. I’ve heard the story from several—Bazley, Brackenshaw’s man, for one. He is getting a new tenant for Offendene.”

“I don’t mean that. Is Miss Harleth there, or is she not?” Grandcourt said, as if he were asking for a single ingredient, not a whole stew of circumstances.

“Upon my soul, I can’t tell,” said Lush, sulkily. “She may have left yesterday. I heard she had taken a situation as governess; she may be gone to it for what I know. But if you wanted to see her no doubt the mother would send for her back.”

The sneer slipped out with the words, unbidden.

“Send Hutchins to inquire whether she will be there to-morrow.”

Lush did not move. He had thought over beforehand what he might say, and now an unexpected irritation shoved those prearranged warnings onto his tongue. The scrape Grandcourt might get into was too tremendous to watch in silence while the first step was taken. He kept a tone of rational friendliness, but he felt his own value and was ready to be bold.

“It would be as well for you to remember, Grandcourt, that you are coming under closer fire now. There can be none of the ordinary flirting done, which may mean everything or nothing. You must make up your mind whether you wish to be accepted; and more than that, how you would like being refused. Either one or the other. You can’t be philandering after her again for six weeks.”

Grandcourt said nothing. He pressed the newspaper down on his knees and began to light another cigar—as calmly as a man in a noisy city might seal his ears by attending only to the scratch of his match, choosing that small clean sound over the world’s coarse clamor. Lush, catching at the sign of permission, went on; he wanted to learn which hesitation would be stronger—fear of refusal or distaste for certain acceptance.

“Everything has a more serious look now than it had before. There is her family to be provided for. You could not let your wife’s mother live in beggary. It will be a confoundedly hampering affair. Marriage will pin you down in a way you haven’t been used to; and in point of money you have not too much elbow-room. And after all, what will you get by it? You are master over your estates, present or future, as far as choosing your heir goes; it’s a pity to go on encumbering them for a mere whim, which you may repent of in a twelvemonth. I should be sorry to see you making a mess of your life in that way. If there were anything solid to be gained by the marriage, that would be a different affair.”

His voice grew more and more unctuous, like oil poured in hopes of making a hard morsel slide down. He had nearly forgotten that he was gambling in argument.

When he stopped, Grandcourt took the cigar from his mouth and looked steadily at its moist end while he adjusted the leaf with delicate fingertips, as if the whole matter were merely a question of tidiness.

“I knew before that you had an objection to my marrying Miss Harleth.” He paused, letting the sentence cool. “But I never considered that a reason against it.”

“I never supposed you did,” Lush answered, dryly. “It was not that I urged as a reason. I should have thought it might have been a reason against it, after all your experience, that you would be acting like the hero of a ballad, and making yourself absurd—and all for what? You know you couldn’t make up your mind before. It’s impossible you can care much about her. And as for the tricks she is likely to play, you may judge of that from what you heard at Leubronn. However, what I wished to point out to you was, that there can be no shilly-shally now.”

“Perfectly,” said Grandcourt. He looked round at Lush and fixed him with narrow eyes. “I don’t intend that there should be. I dare say it’s disagreeable to you. But if you suppose I care a damn for that you are most stupendously mistaken.”

“Oh, well,” said Lush, rising, hands in pockets, venom still warm in him, “if you have made up your mind!—only there’s another aspect of the affair. I have been speaking on the supposition that it was absolutely certain she would accept you, and that destitution would have no choice. But I am not so sure that the young lady is to be counted on. She is kittle cattle to shoe, I think. And she had her reasons for running away before.”

He moved a step or two, standing nearly in front of Grandcourt, not much restrained by consequences. He knew his hold was serviceableness; even after a quarrel the need of him would return like appetite. He foresaw Gwendolen would oust him for a time, and his temper urged him to risk the rupture.

“She had her reasons,” he repeated, more significantly.

“I had come to that conclusion before,” said Grandcourt, with contemptuous irony.

“Yes, but I hardly think you know what her reasons were.”

“You do, apparently,” said Grandcourt, betraying by no eyelash that he cared.

“Yes—and you had better know too, that you may judge of the influence you have over her if she swallows her reasons and accepts you. For my own part I would take odds against it. She saw Lydia in Cardell Chase and heard the whole story.”

Grandcourt made no immediate answer. He went on smoking, as if the only thing in the room worth attending to were the faint crackle of the leaf and the soft exhale—private sounds, controlled, unlike the loud machinery of other people’s feelings. He waited so long that Lush began to move about and look out of the window, unwilling to leave without seeing some effect. He had expected Grandcourt to accuse him of contriving the interview, since Mrs. Glasher was then living at Gadsmere a hundred miles away, and he was ready to admit it. What mattered was that Grandcourt should feel he must approach a girl who had that knowledge and had been frightened by it.

At last Grandcourt looked at Lush again and said, with the same contempt as if discussing a trivial point of stable-management, “What follows?”

It was a neat mate to Lush’s check. However exasperated he was, it would have been idiocy to pretend that another move could do good. He gave a slight shrug and was turning away when Grandcourt—turning on his seat toward the table—said, as quietly as if nothing sharp had passed between them, “Oblige me by pushing that pen and paper here, will you?”

No blustering tyrant could have exercised the spell Grandcourt did in such moments. Men who would never call him master still found themselves moving at his smallest sign, as if his will were a dominant language their bodies understood even when their minds resented it.

Lush pushed the pen and paper. Grandcourt took them and said, “Just wait for this letter.”

He scrawled with ease; a brief note, quickly addressed.

“Let Hutchins go with it at once, will you?” Grandcourt said, pushing it away.

As Lush expected, it was directed to Miss Harleth, Offendene.

When Lush’s irritation cooled, he was glad there had been no explosive quarrel. Yet he felt a notch had been cut against him, and that somehow he was meant to pay. It was clear, too, that his revelation had not softened Grandcourt but hardened him, like sugar boiled too far into brittle. And as to what inward motions had made that hardening—what secret appetite, what resistance to being mastered by another’s knowledge—Lush could only lift his chin in despair of any theory.
