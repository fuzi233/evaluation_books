I rose to continue my march, traversing miles of barren wasteland. My spirit screamed for elevation—a mountain peak or a towering crag from which I might survey the desolate plain and the dried, skeletal channels leading toward some distant hope. Yet, what strategic value could such foresight hold? The internal state of a man dictates his fate, not the external landscape he surveys. Anticipation is not comprehension; otherwise, the prophetic instinct inherent in humanity would manifest with greater frequency.

Halfway to the horizon, the sun cast its dying light upon a rugged ascent of rock. Before I could reach it, the urge to climb evaporated, replaced by a desperate need to collapse. By the time the orb dipped below the line, twilight had swallowed the air. Beneath my feet lay a carpet of emerald moss, a royal cushion worthy of a king. I threw myself onto it, and instantly, the tide of exhaustion receded. The moment my head touched the verdant surface, the third time, I heard the roar of waters below—broken airs and ethereal harmonies clashing against the stones of buried channels. It was a chaotic symphony, the aquatic harp sending endless, recurring gurgle and bell-like drips to my ears. What masterwork could Handel have composed from this mingling, mutually destructive melody?

Listening, my gaze wandered up the steep rocky slope above, reading the geological record etched into the stone: centuries ago, a cataract had thundered here, filling the channels that now guided my weary steps. My heart swelled at the memory of that splendid tumult, where waves danced in helpless descent, massing their power into a single, organ-deafening roar. Soon, the hidden brooks lulled me into sleep, their lullabies weaving themselves into my dreams.

I awoke before dawn and scrambled upward to inspect the vista beyond. Alas, there lay nothing but a sea of fine, powdery sand. No trace remained of the river that had plunged down the rocks; the drift had filled its course to the level of the dreary expanse. Looking back, I saw the river had bifurcated as it fell—the branch whose bank I had followed to the foot of the rocky scaur, and the one I had first crossed to enter the Evil Wood. The wood loomed on the far horizon, trapped between the two arms of the delta. To my left, the desert stretched endlessly; to the right, a subtle lift in the skyline hinted at the forest my hostess had indicated.

I sat, reaching into my pocket for the half-loaf I had carried. Only then did I grasp the meaning behind my hostess's warning. The bread was not sustenance for tomorrow; it had shrunk and hardened into a stone. Discarding it, I resumed my trek.

By noon, I encountered scattered tamarisks and junipers, followed by stunted firs. As I progressed, thicker thickets and larger pines intercepted my path. Eventually, I entered a forest of pine and hardwood identical to that where the Little Ones nurtured their young, convincing me I had circled back to a familiar sector. But geography was irrelevant; *where* mattered not when *everywhere* equaled *nowhere*. I had not yet transformed this void into a place through action. I was not truly alive; I was merely dreaming of existence. I was a consciousness with a view, devoid of substance. In the world I had abandoned, I had been nothing else, but now the truth crystallized. I resolved that if I caught a faint gleam of the mirror in this forest, I would veer sharply away, lest it trap me unawares and drag me back to my old stagnation. Here, amidst the unknown, I might learn to exist by acting. I could not bear the thought of returning with so many beginnings and no conclusion. The Little Ones would meet their appointed fate; the awful witch would remain unseen; the dead would rise without me. I would simply wake to realize I had dreamt, that all my wandering had been futile. Rather than arrive at such a hollow end, I would press onward.

I ventured deeper into the wood, intending to rest within its embrace.

The trees had grown massive, standing in a regular, almost geometric formation with spacious intervals between them. Undergrowth was sparse, granting visibility for leagues in every direction. The forest resembled a vast cathedral—solemn, silent, and empty. I encountered no creature on two legs or four that day. Occasionally, a swift shadow or a sluggish form crossed the field of my vision, but always at a distance, enhancing the profound sense of isolation. I heard the chirping of birds and witnessed a profusion of butterflies, displaying colors of marvelous complexity and dazzling purity.

Reaching a clearing where pines stood further apart, allowing flowering shrubs to thrive, I hoped this signaled proximity to habitation. Hunger drove me to follow the trail of increasingly dense roses, seeking the voice and face of kin—or any sentient soul, human or otherwise, capable of mutual understanding. What a hellish horror it was to wander alone, a bare existence that never expanded outward, never widening its life through connection. Bound by the cords of its own peculiarities, I was an eternal prisoner in the dungeon of my own being. I was learning that solitude was impossible, even for oneself, unless in the presence of others—and then, terrifyingly, it was possible to be utterly alone. Evil thrived only through the absence of good; selfishness was merely a parasite on the Tree of Life. In my former world, I possessed the habit of solitary song; here, not a crooning murmur escaped my lips. There, I sang without thinking; here, I thought without singing. There, I had never known a bosom friend; here, the affection of an idiot would be divinely welcome. "If only I had a dog to love!" I sighed, regarding my past self with wonder. That version of me preferred the company of books and pens to that of men and women; if the author of a tale I enjoyed appeared, I would wish him gone so I might return to his story. I had chosen the dead over the living, the thing thought over the thing thinking. "Any man," I declared now, "is more than the greatest of books!" I had neglected my living brothers and sisters, leaving me now without even the dead to offer comfort.

The woods thinned further, the pines rising like colossal columns eager to support the heavens. Other species joined the canopy; the forest grew richer. The roses had become trees, their blossoms of astonishing splendor.

Suddenly, I spied what appeared to be a great house or castle. Its forms were so strangely indistinct that I could not be certain it was anything more than a chance combination of tree shapes. As I drew nearer, the lines held together, yet neither they nor the structure gained definition. Standing before it, I remained as doubtful as before. Was it a habitable dwelling? Or a ruin overtaken by ivy and roses? Yet, hiding within the foliage, not a single wall remnant could I discern. Again and again, I seemed to perceive a building, only for it to vanish under closer inspection. Could it be, I pondered, that ivy had embraced a huge edifice and consumed it, its interlaced branches retaining the phantom shapes of the walls it had assimilated? Certainty was impossible.

Ahead lay a rectangular vacancy—the ghost of a doorway without a door. Stepping through, I found myself in an open space resembling a great hall. Its floor was covered with grass and flowers; its walls and roof were woven from ivy and vines, interspersed with roses.

This was the perfect place to spend the night. Gathering a quantity of withered leaves, I piled them in a corner and collapsed upon them. A crimson sunset flooded the hall; the night was warm, and my couch restful. Lying there, I gazed up at the living ceiling, tracing its network of branches and twigs, the clouds of foliage, and the peering patches of higher roof. My eyes waded through the greenery until the sun sank and the sky began to darken. Then, the red roses turned black, and soon only the yellow and white blooms remained visible. When they faded, the stars emerged, hanging in the leaves like live topazes, throbbing, sparkling, and flashing myriad colors. I was canopied by a tree from Aladdin's cave.

Then I noticed the hall teemed with nests. Tiny heads, nearly indistinguishable, popped out with a chirp or two before vanishing again. For a while, there were rustlings, stirrings, and little prayers. But as darkness deepened, the small heads stilled. Finally, every feathered mother settled her brood beneath her wings; the chatter in the little beds ceased, and God's bird-nursery rested beneath the waves of sleep. Once more, a few flutterings drew my gaze upward: an owl sailed across. I caught only a glimpse, yet felt the cool wafture of his silent wings multiple times. The mothers did not move; they sensed he sought mice, not children.

Around midnight, I jolted awake, roused by a revelry whose noises were not yet loud. They were close, though attenuated. My eyes were so dazzled that for a moment I saw nothing, until they adjusted.

I lay on my withered leaves in the corner of a splendid hall. Before me stood a crowd of gorgeously dressed men and gracefully robed women, none of whom seemed to notice me. In dance after dance, they vaguely embodied the story of life—its meetings, its passions, its partings.
