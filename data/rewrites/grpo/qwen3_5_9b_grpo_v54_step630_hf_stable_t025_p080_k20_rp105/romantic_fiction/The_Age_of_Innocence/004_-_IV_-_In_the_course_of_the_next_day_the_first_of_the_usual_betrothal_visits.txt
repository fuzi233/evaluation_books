The following day, the rigid rituals of New York betrothal took hold with their customary precision. Newland Archer, accompanied first by his mother and sister to pay respects to Mrs. Welland, then joined her and May for a carriage ride to the ancestral home of Mrs. Manson Mingott, where the matriarch's blessing was the final seal on their union.

For a young man of Archer's standing, a visit to Mrs. Mingott was invariably a spectacle of amusement. Her residence, though historically significant, lacked the austere, grim elegance of the older Fifth Avenue mansions with their rosewood consoles and mahogany bookcases. Instead, Mrs. Mingott had embraced the frivolous opulence of the Second Empire, mixing her family's heirlooms with lighter upholstery. She preferred to sit in a ground-floor window, observing the world with a calm, unshakeable confidence. She believed the chaotic sprawl of the city—the quarries, the one-story saloons, the ragged gardens—would inevitably yield to grander residences like hers, and the clatter of omnibuses would be silenced by the smooth asphalt of Paris. Since everyone she wished to see came to her, she felt no isolation despite her location.

Time had transformed Mrs. Mingott into a natural phenomenon. What was once a plump, active woman had become a vast, august figure, accepting her physical expansion with the same philosophy she applied to life's other trials. Now, in extreme old age, she presented a mirror image of firm, pink flesh, save for the faint traces of a small face waiting to be excavated. Smooth double chins led down to a snowy bosom draped in muslins pinned by a miniature portrait of the late Mr. Mingott, while waves of black silk cascaded over her armchair, framing two tiny white hands that rested like gulls upon the billows.

Her unique arrangement of living quarters defied every New York propriety. Unable to climb stairs, she had moved her reception rooms to the ground floor, leaving the bedrooms upstairs. To a guest seated with her, the open door revealed a startling vista: a bedroom with a low, sofa-like bed and a toilet table adorned with lace, creating an atmosphere reminiscent of French fiction and architectural invitations to immorality. It amused Archer to imagine Mrs. Mingott leading a blameless life within a stage-set designed for adultery; he privately conceded that if a lover had been what she desired, the intrepid woman would certainly have secured him.

Fortunately, the Countess Olenska was absent during the betrothed couple's visit. Mrs. Mingott claimed she had gone out—a seemingly indiscreet move for a woman of her compromised past on a bright, busy shopping day—but her absence spared the young couple the embarrassment of her shadow falling upon their radiant future. The visit proceeded successfully. Mrs. Mingott delighted in the engagement, which had been carefully vetted by family councils, and admired the engagement ring: a large, thick sapphire set in invisible claws.

"It is the new setting," Mrs. Welland had explained, glancing conciliatorily at her future son-in-law. "It shows the stone beautifully, though it looks a bit bare to old-fashioned eyes."

"Old-fashioned eyes? I hope you do not mean mine, my dear? I adore all novelties," the ancestress replied, lifting the stone to her bright, unassisted eyes. "Very handsome. Very liberal. In my time, a cameo set in pearls sufficed. But it is the hand that sets off the ring, is it not, Mr. Archer?" She waved a tiny hand, its fingers tipped with pointed nails and rolls of aged fat encircling the wrist like ivory bracelets. "Mine was modeled in Rome by the great Ferrigiani. You should have May's done; no doubt he will. Her hand is large—these modern sports spread the joints—but the skin is white. And when is the wedding to be?" she asked, fixing her gaze on Archer.

"As soon as possible, if only you will back me up, Mrs. Mingott," Archer smiled at his betrothed.

"We must give them time to know each other better, mamma," Mrs. Welland interjected with practiced reluctance.

"Know each other? Fiddlesticks! Everyone in New York knows everyone. Let the young man have his way; do not wait until the bubble bursts on the wine. Marry them before Lent; I may catch pneumonia this winter, and I wish to host the wedding breakfast," the ancestress declared.

The room filled with expressions of amusement and gratitude as the visit wound down in mild pleasantry. Then, the door opened to admit the Countess Olenska, wearing bonnet and mantle, followed unexpectedly by Julius Beaufort.

A cousinly murmur of pleasure rippled through the ladies. Mrs. Mingott extended Ferrigiani's model to the banker. "Ha! Beaufort, this is a rare favor!" she exclaimed, using her odd foreign custom of addressing men by surname.

"Thanks. I wish it might happen oftener," the visitor replied in his easy arrogance. "I am generally so tied down, but I met the Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she was kind enough to let me walk home with her."

"Ah—I hope the house will be gayer now that Ellen is here!" Mrs. Mingott cried with glorious effrontery. "Sit down, Beaufort; push up the yellow armchair. Now that I have you, I require a good gossip. I heard your ball was magnificent, and I understand you invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers? Well, I have a curiosity to see the woman myself."

She had forgotten her relatives, who drifted out into the hall under Ellen's guidance. Mrs. Mingott, who always professed admiration for Julius Beaufort due to their shared cool dominance and shortcuts through convention, was eager to learn why the Beauforts had invited Mrs. Struthers for the first time. The widow of Struthers's Shoe-polish had returned from a European sojourn to besiege New York's tight little citadel. "Of course, if you and Regina invite her, the matter is settled. We need new blood and new money—and I hear she is still very good-looking," the carnivorous old lady declared.

In the hall, as Mrs. Welland and May donned their furs, Archer noticed the Countess Olenska looking at him with a faintly questioning smile.

"Of course you know already—about May and me," he said, answering her look with a shy laugh. "She scolded me for not giving you the news last night at the Opera; I had her orders to tell you we were engaged, but the crowd was too great."

The smile traveled from the Countess's eyes to her lips; she looked younger, more like the bold brown Ellen Mingott of his boyhood. "Of course I know; yes. And I am so glad. But one does not announce such things first in a crowd." The ladies stood on the threshold, and she held out her hand.

"Good-bye; come and see me some day," she said, her gaze lingering on Archer.

In the carriage, descending Fifth Avenue, they spoke pointedly of Mrs. Mingott—her age, her spirit, her wonderful attributes. No one mentioned the Countess Olenska. Yet Archer knew Mrs. Welland was thinking: *It is a mistake for Ellen to be seen, the very day after her arrival, parading up Fifth Avenue at the crowded hour with Julius Beaufort.* And the young man himself mentally added: *And she ought to know that a man who is just engaged does not spend his time calling on married women. But I daresay in the set she has lived in, they do—they never do anything else.* Despite his pride in his cosmopolitan views, he thanked heaven that he was a New Yorker, about to ally himself with one of his own kind.
