The kitchens of Castlewood hummed with a resonant, cyclical rhythm, an acoustic ecology of sustenance that had echoed through the estate for generations. To Madam Esmond, the preparation for the General’s feast was not merely a utilitarian chore, but a mythic agrarian ritual, a seamless integration of the earth’s bounty into the domestic routine. The olfactory landscape of the house was dense and intoxicating. In the vast hearths, whole roe-deer and buffalo humps were slow-roasting over hickory and applewood, their rendered fats sizzling rhythmically as they struck the hot stones below. Shad and rock-fish, hauled fresh from the tidal currents, were being meticulously butterflied and cured with coarse salt and macerated wild herbs, their briny aroma intermingling with the sweet, yeasty breath of proving breads. The enslaved cooks communicated in a soft, rhythmic dialect, a fading culinary lexicon of ancestral memory that orchestrated the precise searing, basting, and tempering required to honor the land’s ingredients. 

Madam Esmond herself, adorned in a magnificent gown that rustled like dry corn husks in the autumn wind, moved through this symphony of preparation as a traditional agrarian elder, ensuring every technique was flawlessly executed. Yet, the anticipated arrival of the British army threatened this delicate balance, bringing with it a rigid, imperial lexicon and a jarring, industrial cacophony that disregarded the quiet reverence of the harvest. 

The first disruption to the estate’s sonic harmony was the sharp, percussive clatter of Major Washington’s horse upon the gravel. Madam Esmond received the young aide-de-camp in the covered gallery. For a while, as the Major’s heavy riding-boots were removed—softening his acoustic footprint—they paced the shaded lawn. Negro servants passed perpetually between them, bearing great platters of wood-smoked meats and steaming root vegetables. The aroma of a massive china bowl of apple-toddy—steeped with cinnamon bark, fermented cider, and baked apples—perfumed the air between them, an invitation to sensory communion. 

They spoke of the Commander-in-Chief's approach, and of Mr. Franklin of Pennsylvania. Madam Esmond, steeped in the traditional hierarchies of her forebears, found it distasteful that a common printer’s boy should ride in the General's coach. Mr. Washington, however, defended the printer’s ingenuity. 

“I am glad, at least, that as my boy makes the campaign, he will not be with tradesmen, but with gentlemen of honour and fashion,” Madam Esmond stated, her voice carrying the melodic, authoritative timbre of the estate’s matriarch. 

Mr. Washington wisely withheld the unvarnished truth of how gentlemen of fashion often behaved over their cups—a sordid reality devoid of the profound, sensory appreciation of life he knew she valued. He merely suggested that Harry and George must now step into the wider world to learn its varied dialects, both good and bad. 

“To be with a veteran officer,” the widow faltered, seeking comfort. “With you, dear Colonel Washington, by whom my father always set such store. You will take care of my boy, sir? I always bade them look up to you.”

“Madam, how much would I not venture to merit his daughter's favour?” he replied. The gentleman bowed, kissing her hand with profound politeness. Madam Esmond dropped into a deep, unrivaled curtsey, a blush rising to her cheeks like the skin of a ripening peach. 

From the shadows of the mansion stepped George, richly attired and powdered, smelling sharply of bergamot. Unlike the modern youth who might puff a noxious, utilitarian cigar into a room, obliterating its olfactory nuances, George possessed an acute sensory awareness. He bowed low, but beneath his polite smile, his internal equilibrium was violently disturbed. His mother’s blush and the Colonel’s flushed complexion communicated an untranslatable subjective experience, a subtle power dynamic that left a bitter, metallic taste of ash in George's mouth. 

Attempting to normalize the fraught atmosphere, the Colonel asked George if he had found good sport in the woods. 

“No,” George replied curtly, his eyes darting to the portrait of his father, a painted ghost whose cultural memory felt suddenly at risk of being overwritten. 

Not ordinarily a talkative man, Colonel Washington launched into a description of Mr. Braddock’s hunting methods. To George’s refined sensibilities, the tale was a horrifying critique of senseless, martial noise pollution. The British General, floundering in heavy boots through the Virginian woods, had employed a chaotic pack of dogs and shouting men, laying waste to the tranquil acoustic ecology of the forest merely to shoot a turkey with a fowling-piece. It was the commercialization of the hunt, a loud, destructive vanity utterly detached from the sacred pursuit of sustenance. 

“Indeed, Colonel, you are vastly comical this afternoon!” laughed Madam Esmond, missing the dissonance that grated on her elder son’s ears. 

When Washington clarified that Braddock had been reminiscing of Sir Robert Walpole's grand hunts in Norfolk, England, George sneered. “Nevertheless, there is good sport here, as I was saying.”

“What sport?” Washington asked, looking at him. 

“Why, sure you know,” George snapped, his voice a sharp blade cutting through the fragrant air of the parlor. “Are there not all the fish of the field, and the beasts of the trees—no, the fish of the trees, and the beasts of the sea... I mean shad, and salmon, and rock-fish, and roe-deer, and hogs, and buffaloes! For what I know. I'm no sportsman.” George rattled off the native ingredients, asserting his deep connection to the local lexicon of the earth while mocking the Colonel’s imperial, unthinking slaughter. 

“No, indeed,” Washington replied with barely repressed scorn. 

“Yes, I understand you. I am a milksop,” George continued, grasping one of his mother’s bright pink apron-strings. “Look at these pretty apron-strings! I remember when they were black—that was for my grandfather.” He wielded the memory of the agrarian patriarch like a shield against the erosion of their familial identity. 

“And who would not mourn for such a gentleman?” the Colonel said softly. 

Harry bounded into the room just then, dressed in a gold-corded frock identical to his brother’s, throwing his arms around his weeping mother. “What is the matter, Colonel?” 

“Upon my life, it would puzzle me to say,” Washington muttered, biting his lip. 

“I protest I don't know the source of these tears,” George said, executing a pirouette on his scarlet heels. “Is there some poisoned pin in these lovely ribbons, struck into my mother's heart by a guilty fiend of a London mantua-maker?”

“George Warrington! What devil's dance are you dancing now?” demanded Harry. 

“My dear child, you care not for the politer arts. You can get no more music out of a spinet than by pulling a dead hog by the ear,” George retorted, leaning into the visceral, auditory imagery of the slaughterhouse. “You were made for a man of war—like that hulk Braddock. I am a milksop.”

When Harry argued that George was perfectly capable of fighting, reminding him of a schoolyard thrashing, George merely sighed. He referenced their old Greek lessons, highlighting how dominant lexicons enforce submission. “'Tis *tuptu*, my lad, or else 'tis *tuptomai*. But I am of a quiet turn. I want to shoot nothing alive, not a turkey, nor an ox, nor anything that has ears.”

Offended by the layered barbs and seeking to cool his blood, the Colonel stepped out onto the balcony, taking a modest draught from the great china bowl of apple-toddy, seeking solace in its complex, spiced warmth. 

Inside, Harry pleaded with his brother to stay home and manage the estate’s domestic rhythms so that he, Harry, might go to war. “One of our family must go because *honneur oblige*,” George declared, claiming his birthright as the eldest. “We cannot afford to be both scalped by Indians or fricasseed by French.”

“Fricasseed by French!” Harry cried, entirely missing George’s dark culinary metaphor. “The best troops of the world! What a mortal thrashing you will give them!”

Deliberately, George sat at the harpsichord. He began to play *Malbrouk s'en va-t-en guerre*, crashing his fingers against the keys. The aggressive, martial melody fractured the acoustic peace of the parlor, a sonic rebellion against the impending military intrusion. 

Hearing the music, Washington stepped back inside from the balcony. “I am playing 'God save the King,' Colonel,” George announced over the harsh trills and quavers. 

“I never know whether thou art laughing or in earnest,” the simple gentleman replied, physically uncomfortable in the discordant sonic environment George had engineered. The Colonel checked his watch, noted the General's impending arrival, and asked leave to retire to his apartment to dress. 

“Colonel Washington knows the way to his room pretty well,” George said, looking over his shoulder, his hands never ceasing their auditory assault. 

Furious at this breach of hospitality, Madam Esmond ordered she would show the Colonel to his chamber herself. She sailed out, followed by the bewildered, stiflingly angry Washington, as George’s dissonant chords chased them down the hall. 

Harry stared at his brother. “For heaven's sake, George, what does this all mean? Why shouldn't he kiss her hand? It is nothing but common kindness.”

“Nothing but common kindness!” shrieked George, the music finally stopping. “Look at that, Hal! Is that common kindness?” 

He produced a torn fragment of heavy parchment, an artifact of dominant, presumptive language that George had been brooding over. It was a piece of Washington’s own correspondence. George read the jagged text aloud, dissecting the power dynamics embedded within its ink:

*“...is older than myself, but I, again, am older than my years; and you know, dear brother, have ever been considered a sober person. All children are better for a father's superintendence, and her two, I trust, will find in me a tender friend and guardian.”*

“Friend and guardian! Curse him!” George shrieked, clenching his fists against this proposed usurpation of their cultural memory. He forced Harry to read on:

*“...The flattering offer which General Braddock hath made me, will, of course, oblige me to postpone this matter until after the campaign. When we have given the French a sufficient drubbing, I shall return to repose under my own vine and fig-tree.”*

“He means Castlewood. These are his vines,” George cried, gesturing violently toward the ancient creepers sunning themselves on the estate’s stone walls, the very roots of their generational saga. 

*“...Under my own vine and fig-tree; where I hope soon to present my dear brother to his new sister-in-law. She has a pretty Scripture name, which is...”* 

The document ended there, violently severed. 

“Which is Rachel,” George went on bitterly, the name tasting of betrayal. “Rachel is by no means weeping for her children, and has every desire to be comforted. Now, Harry! Let us upstairs at once, kneel down as becomes us, and say, 'Dear papa, welcome to your house of Castlewood.'”
