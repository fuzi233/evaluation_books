She enchanted me in most ways—yet some things unsettled me.

Carmilla stood taller than most women, slender, her movements languid as drifting smoke. No trace of illness marked her, save for that unnatural slowness. Her skin glowed with vitality, her features delicate as if carved by some devoted hand. But it was her eyes that arrested me—dark, depthless, gleaming like wet stones in moonlight. And her hair—God, her hair. Heavy as sin, thick as a curtain of night, spilling over her shoulders in waves of deep brown threaded with gold. I would let it cascade through my fingers, marveling at its weight, its silk, while she reclined in her chair, murmuring in that voice like a secret.

Yet for all her openness in these moments, she was a locked box in other ways. She spoke nothing of her family, her past, her home—only fragments, teasing and vague. A name: Carmilla. A lineage: ancient, noble. A direction: west. No more. No matter how I coaxed, how I swore secrecy, she would not yield. It maddened me. What harm in a name, a place?

Her refusals were never cruel, though. She’d draw me close, her breath at my ear, whispering riddles that curled like fog in my mind:

*"Dearest, your heart bleeds because I wound it. But mine bleeds with yours. This is the way of things—we draw near, we devour, we become. You will know in time. For now, let me keep my secrets, and love me still."*

Then her lips would press to my cheek, soft and burning, and I would feel myself slipping, my will dissolving like sugar in hot tea. A trance. A surrender. And beneath it all, a creeping dread—not of her, but of the thing inside me that thrilled to it.

She was a creature of contradictions. By day, languid, drifting through the halls like a ghost. By night, her eyes burned with a feverish light, her fingers tightening around mine with sudden, desperate strength. Once, as a funeral procession passed—a village girl, dead too young—Carmilla recoiled as if struck, her face twisting into something inhuman.

*"Don’t you hear how it scrapes?"* she hissed, clapping her hands over her ears. *"That hymn—it’s a knife. And why mourn? Death is kinder than they know."*

Then, just as swiftly, the storm passed. She laughed, light as a girl again, as if the darkness had never touched her.

But the shadows gathered. Strange deaths plagued the countryside—girls wasting away, whispering of phantoms at their throats. My father dismissed it as superstition. Carmilla only smiled, her teeth very white.

*"Nature ordains all things,"* she said. *"Life, death… hunger."*

And when the hunchbacked peddler came with his charms and his grinning insinuations—*"A needle’s tooth, my lady, sharp as sin!"*—she recoiled like a thing scalded. For a moment, her mask slipped entirely, fury flashing in her eyes before she smoothed it away.

That night, the doctor came. I caught only scraps of their talk—*hippogriffs, dragons*—but my father’s laughter was uneasy.

And Carmilla, watching from the shadows, said nothing at all.
