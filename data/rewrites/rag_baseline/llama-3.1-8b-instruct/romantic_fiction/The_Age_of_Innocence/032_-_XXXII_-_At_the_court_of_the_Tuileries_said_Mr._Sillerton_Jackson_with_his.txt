Silence enveloped the van der Luyden's drawing room like a damp shroud. I stood beside May, surveying the black walnut table set for dinner. The scent of Skuytercliff carnations hung heavy in the air, a reminder of the couple's hasty return to town. The announcement of Beaufort's failure had sent them scurrying back to New York, to reaffirm their presence and uphold the standards of society. As Mrs. Archer had said, they owed it to society to show themselves at the Opera, and even to open their own doors. But the evening felt forced, a calculated gesture to counterbalance the whispers and speculation that had followed Beaufort's downfall.

May's hand brushed against mine as she leaned in to speak. Her voice was barely audible over the ticking clock on the mantle. "Mother's carriage was seen outside Mrs. Beaufort's door, I'm afraid." The words hung in the air like a challenge, a test of our loyalty and discretion. I felt a flush rise to my cheeks as Mrs. van der Luyden's eyes narrowed, her gaze piercing.

Mrs. Archer intervened, her voice a gentle rebuke. "If it was, I'm convinced it was there without Mother's knowledge." But the damage was done. The seed of doubt had been planted, and I could sense the unease spreading through the room like a stain.

As we sat down to dinner, the conversation turned to the Beaufort affair once more. Mr. van der Luyden spoke of Madame Olenska's kindness and her tendency to surround herself with unconventional friends. Mrs. Archer added her own thoughts, her voice laced with a hint of disapproval. I felt a pang of unease, knowing that May's grandmother, the formidable Mrs. Mingott, had been implicated in the scandal.

The meal passed in a haze of polite conversation and forced laughter. I couldn't shake the feeling that we were all trapped in a delicate web of social expectations and obligations. And then, like a thread pulled loose, Mr. Jackson's words dropped into the conversation. "At the Tuileries, the standard was excessively lax in some respects..." His voice trailed off, leaving us to ponder the implications. The room seemed to hold its breath, waiting for someone to pick up the thread and continue the conversation. But the silence that followed was oppressive, a reminder that some secrets were better left unspoken.
