People who live behind walls—school walls, convent walls, the walls a city sets up between one street and the next—are apt to be mislaid by those who move in open air. Not always from neglect. Life outside rushes like a river in thaw: it snatches letters out of hands, it shakes appointments loose, it carries away the small proofs by which one remembers one is still remembered. After a season of frequent messages—after a knot of little excitements that seem, by their very nature, to bind communication tighter—there will come, without warning, a hush. The post that used to knock will pass in silence; the familiar step will not sound on the stair. A blank begins; it does not explain itself.

There are reasons—excellent reasons—if only the shut-in knew them. The hermit counts his minutes by the tick of a clock that seems to have grown tired; he watches hours go past like travellers who drag their feet and stop too often. But those same days, outside, may be full of movement: errands, consultations, sudden illnesses, sudden recoveries, money-matters, journeys; the swirl of the world’s business which makes seven weeks seem not a winter but a handful of hurried mornings.

If the hermit is wise, he learns a difficult domestic art: to lay his feelings by, as one lays away fruit for a scant season; to fold his thoughts tight, like linen in a drawer, and shut the drawer. Destiny sometimes means us to imitate small creatures that sleep through frost. Make yourself compact, creep into the crevice of your own life, let the drift pile up; endure the cold. Say, because you must: It is right, for so it is.

Yet winter in a human chest is not like winter in a field; it does not always thaw cleanly. Sometimes spring returns, and the sealed place opens; air, sun, and south-wind come in, and the sound of running water is enough to raise one again. Sometimes the frost gets into the heart and never goes out, and what is left is only what scavengers find. Even that would be “right,” in the hard sense: mortals are mortals. “As well soon as syne.”

After that evening at the theatre came for me seven weeks as bare as seven sheets of unused paper. Not a line was written on one. Not a visit, not a token.

Midway through, my mind began to fill the white pages with its own dark ink. Something had happened at La Terrasse, I told myself: an accident, an illness. That middle point is always clouded for the solitary. Expectation strains the nerves as a tight string strains under a finger; doubts, kept down for days, heap themselves, gather weight, and roll back with a kind of revenge. Nights grew unkind. Sleep and I could not agree; I started from dozes as if some hand had shaken me. Dreams came in a sinister band—calamity leading them, desertion marching close behind—and they sat on my bed with cold breath and busy eyes. I did what I could; but one can be brave and still be a poor, pale, wasting creature.

Toward the end of the seven weeks I admitted what I had guarded against admitting: that these blanks were part of my lot; that they came from circumstance, from Fate; that they had an origin one must not question and a sequence one must not complain of. I did not wrong myself by any weak self-accusation. I knew enough justice to understand that suffering is not always a fault. And as to blaming others for silence, my reason acquitted them and my heart acquitted them too. Only, the road was heavy; I longed for a different weather.

I tried to fill my days with decent industry: I began a piece of lace-work so elaborate it ought to have had power to mesmerise the mind; I studied German till the syllables turned dry as husks; I set myself a course of reading in the library—those thick books whose pages smell of dust and old glue, whose knowledge, like dried peas, needs long soaking to be edible. I did all as orthodoxly as I could. The result was like gnawing a file for hunger, or drinking brine for thirst.

My hour of torment was the post-hour. I knew it by the air, by the sound in the passage, by the stir of the house; and I tried to trick myself—uselessly—out of that knowledge. I dreaded the nerve-shock of expectation, and then the sick drop after the ring when nothing followed for me.

I used to think of caged creatures—half-fed—waiting for their portion. So I waited for a letter. And to speak truth, and have done with the pretence of calm: I suffered. I had bitter fears and strange inward trials; hope failed in me as a flame fails when the wick is too short; despair came so near that it breathed through me like a cold draught in a closed room and made the heart seem to move under a weight. The letter—the well-beloved letter—would not come; and it was all of sweetness I had to look for.

At the extremity of want I went again and again to my small hoard: the five letters locked in their case. How splendid that month seemed which had dropped those five stars into my dark! Always at night I visited them. Not daring to ask each evening for a candle below, I bought a wax taper and matches; at study-hour I stole up to the dormitory, and there, with the taper’s thin honey-coloured flame, I feasted—like some famished guest at a Barmecide table—on what looked like bread and proved only air. It did not nourish me. I grew thin as a shadow; but I was not ill.

One night, reading late, I felt even this poor power fading: the letters, from too much poring, were losing sap and savour; my gold seemed turning to leaves in my hands. I was grieving over the disillusion when a quick tripping foot ran up the stairs. I knew Ginevra Fanshawe’s step. She had dined in town; she was returned to fetch shawl or ribbon from the wardrobe.

In she came, bright silk rustling, her shawl slipping from her shoulders, curls half undone by damp night-air, heavy on her neck. I had barely time to shut away my treasures and lock the bureau before she was at my side, out of humour.

“It has been a stupid evening: they are stupid people,” she began.

“Who? Mrs. Cholmondeley? I thought you always found her house charming.”

“I have not been to Mrs. Cholmondeley’s.”

“Indeed! Have you made new acquaintance?”

“My uncle de Bassompierre is come.”

“Your uncle de Bassompierre! Are you not glad? I thought he was a favourite.”

“You thought wrong: the man is odious; I hate him.”

“Because he is a foreigner? or for what other reason of equal weight?”

“He is not a foreigner. The man is English enough, goodness knows, and had an English name till three or four years ago; but his mother was a foreigner—a de Bassompierre—and some of her family are dead and have left him estates, a title, and this name: he is quite a great man now.”

“Do you hate him for that reason?”

“Don’t I know what mamma says about him? He is not my own uncle, but married mamma’s sister. Mamma detests him; she says he killed aunt Ginevra with unkindness: he looks like a bear. Such a dismal evening!” she ran on. “I’ll go no more to his big hotel. Fancy me walking into a room alone, and a great man fifty years old coming forwards, and after a few minutes’ conversation actually turning his back upon me, and then abruptly going out of the room. Such odd ways! I daresay his conscience smote him, for they all say at home I am the picture of aunt Ginevra. Mamma often declares the likeness is quite ridiculous.”

“Were you the only visitor?”

“The only visitor? Yes; then there was missy, my cousin: little spoiled, pampered thing.”

“M. de Bassompierre has a daughter?”

“Yes, yes: don’t tease one with questions. Oh, dear! I am so tired.”

She yawned, and without ceremony threw herself on my bed. “It seems Mademoiselle was nearly crushed to a jelly in a hubbub at the theatre some weeks ago.”

“Ah! indeed. And they live at a large hotel in the Rue Crécy?”

“Justement. How do you know?”

“I have been there.”

“Oh, you have? Really! You go everywhere in these days. I suppose Mother Bretton took you. She and Esculapius have the entrée of the de Bassompierre apartments: it seems ‘my son John’ attended missy on the occasion of her accident—Accident? Bah! All affectation! I don’t think she was squeezed more than she richly deserves for her airs. And now there is quite an intimacy struck up: I heard something about ‘auld lang syne,’ and what not. Oh, how stupid they all were!”

“All! You said you were the only visitor.”

“Did I? You see one forgets to particularize an old woman and her boy.”

“Dr. and Mrs. Bretton were at M. de Bassompierre’s this evening?”

“Ay, ay! as large as life; and missy played the hostess. What a conceited doll it is!”

Her sourness had a plain cause: incense had been withheld; homage diverted; coquetry had failed; vanity smarted. She lay there, fuming in her own perfume.

“Is Miss de Bassompierre quite well now?” I asked.

“As well as you or I, no doubt; but she is an affected little thing, and gave herself invalid airs to attract medical notice. And to see the old dowager making her recline on a couch, and ‘my son John’ prohibiting excitement, etcetera—faugh! the scene was quite sickening.”

“It would not have been so if the object of attention had been changed: if you had taken Miss de Bassompierre’s place.”

“Indeed! I hate ‘my son John!’”

“‘My son John!’—whom do you indicate by that name? Dr. Bretton’s mother never calls him so.”

“Then she ought. A clownish, bearish John he is.”

“You violate the truth in saying so; and as the whole of my patience is now spun off the distaff, I peremptorily desire you to rise from that bed, and vacate this room.”

“Passionate thing! Your face is the colour of a coquelicot. I wonder what always makes you so mighty testy à l’endroit du gros Jean? ‘John Anderson, my Joe, John!’ Oh, the distinguished name!”

My anger rose in a hot, useless wave. There is no grappling with such a creature—light as down, and as irritating. I put out my taper, locked my bureau, and left her there, since she would not leave me. Small-beer as she was, she had turned unendurably acid.

The morrow was Thursday, our half-holiday. Breakfast ended, I withdrew to the first classe. The dreaded post-hour drew near, and I waited for it as a person who believes in apparitions might wait the hour when a spectre crosses a room—fearing it, yet unable not to watch. Less than ever was a letter likely; yet the mere possibility tormented me.

It was a winter day of east wind, and I had come, by long trial, into a dreary fellowship with winds and their changes—an alliance the healthy seldom understand. North and east had for me a tyrannous influence: they sharpened pain, they blackened sorrow. South could soothe; west could sometimes cheer—unless it carried thunder-clouds, under whose weight and warmth all energy dies and the mind turns sick.

Bitter and dark as the January morning was, I remember running down—without bonnet—into the garden, and lingering among stripped shrubs and bare stems in the forlorn hope the postman’s ring might happen while I was out of hearing. There was a particular nerve in me—worn thin by long repetition of the same thought—which could scarcely bear that clang and its consequence: blankness. I stayed as long as I dared without attracting notice. I muffled my head in my apron; I stopped my ears, as if noise itself were torture.

At last I returned to the first classe. It was not yet nine; no pupils had entered. The first thing my eye caught was a white object lying on my black desk—a flat, pale shape like a leaf on charred ground. The post had come—unheard by me. Rosine had visited my little cell, and like an angel, left a token. That bright thing was a letter, a real letter. From three yards off I saw as much; and since I had but one correspondent on earth, it must be from him. He remembered me yet. Gratitude sent a deep pulse through my heart—like warm broth into a chilled body, like bread into a starving stomach.

I drew near, trembling, almost sure of a known hand; and found, instead, a pale female scrawl where I had looked for a firm masculine character. The blow was so sharp I spoke aloud: “This is cruel.”

I got over it. Life remains life, whatever its pangs: eyes still see, ears still hear, though the prospect of what delights be withdrawn, and the sound of what consoles be silenced.

I opened the billet. By that time I knew the hand well enough. It was dated “La Terrasse,” and it ran thus:

“DEAR LUCY,—It occurs to me to inquire what you have been doing with yourself for the last month or two? Not that I suspect you would have the least difficulty in giving an account of your proceedings. I daresay you have been just as busy and as happy as ourselves at La Terrasse. As to Graham, his professional connection extends daily: he is so much sought after, so much engaged, that I tell him he will grow quite conceited. Like a right good mother, as I am, I do my best to keep him down: no flattery does he get from me, as you know. And yet, Lucy, he is a fine fellow: his mother’s heart dances at the sight of him. After being hurried here and there the whole day, and passing the ordeal of fifty sorts of tempers, and combating a hundred caprices, and sometimes witnessing cruel sufferings—perhaps, occasionally, as I tell him, inflicting them—at night he still comes home to me in such kindly, pleasant mood, that really, I seem to live in a sort of moral antipodes, and on these January evenings my day rises when other people’s night sets in.

“Still he needs keeping in order, and correcting, and repressing, and I do him that good service; but the boy is so elastic there is no such thing as vexing him thoroughly. When I think I have at last driven him to the sullens, he turns on me with jokes for retaliation: but you know him and all his iniquities, and I am but an elderly simpleton to make him the subject of this epistle.

“As for me, I have had my old Bretton agent here on a visit, and have been plunged overhead and ears in business matters. I do so wish to regain for Graham at least some part of what his father left him. He laughs to scorn my anxiety on this point, bidding me look and see how he can provide for himself and me too, and asking what the old lady can possibly want that she has not; hinting about sky-blue turbans; accusing me of an ambition to wear diamonds, keep livery servants, have an hotel, and lead the fashion amongst the English clan in Villette.

“Talking of sky-blue turbans, I wish you had been with us the other evening. He had come in really tired, and after I had given him his tea, he threw himself into my chair with his customary presumption. To my great delight, he dropped asleep. (You know how he teases me about being drowsy; I, who never, by any chance, close an eye by daylight.) While he slept, I thought he looked very bonny, Lucy: fool as I am to be so proud of him; but who can help it? Show me his peer. Look where I will, I see nothing like him in Villette. Well, I took it into my head to play him a trick: so I brought out the sky-blue turban, and handling it with gingerly precaution, I managed to invest his brows with this grand adornment. I assure you it did not at all misbecome him; he looked quite Eastern, except that he is so fair. Nobody, however, can accuse him of having red hair now—it is genuine chestnut—a dark, glossy chestnut; and when I put my large cashmere about him, there was as fine a young bey, dey, or pacha improvised as you would wish to see.

“It was good entertainment; but only half-enjoyed, since I was alone: you should have been there.

“In due time my lord awoke: the looking-glass above the fireplace soon intimated to him his plight: as you may imagine, I now live under threat and dread of vengeance.

“But to come to the gist of my letter. I know Thursday is a half-holiday in the Rue Fossette: be ready, then, by five in the afternoon, at which hour I will send the carriage to take you out to La Terrasse. Be sure to come: you may meet some old acquaintance. Good-by, my wise, dear, grave little god-daughter.—Very truly yours,

“LOUISA BRETTON.”

A letter like that sets one to rights. I was not, perhaps, gay after reading it; but I was steadied, eased—like a room after a window is opened and stale air let out. My friends were well; Graham had not met danger; his mother had not sickened—those calamities my mind had been cooking nightly, as if grief were a broth forever simmering on a low flame. Their feeling for me, too, remained what it had been. And yet I could not help looking at Mrs. Bretton’s seven weeks and then at mine, and marvelling at the difference.

How wise it is for those set in an exceptional position to keep silence about the galling of it. The world understands perishing for lack of food: hunger is a common tongue. But the starvation that comes from solitary confinement—how the mind thins, how the nerves first burn and then go numb, how the senses, deprived of their natural nourishment, begin to invent phantoms—is too intricate for easy sympathy. Speak of it, and you might as well stand in a market-place and utter riddles in some dead dialect. Long may it be that few comprehend such themes; long may it be that physical privations alone are thought real, and the rest a fancy. Perhaps it is kinder so. In old days moral trial was a rarer mystery: in all Israel, there was but one Saul, and but one David to soothe him.

The keen still cold of morning sharpened, later, into a harsher breath from far wastes: the cold zone sighed over the temperate and froze it fast. A heavy sky—dull, thick with snow—sailed from the north and settled over expectant Europe. Toward afternoon the descent began. The storm was so dense and wild I feared no carriage would venture; but trust my godmother: she asked, and she would have her guest.

About six o’clock I was lifted from the carriage over front steps already blocked with drifted white, and set within the door of La Terrasse.

I ran through the vestibule and up-stairs to the drawing-room. There was Mrs. Bretton—a summer day in her own person, with warmth in her clasp and a kind kiss that thawed what the east wind had frozen. After months of bare boards, black benches, desks, and the sour breath of school stoves, the blue saloon struck my senses like richness: not only sight, but smell—the clean resinous scent of a good fire; the faint perfume of tea and wax and polished wood. The hearth glowed with a Christmas-like crimson splendour that dazzled me.

She held my hand awhile; she chatted; she scolded me for growing thinner; then declared the snow-wind had disordered my hair, and sent me up-stairs to make it neat and remove my shawl.

In my own little sea-green room I found a bright fire, and candles already lit: tall waxlights stood on either side of the great looking-glass. But between those candles, and before the glass, something moved—something that seemed to be dressing itself: slight, white, airy; like a winter spirit come to warm its hands at my hearth.

For one instant I thought of Graham’s spectral fancies. I watched, cautious. The thing wore white, sprinkled with small drops of scarlet; a red girdle defined its slenderness; in its hair was something leafy yet shining—a wreath with evergreen gloss. Frightful it was not; only strange in that quiet room.

It turned quickly. A large eye, under long dark lashes, flashed full upon me; the lashes softened the clear brilliance they guarded. The voice that came was low and gentle.

“Ah! you are come!” she breathed, and smiled slowly, looking at me with an intentness that felt like recognition.

I knew her then. One does not forget that cast of fine, delicate feature once seen.

“Miss de Bassompierre,” I said.

“No,” she answered, “not Miss de Bassompierre for you!” She did not explain at once; she let the moment ripen, as if taste must be given time upon the tongue.

“You are changed, but still you are yourself,” she said, coming nearer. “I remember you well—your countenance, the colour of your hair, the outline of your face….”

I moved toward the fire; she stood opposite and looked into me. While she gazed, her face grew more and more full of thought and feeling, until a dimness rose and quenched the clear light of her eyes.

“It makes me almost cry to look so far back,” she said. “But as to being sorry, or sentimental, don’t think it: on the contrary, I am quite pleased and glad.”

I was interested, yet utterly at fault; words would not come readily. At last I stammered, “I think I never met you till that night, some weeks ago, when you were hurt…?”

She smiled—a smile that seemed to bridge time. “You have forgotten then that I have sat on your knee, been lifted in your arms, even shared your pillow? You no longer remember the night when I came crying, like a naughty little child as I was, to your bedside, and you took me in. You have no memory for the comfort and protection by which you soothed an acute distress? Go back to Bretton. Remember Mr. Home.”

All at once the past unfolded—like a folded paper opened and smoothed flat.

“And you are little Polly?” I breathed.

“I am Paulina Mary Home de Bassompierre.”
