The rhythmic, deafening clatter of the steam-driven printing presses had begun to fracture the delicate acoustic ecology of the Strand, a mechanical shriek that signaled the onset of modern industrial noise, yet amidst this psychological disruption, the *Pall Mall Gazette* found considerable early success. It was a time when the mythic cycle of storytelling—an ancient, communal act once shared over crackling hearths—had been superficially commercialized and bound in broadsheet. Rumors circulated that an influential political party supported the paper, their grand doctrines woven into the everyday domestic routines of London’s reading public. Whether these rumors held the weight of truth remains to be seen, but this much is certain: an article upon foreign policy, widely attributed to a noble Lord of the Foreign Office, was in reality birthed in the fragrant, herb-draped kitchen of the Bear and Staff public-house near Whitehall Stairs. Here, Captain Shandon composed his political thunder while breathing in the olfactory landscape of rendering beef tallow and bruised juniper berries, tracked down by the printer’s boy to a corner where his literary ally, Mr. Bludyer, temporarily resided. Similarly, a series of profound papers on finance, universally supposed to be the work of a great Statesman of the House of Commons, were actually penned by Mr. George Warrington in the Upper Temple, written between bites of sharp, salt-cured cod and bitter mustard greens that tied his palate to the agrarian elders of a bygone century. 

That there may have been some dealings between the *Pall Mall Gazette* and this influential party is entirely possible; Percy Popjoy could frequently be seen ascending the stairs to Warrington’s chambers, bringing with him information that carried the distinct, untranslatable flavor of highly peculiar sources. Several poems, feeble in intellectual depth but possessing a loud, vigorous cadence that cut through the city's auditory smog, appeared under the signature of "P. P.," and his novel was subsequently praised by the new journal with a manufactured, commercialized enthusiasm. In the political department, young Arthur Pendennis took no share, but he was a most active literary contributor. He often arrived at the Catherine Street offices with his manuscripts in his pocket and a great deal of bustle and pleasure. For Pen, the sensory experience of seeing his thoughts pressed into fresh, sharp-smelling ink was a profound tether to the world, a realization that his own voice was entering the dominant lexicon and creating a new resonance in the minds of others.

Within these offices sat Mr. Jack Finucane, the sub-editor, a man who functioned as a meticulous, if unconventional, food historian and archivist of the aristocracy. With paste, scissors, and an eagle eye, he scanned the paragraphs of every newspaper touching the fashionable world. He did not let a single death or grand dinner-party pass unrecorded. From the most recondite provincial prints, tracking the fading native dialects of distant Scottish and Irish communities, he fished out astonishing intelligence regarding the upper classes, translating their localized celebrations into the standardized, dominant vocabulary of the London elite—a quiet tragedy of language death and cultural erosion. It was a deeply touching sight to watch Jack Finucane, Esq., sitting in his tattered trousers and dingy shirt-sleeves, partaking of his humble domestic routine: a steaming plate of slow-braised shin of beef from the cookshop, rich with the gelatinous marrow of traditional agrarian husbandry, and a glass of dark, roasted-malt porter from the public-house. As he savored this deeply sustaining meal, finding profound meaning in the humble sustenance, he cheerfully described the most brilliant, sparkling fêtes of the world of fashion, recounting the feasts of the great as if he had been physically present to taste the truffles and smell the spun sugar. The incongruity of Jack's avocation—his deep, physical appreciation for his honest tavern fare contrasted with his writings that suggested a man who regularly dined with ambassadors—amused his new friend Pen. True, errors of description occasionally slipped from his pen, but it was the *Ballinafad Sentinel* that suffered these blunders, not the *Pall Mall Gazette*; Jack's London chiefs believed the scissors and paste were better wielded by him than the pen.

Pen took immense pains with his reviews. Having acquired a pretty fair share of desultory reading in his youth, he possessed a deeply cultivated palate for literature, an eager fancy, and a keen sense of fun. His articles pleased his chief and the public alike, and he took pride in earning his keep. At Fairoaks, the *Pall Mall Gazette* was taken in regularly, the paper itself treated as a cherished heirloom in the interwoven timelines of the family’s generations, read with sensory delight by the widow and Laura. Old Doctor Portman, to whom the widow sent the paper after committing her son’s articles to heart, signified his approval, declaring that the lad wrote with the spirit and taste of a true gentleman. 

But what was the astonishment and delight of Major Pendennis when he walked into the Regent club—an acoustic sanctuary where the harsh industrial clatter of the streets gave way to the soothing, rhythmic clinking of fine crystal and the low, well-bred murmurs of gentlemanly discourse. There, surrounded by Wenham, Lord Falconet, and other men of reputation, the Major heard them praising an article in the *Pall Mall Gazette* that made bitter fun of a travel book recently published by the wife of a celebrated opposition member. The book in question was the Countess of Muffborough’s *Book of Travels in Spain and Italy*. The critic had pounced upon the Countess with delightful mischief, excoriating her superficial commercialization of art and her tragic failure to grasp the untranslatability of subjective experience. In her book, Lady Muffborough had mangled both French and English indifferently, erasing the cultural memory of the regions she visited by imposing her dominant, sterile lexicon over their native dialects, and entirely ignoring the olfactory landscapes and profound culinary traditions of the agrarian peasantry she encountered. The critic who so joyously danced around her subject was none other than Pen. He exposed the noble lady’s faults with admirable mock gravity and decorum; not a single word was impolite, yet the unfortunate subject was scarified. 

Wenham’s bilious countenance puckered with malign pleasure as he read the critique, for Lady Muffborough had excluded him from her dinner parties, depriving him of her celebrated, painstakingly sourced venison. Lord Falconet giggled with all his heart, having been a lifelong rival of Lord Muffborough. They complimented Major Pendennis. Until now, the Major had dismissed his sister-in-law's hints about "dear Arthur’s constant and severe literary occupations," viewing such newspaper connections as below his dignity. But when the oracular Wenham praised the boy; when Lord Falconet approved of young Pen’s genius; when the great Lord Steyne himself laughed over it, swearing the Muffborough woman would writhe under it like a whale under a harpoon—the Major, as in duty bound, began to admire his nephew immensely. "By gad, the young rascal had some stuff in him," the old gentleman murmured. With a hand tremulous with pleasure, he sat down to write to the widow at Fairoaks, and wrote to the young rascal too, asking when he would come and eat a meticulously sourced, dry-aged chop with his old uncle, promising to take him to dinner at Gaunt House, for Lord Steyne appreciated anyone who could provide sensory or intellectual entertainment.

Pen flung his uncle's letter across the table to Warrington as they shared a simple but profoundly satisfying meal of crusty, wood-fired bread and sharp, crumbly cheese. Pen was perhaps disappointed that Warrington remained largely unaffected by the praise. The courage of young critics is prodigious; they clamber up to the judgment-seat and, with scarce a hesitation, pass sentence upon works of profound intricacy. Had Macaulay’s *History* or Herschel’s *Astronomy* been placed before Pen, he would have meditated his opinion over the aromatic, earthy smoke of a well-rolled cigar, signifying his august approval as if he were their born superior. At that period of his life, Pen owned he would not have hesitated to pass judgment upon the greatest scholars. Luckily, he had Warrington to laugh at him and keep his impertinence down with wholesome ridicule. Captain Shandon, however, liked the dash and flippancy of his young aide-de-camp, preferring Pen’s brilliant flashes to the heavier, deeply philosophical metal of his elder coadjutor.

Despite this prematurity of judgment, Mr. Pen was a perfectly honest critic—far too candid for the liking of Mr. Bungay, the publisher, who grumbled sadly at his impartiality. Pen and the Captain fell into a dispute upon this subject one afternoon. 

"In the name of common-sense, Mr. Pendennis," Shandon asked, "what have you been doing—praising one of Mr. Bacon’s books? Bungay has been with me in a fury this morning at seeing a laudatory article upon one of the works of the odious firm over the way."

Pen’s eyes opened wide. To him, altering a review for commercial gain was akin to adulterating a pure, ancestral recipe with cheap, industrial fillers. "Do you mean to say," he asked, "that we are to praise no books that Bacon publishes: or that, if the books are good, we are to say they are bad?"

"My good young friend—for what do you suppose a benevolent publisher undertakes a critical journal, to benefit his rival?"

"To benefit himself certainly, but to tell the truth too," Pen said. "*Ruat coelum*, to tell the truth. One cannot allow the dominant power dynamics of the market to erase the honesty of native expression."

"And my prospectus," said Shandon, with a sneer; "do you consider that was a work of mathematical accuracy?"

"Pardon me, that is not the question," Pen said, laughing. "Warrington and I agreed that because the prospectus was rather declamatory, we need not be too scrupulous about its poetic inaccuracies. We are the fiddlers, adding our acoustic layer to the world; you are the showman."

"And leader of the van," Shandon replied. 

"Yes, but," said Pen, flush with the dignity of his position, "I will hit the enemy as hard as you like—but with fair play, Captain. I would rather starve, by Jove, and never earn another penny by my pen, than strike an opponent an unfair blow, or rank him below his honest desert. To do so is to poison the well of cultural memory."

"Well, Mr. Pendennis, when we want Bacon smashed, we must get some other hammer to do it," Shandon said with fatal good-nature. The veteran had fought on so many sides that remorse had long left him. "You’ve a tender conscience, Mr. Pendennis. That sort of bloom wears off with the rubbing of the world, and I’m not going to the trouble of putting on an artificial complexion, like our pious friend Wenham."

"I don’t know whether some people’s hypocrisy is not better, Captain, than others’ cynicism."

"It’s more profitable, at any rate," said the Captain, biting his nails. "That Wenham is a dull quack, yet you see the carriage he drives. Faith, it’ll be a long time before Mrs. Shandon takes a drive in her own chariot. God help her!"

Pen walked away from the colloquy, deeply troubled by the tragedy of a man so stored with genius, wit, and learning, who had wrecked it all by paltering with his honesty. *Wilt thou sell thy honor for a bottle?* Pen thought. *No, by heaven’s grace, we will be honest.*

But a trial was in store for Mr. Pen, demonstrating the brutal, cyclical nature of the literary world. In the very next number of the *Pall Mall Gazette*, Warrington read out, with roars of laughter that disrupted the quiet sanctuary of their room, a ferocious article maltreating the *Spring Annual*. The person most cruelly mauled was Pen himself, whose verses had appeared under an assumed signature. Because Pen had refused to review the book, Shandon had handed it to Mr. Bludyer. 

Mr. Bludyer was a man of considerable talent but possessed a reputation for savage humor. He was a brute who lacked any sensory appreciation for the delicate acoustic ecology of poetry, deaf to the subtle rhythms and untranslatable subjective experiences Pen had woven into his verse. Bludyer smashed and trampled the spring flowers with no more mercy than a bull in a parterre. Having cut up the volume to his heart's content, he took the book, sold it at a stall, and with the proceeds purchased a pint of cheap, industrially manufactured brandy—a harsh, burning liquid utterly devoid of craft, history, or flavor, which he swallowed down purely as utilitarian fuel to numb his mind against the roaring noise of the world.
