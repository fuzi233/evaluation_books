The night of May hung heavy over Wadi Halfa, a suspended breath between the heat of the day and the chill of the coming dawn. Above the Nile, on a grassy knoll that served as a sentinel overlooking the river, three officers sat in their lounge chairs, the smoke of their cigars curling into a sky that had been scrubbed clean of stars. The moon was full, a blinding coin that had stolen the lustre from the planets themselves, leaving the firmament washed in a pearlescent, luminous haze where only the smallest pinpricks of light dared remain hidden. Below them, the Nile flowed with a sluggish, hypnotic rhythm, its surface polished to the gleam of a burnished mirror reflecting the opposite bank—a vast, white expanse of desert stretching to infinity, dotted with hummocks that sparkled like jewels set in a hoarfrost. Behind the officers, the verandah of the mess-room cast a solid, impenetrable shadow, a block of absolute blackness against the pale world beyond.

One of the men struck a match, the flare illuminating a face etched with anxiety and worry. He tossed the burning match aside, his voice low. "I hope no harm has come to him," he murmured. "Though I fear I cannot truly believe it."

The speaker was Colonel Dawson, a man of middle age commanding a Sudanese battalion. His response came from Lieutenant Calder of the Engineers, whose hair had indeed turned grey—a common affliction in the Sudan—but whose unlined face betrayed a youthfulness that clashed sharply with the gravity of the situation. Yet, in this instance, youth offered no shield against the Colonel's dread.

"He departed Halfa eight weeks ago," Calder said, his tone devoid of optimism.

"Precisely eight weeks today," Dawson replied.

A third figure, a tall, spindly Major of the Army Service Corps, broke the somber silence with a cheerful prophecy. "To conclude Durrance is lost is premature," he argued, leaning back. "You know Durrance. Place him in a desert campfire with a circle of sheikhs, and he would dance for a month without a hint of boredom. Here, amidst letters, offices, and desks—the very things he despises—he is stifled. He will appear, surely, though he lacks the urgency of a man running from death."

"He is three weeks overdue," Dawson countered, his voice tightening. "He is methodical, in his own way. I am afraid."

Major Walters gestured toward the white emptiness of the desert across the river. "If he had ventured westward, I might concede your fears," he noted. "But Durrance traveled east, through the rugged terrain toward Berenice and the Red Sea. Even in the darkest hours, when Osman Digna threatened Suakin, the tribes in those regions remained quiet."

Colonel Dawson found no solace in Walters's logic. He tugged nervously at his moustache, repeating the phrase like a prayer: "Three weeks overdue."

Lieutenant Calder tapped ash from his pipe, refilling it with deliberate slowness. Leaning forward, pressing the tobacco down with his thumb, he spoke softly. "Perhaps a trap was laid. I hesitate to say it aloud, for until recently I suspected nothing. But now... I wonder. Do you recall the night before his departure?"

"Yes," Dawson admitted, pulling his chair closer. Of all the men in Wadi Halfa, Calder possessed a unique intimacy with Durrance. Their ranks differed, but their ages were similar; Calder, fresh from England with an ardent hunger for experience, had found in Durrance a reticent but genuine friend.

"I remember that night clearly," Walters interjected. "Durrance dined at the mess and retired early to prepare."

"His preparations were complete," Calder corrected gently. "He did not return to his quarters. Instead, he walked along the riverbank to Tewfikieh."

Tewfikieh lay a mile north, a frontier town separated from Halfa by the river's edge. It was a chaotic tapestry of Greek storekeepers, barefoot cafes, native cookshops, and tobacconists, where the dialects of the Dinka and the Delta collided in a noisy cacophony. Yet, beneath the clamor, there existed a profound, brooding silence—the silence of the desert and the East. The crowds walked on sand, mostly barefoot, moving so noiselessly that if the shouts and voices momentarily ceased, the figures of men and women would seem to flit by like ghosts. Even at night, when the streets teemed with life, a deep hush seemed to lie beneath the uproar, almost audible to the ear.

"Durrance arrived in Tewfikieh at ten o'clock," Calder recounted. "I reached his quarters at eleven. He was nowhere to be found. He was scheduled to depart eastward at four in the morning, and I needed to speak with him regarding a specific detail of business. I waited. He returned shortly after, looking agitated, restless, vibrating with an unusual emotion. He barely listened to me, answering at random. At one moment, his expression suggested fury; the next, he laughed, as if compelled by joy. Then, abruptly, he dismissed me, instructing his servant to retire, claiming he would receive his visitor personally."

"And who was this visitor?" Dawson asked.

"I do not know," Calder answered. "All I know is that when his servant roused him at four o'clock, Durrance was still seated on the verandah outside his quarters, waiting. The visitor had not appeared."

"Did he leave a note?"

"No. I was awake before he left. I thought he was confused, worried, intending to explain. Perhaps he couldn't decide what to say. Even as he mounted his camel, he turned to look at me. He hesitated—perhaps thinking better of speaking, or worse—and then rode slowly past the post office and into the desert, his head buried in his breast. I wonder if he rode into a trap. Who could this secret visitor be? What business required such secrecy? Important, troubling business, undoubtedly. And yet, he failed to conduct it. Was the entire encounter a lure? Like Colonel Dawson, I am afraid."

Silence settled over the group until Major Walters broke it, offering no defense, only a renewed assertion of his cheerfulness. "I do not believe Durrance is lost," he declared, rising from his chair.

"I know what I must do," Dawson said firmly. "Tomorrow morning, I dispatch a strong search party."

The following morning, as they gathered for breakfast on the verandah, Dawson outlined the details of the expedition. Major Walters, despite his earlier optimism, had evidently given weight to Calder's tale during the night. He leaned across the table toward Calder. "Did you never inquire who Durrance spoke with in Tewfikieh that night?"

"I did," Calder replied, a puzzle forming in his voice. He sat with his back to the Nile, facing the glass doors of the mess-room, addressing Walters who sat opposite. "I discovered he spoke to only one person, and that individual could not possibly have been the visitor he awaited. Durrance stopped before a café where wandering musicians played for their nightly lodging. A Greek, I was told, stepped out to collect his hat. Durrance tossed a sovereign into it, the man turned to thank him, and they conversed briefly."

As he reached this point, Calder raised his head. Recognition flashed in his eyes, transforming swiftly into bewilderment. He gripped the table edge, leaning forward, ready to spring up, but restrained himself. He glanced around; Colonel Dawson poured cocoa, Major Walters stared at his plate. No one noticed his sudden arrest of motion. Calmer now, he leaned back, staring intently ahead. "But I never heard him speak to anyone else. He seemed merely to stroll through the village and return to Halfa."

"That offers little aid," the Major observed.

"And is that all you know?" Dawson asked.

"No, not entirely," Calder said slowly. "I know, for instance, that the man we are discussing is staring straight at me."

Instantly, every eye at the table turned toward the mess-room entrance.

"Durrance!" Dawson shouted, leaping to his feet.

"When did you arrive?" the Major demanded.

Durrance stood in the doorway, the dust of his journey powdering his clothes, his face burned to the color of red brick. He listened with remarkable intentness to the voices of his comrades. Notably, Calder, his friend, did not rise or offer greeting. He remained seated, watching with curiosity and perplexity. But as Durrance descended the three steps onto the verandah, a look of troubled comprehension swept across his features.

"We expected you three weeks ago," Dawson said, pulling a chair from an empty spot at the table.

"The delay was unavoidable," Durrance replied, taking the chair and drawing it close.

"Do my explanations suffice?" Calder asked.

"Not at all. It was the Greek musician I awaited that night," Durrance explained, a laugh escaping him. "Curiosity drove me to ask what misfortune had cast him out to play the zither for a night's lodging in a Tewfikieh café. That was all," he added, his voice softening. "Yes, that was all."

"Yet you neglect your breakfast," Dawson remarked, rising. "What shall you have?"
