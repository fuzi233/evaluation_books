Elinor stood motionless long after Willoughby’s departure, the echo of his carriage wheels fading into silence. Her mind was a tumult of conflicting thoughts, each more sorrowful than the last. How strange it was—that Willoughby, whom she had so recently despised as the worst of men, could now stir in her such aching pity. His suffering, born of his own faults, softened her heart toward him, and she found herself thinking of him with a tenderness she had not expected. It was not his merits that moved her, she knew, but the lingering traces of what he had once been: his charm, his warmth, his unfeigned love for Marianne—a love that should have been innocent but was now tainted beyond repair.

Yet even as she acknowledged these feelings, she fought them. It was long before she could steady herself enough to return to Marianne’s bedside.

Her sister, at least, was peaceful—revived by deep, healing sleep, her face no longer shadowed by pain. Elinor watched her, her own heart too full for words. The past, the present, the future—Willoughby’s visit, Marianne’s fragile recovery, their mother’s imminent arrival—all pressed upon her at once, leaving no room for exhaustion. She could only pray that her composure would hold.

But there was no time for lingering unease. Within half an hour, another carriage arrived. Elinor flew to the door, desperate to spare her mother even a moment of dread. Mrs. Dashwood, pale with terror, stepped inside, her fear so overwhelming that she could not speak. Elinor needed no words; she clasped her mother’s hands and at once assured her—Marianne was safe.

The relief was instant. Mrs. Dashwood, trembling, embraced her daughter again and again, then turned to Colonel Brandon with tearful gratitude, pressing his hand as if words could not capture what she owed him. The Colonel, ever restrained, accepted her thanks in silence.

As soon as she had gathered herself, Mrs. Dashwood hurried to Marianne’s side. There, she found her beloved child weakened but alive—dearer now than ever after such peril. Elinor, watching them, felt a swell of joy, though she feared the excitement might disturb Marianne’s rest. But Mrs. Dashwood, for all her usual exuberance, was gentle, careful—prudent where her daughter’s life was concerned. Marianne, content merely to have her mother near, did not stir.

That night, Mrs. Dashwood insisted on keeping vigil. Elinor, though weary, found sleep impossible. Her thoughts circled back to Willoughby—*poor Willoughby*—as she now permitted herself to call him. She could not dismiss his anguished defense, nor quiet her own guilt for having judged him so harshly. Yet the thought of recounting it all to Marianne filled her with dread. What would it do to her sister’s heart? Would she ever recover? For a fleeting, shameful moment, Elinor even wished Willoughby free of his marriage—then recoiled at herself, remembering Colonel Brandon’s quiet devotion.

The Colonel’s arrival at Barton had been softened for Mrs. Dashwood by her own mounting fear; she had already resolved to leave for Cleveland that very day, unwilling to wait for further news. Marianne’s improvement brought radiant joy to her mother, who declared herself the happiest of women. Elinor, though glad, could not help wondering—did their mother ever think of Edward? But Mrs. Dashwood, buoyed by Elinor’s own measured account of her disappointment, allowed herself only thoughts that amplified her happiness.

Then came the revelation.

"At last we are alone," Mrs. Dashwood whispered eagerly when they had a private moment. "My Elinor, you do not yet know all my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself."

Elinor was silent—surprised, yet not entirely so.

"You are never like me, dear Elinor," her mother continued, "or I should wonder at your composure. If I had prayed for any blessing upon this family, it would have been for Colonel Brandon to marry one of you—and I believe Marianne will be happier with him than you would have been."

Elinor nearly asked why—for she could think of no impartial reason—but held her tongue. Her mother’s imagination, once seized upon an idea, would not be checked.

"He confessed it all to me as we traveled," Mrs. Dashwood went on, her voice warm with conviction. "His love has been steadfast from the very beginning, enduring even Marianne’s ill-fated attachment to Willoughby. And never once did he act selfishly! He would have stood aside if she had been happy with another. Such nobility! Such sincerity!"

Elinor smiled faintly. "Colonel Brandon’s character is beyond reproach."

"Indeed it is," her mother agreed. "Had it been otherwise, I should never have encouraged such feelings. But his kindness—his devotion—proves his worth beyond doubt."

Elinor nodded. "I esteem him highly. If Marianne can find happiness with him, I will rejoice as much as you."

Her mother clasped her hands. "I told him—if she lives, as I pray she will—nothing would make me happier than their union. And now that she is safe, I have repeated it. A little time is all that is needed. Marianne’s heart will not remain bound to Willoughby forever."

Yet Elinor noted the Colonel’s restraint. "He does not share your confidence?"

"No," Mrs. Dashwood admitted. "He fears her attachment is too deep, that even if she recovers from it, he himself—with his age and temperament—could never win her. But he is wrong. His maturity is an advantage, his disposition precisely what she needs. And his manners—so sincere, so gentle—are far better suited to her than Willoughby’s showy charm ever was."

Elinor said nothing, though she did not entirely agree.

"Besides," her mother added brightly, "Delaford is but a short distance from Barton! And surely there is some cottage nearby where we might settle."

Elinor nearly laughed at this fresh scheme to bring her to Delaford, though her resolve remained firm.

"And his fortune!" Mrs. Dashwood continued. "Not that I care for such things, but one must be practical—"

Their conversation was interrupted then, and Elinor withdrew, turning it all over in her mind. She wished fervently for her friend’s happiness—even as the thought of Willoughby’s sorrow pierced her heart anew.
