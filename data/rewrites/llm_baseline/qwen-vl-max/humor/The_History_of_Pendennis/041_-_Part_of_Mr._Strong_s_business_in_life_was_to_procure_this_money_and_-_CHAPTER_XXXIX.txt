On the appointed day, Major Pendennis—whose social calendar was about as exciting as a dustbin’s—arrived at Sir Francis Clavering’s residence alongside Arthur, who had no greater desire than to avoid the entire affair. They were greeted by the usual suspects: Sir Francis and his wife, and Captain Strong, whom Arthur was delighted to see, though the Major scowled like a man who’d just been told his favorite soup was made of turnips. “Clavering’s d—— house-steward,” he muttered under his breath, as if Strong were some kind of domestic usurper.

But before the Major could fully seethe, Mr. Welbore Welbore—a gentleman so dull he seemed to have been manufactured in a factory for boring people—arrived with the solemnity of a clock striking midnight. Welbore, though possessing seven thousand pounds a year and a pedigree longer than a Christmas list, contributed precisely zero to conversation. He sat there like a stone statue, only more depressing. But the Major found comfort in such company: it was familiar, safe, and required no effort.

Then came the Dowager Lady Rockminster (who had her own reasons for being nice to the Claverings), Lady Agnes Foker, and her son Harry—our old friend, now slightly less awkward but still prone to blushing like a beetroot when near a pretty face. Mr. Pynsent couldn’t make it—he was trapped in Parliament, where duty, unlike wine, never makes you feel better. Miss Blanche Amory arrived last, resplendent in a white silk dress that clung to her like gossip clings to a scandal. Foker leaned over and whispered to Pen, “She’s a stunner.” Pen nodded, not because he agreed, but because he didn’t want to appear rude.

Blanche was extra sweet on this occasion. She held out her hand with all the warmth of a summer’s day, asked after Laura and Mrs. Pendennis, and declared she longed to return to the country. She was simple, affectionate, and entirely unguarded—like a lamb who doesn’t know wolves exist. Harry Foker, who usually spoke only when cornered by a fire hydrant, suddenly became fluent. He chattered about Taglioni, the Sylphide, and the mystery of how cherries end up inside jelly. (“How do they get those cherries inside? Do they feed the jellies?”) He even hummed the ballet tune, which brought a smile to Blanche’s lips and a sneer to Pen’s soul.

Pen, ever the romantic cynic, remembered that Blanche once said similar things to him—about singing duets, about music, about feeling *seen*. Now she was offering them to Foker. And while Pen didn’t say anything aloud, he thought: *How many men has she sung with since I last saw her?* But he kept quiet. Instead, he smiled and said, “I’d love to hear you sing again, Miss Blanche. Your voice is… remarkable.”

“Oh, but you liked Laura’s,” she teased.

“Laura’s a contralto,” Pen replied, sounding bitter enough to curdle milk. “And contraltos are often out of tune. I’ve heard too much music in London. It’s all too loud, or too fake. I only care for what I heard when I was young.”

“I adore English ballads,” Blanche said, eyes bright. “Sing me one after dinner?”

Pen, heart full of longing and jealousy, pleaded: “Please. Just one.”

But then she turned to Foker. “Shall I sing you an English song after dinner?” she asked, flashing him a look that could melt ice in Siberia.

“If you promise to come up soon,” he said, grinning like a cat who’d just found the cream.

“I’ll come up fast enough,” he added, already planning his next move.

He wasn’t picky about wine either. “I don’t care for much after dinner—I take my whack at dinner, you know. When I’ve had enough, I toddle off to tea. I’m a domestic character. Simple habits. And when I’m happy, I’m generally in good spirits—ain’t I, Pen?”

And thus, the artless youth prattled on, while Blanche listened with infinite patience. When the ladies retired, she gave both boys a little nod and dropped her gloves beside Foker and her handkerchief beside Pen—equal favors, perhaps, but Foker felt the slight edge of preference. As she left, her final glance passed over Strong’s broad white waistcoat and landed directly on Harry’s face. The door closed. He sighed. Then drank a bumper of claret.

The dinner had begun earlier than usual—because it wasn’t a grand soirée, but a modest gathering—and the ladies departed while daylight still lingered. From the balconies, they could see Kensington Gardens, couples strolling, carriages rolling past. The sun hadn’t set yet; it still gilded the statue of the Duke of Wellington, erected by the ladies of England as a tribute to their national hero. Inside, the dining room was open to the breeze, and passersby could glimpse six gentlemen in white waistcoats, decanters gleaming, fruit piled high. A little boy at the railing shouted, “Hi hi, Jim! Wouldn’t you like to be there and have a cut of that pineapple?”

Meanwhile, outside, a peculiar figure caught the eye of Jeames, the footman, standing pensively by the door. This man was dressed like a king’s cousin: chains, jewels, shiny boots, brass buttons, and wristbands that looked like they’d been stolen from a circus. His hair was suspiciously black, his whiskers a deep purple—Tyrian, possibly. He was clearly drunk, swaying like a tree in a hurricane, hat askew, eyes bloodshot.

And then—laughter erupted from within. Strong had just delivered a particularly vulgar joke. The man outside laughed too—loudly, strangely, slapping his thigh. He winked at Jeames. “Plush, my boy, isn’t that a good story?”

Jeames, whose job did not include dealing with drunken noblemen, muttered later, “A holtercation with a feller in the streets is never no good.” So he called out, “Pleaceman!” and pointed silently at the man.

Policeman X marched forward, Berlin glove tucked into his belt. “Now, sir, will you have the kindness to move on?” he asked.

The man didn’t hear. He nodded, grinned, waggled his head. “Not stare—ho ho—that’s a good one!”

“Move on,” X repeated, louder.

The man staggered back, squared up, and roared, “Hullo! Keep your hands off a gentleman!” Then he swore—something best left unsaid.

“Move on,” X insisted. “Don’t block the pavement.”

“Who’s to stop me?” the man sneered. “Looking at my friends, if I like. Not you, old highlows.”

“Friends, eh?” X said. “Move on.”

“If you touch me, I’ll pitch into you!” the man bellowed.

At that moment, a cry came from inside: “It’s that d—— Altamont!” Sir Francis gasped.

Strong, rising instantly, ran to the door. The scene outside was now a spectacle: a bareheaded man in a white waistcoat, a policeman, and a flashy drunk nearly fighting on the sidewalk. A small crowd began to gather.

Strong grabbed the man’s arm. “For God’s sake, come in.”

“Send for a cab, James,” he whispered.

They dragged the man inside—though he resisted, roaring, “That ain’t the door! That’s the dining-room door—where the drink’s going on! I’ll go and have some, by Jove!”

The butler blocked the way. Sir Francis appeared, pale as a ghost.

“I will have some,” the man roared. “Get us a bottle of the yellow seal—the very best. A hundred rupees a dozen, no mistake.”

Sir Francis hesitated. Then, with a forced laugh, said, “Well, Altamont, come in. I’m glad to see you.”

Colonel Altamont—revealed to be the Ambassador of the Nawaub of Lucknow—reeled into the room, triumphant, and sank into the nearest chair. He stared around, frowning, then smiling, hiccuping compliments about the wine. “Very singular man,” Strong said gravely. “Resided long in a native court. They get very singular habits.”

“Very,” said Major Pendennis, dry as dust.

Foker whispered to Pen, “It’s the man who sang the Malay song at the Back Kitchen.”

“Try this pine,” Foker offered Altamont.

“Pines? I’ve seen ’em feed pigs on pines,” Altamont said.

“All the Nawaub’s pigs are fed on pines,” Strong whispered to the Major.

“Oh, of course,” the Major replied, unimpressed.

Altamont then fixed his gaze on Pen. “I know you, young fellow. Baymouth ball. Wanted to fight the Frenchman. I remember you.”

Pen bowed stiffly. “I remember the circumstance perfectly.”

“What’s his name?” Altamont demanded.

“Mr. Pendennis,” Strong said.

“Pendennis!” Altamont roared. “Pendennis be hanged!” He slammed his fist on the table.

“My name is Pendennis,” the Major said, dignity cracking. “And I’d appreciate it if you didn’t curse it so loudly.”

Altamont turned. For a moment, he seemed to sober. He raised a hand to his forehead, dislodging his wig slightly. His eyes locked with the Major’s. They stared—two warriors, one drunk, one dignified. Then Altamont buttoned his coat, rose abruptly, and reeled toward the door, muttering, “Captain Beak! Captain Beak, by jingo!”

And just like that, he was gone.

Less than fifteen minutes had passed since his arrival. The guests blinked. Clavering looked terrified. “Do you know him?” he asked the Major.

“I’ve seen the fellow,” the Major said. “Deserter from the Horse Artillery. Got into the Nawaub’s service. I remember his face.”

“Oh!” Clavering sighed, relief flooding his face.

The cab arrived. Strong helped Altamont in. Coffee was served. The gentlemen ascended to the drawing-room, where Miss Amory played the piano.

Foker was enchanted. Pen pretended to talk to others, but Blanche pulled him to the piano by singing his own words—words he’d written years ago, now set to music. He stayed by her side, turning pages like a devoted servant.

Later, Foker sighed, “Gad! How I wish I could write verses like you, Pen.”

“If I could, wouldn’t I?” Pen replied. “But I never was a dab at writing. I was too idle at school.”

No one mentioned the chaos downstairs. Pen almost did—but then he saw Clavering’s face: pale, panicked. So he kept quiet.

The evening was dull. Welbore fell asleep mid-song. The Major, usually a fountain of scandal, sat silent, watching Blanche play. When it was time to leave, he rose and complimented Miss Amory lavishly. “Your daughter, Lady Clavering,” he declared, “is a perfect nightingale—a perfect nightingale, begad! I’ve scarcely ever heard anything equal to her. Her pronunciation of every language—begad, of every language—is perfect. And the best houses in London must open before a young lady with such talents—and, allow an old fellow to say, Miss Amory, such a face.”

Blanche stared. Pen stared. The Major had just praised her as if she were royalty.

They walked home together. Foker, puffing his cigar like a chimney, clung to them until they reached Bury Street. The Major wished them goodnight.

“And I say, Pen,” he whispered, pulling his nephew aside, “mind you call in Grosvenor Place tomorrow. They’ve been uncommonly civil—mons’ously civil and kind.”

Pen promised, though he wondered why. Then the Major’s door closed behind him.

Foker took Pen’s arm and walked on, silent, until Charing Cross. Then he broke into a monologue about poetry, regret, and Blanche Amory—his heart laid bare like a wet towel on a cold rail. All the way down the Strand and up to the Temple stairs, he talked—about singing, about her voice, about how he’d wasted his life.

And Pen, walking home, thought: *I may have lost her to a man who sings about pigs and eats pineapple like it’s a sacrament—but at least he’s honest about it.*
