"I ask Thee for a thoughtful love, Through constant watching wise, To meet the glad with joyful smiles, And to wipe the weeping eyes; And a heart at leisure from itself To soothe and sympathise." — *Anon.*

Margaret Hale proved herself a remarkably attentive listener to her mother's myriad schemes for alleviating the hardships of Helstone's poorest parishioners. Yet, beneath her polite attention, each new proposal struck a fresh blow to her heart. By the onset of the frost, they would be forced to depart. Old Simon's rheumatism would worsen, his failing sight would go unassisted, and the comforting visits of broth and warm flannel would be rendered impossible—or entrusted to strangers who could not fill the void left by her absence. Little Mary Domville's crippled son would crawl futilely to the gate, searching for a face that would never appear. These neighbors would never comprehend why she had abandoned them, nor would they understand the gravity of the situation. "Father has always expended his entire stipend within the parish," Mrs. Hale explained, her voice heavy with concern. "I fear I am encroaching upon future funds, but the winter promises to be severe, and our people require aid."

"Oh, Mother, let us do everything we can," Margaret urged, her eagerness masking the profound sadness she felt. She clung to the notion that this assistance would be their final act together; "We may not remain here long."

"Do you feel unwell, my darling?" Mrs. Hale asked, her anxiety rising as she misinterpreted Margaret's melancholy. "You look pale and exhausted. Is it this soft, damp, unhealthy air?"

"No, Mother, it is not that. The air is delightful; it carries the scent of purity, a stark contrast to the choking smoke of Harley Street. However, I am weary; surely it is nearly bedtime."

"Not far off—it is half-past nine. You should retire immediately. Ask Dixon for some gruel. I shall check on you once you are settled. I fear you have caught a chill, or perhaps breathed in the miasma from those stagnant ponds—"

"Oh, Mother," Margaret murmured, offering a faint smile as she kissed her cheek, "I am perfectly well. Do not trouble yourself; I am simply tired."

Retiring upstairs, Margaret complied with her mother's request, drinking the bland gruel to ease her conscience. Languidly propped against her pillows, she waited until Mrs. Hale ascended to lock her own door. The moment the latch clicked, Margaret sprang from the bed, draped her dressing gown around her shoulders, and paced the floor until the creaking of the wood warned her to cease. She collapsed onto the window seat in the small, recessed bay. Earlier that morning, the sight of the church tower glowing brightly under a clear sky had filled her with hope, promising a sunlit day. Now, sixteen hours later, she sat paralyzed by a grief so deep it stifled tears, replaced instead by a cold, dull ache that seemed to drain the vitality from her very soul.

Mr. Henry Lennox's visit, his generous offer, felt like a distant dream, severed from her reality. The harsh truth remained: her father had allowed doubt to fester until he became a schismatic, an outcast. All the upheaval of their lives revolved around this single, devastating fact.

Gazing out at the dark grey silhouettes of the church towers, sharp and square against the deep, transparent blue of the night sky, Margaret felt a terrifying emptiness. She stared endlessly into the distance, seeking a sign of God, finding none. To her, the earth seemed more desolate enclosed by an iron dome than exposed to the infinite void. Those endless spaces of serene darkness mocked her more cruelly than any physical barrier, shutting out the cries of suffering humanity. Perhaps those voices would ascend to the divine throne only to vanish forever, lost in the vastness before reaching Him.

In this mood, her father entered silently. The moonlight revealed his daughter in her solitary vigil. He approached and gently touched her shoulder.

"Margaret, I heard you were awake. I could not resist coming in to ask you to pray with me—to recite the Lord's Prayer. It would benefit us both."

Mr. Hale and Margaret knelt by the window. He looked upward; she bowed her head in humble shame. God was present, close and listening to her father's whispered words. Even if her father was a heretic, had not she, moments prior, displayed a skepticism equally absolute? She said nothing, retreating to her bed like a child fleeing from a perceived fault. If the world presented insurmountable puzzles, she trusted that God would reveal the necessary step for the hour. Yet, the memory of Mr. Lennox haunted her dreams. He climbed a mythical tree to retrieve her bonnet, fell, and she struggled in vain to save him, held back by an invisible force. Then, the scene shifted: she was back in the Harley Street drawing room, conversing with him as of old, yet perpetually aware that he had already perished in that fatal fall.

It was a miserable, restless night, a poor preparation for the dawn. She woke abruptly, unrefreshed, sensing a reality far more painful than her feverish visions. The sorrow returned, compounded by a terrible dissonance. How far had her father wandered, led astray by doubts that appeared to her as temptations from evil? She longed to ask, yet dreaded the answer.

Morning broke crisp and clear, lifting Mrs. Hale's spirits at breakfast. She chattered happily about village charities, ignoring her husband's silence and Margaret's monosyllabic responses. Before the meal concluded, Mr. Hale rose, leaning heavily on the table.

"I shall not return until evening. I am going to Bracy Common to speak with Farmer Dobson regarding dinner. I expect to be back for tea at seven."

He avoided looking at either woman, but Margaret understood the subtext. By seven, the announcement must be delivered. Mr. Hale might have hesitated, but Margaret possessed a different constitution. She could not endure the mounting dread weighing on her mind all day; it was better to confront the worst immediately. The day was too short to console her mother with false hopes. Standing by the window, calculating how to begin, she waited for the servants to vacate the room. Just then, Mrs. Hale descended the stairs, dressed briskly for school, her mood unusually energetic.

"Mother, please join me in the garden this morning. Just one walk," Margaret suggested, linking her arm through her mother's waist.

They stepped through the open window. Mrs. Hale spoke, but Margaret could not decipher her words. Her gaze caught a bee diving into a deep bell-shaped flower. When the insect emerged laden with pollen, that would be the signal. It flew out.

"Mother!" Margaret blurted out, unable to contain the news. "Father is leaving Helstone! He is abandoning the Church and moving to Milton-Northern."

There lay the three crushing facts, barely articulated.

"What leads you to say that?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice trembling with surprise and disbelief. "Who has been spreading such nonsense?"

"Father himself," Margaret replied, wishing she could offer gentleness but lacking the words. They reached a garden bench. Mrs. Hale sat and began to weep.

"I do not understand," she choked out. "Either you have made a grave error, or I fail to grasp your meaning."

"No, Mother, I have made no mistake. Father wrote to the Bishop stating that his doubts prevent him from remaining a priest of the Church of England. He must resign Helstone. He has also consulted Mr. Bell—Frederick's godfather—and it is agreed we move to Milton-Northern." Throughout her explanation, Mrs. Hale watched her daughter's face; the shadow there confirmed her belief in the truth of the words.

"I cannot believe it," Mrs. Hale finally whispered. "He would surely have told me before it came to this."

A sudden pang of guilt struck Margaret. Her mother should have been informed earlier. Regardless of her own complaints, it was a failure on her father's part to allow his daughter to discover his spiritual crisis and impending exile through her own superior understanding. Margaret sat beside her mother, cradling her head against her breast, pressing her soft cheek tenderly against her mother's.

"Dearest, darling Mother! We feared causing you pain. Father suffered acutely. You know you are not robust, and the suspense must have been agonizing."

"When did he tell you, Margaret?"

"Only yesterday," Margaret replied, sensing the underlying jealousy in the question. "Poor Father!" She attempted to steer her mother toward compassion for her father's struggle. Mrs. Hale lifted her head.

"What does he mean by 'doubts'?" she asked. "Does he not mean he believes differently—that he knows better than the Church?"

Margaret shook her head, tears filling her eyes as her mother touched the raw nerve of her own regret.

"Cannot the Bishop correct him?" Mrs. Hale asked, half-impatient.

"I fear not," Margaret said softly. "But I did not inquire. I could not bear to hear his response. Nevertheless, it is settled. He departs in two weeks. I am unsure if he has not already submitted his formal resignation."
