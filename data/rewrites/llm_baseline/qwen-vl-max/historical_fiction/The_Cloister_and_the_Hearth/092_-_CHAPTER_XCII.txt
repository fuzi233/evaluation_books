Yes, the hermit of Gouda was in truth the vicar of Goudー—though he knew it not, so complete was his retreat from the world. My reader will recall that as soon as the fire of passion within him began to fade, a wave of remorse crashed over him. He had been undone by love, and now he trembled at himself. Only those who have climbed high in spiritual devotion, only those who have fallen from such heights, can grasp the horror he felt. It was as if he stood on the edge of a chasm, betrayed by his own confidence.

“Ah, good Jerome,” he cried aloud, “you knew me better than I knew myself! How bitter yet how healing your warning was!”

He had long been accustomed to examining his soul, and now he saw plainly what had stirred his fury: Margaret. She was the root of it all.

“I love her more than God,” he whispered, broken with despair. “More than the Church. What can come of such love—for me? For her?”

The thought sent a chill through him. “Let the strong fight temptation,” he said. “It is for the weak to flee. And who is weaker than I have shown myself? My religion, my penance—what are they but a house of cards, built slowly, carefully, only to collapse at the breath of earthly desire? I must begin again. On a firmer foundation.”

And so he resolved: he would leave Holland at once, spend years in some distant monastery far from her, where time and solitude might harden him against the lure of the flesh. By then, perhaps, the flame would be quenched. Perhaps, older and stronger, he could return—not as a man, but as a monk. And Margaret—she would forget him. She would marry another, and love him. The very pain this thought caused him proved that he was walking the path of duty.

Yet even as he turned away from her, he did not abandon her entirely. Her well-being still mattered. In fact, the thought that he could provide for her, make her secure and rich in this world—though she was not bound to despise wealth—gave him strength. It sustained him in the agony of silence, of leaving without a word, without a glance.

“Oh, what will she think of me?” he groaned. “Will I seem the most cruel, the most heartless creature alive? But so it must be. Better she hate me—wretched though I am—than that I fail in my purpose. Heaven is merciful. It gives me this comfort: I can force that villain to restore her fortune. She shall never lose another true lover because of poverty. Another? Ah, me! Ah, me! God and the saints, help me!”

Thus he set forth on his mission. But first, as you may remember, he went at night to hear the confession of the dying hermit of Gouda.

He found the old man near death, and stayed by his side until his eyes closed and he was buried beneath the floor of the small oratory attached to his cell. The end was peaceful—a quiet close to a stormy life. Once a soldier, the hermit still wore a steel corselet beneath his skin, saying he was now Christ’s warrior, just as he had once been Satan’s.

Clement confessed him, prayed beside him, and then, in turn, sought counsel from one who was dying in such holy calm. The hermit advised him to take up his own place in this secluded retreat.

“It has been a hard battle against the world, the flesh, and the devil,” he said. “I never truly won until I withdrew into the fortress of solitude.”

These words, and the serene passing of the hermit, left a deep mark upon Clement. There was no resistance in him to such a calling. The solitary life was still revered in the Church—whether in anchorites sealed in cells, or in independent hermits living apart in wild places. And Clement knew much about them. At the monastery near Tergou, he had studied their lives; in the Vatican manuscripts, he had devoured their stories with awe. He had talked earnestly with mendicant friars of various orders, who, before the invention of printing, were the keepers and carriers of these sacred annals.

His teacher, Jerome, had spent three years as an anchorite on the heights of Camaldoli—a place where for over four centuries the ancient Thebaid had lived again. Though Jerome was cold and reserved on most matters of faith, he spoke passionately of the hermits. He had pored over the records of St. John Baptist’s abbey, where caves dotted the mountainside, and told Clement of nobles and warriors who had ended their days there—of bishops and archbishops who had passed from sees to hermitages, or from hermitages to sees.

Among them: the Archbishop of Ravenna, and Pope Victor the Ninth. He described their severe disciplines—the way each hermit sought out his weakest point and attacked it relentlessly until he became strongest there. He spoke of the seven daily prayers, held every day regardless of weather or season—matins, prime, tierce, sext, none, vespers, compline—when the solitaries would climb from distant peaks, over treacherous paths, to worship together.

He even named some of the anchorites he had known: Moncata, Duke of Moncata and Cordova; Hidalgo of Spain, who left worldly splendor in his youth; Father John Baptist of Novara, once a general, now a private soldier of Christ; Cornelius, Samuel, and Sylvanus.

Of Sylvanus, Jerome told a tale with grim pride: when the great Duchess de’ Medici obtained papal permission to visit Camaldoli—something long denied—he met her at the first wooden cross. Surrounded by courtiers and flatterers, he rebuked her, warned her, pleaded with her not to profane the holy mountain, where no woman had set foot in centuries. And she, struck by the place and the man, turned back—her entire retinue following her—before a single word was spoken.

In Basel, Clement had gathered more tales, especially of German and English anchorites. He had even compiled a *Catena Eremitarum*, a chain of hermits, beginning with Paul of Thebes in 250 AD, who lived ninety years in solitude, down to 1470. He called them *Angelorum amici et animalium*—friends of angels and animals.

So, though he had never imagined becoming a recluse, the path was already laid. When the dying hermit blessed the citadel of solitude—where he had fought and won—and offered Clement his breastplate of faith, Clement whispered: “Heaven itself led my foot here.”

It struck him too as a sign that his patron saint, St. Bavon, had been a hermit—a stern one, a knight of the solitary cell.

Once reconciled with Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, Clement hurried to the hermit’s dwelling, hoping it had not already been claimed. Such places rarely remained empty long.

He found the rough stone door slightly ajar. For a moment, he feared it was too late. Then he stepped inside—softly, reverently.

No. The cell was empty. And there, untouched, lay the hermit’s possessions: a large ivory crucifix, pens and ink, seeds, a skull—memento mori—his cilice of hair and bristles, a worn sheepskin cloak and hood, his hammer, chisel, and psaltery.

People had passed by, but no one had dared enter. No one had stolen. Faith and simplicity guarded the door more fiercely than iron bars or moats ever could.

As Clement settled into the cell, a sudden flutter broke the silence. A great brown owl dropped from a corner and flew out the window, its wings brushing cold air against his face.

He started. Was this a demon, trying to deter him from his soul’s salvation?

Then he reconsidered: could it be a spirit, a guardian left by the hermit? Or simply an owl?

Finally, he decided: it was just an owl. And he would try to befriend it.

He knelt and began his new life with prayer.

But Clement had not only a passion to subdue—one whose power made him fear for his eternal fate—but also a penance to fulfill. He had yielded to anger, his greatest failing, and cursed his brothers. Now he must atone.

He looked around the modest cell—furnished with comfort—and compared it to the images in his mind: the terrible desert of holy Jerome, who wrestled with sickness and despair for four years; the thorny niche where young Benedict hid, surviving on scraps shared with a raven; the hollow tree of St. Bavon; the cavern at Fribourg where a nameless saint lived, eyes chilled by perpetual darkness, ears deafened by endless waterfalls; the pillar of St. Simeon Stylita, where he stood forty-five years; the stone box of St. Dunstan, barely room to sit or lie; the sealed tombs of Thais and Christina; the damp dungeon of St. Alred.

All these places—dozens more—came to mind. And he said to himself: “This sweet refuge is for the safety of the soul. But what of penance? Jesu, aid me against future faults. And for this fault, I vow: I will see no human face for a year and a day.”

He had precedents. The original occupant of this very cell had likely taken the same vow—hence the two narrow openings, through which he could speak and be answered without being seen.

Adopting the ancient rule of hermits, Clement divided his day into the seven canonical hours, ignoring the shifting light. He memorized the Psalter. In every gap between prayer and sleep, he worked with his hands.

But this self-imposed vow created a problem: what labor could he do unseen, that would benefit others?

For the hermit was to work only when necessity demanded it. Yet Clement’s needs were met by the villagers.

So on moonlit nights, he crept out like a thief and dug gardens for the poor. He wove baskets and slipped them quietly into humble homes.

And since he could not serve travelers during the day, he went out at midnight with hammer and chisel, carving moral and religious sayings into the sandstone along the road.

“Who knows?” he mused. “A chance word may strike deep.”

Oh, sore heart, comfort the poor and bereaved with holy words in their own tongue. For he said: “Well, ‘tis *clavis ad corda plebis*”—the key to the people’s hearts.

He remembered Colonna telling him of Eastern kings who inscribed their victories on mountains. “What,” Clement asked, “if those monarchs are wise to carve their glory on rock, making blood-bubbles endure as long as earth? Shall I let the stones around me remain silent to the King of Glory, whose word made them, and whose breath will reduce them to dust? No. These stones shall speak of eternal peace, of the Lamb—whose frail, afflicted, yet happy servant carves them.”

At that time, the inspired words that had comforted generations were not yet printed in Dutch. So these carved sentences—gold from the Gospel—seemed fresh oracles from heaven, like dew on parched flowers. They softened hearts, lifted spirits, and sent weary travelers singing.

These holy messages, appearing as if by magic; his wise answers given through the window to those seeking counsel; above all, his invisibility—soon brought him a great reputation.

It grew stronger still when, under tears and pleading, he gave medical advice—though always reluctantly. If patients recovered, they credited the holy hermit. If not, they blamed the devil.

“I think he killed nobody,” someone said, “for his remedies were weak and womanish.”

Sage, wormwood, sion, hyssop, borage, spikenard, dog’s-tongue, our Lady’s mantle, feverfew—and faith, above all. And only in small doses—except faith.

When villagers brought him eggs and milk, he refused them with horror.

“Do you not know the hermit’s rule? Bread, herbs, water. Eggs are birds in disguise. When the bird dies, the egg rots. Milk is little better than white blood.”

When they brought too much bread, he refused it.

“You who go among the poor can give it to them.”

“But, holy father, we want you to give it.”

“No. To live on charity and pretend to be generous is to lie with the right hand. Giving what belongs to others to the poor, I would steal their thanks and cheat you, the true giver. That is what thieves do—they boast of bleeding the rich into the lap of the poor. *Occasio avaritiae nomen pauperum.*”

When nothing else convinced them, this Latin phrase always turned them around. So did a line from Virgil’s *Aeneid*.

Thus, his fame grew—yet within, he was deeply critical of himself.

“I cannot deceive myself,” he said. “I cannot deceive God’s creatures. See the birds—how shy they are. I feed them, I long for their trust, yet they never come near. Why? Because no Paul, no Benedict, no Hugh of Lincoln, no Columba, no Guthlac dwells here. The hunted deer does not come. No Fructuosus, no Aventine, no Albert of Suabia. Not even a squirrel dares approach for the acorns I’ve stored. No Columban. Even the owl has fled. They are not deceived. I have a Pope’s word for that. May Heaven rest his soul.”

Clement had one advantage over the image of Margaret he carried in his heart: he had suffered loss, and survived. The mind cannot fully repeat such pain. He had learned to regard her as dead. After that scene in the churchyard of St. Laurens, that habit had been shattered—but enough remained to rebuild it. He saw her as dead to him.

Through strict division of time, constant prayer, and hard labor, he managed, in about three months, to numb the earthly part of his heart.

But then—within days of this fragile peace—there came a crushing despair. Words cannot describe it. Despair has no likeness.

Voices whispered in his ear: “Kill yourself! Kill! Kill! Kill!”

And he longed to obey. Life was unbearable.

He wrestled with the darkness—praying, weeping. “Oh, Lord,” he begged, “let my enemy scourge me with red-hot whips, drag me across rocks by the hair, attack me like a lion, gnaw me with serpent fangs—any pain, any terror—but not this soul-deep gloom that shuts me from Your light and the saints.”

Then a chilling thought struck him: what if the triumphs of darkness in deserts had been hidden, while only the defeats were recorded? Ancient writings hinted at such things—now they seemed to grin with terrifying meaning.

“THEY WANDERED IN THE DESERT AND PERISHED BY SERPENTS,” wrote an early father of hermits. “And were seen no more.”

Another, later, wrote: *Vertuntur ad melancholiam*—they turn to gloomy madness.

Were these not the same? The fathers never mourned bodily death. No—the lost hermits were lost souls. The serpents were diabolical thoughts, born of solitude.

St. Jerome entered the desert with three companions. One fled in the first year. Two died—how? The only one who endured was a giant soul with an iron body.

The chronicler made no comment. No wonder.

So what if this was the truth? That for every mighty soul who survived, many others perished in silence?

Now, the darkened recluse searched history for weapons against despair. The greatest was prayer.

Alas—he could not pray with true fervor. His mechanical routine had dulled his heart. He prayed louder than ever, but his soul remained cold. The words rose alone.

“Poor wingless prayers,” he cried, “you’ll never reach halfway to heaven.”

Once, a fiend of this sort had been driven from King Saul by music.

Clement picked up the hermit’s psaltery. With effort, he mended the strings, tuned it.

But he could not play. His soul was out of tune. The sounds jarred him, nearly drove him mad.

“Ah, wretched me!” he cried. “Saul had a saint to play for him. He was not alone with darkness. But here—no sweet bard of Israel plays for me. I, alone, crushed, with a black fiend sitting mountain-high upon my heart, must make music to lift it to heaven. It cannot be.”

And he fell to the earth, weeping, tearing his hair.
