The evening of February 15, 1587, found Mrs. Leigh pacing the terrace-walk at Burrough House. For three weary years, she had performed this ritual every night, regardless of the weather, gazing out over the winding river, the hazy sandhills, and the vast, western sea. Three years had passed since Frank and Amyas sailed away with their gallant ship and its brave crew, vanishing into the infinite unknown much like John Oxenham before them. Yet, no news had arrived. The loving eyes of Bideford, Appledore, Clovelly, and Ilfracombe had grown hollow from watching and weeping for those lost to the West.

There had been a fleeting flush of hope when Sir Richard, unbeknownst to Mrs. Leigh, sent a horseman to Plymouth upon hearing that Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle had returned from the Spanish Main. The rider brought glorious tidings of the sackings of Cartagena and San Domingo, and the relief of Raleigh's Virginian Colony, but no word of the *Rose* or its passengers. Mrs. Leigh had bowed her head, whispering, "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord!"

Her hair was now gray, her cheeks wan, and her step feeble. She rarely left home save for church or the neighboring cottages. She never spoke her sons' names, allowing not a single word to escape her lips that might betray her thoughts of them. Yet, daily, when the tide rose high and the red flag on the sandhills signaled water over the bar, she paced the terrace, devouring the sea with greedy eyes in search of a sail that never came. The stately ships entered and exited as of old; white sails hung off the bar for hours, days, months, and years. But an instinct within her told her that none were the vessel she sought. She knew that ship, every line and cut of cloth; she could have identified it from miles away among a whole fleet, but it never appeared. Mrs. Leigh bowed her head in worship, moving among the poor who regarded her as an awe-inspiring figure, one whom God had drawn near to Himself in that mysterious heaven of sorrow. Lone women and bedridden men looked into her steadfast eyes, finding strength there, for they knew—though she never spoke of her own grief—that she had descended into the fiercest depths of the fiery furnace and walked there unhurt by the side of One whose form was as of the Son of God. All the while, she blamed herself for her "earthly" longings, confessing nightly to Heaven the weakness that drew her feet to the terrace beneath the wind-clipped trees.

That evening, however, Northam was in a stir. The pebble ridge thundered far below, but Northam was noisy enough without the rolling surge. The tower rocked with pealing bells; people filled the streets shouting and singing around bonfires. They burned the Pope in effigy, drank to the Queen's health, and cried, "So perish all her enemies!" The hills glowed red with bonfires in every village. Far away, the bells of Bideford answered those of Northam as they had seven years prior when Amyas returned from circumnavigating the globe. Today, news had arrived that Mary, Queen of Scots, had been beheaded at Fotheringay. All England, like a dreamer shaking off a hideous nightmare, leapt up in a tremendous shout of jubilation, lifting the terror and danger of seventeen anxious years from its heart forever.

She was gone to answer at a higher tribunal for the noble English blood poured out for her, and the noble English hearts she had tempted into treachery, rebellion, and murder. Elizabeth's words had finally been fulfilled:

"The daughter of debate,
That discord aye doth sow,
Hath reap'd no gain where former rule
Hath taught still peace to grow."

Now she could do evil no more. Murder, adultery, the unforgiving heart, and the tongue that could not speak truth even for its own interest had passed, perhaps atoned for. Her fair face remained a pitiful dream in the memory of those who knew that either she or England must perish.

"Nothing is left of her
Now, but pure womanly."

Even Mrs. Leigh, a Protestant, breathed a prayer for mercy on that soul, "clear as diamond, and as hard," as she had once described herself. She pondered the final scene before the fatal block; it could not have been mere acting. Mrs. Leigh had learned many lessons in the last seven years; might not Mary Stuart have learned something in seventeen? She had been a courtier and knew, as far as a chaste Englishwoman could know, of the godless profligacy of the French court in which poor Mary had been trained among the Medicis, the Guises, and Cardinal Lorraine. She shuddered and sighed to herself: "To whom little is given, of them shall little be required!" Yet the bells continued to peal.

What answered them from the fast-darkening twilight? A flash, followed by the thunder of a gun at sea.

Mrs. Leigh stopped. The flash was right outside the bar. It could not be a ship in distress; the wind was light and westerly, and it was a high spring tide. What could it be? Another flash, another gun. The noisy folk of Northam hushed instantly and hurried into the churchyard overlooking the broad flats and the river.

There was a gallant ship outside the bar, running in with all sails set. It was a large vessel, nearly a thousand tons, but not of English rig. What was its meaning? A Spanish cruiser preparing reprisals for Drake's raid along the Cadiz shore? Unlikely. The Don favored scientific warfare with admirals, vice-admirals, and transports, not such dare-devil tactics.

Easily, on the flowing tide and fair western wind, she slipped up the channel between the two lines of sandhills. She was almost off Appledore. She was no enemy; if she were a foreigner, she was a daring one, for she had never veiled her topsails. Every foreign ship must veil its topsails within sight of an English port, or risk war, as the Spanish admiral discovered many years prior when he sailed into Plymouth Sound without veiling his topsails or lowering the flag of Spain. John Hawkins, the port admiral, had rushed out in his famous *Jesus of Lübeck*, sent a shot between the admiral's masts, and when that failed, ran alongside and fired a shot that pierced the admiral through and through. The offending flag came down, apologies were made, but not accepted for a long time by the stout guardian of her Majesty's honor. If John Hawkins acted thus for a Spanish fleet in peacetime, there were many old sea-dogs in Appledore who would do the same for a single ship in wartime if they could find even an iron pot to burn powder with.

The strange sail passed behind the hill of Appledore, and then a cheer rose from a hundred throats in the quiet evening air. Mrs. Leigh stood still, listening. Another gun thundered among the hills, followed by another cheer.

It took twenty minutes for the vessel to hove in sight again around the dark rocks of the Hubbastone as she turned up the Bideford river. Mrs. Leigh had stood perfectly motionless during that time, a pale, scarcely breathing statue, her eyes fixed upon the Viking's rock.

Round the Hubbastone she came at last. Music blared on board—drums, fifes, shawms, and trumpets—waking ringing echoes from every knoll of wood and slab of slate. As she opened fully onto Burrough House, another cheer burst from her crew, rolling up to the hills from the silver waters far below.

Mrs. Leigh walked quickly toward the house and called her maid, "Grace, bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home!"

"No, surely? O joyful sound! Praised and blessed be the Lord! But, madam, how did you know?"

"I heard his voice on the river; but I did not hear Mr. Frank's with him, Grace!"

"Oh, be sure, madam, where the one is, the other is. They'd never part company. Both come home or neither, I'll warrant. Here's your hood, madam."

Mrs. Leigh, with Grace behind her, started with rapid steps toward Bideford. Was it true? Was it a dream? Had the divine instinct of the mother enabled her to recognize her child's voice among all the rest, or was her brain turning from the long effort of her supernatural calm?

Grace asked herself the same question many times between Burrough and Bideford. When they arrived on the quay, the question answered itself.

As they came down Bridgeland Street—which would later house tobacco warehouses for the Virginia trade, but then was merely a row of rope-walks and sailmakers' shops—they could see the strange ship already at anchor. Just reaching the lower end of the street, a great mob swept round the corner: sailors, women, apprentices, hurrahing, questioning, weeping, laughing. Mrs. Leigh stopped; the mob stopped also.

"Here she is!" shouted someone. "Here's his mother!"

"His mother? Not their mother!" Mrs. Leigh said to herself, turning very pale, though her heart was long past breaking.

The next moment, the giant head and shoulders of Amyas swept round the corner, far above the crowd.

"Make a way! Make room for Madam Leigh!" Amyas fell on his knees at her feet.

She threw her arms round his neck, bending her fair head over his, while sailors, apprentices, and coarse harbor-women were hushed into holy silence, forming a ring round the mother and son.

Mrs. Leigh asked no questions. She saw that Amyas was alone.

At last, he whispered, "I would have died to save him, mother, if I could."

"You need not tell me that, Amyas Leigh, my son."

Another silence.

"How did he die?" whispered Mrs. Leigh.

"He is a martyr. He died in the—"

Amyas could say no more.

"The Inquisition?"

"Yes."

A strong shudder passed through Mrs. Leigh's frame, then she lifted her head.

"Come home, Amyas. I little expected such an honor—such an honor—and such a fair young martyr, too; a very St. Stephen! God, have mercy on me; and let me not go mad before these folk when I ought to be thanking Thee for Thy great mercies! Amyas, who is that?"

She pointed to Ayacanora, standing close behind Amyas, watching keenly.

"She is a poor wild Indian girl—my daughter, I call her. I will tell you her story hereafter."

"Your daughter? My grand-daughter, then. Come hither, maiden, and be my grand-daughter."

Ayacanora came obediently and knelt, having seen Amyas kneel.

"God forbid, child! Kneel not to me. Come home, and let me know whether I am sane or mazed, alive or dead."

Drawing her hood over her face, she turned to go back, holding Amyas tight by one hand and Ayacanora by the other.

The crowd let them depart some twenty yards in respectful silence before bursting into a cheer that made the old town ring.

Mrs. Leigh stopped suddenly.

"I had forgotten, Amyas. You must not let me stand in the way of your duty. Where are your men?"

"Kissed to death by this time; all of them, that is, who are left."

"Left?"

"We went out a hundred, mother, and we came home forty-four—if we are at home. Is it a dream, mother? Is this you? And this old Bridgeland Street again? As I live, there stands Evans the smith, at his door, tankard in hand, as he did when I was a boy!"

The brawny smith crossed the street but stopped when he saw Amyas, yet no Frank.

"Better one than neither, madam!" he said, offering rough comfort. Amyas shook his hand as he passed, but Mrs. Leigh neither heard nor saw him nor anyone else.

"Mother," said Amyas as they passed the causeway, "we are rich for life."

"Yes; a martyr's death was the fittest for him."

"I have brought home treasure untold."

"What, my boy?"

"Treasure untold. Cary has promised to see to it tonight."

"Very well. I would that he had slept at our house. He was a kindly lad and loved Frank. When did he?"

"Three years ago, and more. Within two months of our sailing."

"Ah! Yes, he told me so."

"Told you so?"

"Yes; the dear lad has often come to see me in my sleep; but you never came. I guessed how it was—as it should be."

"But I loved you none the less, mother!"

"I know that, too: but you were busy with the men, you know, sweet; so your spirit could not come roving home like his, which was free. Yes—all as it should be. My maid, and do you not find it cold here in England, after those hot regions?"

"Ayacanora's heart is warm; she does not think about cold."

"Warm? Perhaps you will warm my heart for me, then."

"Would God I could do it, mother!" said Amyas, half reproachfully.

Mrs. Leigh looked up in his face and burst into a violent flood of tears.

"Sinful! Sinful that I am!"

"Blessed creature!" cried Amyas. "If you speak so, I shall go mad. Mother, mother, I have been dreading this meeting for months. It has been a nightmare hanging over me like a horrible black thunder-cloud; a great cliff miles high, with its top hid in the clouds, which I had to climb and dared not.
