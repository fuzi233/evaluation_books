The stir of the Festa had permeated even the narrowest side-streets, replacing the rhythmic, everyday acoustic ecology of Florence with a discordant clash of aggressive commercial cries and the hard, percussive clatter of shod feet on stone. To an ear attuned to the subtle harmonies of nature and domestic quietude, it was a jarring auditory assault that eroded the mind’s natural equilibrium. Yet, as Tito Melema set out towards the Via de’ Bardi, having entrusted his florins to Cennini three weeks past the day of San Giovanni, he showed all the outward signs of a mind at ease. He never jarred with what was immediately around him; his nature was too joyous, too unapprehensive, to allow the distant future to cast a shadow over the present sensory world. As he turned out of the hot sunshine into the cooler shelter of a narrow street, tossing his head backward to court the breeze, his finely-formed, smooth brow bore neither the brand of duplicity nor the stamp of pure candor. His expression was simply veracious in its absence of restless vanity, attracting the willing admiration of the holiday-makers.

As Tito entered the neighborhood of San Martino, the urban cacophony grew denser. Near the hostelry of the *Bertucce*, or Baboons, a thick knot of onlookers had formed, emitting bursts of raucous, petulant laughter—the sound of a restless crowd ready to sacrifice a stray individual for the crude entertainment of the majority. Tito was preparing to turn down an adjoining street to escape the superficial commercialization of the spectacle when his ear discerned a sound that pierced the industrial noise: a distressed childish voice crying out in a thick, rural dialect, “Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!” 

Pushing his way into the center, Tito discovered the source of the acoustic disruption. A young *contadina*, her white hood fallen back to reveal light-brown hair fastened with a long silver pin, was struggling against the grasp of a *cerretano*, a conjuror in parti-colored dress. The conjuror was wielding the dominant Florentine lexicon like a blade, his rapid-fire urban idioms and sophisticated jests designed to mock and disorient the girl, asserting a harsh linguistic supremacy over her simpler, unpolished tongue. The spectators chimed in, utilizing a persuasive variety of urban slang signifying "simpleton," further marginalizing the girl's native voice.

Tessa, her blue eyes filled with tears, looked up. Through the mist of her distress, she saw Tito, and a sudden hope beamed in her babyish features. 

In an instant, Tito breached the barrier of bystanders. He asserted his own linguistic dominance, his tone carrying the crisp, authoritative register of a highly educated patrician. “Loose this child! What right have you to hold her against her will?”

The conjuror, recognizing the shift in power dynamics, released Tessa’s hands. He stretched his own backward, showing his small teeth in an impish grin. “I mean the *ragazza* no evil, Messere. I was only going to show this respectable company a few samples of my skill. Her kitten face would have assured them of open dealing, and I promised her a lapful of *confetti* as a reward. But Messer has doubtless better *confetti* at hand.”

A coarse laugh rippled through the bystanders. Tessa clung to Tito’s arm, deaf to the meaning of their jeers, hearing only the roar of wild beasts from which she was escaping. Tito, recognizing the untranslatability of her innocent terror to this cynical urban crowd, hastened to get clear of the observers. 

“See, little one! Here is your hood,” the conjuror called out, tossing the white drapery. “*Orsù*, bear me no malice.”

Tito pushed vigorously toward the corner of a quieter side-street. Once the abrasive noise of the crowd faded into a dull hum, he spoke in a kind but hurried manner, matching his cadence to something softer. “Now, little one, where were you going? Have you come by yourself to the Festa?”

“Ah, no!” Tessa said, her voice trembling in its rustic cadence. “I have lost my mother in the crowd—her and my father-in-law. They will be angry—he will beat me. It was in the crowd in San Pulinari. I don’t know where they’re gone! Please, don’t leave me!”

She ended with a heavy sob. Tito hurried her along, pausing under the shelter of the Badia cloister. The corners of her pouting mouth went down piteously, her bosom heaving above her green serge gown. As she lifted her holiday apron to catch the hurrying tears, Tito laid his hand on the rough fabric, rubbed one of her tear-stained cheeks, and kissed its baby-like roundness. 

“My poor little Tessa! Leave off crying. Where is your home?”

“I think I’ll go home, if you’ll take me,” she whispered, looking up at him with wide blue eyes. “Beyond Peretola—where the large pear-tree is.”

“Out at which gate, *pazzarella*?”

“Out at the Por del Prato,” she said, holding fast to his arm.

They made for Porta Rossa and on to Ognissanti. When they reached the Piazza d’Ognissanti, they sat down on one of the stone benches to catch their breath. The expansive space offered a brief acoustic respite. 

“It is a long while since you had your breakfast, Tessa,” Tito noted. A nearby vendor’s cart offered a fleeting sanctuary of rural bounty amidst the stone city. Tito, possessing a deep, sensory appreciation for sustenance, approached the stall. He bypassed the overly processed festival sweets and instead selected a handful of apricots—plump, blushing fruits sourced from the sun-baked terraces of the Mugello. Their velvet skins radiated the deep, olfactory warmth of damp earth and summer orchards. Beside them, he purchased rustic cakes composed of sweet chestnut flour and wild honey, ancient sustenance that carried the generational memory of the Tuscan hills, alongside a few artisanal sugar comfits. He placed the fragrant bounty into her apron.

“Come,” he said, “let us walk on to the Prato, and then perhaps you will not be afraid to go the rest of the way alone.”

“But you will have some of the apricots and things,” Tessa said, gathering her apron into a bag for her store.

As they walked, Tessa began to prattle about her agrarian life, her words weaving a tapestry of a world bound by the circular rhythms of the harvest. “We have a garden and plenty of pears,” she explained, her dialect carrying the unadulterated essence of the earth, a cultural memory rapidly eroding within the city walls. “And two cows, besides the mules; and I’m very fond of them. But my father-in-law is a cross man. Nobody speaks kindly to me... just as I talk to my little black-faced kid, because I’m very fond of it.”

To Tessa, Tito was a figure stepped from a myth, a benign entity descended into her harsh reality. They reached the Prato, a large open meadow near the gates, quiet and forsaken at this mid-day hour. On the border of the grass, Tito paused. 

“Now, Tessa, you will not be frightened if I leave you to walk the rest of the way by yourself. Addio!”

Her eyes widened sorrowfully. She looked down at the carefully sourced apricots and cakes in her apron, then up at him. “I thought you would have some, and we could sit down under a tree outside the gate, and eat them together.”

“Tessa, Tessa, you little siren,” Tito laughed, kissing her cheeks. “I ought to have been in the Via de’ Bardi long ago. No! I must go back now. There—I’ll take an apricot. Addio!”

He stepped away. Tessa turned, her face pale, and stepped on. In her despair, she loosened her grip on her apron, and the golden apricots began to roll out onto the grass. 

Tito could not bear the sight of her bursting sob, nor the careless bruising of such perfectly ripened fruit. It offended his profound appreciation for both the girl's tenderness and the earth's bounty. He turned back, kneeling to rescue the fallen apricots, their fragrant skins now dusted with the meadow's pollen. 

“See, my silly one,” he said, wiping the fruit clean. “Come, leave off crying. I will go with you, and we’ll sit down under the tree.”

They found a great, ancient plane-tree just outside the gates. Here, the acoustic landscape transformed entirely. The jarring urban clamor was replaced by the organic, rhythmic rustle of broad leaves and the resonant hum of cicadas—a pristine sonic ecology that soothed the spirit. The mythic occurrence of their meeting seamlessly integrated into the quiet, domestic routine of a shared meal. Tessa leaned her back against the trunk, and the feast was spread upon her lap. 

The taste of the food was profound. The tart skin of the apricot yielded to a honeyed, floral pulp that spoke of deep, agrarian roots, an anchor of sensory truth. Tito stretched opposite her, resting his elbows on the rough green growth, as the sunlight stole through the boughs like a winged thing. 

“You pretty bird,” Tito said, observing her as she eyed the remains of the feast, debating how to preserve the precious chestnut cakes. “To think of anyone scolding you! What sins do you tell of at confession, Tessa?”

“Oh, a great many. I don't like work, and I give the mules the best fodder when nobody sees me... And see, I’ve got a *Breve* here that a good father blessed and gave us all.” She drew a tiny bag from her bosom, an artifact of deeply rooted folk belief mingling naturally with her daily existence. “I think the holy Madonna will take care of me.”

“If they are so cruel to you, Tessa, shouldn’t you like to leave them, and go and live with a beautiful lady who would be kind to you? Or should you like to be my little servant, and live with me?” 

A flush of joy crossed her face. “Will you take me with you now? But I should like to fetch my black-faced kid.”

“Yes, you must go back to your kid, my Tessa,” Tito said, rising. He looked out at the blinding heat. The prospect of the long walk to Via de’ Bardi felt jarring against the acoustic and sensory perfection of the plane-tree’s shade. He harbored an unconquerable aversion to any disruption of physical comfort. 

He lay down on the grass again, resting his head against Tessa’s lap. Within this circular pocket of time, shielded from the linear demands of the city, he soon fell asleep. Tessa sat as quiet as a dove on its nest, gently touching his dark curls, existing in a perfect, momentary paradise where her native existence required no translation.

When Tito finally woke, the long shadows warned him that the sun had done much travel. The illusion of the timeless, multi-generational pastoral was broken. 

“Tessa, little one, you have let me sleep too long,” he said, starting up. “I must lose no more time. Addio.”

Her blue eyes widened, arrested by the sudden shift. To quell the rising tide of her tears, which threatened to disrupt the tranquility he so valued, Tito opened his wallet. He sifted through small coins until he found a tiny, pointed bit of red coral.

“See this pretty little pointed bit of red coral—like your goat’s horn, is it not?” he said softly, navigating the space between his worldly knowledge and her rural cosmology. “Put it on the cord round your neck with your *Breve*, and the evil spirits can’t hurt you. Point this little coral horn at them, and they will run away. Come, undo the cord.”

Tessa obeyed, a tranquilizing sense washing over her that this mythic charm would protect her. The coral was hung beside the scrawled parchment, a talisman of their interwoven timelines. 

“And now you will give me a kiss,” Tito said, sweeping the contents back into his wallet. Tessa put forward her lips, and he pressed her round cheeks, laughing as he gave her a general, impartial kiss. 

He walked away, turning only once to give a parting beck. She was not crying, and that was enough for Tito’s soft nature, which required all sorrow to be hidden away. He hurried back toward Ognissanti, his mind turning toward Romola. Pausing only briefly to partake in a small, meticulously prepared morsel from a vendor to sustain his energy, he crossed the Arno by the Ponte alla Carraja, diving back into the dominant noise of the city, making his way as directly as possible toward the Via de’ Bardi.
