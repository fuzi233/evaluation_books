“And after every single blasted thing I’ve done for her!” wailed Mr. Reginald Cracknell, voice wobbling like a jelly on a bouncy castle, eyes glistening from a potent mix of romantic heartbreak and one too many snifters of his famous top-shelf private moonshine. “After all that, she dumps me flat!”

Sally didn’t waste breath replying. The Flower Garden’s orchestra blared so loud you’d need a foghorn to make yourself heard over the brass section, and she was far too busy doing advanced parkour just to keep her own feet from getting squashed under Mr. Cracknell’s unpredictable, drunken dance steps to pay attention to much else. They jolted and lurched past a table where Miss Mabel Hobson, the club’s newest resident hostess, surveyed the chaos with the icy hauteur of a queen who just spotted a peasant wearing her spare crown. Draped in old gold and black, she looked so regal you half expected the crowd to curtsy as you passed, and poor smitten Reggie let out a sorrowful gurgle as he shambled past her line of sight.

“If I told you what I’ve blown on that woman, you’d call me a liar!” he moaned directly into her ear, stomping hard enough on her ankle to make Sally choke. “Sorry! No idea what my feet are doing tonight… And all because I said that hat she bought was hideous. She hasn’t spoken to me in a week! Won’t even pick up the phone when I ring! And I was right! It was a terrible hat. Looked like a seagull got into a glitter factory and glued itself to a hatbox. But that’s women for you!” he finished glumly.

Sally yelped as his wandering boot came down on her shin before she could yank it out of the way. Reggie misinterpreted the noise as outrage at his sweeping generalization, and gallantly backtracked.

“Not you! You’re different. Spotted that the second I laid eyes on you. You’ve got sympathy. That’s why I’m spilling my whole sad story. You’re the sort of broad who’s got common sense, who gets it. I got her this job here, you wouldn’t believe the number they’re paying her. Once I even put her center stage in a whole Broadway show! You saw those pearls she was wearing around her neck? I bought those! And she won’t even glance my way, all over a stupid hat. I drag myself here every single night—” Sally could confirm that; she’d spotted him camped at the back table every shift, but this was the first time Lee Schoenstein, the suave MC, had dumped him on her as a dance partner. “I dance past her table every ten minutes and she stares right through me. What would you do, eh?” He teared up, pale eyes glistening like wet marbles.

“Honestly? No clue,” Sally said frankly.

“Me neither! Wait, don’t tell a soul, I’ve got a last-ditch plan tonight. You won’t spill, will you? I know you won’t, you’re so sensible… I’m sending her a little gift here tonight. Ought to soften her up, right?”

“Big gift would work faster,” Sally pointed out.

Reggie kicked her shin in a sudden panic, like she’d just handed him the worst possible news.

“Never thought of that! You’re right, but it’s too late now. Still, it might work, right? D’you think?”

“Sure,” Sally said.

“Knew you’d see it my way,” Reggie sighed in relief.

The orchestra cut out with a final bang and brassy flourish, leaving Reggie clapping weakly in the middle of the dancefloor like a confused seal. Sally bolted back to her table, while her ex-partner blinked around like he’d misplaced his car keys and forgotten what they looked like, before zigzagging off to find his own seat. The noise of a hundred overlapping conversations, drowned out all night by the music, roared back twice as loud. The hot, stale air reeked of cigarette smoke and cheap champagne, and Sally pressed her palms to her closed eyes, suddenly remembering she had a headache bad enough to make her consider moving to a remote cave with no brass bands.

Nearly a month had dragged by since she’d gone back to working for Mr. Abrahams. It had been a dull, leaden stretch of days, the sort of monotonous slog that feels like a bad dream you can’t wake up from. She’d barely seen a single familiar face in weeks, none of her old boarding-house pals had crossed her path, Fillmore was obviously avoiding her out of guilty conscience, and Ginger was off doing his vaudeville bit somewhere on Long Island’s south shore.

She lowered her hands and blinked out at the crowd. The Flower Garden was packed, per usual. One of the thousands of new dance clubs that had popped up riding New York’s dance craze wave, and unlike half its rivals that had gone bust after two weeks, it was thriving—Abrahams swore blind it was “a nice place, run nicely,” and the crowd ate that line up. The ads called it a “supper club for post-theater dining and dancing,” bragged it was “spacious, sumptuously appointed, one of the city’s greatest wonders, with an unbeatable dance floor, heavenly music, five-star food and luxury service.” You didn’t even have to meet Abrahams to know he thought this joint was the eighth wonder of the world.

Sally had liked it once, back when she first started working there, found it fun and chaotic and full of silly little bits of entertainment. But back then she didn’t get splitting headaches every night, or that heavy, listless depression that made every shift feel like hauling bricks uphill.

“Miss Nicholas?”

The orchestra, which never shut up for longer than 90 seconds max at the Flower Garden, started up again, and Lee Schoenstein had arrived with her next dance partner. She stood up on autopilot.

“First time I’ve ever stepped foot in this sort of place,” the guy said as they bounced and bumped across the dancefloor. He was huge, clumsy, the sort of man who moves through crowds like a wrecking ball. “Swanky joint! I’m from upstate, we don’t have nothing half this fancy back home.” He plowed a path through the crowd using Sally as a human battering ram, and Sally, who’d thought dancing with Reggie was bad, found herself missing Reggie actively. This man was unequivocally the worst dancer on the entire North American continent.

“Give me old New York, huh!” he said proudly. “Nothing beats it. I’ve seen every show in town since I got here. You catch this year’s Follies?”

“No,” Sally said.

“You gotta go! S’the best thing you’ll ever see. You seen *Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath*?”

“I don’t get to the theater much,” Sally said.

“Go! It’s a scream! I’ve been to a show every single night since I got into the city. All of ‘em great, except last night’s dud. Took a chance, y’know, it was a brand new opening. Thought I could tell all the folks back home I went to a New York opening night! Cost me two seventy-five with tax, and I’d rather have flushed the cash down the toilet. Called it *The Wild Rose*,” he said, sneering like he’d just caught the management trying to sell him a glass of tap water as champagne. “*The Wild Rose*! It sure made me wild, all right. Before the second act even hit halfway, the whole audience was sprinting for the exits, and if someone hadn’t yelled ‘women and children first’ there would’ve been a full stampede.”

Something clicked in Sally’s head. *The Wild Rose*. Oh, of course. That was Gerald’s new play. She’d been dodging the thought of running into him in their shared apartment building for weeks, until she’d spotted a tiny newspaper note saying he was out on tour with a new production.

“Is that Gerald Foster’s play?” she asked quickly.

“Dunno who wrote it, but that guy ought to get ten years hard labor for writing it,” the upstate guy grumbled. “They’ve got guys breaking rocks up at Ossining prison who did way less to deserve a sentence.”

Before she knew it, Sally was back at her table, no memory of how she’d gotten there.

“Miss Nicholas.”

She jumped to her feet, and realized this wasn’t Lee Schoenstein, calling her to another shift through his gold-filled teeth. The man who’d spoken her name had sat down beside her, talking in crisp, precise accents that she recognized instantly. The fog cleared from her eyes, and it was Bruce Carmyle.

“I stopped by your apartment,” Bruce said, lighting a cigarette with what he imagined was the air of a cool, collected man of the world, though his fingers trembled so bad he nearly burned his thumb on the match. “The hall porter told me you worked here, so I came to find you. Hope you don’t mind? I landed in New York tonight, by the way.”

“Tonight?” Sally blinked.

“We docked at ten.” He leaned back, pleased with that little bombshell, and puffed at his cigarette like he didn’t have a single care in the world. In reality he was so over-excited he felt like he was about to vibrate out of his chair. He was a firm believer in being the sort of strong, stoic man who never lets his emotions show, and was very proud of himself for not yelling his head off the second he spotted her across the room. He glanced at her sidelong, and decided she looked prettier than she ever had back in the garden at Monk’s Crofton, that day he’d first spilled his heart out. The ghost of his stuffy old Uncle Donald, who’d ranted at him for hours about not chasing after some American girl across the Atlantic, faded right out of existence the second he looked at her.

“When did you get off the boat?” Sally asked, scrambling for something to say. Her head was all fuzzy, she couldn’t decide if she was glad to see him or not, but mostly… relieved. He was stiff, proper, English, nothing like Reggie the drunk sad sack, or the upstate dancing wreck, and he felt far, far away from this hot, noisy, exhausting place. Looking at him was like sticking your head out a stuffy window to get a breath of cool fresh air.

“I disembarked at ten,” he repeated, turning to face her dead on. “Came straight here.”

Sally fell quiet. She knew exactly what that meant. But she was so tired, so worn out from weeks of this grind, that she couldn’t even summon the energy to fight. She felt like a swimmer who’d been paddling for hours and was ready to just let the tide take them. The heat of the club was pressing down on her like a wet blanket, her nerves were screaming under the blare of the music and the clatter of a hundred conversations.

“Shall we dance?” he asked.

The orchestra had started up the latest overplayed Broadway hit, a creamy, overfamiliar tune Sally had heard a hundred times on repeat.

“Sure,” she said.

Bruce Carmyle was the sort of man who didn’t try anything he wasn’t absolutely sure he could nail perfectly. He’d decided dancing was a required skill for a gentleman years ago, and paid a professional instructor a fortune to make him perfect. Sally, who’d tensed up ready for the usual bump-and-grind dancefloor wrestling match, suddenly found herself gliding across the floor like she was floating. No toes crushed, no one using her to clear a path, he was a better dancer than she was, and for the first time in months her taut, tight nerves unspooled. She half closed her eyes, let the rhythm carry her, and that old feeling of quiet distaste she’d always had for him melted away completely. The barrier that had been between them since they first met was gone.

“Sally!”

His arm tightened around her, she felt the muscles in his arm quiver. She looked up at his face, his dark eyes blazing into hers, and she stumbled a little, caught off guard. He was proposing again, right there, the same words he’d said to her in the soft English spring at Monk’s Crofton, his voice low enough that only she could hear it over the music. She felt a flash of irritation—this was hardly a fair place to corner someone for a life-altering decision—but her brain was too tired to push back.

The music cut off abruptly. The crowd clapped loud enough to make your ears ring, and the orchestra started the song again, but Sally had already turned and was heading back to her table, Bruce trailing right behind her like a lost shadow. Neither of them said a word. Bruce had said his piece, and Sally was staring at nothing, trying to make her fuzzy, tired brain work. Was it worth fighting anymore? What was even the point of struggling? She was tired, right down to her bones. Her eyes burned. The music stopped again, no amount of clapping could get the orchestra to play one more song. The dancefloor emptied, the shuffling of feet died down. The Flower Garden was as quiet as it could ever get. Even the loudest chatterboxes had hushed their voices. Sally closed her eyes, and somewhere near the ceiling, a bird started to sing.

Isadore Abrahams was a man of his word. He named his club the Flower Garden, so he’d done his level best to make a stuffy, overcrowded Broadway dance joint feel like an actual garden. Paper roses strung all over the walls, real tulips in tubs by every pillar, and cages of songbirds hung right from the ceiling. One of them, woken up by the sudden lack of noise, had decided to perform.

Sally had felt bad for those caged birds forever, she’d begged Abrahams a dozen times to let them go, but right then she didn’t think of that bird begging to get out of the noise. To her, sitting there worn thin, fighting with the decision of her life, the song felt cheerful, soft, like a lullaby. And suddenly she could see Monk’s Crofton in her head, green and cool, peaceful under the soft English sun, calling to her like an oasis to a man dying of thirst in the desert.

Then she realized Bruce’s hand was on top of hers, squeezing tight. She looked down, and shivered a little. She’d always hated Bruce’s hands, strong, bony, covered in dark black hair on the back. For months she’d had a gut instinct that the last thing she wanted was those hands touching her. But she didn’t pull away. That vision of the old garden, quiet, no noise, no dancing oafs, no endless shifts, flickered again. A place she could finally rest.

He leaned in, whispering in her ear. The room was hotter than ever, but the bird was singing again, and all she could think was *take me out of this*. Did anything else even matter? How, or where, or who took her, none of it mattered. As long as she got to get out of here.

Monk’s Crofton looked so cool, so green, so quiet.

“All right,” Sally said.

Bruce Carmyle, newly minted accepted suitor, found himself completely stumped. He wasn’t upset that Sally hadn’t thrown her arms around him and squealed with delight—he was more than happy to wait for that warmth to show up. What was bugging him was that he’d somehow just had his big, life-changing proposal in the loudest, tackiest spot in the whole of New York. He’d always imagined proposals happening under moonlight, in quiet, private gardens, not in a overheated dance club with brass music blaring and waiters yelling at each other two feet behind his chair.

The band started up again, but it was a brassy, loud, ribald one-step, the sort of tune that would kill the romantic mood of even the most devoted lover. Couples were bouncing and slamming into each other everywhere he looked, two waiters right behind him had stopped to have a screaming, fast-paced argument in a language that sounded like half Italian menu, half gibberish. There was no way to pull off the soft, tender romantic speech he’d been practicing for months. Bruce, engaged man of the world, was forced to drop straight into boring small talk.

“Good lord, this is noisy,” he grumbled.

“Yeah,” Sally said.

“Is it always this bad?”

“Pretty much.”

“Dreadful racket!”

“Mhm.”

The romantic part of Bruce’s soul wanted to cry. He’d spent weeks daydreaming about this exact moment, after the girl he loved said yes, him murmuring soft, poetic lines to her, making the whole moment perfect. How was a man supposed to murmur anything tender when two waiters were having a verbal brawl right behind his head? He got grumpy fast.

“Do you come here often?”

“Yep.”

“What for?”

“To dance.”

Bruce seethed, completely at a loss. This was supposed to be the greatest moment of his life, and it felt exactly like the night he was 20, at his very first formal ball, stuck behind a potted palm, sweating through his too-tight collar, trying to make small talk with a terrifying woman in a pink ballgown. He’d barely survived that night, and he hated being reminded of it. He shifted in his chair, annoyed, and tried to get back on the romantic track.

“Darling,” he murmured, shifting his chair two feet to the right and leaning far over so only she could hear him. “You have made me so—”

“BATTI BATTI! PRESTO RAVIOLI HOLLANDAISE!” the first waiter shrieked right in his ear.

“LA DONNA E MOBILE SPAGHETTI NAPOLI TETRAZZINA!” the second waiter yelled back, firing back.

“—You have made me so—”

“INFANTA ISABELLA LOPE DE VEGA MULLIGATAWNY TORONTO!” the first waiter fired back, wheezing a little but still going strong.

“—so happy—”

“FUNICULI FUNICULA VINCENTE Y BLASCO IBAÑEZ VERMICELLI RISOTTO!” the second waiter yelled, landing the knockout blow.

Bruce gave up, lit a very moody cigarette. He’d never felt so strongly that this whole situation was completely wrong.
