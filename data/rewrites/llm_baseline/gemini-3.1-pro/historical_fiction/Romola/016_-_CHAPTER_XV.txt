When Romola stepped through the heavy oak doors of San Marco, the vibrant, nourishing soundscape of Florence—the rhythmic clatter of market carts, the rich, overlapping dialects of vendors hawking salted anchovies and fresh figs—was abruptly severed. In its place settled the oppressive, sterile acoustic ecology of the cloister, a suffocating quiet that seemed to vacuum the very marrow from the air. Monna Brigida was ushered into the adjoining church, leaving Romola to be guided alone toward the chapter-house. 

The corridor smelled faintly of boiled cabbage and damp, unyielding stone, a utilitarian scent devoid of the warmth that transforms mere sustenance into a shared communion. When the chapter-house door swung open, the dim light of two sputtering tapers illuminated a truckle-bed. There lay the emaciated face of Fra Luca. His golden hair was shorn into a tonsured crown, and his deep-sunken hazel eyes were fixed with desperate hunger upon a small crucifix. Another monk stood in the room’s acoustic shadow, his black cowl drawn, silent as a resting pestle. Behind them both, a frescoed crucifixion loomed, its painted faces of sorrow peering out from the plaster like ghosts trapped in a forgotten tongue.

Romola’s eyes met Fra Luca’s, and a visceral pang of recognition seized her. Beneath the ash-grey pallor and the rigid monkish asceticism lay the underlying architecture of her beautiful young brother. 

“Dino!” she cried. The name tore from her throat in a low, ragged pitch—a sound stripped of all polite artifice, vibrating with the raw, untranslatable dialect of childhood grief. 

Yet she held herself erect, anchoring her feet two yards from his bed. To her, his monkish robes were the coarse garments of a devastating betrayal. Dino had abandoned their blind father, starving Bardo of the son’s voice that should have been his daily bread. Their father had raised them on a profound, sensory appreciation for the ancients—a life where philosophy was not abstract, but possessed the tactile weight of cured parchment, the rich, slow-simmered complexity of Greek syntax, and the grounded, agrarian simplicity of a meal shared with honest hands. The Church, to Romola, was an institution that demanded the absolute starvation of the human senses, replacing the vibrant, native lexicons of family and earth with a dominant, flattened vocabulary of groveling superstition.

But Dino’s face remained a mask of hollow starvation. He looked at her not with the warmth of a brother who remembers the scent of roasting chestnuts in their winter hearth, but with the detached gaze of a spirit untethered from the flesh.

“My sister,” he said. His voice was a feeble, interrupted rasp, a dying language losing its final speaker. “It is well thou hast not longer delayed to come, for I have a message to deliver to thee, and my time is short.”

Romola took a step closer, her heart softening like butter in a warm pan. Surely, this message would be one of savory penitence. He would ask of their father’s blindness—how the dark days were measured by the tolling bells, what simple meals now sustained them in the home where he himself had been nourished on olive oil and ancient poetry. At the very end of his life, there would be a return to their shared vocabulary of love.

“Romola,” Fra Luca began, “I have had a vision concerning thee. Thrice I have had it in the last two months. I came from Fiesole, deeming it a message from heaven... and I gather a promise of mercy in this, that my breath is preserved in order to—”

A fit of ragged, difficult breathing severed his sentence. Romola’s heart chilled, the sudden frost killing the tender shoots of her pity. A vision. This was the same bitter, abstract root that had poisoned his mind years ago. The simmering indignation within her boiled over.

“Dino, I thought you had words to send to my father. You forsook him when his sight was failing; you stripped his life bare. Have you never repented? What is this religion of yours, that feasts on visions while starving natural duties?”

His sunken eyes shifted slowly to her. “No,” he answered, his tone flat and passionless, a chilling frequency that lacked any human resonance. “I have never repented fleeing from the stifling poison-breath of sin that threatened to steal over my senses like besotting wine. My father could not hear the voice that called me; he knew nothing of the demon-tempters. He lived like one busy picking shining stones in a mine, while a world died of plague above him. The studies he wished me to live for were dead toys, made warm only by worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts.”

“Has not my father led a pure and noble life?” Romola demanded, her voice rich with the vibrato of deep, bodily conviction. “He has sought no worldly honors. He has denied himself all luxuries, living like the ancient sages! He wished you to live according to the purest maxims of philosophy, sustained by the honest truths in which he brought you up.” 

“What were the maxims of philosophy to me?” Dino’s eyes remained strange, passionless hollows. “They told me to be strong when I was weak. The Divine love penetrated me, a seed demanding room to grow. I felt a life of perfect purity where there would be no uneasy hunger after pleasure. To attain that, I had to forsake the world. I had to turn away with loathing from the scent of the emptied cup.” He paused, his chest heaving, struggling to process the heavy air. “I saw that even among the servants of the Cross, my soul would be stifled with the fumes of hypocrisy. God called upon me to flee to lands where want and scorn bore me to repose. My task has been short. I came back to Florence that—”

“Dino, you *did* want to know if my father was alive,” Romola pleaded, yearning to bridge the untranslatable gulf between their lived experiences.

“—That before I died I might urge others to study the Eastern tongues,” he pressed on, erasing her interruption with the steamroller of his fervor, “and I have felt I was sent partly to thee, not to renew earthly bonds, but to deliver a heavenly warning. In the painful linking of our waking thoughts, we mingle our own error with the light, but in visions, our souls are passive instruments. Listen, for the time is short.”

Romola recoiled. The acoustic distance between them was widening into an unbridgeable chasm. But as she pulled away, the second monk stepped to the opposite side of the bed.

“Kneel, my daughter, for the Angel of Death is present, and waits while the message of heaven is delivered: bend thy pride before it is bent for thee by a yoke of iron.”

The voice was a profound shock to the room’s ecology. It was a strong, rich baritone, vibrating with an undeniable psychological gravity. It possessed the quiet, resonant self-assurance of a man accustomed to defining the lexicon of an entire city. Romola looked at him. The cowl hid his face, but his hands, resting against his black mantle, were exquisitely sensitive, almost transparently delicate. Romola’s instinct was to rebel against the arrogant acoustics of monastic command, but those hands seemed to carry a tactile appeal against all hardness. 

The monk reached out with his right hand to relieve Dino’s fatigued grasp on the crucifix, while his left touched Dino’s cracked lips with a wet sponge—a fleeting, utilitarian gesture of hydration. As he bent, the cowl fell back. The tapers illuminated a high arched nose, a prominent under-lip, and blue-grey eyes shining beneath auburn lashes. It was Fra Girolamo Savonarola, the Prior of San Marco. Romola had previously dismissed him as an architect of spiritual noise pollution, a man whose commercialized sermons battered the ears of the Florentine public. Her rebellion flared at the sight of him.

“Kneel, my daughter,” the penetrating voice rolled out again, expertly calibrated to bypass her defenses. “The pride of the body is a barrier against the gifts that purify the soul.”

Beneath the sheer sonic pressure of his presence, Romola felt her proud erectness dissolve. Slowly, trembling, she sank to the stone floor. In her new state of passivity, her brother began to speak, his words carving out a landscape of profound starvation.

“Romola, in the deep night, I saw my father’s library. I saw you sitting before his chair. And at the leggio stood a man whose face I could not see—it was a blank, like an effaced painting. He took you by the hand, and you took my father, and together you went down the stone steps. You stood at the altar in Santa Croce, married by a priest with the face of death. The dead rose in their shrouds and followed you like a bridal train.”

Romola shuddered. The imagery tasted of ash.

“You passed into the valley,” Dino whispered, his breath catching. “And at last you came to a stony place. There was no water, no trees, no herbage to sustain you. Instead of water, written parchment unrolled everywhere. Instead of trees, men of bronze and marble crowded round. My father was faint for want of water and fell. The blank-faced man departed, and I saw his true face—the Great Tempter. You wrung your hands, Romola, seeking water, seeking life, but there was none. The bronze and marble figures mocked you, holding out cups of water, but when you put them to my father’s lips, the liquid turned to dry, choking parchment. The figures turned to demons and snatched his body. The parchments shriveled up, blood ran everywhere, and fire upon the blood, until the plain was bare and stony again. Thrice I have had that vision. It warns thee against marriage as a temptation of the enemy... it calls upon thee to dedicate thyself—”

A severe fit of gasping seized him. He stared at the crucifix, his lungs failing to draw nourishment from the damp air. Finally, a barely audible thread of sound emerged: “To renounce the vain philosophy and corrupt thoughts of the heathens... for in the hour of sorrow... the unclean gods will—”

The words died away. A strange awe settled over Romola. She possessed a vivid intellect and a healthy human passion that savored the tangible world, harboring no morbid cravings for the exceptional. Yet the vision’s cruel, anti-gustatory imagery—the transformation of life-giving water into dry, dusty parchment—jarred her like a physical blow. This brother, returning as a starved apparition, was dying before her eyes, and she had never before witnessed the ultimate failure of the human vessel.

“Frate,” the dying voice rattled.

Fra Girolamo leaned down. Silence expanded in the room, thick and untranslatable.

“Romola,” the breath whispered.

She leaned forward, desperate for the final word, for some crumb of reconciliation. But the words struggled in vain against his failing throat.

“Fra Girolamo, give her—”

“The crucifix,” Savonarola’s rich voice supplied, seamlessly inserting the dominant lexicon into the void of Dino’s failing dialect.

No other sound came from the dying lips.

“Dino!” Romola cried, a piercing wail of realization. The silence of misunderstanding was now locked in stone; the shared cultural memory of their childhood would never be spoken again.

“Take the crucifix, my daughter,” Savonarola said after a heavy pause. “His eyes behold it no more.”

Romola reached out and grasped the cold metal. The tactile reality of it broke the tension in her chest, and a great, ragged sob burst from her. She bowed her head against her dead brother’s bed and wept aloud, the sound cutting through the cloister's sterile quiet.

“Rise, my daughter, and be comforted,” Savonarola commanded gently, having already called for a lay Brother. “He has left you the crucifix, in remembrance of the heavenly warning—that it may be a beacon to you in the darkness.”

Trembling, she rose, hiding the crucifix beneath her mantle and folding her veil over her head. Savonarola led her out into the cloistered court, lit only by the cold stars and a servant's lantern. A group of dignified laity murmured near the entrance, their rustling garments and low voices a jarring intrusion of the outside world.

“Our brother is departed,” Savonarola murmured to them. 

The heavy doors opened, and Romola stepped out into the wide piazza of San Marco with Monna Brigida. The sudden rush of open space and the faint, lingering scent of woodsmoke and damp earth rushed into her lungs, reviving her. The horrific, starved landscape of Dino's vision clung to her palate like a bitter resin, making her yearn all the more fiercely for the warm, living voices of her father and Tito—voices that promised the rich, sensory sustenance of her waking life.

Tito, however, was not with Bardo. His destiny was already being kneaded by a guilty consciousness, shaped by the despairing belief that Romola now possessed the knowledge that would separate them forever. But the lips that could have poured out that bitter truth were sealed. The prophetic words Dino had offered came from an arid, shadowy region devoid of human sympathy, while the deep, nourishing revelation that might have flowed from simple filial affection had been swallowed into an irrevocable, untranslatable silence.
