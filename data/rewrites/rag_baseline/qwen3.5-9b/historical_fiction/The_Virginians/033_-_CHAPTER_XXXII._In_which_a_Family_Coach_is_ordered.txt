The air inside the Oakhurst parlor shifted, a subtle pressure change preceding the storm of anticipation. The secret Mr. Lambert had whispered to his wife—the destination of Tunbridge Wells—hung suspended, dissolving the tension of the previous days. With the hay secured and the corn untouched, the farm horses were free. The logic was simple, almost arithmetic: if the beasts were idle, the family might move.

"Put them in the coach," Colonel Lambert proposed, the words landing with the weight of a decree. "We shall all go."

The reaction was immediate and visceral. Mamma embraced the idea, her posture softening into relief. The children leaped, a chaotic burst of kinetic energy. The girls vanished instantly, a whirlwind of fabric and motion, scouring the presses for their finest garments. Calamancoes, paduasoys, falbalas, furbelows—they emerged from the dark recesses of the wardrobe, items reserved for solemn occasions, now repurposed for a journey. Mamma's robes, usually retired to lavender and silence, were laid out. The Colonel produced his laced hat, his waistcoat, and the silver-hilted hanger, the metal gleaming under the lamplight. Charley donned a rasee holiday suit, a garment worn by his father during his own marriage, which Mrs. Lambert cut up later with a pang of loss. Even the animals participated in the ritual; Ball and Dumpling had their tails and manes bound with ribbon. Chump, the old white cart-horse, was designated the unicorn leader, tasked with guiding the carriage-horses up the initial incline from Oakhurst to Westerham.

The coach itself was an artifact of history, an ancient vessel rumored to have transported George I from Greenwich to London upon his accession. It had belonged to Mr. Lambert's father, and for generations, the family had regarded it as one of the most splendid conveyances in the three kingdoms. Brian, the coachman—and ploughman—occupied the box, flanked by Mr. Charley. The precious clothes were stacked in imperials atop the roof. The Colonel's pistols found their place in the carriage pockets, while the blunderbuss hung behind the box, within reach of Brian, a veteran of the old wars.

No highwayman harassed the convoy. No innkeeper levied contributions. Colonel Lambert, with his slender purse and large family, was beyond the reach of such depredators. A modest lodging had been arranged by young Colonel Wolfe at his customary house, a location he accompanied them to on horseback.

These lodgings lay opposite Madame Bernstein's establishment. As the Oakhurst family settled into their quarters on a Saturday evening, the scene outside their window unfolded with a rhythmic, almost hypnotic regularity. Chair after chair discharged powdered beaux and patched beauties onto the pavement before the Baroness's door. She held one of her many card-parties. The sun had not yet fully surrendered the sky; the ancestors of this era began their dissipations early, indulging in meat, drink, or cards anytime after three in the afternoon until the break of dawn. From their vantage point, the young country ladies and their mother watched the stream of personages entering the Bernstein rout.

Colonel Wolfe identified the arrivals for the girls. It was nearly as delightful as attending the party itself. Hetty and Theo observed not only the guests arriving but peered through the Baroness's open casements, watching the figures settle within. Among the passing faces was the Duchess of Queensberry. When Mr. Wolfe explained her identity, Martin Lambert was quick to recite a score of lines from his favorite Mat Prior regarding "Kitty, beautiful and young."

"Think that that old lady was once like you, girls!" the Colonel cried.

"Like us, papa?" Miss Hetty tossed her head, defiant. "Well, certainly we never set up for being beauties!"

"Yes, like you, you little baggage; like you at this moment, who want to go to that drum yonder:"

*"Inflamed with rage at sad restraint Which wise mamma ordained, And sorely vexed to play the saint Whilst wit and beauty reigned."*

"We were never invited, papa," the satirist of the family interjected. "And I am sure if there's no beauty more worth seeing than that, the wit can't be much worth the hearing."

"Oh, but he's a rare poet, Mat Prior!" the Colonel continued, ignoring the interruption. "Though, mind you, girls, you'll skip over all the poems I have marked with a cross. A rare poet! And to think you should see one of his heroines! 'Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way' (she always will, Mrs. Lambert!)"

"I am sure it must have been very inflammable," Mamma remarked.

"So it was, my dear, twenty years ago, much more inflammable than it is now," the Colonel noted.

"Nonsense, Mr. Lambert," Mamma answered sharply.

"Look, look!" Hetty cried, running forward to point at the little square and the covered gallery where the door to Madame Bernstein's apartments stood. A crowd of street urchins, idlers, and yokels surrounded it, watching the company.

"It's Harry Warrington!" Theo exclaimed, waving a handkerchief. But Warrington did not see Miss Lambert. The Virginian walked arm-in-arm with a portly clergyman in a crisp, rustling silk gown, disappearing into Madame de Bernstein's door.

"I heard him preach a most admirable sermon here last Sunday," Mr. Wolfe said. "A little theatrical, but most striking and eloquent."

"You seem to be here most Sundays, James," Mrs. Lambert observed.

"And Monday, and soon till Saturday," the Colonel added. "See, Harry has beautified himself already; hath his hair in buckle, and I have no doubt is going to the drum too."

"I had rather sit quiet generally of a Saturday evening," said sober Mr. Wolfe. "At any rate, away from card-playing and scandal; but I own, dear Mrs. Lambert, I am under orders. Shall I go across the way and send Mr. Warrington to you?"

"No, let him have his sport. We shall see him tomorrow. He won't care to be disturbed amidst his fine folks by us country-people," said meek Mrs. Lambert.

"I am glad he is with a clergyman who preaches so well," Theo murmured, her eyes suggesting, *You see, good people, he is not so bad as you thought him.* "The clergyman has a very kind, handsome face."

"Here comes a greater clergyman," Mr. Wolfe announced. "It is my Lord of Salisbury, with his blue ribbon, and a chaplain behind him."

"And whom a mercy's name have we here?" Mrs. Lambert broke in as a sedan-chair, covered with gilding and topped with no less than five earl's coronets, approached the door. Carried by bearers in richly laced clothes and preceded by three footmen in matching splendor, it halted before the Baroness's entrance. The Bishop, about to enter, stopped, ran back with respectful bows and curtseys to the sedan-chair, offering his hand to the lady who stepped forth.

"Who on earth is this?" Mrs. Lambert asked.

"Sprechen sie Deutsch? Ja, meinherr. Nichts verstand," the waggish Colonel replied.

"Pooh, Martin."

"Well, if you can't understand High Dutch, my love, how can I help it? Your education was neglected at school. Can you understand heraldry? I know you can."

"I make," Charley cried, reciting the shield, "three merions on a field or, with an earl's coronet."

"A countess's coronet, my son. The Countess of Yarmouth, my son."

"And pray who is she?"

"It hath ever been the custom of our sovereigns to advance persons of distinction to honour," the Colonel continued gravely. "And this eminent lady hath been so promoted by our gracious monarch, to the rank of Countess of this kingdom."

"But why, papa?" the daughters asked together.

"Never mind, girls!" Mamma said.

But the incorrigible Colonel persisted.

"Y, my children, is one of the last and the most awkward letters of the whole alphabet. When I tell you stories, you are always saying Why. Why should my Lord Bishop be cringing to that lady? Look at him rubbing his fat hands together, and smiling into her face! It's not a handsome face any longer. It is all painted red and white like Scaramouch's in the pantomime. See, there comes another blue-ribbon, as I live. My Lord Bamborough. The descendant of the Hotspurs. The proudest man in England. He stops, he bows, he smiles; he is hat in hand, too. See, she taps him with her fan. Get away, you crowd of little blackguard boys, and don't tread on the robe of the lady whom the King delights to honour."

"But why does the King honour her?" the girls asked once more.

"There goes that odious last letter but one! Did you ever hear of her Grace the Duchess of Kendal? No. Of the Duchess of Portsmouth? Non plus. Of the Duchess of La Valliore? Of Fair Rosamond, then?"
