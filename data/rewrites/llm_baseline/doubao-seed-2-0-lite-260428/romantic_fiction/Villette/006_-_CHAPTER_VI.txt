The first of March dawned soft and smudged with fog, and when I woke, rose, and drew back my bed curtains, I found the risen sun fighting hard to pierce the mist. Above my head, above the gabled house-tops, nearly level with the low-hanging clouds, hung a solemn, rounded mass of dim dark blue: THE DOME of St Paul’s. As I stared, something long dormant in my chest shifted; the spirit that had dragged its fettered wings for years half-loosened them, and a sudden, bright feeling swept over me— that I, who had never truly lived a life of my own, was finally, finally about to taste it. My soul grew that morning as fast as Jonah’s gourd, green, sudden, and bursting with new possibility.

“I did well to come,” I murmured, dressing quickly and with more care than I had brought to my appearance in years. “I love the pulse of this great London thrumming all around me. What coward would waste their whole existence in tiny hamlets, letting their wits and wills rust away in obscurity?”

When I descended the stairs, I was not travel-worn or haggard, but tidy, rested, and light-footed. When the grey-haired elderly waiter brought my breakfast, I spoke to him calmly, cheerfully, and we talked for ten minutes that left us usefully well-acquainted. He had worked at the inn for twenty years, he told me, and when I mentioned the names of my uncles Charles and Wilmot, who had been frequent visitors fifteen years prior, he recalled them clearly and with great respect. Once I had shared that family connection, my standing in his eyes was settled and secure: he said I bore a strong likeness to Uncle Charles, a compliment I could not doubt, for Mrs. Barrett had often said the very same. His previously uncertain, distant manner softened into ready, obliging courtesy, and I knew I would never struggle to get a kind, prompt answer to any reasonable question I might ask.

The street outside my small sitting-room window was narrow, quiet, far from squalid, and the few passers-by moved at the same unhurried pace one might see in any provincial town. There was nothing daunting here: I felt sure I could venture out alone without fear.

After breakfast, I stepped out, elation bubbling light in my chest. To walk unescorted through London felt like a grand adventure all its own. Before long I found myself in Paternoster Row, that storied stretch of booksellers, and stepped into Mr. Jones’s shop. I splurged on a tiny volume, a little extravagance I could barely afford, but I thought I would send it one day to Mrs. Barrett as a small token of my affection. The dried, sharp man of business behind the desk felt like one of the most important people in the world, and I felt like one of the happiest.

That morning I lived more fully than I had in all the prior years of my quiet, staid life. I reached St. Paul’s, stepped inside, and climbed all the way up to the dome, where I looked out over the whole spread of London: the silver curve of the Thames, its bridges, its spired churches, old Westminster, and the green sun-dappled stretch of Temple Gardens, all laid out under a soft early spring blue sky veiled by thin, wispy haze.

I wandered aimlessly after that, still high on freedom and joy, until I found myself right in the thrumming heart of the city. I had found real London at last: I walked the Strand, climbed Cornhill, mingled with the crowds hurrying past, dared the chaos of busy street crossings all on my own. It was an irrational, unaccountable pleasure, but a sharp, real one. In later years I would see the West End, its parks and its grand squares, but I will always love the working city far better. It is earnest, alive: its rush, its roar, its industry are not idle amusements. The city is earning its living, while the West End only plays at its pleasure. You might be entertained in the grand squares, but in the heart of the city you feel alive, stung awake by excitement.

By two o’clock I was faint and hungry— a deep, healthy hunger I had not felt in years— and I returned to my dark, old, quiet inn. I dined on a plain roast joint and vegetables, both of which tasted far better than the tiny, dainty little meals Miss Marchmont’s cook used to send up for my kind dead mistress and me, neither of whom ever had half an appetite enough to finish them. Delightfully tired, I lay back across three hard chairs, for my small room had no sofa, and slept for an hour before waking to think long and deep for two more.

Every circumstance of my life right then pushed me to take a new, resolute, daring course— perhaps even a desperate one. I had nothing to lose. My utter loathing of the empty, desolate life I had left behind forbade me from ever turning back. If I failed in the plan I was about to form, who but myself would suffer? If I died far from England, the only land I had ever called anything close to home, who would weep for me?

I was well used to hardship, I told myself. Death held none of the terrible terrors for me that it holds for the gently reared and coddled. I had long looked on the idea of death with quiet, unflinching eyes. Prepared for whatever consequences might come, I laid my plans.

That very evening, my new friend the waiter told me all he knew about ships sailing for the continental port of Boue-Marine. There was no time to waste: I must take my berth that very night. I could have waited till morning to board, but I would not risk missing the ship.

“Best go claim your berth right now, ma’am,” he advised. I agreed, settled my bill, and tipped him a sum I now know was absurdly generous, so much so that he smiled a faint, knowing little smile as he pocketed the cash, quietly judging my lack of worldly sense. He called a coach, recommended me to the driver with strict instructions not to leave me to the mercy of the rough watermen, but the man broke his promise the second we reached the wharf: he set me down right in the middle of a huddle of jostling watermen, took his fare, and drove off at once.

It was an uncomfortable, dangerous moment. The night was dark, the river wharves reeked, and the watermen began fighting each other over my trunk and over me, their oaths loud and coarse enough to shake the steady calm I had held till then. One man seized my trunk, and I waited quiet, but when another laid a hand on my arm, I spoke up sharply, shook off his grip, stepped straight into the nearest empty boat, and told the man who owned it to set my trunk down beside me on the bench. He did it at once, for I was now his fare, and he pushed off into the black water before any of the other men could protest.

The river was as dark as a torrent of ink, lights glinting off its surface from the surrounding warehouses, ships rocking soft on its breast as we glided past. We rowed past *The Ocean*, *The Phoenix*, *The Consort*, *The Dolphin*, checking each name painted in great white letters on the dark hull under the lantern light. My ship, *The Vivid*, lay further down the sable flood.

I thought of the Styx, of Charon ferrying solitary souls to the land of shades, as the cold wind bit my face and a fine midnight rain began to fall, the two rude oarsmen beside me still swearing under their breath. But I was not wretched, not terrified. I had been far more miserable, far more afraid, in far safer, cozier rooms back in England. I was only alert, bright, alive.

At last *The Vivid* loomed up, white and bright against the black night. “Here you are,” the waterman said, and instantly demanded six shillings for the fare.

“You ask far too much,” I said. He pulled back from the ship’s side and swore he would not bring me aboard till I paid. A young man I would later learn was the steward leaned over the rail, grinning in anticipation of the fight, so I paid the money at once to disappoint him. I had overpaid three times that afternoon, handing out crowns where I ought to have given shillings, but I consoled myself: this is the price of experience.

“They’ve cheated you!” the steward cackled exultantly when I climbed aboard. I told him calmly that I knew that already, and made my way below.

A stout, handsome, showy woman stood in the ladies’ cabin, and when I asked to be shown my berth, she stared hard at me, muttering that it was most unusual for a passenger to turn up in the middle of the night, clearly inclined to be unkind. Her face was comely, yes, but stamped all over with insolence and selfishness.

“I am on board now, and I am certainly staying,” I said firmly. “Please show me my berth.”

She complied, sullenly. I took off my bonnet, arranged my few belongings, and lay down. I had won a small victory, pushed through a string of small difficulties, and my homeless, unsupported mind could rest for a little while. No further action would be required of me till we docked at Boue-Marine. I dared not think past that point, and exhausted, I drifted into a half-trance.

The stewardess talked the whole night through, not to me, but to the young steward, her son, who looked exactly like her. He wandered in and out of the cabin constantly, and the pair quarrelled, made up, and quarrelled again twenty times over the course of the dark hours. She said she was writing a letter home to her father, reading snippets out loud as if I were no more than a wooden post, recounting family secrets about her younger sister Charlotte, who was on the brink of making a romantic, imprudent match she was determined to stop. Her dutiful son laughed her whole letter to scorn, and she raved at him in response. She was a strange pair, nearly forty years old but buxom and blooming as a girl of twenty, loud, vain, vulgar, hard as brass. She talked as dawn neared about the Watsons, a wealthy family of passengers she adored for the generous fees they always gave her, saying a crossing with the Watsons was as good as a small fortune for her.

At dawn everyone stirred, and by full sunrise the rest of the passengers came aboard. The stewardess greeted the Watson family with boisterous excitement, making a great fuss over the four of them: two men, two women, both young women richly and gaily dressed in velvet cloaks, silk gowns, bonnets wreathed in bright flowers far better suited for a park promenade than a damp packet ship deck. The men were short, plain, fat, and vulgar, and I soon learned the oldest, greasiest, broadest man was the husband of the beautiful young girl. I was stunned to see that instead of looking miserable trapped in such a union, she was giddy to the point of recklessness, laughing loud and bright. I thought her laughter must be the frenzy of despair, but she tripped over to me, a total stranger, holding a camp stool, and offered it to me with a light, careless smile, showing off a perfect set of white teeth. I declined politely, and she danced off again, light as a feather. The man she had married looked as much like an oil barrel as a man.

The other passenger, escorted by a gentle, languid-looking gentleman, was a pretty, fair young girl dressed in the plainest of print dresses, untrimmed straw bonnet, and large soft shawl, simple enough to suit a Quaker, but graceful on her. Before the gentleman left her to return to shore, he scanned all the passengers carefully, clearly assessing the company his ward would be left in, looked askance at the flaunting Watsons, glanced at me, and murmured something to the girl, who curled her pretty short lip slightly in my direction. It could have been me, or my plain mourning dress, that earned her contempt— likely both. A bell rang, he kissed her goodbye, and the ship set sail.

Foreigners often marvel at the daring of English parents who trust their daughters to travel alone, calling their intrepidity unladylike and improper. Whether this particular girl was suited to unchaperoned travel I could not say then, but I soon learned the dignity of solitude did not suit her at all. She paced the deck twice, staring in disdain at the loud, laughing Watsons, before she came over to speak to me.

“Do you like sea voyages?” she asked.

I told her my fondness for the sea had yet to be tested, for I had never been on a voyage before.

“Oh, how charming!” she cried. “I envy you the novelty! First impressions are always so delightful. I have crossed the Channel ten times now, I can barely remember my first voyage— I am quite blasé about the whole thing.”

I smiled, and she huffed, half testy, half frank. “Why are you laughing at me?”

“Because you are far too young to be blasé about anything,” I said. “You barely look sixteen.”

“I am seventeen!” she said, piqued. “Do you like travelling alone?”

“Bah! I don’t mind it. I never stay alone long, I always make friends. You won’t make many friends on this voyage, will you?” I said, glancing at the loud, vulgar Watsons.

“Certainly not those horrid people! They ought to be in steerage. Are you going to school?”

“No.”

“Where are you going, then?”

“I have not the faintest idea past the port of Boue-Marine,” I told her. She stared, then rambled on, introducing herself as Ginevra Fanshawe. She was on her way to a school in Villette, the capital of Labassecour, listing all the schools she had attended across Europe, gushing about how many handsome students there had been in Bonn, where every nice girl in her school had an admirer who walked the promenade to pass them and murmur “schönes mädchen.” She confessed she was shockingly uneducated, could barely read or write French or German, knew almost no history, geography, or arithmetic, could not even tell the difference between Protestantism and Catholicism, and did not care to.

“Where are you going, really?” she asked, turning back to me.

“Wherever Fate leads. I intend to earn my living wherever I can find work.”

“Earn your living?” she gasped, horrified. “Are you poor then?”

“As poor as Job,” I said. She paused for a moment, then shrugged, saying she knew what poverty was like at home, with her half-pay captain father and her nine siblings, their only support her wealthy godfather M. de Bassompierre who paid for her schooling. Her older sister Augusta had already married a much older man who had survived yellow fever in India and was rich enough to give her a carriage and a fine house, and Ginevra fully expected to marry an elderly wealthy gentleman one day too, far better than “earning a living.”

She soon admitted she would be horribly seasick the second we cleared the harbour, and tripped below to bully the rude stewardess into waiting on her hand and foot. The deck was quiet after that, and I stayed out in the sea breeze, drinking in the sharp, sweet air, watching the waves heave, the gulls glide over their crests, the white sails far off on the dark blue distance. I daydreamed of the continent stretched far ahead of me, a wide dreamland of sunlit coast, clustered towns, snow-gleaming towers, deep woods and smooth pastures, with a great arch of rainbow hope striding across the solemn dark blue sky above it. I soon had to laugh at my own fancy: daydreams are delusions of the demon, after all, and I was suddenly seized with a violent fit of seasickness that made me stagger down to the cabin.

Ginevra’s berth was right next to mine, and she tormented me the whole rough crossing, her selfish impatience and peevishness making her far harder to bear than the Watsons, who were all violently sick but stoic in their misery. I finally snapped at her to hold her tongue, and the rebuke did her good: she stopped complaining, and even seemed to like me better for it. As full dark fell the sea roughened, great waves crashing against the hull, furniture tumbling across the cabin floor till it had to be lashed down, every passenger groaning in distress. Ginevra swore she was about to die, but the stewardess laughed and told her they were nearly in port. A quarter of an hour later, the ship went still, and the voyage ended at midnight.

I was sorry to dock. My little rest was over, my hard difficulties were starting again. The cold dark air hit my face when I stepped on deck, the lights of the foreign port glimmering across the harbour like a thousand strange, unknowing eyes. Crowds of friends rushed aboard to greet the Watsons, Ginevra’s whole large family swarmed up to carry her away, and I was left alone on the deck, no one to meet me, no one to welcome me. I dared not dwell on the sharp contrast between my position and theirs.

But I had to go somewhere, and necessity could not afford to be fussy. I gave the stewardess her fee, a sum that clearly shocked her far more generous than she had expected, and asked her to direct me to a quiet, respectable inn for the night. Not only did she give me clear directions, she called a commissionaire, and told him to take good care of me— though my trunk had already been sent off to the custom-house, to be claimed later.
