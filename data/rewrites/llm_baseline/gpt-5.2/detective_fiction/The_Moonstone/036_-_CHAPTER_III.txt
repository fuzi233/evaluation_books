The one man at our host’s table who carried with him, as naturally as another gentleman carries his watch-chain, the sense of distant places and hidden dangers, was Mr. Murthwaite. London had made a show of him on his return—as it always makes a show of any traveller who has escaped alive from countries where the map turns vague and the air turns murderous. He had announced, with that calm improvidence which society calls courage, that he meant to go back again, and press farther in—into regions left, by common consent, to the silence of their own mountains.

It is not an everyday thing to dine with a celebrated personage and to have, at the back of one’s mind, the perfectly reasonable expectation that the next public mention of him may be in the form of an obituary, or a rumour of violent death. The idea did him no injustice; it was only the tribute which civilised life pays to a man who repeatedly stakes it against the unknown.

When the ladies withdrew, and the servants had closed the door, the room altered at once—as rooms do. The talk fell, by that English inevitability which needs no more prompting than a decanter, into politics. The voices grew broader; the opinions grew louder; and the very knives and glasses seemed to take on a sharper ring, as if they too were enlisted under party colours.

To me, politics has always been the thinnest soup on the table: much heat, little nourishment. I glanced at Murthwaite when the wine had made its first circuit. He was performing, with admirable consideration for our host, the difficult feat of listening without hearing. His eyelids had the look of shutters half-drawn against a dreary street-noise; he was settling himself, inch by inch, into that genial doze which good claret encourages and bad discussion compels.

It occurred to me that there was one subject in the world which might rouse him: a subject in which the marvellous and the practical had been mixed together, like spice in ordinary bread, until no man could separate them. I leaned towards him.

“If I am not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite, you knew the late Lady Verinder, and took some interest in the strange succession of events which ended in the loss of the Moonstone?”

He woke as if the words had been a bell sounded close to his ear.

“Who are you, sir?” he asked.

I told him—my name, my profession, and my past connection with the Herncastle family; not forgetting the odd position I had once held in relation to the Colonel and his Diamond. At that, Murthwaite shifted his chair a little, so that the Conservatives and Liberals—equally monotonous at the moment—were left behind his shoulder, and he could give his whole attention to me.

“Have you heard anything lately,” he said, “of the Indians?”

“I have every reason to believe,” I answered, “that one of them had an interview with me, in my office, yesterday.”

I had thought him hard to astonish. He stared at me now as if, for once, the improbable had forced its way into his experience. I related, exactly, what had happened to Mr. Luker, and what had happened to myself.

“It is clear the Indian’s parting inquiry had an object,” I concluded. “Why should he be so anxious to know the time at which a borrower of money is usually privileged to pay the money back?”

“Is it possible you don’t see his motive, Mr. Bruff?”

“I am ashamed of my stupidity, Mr. Murthwaite—but I certainly don’t see it.”

He looked at me with that quiet relish for clearness which belongs to men who have lived among dangers where a mistake means a grave. Then, very patiently, he began to probe my dulness to the bottom.

“Let me ask you one question. In what position does the conspiracy to seize the Moonstone now stand?”

“I can’t say,” I answered. “The Indian plot is a mystery to me.”

“The Indian plot can only be a mystery to you,” he said, “because you have never seriously examined it. Shall we run it over together—from the time when you drew Colonel Herncastle’s Will, to the time when the Indian called at your office? In your position it may be of very serious importance to Miss Verinder’s interests, that you should take a clear view of this matter in case of need. Tell me—will you penetrate the Indian’s motive for yourself? or do you wish me to save you the trouble?”

There was no mistaking the practical kindness under his tone. I saw his purpose at once, and chose the first alternative.

“Very good,” he said. “We will begin with the ages of the three Indians. I can testify they all look much about the same age—and you can judge whether the man you saw was, or was not, in the prime of life. Not forty, you think? My idea too. We will say not forty.

“Now look back to the time when Colonel Herncastle came to England, and when you were concerned in the plan he adopted to preserve his life. I don’t want you to count the years. It is clear that these present Indians, at their age, must be the successors of three other Indians—high-caste Brahmins, all of them, when they left their own country—who followed the Colonel to these shores.

“These present men have succeeded to the men who were here before them. If they had only done that, the matter would not be worth inquiry. But they have done more: they have succeeded to the organisation their predecessors established in this country. Don’t start. It is a trumpery affair, according to our notions. Reckon it as including command of money; the services, when needed, of that shady sort of Englishman who lives in the byways of foreign life in London; and, lastly, the secret sympathy of such few men of their own country—and formerly, at least, of their own religion—as happen to be employed in ministering to some of the multitudinous wants of this great city. Nothing formidable. But worth notice at starting; we may have occasion to refer to it.

“Now I ask you a question, and I expect your experience to answer it. What event gave the Indians their first chance of seizing the Diamond?”

“The first chance,” I said, “was clearly offered to them by Colonel Herncastle’s death. They would be aware of his death, I suppose?”

“As a matter of course. And his death gave them their first chance. Up to that time the Moonstone was safe in the strongroom of the bank. You drew the Colonel’s Will leaving the jewel to his niece; and the Will was proved. As a lawyer, you know what course the Indians would take—under English advice—after that.”

“They would procure a copy of the Will from Doctors’ Commons.”

“Exactly. One of those English intermediaries would get them the copy. It would inform them that the Moonstone was bequeathed to Lady Verinder’s daughter, and that Mr. Blake the elder, or a person appointed by him, was to place it in her hands. Information about Lady Verinder and Mr. Blake would be easy to obtain.

“The only difficulty for the Indians would be whether to attempt the Diamond in its removal from the bank, or wait until it was taken down to Yorkshire to Lady Verinder’s house. The second way is the safer way—and there is your explanation of the Indians at Frizinghall, disguised as jugglers, waiting their time.

“In London, they had their organisation to keep them informed. Two men would do it: one to follow anybody who went from Mr. Blake’s house to the bank; and one to treat the lower servants with beer and hear the house-news. Those precautions would tell them Mr. Franklin Blake had been to the bank, and that Mr. Franklin Blake was the only person going to Lady Verinder. What followed on that discovery you remember.”

I did remember. Franklin Blake had detected one of the spies; he had hastened his journey northward; and, by old Betteredge’s sensible counsel, had placed the Diamond in the bank at Frizinghall before the Indians were ready even to suspect him in the neighbourhood.

But one question had always troubled me. The Indians, ignorant of those precautions, must have supposed the Diamond was in the house—why did they make no attempt on Lady Verinder’s home during the whole interval before Miss Verinder’s birthday? I put the difficulty plainly, adding that I knew of the boy and the drop of ink and all the rest, and that any explanation founded on clairvoyance would carry no weight with me.

“Nor with me,” Murthwaite said. “The clairvoyance here is merely the romantic side of the Indian character. It refreshes and encourages those men—quite inconceivable, I grant you, to the English mind—to surround their perilous errand with a halo of the marvellous. Their boy is unquestionably sensitive to mesmeric influence; and, under it, he has reflected what was already in the mind of the person mesmerising him. I have tested clairvoyance and never found it go beyond that. The Indians do not investigate so; they believe their boy a Seer. In that marvel they find a new interest in the purpose that unites them.

“But we have nothing to do with mesmerism or anything hard of belief. My object is to trace results, by rational means, to natural causes. Have I satisfied you so far?”

“Not a doubt of it. But I am waiting, with some anxiety, for the rational explanation of my difficulty.”

He smiled, as if the thing I feared most was the easiest for him to lift.

“It is the easiest difficulty of all. Admit your statement: the Indians were not aware of what Mr. Franklin Blake had done with the Diamond—for we find them making their first mistake on the first night of his arrival.”

“Their first mistake?”

“The mistake of letting themselves be surprised, lurking on the terrace at night, by Gabriel Betteredge. However, they had the merit of seeing they had taken a false step—for, with plenty of time, they never came near the house for weeks afterwards.”

“Why? That is what I want to know.”

“Because no Indian ever runs an unnecessary risk. The clause you drew in the Colonel’s Will informed them that the Moonstone was to pass absolutely into Miss Verinder’s possession on her birthday. Now tell me—what was the safest course? To attempt the Diamond while it was under the control of Mr. Franklin Blake, who had already shown he could suspect and outwit them? Or to wait till the Diamond was at the disposal of a young girl, who would innocently delight in wearing a magnificent jewel at every opportunity?

“Want proof? Take their own conduct. They appeared after waiting all those weeks on Miss Verinder’s birthday, and were rewarded for their patience by seeing the Moonstone in the bosom of her dress. When I heard the story later that evening, I felt sure what risk Mr. Franklin Blake had run—they would certainly have attacked him had he not ridden back with other people—and I was convinced an even worse risk was in store for Miss Verinder. I recommended following the Colonel’s plan, destroying the identity of the gem by cutting it into separate stones. How its extraordinary disappearance that night made my advice useless, defeated the Hindoo plot, and how all further action by the Indians was paralysed next day by their confinement as rogues and vagabonds—you know as well as I do. The first act in the conspiracy closes there. Have I met your difficulty?”

I could not deny it. His knowledge of Indian character, and his having no pile of other wills clouding his memory, gave him an advantage I was obliged to admit.

“So far, so good,” he resumed. “The first chance was lost when they were committed to prison at Frizinghall. When did the second chance offer itself? While they were still in confinement—as I can prove.”

He drew out his pocket-book and opened it at a certain page.

“I was staying with friends at Frizinghall at the time. A day or two before the Indians were set free—on a Monday, I think—the governor came to me with a letter. It had been left for the Indians by Mrs. Macann, from whom they had hired their lodging; and it had arrived by ordinary post the morning before. The prison authorities noticed the postmark ‘Lambeth,’ and that the direction, though in correct English, was oddly unlike the customary method. On opening it they found a foreign language, which they guessed—rightly—was Hindustani. They came to me to translate it. I copied the original and the translation into my pocket-book.”

He handed it to me. The address had been copied first, all in one unpunctuated paragraph:

“To the three Indian men living with the lady called Macann at Frizinghall in Yorkshire.”

Below were the Hindoo characters. At the end came his English rendering, brief and uncanny:

“In the name of the Regent of the Night, whose seat is on the Antelope, whose arms embrace the four corners of the earth.

“Brothers, turn your faces to the south, and come to me in the street of many noises, which leads down to the muddy river.

“The reason is this.

“My own eyes have seen it.”

There was neither date nor signature.

I handed back the book, confessing that the letter puzzled me.

“I can explain the first sentence,” he said, “and the conduct of the Indians explains the rest. The moon-god in Hindoo mythology is a four-armed deity seated on an antelope; one of his titles is Regent of the Night. That looks suspiciously like an indirect reference to the Moonstone.

“Now, what did the Indians do after they were allowed to receive the letter? On the very day they were set free they went straight to the station and took the first train to London. We thought, at Frizinghall, it was a pity their proceedings were not watched; but after Lady Verinder dismissed the police-officer and stopped inquiry, no one could presume to stir. They were free to go—and they went. What was the next news of them?”

“They were annoying Mr. Luker,” I said, “by loitering about his house at Lambeth.”

“Did you read the report of his application to the magistrate?”

“Yes.”

“He referred, if you remember, to a foreign workman in his employment whom he had dismissed on suspicion of attempted theft, and whom he suspected of collusion with the Indians. The inference is plain as to who wrote this letter—and as to which of Mr. Luker’s Oriental treasures the workman attempted to steal.”

The inference was indeed too plain. I had never doubted that the Moonstone had found its way into Luker’s hands at that time. The only question had been how the Indians discovered the circumstance; and now, like the rest, the answer lay before me in daylight.

I told Murthwaite I felt as if I could trust him to lead me blindfold through any remaining turns of this maze. He accepted the compliment with quiet good humour.

“You shall give me information in your turn before we go on,” he said. “Somebody must have taken the Moonstone from Yorkshire to London. Somebody must have raised money on it, or it would never have been in Luker’s possession. Has there been any discovery of who that person was?”

“None that I know of.”

“There was a story—was there not?—about Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. I am told he is an eminent philanthropist—which is decidedly against him, to begin with.”

I agreed, heartily, on that general principle; but I felt bound—without mentioning Miss Verinder’s name—to inform him that Ablewhite had been cleared on evidence I could answer for as entirely beyond dispute.

“Very well,” Murthwaite said. “Let time clear it up. Meanwhile we must get back to the Indians, on your account.

“Their journey to London ended in another defeat. The loss of their second chance is mainly attributable to Luker’s cunning and foresight—he doesn’t stand at the top of the ancient profession of usury for nothing. By dismissing the man in his employment, he deprived the Indians of their confederate’s help in getting into the house. By promptly transporting the Moonstone to his banker’s, he took them by surprise before they could frame a new plan. How they suspected what he had done, and how they contrived to possess themselves of his banker’s receipt, are too recent to dwell on. Let it be enough that they know the Moonstone is once more out of their reach, deposited—under the general description of ‘a valuable of great price’—in a banker’s strong room.

“Now, Mr. Bruff—what is their third chance of seizing the Diamond? And when will it come?”

As he asked the question, the meaning of the Indian’s visit to my office sprang into view at last, as if a shutter had been flung back and the whole room flooded with light.

“I see it!” I cried. “They take it for granted, as we do, that the Moonstone has been pledged; and they want to be certainly informed of the earliest period at which the pledge can be redeemed—because that will be the earliest period at which the Diamond can be removed from the bank!”

“I told you,” he said, “you would find it out for yourself if I gave you a fair chance. In a year from the time when the Moonstone was pledged, the Indians will be on the watch for their third chance. Luker’s own lips have told them how long they must wait; and your respectable authority has satisfied them he spoke the truth. When do we suppose—roughly—that the Diamond found its way into the money-lender’s hands?”

“Towards the end of last June,” I answered, “as well as I can reckon it.”

“And we are now in the year ’forty-eight. Very good. If the unknown person who has pledged the Moonstone can redeem it in a year, the jewel will be in that person’s possession again at the end of June, ’forty-nine. I shall be thousands of miles from England and English news then. But it may be worth your while to take a note of it, and arrange to be in London at the time.”

“You think something serious will happen?” I asked.

“I think,” he answered, “I shall be safer among the fiercest fanatics of Central Asia than I should be if I crossed the door of the bank with the Moonstone in my pocket. The Indians have been defeated twice running. It’s my firm belief they won’t be defeated a third time.”

That was the last he said. Coffee came in; chairs moved; the talk broke up and dispersed, like smoke drifting into corners; and we went upstairs to the ladies.

I made my note at once, and I close my narrative by repeating it here:

June, ’forty-nine. Expect news of the Indians, towards the end of the month.

And having done so, I lay down the pen, which I have no further claim to use, and leave it to the writer who follows me next.
