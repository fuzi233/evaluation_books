Thus, Ayacanora established herself within Lucy's cabin, officially accepted as a regular member of the crew. Yet, she proved to be a most troublesome addition. It was now that she entered that perilous crisis common to all savages upon first mingling with white men—a crisis that, mere years later, would hasten the extermination of the North American tribes. Had it not been for Amyas's admirable good sense and constancy, Ayacanora might have met a fate even more miserable than that of Pocahontas, the famous daughter of the Virginia king. After being welcomed into court by the pedantic King James I with the honors of a sister-sovereign, and becoming the reputed ancestress of numerous ancient families, she ultimately ended her days in wretchedness in a garret near Wapping.

The mind of the savage, crushed by witnessing the white man's superior skill, wealth, and wisdom, initially loses its self-respect. Meanwhile, his body, pampered by easily obtained luxuries rather than earning necessities through heavy toil, forfeits its self-reliance. With self-respect and self-helplessness gone, the few and fragile savage virtues vanish, leaving the path to begging, stealing, drunkenness, and idleness clear and inviting.

It seemed, indeed, that poor Ayacanora was walking swiftly down this road. The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas soon appeared to be nothing more than a very naughty child. The Diana of the Meta, after satisfying her simple wonder at the great floating vessel by roaming from deck to deck and peering into every cupboard and crevice, developed a strong propensity to steal and hide every trifle that caught her fancy (too proud or too shy to ask). When Amyas forbade her to take anything without permission, she threatened to drown herself and spent the entire day sulking in her cabin. Nevertheless, she obeyed him, except regarding sweets. Perhaps she craved the vegetable foods of her native forests; certainly, the Bishop's stores of fruit and confections dwindled rapidly. Worse still, so did the sweet Spanish wine Amyas had reserved as a daily cordial for the ailing Lucy.

Upon receiving another severe lecture from Amyas regarding how mean it was to rob poor sick Lucy, Ayacanora, as usual, threatened suicide. She ran onto the deck to carry out her threat, but Amyas caught her and forgave her. This elicited a violent fit of weeping, profound penitence, and endless promises. A week later, Amyas discovered she had cheated both Satan and her conscience by tormenting the Portuguese steward into providing her with alternative wine. Fortunately for her, Amyas's warnings about alcohol proving madness had begun to manifest; she performed several foolish acts one evening and suffered a terrible headache the next morning. Consequently, the "murder" plot was exposed, and Amyas ordered the steward a sound flogging. However, Ayacanora, acting honorably, not only begged him off but offered to be whipped in his stead, confessing that the poor fellow had spoken truly when he swore she had threatened his life and that he had given her the wine only in fear for his safety.

Her own headache and Amyas's cold demeanor served as sufficient lessons. After another attempted drowning, the wilful beauty settled down for a while; remarkably, she could hardly be persuaded, to her dying day, to touch fermented liquors.

Meanwhile, poor Amyas endured countless headaches of his own, pondering how to tame a lady who, at the slightest provocation, fled to suicide. He dared not punish her, even if he possessed the heart to do so. As for setting her ashore, he harbored an instinct—not necessarily superstitious—that her strange affection for the English was sent by Heaven. He believed God had entrusted her into his charge and would demand an accounting for the soul of that fair lost lamb.

Driven nearly to his wits' end, the good simple fellow prayed repeatedly for guidance on how to handle her before she destroyed herself or someone else on the crew. It seemed best to him to have Parson Jack teach her the rudiments of Christianity, intending to baptize her properly upon reaching England.

A new difficulty immediately arose: she flatly refused to learn from Jack or anyone but Amyas himself. Since Amyas had ample reasons to decline the role of schoolmaster, Ayacanora remained untaught for a week or two, aside from the English language, which she acquired with marvelous speed.

Next, as if troubles would never cease, she developed a violent dislike for John Brimblecombe, openly mimicking his gait and voice for the amusement of the men, and for Will Cary, whom she forbade to speak to or approach. Perhaps she was jealous of his intimacy with Amyas; or perhaps, possessing the subtle instinct of a woman, she sensed he was the only other man capable of daring to woo her (though Will, to his credit, was as guiltless of such intentions as Amyas). When remonstrated with, her sole reply was that Cary was a *cacique* as well as Amyas, and there ought not to be two *caciques*. One day, she actually proposed to Amyas that he kill his supposed rival and take the ship entirely for himself, sulking for several days afterward when Amyas, amidst the crew's laughter, recounted her precious advice to its intended victim.

Furthermore, the negroes bore the brunt of her disdain. She viewed them with unspeakable repugnance, first hiding from them whenever possible, then throwing whatever she could find at them until the poor Quashies, fearing for their lives, complained to Amyas and secured temporary respite. Over the rest of the sailors, she ruled like a princess, summoning them from their labor to run errands and make toys, occasionally enforcing her commands with a sharp slap across the ear. The good fellows, particularly old Yeo, treated her like a tame leopard—petting, obeying, and jesting with her, knowing full well her claws could be unsheathed at any moment. Yet, she amused them, and even Amyas. They needed a pet, and what prettier one could they have? As for Amyas, the constant interest of her presence, coupled with the anxiety of her wilfulness, kept his mind occupied, driving away many sad forebodings regarding his meeting with his mother and the tragedy he must reveal to her. Without these burdens weighing so heavily, his worldly success and miraculous deliverance would have seemed worthless, crushing his spirit with melancholy.

Finally, as often happens, matters reached a climax.

The ship had been sailing slowly but steadily for many days under a favorable breeze. Having passed the ring of the West Indies, she was now crawling safely through the vast weed-beds of the Sargasso Sea. For the first time, it was deemed safe to relax the strict discipline maintained thus far and to "rummage" their noble prize. What they found in gold, silver, jewels, and merchandise need not concern the reader; suffice it to say, there was enough treasure, alongside the others, to make Amyas wealthy for life after paying off Cary's and the crew's shares, not to mention Mr. Salterne's third as the shipowner. In the captain's cabin, however, were discovered two chests: one filled with gorgeous Mexican feather dresses, the other with Spanish and East Indian finery destined for some *señora* in Caracas. These two chests were voted, amid the crew's acclamations, to Ayacanora as her due share of the plunder, considering her Amazonian prowess and valuable services.

The poor child seized the trifles greedily, had them carried to Lucy's cabin, and knelt gazing upon them for hours. She chose to despise the Mexican work as savage, but the Spanish dresses were a treasure. For two or three days, she appeared on the quarterdeck, sunning herself like a peacock before Amyas's eyes in Seville mantillas, Madrid hats, Indian brocade farthingales, and countless other gewgaws, wearing them with daring audacity.

The crew tittered; Amyas felt more inclined to weep. Nothing is so pathetic as a child's vanity, unless it is an adult imitating a child's vanity, or a child suffering the agony of disappointment when realizing it has been mocked rather than admired. Amyas wished to speak but feared to intervene. However, the evil brought its own remedy. The pageant proceeded, according to the performer's belief, successfully for three days. Finally, unable to contain herself, the duped girl appealed to Amyas: "Ayacanora is quite an English girl now, is she not?" Hearing a titter behind her, she glanced around, saw a dozen honest faces grinning broadly, comprehended everything instantly, darted down the companion ladder, and vanished.

Amyas, fully expecting her to jump overboard, followed as fast as he could. But she had locked herself inside with Lucy. He could hear her violent sobbing and Lucy's faint voice entreating to know what was wrong.

He knocked in vain. She refused to emerge all day, and by evening, they were forced to break down the door to prevent Lucy from starving.

Inside sat Ayacanora, her finery half-torn off and scattered across the floor in defiance, crying as though her heart would break; poor Lucy wept too, partly from fright and hunger, partly for companionship.
