To understand Mr. Yorke, one must first accept that he is a creature of stark contrasts, a man carved from the raw granite of the North rather than the polished marble of the South. At fifty-five, though appearing older due to a crown of silver hair, he possessed a visage that was undeniably, thoroughly English. It lacked the Norman curve, the aristocratic grace, or the classical symmetry that fine folks often prize. Instead, his face was a map of unvarnished reality: broad, fresh, and marked by a harsh, northern severity that mirrored the steel in his voice. Every lineament spoke of a rugged, unclassical originality—a countenance that sensible souls might term "characteristic," while the shrewd would delight in its biting sagacity. Yet, it was also a face of indocility and scorn, etched with a sarcasm that defied leadership and mocked direction. Tall, wiry, and carrying a stately integrity, he bore no trace of the clown, projecting an aura of formidable, solitary power.

If you seek a benevolent sage or a kindly philanthropist in Mr. Yorke, you will find yourself disappointed. While he occasionally spoke with sense and warmth to friends like Mr. Moore, his default setting was far colder. His mind, the labyrinthine core of his being, suffered from three distinct deficiencies. First, he lacked the organ of veneration; to kings, nobles, priests, and parliaments, he offered only disdain. He viewed their institutions as rubbish, believing that if the high places were razed and their occupants crushed, the world would emerge cleaner and freer. This absence of awe dried up the electric delights of admiration, leaving him blind to the sublime in beauty or virtue. Second, his inability to compare stripped him of true sympathy. While he preached mutual toleration, he harbored a bigoted hatred for specific classes—particularly "parsons" and "lords"—speaking of them with a harshness that bordered on insolence. He could not step into their shoes to weigh their burdens against their flaws; instead, he projected his own frustrations onto them, wishing for arbitrary, even cruel, solutions to the problems of inequality. And third, he possessed scant benevolence, rendering him impatient with weakness and unforgiving of faults that grated on his sharp nature. His sarcasm was a weapon wielded without mercy, often wounding repeatedly without a second thought for the pain inflicted.

Yet, let us not paint him entirely in shadows. Beneath this rough exterior lay a man of considerable honor and capability, highly respected even by those who loathed him. To his workmen, he was a father figure, considerate and cordial. When dismissing a worker, he went out of his way to help them find new employment or relocate their families. However, his kindness to the downtrodden coexisted with a terrifying tyranny over those beneath him; like his ancestors, he could not bear control, and he crushed rebellion in its germ with the vigor of a gardener pulling weeds. He was a proud man, content to submit to the natural hierarchy of labor, yet haughty as Beelzebub toward anyone he deemed superior. His religion, if it could be called that, was devoid of the tender awe that moves the soul. He believed in a God and a Heaven, but they were concepts stripped of imagination and emotion, belonging to a man who found comfort in the stark logic of the physical world rather than the ethereal.

Regarding poetry and idealism, Mr. Yorke held a pragmatic, if dismissive, view. He lacked the poetic imagination himself, considering it a superfluous, even dangerous, attribute in others. He could appreciate the result of a painter's brush or a musician's instrument—the beauty itself—but he held little regard for the internal struggle, the "fire" and "force" that fueled the artist. To him, the quiet poet who could not play the game of commerce, the tradesman who failed to thrive in the Piece Hall, was destined for obscurity and scorn. He understood, perhaps instinctively, why the true poet needs a truculent spirit beneath a placid surface, why they must laugh in their sleeves when the world condemns them as useless. But these were reflections for the sensitive soul, not for the utilitarian mind of Hiram Yorke.

Despite his faults, Mr. Yorke was a man of influence, the head of the oldest family in the district. His education had been sound, and his travels on the Continent before the French Revolution had left him fluent in French and Italian. His home was adorned with treasures gathered during a two-year sojourn in Italy, and when he chose, his manners were those of a gentleman of the old school. Yet, he preferred the company of the shrewd workman or the sagacious old cottager to the fashionable, insipid fine gentleman. He valued the "Yorkshire burr" over the Cockney lisp, believing the former sounded like a bull's bellow compared to the latter's squeak.

Among his guests, the dynamic between Mr. Yorke and Mr. Moore was complex. Yorke entertained a faint partiality for the younger man, drawn by the foreign accent of his speech and the distinctly non-Yorkshire lines of his face. These trivialities, perhaps, served as a portal to Yorke's own youth and travels, reviving memories of Parisian cafés and Italian streets. Furthermore, a business connection with Moore's father, complicated by shared financial losses, tethered them, as did Yorke's role as guardian of the estate on which Hollow's Mill stood. Finally, he recognized in Robert Moore a sharp businessman, resolved and acute, qualities Yorke respected.

In stark opposition stood Mr. Helstone. Between the free-thinking industrialist and the formalist clergyman ran a double antipathy: the clash of nature and the weight of circumstances. Rumors swirled that they had once been rival suitors for the same lady. Indeed, Mr. Yorke, in his youth, had preferred spirited, dashing women, until he fell utterly, unexpectedly in love with a girl of "living marble" silence—Mary Cave. She was the antithesis of the lively belles he usually courted, answering in monosyllables and offering no smile, yet she became his obsession. Conversely, Mr. Helstone, then a curate, had loved Mary as well, or so he fancied. Unlike Yorke's consuming passion, Helstone approached her with a calm, almost clinical observation. He saw her clearly, without the illusions that clouded the eyes of other admirers. He accepted her readily, marrying her before the depth of his feelings or hers could fully mature.

Tragically, nature had not designed Mr. Helstone to be a husband to a quiet wife. He operated under a delusion that silence equaled contentment, failing to perceive that a woman who did not complain was simply holding her breath. He regarded women as a separate, inferior order of existence, incapable of being companions or confidantes. After a brief period of marriage, Mary ceased to hold any significance for him. Her eventual death, sudden and mysterious, left behind a still, beautiful mold of clay. While the local housekeeper and servants whispered of a broken heart, driven by harsh treatment, Mr. Helstone remained dry-eyed and stoic, scandalizing the neighborhood with his lack of visible grief. Unbeknownst to him, rumors of his cruelty had taken root, feeding on the truth that he had indeed neglected his wife's emotional needs. Mr. Yorke, hearing these tales, felt a bitter, rooted animosity toward his former rival. He could not forgive the fact that what he had cherished so deeply had been "neglected, perhaps abused" by another man. If Helstone had known the extent of Yorke's lost love, he likely would never have crossed his threshold again.

Back in the parlour, the conversation shifted from personal history to the turbulent state of the country. The recent depredations on mill-properties sparked a fierce debate. Mr. Helstone, representing the establishment, argued that the masters were aggrieved and the workpeople unreasonable. He advocated for vigorous government interference, strict magisterial vigilance, and if necessary, prompt military coercion to quell the spirit of disaffection. He viewed the unrest as a dangerous deviation from inevitable evils that required firm correction.

Mr. Yorke, however, scoffed at the notion of inevitable suffering. He asked if military force would feed the hungry or provide work for the unemployed. To him, public patience was a camel already overloaded; the last straw had been laid, and resistance was now a moral duty. He identified the "corrupt, base, and bloody" government as the true tyrant, blaming figures like Pitt and Castlereagh for a ruinous war and oppressive taxation that hung a millstone around England's neck. He denounced the hereditary legislators and the "pampered, persecuting" Church as insults to common sense, maintaining that the widespread spirit of defiance against authority was the most promising sign of the age.

Mr. Helstone rose, donning his shovel-hat, to deliver a final rebuke. He warned that sentiment, however brave, crumbled when the "keepers of the house trembled." He recounted a time when he had read prayers for a dying foe, a man stricken with remorse who could find no penance. He declared that blasphemy against God and King was a deadly sin, invoking the concept of "judgment to come."
