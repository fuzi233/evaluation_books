It takes between four and five days to sail from the Cape to Durban, depending on the ship’s speed and the weather. Sometimes, if the landing at East London is rough—where they’ve yet to build the grand harbor they talk so much about—a vessel may be held up for twenty-four hours while cargo boats can reach it. But this time, we had no delay. The Bar was calm, and tugs arrived promptly with their long lines of flat-bottomed boats, ready to haul our goods ashore. Packages were thrown into the boats with little care—china, wool, or anything else met the same rough treatment. I watched as a case of champagne, holding forty-eight bottles, shattered in the bottom of one boat, spilling its contents into the dirty water. It was a terrible waste. The Kafirs aboard found two unbroken bottles, knocked off the necks, and drank deeply. But the fizzing wine caused them to swell, and soon they rolled about in panic, shouting that the liquor was “tagati”—bewitched. I called down from the deck, telling them it was the white man’s strongest medicine and that they were as good as dead. They scrambled ashore in terror, and I doubt they’ll ever touch champagne again.

All through the journey up the coast, I pondered Sir Henry Curtis’s proposal. We didn’t speak of it again for a few days, though I entertained them with true hunting tales—no need to lie when such strange things happen in the wild. But eventually, on a perfect January evening—the hottest month in these parts—we passed the Natal coast, expecting to reach Durban Point by sunset.

The shoreline from East London onward is stunning: red sandhills, wide stretches of emerald green, scattered Kafir kraals, and a ribbon of white surf that crashes into foam against the rocks. As we neared Durban, the landscape deepened in richness. Steep kloofs carved by centuries of rain glistened with rushing rivers; the bush glowed with deep green, alongside mealie fields and sugar patches. Here and there, a white house peeked out toward the sea, adding warmth and life to the scene. To me, no view is complete without signs of human presence—perhaps because I’ve spent so much time in the wilderness, where civilization feels like a rare gift, even if it drives away the game. Eden may have been beautiful before Adam, but I always believe it became more so when Eve walked among the trees.

We miscalculated slightly, and the sun had already dipped below the horizon when we dropped anchor off the Point. A cannon fired, signaling to the town that the English Mail had arrived. Too late to cross the Bar that night, we settled in for dinner after the mailbags were carried off in the lifeboat. Later, under a full moon, the sea and shore shimmered in silver light, nearly outshining the lighthouse flashes. Sweet, spicy scents drifted from the shore—reminding me of hymns and missionaries—and hundreds of lights sparkled in homes on the Berea. Nearby, sailors aboard another brig sang as they raised their anchor. It was a perfect Southern African night, peaceful and serene. Even the bulldog belonging to a sporting passenger seemed enchanted, snoring contentedly at the cabin door, dreaming he’d bested the baboon in the cage.

Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, and I sat quietly by the wheel. After a moment, Sir Henry asked, “Mr. Quatermain, have you thought about my offer?”

Captain Good chimed in, “What do you say? Will you join us to Solomon’s Mines—or wherever that man Neville might be?”

I rose, knocked the ashes from my pipe, and paused. I hadn’t decided yet—but just as the last ember fell into the sea, I knew. That extra second made all the difference. Often, when you’ve wrestled with a decision for too long, it’s the brief pause that settles it.

“Yes,” I said, sitting back down. “I’ll go. And I’ll tell you why—and on what terms.”

First, I laid out my conditions:

1. All expenses are covered by you. Any ivory or valuables we find are split equally between Captain Good and myself.
2. I want £500 upfront for my services, payable before we begin. I’ll serve faithfully until you abandon the mission, succeed, or disaster strikes.
3. Before we depart, you must sign a deed promising to pay my son Harry—currently studying medicine at Guy’s Hospital in London—£200 a year for five years, should I die or become disabled. That’s all I ask.

“No,” replied Sir Henry. “I accept gladly. This venture means everything to me, and I’d pay far more for your expertise.”

“I won’t retract now,” I said. “But let me explain why I’ve decided to go.”

First, I’ve observed both of you closely. I like you. I believe we’ll work well together. That matters on a journey like this.

Now, about the journey itself—I’m honest: I don’t think we’ll survive if we try crossing the Suliman Mountains. What happened to Dom da Silvestra three hundred years ago? His descendant twenty years ago? Your brother? Their fates suggest ours will be the same.

I paused, watching their reactions. Captain Good looked uneasy. Sir Henry remained steady.

“We take our chances,” he said.

“You might wonder,” I continued, “why I—someone who admits to being timid—would risk such a trip. Two reasons. First, I’m a fatalist. I believe my time is fixed, regardless of my actions. If I’m meant to die in those mountains, I will. God knows His plan. Second, I’m poor. For nearly forty years, I’ve hunted and traded, but never made more than a living. And the average elephant hunter lives only four to five years in the trade. I’ve lived through seven generations of such men. My time may not be far off. If I die in ordinary circumstances, my debts would leave nothing for Harry. But now, he’ll be supported for five years—enough time to stand on his own, if he’s worth his salt.”

Sir Henry listened intently. “Mr. Quatermain,” he said, “your reasons show great courage. Whether you’re right or wrong, I intend to see this through, come what may. If we’re to die, I hope we get some good shooting first, eh, Good?”

“Indeed,” the captain added. “We’ve all faced danger before. No point turning back now. Let’s go down to the saloon and take an observation—for luck.”

And we did—through the bottom of a tumbler.

The next day, we went ashore. I took Sir Henry and Captain Good to my small home on the Berea—a modest place of three rooms and a kitchen, built of green brick with a galvanized iron roof. But it has a fine garden, with the best loquat trees I know and young mangoes gifted by the botanical gardens’ curator. An old hunter named Jack tends it—he broke his thigh badly in Sikukunis country and can no longer hunt, but he’s a skilled gardener, born a Griqua. Zulus aren’t interested in gardening—it’s too peaceful for their nature.

Sir Henry and Good slept in a tent beneath my orange trees, where blooms and fruit hang together in golden clusters. Mosquitoes are rare here unless the rains are unusually heavy. It’s a pleasant spot.

Now, having decided to go, I began preparations. First, I secured the deed from Sir Henry, ensuring Harry’s future. It was tricky—Sir Henry was a foreigner, and the property was overseas—but with help from a lawyer (who charged £20, which I considered exorbitant), it was done. Then I collected my £500.

With that settled, I bought a wagon and team of oxen on Sir Henry’s behalf. The wagon was twenty-two feet long, with iron axles, built of stinkwood—strong, light, and seasoned from a previous journey to the Diamond Fields. It was “half-tented,” meaning only the rear twelve feet were covered, leaving room for supplies. Inside, there was a hide bed for two, rifle racks, and other useful fittings. I paid £125—well worth it.

Next, I acquired twenty Zulu oxen—four more than usual, to account for losses. These cattle were smaller than Africander oxen, but hardier. They could endure harsh terrain and make five miles a day with moderate loads. Most importantly, they were “salted”—they’d worked across South Africa and were resistant to red water, a deadly illness that often wipes out teams. They’d also been inoculated against “lung sick,” a form of pneumonia. This involved cutting a slit in the tail and inserting diseased lung tissue, causing a mild infection that cured them of future attacks. Though it left them tailless—looking like prize bulldogs without tails—it saved their lives.

Then came provisions and medicine. We needed to avoid overloading the wagon while carrying everything essential. Fortunately, Captain Good had medical training—though not formally qualified, he knew more than many doctors. He had a superb travel medicine chest and surgical tools. While in Durban, he amputated a Kafir’s big toe with skill and precision. The patient calmly asked him to replace it—with a “white one” if necessary. Good was baffled.

Arms were next. From Sir Henry’s collection and my own, we selected:

- Three heavy breech-loading double-eight elephant guns (15 lbs each, 11 drachms black powder)
- Three double-500 Express rifles (6 drachms charge)
- One double No. 12 central-fire shotgun (full choke)
- Three Winchester repeating rifles (spare guns)
- Three Colt revolvers (American pattern cartridges)

All weapons were uniform in caliber—critical for ammunition interchangeability. Every experienced hunter knows how vital proper arms are.

For crew, we planned five: a driver, a leader, and three servants. I hired two Zulus—Goza and Tom—as driver and leader. Finding trustworthy servants proved harder. I secured two: Ventvögel, a Hottentot tracker of unmatched skill, though prone to drink—useless near grog-shops, but irrelevant beyond them—and Khiva, a small Zulu who spoke English perfectly.

Still missing one servant, we decided to wait. But the evening before departure, Khiva told me a Kafir waited to see me. I told him to bring him in.

A tall, striking man entered—about thirty, lighter-skinned than most Zulus. He lifted his knob-stick in salute and squatted silently in the corner. I waited—important not to rush conversation with a Zulu. I noticed he wore a “Keshla” ring in his hair, a mark of dignity. His face seemed familiar.

“Well,” I said finally. “What’s your name?”

“Umbopa,” he answered slowly.

“I’ve seen your face before.”

“Yes,” he said. “The Inkoosi, my father, saw me at the Place of the Little Hand—Isandhlwana—on the day before the battle.”

I remembered. I’d been Lord Chelmsford’s guide during the Zulu War. On the eve of battle, I’d spoken with this man—a minor officer in the native auxiliaries—who warned me the camp was unsafe. I’d dismissed him then. But later, I recalled his words.

“I remember,” I said. “What do you want?”

“It is this, ‘Macumazahn.’” That was my Kafir name—“the man who wakes in the middle of the night.” “I hear you’re going on a great expedition far north with white chiefs from across the sea. Is it true?”

“It is.”

“And you go to the Lukanga River, beyond Manica? Is that so?”

“Why do you ask? What concern is it of yours?”

“I wish to travel with you.”

His tone carried dignity—especially using “O white men” instead of “Inkosis.” I sensed something unusual.

“You forget yourself,” I said. “Who are you? Where is your kraal?”

“My name is Umbopa. I am of the Zulu people, yet not of them. My tribe’s home lies far north—left behind when the Zulus migrated south, long before Chaka ruled. I have no kraal. I’ve wandered for years. I came south as a child. I served Cetewayo in the Nkomabakosi Regiment, under Umslopogaasi of the Axe, who taught me to fight. Later, I fled Zululand to see the white man’s ways. Then I fought against Cetewayo. Now I work in Natal. But I’m tired. I want to return north. This is not my place. I ask no pay. I am brave. I am worth my place and food. I have spoken.”

I was intrigued. He seemed truthful, yet different. I translated to Sir Henry and Good. Sir Henry instructed me to ask him to stand.

Umbopa rose, removing his military greatcoat, revealing himself naked except for a leather belt and a necklace of lion claws. He stood six foot three—broad, shapely, and powerful. His skin was dark, but marked with deep scars from assegai wounds. Sir Henry stepped forward, examining his face.

“They make a good pair,” said Good. “One as big as the other.”

“I like your looks, Mr. Umbopa,” Sir Henry said. “I’ll take you as my servant.”

Umbopa understood. “It is well,” he replied in Zulu. Then, glancing at Sir Henry’s height and breadth, he added, “We are men, thou and I.”
