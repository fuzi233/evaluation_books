George Warrington had recounted his tale to Madame de Bernstein the prior evening, though the performance was far less successful than it had been in Hill Street. The old lady, prone to nodding off whenever the narrative grew intense, would only rouse herself when he paused, declaring it "most interesting" and commanding him to proceed. Consequently, the young gentleman stammered, hesitated, flushed crimson, and spoke with a halting cadence that belied his confidence before his intimate circle. In contrast, the warm reception of Hetty's wondering gaze, Theo's sympathetic expression, and the gentle smiles of his parents—despite his mother's theatrical tendency to weep at the slightest provocation, a trait the General likened to a professional tragedienne—had served as sufficient encouragement for his modest eloquence. It was precisely to avoid such a cynical auditor that they had selected Lord Wrotham's residence as the venue for George's debut, hoping his words would land with favor rather than falling on weary ears.

"Oh, very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir," Madame Bernstein cooed, raising three slender fingers encased in a lace mitten to mask a sudden, involuntary twitch of her lips. "And I daresay your mother was quite delighted to see you."

George gave a slight shrug and bowed low as his aunt lifted her keen, aged eyes to inspect him.

"She certainly was," the old lady continued, her tone dry and cutting, "delighted to see you, killed the fatted calf, and engaged in all such appropriate niceties. Though, nephew George, I confess I cannot understand why I call it a calf. You were never the prodigal son. I might as well address thee as 'poor Harry!' For you have undoubtedly wandered among swine. Indeed, evil companions have stripped both thy purse and thy coat clean."

"He returned to his family in England, madam," George interjected, his voice warming with defense, "and his friends were, of course, your ladyship's."

"Nay, he could not have sought counsel from worse advisors," the old lady retorted, tossing her head with dramatic flair. "I should have informed my sister sooner, had she deigned to write through him as she has done through you." Turning to her waiting-maid, she whispered excitedly, "By Jove! This young gentleman's mother is half-sorry that he has come back to life. I saw it in his face! Half sorry, and I am perfectly furious! Why did he not lie still beneath that tree? Why did that young Florac drag him to the fort? I knew those Floracs in the days of Monsieur le Régent. They were of the House of Ivry. No great house before Henri IV. His ancestor was the King's favorite. His ancestor—he! he!—his ancestress! Brett! Entendez-vous? Hand me my card-case. I detest the grand airs of this Monsieur George; yet, he bears a striking resemblance to his grandfather—the same look, and occasionally the same tones. Have you heard of Colonel Esmond when I was young? This boy has his eyes. I suppose I favored the Colonel because he loved me."

Engaged immediately to a card party—an amusement she never missed, weekday or Sabbath, so long as she possessed the strength to hold trumps or sit upright—the old lady soon dismissed her two nephews. To the elder, George, she offered a couple of fingers and a stiff, stately curtsy; to the younger, Harry, she extended two hands and bestowed a kindly pat upon his cheek.

"My poor child," she murmured, her voice softening, "now that thou art disinherited, thou shalt see how differently the world treats thee!" She pressed a small leather pocket-book into his hand. "There is only, in all London, a wicked, heartless old woman who will treat thee as before. Do not lose it tonight at Ranelagh. That suit of thine does not become thy brother half so well as it did upon thee! Present thy brother to everyone, and pace the room for at least two hours. Were I in thy shoes, I would retreat to the Chocolate-House and play as if nothing had occurred. While thou art there, perhaps thy brother may return to dine with me upon some chicken. My Lady Flint serves wretched suppers, and I wish to discuss thy mother's letter with him. Adieu, gentlemen!" With that, she departed toward her toilet, her chairmen and torchbearants already awaiting at the door.

The gentlemen proceeded to Ranelagh, where only a handful of Mr. Harry's acquaintances happened to be present. They strolled along the circular promenade, encountering Mr. Tom Claypool and several country friends, listened to the strains of music, and sipped tea within a private box. Harry assumed the role of host, introducing his brother to the various curiosities of the pleasure palace. Meanwhile, George proved far more agitated than his sibling had been on his initial visit. George possessed a profound love for music, having never before heard a full orchestra perform a composition by Mr. Handel with such satisfaction. A notable testament to Harry's newfound humility and fraternal regard was his ability to tolerate George's passion for the fiddle—a pursuit often deemed effeminate and unmanly in England, where patriotic pamphlets daily urged citizens to mock the frivolous accomplishments of foreign "Squallinis" and "Monsieurs." Yet, the spirit of the age seemed shifting; pride in ignorance was fading, and arrogance had vanished from polite society. Art had secured her letters of naturalization, residing here on terms nearly equal to the native born. Even if Mrs. Thrale chose to wed a music master today, her friends need not shudder at the union, nor withhold civility from Mr. Piozzi, provided she maintained a good fortune and a competent cook.

Following Ranelagh, and acting on Madame Bernstein's counsel, George returned to her ladyship's house, while Harry presented himself at the club, where gentlemen traditionally assembled for supper followed by gambling. Naturally, no one alluded to Mr. Warrington's brief, mysterious absence. His former landlord, Mr. Ruff, attended to him with the utmost gravity and politeness, as if no rift had ever existed between them. Mr. Warrington had ensured his trunks and belongings were removed from Bond Street in the morning, and he and his brother were now quartered in separate lodgings.

However, once supper concluded and the gentlemen prepared to ascend to the macco-table upstairs, Harry declared he would not wager further. "I have already burned my fingers," he remarked, "and can afford no further extravagance."

"Why, Mr. Morris," said a gentleman in a somewhat flippant tone, "you must have won more than you have lost, after all is said and done."

"And of course I know your business less well than you do, Mr. Morris," Harry replied sternly, remembering the other man's behavior upon learning of his arrest. "But I have another reason. A few months—or days, rather—ago, I was heir to a vast estate and could afford to lose a trifling sum. Now, thank God, I am heir to nothing." He glanced around, flushing deeply, at the cluster of gentlemen lounging near the tables or gathered by the fire.

"How do you mean, Mr. Warrington?" cried Lord March, eyes lighting up. "Hast thou lost Virginia, too? Who hath won it? I always fancied playing thee myself for that stake."

"And thereby produce an improved breed of slaves in the colony," suggested another voice.

"The rightful owner hath claimed it. Hast thou not heard of my twin elder brother?"

"Yes," came the response, "who perished in the affair of Braddock two years hence. Gracious goodness, my dear sir, I trust in Heaven he hath not returned to life!"

"He arrived in London two days past," Harry explained. "He was a prisoner in a French fort for eighteen months, escaping only recently. He left our house in Virginia shortly after his liberation."

"Haven't thou had time to order mourning, I suppose?" asked Mr. Selwyn, his nature good-humored and simple, though Harry scarcely grasped the jest until his brother elucidated it.

"Blasphemy, if I am not mistaken!" cried Lord March as they continued to discuss the matter after the young Virginian had taken his leave. "These savages practice the simple virtues of affection—they are barely civilized in America yet."

"They cherish their kin, and scalp their foes," chimed Mr. Walpole with a smirk. "It is not Christian, but natural. Wouldst thou care to attend a scalping match, George, and behold a fellow skinned alive?"

"A man's elder brother is his natural enemy," observed Mr. Selwyn calmly, arranging his money and counters before him.

"Torture is akin to broiled bones and pepper," Horry added. "Thou wouldst not relish simple hanging afterward, George!"

"I'm hanged if there's any man in England who would desire to see his elder brother alive," declared Lord March.

"No, nor his father either, my lord!" shouted Jack Morris.

"For the first time, I learn thou hast one, Jack. Give me counters for five hundred."

"I say, 'tis all mighty fine concerning dead brothers returning to life," Jack mused. "Who knows if this was not a scheme contrived between these two fellows? Behold a young fellow calling himself the Fortunate Youth, claiming to be a Virginian Prince and devil-knows-what, entering our society..."
