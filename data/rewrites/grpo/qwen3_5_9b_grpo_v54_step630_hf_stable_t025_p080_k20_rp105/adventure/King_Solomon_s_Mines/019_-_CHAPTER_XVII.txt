While we lay recovering from our terror, inspecting the grisly remains of the Place of Death, Gagool was busy elsewhere. With surprising agility, she scrambled onto the great table and approached Twala's frozen form beneath the dripping rock. Whether she was checking on his "preservation" or serving some darker purpose, she bent to kiss his icy lips with feigned affection before hobbling back. She paused intermittently to murmur unintelligible greetings to the shrouded figures, treating them as old acquaintances. Once this macabre ritual concluded, she squatted directly beneath the White Death and began to chant prayers to the arch-enemy of mankind. The sight of this wicked creature supplicating the devil was so unnerving that it spurred us to hasten our investigation.

"Now, Gagool," I whispered, for the air itself seemed to forbid loud speech, "lead us to the chamber."

The old witch descended promptly. "My lords are not afraid?" she leered.

"Lead on."

"Good, my lords," she hobbled around the rear of the great stone. "Here is the chamber. Light the lamp and enter." She set a gourd of oil on the floor and leaned against the cave wall. I struck a match from our dwindling supply, lighting a rush wick, but before us lay only solid rock. Gagool grinned. "The way is there, my lords. Ha! ha! ha!"

"Do not jest with us," I commanded sternly.

"I jest not. See!" she pointed.

As she raised the lamp, we watched in disbelief as a massive slab of stone rose slowly from the floor, sliding upward into a cavity in the ceiling. The monolith, roughly ten feet high and five feet thick, weighed perhaps thirty tons, operated by a simple counter-weight system akin to a modern window sash. We did not see the trigger mechanism, for Gagool avoided it, but I suspected a hidden lever required only slight pressure to tip the balance. Slowly, the stone vanished, revealing a dark maw leading to Solomon's treasure.

Our excitement was palpable. Was this a hoax, or did vast hoards await us? We would know soon enough.

"Enter, white men from the Stars," Gagool called, stepping into the darkness. "First, hear your servant. The bright stones you seek were dug from the pit of the Silent Ones and stored here long before my memory begins. This place has been entered only once since those who hid the stones fled in haste. A white man arrived from over the mountains, perhaps also 'from the Stars,' and was welcomed by the king. He and a local woman journeyed here; by chance, the woman learned the secret of the door—a secret you would search a thousand years for in vain. They entered, found the stones, and filled a small goat-skin carried by the woman. As he departed, he took one final, large stone in his hand."

She paused. "Well," I breathed, "what happened to Da Silvestra?"

The hag started. "How knowest thou the dead man's name?" she snapped, then continued. "None can tell. The white man was frightened; he dropped the goat-skin and fled with only the single stone, which the king took. It is that very stone you, Macumazahn, removed from Twala's brow."

"Have none entered since?" I asked, peering down the dark passage.

"None, my lords. Only the kings have opened it, though none have entered. There is a saying: those who enter die within a moon, just as the white man died in the mountain cave. Therefore, the kings do not enter. Ha! ha! Mine are true words."

Her gaze locked with mine, and I felt a chill. How did she know all this?

"Enter, my lords. If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies on the floor; if death awaits us, you will learn later. Ha! ha! ha!" She hobbled through, carrying the light, though I hesitated.

"Oh, confound it!" Good declared. "Here goes. I will not be frightened by that old devil." Followed by Foulata, who shivered violently despite her fear, he plunged into the passage. We quickly followed.

A few yards down, Gagool waited. "See, my lords," she illuminated, "those who stored the treasure fled in haste, lacking time to finish their work." She pointed to square blocks of stone laid across the passage, forming a partial wall. Alongside were more blocks, a heap of mortar, and trowels identical to those used by workmen today.

Foulata, overcome by fear and faintness, collapsed. We set her down on the unfinished wall beside her basket of provisions and left her to recover.

Pressing onward for fifteen paces, we encountered an elaborately painted wooden door, standing wide open. Across the threshold lay a goat-skin bag, heavy and jingling.

"Hee! hee!" Gagool sniggered as the light fell upon it. "What did I tell you? The white man fled in haste and dropped the woman's bag. Look within; you will find a water-gourd among the stones."

Good lifted the bag. "By Jove! Full of diamonds," he whispered in awe.

"Go on," Sir Henry urged impatiently, taking the lamp. He stepped through, followed by us, momentarily forgetting the diamonds, and found ourselves in the treasure chamber.

The lamp's faint glow revealed a room hewn from living rock, perhaps ten feet square. Stacked against the arch of the roof were hundreds of elephant tusks of the finest quality—enough ivory to make a man wealthy for life. On the opposite side stood eighteen wooden boxes, painted red, resembling ammunition crates.

"There are the diamonds," I cried. "Bring the light."

Sir Henry held the lamp to the top box. The lid, rotted by time, had been smashed, likely by Da Silvestra. Pushing my hand through the hole, I withdrew it full not of diamonds, but of gold pieces stamped with Hebrew characters.

"We shan't go back empty-handed," I said. "There must be thousands of pieces here. Probably payment for workmen and merchants."

"I don't see any diamonds," Good noted. "Unless the old Portuguese put them all in his bag."

"Look where it is darkest," Gagool interpreted our silence. "There is a nook with three stone chests."

"How do you know this if no one has entered since the white man?" I asked.

"Some live long, and some have eyes that see through rock," she mocked.

"Look in that corner, Curtis."

"Hullo!" Curtis cried. "Here's a recess. Great heavens!"

We hurried to the niche, shaped like a bow window. Against the wall sat three stone chests. Two were sealed; the third lay open.

"See!" Curtis hoarsely held the lamp over the open chest.

For a moment, a silvery sheen blinded us. When our eyes adjusted, we saw the chest was three-quarters full of uncut diamonds, many of considerable size. I picked one up; the unmistakable soapy feel confirmed it.

I gasped. "We are the richest men in the whole world. Monte Christo was a fool to us."

"We shall flood the market," Good said.

"Get them out first," Sir Henry suggested.

We stood staring at the gems, lantern between us, feeling like conspirators rather than fortunate men.

"Hee! hee! hee!" Gagool cackled behind us, flitting like a vampire bat. "Take them, run them through your fingers, eat them, drink them, ha! ha!"

The absurdity of eating diamonds broke the tension, and we erupted into laughter, a sound echoing strangely in the tomb. We shrieked over the gems meant for us, stored millennia ago by Solomon's overseer. Solomon never got them, nor Da Silvestra. We had them: millions of pounds in diamonds, gold, and ivory waiting to be taken.

Suddenly, the laughter ceased.

"Open the other chests," Gagool croaked. "Take your fill!"

We broke the seals on the remaining two. The second was full to the brim; the third, though only a quarter full, contained picked stones, many over twenty carats, some as large as pigeon eggs. Several were slightly yellow, "off-coloured" by Kimberley standards.

What we did not see was the look of fearful malevolence Gagool fixed upon us as she slithered out of the chamber and down the passage toward the rock door.

Then, cries rang up the vaulted path. Foulata's voice!

"Oh, Bougwan! Help! The stone falls!"

"Leave her go!"

"Help! She has stabbed me!"

We ran down the passage. The rock door was closing, mere feet from the floor. Near it, Foulata and Gagool struggled. Blood ran to Foulata's knee, but the brave girl held the witch, who fought like a wild cat. Suddenly, Foulata fell. Gagool threw herself on the ground, twisting like a snake through the crack of the closing stone. She was under—but too late! The stone nipped her, and she screamed in agony. Down, down it came, thirty tons of rock slowly crushing her old body against the bedrock. Shrieks followed by a sickening crunch, and the door slammed shut just as we hurled ourselves against it.

It was all done in four seconds.

We turned to Foulata. She was mortally wounded.

"Bougwan, I die!" she gasped. "She crept out... the door began to fall... she came back... she stabbed me... I die, Bougwan!"

"Poor girl!" Good cried, falling to kiss her.

"Bougwan," she whispered, "is Macumazahn there? It grows dark..."

"Here I am, Foulata."

"Say to my lord Bougwan... I love him... I am glad to die because the sun may not mate with the darkness... Say that if I live again, I may see him in the Stars... Oh, hold me closer..."

"She is dead!" Good muttered, rising in grief.

"You need not let that trouble you," Sir Henry said grimly.

"Eh? What do you mean?"

"I mean you will soon be in a position to join her. Man, don't you see we are buried alive?"

Only then did the full horror strike us. The rock door had closed, sealing us in. The only one who knew the secret, Gagool, was crushed beneath it. We were trapped on the wrong side of a door no army could breach.

"This will never do," Sir Henry said hoarsely. "The lamp will soon go out. Let us find the spring that works the rock."

We searched desperately along the door and walls, but found no knob or lever.

"It does not work from the inside," I realized. "If it did, Gagool wouldn't have risked crawling under it."

"Retribution was swift," Sir Henry laughed bitterly. "Her end was almost as awful as ours. We can do nothing with the door; let us go back to the treasure room."

We returned, bringing Foulata's corpse to lie beside the boxes of coin. We seated ourselves against the stone chests containing the priceless loot.

"Divide the food," Sir Henry ordered. "Make it last."

We rationed the biltong and two quarts of water, enough for perhaps two days.

"Now," Sir Henry said grimly, "let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die."

We ate small portions, our appetites gone. Then we examined the walls of our prison-house, sounding them carefully. There was no exit.

"The lamp burns dim," I noted. "Quatermain, what is the time?"

"It is six. We entered at eleven."

"Infadoos will miss us," I suggested. "Curtis, if we don't return tonight, he will search in the morning."

"He may search in vain. He doesn't know the secret, nor where it is. No living person knew it yesterday except Gagool. Today, no one knows it. Even the Kukuana army couldn't break five feet of living rock. My friends, we must bow to the will of the Almighty. The search for treasure brings many to a bad end; we shall go to swell their number."

The lamp flickered and finally expired.

In the darkness, the scene remained: the great mass of white tusks, the boxes of gold, Foulata's corpse, the goat-skin of treasure, the glimmer of diamonds, and the wild, wan faces of the three white men seated there, awaiting death by starvation.
