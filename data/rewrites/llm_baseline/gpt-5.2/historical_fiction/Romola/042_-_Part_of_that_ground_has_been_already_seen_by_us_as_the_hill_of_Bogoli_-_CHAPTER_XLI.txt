“Rise, my daughter,” Fra Girolamo said at last, and his voice—spare as bread in a year of scarcity—seemed to draw a straight line through the hush of the hill. “Your servant waits not far off with the mules. It is time that I go onward to Florence.”

Romola rose from her knees. The stone had chilled them through the serge; the cold had crept, thread by thread, up her bones. Yet that very chill, accepted without flinching, had been to her like a consecration. Kneeling there, she had made her yielding into an act, and the act had bruised her will so thoroughly that even the smallest motion—whether to speak, whether to beckon—felt as if it must be ordered for her, lest she fall back into the old self-command that had led her to revolt.

She lifted the edge of her cowl. On the hill’s rim, ten yards away, Maso and another Dominican stood with their backs turned, as if the world behind them were a temptation they had agreed not to taste. Below, Florence lay muffled in wintry air: a city whose sounds rose even here, faint as a distant psalm—cart wheels softened by damp earth, a bell that seemed to ring through wool, the far cry of a vendor made thin by cold. Romola looked from them back to Savonarola without speaking. The summons to Maso, she felt, must come from him and not from her—like the blessing before a meal, without which even honest hunger feels unlawful.

“I will go and call them,” he said, answering the appeal in her eyes. “And I will recommend you, my daughter, to the brother who is with me. You desire to put yourself under guidance, and to learn that wisdom which has been hitherto as foolishness to you. A chief gate of that wisdom is the sacrament of confession. You will need a confessor; and I desire to put you under the care of Fra Salvestro, one of the brethren of San Marco, in whom I most confide.”

“I would rather have no guidance but yours, father,” Romola said. The words came quickly, like breath drawn when a door is suddenly opened on a draught: she wanted the warmth she had just felt near him to continue, as one wants the hand that has steadied a knife to remain upon the wrist until the cut is finished.

“My daughter, I do not act as a confessor. The vocation I have withdraws me from offices that would force me into frequent contact with the laity, and interfere with my special duties.”

“Then shall I not be able to speak to you in private? if I waver, if—” The sentence broke. A sharp fear rose in her—fear not of punishment, but of losing the one influence that had held her like a strong spice whose scent cannot be forgotten once it has entered the cloth.

“My daughter, if your soul has need of the word in private from my lips, you will let me know it through Fra Salvestro, and I will see you in the sacristy or in the choir of San Marco. And I will not cease to watch over you. I will instruct my brother concerning you, that he may guide you into that path of labour for the suffering and the hungry to which you are called as a daughter of Florence in these times of hard need. I desire to behold you among the feebler and more ignorant sisters as the apple-tree among the trees of the forest, so that your fairness and all natural gifts may be but as a lamp through which the Divine light shines the more purely. I will go now and call your servant.”

He turned away; his robe moved with the dry, disciplined sound of wool brushed by winter air. Romola heard his steps go—measured, unseduced by haste—and the small ecology of the hill came forward again: the whisper of bare twigs, the thin scratch of grit under a sandal, the breath of a mule somewhere below shifting its load and letting out a low, patient sound like a sigh held long.

When Maso had been sent a little way in advance, Fra Salvestro came forward, and Savonarola led Romola towards him. All along, her mind had been braced to obey what was hard rather than what was easy; to resist Savonarola’s counsel now would be to snatch back independence at the very moment she was living by its renunciation, as if a fasting person should suddenly demand delicacies in the name of freedom. Still, as she came nearer, she felt the old inward shrinking from a guide who was a stranger—unknown hands at the kneading-trough of her conscience.

She bowed reverently before looking at him; but when she raised her head and saw him fully, reluctance changed into a quick, uneasy doubt that fluttered at her heart.

There are men whose mere presence is a shelter—a hearth you trust because the smoke draws clean and the sound of the fire is steady. And there are others to whom one must bring trust ready-made, like food carried in a cloth to a house where nothing is set out. That difference struck Romola at once, as Savonarola’s stern, kindled countenance gave place to Fra Salvestro Maruffi.

It was not that Fra Salvestro was repulsive. His face was handsomer than Fra Girolamo’s, his person a little taller; his manner had practiced smoothness, as of one long accustomed to the confessional whispers of noble houses, where sins are told with a careful tongue. He had been accepted as director by many among the chief in Florence, and experience had given him the phrases of exhortation as a cook has the common receipts—how much salt of warning, how much oil of comfort, when to add the name of God as if it were saffron that must not be wasted.

But his features had the uncertain look of a mind that could not pour itself wholly into one great passion or belief. The muscles of his face seemed always ready to relax out of lofty meaning, as if prose insisted on coming when poetry was demanded, not because he lied, but because he could not be possessed. The expression was like diluted wine—still wine, yet unable to warm. To an ardent nature, such shallowness is more chilling than open harshness.

Fra Salvestro, too, had that peculiar liability to visions, born—so men said—of a constitution inclined to somnambulism. Savonarola believed in the supernatural character of these visitations, while Fra Salvestro had once resisted such an interpretation and even rebuked Savonarola’s prophetic preaching. In a world thick with marvels and credulities, that contrast was no measure of greatness: the greater spirit was the one that could use belief as a flame and not as a fog.

As Fra Salvestro began to address her—offering encouragement, urging her to patience and obedience—Romola felt a faint chill steal back over her. After her first angry resistance to Savonarola had melted, she had forgotten the old dread of being drawn into sour, monkish fanaticism. But now the fear returned, not as a command, only as a weather-change: like grey cloud closing over sunrise, making the road ahead look longer, more level, more without promise.

And perhaps of all sombre roads, the one most trying is that by which we go back after having trodden it with a strong resolution. It is easy to rush forward into the unknown; it is harder to turn again toward the familiar threshold, carrying in one’s hands the newly accepted burden.

When they re-entered the city gates, light snow began to fall. It did not come down with any loud insistence—no storm with a roaring voice—but softly, in innumerable small touches. The flakes made the air seem quieter than before; they damped the far city clamour, as if Florence were wrapped in linen. Romola, the grey sister, walked hastily homeward from the Piazza di San Marco; her steps made dark marks on the thin whitening of the stones, each footprint a brief stain, as if decision itself left traces that could not be hidden.

She trod the bridge again. Beneath, the river’s sound had changed with the cold—less garrulous, more secret, as water is when it runs under a skin of ice. She turned in at the large door in the Via de’ Bardi; the familiar hinges gave their accustomed complaint, and the house received her with its own enclosed silence—a domestic silence that held stored scents: old wood, faded herbs hung long ago, the faint remnant of lamp-oil. Her cowl, laden and damp, clung about her face; the wet wool had that raw sheep-smell that winter brings out, and it made her human, bodily, unromantic in the very hour when her spirit had tried to be pure flame.

She went up to her room. She threw off the serge. She destroyed the parting letters—paper that had been meant to travel, now fed to secrecy with quick hands. She replaced all her precious trifles, as one lays back small household relics in their right places—each object a syllable of memory in a language only she could speak. She unbound her hair. She put on her usual black dress.

Instead of taking a long, exciting journey, she was to sit down again in her accustomed place. The same walls; the same window; the same narrow portion of Florence framed in stone. The snow thickened against the panes, a white, soundless persistence; and she was alone.

She felt the dreariness—keenly, as the mouth feels plain bread after spiced meats. Yet her courage was high, like that of a seeker who has struck new signs of gold in a stream-bed and will endure cold fingers and numb feet for the promise of a gleam. She was going to thread life by a fresh clue. She had thrown all the energy of her will into renunciation.

The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino’s crucifix outside it—wood and carved pain set where the eye must meet it, as if to say that what she had shut away within herself would now be watched from without. Nothing broke the outward monotony of her solitary home until night came like a white ghost at the windows—paler than the snow, because darkness itself seemed diluted by the falling flakes.

Yet of all her days, this was to Romola the most memorable Christmas-eve: the year 1494, when the city’s bells rang through muffled air and hunger crouched in many doorways, and she sat in silence learning how large a life can be when it is reduced to one room, one conscience, and the sound of the world kept at a distance by snow.
