Dawning Hopes.

As Maso ushered in the two visitors, Nello stepped forward with Tito, leading him towards Romola with a gentle reverence. "Messer Bardo," he said, his voice measured and respectful, "I present to you Tito Melema, a Greek scholar whose erudition and storied collections have earned him a reputation throughout the land. His recent misfortune, compounded by a shipwreck, has left him in need of patronage and support." Tito's eyes, dark as the finest Tuscan olives, met Romola's, and for an instant, the world held its breath. Her gaze, initially wary, softened as she beheld the young scholar's radiant face, reminiscent of a spring morning bursting forth in the midst of winter's chill.

Romola's astonishment knew no bounds, for Nello had spoken little of Tito's age or appearance, and among her father's learned guests, she had rarely encountered men as young and vibrant as this stranger. Yet, her habitual reserve, forged from her father's tales of a world unfair and unjust, stood firm, like a wall of snow guarding against the warmth of admiration. As Tito bowed low, his rich-tinted skin glowed beneath the dark folds of his sajo, a garment that seemed woven from the very fabric of the Mediterranean night. It was as if a bouquet of wildflowers had been dropped into her bleak, book-lined existence, releasing a fragrance that hinted at distant lands, forgotten loves, and the promise of rediscovered joys.

Bardo's voice broke the spell, his words dripping with a mixture of condescension and curiosity. "Ah, Tito Melema, welcome. Misfortune entwined with learning, especially Greek learning, is a passport to the esteem of any cultured Florentine. Our city's scholars have long recognized the value of direct knowledge, unencumbered by the derivative teachings of the Latins. Since Manuelo Crisolora's illuminating presence in our city, nearly a century past, we hold scholars who merely parrot the classics in contempt, labeling them opici or barbarians. I, alas, am blind, a fate to which our fair city is peculiarly susceptible, due perhaps to the biting spring winds from the Apennines or the sudden transition between our cool, dim homes and the blinding sunlight of our summers."
