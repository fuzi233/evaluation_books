No penitence and no confessional, no priest to allot the penance—only the long compelled sitting amid the gray ash of what has burned down, while life still asks for breakfast and lessons and the small decencies of an afternoon.

Gadsmere had that look of old country comfort which, in another county, might have been praised as picturesque: the best of it built in sober gray stone, with a red-tiled roof softened by years of rain; a round tower thrusting itself out at one corner, capped with a conical darkness where a weathercock turned, not bravely, but with the patient compliance of metal submitted to wind. Great trees laid their shade over gravel and grass; a broken bank showed the naked grip of roots where Scotch firs clung; beyond, a rookery kept its harsh parliament. On the other side, a pool lay under bushes, and waterfowl made their sudden flutters and screams—sharp, living cries that cut across the November hush like a knife across crust.

But outside the gate, the country had been darkened into another element. What had once been hedgerow and pasture now wore coal like mourning: black roads, black mounds, and men emerging with candles fixed in their hats, their faces so stained that children, honest in their instincts, called them devils and drew close to nursemaids’ skirts. The new world sounded different too: the distant clank of iron, the low continuous grind, the occasional whistle that seemed to split the air for the sake of commerce—no birds’ music there, only industry insisting upon itself. At Gadsmere, the seclusion purchased by ugliness was exact to Mrs. Glasher’s taste. No carriages of neighboring gentry met her waggonet; no eyes at church were charged with old knowledge. The curate and his wife, whether ignorant or politely incurious, received her as a widow tenant. In that district, Grandcourt was a name less vital than Fletcher and Gawcome, lessees of the collieries—names spoken with the respect given to whoever makes the ground profitable, even if it must first be wounded.

It had been ten years since the elopement that had rippled through drawing-rooms like gossip stirred into tea: an Irish officer’s beautiful wife running away with young Grandcourt, and afterwards a duel in which the bullets found only air. Those who still remembered the affair, remembered it as they remembered a tragedy that had happened to other people at some foreign resort—interesting while the sound of it was fresh, then folded away. They supposed, naturally, that Grandcourt had tired of the woman; and they supposed, just as naturally, that a woman who had left child and husband had fallen lower. Grandcourt, in their imagining, lay safe in harbor—colors flying, seaworthy, ready for a suitable marriage with a noble daughter. Mrs. Glasher was a wreck no one sent out boats to find.

Yet the truth had never been so neat. He had not disentangled himself; he had merely let passion crack and sour like a flute whose music can no longer be trusted, leaving behind a dull habit of return. When her husband died three years ago, that habit had even prompted him now and then toward the old understanding spoken in the days when his ardor had been a religion—that he would marry her if the way were free. Once, in that early heat, he would have paid for a divorce; but the husband would not. He did not desire a second marriage, and he did not wish the evidence of his domestic habits paraded as print.

Time had altered Lydia in the opposite fashion from Grandcourt. At first, marriage had mattered to her less than escape and the intoxication of being loved with youth’s extravagance: to be out of a rough husband’s reach, to be clasped by a handsome lover who lived in the best style—equipage, talk, the easy insolence of men who have seen everything and are bored by most of it. She had wanted adoration; she had been made raw by five years of marital rudeness; and relief itself was so strong that it quieted anxiety about the future. An equivocal position, then, had seemed a small price for such sweetness. She did not envy the dull honors of a disregarded wife.

Only one bruise had lain under the velvet: the boy she had left, three years old, who died two years after; and in her memory the first tones of “mamma” from his lips kept a difference no later child could repeat—a dialect of tenderness now extinct. But as years went on, her cheek and throat changed; and with them changed the whole meaning of her life. Marriage with Grandcourt became not a romance but an urgent necessity—less for herself than for the children who sat at her knees like a debt she must pay back with interest. The shame that had once seemed hers alone now struck them. If he married her, the past could be folded, the children could stand in the world without disadvantage; her son could be made heir. That hope hardened into a single purpose so intense it could bear the look of tragic calm. She asked for nothing less. Grandcourt kept all gifts voluntary; he had said he would settle nothing except by will. Sometimes—when she forced herself to look at ugly alternatives—she thought: even if he never married her, he might die without a legitimate son; her boy might yet be named heir to the best of his estates. The child had, at that early age, too much of his father’s physique to be overlooked.

Lush had even believed the marriage possible; and when he fancied Grandcourt meant to win Miss Arrowpoint at Diplow, he had fanned that project as a tacit renunciation of Lydia. But Gwendolen Harleth’s appearance had altered all those airy calculations; and it had been easy for Lydia, with passion stored like venom, to enter into Lush’s plan of frightening the girl away by setting a barrier in her mind.

After that, Lydia heard from Lush that Gwendolen had gone, and danger was likely over; but no one wrote to tell her that danger had returned and hardened into certainty. Lydia, as she did habitually, had written to Grandcourt; he had delayed his answer longer than usual. She guessed he might come to Gadsmere; she could not help hoping—hope, that stubborn cook, will season any scrap into a feast—that a disgust from frustrated courtship might make him slide back into the old intention as into a familiar chair.

Grandcourt, when he came, carried two grave purposes with him: to deliver news of his approaching marriage in person, making the first difficulty final; and to retrieve his mother’s diamonds, confided long ago to Lydia and worn by her with an effect he had liked—diamonds that, on her, looked as if they were worth the money paid. They were not mountains of light: peas and little haricots for ears, neck, and hair; but worth thousands, and he meant them for his wife. Once, asking for them back on the pretext of safety and a bank, he had been refused with Lydia’s quiet absolutism. “If you ever marry another woman I will give them up to her: are you going to marry another woman?” At that time he had not persisted. In him there was a peculiar forbearance toward Lydia: the old power she had had over him did not die, it merely changed its form, leaving him disinclined to provoke her. A harder interpreter might say that her dependence was a standing banquet to his love of domination; but even he had kept that propensity oddly asleep in her presence. Now, however, novelty had taken hold of him, and urged him toward its own completion.

Mrs. Glasher sat in the room where she spent her mornings, the children gathered as naturally as crumbs gather birds. A square projecting window looked on broad gravel and grass that sloped toward a little brook running into the pool. The top of a low black cabinet, an old oak table, chairs in tawny leather, were all littered with toys, books, and the small garments of garden and play. On the wall a maternal lady in pastel smiled down as if indulgence were an inherited property.

The children were all there. The three girls sat near their mother by the window—dark-eyed, delicate-featured little brunettes with rich bloom on their cheeks, their tiny nostrils and brows finished with the precision of miniatures. The eldest, Josephine, was barely nine. The boy, Henleigh, sat on the carpet at some distance, bending his fair head over the animals from a Noah’s ark, admonishing them with fierce little commands, then licking the spotted ones to see whether the colors would hold—testing the world with his tongue, as if truth were always, in some degree, a taste.

Josephine was at her French lesson; the two younger girls, dolls in their laps, had the demure stillness of Madonnas painted small. Mrs. Glasher’s dress had been made with care. Each day now she said to herself that Grandcourt might come. Her head—emaciation notwithstanding—kept a beauty that could not be effaced: fine profile, crisp curves of hair, eyebrows clearly marked as if drawn with resolve. Above bronze-colored silk and velvet, the gold necklace he had clasped round her years ago lay against the hollowness at her throat like a memory that would not melt.

She did not take pleasure in her toilet. The mirror offered her one recurring sentence: How changed. Yet the remnant of good in life—what could be held—she would hold. If her chief wish were fulfilled, she could imagine taking on again the comeliness of a matron fit for the highest rank. The little faces beside her, almost exact reductions of her own, told of blooming curves where her pallor now was. But they kissed her cheeks and found no deficiency. Their love—warm, unquestioning, hungry as young mouths—was the one end of her life.

Suddenly she turned her head from Josephine’s book and listened. Sound at Gadsmere was intimate: you could tell footsteps by the way gravel yielded, could hear a gate-latch’s complaint, could distinguish the soft scramble of dogs from the heavier tread of a stranger. Even the distant collieries had their own bass note, like a far organ held down.

“Hush, dear,” she whispered. “I think some one is coming.”

Henleigh sprang up. “Mamma, is it the miller with my donkey?”

He repeated the question, insistent, at her knee. She did not answer. The door opened, and the servant announced Mr. Grandcourt.

Mrs. Glasher rose, agitated as if her blood had received an order. Henleigh frowned at the disappointment of no donkey; the little girls lifted timid eyes to the visitor. They had never loved this friend of mamma’s. When Grandcourt took Lydia’s hand and then reached to lay his other hand on Henleigh’s head, the boy beat his arm away with small fists, indignant at being touched without consent. The girls submitted bashfully to being patted beneath the chin and kissed, but there was relief when Lydia sent them into the garden. Soon they were out on the gravel, dancing, chatting, the dogs leaping and circling—light sounds and quick breaths making a brief music against the November barrenness.

“How far are you come?” Lydia asked, as Grandcourt put away hat and overcoat.

“From Diplow,” he said slowly, seating himself opposite her. He looked at her with a gaze so unnoting that she felt the omission like a pinch.

“You are tired, then.”

“No. I rested at the Junction—a hideous hole. These railway journeys are always a confounded bore. But I had coffee and smoked.”

He drew out his handkerchief, rubbed his face, and when he returned it to his pocket he glanced down at his crossed knee and blameless boot, as if any stranger sat opposite rather than a woman quivering with suspense. Yet he was not absent; he was measuring, arranging, anticipating—the way a man does who means to pass through disagreeable business with the least possible stain.

“I expected to see you,” Lydia said. “It was so long since I had heard from you. I suppose the weeks seem longer at Gadsmere than they do at Diplow.” Her speech had a quick incisive edge, like the bright strain of a violin whose body is shaped for sharpness.

“Yes,” Grandcourt drawled. “But you found the money paid into the bank.”

“Oh, yes,” she said curtly, tingling. Always before—at least she believed it—he had noticed her and the children more than he did today.

“Yes,” he went on, playing with his whisker, not looking at her at first. “The time has gone on at rather a rattling pace with me; generally it’s slow enough. But there has been a good deal happening, as you know.” He turned his eyes on her then.

“What do I know?” she said sharply.

He paused, then said without change of manner, “That I was thinking of marrying. You saw Miss Harleth?”

“She told you that?” Lydia’s cheeks whitened further; the fierce brightness in her eyes made the pallor look lit from within.

“No. Lush told me.”

It was like watching the tools of torture laid out—thumb-screw and iron boot—by hands that moved with careful patience.

“Good God! say at once that you are going to marry her,” Lydia burst out. Her knees shook; her hands clasped tight as if to keep her bones from coming apart.

“Of course, this kind of thing must happen some time or other, Lydia,” he said, and there was even a shade of unwillingness to drive the screw too fast. “I see it now.”

In those few undertoned words she felt resistance as absolute as iron. She knew how useless beseeching was with him: crying into a dead ear, clinging to dead knees, only to meet an immovable face and rigid limbs. She did not weep. She did not speak. The certainty came with a chill sickness that made thought and feeling swim together. Hope, defeated, left her with a horrible bodily sensation—like a clutching hand suddenly empty.

At last she rose with a spasmodic effort and pressed her forehead against the window-glass, hard and cold. In the garden the children saw and mistook: running forward, sweet faces upturned, they stood expectant as if called. The sight roused her. She shook her head, waved them away, and with the painful exertion sank into the nearest chair.

Grandcourt had risen too, leaning against the corner of the fireplace. He was annoyed—by the scene, and by the fact that no imperiousness could save him from it. Still, the business had to be done, and he must arrange matters for future quiet.

Lydia looked up at him and said bitterly, “All this is of no consequence to you. I and the children are importunate creatures. You wish to get away again and be with Miss Harleth.”

“Don’t make the affair more disagreeable than it need be, Lydia. It’s of no use to harp on things that can’t be altered. Of course, it’s deucedly disagreeable to me to see you making yourself miserable. I’ve taken this journey to tell you what you must make up your mind to—you and the children will be provided for as usual—and there’s an end of it.”

Silence. She dared not answer. Mother’s anguish had put iron into her soul, and sometimes it enabled a repression harder than shrieking. But under the silence there sprang hatred, vindictiveness: she wanted the marriage to bring wretchedness on others too, not only herself. Presently he continued, slow and administrative:

“It’ll be better for you. You may go on living here. But I think of by-and-by settling a good sum on you and the children, and you can live where you like. There’ll be nothing for you to complain of then. Whatever happens, you’ll feel secure. Nothing could be done beforehand. Everything has gone on in a hurry.”

He stopped, as if that were a reasonable conclusion. He did not expect thanks, but he thought she might be contented—if contentment were possible for Lydia. She did not change. After a minute he said,

“You’ve never had any reason to fear that I should be illiberal. I don’t care a curse about the money.”

“If you did care about it, I suppose you would not give it us,” Lydia said, and the sarcasm came out like a spasm.

“That’s a devilishly unfair thing to say,” he answered, voice lowered. “And I advise you not to say that sort of thing again.”

“Should you punish me by leaving the children in beggary?” The one outlet of venom had opened the other.

“There is no question about leaving the children in beggary,” he said, still low. “I advise you not to say things that you will repent of.”

“I am used to repenting,” she said bitterly. “Perhaps you will repent. You have already repented of loving me.”

“All this will only make it uncommonly difficult for us to meet again. What friend have you besides me?”

“Quite true.”

The words fell like a low moan. And in the same instant, like a flash that shows a landscape only to make it darker after, she imagined him miserable and lonely with his new wife, driven back to her by emptiness, craving some memory of being young, glad, hopeful. But no—he would go scathless. It was she who must bear the ash.

After that, the scorching words ended, not because the fire was out, but because both knew speech only widened the wound. Grandcourt had meant to stay until evening; he wished to curtail the visit, but there was no earlier train than the one he had arranged. Hours had to pass. There was eating to be done; the children came in and out; servants moved with the careful deafness of good training. All that mechanism of life went on with the dreary constraint felt in household quarrels, when the teacups clink too loud and the ticking clock seems like mockery.

To Lydia it was a slight relief to have the children present: their loveliness rose in her like a savage pride, as if their faces could taunt him with what he was throwing away. She watched him watch them, hungry for some sign—any softening, any hesitation. He acquitted himself with the ease of a man whose grace has been molded by long practice in boredom. He nursed little Antonia, who sat with hands crossed and eyes upturned at his bald head with solemn curiosity; and he propitiated Henleigh by promising a beautiful saddle and bridle. Only the two eldest girls had known him as a continual presence; now intervening years had covered their infant memory with bashfulness that his bearing would never dissolve.

He and Lydia, when servants were near, exchanged occasional conventional remarks, as if words were merely furniture to fill an emptiness. Otherwise they did not speak. In Grandcourt’s mind all the while lay a stagnant irritation: his own folly in giving her those diamonds, which now obliged him to ask for them back. He had a deep care for what belonged to his caste; about property he liked to be lordly. And he hated the consciousness of needing to ask anyone for anything. Yet, whatever independence he asserted, he had forged for himself a past that pulled harder than any yoke he could fasten on others. He had promised the diamonds to Gwendolen. He must have them.

At last they were alone again, candles lit above them, facing each other with the room’s dim corners pressing inward. Grandcourt looked at his watch, then said in a tone made indifferent by practice, “There is one thing I had to mention, Lydia. My diamonds—you have them.”

“Yes, I have them,” she answered promptly, rising. She stood with her arms thrust down, fingers threaded tight, while he sat still. She had expected this; during the long hours she had shaped her resolve, and now she meant—if possible—to carry it out without exasperating him. She was aching to take back her earlier words, not from softness but from strategy: they had only widened the breach.

“They are in this house, I suppose?” he said.

“No; not in this house.”

“I thought you said you kept them by you.”
