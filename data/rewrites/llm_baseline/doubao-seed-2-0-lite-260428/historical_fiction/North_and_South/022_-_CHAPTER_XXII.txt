“But work grew scarce, while bread grew dear,
And wages lessened, too;
For Irish hordes were bidders here,
Our half-paid work to do.”

Margaret Hale was ushered into the Thorntons’ drawing-room, the space fallen back to its usual state of dust sheets and furniture covers after the small party of days prior. The summer heat hung thick and heavy, so the windows were propped half open, Venetian blinds pulled down over the glass to block the direct glare—all that filtered through was a grim, gray reflected glow bouncing off the paved street below, twisting every shadow into unnatural shape, tinged green by the slats of the blinds. When Margaret caught her own reflection in the wall mirror, she looked ghastly and wan, her face sallow as unglazed porcelain. She sat waiting, no one coming to greet her. At intervals, a distant, low, multitudinous murmur seemed to drift closer, though there was no wind to carry it; it faded away again into a profound, eerie stillness that hummed in her ears.

At last Fanny Thornton bustled in, cheeks pink and her bonnet askew.
“Mamma will be with you directly, Miss Hale, she bade me apologise for the delay. I expect you’ve heard my brother brought over new labourers from Ireland, haven’t you? The whole of Milton’s turned savage over it, as if he hadn’t every right to hire labour where he can find it. Those stupid, greedy oafs around here refused to work for his offered rate, so now they’ve threatened these poor starving Irish folks so fiercely we daren’t let them step foot outside the mill grounds. You can see them huddled in that top back room of the mill, and they’re going to sleep there tonight to keep them safe from the brutes who won’t work themselves and won’t let any other soul work either. Mamma’s sorting out their food right now, and John’s in there speaking to them—some of the women are crying their eyes out, begging to go back home. Oh, here’s mamma now!”

Mrs. Thornton crossed the threshold with a face set in lines of black, unyielding sternness, and Margaret realised instantly she had arrived at the worst possible moment to press her request. Still, she had only come in answer to Mrs. Thornton’s own repeated invitation to call on them for any assistance she needed while her mother lay ill. She spoke gently, modestly, explaining how her mother had been restless and wracked with pain, and Dr. Donaldson had prescribed a water-bed to ease her symptoms.

She fell silent. Mrs. Thornton did not reply at once. Then she started to her feet with a sharp cry.
“They’re at the gates! Call John, Fanny—run fetch him out of the mill! They’re battering the gates down! Call him, I say!”

At the very same moment, the heavy, trampling tramp of hundreds of feet that Mrs. Thornton had been straining to listen to, ignoring Margaret’s every word, swelled loud just outside the mill wall. A din of angry, wordless voices raged behind the heavy wooden gates, which shook as if the maddened crowd were using their own bodies as battering rams, drawing back a few paces before throwing their full weight against the barrier again. The strong iron-bound gates quivered like thin reeds in a gale under the force of their blows.

Every woman in the house clustered at the windows, transfixed even as they were terrified: Mrs. Thornton, the two housemaids, Margaret all pressed close to the glass. Fanny had already fled, screaming up the stairs as if hounds were at her heels, and thrown herself onto the sofa in the upper parlour, heaving in hysterical sobs. Mrs. Thornton kept her gaze fixed on her son, who was still inside the mill yard. He stepped out of the factory door, looked up at their pale, frightened faces grouped at the window, and gave them a smile meant to steady their courage before he locked the factory door behind him. He called up, bracing his voice, for one of the women to come down and unlock the house’s front door, which Fanny had slammed and bolted behind her in her mad dash upstairs. Mrs. Thornton herself went to do it. The sound of his deep, commanding voice drifting out the door acted on the crowd like the taste of blood to a pack of wolves. Until that moment they had been silent, pouring all their breath into the work of breaking down the gates, but now they let loose a fierce, unearthly groan so raw with hatred that even Mrs. Thornton went white with fear as she stepped back into the parlour ahead of her son.

Thornton stepped inside, a faint flush on his cheeks, his eyes blazing bright as if the call of danger had woken something fierce and noble in him. He looked prouder, taller, a man made grander by defiance even if he was not conventionally handsome. Margaret had always feared she would prove a coward in any real emergency, but now, faced with very real, tangible terror, she forgot her own fear entirely, swept up in a sharp, painful sympathy for every soul trapped in the mess: Thornton, the Irish strangers, even the ragged, starving men raging outside.

Thornton stepped forward frankly.
“I am dreadfully sorry, Miss Hale, that you have walked right into this catastrophe. I fear you may be caught up in whatever risk we have to bear here. Mother—would it not be wiser to move everyone to the back rooms? I cannot be certain the crowd hasn’t already broken through from Pinner’s Lane into the stable yard. You will be far safer back there than at these front windows. Go, Jane!” he added, addressing the head housemaid, and she hurried away, the other servant trailing after her.

“I will not budge,” his mother said flatly. “Where you stand, I stand.” Retreat to the back rooms would have done no good anyway: the crowd had already circled the outbuildings at the rear, and their awful, threatening roar boomed from behind the house too. The two maids fled up to the garrets, screaming and wailing as they went. Thornton smiled a thin, scornful smile as he heard their cries. He glanced over at Margaret, who stood alone by the window nearest the mill. Her eyes were bright, a high flush had risen on her cheeks and lips. She felt his gaze, and turned to ask the question that had been turning over in her head for minutes.
“Where are those poor imported workpeople? Are they inside the factory?”

“Aye. I left them huddled up in a small closet at the top of a back staircase, told them to bolt themselves in there and run for the back exit if they heard the mob break through the mill doors. But it is not the Irish they want. It is me.”

“How long until the soldiers get here?” his mother asked, her voice low but steady.

Thornton pulled out his pocket watch with the same calm composure he brought to every part of his work, did a quick mental calculation.
“Assuming Williams ran straight for the barracks the moment I told him to, and did not have to weave and dodge through the crowd, it will be at least twenty minutes yet.”

“Twenty minutes!” his mother echoed, and for the first time, terror crept into her tone.

“Shut the windows at once, mother,” he exclaimed sharply. “Those gates will not survive another full blow from the crowd. Miss Hale, shut that window, please.”

Margaret pulled the sash down, then crossed to help Mrs. Thornton fumble with the fastenings, her fingers trembling.

For several long, endless minutes, the noise outside fell completely quiet. Mrs. Thornton stared wild-eyed at her son’s face, trying to read the meaning of that sudden stillness in his expression. His features were set into rigid lines of contemptuous defiance, no trace of hope or fear visible there.

Fanny lifted her head from the sofa where she had been sobbing.
“Have they gone?” she whispered.

“Gone?” Thornton said. “Listen.”

They all heard it then: one great, straining collective heave, the slow, groaning creak of wood giving way, the sharp wrench of iron bolts tearing free, and the great, thunderous crash of the massive gates falling flat to the ground. Fanny tottered to her feet, stumbled a step or two towards her mother, and fainted dead away in her arms. Mrs. Thornton lifted her up with a strength born as much of iron will as physical force, and carried her out of the room.

“Thank God for that,” Thornton muttered, watching her go. “Miss Hale, had you not better go upstairs with them?”

Margaret’s lips formed the word No, but he could not hear her speak over the thunder of hundreds of pairs of feet tramping right past the house wall, and the low, deep, ferocious murmur of angry voices that hummed with a horrible, satisfied greed far worse than their frustrated roars of minutes before.

“Do not be afraid,” he said, trying to reassure her. “I am very sorry you have been trapped in all this alarm, but it will not last much longer. A few more minutes, and the soldiers will be here.”

“Oh God!” Margaret cried out suddenly. “That is Boucher! I know his face, even though he is livid with rage. He is pushing and shoving his way to the very front of the crowd—look, look!”

“Who is Boucher?” Thornton asked coolly, stepping up to the window to peer at the man Margaret was pointing out. The moment the mob caught sight of Thornton’s face at the window, they let loose a yell so inhuman it sounded like the snarl of a wild beast denied the food it has been ravening for. Even Thornton drew back for a second, staggered by the sheer intensity of the hatred he had roused.

“Let them yell,” he said. “In five more minutes—. I only hope those poor Irish folk do not get scared out of their wits by that fiendish noise. Keep your courage for five more minutes, Miss Hale.”

“Do not worry about me,” she said hastily. “But can you do nothing at all to soothe these poor people? It is awful to watch them suffer like this.”

“The soldiers will be here directly, and that will bring them to their senses.”

“Bring them to their senses?” Margaret said quickly. “What kind of sense?”

“The only kind that works on men who have turned themselves into wild beasts. By heaven! They are turning for the mill doors!”

“Mr. Thornton,” Margaret said, shaking all over with the force of her passion, “go down this instant, if you are no coward. Go out and face them like a man. Save those poor strangers you invited here. Speak to your workmen as if they are human beings. Speak to them kindly. Do not let the soldiers come in and cut down men who are half driven mad by hunger. I see one of them right there who is. If you have any spark of courage or honour in you, go out to them, speak man to man!”

He turned and looked at her as she spoke, a dark, heavy shadow crossing his face, his teeth clenched tight.
“I will go. Perhaps I can ask you to follow me downstairs, and bar the front door behind me. My mother and sister will need that extra protection.”

“Oh, Mr. Thornton! I do not know—maybe I am wrong—only—”

But he was already gone, thudding down the stairs to the hall, unbolting and unbarring the heavy front door. All Margaret could do was rush after him, slam the door shut and bolt it behind him, then stumble back up the stairs with a sick, heavy heart and a dizzy head. She took her place again at the farthest window. There he was, standing on the front steps, hundreds of angry eyes fixed on him. She could not see his face, nor could she hear anything above the low, rolling murmur of the crowd’s savage satisfaction.

She wrenched the window wide open. Most of the men in the crowd were just boys: cruel, thoughtless boys, cruel precisely because they were too young to think through the consequences of their actions. The rest were men, gaunt as starving wolves, wild for relief. Margaret knew exactly what drove them. They were like Boucher, with starving children waiting at home, and they had counted on winning higher wages by holding out together—only to find Irish men brought in to steal the bread right out of their little ones’ mouths. She could read that desperation written all over Boucher’s face, forlorn and livid with rage. If only Thornton would speak to them, let them hear his voice, it would be better than this wild, useless beating against the stony silence that gave them not even a word of anger or reproach.

For a second the noise fell quiet, still and wordless as the hush before a pack of wolves charges. Margaret tore off her bonnet, leaned far out of the window to hear. She saw it all then: Thornton had tried to speak, but the momentary hush of attention had already passed, and the crowd was raging worse than ever. He stood with his arms folded across his chest, still as a marble statue, his face pale with repressed fury. The men were jeering at him, trying to make him flinch, each urging the next on to some sudden act of personal violence. Margaret knew instantly that the violence was only seconds away: the first touch would spark an explosion, and among hundreds of infuriated men and reckless boys, even Thornton’s life would not be safe. Even as she watched, lads at the back of the crowd stooped to yank off their heavy wooden clogs, the readiest missiles they could lay their hands on. That was the spark to the gunpowder. With a cry no one could hear, Margaret ran out of the room, down the stairs. She hefted the great iron bar off the front door with a strength that felt like it did not belong to her, wrenched the door wide open, and stepped out face to face with that roaring sea of angry men. Her eyes blazed at them, sharp with reproach. The clogs hung arrested in the lads’ hands. Faces that had looked fiendish a minute before now looked confused, uncertain, wondering what on earth a fine lady was doing stepping out into the middle of their riot.

She could not speak for breath at first, only held her arms out towards them until she could catch her voice.
“Oh do not use violence! He is one man, and you are hundreds,” she called out, but her voice was hoarse, no more than a whisper, her words barely carrying. Thornton had stepped a little to one side, moving out from behind her as if he resented anything standing between him and the danger he was meant to face.

“Go!” she cried again, this time her voice ringing out like a sob. “The soldiers have been sent for—they are on their way. Go peaceably. Leave now. You will get relief for whatever grievances you have, I promise you.”

“Will you send those Irish blackguards back home, then?” a man shouted from the crowd, his voice thick with threat.

“Never, at your bidding!” Thornton roared back. The storm broke at once. Hoots and jeers rose to fill the air, but Margaret did not hear them. Her eyes were fixed on the group of lads who had armed themselves with their clogs. She saw their arm drawn back, she knew what they meant to do. If that clog hit Thornton, he would be struck down dead. She did not think of herself at all, only of saving him. She threw her arms around him, using her own body as a shield between him and the crowd. He shook her off roughly, his arms still folded.

“Go away,” he rumbled, his voice deep and hard. “This is no place for a woman.”

“It is,” she said. “You did not see what I saw.” She had thought her presence, her sex, would stop the crowd, make them pause, think, slink away ashamed. She was wrong. Their reckless passion had carried them too far to turn back, the cruel, excitable boys at the front leading the charge, reckless of what blood they spilled. A clog whizzed through the air. Margaret watched it fly, her eyes fixed. It missed its mark, and she turned sick with fright, but did not move an inch, only buried her face against Thornton’s arm.

“For God’s sake!” she called out to the crowd. “Do not ruin your own cause with this violence. You do not know what you are doing.” She was forcing her voice to be clear and steady, when a sharp pebble flew through the air, grazing her forehead and her cheek. A blinding sheet of white light burst before her eyes, and she slumped like a dead weight against Thornton’s shoulder.

He unfolded his arms then, holding her for a single second against his chest.
“You do well!” he roared at the crowd, his voice shaking with rage. “You come to turn out innocent, starving strangers. You hundreds, against one lone man, and when a woman steps out to beg you for your own sakes to act like reasonable human beings, your cowardly wrath falls on her! You do well!”

The crowd went dead silent. They stared, open-mouthed and wide-eyed, at the thin thread of dark red blood trickling down her face, the sight jolting them straight out of their maddened trance. The men nearest the gate slipped away, ashamed. A ripple of retreat moved through the whole mob. Only one voice called out as they fled:
“That stone was meant for thee! Thou wert only hiding behind a woman!”

Thornton quivered with rage. The flow of blood had roused Margaret to faint, dim consciousness. He laid her gentle down on the front door step, her head resting against the frame.
“Can you rest there for a moment?” he asked. Without waiting for an answer, he stepped slowly down the steps, right into the middle of the last lingering knots of the crowd. “Now kill me, if that is your brutal will. There is no woman to shield me here. You may beat me to death—you will never, ever make me change my mind on this matter. Not you!” He stood amongst them, his arms still folded across his chest, exactly the same defiant pose he had held on the steps.

The retreat turned into a full, panicky flight. Maybe it was the sound of the soldiers’ boots clanking in the distance, maybe it was the sight of Margaret’s pale upturned face, still and still as marble, tears seeping out through her tangled eyelashes, and the slower, heavier drip of blood falling down from her temple.
