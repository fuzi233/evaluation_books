The Virginian colonel waited in the dim-lit tavern chamber, his thoughts heavy as he prepared for the impending duel. Across the hall, his young adversary, George Warrington, dictated a letter to his brother Harry—a grand, solemn farewell to their mother, laced with bitter irony.

“*As probably will be the case,*” George declared, his voice cold.

Harry’s quill faltered. “Don’t say that, Georgy!”

“But it is the truth,” George replied. “Colonel Washington is a marksman. And I? Well, I can hold my own. One of us may fall—likely both.” His tone sharpened as he added, “*I scarcely suppose you will carry out the intentions you have at present in view.*”

Harry’s eyes stung. He smudged the page, ink and tears mingling. “I can’t write properly—not now!”

With an impatient sigh, George took the quill himself. His words grew loftier, laced with Latin flourishes and cutting wit. He willed freedom to his servant Sady, his books to his tutor, his silver fruit-knife to little Fanny Mountain. And to Harry—a lock of his hair, a keepsake of a brother’s love. He sealed it with their grandfather’s crest.

Then came the watch—their grandfather’s gold timepiece. George pressed it into Harry’s hand. “It’s yours now.”

Harry flung his arms around him. “If he fights you, I’ll fight him too! If he kills you, by God, I’ll—” His voice broke into curses, raw and desperate.

Meanwhile, Colonel Washington had written his own letters—one to his mother, one to his brother, one marked *M.C.*, and one to General Braddock. And, as the tavern landlord informed the boys with a grim smile, one for Madam Esmond herself.

George’s face darkened. Another oath tore from his lips—one that would surely trouble the celestial scribes.

Outside, the world moved on, indifferent. The landlord, in his old militia coat, announced that the Colonel waited in the garden, pacing like a man already haunted. The English officers, roused from their drink, watched with bleary interest.

Then—chaos. A thunder of hooves, wild shouts. Sady, George’s servant, came galloping back, pistols in hand, whooping like a madman. Behind him, riding hard, came Mr. Dempster and—*Mrs. Mountain?*

She stumbled from her horse, breathless, and seized George in a frantic embrace. “It was a mistake! All my fault!”

George stiffened. “What mistake?”

“That paper—the one about the Colonel marrying a widow with two children—I thought it meant your mother! But no, it’s *Mrs. Custis*! He told Madam so himself today!”

Silence.

Harry exhaled. “Thank God.”

George’s jaw tightened. “Sady disobeyed me. I told him to keep silent.”

“Thank Heaven he didn’t!” Harry cried.

Then Harry was running—across the garden, to where Washington stood. Words poured out, clumsy and earnest. And the Colonel, after a moment’s stillness, pulled him into an embrace. “Thank God,” he murmured.

When George approached, the greeting was stiff, formal.

“I wronged you, Colonel,” George admitted. “I apologize—not for the error, but for my conduct since.”

Washington’s reply was curt. “The fault was not yours alone. Had my private papers remained private, much pain might have been spared.” He turned to Harry, clasping his shoulder. “Be less quick to think ill of friends, George. We meet again at camp—save your fire for the enemy.”

With a final bow to the English officers, he mounted his horse and rode away—leaving behind a silence thick with shame and relief.
