In the evening she sent for him again, as though the day could not close upon her without his presence to hold it in some shape that would not break her. The hour was near that in which, the night before, they had brought her in from the sea; and the room, with its blinds half-drawn and the windows open, had that subdued, salt-damp coolness which makes even a lamp-flame seem to breathe softly. Beyond, the sea lay in a long grey quiet, and from it came a low, incessant wash—like a careful hand forever smoothing a sheet—broken now and then by the creak of a rope, the faint clink of a ring, the far, thin call of some boatman. Those sounds, natural and ancient, should have been a kind of peace; yet to her they were as if the world insisted on continuing, indifferent to the one moment where her will had turned black.

She sat gazing fixedly outward, her cheek on her hand. She looked less shattered than when he had left her, but in her face there was a melancholy so deep that, as he drew near, it changed into an anxious timidity—as if she were afraid of her own voice and what it might carry into the air.

She did not put out her hand. “How long ago it is!” she said, and then, after a breath that trembled like a caught sob, “Will you sit near me again a little while?”

He placed himself at her side as he had done before, and because he saw that she turned to him with that look which means a wish to speak—something hovering on the edge of words—he waited. Again she looked toward the window, silent; again she turned with the same helpless expression, and still nothing came. Fear held her as if it had a physical grip. To ease her he averted his face, making a little space for her courage to enter.

Presently he heard her cry, imploring, as if the words were torn out of her—“You will not say that any one else should know?”

“Most decidedly not,” said Deronda. His voice, in the hush of the room, seemed to fall among the sea-sounds like a steady oar-stroke. “There is no action that ought to be taken in consequence. There is no injury that could be righted in that way. There is no retribution that any mortal could apportion justly.”

She was so still afterwards that it seemed she was holding her breath to keep herself together, before she whispered, “But if I had not had that murderous will—that moment—if I had thrown the rope on the instant—perhaps it would have hindered death?”

“No—I think not,” he said, slowly, as if he would not allow himself the comfort of easy assurance. “If it were true that he could swim, he must have been seized with cramp. With your quickest, utmost effort, it seems impossible that you could have done anything to save him. That momentary murderous will cannot, I think, have altered the course of events. Its effect is confined to the motives in your own breast. Within ourselves our evil will is momentous, and sooner or later it works its way outside us—it may be in the vitiation that breeds evil acts, but also it may be in the self-abhorrence that stings us into better striving.”

The sea breathed on, and the faint noises of the place—so much older than their misery—seemed to her like witnesses that could not be bribed into silence. She spoke again, hesitating, as if she feared he might suspect her even where she was trying to clear herself.

“I am saved from robbing others—there are others—they will have everything—they will have what they ought to have. I knew that some time before I left town. You do not suspect me of wrong desires about those things?”

“I had not thought of them,” said Deronda. “I was thinking too much of the other things.”

“Perhaps you don’t quite know the beginning of it all,” she said, slowly, as if each syllable were a step on ground that might give way. “There was some one else he ought to have married. And I knew it, and I told her I would not hinder it. And I went away—that was when you first saw me.” The words made a narrow bridge back into her past; her eyes did not leave the dim line of the water. “But then we became poor all at once, and I was very miserable, and I was tempted. I thought, ‘I shall do as I like and make everything right.’ I persuaded myself. And it was all different. It was all dreadful. Then came hatred and wicked thoughts. That was how it all came. I told you I was afraid of myself.”

Her fingers, lying on the chair-arm, moved as if they were feeling for some thread to wind round her and hold her in. “And I did what you told me—I did try to make my fear a safeguard. I thought of what would be if I—” She stopped, and when she went on her voice had that strange, strained clearness which comes when the mind is haunted and cannot sleep. “I felt what would come—how I should dread the morning—wishing it would be always night—and yet in the darkness always seeing something—seeing death.”

Outside, some distant sound—perhaps a shutter touched by wind—made a sharp little clap. She started, then went on hurriedly, as if she must pour the whole thing out before her courage failed. “If you did not know how miserable I was, you might—but now it has all been no use. I can care for nothing but saving the rest from knowing—poor mamma, who has never been happy.”

Silence fell, heavy as a closed door; then she said, with a repressed sob that seemed to shake her from within, “You cannot bear to look at me any more. You think I am too wicked. You do not believe that I can become any better—worth anything—worthy enough—I shall always be too wicked to—” Her voice broke helplessly, as if even language refused her at the end, leaving only that raw sound of a creature asking not to be cast out.

Deronda’s heart was pierced. He turned his eyes on her poor beseeching face and said, “I believe that you may become worthier than you have ever yet been—worthy to lead a life that may be a blessing. No evil dooms us hopelessly except the evil we love, and desire to continue in, and make no effort to escape from. You have made efforts—you will go on making them.”

“But you were the beginning of them.” She leaned forward, clasping her hands on the arm of her chair as if she would hold him there by the mere force of need; her face bore piteous traces of a whole life’s change crowded into twenty-four hours—this new terrible life lying on the other side of the deed that fulfills a criminal desire. “You must not forsake me,” she said. “I will bear any penance. I will lead any life you tell me. But you must not forsake me. You must be near. If you had been near me—if I could have said everything to you, I should have been different. You will not forsake me?”

“It could never be my impulse to forsake you,” said Deronda promptly—his voice and eyes having that ready tenderness which, without intending it, makes sympathy seem like a promise meant for one soul alone. And in that instant he was not himself quite free from a foreboding: he felt how words spoken to keep a despairing creature from sinking might bind him later as if they were vows. Strong pity would not hinder the rushing images of future difficulty. Yet he met her appealing eyes and spoke on, with the painful consciousness that to her ear his words might carry a pledge which one day would seem unfulfilled—an indefinite promise to an indefinite hope.

Anxieties, both immediate and distant, crowded on his thought; and under their pressure, after a moment’s silence, he said, “I expect Sir Hugo Mallinger to arrive by to-morrow night at least; and I am not without hope that Mrs. Davilow may shortly follow him. Her presence will be the greatest comfort to you—it will give you a motive to save her from unnecessary pain.”

“Yes, yes—I will try.” Her assent came quickly, as if she caught at any task that could be called duty. “And you will not go away?”

“Not till after Sir Hugo has come.”

“But we shall all go to England?”

“As soon as possible,” he said, not wishing to enter into particulars.

She looked toward the window again, and her face altered with an expression like a gradual waking—like someone who, after being stunned, begins to feel the bruises and remember the blow. The twilight was deepening; the sea-sound seemed louder by contrast, and every faint creak and distant call entered the room as if it had a right to be heard. Deronda could see a movement in her eyes and hands that belonged to returning perception.

“You will always be with Sir Hugo now!” she said presently, turning and looking at him. “You will always live at the Abbey—or else at Diplow?”

“I am quite uncertain where I shall live,” said Deronda, coloring.

The changed color warned her that she had spoken too rashly; she fell silent, as if she had put her foot upon a hidden sore. After a while she began again, looking away, her voice low and drained: “It is impossible to think how my life will go on. I think now it would be better for me to be poor and obliged to work.”

“New promptings will come as the days pass. When you are among your friends again, you will discern new duties,” said Deronda. “Make it a task now to get as well and calm—as much like yourself as you can, before—” He hesitated.

“Before my mother comes,” said Gwendolen.

“Ah! I must be changed.” Her hand went up, not quite touching her face, as if she dreaded the feel of her own skin. “I have not looked at myself. Should you have known me,” she added, turning toward him, “if you had met me now?—should you have known me for the one you saw at Leubronn?”

“Yes, I should have known you,” said Deronda, mournfully. “The outside change is not great. I should have seen at once that it was you, and that you had gone through some great sorrow.”

“Don’t wish now that you had never seen me; don’t wish that,” she said, imploringly; and as the tears gathered, her voice took on a childlike terror—of being erased, of being judged unworthy even of memory.

“I should despise myself for wishing it,” said Deronda. “How could I know what I was wishing? We must find our duties in what comes to us, not in what we imagine might have been. If I took to foolish wishing of that sort, I should wish—not that I had never seen you, but that I had been able to save you from this.”

“You have saved me from worse,” said Gwendolen, sobbing. “I should have been worse if it had not been for you. If you had not been good, I should have been more wicked than I am.”

“It will be better for me to go now,” said Deronda, worn in spirit by the perpetual strain—as if the room’s salt air and the sea’s tireless breath had been passing through him, taking strength away drop by drop. “Remember what we said of your task—to get well and calm before other friends come.”

He rose, and she gave him her hand submissively, as if obedience could keep him from vanishing. But when he had left her she sank on her knees, breaking into hysterical crying—the body at last uttering what the words had been forced to discipline. The distance between them was too great. She was a banished soul, beholding a possible life which she had sinned herself away from.

They found her crushed on the floor in that posture of grief which seemed natural enough in a poor lady whose husband had been drowned in her presence—while the sea outside went on murmuring its old speech, indifferent to the names and stories men give to their dead.
