When the Dashwood family first arrived in Devonshire, they had not expected their days to fill so swiftly with invitations, gatherings, and social engagements that there would be little time left for quiet reflection or serious pursuits. Yet such was the reality. As Marianne regained her strength, Sir John’s long-planned entertainments came to life—private balls at Barton Park, excursions on the water whenever the autumn weather permitted. In every gathering, Willoughby was present, his easy charm and natural familiarity making him a constant companion to the Dashwoods. These occasions only deepened his connection with Marianne, offering him ample opportunity to admire her spirit, to express his growing affection openly, and to receive from her the most unmistakable signs of her own devotion.

Elinor could not deny the sincerity of their bond. But she wished it were less visible. On more than one occasion, she gently urged Marianne to exercise some restraint. To Marianne, however, such caution seemed both unnecessary and unfeeling. She believed that when feelings were pure and honorable, concealment was not only unjust but a surrender to convention—a submission to the dullness of society’s false rules. Willoughby shared this belief, and their behavior reflected it plainly. When he entered a room, her attention was entirely fixed upon him. Everything he said seemed brilliant; everything he did, admirable. At card games, he subtly ensured she received the best hand—even if it meant playing unfairly. When dancing was the evening’s delight, they danced together half the night, and when separated for a few rounds, they stood side by side, exchanging glances and whispers, scarcely speaking to anyone else. Their conduct drew laughter from others, but neither shame nor irritation touched them. Ridicule rolled off like rain from a stone.

Mrs. Dashwood, warm-hearted and emotionally open, embraced their passion without reservation. To her, their display was simply the honest expression of young love—natural, even noble. For Marianne, this was the happiest season of her life. Her heart belonged wholly to Willoughby, and the deep attachment she once held for Norland began to fade under the glow of his presence. The new home, once foreign and incomplete, now felt enchanted by his company.

Yet Elinor found no such joy. Though she smiled and participated in the festivities, her heart remained heavy. None of these new acquaintances could replace what she had lost—the familiar voices, the quiet corners of Norland, the comfort of old memories. Lady Middleton offered little in conversation, though her silence was at least unburdensome. Mrs. Jennings, on the other hand, spoke ceaselessly—her stories repeated over and over, as if each retelling brought her fresh delight. By now, Elinor had heard the full account of Mr. Jennings’s final illness, word for word, including his last words to his wife, which she recited with solemn gravity. But beyond that, there was little depth.

Lady Middleton, while quieter than her mother, lacked any real warmth or thought. Her manner was calm, but it was the stillness of emptiness—not wisdom. She spoke the same things day after day, her spirits unchanged, her interest in anything beyond her children negligible. Though she allowed her husband’s plans to proceed, provided they were done with proper formality, she seemed no more engaged in the pleasures of the evening than if she had stayed at home. Her presence added nothing to the conversation, and often the others forgot she was there—until one of her restless boys caused a commotion and reminded them of her watchful care.

Only one man among all the new faces stirred Elinor’s respect: Colonel Brandon. He possessed a quiet dignity, a thoughtful nature, and a sense of integrity that made him a true companion. While Willoughby captivated Marianne, he remained distant from Elinor—not because he lacked charm, but because his affections were already claimed. And yet, in his quiet conversations with Elinor, she sensed a depth of feeling that went beyond mere politeness.

It was during one evening at the park, as they sat apart while others danced, that he revealed something unexpected. His gaze lingered on Marianne, and after a moment of silence, he spoke with a faint, wistful smile. “Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments.”

“No,” Elinor replied, “she holds romantic ideals.”

“Or rather,” he continued, “she believes them impossible.”

“I believe she does,” Elinor said. “Though I wonder how she reconciles that with her father, who had two wives himself.”

A pause. Then she added, “But perhaps time will bring her to a more balanced view—one grounded in reason and experience.”

“It may,” he said softly. “And yet there is something tender in the convictions of youth—so innocent, so pure—that one regrets seeing them yield to the world’s colder judgments.”

Elinor shook her head. “I cannot agree. There are risks in such feelings—blindness to duty, disregard for propriety. Marianne’s principles, though charming, can lead to harm. It is only through experience that she will learn to temper her heart with wisdom.”

He fell silent for a moment, then asked quietly, “Does your sister make no exceptions? Is a second attachment always wrong—no matter the circumstances? Must those who have suffered disappointment in love remain indifferent forever?”

“I don’t know the details of her thinking,” Elinor admitted. “But I’ve never heard her allow even one case where it might be forgiven.”

“That cannot be right,” he murmured. “Yet sometimes change comes not by choice, but by necessity. A complete transformation of the heart—” He stopped abruptly, as if realizing he had spoken too much. His face darkened slightly, and Elinor saw the flicker of pain beneath his composure.

She said nothing. But in that moment, she understood—without being told—that he had loved someone once, deeply, and that love had been lost. Perhaps it had ended in sorrow, or betrayal, or fate’s cruel twist. He had known heartbreak, and now, in his quiet regard for Marianne, she glimpsed the echo of a past that had shaped him.

Had Marianne been listening, she would have imagined the whole story—every detail, every tragic turn—filling in the gaps with dramatic certainty. But Elinor, more reserved, let the silence speak for itself. And in that stillness, she felt a deeper compassion for the colonel than she had before.
