We Compared Notes

Our gazes lingered on the _cortege_ until the wheels vanished into the churning mist of the woods, the rhythmic clatter of hooves swallowed whole by the suffocating silence of the night. Nothing remained but the girl, who suddenly blinked open her eyes. Her face was turned away, but she raised her head, searching the darkness with evident confusion. A voice, sweet and trembling, cut through the air, laced with complaint: "Where is Mamma?"

Madame Perrodon answered gently, offering words of comfort and reassurance. Then the girl's questions became sharper, disoriented. "Where am I? Where is this place? I cannot see the carriage! And Matska? Where is Matska?"

Madame answered what she could understand. Gradually, fragments of the nightmare coalesced in the girl's mind. She learned that no one aboard the carriage—or among the attendants—had been harmed. But when she realized her mother had abandoned her there, promising a return in three months, tears streamed down her pale cheeks.

I stepped forward to offer my own consoling words, but Mademoiselle de Lafontaine caught my wrist, pulling me back. "Do not approach," she whispered urgently. "One person at a time is all she can handle now. Too much excitement could break her completely."

*Once she is safely in bed,* I thought, *I must go to her chamber.*

Meanwhile, my father had dispatched a servant on horseback to fetch the physician, who resided two leagues distant, while a bedroom was scurried ready for the stranger. As the woman rose, leaning heavily on Madame's arm, she shuffled slowly across the drawbridge and through the castle gates. Servants awaited in the hall, guiding her immediately to her quarters.

Her room—the space we used as our drawing room—was long, dominated by four windows overlooking the moat and the desolate forest scene I had just witnessed. It was furnished in heavy, old carved oak, filled with massive cabinets. The chairs were upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet, and the walls were draped in tapestries framed in vast gold borders. These depicted ancient, curious figures engaged in hunting, falconry, and wild festivities. It was a stately yet undeniably comfortable space. Here, later that night, we drank tea. My father, with his usual patriotic fervor, insisted that the national beverage accompany our coffee and chocolate.

Sitting by candlelight, the group discussed the evening's terrifying adventure. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine were present, but the young stranger had already succumbed to a deep slumber, left in the care of a servant.

"How do you like our guest?" I asked as Madame entered. "Tell me everything."

"I like her extremely," Madame replied. "She is, I almost believe, the prettiest creature I have ever seen. About your age, so gentle, so sweet."

"She is absolutely beautiful," interjected Mademoiselle, who had peeked inside the stranger's room. "And what a voice!"

"Did you notice anything else?" Mademoiselle asked. "A woman in the carriage, after it started again, who didn't exit? Someone who merely stared from the window?"

"No," I admitted. "We saw no one."

Then she described a horrid figure: a black woman wearing a sort of colored turban, gazing relentlessly from the carriage window. This figure nodded and grinned derisively at the ladies, her eyes gleaming, her whites enlarged and stark, her teeth clenched in a mask of fury.

"And the servants?" Mademoiselle asked. "Did you notice what an ill-looking pack of men they were?"

"Yes," my father said, stepping into the circle. "Ugly, hang-dog fellows as I have ever beheld in my life. I pray they do not rob the poor lady in the forest. Yet, they are clever rogues; they restored everything to order in a single minute."

"They must be exhausted from such long travel," Madame suggested.

"But beyond their wicked looks, their faces were strangely lean, dark, and sullen," my father continued. "I am very curious, I own. Perhaps the young lady will explain tomorrow, if she is recovered."

"I doubt she will," my father said, a mysterious smile playing on his lips, accompanied by a slight nod as if he knew secrets he preferred to keep.

This cryptic comment only fueled our curiosity regarding the brief, earnest interview that had occurred between him and the lady in black velvet just prior to her departure.

Scarcely alone, I implored him to speak. He required little persuasion.

"There is no particular reason why I should not tell you," he began. "She expressed a reluctance to burden us with the care of her daughter, citing delicate health and a nervous constitution. However, she volunteered explicitly that she was not subject to seizures nor to any illusions—in fact, she declared herself perfectly sane."

"How odd to say that!" I interrupted. "It seemed unnecessary."

"At least it _was_ said," he laughed softly. "Since you wish to know the full account, which was indeed brief, let me recount it. She stated, 'I am making a long journey of _vital_ importance—I emphasized the word—rapid and secret.' She promised to return for her child in three months, and in the meantime, she would remain silent as to who we were, whence we came, or whither we traveled. That was all. She spoke very pure French. When she uttered the word 'secret,' she paused for several seconds, staring sternly, her eyes locked onto mine. I suspect she places immense weight on that concept. You saw how swiftly she departed. I hope I have not committed a foolish act in taking charge of the young lady."

For my part, I was delighted. I had been longing to see and converse with her, merely waiting for the physician's approval. Those who dwell in towns cannot imagine how monumental an event the introduction of a new friend becomes in such profound solitude.

The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o'clock, but I could no longer have retired to sleep than I could have overtaken the carriage of the princess in black velvet on foot. When the physician descended to the drawing room, his report was remarkably favorable. His patient was sitting upright, her pulse regular, appearing perfectly well. She had sustained no injuries, and the minor shock to her nerves had passed harmlessly. With his explicit permission, I immediately sent to inquire if she would allow me to visit her for a few moments.

The servant returned instantly, confirming that she desired nothing more.

I need not delay in availing myself of this permission.

Our visitor lay in one of the handsomest chambers in the _schloss_. Perhaps it was slightly too stately. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a somber piece of tapestry depicting Cleopatra with the asp coils around her bosom; other solemn classical scenes, now a little faded, adorned the remaining walls. Yet, the gold carvings and rich, varied colors of the other decorations more than redeemed the gloom of the ancient hangings.

Candles burned at the bedside. She sat upright, her slender, pretty figure enshrouded in a soft silk dressing gown embroidered with flowers, lined with thick quilted silk—the garment her mother had thrown over her as she lay upon the ground.

What struck me dumb, causing me to recoil a step or two as I approached the bedside, was the sight before me. It was the very face that had visited me in my childhood nightmares, the visage fixed in my memory, one upon which I had ruminated with horror for years, hidden from suspicion.

It was pretty, even beautiful, wearing initially the same melancholy expression I remembered. But almost instantly, it lit into a strange, fixed smile of recognition.

A silence of a full minute stretched between us, broken only when she finally spoke. I could not.

"How wonderful!" she exclaimed. "Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since."

"Wonderful indeed!" I repeated, struggling to overcome the sudden suspension of speech induced by a flash of old horror. "Twelve years ago, in vision or reality, I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since."

Her smile softened. Whatever strangeness I had detected vanished, replaced by a delightful, intelligent beauty. Her dimpling cheeks became warm.

Feeling reassured, I continued in the vein of hospitality, welcoming her warmly and expressing how much pleasure her accidental arrival had brought to all of us, particularly what a happiness it had been for me. I took her hand as I spoke. Shy, as lonely people often are, the situation made me eloquent, even bold. She pressed my hand, laying hers upon it, her eyes glowing as she looked hastily into mine, smiling again and blushing.

She responded to my welcome with charming grace. Sitting beside her, still bewildered, she told me of her vision concerning me.

"It is so very strange," she said, "that you and I should have had such vivid dreams of one another, each seeing the other exactly as we appear now, when of course we were both mere children."

"I was about six years old," she continued.
