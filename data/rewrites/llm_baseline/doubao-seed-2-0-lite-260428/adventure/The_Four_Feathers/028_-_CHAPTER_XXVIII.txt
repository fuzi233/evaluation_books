For three whole days, Feversham drifted in and out of fevered delirium, his speech a jumble of disjointed, half-mad memories. For every one of those days, Trench tended to him uncomplainingly: trekking down to the Nile to fetch him cool, fresh water, splitting his own meagre dhurra rations, fending off ravenous fellow prisoners who would have stolen even the scraps meant for the sick, helpless man. For three nights running too, he stood shoulder to shoulder with the loyal guard Ibrahim, fending off violent drunks and raving madmen that roamed the dark corridors of the House of Stone, to keep the unconscious Feversham from harm.

On the fourth morning, Feversham woke sharp to his senses, blinked, and found Trench’s face bending over him. At first he mistook it for one of the nightmare visions that had haunted his boyhood nights, the faces that swelled too large and crept too close out of the dark, and he pushed the weak arm reaching for him away. But when he gazed around, the scene sank in: he was lying in the dappled shadow of the prison walls, the hard, unforgiving blue sky stretched overhead, brown, trampled bare dust was under his back, and all around him fellow prisoners dragged heavy iron chains, or lay prone in the throes of fever and starvation. He scrambled to grab Trench’s wrist, as if scared the man would vanish the next instant, and a relieved, soft smile spread over his face.
“I’m in the Omdurman prison,” he breathed. “Actually here. Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It feels too good to be true.”

He leaned back against the wall, heavy with relief that Trench at first mistook for bitter irony. He thought only a man who posed as completely numb to all joy and pain could speak that way – but Feversham’s three days of delirium had proven he was no such man. This satisfaction was real. The peril of Dongola was behind him, he had found the man he had crossed hundreds of miles of desert to reach, and he was exactly where he had fought so hard to get. He could have been strung up on a gibbet miles down the Nile, circled by vultures, his entire life’s purpose unfulfilled. But he was here, inside the thorn zareeba of the House of Stone.
“You’ve been here a long time,” Feversham said.
“Three years.”

Feversham scanned the ragged, chain-dragging figures around the enclosure. “Three whole years. I was terrified I’d find you long dead.”
“The nights are the worst,” Trench said quietly. “The nights locked inside that stone building. It’s a wonder any man survives a week of them. I’ve survived a thousand. Even to me, that sounds impossible.”
“But we get to walk to the Nile during the day, right?” Feversham jolted upright, suddenly alarmed as he stared at the sharp thorn fence surrounding them. “An Arab at Wadi Halfa told us we’re allowed that much liberty.”
“It’s true,” Trench said, pointing to the earthen bowl of water at his side. “I filled that at the river at dawn.”
“Then I have to go this morning,” Feversham said, rising unsteadily to his feet, and Trench hissed a quiet warning.
“Hush. There are scores of prisoners here, and half of them are tale-bearers. If they see two men talking in earnest, word will reach Idris within the hour, the Khalifa before sunset. We’ll get heavier fetters and a lashing with the courbatch before the sun dips low.”
Feversham sank back to the ground, half from weakness, half to obey the warning. They napped side by side until noon, Trench’s face slack in sleep showing every deep mark of the three brutal years he had spent in the prison camp.

When the zareeba gate swung open at last for prisoners’ families and friends to bring their daily rations, the enclosure turned into a cage of starving wild beasts. The thirty gaolers, each armed with a rhino-hide courbatch whip, took the largest share of the moist, pounded dhurra cake called aseeda at the gate, leaving barely enough scraps to keep a man breathing. The strong fought the weak for every crumb, groups of four battled groups of three for a single handful of food, gaunt men hopping as fast as their chains would let them towards the entrance. One starving man tripped and fell, and lay still in the dust, knowing no food would be left for him that day. Others tore rations straight from the hands of the bearers, ignoring the lashes that sliced open their backs.
Hidden at the back of the crowd, a small boy gazed around the zareeba irresolutely. He was quickly spotted, knocked down, and his food stolen – but his screams for help brought the head gaoler Idris-es-Saier running to chase off his attackers. When Idris asked who he had come to see, the boy said “The Greek, Joseppi,” and Idris pointed him straight to Feversham’s corner. He knelt beside Feversham, chattering loudly in Arabic about the theft of his rations, while he slipped a gazelle skin hidden under his jibbeh loose from his waist and left it on the dust beside the man. Inside was a whole chicken, enough for Feversham and Trench to eat their fill for the entire day.

An hour later, they were permitted to leave the zareeba and walk slowly down to the Nile, stopping every few steps to rest. When they were far enough from the gaolers that no one could overhear them, Trench spoke.
“You told me everything over those three days you were delirious,” he said. “I know you didn’t get captured by accident on the Arbain road, pretending to be a mad wandering zither-player from Wadi Halfa. You came here on purpose, to find me. Because five years ago, I sent you a white feather.”
Feversham froze, alarmed. “What else did I say?”
“About a girl,” Trench said quietly, not looking at him. “The one you brought up when we dined with Willoughby and Durrance in London, long ago. I know her name now. She was there when the three of us sent you the feathers. She plucked a fourth one from her fan and added it to the pile. I’m sorry. We never thought of that.”
Feversham shook his head, smiling softly. “I’m not sorry. That fourth feather is what got me through three years in Suakin, when all I wanted to do was slip onto a boat back to Suez, when I saw all of you going about your regimental work every day. Without that feather, I never would have walked out into the desert to reach Berber, I never would have made it all the way here to Omdurman. I don’t blame you, and I don’t blame her.”

For a long minute, they both gazed out over the Nile, at the crowds of shouting Arabs and negroes unloading dhows on the muddy banks, at the line of palm trees marking the far shore of Khartum against the cloudless western sky. Trench held his hand out to Feversham.
“I take my feather back now,” he said.
Feversham shook his head. “Not yet.”
Hope, a hope Trench had spent three years trying to crush to save himself from heartbreak, flared bright in his chest. “Not yet? So you have an actual escape plan? When you were delirious you never breathed a word of it, did you?”
“Not a single one,” Trench said. Feversham exhaled in relief – he had been terrified he had rambled about the plan in Dongola, where a former resident of Khartoum who spoke broken English had been sent to question him, when his fever had almost made him spill every secret. He had deliberately focused all his wandering thoughts on Ethne, to keep the escape details locked tight in his mind.
“Camels are waiting for us ten miles west of Omdurman right now,” Feversham said, grinning. “Rifles, ammo, water, and provisions are buried near them. There are more fresh camels waiting for us at Metemneh, more at Berber, and we ride the Kokreb pass all the way to Suakin. I just need to get my strength back enough to sit a camel for a week.”
He nodded towards a man in a blue linen robe loitering halfway between them and the gaol. That was their agent, waiting for a month for Feversham to give the signal to move.
“Even easier than digging out of the zareeba wall,” Trench laughed, pointing to a chained prisoner who had tripped and fallen face-first in the shallow water, struggling uselessly to get back up under the weight of his load and his shackles. “We bribe Idris. He’ll come to you soon, lecture you about the mercy of Allah for bringing you to the holy city of Omdurman, warn you about the magic spy spirit Nebbi Khiddr that reports every prisoner’s move to the Khalifa, then end with a long, sad speech about his starving wife and children. If you give him fifteen dollars of the money he stole from you, he’ll let you sleep outside the stone prison in the open zareeba. Then we bribe him a second time to let me sleep out there with you.”

The plan worked exactly as Trench predicted. That very afternoon, Idris cornered Feversham for an overlong lecture about the destruction of Hicks Pasha’s army at the hands of the Mahdi’s angels, then begged him for money for his starving family. Feversham handed over the fifteen dollars, and that night he slept out in the open air, free of the stinking, crowded stone prison. Two nights later, Trench was lying beside him, staring up at the blazing, bright southern stars.
“Three more days,” Feversham murmured.
Trench’s voice was thick with emotion when he spoke. “I’m not a marrying man, so this isn’t that kind of sentiment. But all these years in this hell, all I’ve wanted is to die at home. Not just in England, but in my little village, buried under the trees I grew up climbing, where I can see the old church and the trout stream I fished every day as a boy. I’m so scared I’d die here, and never get back.”
Feversham said nothing, but Trench could tell he was not laughing. “I understand,” he said quietly.

For hours that night, side by side under the open sky, they talked. They talked about the sharp cry of “Mark!” on English shooting estates, the rattle of a fishing reel on the banks of a cold northern trout stream, the old West End plays they had seen six years before, the books they had read in their barracks mess. The shouts and screams from inside the House of Stone sounded faint and far away, muffled by the dark. They did not notice when the tail of the Great Bear dipped below the thorn hedge of the zareeba, or when the cool desert night began to turn cold. They were not prisoners in Omdurman anymore. They were home, walking the lanes they knew, beneath their old familiar dim, small northern stars.
“Only three more days,” Trench said, half asleep.
“Only three more days,” Feversham agreed, and a minute later, both men slipped into deep, peaceful sleep, the stars marching unnoticed over their heads towards dawn.
