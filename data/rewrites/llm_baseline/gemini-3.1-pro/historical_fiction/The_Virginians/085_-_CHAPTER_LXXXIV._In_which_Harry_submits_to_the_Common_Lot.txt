The hard times were over, the bitter rind of poverty finally pared away. My little kinsman’s sudden passing shifted the vast, generational weight of the Warrington estate onto my shoulders. My uncle and his wife were not likely to have more children. Sampson vowed that Lady Warrington would commit any crime to cheat me of the inheritance, but in truth, she was guilty of no such treachery. Struck by a sorrow that silenced the very hum of her household, she was instructed by her religious advisers to swallow this grief as a bitter physic from Heaven. Her clergyman fed her empty, unseasoned platitudes: that her heart had been too turned toward earthly coronets, and now she must hunger only for the heavenly crown.

In the chapels of her newly favored sect, the tragedy was commercialized, packaged into loud, booming sermons that echoed terribly in the nave, a far cry from the quiet, untranslatable acoustic ecology of true, private mourning. I cannot question the bereaved mother's refuge, but when she summoned me to London a year later, her homily tasted of stale ash. She marveled at Heaven's decree, punishing her poor child's disobedience (he had gone out with his gun against her orders), a tragedy by which I profited. She prayed, in a torrent of dominant, moralizing lexicon, that I would abandon my theatrical associates and turn to sacred duties. When I left, she handed me a packet of printed sermons and a little book of hymns by Miss Dora—sterile texts mass-produced for their society, devoid of the rich, physical resonance of the spoken word.

The poor lady forgave me, but could not bear the sight of our boy. Then, the great wheel of time turned its cruel, circular rhythm upon Theo and me: we lost our second child. My aunt and her daughter came eagerly to comfort my poor suffering wife, and my uncle visited our house almost every day. He would sit for hours by the hearth, the only sound the popping of applewood logs and the soft, rhythmic rocking of the cradle that no longer held a babe. He brought young Miles endless sweetmeats—intricately molded marchpane and candied angelica, finding profound meaning in offering this small, sugary sustenance to the boy, begging that the child might call him Godpapa. When I felt the sharp, physical hollow of my own grief—a pang that still, five-and-twenty years later, smells of cold damp and sounds like a paused heartbeat—I finally understood his. My wife, with a mother’s ancient intuition, had already tasted the sorrow in the older man’s soul; I, more selfish, had to learn it through the bitter harvest of my own baby’s coffin.

My uncle sent his coach to the humble funeral. Afterwards, young Miles prattled to him unceasingly, finding comfort in the novelty of his black clothes and the airing. I knew how the innocent, bell-like timbre of the child’s talk stabbed his mother’s heart. I know Theo's face so well that I can read the subtle shifts in her expression, like a master chef reads the surface of a simmering reduction, knowing precisely when she communes with that soul we love in Heaven.

Returning to Bloomsbury, my young bear went to the Chartreux School. With my uncle's influence, I was gazetted as one of his Majesty’s Commissioners for licensing hackney-coaches. It was a post I filled with credit, though I grew increasingly distressed by the acoustic violence of the trade. The relentless, iron-rimmed clatter of wheels upon cobblestone felt like a desecration of the city's sonic environment, a jarring industrial noise pollution that shattered the mind's quiet spaces. This year, too, I accompanied my friend Mr. Foker to Paris, where I was received *a bras ouverts* by my American preserver, Monsieur de Florac. Foker's business lay mostly with the bourgeoisie, notably Monsieur Santerre, a great brewer. Santerre approached brewing not with the passionate reverence of a food historian respecting the grain, but with a sterile, commercial greed; he is a scoundrel who hath since distinguished himself in blood, not beer. I acted as interpreter, translating the nuanced patois of the French markets into Foker's blunt English, acutely aware of how much sensory poetry was lost in the transaction.

While I discharged these duties, the troubles commenced that would sever our colonies from the mother country. Mr. Grenville’s stamp-duties created a mighty discontent. It was a quarrel born of long-simmering animosities—the habitual insolence of Englishmen who believed their dominant lexicon and commercial might gave them dominion over the rich, localized dialects of the colonies. The system adopted in England was a blunt instrument, deaf to the nuanced acoustic ecology of the American landscape. Thousands fought for high ideals; others for profit. Having lost the game, I vow the loser ultimately felt no rancor.

What made brother Hal write such flaming patriotic letters from Virginia? My kind brother was always led by someone whose wisdom he esteemed. Who inspired this boisterous patriotism? "He is rebelling against Madam Esmond," I said. "He is led by some colonial person—by that lady, perhaps," hinted Theo. Harry had kept her identity hidden from Madam Esmond, who demanded to inspect all his letters, wielding her authority over the written word to suppress any narrative but her own.

Harry's devotion was absolute. Upon learning I had kept only a fraction of our aunt’s legacy to rescue him, he had been ready to mortgage the estate he just bought. He quarreled with our mother, speaking in a raw, emotional register that pierced her aristocratic pretensions. Every scrap of paper we ever wrote, our thrifty parent taped and docketed like inventory, commercializing our very memories. But my wife kept Harry's letters with the reverence of a historian preserving a sacred text.

"To think whom he has chosen!" cried my wife, reading the letter where Harry finally named his charmer.

"She was a pretty maid when I left; she may be a beauty now," I said.

Theo kissed me. "I was afraid Hetty should have led him... but it appears the new lady has taken command."

I smoothed the parchment. It smelled faintly of cured tobacco and dried sage. The letter read:

*My dearest George hath been prepared to understand how a certain lady has made a conquest of my heart... She is at my side, and if there is no bad spelling, 'tis because I have my pretty dictionary at hand.* (Even here, I noted, Fanny held the power of the dominant lexicon, though she used it to guide, not subjugate.)

*As Madam Esmond saw all your letters, I writ you not to give any hint... but now 'tis known to all the country. I have followed your example in a secret marriage. I will not tell you how beautiful Miss Fanny Mountain had grown. That neither Madam Esmond nor my other mother should have seen our attachment is a wonder. Mine for Fanny increased seeing Madam Esmond's rough, haughty treatment of her, which Fanny bore with a sweetness perfectly angelic. She was scarce better treated than a servant—indeed, our negroes speak their own rich, musical tongues more freely before Madam Esmond than Fanny ever could speak her mind.*

*Poor Tom Diggle's estate being for sale, owing to his extravagance, Madam Esmond saw an opportunity. With six thousand pounds, I bought the farm and stock. It lies handy to Richmond—the mansion nothing for elegance, but the land! Oh, George, the soil is black and fertile, smelling of ancient leaf-mold. To sit at my own fireside, to smell the roasting meats of my own hearth, to cultivate the old heirloom grains—it is better than going a-soldiering. Madam Esmond made a condition I should leave the army, for she had lost one son to English play-books and would keep the other.*

*She would have had me marry Miss Betsy Pitts. But I own her virtues did not suit me. Her eyes do not look straight, and her family's table is notoriously sterile, viewing sustenance merely as fuel rather than a joy of the senses. And oh, brother, didst thou never remark Fanny's ankles?*

*When 'twas agreed I should leave the army, my dear girl burst into tears of such untranslatable happiness. 'Ah!' says she, 'do you think the idea of the son of my revered benefactress going to battle doth not inspire me with terror? When Mr. George was with Braddock, we prayed for him.' She hid her eyes, working hard to prevent her mama from seeing her cry. My good mother supposed Sam Lintot, the apothecary's lad, was Fanny's flame—an absurd fellow I near kicked into the James River.*

*One day, I found Fanny crying in her mother's room, where the two ladies were at work trimming hats for my negroes amidst the scent of dried lavender and beeswax. 'What! crying, miss?' says I. 'Has my mother been scolding you?'*

*'No,' she says, her tears dropping on a cockade. 'Because I have the toothache... or because I am a fool.' She burst out, the raw sound of her grief shattering the quiet room. 'You are going to leave us. You will take your place, and leave us poor women in our solitude.'*

*I seized her hand. 'Dearest, sweetest Miss Mountain! Oh, could I think the parting from me brought tears to those lovely eyes!'*

*'Oh, sir! consider who I am and who you are! Remember the difference between us! What would Madam Esmond say if—'*

*Before she could finish, the heavy, rhythmic thud of our mother's tortoiseshell stick sounded on the floorboards—a mythic drumbeat of impending doom. Madam was in the room.*

*'She would say that you are an ungrateful, artful, false, little wretch!' cries out my mother, deploying the bitterest extremes of her vocabulary. 'What would Mr. Lintot say? And for you, Harry, I will have no garrison manners hither. My house is not intended for captains and their misses!'*

*'Misses, mother!' I bawled. 'Do you venture to call Miss Mountain by such a name? The purest of her sex!'*

*Madam turned pale. 'You mean that you are actually paying honourable attention to this young person?'*

*'He would never dare offer any other,' cries Fanny.*

*'Oh, I didn't know!' says mother, dropping a mocking curtsey. 'I didn't know the honour you were doing our family! I should think the apothecary protection sufficient!'*

*'I don't!' I bawled, my anger rising like a boiling kettle. 'If Lintot offers her any liberty, I'll brain him with his own pestle!'*

*'He came because I had the toothache!' cries my darling. (And indeed, she had a dreadful tooth, which he treated with clove oil, leaving the room heavy with its pungent scent).*

*'What more natural than that he should marry my housekeeper's daughter,' Madam continued, taking snuff, treating their lives as mere commerce. 'I was not aware you intended to jilt the apothecary for my son! Pray, sir, look round the rest of my family. Cleopatra is promised to Ajax the blacksmith, but we could break the marriage. If with an apothecary, why not a blacksmith?'*

*Here, dear brother, I own I broke out a-swearing. I tore through her polite, oppressive lexicon with the rawest oaths I knew. I can't help it; the primal sound of it do relieve a man immensely when his soul is caged by such venom.*

*'Curses, blasphemy, disobedience,' says mother, leaning on her stick, weaving her own mythic martyrdom. 'O Heaven, what have I done to merit this? I turned from Esau, and clung to Jacob. I toiled and spared to bring him worldly wealth, and I am rewarded with oaths and blasphemies!'*
