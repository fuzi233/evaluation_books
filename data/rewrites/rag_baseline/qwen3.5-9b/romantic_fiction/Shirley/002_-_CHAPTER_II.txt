The evening had descended into a suffocating obscurity, a pitch-black void where the stars and moon were extinguished beneath a canopy of slate-gray clouds. Even by day, these clouds wore a somber hue, but under the cover of night, they transformed into a sable shroud, swallowing the light entirely. Malone, however, was not a man inclined to scrutinize the subtle dialectics of nature; the shifting moods of the earth and sky passed him by, largely unnoticed. He could traverse miles on a variable April morning without witnessing the celestial dance between soil and atmosphere—the moment a sunbeam kisses a hilltop to reveal a verdant smile, or when a shower weeps over the landscape, veiling its crests in the disheveled tresses of passing clouds. Consequently, he felt no compulsion to contrast the current sky—a muffled, streaming vault of ink, broken only by the tremulous, lurid shimmer of Stilbro' ironworks furnaces glowing toward the east—with the serene clarity of an unclouded frosty night. He did not trouble himself to locate the vanished constellations or lament the loss of the "black-blue" serenity of the air-ocean, punctuated by those white islets of stars, now replaced by a heavier, denser ocean rolling below and concealing the heavens. With a dogged persistence, he pressed onward, leaning slightly forward in his stride, his hat worn rakishly on the back of his head, a distinctively Irish affectation. *Tramp, tramp* echoed along the causeway, where the road afforded such a privilege; *splash, splash* followed through the mire-filled cart ruts, where the dry flags yielded to soft, sucking mud. His gaze fixed solely on specific landmarks: the distant spire of Briarfield Church, and further still, the flickering lights of Redhouse. This was an inn, and as he approached, the warm glow of a fire filtering through a half-drawn curtain, the vision of glasses arranged on a round table, and the silhouettes of revelers lounging on an oaken settle, nearly diverted the curate from his solitary path. He sighed with longing at the prospect of a tumbler of whisky-and-water. In a stranger land, he would have instantly embraced this dream; but the company assembled in that kitchen were Mr. Helstone's own parishioners, faces familiar enough to offer no sanctuary. He exhaled a weary sigh and continued his journey.

The main highway now had to be abandoned, for the remaining distance to Hollow's Mill could be significantly shortened by cutting directly across the fields. These fields were level and monotonous, stretching out like a flat, endless plain. Malone chose a direct course, leaping effortlessly over hedges and walls. He passed but one building in this stretch, a structure that appeared large and hall-like despite its irregularity. A high gable rose above, followed by a long front, then a low gable, capped finally by a thick, lofty stack of chimneys. Trees stood sentinel behind it. Darkness reigned absolute; not a single candle illuminated any window. The only sounds were the relentless rain dripping from the eaves and the somewhat wild, yet very low whistle of the wind circling the chimneys and threading through the branches.

Having passed this edifice, the fields, previously flat, began a rapid descent. Clearly, a valley lay below, through which the murmur of running water could be faintly heard. A single light glimmered in the depths. For that beacon, Malone steered his course.

He arrived at a small white house, visible even through the dense gloom, and knocked upon the door. A fresh-faced servant opened it, revealing a narrow passage that terminated in a steep staircase. Two doors covered in crimson baize, a strip of crimson carpet running down the steps, contrasted sharply with the light-colored walls and white floor, lending the tiny interior an appearance of cleanliness and freshness.

"Mr. Moore is at home, I suppose?" Malone asked.

"Yes, sir, but he is not in."

"Not in! Where is he then?"

"At the mill—in the counting-house."

At this, one of the crimson doors swung open.

"Are the wagons come, Sarah?" a female voice inquired, accompanied immediately by the appearance of a female head. It might not have been the visage of a goddess—indeed, a coil of curl-paper on either side of her temples quite forbade such a supposition—but neither was it the head of a Gorgon; yet, Malone seemed to perceive it through the lens of the latter. Despite his imposing stature, he shrank bashfully backward into the rain at the sight of her, muttering, "I'll go to him," and hurried down a short lane, across an obscure yard, toward a massive black mill with evident trepidation.

The work hours had ended; the "hands" were gone. The machinery rested silent, the mill shut tight. Malone circled the structure until he found another chink of light in its great, soot-stained flank. He knocked at another door, using the thick end of his shillelah to beat a rousing tattoo. A key turned; the door unlatched.

"Is it Joe Scott? What news of the wagons, Joe?"

"No; it's myself. Mr. Helstone would send me."

"Oh! Mr. Malone." The voice uttering this name carried the slightest possible cadence of disappointment. After a brief pause, it continued, politely but with a formal stiffness,—"I beg you will come in, Mr. Malone. I regret extremely that Mr. Helstone should have deemed it necessary to trouble you so far. There was no necessity—I told him so—and certainly not on such a night; but walk forwards."

Through a dim apartment of indistinguishable character, Malone followed the speaker into a light and bright room within. Very light and bright indeed it seemed to eyes that, for the past hour, had struggled to penetrate the double darkness of night and fog. Yet, apart from an excellent fire and a lamp of elegant design casting a vivid lustre upon a table, it was a very plain place. The boarded floor was bare of carpet; the three or four stiff-backed, green-painted chairs seemed once to have furnished the kitchen of some farmhouse; a desk of strong, solid formation, the aforementioned table, and some framed sheets on the stone-colored walls bearing plans for buildings, gardens, designs of machinery, etc., completed the sparse furniture.

Plain as it was, the setting satisfied Malone. Upon removing and hanging up his wet surtout and hat, he drew one of the rheumatic-looking chairs to the hearth, settling his knees almost within the bars of the red grate.

"Comfortable quarters you have here, Mr. Moore; and all snug to yourself."

"Yes, but my sister would be glad to see you, if you would prefer stepping into the house."

"Oh no! The ladies are best alone; I never was a lady's man. You don't mistake me for my friend Sweeting, do you, Mr. Moore?"

"Sweeting! Which of them is that? The gentleman in the chocolate overcoat, or the little gentleman?"

"The little one—he of Nunnely; the cavalier of the Misses Sykes, with the whole six of whom he is in love, ha! ha!"

"Better be generally in love with all than specially with one, I should think, in that quarter."

"But he is specially in love with one besides, for when I and Donne urged him to make a choice amongst the fair bevy, he named—which do you think?"

With a queer, quiet smile, Mr. Moore replied, "Dora, of course, or Harriet."

"Ha! ha! You've an excellent guess. But what made you hit on those two?"

"Because they are the tallest, the handsomest, and Dora, at least, is the stoutest; and as your friend Mr. Sweeting is but a little slight figure, I concluded that, according to a frequent rule in such cases, he preferred his contrast."

"You are right; Dora it is. But he has no chance, has he, Moore?"

"What has Mr. Sweeting besides his curacy?"

This question seemed to tickle Malone immensely. He laughed for full three minutes before answering.

"What has Sweeting? Why, David has his harp, or flute, which comes to the same thing. He has a sort of pinchbeck watch; ditto, ring; ditto, eyeglass. That's what he has."

"How would he propose to keep Miss Sykes in gowns only?"

"Ha! ha! Excellent! I'll ask him that next time I see him. I'll roast him for his presumption. But no doubt he expects old Christopher Sykes would do something handsome. He is rich, is he not? They live in a large house."

"Sykes carries on an extensive concern."

"Therefore he must be wealthy, eh?"

"Therefore he must have plenty to do with his wealth, and in these times would be about as likely to think of drawing money from the business to give dowries to his daughters as I should be to dream of pulling down the cottage there, and constructing on its ruins a house as large as Fieldhead."

"Do you know what I heard, Moore, the other day?"

"No. Perhaps that I *was* about to effect some such change. Your Briarfield gossips are capable of saying that or sillier things."
