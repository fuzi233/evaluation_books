Mr. Home lingered at Mrs. Bretton’s hearth for two full days, and not all the cajolery in the world could draw him out of the warm, low-lit parlour. He sat slouched in his armchair by the fire from dawn till dusk, sometimes wrapped in heavy silence, sometimes listening and responding to Mrs. Bretton’s easy chatter—exactly the right tone for a man raw with quiet grief, never cloyingly sympathetic, never so detached he felt cast adrift, sensible, softened by a gentle, maternal warmth earned by the years she had over him.

As for little Paulina, the child was a strange blend of brightness and hush, half-busy, half-watchful, brimming with a quiet joy she dared not waste. Her father would lift her to rest on his knee for hours at a time, and she would stay perfectly still until the very second she sensed he might grow restless, then murmur soft and shy, “Papa, put me down; my weight will tire you.” That tiny “mighty burden” would slip light as a feather to the rug, settle on the carpet or a low stool right at his feet, and pull out her white work-box and that scarlet-speckled handkerchief she was stitching to give him as a keepsake before he left. It needed a score of stitches every half hour, and she worked at it with the utmost, unwavering gravity of a master craftsman on a royal commission.

When evening rolled around, Graham would trudge in the door home from school, and the whole house flooded with bright, crackling energy—energy that only spiked higher with the playful, half-hostile antics that always unfolded between him and little Miss Paulina.

Ever since he’d teased her that first night, she’d adopted a lofty, haughty reserve around him; whenever he spoke to her, her default answer was, “I can’t attend to you. I have other things to think about.” When he begged her to name those things, she’d lift her small chin and say, “Business.”

Graham set out to lure her attention anyway, flinging open his desk to show off its treasure trove: bright wax sticks, sharp penknives, fancy seals, and a stack of colourful engravings he’d hoarded for years. The temptation was far too strong to resist entirely; her eyes would dart up furtively from her stitching again and again, sneaking peeks at the pictures scattered across his writing table. One soft etching of a little child playing with a fluffy Blenheim spaniel fluttered right to her feet.

“Pretty little dog,” she breathed, delighted before she could catch herself.

Graham pretended not to hear. Before long, she’d slipped off her stool and crept over to get a closer look at the treasure, those great soft spaniel eyes and long floppy ears, the child’s feathered hat, too charming to pass up. “Nice picture,” she pronounced, magnanimous.

“You can have it,” Graham offered.

She hesitated. She ached to take it, but accepting would mean surrendering her hard-won dignity. No. She set it back down carefully and turned away.

“You won’t have it, Polly?”
“I would rather not, thank you.”
“Guess what I’ll do with it if you refuse.”
She half-turned, curious despite herself.
“Cut it into strips to light the taper.”
“No!”
“But I will.”
“Please—don’t.”

Graham acted inexorable the second he heard that soft pleading tone, grabbing a pair of scissors from his mother’s work basket. “Here goes!” he flourished the blades, menacing. “Right through Fido’s head, and splitting little Harry’s nose.”

“Then come to me. Come quickly, or it’s done.”

She hesitated, lingered, then complied, her small feet padding across the carpet to stand before him.
“Now will you have it?” he asked.
“Please.”
“But I want payment.”
“How much?”
“A kiss.”
“Give the picture to me first.”

Polly looked distinctly untrusting as she said that, so Graham handed it over. She grabbed it, darted away like a little thief who’d escaped the bailiffs, and scrambled to hide on her father’s knee. Graham rose in mock, dramatic wrath and chased after her. She buried her face in Mr. Home’s waistcoat, muffled. “Papa—papa—send him away!”

“I won’t be sent away,” Graham laughed.
Face still averted, she stuck out a small hand to fend him off.
“Then I’ll kiss the hand,” he said—only for that hand to curl into a tiny, tight fist, and thump him right on the mouth with a coin far from the kiss he’d asked for.

Graham, just as wily as his little playmate, retreated, acting thoroughly and dramatically wounded. He flopped onto the sofa, rested his head on the cushion, and lay there like he was in terrible pain. Polly, finding him strangely silent, soon peeked over at him. His hands were covering his eyes and his whole face. She twisted around on her father’s knee and gazed at her supposed “enemy” long and anxiously, until Graham let out a loud, dramatic groan.

“Papa, what’s the matter?” she whispered.
“You had better ask him, Polly.”
“Is he hurt?” (another groan.)
“He makes a noise as if he is,” said Mr. Home, smiling faintly.
“Mother,” Graham feebly moaned, “I think you’d better send for the doctor. Oh my eye!” (A long silence, broken only by his dramatic sighs.) “If I were to go blind—”

The little chastiser could not bear that thought for a second. She was at his side in an instant. “Let me see your eye! I didn’t mean to hit it, only your mouth, and I didn’t think I hit so very hard!”

Graham said nothing. Her face crumpled. “I’m sorry! I’m so sorry!” Then the tears came, thick and fast, her shoulders shaking as she sobbed.

“Stop tormenting that child, Graham,” Mrs. Bretton laughed.
“It’s all nonsense, my pet,” Mr. Home assured her.

Graham snatched her up off the floor before she could protest, and she punished him again, tugging at his thick, lion’s mane of hair while she called him “the naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever lived.”

On the morning Mr. Home was set to leave, he drew his daughter aside in the window recess, and I caught snippets of their quiet conversation.
“Couldn’t I pack my box and go with you, papa?” she whispered, earnest as anything.
He shook his head.
“Am I a trouble to you?”
“Yes, Polly.”
“Because I’m little?”
“Because you’re little and tender. Only great, strong people should travel long distances. But don’t look sad, my little girl—you break my heart. Papa will come back to his Polly very soon.”
“Indeed, indeed I’m not sad, scarcely at all.”
“Polly would be sorry to give papa pain, wouldn’t she?”
“Sorrier than sorry.”
“Then Polly must be cheerful. No crying at parting, no fretting after I leave. Look forward to when we meet again, and try to be happy in the meantime. Can you do that?”
“She will try.”
“I see she will. Farewell, then. It’s time to go.”
“Now? Just now?”
“Just now.”

She lifted up her quivering lips for a kiss. Her father sobbed, but Polly did not shed a tear. When he set her down, he shook hands with the rest of us, and walked out the door. The second the street latch clicked shut behind him, she dropped to her knees by the nearest chair and cried out, soft and long: “Papa!” It was a cry so raw, so full of that quiet “why have you forsaken me?” agony that no child her age should have known. For long minutes she endured that pain, and in that short span of her young life, she felt emotions many people never experience in a lifetime—such was the depth of her nature, and I knew if she lived, there would be many more such moments for her. Nobody spoke. Mrs. Bretton, a mother, shed a tear or two. Graham, who had been writing, lifted his pen and stared at her. I, Lucy Snowe, was calm, and watched.

Left unharassed, the little creature fought that intolerable grief on her own, no coddling from others needed, and soon managed to push it down to a bearable ache. That day she would accept no comfort from anyone, nor the next. Slowly, after that, she grew more passive, more quiet.

On the third evening, she sat on the floor, worn thin and still, when Graham came in. He picked her up gently, not a word said. She did not resist. She nestled right into his arms, like she was exhausted from carrying all that weight alone. When he sat down, she rested her head on his chest, and within minutes she was fast asleep. He carried her upstairs to bed, and I was not at all surprised that the very next morning, the first thing she asked for when she woke was, “Where is Mr. Graham?”

Graham skipped breakfast that day, tied up writing exercises for his morning class, and asked his mother to send a cup of tea to his study. Polly volunteered to carry it—she had to be busy, had to look after someone, after all. Mrs. Bretton handed it to her, trusting her care even if she was restless. The study door faced the breakfast room across the passage, so my eye followed her every step.

“What are you doing?” she asked, pausing on the threshold.
“Writing,” Graham said.
“Why don’t you come have breakfast with your mamma?”
“Too busy.”
“Do you want any breakfast at all?”
“Of course I do.”
“Then here.” She set the tea cup down on the carpet like a jailer pushing a prisoner’s water pitcher through a cell door, then retreated. She was back a few minutes later. “What else do you want besides tea? Something to eat?”
“Anything good. Bring me something particularly nice, that’s a kind little woman.”

She trotted back to Mrs. Bretton. “Please ma’am, send your boy something good.”
“You choose for him, Polly. What should my boy have?”

She picked out every nicest thing on the table, then came back to whisper a request for marmalade that wasn’t already set out. Mrs. Bretton indulged every last one of them, and soon we heard Graham praising her to the skies, promising that when he had a house of his own, she could be his housekeeper, and maybe—if she showed enough culinary talent—his cook. When she didn’t come back, I went to check, and found them breakfasting tête-à-tête: she stood at his elbow, sharing his fare, except for the marmalade, which she refused to touch, no doubt determined no one would ever think she’d fetched it for her own benefit, not his. She was always full of those sharp, delicate, lovely little instincts.

Their friendship, once forged, never frayed. The more time passed, the tighter their bond grew, even though they were worlds apart in age, sex, and pursuits. Paulina’s true character only ever came out when Graham was around. With Mrs. Bretton, she was docile enough, sitting for hours on a stool at her feet learning her lessons, sewing, or scribbling figures on a slate, never once sparking to the wild, bright originality that made her special. I soon stopped watching her when she was quiet like that—she was no fun. But the second Graham’s knock sounded on the door in the evening, she was instantly at the top of the staircase, waiting, her first greeting usually a scolding or a threat.

“You didn’t wipe your shoes properly on the mat! I’m going to tell your mamma.”
“Little busybody! Are you up there?”
“Yes—and you can’t reach me. I’m higher up than you.” She peeked through the banister rails, too small to see over the top.
“Polly!”
“My dear boy!” she’d mimicked Mrs. Bretton, one of her favourite pet names for him.
“I’m fit to faint with fatigue,” Graham would declare, leaning against the passage wall, pretending to be all but dead. “Dr. Digby worked me half to death at school today. Come down and help me carry my books upstairs.”
“Ah! You’re cunning!”
“Not at all. I’m weak as a rush. Come down.”
“Your eyes are as quiet as a cat’s, but you’re going to spring on me.”
“Spring? I don’t have the strength. Come down.”
“Maybe I will—if you promise not to touch me, not to snatch me up, not to whirl me around.”
“Me? I couldn’t lift a finger!” He sank into a chair, dramatic.
“Then put the books on the first step, and go three yards away.”

Of course, the second she descended warily, never taking her eyes off him for a second, he’d spring to life, and the romping would start. Sometimes she’d pretend to be cross, sometimes she’d let it slide, leading him up the stairs by the hand, saying soft and motherly, “Now, my dear boy, come and have your tea. I know you must be starving.”

It was endlessly comical to watch her sit beside him while he ate. When he was gone, she was a quiet, reserved little thing, but with him, she was the most fussy, officious little caretaker in the whole house. I often wished she’d sit still and mind her own business, but no—she forgot herself entirely around him. He was treated better than the Grand Turk in her eyes. She’d assemble every plate within his reach, and just when you thought he had everything he could possibly want, she’d remember something else, whispering to Mrs. Bretton, “Ma’am, maybe your son would like a little sweet cake, you know the one in the sideboard cupboard.” Mrs. Bretton usually disapproved of sweet cake at tea, but Polly would beg, “Just one tiny piece, only for him. Girls like me and Miss Snowe don’t need treats, but he’d like it.”

Graham loved that cake, and he almost always got it. To do him justice, he’d try to share it with her, but she would never, ever accept. Even trying to force it on her would ruffle her mood for the whole evening. Standing by his knee, monopolizing his attention, was the only reward she wanted—not a bite of cake.

She adapted to every one of his interests faster than light. You’d think the child had no mind, no life of her own, after her father left—she nestled to Graham, felt his feelings, existed in his existence. She learned all his schoolfellows’ names in a trice, memorized every one of their personalities as he described them, never mixed a single one up. She could mimic the strict under-master Graham hated perfectly, after seeing him only once, and act him out for Graham’s amusement—till Mrs. Bretton found out and forbade it.

They almost never fought, but once, a sharp rupture left her feelings badly bruised. It was Graham’s birthday, and he’d invited a group of his school friends, boys his own age, over for dinner. Paulina had heard him talk about these friends for ages, and waited for their arrival with bated breath. After dinner, the boys shut themselves in the dining room, laughing loudly and making a racket. I found Polly sitting alone on the lowest step of the staircase, staring at the glossy dining room door panel, her little brow furrowed in deep thought.

“What are you thinking about, Polly?”
“Nothing much. I just wish that door was all clear glass, so I could see through it. They sound like they’re having so much fun. I want to go to them. I want to be with Graham, and meet his friends.”
“What’s stopping you?”
“I’m scared. But do you think I can try? Knock on the door and ask to be let in?”

I thought the boys wouldn’t mind her as a playmate, so I encouraged her. She knocked, softly at first, no one heard, then a little harder. The door swung open, Graham’s bright, flushed face peeking out, giddy and impatient.

“What do you want, you little monkey?”
“To come in with you.”
“Do you indeed? As if I’d be troubled by you! Go back to mamma and Miss Snowe, and tell them to put you to bed.” He vanished, and the door slammed shut hard. She stood there, stunned.

“Why did he talk like that? He never talked like that before,” she said, horrified. “What did I do?”
“Nothing, Polly. Graham is just busy with his friends.”
“And he likes them better than me! He’s turning me away now they’re here!”

I was about to comfort her, pull out a few of my usual philosophical maxims to soothe her, but she stuck her fingers in her ears the second I spoke, then laid face down on the hall mat, pressed against the cold stone flags. Neither the cook nor the maid could get her to move, so we left her there till she chose to get up on her own.

Graham forgot his impatience that same evening. When his friends left, he tried to greet her as usual, but she wrenched herself out of his hand, her eyes flashing, refused to say goodnight, refused to even look at his face. The next day he treated her with cold indifference, and she turned as still and hard as marble. The day after that, he teased her to tell him what was wrong, and her lips stayed sealed. He couldn’t stay angry for long, he soon softened and coaxed her. “Why are you so upset? What did I do?” Before long the tears came, he petted her, and they made up. But she never forgot that snub. I never saw her chase after him again, or follow him, or beg for his notice. Once I told her to take a book to Graham in his study. “I’ll wait till he comes out,” she said, proud as a little queen. “I won’t make him get up and open the door for me.”
