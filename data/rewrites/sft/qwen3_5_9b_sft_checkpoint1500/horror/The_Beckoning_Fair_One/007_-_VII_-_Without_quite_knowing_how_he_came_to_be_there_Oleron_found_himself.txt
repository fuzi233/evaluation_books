Without fully understanding how he arrived there, Oleron found himself striding over the loose board Miss Bengough had temporarily laid upon the broken step. Hatless and descending the stairs, he felt a hazy recollection return only afterward: he had left the candle burning on the table, opened the door just wide enough for his frame, and slipped out, softening the sound of the exit. At the bottom of the stairs, a jolt awaited him. A shape darted upward from the disused cellars in a flurry and vanished through the doorway. It was merely a cat, yet Oleron let out a childish sob.

He crossed the threshold of the gate and paused beneath the "To Let" boards, nervously picking at his lip while staring up at the faint glow filtering through one of his red blinds. Still glancing back over his shoulder, he stumbled up the square. A small public house rounded the corner—a haunt he had never visited—yet he pushed inside and dropped a shilling onto the counter, missing the edge by mere inches.

"B—b—bran—brandy," he stammered, then bent to retrieve the coin.

The little, sawdust-covered bar belonged entirely to him. The scattered company of carters, laborers, and local tradesmen huddled in the far compartment, separated from the white-haired landlady who moved silently among her taps and bottles. Oleron sank onto a hard wooden settee with a perforated seat, drained half the glass, and then, deciding it was better spilled than wasted, finished the remainder.

His thoughts drifted to the men whose voices echoed in the distance; he wondered which of them would undertake moving his effects the following morning. In the interim, he ordered another measure of brandy.

He resolved not to return to the room where the candle still burned. No! He could not face the entryway, let alone the staircase with its treacherous step—least of all confront that pith-white, mesmerizing chamber. Instead, he intended to revert to his former arrangement: a workroom and separate quarters. He would visit his old landlady immediately, perhaps shortly after finishing his drinks, and ask if she could shelter him for the night. His glass was empty now.

He stood, signaled for a refill, and resumed his seat.

If anyone questioned why he was fleeing again? Oh, he possessed ample reasons! Nails that magically reseated themselves to gash fingers, steps that shattered underfoot, and women who invaded a man's domain to groom their tresses in the dark—these were sufficient causes! He felt querulous and aggrieved. He had secured the premises for himself, not for unseen females to coiffure their hair within! That solicitor in Lincoln's Inn ought to be informed of such improprieties before sundown; it was outrageous to permit such intrusions under agreements of this nature!

A cut-glass partition divided Oleron's compartment from the space where the landlady operated, rising only seven or eight inches above the counter. Beyond that, there was no barrier at the farther bar. Soon, Oleron raised his gaze and perceived faces watching him through the gap. When he stared directly at them, the watchers vanished.

He shifted to a corner where he remained unseen from the opposite bar, though this maneuver aligned him squarely with the landlady. She recognized him from frequent passings, and eventually remarked upon the weather. Oleron offered a reply he could scarcely recall, yet it prompted her observation that the winter had been dreadful for influenza, though spring seemed imminent. Even this trivial engagement with the mundane steadied Oleron slightly; an idle, budding curiosity regarding whether the landlady combed her hair nightly—and if so, whether it emitted those tiny electric crackles—snapped shut instantly. Oleron felt better.

With his next glass, he considered returning to his flat. Back? Indeed, he would return! They would soon learn whether he would be ejected from his lodging like that! He began to question why he was performing this rather unusual act for himself—sitting hatless, drowning sorrows in brandy within a pub. What if he confessed to the white-haired landlady everything? If he told her that a caller had scratched her hand on a nail, followed by the misfortune of stepping through a rotting stair, and that he himself, trapped in an ancient house filled with squeaks, creaks, and whispers, had fled in terror at a faint noise—what would she think? That he was mad, naturally.... Pshaw! The true reality was simply that he had not worked sufficiently to fill his time. He had idled his days away, filling his mind with moonshine about a new *Romilly* (as if the original was not adequate!), and now he was startled that the devil should inhabit an empty head!

Yes, he would return. First, a walk in the air—he had lacked exercise recently—then he would take charge, settle the mess of the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly* (imagine his folly in considering destroying fifteen!), and thenceforth remember his obligations to mankind and the work awaiting him. The issue was neatly summarized.

He finished his brandy and stepped outside.

He walked for some duration before any perspective other than his own internal state surfaced. Initially, the fresh air amplified the heady effects of the liquor; subsequently, his mind sharpened beyond its morning clarity. As lucidity increased, his boastful self-assurances waned, replaced by a firm conviction that, despite exhaustive explanation, certain elements remained inexplicable. The hysteria of the prior hour dissipated; he grew steadily calmer, yet a disturbing certainty persisted. A profound dread seized hold of him. It was a fear for Elsie.

Something within his residence was hostile to her safety. Her two accidents alone might not have convinced him, but she herself had spoken: "_I'm not wanted here_..." She had declared the place itself was amiss, having sensed it before he did. Perfect. One fact stood clear: if this were true, she must be kept away for reasons far deeper than those that had so humiliated Oleron. Fortunately, she had voiced her intent to stay away; he must ensure she adhered to it. He must attend to this personally.

Moreover, he realized his initial impulse—to never set foot in the place again—was absurd. Ordinary people did not behave thus. With Elsie secured, he could not, with any regard for his own dignity, allow himself to be driven out by a shadow, nor even by a mere peril simply because it existed. He required a home, and he would reside there. He must return.

He quelled the faint chill of fear accompanying this decision, and abruptly turned his direction. Should the terror resurface, he might, perhaps, consume one final glass of brandy....

Yet by the time he reached the short street leading to the square, it was too late for another drink. The pub remained lit but closed, with one or two men standing conversing on the curb. Oleron noted a sudden, heavy silence fall over them as he passed, and observed further that the long-nosed Barrett, whom he encountered slightly below, did not acknowledge him with a nod. He turned into the broken gate, hesitated for an instant in the alley, and then ascended the stairs once more.

Only an inch of wax remained in the Sheffield stick, and Oleron refused to relight it. Deliberately, he forced himself to gather the candle and inspect each of his five rooms before retiring. As he traversed back from the kitchen across his small hall, he spotted a letter lying on the floor. He carried it into the sitting-room and glanced at the envelope before breaking the seal.

It was unstamped and had been slid through the door by hand. The handwriting was clumsy, running continuously from start to finish without commas or periods. Oleron read the first line, turned to the signature, and completed the reading.

It was from Barrett, informing Oleron that he would be most obliged if Mr. Oleron would arrange alternative provisions for preparing breakfasts and cleaning the premises. The sting resided in the postscript, a biblical text containing an allusion that could only refer to Elsie Bengough....

A rarely seen frown carved deeply into Oleron's brow. So! That was the connection! Very well; they would address this tomorrow.... For the remainder, this appeared to be merely another justification for Elsie to remain absent....

Then his suppressed rage erupted.

Foul-minded wretches! The devil himself could not have sneered more grotesquely at anything that had transpired between Paul Oleron and Elsie Bengough, yet this nosing scoundrel dared pry and speak!...

Oleron balled the paper, held it to the flickering candle flame until it blackened, and then ground the ash beneath his heel.

However, the letter served one useful purpose: it ignited a wrathful blaze within Oleron that effectively banished the pale shadows. Nevertheless, one final puzzle lingered to conclude the day. As he undressed, he cast a casual glance at his bed. The coverlets bore an impression as if someone had lain upon them. Oleron could not recall settling down during the day—offhand, he would assert certainly he had not—but he could not be absolutely sure. His indignation for Elsie, potentially mingled with the residual brandy, excluded all other considerations; he extinguished the candle, lay down, and slipped immediately into a deep, dreamless sleep, lasting nearly twenty-four hours in the absence of Mrs. Barrett's morning summons.
