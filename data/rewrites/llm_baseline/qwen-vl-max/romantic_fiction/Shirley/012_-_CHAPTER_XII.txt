Shirley proved her sincerity in saying she would welcome Caroline’s company by seeking it often. Indeed, without such effort, she might never have had it—Miss Helstone was slow to open herself to new acquaintances. She always hesitated, convinced that others could not possibly want her, that she could not entertain them. A bright, cheerful young woman like the heiress of Fieldhead seemed to her too self-sufficient, too radiant in her own world, to ever truly value someone so quiet and unassuming as herself.

Yet Shirley might shine, and perhaps be happy, but no one is truly independent of warmth and companionship. Though she had made the acquaintance of nearly every family in the surrounding parishes within a month, and was on easy, familiar terms with the Misses Sykes, the Misses Pearson, and even the two celebrated Misses Wynne of Walden Hall, none of them seemed to stir her spirit deeply. She did not form close bonds with any of them—not really. If she had been born Shirley Keeldar, Esq., lord of Briarfield Manor, there wasn’t a single young lady in these parts whom she’d consider asking to become Mrs. Keeldar, mistress of the estate. She confessed this to Mrs. Pryor, who took it calmly, as she did most of Shirley’s bold remarks. “My dear,” she replied gently, “do not let this habit of speaking of yourself as a gentleman grow stronger. It’s unusual. Those who don’t know you might think you’re trying to imitate masculine ways.”

Shirley never mocked her former governess—even the smallest formalities or quirks of Mrs. Pryor were respected in her eyes. To laugh at her would have shown weakness; only the weak turn dignity into ridicule. So Shirley accepted the gentle reproof in silence. She stood quietly near the window, watching the great cedar tree on her lawn, where a bird perched on one of its lower branches. Then, softly, she began to chirrup. The sound grew clearer, then turned into a whistle. Soon, she was whistling a tune—sweetly, skillfully.

“My dear!” Mrs. Pryor exclaimed.

“Was I whistling?” Shirley asked, startled. “I didn’t realize. I’m sorry, ma'am. I promised myself I wouldn’t do it in front of you.”

“But Miss Keeldar, where did you learn to whistle? You must have picked it up since coming to Yorkshire. I never knew you to do it before.”

“Oh, I learned long ago.”

“Who taught you?”

“No one. I just listened and copied. I stopped for years. But yesterday evening, walking up our lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that very tune from the field beyond the hedge. That brought it back.”

“What gentleman was it?”

“We have only one gentleman hereabouts, ma'am—Mr. Moore—at least, he’s the only one who isn’t gray-haired. My two beloved elders, Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, are fine old gentlemen, infinitely better than any of the dull young ones.”

Mrs. Pryor said nothing.

“You don’t care much for Mr. Helstone, do you?”

“My dear, his position protects him from criticism.”

“You usually leave the room when he arrives.”

“Are you going out today, my dear?”

“Yes. I’ll visit the rectory and find Caroline Helstone. I’ll coax her into a walk. She shall have a brisk stroll across Nunnely Common.”

“If you go that way, please remind Miss Helstone to wrap up well. There’s a fresh wind, and she seems to need care.”

“I’ll obey you exactly, Mrs. Pryor. And will you come with us?”

“No, my love. I’d only hold you back. I’m not as fleet-footed as you’d like.”

Shirley easily persuaded Caroline to join her. Once they were on the peaceful road crossing the wide, empty stretch of Nunnely Common, she drew her into conversation with ease. After the first shyness passed, Caroline found herself eager to talk with Miss Keeldar. Just a few brief exchanges told each of them what kind of person the other was.

Shirley admired the green sweep of the common and especially the heather along its ridges—it reminded her of moors. She remembered traveling near the Scottish border, journeying all afternoon through an endless sea of deep heath under a sultry, clouded sky. From noon until sunset, they saw nothing but wild sheep, heard only the cries of birds.

“I can picture how the heath would look on such a day,” Caroline said. “Purple-black—darker than the sky itself, which would be sickly pale.”

“Yes,” Shirley agreed. “Quite livid, with brass-edged clouds, and here and there a flash of white, more chilling than the red glow—so sharp you’d expect it to burst into lightning.”

“Did it thunder?”

“It rumbled far off, but the storm broke only after we reached our inn—an isolated house at the foot of mountains.”

“Did you watch the clouds roll over the hills?”

“I did. I stood at the window for an hour. The mountains looked wrapped in sullen mist. When the rain came down in sheets, they vanished entirely—washed away from sight.”

“I’ve seen storms like that in Yorkshire’s hills. At their peak, when the sky was all waterfalls and the earth flooded, I’ve thought of the Deluge.”

“It’s oddly refreshing afterward—when calm returns and sunlight breaks through the clouds, soft and comforting, reminding you the sun hasn’t died.”

“Miss Keeldar, stop now and look down at Nunnely Dale and the wood.”

They paused on the green slope. Below lay the deep valley dressed in May’s greenery—meadows dotted with daisies and golden king-cups. The light danced across the land in emerald and amber hues. Nunnwood—the last remnant of ancient British forest in a region once covered in woodland—rested beneath a shadow. Distant hills shimmered in silvery blues, soft purples, fleeting greens and rose tints, melting into fleecy white clouds as pure as snow. The air was crisp, sweet, and invigorating.

“Our England is a lovely island,” Shirley said. “And Yorkshire is one of its loveliest corners.”

“You’re a Yorkshire girl too?”

“I am—by blood and birth. Five generations of my family rest beneath the arches of Briarfield Church. I drew my first breath in the old black hall behind us.”

At that, Caroline offered her hand. Shirley took it, and they shook. “We are countrywomen,” she said.

“Yes,” Shirley nodded solemnly.

“And that,” Miss Keeldar asked, pointing to the forest, “is Nunnwood?”

“It is.”

“Have you ever been there?”

“Many times.”

“To the heart of it?”

“Yes.”

“What’s it like?”

“It feels like an encampment of giant sons of Anak. The trees are immense and ancient. Standing at their roots, their crowns seem to belong to another world. The trunks stand firm like pillars, while the branches sway with every breeze. Even in stillness, the leaves whisper; in high wind, it sounds like a river rushing, a sea roaring above you.”

“Was it one of Robin Hood’s hiding places?”

“Yes. And traces remain. To enter Nunnwood is to step back into the dim past. Can you see a break in the trees, near the center?”

“Yes, clearly.”

“That’s a hollow—a deep cup lined with grass as short and green as this common. The oldest oaks, twisted and mighty, crowd the edge. In the bottom lie the ruins of a nunnery.”

“We’ll go—you and I alone, Caroline—to that wood one bright summer morning. We’ll spend the whole day there. We can bring pencils, sketchbooks, books we enjoy, and food. I have two small baskets—Mrs. Gill could pack our provisions—and we can carry our own. Won’t it tire you?”

“Oh no. Especially if we rest all day. I know every lovely spot. I know where to find nuts in autumn, where wild strawberries grow. I know hidden glades, untouched by feet, carpeted with strange mosses—some golden like gilding, some gray, some gem-green. I know clusters of trees that delight the eye: rough oaks, delicate birches, glossy beeches, all contrasted; ash trees tall as Saul, standing alone; ancient giants draped in ivy. I could show you.”

“You wouldn’t find me dull?”

“I wouldn’t. I think we’d suit. And who else, of our age, could join us without spoiling the joy?”

“Indeed, I know no lady nearby. As for gentlemen…”

“An outing changes completely when gentlemen are involved,” Caroline interrupted.

“I agree. It becomes something different altogether.”

“We meant simply to see the old trees, the old ruins—live a day in the past, surrounded by silence and peace.”

“You’re right. Gentlemen ruin that charm. If they’re the wrong sort—like your Malones, your young Sykes, your Wynnes—irritation replaces serenity. Even if they’re good men, there’s still a shift—hard to name, easier to feel.”

“We forget Nature, _imprimis_.”

“And Nature forgets us. She veils her calm face, hides her beauty, and withdraws the quiet joy she’d give us—if we’d only worship her alone.”

“What does she give instead?”

“More excitement, more anxiety. Time flies, and trouble stirs the hours.”

“Our ability to be happy depends largely on ourselves,” Caroline said wisely. “I’ve gone to Nunnwood with large groups—curates, gentry, ladies—and found it tedious. But I’ve gone alone, or with Fanny, who stayed in the woodman’s hut sewing or chatting with the wife, while I wandered, sketched, read. I’ve known real peace. But that was two years ago.”

“Have you ever gone with your cousin Robert Moore?”

“Yes. Once.”

“What’s he like as a companion?”

“A cousin is different from a stranger.”

“I know. But cousins, if dull, are worse than strangers—because you can’t easily avoid them. But your cousin isn’t dull?”

“No. But…”

“Well?”

“If foolish company irritates, wise company brings its own ache. When a friend’s goodness or talent is beyond question, you begin to doubt whether you deserve to be near him.”

“Oh! I can’t follow that. Such thoughts don’t appeal to me. I believe I’m worthy to be the companion of the finest men—gentlemen, I mean—though that’s saying much. Where they’re good, they’re very good. Your uncle, by the way, is a decent example of an older gentleman. I always enjoy seeing his keen, sensible face—whether in my home or elsewhere. Do you love him? Is he kind to you? Now, speak honestly.”

“He raised me from childhood, no doubt, as he would have done his own daughter. That’s kindness. But I don’t love him. I’d rather be away from him than near.”

“Strange, when he seems so pleasant.”

“Yes, in public. But at home, he’s stern and silent. He puts away his cane and hat in the hall—as if he locks away his cheerfulness in his study. The brow knits, words are brief by the fire; smiles, jests, wit—reserved for company.”

“Is he tyrannical?”

“Not at all. He’s neither tyrant nor hypocrite. He’s simply a man more liberal than kind, more brilliant than warm, more scrupulously fair than truly just—if you can grasp such subtle differences.”

“Oh yes. Good-nature implies indulgence—he lacks that. Geniality means warmth—he doesn’t possess it. True justice comes from sympathy, which I imagine your bronzed old friend lacks entirely.”

“I often wonder, Shirley, if most men are like my uncle at home. Do they need strangeness to seem likable? Is it impossible for them to keep affection alive for those they see daily?”

“I don’t know. I ponder the same questions. But let me tell you a secret: if I believed men were inherently fickle—soon cold, unfeeling—I’d never marry. I couldn’t bear to discover that what I loved didn’t love me, that it was tired of me, that no effort would matter, because change is in their nature. Once I knew that, what would I desire? To leave, to escape from a presence where my company brought no joy.”

“But you couldn’t leave if married.”

“No. That’s the horror. I’d lose my freedom forever. A terrible thought—it chokes me! Nothing burdens me more than the idea of being a burden, a bore—inevitable, endless. Now, when I feel unwanted, I can wrap myself in independence like a cloak, lower my pride like a veil, and retreat to solitude. Married, I couldn’t.”

“I wonder we don’t all decide to stay single,” Caroline said. “We would, if we listened to experience. My uncle always calls marriage a burden. He sees every man who marries as a fool—or at least doing a foolish thing.”

“But not all men are like your uncle. Surely not.”

She paused, thoughtful.

“I suppose we each find an exception—in the one we love—until we’re married,” Caroline suggested.

“I suppose so. And we believe that exception is made of true worth. We imagine harmony. We hear his voice and trust it speaks a heart that won’t harden. We see in his eyes a faithful love. I don’t trust passion, Caroline. I think it’s just dry tinder—blazing fast, dying quick. But we watch him—kind to animals, children, the poor. Kind to us. Patient with women, comfortable in their presence. He likes them not for vanity, but because he likes them—just as we like him. We see he’s honest, just, conscientious. We feel joy when he enters a room, sadness when he leaves. We know he was a kind son, a kind brother. Would anyone dare say he won’t be a kind husband?”

“My uncle would say yes—‘He’ll be sick of you in a month.’”

“Mrs. Pryor would hint the same.”

“Mrs. Yorke and Miss Mann would suggest it darkly.”

“If they’re true prophets, it’s best never to fall in love.”

“Very true—if you can avoid it.”

“I choose to doubt them.”

“I fear that proves you’re already caught.”

“Not I. But if I were, whose advice would I seek?”

“Tell me.”

“Neither man nor woman, young nor old: the barefoot Irish beggar who knocks at my door; the mouse that slips from the wall; the bird that pecks at my window in winter; the dog who licks my hand and rests beside my knee.”

“Have you ever seen anyone kind to such creatures?”

“Have you ever seen creatures drawn to someone instinctively—following, trusting, relying?”

“We have a black cat and an old dog at the rectory. I know someone the cat loves to climb onto, who purred against his shoulder. The dog wags and whines whenever he passes.”

“And what does he do?”

“He strokes the cat gently, lets her sit as long as possible. When he must rise, he sets her down softly, never throws her off. He whistles to the dog, gives him a pat.”

“He does? It’s not Robert?”

“It is Robert.”

“Handsome fellow!” Shirley said, her eyes shining.

“Isn’t he? Fine eyes, strong features, noble forehead?”

“He has all that. Bless him—he’s both graceful and good.”

“I knew you’d see it. When I first saw your face, I knew you would.”

“I liked him before I met him. Liked him when I saw him. Admire him now. Beauty has charm in itself, Caroline—but when joined with goodness, it’s powerful.”

“When mind is added, Shirley?”

“Who can resist it?”

“Remember my uncle, Mrs. Pryor, Mrs. Yorke, Miss Mann.”

“Remember the frogs of Egypt. He’s noble. When men are good, they are lords of creation—sons of God. Made in His image, even the smallest spark of His spirit lifts them almost above death. Undeniably, a great, good, handsome man is the finest thing created.”

“Above us?”

“I’d scorn to claim equality with him. Should my left hand challenge my right? My heart quarrel with my pulse? My veins resent the blood within?”

“Men and women, husbands and wives, quarrel terribly, Shirley.”

“Poor things. Poor, fallen, broken things. God made them for higher purpose, deeper feelings.”

“But are we equal to men—or not?”

“Nothing charms me more than meeting my superior—one who makes me feel he truly is.”

“Have you ever met him?”

“I’d welcome him any day. The higher, the better. To stoop is degrading; to look up is glorious. What troubles me is that when I try to admire, I’m thwarted. When I feel spiritual, I find only false gods to worship. I refuse to be a pagan.”

“Miss Keeldar, shall we go in? We’re at the rectory gates.”

“Not today. But tomorrow, I’ll come for you. Caroline Helstone, if you’re truly as you seem, we’ll suit. I’ve never talked to a young woman as freely as I have with you today. Kiss me—and goodbye.”

* * * * *

Mrs. Pryor, too, seemed inclined to foster a friendship with Caroline. She, who rarely visited anywhere, called early one afternoon at the rectory. The rector was out. It was a hot day, and the warmth had flushed her cheeks. She appeared unsettled, perhaps unnerved by entering a new house—her habits were quiet and secluded. When Caroline found her in the dining room, she sat trembling on the sofa, fanning herself with her handkerchief, struggling with a nervous tremor that threatened to become full-blown distress.

Caroline was surprised—this was unlike a woman of her age and strength. Mrs. Pryor quickly blamed the heat, the walk, the exertion. Yet, as she repeated these excuses in haste and confusion, Caroline gently helped her—unfolding her shawl, removing her bonnet. Such attentions, Mrs. Pryor would not accept from everyone. Usually, she recoiled from touch, from closeness, with a mix of embarrassment and coldness that discouraged kindness. But with Miss Helstone’s light hand, she yielded peacefully, and seemed calmed by the contact. Within minutes, the trembling ceased, and she regained composure.

Once settled, she resumed conversation. In mixed company, Mrs. Pryor seldom spoke. If she did, it was constrained, and thus not effective. But in dialogue, she was articulate and thoughtful. Her language was precise, her ideas just, her knowledge broad and accurate. Caroline found it pleasing to listen—more so than she expected.

On the wall opposite the sofa hung three portraits: a woman in the center, above the mantel, flanked by two men.

“That’s a beautiful face,” Mrs. Pryor said, breaking a brief silence after half an hour of lively talk. “The features are perfect—no sculptor could improve them. It’s a portrait from life, I assume?”

“It’s Mrs. Helstone.”

“Mrs. Matthewson Helstone? Your uncle’s wife?”

“Yes. It’s said to be a good likeness. Before her marriage, she was considered the district’s greatest beauty.”

“I’d say she earned the title. Every line is exact. Still… it’s a passive face. The original couldn’t have been what people call ‘a woman of spirit.’”

“She was remarkably quiet and reserved.”

“One would scarcely expect, my dear, that your uncle would choose such a partner. Doesn’t he enjoy lively conversation?”

“In company, yes. But he says he could never manage a talkative wife. He needs peace at home. You go out to chat, he says; you come home to read and reflect.”

“Mrs. Matthewson lived only a few years after her marriage, I believe?”

“About five.”

“Well, my dear,” Mrs. Pryor said, rising to leave, “I hope it’s understood that you’ll visit Fieldhead often. I hope you will. You must feel lonely here, with no female relative nearby. You must spend much time alone.”

“I’m used to it. I’ve grown up by myself. May I help you with your shawl?”

Mrs. Pryor allowed it.

“If you need help with your studies,” she said, “you may command me.”
