Another figure, easily recognizable to us, approached the Piazza that morning. Unlike the mourners draped in black, she wore the vibrant hues of the old days—red, green, and white—as she prepared to witness the Carnival. She hailed from a different direction entirely; Tessa no longer resided on the Hill of San Giorgio. Following the tragic events involving Baldassarre there, Tito had deemed it prudent to relocate her, citing various reasons, yet he ensured her new home remained within a quiet, airy quarter. She now lived in a house bordering the expansive gardens north of the Porta Santa Croce.

Tessa had not ventured out sightseeing without explicit permission. The evening prior, Tito had accompanied her. She had held back the plea swelling in her heart and throat until she witnessed him in a state of radiant ease. With one arm around the sturdy Lillo and the other resting gently on her shoulder, he helped steady the tiny Ninna on her legs. Tessa observed that the weariness which had previously caused him to collapse into his chair upon entering had completely vanished from his brow and lips.

Tessa was quick to learn the subtle stratagems required to avoid vexing Naldo while securing a measure of her own desires. Though she could read nothing else, she had mastered the art of reading her husband's face. Indeed, the charm of that bright, gentle-humored Tito—who had awakened beneath the Loggia de' Cerchi on a Lenten morning five years ago, offering no hostages to deceit—had never returned so fully as it did in the person of Naldo, seated in the straight-backed, carved armchair provided for his comfort during visits to Tessa and the children.

Tito himself was surprised by the growing sense of relief he felt in these moments. No guile was necessary toward Tessa; she was too ignorant and too innocent to suspect him of anything. Furthermore, the little voices calling him "Babbo" were sweet in his ears for the brief duration he heard them. When contemplating leaving Florence, he never imagined abandoning Tessa and the little ones. He cherished these round-cheeked, wide-eyed creatures who clung to him, knowing no evil. Wherever affection springs, it resembles the green leaf and the blossom—pure and breathing purity, regardless of the soil in which it grows.

Poor Romola, despite her self-sacrificing efforts, was inadvertently hardening Tito's nature, chilling it with a positive dislike that had previously seemed impossible. Yet, Tessa kept the fountains of kindness flowing.

"Ninna is very good without me now," Tessa began, feeling her request rise high in her throat. She allowed Ninna to sit on the floor. "I can leave her with Monna Lisa anytime. If she cries in the cradle, Lillo is as sensible as can be; he goes and thumps Monna Lisa."

Lillo, whose great dark eyes appeared even darker against his light-brown curls (inherited from his mother), jumped off Babbo's knee. He immediately demonstrated his intelligence by thumping Monna Lisa, who sat shaking her head slowly over her spinning wheel at the other end of the room.

"A wonderful boy!" Tito laughed.

"Isn't he?" Tessa asked eagerly, moving slightly closer. "And might I go to see the Carnival tomorrow, just for an hour or two? Mightn't I?"

"Oh, you wicked pigeon!" Tito replied, pinching her cheek. "Those are your longings, are they? What have you to do with carnivals now that you are an old woman with two children?"

"But old women like to see things," Tessa said, her lower lip trembling slightly. "Monna Lisa said she would like to go, too, but she is so deaf she cannot hear what is behind her, and she fears we cannot manage both children."

"No, indeed, Tessa," Tito said, his expression turning grave. "You must not think of taking the children into the crowded streets, or I shall be angry."

"But I have never been into the Piazza without leave since Holy Saturday," Tessa pleaded in a frightened tone. "I think Nofri is dead; you know the poor *madre* died. I shall never forget the Carnival I saw once—it was so pretty, all roses and a king and queen beneath them, with singing. I liked it better than San Giovanni."

"There is nothing like that now, my Tessa," Tito replied. "They intend to make a bonfire in the Piazza—that is all. But I cannot allow you to go out alone in the evening."

"Oh no, no! I do not wish to go in the evening. I only want to see the procession by daylight. There *will* be a procession, is it not true?"

"Yes, after a sort," Tito conceded. "As lively as a flight of cranes. Do not expect roses and glittering kings and queens, my Tessa. However, I suppose any string of people to be called a procession will please your blue eyes. There is a structure they have raised in the Piazza de' Signori for the bonfire; you may like to see that. But come home early, and look like a grave little old woman. If you see any men with feathers and swords, keep out of their way; they are very fierce and like to cut old women's heads off."

"Santa Madonna! Where do they come from?" Tessa exclaimed, realizing he was joking. "Ah! You are laughing; it is not so bad. But I will keep away from them. Only..." She leaned forward in a whisper, placing her lips near Naldo's ear. "If I might take Lillo with me! He is very sensible."

"But who will thump Monna Lisa then, if she does not hear?" Tito asked, struggling to maintain a serious demeanor.

"No, Tessa, you could not take care of Lillo in a crowd, and he is too heavy for you to carry," Tito insisted.

"It is true," Tessa said, somewhat sadly. "He likes to run away. I forgot that. Then I will go alone. But now look at Ninna—you have not looked at her enough."

Ninna was a blue-eyed creature of the tottering, tumbling age—a fair solid object that, like a loaded die, found its base with a constancy warranting prediction. Tessa snatched her up. While Babbo paid due attention to Ninna's recent teeth and other marvels, Tessa whispered, "And shall I buy some confetti for the children?"

Tito drew small coins from his *scarsella* and poured them into her palm.

"That will buy no end," Tessa said, delighted by the abundance. "I shall not mind going without Lillo so much if I bring him something."

Thus, Tessa set out in the morning toward the great Piazza where the bonfire was to be erected. She did not consider the February breeze cold enough to require more than her green woolen dress. A mantle would have been oppressive, hiding a new necklace and a new clasp mounted with silver—the only ornamental gifts Tito had ever bestowed upon her. Tessa did not think of displaying her figure, for no one had ever told her it was pretty; however, she was certain her necklace and clasp were among the finest ever worn by the wealthiest peasant woman. She arranged her white hood so that the front of her necklace might be prominently displayed. In her mind, these ornaments must inspire respect, signaling that she was the wife of someone capable of affording such luxuries.

She tripped along cheerfully in the February sunshine, thinking mostly of the treats for the little ones to fill her small basket, oblivious to anyone watching her. Yet, her descent from her upper story into the street had been observed. She was being kept in sight by a person who had often waited in vain to confirm if Tessa indeed lived in the house to which he had once dogged Tito.

Baldassarre carried a package of yarn. Constantly employed in this manner to earn his scanty bread and keep the sacred fire of vengeance alive, he had come out of his way this morning, as he often did, merely to pass the house where he had followed Tito in the evenings. His long imprisonment had intensified his timid suspicion and his belief in a diabolic fortune favoring Tito. He dared not pursue him openly, fearing that if Tito's eyes fell upon him, he would again be subjected to obloquy, dragged away, stripped of his weapon, and cast helpless into a prison cell. His fierce purpose had become as stealthy as a serpent, depending on a single dart of the fang for its prey. Justice was weak and unfriended; he could no longer hear the voice promising vengeance echoing in the Duomo. He had visited it repeatedly, but that voice seemed stifled by cunning, strong-armed wickedness.

For a long while, Baldassarre's sole objective was to ascertain whether Tito still wore armor, for now his fainting hope would have been contented with a successful stab before the grave. Yet, he would never risk his precious knife again. The knowledge that the sharp steel was useless, and that he had no hope but in some new device, weighed heavily on his enfeebled mind. A dim vision of winning one of the two wives to aid him continually formed and dissolved before him. The wife who had lived on the hill was no longer there. If he could find her again, he might grasp a thread of a project and work his way toward clarity. This morning, he had succeeded. He was certain of her location.
