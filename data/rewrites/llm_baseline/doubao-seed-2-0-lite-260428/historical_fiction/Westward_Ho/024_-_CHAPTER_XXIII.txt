> “My mariners, Souls that have toil'd, and wrought, and thought with me--Death closes all: but something ere the end, Some work of noble note, may yet be done, Not unbecoming men that strove with gods!”
> —TENNYSON'S *Ulysses*

Nearly three long years have slipped by since that small band knelt for evensong beneath the colossal ceiba of Guayra—years of wandering so unmarked by any European map that only scattered half-memories and misspelt native place names trace their path. They have trekked across eight hundred miles of untrodden hill and forest, four hundred miles deep into the interior, hunting the fabled Golden City of Manoa, and they have found nothing but disappointment.

They traced the wooded banks of the Orinoco, dared the roaring foam-choked falls of Maypures, and pushed up the vast upper waters of the Amazon. They sailed deep into Peru itself, and trod the cinchona groves of Loxa, unaware, as the whole world then was, of the fever-healing power hidden in the tree’s bark. They watched the virgin snows of Chimborazo glow white above roiling thunderheads, and saw the smouldering black cone of Cotopaxi glower down at them from the heights, only days before lava streams poured down its slopes. Foiled in their search behind the Andes’ western flanks, they turned east again, plunging from the alpine cliffs into what old travellers called “the green misty ocean of the Montana.” They fought their way slowly, painfully, north once more along the eastern foot of the inland Cordillera, and now they are camped on the banks of one of the many tributaries of the Meta, that tumbles down from the Suma Paz range into the wide, forest-choked plains.

Their watchfires glint on the dark river surface under the shadow of enormous hardwoods. Amyas Leigh, Will Cary, Jack Brimblecombe, old Salvation Yeo, and the young Indian lad who has followed them through every twist of their wanderings sit alive and well by the flames—still as far as ever from Manoa, its fairy lake, its gilded palaces, and every wondrous tale the native peoples once told them. Again and again across their miles they caught faint whispers of the city, struck off in a new direction to follow the rumour, and met only fresh letdown, that deferred hope which sours the heart.

Of the eighty-four men who knelt with them under the Guayra tree, only forty-four remain. Where are the rest?

Drew, their sailing master, lies buried on the Rio Negro’s banks, five brave fellows at his side, killed by poisoned Indian arrows in a doomed attempt to force the mountain gorges of Parima. Two more froze to death in the Andean valleys, struck down by the biting slate hail that sweeps down from the condor’s nesting crags. Four drowned in the Orinoco’s churning rapids. Five or six badly wounded men were left behind at another set of falls in the care of friendly local tribes, to be fetched later if they healed—if the men ever made their way back at all. Month by month, fever, venomous snakes, jaguars, alligators, man-eating piranha, and numbing electric eels have thinned their ranks. No trace of their march through the untouched wilderness remains but those lonely, unmarked graves.

The survivors sit now beside the silent stream under the glow of the tropic moon: sun-dried, lean, but strong and bold as the day they left Devon. The quiet, unshakable fire of English courage burns undimmed in every eye, the easy, genial smile of English mirth still rests on every lip. They jest at danger, turn their toil into sport, as cheerily as they did when they sailed over Bideford bar in those days that now feel like they belong to a past life before they were even born. Their beards hang long down to their chests, their hair knotted thick on their heads like women’s to shield their scalps from the burning sun. Their leggings are stitched from the soft hide of the delicate Guazu-puti deer, their shirts patched with woven Indian cotton. Trophies of jaguar, puma, and monkey fur hang slung over their shoulders.

Their gunpowder ran out long ago, their muskets rotted through by the perpetual steam bath of the rainforest, left abandoned and useless in a cave above an Orinoco cataract—but their broadswords still gleam bright and terrible. They carry longbows of a strength no Indian arm can draw, arrows tipped with shards of their old dented armour, and many of them wield the native pocuna blowgun: deadlier still, because it strikes in total silence, no loud report to warn prey or enemy. They wander as unchallenged lords of the forest and its beasts, feared by all hostile native bands but kind, just, and generous to every people that deals fairly with them. A dozen smooth-faced Carib, Ature, Solimo, and Guahiba storytellers recount with awe the righteousness of these bearded heroes, who proclaimed themselves the sworn foes of the treacherous, murderous Spaniards, and spoke of the great, good queen across the sea who would send her warriors to deliver and avenge all oppressed native peoples.

The rest of the men sleep among the trees, some on the packed earth, others strung in grass hammocks slung between tree trunks. The whole forest is quiet save for the heavy splash of a tapir wading in the river to tear up water weeds for its night feed. Now and then a jaguar leaps from treetop to treetop chasing prey, waking the whole horde of monkeys clustered on the boughs, who rouse the roosting birds, and for ten minutes unearthly roars, howls, shrieks, and cackles ring through the woods as if all pandemonium has broken loose. But the racket dies away fast, and even while it lasts it is too familiar to wake the sleeping men, let alone interrupt the small war council gathered around the watchfire: Amyas, Cary, Brimblecombe, and the faithful Yeo.

They have held a hundred such councils before, every one fruitless, and for all they know this one will be no different. But this one is far more solemn than the rest: the two years they agreed to spend searching for Manoa are long expired, and they must pick a new path now, unless they intend to spend the rest of their lives lost in this green wilderness.

“Well,” says Will Cary, puffing smoke from a stolen cigar he traded for with the last band of friendly Indians they met, “at least we came away with something from those last natives. It’s a pure comfort to have a proper smoke after three weeks of going without.”

“For me,” Jack Brimblecombe chimes in, laughing, “Heaven forgive me, but when I get that magical leaf between my lips again I feel tempted to sit as still as a chimney and smoke till my dying day, never stirring hand or foot.”

“Then I shall take your tobacco away, Parson,” Amyas grins, “for we have to be up and away at first light. We’ve lazed here three whole days already, and nothing’s to show for it.”

“Shall we ever find anything? I swear the gold of Manoa’s like the gold at the foot of the rainbow—always in the next field you never quite reach.”

Amyas falls silent for a long minute, and the rest go quiet with him. No one can deny their hopes are all but spent. In the thousands of miles they have travelled, they have met nothing but disappointment.

“There’s only one last chance,” he says at last, “and that’s the mountains east of the Orinoco, where we failed the first time. The escaped Inca royals might well have moved their people there to hide.”

“Why not?” Cary nods. “They’d put all these forests, the wide grassy Llanos, and half a dozen great rivers between themselves and those Spanish dogs, wouldn’t they?”

“Shall we make one more try?” Amyas says. “This river must flow straight into the Orinoco. Once we hit that main waterway, we’ll be right at the foot of those mountains. What do you say, Yeo?”

“I can’t help but mind, your worship,” the old gunner says slowly, “that when we first sailed up the Orinoco the Indians told us terrible tales of those peaks. They stretch for hundreds of miles, they said, almost impossible to cross, with sheer cliffs up high and forests so thick in the valleys you can barely squeeze through. And haven’t we already lost five good men there?”

“What do we care? No forest can be thicker than the ones we’ve already hacked our way through. Half the time I wished I had a monkey’s tail to swing along the treetops, that would have been far nicer than tripping over hanging withes and getting eaten alive by crawling bugs from dawn till dark.”

“But don’t forget,” Jack adds, his voice a little nervous, “they warned us about the Amazons there, too.”

“What, Jack? Scared of a parcel of women?”

“Why wouldn’t I be?” Jack huffs. “I’d never run from a man, you know that. But a whole nation of women warriors? That’s not natural. They must be witches or devils. The Caribs were terrified of them. They said there were men there too with no necks, their eyes set right in their chests. How’s a Christian supposed to fight a creature like that?”

“He couldn’t very well cut their heads off, that’s certain,” Cary snorts, “but I reckon a good thump in the ribs will drop them just the same as anyone else.”

“Well,” Jack says, “if I’m going to fight, let me fight honest flesh and blood, no outlandish monsters. Who’s to say they aren’t invulnerable to blades, thanks to witchcraft?”

“Who’s to say they are?” Amyas laughs. “And as for those Amazons,” Cary adds, “a woman’s a woman the whole world over. I’ll bet you could wheedle them all round with a few pretty compliments, the same as you’d charm a Devonshire burgher’s wife. Pity I haven’t got my court suit and a fine Spanish feathered hat. I’d take an orange in one hand and a lace handkerchief in the other, go to them all alone as ambassador, and in a week I’d be as great a favourite with Queen Blackfacealinda as Walter Raleigh is with the queen at Whitehall.”

“Gentlemen!” Yeo speaks up, his deep voice steady, “where you go, I go. And not just me—every last man of us will follow, I’ve no doubt. But we’ve lost half our company now, used up all our shot, and without our good swords we’re no better off than the naked heathens around us. It was the noise and wonder of their ordnance, not to mention those breakneck horses I’d never trust a yard, that let Cortez and Pizarro, those imps of Satan, work their golden conquests. If we’d had the guns to astound the people of Manoa—”

“Assuming we ever found the said people,” Amyas laughs dryly. “It’s the old fable. Every tradesman thinks his own craft is the only thing holding the world together.”

“Well, your worship,” Yeo grins, “it may be that being an old gunner I prize guns too high. But you don’t need slate and pencil to work this sum out: are forty men with no shot as good as eighty who have plenty?”

“You’re right, old fellow, dead right,” Amyas says, his smile fading. “I was only jesting out of sheer sadness, laughing so I don’t start weeping. I think our chance of Manoa is gone, though I daren’t admit that to the men.”

“Sir,” Yeo says, his voice soft and grave, “I’ve had a feeling on me that the Lord’s hand is against us in this quest. Whether He means to keep that wealth for worthier men than us, or to hide that great city in the secret of His presence far from the greed of fighting men, to save those people from sinful man’s covetousness, and save England from that same sin and luxury I’ve seen gold breed among the Spaniards, I don’t know, sir. Who can ever know the counsels of the Lord? But for a long time a voice inside me has said, ‘Salvation Yeo, you will never see the earthly Golden City where heathens worship the sun and moon and the hosts of heaven. Be content then to look for that Golden City above, where there is no sun nor moon, for the Lord God and the Lamb are the light thereof.’”

There was a simple, quiet majesty about old Yeo when he spoke like this, that made his comrades—even Amyas and Cary—look on him the way pious Muslims look on holy madmen, as someone touched with flashes of secret inspiration. Brimblecombe, whose pious heart had long revered the old Anabaptist hero far above any petty churchman’s prejudice against his faith, answered gently, “Amen! Amen, all of us. It has been on my mind for months too, that Providence has turned us back every time we tried to push east. Look how these past two years, every time we struck east we ran into trouble and lost good men. Every time we turned westward-ho, we thrived, and still thrive to this day.”

“And what’s more, gentlemen,” Yeo adds, “if Scripture tells us dreams can come from the Lord, I swear mine last night came straight from Him. I lay by the fire, sirs, and I heard my little maid’s voice calling me, as plain as I ever heard it in my life. She was crying the very same words me and my old comrade William Penberthy taught her: ‘Westward-ho! jolly mariners all!’ It’s a silly, ungodly old sea shanty from our wild younger days, but there she stood calling it, plain as day, till I called back, ‘Coming, my maid, coming!’ And then the dear little lass fell silent—God grant I find her yet!—and I woke.”

Cary had long since stopped laughing at Yeo for talking about his lost little maid, and Amyas nodded. “So be it then, Yeo, if the rest agree. But what do we do sailing west?”

“Do?” Cary grins. “There’s plenty of work for our swords. They say there’s no end of gold, and no end of Spaniards, on the other side of these mountains. Our blades won’t be rusting for lack of adventure, my merry knights errant.”

They chatted on long into the night, and before the midnight hours had passed a desperate new plan was fully formed—what did those bold men care for risk? They would cross the Cordillera range to Santa Fe de Bogota, a city Yeo and Amyas had heard tales of for years when they sailed the Pacific, fabulously wealthy. They would try to seize the town, or at the very least intercept a gold convoy bound out of it, then make for the large north-flowing river they were told ran from that region, build new canoes, and fight their way back out to the Caribbean Sea. If fortune favoured them, they could seize a Spanish merchant galleon and sail home to England—not with the endless gold of Manoa, but with a fine, honest load of plundered Spanish treasure. It was a wild scheme, but no wilder than the one Francis Drake had already pulled off, and far less foolish than the one that cost Oxenham his life through his own reckless folly.

Amyas sat watching by the fire late that night, heavy of heart. To give up the dream he had cherished for years was hard. To face his mother back in Devon without the golden fortune he had promised her felt harder still. But he knew it was the only right path, for the sake of his surviving men. He laid the new plan before the camp at dawn, and the men cheered it wholeheartedly. They would sail upriver to the foothills of the mountains, rest a while, and if possible fetch back the wounded men they had left behind with their native allies, before setting off on their new venture, braced for whatever new dangers might come their way—they were long inured to hardship now.

They set off at sunrise the next morning, as cheerful as ever. For three hours they paddled easily up the glassy, windless stretches of the Meta, between two green walls of forest draped in bright flowering vines, alive with thousands of birds and iridescent butterflies. Long trailing vines hung from the overhanging boughs down to the water’s edge, their gorgeous blooms reflected clearly in the river’s still surface as if they were admiring their own beauty. River, trees, flowers, birds, insects—it was all a fairyland, but a colossal one, and the voyagers barely paid it any mind. It was an everyday sight to them now, to see two hundred foot trees smothered in yellow or purple bloom from root to highest twig, every branch draped in hanging gardens of crimson and orange orchids and vanilla vines. They had long grown used to the fantastic, enormous shapes Nature wears beneath the tropic sun and rain, to birds, fish, and butterflies brighter and stranger than any opium addict ever dreamed. The long processions of monkeys that kept pace with their canoes along the treetops, chattering, hooting, and howling, no longer made them laugh, the distant roar of a jaguar and rustle of a boa constrictor no longer made them start. When a brilliant green and rose-coloured piranha, flat-bodied like a bream, finned like a salmon, and saw-toothed like a shark, leapt straight into their canoe to escape the rush of a massive alligator—whose disgusting snout slammed against the canoe’s gunwale a foot away from Jack Brimblecombe’s hand—Jack did not turn pale, the way he once had at the sight of sharks off the South American coast. He coolly picked the fish up, laughed, and said, “Four pound weight! If you want to keep catching piranha for us like that, old fellow, you can hang around in our wake, and we’ll give you all the fish guts for wages.”

The human appetite for wonder is not nearly so infinite as folks back home fancy. All that craving for novelty, so ravenous in the grey everyday world of Europe, sates as easily as any other, leaving a man’s senses as dull to wild natural marvels as a city glutton’s is dull to fine feasts after a Lord Mayor’s banquet.
