That evening, the words arrived that would alter everything. Ethne retreated to her farmhouse, sinking into the parlour chair as the shadows of the summer dusk deepened outside. She felt a chill that had nothing to do with the cooling air; instead, she ordered the fire lit, staring into the glowing embers with a stillness that betrayed the storm raging within her. The moment she had awaited with such feverish anticipation—the brief, stolen hour when Harry Feversham would be hers—had finally come and gone. Now, she was utterly alone in her isolated hilltop village, cut off from the world below, feeling more desolate than she had since Willoughby sailed away that August morning down the Salcombe estuary.

From the day Willoughby arrived, Ethne had lived for that single half-hour. It had arrived, it had passed, and now the silence was deafening. She knew the weight of this emptiness; she had always anticipated the hollow ache that followed sending Harry away, the desperate urge of her heart to summon him back. Yet, she forced herself to endure the shivering cold, reminding herself that she had seen this coming. Tomorrow, she would recall the parting at Lennon House, realizing how trivial this separation truly was compared to that night. Her mind drifted to Harry, constructing a future of glory and distinction for him, while Dermod's ancient collie nudged her hand with an instinctive understanding of her distress. Ethne rose, cupping the old dog's head in her hands and kissing him. She thought of his impending death, and the vast expanse of years that might stretch between losing him and facing her own final moment.

Just as her thoughts darkened, a knock sounded at the door. A servant announced that Colonel Durrance was waiting.

"Yes," Ethne replied, rising immediately. She did not retreat into the sanctuary of her grief; she stepped forth to meet him as if nothing extraordinary had occurred an hour prior. They spoke of their marriage plans and the restoration of Lennon House, though the conversation felt fragile. It was difficult, but Ethne had grown accustomed to navigating such complexities. That night, however, Durrance made her path steeper. After the maid served tea, he requested that she play the *Mussolini Overture* on her violin.

"Not tonight," Ethne hesitated, feigning fatigue. "I am rather tired."

She had scarcely finished speaking before she changed her mind. Ethne resolved that in both grand gestures and mundane details, she must not shy away. The small, daily occurrences demanded her utmost vigilance. "Still, I believe I can manage the overture," she said, forcing a smile as she retrieved her violin.

She played from start to finish. Durrance stood by the window, his back to her, until the final note faded. Then, he approached her side.

"I was rather a brute," he murmured softly, "to ask you to play that piece tonight."

"I wasn't eager to play," Ethne admitted, setting the instrument down.

"I know. But I needed to discover something, and I knew no other way."

Ethne turned to him, her face alight with sudden alarm. "What do you mean?"

"You are rarely off your guard," Durrance continued calmly. "Only when you play. Once before, when you performed this overture, you were completely unguarded. I realized that if I could induce you to play it again tonight—that specific tune once strummed in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—I might catch you in that vulnerable state."

His words stole her breath and drained the color from her cheeks. She rose slowly, eyes wide, unable to comprehend. How could he possibly know? It was impossible. Yet, Durrance pressed on.

"Well? Did you return the feather? The fourth one?"

These were the unthinkable words. Durrance spoke them with a gentle smile, and it took Ethne a long moment to realize he meant exactly what he said. Her senses seemed overwhelmed; she wondered if she were hallucinating. But he repeated the question, leaving no room for denial.

"Who informed you of a fourth feather?" she whispered, her voice trembling.

"Trench," Durrance answered simply. "I encountered him at Dover. However, he mentioned the fourth feather only because I already knew of the first three. If I hadn't known of the initial three, I wouldn't have recognized him upon his arrival from the steamer, nor would I have asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' Knowing of the three was sufficient."

"How do you know?" Ethne cried out, a wave of despair washing over her. As she moved closer, he gently grasped her arm.

"But since I know," he protested warmly, "does the method of my knowledge matter? I have suspected for a long time, ever since Captain Willoughby brought the first feather to The Pool. I chose to wait until Harry Feversham returned before revealing my certainty, and he has returned today."

Ethne sank back into her chair, stunned. She had guarded her secret so meticulously, yet to learn that for a year it had been transparent to another was a shocking blow. Even amidst her confusion, she gathered her wits, attempting to buy time by discussing trivialities.

"Were you in the church? Did you hear us on the steps? Or did you encounter him as he rode away?"

None of her guesses were correct, Durrance smiled. Ethne had inadvertently chosen the perfect topic to delay the inevitable revelation. Like anyone else, Durrance possessed his own vanities; since becoming blind, he particularly prized the acuteness of his perception. It delighted him to make discoveries that others deemed impossible for a blind man, and to explain the intricate steps of his deduction to a baffled audience.

"Not one of your conjectures is right, Ethne," he said, inviting her to probe further.

"Then how did you uncover the truth?"

"I knew from Trench that Harry Feversham would arrive soon. This afternoon, I passed the church. Your collie barked at me, confirming you were inside. Beside the gate, a saddled horse was tethered. Someone else was with you, certainly not a villager. Then, I persuaded you to play, and that performance revealed the identity of the rider."

"Yes," Ethne murmured, barely listening. "Yes, I see."

Regaining her composure, she spoke with a steady voice. "You departed for Wiesbaden for a year, shortly after Captain Willoughby's arrival. Was that the reason for your departure?"

"I left because neither you nor I could sustain the charade we were maintaining," Durrance explained. "You pretended to harbor no thoughts for Harry Feversham, caring little for his fate. I pretended not to have discovered that, above all else, you loved him. Eventually, one of us would have faltered. I dared not fail, nor did I want you to. I could not allow you, who declared that 'two lives must not be ruined because of me,' to spend a year believing two lives had been sacrificed. Similarly, you could not bear for me, who stated that 'marriage between a blind man and a woman requires more than friendship,' to remain unaware that on your side, there existed only friendship. We were perilously close to collapse, so I withdrew."

"You did not fail," Ethne said quietly, her tone heavy with self-reproach. "It was only I who failed."

She berated herself bitterly. She had dedicated herself to shielding this man from knowledge that would compound his misfortunes, and she had failed. Conversely, he had protected her from realizing her own failure, succeeding where she had stumbled. It was not merely the humiliation of being outwitted by the man she believed she had deceived that troubled her. Rather, she felt she ought to have succeeded, for her failure had deprived him of his final opportunity for happiness. That was the true sting.

"But it was not your fault," Durrance reassured her. "On occasion, you slipped your guard, but the conclusive evidence did not reveal itself solely through those moments. When you played the *Mussolini Overture* previously, on the night Willoughby brought you such joy, I absorbed the happiness radiating from your performance. Do not blame yourself. Instead, rejoice that I have uncovered the truth."

"Glad?" she exclaimed, incredulous.

"Yes, for my sake, be glad." Gazing at him in awe, he continued, "Two lives should not be ruined because of you. Had you prevailed, had I remained ignorant, not two but three lives would have been destroyed because of your loyalty—yours, Harry's, and mine."

"Three?"

"Yours. Yes—yours, Feversham's, and mine. Maintaining the pretense during our few weeks in Devonshire was arduous enough. Admit it, Ethne! When I traveled to London to consult my oculist, it provided a respite, a pause allowing you to drop the mask and be yourself. It could not have endured much longer in Devonshire. But consider what happens when we live under the same roof, seeing one another hourly, with no visits to the doctor to offer excuses? Truth must eventually surface. It might have emerged gradually, suspicion stacking upon suspicion until doubt vanished entirely. Or it might have struck in a single, terrifying instant. Either way, clarity would have been achieved. And then, Ethne? What then?"
