Such a letter as the Major despatched—thin, sharp, and folded with the neat cruelty of a man who likes his bad news to arrive punctually—could not but set Doctor Portman in motion. He went to Fairoaks with the briskness common to worthy people when they have to perform a painful duty upon somebody else. A dentist, if you mark him, will sigh over your jaw, lament the necessity, call the instrument “unpleasant,” and then, with the air of a philanthropist saving your life, will tug as though the tooth were a personal enemy. So the Doctor, having read the epistle aloud to Myra and Mrs. Portman, seasoning every line with pious thunder against the young scapegrace who was “going down, down,” left the ladies to transmit the intelligence through Clavering with that speed and precision for which neighbourhoods are famed, and strode off to Fairoaks to administer his moral lancet to the widow.

He was forestalled. She had the news already.

Helen had read Pen’s own letter; and in some strange domestic arithmetic (which is not taught at universities, and never appears in Portman’s sermons), it had eased her. For months and months she had lived with a shadow in the house, a weight in the air, as if the very curtains listened for a misfortune. Now the thing had a name, a handwriting, a seal. The worst, whatever it was, had spoken. And—this was all her heart needed—her darling boy was coming home, repentant, tender-hearted, her boy again. Did she want a degree, if she had her son? What were these academical laurel-leaves, which sensible men and good divines shook like a rattle over a mother’s cradle? What good would the parchment do Pen, compared with the sight of him at her own table, hearing his own household sounds again—the kettle’s small singing, the clock’s faithful click, the soft fall of a foot on the stair?

The Rector fired his arrows of indignation; they fell upon her gentleness and dropped, blunted, to the carpet. Debts must be paid—certainly: but whose money was it but his? Why send him amongst temptations in the first place? Why not leave him at home, where the very air had known him as a child and would have kept him decent? She had arguments enough to bewilder virtue itself; and the Doctor went on, frowning and instructing, only to find that a mother’s forgiveness is a kind of armour: it does not ring with the blow; it merely closes more softly.

Meanwhile, another change had been growing at Fairoaks—a change more startling, to Mr. Arthur at least, than any examination-paper. There is a practice, ancient as brothers and sisters, by which they express affection with an innocent freedom; and this custom, between Pen and his little sister Laura, had been dropped of late by mutual consent, as quietly as one puts away childish shoes. For when Arthur came home after months of absence, he found that the simple girl he had left had become a tall, slim young lady, who offered him—not her cheek—but a curtsey, a hand, and a blush precisely upon the very spot where, in former days, he had been used to bestow his brotherly salute. It is astonishing how swiftly propriety grows up between two people who have not altered their hearts at all, only their height.

Miss Laura Bell at sixteen was what Clavering called “tall” and what small ladies called “gawky,” which is the revenge of the short upon the long. If she was a maypole, she had roses about her—those quick colourings that come and go like a sudden flame under thin skin. Her eyes, always large, were blamed for shining, as though Nature had committed an indiscretion in providing her with light; and when she lowered them behind their long black lashes, the same critics declared she did it to show the lashes. One would think some people would prefer women to look and see nothing at all. Her voice, low and sweet, had in it that domestic music which makes a room kinder—the very opposite of the hard clatter of the world outside; and though Miss Piminy, whose own appetite would have swallowed a button with difficulty only from sheer reluctance to share it, averred that Laura’s mouth was rather large, everybody allowed that her smile was charming and her teeth as neat as peeled almonds laid out for dessert.

This young lady, now that she had begun to think for herself (and in two years her mind and person had both shot up like a well-tended sapling), had been only half pleased with her brother’s late behaviour. His letters had grown rare and short—like the notes of a miser. Helen excused him: his studies, his engagements, his uncle’s plans. Laura would not have it.

“It would be better he should lose a prize,” she said, “than forget his mother. And I don’t see that he gets many prizes. Why doesn’t he come home and stay with you, instead of passing his vacations at fine houses? Nobody there will love him half so much as—as you do.”

“As I do only, Laura?” Helen sighed; and Laura, stoutly declaring she did not love Pen a bit when he neglected his duty, would not be convinced by any of those maternal arguments which mothers invent and children disbelieve—about men’s “ties” and “calls,” about acquaintance to be cultivated, and the necessity of a way in the world. Perhaps Helen did not quite believe her own excuses; but she tried to believe she believed them, which is a mother’s principal occupation.

And there was more than silence in Pen that vexed Laura. There had been a laxness in his talk, a lightness, an audacity—nothing gross, for he was always polite to women by nature and breeding; but he spoke of women in general with an easy disrespect, and in a dozen little offices of life he was less courteous in action than in phrase. It offended her that he smoked his horrid pipes in the house, turning a clean parlour into a travelling tavern; that he refused to go to church with his mother, or on walks and visits, and lay in his dressing-gown yawning over a novel when the widow returned from duty. The Pen of her childhood—of those evenings when Helen had told story after story of the boy’s virtues, bravery, and tenderness—had been a bright figure indeed; the Pen she now saw could be brilliant, yes, and bold, and sarcastic, and defiant, and seemed to scorn the gentle occupations and devotions of the two women who loved him most, quitting them on any light pretext, as though affection were a chair one rises from when bored.

Then came that Fotheringay affair. Laura heard of it first by Major Pendennis’s sarcastic allusions on a visit, and then by the neighbours, who, being charitable, had a plentiful supply of facts. The notion struck her like smoke in a nursery: Helen’s boy—galloping day after day to fall on his knees to an actress, to drink with her “horrid father,” to dream of bringing such people into his home, setting that woman over his mother! Laura blazed out.

“I would have run away, mamma; I would, if I had had to walk barefoot through the snow!”

“And you would have left me too, then?” Helen answered; at which Laura instantly retreated from her heroism, and the two women fell into one another’s arms with that warmth and foolish tenderness which sober historians pretend not to understand, though they profit by it all their lives.

Where had Laura’s indignation come from? Perhaps from hearing the story dressed in the bitter dialect of Mrs. Portman’s circle; perhaps from that old Mrs. Portman’s especial dislike since Pen had behaved “impertinently” to the Doctor and—crowning crime—had smoked cigars during church-time. Or perhaps, if one must rummage for explanations, it was jealousy; but that is a vice in which ladies, we are told, very seldom indulge. At any rate, against Helen she had no resentment. Her devotion to the widow was complete: passionate, foolish, inexhaustible—the sort of affection women give to the near female heart when their own is unattached elsewhere; the sort of affection that fills a house with whisperings and soft footfalls, and makes the very silence between two rooms feel inhabited.

And yet, the moment she heard that Pen was unfortunate and unhappy, all her wrath vanished like steam from a kettle when the lid is lifted. The Pen of old came back to her imagination at once—frank, generous, affectionate. She took Helen’s side against Doctor Portman with a courage that, in a general, would have earned medals.

Debts? What were debts? A trifle, a mere kitchen account, compared with the bill the world sends young men for being young in expensive company. Disgraced for not getting his degree? He must have been ill. His mind could not attend to “mathematics and stuff” with such worries. The tutors were jealous; examiners had favourites; other people were unfair and cruel: she was certain. So reasoned this young advocate, cheeks flushing, eyes bright with anger. She went up, seized Helen’s hand, kissed it in the Doctor’s presence, and looked at that good man as though to ask by what right he dared to say a syllable against her darling mother’s Pen.

The Doctor departed, discomfited and amazed to find two women obstinate against common sense and antiquity. Laura repeated her arguments tenfold once he was gone; and Helen, who wanted to believe there must be jealousy against Pen, found Laura’s passionate logic extraordinarily convincing. And truly, the calamity vexed them very little. Pen, in London, tortured with shame and remorse, fancying his mother stricken with grief, would have been astonished to see how lightly she bore it. Misfortune is sometimes welcome to women—if it brings the truant heart home again. Reduce a mistress to a crust, and she will eat the smallest bit herself, provided you will eat the rest beside her.

Indeed, directly Portman had gone, Laura flew to practical mercy. Fires were ordered for Mr. Arthur’s rooms; bedding aired; the very curtains seemed to shake out their dust and prepare to receive the prodigal. Helen wrote at once a letter so tender that each line might have been warmed at the grate before being put to paper. When she had finished, Laura, smiling with that fond business-like happiness young women have in doing kind deeds, took her mamma by the hand and led her into Pen’s apartments, where the fires blazed cheerfully. There the two sate down upon the bed and talked of Pen ever so long—of his childhood, his voice, the way he used to laugh, the looks he had when he was good and when he was naughty. Laura added a postscript, calling him her dearest Pen, bidding him come home instantly, and underlining the command with two of the handsomest dashes, as though punctuation could drag a son down the road by the collar.

That night they read their Bibles more than usual, as people do when they are at once frightened and hopeful; and as they passed to their own room they took a look into Pen’s, as if by seeing the empty bed they could summon its occupant.

In the middle of the night, Laura cried suddenly, “Mamma, are you awake?”

“Yes,” Helen answered.

The truth was, though she lay still as death, she had not slept for an instant, but watched the night-lamp in the chimney and listened to the house as if each creak were Pen’s foot upon the stair. Laura, too, had been lying with the hypocrisy of the innocent—motionless, silent, and wide awake, her thoughts as busy as mice in a pantry. Her head not unfrequently chose to occupy that pillow where the late Mr. Pendennis’s nightcap had been wont to press; and now, in the dark, she unfolded a plan, notable, triumphant, and as easy in her telling as making toast.

“You know, mamma,” she began, “I have been living with you for ten years, and you have never taken any of my money, but treated me as if I was a charity girl. This has offended me extremely, because I am proud and do not like to be beholden. If I had gone to school—only I wouldn’t—it must have cost at least fifty pounds a year; so clearly I owe you fifty times ten pounds, which you have put in the bank at Chatteris for me, and which doesn’t belong to me a bit. To-morrow we will go to Chatteris and see that nice old Mr. Rowdy with the bald head and ask him for it—not for his head, but for the five hundred pounds. I dare say he will send you two more, which we will save and pay back. And we will send the money to Pen, and he can pay all his debts without hurting anybody; and then we will live happy ever after.”

What Helen replied was made up of incoherent exclamations, kisses, embraces, and such soft nonsense as women, when they are noble, are apt to indulge in. But after that, the two slept—soundly, sweetly—like people who have put a heavy box down at last.

When the night-lamp went out with a little splutter and the sun rose over the purple hills, and the birds began their clear January piping among leafless branches and glistening evergreens on the Fairoaks lawn, Helen woke and looked at the sleeping girl beside her. Laura’s lips were parted with a faint smile; her cheeks held those shy roses that come even in dreams; and as the child’s breath rose and fell like a gentle tide, Pen’s mother felt thankful beyond what words can carry—thankful with that kind of gratitude which turns itself, as naturally as a bird turns to light, toward the Giver of love and mercy.

Although it was January and cold, so sincere was Pen’s remorse, and so determined his new economy, that he would not take an inside seat in the coach, but sat up behind with the Guard, who remembered his former liberality and lent him great-coats enough to make a small regiment. Perhaps it was the cold that made his knees tremble when he got down at the lodge-gate; or perhaps it was the thought of the kind creature for whose love he had made so selfish a return. Old John was waiting for the baggage, but appeared in fustian, and no longer wore the drab and blue livery.

“I’se garner and stable man, and lives in the ladge now,” John remarked with a welcoming grin and a blush, as if even his employment had grown shy; and then, as Pen turned the shrubbery corner and the coach was out of sight, Helen herself came, her face beaming with love and forgiveness—for forgiving is, with some women, the dearest feast of all.

She had written already to Pen of Laura’s offer—the noble, magnanimous, magnificent offer—filling her letter with blessings upon both her children; and this knowledge, no doubt, caused Pen to blush when he saw Laura waiting in the hall. On this occasion—on this occasion only—she broke through the little compact of handshakes and curtseys which had been established of late between them; but there has been far too much kissing in this history already, and the reader may supply such embraces as are necessary without further assistance.

So the prodigal came home; the fatted calf was killed (and, to do Fairoaks justice, killed properly, with a respect for soup, roast, and cold collations that would have pleased any serious student of English domestic cookery); and he was made as happy as two simple women could make him. No one alluded to Oxbridge. No one questioned him as to the future. The house, which had listened so long for sorrow, now listened for him: the crackle of the parlour fire, the soft clink of tea-things, the whisper of skirts, the spoon’s small tap against china—sounds that are a kind of music, and far better for the mind than the roar and grinding din of the great world’s streets. Even Pen, moody as he was, could not but feel the relief of it, as one feels relief in stepping out of a factory into a garden.

Yet he debated everything anxiously in his own room. A few days after his return he rode to Chatteris on his horse and came back atop of the coach—a change of dignity which he called economy. He informed his mother he had left the horse to be sold; and when it was sold, handed her the cheque. Helen (and perhaps Pen too) thought this an act of uncommon virtue and self-denial; Laura pronounced it strict justice, which is a colder dish and healthier.

He rarely mentioned the loan Laura had devised, and which Helen had accepted with certain modifications; but once or twice, with hesitation and stammering, he thanked her. It plainly pained his vanity to be beholden to the orphan for succour. He was wild to find some means to repay her—wild, too, to scrape dignity out of the ashes of his failure.

He left off wine, and took to whisky-and-water, but with moderation, as if even his vices were now under tutoring. He gave up cigar-smoking; but it must be confessed he had come to like pipes as well or better, so the sacrifice was not so martyr-like as it sounded. He fell asleep a great deal after dinner when he joined the ladies in the drawing-room; and he was certainly moody, melancholy, and prone to stare at nothing as though nothing were a landscape. He watched the coaches with interest, walked into Clavering to read the papers assiduously, dined with anybody who would ask him (the widow being glad he should have any entertainment in their solitary place), and played much cribbage with Captain Glanders. He avoided Doctor Portman; and the Doctor, whenever Pen passed, gave him severe looks from under his shovel-hat, as though a glare were a sacrament.

Pen went to church with his mother, however, very regularly, and read prayers at home to the diminished household. Diminished it was indeed. Two maids now did the work of the house at Fairoaks; the silver dish-covers never saw daylight. John put on his livery for church on Sundays to assert his dignity, but it was only for form’s sake: he was gardener and outdoor man now, vice Upton resigned. There was little fire in the kitchen; John and the maids drank their evening beer by a single candle, and the house’s old cheerful hum seemed pared down to a thin thread of sound. It was all Pen’s doing—his economy, his penance—and the austerity did not brighten him. A quiet house can be a comfort; but a house made quiet by want can feel, even to the penitent, like being shut up inside one’s own bad conscience.

For a time Pen declared no power on earth could induce him to return to Oxbridge after his failure. Then one day Laura, with many blushes, said she thought that, as a reparation—as a punishment upon himself for his idleness—he ought to go back and get his degree, if he could fetch it by doing so. And so back Mr. Pen went.

A plucked man is a dismal creature in a university. He belongs to no set and is owned by no one. Pen felt himself stripped indeed of all his fine feathers—those brilliant years’ plumage—rarely appearing out of college, going regularly to morning chapel, and shutting himself up of nights away from the noise and suppers of undergraduates. There were no duns at his door now: they were paid. There were scarcely any cards: the men of his year had taken their degrees and were gone. He went in for a second examination and passed with perfect ease; and when he wore his bachelor’s gown, he felt—if not happy—at least less naked.

On his way back he paid a visit to his uncle in London. The old gentleman received him with cold looks and would scarcely give him his forefinger to shake. Pen called a second time; Morgan said his master was from home. So Pen came back to Fairoaks—and to books, and to idleness, and to loneliness and despair.

He commenced several tragedies and wrote many copies of verses of a gloomy cast. He formed plans of reading and broke them. He thought of enlisting—of the Spanish legion—of a profession. He chafed against his captivity and cursed the idleness that had caused it. Helen said he was breaking his heart, and grieved to see his prostration. As soon as they could afford it he should go abroad—he should go to London—he should be freed from the dull society of two poor women.

It was dull—very, certainly. The widow’s habitual melancholy deepened into a sadder gloom; and Laura, listening with a girl’s quick ear to the house’s changing sounds—the fewer footsteps, the softer voices, the longer silences—saw with alarm that her dear friend became every year more languid and weary, and that the pale cheek grew more wan.
