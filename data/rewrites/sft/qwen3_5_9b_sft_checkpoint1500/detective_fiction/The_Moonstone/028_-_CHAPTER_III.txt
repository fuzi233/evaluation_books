My conscience, burdened by the gravity of Lady Verinder's frailty, forbade me to whisper a single word of my suspicion regarding the diamond's whereabouts before she had spoken. I sat in a respectful silence, internally arranging a few words of comfort to offer at the earliest opportune moment, bracing myself for whatever pain or shock might befall me.

"I have been seriously ill, Drusilla," my Aunt began, her voice thin but steady. "For some time past. And strangely, without realizing it myself."

A thought crossed my mind: the countless souls perishing in spiritual darkness, unaware of their condition. I trembled lest my poor aunt be among them. "Yes, dear," I replied softly. "Yes."

"I brought Rachel to London specifically for medical counsel," she continued. "I deemed it prudent to consult two physicians."

Two doctors! And, heaven help us, not a single clergyman! "Yes, dear?" I asked again, my heart sinking.

"One of these gentlemen was a stranger to me," she explained. "The other had been an intimate friend of my late husband and had always harbored a deep affection for me. After examining Rachel, he requested a private audience with me in another room. Naturally, I anticipated specific instructions regarding my daughter's treatment. Instead, he seized my hand with grave seriousness and declared, 'Lady Verinder, I regard you with both professional and personal concern. You are, I fear, in far more urgent need of medical aid than your daughter.' He questioned me, at first with queries I dismissed lightly, but his countenance darkened as my answers grew serious. He concluded by scheduling a visit for the following day, accompanied by a colleague, at an hour when Rachel would be absent."

"The outcome of that consultation," she whispered, tears welling in her eyes, "was delivered to me with gentle kindness. Both physicians agreed that precious time had been squandered, irretrievably lost, and that my case had now slipped beyond the reach of their art. For over two years, I have suffered from a treacherous form of heart disease. It showed no alarming symptoms, yet it has slowly, imperceptibly, destroyed me. The doctors cannot predict whether I shall survive a month or perish within the week. Their verdict is bleak."

She paused, composing herself. "It would be futile for me to deny that I have endured misery since learning the truth. Yet, I am more resigned than before, and I am diligently organizing my worldly affairs. My sole, crushing anxiety is ensuring that Rachel remains ignorant of my condition. If she knew, she would immediately attribute my decline to worry over the diamond and reproach herself bitterly for a guilt that is entirely hers not to bear. Both doctors concur that the deterioration began two, perhaps three years ago. I trust you, Drusilla, to keep this secret. I see genuine sorrow and sympathy in your gaze."

"Sorrow and sympathy!" I thought inwardly. "What pagan emotions to expect from a Christian woman so firmly anchored in her faith!"

Yet, as my aunt finished her tale, a flood of devout gratitude surged through me. Here lay a field of labor opened before me! Here stood a beloved relative, a dying soul, utterly unprepared for the transition, divinely led to entrust her fate to *me*. How could I articulate the joy that flooded my spirit as I recalled the vast network of clerical friends upon whom I could rely? Counted not by ones or twos, but by tens and twenties! I drew my aunt into an embrace, my tenderness overflowing. "Oh!" I cried fervently, "the profound interest you inspire in me! Oh, the good I intend to do for you, dear, before we separate!"

After offering a few earnest warnings, I presented her with a choice of three virtuous friends, all engaged in the work of mercy within her neighborhood, all tireless in their exhortations, all ready to serve at my command. Alas, the result was disheartening. Lady Verinder looked bewildered and terrified, rejecting my suggestions with the pragmatic objection that she lacked the strength to face strangers. I yielded—for the moment, at least. My extensive experience, gathered from fourteen years of visiting under the guidance of cherished clergymen, told me this required preparation through literature. I owned a small library of works tailored precisely to this emergency, designed to awaken, convince, prepare, illuminate, and fortify her spirit.

"You will read, dear, will you not?" I asked, employing my most charming demeanor. "If I bring you my own precious volumes? Turned down at the crucial passages, Aunt. Marked in pencil where you must pause and ask yourself, 'Does this apply to me?'" Even this simple appeal—an approach so thoroughly secularized by the world's influence—startled my aunt. "I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you," she murmured, her expression a mixture of surprise and unease that was both instructive and terrifying to behold.

Time was of the essence. The clock on the mantelpiece indicated I had just enough time to hasten home, procure a curated selection of twelve essential readings, and return in time to meet the lawyer and witness the signing of Lady Verinder's Will. Vowing faithfully to return by five, I departed on my mission of mercy.

When personal stakes are low, I am content to travel by omnibus. However, let me record the extent of my devotion to my aunt's welfare: on this occasion, I committed the sin of hiring a cab.

I drove home, selected and marked my first batch of readings, and returned to Montagu Square with a carpet-bag containing a dozen volumes, artifacts unique, I firmly believed, to European literature. I paid the cabman his exact fare. He received it with a profane oath; instantly, I threw him a religious tract. If I had brandished a pistol, this wretch could scarcely have displayed greater consternation. He leaped onto his box, shouting curses, and drove off furiously. Fortunately, my efforts were not in vain! I had successfully sown a good seed by tossing a second tract into the cab window.

The servant who answered the door was not the footman with ribbons, to my immense relief, but the footman proper. He informed me that the doctor had called and remained sequestered with Lady Verinder. Mr. Bruff, the family solicitor, had arrived a moment prior and was awaiting me in the library. I was ushered in to join him.

Mr. Bruff appeared surprised to see me. As the family solicitor, we had met previously under Lady Verinder's roof. He was a man, I regret to say, aged and gray-haired in the service of the world. A man whose business hours were devoted to the prophecies of Law and Mammon, yet whose leisure hours were equally suited for devouring novels or tearing up tracts.

"Have you come to reside here, Miss Clack?" he asked, glancing at my carpet-bag.

To disclose the contents of my holy repository to a soul of such profligacy would have invited a torrent of blasphemy. Lowering myself to his earthly plane, I stated my business.

"My aunt has informed me that she intends to sign her Will," I answered. "She has graciously asked me to serve as a witness."

"Aye? Aye? Well, Miss Clack, you shall do. You are over twenty-one, and you hold no pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder's estate."

"No pecuniary interest"? Oh, how thankful I felt to hear those words! If my aunt, possessing thousands, had remembered poor me—to whom five pounds is a fortune—and had included a modest legacy in her Will, my enemies might have questioned the motives behind loading me with the choicest treasures of my library and spending prodigally on a cab. Now, however, no scathing mocker could doubt the purity of my intent. It was infinitely better! Oh, surely, infinitely better!

These consoling reflections were interrupted by Mr. Bruff's voice. My meditative silence seemed to weigh heavily upon his spirits, forcing him to converse with me against his will.

"Well, Miss Clack, what is the latest news from the charitable circles? How fares your friend, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, following the beating he received from the rascals in Northumberland Street? Gad! They are spinning a rather convincing yarn about that charitable gentleman at my club!"

I had already glossed over his inquiry regarding my age and lack of financial stake. But the tone in which he alluded to dear Mr. Godfrey exceeded my patience. Bound, after the events of that afternoon, to assert the innocence of my admirable friend whenever it was questioned, I felt compelled to deliver a stinging rebuke to Mr. Bruff.

"I live largely outside the world," I stated. "I do not enjoy the privilege of belonging to a club. Nevertheless, I am acquainted with the story you mention, and I know that a vile falsehood has never been told."

"Yes, yes, Miss Clack—you believe in your friend. Natural enough. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite will not find the world at large as easy to convince as a committee of charitable ladies. The evidence is stacked against him. He was in the house when the diamond vanished, and he was the first to depart for London afterward. These are grim circumstances, viewed through the lens of subsequent events."
