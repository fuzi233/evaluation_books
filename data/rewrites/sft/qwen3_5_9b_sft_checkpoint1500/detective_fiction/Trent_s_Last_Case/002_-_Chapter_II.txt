A sharp vibration shattered the silence of the sole comfortably appointed chamber within the *Record*'s offices. Sir James Molloy did not rise; instead, he tapped his pen against his desk, a silent command to his secretary, Mr. Silver. With practiced efficiency, Silver abandoned his paperwork and moved to the ringing instrument.

"Who is calling?" Silver asked, his fingers deftly maneuvering the receiver. "Hello?... I'm afraid you'll have to shout, sir, the line is dead."

"Oh, it's Mr. Bunner!" Silver exclaimed, lowering his voice immediately. "Sigsbee Manderson's right-hand man. He says it's the most terrible news imaginable. He's calling from Bishopsbridge, so please keep it down, sir."

Silver held the receiver aloft. "Mr. Bunner speaks to you directly. He claims it is a matter of grave importance."

Sir James looked at the phone with a glint of professional hunger rather than affection. He snatched the receiver. "Speak, man," he commanded, his voice a resonant boom.

Silence followed for a heartbeat, broken only by the static of the line. Then, a voice crackled through, heavy with urgency. "It's murder, Molloy. Done in cold blood."

Sir James's composure cracked. A look of sheer astonishment, swiftly followed by a grim horror, washed over his swarthy face. "God Almighty!" he whispered, clutching the instrument as if it were a lifeline. He rose abruptly, his movements jerky, and leaned closer to the mouthpiece. "Yes... yes..." he repeated, his eyes darting around the room. His gaze snapped to the clock. Three fifteen.

"Hurry!" Sir James barked, turning to Mr. Silver. "Fetch Figgis and young Williams. Now!"

Mr. Silver bolted from the room.

Sir James turned back to the receiver. "Bunner, listen closely. Are you certain?" He listened intently, his brow furrowed beneath his thick black moustache. "Has anyone known for weeks? And the servants? Have they heard nothing? Very well. I must act. Thank you, old chap. I owe you a debt. Go to hell, no—go to town and find me the first moment you arrive."

He slammed the receiver down, the click echoing like a gavel strike. With sudden, predatory energy, Sir James seized a railway timetable from the rack. He scanned it rapidly, his eyes narrowing as he calculated the geography of the tragedy. "Marlstone," he muttered. "White Gables."

With a decisive jerk, he threw the timetable onto the desk. The door swung open, and Mr. Silver returned, ushering in a man of sharp features and spectacles, accompanied by a youthful clerk with an alert, eager eye.

"Figgis," Sir James said, his agitation vanishing like mist before the sun, replaced by a chilling, rapid calm. "Take notes. Every fact you gather goes straight into a special edition of the *Sun*. Make it fly."

Figgis nodded, pulling a notebook from his pocket and seating himself at the great writing table. "Silver," Sir James continued, addressing the secretary, "send a wireless to Jones, our local correspondent. Tell him to abandon everything and ride for Marlstone immediately. Do not reveal the cause in the telegram. Not a single word until the *Sun* hits the streets. You understand?"

"Yes, sir," Silver said, already moving.

"And you, Williams," Sir James pointed to the young clerk, "cross over to Mr. Anthony's office. Tell him he needs to prepare a two-column lead that will shake London to its foundations. Call it a scoop. Tell him Figgis will be there in five minutes with the facts, and instruct him to let Figgis write in privacy. On your way back, fetch Miss Morgan and demand her presence here. Also, check if we can get Mr. Trent on the wire."

Williams vanished with the speed of a shadow.

Sir James turned to Figgis, who sat poised with a pencil hovering over fresh paper. "Sigsbee Manderson is dead," Sir James announced, pacing the small space with his hands clasped behind his back. "Shot through the brain."

Figgis scratched frantically at his pad, his shorthand flowing with a mechanical precision that belied the gravity of the words. "Manderson and his wife, plus two secretaries, have been residing at White Gables, near Bishopsbridge, for the last month. He purchased the estate four years prior. Last night, around 11:30 PM, he retired as usual. He was not missed until this morning. A gardener discovered the body about ten o'clock, lying near a shed. One gunshot wound to the head, left eye. Instant death. The body was untouched, yet there are bruised wrists suggesting a fierce struggle. Dr. Stock has been summoned for the autopsy. Local police from Bishopsbridge are present but offer no leads regarding the killer. That is the dossier, Figgis. Mr. Anthony awaits you."

As Figgis prepared to depart, he raised an eyebrow. "An able detective from Scotland Yard has been assigned to the case," he noted casually. "Standard procedure."

"If you wish to believe it," Sir James allowed dryly.

"And the widow?" Figgis pressed. "Was she present?"

"Yes. What of her?"

"She appears prostrated," Figgis ventured, adopting a tone of sympathetic journalism. "Struck dumb by the shock. Pure human interest."

Before Sir James could respond, a soft voice chimed from the corner. "I beg you, Mr. Figgis, do not publish that sentiment."

Miss Morgan stepped forward, a pale, graceful figure who had materialized during the briefing. "I have seen Mrs. Manderson," she stated quietly. "She looks entirely composed, intelligent, and lucid. I doubt the shock has rendered her catatonic. She is likely assisting the police with whatever information she possesses."

Sir James offered a fleeting, amused smile. "That suits your style better, Miss Morgan. Your efficiency is legendary. Cut the drama, Figgis. Go."

"As you command," Figgis muttered, gathering his notes.

"Now, Madam," Sir James addressed Miss Morgan, "do you know what I require?"

"Our biography of Manderson is current," she replied, her dark lashes dropping thoughtfully. "I reviewed it merely months ago. It stands ready for tomorrow. However, I suggest the *Sun* utilizes the sketch from two years ago, penned when he settled the potash dispute in Berlin. It remains their finest piece. Our archives contain numerous cuttings, though largely trivial. I shall dispatch our two exclusive portraits immediately. The superior one is a drawing by Mr. Trent, executed when they shared a voyage together. It surpasses any photograph, though the public often craves the grainy reality of a bad photo over the artistry of a drawing. I will send them down promptly for your selection."

She paused, her expression shifting slightly. "However, I foresee a logistical hurdle. Unless you dispatch a special agent immediately, the *Record* will lack a field report for tomorrow's issue."

Sir James let out a long, weary sigh. "Pray, tell me, what value does our organization retain in such catastrophes?" he asked Mr. Silver, who had returned to his desk. "Even she recites Bradshaw by heart."

Miss Morgan adjusted her cuffs with a serene patience. "Anything further, sir?" she asked as the telephone bell jangled insistently.

"One final matter," Sir James said, answering the call. "Miss Morgan, I need you to commit a blunder. An 'everlasting bloomer,' if you will. Something to give us plausible deniability."

A fraction of a charming smile touched Miss Morgan's lips as she departed.

Sir James dialed the number of the rival newspaper, the *Sun*. "Mr. Anthony?" he asked. He engaged in a hushed, urgent consultation with the editor, a man described as the Murat of Fleet Street, who thrived on chaos and speed. Sir James rarely visited the *Sun*'s offices in person; he preferred to operate from a distance, respecting the chaotic atmosphere of the evening press.

Five minutes later, a uniformed page rushed in. "Mr. Trent is on the wire, sir!"

Sir James cut his conversation short. "Put him through immediately."

"Hullo!" Sir James shouted into the receiver.

"Hullo, be blowed!" the voice roared back. "What do you want?"

"This is Molloy," Sir James said firmly.

"I know it is," the voice replied. "This is Trent. I am currently painting a canvas and have been interrupted at a critical juncture. I hope this warrants a holiday, that's all!"

"Trent," Sir James said, his voice dropping an octave, imbued with authority. "This matters. I require your assistance."

"Some play, you mean?" the voice scoffed. "Do not delude yourself. I harbor no desire for leisure. My creative fervor is at a fever pitch. I am producing masterpieces. Why cannot you simply let a man work?"

"A grave incident has occurred."

"What?"

"Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered. Shot through the brain. The perpetrator remains unknown. The body was located this morning at his residence near Bishopsbridge." Sir James relayed the facts with brutal clarity. "What is your assessment?"
