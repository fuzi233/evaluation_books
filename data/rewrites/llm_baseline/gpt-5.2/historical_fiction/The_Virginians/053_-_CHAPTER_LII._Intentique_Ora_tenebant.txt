We dragged on through months of that weary fort-life: the commandant and I snarling and making it up again like ill-sorted spouses; greasy packs of cards slapping the table; his asthmatic flute wheezing alongside my cracked guitar; the drum at retreat cutting clean through any little comfort our noise contrived. Outside the palisade the woods kept their own music—wind in the high leaves, the low talk of water where the rivers met—yet within, the fort’s sounds were meaner: a cough, a curse, a bottle knocked against a cup, a sentry’s boots pacing the plank. It is a curious tyranny, that of a place: it teaches you how a man’s temper can be tuned by nothing more than the pitch of a whistle and the constant rasp of a file on iron.

La Biche—“the Fawn,” as they called her, with that brutal fondness by which conquerors make pets of what they have broken—took her beatings and her drams as Museau dispensed them. She was made to nurse everything that needed nursing: her own papoose, the commandant when the gout made him howl, and once, when fever shook me like a dog in winter, me too. She moved about the fort’s little domestic labours with a quiet exactness that only those learn who must keep order in a life where they are allowed none. She fetched in from the river and the fields the small necessities of our table—corn, onions, a handful of herbs bruised between her fingers to wake them; she made a thin soup out of what there was, and, when she could, she thickened it with a trick of patience, letting it simmer until even poor roots tasted of something like home. Her face rarely altered; but her ears—hers were always working. She listened as if the very air might betray a danger before eyes could.

At the beginning of the fall of last year a hunter came in with news, and the fort, which had been languid as a sickroom, stirred like a pot just set over a high fire. Montcalm, they said, had sent up a considerable detachment to garrison the posts already in French hands and to thrust further into the enemy’s—meaning our—possessions. The troops had left Quebec and Montreal and were coming up the St. Lawrence and the lakes in bateaux, bringing artillery and provisions and all the heavy furniture of war. Museau would be superseded by an officer of higher rank; and such a change, while it made the soldiers clap and sing and swear of victories to come—Albany, Philadelphia, the Rosbifs driven into the sea—put an altogether different taste in my mouth. A new commander might exchange me; or, with a shrug, might hand me to the Indians in reprisal for cruelties our own backwoodsmen had practised on captured Frenchmen. In these border wars, men paid for other men’s sins as if guilt were a coin passed from palm to palm.

The garrison swaggered about their conquest of all America from Mississippi to Newfoundland; yet, for all their boasting, I noticed that the prospect did not sweeten Museau’s humour.

“Eh, commandant,” said I, “’tis fort bien. But meanwhile—your farm in Normandy, your pot of cider, your trippes à la mode de Caen—where are they?”

“Ay,” says he, making a face as if he could already smell the apple-press and the stewing onions of his parish, “it is very well, my garcon. But where will you be when old Museau is turned out? Other officers are not companions like me. Very few men in the world have my humanity. When there is a great garrison here, will my successor give you indulgences as honest Museau has? You will be kept in a sty like a pig for killing. And as sure as one of our officers falls into your frontier brigands’ hands and harm comes to him, so surely will you pay with your skin. You will be given to our red allies—to La Biche’s brethren. Did you see last year what they did to your countrymen we took with Braddock? Roasting was the smallest punishment—was it not, La Biche?”

Then, with that ghastly cheerfulness some men assume when they talk of horrors as if they were recipes, he went on with a list of torments—eyes burned, teeth and nails wrenched, flesh gashed and hacked—until the very air of the room seemed thick with imagined smoke. La Biche did not flinch. She had seen such work. She came of the Senecas by the great cataract between Ontario and Erie; her people had fought now for the English, now against them; and in the wars among tribes and between tribes and whites, it is hard to say which skin has worn the more savage heart.

“They may chop me into cutlets and broil me, ’tis true, commandant,” said I, keeping my voice cool. “But again I say: you will never have the farm in Normandy.”

“Go get the whisky-bottle, La Biche,” said Museau.

“And it is not too late even now,” said I, leaning into the one appetite that ruled him best. “I will give the guide who takes me home a large reward. And I promise, as a man of honour, ten thousand livres—to whom shall I say? To one who brings me any token—my watch and seal, with my grandfather’s arms—which I have seen in a chest somewhere in this fort.”

“Ah, scelerat!” he roared with hoarse laughter. “You have eyes! All is good prize in war.”

“Think,” I whispered, as if to myself, though I meant every word for him, “of a house in your village; a fine field hard by; half a dozen cows; an orchard covered with fruit.”

“And Javotte at the door with her wheel,” he whimpered suddenly, “and a rascal child or two, cheeks as red as the apples! O my country! O my mother! Quick, La Biche—the whisky!”

All that night he sat deep in thought, and La Biche, too, silent and melancholy. She kept apart nursing her infant; and whenever my eyes drifted to her, I found hers fixed on me—not boldly, not beseeching, but with a steady attention, as if she were holding in her mind some path that must not be forgotten. The little one began to cry, and Museau—his tenderness always reserved for the idea of Normandy—ordered her away with foul language to the hut where she slept with her child. When she was gone, the commandant and I spoke freely, and I laid before him such reasons as his cupidity could not resist.

“How do you know,” he asked, “that this hunter will serve you?”

“That is my secret,” said I. And, since we were dealing in anything but honour, I may as well say how it came to pass.

When hunters came in for trade, they often stayed a day or two for rest and drink and company, and our new friend loved all three. He gambled with the men; he set his furs against their liquor; he sang and danced with them. There being little else to fill the hours, I was often made to sing and scrape my guitar, and there would be a kind of concert in the smoky room: men clapping time with their palms, boots stamping, voices joining in chorus until the drum called them back to discipline, that blunt instrument which flattens all human noise into obedience.

The hunter was present at one such evening, and I watched him as I sang our French catches. Then, as if on a whim, I said, “My lads, I will give you an English song.” To the tune of “Over the hills and far away”—an air my good old grandfather used to hum, loving it as a soldier loves what carries him out of mud and hunger—I made doggerel words of my plight:

“This long, long year, a prisoner drear;
Ah, me! I’m tired of lingering here:
I’ll give a hundred guineas gay,
To be over the hills and far away.”

“What is it?” says the hunter. “I don’t understand.”

“’Tis a girl to her lover,” I answered. But I saw by the twinkle in his eye that he understood well enough.

Next day, when no one stood within hearing, he passed me and hummed low, in perfectly good English, “Over the hills and far away”—the burden of my nonsense.

“If you are ready,” says he, “I am ready. I know who your people are, and the way to them. Talk to the Fawn; she will tell you what to do. What! you will not play with me?” Here he pulled out cards as two soldiers came up, and shifted into French with a grin. “Milor est trop grand seigneur? Bonjour, my lord!”

He mocked me with a bow and went off to drink elsewhere, shrugging as if my fate were merely another hand dealt and thrown away.

I knew now that La Biche was to be the agent, and that my offer had found its mark in Museau. The poor Fawn performed her part with a dexterity that would have been admired in any court where people call cunning “policy.” She had long been allowed free communication with me: she had tended me when I lay weak, helped with the work of my little chamber, my cooking, and so forth. (Prison fare is mostly noise and want; yet even there the body begs for some small mercy of the senses—warmth, salt, the remembered smell of bread. Many a day I watched her coax a better flavour out of mean stores: a sprig of potherb bruised and dropped at the right moment; corn boiled until it lost its rawness; a bone kept back to give tomorrow’s pot a ghost of richness. Such arts are a language too, and the conquerors did not even know they were hearing it.)

She was free to go out of the fort, to the river and the fields whence our scant corn and garden-stuff were brought. Meanwhile the trapper—Silverheels was the name he went by—having gambled away most of the money he got for his peltries, gathered his store of flints, powder, and blankets and took his leave. Three days after his departure, La Biche gave me the sign that the time was come.

When first wounded, kind Florac had placed me on his own bed in the officers’ room. Later, when the fort was emptied of all officers save the old lieutenant left in command, I had been allowed to remain in my quarters—sometimes pretty free, sometimes locked and fed on prisoners’ rations, sometimes invited to share Museau’s mess when my gaoler was in a good humour and a bad sobriety.

This officers’ house was of logs like the others, one of a half-dozen buildings within a fort that mounted only four small guns. One piece stood on the bastion behind my cabin. Looking westward over that gun you could see a small island at the meeting of the Ohio and Monongahela, where Duquesne sat; and on the opposite shore some trees stood in a clump.

“You see those trees?” La Biche said to me the day before, in her broken French. “He wait for you behind those trees.”

In daylight my door was open, and she came and went. That day she returned from the fields with a pick in her hand and a basketful of vegetables and potherbs for soup—the smell of damp earth clinging to the roots, the sharp green of bruised leaves rising when she shifted them. She sat on the bench at my door with the pick leaning against it and the basket at her side. I stood talking to her, stupid with anxiety; I believe I should never have thought to use that pick even if she had struck the wall with it before my eyes. At last, with a motion swift as a bird’s, she pushed it into my doorway so it fell into my room.

“Hide it,” she whispered. “Want it soon.”

And that afternoon she pointed again, silently, to the clump of trees.

Next day she came pretending to be very angry, calling out loud in her jargon, “My lord! my lord! why you not come to commandant’s dinner? He very bad! Entendez-vows?” As she peeped in, she flung a coil of rope at me.

“I am coming, La Biche,” said I, and hobbled after her on my crutch. As we went, she breathed, “Pour ce soir.” Then I knew.

Museau, for his part, knew nothing—nothing at all. He growled at me that the soup was cold. He looked steadily in my face and talked of this and that, not only while his servant was present but afterwards as we smoked and played piquet, while La Biche, in her wonted posture, sat cowering in a corner like a shadow. The soup—thin as it was—carried the faint taste of the garden she had brought in; it was a domestic thing in a place made for murder, and the contradiction sickened me.

Museau’s whisky-bottle was empty. With a knowing look he said he must have another glass—we must both have a glass that night. Rising, he stumped to the inner room where he kept his fire-water locked, away from La Biche, who could no more resist that temptation than a starving man can resist bread.

As he turned his back, La Biche raised herself. He was scarcely gone before she was at my feet, kissing my hand, pressing it to her heart, and bursting into tears over my knees—silent at first, then shaking as if some dam had broken in her. I had not understood—until that instant—the full measure of her attachment, nor how, in helping me, she was perhaps reaching for the one act in her life that belonged to her alone. When Museau returned I had not recovered my composure, though La Biche had slipped back to her corner and pulled her blanket up, face hidden.

He remarked nothing. We sat down again to cards, though my mind was so preoccupied the painted kings and knaves seemed like strangers.

“I gain everything from you to-night, milor,” says he grimly. “We play upon parole.”

“And you may count upon mine,” I answered.

“Eh! ’tis all that you have!” says he.

“Monsieur,” said I, “my word is good for ten thousand livres.” And the game went on, the cards whispering on the table like dry leaves.

At last he said he had a headache and would go to bed, and I understood the order that I was to retire too.

“I wish you a good night, mon petit milor,” says he. “Stay—you will fall without your crutch.” His eyes twinkled; his mouth wore a sarcastic grin. In the agitation of the moment I had forgotten my lameness and was walking away at almost a grenadier’s pace.

“What a vilain night!” says he, looking out; and indeed a tempest was abroad, roaring in the trees, rain driving in sheets. “Bring a lanthorn, La Tulipe, and lock my lord comfortably into his quarters!” He stood a moment in his doorway watching me, and I caught a glimpse of La Biche behind him, still as a carved figure.

The night was so wet that the sentries clung to their boxes and did not trouble me. The log-house was built with upright posts sunk deep, with horizontal logs laid on them. With the pick hidden in my room I began to dig under the lowest timber soon after tattoo. The earth was cold and heavy; each scrape sounded in my skull like a shout, though the storm kindly swallowed it. I worked in darkness until my hands were raw and my breath came short. It was some while after midnight when my fingers, feeling upward, found rain falling from without—the first cold touch of freedom. For two hours after that I worked more cautiously, widening the hole enough to admit my body.

Then I crept through to the parapet and flung my rope over the gun, not without a tremor lest the sentry should look down and send a charge of lead into me. The wall was but twelve feet; my drop into the ditch easy enough. I waited there a moment, letting my eyes accustom themselves, peering under the gun to find the river and the island. Above me the sentry paced and hummed—careless, domestic, as if the storm and the dark were his kitchen and he the master of it.

The darkness clarified; the moon rose, and I saw the river shining and the island’s rocks and trees crouched in the water. I made for the clump of trees as swiftly as my weakened leg allowed. Oh, the relief—sharp as the first mouthful of hot broth to a starving man—when I heard, low among the trunks, a voice humming, “Over the hills and far away.”

When I came to this part of my narrative, Miss Theo—seated by the harpsichord—turned round and dashed off the tune, and all the little company broke into the merry chorus; for people who sit warm by candlelight love to borrow the edge of danger and turn it into music.

“Our way,” I continued, “lay through a level tract of forest on the right bank of the Monongahela, which my guide knew as well as a cook knows the shelves of his pantry. He moved by sound as much as sight—the hush of a hollow, the different rustle of oak and maple, the far-off complaint of a night-bird—choosing the quieter paths, for even the wilderness has its alarms, and the loudest thing in it is often a man.”

By daylight we came to clearer ground, and Silverheels asked had I ever seen the spot before. It was the fatal field where Braddock fell, whence I had been wonderfully rescued the previous summer. Now the leaves were beginning to tint with our magnificent autumn—maples turning like embers, oaks bronze and wine-coloured, the whole forest as if some vast hearth had been stirred. Yet even that splendour had its own sorrow: it was a language the season spoke for a brief while, and then forgot, leaving bare branches that could not translate their own former glory.

“Ah, brother!” cries Harry, seizing my hand, when I told it. “I was gambling and making a fool of myself at the Wells and in London, when my George was flying for his life in the wilderness! Oh, what a miserable spendthrift I have been!”

“But I think thou art not unworthy to be called thy mother’s son,” said Mrs. Lambert very softly, with wet eyes. And indeed, if Harry had erred, the mark of his repentance—his love, his unselfish joy and generosity—made one feel there was hope even for the humbled young sinner.

“We presently crossed the river,” I resumed, “and took our course along the base of the western slopes of the Alleghanies, through grand forest of oaks and maples and enormous poplars rising a hundred feet without a branch. It was Indians we had chiefly to avoid, besides the outlying French. Always of doubtful loyalty, the savages have been specially against us since our ill-treatment of them and the French triumph two years ago.

“I was weak still, and our journey lasted a fortnight or more. As we advanced, the woods grew redder and redder; frost nipped sharply at night. We lighted fires at our feet and slept in blankets as best we might, listening always—listening for a twig snapped where it ought not, for a far cry, for that sudden hush in which even insects seem to hold their breath. At that time of year mountain hunters get their sugar from maples. We came on more than one such family camped by their trees near mountain streams; they welcomed us at their fires, and gave us venison—smoked, or roasted on spits, the fat hissing as it fell into the coals. The smell of it in the cold air was like a promise, and the taste brought back, for a moment, the idea of a table where people speak their own tongue without fear of who might overhear and punish the very words.

“So we crossed the two ranges of Laurel Hill and the Alleghanies. The last day’s march took us down that wild pass of Will’s Creek, a valley between cliffs near a thousand feet high—bald, white, broken into towers like fortifications—with eagles wheeling round the summits and watching their nests among the crags. Their cries rang thin and clean above us, and after the fort’s petty noises it seemed a kind of liberty merely to hear a sound that no man had trained or priced.

“And thence we descended to Cumberland, whence we had marched the year before, and where now there was a considerable garrison of our people. Oh, you may think it was a welcome day when I saw English colours again on the banks of our native Potomac.”
