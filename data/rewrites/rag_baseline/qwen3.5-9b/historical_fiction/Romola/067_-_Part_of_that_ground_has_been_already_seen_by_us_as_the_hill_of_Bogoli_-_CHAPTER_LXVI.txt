Palm Sunday, or as the olive-growers of the Valdarno knew it, Olive Sunday, arrived with a sky washed clean by the previous night's storm. The sun burned with a terrifying clarity, illuminating the dust of a city that had barely slept. Inside San Marco, Savonarola climbed the pulpit once more, greeted by a congregation whose faith remained unbroken, yet their eyes held a hollow reverence. With a calm that bordered on the tragic, he proclaimed his readiness to die, seeing his own execution in every shadow cast by the candlelight. He delivered the benediction, watching the sea of upturned faces, and then descended the steps, turning his back on the adoration forever. Before the sun dipped below the horizon, Florence erupted.

The tensions that had simmered since the previous day finally boiled over, fueled by incitement and tacit approval from the highest offices. The violence began at the Duomo, where Compagnacci attempted to disrupt the evening sermon intended for the Piagnoni. As blood rose in the throats of the mob and the disturbance escalated into a riot, the cry rang out: "To San Marco! Burn San Marco!" Long before darkness fell completely, the church and convent were besieged by a swelling tide of rage. Resistance was fierce; the monks, aware of the encroaching threat, wielded weapons within their walls, fighting with the vigor of Knights Templar in their long white robes. Even Savonarola's commands could not quell the instinct for self-defense among men whose muscles were still strong beneath their Dominican habits. Laymen who had refused to flee joined the fray, hurling stones and burning embers from the roof while swords clashed violently in the cloisters. Despite the overwhelming numbers of the attackers, the assault dragged on until the deepest hour of the night.

Throughout the conflict, the government's stance remained singularly hostile toward the convent. Early in the chaos, guards were dispatched not to disperse the rioters, but to order everyone inside to surrender their arms, command all laymen to depart, and demand that Savonarola leave Florentine soil within twelve hours. Had he complied then, he likely would have been torn apart; he was willing to go, but his friends obstructed him. Many high-ranking laymen deemed it too dangerous to exit via the garden wall, yet Francesco Valori chose to leave, hoping to rally reinforcements from outside. By the time the struggle reached its nadir—when the Compagnacci might have breached the library where Savonarola prayed with unarmed brethren—a second contingent of guards arrived, commissioned by the Signoria to arrest Fra Girolamo and his two coadjutors, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

When the lantern-light revealed the Friar emerging from the convent doors, escorted by a guard offering no protection beyond the prison walls, a roar of triumphant hatred tore through the crowd. The battle shifted instantly to the narrow streets leading to the Old Palace. Every man scrambled to position himself for the ultimate humiliation: to reach the Prophet's ear with insults, or worse, to strike and kick him. Armed Compagnacci, unimpeded by protocol, surged forward. As Savonarola was dragged and shoved through the jeering throng, illuminated by lanterns reflecting derisive faces, spat upon, and beaten with coarse epithets, he felt the bitterest sting of life pass. If judgment had fallen, death was the only remaining sentence. Yet the deepest despair lies not in martyrdom, but in the silent realization that one is unworthy of the title, that truth may prosper, but not through one's hand. This brief, imperfect victory of the mob ended as quickly as it began; the Friar vanished beneath the archway of the palace.

For the Compagnacci, however, the satisfaction was fleeting, akin to a tiger tasting blood. Their attention turned to the houses of the hypocrites' allies. Half the armed multitude, previously kept at bay during the siege, now moved with purposeful efficiency to sack wealthy residences. This was not random looting; it was a targeted operation against the homes of chief Piagnoni, proving the riot was orchestrated and led by sword-wielding officers. Next on the list was Francesco Valori, suspected of plotting with the Friar to establish a lifelong doge or gonfalonier. Then came the grey-haired man who had demanded justice eight months prior, only to escape San Marco to witness a different kind of retribution: his home surrounded by a greedy horde, his wife struck down by an arrow, and himself murdered en route to the Palazzo by the blades of the Ridolfi and Tornabuoni families. Thus, the Masque of the Furies played out across Florence through the night and into the dawn.

Yet the mastermind remained invisible, issuing orders from a secluded retreat, his reputation too lofty to allow his red plume to wave amidst the carnage. The location was a quiet house between Santa Croce and San Marco, the same chamber where Tito had recently visited Dolfo Spini. Here, the Captain of the Compagnacci spent the memorable night, receiving a revolving door of visitors—some disguised as armed brothers, others cloaked in obscurity. Wine flowed freely, cups raised for opportunists, though Spini, wary of excess, drank sparingly to fuel the adrenaline of the incoming reports. Among the shadowed guests, Ser Ceccone was a constant presence, accompanied late into the twilight by Francesco Cei, who hid in Florence awaiting the revocation of his exile upon the Friar's fall.

Candles burned down to ragged, shapeless stubs, and holes in the shutters glowed with a somber external light. Suddenly, Spini leaped from his chair, pacing with a flushed, angry countenance, reacting to the latest gossip from his two unmilitary associates. "The devil spit him! He shall pay," he roared, a manic laughter escaping him. "Ha, ha! The claws will descend when he least expects it. So *he* was to be the great man after all! Pretending to throw everything at my feet as if I were a blind beggar, while he winked and filled his own purse. I'd hang skins about him and set my hounds loose! And he stole that fine ruby of mine—I was a fool to gift it yesterday. Malediction! He laughed at me in his sleeve two years ago, ruining the finest plan ever devised. I trusted a rascal whose twisted schemes remain invisible to anyone but himself."

"A Greek, too," interjected Francesco Cei, a faint smile playing on his lips, "who dropped into Florence with gems packed about him."

"You did not choose your confidant wisely, my Dolfo," Cei remarked.

"He is cunning beyond you, Francesco, and handsome as well," Spini retorted, turning on his associate with a predatory glare.

"I humbly conceive," Ser Ceccone chimed in, "that Messer Francesco's poetic genius will outweigh—"

"Yes, yes, rub your hands! I detest that notary's trick," Spini snapped, impatient with the delay. "But here comes Taddeo, or someone else: now is the moment! What news?" Two Compagnacci entered, their faces heated.

"Bad," one reported. "The populace has decided they want to sack Soderini's house. We've blocked them, but if we don't act, they'll turn on us. I suspect Medicean agitators are buzzing among them; they may attack your palace over the bridge shortly unless we provide another bait."

"I have it!" Spini shouted, seizing Taddeo by the belt and dragging him aside for instructions while the other continued briefing Cei on the Signoria's interference regarding Soderini.

"Ecco!" Spini commanded moments later, pushing Taddeo toward the door. "Go, and make quick work."
