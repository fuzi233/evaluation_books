The following day, Mrs. Dashwood visited Lady Middleton, accompanied by two of her daughters. Marianne excused herself from the party under a trivial pretext of employment, leaving her mother content with the belief that Willoughby had promised to call upon them during their absence. Upon their return from the park, they discovered Willoughby's curricle and servant waiting at the cottage, confirming Mrs. Dashwood's conjecture. However, the moment they crossed the threshold, the scene unfolded that no foresight had prepared them for. Hardly had they stepped into the passage when Marianne emerged from the parlour, visibly distraught, her handkerchief clutched to her eyes. Without offering a greeting, she fled upstairs. Alarmed, Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed immediately into the room Marianne had vacated. Inside, they found only Willoughby, leaning against the mantelpiece with his back turned. As they entered, he spun around, his countenance betraying the same profound emotion that had overwhelmed his sister.

"Is anything the matter with her?" Mrs. Dashwood cried upon entering. "Is she ill?"

"I hope not," Willoughby replied, attempting to project cheerfulness, though a forced smile quickly followed. "In fact, it is I who may rather expect to be ill—for I am now suffering under a very heavy disappointment!"

"Disappointment?"

"Yes. I am unable to keep my engagement with you. Mrs. Smith has this morning exercised the privilege of riches upon a poor dependent cousin by sending me on urgent business to London. I have just received my dispatches and taken my farewell of Allenham; and, by way of exhilaration, I have come to take my farewell of you."

"To London! And are you going this morning?"

"Almost this moment."

"This is very unfortunate. But Mrs. Smith must be obliged; and her business will not detain you from us long, I hope."

Willoughby flushed as he answered. "You are very kind, but I have no idea of returning to Devonshire immediately. My visits to Mrs. Smith are never repeated within the twelvemonth."

"And is Mrs. Smith your only friend? Is Allenham the only house in the neighbourhood to which you will be welcome? For shame, Willoughby, can you wait for an invitation here?"

His color deepened further. With his eyes fixed on the floor, he could only mutter, "You are too good."

Mrs. Dashwood exchanged a look of surprise with Elinor, who felt equal astonishment. A heavy silence descended upon the room. Breaking the quiet, Mrs. Dashwood spoke first. "I have only to add, my dear Willoughby, that at Barton Cottage you will always be welcome. I will not press you to return here immediately, for only you can judge how far that might please Mrs. Smith; and on this head, I shall be no more disposed to question your judgment than to doubt your inclination."

"My engagements at present," Willoughby replied, his voice trembling slightly, "are of such a nature—that... I dare not flatter myself—" He trailed off.

Mrs. Dashwood was too stunned to speak, and another pause ensued. Willoughby broke it with a faint, strained smile. "It is folly to linger in this manner. I will not torment myself any longer by remaining among friends whose society it is impossible for me now to enjoy."

With hurried efficiency, he took leave of them all and departed. They watched him mount his carriage; within a minute, it vanished from sight. Mrs. Dashwood felt too overwhelmed for words and instantly withdrew to the adjoining room, seeking solitude to process the alarm this sudden departure had ignited. Elinor's uneasiness matched her mother's. She replayed the interaction with growing anxiety and distrust. Willoughby's behavior in taking leave—their shared embarrassment, his affected cheerfulness, and above all, his refusal to accept her mother's invitation—was profoundly disturbing. His reluctance was so unlike a lover, so alien to his own character, that it shook her foundations. One moment she feared no serious design had ever existed on his part; the next, she imagined some catastrophic quarrel had erupted between him and Marianne. The distress in which Marianne had retreated was precisely what a serious dispute would cause, yet considering the intensity of Marianne's love, a quarrel seemed almost inconceivable. Regardless of the specifics, her sister's affliction was undeniable. Elinor thought with tender compassion of that violent sorrow, fearing Marianne was not merely venting pain as a relief, but feeding and encouraging it as a duty.

Half an hour later, her mother returned. Though her eyes were red-rimmed, her countenance remained composed. "Our dear Willoughby is now several miles from Barton, Elinor," she said, sitting down to her work. "And with how heavy a heart does he travel?"

"It is all very strange. So suddenly to be gone! It seems but the work of a moment. Last night he was with us so happy, so cheerful, so affectionate? And now, after only ten minutes' notice—gone, without intending to return! Something more than what he confessed to us must have occurred. He did not speak, he did not behave like himself. You must have seen the difference as clearly as I. What can it be? Could they have quarreled? Why else such unwillingness to accept your invitation?"

"It was not inclination that he lacked, Elinor; I could plainly see that. He had not the *power* to accept it. I have thought it all over, I assure you, and I can perfectly account for everything that initially seemed strange to both of us."

"Can you, indeed!"

"Yes. I have explained it to myself in the most satisfactory way—but you, Elinor, who love to doubt where you can, it will not satisfy you, I know. Yet you shall not talk me out of my trust in it. I am persuaded that Mrs. Smith suspects his regard for Marianne, disapproves of it—perhaps because she holds other designs for him—and on that account is eager to remove him. The business she sends him to transact is likely an invention designed to dismiss him. This is what I believe to have happened. Furthermore, he is aware that she disapproves of the connection; he dares not, in his current dependent state, confess his engagement to Marianne, and thus feels obliged to submit to her schemes and abscond from Devonshire temporarily. You will tell me, I know, that this may or may not have occurred; but I will listen to no cavil unless you can offer a more satisfactory explanation. And now, Elinor, what have you to say?"

"Nothing, for you have anticipated my answer."

"Then you would have said it might or might not have happened. Oh, Elinor, how incomprehensible your feelings are! You prefer to take evil upon credit rather than good. You look out for misery for Marianne and guilt for poor Willoughby rather than an apology for the latter. You are resolved to deem him blameable simply because he took leave with less affection than his usual behavior suggests. Is no allowance to be made for inadvertence, or for spirits depressed by recent disappointment? Are no probabilities to be accepted merely because they are not certainties? Is nothing due to the man whom we all hold in such high esteem, and for whom there is no reason to feel ill-disposed? To the possibility of motives unanswerable in themselves, though unavoidably secret for a while? And after all, what exactly do you suspect him of?"

"I can hardly tell myself. But suspicion of something unpleasant is the inevitable consequence of such an alteration in his demeanor. There is great truth, however, in what you have now urged regarding the allowances we ought to make for him, and it is my wish to be candid in my judgment of everyone. Willoughby may undoubtedly have very sufficient reasons for his conduct, and I will hope that he does. But it would have been more characteristic of Willoughby to acknowledge them at once. Secrecy may be advisable; still, I cannot help wondering at its practice by him."

"Do not blame him for departing from his character where the deviation is necessary. But you really do admit the justice of what I have said in his defense? I am happy—and he is acquitted."

"Not entirely. It may be proper to conceal their engagement—if they are engaged—from Mrs. Smith, and if so, it must be highly expedient for Willoughby to remain away from Devonshire for the present. But this is no excuse for concealing it from us."

"Concealing it from us! My dear child, do you accuse Willoughby and Marianne of concealment? This is strange indeed, especially given that your eyes have been reproaching them daily for their incautiousness."

"I want no proof of their affection," Elinor insisted, "but of their engagement, I do."

"I am perfectly satisfied of both."

"Yet not a syllable has been said to you on the subject by either of them."
