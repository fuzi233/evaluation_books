The Nuns' House was preparing for a solemn silence. The Christmas break was imminent. What Miss Twinkleton had once, with scholarly pride, termed "the half"—now rebranded as the more elegant and strictly collegiate "term"—was set to expire tomorrow. For several days, the rigid discipline of the institution had softened noticeably. Club suppers had migrated to the bedrooms, accompanied by the carving of dressed tongues with scissors and the passing around of curling tongs. Marmalade was served on plates crafted from curling paper, and cowslip wine flowed from the small, squat measuring glass usually reserved for little Rickitts's daily steel drops. The housemaids had been bribed with scraps of ribbon and pairs of shoes worn thin at the heels to ignore crumbs found in the beds. Festive attire adorned the young ladies, and the daring Miss Ferdinand even startled the assembly with a spirited solo on a comb-and-paper instrument, only to be smothered by two long-haired accomplices beneath her own pillow.

These were not the only signs of impending dispersal. Packing boxes appeared in rooms that were otherwise tidy, and a frantic amount of luggage was prepared, disproportionate to the actual needs of the students. Gifts of cold cream, pomatum, and hairpins were liberally distributed among the staff. Under oaths of absolute secrecy, whispers circulated regarding the "golden youth" of England expected to arrive shortly. Miss Giggles, lacking in sentiment, claimed she honored this prospect by making faces, but she was overwhelmingly outvoted by her peers. By tradition, the final night before recess was declared a point of honor where no one slept, and ghosts were actively encouraged—a pact that invariably collapsed within minutes, resulting in everyone falling asleep early and rising early the next morning.

At noon on the day of departure, the concluding ceremony took place in Miss Twinkleton's drawing room, draped in brown holland. Supported by Mrs. Tisher, Miss Twinkleton presided over a table laden with white wine and slices of pound cake. "Ladies," she announced, "another revolving year has brought us to this festive season when the first impulses of our nature leap within our... bosoms!" She paused annually at this word, substituting "hearts" instead. "Our hearts! Like the sailor in his ship, the warrior in his tent, the prisoner in his dungeon, or the traveler in his carriage, we yearn for home. Do we not recall the opening lines of Mr. Addison's tragic masterpiece: 'The dawn is overcast, the morning lowers...'?"

"Not so," she corrected cheerfully. "From horizon to zenith, everything is *couleur de rose*, filled with the scent of our relations and friends. May they prosper as we expect, and may we prosper as they desire! Ladies, with our mutual love, we bid you farewell and wish you happiness until we meet again. And when the time comes to resume our studies—those pursuits which, ahem, require further discussion—let us remember the words of the Spartan General at a battle too famous to need naming: 'Hold fast.' The maids, wearing their finest caps, carried the trays; the young ladies sipped and crumbled cakes as the hired coaches began to clog the street. Departures were swift. Miss Twinkleton kissed each student's cheek and handed her a neatly folded letter addressed to her next-of-kin, marked "With Miss Twinkleton's best compliments." Delivered with an air of innocent surprise, despite being attached to the bill, the gesture masked the reality of the separation.

Rosa had witnessed many such departures. Having known no other home, she was content to stay, especially with her latest friend beside her. Yet, a void existed in that friendship. Helena Landless, privy to Rosa's brother's revelations and bound by a silent agreement with Mr. Crisparkle, avoided mentioning Edwin Drood entirely. The reason remained a mystery to Rosa, though she sensed the weight of it. Without Helena's counsel, Rosa could not resolve her own doubts, left to wonder why the name of Edwin was so rigorously suppressed, even as Helena assured her that a reconciliation awaited Edwin's return.

As the coaches departed, the High Street, usually drab, sparkled with youthful energy. The cry of "Good-bye, Rosebud darling!" echoed in silver voices. An effigy of Mr. Sapsea's father loomed over the doorway, seemingly urging the crowd to admire the remaining girls with appropriate spirit. Soon, however, the street drained of color and sound, returning Cloisterham to its usual stillness.

Inside, if Rosa waited anxiously for Edwin, Edwin was equally uneasy. Unlike the fairy-queen-like Rosa, whose beauty commanded adoration, Edwin possessed a conscience. Mr. Grewgious's moral compass had become immovable. Had it not been for the dinner at Staple Inn and the ring hidden in his breast pocket, Edwin might have drifted blindly toward marriage, trusting fate. But the gravity of his oath to the living and the dead had halted him. He faced a binary choice: give the ring to Rosa, or return it. Confronted with this narrow path, he found himself considering Rosa's claims with unprecedented selflessness, losing the certainty he once enjoyed.

"I will follow her lead," he decided as he walked from the gatehouse toward the Nuns' House. "Whatever happens, I will heed her words and strive to honor both the living and the dead."

Rosa, dressed for travel, awaited him. It was a crisp, frosty day, and Miss Twinkleton had graciously permitted outdoor exercise. Together, they stepped out before propriety demanded a formal offering to the shrine of convention.

"My dear Eddy," Rosa said softly as they turned onto the quiet paths near the cathedral and river, "I wish to say something very serious. I have been contemplating it for a long time."

"I intend to be serious and earnest with you, Rosa dear."

"Thank you, Eddy. Will you not deem me unkind for starting? Will you not think I speak only for myself? That would not be generous, would it? And I know you are generous!"

"I hope I am not ungenerous to you, Rosa." He ceased calling her "Pussy"; that era was over.

"There is no fear of quarreling, is there?" Rosa continued, clasping his arm. "Because, Eddy, we have ample cause to be lenient with one another."

"We will be, Rosa."

"That's a dear good boy! Eddy, let us be brave. Let us become brother and sister from this day forward."

"Never husband and wife?"

"Never!"

Silence stretched between them. Finally, Edwin spoke with effort. "Of course I know this has been in both our minds, Rosa. And I must confess freely that it did not originate with you."

"No, nor with you, dear," she replied with poignant sincerity. "It grew between us. You are not truly happy in our engagement; I am not either. Oh, I am so sorry, so sorry!" Tears welled in her eyes.

"I am deeply sorry too, Rosa. Deeply sorry for you."

"And I for you, poor boy! And I for you!"

This pure, gentle reciprocity cast a softening light on their situation. Their relationship no longer appeared wilful or a failure; it transformed into something noble, self-sacrificing, and true.

"If we had known yesterday—and we did know, on countless past days—that we were ill-suited for these chosen bonds, what better course could we take today than to alter them? It is natural to feel regret, and you see how deep ours is. But how much better to regret it now rather than later!"

"When, Rosa?"

"When it would be too late. And then anger would surely follow."

Another silence descended.

"You know," Rosa said innocently, "you wouldn't have liked me then; but you can always like me now, for I shall not be a burden or a worry. And I can always like you now, and your sister will cease to tease or toy with you. I often did when we were not siblings, and I beg your pardon."

"Don't let us reach that point, Rosa; or I shall require more forgiveness than I care to contemplate."

"No, indeed, Eddy; you are too harsh upon yourself, my generous boy. Let us sit on these ruins, brother, and let me explain how it unfolded. I believe I understand, having reflected deeply since your last visit. You liked me, didn't you? You thought I was a pleasant little thing?"

"Everyone thinks that, Rosa."

"Do they?" She furrowed her brow thoughtfully, then flashed a bright insight. "Well, suppose they do. Surely it wasn't sufficient that you viewed me merely as others did, was it?"

The point stood firm. It was insufficient.

"That is precisely what I mean," Rosa said. "You liked me well, accustomed yourself to me, and grew used to the notion of marriage. You accepted the situation as inevitable, didn't you? It was destined to be, so why question it?"
