Fra Colonna had the freedom of the Pope’s library, and by that freedom Gerard had, as it were, a key to Rome itself. Many a day, when the ink in the Vatican had dried upon their labours, the friar would shut a manuscript with the flat of his hand, and the two would go out together, walking where the streets were narrow, and the stones underfoot were a patchwork of the living and the dead.

To Gerard, whose eye could never keep itself from beauty, Rome came upon him like a feast brought in course after course: churches like vast loaves broken open to show gold within; palaces glittering as if sugared; ruins that had the savour of antiquity, dry and wholesome as old wine. The city’s very air seemed seasoned—smoke from braziers, damp from fountains, incense from open doors; and always, underneath, the hum and clink of a populous place: sandals and wheels, bells, a snatch of chant, a vendor’s cry. It was a music that fed the spirit as broth feeds the body.

The friar granted him the ruins—granted them as a man grants bread to the hungry—yet he dashed cold water on the rest.

“This place Rome?” said he. “It is but the tomb of mighty Rome.”

And, as if he were a cook turning the lid off a pot to show what lies beneath, he made Gerard look down—down through the present into the buried past. Here, twenty or thirty feet of a triumphal arch lay sunk as if the earth had swallowed it. There, a modern street ran right over what had been a palace; and columns that once bore roofs and emperors’ dreams now showed only their crowns, like bones through thin soil. He pointed with a dry finger, and his voice took that relish it always had when he could season a lesson with a sharpness from antiquity.

“I tell thee this village they call Rome is but as one of those swallows’ nests ye shall see built on the eaves of a decayed abbey.”

“Old Rome must indeed have been fair then,” said Gerard, with the simple reverence of a man who feels greatness as he feels warm bread in the hand.

“Judge for thyself, my son. Thou seest the great sewer, the work of the Romans in their very childhood, and it shall outlast Vesuvius. Thou seest the fragments of the Temple of Peace. How wouldst thou look couldst thou see also the Capitol with its five-and-twenty temples? Note this Monte Savello: what is it, an it please you, but the ruins of the ancient theatre of Marcellus? And as for Testacio—one of the highest hills in modern Rome—it is but an ancient dust heap; the women of old Rome flung their broken pots and pans there, and lo—a mountain.”

Then, as if the sound of Latin itself were a savour he could not be without, he added, “Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem.”

Gerard listened, as he always did, with the respect due to learning; but when the friar, by a glide of words, began to hint that the moral greatness of those heathen must have matched their stone and their works, Gerard’s heart rose against it.

“Has then the world lost by Christ His coming?” said he; and he blushed for the boldness of it, for he knew he spoke to a benefactor.

“Saints forbid!” cried Fra Colonna. “‘Twere heresy to say so.”

Yet the friar had an art of conceding with the front of his face, and retreating with the rest of him by a back stair of subtlety; and while he fenced and circled, they came to a church where a knot of folk stood in the porch. Within, a demon was being exorcised.

Now Fra Colonna had a certain little moan—half pity, half disdain—that he would utter when men brought him things Zeno or Epicurus would not have swallowed, and called them fact. It was a sound that belonged as much to him as his thin hand and his quick, keen eye. He moaned now.

“Then come away.”

“Nay, father, prithee! prithee!” said Gerard, his youth eager for marvels as for spices he had never tasted. “I ne’er saw a divell cast out.”

The friar went in with him, but first gave such a shrug as would have shaken dust from a cassock.

Within, they found the demoniac forced down on his knees before the altar. A scarf was tied round his neck, and by it the priest held him as one holds a dog in a chain. There were not many present; fame had whispered that the last demon cast out in that place went no farther than into one of the company—as a cony ferreted from one burrow runs to the next.

When Gerard and the friar drew near, the priest seemed to count the spectators sufficient, and began as a man begins a task he has done often and with pride.

He faced the demoniac, breviary in hand, and set himself to learn the name of the guest within.

“Come out, Ashtaroth. Oho! it is not you then. Come out, Belial. Come out, Tatzi. Come out, Eza. No; he trembles not. Come out, Azymoth. Come out, Feriander. Come out, Foletho. Come out, Astyma. Come out, Nebul. Aha! what, have I found ye? ’Tis thou, thou reptile; at thine old tricks. Let us pray!”

And he prayed with a zeal that would have been better bestowed upon bread for the hungry, thought Gerard, had he been free to judge; yet the sound of the prayer filled the vaulting as steam fills a kitchen.

“Oh, Lord, we pray thee to drive the foul fiend Nebul out of this thy creature: out of his hair, and his eyes, out of his nose, out of his mouth, out of his ears, out of his gums, out of his teeth, out of his shoulders, out of his arms, legs, loins, stomach, bowels, thighs, knees, calves, feet, ankles, finger-nails, toe-nails, and soul. Amen.”

Then, rising, the priest turned with a genial quietness to the little company. “Gentles, we have here as obstinate a divell as you may see in a summer day.” And facing the patient again, he spoke with great rigour, now to the man, now to the fiend; and through that one mouth came two sorts of answer—now hatred of the holy names, now complaint of pains within.

But it was the priest himself who first muddled victim and culprit in the eyes of the onlookers. He fell upon the poor wretch as if beating the devil could bruise the sin out of him: cuffed him soundly, kicked him, spat in his face again and again. He took a candle, lit it, turned it down, and burned it till it scorched his own fingers, then dropped it hastily. He fetched the custodial and showed the Corpus Domini. He burned another candle, more wary. At last he spoke civilly to the demoniac in his human character, dismissed him, and received the compliments of the company as if he had carved a roast and served it well.

“Good father,” said Gerard, when they were out, “how you have their names by heart. Our northern priests have no such exquisite knowledge of the hellish squadrons.”

“Ay, young man,” said the priest, overhearing and pleased, “here we know all their names, and eke their ways, the reptiles. This Nebul is a bitter hard one to hunt out.”

And he told them, affably, of feats he had done; ending with yesterday’s triumph, when he had driven a great hulking fiend out of a woman by her mouth, leaving nails and pins and a tuft of his own hair behind, and crying in anguish, “’Tis not thou that conquers me. See that stone on the window sill. Know that the angel Gabriel coming down to earth once lighted on that stone: ’tis that has done my business.”

Fra Colonna moaned his small moan. “And you believed him?”

“Certes! who but an infidel has discredited a revelation so precise?”

“What, believe the father of lies? That is pushing credulity beyond the age.”

“Oh, a liar does not always lie.”

“Ay doth he whenever he tells an improbable story to begin, and shows you a holy relic; arms you against the Satanic host. Fiends (if any) be not so simple. Shouldst have answered him out of antiquity—Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes. Some blackguard chopped his wife’s head off on that stone, young man; you take my word for it.” And, having given that, he hurried Gerard away.

“Alack, father,” said Gerard, troubled, “I fear you abashed the good priest.”

“Ay, by Pollux,” said the friar, with a chuckle that rang like a dry nut cracked between teeth. “I blistered him with a single touch of Socratic interrogation. What modern can parry the weapons of antiquity?”

One afternoon, when Gerard had finished his day’s work, a fine lackey came and demanded his attendance at the Palace Cesarini. Gerard followed, and was ushered into a noble apartment, where a girl sat working tapestry. She rose, went out, and said she would let her mistress know.

A full hour Gerard cooled his heels there, listening to the palace-sounds—soft steps, a far-off laugh, a door murmuring shut; and beneath all, the faint city-noise like the sea heard through walls. At last he began to fret. These nobles thought nothing of a poor fellow’s time. Yet just as he was gathering himself to slip away, the door opened, and a superb beauty entered, followed by two maids.

It was the young princess of the house of Cesarini. She came in talking loudly and haughtily to her dependents; but at sight of Gerard her voice dropped into a tone as sweet as it was sudden.

“Are you the writer, messer?”

“I am, Signora.”

“’Tis well.”

She seated herself on a chair so high it was almost a throne. Gerard and her maids remained standing until she bade him.

“What is your name, good youth?”

“Gerard, signora.”

“Gerard?” she cried. “Body of Bacchus! is that the name of a human creature?”

“It is a Dutch name, signora. I was born at Tergou, in Holland.”

“A harsh name, girls, for so well-favoured a youth; what say you?”

The maids agreed with an alacrity that showed their training.

“What did I send for him for?” said the lady, with lofty languor, as if even her own wishes were beneath her memory. “Ah, I remember. Be seated, Ser Gerardo, and write me a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover; at least he says so.”

Gerard sat, drew forth paper and ink, and looked up, waiting as a scribe waits for dictation.

She, enthroned, looked down at him, eyes inquisitive as a cat’s.

“Well, Gerardo.”

“I am ready, your excellence.”

“Write, then.”

“I but await the words.”

“And who, think you, is to provide them?”

“Who but your grace, whose letter it is to be?”

“Gramercy! what, you writers, find you not the words? What avails your art without the words? I doubt you are an impostor, Gerardo.”

“Nay, Signora, I am none. I might make shift to put your highness’s speech into grammar, as well as writing. But I cannot interpret your silence. Therefore speak what is in your heart, and I will empaper it before your eyes.”

“But there is nothing in my heart. And sometimes I think I have got no heart.”

“What is in your mind, then?”

“But there is nothing in my mind; nor my head neither.”

“Then why write at all?”

“Why, indeed?” cried she, and her lip curled with amusement. “That is the first word of sense either you or I have spoken, Gerardo. Pestilence seize him! why writeth he not first? then I could say nay to this, and ay to that, withouten headache. Also is it a lady’s part to say the first word?”

“No, signora,” said Gerard. “The last.”

“It is well spoken, Gerardo. Ha! ha! Shalt have a gold piece for thy wit. Give me my purse!”

And she paid him on the nail, in the frank medieval fashion. The weight of the gold warmed his palm; money never yet chilled zeal. Having got it so cheaply, he felt bound to pull her out of her difficulty if the wit of man might do it.

“Signorina,” said he, “these things are only hard because folk attempt too much, are artificial and labour phrases. Do but figure to yourself the signor you love—”

“I love him not.”

“Well, then, the signor you love not—seated at this table, and dict to me just what you would say to him.”

“Well, if he sat there, I should say, ‘Go away.’”

Gerard, who had lifted his pen ready to run, laid it down with a groan.

“And when he was gone,” put in Floretta, one of the maids, quick with mischief, “your highness would say, ‘Come back.’”

“Like enough, wench.” The princess frowned, and the maids fell silent in a breath. She turned her head away, and fell into deep thought, her noble chin resting on her white hand, half clenched. “Now silence, all, and let me think. He pestered me to write, and I promised; so mine honour is engaged. What lie shall I tell the Gerardo to tell the fool?”

She sat so still, so lovely, so statuesque, that for a moment the room seemed to hold its breath. Even the faint palace-sounds seemed to retreat, as if they too waited upon her thought. Gerard forgot letter and Orsini and all else. Here was art offered to him as a dish set down steaming. He took his paper and, with hungry delight, began to draw that exquisite profile—pure line, pure light, the curve of cheek and chin that made her beauty imperial even in repose.

He had nearly finished when the fair statue turned brusquely and caught him.

“Nay, Signora,” said he, a little peevishly, like a cook whose dish is jostled at the last moment, “for Heaven’s sake change not your posture—’twas perfect. See, you are nearly finished.”

Instantly all eyes were on the work, and all tongues active.

“How like! and done in a minute: nay, methinks her highness’s chin is not quite so.”

“Oh, a touch will make that right.”

“What a pity ’tis not coloured. I’m all for colours. Hang black and white! And her highness hath such a lovely skin. Take away her skin, and half her beauty is lost.”

“Peace,” said the princess, but her eyes had kindled. “Can you colour, Ser Gerardo?”

“Ay, signorina. I am a poor hand at oils; there shines my friend Pietro; but in this small way I can tint you to the life, if you have time to waste on such vanity.”

“Call you this vanity? And for time, it hangs on me like lead. Send for your colours now—quick, this moment—for love of all the saints.”

“Nay, signorina, I must prepare them. I could come at the same time.”

“So be it. And you, Floretta, see that he be admitted at all hours. Alack! Leave my head! leave my head!”

“Forgive me, Signora,” said Gerard. “I thought to prepare it at home to receive the colours. But I will leave it. And now let us despatch the letter.”

“What letter?” she asked, as if the very word were tasteless.

“To the Signor Orsini.”

“And shall I waste my time on such vanity as writing letters—and to that empty creature, to whom I am as indifferent as the moon? Nay, not indifferent, for I have just discovered my real sentiments. I hate him and despise him. Girls, I here forbid you once for all to mention that signor’s name to me again; else I’ll whip you till the blood comes. You know how I can lay on when I’m roused.”

“We do. We do,” murmured the maids, cowed and accustomed.

“Then provoke me not to it;” and her eye flashed daggers; then, in the same heartbeat, she turned to Gerard all honey. “Addio, il Gerardo.”

And Gerard bowed himself out of that velvet tiger’s den.

He came next day and coloured her; and the next he was set to make a portrait of her on a large scale; and then a full-length figure. He had to set apart two hours each afternoon for drawing and painting this princess, whose beauty and vanity were alike prodigious, and whose admirers were numerous enough to make her likeness a kind of coin. The thriving Gerard found there a new and fruitful source of income—income that, like a well-kept store of grain, made the thought of Margaret feel nearer and nearer.

It was Holy Thursday. No work that day. Fra Colonna and Gerard sat in a window and saw the religious processions pass beneath, like rivers of men flowing between stone banks. Their number, their ardour, the press of bodies and voices—chants rolling like waves, bells striking, the beat of hoofs and the shiver of armour—thrilled Gerard with devotion. It was as if the whole city, lately bent on earthly joys, had turned in one hour to a great act of piety; and the sound of it all—the city’s breath made audible—entered him, and moved him.

Presently the Pope came pacing majestically at the head of his cardinals: red hat, white cloak, a capuchin of red velvet, riding a lovely white Neapolitan barb, caparisoned with red velvet fringed and tasselled with gold. Behind him rode a hundred horsemen armed cap-a-pie, lances erected, butt-ends resting on their thighs. The cardinals went uncovered, all but one—de Medicis—who rode close and conversed with the Pope as with an equal. At every fifteen steps the Pope halted a single moment, blessed the people, and went on.

Gerard’s heart beat fast, as it does when one hears a great organ fill a church; and with it came, half-unbidden, his own homelier memories—bread broken at home, voices in his native tongue—things no Latin blessing could translate, and which yet rose in him at the sound of so many voices made one.

The two came down, and threading by-streets reached the portico of one of the seven churches. It was hung with black. Soon the Pope and cardinals, who had entered by another door, issued forth and stood with torches on the steps, separated by barriers from the people. Then a canon read a Latin Bull, excommunicating several by name, especially such princes as kept the Church out of any of her temporal possessions.

At the awful words, Gerard trembled, and the crowd with him, as when a sudden hush falls over a market and even the hawkers swallow their cries. But two cardinals laughed unreservedly the whole time, and their laughter, light and careless in the midst of thunder, jarred upon the ear like tin struck in a chapel.

When it was ended, the black cloth was removed, and a gay panoply was revealed; the Pope blessed the people, and ended by throwing his torch among them. Two cardinals did likewise. Instantly there was a scramble. The torches were fought for and torn in pieces with a devotion that paid for splinters with black eyes, bloody noses, and burnt fingers. In that hurtling, his holiness and suite withdrew in peace.

Then a cry rose; the crowd rushed to a square where a large open stage had been set. Several priests were upon it praying. They rose, and with great ceremony donned red gloves. One knelt, and with signs of the lowest reverence drew forth from a shrine a square frame like that of a mirror, and within it, as it were, the impression of a face.

It was the Verum icon—the true impression of our Saviour’s face, taken at the very moment of His most mortal agony for men. Received without a grain of doubt, it moved every Christian heart as the smell of fresh bread moves the hungry: instantly, without argument, straight to the centre.

The people threw themselves on their faces when the priest raised it on high; cries of pity rose everywhere, and tears stood in almost every eye. After a time they rose; then the priest went round the platform, showing it for a single moment to the nearest; and at each glimpse loud cries of devotion burst forth, wave upon wave.

Soon after, they fell in with a procession of Flagellants, flogging their bare shoulders till blood ran streaming down; yet there was no pain in their faces, and many of them laughed and jested as they lashed. The bystanders, out of pity, offered them wine. They took it—few drank it; most used it to soften and cleanse the tails of the cat, hard with clotted blood, that the next stroke might bite the better. Many were boys. A young woman took pity on one fair urchin.

“Alas! dear child,” said she, “why wound thy white skin so?”

“Basta,” said he, laughing, “’tis for your sins I do it, not for mine.”

“Hear you that?” said the friar. “Show me the whip that can whip the vanity out of man’s heart! The young monkey; how knoweth he that stranger is a sinner more than he?”

“Father,” said Gerard, sickened by the sound of laughter mingled with the wet smack of scourges, “surely this is not to our Lord’s mind. He was so pitiful.”

“Our Lord?” said Fra Colonna, crossing himself. “What has He to do with this? This was a custom in Rome six hundred years before He was born. The boys used to go through the streets at the Lupercalia flogging themselves; and the married women used to shove in and try and get a blow from the monkeys’ scourges; for these blows conferred fruitfulness in those days. A foolish trick, this flagellation; but interesting to the bystander; reminds him of the grand old heathen. We are so prone to forget all we owe them.”

So even here, amid blood and prayer, the friar’s mind returned to older names, older rites—memory laid like one language over another, the later words drowning the first, yet never quite silencing it.

Next they got into one of the seven churches and saw the Pope give the mass. The ceremony was imposing; incense rose like visible breath, the chant flowed and echoed, candles trembled in their own heat—yet again it was spoiled by the inconsistent conduct of the cardinals and prelates, who sat about the altar with hats on, chattering all through the mass like a flock of geese. Their talk, that small domestic noise of pride and ease, crept into the sacred soundscape and soured it, as smoke from a bad fire will taint a whole pot.

The eucharist in both kinds was tasted by an official before the Pope would venture on it; and this surprised Gerard beyond measure.

“Who is that base man? and what doth he there?”

“Oh, that is the Preguste,” said the friar, “and he tastes the eucharist by way of precaution. This is the country for poison; and none fall oftener by it than the poor Popes.”

“Alas! so I have heard; but after the miraculous change of the bread and wine to Christ His body and blood, poison cannot remain; gone is the bread with all its properties and accidents; gone is the wine.”

“So says Faith; but experience tells another tale. Scores have died in Italy poisoned in the host.”

“And I tell you, father,” cried Gerard, hot with certainty, “that were both bread and wine charged with direst poison before his holiness had consecrated them, yet after consecration I would take them both withouten fear.”

“So would I,” said Fra Colonna, “but for the fine arts.”

“What mean you?”

“Marry, that I would be as ready to leave the world as thou, were it not for those arts which beautify existence here below, and make it dear to men of sense and education. No; so long as the Nine Muses strew my path with roses of learning and art, me may Apollo inspire with wisdom and caution, that knowing the wiles of my countrymen, I may eat poison neither at God’s altar nor at a friend’s table; since, wherever I eat it or drink it, it will assuredly cut short my mortal thread; and I am writing a book—heart and soul in it—The Dream of Polifilo, the man of many arts. So name not poison to me till that is finished and copied.”

And now the great bells of St. John Lateran rang with a clash at short intervals, as if the city itself were struck like a great brazen vessel. The people hurried thither to see the heads of St. Peter and St. Paul.

Gerard and the friar got a good place within. A great curtain hung; long, breathless expectation held the multitude in a silence broken only by the restless rustle of bodies and the whisper of prayers. Then the curtain was drawn by jerks, and at a height of about thirty feet were two human heads with bearded faces that seemed alive. They were shown no longer than the time to say an Ave Maria; then the curtain fell again. But they were shown thus three times.

St. Peter’s complexion was pale, his face oval, his beard grey and forked; his head crowned with a papal mitre. St. Paul was dark-skinned, with a thick square beard; his head and face were more square and massive, full of resolution.

Gerard was awe-struck, as a child is when first he tastes wine and feels it go straight to the blood. The friar approved—after his fashion.

“This exhibition of the imagines,” said he, “or waxen effigies of heroes and demigods, is a venerable custom, and inciteth the vulgar to virtue by great and invisible examples.”

“Waxen images?” cried Gerard, startled, as if told that a loaf was only painted. “What, are they not the apostles themselves, embalmed, or the like?”

The friar moaned.

“They did not exist in the year 800. The great old Roman families always produced at their funerals a series of these imagines, thereby tying past and present history together, and showing the populace the features of far-famed worthies. I can conceive nothing more thrilling or instructive. But then the effigies were portraits made during life or at the hour of death. These of St. Paul and St. Peter are moulded out of pure fancy.”

“Ah! say not so, father,” pleaded Gerard, who felt in himself the ache of the untranslatable—the way one man’s certainty cannot be poured into another man’s cup.

“But the worst is,” continued the friar, “this humour of showing them up on a shelf, and half in the dark, and by snatches, and with the poor mountebank trick of a drawn curtain.

Quodcunque ostendis mihi sic incredulus odi.

Enough; the men of this day are not the men of old. Let us have done with these new-fangled mummeries, and go among the Pope’s books; there we shall find the wisdom we shall vainly hunt in the streets of modern Rome.”

And the idea, once planted, took root in him at once. He plunged and tore through the crowd, looking neither right nor left, till he had escaped the glories of Holy Week that had brought fifty thousand strangers to Rome, and had got quiet among the dead in the Vatican library—where the loudest sound was a page turned, and the air smelled not of sweat and torches but of vellum, dust, and the faint sharpness of ink.

Presently, going into Gerard’s room, he found a hot dispute afoot between him and Jacques Bonaventura. That spark had come in all steel from head to toe; he doffed his helmet, puffed, and railed with scorn upon a ridiculous ceremony at which he and his soldiers had been compelled to attend the Pope: to wit, the blessing of the beasts of burden.

Gerard said it was not ridiculous; nothing a Pope did could be ridiculous.

The argument grew warm. Fra Colonna stood grimly neuter, waiting like the stork in the fable that ate frog and mouse at the close of their combat—to grind them both between the jaws of antiquity. When lo, the curtain was gently drawn, and there stood a venerable old man in a purple skull-cap, with a beard like white floss silk, looking at them with a kind though feeble smile.

“Happy youth,” said he, “that can heat itself over such matters.”

They all fell on their knees. It was the Pope.

“Nay, rise, my children,” said he, almost peevishly, as one who had stepped aside for quiet and found ceremony thrust upon him. “I came not into this corner to be in state. How goes Plutarch?”

Gerard brought his work, and kneeling on one knee presented it. His holiness seated himself; the others stood.

He inspected the writing with interest. “’Tis excellently writ,” said he.

Gerard’s heart beat with delight; praise from that mouth seemed to make even the ink on his page more black and sure.

“Ah, this Plutarch,” said the Pope to Francesco, “he had a wondrous art. How each character standeth out alive on his page: how full of nature each, yet how unlike his fellow!”

Jacques Bonaventura, who cared for nothing that did not march or strike, broke in bluntly. “Give me the Signor Boccaccio.”

“An excellent narrator, capitano,” said the Pope, “and writeth exquisite Italian. But in spirit a thought too monotonous. Monks and nuns were never all unchaste: one or two such stories were right pleasant and diverting; but five score paint his time falsely, and sadden the heart of such as love mankind. Moreover, he hath no skill at characters. Now this Greek is supreme in that great art: he carveth them with pen; and turning his page, see into how real and great a world we enter—of war, and policy, and business, and love in its own place: for with him, as in the great world, men are not all running after a wench. With this great open field compare me not the narrow garden of Boccaccio, and his little mill-round of dishonest pleasures.”

“Your holiness,” said Jacques, half-curious, half-mocking, “they say, hath not disdained to write a novel.”

“My holiness hath done more foolish things than one,” replied the Pope, and the tiredness in his voice made him sound suddenly older than his beard, “whereof it repents too late. When I wrote novels I little thought to be head of the Church.”

“I search in vain for a copy of it,” said Jacques, “to add to my poor library.”
