The doctor’s pretty young housemaid stood holding the street door open for me, bright morning sunlight flooding straight down the hall to catch Ezra Jennings full in the face the second I turned back to glance at him.

Betteredge had been entirely correct in his assessment: to any casual onlooker, the man’s appearance worked violently against him. His swarthy gipsy complexion, sunken fleshless cheeks, sharp gaunt facial bones, far-off dreamy eyes, and that uncanny streaked two-toned hair blended into a figure that defied age entirely—no one could say if he was twenty or fifty, and every odd quirk of him stacked up to put strangers instinctively on guard. But for all that, I could not shake a strange, unaccountable pull of sympathy that rooted me to the spot. My better sense, honed by years of worldly caution, told me to nod politely, confirm Mr. Candy looked shockingly unwell, and proceed on my way to my aunt Mrs. Ablewhite’s. But my fascination for this strange, lonely man held me fast, and I knew he had been waiting precisely for this chance to speak to me in private, away from the prying eyes of the household.

“You’re heading out too, Mr. Jennings?” I said, noticing he already had his hat in his hand. “I’m bound for my aunt’s place, not far from here.”
He replied that he had a patient to visit, and his route ran the exact same way as mine.

We stepped out together. The same pretty housemaid who had greeted me with bright grins and easy amiability when I bade her good morning pursed her lips tight the second Jennings tried to pass on a quiet, unassuming message about what time he would be back, and deliberately stared at the wall rather than meet his eye. The man was no favourite even under his own employer’s roof. Out on the steps, I remembered Betteredge had said no one anywhere in Frizinghall cared for him. What a miserable, friendless life he must lead, I thought.

Jennings had already broached Mr. Candy’s illness earlier, and now he held his tongue, clearly signalling that it was my turn to bring the subject up. I had my own urgent reasons to dig into the details of the doctor’s condition, so I spoke first.
“From how altered he looks today, his illness must have been far more serious than anyone let on?”
“It is nothing short of a miracle he survived,” Jennings replied.
“Is his memory never any clearer than it was when I saw him just now? He kept trying to speak to me about something before he fell sick—”
“Something that happened the night before he took ill?” Jennings cut in, reading my hesitation perfectly.
“Exactly.”
“His memory of that period is all but destroyed,” he said, his tone softening. “It would be kinder for him if he had lost all recollection entirely, instead of clinging to that half-echo of plans he made, things he swore he had to say or do. He cannot for the life of him name what those things were, and he’s tortured by the gap, constantly fighting to hide how broken his memory has become. Perhaps we would all be happier, if we could wipe the worst parts of our past clean away entirely.” He added that last line with a thin, sad smile.
“Surely there are memories no man would ever want to lose entirely?” I pushed back.
“That may be true for most people, Mr. Blake. It is not true for all.” His dark eyes locked on mine. “Do I gather that the lost memory Mr. Candy was fumbling to recover when you visited him this morning matters a great deal to you?”

He had landed right on the exact question I had been hoping to bring up, and every word he had spoken so far convinced me he was a man of fine, quiet breeding, the kind of unforced, unassuming self-possession no amount of mimicry can fake. I had no qualms about speaking frankly.
“I have a very great stake in recovering that lost memory. Can you suggest any way to jog his recollection?”
A sharp, bright spark of interest lit up his otherwise dreamy brown eyes.
“I’ve tried dozens of times already. His own memory is entirely beyond saving,” he said.
That disappointed me, and I did not hide it. “I was hoping for a far more hopeful answer than that.”
“Wait,” he smiled. “It may not be the final answer. We do not need Mr. Candy to remember at all. We can reconstruct that lost event without ever asking him to recall a single thing.”
I leaned forward, eager. “How?”
“I will spare you none of the messy professional details of his illness, if you are willing to hear them out.”
I assured him I was all attention. By that point we had left the last houses of the small town behind us. Jennings paused to pluck a small bunch of wild flowers from the hedgerow, holding them up gently. “They are so beautiful. Almost no one in England ever stops to notice them the way they deserve.”
“You did not grow up in England, then?” I asked.
“No. I was born in one of the colonies, raised there for half my childhood. My father was English, but my mother—” he cut himself off, shaking his head. “My apologies, we are straying from the subject. Those little flowers hold old, painful memories for me. Let us get back to Mr. Candy.” The small slip of his life story, paired with that bleak view of forgetting the past to be happy, confirmed what his face had already told me: he had suffered more than most men ever do, and there was foreign, non-English blood in his veins.

“You have heard, I assume, how he fell ill in the first place?” Jennings resumed. “The night of Lady Verinder’s dinner party, it poured without stopping. He drove home in his gig soaked to the skin, and found an urgent message waiting for him the second he stepped in the door. He foolishly drove straight out to see that patient without ever stopping to change his wet clothes. I was tied up with a serious case miles outside Frizinghall that same night, and when I got back at dawn, his groom was waiting for me in a total panic, to drag me up to his sickroom. The fever had already taken hold. It was a strange, undefined fever, no distinct diagnosis, and the two senior local doctors I called in immediately disagreed violently with me on treatment. They said his rapid pulse proved he needed a lowering diet of gruel, lemonade, barley water. I agreed his pulse raced—but I also saw it was shockingly weak, a sign his whole system was crashing, that he needed heavy doses of stimulants: champagne, brandy, ammonia, quinine. They threatened to walk out if I ignored their advice. I sent a man down to the cellar for a bottle of champagne, poured half a glass down Mr. Candy’s throat myself, and the two men left in a huff. I owed that man my life—he was the only person in the world who had ever been kind to me. I sat by his bedside for twenty hours out of every day, fighting Death through those terrible small hours between two and five a.m., when the life force of even the healthiest man is at its lowest. When wine stopped working, I used brandy. When that faded, I doubled the dose. After what felt like an eternity of waiting, his pulse slowed just a little, then steadied, then grew strong. I knew I had saved him, and I broke down crying, right there. The medical books are right: some men are born with nervous, sensitive constitutions. I am one of them.”

He brushed past his own display of emotion like it was a triviality, and went on. “I have spent years writing an unpublished medical book, about delirium and the workings of the nervous system, something no one else will ever read. It got me through all those long, silent hours at Mr. Candy’s bedside. I have long suspected that even when a delirious man cannot speak a single coherent sentence, his mind may still be thinking in perfectly logical, connected lines. I know shorthand. I wrote down every single disjointed word that fell from Mr. Candy’s lips while he was raving. Later, I filled in the gaps between those broken fragments like assembling a jigsaw puzzle, until I had stitched all his rambling lines together into a single coherent, intelligible narrative. Your name comes up in one of those long, delirium sessions. For an entire night, his mind was fixed entirely on something that happened between you and him, the night of Lady Verinder’s dinner party—something he had fully intended to tell you, before his sickness hit and wiped the memory clean.”

“Then it is exactly the lost memory he was trying to reach!” I burst out. “Let us turn back right now and look at those notes.”
“Absolutely impossible,” Jennings said, steady.
“Why?”
“Would you hand over the private, unguarded ramblings of your most helpless sick friend to a stranger, before you were certain there was a very urgent, justified reason to do so?”
I had no good argument against that, and fumbled to offer only vague, half-hearted excuses about a personal matter that did not involve the Moonstone.
Jennings shook his head. “I can promise you, not one single word Mr. Candy raved about that night has anything to do with the Diamond. No mention of the theft, no mention of the Moonstone at all.”

We reached the fork in the road just then. One path led to Mr. Ablewhite’s house. The other ran out towards a remote moorland village two or three miles distant. Jennings stopped at the second path.
“This is my way. I truly am sorry I cannot be of more help to you.” He bowed, turned, and started walking towards the moor.

I stood there for a full minute, watching him get further and further away, the only possible clue that could clear my name vanishing into the distance behind him. What was I doing, letting my stupid pride keep me from stopping him? I called his name before I could overthink it.
He turned right around and walked back to me.
“I was not fair to you just now,” I said. “My interest in that lost memory has nothing to do with the Moonstone. I am the prime suspect in the theft of the Diamond, and it is the most humiliating, painful thing I have ever had to say out loud. I cannot keep lying to you. If you help me, I will swear to keep whatever you tell me entirely secret.”

A terrible, raw emotion seized Jennings the second the words left my mouth. His gipsy-dark face bleached to a livid, greyish pallor, his soft brown eyes blazed wild and glittering, and his voice dropped low, sharp, and rock steady, a note I had never heard from him before.
“Before you place any trust in me at all, there is one small thing you need to know, the short story of why I even have a roof over my head under Mr. Candy’s roof. I never tell anyone my full story. It will die with me. But you have the right to know exactly who you are sharing your secret with. Shall we walk a little?”
We had gone a few hundred yards further along the road before he stopped at a gap in the rough dry-stone wall that separated the open moor from the highway.
“Would you mind stopping to rest for a moment, Mr. Blake?” he asked. “I am not half as steady as other men. Some things still shake me right down to my bones.”
