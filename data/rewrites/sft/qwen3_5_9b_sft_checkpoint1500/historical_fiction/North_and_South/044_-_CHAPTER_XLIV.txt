For Margaret, the profound silence of the Harley Street residence, maintained throughout Edith's convalescence from childbirth, offered a necessary sanctuary. It afforded her the rare luxury of time to assimilate the seismic shift in her fortunes over the past two months. She found herself an inhabitant of a palace, where the mere awareness of any burden or anxiety seemed to have evaporated entirely. The machinery of domestic life ran with a frictionless, oil-lubricated smoothness. Mrs. Shaw and Edith showered Margaret with affection, insisting it was her home, yet she harbored a secret, gnawing guilt. The vicarage at Helstone—the humble cottage at Milton with her worried father and frail mother, surrounded by the modest chores of relative poverty—remained the truest definition of home in her spirit. Edith, eager to regain health, planned to flood Margaret's chamber with the soft comforts and decorative curiosities that adorned her own. Meanwhile, Mrs. Shaw and her maids labored tirelessly to restore Margaret's wardrobe to a state of elegant opulence. Captain Lennox, gentle and gentlemanly, spent hours playing with his young son, lingering in Edith's dressing room, or drifting away to his club whenever he was not dining out.

Just as Margaret had begun to crave quietude and reprieve, Edith descended the stairs, resuming her role in the household. Margaret reverted to her old instincts, observing and serving her cousin, gladly assuming the trivialities of duty—answering correspondence, managing schedules, and offering tender care—which led her to suspect she was feigning illness herself. Yet the rest of the family was immersed in the frenetic business of the London Season, leaving Margaret frequently solitary. In these moments, her thoughts drifted back to Milton, struck by a jarring contrast between that earnest, striving life and her current existence. She became increasingly bored by an uneventful ease that demanded no struggle or effort. A creeping fear took hold: she dreaded becoming sleepily deadened to everything outside the gilded cage of luxury surrounding her. Though London teemed with laborers and strugglers, she never saw them; the servants existed in a subterranean realm, invisible save when summoned by a whim of their masters. A strange, unsatisfied vacuum yawned within Margaret. Once, she had tentatively hinted at this malaise to Edith. Wearied from a night of dancing, Edith had languidly stroked Margaret's cheek as the younger woman sat on a footstool beside her resting cousin.

"Poor child!" Edith had sighed. "It is somewhat melancholy to be left alone night after night when the whole world is so gay. But soon we shall host dinner parties—as soon as Henry returns from circuit—and there will be a delightful variety for you. No wonder you are in such a mood, my darling!"

Margaret did not believe dinner parties could cure her ennui. Yet Edith took pride in her social gatherings, boasting they were "so different" from the staid, dowager-led meals of her mother's regime. Even Mrs. Shaw seemed to derive a peculiar pleasure from these modern, informal entertainments suited to Captain and Mrs. Lennox's tastes, mirroring her enjoyment of the more formal, ponderous events she herself used to organize. Captain Lennox remained consistently kind and brotherly toward Margaret, though a flicker of irritation sparked within her whenever he turned his anxious attention to Edith's attire, scrutinizing her beauty with the intent to impress the world. Then the dormant Vashti within Margaret rose, and she struggled to suppress her feelings.

Her days followed a predictable rhythm: a couple of quiet hours before a late breakfast, an unpunctual meal eaten lazily by weary, half-asleep guests, at which she was nonetheless expected to preside due to the subsequent discussions of plans that followed—discussions she could influence with sympathy but rarely with advice. An endless stream of notes awaited her pen, accompanied by Edith's effusive praise of her *eloquence du billet*. There were playful interludes with Sholto returning from his walks, the care of the children during the servants' dinner, drives, calls, and engagements for her aunt and cousins. These activities, while freeing, left her drained, succumbing to depression and delicate health amidst the inactivity of the day.

She looked forward with a silent, yearning interest to Dixon's return from Milton, where the old servant had been busy winding up the Hale family's affairs. This complete cessation of news regarding her former community felt like a sudden famine to her heart. True, Dixon's business-like letters occasionally quoted opinions from Mr. Thornton regarding furniture or landlords, but references to Milton itself were sparse. Sitting one evening alone in the Lennox drawing-room, holding Dixon's letters but unable to read them, Margaret pondered the bustling life she had left behind, wondering if it continued unchanged as if she and her father had never existed. She questioned whether anyone in that throng truly missed her—or Higgins, whom she did not consider at that moment. Suddenly, the door opened and Mr. Bell was announced. Margaret hastily tucked the letters into her work-basket, rising with a blush that suggested she had committed some transgression.

"Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought of seeing you!"

"But you give me a welcome, I hope, as well as that very pretty start of surprise."

"Have you dined? How did you come? Let me order you some dinner."

"If you're going to have any. Otherwise, you know, there is no one who cares less for eating than I do. But where are the others? Gone out to dinner? Left you alone?"

"Oh yes! and it is such a rest. I was just thinking—But will you run the risk of dinner? I don't know if there is anything in the house."

"Why, to tell you the truth, I dined at my club. Only they don't cook as well as they did, so I thought, if you were going to dine, I might try and make out my dinner. But never mind, never mind! There aren't ten cooks in England to be trusted at impromptu dinners. If their skill and their fires will stand it, their tempers won't. You shall make me some tea, Margaret. And now, what were you thinking of? You were going to tell me. Whose letters were those, god-daughter, that you hid away so speedily?"

"Only Dixon's," replied Margaret, turning crimson.

"Whew! Is that all? Who do you think came up in the train with me?"

"I don't know," said Margaret, resolving against guessing.

"Your what d'ye call him? What's the right name for a cousin-in-law's brother?"

"Mr. Henry Lennox?" asked Margaret.

"Yes," replied Mr. Bell. "You knew him formerly, didn't you? What sort of a person is he, Margaret?"

"I liked him long ago," said Margaret, glancing down briefly before lifting her gaze and speaking naturally. "You know we have been corresponding about Frederick since; but I have not seen him for nearly three years, and he may be changed. What did you think of him?"

"I don't know. He was so busy trying to find out who I was, in the first instance, and what I was in the second, that he never let out what he was; unless indeed that veiled curiosity of his as to what manner of man he had to talk to was not a good piece, and a fair indication of his character. Do you call him good-looking, Margaret?"

"No! certainly not. Do you?"

"Not I. But I thought perhaps you might. Is he a great deal here?"

"I fancy he is when he is in town. He has been on circuit now since I came. But—Mr. Bell—have you come from Oxford or from Milton?"

"From Milton. Don't you see I'm smoke-dried?"

"Certainly. But I thought that it might be the effect of the antiquities of Oxford."

"Come now, be a sensible woman! In Oxford, I could have managed all the landlords in the place, and had my own way, with half the trouble your Milton landlord has given me, and defeated me after all. He won't take the house off our hands till next June twelve-month. Luckily, Mr. Thornton found a tenant for it. Why don't you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He has proved himself a very active friend of yours, I can tell you. Taken more than half the trouble off my hands."

"And how is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?" asked Margaret hurriedly, lowering her voice despite her effort to project.

"I suppose they're well. I've been staying at their house till I was driven out of it by the perpetual clack about that Thornton girl's marriage. It was too much for Thornton himself, though she was his sister. He used to go and sit in his own room perpetually. He's getting past the age for caring for such things, either as principal or accessory. I was surprised to find the old lady falling into the current, and carried away by her daughter's enthusiasm for orange-blossoms and lace. I thought Mrs. Thornton had been made of sterner stuff."

"She would put on any assumption of feeling to veil her daughter's weakness," said Margaret in a low tone.
