One crisp morning during the height of London's social season, Major Arthur Pendennis arrived at his favorite club in Pall Mall, just as he did every day. To the members of this exclusive establishment, he was less a guest and more the undisputed manager. As one of England's premier connoisseurs of wine and a man whose inquiring spirit dominated every conversation, he sat atop the committee, treated by stewards and waiters with the deference usually reserved for Dukes or Field Marshals.

By ten fifteen, the Major made his entrance. He was a study in impeccable military fashion *en retraite*. His boots were the blackest in London, his cravat checked and untouched by wrinkles until dinner, and his buff waistcoat boasted buttons stamped with the royal crown. His linen was so pristine that Beau Brummel himself reportedly paused to ask the name of his laundrywoman (though Brummel, forced into exile by bad luck, never got the chance to hire her). From his perfect white gloves and "Wellington-pattern" nose to the gold buttons gifted by the Duke of York and the rings bearing the Pendennis crest, every detail screamed aristocratic authority. Even his cane was flawless. While a casual glance might suggest he was barely thirty, a closer look revealed the artificial sheen of his brown hair and the faint crow's feet around his handsome, mottled eyes.

He claimed his usual corner table by the fire, a spot so fiercely guarded that no one dared challenge him. Formerly, a few reckless louts had tried to evict him, but the sheer quiet dignity with which the Major settled in, surveying the room through his gold-rimmed spectacles, ensured his reign was unbroken. This table became his throne. Letters awaited him there, piled high with seals and franks that bewildered the younger set. Whether asking about proper etiquette, marital status, or the exact age of a specific Duke, everyone turned to Pendennis for the answer. Ladies-in-waiting drove up specifically to deliver notes, and young men flocked to walk beside him in the Park, simply because touching his hat to him felt like shaking hands with royalty.

As the waiters brought his toast and the morning papers, Pendennis began his daily ritual: sorting his correspondence. He carried his glasses with such flair that they seemed mere accessories rather than tools of vision. He methodically arranged the notes—solemn dinner cards promising heavy conversation, intimate missives from women, and official invites. There was a summons from the Marquis of Steyne to a party at the Star and Garter, requiring fluent French (which the Major spoke perfectly), and an invitation from the Bishop of Ealing and Mrs. Trail. He read them all with graceful satisfaction, particularly enjoying the spectacle of Glowry, the Scottish surgeon sitting opposite, glaring with envy at the mountain of invitations that never included the poor doctor.

With a swift calculation in his pocket-book, the Major decided his schedule. He tossed aside a dinner with Cutler, the East India Director, to prioritize Lord Steyne's French-speaking soirée. He accepted the Bishop's invite, reasoning that despite the slow service, dining with clergy was essential. Having cleared his calendar based on whim and interest, he turned to the newspapers, checking the birth and death columns and ensuring his name appeared on the guest lists of upcoming fêtes. Between glances at the gazette, he chatted cheerfully with the room's occupants.

But amidst the whirlwind of high society, one letter stood apart. It lay solitary, marked with a rustic country postmark and a simple seal, ignored while the grandees of London vied for attention. Written in a delicate female hand and stamped "Immediate" with a dash of anxious urgency, it had been waiting patiently in the metaphorical ante-chamber. Finally, it was its turn.

Breaking the seal, which bore the engraving "Fairoaks," the Major opened the envelope first. Inside, the letter from his sister, Helen, read:

*I beg and implore you to come to me immediately. I am in the very greatest grief and perplexity. My dearest boy, who has been everything a fond mother could wish, is grieving me dreadfully. He has formed—an infatuation—for an actress currently performing here. She is at least twelve years older than Arthur, who will not be eighteen until next February. The wretched boy insists upon marrying her.*

*Please, my dear friend, come instantly upon receiving this. As his guardian, I entreat and command you to dissuade this most deplorable resolution."*

"Fairoaks, Tuesday," the Major muttered, his face draining of color. "A damned pretty business at Fairoaks on Tuesday. Now let us see what the boy has to say."

He tore open the second letter, written in a frantic, floundering hand and sealed with a massive Pendennis signet, splattered with extra wax to indicate the writer's trembling agitation.

*In informing you of my engagement with Miss Costigan, daughter of J. Chesterfield Costigan, Esq., of Costiganstown—but better known professionally as Miss Fotheringay of the Theatres Royal Drury Lane and Crow Street, and the Norwich and Welsh Circuit—I know I offer news unlikely to please current societal prejudices. My dearest mother, whom I wish to spare no unnecessary pain, is deeply moved and grieved by this intelligence. I beseech you, my dear Sir, to come down, reason with her, and console her.*

*Though driven by poverty to maintain an honorable livelihood through her splendid talents, Miss Costigan's family is as ancient and noble as ours. Our ancestor, Ralph Pendennis, landed with Richard II in Ireland; my Emily's forefathers were kings of that land. Mr. Costigan, a military man like yourself, confirms this lineage.*

*Logic fails against my mother's prejudices. She refuses to welcome into her arms one who wishes to be her most affectionate daughter forever. Though Miss Costigan is several years older, this does not hinder my affection nor its duration. A love like mine is contracted once and for all. Never having known love until I saw her, I feel I shall die without experiencing another passion. It is the fate of my life.*

*It was Miss C.'s own delicacy suggesting the age gap might be a barrier, but having loved once, I would despise myself if I hesitated to follow my passion. I intend to give all where I feel all. I press for a speedy marriage—why delay when doubt is unworthy?*

*I entreat you, my dear Uncle, to reconcile my mother to our union. Addressing you as a man of the world (*qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes*), I trust you will not share the weak scruples that agitate a lady who has scarcely left her village. Come down immediately. Apart from fortune, I am confident you will admire and approve of my Emily.*

When the Major finished reading, his countenance twisted into an expression of pure rage and horror. Glowry, the surgeon, instinctively reached for his lancet in his card case, fearing his friend was having a stroke. The news was staggering: the head of the Pendennis house planning to marry an actress ten years his senior; a headstrong youth plunging blindly into matrimony.

"The mother has spoiled the young rascal!" the Major groaned internally. "Cursed sentimentality and romantic rubbish. My nephew marrying a tragedy queen! Good heavens, people will laugh until I dare not show my head!"

He realized with a sinking pang that he must cancel Lord Steyne's dinner at Richmond. Instead of enjoying select English society, he would spend the night in a foul-smelling mail coach. Worse still, he would have to cancel all other engagements indefinitely. Who knew how long this domestic crisis would drag on?

Quitting his breakfast table, the Major retreated to the adjacent writing room. With a rueful sigh, he penned formal refusals to the Marquis, the Earl, the Bishop, and every other host who had invited him. Then, he instructed his servant to book seats on the evening mail coach, naturally charging the exorbitant cost to the account of the widow and the young scapegrace he was sworn to protect.
