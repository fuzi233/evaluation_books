The post-chaise arrived at Aswarby Hall on a chill September evening in 1811, its wheels crunching over the gravel as it came to a halt before the imposing red-brick façade. Inside, a lone passenger—a small, wide-eyed boy—pressed his face against the glass, taking in the grand yet somber visage of his new home.

The house loomed tall and square, its many narrow windows glinting like dull embers in the fading light. A stone-pillared porch, added in a later era, lent it an air of austere dignity. To either side stretched curious glazed galleries, connecting the main block to the stables, each crowned with a cupola where a gilded weathervane caught the last gleam of sunset. Beyond the house lay a flat parkland, studded with ancient oaks and edged with dark firs that stood sentinel against the twilight sky.

The boy, Stephen Elliott, scarcely twelve years old and newly orphaned, had been summoned here by his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney—a man known more for his reclusive scholarship than for acts of familial kindness. Few in Lincolnshire could fathom why the stern antiquarian had taken an interest in the child, and yet, when the door swung open, it was Mr. Abney himself who emerged, his gaunt face alight with an unsettling eagerness.

“Ah! Stephen, my boy!” he exclaimed, rubbing his thin hands together. “How old did you say you were? Twelve, yes? And your birthday—the eleventh of September, is it not?” His eyes gleamed as he noted this down in a small book, murmuring to himself before abruptly ushering the boy inside.

Mrs. Bunch, the housekeeper, became Stephen’s sole comfort in the eerie stillness of Aswarby. She was a woman of endless tales, and over the fire one evening, she spoke of two other children who had once lived in the house—a girl, taken in years prior, who vanished without a trace, and a foreign boy with a hurdy-gurdy, who disappeared just as mysteriously.

“Master was right put out,” Mrs. Bunch said, lowering her voice. “Had the ponds dragged, he did. But mark my words, them gipsies had a hand in it.”

Stephen’s nights grew restless. He dreamed of a figure in the disused bathroom—a wasted, spectral thing lying in the lead-lined tub, its lips parted in a silent moan. He woke to find himself standing in the moonlit corridor, his nightshirt torn inexplicably over the left side of his chest, as though scored by long, careful nails.

Then came the night of the equinox, when the wind howled through the park like a chorus of lost voices. Mr. Abney summoned Stephen to his study at eleven o’clock, warning him to tell no one. Passing the library door, the boy glimpsed strange preparations—a brazier glowing with incense, an ancient cup filled with dark wine, papers scrawled with cryptic notes.

But before the appointed hour, Stephen saw them—the children. They stood on the terrace beneath his window, their moonlit faces ghastly and familiar. The girl, her hands clasped over her heart, smiled faintly. The boy, ragged and thin, raised claw-like hands, revealing a yawning black wound in his chest. A soundless scream echoed in Stephen’s mind, and then they were gone.

Trembling, he rushed to Mr. Abney’s study. The door was unlocked, yet it resisted him. From within came choked, guttural sounds—then silence. When Stephen finally forced his way in, he found his cousin dead, his face frozen in terror, his chest torn open where the heart should have been.

The papers on the desk told the truth. Mr. Abney had sought immortality through an ancient rite—one that required the hearts of three young souls. The first two had been taken long ago. The third was meant to be Stephen’s.

But the dead, it seemed, had come to collect what was theirs.
