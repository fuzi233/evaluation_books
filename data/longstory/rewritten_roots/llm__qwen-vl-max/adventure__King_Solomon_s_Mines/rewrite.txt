This true yet modest account of an extraordinary journey is respectfully dedicated by the narrator, ALLAN QUATERMAIN, to all young readers—both big and small—who dare to dream beyond the ordinary.

The author takes this moment to express gratitude to his readers for their warm reception of this tale across twelve years and many editions. He hopes that in its current form, it may reach even more hearts and continue to stir wonder in those who still carry the spirit of adventure, drawn to stories of hidden riches, fierce battles, and untamed lands.

Ditchingham, 11 March, 1898.

Now, in 1907, with the release of this new edition, I can only say how delighted I am that this story continues to captivate so many. Imagination has met reality—the legendary King Solomon’s Mines, once a dream, have been found, yielding gold—and now, diamonds too. The Kukuanas, or more accurately, the Matabele, have been subdued by modern arms, yet the tale lives on. It remains a joy to see that so many still find delight in these simple pages. May they bring pleasure not just to today’s readers, but to generations yet to come—just as our dear friend Allan Quatermain would have wished.

H. Rider Haggard
Ditchingham, 1907.

Now that this book is ready to be shared with the world, I feel a strong sense of its limitations—both in how it’s written and in what it includes. I must admit, it does not aim to be a complete record of everything we saw or did during our journey into Kukuanaland. There are many things I would have liked to explore in greater depth, but which have only been briefly mentioned here.

For instance, I gathered fascinating legends about the chain armor that saved us from death in the great battle of Loo—and also about the “Silent Ones,” the towering figures found at the entrance to the stalactite cave. These stories could have filled pages with mystery and wonder. I also wanted to examine the differences between the Zulu and Kukuana languages—some of which struck me as deeply meaningful. A few pages on the plants and animals native to Kukuanaland might have added richness too. I discovered eight new kinds of antelope and countless unfamiliar plants, mostly bulbs, none of which I had seen before.

Then there’s the remarkable military system of the Kukuanas—a topic barely touched upon. In my view, it surpasses even Chaka’s famed Zulu army, allowing faster mobilization without the harsh rule of forced celibacy. And yet I’ve said little about their family customs, which are often strange and intriguing, or their advanced skill in metalworking. Their “tollas”—heavy throwing knives—are made with iron backs and edges forged from fine steel, welded with astonishing precision. This craft shows a deep mastery of metallurgy.

But Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good and I agreed: the best approach was to tell the story simply and directly. We decided to leave deeper explorations for another time, should the need arise. For now, I’m happy to share any details I can with those who are curious.

I must also apologize for my plain style of writing. I’ve spent more time handling a rifle than a pen, so I cannot pretend to the elegance and flourishes seen in novels—though I do enjoy reading them sometimes. Perhaps such stylistic touches are expected. I regret not being able to offer them. Still, I believe truth told plainly carries more weight. Simple words can speak louder than fancy ones.

As the Kukuanas say: “A sharp spear needs no polish.” So too, I hope, a true story—even one as extraordinary as this—does not need华丽 language to be powerful.

It’s strange, at fifty-five—just past my last birthday—that I should find myself picking up a pen to write down a story. I wonder what kind of tale it will become when I’m done—if I ever finish it at all. I’ve lived a long life, though not one that most would call ordinary. I began working young, before many boys had even left school, earning my living as a trader in the old Colony. Since then, I’ve traded, hunted, fought, and mined—all across the wild corners of Africa. And yet, just eight months ago, I finally made my fortune. It’s a sizable pile now—though I still don’t know exactly how big—and I wouldn’t go through the last fifteen or sixteen months again for all the gold in the world. Not even if I knew I’d come out unscathed.

I suppose it’s because I’m naturally cautious. I hate violence. And lately, I’ve grown weary of adventure. So why am I writing this? It’s not something I’d normally do. I’m no writer. But I have my reasons—four of them, to be precise.

First: Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked me to. Second: I’m laid up here in Durban with pain in my left leg. Ever since a lion clamped his jaws around it, I’ve been plagued by flare-ups—especially around the same time each year. There must be some poison in those teeth, or else why would a healed wound reopen like that? It’s hard enough to take when you’ve shot sixty-five lions in your lifetime, only for the sixty-sixth to chew your leg like a piece of tobacco. It disrupts everything. And I’m an orderly man—I dislike disorder.

Third: I want my son Harry, who’s studying medicine at a hospital in London, to have something to read. Hospital work can grow dull—cutting up dead bodies, day after day. This story won’t be dull. It’ll give him a little excitement while he’s reading about our journey.

And fourth: Because I’m going to tell the strangest tale I’ve ever lived through. A tale without women—well, almost. There’s Foulata, and perhaps Gagaoola, though she was more monster than woman. She was over a hundred, so marriageable she wasn’t. No petticoats in this book, I promise.

Now, let’s get started. It’s a heavy task, and I feel as though I’m stuck in mud up to the axle. But “sutjes, sutjes,” as the Boers say—slow and steady wins the race. A strong team will pull through, if they’re not too weak. You can’t do much with poor oxen.

So here goes: I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, begin this account—not with a legal oath, as I did when giving testimony about the deaths of Khiva and Ventvögel—but simply as a man recalling events from memory. I’m not sure I’m a gentleman. What is a gentleman? I don’t know. But I’ve known men—white men with money—who weren’t, and others—natives—who were. I was born into a decent family, though I’ve spent most of my life as a hunter and trader. Whether I’ve stayed one, well, that’s for you to judge. I’ve killed men—many of them—but never without cause. Never innocent blood. I believe God gave us life to defend, and I’ve always acted on that. Heaven knows, I’ve tried.

Still, the world is cruel and wicked. And though I’m timid by nature, I’ve found myself in plenty of fights. I can’t justify every action, but I’ve never stolen—except once. I cheated a Kafir out of a herd of cattle. He’d wronged me first, and I’ve regretted it ever since.

But let’s return to the beginning. It was eighteen months ago when I first met Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good.

I’d been elephant hunting beyond Bamangwato, and things had gone badly. Everything went wrong. Then I caught a severe fever. When I recovered, I sold my ivory, my wagon, and my oxen, discharged my hunters, and took the post-cart to the Cape.

After a week in Cape Town—where I found the hotel overpriced and saw the botanical gardens (a fine thing for the country) and the new Parliament buildings (which I doubt will amount to much)—I decided to return to Natal by sea. The *Dunkeld* was waiting at dock, bound for England via the *Edinburgh Castle*. I booked a berth and boarded the ship.

That afternoon, passengers from the *Edinburgh Castle* transferred over, and we set sail. Among them were two men who caught my attention.

The first was a tall, broad-shouldered man of about thirty—perhaps the largest-chested and longest-armed fellow I’d ever seen. He had thick yellow hair and beard, sharp features, and deep-set grey eyes. He looked like an ancient Dane—or at least like someone from a painting I once saw of Norsemen drinking from horns, their long hair flowing down their backs. If he’d let his hair grow, slipped on a chain shirt, and gripped a battle-axe, he might have stepped straight out of that picture. Later, I learned his name was Sir Henry Curtis—and yes, he had Danish blood. He reminded me of someone else, too, though I couldn’t place who at the time.

The second man was shorter, dark, and stocky. I sensed immediately he was a naval officer—something about the way he stood, the set of his jaw. I’ve traveled with navy men before, and they’ve always been brave, honest, and good company—though some could swear like sailors.

I soon confirmed my guess. His name was Captain John Good—a lieutenant turned commander after seventeen years of service, but forced out of the Royal Navy with no prospects. That’s the fate of many who serve the Queen: trained, experienced, and then cast aside when they’re at their peak. I pity them. I’d rather earn my bread as a hunter. The pay may be less, but you don’t get kicked as often.

Captain Good was neat, clean-shaven, and wore an eye-glass in his right eye—no string, just fixed there like a part of him. He never removed it except to wipe it. At first, I thought he slept in it, but later I discovered he tucked it in his pocket along with two sets of false teeth—so perfect, in fact, that I’ve often envied them.

The weather turned foul shortly after we sailed. A cold wind blew in from shore, bringing a thick, driving mist. The *Dunkeld*, being flat-bottomed and light, rolled heavily—so much so it seemed she might capsize. I stood near the engine room, where it was warm, watching the pendulum swing back and forth with each roll.

“That pendulum’s off,” said a voice beside me. “It’s not properly weighted.”

I turned. It was Captain Good.

“Indeed?” I replied. “How so?”

“I don’t think—it’s obvious. Look—when the ship rights herself, if it truly showed the angle, she’d never come back up. But these merchant skippers are always careless.”

Just then, dinner was called. I was glad. Nothing worse than listening to a naval officer complain about merchant captains—unless it’s hearing a captain complain about the navy.

We went down together. Sir Henry Curtis was already seated. He and the captain sat side by side; I took the seat opposite. We fell into conversation—about hunting, animals, the land. Then Captain Good brought up elephants.

“Ah,” said a voice nearby, “you’ve found the right man. Hunter Quatermain knows elephants better than anyone.”

Sir Henry, who had been quiet, suddenly leaned forward.

“Excuse me, sir,” he said, his voice low and deep—fitting for such a large man—“but is your name Allan Quatermain?”

“It is,” I answered.

He said nothing more, but muttered “fortunate” under his breath.

After dinner, Sir Henry invited me to his cabin. I accepted. It was a spacious deck cabin—originally two, but knocked together when Sir Garnet Wolseley passed through. There was a sofa, a table, and a lamp. He sent for whisky, and we lit our pipes.

“Mr. Quatermain,” he said, once the lamp was lit, “the year before last, around this time, you were at Bamangwato, north of the Transvaal?”

“I was,” I replied, surprised he knew.

“You were trading there?”

“Yes. I brought a wagonload of goods, set up camp outside the settlement, and waited until I sold them.”

Sir Henry leaned forward, his grey eyes fixed on mine. There was something urgent in them.

“Did you meet a man named Neville?”

“Oh, yes. He stopped beside me for two weeks to rest his oxen before heading deeper into the interior. I received a letter from a lawyer asking if I knew what became of him. I answered as best I could.”

“Your letter reached me,” Sir Henry said. “You mentioned that Neville left Bamangwato in May with a driver, a voorlooper, and a Kafir hunter named Jim. He planned to reach Inyati—the farthest trading post among the Matabele—sell his wagon, and continue on foot.”

“Yes,” I said.

A silence followed.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “do you know anything more about the purpose of his journey? Or where he intended to go?”

“I heard something,” I said, hesitating.

They exchanged glances. Captain Good nodded.

“Then I shall tell you a story,” Sir Henry continued. “And ask for your advice—and perhaps your help. The agent who forwarded your letter told me I could trust you implicitly. You’re well known in Natal—respected, discreet.”

I bowed. “I do my best.”

“Mr. Neville,” Sir Henry said, “was my brother.”

I started. Now I understood why he reminded me of someone. His brother was smaller, with a dark beard, but the eyes—same shade of grey, same sharpness. The features were similar.

“He was my only younger brother,” Sir Henry went on. “We were inseparable until five years ago, when a quarrel tore us apart. I behaved unjustly in my anger.”

Captain Good nodded slowly.

“At that time, our father died intestate. He’d delayed making a will. As a result, the estate passed to me—his eldest son. My brother, who had no profession, was left with nothing. I should have provided for him. But the quarrel was bitter, and I waited for him to reach out. He didn’t. To my shame, I did nothing.”

He sighed. “I’ve come to understand that blood is thicker than water.”

“I’ve learned that too,” I said, thinking of Harry.

“And I would have given half my fortune,” Sir Henry said, “to know my brother George was safe. To see him again.”

“But you never did,” Captain Good interrupted, looking at him sharply.

“No,” Sir Henry said quietly. “Time passed. I grew anxious. I began searching. Your letter was one result. It proved George was alive—at least recently. But it didn’t tell me where he was. So I decided to come to Africa myself. Captain Good offered to join me.”

“Nothing else to do,” the captain said. “Turned out by my Lords of the Admiralty. Half-pay and no prospects. So I came.”

He turned to me. “Now, Mr. Quatermain—what do you know of this man Neville?”

“What did you hear about your brother’s journey at Bamangwato?” Sir Henry asked, pausing as I stopped to refill my pipe before responding to Captain Good.

“I heard this,” I replied, “and I’ve never spoken of it to anyone until today. I learned that he was setting out for Solomon’s Mines.”

“Solomon’s Mines?” both men exclaimed in unison. “Where are they?”

“I don’t know exactly,” I said. “But I know where they’re said to be. Once, I saw the peaks of the mountains that border them—though there were over a hundred miles of barren desert between me and those heights, and I believe no white man has crossed that stretch save one. Still, perhaps the best way to explain is to tell you the legend of Solomon’s Mines as I’ve come to know it—on the condition that you promise not to repeat any part of what I say without my permission. Do you agree? I have reasons for asking.”

Sir Henry nodded slowly. Captain Good answered at once: “Certainly. Without hesitation.”

“Well,” I began, “as you might imagine, elephant hunters aren’t usually the kind of men who concern themselves with history or folklore. We live by the blade and the rifle, and care little beyond survival and game. But every now and then, you meet one who takes an interest in native traditions, collecting stories from the tribes, trying to piece together fragments of the past from this vast, shadowed land. It was such a man who first told me the tale of Solomon’s Mines—nearly thirty years ago, when I was on my first elephant hunt in Matabeleland. His name was Evans. He died the following year, killed by a wounded buffalo, and lies buried near the Zambesi Falls.

One night, I was recounting to him some remarkable ruins I’d stumbled upon while hunting koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. The site had a wide wagon road carved directly into solid rock, leading to the entrance of an underground gallery. Inside, stacks of gold quartz lay piled up, ready for roasting—clear evidence that whoever worked there had fled suddenly. Twenty paces in, the passage was blocked by a wall of finely cut stone, so skillfully built it looked like ancient craftsmanship.

‘Ah,’ Evans said, ‘but let me tell you something even stranger.’ And then he spoke of a ruined city deep in the interior—a place he believed to be Ophir, the biblical land of riches. Other scholars, he added, had long since suggested the same. I listened intently. I was young then, and the idea of a forgotten civilization, of Jewish or Phoenician traders amassing wealth in a land long fallen into darkness, stirred my imagination deeply.

Then, suddenly, he leaned forward. ‘Lad,’ he said, ‘have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains, far to the northwest of Mushakulumbwe country?’”

I shook my head. “No,” I admitted.

“Ah,” he said, “that’s where Solomon really had his mines—the diamond mines, I mean.”

“How do you know that?” I asked.

“Know it? Why, ‘Suliman’ is just a corruption of Solomon.” [3] “Besides,” he continued, “an old Isanusi witch doctor from Manica told me everything. She claimed the people beyond those mountains were a branch of the Zulus—speaking a dialect of Zulu—but taller, stronger, and more noble. Among them lived great wizards who had learned their magic from white men in ancient times, when the world was still dark. These sorcerers guarded the secret of a mine filled with ‘bright stones’—diamonds.”

I laughed then, though I found the story intriguing. After all, the Diamond Fields hadn’t been discovered yet. But Evans died soon after, and for twenty years I forgot the tale entirely.

Then, exactly twenty years later—twenty years, gentlemen, which is a long time for a hunter’s life—I heard something more concrete. I was north of Manica, at a place called Sitanda’s Kraal—a wretched spot, barely fit for survival. Game was scarce, and I was struck with fever. One day, a Portuguese man arrived with a half-breed companion. Now, I’ve seen many Delagoa Bay Portugees—men who live off suffering and slave trade, cruel and heartless. But this one was different. Tall and thin, with large dark eyes and curling gray mustaches, he reminded me more of the polite *doms* I’d read about in books. He could speak broken English, and I understood a little Portuguese. His name was José Silvestre, and he had a home near Delagoa Bay.

The next morning, as he left with his companion, he tipped his hat and said, ‘Good-bye, señor. If we meet again, I shall be the richest man in the world—and I will remember you.’

I smiled weakly—I was too ill to laugh properly—and watched him vanish into the great desert westward, wondering if he was mad.

A week passed. My fever broke. One evening, I sat outside my tent, chewing the last leg of a chicken I’d bought from a native for cloth worth twenty birds, staring at the red sun sinking over the desert. Then, three hundred yards away, I saw a figure on the slope opposite—wearing a coat, unmistakably European. The figure crawled on hands and knees, then staggered forward a few steps before collapsing again.

I sent one of my hunters to help. When he returned, he brought back a man—or what remained of one.

José Silvestre.

Or rather, his skeleton, wrapped in skin. His face was yellow with bilious fever, his eyes bulging from their sockets, his lips cracked and dry. His tongue protruded, swollen and blackened.

“Water!” he moaned. “For Christ’s sake, water!”

I gave him milk mixed with water—he drank nearly two quarts in gulps. I refused to give him more. Then the fever seized him again, and he began raving about Suliman’s Mountains, diamonds, and the desert.

I carried him into the tent and did what little I could. I knew he wouldn’t survive.

At eleven, he grew quiet. I lay down and fell asleep.

At dawn, I woke to see him sitting upright, gaunt and hollow-eyed, gazing toward the desert. Then, the first ray of sunlight pierced the plain, striking the distant crest of the tallest mountain in the Suliman range—more than a hundred miles away.

“There it is!” he whispered in Portuguese, pointing with a bony arm. “But I shall never reach it… Never. No one will ever reach it…”

He paused, then turned to me. “Friend,” he said, “are you there? My eyes grow dark.”

“Yes,” I answered. “Yes, lie down. Rest.”

“Ay,” he murmured. “I shall rest soon. All eternity. Listen—I am dying. You’ve been good to me. I’ll give you the writing. Perhaps you can reach it—if you live to cross the desert, which has taken my servant and me.”

He fumbled inside his shirt and pulled out what looked like a Boer tobacco pouch made from sable antelope hide. It was tied with a strip of leather—what we call a *rimpi*. He couldn’t loosen it.

“Untie it,” he whispered.

I did. Inside was a torn piece of yellow linen, stained with rust-colored letters. Within that, a folded paper.

He went on, voice fading. “The paper contains everything written on the linen. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor—José da Silvestra—was a political refugee from Lisbon, one of the first Portuguese to set foot here. He wrote this as he died on those mountains no white man had ever touched before or since. He lived three hundred years ago. His slave waited below, found him dead, and brought this message to Delagoa Bay. It stayed in the family, unread—until I finally opened it. I lost my life over it, but perhaps another may succeed—and become the richest man in the world. Only—give it to no one, señor. Go yourself.”

Then he drifted into delirium. An hour later, he was gone.

God rest him. He died peacefully. I buried him deep, with large boulders placed on his chest. I doubt the jackals could dig him up.

And then I left.

“Ah, but the document?” Sir Henry said, voice heavy with curiosity.

“Yes—the document,” added Captain Good. “What was written?”

“Well,” I said, “if you wish, I’ll tell you. I’ve never shown it to anyone—except a drunken Portuguese trader who translated it for me, then forgot it by morning. The original rag is at my home in Durban, along with poor Dom José’s translation. But I carry the English version in my pocket book—and a copy of the map, if it can be called a map. Here it is.”

*I, José da Silvestra, now dying of hunger in the small cave where no snow lies on the northern side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I named Sheba’s Breasts, write this in the year 1590, using a split bone on a scrap of my clothing, my blood as ink. If my slave finds this when he comes, and brings it to Delagoa, let my friend (name illegible) bring it to the king’s attention, so that he may send an army. If they survive the desert and the mountains, and overcome the brave Kukuanes and their devilish arts—many priests should accompany them—they may become the richest king since Solomon. With my own eyes, I saw countless diamonds stored in Solomon’s treasure chamber behind the White Death. But through the treachery of Gagool, the witch-finder, I could take nothing—scarcely my life. Let him who follows follow the map, climb the snow on Sheba’s left breast until reaching the nipple, on the north side of which lies the great road Solomon built. From there, three days’ journey to the King’s Palace. Kill Gagool. Pray for my soul. Farewell. JOSÉ DA SILVESTRA.*

[4]

When I finished reading, and showed the sketch map drawn by the dying hand of the old Dom—with blood for ink—there was silence. A stunned, breathless silence.

“Well,” Captain Good said at last, “I’ve sailed around the world twice, docked in every port, but I swear I’ve never heard anything like this—neither in a storybook nor out of one.”

“It’s a strange tale, Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said. “Are you sure you’re not making it up? Some men think it’s sport to fool newcomers.”

“If you think that, Sir Henry,” I said, offended, slipping the paper into my pocket—because I detest being mistaken for a liar, one of those fools who boast of adventures that never happened—“then there’s no point continuing.” I stood to leave.

Sir Henry laid his hand on my shoulder. “Sit down, Mr. Quatermain,” he said. “I beg your pardon. I see clearly you’re not deceiving us. It’s just that the story sounds so impossible I could scarcely believe it.”

“You’ll see the original map and writing when we reach Durban,” I replied, somewhat calmed. “But I haven’t told you about your brother. I knew the man Jim who traveled with him. A Bechuana by birth, a skilled hunter, clever for a native. On the morning Neville departed, I saw Jim standing by my wagon, cutting tobacco on the disselboom.

‘Jim,’ I said, ‘where are you going this trip? Elephants?’

‘No, Baas,’ he answered. ‘We’re after something worth more than ivory.’

‘And what might that be?’ I asked. ‘Gold?’

‘No, Baas. Something worth more than gold.’ He grinned.

I didn’t press further. But I was curious. Soon, Jim finished his work.

‘Baas,’ he said.

I ignored him.

‘Baas,’ he repeated.

‘Eh, boy? What is it?’

‘Baas—we’re going after diamonds.’

‘Diamonds? Then you’re heading in the wrong direction. You should go to the Fields.’

‘Baas, have you ever heard of Suliman’s Berg?’—that is, Solomon’s Mountains, Sir Henry.

‘Ay.’

‘Have you ever heard of the diamonds there?’

‘I’ve heard a foolish tale, Jim.’

‘It’s no tale, Baas. Once I knew a woman who came from there. She reached Natal with her child. She told me—she’s dead now.’

‘Your master will feed the vultures, Jim,’ I said, ‘if he tries to reach Suliman’s land. And you’ll join him, if they find anything to feast on.’

He grinned. ‘Mayhap, Baas. Man must die. I’d rather try a new country myself. The elephants are getting scarce.’

‘Ah! my boy,’ I said, ‘wait till the pale old man grips your yellow throat, and then we’ll hear what tune you sing.’

Half an hour later, I saw Neville’s wagon move off. Then Jim returned, running.

‘Good-bye, Baas,’ he said. ‘I didn’t want to leave without saying farewell. You’re probably right—we may never trek south again.’

‘Is your master really going to Suliman’s Berg, Jim? Or are you lying?’

‘No, Baas. He is. He said he had to make his fortune somehow—or try to. So why not aim for diamonds?’

‘Oh!’ I said. ‘Wait—Jim, will you take a note to your master? Promise not to give it to him until you reach Inyati?’ That was about a hundred miles away.

‘Yes, Baas.’

So I tore off a scrap of paper and wrote: *Let him who comes… climb the snow of Sheba’s left breast, till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which is Solomon’s great road.*

‘Now, Jim,’ I said, ‘when you give this to your master, tell him to follow it exactly. Don’t give it to him now—because I don’t want him coming back with questions I won’t answer. Now go—you idle fellow, the wagon’s nearly out of sight.’

Jim took the note and left. That’s all I know, Sir Henry. But I’m afraid—”

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry interrupted, “I’m going to find my brother. I’ll trace him to Suliman’s Mountains, and beyond if necessary, until I find him—or know he’s dead. Will you come with me?”

I am, as I’ve said, a cautious man—perhaps even timid. This idea frightened me. To attempt such a journey seemed to invite certain death. And aside from that, I had a son to support. I couldn’t afford to die.

“No, thank you, Sir Henry,” I said. “I think I’d rather not. I’m too old for such wild-goose chases. We’d only end like poor Silvestre. I have responsibilities.”

Both Sir Henry and Captain Good looked disappointed.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “I’m well-off. I’m determined to do this. Name your price—any reasonable amount—and I’ll pay you before we start. Also, I’ll ensure your son is provided for if anything happens to us. You’ll see how essential I consider your presence. And if we find diamonds—well, they’ll belong to you and Good equally. I don’t want them. Of course, that promise means nothing—but the same applies to any ivory we might get. Make your terms with me, Mr. Quatermain. I’ll cover all expenses.”

“Sir Henry,” I said, “this is the most generous offer I’ve ever received—no hunter or trader could sneeze at it. But this is the biggest venture I’ve ever heard of. I need time to think. I’ll give you my answer before we reach Durban.”

“Very good,” he replied.

I said goodnight and turned in. That night, I dreamed of poor Silvestre—and of diamonds gleaming in the dark.

It takes between four and five days to sail from the Cape to Durban, depending on the ship’s speed and the weather. Sometimes, if the landing at East London is rough—where they’ve yet to build the grand harbor they talk so much about—a vessel may be held up for twenty-four hours while cargo boats can reach it. But this time, we had no delay. The Bar was calm, and tugs arrived promptly with their long lines of flat-bottomed boats, ready to haul our goods ashore. Packages were thrown into the boats with little care—china, wool, or anything else met the same rough treatment. I watched as a case of champagne, holding forty-eight bottles, shattered in the bottom of one boat, spilling its contents into the dirty water. It was a terrible waste. The Kafirs aboard found two unbroken bottles, knocked off the necks, and drank deeply. But the fizzing wine caused them to swell, and soon they rolled about in panic, shouting that the liquor was “tagati”—bewitched. I called down from the deck, telling them it was the white man’s strongest medicine and that they were as good as dead. They scrambled ashore in terror, and I doubt they’ll ever touch champagne again.

All through the journey up the coast, I pondered Sir Henry Curtis’s proposal. We didn’t speak of it again for a few days, though I entertained them with true hunting tales—no need to lie when such strange things happen in the wild. But eventually, on a perfect January evening—the hottest month in these parts—we passed the Natal coast, expecting to reach Durban Point by sunset.

The shoreline from East London onward is stunning: red sandhills, wide stretches of emerald green, scattered Kafir kraals, and a ribbon of white surf that crashes into foam against the rocks. As we neared Durban, the landscape deepened in richness. Steep kloofs carved by centuries of rain glistened with rushing rivers; the bush glowed with deep green, alongside mealie fields and sugar patches. Here and there, a white house peeked out toward the sea, adding warmth and life to the scene. To me, no view is complete without signs of human presence—perhaps because I’ve spent so much time in the wilderness, where civilization feels like a rare gift, even if it drives away the game. Eden may have been beautiful before Adam, but I always believe it became more so when Eve walked among the trees.

We miscalculated slightly, and the sun had already dipped below the horizon when we dropped anchor off the Point. A cannon fired, signaling to the town that the English Mail had arrived. Too late to cross the Bar that night, we settled in for dinner after the mailbags were carried off in the lifeboat. Later, under a full moon, the sea and shore shimmered in silver light, nearly outshining the lighthouse flashes. Sweet, spicy scents drifted from the shore—reminding me of hymns and missionaries—and hundreds of lights sparkled in homes on the Berea. Nearby, sailors aboard another brig sang as they raised their anchor. It was a perfect Southern African night, peaceful and serene. Even the bulldog belonging to a sporting passenger seemed enchanted, snoring contentedly at the cabin door, dreaming he’d bested the baboon in the cage.

Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, and I sat quietly by the wheel. After a moment, Sir Henry asked, “Mr. Quatermain, have you thought about my offer?”

Captain Good chimed in, “What do you say? Will you join us to Solomon’s Mines—or wherever that man Neville might be?”

I rose, knocked the ashes from my pipe, and paused. I hadn’t decided yet—but just as the last ember fell into the sea, I knew. That extra second made all the difference. Often, when you’ve wrestled with a decision for too long, it’s the brief pause that settles it.

“Yes,” I said, sitting back down. “I’ll go. And I’ll tell you why—and on what terms.”

First, I laid out my conditions:

1. All expenses are covered by you. Any ivory or valuables we find are split equally between Captain Good and myself.
2. I want £500 upfront for my services, payable before we begin. I’ll serve faithfully until you abandon the mission, succeed, or disaster strikes.
3. Before we depart, you must sign a deed promising to pay my son Harry—currently studying medicine at Guy’s Hospital in London—£200 a year for five years, should I die or become disabled. That’s all I ask.

“No,” replied Sir Henry. “I accept gladly. This venture means everything to me, and I’d pay far more for your expertise.”

“I won’t retract now,” I said. “But let me explain why I’ve decided to go.”

First, I’ve observed both of you closely. I like you. I believe we’ll work well together. That matters on a journey like this.

Now, about the journey itself—I’m honest: I don’t think we’ll survive if we try crossing the Suliman Mountains. What happened to Dom da Silvestra three hundred years ago? His descendant twenty years ago? Your brother? Their fates suggest ours will be the same.

I paused, watching their reactions. Captain Good looked uneasy. Sir Henry remained steady.

“We take our chances,” he said.

“You might wonder,” I continued, “why I—someone who admits to being timid—would risk such a trip. Two reasons. First, I’m a fatalist. I believe my time is fixed, regardless of my actions. If I’m meant to die in those mountains, I will. God knows His plan. Second, I’m poor. For nearly forty years, I’ve hunted and traded, but never made more than a living. And the average elephant hunter lives only four to five years in the trade. I’ve lived through seven generations of such men. My time may not be far off. If I die in ordinary circumstances, my debts would leave nothing for Harry. But now, he’ll be supported for five years—enough time to stand on his own, if he’s worth his salt.”

Sir Henry listened intently. “Mr. Quatermain,” he said, “your reasons show great courage. Whether you’re right or wrong, I intend to see this through, come what may. If we’re to die, I hope we get some good shooting first, eh, Good?”

“Indeed,” the captain added. “We’ve all faced danger before. No point turning back now. Let’s go down to the saloon and take an observation—for luck.”

And we did—through the bottom of a tumbler.

The next day, we went ashore. I took Sir Henry and Captain Good to my small home on the Berea—a modest place of three rooms and a kitchen, built of green brick with a galvanized iron roof. But it has a fine garden, with the best loquat trees I know and young mangoes gifted by the botanical gardens’ curator. An old hunter named Jack tends it—he broke his thigh badly in Sikukunis country and can no longer hunt, but he’s a skilled gardener, born a Griqua. Zulus aren’t interested in gardening—it’s too peaceful for their nature.

Sir Henry and Good slept in a tent beneath my orange trees, where blooms and fruit hang together in golden clusters. Mosquitoes are rare here unless the rains are unusually heavy. It’s a pleasant spot.

Now, having decided to go, I began preparations. First, I secured the deed from Sir Henry, ensuring Harry’s future. It was tricky—Sir Henry was a foreigner, and the property was overseas—but with help from a lawyer (who charged £20, which I considered exorbitant), it was done. Then I collected my £500.

With that settled, I bought a wagon and team of oxen on Sir Henry’s behalf. The wagon was twenty-two feet long, with iron axles, built of stinkwood—strong, light, and seasoned from a previous journey to the Diamond Fields. It was “half-tented,” meaning only the rear twelve feet were covered, leaving room for supplies. Inside, there was a hide bed for two, rifle racks, and other useful fittings. I paid £125—well worth it.

Next, I acquired twenty Zulu oxen—four more than usual, to account for losses. These cattle were smaller than Africander oxen, but hardier. They could endure harsh terrain and make five miles a day with moderate loads. Most importantly, they were “salted”—they’d worked across South Africa and were resistant to red water, a deadly illness that often wipes out teams. They’d also been inoculated against “lung sick,” a form of pneumonia. This involved cutting a slit in the tail and inserting diseased lung tissue, causing a mild infection that cured them of future attacks. Though it left them tailless—looking like prize bulldogs without tails—it saved their lives.

Then came provisions and medicine. We needed to avoid overloading the wagon while carrying everything essential. Fortunately, Captain Good had medical training—though not formally qualified, he knew more than many doctors. He had a superb travel medicine chest and surgical tools. While in Durban, he amputated a Kafir’s big toe with skill and precision. The patient calmly asked him to replace it—with a “white one” if necessary. Good was baffled.

Arms were next. From Sir Henry’s collection and my own, we selected:

- Three heavy breech-loading double-eight elephant guns (15 lbs each, 11 drachms black powder)
- Three double-500 Express rifles (6 drachms charge)
- One double No. 12 central-fire shotgun (full choke)
- Three Winchester repeating rifles (spare guns)
- Three Colt revolvers (American pattern cartridges)

All weapons were uniform in caliber—critical for ammunition interchangeability. Every experienced hunter knows how vital proper arms are.

For crew, we planned five: a driver, a leader, and three servants. I hired two Zulus—Goza and Tom—as driver and leader. Finding trustworthy servants proved harder. I secured two: Ventvögel, a Hottentot tracker of unmatched skill, though prone to drink—useless near grog-shops, but irrelevant beyond them—and Khiva, a small Zulu who spoke English perfectly.

Still missing one servant, we decided to wait. But the evening before departure, Khiva told me a Kafir waited to see me. I told him to bring him in.

A tall, striking man entered—about thirty, lighter-skinned than most Zulus. He lifted his knob-stick in salute and squatted silently in the corner. I waited—important not to rush conversation with a Zulu. I noticed he wore a “Keshla” ring in his hair, a mark of dignity. His face seemed familiar.

“Well,” I said finally. “What’s your name?”

“Umbopa,” he answered slowly.

“I’ve seen your face before.”

“Yes,” he said. “The Inkoosi, my father, saw me at the Place of the Little Hand—Isandhlwana—on the day before the battle.”

I remembered. I’d been Lord Chelmsford’s guide during the Zulu War. On the eve of battle, I’d spoken with this man—a minor officer in the native auxiliaries—who warned me the camp was unsafe. I’d dismissed him then. But later, I recalled his words.

“I remember,” I said. “What do you want?”

“It is this, ‘Macumazahn.’” That was my Kafir name—“the man who wakes in the middle of the night.” “I hear you’re going on a great expedition far north with white chiefs from across the sea. Is it true?”

“It is.”

“And you go to the Lukanga River, beyond Manica? Is that so?”

“Why do you ask? What concern is it of yours?”

“I wish to travel with you.”

His tone carried dignity—especially using “O white men” instead of “Inkosis.” I sensed something unusual.

“You forget yourself,” I said. “Who are you? Where is your kraal?”

“My name is Umbopa. I am of the Zulu people, yet not of them. My tribe’s home lies far north—left behind when the Zulus migrated south, long before Chaka ruled. I have no kraal. I’ve wandered for years. I came south as a child. I served Cetewayo in the Nkomabakosi Regiment, under Umslopogaasi of the Axe, who taught me to fight. Later, I fled Zululand to see the white man’s ways. Then I fought against Cetewayo. Now I work in Natal. But I’m tired. I want to return north. This is not my place. I ask no pay. I am brave. I am worth my place and food. I have spoken.”

I was intrigued. He seemed truthful, yet different. I translated to Sir Henry and Good. Sir Henry instructed me to ask him to stand.

Umbopa rose, removing his military greatcoat, revealing himself naked except for a leather belt and a necklace of lion claws. He stood six foot three—broad, shapely, and powerful. His skin was dark, but marked with deep scars from assegai wounds. Sir Henry stepped forward, examining his face.

“They make a good pair,” said Good. “One as big as the other.”

“I like your looks, Mr. Umbopa,” Sir Henry said. “I’ll take you as my servant.”

Umbopa understood. “It is well,” he replied in Zulu. Then, glancing at Sir Henry’s height and breadth, he added, “We are men, thou and I.”

I won’t go into every detail of our journey from Durban to Sitanda’s Kraal, near where the Lukanga and Kalukwe Rivers meet. It was over a thousand miles—three hundred of which we walked, thanks to the dreaded tsetse fly, whose bite kills most animals but leaves men and donkeys unharmed. We left Durban at the end of January and reached our destination in mid-May. Along the way, we faced countless trials—some common to any African explorer, others unique—but only one incident stands out as truly worth telling. The rest, I’ll leave aside, lest this tale grow too long.

At Inyati, a trading post on the edge of Matabeleland ruled by the ruthless Lobengula, we said goodbye to our wagon. Of the twenty oxen I’d bought in Durban, only twelve remained. One died from a cobra bite, three starved or succumbed to lack of water, one wandered off, and three more ate the poisonous "tulip" plant. Five others fell ill from it, but we saved them with a tea brewed from boiled tulip leaves—an effective antidote if given early. The wagon and surviving oxen were left in charge of Goza and Tom, our trusted driver and guide, under the watchful eye of a kind Scotch missionary who lived nearby.

With Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and half a dozen local bearers hired along the way, we set out on foot. The moment we stepped away from the wagon, there was silence among us. Each man seemed to wonder whether we’d ever see that wagon again. For me, it felt like a farewell to the world I knew.

We marched for a while in quiet, until Umbopa, walking ahead, began singing an old Zulu chant about brave men who abandoned comfort to wander into unknown lands—only to find, after great hardship, a land rich with game, cattle, and enemies to fight. When he finished, we all laughed—it felt like a sign.

Umbopa had a rare gift: he could lift spirits even in the worst conditions. He wasn’t always cheerful—he often fell into deep, brooding moods—but when he was himself, he brought light to the camp. We came to love him.

Now, for the story I’ve been waiting to tell—one that any hunter would remember.

About two weeks from Inyati, we entered a stretch of lush woodland, cradled between hills. Thick bush covered the ravines—what the natives called *idoro*—and patches of *wacht-een-beche*, or “wait-a-little thorn,” grew alongside towering *machabell* trees, heavy with golden fruit and massive seeds. Elephants loved these trees, and the signs were everywhere: broken branches, uprooted trunks, deep hoofprints. These creatures were not gentle feeders—they tore through the forest like storms.

One evening, after a long march, we reached a beautiful spot. At the base of a wooded hill lay a dry riverbed, dotted with crystal pools surrounded by animal tracks. Opposite stood a wide plain scattered with flat-topped mimosa and glossy machabells, all bordered by endless, silent bush.

As we stepped into the riverbed, we startled a herd of giraffes. They moved with their strange, gliding gait, tails curled over their backs, hooves clattering like castanets. They were three hundred yards away—too far to shoot. But Good, walking ahead with his express rifle loaded with solid ball, couldn’t resist. He raised his gun and fired at the last, a young cow.

By some miracle, the bullet struck her neck directly, shattering her spine. She collapsed instantly, rolling forward like a rabbit. I’d never seen anything so odd.

“Curse it!” Good exclaimed—though I should warn you, he had a habit of swearing when excited, likely picked up during his time at sea. “I’ve killed her!”

“_Ou_, Bougwan!” the Kafirs shouted. “_Ou! ou!_” They called him “Bougwan”—Glass Eye—for his monocle.

“Oh, ‘Bougwan!’” echoed Sir Henry and I. From that day on, Good became legendary among the Kafirs for his shot—even though he wasn’t really much of a marksman. But we forgave his misses because of that giraffe.

We sent some of the boys to cut meat from the carcass, then built a *scherm*—a circular thorn hedge—near one of the pools. Inside, we smoothed the ground, laid down dry grass, and lit fires. By the time we finished, the moon rose, and our dinner of giraffe steak and roasted marrowbones was ready.

That marrowbone was a treat—cracking it was hard, but once inside, the richness was unmatched. Only elephant heart rivals it, and we’d have that tomorrow.

We ate by moonlight, laughing and praising Good’s skill. Then we smoked and talked, squatting around the fire. The scene must have looked strange: I, short and dark, with grizzled hair standing upright; Sir Henry, tall and fair, with long yellow locks; and Captain John Good, sitting neatly on a leather bag, dressed like he’d just stepped out of a country estate.

He wore a brown tweed shooting suit, matching hat, and neat gaiters. His face was shaved clean, his false teeth and monocle perfectly in place. Even his collar—made of white gutta-percha—was fastened tight. “They weigh nothing,” he told me when I asked why he carried them. “And I always like to look like a gentleman.”

Ah, if only he’d known what awaited him.

There we sat, three men in the wild, sharing stories under the stars, while the Kafirs sipped their intoxicating *daccha* from an eland-horn pipe. One by one, they rolled into blankets and slept—except Umbopa, who sat apart, chin in hand, lost in thought. He rarely joined the others.

Then, from the bush behind us, came a deep, rumbling *woof, woof*.

“That’s a lion,” I said.

We all stood alert.

Almost at once, from the pool a hundred yards away, came a thunderous trumpet—a sound no one could mistake.

“_Unkungunklovo_! _Indlovu_!” the Kafirs whispered. “Elephant! Elephant!”

A few minutes later, shadowy forms emerged from the water, moving slowly toward the trees.

Good leapt up, eager for a kill, perhaps thinking elephants were as easy as giraffes. I grabbed his arm.

“It’s no good,” I whispered. “Let them go.”

Sir Henry, however, broke in. “This is paradise. Let’s stay a couple of days. We can try for them.”

I was surprised—he’d always pushed forward, especially since learning that an Englishman named Neville had sold his wagon at Inyati and vanished into the interior. But now, his hunting instincts took over.

Good jumped at the idea. So did I—though I felt guilty letting such a herd pass without a shot.

“All right,” I said. “Let’s rest. We’ll be up before dawn and catch them feeding.”

We agreed. Good undressed carefully, shook his clothes, placed his monocle and false teeth in his pocket, folded everything neatly, and put it under his mackintosh to keep it dry. Sir Henry and I made do with simpler arrangements, then curled into our blankets and fell into a deep, dreamless sleep.

Then—*going, going, go*—what was that?

From the direction of the water came loud scuffling, followed by a series of terrifying roars.

No doubt about it—lions.

We sprang up, staring toward the pool. A shape—yellow and black—staggered toward us, tumbling over itself.

We grabbed rifles and slipped on our veldtschoons—shoes of untanned hide—and ran.

The mass had fallen. Now we saw what it was: a dead sable antelope bull, magnificent in its dark coat and sweeping horns. Transfixed through its skull was a great black-maned lion—also dead.

It was clear what happened: the antelope came to drink. The lion pounced—but was caught by the horns, impaled. Unable to escape, it tore at the bull’s neck, which charged blindly until both dropped dead.

We examined the bodies, then dragged them to the scherm. After that, we returned to bed, waking only at dawn.

At first light, we prepared for the hunt. We took the three eight-bore rifles, ammunition, and large water bottles filled with weak cold tea—the best thing to drink before a shoot, I’ve found.

After a quick breakfast, we set out, with Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel in tow. The others stayed to skin the lion and cut up the antelope.

We found the elephant trail easily—broad and fresh. Ventvögel estimated twenty to thirty elephants, mostly full-grown bulls. But they’d moved on during the night. It wasn’t until nine o’clock, when the sun beat down fiercely, that we spotted signs: broken trees, crushed bark, and steaming dung.

Then, we saw them—about two hundred yards away—standing in a hollow, ears flapping, having just fed.

It was a glorious sight.

I tossed dry grass into the air to check the wind. If they smelled us, they’d vanish before we could fire. Fortunately, the breeze blew from them to us.

We crept forward, using the cover, and got within forty yards. Three huge bulls stood broadside—especially one with enormous tusks.

I whispered my plan: I’d take the middle one, Sir Henry the left, and Good the big-tusked bull.

“Now,” I said.

*Boom! Boom! Boom!* The rifles cracked.

Sir Henry’s elephant fell dead—shot through the heart. Mine dropped to its knees, but rose and charged past me. I fired again—into its ribs—and it went down for good. I rushed forward, reloaded, and ended it with a shot to the brain.

Then I turned to Good. He was agitated. The big bull had charged him after being hit, forcing him to dive aside. It passed blindly, heading back toward camp.

The herd bolted in panic.

We debated chasing the wounded bull or the herd. We chose the herd, thinking we’d seen the last of those tusks.

But I’ve often wished we hadn’t.

Following the elephants was easy—their trail was like a road, crushing the bush as if it were straw. But catching them was harder. We trudged under the blazing sun for over two hours before finding them.

All but one bull stood together. They were restless, lifting trunks to sniff the air. The lone bull stood fifty yards off, acting as lookout—sixty yards from us.

We decided not to approach. Instead, we aimed at the sentry bull and, at my signal, fired.

The three shots hit. He fell dead.

The herd stampeded again—this time straight into a nullah, a dried-up watercourse with steep banks. It reminded me of the spot where Prince Imperial was killed in Zululand.

The elephants plunged in, scrambling wildly to climb the other side. They screamed and cried, pushing each other aside in blind terror—just like panicked humans.

Now was our chance.

We fired as fast as we could reload. Five elephants fell. Had they not suddenly stopped trying to climb and instead charged down the nullah, we might have taken them all.

We were tired, and perhaps a little sick of killing. Eight elephants in one day was enough.

After resting and cutting hearts from two of the dead beasts for supper, we headed back—pleased with our work. We planned to send the bearers next day to collect the tusks.

Shortly after passing where Good had wounded the old bull, we saw a herd of eland. We didn’t shoot—we already had meat. They trotted past, then paused behind a patch of bush a hundred yards away, turning to watch us.

Good wanted a closer look. He handed his rifle to Umbopa and, followed by Khiva, strolled toward the bush.

We sat down, happy for a break. The sun was setting, painting the sky crimson. Sir Henry and I admired the view—then we heard a scream.

An elephant, huge and furious, appeared silhouetted against the dying sun, trunk and tail raised.

Then we saw Good and Khiva running back, the wounded bull charging after them.

For a moment, we dared not fire—fear of hitting them.

Then—disaster.

Good tripped. His polished boot slipped on the dry grass, and he fell face-first, just feet from the beast.

We gasped. We knew he was done.

But something else happened.

Khiva, the Zulu boy, saw his master fall. Without hesitation, he threw his assegai straight into the elephant’s face.

It stuck in the trunk.

The beast roared in pain, seized Khiva, hurled him to the ground, placed a foot on his body, and wrapped its trunk around his upper half—*tore him in two*.

We rushed forward, firing wildly, until the elephant finally fell atop the pieces.

Good stood up, wringing his hands over the boy who’d died saving him. Even I, hardened as I was, felt a lump rise in my throat.

Umbopa stared at the dead elephant and the shattered remains of Khiva.

“Ah, well,” he said quietly. “He is dead—but he died like a man.”

We had killed nine elephants, and it took us two days to remove the tusks, bury them carefully in the sand beneath a large tree that stood like a landmark for miles around. The ivory was exceptional—each tusk averaging between forty and fifty pounds, with the pair from the great bull that killed poor Khiva weighing nearly one hundred and seventy pounds together.

Khiva himself we buried in an ant-bear hole, along with an assegai, to guard him on his journey to whatever world awaited him. On the third day, we set out again, hoping someday to return and reclaim our treasure. After a long and grueling march, filled with trials I cannot fully recount here, we reached Sitanda’s kraal near the Lukanga River—the true beginning of our quest.

I remember vividly our arrival at this place. To the right lay a scattered native settlement: stone cattle enclosures, small fields where grain struggled to grow by the water’s edge, and beyond, vast stretches of open veld covered in tall grass, where herds of smaller game wandered freely. To the left stretched the endless desert. This spot marks the edge of fertile land, and no one can say what natural force causes such a sudden shift in terrain. Yet so it is.

Just below our camp flowed a narrow stream. On the far side rose a rocky slope—the same path down which, twenty years earlier, I had seen Silvestre creeping back after his failed attempt to reach Solomon’s Mines. Beyond that slope began the waterless wasteland, blanketed in karoo scrub.

It was evening when we pitched our tents. The sun sank into the desert, painting the sky in brilliant hues of gold and crimson, casting long shadows across the dunes. Leaving Good to oversee the camp setup, I walked with Sir Henry to the top of the opposite slope and looked out over the barren expanse. The air was clear, and far in the distance I could make out the faint blue outlines of the Suliman Berg, capped here and there with snow.

“There,” I said, “lies the wall surrounding Solomon’s Mines. But God only knows if we’ll ever climb it.”

“My brother must be there,” replied Sir Henry, his voice calm and steady. “If he is, I will find him.”

“I hope so,” I answered, turning to return, when I noticed we were not alone. Standing behind us, gazing intently toward the distant mountains, was Umbopa, the great Kafir warrior.

He spoke directly to Sir Henry, who had grown close to him.

“Is it to that land you go, Incubu?” he asked, pointing with his broad assegai. (Incubu means “elephant” in their tongue—a name they gave Sir Henry.)

I frowned. “Why do you address my master so familiarly? Natives may have names among themselves, but they should not call a white man by such heathen terms.”

The Zulu smiled quietly—a smile that irritated me.

“How do you know I am not equal to the Inkosi I serve?” he replied. “He is of royal blood, I see it in his bearing. Perhaps I too am noble. At least, I am no less a man. Speak for me, Macumazahn. Tell your master what I wish to say.”

I was angry—but something about him made me pause. He carried himself with quiet power, and I wanted to hear what he had to say. So I translated, adding my own judgment: “He is bold, even insolent.”

“Yes, Umbopa,” said Sir Henry. “I would go there.”

“The desert is wide,” Umbopa continued. “No water flows through it. The mountains are high and cold. No man knows what lies beyond the sunset. How will you reach it, Incubu? And why?”

I passed on the question.

“To seek a brother,” Sir Henry replied. “One of my own blood, who may have gone before me.”

“That is true,” Umbopa said. “A Hottentot traveler told me a white man entered the desert two years ago, heading toward those peaks, with a single servant—a hunter named Jim, a Bechuana who wore clothes.”

Sir Henry leaned forward. “How do you know it was my brother?”

“I do not,” Umbopa admitted. “But the Hottentot described a man with your eyes and a black beard. That is enough for me.”

I nodded. “I knew Jim well.”

Sir Henry sighed. “I always knew George would do it. If he set his mind to something, he did it. As a boy, he never stopped until he succeeded. If he meant to cross the Suliman Berg, he crossed it—unless fate caught him. We must look for him beyond.”

Umbopa understood English, though he rarely used it.

“It is a long journey, Incubu,” he said.

I translated.

“True,” Sir Henry replied. “But no journey on earth is impossible if a man sets his heart upon it. There is nothing he cannot do, no mountain he cannot climb, no desert he cannot cross—except perhaps one guarded by death. But if love drives him, and he holds life lightly, ready to keep or lose it as Heaven commands… then all things are possible.”

I relayed the words.

“Great words, my father,” Umbopa said. “Words fit for a king. You are right. Listen—what is life? It is a feather, a seed blown by the wind, sometimes dying in the act of multiplying, sometimes carried into the heavens. But if the seed is strong, it may travel far. It is good to try, to fight the air. Man must die. At worst, he dies sooner. I will go with you across the desert and over the mountains—unless I fall along the way, my father.”

He paused, then launched into a rare burst of poetic speech, full of repetition yet rich in emotion—typical of the Zulu tongue.

“What is life? Tell me, O wise white men, who know the stars and the worlds beyond them, who send your voices across space without sound—tell me, what is life? Where does it come from? Where does it go?

“You cannot answer. I will. We come from darkness, and into darkness we return. Like a storm-tossed bird at night, we fly from nowhere, briefly lit by firelight, then vanish again. Life is nothing. Life is everything. It is the hand that pushes back death. It is the glow-worm shining at night and gone by morning. It is the breath of oxen in winter. It is the shadow that races across the grass and vanishes at dusk.”

Sir Henry stared. “You are a strange man.”

Umbopa laughed. “Perhaps we are alike, Incubu. Perhaps *I* too seek a brother over those mountains.”

I looked at him sharply. “What do you mean? What do you know of those lands?”

“A little,” he said. “A strange country—of magic, beauty, brave people, rivers, trees, snowy peaks, and a great white road. I have heard of it. But what good is talk? It grows dark. Those who live to see will see.”

I studied him. He knew more than he let on.

“You need not fear me, Macumazahn,” he said, reading my thoughts. “I plot nothing. I dig no traps. If we cross those mountains, I will tell you all I know. But Death watches them. Turn back. Go hunt elephants. I have spoken.”

With that, he raised his spear in salute and walked back to camp, where we soon found him cleaning a rifle like any other warrior.

“That man is odd,” Sir Henry remarked.

“He is too odd,” I agreed. “He hides something. But what can we do? We’re stepping into mystery, and a mysterious guide may not matter much.”

The next day, we prepared for departure. Our heavy rifles and gear could not be carried across the desert. So we left them with an old native whose kraal was nearby, promising him safekeeping until our return. It pained me to abandon such fine tools to a thief’s mercy—his eyes already gleaming with greed.

But I took precautions. I loaded every rifle, cocked them, and warned him that touching them would cause them to fire. He tested my eight-bore immediately—and it went off, blowing a hole through one of his oxen and throwing him to the ground. He rose, stunned and furious, demanding payment for the animal. After that, he refused to touch the guns again.

“Put the living devils up in the thatch,” he muttered. “Or they’ll kill us all.”

Then I told him, if we returned and found anything missing, I’d curse him—kill him by witchcraft, turn his cattle mad, sour his milk, summon the devils in the guns to speak to him in ways he wouldn’t like. He promised to guard them as if they were his father’s spirit. Superstitious and villainous, yes—but now he’d obey.

With our excess gear secured, we packed only what we needed—five men: Sir Henry, Good, myself, Umbopa, and the Hottentot Ventvögel. Our load weighed about forty pounds each:

- Three express rifles and 200 rounds.
- Two Winchester repeaters (for Umbopa and Ventvögel) with 200 cartridges.
- Five Cochrane water bottles (four pints each).
- Five blankets.
- Twenty-five pounds of biltong (sun-dried meat).
- Ten pounds of mixed beads for trade.
- Medicine: quinine, surgical tools.
- Knives, compass, matches, filter, tobacco, trowel, brandy, and clothing.

It was light by necessity, but still heavy under the desert sun. Every ounce mattered. We could not reduce it further.

I persuaded three natives from the village to join us for the first twenty miles, offering each a hunting knife. They carried large gourds of water—one gallon each—so we could refill our bottles after the first night. I told them we were going to hunt ostriches, which were plentiful. They scoffed, calling us fools who would die of thirst. But knives were rare treasures, so they agreed—probably thinking our deaths wouldn’t concern them.

All day we rested. At sunset, we ate fresh beef and tea—our last for many days, Good noted sadly. Then, after final preparations, we waited for the moon.

At nine o’clock, she rose—bright and full—casting silver light across the desert. It looked as still and alien as the stars above. We stood ready, hesitating. Such moments weigh heavily on the soul.

Sir Henry stepped forward.

“Gentlemen,” he said, his voice deep and firm. “We are setting out on one of the strangest journeys a man can make. Success is uncertain. But we stand together—three men—for better or worse. Before we begin, let us pray to the Power who guides destinies, who shaped our paths long ago, that He may lead our steps according to His will.”

He removed his hat, covered his face, and bowed his head. Good and I did the same.

I’m no great prayer man—hunters rarely are. Sir Henry had never spoken so before. But I felt it deeply. In that moment, facing the unknown, I prayed more sincerely than I ever had. Something about the vast silence brought me closer to God.

“And now,” said Sir Henry, “_trek_!”

We started.

Our only guides were the distant mountains and José da Silvestra’s ancient chart—a fragment of linen drawn by a dying man centuries ago. It was crude, unreliable. Yet our only hope lay in it. If we missed the pool of bad water marked sixty miles ahead, midway between us and the mountains, we would surely die of thirst. Finding it in that sea of sand seemed nearly impossible. Even if it existed, might it not have dried up, been buried, or trampled flat?

We moved silently through the night, feet sinking into soft sand, karoo bushes snagging our boots. Sand filled our shoes, forcing frequent stops to empty them. The air was thick, creamy, and cool. Progress was slow but steady.

The desert was silent—oppressively so. Good tried to whistle “The Girl I Left Behind Me,” but the tune sounded mournful in the emptiness. He stopped.

Then came a sudden noise—an exclamation, then chaos. Snorts, groans, galloping hooves. Shapes flashed past in the dim light. The natives dropped their loads and cowered, shouting it was ghosts. Sir Henry and I froze—until we saw Good racing away, seemingly riding a horse, yelling wildly.

Then he fell with a thud.

I realized what had happened: we had stumbled onto a herd of sleeping quagga. Good had landed on one, and the beast bolted with him. I ran to help, fearing injury—but found him sitting in the sand, eye-glass still in place, shaken but unharmed.

We laughed, then resumed.

By one o’clock, we halted. We drank sparingly—water was precious—and rested half an hour before continuing.

On we went, until dawn began to blush in the east. Pale light turned to golden bars, stars faded, the moon grew pale, and the desert shimmered in golden glow. It was day.

Still, we marched on, knowing once the sun rose, walking would be nearly impossible.

About an hour later, we spotted a cluster of rocks. Beneath one lay a smooth, shaded hollow—perfect shelter. We crawled under, drank water, ate biltong, and slept deeply.

We woke at three. Our bearers were leaving—they had seen enough. Nothing could tempt them farther. We refilled our bottles from their gourds and watched them walk home.

At four-thirty, we set off again. The landscape was desolate—only ostriches broke the emptiness. Game avoided this dryness. We saw a few cobras, but mostly flies—houseflies, swarming in battalions. They followed us everywhere, buzzing relentlessly. I’ve seen them preserved in amber, half a million years old, unchanged. I suspect they’ll be the last creatures on Earth when the final man dies.

At sunset, we stopped, waiting for the moon. She rose serene and bright. With a brief halt at two in the morning, we trudged through the night until sunrise.

We drank, collapsed on the sand, and slept—no watch needed. The desert held no threats except heat, thirst, and flies.

This time, no rock offered shade. By seven, we woke feeling like steaks on a grill. The sun burned through us, sucking our blood. We sat up, gasping.

“Phew,” I said, swatting the halo of flies around my head. “They don’t feel it.”

“My word,” said Sir Henry.

“It’s hot!” echoed Good.

It was unbearable. No shelter anywhere—just endless glare, shimmering like a red-hot stove.

“What do we do?” asked Sir Henry. “We can’t endure this.”

We looked at each other, helpless.

“I’ve got it,” said Good. “We dig a hole, get in, and cover ourselves with scrub.”

It wasn’t ideal—but better than nothing. Using the trowel and our hands, we dug a ten-by-twelve-foot pit two feet deep. We cut low shrubs and piled them over us—except Ventvögel, who seemed unaffected by the heat.

Inside, it was stifling. The Black Hole of Calcutta would have been a paradise. We lay panting, sipping water slowly. Had we drunk freely, we’d have run out in hours. But we knew—without water, we died.

There we stayed, trapped in our makeshift grave, enduring the day, alive only by sheer will.

Two hours passed—about four o’clock—and I awoke, not from rest, but from the relentless grip of thirst. The exhaustion that had finally claimed my body earlier had been replaced by a deeper agony: my mouth burned, my throat felt like cracked leather, and every breath was a struggle. Sleep was impossible now. I’d been dreaming of cool water rushing over smooth stones beneath a green canopy of trees, only to wake in this endless, sun-baked wasteland. And then it came back—the truth Umbopa had warned us about: if we didn’t find water today, we would die.

No human could survive long without water in this furnace. I sat up, rubbing my grimy face with hands so dry and rough they felt like sandpaper. My lips were stuck shut, my eyelids glued together. It took effort just to open them. Dawn was near, but there was no freshness in the air—only a thick, oppressive heat that clung like smoke. The others still slept, their breathing shallow and uneven.

As light began to creep across the desert, I pulled out my pocket copy of *The Ingoldsby Legends*—a small book I’d brought for distraction—and opened it to “The Jackdaw of Rheims.” When I read the line, “A nice little boy held a golden ewer, Embossed, and filled with water as pure As any that flows between Rheims and Namur,” I snapped my cracked lips together—trying, failing—then laughed aloud. Not a real laugh, more like a broken cackle. The thought of that pure water drove me mad. If the Cardinal had been standing there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have lunged forward and drained that sacred basin, even if it was full of soap suds fit for washing the Pope’s hands. I’d have taken the curse of the Church upon me—anything to quench this fire inside.

I must have been half delirious. I imagined myself, a scorched, grizzled elephant hunter, suddenly appearing between the Cardinal and his boy, diving headfirst into the ewer, gulping down every drop. The image made me laugh again—loud enough to wake the others. They stirred, rubbing their faces, prying apart their gummed lips and eyes, groggy and disoriented.

Once fully awake, we gathered around, silent at first, then speaking in low voices. The situation was desperate. Not a single drop remained. We turned our bottles upside down, licked the rims—nothing. Good, who carried the flask of brandy, stared at it longingly, but Sir Henry snatched it away. “Drinking raw spirit will only kill us faster,” he said.

“If we don’t find water, we’re done for,” I said.

“If the old Dom’s map is accurate, there should be some,” I added.

But no one believed it. We all knew better than to trust a sketch drawn on a shirttail.

Then Ventvögel stood. The Hottentot moved slowly, scanning the ground, his eyes sharp. He stopped abruptly, exclaiming something guttural, pointing at the sand.

“What is it?” we shouted, rising instantly.

“Springbok tracks,” I said, stepping forward. “So what?”

“Springbucks never go far from water,” he replied in Dutch.

“No,” I said, “I forgot. Thank God.”

That tiny clue lit a spark. In moments like these, when hope is dead, even a faint sign can revive the soul. A single star in a black sky is better than none.

Ventvögel raised his snub nose and sniffed the air, like a ram scenting danger. Then he spoke again.

“I _smell_ water,” he said.

We nearly cheered. These men, born of the wild, had instincts sharper than any animal’s.

Just then, the sun rose—glorious, golden, blazing. And what it revealed stunned us.

There, not fifty miles away, glittering like silver under the morning rays, stood Sheba’s Breasts—two colossal mountains, linked by a sheer cliff, towering into the sky. Beyond them stretched the Suliman Berg, a vast chain of cliffs and plateaus, broken only by flat-topped peaks like those near Cape Town. The sight was beyond words. Two enormous peaks, each at least fifteen thousand feet high, shaped like breasts, their snow-capped summits like nipples. At times, mist curled beneath them, forming the outline of a sleeping woman, veiled in mystery.

Their bases swelled gently from the plain, smooth and round. The connecting cliff soared thousands of feet, sheer and unclimbable. On either side, ridges of rock ran into the distance. The scale was terrifying—so grand, so solemn, that we fell silent, awestruck.

For a moment, the sunlight danced on the snow and stone. Then clouds rolled in, wrapping the mountains in soft, ghostly mist. They vanished—like a secret hidden from view.

But our thirst returned, burning hotter than ever.

Ventvögel still insisted he smelled water. But we saw nothing. Only endless sand and scrub. We walked around the nearby hillock, searching every direction. No pan. No spring. Nothing.

“You’re a fool,” I snapped. “There’s no water.”

He lifted his nose again. “I smell it, Baas. It’s in the air.”

“Yes,” I said bitterly, “probably in the clouds. It’ll fall in two months—too late to wash our bones.”

Sir Henry stroked his beard. “Maybe it’s on top of the hill?”

“Rubbish!” Good scoffed. “Water on a hilltop? Never heard of such a thing!”

“Let’s go look,” I said. And so, with no hope left, we climbed the sandy slope, Umbopa leading.

Then he froze—petrified.

“_Nanzia manzie!_” he yelled. “Here is water!”

We rushed to him. And there, in a deep hollow at the very top of the koppie, lay a pool—dark, muddy, foul-looking. But it was water. Real water.

We didn’t hesitate. We dropped to our stomachs and drank—gulping, sucking, devouring it like gods tasting nectar. Heaven help us, we drank until we couldn’t breathe.

Then we stripped off our clothes and plunged into the pool, letting the tepid, brackish water soak into our parched skin. For those of you who’ve only known water from taps—hot and cold at your command—you cannot imagine the joy of that muddy bath. It was ecstasy.

Afterward, we ate what biltong remained—barely able to chew it before. We smoked a pipe and fell asleep under the shadow of the bank, beside the blessed pool, until noon.

All day we rested, grateful for the water, however bad. We gave thanks—not only to luck, but to the memory of da Silvestra, whose crude map had led us here. How the pool lasted so long, we couldn’t say. But I suspect it was fed by a hidden spring deep in the sand.

We refilled our bottles, reloaded our packs, and set off again under the moon. That night, we covered nearly twenty-five miles. No water found. But the next day, we found shade behind ant hills—a small mercy.

At dawn, the mists cleared, and Sheba’s Breasts loomed ahead—now only twenty miles distant. They seemed to rise directly above us, more magnificent than ever.

By evening, we marched again. By daylight the next morning, we stood on the lower slopes of the left breast. Our water was gone again. Thirst gnawed at us. Ahead, only snow—far, far above.

We rested briefly, driven by desperation, then continued, climbing the lava slopes. The mountain’s base was ancient lava, cooled and cracked from some forgotten eruption. The clinker beneath our feet was rough, tearing our soles. By eleven, we were exhausted—worn down by heat, hunger, and pain.

A few hundred yards ahead, large lava rocks offered shade. We limped toward them, collapsing beneath their shelter. There, on a ridge nearby, I noticed something strange—green growth covering the clinker. Soil formed from decomposed lava had collected, seeds carried by birds. But grass wouldn’t feed us.

Then I saw Umbopa rise, hobbling toward the patch. Minutes later, he was dancing, shouting, waving something green.

We scrambled over—hope flaring.

“It’s food and water!” he cried in Zulu. “Macumazahn!”

And then I saw it—wild melons. Thousands of them, ripe and heavy. We grabbed them, tore them open, and devoured them. Six each—perhaps more. They were sour, watery, but divine. We cut the rest in half and set them upright in the sun to cool through evaporation.

But melons aren’t filling. Soon we were ravenous again. Biltong remained, but our stomachs rebelled. We had to ration it.

Then—luck.

Across the desert, a flock of ten large birds flew straight toward us.

“_Skit, Baas, skit!_” whispered Ventvögel, dropping to his belly. We followed suit.

They were _pauw_—bustards. They passed within fifty yards. I grabbed a Winchester, waited, then jumped up. The birds clustered together—exactly as I’d hoped. Two shots. One fell—twenty pounds of meat.

In half an hour, we had a fire built from melon stalks, and the bird was roasting. We feasted—every scrap eaten, bones and beak left. We felt stronger.

That night, we carried melons and climbed higher. The air grew cooler—relief. By dawn, we were close to the snow line.

More melons appeared. Water was no longer a worry. But the climb grew steeper—barely a mile per hour. That night, we ate our last piece of biltong.

Still, no game. No streams. Odd, given the snow above. But we later learned all meltwater flowed down the north face.

Now, food became our fear.

For three days, our condition worsened. I’ll quote from my journal:

**21st May** —Started at 11 a.m. Cold enough to travel by day. Carried melons. Struggled all day. No more melons. No game. Halted at sundown. No food for hours. Suffered greatly from cold.

**22nd** —Started at sunrise. Faint, weak. Made five miles. Found patches of snow—ate some. Camped under a plateau. Bitter cold. Drank a little brandy. Huddled together, wrapped in blankets. Starvation and weariness crushing us. Ventvögel nearly died.

**23rd** —Started at sunrise. Frozen limbs thawed slightly. In dreadful shape. Fearful we won’t survive. Little brandy left. Good, Sir Henry, and Umbopa hold on. Ventvögel worse—Hottentots can’t endure cold. Hunger dulls, but stomach feels numb. Now level with the lava wall linking the two peaks. View is glorious. Desert behind us. Snow ahead, swelling upward. Nipple of the mountain rises four thousand feet. No living thing. God help us—we may be at the end.

I’ll stop the journal here. What follows needs full telling.

All day on the 23rd, we trudged slowly up the snow, stopping often to rest. We looked like ghosts—gaunt, burdened, dragging ourselves across the blinding white plain. Eyes scanned the horizon—useless. Nothing to eat.

We managed seven miles. Just before sunset, we stood beneath the nipple of Sheba’s left breast—a massive dome of frozen snow, soaring thousands of feet.

Weak as we were, we couldn’t help but admire the scene. The setting sun painted the snow blood-red, crowning the peak with golden glory.

“Look,” gasped Good. “We should be near the cave the old man wrote about.”

“Possibly,” I said. “If it exists.”

“Come, Quatermain,” Sir Henry urged. “Don’t doubt the Dom. Remember the water. We’ll find it.”

“If we don’t before dark,” I said, “we’re dead.”

Ten minutes passed in silence. Then Umbopa, walking beside me, gripped my arm.

“Look!” he said, pointing.

Two hundred yards away, in the snow, was a hole.

“The cave,” he said.

We hurried toward it. Yes—it was a cavern. Likely the same one da Silvestra described.

We reached it just as the sun vanished—no twilight here. Darkness fell fast.

We crawled inside. The cave was small. We huddled together, sharing the last drops of brandy—barely a sip each. Tried to sleep.

But the cold was unbearable. I believe the temperature was fifteen degrees below freezing. For us—drained by heat, hunger, and exhaustion—it was torture. Frost crept over us, biting fingers, toes, faces. We pressed closer, but our bodies had no warmth left.

Sleep came in fits—brief, uneasy. Perhaps that saved us. Had we slept deeply, I doubt we’d have woken.

Near dawn, I heard Ventvögel sigh. His teeth had chattered all night like castanets. Then—silence.

I didn’t think much of it. Thought he’d fallen asleep. His back leaned against mine. But it grew colder—then icy.

Then the sky lightened. Golden arrows pierced the snow. The sun rose above the lava wall, casting cold light into the cave.

And there, sitting among us—was Ventvögel. Stone dead.

We dragged ourselves from the corpse—fear of death too strong to bear its presence. Left him sitting, arms crossed over his knees.

Sunlight flooded the cave. Suddenly, someone screamed.

I turned.

At the far end of the cavern—only twenty feet long—sat another figure. Head bowed, arms hanging. I stared.

It was a dead man.

And he was white.

The others saw it too.

Our shattered nerves broke. One by one, we scrambled out of the cave—half-frozen, half-mad—leaving the dead behind.

We stepped out of the cavern, blinking in the sudden brightness, feeling absurdly foolish. The cold air bit at our faces, and the weight of exhaustion pressed down on us like a physical force.

“I’m going back,” Sir Henry declared abruptly.

“Why?” asked Good.

“Because it just occurred to me—what we saw… might be my brother.”

That stopped us all. A new possibility, sharp and chilling, had pierced through our weariness. We turned and re-entered the cave, stepping cautiously into the oppressive gloom. After the blinding glare outside, our eyes struggled to adjust, blurred by fatigue and the lingering afterimage of snow. But slowly, shapes began to emerge from the shadows.

Sir Henry knelt beside the body, peering closely at the face.

“Thank God,” he breathed, relief washing over him, “it’s not my brother.”

I moved forward, drawn by an almost magnetic pull. The corpse was that of a tall man, middle-aged, with strong aquiline features, grizzled hair, and a long black mustache. His skin was yellowed, stretched tight over his bones like parchment over a frame. His clothes had been stripped away—except for what remained of woolen hose—leaving only the skeletal form exposed. Around his neck, frozen stiff, hung a yellow ivory crucifix.

“Who on earth could this be?” I whispered.

“Can’t you guess?” Good asked, his voice low but edged with excitement.

I shook my head.

“José da Silvestra, the old Dom, of course—who else?”

“That’s impossible,” I gasped. “He died three hundred years ago!”

“And what stops him from lasting three thousand in this climate?” Good countered. “If the temperature is low enough, flesh can preserve itself as well as New Zealand mutton. And here? It’s colder than the heart of winter. No sun reaches this place. No animals come near. Nothing disturbs it. No doubt his servant—the one mentioned in the writing—took off his clothes before he died. He couldn’t have buried him alone.” He bent down, picking up a strangely shaped bone, sharpened at one end. “Look—this is the ‘cleft bone’ Silvestra used to draw his map.”

We stared, stunned. The realization settled over us like frost: this was the man whose words had led us across deserts and mountains. Here was the very tool he’d used to write them. And around his neck, the crucifix he’d kissed with his dying breath.

A shiver ran through me—not from cold, but from awe. I could almost see the final moments: the explorer, starving, freezing, yet driven to leave a message for the world. The loneliness of it—the silence, the emptiness—was written in every line of the dead man’s face. I even thought I saw a resemblance to Silvestre, my friend who had died twenty years prior, though perhaps that was merely imagination. Still, there he sat, a monument to the fate that awaits those who dare enter the unknown—a silent sentinel, frozen in time, waiting for the next wanderer to stumble upon his grave.

The sight overwhelmed us, already weakened by hunger and cold.

“Let’s go,” Sir Henry said quietly. Then, after a pause, “Wait—we’ll give him a companion.” He lifted the body of the Hottentot Ventvögel and placed it beside the old Dom. Then, fingers numb and clumsy, he tore the string holding the crucifix, unable to untie it. I believe he still has it. I took the bone pen—it rests before me now, sometimes used to sign my name.

Leaving the two men—one noble, one humble—to keep eternal watch among the snows, we crept out into the sunlight and resumed our journey, each of us wondering how many hours would pass before we too were as still as they.

After walking half a mile, we reached the edge of the plateau. The mountain peak rose slightly off-center, not directly from the middle, though from the desert side it had seemed otherwise. Below us, the landscape was veiled in morning fog. But gradually, the higher mist lifted, revealing a patch of green grass five hundred yards below, threaded by a running stream. And beyond that—ten to fifteen large antelopes, basking in the sun.

Joy surged through us, unreasoning and fierce. Food—plenty of it—if only we could reach it. But six hundred yards was a long shot, especially when our lives depended on it.

We debated stalking them, but dismissed the idea. The wind was wrong. And against the blinding white snow, we’d be seen no matter how carefully we moved.

“Well,” said Sir Henry, “we’ll try from here. Repeating rifles or expresses?”

Another dilemma. Our Winchesters were sighted for a thousand yards. The expresses, only three hundred fifty—beyond that, luck. But if they hit, the expanding bullets were more likely to bring the animal down. I decided we had to take the risk.

“Each of us takes the buck opposite,” I said. “Aim high, just behind the shoulder. Umbopa, give the signal so we fire together.”

We paused, each aiming with desperate care. Life hung on this shot.

“Fire!” Umbopa called in Zulu.

Three rifles cracked simultaneously. Smoke curled upward, echoes ricocheted across the snow. Then, through the haze—joy! A great buck lay on its back, kicking wildly in death throes.

We roared in triumph. We were saved. We wouldn’t starve.

Weak as we were, we rushed down the slope. Within ten minutes, the heart and liver were laid out before us. But then came a new problem: no fuel. No fire. No way to cook.

“Starving men shouldn’t be picky,” Good said grimly. “We eat raw meat.”

No other choice. Hunger dulled any disgust. We buried the organs in snow to cool them, washed them in icy water, then devoured them greedily. Horrible as it sounds, I’ve never tasted anything better. In less than fifteen minutes, strength returned. Our pulses quickened, blood flowed warm again. But we stopped before fullness—knowing the danger of overeating on empty stomachs.

“Thank Heaven,” Sir Henry said. “That beast saved us. What kind of animal was it, Quatermain?”

I went to examine it. About the size of a donkey, with curved horns, brown fur, faint red stripes, thick coat. Unfamiliar. Later I learned the natives call them *inco*—rare creatures found only at high altitudes where no other game survives. The bullet had struck high in the shoulder—whose shot brought it down, we couldn’t tell. But Good, remembering his perfect aim at the giraffe, probably believed it was his. We didn’t argue.

We’d been too busy eating to notice our surroundings. Now, with Umbopa cutting meat for carrying, we looked around. The mist had vanished—eight o’clock, the sun had burned it away. Before us unfolded a panorama beyond description.

Behind us, Sheba’s snowy peaks rose like ancient sentinels. Below, five thousand feet down, lay a vast, fertile land. Forests stood dense and dark; a silver river wound through the valley. To the left, rolling grasslands stretched endlessly, dotted with herds—perhaps cattle, perhaps game—too far to distinguish. Mountains ringed the horizon. To the right, hills rose from the plain, with cultivated fields and dome-shaped huts nestled between.

It was breathtaking. Rivers flashed like silver snakes. Snow-capped peaks twisted like frozen lace. Sunlight bathed everything in golden life.

Two things struck us: first, this land was at least three thousand feet higher than the desert we’d crossed. Second, all rivers flowed south to north. We knew the southern side of the range held no water. But here, on the northern face, streams poured down, feeding the great river we could now trace far into the distance.

We sat in silence, awed.

Then Sir Henry spoke.

“Isn’t there something about Solomon’s Great Road on the map?”

I nodded, still gazing.

“Look,” he said, pointing slightly to the right.

We turned—and there it was: a wide, paved road winding toward the plain. At first we hadn’t seen it because it disappeared behind broken terrain.

“It must be close,” Good said. “Shouldn’t we start?”

Good advice. After washing our faces in the stream, we set off. For miles we walked over boulders and patches of snow, until suddenly, at the top of a rise, the road lay beneath us.

It was magnificent—cut from solid rock, fifty feet wide, seemingly well-maintained. Yet it appeared to begin right there. Behind us, toward Sheba’s Breasts, it vanished entirely—only boulders and snow remained.

“What do you make of this, Quatermain?” Sir Henry asked.

I shook my head. I had no answer.

“I’ve got it!” Good exclaimed. “The road once crossed the entire range and continued into the desert—but sand covered it. Above us, lava flows buried it.”

Plausible. We accepted it and continued downward.

Descending on the road was vastly different from climbing over snow—especially with full stomachs. If not for the memory of Ventvögel’s fate and the grim cave, we might have felt cheerful. Each mile, the air grew warmer, softer. The land shimmered with beauty. The road itself was an engineering marvel—comparable, Sir Henry said, to the St. Gothard Pass in Switzerland.

At one point, we came to a ravine three hundred feet wide, a hundred deep. It was filled with massive dressed stones, arches carved beneath for water flow. The road passed over it as if nothing had been there.

Elsewhere, it zigzagged down cliffs five hundred feet high. Elsewhere, it tunneled through ridges—thirty yards long.

Inside the tunnel, the walls bore strange carvings: armored figures in chariots. One scene showed a battle, captives marched away.

“Well,” Sir Henry said after studying it, “they may call it Solomon’s Road, but I suspect the Egyptians—or Phoenicians—were here first. This looks like their work.”

By midday, we reached woodlands. Scattered bushes gave way to a vast grove of silver trees—like those on Table Mountain at Cape Town. I’d never seen them outside the Cape. Their presence here amazed me.

“Ah!” Good said, delighted. “Wood! Let’s stop and cook. I’ve digested that raw heart.”

No objections. We left the road, found a nearby stream, built a fire. We cut chunks from the *inco*, skewered them on sticks, and roasted them Kafir-style. We ate heartily, then lit pipes and relaxed.

The brook sang beside us, banks thick with giant maidenhair ferns and wild asparagus. Soft air rustled through silver leaves. Doves cooed. Bright birds darted like jewels. It felt like paradise.

The magic of the place, combined with the sense of danger left behind, lulled us into quiet. Sir Henry and Umbopa talked softly in broken English and Zulu. I lay back on the fragrant ferns, watching.

Then I noticed Good was gone. I looked—there he was, by the stream, bathing. Only his flannel shirt remained. His natural fastidiousness had returned. He’d washed his gutta-percha collar, shaken out trousers, coat, waistcoat—folded them neatly, shaking his head at the tears. He scrubbed his boots with fern, rubbed them with fat from the *inco*, making them look presentable. Then, using a tiny mirror in a pocket comb, he examined himself.

Still unsatisfied. He began grooming his hair. Then paused—felt his chin, where a ten-day beard had grown.

“Surely,” I thought, “he’s not going to shave.”

But he was. He washed the fat from his boots, dipped it in water, pulled out a safety razor. Rubbed his face with the fat, then began shaving.

It was painful. He groaned. I watched, laughing inwardly—how odd, in such a place, to bother with grooming. He managed one side of his face when—suddenly—I saw a flash of light near his head.

Good leapt up, cursing (if it hadn’t been a safety razor, he’d have slit his throat). I jumped too.

And there—no more than twenty paces away—stood a group of men. Tall, copper-skinned. Some wore black feather plumes, leopard-skin cloaks. In front, a youth, seventeen, hand raised, body poised like a Greek statue—just after throwing a spear.

The flash had come from the weapon.

An older man stepped forward, grabbed the youth’s arm, said something. Then they advanced.

Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa raised rifles, threateningly. The natives kept coming.

“They don’t know what rifles are,” I realized. “Or they’d hesitate.”

“Put down your guns!” I shouted. “Our only chance is peace.”

They obeyed. I stepped forward, addressed the elder.

“Greeting,” I said in Zulu.

To my surprise, he replied—in a dialect so close to Zulu that both Umbopa and I understood it easily. It was an old form, like Chaucerian English compared to modern speech.

“Whence come you?” he asked. “Who are you? Why are three of your faces white, and the fourth like our own?” He pointed to Umbopa.

I glanced at him. Indeed—he looked like these men. Same build, same features.

“We are strangers,” I answered slowly. “We come in peace. This man is our servant.”

“You lie,” the elder said. “No stranger crosses these mountains—none survive. But why speak lies? If you are strangers, you must die. No strangers live in the land of the Kukuanas. It is the king’s law. Prepare to die, O strangers.”

I was startled—especially as I saw hands slipping to knives at their sides.

“What does he say?” Good asked.

“He says we’re going to be killed,” I replied grimly.

“Oh, Lord!” Good groaned. Then, as always when confused, he touched his false teeth—dragging the upper plate down, letting it snap back.

It was a lucky move.

The Kukuanas shrieked in horror and bolted back.

“What’s happening?” I asked.

“It’s his teeth,” Sir Henry whispered urgently. “He moved them! Take them out, Good!”

Good slipped the dentures into his sleeve.

Curiosity overcame fear. The men crept forward.

“How is it,” the elder asked solemnly, “that this fat man—clothed yet bare-legged, with hair on one side of his face and not the other, with one shining eye—how is it that his teeth move, vanish, and return?”

“Open your mouth,” I told Good.

He grinned—revealing two thin lines of gum, utterly toothless.

The crowd gasped.

“Where are his teeth?” they cried. “We saw them!”

Good turned slowly, swept his hand across his mouth with contemptuous disdain. Then grinned again.

There—two rows of perfect teeth.

The young spear-thrower collapsed, wailing in terror. The elder trembled, knees knocking.

“I see,” he whispered. “Ye are spirits. Never has man born of woman had such a face, such an eye, such teeth that melt and grow again. Pardon us, O lords.”

Luck—pure luck.

I seized the moment.

“It is granted,” I said, smiling imperially. “Nay, ye shall know the truth. We come from another world—though we are men like you. From the biggest star that shines at night.”

“Oh! oh!” they moaned in unison, awestruck.

And so, by the power of illusion, we lived.

The afternoon sun cast long shadows as we traveled along the magnificent roadway, stretching steadily toward the northwest. Infadoos and Scragga walked beside us, while their men marched ahead—about a hundred paces distant, like a silent guard escorting royalty. The road itself was an engineering marvel, smooth and broad, carved through the land with precision that spoke of ancient hands.

“Infadoos,” I said at length, “who built this road?”

“It was made long ago, my lord,” he replied, his voice grave. “No one knows when or by whom—not even Gagool, the wise woman who remembers generations past. We are too young to recall its making. No one today could fashion such a path. But the king commands that no grass grow upon it—so it remains clear and strong.”

“And those carvings on the cave walls?” I asked, recalling the Egyptian-like figures we’d seen earlier. “Who inscribed them?”

“The same hands that laid the stones wrote the words,” Infadoos answered. “We do not know who they were.”

I turned to him again. “When did your people, the Kukuana, come into this land?”

“Ten thousand thousand moons ago,” he said, pointing northward, beyond the snow-capped peaks. “They came down like a storm from the great lands beyond. They could go no further—the high mountains encircle this country, as our ancestors told us, and as Gagool confirms. The land was fertile, so they settled, grew strong, and now their numbers are as countless as the sands of the sea. When Twala calls his regiments, their plumes cover the plain as far as the eye can see.”

“But if the mountains surround you,” I pressed, “who do your warriors fight?”

“Not all is walled in, my lord,” he said. “To the north, the land opens. From time to time, warlike strangers descend upon us in great numbers—from lands we do not know. We meet them and destroy them. It has been three lifetimes since the last war. Thousands died, but we broke their strength. Since then, peace has held.”

“Then your warriors must grow restless,” I observed.

“There was one war,” Infadoos admitted, “after the great battle. But it was among ourselves—a civil war. Dog ate dog.”

“How so?” I asked.

“My lord, the king—my half-brother—had a twin born at the same hour, from the same mother. Our custom forbids twins to live; the weaker must die. But the queen hid the younger child, for her heart could not bear to lose him. That child is Twala, the king. I am his younger brother, born of another wife.”

“And what happened after?”

“Kafa, our father, died when we came of age. My brother Imotu became king. He ruled well and had a son by his favorite wife. When the boy was three, just after the great war, famine struck. The land failed. People starved, and murmured like lions denied prey.

Then Gagool—the wise, the terrible, the immortal—rose and proclaimed: ‘Imotu is not your king!’ At that time, Imotu lay wounded, unable to move.

Gagool entered a hut and brought forth Twala—my half-brother, hidden since birth. She stripped away his waistcloth and revealed the sacred mark of the coiled snake around his belly—the sign of the eldest son. And she cried out: ‘Behold your king! I have saved him for you!’”

The people, mad with hunger and reasonless, roared: “_The king! The king!_” But I knew it was false. Imotu was older. He was the rightful ruler.

Just then, though gravely ill, Imotu crawled from his hut, hand in hand with his wife—and behind them, their son, Ignosi—meaning ‘Lightning’ in our tongue.

‘What is this noise?’ he demanded. ‘Why cry ye _The king!_?’

Twala ran to him, seized his hair, and stabbed him through the heart with his knife.

The people, fickle and quick to worship new power, clapped and shouted: ‘_Twala is king!_ Now we know it!’

“And what of Imotu’s wife and child?” I asked.

“She fled,” Infadoos said. “When she saw her husband fall, she took the boy and ran. Two days later, she reached a kraal, hungry and broken. None gave her food—no one welcomes the fallen. But a little girl crept out at night and brought her corn. She blessed the child and vanished into the mountains before dawn. No one has seen her since. Nor Ignosi.”

“So if Ignosi lived, he would be the true king?”

“Yes, my lord. The sacred snake marks him. If he lives, he is king. But alas—he is long dead.”

He pointed ahead. Below us, nestled in the plain, lay a vast kraal—huts clustered within a fence, surrounded by a deep ditch. “That is where the queen and her son were last seen,” Infadoos said. “We shall rest there tonight—if, indeed, my lords sleep at all.”

“We do as the Kukuanas do,” I replied with dignity, turning to address Good, who trudged behind, preoccupied with his flapping shirt.

But I nearly collided with Umbopa, who stood directly behind me—listening intently. His face bore a strange expression, as though some forgotten memory stirred beneath the surface.

We continued forward. The mountains we had crossed now rose above us, their peaks veiled in mist. Sheba’s Breasts loomed softly, draped in gauzy clouds. The landscape grew ever more beautiful—lush, yet not tropical; warm under the sun, but never harsh. A gentle breeze carried scents of earth and wild herbs. This land felt like paradise—richer, fairer, more perfect than any I had known. The Transvaal was grand, but Kukuanaland surpassed it.

Infadoos had sent a runner ahead to warn the kraal of our coming. The man had sprinted off at incredible speed—running, he explained, was a common and valued skill among his people.

Now, as we neared the kraal, we saw companies of warriors emerging from its gates, marching toward us in orderly ranks. Sir Henry touched my arm. “Looks like a warm welcome,” he remarked, his tone cautious.

Infadoos noticed. “Do not fear, my lords,” he said quickly. “This regiment is under my command. They come to greet you.”

I nodded, though unease lingered.

Half a mile from the gate, the ground sloped gently upward. There, the companies formed—each about three hundred strong. They charged up the rise, spears flashing, plumes waving, taking position along the road.

By the time we reached the slope, twelve companies—three thousand six hundred men—had lined the path. Then came the first unit.

We stopped. Before us stood the most magnificent warriors I had ever seen. All were mature men—veterans of forty or more—none shorter than six feet, many towering at six-three or four. On their heads, heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers. Around their waists and knees, circlets of white ox tails. In their left hands, round shields—twenty inches across—made of iron plates covered in milk-white ox-hide.

Their weapons were simple but deadly: short, thick, double-edged spears—meant only for close combat. Each blade measured six inches wide. These were not thrown, but thrust like Zulu *bangwan*—inflicting terrible wounds.

Each warrior carried three large knives—called *tollas*—weighing two pounds each. One hung at the hip, two fastened to the back of the shield. These replaced the throwing assegai. The Kukuana could hurl them fifty yards with deadly accuracy. As they charged, they would release a volley before closing in.

Each company stood motionless—like bronze statues—until we passed. Then, at a signal from their commander—distinguished by a leopard-skin cloak—they raised their spears and roared: “_Koom!_”—the royal salute.

Then they fell in behind us, marching in formation. Soon, the entire regiment—the “Greys,” named for their white shields—marched in our wake, their tread shaking the earth.

At last, we branched off from Solomon’s Great Road and approached the wide fosse surrounding the kraal—over a mile in circumference, protected by a strong palisade of tree trunks. At the gate, a primitive drawbridge lowered for us to cross.

Inside, the kraal was impressively laid out. A wide central path crossed at right angles with others, dividing the area into square blocks—each housing a company. The huts were dome-shaped, built of wattle and beautifully thatched with grass. Unlike Zulu huts, these had doorways—large enough for men to walk through—and were much larger, surrounded by verandas paved with hard-packed lime.

Along the central path, hundreds of women stood—drawn by curiosity. For a native race, they were strikingly beautiful—tall, graceful, with fine figures. Their hair was short but curly, not woolly. Faces often aquiline, lips not overly thick. But what amazed us most was their quiet dignity. They watched us with calm eyes, no expressions of shock or rudeness. Even when Infadoos quietly pointed to Good’s pale legs—his skin so white it seemed almost luminous—they stared, then looked away—without comment.

It was enough for Good, who blushed deeply.

At the center of the kraal, Infadoos halted before a large hut, ringed by smaller ones.

“Enter, Sons of the Stars,” he declared in a grand tone. “Deign to rest in our humble homes. Food will be brought—milk, honey, an ox or two, sheep. Not much, my lords—but enough.”

“It is good,” I said. “We are weary from journeying through realms of air. Let us rest.”

We stepped inside. The hut was prepared—couches of tanned hides, water for washing. Soon, voices shouted outside. At the door, we saw lines of girls bearing milk, roasted mealies, honey. Behind them, youths drove a fat young ox.

We accepted the gifts. A youth drew his knife and swiftly cut the ox’s throat. Within ten minutes, it was dead, skinned, and jointed. The best cuts were set aside for us. The rest, I offered to the warriors, who took it reverently and distributed it as a gift from the “white lords.”

Umbopa, with the help of a charming young woman, began boiling our portion in a large earthen pot over a fire outside. When it was nearly ready, I sent word to Infadoo and Scragga, inviting them to join us.

They arrived shortly, sitting on small stools—unlike the Zulus, who squat. They ate with us. Infadoos was courteous and affable. But Scragga watched us with wary eyes. At first, he had been awed by our pale skins and strange ways. But now, seeing us eat, drink, and smoke, his awe faded—replaced by suspicion. It unsettled me.

During dinner, Sir Henry suggested we ask about his brother. But I thought it best to wait. How could we explain a man lost from “the Stars”?

After supper, we lit our pipes. The sight stunned Infadoos and Scragga. Tobacco-smoke was unknown here. Though they grew the plant, they used it only as snuff. They stared, baffled, at the rising curls of smoke.

Later, I asked Infadoos when we would continue.

“We leave at dawn,” he said. “Messengers have already gone to Twala. He is at Loo, preparing for the great annual feast—held in early June. Then all regiments gather, except those guarding the borders. The great witch-hunt also takes place. You will reach Loo by nightfall on the second day—unless rivers swell or accident delays.”

They bid us goodnight. We arranged watches—three slept, one kept vigil—for the night.

And so, under the stars, we rested—exhausted, yet alive in a world both wondrous and perilous.

There was no need to recount every detail of our journey to Loo. It took two full days, following Solomon’s Great Road—a wide and steady path that cut straight through the heart of Kukuanaland. As we traveled, the land grew richer, the fields more abundant, and the kraals—each surrounded by broad bands of cultivated soil—more frequent. All were built like the first camp we had seen, guarded by strong detachments of warriors. In Kukuanaland, as among the Germans, Zulus, and Masai, every able-bodied man is a soldier. The entire population can be called upon for war, whether offensive or defensive.

We were overtaken by thousands of warriors marching toward Loo for the great annual review and festival. They moved in proud formations, their plumes waving, spears gleaming, shields polished. I had never seen troops so magnificent.

At sunset on the second day, we halted atop a range of hills where the road passed. Below us lay Loo—an immense native town, perhaps five miles around, with outlying kraals extending like military camps on grand occasions. A curious horseshoe-shaped hill stood about two miles north, one we would soon come to know well. The town was beautifully situated, bisected by a river that ran through its center, crossed at several points by bridges—the same river we had glimpsed from Sheba’s Breasts. Sixty or seventy miles away, three snow-capped mountains rose sharply from the plain, forming a triangle. Unlike Sheba’s Breasts, these peaks were sheer and jagged, not smooth and rounded.

Infadoos noticed us gazing at them and spoke up.

“The road ends there,” he said, pointing to the mountains known as the “Three Witches.”

“Why does it end?” I asked.

“Who knows?” he shrugged. “The mountains are filled with caves, and between them lies a great pit. That is where the wise men of old came to find what they sought—and now, it is where our kings are buried in the Place of Death.”

“What did they seek?” I pressed.

“I do not know,” he replied. “My lords who have fallen from the Stars should know.” He glanced at me quickly—evidently, he knew more than he would say.

“Yes,” I said, “you’re right. From the Stars, we learn many things. I’ve heard the wise men came here for bright stones, pretty playthings, and yellow iron.”

“My lord is wise,” Infadoos answered coldly. “I am but a child, unable to speak with my lord on such matters. You must speak with Gagool the old, at the king’s place. She is as wise as my lord.” And he walked away.

As soon as he was gone, I turned to the others and pointed at the mountains.

“There,” I said, “are Solomon’s diamond mines.”

Umbopa stood nearby, lost in one of his silent reveries. He caught my words.

“Yes, Macumazahn,” he said in Zulu, “the diamonds are surely there. And you shall have them, since white men love toys and money.”

“How do you know that?” I asked sharply, uneasy at his cryptic ways.

He laughed. “I dreamed it last night, white men.” Then he turned and left.

“Well,” Sir Henry said, “what’s our dark friend trying to tell us? He knows more than he lets on. By the way, Quatermain—has he heard anything of my brother?”

“Nothing,” I replied. “He’s asked everyone he’s met, but all say no white man has ever been seen here before.”

“Do you think he made it?” Good asked. “We reached this place by miracle. How could he have come without the map?”

“I don’t know,” Sir Henry said grimly. “But somehow, I feel I’ll find him.”

The sun sank slowly, then suddenly—like a physical force—darkness swept over the land. There was no twilight here, no gentle fade from day to night. One moment it was light; the next, the world was wrapped in shadow. But not for long. In the west, a glow appeared—then silver rays, and finally the full moon rose, flooding the plain with radiant light. Its beams pierced the darkness, turning the earth into a shimmering expanse. We stood in awe, watching the stars grow pale beneath the moon’s brilliance, feeling uplifted by beauty too vast to describe. My life has been rough, but I am grateful for moments like this—one of them being the sight of that moon rising over Kukuanaland.

Our silence was broken by Infadoos.

“If my lords are rested, we may proceed to Loo. A hut awaits you tonight. The moon is bright—no danger of falling.”

We agreed. Within an hour, we reached the outskirts of the city. The sprawl of fires lit the landscape, making the town seem endless. Good, ever fond of wit, dubbed it “Unlimited Loo.” Soon we came to a moat with a drawbridge. Arms rattled, a sentry bellowed a challenge. Infadoos gave a password I couldn’t catch. It was answered with a salute, and we passed through the central street of the grass city.

After nearly half an hour of walking past countless huts, Infadoos stopped at a small cluster of dwellings surrounding a courtyard of powdered limestone. These, he said, were our “poor” quarters.

Inside, each of us had a hut. They were better than any we’d seen—each with a comfortable bed of tanned hides laid over mats of aromatic grass. Food was ready: roasted meat and mealie cobs served on wooden platters by young women of striking appearance, who bowed deeply as they presented the meals.

We ate and drank. Then, at our request, the beds were moved into one hut—a precaution that brought amused glances from the attendants. Exhausted, we fell asleep.

When we woke, the sun was high. The female servants, unbothered by modesty, were already inside, ordered to help us “make ready.”

“Make ready indeed,” Good grumbled. “With just a flannel shirt and boots, it won’t take long. Quatermain, ask for my trousers.”

I asked. I was told they’d already been taken to the king—he would see us soon.

To the surprise of the girls, we insisted on dressing ourselves. Good even shaved the right side of his face again—the left, now thick with whiskers, we warned him not to touch. For the rest of us, a good wash and combing sufficed. Sir Henry’s golden hair now reached his shoulders—he looked more like an ancient Dane than ever. My own grizzled beard was fully an inch long, far beyond my usual limit.

By the time we’d eaten breakfast and smoked a pipe, Infadoos arrived with a message: Twala, the king, was ready to receive us—if we wished to come.

We replied we’d prefer to wait until the sun climbed higher—we were still weary from travel. It’s always wise, when dealing with uncivilized peoples, not to rush. They often mistake politeness for fear. Though eager to meet the king, we sat and waited an hour, using the time to prepare gifts: the Winchester rifle once used by poor Ventvögel, and some beads.

We decided to give the rifle and ammunition to the king, and the beads to his wives and courtiers. We’d already given a few to Infadoos and Scragga, who adored them—never having seen such things before.

At length, we declared ourselves ready. Umbopa carried the rifle and beads as we followed Infadoos toward the audience.

After walking a short distance, we came to a vast enclosure—fifty times larger than the one housing our huts. It covered six or seven acres. Along the outer fence stood rows of huts, homes of the king’s wives. Opposite the gate, across the open space, stood a large hut—Twala’s residence. The rest was bare ground, except for the eight thousand warriors standing in perfect silence, their spears gleaming, plumes fluttering, ox-hide shields polished.

In front of the king’s hut stood several stools. At Infadoos’s signal, we sat on three of them. Umbopa stood behind us. Infadoos took position near the door.

We waited—ten minutes, more—in complete silence, aware that every eye of the eight thousand warriors was fixed upon us. It was tense, but we held our composure.

Then the hut door opened. A towering figure stepped out, draped in a tiger-skin cloak. Behind him came Scragga and a strange, shriveled creature wrapped in fur, resembling a monkey. The giant seated himself. Scragga stood behind. The creature crawled into the shade and crouched low.

Still silence.

Then the giant removed his cloak and rose—a terrifying sight. He was enormous, with a face so repulsive we had never seen its like. His lips were thick, his nose flat. One eye gleamed black; the other was a hollow. His expression was cruel, sensual. A plume of white ostrich feathers crowned his head. He wore chain armor, ox-tail ornaments at waist and knee. In his hand, a massive spear. Around his neck, a thick gold torque. On his forehead, a single, dull, uncut diamond shone dimly.

Silence again—until the king raised his spear.

Instantly, eight thousand spears rose in answer. From eight thousand throats roared the royal salute: “_Koom!_” Three times it echoed, shaking the ground like thunder.

“Be humble, O people,” piped a thin voice from the shadow—coming from the monkey-like figure.

“It is the king!” thundered the crowd.

“Be humble, O people—it is the king!”

Then silence returned.

Suddenly, a shield clattered to the ground.

Twala turned his single eye toward the noise.

“Come here,” he said coldly.

A young warrior stepped forward.

“It was your shield that fell, clumsy dog. Do you mock me before these strangers from the Stars? What do you say?”

The youth paled.

“It was chance, O Calf of the Black Cow.”

“Then pay for that chance. You have made me look foolish. Prepare to die.”

“I am the king’s ox,” he whispered.

“Scragga,” the king roared, “show me how you wield your spear. Kill this fool.”

Scragga grinned, lifted his spear. The victim covered his eyes. We froze.

“Once… twice…” the boy waved the weapon—and struck. The spear emerged a foot from his back. He threw up his hands and fell dead.

A murmur rippled through the crowd—then died. The body lay there. Sir Henry leapt up, swearing violently, then collapsed back, overwhelmed by silence.

“The thrust was good,” the king said. “Take him away.”

Four men carried the corpse off.

“Cover the blood,” the thin voice cried. “The king’s word is spoken. The king’s doom is done!”

A girl brought a jar of lime and scattered it over the stain.

Sir Henry seethed with rage. We barely kept him quiet.

“Sit down,” I whispered. “Our lives depend on it.”

He obeyed.

Twala remained silent until the blood was hidden. Then he addressed us.

“White people,” he said, “who come here from lands unknown, greeting.”

“Greeting, Twala, King of the Kukuanas,” I replied.

“Where do you come from? What do you seek?”

“We come from the Stars. Ask not how. We come to see this land.”

“And that man beside you—does he also come from the Stars?”

“Indeed. There are people of your color in the heavens. But ask not of things too high for you, Twala.”

“You speak loudly, people of the Stars,” the king replied, uneasily. “Remember—Stars are far. You are here. What if I make you like the one they bore away?”

I laughed aloud, though my heart was heavy.

“O king,” I said, “be careful. Walk on hot stones, lest you burn your feet. Hold the spear by the handle, lest you cut your hands. Touch one hair of our heads, and destruction will fall upon you. Have not Infadoos and Scragga”—pointing to the boy, who was scrubbing blood from his spear—“told you what kind of men we are? Have you seen the like of us?” I gestured to Good, certain the king had never seen anyone quite so bizarre.

“It is true,” the king admitted, studying Good.

“Have they told you how we strike death from afar?”

“They have. But I do not believe. Show me. Kill one of those men yonder,” he pointed across the kraal, “and I will believe.”

“No,” I said. “We kill only in justice. But if you wish, send a young ox through the gate. Before it runs twenty paces, I will strike it dead.”

“No,” the king laughed. “Kill a man, and I will believe.”

“Very well, O king,” I said coolly. “Walk across the open space. Before your feet reach the gate, you shall be dead. Or—if you prefer—send your son Scragga.” (At that moment, I would have gladly shot him.)

Scragga let out a howl and fled into the hut.

Twala frowned.

“Bring a young ox,” he said.

Two men ran off.

“Now, Sir Henry,” I said. “You shoot. I want to show this brute I’m not the only magician here.”

Sir Henry took his “express” rifle, prepared.

“I hope I hit,” he groaned.

“You must,” I said. “If you miss the first barrel, use the second. Aim for 150 yards. Wait until the beast turns broadside.”

We waited. Then we saw an ox charging through the gate. It paused, turned, bellowed.

“Now!” I whispered.

The rifle rose.

Bang! _Thud!_ The ox fell, kicking, dead—shot through the ribs. The semi-hollow bullet had done its work. A gasp of astonishment rose from the crowd.

I turned calmly.

“Have I lied, O king?”

“No,” he said, awed. “It is truth.”

“Listen, Twala. You have seen. Now know—we come in peace, not war. See,” I said, holding up the Winchester repeater. “Here is a hollow staff that lets you kill as we do—but I place a charm upon it. You shall not kill a man with it. If you aim at a man, it will kill you. Watch—I will prove it.”

I instructed the king to have a soldier stand forty paces away and plant a spear upright, blade facing us.

It was done.

“Now,” I said, “I will break that spear.”

I took careful aim and fired. The bullet struck the blade—shattering it into fragments.

Another wave of astonishment swept through the crowd.

“Now, Twala,” I said, handing him the rifle, “we give you this magic tube. I will teach you how to use it—but beware. Do not turn the magic of the Stars against a man of earth.”

The king took the rifle cautiously and placed it at his feet. As he did, the wizened figure crept from the shadows. It moved on all fours, but when it reached the king, it rose—then threw off its furry covering.

Revealed was a woman of terrible age, her face so shriveled it seemed no larger than a child’s. Deep yellow wrinkles formed a grotesque mask. Her mouth was a sunken slit; her chin curved outward sharply. No nose—only a hollow. Yet her eyes—large, black, fiery—glowed like jewels in a charnel house. Her skull was bald, yellow, and constantly twitching like a cobra’s hood.

She stood still. Then, with a clawed hand, she touched the king’s shoulder and began to speak in a thin, piercing voice:

“Listen, O king! Listen, O warriors! Listen, O mountains, plains, rivers—home of the Kukuana race! Listen, O skies, sun, rain, storm, mist! Listen, O men, women, youths, maidens, unborn babes! Listen, all things that live and die! Listen, all dead things that shall rise again—only to die! Listen—the spirit of life is in me, and I prophesy. I prophesy! I prophesy!”

Her voice trailed into a faint wail. Fear gripped us all.

“Blood! Blood! Blood! Rivers of blood—everywhere! I see it, smell it, taste it—salt! It flows red on the ground, rains from the sky!

“Footsteps! Footsteps! Footsteps! The tread of the white man comes from afar. It shakes the earth. The earth trembles before its master.

“Blood is good—red blood is bright. No scent like new-shed blood. Lions will lap it and roar. Vultures will bathe their wings and shriek with joy.

“I am old! I am old! I have seen much blood—_ha, ha!_ But I shall see more before I die—and laugh. How old am I? Your fathers knew me. Their fathers knew me. Their fathers’ fathers’ fathers knew me. I have seen the white man. I know his desires.

“I am old—but the mountains are older. Who made the great road? Who painted the rocks? Who raised the Three Silent Ones yonder, who gaze across the pit?” She pointed at the mountains.

“You do not know—but I do. A white people came before you. They will come again when you are gone. They will eat you up and destroy you. _Yea! yea! yea!_

“And why did they come? The White Ones, the Terrible Ones, skilled in magic and learning, strong and unswerving? What is that bright stone on your forehead, O king? Whose hands forged the iron on your chest? You do not know—but I do. I, the Old One. I, the Wise One. I, the _Isanusi_, the witch doctor!”

She turned her vulture-head toward us.

“What do you seek, white men of the Stars—ah, yes, of the Stars? Do you search for a lost one? You will not find him here. He is not here. Never in ages has a white foot trod this land—except once. And I remember: he left only to die.

“You come for bright stones. I know it. You will find them when the blood dries. But will you return? Or stay with me? _Ha! ha! ha!_

“And you—thou with dark skin and proud bearing,” she pointed at Umbopa, “who art thou? What dost thou seek? Not shining stones, not yellow metal—those you leave to the ‘white men from the Stars.’ I know you. I smell the blood in your heart. Strip off the girdle—”

Her features contorted. She fell to the ground, foaming, seized by an epileptic fit. She was carried into the hut.

The king rose, trembling. He waved his hand. Instantly, the regiments filed away. In ten minutes, only we, the king, and a few attendants remained.

“White people,” he said, “it crosses my mind to kill you. Gagool has spoken strange words. What say you?”

When we reached our hut, I gestured for Infadoos to step inside with us.

“Now, Infadoos,” I said, “we wish to speak with you.”

“Let my lords speak,” he replied.

“It seems to us,” I continued, “that Twala, the king, is a cruel man.”

“He is indeed, my lords,” Infadoos answered. “The land groans under his tyranny. Tonight, you will see—tonight is the great witch-hunt. Many will be accused of sorcery and killed. No one is safe. If the king desires a man’s cattle, or his wife, or fears him as a threat to his rule, then Gagool—the old woman you saw—or one of her trained witch-finders will mark him as a wizard, and he shall die. Before the moon fades, many will fall. It always happens this way. Perhaps even I may be among them. I have been spared so far because I am skilled in war and beloved by the soldiers, but I do not know how much longer I shall live. The people suffer under Twala’s cruelty. They are weary of him and his bloodthirsty ways.”

“Then why do they not rise against him?” I asked.

“Because he is the king,” Infadoos said. “If he were slain, Scragga would take his place—and Scragga’s heart is blacker than his father’s. Under Scragga, the yoke upon our necks would be heavier still. If Imotu had not died, or if Ignosi, his son, had survived, things might have been different. But both are gone.”

“How do you know Ignosi is dead?” a voice broke in behind us. We turned sharply, startled. It was Umbopa.

“What do you mean, boy?” Infadoos demanded. “Who gave you leave to speak?”

“Listen,” Umbopa said quietly. “I will tell you a story. Long ago, King Imotu was killed in this land, and his wife fled with their son, Ignosi. Is that not true?”

“It is.”

“They say the mother and child perished on the mountains. Is that not so?”

“It is.”

“But they did not die. They crossed the mountains and were led by a wandering tribe of desert people across the sands until they found water, grass, and trees once more.”

“How do you know this?”

“Listen. They journeyed for many months until they came to a land ruled by the Amazulu—a people of Kukuana descent who live by war. There they stayed for years. Then the mother died. Her son, Ignosi, became a wanderer again. He traveled into a land of wonders where white men dwell, and there he spent many years learning their ways.”

“It sounds like a tale,” Infadoos said, skeptical.

“For years, he lived as a servant and soldier, yet never forgot what his mother told him of his homeland. He waited, planning how he might return before death claimed him. At last, he met white men seeking this unknown land, and joined them. Together, they crossed the burning deserts, climbed snow-capped mountains, and finally reached the land of the Kukuanas—where they found *you*, O Infadoos.”

“You must be mad,” Infadoos said, stunned.

“Perhaps I am,” Umbopa replied. “But watch. I will show you, O uncle.”

“I am Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas!”

With a swift motion, Umbopa removed his *moocha*—his girdle—and stood naked before us.

“Look,” he said, pointing to a blue tattoo circling his waist: a great snake coiled with its tail in its open mouth, just above the junction of his thighs.

Infadoos stared. His eyes widened, then he dropped to his knees.

“Koom! Koom!” he gasped. “It is my brother’s son! It is the king!”

“Did I not tell you, uncle?” Umbopa said. “Rise. I am not yet king—but with your help, and the help of these brave white men who are my friends, I shall be. And Gagool was right—the land will run with blood first. Hers too, if she can die. She killed my father with her words and drove my mother away. Now, Infadoos, choose: will you place your hands in mine and become my man? Will you share the dangers ahead and help me overthrow this tyrant? Or will you not? Choose.”

Infadoos pressed his hand to his head, thinking. Then he rose, stepped forward, knelt before Umbopa, and took his hand.

“Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas,” he said, “I place my hand in yours. I am your man until death. When you were a babe, I held you in my arms. Now, my old arm shall fight for you and freedom.”

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “If I win, you shall be the greatest man in the kingdom after the king. If I fail, you can only die—and death is near enough already. Rise, uncle.”

“And you, white men,” Ignosi said, turning to us, “will you stand with me? What can I offer? The white stones! If I succeed and find them, you shall have as many as you can carry. Will that suffice?”

I translated.

“Tell him,” Sir Henry replied, “that an Englishman does not sell himself for riches. Wealth is good—if it comes our way, we’ll take it. But a gentleman doesn’t trade honor for gold. Still, speaking for myself—I’ve always liked Umbopa. So long as I can, I’ll stand by him. It will be a pleasure to face that cruel beast Twala. What about you, Good? And you, Quatermain?”

“Well,” Good said, “to use their dramatic language—this sounds like a fine row. Warms the cockles of the heart. As for me, I’m his boy. Only condition: I insist on wearing trousers.”

I relayed the message.

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “And you, Macumazahn? Are you with me, old hunter—cleverer than a wounded buffalo?”

I paused, scratching my head.

“Umbopa—or Ignosi,” I said, “I don’t care for revolutions. I’m a man of peace and a bit of a coward”—Umbopa smiled—“but I stand by my friends. You’ve stood by us, played the part of a man. I’ll stand by you. That said, I’m a trader—I need to earn my living. So yes, I accept your offer of diamonds, should we ever reach them. One other thing: we came here to find Incubu’s lost brother. You must help us locate him.”

“I will,” Ignosi promised. “Stay, Infadoos. By the sign of the snake around my middle, tell me the truth: has any white man ever set foot in this land?”

“None, O Ignosi.”

“If one had been seen or heard, would you have known?”

“I would have known.”

“Then hear this, Incubu,” Ignosi said to Sir Henry. “He has not been here.”

“Well,” Sir Henry sighed, “there it is. I suppose he never made it. Poor fellow. All for nothing. God’s will be done.”

“Now, business,” I said, eager to move on. “Fine to be king by right, Ignosi—but how do you plan to become king in truth?”

“I do not know,” he admitted. “Infadoos, do you have a plan?”

“Ignosi, Son of the Lightning,” Infadoo replied, “tonight is the great dance and witch-hunt. Many will be marked and slain. Others will burn with grief and fury at the king. After the dance, I will speak to the great chiefs. If I can win them over, they will rally their regiments. I will speak softly at first, showing them you are truly the king. By tomorrow’s light, I believe you will command twenty thousand spears. But now I must go—think, listen, prepare. After the dance, if I live and you live, I will meet you here. At best, there will be war.”

At that moment, a cry rang out: messengers from the king had arrived. We ordered them in. Three men entered, each bearing a gleaming shirt of chain mail and a magnificent battle-axe.

“The gifts of my lord the king to the white men from the Stars!” announced a herald.

“We thank the king,” I said. “Withdraw.”

They left. We examined the armor with fascination. It was the finest chainwork any of us had ever seen—so tightly linked that the entire coat formed a mass barely large enough to cover both hands.

“Do you make these in this land, Infadoos?” I asked. “They are beautiful.”

“No, my lord,” he said. “They came down from our ancestors. We do not know who forged them. Few remain. Only those of royal blood may wear them. They are magical—no spear can pierce them. Those who wear them are nearly invincible in battle. The king sends them either out of favor—or fear. Wear them tonight, my lords.”

We spent the rest of the day resting, discussing our situation—exciting enough. As dusk fell, the thousand watchfires blazed to life. In the dark, we heard the tramp of thousands of feet, the clash of hundreds of spears—as regiments marched to their posts. Then the full moon rose, brilliant and clear. Infadoos arrived, dressed in his war gear, escorted by twenty men. As he advised, we had already donned the chain shirts beneath our clothes—surprisingly light and comfortable. The armor, clearly made for larger men, hung loosely on Good and me, but fit Sir Henry like a glove. We strapped revolvers at our waists, took up the battle-axes, and set off.

At the great kraal—where we’d been received by the king earlier—we found it packed with some twenty thousand men arranged in regiments. Each regiment was divided into companies, with narrow paths between them for the witch-finders to pass. Never had I seen such an imposing sight—silent, orderly, armed to the teeth. The moonlight bathed their raised spears, their proud forms, their plumed heads, and the colorful shields. Everywhere, rows of dim faces topped by shimmering spear tips.

“Surely,” I said to Infadoos, “the whole army is here?”

“No, Macumazahn,” he replied. “Only a third. Another third stands ready outside in case of trouble. Ten thousand guard the outposts around Loo. The rest watch the kraals. This is a mighty people.”

“They are very quiet,” Good observed. Indeed, the silence among so many men was almost suffocating.

“What says Bougwan?” Infadoos asked.

I translated.

“Those shadowed by Death are silent,” he answered grimly.

“Will many die?”

“Many.”

“It seems,” I said to the others, “we’re attending a gladiatorial spectacle—without mercy.”

Sir Henry shivered. Good wished we could escape.

“Are we in danger?” I asked Infadoos.

“I cannot say, my lords. I hope not. But do not appear afraid. If you survive the night, all may yet go well. The soldiers mutter against the king.”

As we advanced toward the center, where stools were placed, another group approached from the royal hut.

“It is the king Twala, his son Scragga, and Gagool the old,” Infadoos whispered. “And look—those who kill.” He pointed to a dozen enormous, savage men, each armed with a spear and a heavy club.

Twala sat on the central stool. Gagool crouched at his feet. The executioners stood behind.

“Greeting, white lords,” Twala called. “Be seated—time is precious. The night is too short for what must be done. You come at a good hour. You shall witness a glorious show. Look around, white lords—look around!” He rolled his single, wicked eye across the ranks. “Can the Stars show you such a sight? See how they tremble in their wickedness—all those with evil hearts, fearing the judgment of ‘Heaven above.’”

“_Begin! Begin!_” shrieked Gagool, her voice thin and piercing. “The hyenas are hungry—they howl for food. _Begin! Begin!_”

For a moment, silence gripped the place—horrible, expectant.

The king lifted his spear. Instantly, twenty thousand feet rose as one and slammed down on the earth. Three times. The ground shook. Then, from the edge of the circle, a lone voice began a mournful song:

“_What is the lot of man born of woman?_”

The answer echoed from every throat:

“_Death!_”

Gradually, company after company joined in. The song swelled—now love, now war, now a death dirge—ending in a single, heart-wrenching wail that rolled across the darkness like thunder.

Silence returned. Then the king raised his hand.

A pattering of feet. From the ranks, strange figures emerged—women, mostly old, their white hair adorned with fish bladders, faces painted in yellow and white stripes, snake skins trailing down their backs, human bones rattling at their waists. Each carried a forked wand. Ten of them. They halted before us.

One pointed at Gagool and cried:
“Mother, old mother—we are here.”

“_Good! Good! Good!_” Gagool croaked. “Are your eyes keen, _Isanusis_—seers in dark places?”

“Mother, they are keen.”

“_Good! Good! Good!_ Are your ears open, _Isanusis_, who hear words not spoken?”

“Mother, they are open.”

“_Good! Good! Good!_ Are your senses awake? Can you smell blood? Can you purge the land of those who plot against the king? Are you ready to do the justice of ‘Heaven above’—you whom I have taught, who have eaten my bread and drunk my magic?”

“Mother, we can.”

“Then go! Tarry not, ye vultures. The slayers”—she gestured to the executioners—“sharpen their spears. The white men from afar hunger to see. _Go!_”

With a wild yell, the witch-finders scattered like shards from a shell, bones rattling, racing through the crowd.

We watched the nearest one. She danced wildly, spinning faster and faster, screaming:
“I smell him—the evil-doer!”
“He is near—he who poisoned his mother!”
“I hear the thoughts of him who plots against the king!”

Her frenzy grew—foam flew from her jaws, her eyes bulged, her flesh quivered. Then she froze—like a hunting dog scenting prey. With her wand outstretched, she crept forward, low and slow.

The warriors before her stiffened. Their stoicism cracked. They recoiled.

She crept closer—then suddenly lunged, touching a tall warrior with her wand.

Instantly, two comrades seized him—one arm each—and dragged him toward the king.

He didn’t resist. But his limbs dragged as if paralyzed. His fingers, limp as a corpse’s, let the spear fall.

Two executioners stepped forward. They met. The king looked on.

“_Kill!_” he barked.

“_Kill!_” squealed Gagool.

“_Kill!_” echoed Scragga, chuckling.

Before the words faded, the deed was done. One drove a spear into the man’s heart. The other smashed his skull with a club.

“_One_,” counted Twala—like a madwoman tallying lives.

The body was dragged aside.

Another victim came—this one wearing a leopard-skin cloak, a man of rank. Same ritual. Same fate.

“_Two_.”

And so it went—until a hundred bodies lay in rows behind us.

I’ve heard of Roman gladiator games, Spanish bullfights—but none could match the horror of this witch-hunt. At least those spectacles entertained. This was pure terror. Even the most hardened seeker of thrills would flee if he knew he might be next.

Once, we tried to protest. Twala silenced us.

“Let the law run its course, white men. These dogs are wizards and evildoers. It is well they die.”

Around ten-thirty, the hunt paused. The witch-finders gathered, exhausted. We thought it was over.

But no. Gagool rose—leaning on a stick—and staggered into the open space. The sight was grotesque: a bent, ancient hag, slowly gathering strength, until she darted about like her own pupils. She chanted, weaving through the crowd—then suddenly lunged at a tall man in front of a regiment.

A groan rose from his unit.

Officers seized him. He was brought forward.

He was slain. Twala counted: “One hundred and three.”

Then Gagool danced again—closer and closer to us.

“By the gods,” Good muttered, “I think she’s coming for us.”

“Nonsense,” Sir Henry said.

But when I saw that fiend advancing, my heart sank. I glanced at the rows of corpses. I shivered.

Closer she came—waltzing like a twisted stick, her eyes glowing with unholy fire.

Closer. Closer.

The entire assembly watched, breathless.

Then she stopped. Pointed.

“Which one?” Sir Henry muttered.

In a flash, she rushed forward and touched Umbopa—Ignosi—on the shoulder.

“I smell him!” she shrieked. “Kill him! Kill him—he is full of evil! Slay the stranger before blood flows from him! Kill him, O king!”

A pause.

I seized the moment.

“O king!” I shouted, rising. “This man is the servant of your guests. He is our dog. Whoever sheds his blood sheds ours. By the sacred law of hospitality, I claim protection for him!”

“Gagool has marked him,” Twala growled. “He must die.”

“Nay,” I replied. “He shall not die. Any who touch him shall die instead.”

“Seize him!” Twala roared.

The executioners advanced—eyes red with blood.

They hesitated.

Ignosi gripped his spear, ready to fight.

“Stand back, dogs!” I yelled. “If you want to see tomorrow’s sun, stay back. Touch one hair of his head—and your king dies.”

I leveled my revolver at Twala. Sir Henry and Good drew theirs—Sir Henry aimed at the lead executioner, Good at Gag。

Twala flinched as my barrel lined up with his chest.

“Well,” I said, “what is it to be, Twala?”

He spoke.

“Put away your magic tubes,” he said. “You have invoked hospitality. For that reason—and not from fear—I spare him. Go in peace.”

“It is well,” I said calmly. “We are weary of slaughter. May we sleep? Is the dance ended?”

“It is ended,” Twala said sulkily. “Let these dead dogs”—he gestured to the corpses—“be thrown to the hyenas and vultures.”

He raised his spear.

The regiments began to file out in perfect silence. A small party remained to drag away the dead.

The witch-hunt was over.

For a long while—perhaps two hours—we sat in silence, overwhelmed by the horrors we had witnessed. Words failed us; even thought seemed too heavy to carry. The night was drawing near its end when, just as we began to consider retiring, we heard the sound of approaching footsteps. Then came the sharp challenge of a sentry posted at the kraal gate. Though no voice replied audibly, the steps continued forward, and in moments Infadoos entered the hut, followed by half a dozen imposing chiefs, their bearing proud and grave.

“My lords,” he said, “I have come as I promised. Here are the great men of our people—each commanding three thousand warriors who live only to obey the king’s will. I have told them what I have seen and heard. Now let them see the sacred snake upon Ignosi, and hear his tale, so that they may decide whether they will join him in battle against Twala, the false king.”

At once, Ignosi stripped off his girdle and revealed the intricate tattoo of the serpent coiled around his body. One by one, the chiefs stepped forward, peering closely at the mark under the dim glow of the lamp. Each examined it silently before stepping aside without a word.

Then Ignosi resumed his seat and repeated the story he had told earlier—the tale of his birthright, his exile, and the cruel reign of Twala.

“Now you have heard,” Infadoos said when he finished. “What say you, chiefs? Will you stand with this man and help him reclaim his father’s throne—or will you not? The land cries out under Twala’s rule. Blood flows like spring rain. You saw tonight—two other chiefs I meant to speak with—where are they now? The hyenas feast on their bones. Soon you too may be among them if you do not act. Choose, my brothers!”

The eldest chief, a short, broad-shouldered warrior with white hair, stepped forward.

“Your words are true, Infadoos. The land does cry out. My own brother died tonight. But this is no small matter. How can we know that if we raise our spears, we are not aiding a thief or a liar? This is a great thing—no one can foresee its end. For certain, blood will flow in rivers before this is done. Many still follow Twala, for men worship the sun that shines bright rather than the one yet unseen. These white men from the Stars possess great magic. If Ignosi is truly the rightful king, then let them give us a sign—one that all can see. Only then will the people believe, and stand with us.”

“I have the sign of the snake,” I said.

“No,” the chief replied firmly. “It is not enough. The mark could have been placed there since childhood. Show us a sign—and it shall suffice. But we will not move without a sign.”

The others nodded in agreement. I turned to Sir Henry and Good, perplexed.

“I think I’ve got it,” Good declared suddenly, eyes gleaming. “Give us a moment to think.”

I relayed the request, and the chiefs withdrew. As soon as they were gone, Good hurried to his medicine box, unlocked it, and pulled out a notebook. In the flyleaf was an almanac.

“Look here,” he said. “Isn’t tomorrow June 4th?”

We confirmed it—our days had been carefully tracked.

“Excellent,” he said. “Here it says: ‘June 4, total eclipse of the moon begins at 8:15 Greenwich time—visible in Teneriffe, South Africa, etc.’ There’s your sign. Tell them we’ll darken the moon tomorrow night.”

The idea struck us all as brilliant—though dangerous. The only flaw was the fear that Good’s almanac might be wrong. If we failed, our credibility would vanish, and so would Ignosi’s chance of the throne.

“What if the almanac is incorrect?” Sir Henry asked, watching Good calculate something on a blank page.

“I see no reason to doubt it,” Good answered. “Eclipses happen on schedule—always, in my experience. And it specifically states this one will be visible in South Africa. I’ve worked out the timing as best I can without knowing our exact position. It should begin here around ten o’clock tonight and last until past midnight. For an hour and a half, nearly total darkness.”

“Well,” Sir Henry said, “I suppose we must risk it.”

I hesitated. Eclipses were unpredictable—clouds could ruin everything. But we had no choice. I sent Umbopa to summon the chiefs back.

They returned, and I addressed them:

“Great men of the Kukuanas, and thou, Infadoos—listen well. We do not wish to reveal our powers, for such things disturb nature and bring fear. But this matter is too great. We are angered by the slaughter we saw, and by the treachery of Gagool, who sought to kill our friend Ignosi. So we break a rule and offer a sign that all may witness. Come here.” I led them to the door and pointed to the full moon glowing red above the horizon. “What do you see?”

“The sinking moon,” the spokesman replied.

“It is so. Now tell me—can any mortal extinguish that moon before its time? Can any man bring blackness over the land?”

The chief chuckled slightly. “No, my lord. No man can do such a thing. The moon is stronger than any who gaze upon her. She follows her course unshaken.”

“You say so. Yet I tell you—tomorrow night, about two hours before midnight, we will cause the moon to be devoured. For an hour and a half, darkness will fall across the earth. This shall be the sign that Ignosi is truly king of the Kukuanas. If we do this, will you believe?”

“Yes, my lords,” the old chief replied with a smile that spread across the faces of the others. “If you do this, we shall believe indeed.”

“It shall be done,” I said. “We three—Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn—have spoken, and it shall be done. Do you hear, Infadoos?”

“I hear, my lord. But it is a wondrous thing to promise—to extinguish the moon, mother of the world, when she is at her fullest.”

“Yet we shall do it,” I said.

“It is well,” Infadoos replied. “Twala will send for you two hours after sunset. You are to witness the girls’ dance. One hour after it begins, the girl he deems fairest will be killed by Scragga, his son, as a sacrifice to the Silent Ones who watch from those mountains yonder.” He gestured toward the distant peaks where Solomon’s Road was said to end. “Then, when the moon darkens, save the maiden’s life—and the people will believe.”

“Ay,” the old chief murmured, still smiling. “They will believe indeed.”

“Two miles from Loo,” Infadoos went on, “there is a hill shaped like a new moon—a stronghold. My regiment, and three others commanded by these chiefs, are stationed there. Tomorrow morning, we will plan to move more regiments into position. If you truly darken the moon, I will take you by the hand and lead you safely out of Loo to that hill, where you will be secure. From there, we can rise against Twala.”

“It is good,” I said. “Now go—let us rest and prepare our magic.”

Infadoos bowed and left with the chiefs.

As soon as they were gone, Ignosi spoke.

“My friends,” he said, “can you truly do this miracle? Or did you speak only empty words to the captains?”

“We believe we can, Umbopa—Ignosi, I mean.”

“It is strange,” he replied. “Had you not been Englishmen, I would not have believed. But I have learned that English gentlemen do not lie. If we survive this, I swear I will repay you.”

“Ignosi,” Sir Henry said, “promise me one thing.”

“I will promise, Incubu, before I even hear it,” Ignosi answered with a smile. “What is it?”

“This: if you become king, abolish the hunting of wizards as we saw last night. And no man shall die without trial or judgment.”

Ignosi paused, then said, “The ways of black men are not the ways of white men. We do not value life as you do. Yet I promise. If I can stop them, the witch-finders shall hunt no more. No man shall die without trial.”

“That’s a bargain,” Sir Henry said. “Now let us rest.”

Exhausted, we fell asleep quickly and slept until Ignosi woke us around eleven. We rose, washed, and ate a hearty breakfast. Then we walked outside, studying the structure of the huts and observing the women’s customs.

“I hope the eclipse comes through,” Sir Henry said.

“If it doesn’t,” I replied, “we’re done for. Some chief will tell Twala, and then there’ll be another kind of eclipse—one we won’t enjoy.”

After lunch, we spent the day receiving ceremonial visits and curious onlookers. At dusk, the sun set, and we passed a few quiet hours, though our minds were restless with dread. Around half-past eight, a messenger arrived from Twala, inviting us to the annual “dance of the girls.”

We hastily donned the chain shirts the king had given us, took our rifles and ammunition, and set off—brave outwardly, but trembling within. The space before the king’s kraal looked entirely different. Instead of armed warriors, rows of Kukuana girls danced in graceful procession. Barely dressed, each wore a flower crown, holding a palm leaf and a white arum lily. In the center sat Twala, with Gagool at his feet, flanked by Infadoos, Scragga, and twelve guards. A dozen chiefs were present, including several we recognized.

Twala greeted us warmly, though his single eye fixed coldly on Umbopa.

“Welcome, white men from the Stars,” he said. “This is a different sight from last night’s moonlight horror—but not as good. Girls are pleasant. Without them, none of us would be here today. But men are better. Kisses and soft words are sweet—but the clash of spears and the scent of blood are sweeter still! Would you take wives from our people? Choose the fairest, and they shall be yours—any number you desire.”

Good glanced at the girls, clearly tempted. But I cut in swiftly.

“Thank you, O king, but we white men wed only white women. Your maidens are fair—but not for us!”

Twala laughed. “A wise choice. Our proverb says, ‘Women’s eyes are always bright, whatever the color.’ Another says, ‘Love the woman who is near, for the one afar is false.’ But perhaps such truths do not hold in the Stars. In a land where men are white, all things are possible. Very well—your choice is made. Welcome again. And welcome, black one. Had Gagool prevailed, you’d be stiff and cold by now. Lucky for you—you too came from the Stars! Ha! ha!”

“I can kill you before you kill me, O king,” Ignosi said calmly. “And you shall be stiff before my limbs cease to bend.”

Twala started. “You speak boldly, boy. Do not push too far.”

“He speaks truth,” I said. “Truth is a sharp spear—once thrown, it never misses. It is a message from the Stars, O king.”

Twala scowled, his eye flashing, but said nothing.

“Let the dance begin!” he cried.

The girls surged forward, singing softly, waving palms and lilies. Under the moonlight, they moved with ethereal grace—whirling, meeting in mock combat, eddying in patterns both chaotic and beautiful. At last, one girl broke from the ranks and danced alone—her movements fluid and powerful, surpassing all others. When she finished, exhausted, another took her place. But none matched her beauty or skill.

When all had danced, Twala raised his hand.

“Which is the fairest, white men?” he asked.

“The first,” I said, without thinking.

Instantly I regretted it. Infadoos had warned us—the fairest girl would be sacrificed.

“Then my mind agrees with yours,” Twala said. “She is the fairest—and a sorry fate awaits her. She must die!”

“Ay, must die!” Gagool piped, her eyes glinting toward the girl, who stood unaware, ten yards away, nervously pulling petals from her wreath.

“Why, O king?” I asked, barely controlling my anger. “She danced well, pleased us, and is fair. To reward her with death is cruel.”

Twala laughed. “It is our custom. The figures of stone yonder”—he pointed to the peaks—“must have their due. If I fail to offer a fair girl, misfortune will fall upon me and my house. The prophecy says: ‘If the king offers not a sacrifice of a fair girl on the day of the dance, then he shall fall, and his house.’ See, white men—my brother refused, because of the woman’s tears. He fell. I reign in his place. It is finished—she must die!”

He turned to the guards. “Bring her here. Scragga, sharpen thy spear.”

Two guards stepped forward. The girl, now realizing her fate, screamed and tried to flee. But they seized her, dragging her weeping and struggling before us.

“What is thy name, girl?” Gagool croaked.

She remained silent.

“Shall the king’s son do his work at once?”

Scragga advanced, lifting his spear. I saw Good’s hand creep toward his revolver. The girl noticed the glint of steel through her tears and stopped struggling. She clasped her hands and shuddered violently.

“See,” Scragga crowed, “she fears my little plaything even before tasting it!” He tapped the blade.

“If I ever get the chance,” I heard Good mutter, “you’ll pay for that, young hound.”

“Now that thou art quiet, give us thy name, my dear,” Gagool mocked. “Come, speak—fear not.”

“Oh, mother,” the girl whispered, trembling, “my name is Foulata, of the house of Suko. Oh, mother, why must I die? I have done no wrong!”

“Be comforted,” Gagool said, mocking. “Thou must die—as a sacrifice to the Old Ones who sit yonder.” She pointed to the peaks. “But it is better to sleep in the night than toil in the day. Better to die than to live—and thou shalt die by the royal hand of the king’s son.”

Foulata wrung her hands. “Oh, cruel! I am so young! What have I done that I may never see the sun rise again, nor the stars follow in his wake? That I may never gather flowers in the dew, nor listen to the laughter of the streams? Woe is me—I shall never see my father’s hut again, nor feel my mother’s kiss, nor tend the sick lamb! Woe is me—that no lover shall embrace me, nor shall children be born of me! Oh, cruel, cruel!”

She threw herself forward and clutched Good’s legs.

“Oh, white father from the Stars!” she cried. “Shield me with your strength! Let me hide in your shadow! Save me from these cruel men and Gagool’s mercy!”

“All right, my hearty,” Good said in rough Saxon. “I’ll look after you. Come on, get up—there’s a good girl.” He stooped and took her hand.

Twala turned to his son. “Now’s your time,” Sir Henry whispered to me. “What are you waiting for?”

“I’m waiting for the eclipse,” I said. “I’ve watched the moon for half an hour—it looks healthier than ever.”

“Well, you must risk it now, or she dies. Twala is losing patience.”

I cast one final despairing glance at the moon—then saw it. On its edge, a faint shadow crept. Smoke-like darkness gathered. Relief flooded through me.

I stepped between the girl and Scragga’s spear.

“King,” I said, “it shall not be. We will not allow this. Let the girl go in safety.”

Twala rose, furious. A murmur rose from the crowd.

“_Shall not be!_” he roared. “Thou white dog, barking at the lion in his cave! _Shall not be!_ Art thou mad? Be careful, lest this chicken’s fate overtake thee and thine. Who art thou to stand between me and my will? Back! Scragga, kill her! Guards—seize them!”

Armed men rushed from behind the hut.

Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa stood beside me, rifles raised.

“Stop!” I shouted, heart pounding. “Stop! We, the white men from the Stars, say it shall not be. Take one step closer, and we will extinguish the moon like a wind-blown lamp—just as we who dwell in her House can do. Dare to disobey, and taste our magic!”

The men halted. Scragga froze.

“Hear him! Hear him!” Gagool shrieked. “The liar says he’ll put out the moon! Let him do it—and the girl lives. Let him fail—and he dies with her!”

I looked up. The shadow deepened. Joy surged through me.

I raised my hand toward the sky. Sir Henry and Good followed. I quoted lines from *Ingoldsby Legends* in solemn tones. Sir Henry recited a verse from the Old Testament, then added a Latin passage about Balbus building a wall. Good launched into a torrent of classical curses aimed at the Queen of Night.

Slowly, the penumbra crept across the moon. Gasps rose from the crowd.

“Look, O king!” I cried. “Look, Gagool! Look, chiefs and people—see if the white men from the Stars keep their word, or if they are liars!

“The moon grows black before your eyes! Darkness comes—yes, darkness in the hour of the full moon! Ye asked for a sign—it is given. Grow dark, O Moon! Withdraw thy light, thou pure and holy One! Bring the proud heart of the usurper low, and cover the world in shadows!”

A groan of terror burst from the onlookers. Some stood frozen. Others fell to their knees. Twala paled beneath his skin. Only Gagool remained defiant.

“It will pass!” she cried. “I’ve seen this before. No man can extinguish the moon. Do not lose heart—sit still. The shadow will fade.”

“Wait,” I said, hopping with excitement. “O Moon! Moon! Moon! Wherefore art thou so cold and fickle?” I quoted from a romance I’d recently read—though now I realize it was unkind to the Lady of the Heavens, who was proving herself our truest ally.

“Keep it up, Good!” I called. “I can’t remember any more poetry. Curse away—there’s a good fellow!”

Good responded magnificently. For ten minutes, he raged in multiple languages, never repeating himself. Never before had I known such depth of naval invective.

And as the darkness deepened, the heavens themselves seemed to side with us.

By fortune, Infadoos and the chiefs knew every path through the great town of Loo like the back of their hands. We slipped past its outer walls unseen, moving cautiously through shadowed alleys and hidden lanes. Though the night was thick with darkness, we pressed forward steadily, guided only by memory and instinct. For over an hour we traveled in silence, until at last the moon began to reappear—its edge, once swallowed by the eclipse, now slowly emerging from the void. Then, suddenly, a streak of silver light burst forth from it, followed by a deep, ruddy glow that hung like a glowing lantern against the black sky. It was a sight both strange and wondrous, stirring the soul with awe.

Five minutes later, the stars began to fade, and enough light returned for us to see our surroundings clearly. We realized then that we had escaped the city and were approaching a broad, flat-topped hill—some two miles around, rising no more than two hundred feet above the plain. Its shape was like a horseshoe, with steep, rocky slopes scattered with boulders. Atop this plateau lay a vast expanse of grassland, long used as a military encampment. Normally garrisoned by three thousand men, it now held several regiments, their tents and fires dotting the land like a constellation of war.

As we climbed the slope under the returning moonlight, we saw hundreds of soldiers stirred from sleep, huddled together in fear, trembling at the unnatural event they had witnessed—the darkening of the moon. We passed them without word or glance, making our way to a hut near the center of the camp. There, to our astonishment, stood two men waiting—our belongings piled beside them, including Good’s long-lost trousers.

“I sent for them,” Infadoos said simply. “And also for these.” He lifted up the trousers with a smile.

Good let out a cry of joy and rushed forward, pulling them on immediately. “Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs!” Infadoos lamented, half-joking.

But Good refused to be swayed. Once again, the Kukuana people caught a glimpse of those famed limbs—but only once. From then on, they would have to content themselves with the sight of his single whisker, his clear eye, and his famously mobile teeth. Good, modest to the core, never allowed such exposure again.

Still admiring the trousers, Infadoos told us he had ordered the regiments to assemble at dawn. He wished to explain the rebellion, reveal the true heir to the throne—Ignosi—and rally the troops to his cause. As the sun rose, some twenty thousand men—nearly the entire strength of the Kukuana army—gathered in a wide open field. They formed a dense square, three sides filled with armed warriors, the fourth left open for the leaders.

We took our place there, soon surrounded by chiefs and officers. Silence fell, and Infadoos stepped forward. With eloquence and fire, he spoke of Ignosi’s father—how Twala, the tyrant king, had murdered him, driven his wife and child into exile to die. He described the suffering under Twala’s rule, recalling the horrors of the previous night when innocent nobles were dragged from their homes and slain under false accusations.

Then he told how the white lords from the Stars—us—had seen their plight. We had come not by chance, but to restore justice. We had rescued Ignosi, led him across the mountains, and even brought down the moon itself to prove our power. We had destroyed Scragga, Twala’s monstrous son, and saved Foulata. Now, we stood ready to help overthrow tyranny and place the rightful king upon the throne.

His words sparked murmurs of approval. Then Ignosi stepped forward.

He repeated Infadoos’s tale, then added his own call to arms:
“O chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people—you have heard my words. Now choose between me and the man who sits on my throne: the uncle who killed his brother, who chased his nephew into the cold and night. I am the true king. These chiefs know it—they have seen the snake about my middle. If I were not the king, would these white men stand with me, wielding their magic? Look! The darkness they summoned still lingers—even in full moonlight! Is it not proof?”

“It is!” came the reply from the ranks.

“I am the king,” Ignosi declared, raising his battle-axe high. “If any man here denies it, let him step forward. I will fight him now, and his blood shall mark my truth.”

No one moved.

“Then hear this,” he continued. “If you stand with me in battle, and I win, you shall share victory and honor. I will give you oxen, wives, and rank beyond your dreams. If you fall, I will fall with you. And I swear this: when I sit upon my father’s seat, bloodshed shall end. No more shall justice bring death without cause. No more shall witch-finders hunt you down. No man shall die unless he breaks the law. Your kraals shall be safe. You shall sleep in peace. Justice shall walk blindfolded throughout the land. Have you chosen?”

“We have chosen, O king!” thundered the response.

“Then turn and see,” Ignosi said. “Twala’s messengers are already spreading out—east, west, north, south—to gather his armies. Tomorrow—or the next day—he will come with all who follow him. Then I shall see who is truly mine—those who do not fear death for their cause. Such men shall not be forgotten when the spoils are divided. I have spoken. Go now, prepare for war.”

A pause. Then one chief raised his hand. The crowd roared the royal salute: _“Koom!”_

It was the sign of acceptance. The soldiers marched away in disciplined columns.

Half an hour later, we convened a council of war. All regimental commanders were present. It was clear: we would be attacked soon. From our vantage point, we could see troops gathering in Loo, runners speeding outward to summon reinforcements.

We had twenty thousand men—seven of the finest regiments. Twala, we estimated, had thirty to thirty-five thousand in Loo, and could add five thousand more by midday. Desertions might occur, but we couldn’t rely on them. Already, patrols circled the base of the hill. Signs of attack were everywhere.

Infadoos believed no assault would come that day—only preparation. But tomorrow, they said, the storm would break.

So we set to work. Every man was mobilized. Paths up the hill were blocked with stones. Boulder traps were placed along likely routes. Regiments were assigned positions. We fortified what we could, using every ounce of ingenuity.

Just before sunset, a small group approached from Loo—one bearing a palm leaf, a symbol of truce. Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and we descended to meet him.

The herald wore the leopard-skin cloak of a nobleman. “Greeting!” he called. “The king’s greeting to those who wage unholy war against the king. The lion’s greeting to the jackals who snarl at his heels!”

“Speak,” I said.

“The king commands: surrender to his mercy before worse befalls. Already, the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the king drives him bleeding through the camp.”

“What terms does Twala offer?” I asked.

“Merciful ones—worthy of a great king. This is Twala, the one-eyed, mighty, husband of a thousand wives, keeper of Solomon’s Road, beloved of the Silent Ones, Calf of the Black Cow, Elephant whose tread shakes the earth, Terror of the evil-doer, Ostrich whose feet devour the desert—this is Twala’s word: ‘I will spare most. One in ten shall die. The rest go free. But the white man Incubu, who slew my son Scragga; the black man who claims my throne; and Infadoos, my brother, who stirs rebellion—these shall die by torture, offered to the Silent Ones.’ Such is Twala’s mercy.”

After brief counsel, I replied aloud, so all could hear:

“Go back, dog, to Twala. Tell him this: We, Ignosi, true king of the Kukuanas; Incubu, Bougwan, Macumazahn, the wise ones from the Stars who darken the moon; Infadoos of the royal line; and all gathered here—answer: We will not surrender. Before the sun sets twice, Twala’s corpse shall lie stiff at his own gate, and Ignosi, whose father he slew, shall reign in his place. Now go—before we send you back in pieces. Beware how you raise a hand against us.”

The herald laughed. “You frighten no men with such bold words,” he shouted. “Show your courage tomorrow, O you who darken the moon. Be brave, fight, and be merry—before the crows pick your bones whiter than your faces. Farewell. Perhaps we shall meet in battle. Do not flee to the Stars. Wait for me, I pray, white men.”

With that, he turned and vanished. The sun sank below the horizon.

That night, though weary, we worked by moonlight—finishing preparations, sending messengers, holding councils. By midnight, all was done. The camp fell silent, save for the occasional challenge of a sentry.

Sir Henry and I, with Ignosi and a chief, walked the picket lines. As we moved, spears flashed in the moonlight from hidden posts—then disappeared when we gave the password. No soldier slept at his post.

Returning, we passed thousands of sleeping warriors. In the pale light, their forms looked ghostly—arms outstretched, limbs twisted. Their plumes fluttered in the chill wind, their spears gleaming like bones. It was a haunting scene.

“How many of these,” Sir Henry asked, “do you think will live to see this time tomorrow?”

I shook my head. To my tired eyes, Death seemed already among them. I saw which men would fall, felt the weight of fate pressing down. A great sorrow washed over me—the futility of life, the suddenness of death.

Tonight, thousands slept soundly. Tomorrow, many would be dead. Wives would become widows, children fatherless. Their names would vanish. Only the moon would rise, the wind blow, the earth rest—unchanged, eternal.

Yet man does not truly die. His breath stirs the pines. His words echo. His thoughts live on in ours. His passions drive us. His joys and sorrows are ours. Even the end he feared—we too must face.

The universe is full of ghosts—not spirits in sheets, but the living essence of every soul, once born, forever changed, never gone.

Such thoughts filled my mind—as I grow older, I regret to say, I find myself thinking more often.

“Curtis,” I said, “I’m in a state of pitiable fear.”

Sir Henry stroked his beard and laughed. “I’ve heard you say that before, Quatermain.”

“But I mean it now. I doubt if any of us will survive tomorrow night. We’ll be outnumbered. Holding this place is a gamble.”

“We’ll make a good account of ourselves,” he said. “Look, Quatermain—this is ugly business. We shouldn’t be involved. But we are. So we must do our best. Personally, I’d rather die fighting than any other way. And now that we’re unlikely to find my brother, it makes it easier. Fortune favors the brave. We may win. Either way, the battle will be terrible. And since I have a reputation to keep, I’ll be right in the thick of it.”

He said it mournfully, yet his eyes sparkled with something fiercer. I suspect Sir Henry Curtis actually enjoys battle.

We slept a few hours. At dawn, Infadoos woke us—great activity in Loo. Skirmishers were driving in our outposts.

We dressed for war. Each donned chain armor—grateful for it. Sir Henry went further. “When in Kukuanaland, do as the Kukuanas do,” he said, slipping on the steel shirt like a glove.

Then came the full war gear: leopard-skin cloak, black ostrich-feather plume, white ox-tail moocha, sandals, goat-hair leglet, rhino-horn axe, iron shield, throwing-knives—all native accoutrements. Yet he added his revolver.

The look was fierce, savage—but I’ve rarely seen a finer sight. His physique, enhanced by the costume, was magnificent. When Ignosi arrived, similarly clad, I thought: never have I seen two more splendid men.

Good and I fared less well. Good insisted on keeping his trousers—so a stout gentleman with an eyeglass, half-shaved face, and mail shirt tucked into corduroys looked more absurd than fearsome.

I, too, struggled. My chain shirt was too large, so I wore it over everything—making me look bulky. I discarded trousers, wearing only veldtschoons. I decided bare legs would make me lighter, should we need to retreat fast.

My gear: mail coat, spear (which I didn’t know how to use), shield, two tollas, revolver, and a huge plume pinned to my hat for a “bloodthirsty finish.” Plus rifles—carried by bearers, since ammunition was scarce and useless in close combat.

At last equipped, we ate hastily and headed to the battlefield.

On the plateau stood a small brown koppie—used as headquarters and lookout. There, Infadoos waited, surrounded by his regiment—the Greys, the finest in the army. Three thousand five hundred strong, they were held in reserve, lying in companies, watching the enemy emerge from Loo.

Three long columns crawled out—each, we judged, eleven or twelve thousand strong. Once clear, they formed ranks. One marched right, one left, the third advanced slowly toward us.

“Ah,” Infadoos said, “they attack on three sides at once.”

That was serious. Our position, a mile and a half wide, was stretched thin. We couldn’t concentrate forces fully—but we had no choice. Orders were sent: prepare for separate assaults.

The battle loomed.

The three enemy columns advanced slowly, without haste or panic, like shadows creeping across the plain. When they were about five hundred yards away, the central force halted at the base of a narrow strip of open ground that stretched up into the hillside—a natural corridor leading toward our position. This was no accident. The pause allowed the flanking divisions to circle around us, forming a trap. Our own line curved like a horseshoe, its two ends pointing toward the town of Loo, and now the enemy intended to strike from all sides at once.

“Oh, for a Gatling!” groaned Good, his eyes fixed on the dense ranks below. “I’d clear that field in twenty minutes.”

“We don’t have one,” Sir Henry replied calmly, “so let’s not waste time wishing. But how about you, Quatermain? Try a shot—see if you can hit that tall fellow who seems to be in command. Two to one you miss, and I’ll pay you an even sovereign—honestly—if we ever get out of this alive.”

That stung me. I loaded my express rifle with a solid ball, waited until the officer stepped forward ten yards from his men—just far enough to get a better view, accompanied only by a single orderly—and then lay down, resting the rifle on a rock. The weapon was sighted for only three hundred and fifty yards, so I aimed halfway down the man’s neck, calculating the bullet would strike him in the chest. He stood perfectly still, giving me every chance. But something—excitement, wind, distance—I don’t know what—made the shot go wrong. I thought I had him dead center. Instead, when the smoke cleared, he stood unharmed, while his orderly lay sprawled three paces to the left, motionless.

The officer turned sharply and bolted back toward his troops, clearly terrified.

“Bravo, Quatermain!” shouted Good. “You scared him!”

It made me furious. There’s nothing worse than missing in public when your reputation is on the line. And I’m not proud of what happened next. Without thinking, I raised the rifle again, tracked the fleeing figure, and fired the second barrel.

He went down instantly—arms thrown upward, face smacking the earth. This time, there was no mistake. And though I should have been ashamed, I confess I felt a strange satisfaction. Not because I killed him—but because I hadn’t failed again.

The soldiers below erupted into cheers. They saw it as a sign—the magic of the white man working. The general’s own regiment broke ranks and fell back in confusion.

Sir Henry and Good picked up their rifles and began firing. Good worked his Winchester repeater with fierce efficiency, sending bullets into the mass before him. I took a few more shots, and together we managed to bring down six or eight men before they closed the distance.

Then came the sound we dreaded—an ominous roar from our right, followed by another from the left. The other divisions had joined the attack.

At the noise, the main force surged forward, advancing up the grassy ridge in slow trot, chanting a deep, rhythmic war song. We kept up a steady fire—Ignosi joining in whenever he could—but it was like throwing stones at a tidal wave. Men fell, but the tide kept coming.

They reached the foot of the hill, driving through our pickets placed among the rocks. Then they slowed—climbing uphill required effort, and they conserved breath. Our first line held at mid-slope, the second fifty yards behind, the third at the edge of the plateau.

Now they charged, shouting:
“*Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!*”
(Twala! Twala! Smite! Smite!)

Our men answered:
“*Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!*”

They were close now. *Tollas*—throwing knives—flew back and forth like deadly rain. Then, with a deafening cry, the battle met.

A sea of bodies surged and twisted. Warriors fell like leaves in autumn. But soon the weight of numbers told. Our first line bent backward, merging into the second. That line held fiercely, but it too gave way, driven up the slope. Within twenty minutes, the third line was engaged.

By then, the attackers were tired, bloodied, and thinning. Breaking through that final wall of spears proved impossible.

For a time, the lines swayed like waves—neither side gaining ground. Sir Henry watched, eyes blazing, then without a word dashed into the thick of it, followed by Good. I stayed where I was.

The enemy soldiers caught sight of him—a tall figure plunging into chaos—and roared:

“*Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!*”
(Here is the Elephant!)
“*Chiele! Chiele!*”

From that moment, the tide turned. Inch by inch, the attackers were pushed back down the hill. By the time they retreated to their reserves, they were in disarray.

Just then, a messenger arrived: the left flank had been beaten back.

I started to breathe easier—until I saw our right-wing defenders being driven across the plain, chased by swarms of enemy soldiers. They’d broken through there.

Ignosi, standing beside me, read the situation instantly. He barked an order. The reserve regiment—the Greys—spread out in formation. Another command echoed down the line. Before I knew it, I was swept into a charge.

I ducked behind Ignosi’s broad back and stumbled forward, doing my best to look like I belonged. We plunged through scattered groups of our own men, who reformed behind us.

Then—chaos.

All I remember is the thunder of shields colliding, and suddenly a monstrous figure lunging at me, eyes bulging, spear glistening with blood.

But I didn’t falter. I threw myself flat just as he reached me. He couldn’t stop—he crashed over me, headfirst into the dirt. Before he could rise, I rose—and shot him from behind with my revolver.

Moments later, someone knocked me down. I blacked out.

When I woke, I was back at the koppie. Good knelt beside me, holding water in a gourd.

“How do you feel?” he asked, voice tight with concern.

I sat up, shook myself. “Pretty well. Thank you.”

“Thank God,” he whispered. “I thought you were gone when they carried you in.”

“I’m not done yet,” I said. “Just got a blow to the head. How did it go?”

“They’re repulsed on all sides—for now. Losses are terrible. We’ve lost two thousand. They must have lost three times that.” He pointed across the plain. “Look at that.”

Lines of men marched in fours. In the center of each group, borne on a hide tray with corner loops, lay wounded soldiers. Medicine men examined them quickly. If the wound wasn’t fatal, the man was taken away. If it was hopeless—then came the knife.

One doctor would pretend to examine, then swiftly slit an artery. The man died quietly, painlessly. It was done often—especially when the wound was in the torso. The broad Kukuana spears left wounds that couldn’t heal. Most victims were already unconscious. Others didn’t seem to notice the cut.

It was horrifying. I’ve never seen anything more chilling than watching those brave men put to rest by their own healers—except once, when I saw Swazis bury their wounded alive after a battle.

We turned away from the scene and walked to the other side of the koppie. Sir Henry stood there, axe still in hand, talking with Ignosi, Infadoos, and several chiefs.

“Thank God, here you are, Quatermain!” Sir Henry said. “I can’t make out what Ignosi wants. We beat off the attack, but Twala’s bringing in reinforcements. He’s planning to surround us—starve us out.”

“That’s bad,” I said.

“Yes. Especially since Infadoos says the spring has run dry.”

“It is true, my lord,” Infadoos confirmed. “The water fails. Before nightfall, we’ll all be thirsty. Listen, Macumazahn. You are wise. You’ve seen many wars. Tell us—what shall we do? Twala has brought fresh men to replace the fallen. But he has learned. The hawk did not expect the heron to be ready. But our beak pierced his breast. He fears to strike again. He will wait. He will coil around us like a snake around a buck. He will fight the fight of ‘sit down.’”

“I hear you,” I said.

“So, Macumazahn,” Infadoos continued, “we have no water. Little food. We must choose: starve like a lion in his den, try to break northward, or—” He gestured toward the enemy mass—“charge straight into Twala’s heart. Incubu, the great warrior—today he fought like a buffalo in a net. His axe sent Twala’s men down like corn before hail. Incubu says ‘Charge.’ But the Elephant always charges. Now—what says Macumazahn, the wily old fox who loves to bite from behind? The last word is Ignosi’s—king’s right to command war. But let us hear you, O Macumazahn, who watches by night. And him—the one with the transparent eye.”

“What say you, Ignosi?” I asked.

“Nay, my father,” Ignosi replied, dressed now in full war regalia, looking every inch a king. “Speak. Let me, who am but a child in wisdom beside you, listen.”

After consulting briefly with Good and Sir Henry, I gave my opinion: trapped, with failing water, our best hope was to attack—not wait. We must strike now—before wounds stiffened, before fear took hold. Otherwise, some captains might betray us to Twala.

My words were received with respect—something rare among these people. But the decision rested with Ignosi.

After a long silence, he spoke.

“Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—brave white men, my friends. Infadoos, my uncle, chiefs—my heart is set. Today I strike at Twala. I stake my life, and yours, on this blow. Listen—this is how I will strike.”

He gestured at the curve of the hill and the tongue of green land running toward us.

“You see how the hill bends like a half-moon, and the plain stretches like a green tongue within it?”

“We see,” I said.

“Good. It is midday. The men eat and rest. When the sun turns westward, let your regiment, my uncle, advance with one other down the green tongue. Twala will see it and hurl his forces to crush it. But the space is narrow—only one regiment can attack at a time. So they will be destroyed one by one. All eyes will be fixed on this battle—like none ever seen.”

“And with you, my uncle,” Ignosi added, “will go Incubu, so that when Twala sees his axe flashing in the front rank of the Greys, his heart may falter.”

“And I,” Ignosi continued, “will follow with the second regiment—so that if you fall, a king remains. With me will go Macumazahn, the wise.”

“It is well, O king,” Infadoos said, calm as stone—though he was leading his men into certain death. These people feared no end when duty called.

“And while Twala’s army watches that fight,” Ignosi said, “one-third of our remaining men—about six thousand—will creep along the right horn of the hill and fall upon Twala’s left flank. Another third will move along the left horn and strike his right flank. When I see both horns ready to toss Twala, then I will charge with the rest—straight into his face. If fortune favors us, the day is ours. Before Night drives her black oxen from mountain to mountain, we shall sit in peace at Loo.”

He paused. “Now, let us eat and prepare. Infadoos, see that the plan is carried out. And stay—let my white father Bougwan go with the right horn. His shining eye will give courage to the captains.”

The plan was set in motion swiftly. Within an hour, rations were eaten, units formed, orders given. Eighteen thousand men stood ready—except for a guard left with the wounded.

Then Good approached Sir Henry and me.

“Good-bye, you fellows,” he said. “I’m off with the right wing. Came to shake hands—just in case we don’t meet again.”

We shook hands in silence. No words needed. There was emotion—Anglo-Saxon restraint barely holding it back.

“It’s a queer business,” Sir Henry said, voice trembling slightly. “I confess I don’t expect to see tomorrow’s sun. From what I understand, the Greys—where I’m going—are to fight until wiped out, so the wings can slip around unnoticed. Well—so be it. At least it will be a man’s death. Good-bye, old friend. God bless you. I hope you live to claim the diamonds. But if you do—take my advice: never deal with Pretenders again.”

In a flash, Good gripped our hands, then vanished into the ranks.

Infadoos led Sir Henry forward—into the front of the Greys. And with heavy heart, I followed Ignosi to my place in the second attacking regiment.

Minutes passed, and the regiments assigned to flank the enemy had vanished into the shadows, moving silently along the rise of ground to avoid detection by Twala’s sharp-eyed scouts. The air was still, save for the occasional whisper of wind through the grass.

For over half an hour, no movement came from the Greys or their supporting regiment—the Buffaloes—whose role was to form the core of the army and endure the fiercest blows of battle. Both units were nearly at full strength, untouched by the earlier fighting. The Greys had been held in reserve during the morning’s clash, suffering only minor losses when they repelled a breakthrough attempt—an attack I had joined, only to be knocked unconscious for my efforts. The Buffaloes, stationed as the third line on the left, had barely seen action, since the enemy there had failed to break through the second line.

Infadoos, a seasoned commander whose wisdom was matched only by his caution, used this pause to address his men—the Greys—in stirring words. He spoke not of fear, but of honor: the privilege of leading the charge, the glory of standing beside the great white warrior from the stars, and the promise of rich rewards—cattle, rank, and lasting fame—for those who lived through the coming fight.

I looked down upon the long lines of warriors, their black plumes swaying like waves under a stormy sky, their faces grim and set. A deep sorrow filled me. In less than an hour, most—if not all—of these proud veterans, every one of them well past forty, would lie dead or dying in the dust. They were being sacrificed, deliberately, with the cold calculation of a master strategist who knows that sometimes the only way to win is to lose. Yet they knew it. And they accepted it without hesitation. Their duty was clear, their purpose absolute. Even now, facing certain death, not one showed fear. I stood among them, my own heart pounding, and could not help envying their calm courage. Never before had I seen such total devotion to duty, such utter disregard for the cost.

“Behold your king!” Infadoos declared, pointing toward Ignosi. “Go forth and die for him, as brave men do. May the name of any man who turns from death for his king be cursed forever. Behold your king—chiefs, captains, soldiers! Pay homage to the sacred Snake, and then follow, so that Incubu and I may show you a path into the heart of Twala’s host!”

A hush fell. Then, slowly, a murmur rose—a soft, rhythmic tapping, like distant thunder, as six thousand spears struck against shields. It grew louder, swelling into a roar that shook the mountains, echoing across the valley. Then, as suddenly as it began, it faded—until the royal salute burst forth.

Ignosi must have felt the weight of that moment. No Roman emperor, I thought, had ever received such tribute from men who were about to die.

He raised his battle-axe in acknowledgment, and the Greys moved forward in tight triple lines—three thousand strong, each line a thousand men deep. As they advanced, I saw the last companies reach the edge of the plateau. Then Ignosi took command of the Buffaloes, forming them into a similar threefold array, and gave the order to march. I followed, praying desperately that I might survive the ordeal. I’d faced danger before—but never anything so bleak, so hopeless.

By the time we reached the plateau’s edge, the Greys were halfway down the slope, descending onto a narrow strip of grassland that stretched like a tongue into the mountain’s curve. On the plain beyond, Twala’s camp was in chaos. Regiment after regiment surged forward in long, galloping strides, eager to block our advance before we could reach the open ground of Loo.

That tongue of land was scarcely four hundred yards deep at its widest point, narrowing to just ninety paces at the tip. The Greys, having descended in column, reformed into their triple-line formation at the broader end—and halted.

Then we—the Buffaloes—moved forward, taking position about a hundred yards behind the final line of the Greys, slightly higher up. From there, we watched Twala’s army approach. Though they had suffered losses, they numbered at least forty thousand, now pressing forward with fierce urgency. But as they neared the root of the tongue, they hesitated. Only one regiment could enter the gorge at a time. And there, seventy yards inside, guarded by towering boulder-strewn walls on either side, stood the Greys—unassailable except from the front, immovable as a fortress.

They stopped. No one rushed forward. There was no eagerness to meet such a disciplined, unyielding force.

Then, a tall figure emerged—plumed headdress nodding, surrounded by chiefs and attendants. I suspected it was Twala himself. He gave a command. The first regiment charged, screaming into the silence.

The Greys remained motionless until the enemy was within forty yards. Then, with a sudden roar, they lunged forward—spears aloft, shields clashing. The impact echoed like thunder. The two forces collided in a furious tide of steel and flesh. Spears flashed, blood flew, and the air rang with shouts and groans.

But the fight lasted only moments. The attacking line thinned rapidly. Then, like a wave breaking over a reef, the Greys surged forward, sweeping through the enemy ranks. The regiment was destroyed. But the Greys had lost a third of their number. Two lines remained.

They closed ranks, silent once more, waiting. And I caught sight of Sir Henry—his yellow beard visible as he moved between the rows, adjusting formations. He was alive.

We advanced to the battlefield, now littered with thousands of bodies—dead, dying, wounded—soaked in crimson. Ignosi issued an order: no enemy wounded were to be killed. We saw it carried out. It was a horror too terrible to dwell upon.

Now, a second regiment—white-plumed, white-kilted—charged the remaining two thousand Greys. Again, silence. Again, the same distance. Again, the sudden eruption of fury.

Shields met with a thunderous crash. The battle raged. This time, however, the outcome was uncertain. The newcomers were young, fierce, and fought with reckless energy. For a time, they seemed to push the veterans back. Hundreds fell each minute. The cry of “_S’gee, s’gee_”—the victor’s triumph—rose above the din.

Yet discipline held. Experience prevailed. One veteran was worth two youths. Just as we feared the Greys would fall, I heard Sir Henry’s voice cut through the noise. His battle-axe swept high, and the tide turned. The Greys stopped retreating. They stood firm. Like rocks, they weathered the storm. Wave after wave crashed against them, only to recoil.

Then they advanced—slowly, inexorably. The enemy broke. White plumes streamed in the wind as survivors fled. The Greys had won. But at what cost?

Of the three thousand who had marched out, fewer than six hundred remained. Bloodied, exhausted, yet still triumphant, they cheered, waved their spears, and ran forward—pursuing the fleeing foe for a hundred yards. Then they seized a knoll, formed a triple ring around its base, and held fast.

And there, atop the mound, I saw Sir Henry—unharmed—alongside Infadoos. Then Twala’s regiments rolled forward again, and the battle resumed.

At that moment, something strange happened inside me. I, who had always avoided conflict, who had fled from danger whenever possible, felt my chest burn with a fierce, unexpected courage. Warlike verses from ancient tales, bloody passages from scripture, flooded my mind. My blood, once frozen with dread, now raced through my veins. I longed to kill—not out of hatred, but out of some primal urge to stand with these warriors.

I glanced at the ranks behind us—faces taut, hands twitching, eyes blazing with battle-lust. And I wondered: did I look like them? Did I belong here?

Only Ignosi seemed unmoved—calm beneath his leopard-skin cloak, though even he clenched his teeth.

“I cannot wait,” I said. “Are we to stand here while Twala destroys our brothers?”

“Nay, Macumazahn,” Ignosi replied. “Now is the moment. Let us seize it.”

As he spoke, another regiment rushed past the ring on the mound and attacked from the near side.

Ignosi raised his axe. The signal was given. With a wild cry, the Buffaloes charged—like a tidal wave.

What followed, I can barely remember. A blur of motion, a roar of voices, flashes of steel through a red haze. When I regained my senses, I was standing near the top of the mound, close to Sir Henry himself. How I got there, I didn’t know. Later, he told me I’d been carried forward by the charge, then left behind as the Buffaloes were pushed back. He pulled me into safety.

The fighting continued. Again and again, the enemy surged forward, only to be driven back. The Greys stood firm—each man stepping where his comrade had fallen. Bodies piled up. The living stepped over the dead.

Infadoos remained cool, shouting orders, taunts, even jokes—anything to keep morale high. He moved into the thickest fights, bearing his share of the burden.

Sir Henry, his ostrich plumes gone, his hair streaming in the wind, fought like a berserker. His axe, his hands, his armor—all drenched in blood. He roared “_O-hoy! O-hoy!_” with each blow, shattering shields and skulls alike. None dared face him.

Then—suddenly—a cry: “_Twala, y’ Twala!_”

Out of the crowd stepped the king himself—one eye, armored, armed with axe and shield. He stared at Sir Henry.

“Where art thou, Incubu, the white man who slew my son Scragga?” he shouted. “Come, see if thou canst slay me!”

He hurled a _tolla_. Sir Henry caught it on his shield. It pierced the iron plate and stuck fast.

Then Twala charged—blow landing hard on the shield. The shock knocked Sir Henry to his knees.

But the fight ended abruptly. From the surrounding ranks, a cry of alarm rose. I looked up—and saw it.

On both flanks, the plain erupted with charging warriors. The flanking squadrons had arrived.

It was perfect timing. Twala’s army had focused entirely on the battle at the mound. They hadn’t expected the horns to strike. Believing the flanking forces hidden on the hilltop, they were unprepared.

In minutes, the battle was decided. Caught on both sides, shattered by the slaughter, Twala’s troops broke and fled. The plain became a river of running men. The army that had surrounded us melted away like mist.

We stood alone—like a rock after the tide receded.

But what a scene! Around us lay heaps of the dead and dying. Of the once-proud Greys, only ninety-five remained on their feet. Over three thousand four hundred had fallen.

Infadoos surveyed the ruins, binding a wound on his arm. “Men,” he said quietly, “you have upheld the honor of your regiment. This day will be spoken of by your children’s children.” Then he turned to Sir Henry. “Thou art a great captain, Incubu. I have known many brave men in my life—but none like thee.”

The Buffaloes began marching toward Loo. A message came from Ignosi—requesting Infadoos, Sir Henry, and myself to join him.

We left the Greys to tend the wounded, and followed.

Soon, we spotted Good sitting on an ant-heap, a hundred paces ahead. Beside him lay a Kukuana warrior—apparently dead.

“He’s wounded,” Sir Henry said.

Then, the body stirred. The Kukuana sprang up, knocked Good off the heap, and began stabbing at him. We rushed forward, horrified. The warrior delivered one final, vicious thrust—and shouted, “Take that, wizard!” before fleeing.

Good lay still. We approached, fearing the worst. But he was pale, faint—but alive, with a calm smile on his face.

“Capital armour this,” he murmured. “How sold that beggar must have been.” Then he fainted.

We found he’d been hit in the leg by a _tolla_, but the chain mail had saved him. A miracle.

He was placed on a wicker stretcher and carried with us.

At the gates of Loo, we found a regiment guarding the entrance. The officer saluted Ignosi as king and reported that Twala’s army had retreated into the town—Twala himself among them—but that they were broken and likely to surrender.

Ignosi consulted with us, then sent heralds to each gate. They offered safe passage and mercy to any soldier who laid down arms. If not, by nightfall, the town would burn.

Half an hour later, amid cheers, the bridge dropped across the fosse. The gates swung open.

We entered cautiously. Thousands of defeated warriors lined the streets, heads bowed, weapons at their feet. Officers led them in silent submission as Ignosi passed.

We marched straight to Twala’s kraal. When we reached the open space where we’d once seen the review and witch hunt, it was empty—except for Twala himself, seated before his hut, with only Gagool beside him.

It was a tragic sight. The mighty king, stripped of power, alone with a crone. No army, no courtiers, no wives—only silence and shame. Compassion stirred in me, despite his crimes. He was learning the cruel truth: that when you fall, no one remembers your greatness. You are forgotten. And in this case, he deserved no mercy.

We filed through the gate, crossed the courtyard, and approached him. At fifty yards, the regiment halted. Ignosi, with a small guard, advanced.

Gagool spat curses as we drew near.

Twala lifted his head for the first time. His single eye, blazing with rage, fixed on Ignosi.

“Hail, O king,” he said, voice laced with scorn. “Thou who ate my bread, and now, with the aid of the white man’s magic, hast seduced my armies and defeated my host. Hail! What fate dost thou prepare for me?”

“The same fate thou gavest to my father,” Ignosi replied, cold and firm. “The throne thou hast sat upon for so long.”

After the battle ended, Sir Henry and Good were carried into Twala’s hut, where I joined them. Both were utterly drained—exhausted from battle and blood loss—and my own state was little better. Though wiry and accustomed to hardship, I had reached the edge of endurance. The old wound from the lion attack throbbed anew, and a sharp headache from the blow I’d taken earlier in the day made every moment agony. We were three broken men, battered by war and fate. Yet even in our misery, we found solace in knowing we still lived when so many others did not.

With help from Foulata—the beautiful Kukuana girl whose life we had saved—she now served us as devoted handmaiden, especially tending to Good—we managed to remove our chain shirts. They had saved our lives that day, but beneath them lay deep bruises. The steel had blocked blades, but not impacts. Sir Henry and Good bore swollen, tender flesh; I too was marked with pain. Foulata brought us crushed green leaves, fragrant and soothing, which she applied as poultices. The relief was real, though temporary.

But worse than the bruises were the wounds. Good had a clean hole through his thigh—a gash that had bled heavily. Sir Henry bore a deep cut across his jaw, delivered by Twala’s axe. Fortunately, Good was no stranger to medicine. Once he retrieved his small kit, he cleaned both injuries under the dim glow of a primitive lamp, then stitched them carefully. He coated the wounds with antiseptic ointment and wrapped them in what remained of a handkerchief. Then came broth—strong and nourishing—prepared by Foulata. Too weak to eat properly, we drank it down and collapsed onto the thick fur rugs scattered across the hut floor.

And there, on Twala’s own couch, wrapped in his royal karross, Sir Henry slept—the man who had slain the king. But sleep was elusive. The air rang with sorrow: women wailed from every direction, mourning husbands, sons, brothers lost in the slaughter. Over twelve thousand had fallen—nearly one-fifth of the Kukuana army. Their cries were unbearable, a constant reminder of the cost of ambition and power.

By midnight, the wailing lessened. Only one sound remained: a long, mournful howl from a nearby hut. It came from Gagool, keening over her dead king. That sound haunted me, pulling me back into visions of the fight—of the warrior charging at me atop the mountain, of the Greys standing firm against overwhelming odds, of Twala’s severed head rolling past my feet, eyes wide, teeth clenched.

At last, dawn broke. My companions were no better rested. Good, however, was feverish. His temperature rose sharply, and soon he began to grow confused, then spitting blood. Clearly, internal damage had been done during the battle—perhaps from a spear thrust that pierced his armor and struck deeper.

Sir Henry, though stiff and sore, seemed stronger. His face was split and swollen, making eating difficult, laughter impossible. Still, he moved with grim determination.

Around eight, Infadoos arrived. He looked unharmed—remarkable for a man who had fought all night. He greeted us warmly, but his concern for Good was clear. I noticed he addressed Sir Henry with something like awe. As we would later learn, the Englishman was already seen as a supernatural figure among the Kukuana. No mortal could have fought so fiercely or killed Twala in single combat, especially after such a grueling day. That final blow—cleaving Twala’s neck—became legend. From then on, any mighty stroke was called “Incubu’s blow.”

Infadoos told us Twala’s regiments had surrendered to Ignosi, and chiefs from distant lands were sending word of submission. With Scragga dead, no rival claimed the throne. The kingdom was stable.

I remarked that Ignosi had risen to power through bloodshed.

He shrugged. “Yes. But the Kukuana must bleed sometimes to remain calm. Many die—but the women live. New warriors will come. For now, peace will reign.”

Later, Ignosi himself visited. Now crowned king, he walked with regal dignity, flanked by guards. Seeing him, I recalled the tall Zulu who had once begged to join our service at Durban. Fortune had turned full circle.

“Hail, O king!” I said, rising.

“Yes, Macumazahn,” he replied. “King at last—by the might of your three right hands.”

All was well, he said. In two weeks, he would host a great feast to be seen by his people.

I asked about Gagool.

“She is the evil spirit of this land,” he answered. “She shall die—along with all witch doctors. She has lived too long. None remember when she was young. She trained the hunters, twisted the land into darkness.”

“But she knows things,” I warned. “Knowledge is harder to gain than to destroy.”

He paused. “True. Only she knows the secret of the ‘Three Witches’—the place beyond Solomon’s Road, where kings are buried and the Silent Ones sit.”

“And the diamonds,” I added. “Do not forget your promise. You must take us to the mines—even if you spare Gagool to show the way.”

“I will not forget,” he said. “I will consider.”

Afterward, I went to check on Good. He was delirious. The fever had seized him fully, worsened by internal injury. For days, his condition was grave. I believe he would have died without Foulata’s tireless care.

She tended him night and day—feeding him a cooling drink made from milk and tulip bulb juice, fending off flies, watching his breathing. Her movements were swift, gentle, instinctive—like a nurse trained in the finest hospitals. I tried to help, as did Sir Henry, but Foulata refused. She insisted we leave—our presence only disturbed him.

For nights on end, she sat beside his cot, her back against the wall, her eyes soft with exhaustion yet alight with compassion—or perhaps something deeper?

We feared he would not survive. Two days passed with no improvement. But Foulata never wavered. “He will live,” she said.

By order of the king, everyone within three hundred yards of the hut was moved away—only Sir Henry, myself, and Foulata remained, so no noise might disturb the sick man.

On the fifth night, I went to see him before retiring. I stepped quietly into the hut. The lamp flickered, casting shadows. Good lay still—no tossing, no moaning. Had he passed?

A sob escaped me.

“Hush—h—h!” whispered a voice from the dark.

I crept closer. Good was asleep—deeply, peacefully. And beside him, Foulata sat, her fingers tightly clasped in his. The crisis had passed.

He slept for eighteen hours. And throughout, she never moved—not once. When he finally woke, she was carried out—her limbs locked in stiffness, unable to walk. She had endured cramp, hunger, endless vigilance—all for him.

When Good recovered, Sir Henry told him everything. When he heard of her eighteen-hour vigil, fearing to wake him by moving, the sailor wept.

He went straight to her hut, taking me along to interpret.

“Tell her,” he said, “that I owe her my life. I will never forget her kindness.”

I translated. Under her dark skin, she flushed.

Then, with a swift, graceful motion—like a bird in flight—she turned to him, her large brown eyes meeting his.

“Nay, my lord,” she said softly. “You forget. Did you not save *my* life? Am I not your handmaiden?”

She remembered only his role in her rescue—completely ignoring Sir Henry and me. Women, I thought, are always like that. My dear wife was the same.

I left the hut heavy-hearted. I didn’t like the way she looked at Good. Sailors, I knew, could fall in love at the slightest spark. And Good… well, Good was no exception.

A few days later, Ignosi held his grand council—the *indaba*. The elders of Kukuanaland formally recognized him as king. A massive review followed. The surviving Greys were paraded, praised, and each given cattle and promoted to officer rank in the new royal guard.

An edict was issued: wherever we went, we were to be greeted with royal honors, treated as equals to the king. Life and death powers were granted to us.

Ignosi also reaffirmed his promises: no bloodshed without trial, no more witch-hunts.

Afterward, we met him privately. We asked about the mines at the end of Solomon’s Road.

“My friends,” he said, “I know this: the Three Witches—the Silent Ones—are there. It is where kings are buried. Twala rests among them. There is a deep pit—perhaps dug long ago for stones like those spoken of in Kimberley. And a secret chamber, known only to the king and Gagool.”

“But Twala is dead,” he continued. “I do not know the way. Yet there is a tale: long ago, a white man crossed the mountains, led by a woman to the chamber. She showed him riches, then betrayed him. He was driven back. Since then, no man has entered.”

“The story is true,” I said. “We found the bones of that man on the mountains.”

“Yes,” he nodded. “Now I swear to you—if you can reach that chamber, and the stones are there…”

“The diamond on your brow proves they are,” I interrupted, pointing to the gem I had taken from Twala’s forehead.

“Perhaps,” he said. “If they are there, you may take as many as you wish—if you choose to stay.”

“We must first find the chamber,” I said.

“There is only one who can show it—Gagool.”

“And if she refuses?”

“Then she dies,” Ignosi said coldly. “I spared her only for this.”

He called a messenger. “Bring Gagool.”

In minutes, she arrived, dragged by two guards, cursing them as she stumbled forward.

“Leave her,” Ignosi ordered.

The guards stepped back. Gagool collapsed—more like a bundle of rags than a person—her bright, snake-like eyes gleaming.

“What do you want?” she hissed. “You dare not touch me. If you do, I’ll kill you. Beware my magic.”

“Your magic failed Twala,” Ignosi replied. “It cannot harm me. I command you: reveal the chamber where the shining stones lie.”

“Ha! ha!” she cackled. “Only I know it. I will never tell. Let the white devils go empty-handed.”

“You will tell,” Ignosi said. “I will make you.”

“How, O king? Your power cannot force truth from a woman.”

“It is difficult—but I will.”

“How?”

“Thus: if you refuse, you will slowly die.”

“Die?” she shrieked. “You dare not touch me! You do not know who I am. How old do you think I am? I knew your fathers’ fathers’ fathers. When this land was young, I was here. When it grows old, I will still be here. I cannot die unless killed by chance. None dare slay me.”

“Yet I will,” Ignosi said. “See, Gagool—mother of evil—you are so old you no longer value life. What is life to a hag like you? You have no shape, no hair, no teeth—only wickedness and cruel eyes. To end your suffering is mercy.”

“Fool!” she screamed. “Accursed fool! Do you think life is sweet only to the young? It is not. The young feel. They love, they suffer. But the old—ha! ha!—they laugh at death. They laugh at evil under the stars. All they desire is life—the warm sun, the sweet air. They fear the cold, the dark—ha! ha! ha!”

“Cease your lies,” Ignosi snapped. “Will you show us the chamber—or die now?”

He raised a spear above her.

With a wild cry, Gagool leapt up—then fell again, writhing.

“Nay—I will show you. Only let me live. Let me sit in the sun, eat meat, and I will guide you.”

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “Tomorrow, you go with Infadoos and my white brothers. Fail, and you will slowly die. I have spoken.”

“I will not fail,” she laughed. “I always keep my word—ha! ha! ha! Once, a woman showed the chamber to a white man. Evil befell him. Her name was Gagool. Perhaps… I was that woman.”

“You lie,” I said. “That was ten generations ago.”

“Mayhap,” she grinned. “When one lives long, one forgets. Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me. Her name was Gagool too. But mark this: in the place of light, you will find a bag of hide filled with stones. The man filled it—but never took it. Evil befell him—evil befell him! Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me. It will be a merry journey—we can see the bodies of the fallen as we go. Their eyes are gone. Their ribs are hollow. Ha! ha! ha!”

It was dark by the third day after the events of the previous chapter when we made camp in a cluster of huts at the base of the “Three Witches”—the name given to the trio of jagged peaks that marked the end of Solomon’s Great Road. Our party now consisted of just three of us—Sir Henry, Good, and myself—accompanied by Foulata, who attended to our needs, especially those of Good; Infadoos, the loyal but wary Kukuana warrior; Gagool, the ancient witch carried in a litter where she muttered curses all day long; and a small group of guards and servants.

The mountains rose before us like a monstrous triangle, their mass formed from a single violent upheaval of the earth. One peak stood to our right, another to our left, and the third loomed directly ahead, piercing the sky with its icy crown. I shall never forget the sight that greeted us the next morning as dawn painted the world in gold. High above, the peaks soared into the blue, wrapped in swirling snows like gauzy veils. Below the snowline, purple heather clung to the slopes, covering wild moors that stretched upward toward the heavens. Directly in front of us, Solomon’s Great Road wound uphill like a silver ribbon, leading straight to the foot of the central peak—about five miles distant—and then abruptly ending. This was its final point.

I leave it to the reader’s imagination to grasp the intensity of our excitement as we began our march that morning. At last, after weeks of hardship and peril, we were nearing the legendary mines—the very place that had claimed the life of the old Portuguese Dom centuries ago, my dear friend, his doomed descendant, and now, we feared, George Curtis, Sir Henry’s brother. Would we fare any better? Gagool had warned us: “Evil befell them. Will it also befall you?” And though I usually dismissed such talk as superstition, something about this place stirred a deep unease in me—and I sensed it in Good and Sir Henry too.

For over an hour, we marched swiftly along the heather-lined road, so eager that even Gagool’s bearers struggled to keep pace. From within her litter, she called out, her voice rasping through the grass flaps:
“Walk more slowly, white men! Why run to meet the evil that awaits you? You seek treasure, but what do you truly desire?”

She peered out, her wrinkled face twisted in a grotesque grin, her eyes gleaming with malice. Then came that chilling laugh—dry, hollow, and full of menace—that sent a shiver down my spine and drained the fire from our spirits for a moment.

But onward we went, driven by curiosity and fate. Soon, we saw it—a vast circular pit, three hundred feet deep and half a mile around, its sloping sides rising like a natural amphitheater.
“Can’t you guess what this is?” I asked Sir Henry and Good, who stared in stunned silence.

They shook their heads.
“Then you’ve never seen Kimberley’s diamond diggings,” I said. “This is Solomon’s Diamond Mine. Look at the layers of blue clay clinging to the walls—exactly like the Kimberley pipes. And there,” I pointed to a series of worn stone slabs arranged on a gentle slope beneath an ancient watercourse, “those are washing tables—no doubt about it.”

At the edge of this immense crater—precisely the spot marked on the old Portuguese map—the Great Road split into two paths that curved around the pit. In places, the path was built entirely of stone blocks, clearly intended to reinforce the rim and prevent collapses.

We followed the path, drawn forward by the mystery of three towering figures we could glimpse beyond the chasm. As we neared, we realized they were colossal statues—gigantic seated forms, each about thirty feet tall, perched on dark stone pedestals carved with crude symbols of ancient worship. They were separated by forty paces, facing outward across the plain toward Loo.

There were two male figures and one female. The woman, nude and beautifully sculpted though weather-worn, bore crescent-shaped horns upon her brow. The male figures were draped in robes—one with a serene, almost noble expression, the other with a terrifying, devilish countenance.
“This,” Sir Henry murmured, “is the calm of inhuman cruelty—the kind the ancients attributed to gods who could watch mankind suffer without pity.”

These silent guardians, known to the Kukuanas as the “Silent Ones,” sat in eternal stillness, watching over the land. I could not help wondering who had built them, who had dug the mine, who had laid this road.

Then it struck me—Solomon, according to Scripture, had turned to strange gods: Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom. Could these statues represent them?
“Perhaps,” said Sir Henry, a scholar of classical languages. “Ashtoreth was Astarte to the Phoenicians, and she was often depicted with horned brows—just like this figure. Maybe a Phoenician overseer carved them during Solomon’s time.”

Before we could explore further, Infadoos arrived, saluting the Colossi with his spear. He asked whether we would enter the “Place of Death” immediately or wait until after midday. If we were ready, Gagool had offered to guide us.

It was only eleven o’clock, and curiosity burned too fiercely to delay. We decided to go at once. I suggested we take food and water, just in case we became trapped. Gagool’s litter was brought forward, and she emerged, leaning on a stick. Foulata, at my request, packed dried meat and water into a reed basket with a hinged lid.

Straight ahead, fifty paces from the backs of the Colossi, rose a sheer rock wall eighty feet high, sloping upward into the mountain’s heart. Gagool cast us one last malevolent grin and limped toward the wall, stopping before a narrow, arched doorway that looked like the entrance to a mine gallery.

She waited, grinning.
“Now, white men from the Stars—great warriors Incubu, Bougwan, Macumazahn the wise—are you ready? Behold! I am here to serve my king and show you the store of bright stones. _Ha! ha! ha!_”

“We are ready,” I replied.

“Good, good! Strengthen your hearts. You will see things that shake the soul. Will you come, Infadoos? You who betrayed your master?”

Infadoos frowned.
“I will not enter. It is not my place. But you, Gagool—restrain your tongue. I will hold you accountable for every harm done to my lords. If a hair is touched, even if you are fifty times a witch, you will die. Do you hear me?”

“I hear,” she chuckled. “I know you—you always loved grand words. Even as a child you threatened your own mother. That was only yesterday. But fear not—I live only to obey the king. I have served many kings… until they served me. _Ha! ha!_ Now I go to see their faces again—and Twala’s too! Come on!”

She pulled a large oil lamp from beneath her fur cloak—a gourd with a rush wick.
“Are you coming, Foulata?” Good asked in his rough Kitchen Kukuana.

“I fear, my lord,” she whispered.

“Then give me the basket.”

“No, my lord. Where you go, I go.”

“The deuce you will!” I thought to myself. That might prove awkward indeed.

Without hesitation, Gagool stepped into the dark passage, wide enough for two to walk abreast. We followed, guided only by the sound of her voice echoing ahead and the sudden flutter of wings overhead.

“Hullo! What was that?” Good shouted. “Something hit me in the face!”

“Bats,” I said. “Keep moving.”

After perhaps fifty paces, the passage began to grow faintly illuminated. Then, suddenly, we stood in one of the most astonishing places ever beheld by mortal eyes.

Imagine the largest cathedral you’ve ever seen—windowless, yet dimly lit from above by shafts of light piercing the roof. That was the cavern we entered—only far greater. The ceiling arched away a hundred feet overhead, and the space was wider and higher than any human-built structure. But the true wonder lay in the rows of colossal pillars lining the chamber—massive stalactites of pure white spar, some twenty feet thick at the base, rising gracefully to the vaulted roof.

Some were still forming. On the floor, broken columns resembled ancient Greek ruins; above, icicle-like points dangled from the ceiling, occasionally dropping water with a soft *plink* onto the growing formations below.
On one pillar, we found a crude carving: a mummy-like figure beside what appeared to be an Egyptian god. It was etched at about five feet from the ground—where idle workers often carve their names. Yet the column itself was only eight feet tall. Given that we heard a drop fall as we watched, and knowing the rate of growth, we calculated it must grow less than an inch per century—proof that this process had been ongoing for thousands of years.

Other stalagmites took curious shapes—a pulpit intricately laced like lacework, beasts frozen in stone, and delicate fanlike patterns resembling frost on glass. Smaller caves branched off like chapels, including one no bigger than a dollhouse—yet inside, water dripped, icicles hung, and tiny spar columns formed just as in the main hall.

We wanted to explore thoroughly, but Gagool had no interest in beauty. She only wanted to reach her destination. I was frustrated—especially because I hoped to discover how light reached this cave. Was it natural, or man-made? Had it been used in ancient times?

We resolved to return later and pressed on.

Gagool led us to the far end of the cavern, where a second doorway stood—square-topped, like an Egyptian temple.
“Are you prepared to enter the Place of Death, white men?” she asked, relishing our dread.

“Lead on, Macduff,” Good said solemnly, trying to appear brave—though we all felt it except Foulata, who clutched his arm.

“This is getting rather ghastly,” Sir Henry muttered, peering into the darkness.

“Come on, Quatermain—_seniores priores_,” he added, stepping aside for me to lead. I inwardly cursed him.

*Tap, tap,* went Gagool’s stick as she hurried ahead, chuckling. I hesitated, gripped by a premonition of horror.

“Come on, old fellow,” Good urged. “Or we’ll lose our guide.”

Reluctantly, I stepped forward. After twenty paces, we entered a gloomy chamber—forty feet long, thirty wide, thirty high—hollowed from solid rock by hand. It was dimmer than the outer cave. At first, I saw only a long stone table, a massive white figure at its head, and smaller white forms seated around it.

Then I noticed a brown shape seated on the table. My eyes adjusted, and I froze—my legs turning to ice. I tried to flee, but Sir Henry grabbed my collar and held me fast.

I am not easily frightened, nor superstitious—but this scene shattered me. If he hadn’t held me, I would have fled, no matter the cost. Even the promise of all Kimberley’s diamonds wouldn’t have brought me back.

Then Sir Henry’s eyes adjusted. He let go, wiping sweat from his brow. Good swore weakly. Foulata shrieked and clung to him. Only Gagool laughed—long and loud.

It was horrifying.

At the head of the table sat a colossal skeleton—fifteen feet tall—holding a great white spear aloft. One bony hand rested on the table, as if preparing to rise. Its skull grinned at us, jaw slightly open, eyes hollow, neck bent forward, vertebrae jutting like daggers. It was Death himself—carved from a single stalactite, perfectly detailed, anatomically flawless.

“Great heavens,” I whispered. “What is this?”

“And those things?” Good asked, pointing at the white figures around the table.

“And what is *that*?” Sir Henry demanded, pointing at the brown object.

“Hee! hee! hee!” Gagool cackled. “To those who enter the Hall of the Dead, evil comes. _Ha! ha!_”

“Come, Incubu, brave in battle—come and see the one you slew,” she croaked, grabbing Curtis’ coat and pulling him forward.

We followed. She stopped and pointed to the brown form on the table.

Sir Henry gasped. There, naked, the severed head resting on its knees, sat the corpse of Twala, the last king of the Kukuanas. His body was gaunt, skin shriveled, vertebrae protruding like spikes—his face a grim parody of Hamilton Tighe.

A thin, glassy film covered the corpse. We couldn’t understand it—until we saw water falling steadily from the ceiling, *drip! drop! drip!* onto the neck, running down the body, and seeping into a hole in the table.

Then I understood—Twala was being turned into a stalactite.

And the others—seated around the table—were the same. Once human, now petrified. Their bodies had been placed under the constant drip for centuries, transformed into ice-like spar. Through the translucent coating, their features remained faintly visible.

Twenty-seven royal corpses lined the table—each preserved forever. The last was Ignosi’s father. The practice must have begun four centuries ago, assuming each king was buried here.

But the giant skeleton—Death himself—was older. Carved from a single stalactite, it bore the marks of the same artist who shaped the Three Witches. Perhaps it inspired the custom. Or perhaps it was meant to frighten intruders away from the treasures beyond.

No one knows. But this is how it stands.

Such was the White Death. Such were the White Dead.

While we were still recovering from the shock of what had just happened, and examining the chilling relics of the Place of Death, Gagool had slipped away into her own dark ritual. Somehow—though she moved with surprising agility when she chose—she had climbed onto the massive stone table where Twala lay beneath the drip, as if to inspect his state, or perhaps for some deeper purpose of her own. Good whispered that she might be checking how he was “pickling,” but it seemed more likely she was paying homage.

She leaned over, pressed her lips against the cold, lifeless mouth of the king as though greeting an old friend, then hobbled back across the table, pausing now and then to mutter words toward the shrouded figures—words we couldn’t catch, but which carried the weight of ancient ceremony. Once done, she settled herself directly beneath the White Death, squatting low, and began a strange, guttural chant, as if offering prayers to something far darker than mere death.

The sight of this twisted woman praying to the very force of evil sent a chill through us all. We hurried our search.

“Now, Gagool,” I said in a hushed voice—no one dared speak loudly in such a place—“lead us to the chamber.”

Without hesitation, she scrambled down from the table.
“My lords are not afraid?” she asked, grinning up at me with yellowed teeth.

“Lead on,” I replied.

“Good, my lords,” she croaked, limping around to the rear of the great stone figure. “Here is the chamber. Let my lords light the lamp and enter.” She set the gourd of oil on the floor and leaned against the cave wall.

I pulled out a match—one of the few we had left—and lit the rush wick. Holding it aloft, I scanned the rock face before us. There was nothing—just solid stone. Then Gagool let out a cackle.
“The way is there, my lords. _Ha! ha! ha!_”

“Do not jest with us,” I said sharply.

“I jest not,” she replied, pointing. “See!”

As she did, the lantern’s light caught movement—a massive slab of rock slowly rising from the ground, disappearing into the ceiling above. It was wide enough for a door, ten feet high, five feet thick, and must have weighed twenty or thirty tons. It rose with eerie silence, balanced by unseen counterweights—perhaps like a modern window mechanism, only carved from mountain stone centuries ago. How it was triggered, we didn’t see; Gagool avoided the spot entirely. But I suspected a hidden lever, pressed just so, shifting balance and lifting the monolith inch by inch.

It rose slowly until it vanished completely, leaving behind a black maw in the rock.

Our hearts pounded. The entrance to Solomon’s treasure chamber was open at last.

Would it be a trick? Or would Da Silvestra’s tale prove true? Were these the riches that could make us kings of the world?

We stood frozen, breathless.

“Enter, white men from the Stars,” Gagool called, stepping forward. “But first, hear your servant, Gagool the old. The bright stones you will see were dug from the pit guarded by the Silent Ones. They were stored here long before I was born—by whom, I know not. Only once has this chamber been entered since those who hid them fled. The legend spread, yes, but no one knew the secret of the door… until a white man came from beyond the mountains—perhaps also ‘from the Stars’—and was welcomed by the king of that time. He sits yonder,” she pointed to the fifth king on the table of the dead.

“And it came to pass that he and a woman journeyed here. By chance, she discovered the secret of the door—search a thousand years, and you would never find it. So they entered. The man found the stones, filled a goat-skin with them, and as he left, he took one more—a large one—in his hand.”

She paused.

“Well,” I asked, voice trembling, “what happened to Da Silvestra?”

Gagool flinched.
“How do you know the name of the dead man?” she snapped. Then, without waiting: “None can say. But the white man fled—frightened. He dropped the skin and ran, holding only the one stone. The king took it—the same stone Macumazahn removed from Twala’s brow.”

“Has no one entered since?” I asked, peering into the darkness.

“None, my lords. Only the door’s secret remains. Every king has opened it—but none have entered. There is a saying: whoever enters dies within a moon. Just as the white man died upon the mountain, so shall others die here. _Ha! ha!_ Mine are true words.”

Our eyes met. A cold dread washed over me. How could she know so much?

“Enter, my lords,” she urged. “If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies on the floor. And if death follows entry, you shall learn soon enough. _Ha! ha! ha!_” She shuffled through the doorway, carrying the lamp.

I hesitated.

“Confound it!” Good burst out. “Here goes. I’m not letting an old witch scare me.” He stepped in, followed by Foulata—though she trembled violently, clearly terrified. We followed.

A few yards down the narrow passage, Gagool stopped.
“See, my lords,” she said, holding the light. “Those who hid the treasure fled in haste. They meant to seal the way—had time to stack blocks two feet high, but not finish. Look—stones ready, mortar, trowels—tools like those used today.”

Foulata gasped, pale and weak. “I cannot go farther,” she whispered. “I feel faint.”

We sat her down on the half-built wall, placed the basket of provisions beside her, and continued.

After another fifteen paces, we reached a painted wooden door—open. Whoever had been here last hadn’t closed it.

Across the threshold lay a goat-skin bag, bulging with pebbles.

“Hee! hee!” Gagool snickered. “What did I tell you? The white man fled in haste—dropped the woman’s bag. Look inside—you’ll find a water-gourd among the stones.”

Good bent down. The bag was heavy, jingling.
“By Jove,” he breathed, “it’s full of diamonds.”

He was right. The thought alone was staggering.

“Go on,” Sir Henry said impatiently. “Here, old lady—give me the lamp.” He took it, stepped through the door, and raised it high.

We crowded in after him, forgetting the bag for a moment.

And there we stood—inside King Solomon’s treasure chamber.

At first, the dim light revealed only a small room, roughly ten feet square, carved from living rock. Then, piled high to the arched ceiling, we saw rows of elephant tusks—hundreds of them, first-quality ivory. Enough wealth to make any man rich forever. Perhaps this was the source of Solomon’s famed throne of ivory.

On the opposite wall stood about twenty wooden boxes, painted red—like ammunition crates, but larger.

“There are the diamonds!” I exclaimed. “Bring the light!”

Sir Henry held it close to the top box. Its lid was cracked, rotted by time. I reached in—not diamonds, but gold coins—strange shapes, stamped with what looked like Hebrew letters.

“Ah,” I said, pulling one out. “We won’t go home empty-handed. At least two thousand pieces per box. Eighteen boxes. This must’ve paid the workers.”

“Looks like that’s it,” Good said. “No diamonds unless Da Silvestra packed them all in his bag.”

“Look yonder,” Gagool croaked. “In the darkest corner—there is a recess, three stone chests. Two sealed, one open.”

Before I could translate, I asked: “How do you know this? No one’s been here since the white man.”

“Ah, Macumazahn,” she mocked, “the watcher from the stars. Do you not know some live long? Some see through rock? _Ha! ha! ha!_”

“Curtis,” I said, pointing. “Check that corner.”

“Hullo!” he yelled. “There’s a niche—look! Great heavens!”

We rushed to the alcove shaped like a bow window. Three stone chests stood against the wall—two closed, one open.

“_See!_” Curtis cried, holding the lamp over the open chest.

At first, the silvery glow blinded us. When our eyes adjusted, we saw it: uncut diamonds—most large, many huge. I picked some up. The soapy texture confirmed it. Real diamonds.

I dropped them, stunned.
“We’re the richest men in the world,” I whispered. “Monte Christo was a fool compared to us.”

“We’ll flood the market,” Good said.

“First, we’ve got to get them out,” Sir Henry reminded us.

We stood, pale-faced, staring at each other, the lantern flickering between us. It felt less like triumph and more like conspiracy.

“Hee! hee! hee!” Gagool cackled behind us, darting like a bat. “There are the bright stones you love—take them! Run them through your fingers! _Eat_ them! _Drink_ them! _Ha! ha!_”

At that, I burst out laughing—so hard I couldn’t stop. The others joined in, though none knew why. We laughed wildly over the treasure that had waited thousands of years—for *us*. The miners who dug them, the overseer who stored them—none ever saw their value. Solomon never touched them. David never claimed them. Da Silvestra barely escaped with a handful.

But we had them. Millions in diamonds, thousands in gold and ivory—all ours.

Then the laughter faded.

“Open the other chests,” Gagool croaked. “Take your fill, white lords. _Ha! ha!_ Take your fill.”

We obeyed. We broke the seals—felt a pang of sacrilege—then lifted the lids.

Hooray! The second chest was full to the brim. The third, only a quarter full—but every stone was a gem: none smaller than twenty carats, some the size of pigeon eggs. Many were slightly yellow, “off-coloured,” as they’d say at Kimberley.

But I noticed something else—the look on Gagool’s face. As she crept silently from the chamber, slithering down the passage like a snake, her eyes burned with hatred.

Then—suddenly—cries echoed through the tunnel.

“_Oh, Bougwan! Help! Help! The stone falls!_”

“Leave go, girl! Then—”

“_Help! Help! She stabbed me!_”

We sprinted down the passage.

The rock door was closing—slowly, inexorably. Only three feet from the floor.

Near it, Foulata and Gagool struggled. Blood soaked Foulata’s leg. She clung to the witch, who fought like a wild animal.

Then—Gagool broke free. Foulata collapsed. The old hag threw herself to the ground, twisting through the narrowing crack.

She made it—just—under the stone.

Then—*crunch!*

The thirty-ton slab descended. It crushed her. A scream tore from her throat—long, agonizing—then silence. The door sealed shut.

We hurled ourselves against it—too late.

Four seconds. That was all.

We turned to Foulata. She was dying.

“Ah! Bougwan, I die,” she gasped. “She crept out—I didn’t see her. The door began to fall. Then she came back—through the falling stone—I saw her and caught her. She stabbed me. And now—_I die_, Bougwan.”

“Poor girl,” Good sobbed, kneeling beside her. He kissed her again and again.

“Bougwan,” she whispered, “is Macumazahn there? It grows so dark…”

“Here I am, Foulata.”

“Macumazahn, be my tongue. I cannot speak to him. Say… I love him. And I am glad to die, because I know he cannot carry such as I am—sun cannot mate with darkness, white with black.

Say… since I saw him, sometimes I felt a bird in my heart—ready to fly away and sing elsewhere. Even now, though I cannot move, my heart does not die. It is full of love—could live ten thousand years and still be young.

Say—if I live again, I may see him in the Stars. And I will search them all—even if I am still black, and he… still white.

Say—nay, Macumazahn, say no more. Just that I love—oh, hold me closer, Bougwan—_oh! oh!_”

“She’s gone,” Good murmured, tears streaming. “She’s dead.”

“Don’t let that trouble you,” Sir Henry said quietly. “You’ll soon join her.”

“What?” Good stammered.

“You don’t see it?” Sir Henry said grimly. “We’re buried alive.”

Until he spoke, we hadn’t fully grasped it. But now it hit us—Gagool had planned this. She’d known the door would trap us. Her mockery about eating and drinking diamonds—now it made sense. Someone had tried it before. Da Silvestra had fled. Now we were trapped—with the treasure we’d come for.

“This won’t do,” Sir Henry said hoarsely. “The lamp will burn out. Let’s find the spring.”

We searched frantically—hands in blood, feeling the walls, the floor, the sides. Nothing. No lever. No knob.

“It doesn’t work from inside,” I said. “If it did, Gagool wouldn’t have risked crawling under. She knew it was triggered from outside.”

“At least,” Sir Henry said with a dry laugh, “retribution was swift. Hers was worse than ours.”

We gave up. Returned to the chamber.

I spotted the basket of food near the unfinished wall. I picked it up and brought it in.

Then we carried Foulata’s body into the chamber, laid her beside the gold boxes.

We sat back against the stone chests, backs to the treasure.

“Let’s divide the food,” Sir Henry said. “Make it last.”

We did. Four tiny meals each. Enough for maybe two days. Two gourds of water—each holding a quart.

“Now,” Sir Henry said grimly, “let us eat and drink. For tomorrow, we die.”

We ate a small bite of dried meat, drank a sip of water. Not hungry, but needed it. Afterward, we stood and examined every inch of the chamber—walls, floor, ceiling. No exit. No secret passage. None existed. A treasure vault wasn’t meant to be escaped from.

The lamp flickered. The fat was nearly gone.

“Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “what time is it?”

I checked my watch. Six o’clock. We’d entered at eleven.

“Infadoos will miss us,” I said. “He’ll search tomorrow.”

“But he doesn’t know the door’s secret,” Sir Henry replied. “No one does. Not even if he finds it. Five feet of solid rock—nothing short of dynamite could break it. All the Kukuana army couldn’t breach it. My friends, we must accept fate. The hunt for treasure has ended many lives. We shall join them.”

The flame dimmed further. Then—suddenly—it flared.

For a moment, it illuminated everything: the towering pile of ivory, the gold boxes, Foulata’s corpse, the goat-skin full of jewels, the glimmering diamonds, and the three of us—pale, exhausted, sitting in silence, awaiting death by starvation.

Then the flame sank.

And died.

I cannot begin to describe the horror of the night that followed. Mercifully, sleep offered some relief—though even in our dire straits, exhausted nature still found a way to claim its due. Still, I could not rest much. Beyond the crushing dread of what lay ahead—any man, no matter how brave, might crumble under such a fate—I was haunted by something far more oppressive: silence.

You may have lain awake at night and felt the quiet weigh on your mind, but you’ve never known true silence. On Earth, there is always a whisper—a breeze, a distant bird, the hum of life. It may be faint, but it dulls the edge of emptiness. Here, nothing. We were buried deep within a snow-capped mountain, miles beneath the sky where wind roared over ice and snow. Not a sound reached us. The tunnel sealed off the chamber of the dead, and the dead make no noise. And we knew this well—lying beside poor Foulata’s cold form.

Even if every cannon on Earth had fired at once, we would not have heard it. We were cut off from the world, as though already in the grave.

Then came the irony: around us lay riches beyond imagination—enough to pay a nation’s debt, enough to build an entire fleet—and yet, we would have traded it all for a single breath of fresh air, a sip of water, or even just a swift end to our suffering. Wealth, which men spend their lives chasing, meant nothing now. It was ash in our mouths.

The hours crawled by. Then, finally, Sir Henry’s voice broke the stillness—deep, hoarse, and terrifying in the quiet.

“Good,” he said, “how many matches are left?”

“Eight,” Curtis replied.

“Strike one. Let’s see the time.”

He did. The flame burst into life, blinding in the blackness. My watch read five o’clock. Outside, dawn was staining the snow above with rose and gold. The night mists must have been lifting in the valleys.

“We should eat something,” I suggested. “Keep our strength.”

“What’s the point?” Good answered. “The sooner we die, the better.”

“While there’s life, there’s hope,” Sir Henry said firmly.

We ate a little, drank sparingly. Then, after a while, Sir Henry proposed we try shouting—just in case someone outside might hear us.

Good, whose voice carried far from years at sea, crept down the passage and began to yell. He made a hellish racket—like a beast in torment. But it might as well have been a mosquito buzzing. Nothing stirred. After a few minutes, he gave up, parched and drained. We stopped yelling—we couldn’t afford to waste water.

We sat again, back against the chests of diamonds, useless and heavy as stone. That dreadful inaction was perhaps the worst part. I broke down. I leaned my head on Sir Henry’s shoulder and wept. I think I heard Good choking back sobs on the other side, cursing himself for weakness.

But oh, what a man he was! If we had been children lost in the dark, and he our guardian, he could not have cared for us more tenderly. He forgot his own pain, soothing our nerves with stories of men who had survived impossible odds. When those failed, he reminded us that death was inevitable, that this was merely hastening it—and that dying slowly was merciful (which it isn’t). Then, quietly, as he had done before, he urged us to trust in something greater than ourselves. I did so, wholeheartedly.

His character—quiet, unassuming, yet strong as steel—was a light in that darkness.

And so the day passed, like the night before, though the word “day” seemed meaningless when all was pitch black. I lit another match—only three remained—and saw it was seven o’clock. We ate again, and then something struck me.

“How,” I asked, “is the air here still fresh? It’s thick, yes—but clean.”

“Great heavens!” Good jumped up. “I never thought of that. It can’t come through the door—it’s sealed tight. There must be another source. If there were no airflow, we’d have suffocated long ago. Let’s look.”

That tiny spark of hope changed everything. In seconds, we were crawling on hands and knees, feeling every inch of the floor, searching for a draft.

Then I touched something cold—Foulata’s face. I recoiled. For hours we searched, banging heads on tusks and chests, until Sir Henry and I gave up. But Good kept going, muttering that it was better than doing nothing.

“Come here,” he called suddenly, his voice strained.

We scrambled to him.

“Quatermain, put your hand where mine is. Feel anything?”

“I think… I feel air.”

He stood and stamped. A hollow thud echoed through the chamber.

Hope flared.

With trembling hands, I lit a match. Only three left. We were in the far corner—explaining why we hadn’t noticed the sound before. As the flame flickered, we saw it: a seam in the rock floor. And set level with it—stone ring.

We didn’t speak. Our hearts pounded too hard.

Good pulled out a knife with a hoof-pick at the back. He scratched around the ring. Then, gently, he levered it upward. Stone, unlike iron, hadn’t rusted. Slowly, it lifted.

He tried to pull it open. Nothing moved.

“Let me,” I said, impatient. The angle made it impossible for two to pull at once.

I tugged. No good.

Sir Henry tried. Failed.

Good scraped the crack again. Then:

“Curtis,” he said, “you’re strongest. Tackle it. Put your back into it.”

He took off his silk handkerchief and threaded it through the ring.

“Quatermain, grab Curtis’s waist. Pull when I say—_now._”

Sir Henry braced himself. Good and I pulled with every ounce of strength.

“Heave! Heave! It’s moving!”

I heard the crack of muscles. Then—grinding stone, rushing air—and we were thrown backward, crushed beneath a fallen flagstone.

Sir Henry’s strength had done it.

“Light a match,” he gasped, once we were up.

I did.

And there—beyond the stone—was the first step of a stair.

“Now what?” Good asked.

“Down the stairs,” Sir Henry said. “Trust in Providence.”

“Wait,” he added. “Quatermain, get the last biltong and water. We may need them.”

I went back to the chests. As I turned, a thought occurred: the diamonds. We hadn’t thought of them in days—their very idea sickened us. But what if we escaped? Might as well carry some.

So I plunged my fist into the nearest chest, filling every pocket of my coat and trousers. Then, with a grin, I scooped handfuls of large stones from a third chest. And stuffed Foulata’s basket full.

“Hey!” I shouted. “Want some diamonds? I’ve got pockets full and the basket packed.”

“Come on, Quatermain!” Sir Henry snapped. “Forget the diamonds!”

“I hope I never see another,” he muttered.

Good said nothing. I think he was saying goodbye to her.

And yet, reader, though you sit safe and warm, thinking of the wealth we left behind—vast, immeasurable—I assure you: if you’d spent twenty-eight hours in that tomb with nothing to eat or drink, you wouldn’t care about diamonds either. You’d rather die light.

Still, habit made me carry them. It wasn’t greed—it was instinct.

“Come on,” Sir Henry repeated, already descending.

“Steady. I’ll go first.”

“Mind your feet,” I warned. “There might be a drop.”

“More likely another room,” he said, counting steps.

At fifteen, he stopped.

“Bottom,” he said. “Looks like a passage. Follow me.”

Good went next. I brought up the rear, carrying the basket. At the bottom, I lit the last match.

By its weak glow, we saw a narrow tunnel branching right and left. The match burned my fingers and died.

Now came the question: which way?

We had no clue. One path might lead to salvation, the other to doom.

Then Good remembered: when I lit the match, the flame bent left. Air flows inward, not outward.

“Let’s go against the draught,” he said.

We followed the wall, feeling each step, testing the ground. We left the cursed treasure chamber behind—our only trace: open chests, empty lamp, and Foulata’s bones.

For nearly an hour, we crawled through the tunnels. They twisted, branched, looped. We were lost in a labyrinth—likely ancient mine shafts, following veins of ore. That was the only explanation.

Eventually, worn out, we stopped. Our biltong was gone. Our water—just a final sip. Throats like furnaces.

It seemed we’d escaped death in the chamber only to meet it here.

Then—I thought I heard something. A faint murmur.

I called the others.

Yes. A sound. Distant, soft—but real. Water.

“Running water,” Good whispered. “Come on.”

We moved forward. The sound grew louder—soon, unmistakable swirls, like a river racing through stone.

How could water flow underground?

We were close. Good swore he could smell it.

“Go slow,” Sir Henry said. “We’re near.”

Then—splash!

A cry.

Good had fallen in.

“Good! Where are you?”

A choked reply: “All right. Got hold of a rock. Light a match.”

I lit the last one.

In its dim glow, we saw dark water flowing at our feet. Wide, we couldn’t tell. Ahead—Good clinging to a rock.

“Stand clear,” he yelled. “I’m swimming.”

Splash. Struggle. Then—hands grasping. Sir Henry pulled him up.

“My word,” he gasped, “that was close. If I hadn’t caught that rock… it runs like a mill-race. No bottom.”

We dared not follow the riverbank—too dangerous in the dark. After resting, drinking sweet water, washing our faces, we turned back.

Good dripped ahead.

Then, a new gallery to the right.

“Might as well take it,” Sir Henry said wearily. “All paths are the same. We keep going till we collapse.”

We shuffled on, utterly drained.

I considered dropping the basket again. But no. Why not die rich?

Suddenly, Sir Henry stopped.

We bumped into him.

“Look,” he whispered. “Is my brain failing, or is that light?”

We stared.

Far ahead—a faint glimmer, no bigger than a windowpane. So dim, only eyes used to total darkness could see it.

Hope surged.

We pushed forward.

In minutes, it was undeniable. Light.

Then—air. Real air. Fresh, alive.

We stumbled onward.

The tunnel narrowed. Sir Henry knelt. Smaller. Smaller. Now only a foxhole-sized gap—earth, not rock.

A squeeze. A struggle.

And then—freedom.

Sir Henry emerged. Then Good. Then I, dragging the basket.

Above us—stars! And air, sweet and free, filling our lungs.

Then—crack! We rolled down a slope, tumbling through grass and roots. The basket snagged, and I stopped.

I sat up and shouted.

An answering cry from below—Sir Henry had landed safely.

We found Good stuck in a forked root. Bruised, shaken, but alive.

We sat together on the grass. The emotion was overwhelming. We laughed. We wept. We had escaped the tomb—nearly our grave.

Some higher power must have guided us to that jackal hole at the tunnel’s end.

And there—on the mountains—dawn blushed red, as if welcoming us back.

As light grew, we saw we were at the bottom of the great pit, opposite the cave entrance.

The Colossi loomed on the edge, silent sentinels.

Those endless tunnels—once part of the diamond mine, perhaps.

The underground river—its source, its course—unknown. And I don’t care to know.

Daylight brightened. We could see each other now.

Gaunt, hollow-eyed, filthy, bleeding, our faces etched with terror—yet Good’s eye-glass still sat firm in his eye. Never removed. Not even during the plunge, the fall, the roll.

Then we rose, fearing stiffness, and began the slow climb up the blue clay, using roots and grass for support.

I no longer thought of abandoning the basket. Nothing would separate us now.

At last, we reached the top—standing on the road, facing the huts.

A fire burned. Figures gathered.

We staggered toward them, supporting each other.

One figure rose—saw us—and fell to the ground, shrieking.

“Infadoos! Infadoos! It is you—come back from the dead!”

He rose, ran to us, shaking with joy.

“Oh, my lords! My lords! You’ve returned from the dead!”

And the old warrior threw himself at our feet, clasping Sir Henry’s knees, weeping aloud—for joy.

Ten days after that fateful morning, we found ourselves back in our old quarters at Loo—safe, sound, and remarkably unscathed by the horrors we had endured, save for a few telltale signs. My once-thick hair had turned three shades grayer from the ordeal, and Good, though still as sharp as ever, carried a quiet sorrow in his eyes ever since Foulata’s death. It was a loss that deeply moved him, and I could not help but feel it was better this way. From my perspective as a seasoned traveler of the world, her passing spared us future entanglements. She was no ordinary girl—she was strikingly beautiful, with a dignity and grace rare among her people, and possessed a mind far beyond the usual. Yet, as she herself once said, “Can the sun mate with darkness? Can white unite with black?” No amount of beauty or refinement could have made such a union wise—or desirable.

We never returned to Solomon’s treasure chamber. After two days of rest to recover from our exhaustion, we descended into the great pit where we’d escaped, hoping to locate the narrow passage that had led us out. But rain had washed away our tracks, and the sheer cliffs were riddled with ant-bear burrows and other crevices—so many that we couldn’t be sure which one had saved our lives. The mystery remained.

On the eve of our departure, we revisited the stalactite cave, drawn by a restless curiosity. Once more, we stepped into the Chamber of the Dead, passing beneath the spear of the White Death. There, before us, lay the massive rock that had sealed our escape route—impenetrable now, like a tombstone over an ancient secret. We stared at it, transfixed, thinking of the treasures beyond, of Gagool’s mangled remains crushed beneath it, and of Foulata, whose final resting place it guarded. Yet despite our efforts, no trace of the sliding door remained visible. No mechanism could be found—no lever, no pressure plate, no hidden switch. The device was gone, lost to time, its operation forever forgotten. It was a marvel of engineering—massive, silent, and utterly mysterious—a relic of a civilization long vanished. Even if the stone had risen before our eyes, I doubt any of us would have dared step across the bones of the witch to re-enter the chamber, even knowing it held diamonds beyond measure.

And yet… I could have wept at the thought of leaving behind what may well be the greatest hoard of wealth ever gathered in one place. But there was nothing to be done. Only dynamite could break through five feet of solid rock. So we left it. Perhaps one day, in some distant age, another explorer will discover the key—the “Open Sesame”—and unleash those gems upon the world. But I suspect not. Something in me feels that those tens of millions in jewels, locked in their stone coffers, are destined never to shine on human necks. They will remain cold companions to Foulata’s bones until the end of time.

With heavy hearts, we turned back toward Loo. And yet, our disappointment was misplaced. For I had taken care—before fleeing the cave—to fill every pocket of my old shooting coat and trousers with gems. And Foulata’s basket, though partly occupied by a water bottle, still held twice as many more. Some stones tumbled out during our descent down the pit—several large ones, too, which I’d stuffed into my coat pockets—but the majority remained. Among them were ninety-three magnificent diamonds, ranging from seventy to over two hundred carats. Between my coat, my trousers, and the basket, we carried enough treasure to make us rich beyond imagination—not millionaires by American standards, perhaps, but wealthy beyond dreams, with enough gemstones left for each of us to possess the finest collections in Europe. We hadn’t come out empty-handed.

At Loo, Ignosi received us warmly. He was well, busy rebuilding his kingdom, reorganizing his regiments after the great war with Twala. He listened intently to our tale, but when we spoke of Gagool’s death, he fell silent. Then he called to an ancient Induna, seated with others in a circle near him.

“Come hither,” he said.

The old man rose, bowed, and sat before the king.

“You are aged,” Ignosi said.

“Ay, my lord,” replied the Induna. “Thy father’s father and I were born on the same day.”

“Tell me—when you were young, did you know Gagaoola, the witch-doctress?”

“I did, my lord.”

“What was she like? Young, as you?”

“No, my lord. She was as she is now—as she was in the days of my great-grandfather: old, shriveled, ugly, full of evil.”

“She is dead.”

“So, O king. Then the ancient curse is lifted from the land.”

“Go.”

“_Koom!_ I go, Black Puppy, who tore out the old dog’s throat. _Koom!_”

“See, brothers,” said Ignosi, “this woman was strange indeed. I am glad she is gone. She would have let you die in the dark place—and perhaps later have killed me, as she killed my father and raised Twala, whom her black heart loved, to take his place. Now, continue your tale. Never has there been anything like it!”

After I recounted our escape—just as we had agreed—I turned to Ignosi with a request.

“And now, Ignosi,” I said, “the time has come for us to bid thee farewell and return to our own land. You came to us as a servant; now we leave you a mighty king. If you are grateful, remember your promise: rule justly, honor the law, and spare life without cause. In doing so, you shall prosper. Tomorrow, at dawn, send us an escort to guide us across the mountains. Is it not so, O king?”

Ignosi covered his face with his hands and remained silent for a long while.

“My heart is torn,” he said at last. “Your words split it in two. What have I done to you—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—that you should leave me so desolate? You stood by me in rebellion, in battle—will you abandon me now, in peace and victory? Would you not stay? Wives? Choose from my maidens. A home? Take all the land you can see. Houses? I will learn my people to build them. Cattle? Every married man shall bring you an ox or cow. Game? The elephant walks through my forests, the river-horse sleeps in the reeds. War? My Impis await your command. If there is anything else you desire, I will give it.”

“We want none of these things,” I replied. “We seek only our own place.”

Then Ignosi spoke bitterly, his eyes flashing.

“Now I understand,” he said. “You love the bright stones more than me, your friend. You have the stones. Now you wish to go to Natal, cross the black waters, sell them, and grow rich—just as a white man desires. Cursed be the white stones! Cursed be the man who seeks them! Death to anyone who sets foot in the Place of Death to find them. I have spoken. Go, white men. You may leave.”

I placed my hand on his arm.

“Ignosi,” I said, “tell me—when you wandered in Zululand and among the white people of Natal, did your heart not turn toward your mother’s land, the place where you first saw light, where you played as a child? That was your true home.”

“It was so, Macumazahn.”

“In the same way, Ignosi, our hearts turn toward our own lands. Our homes call to us.”

A silence followed. Then Ignosi spoke again, softer now.

“I see now, as ever, that your words are wise and full of reason. The bird that flies does not wish to run on the ground. The white man does not wish to live among the kraals of the black. Well, you must go. And I shall be left alone, for you will be as dead to me—since no word from you can reach me.”

“But listen,” he continued, “and let all your brothers hear. No other white man shall cross these mountains—no trader with guns and gin. My people shall fight with spears and drink water, as their ancestors did. I will not allow praying-men to frighten men’s hearts, to stir up rebellion against the king, or open the way for white folk to follow. If one white man comes to my gates, I will send him back. If a hundred come, I will push them back. If armies come, I will wage war with all my strength, and they shall not prevail. None shall seek the shining stones—not even an army. If they come, I will send a regiment to fill the pit, collapse the white columns in the caves, and bury them under rocks, so that no one may reach even the door you speak of—whose secret is now lost.

“But for you three—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—the path is always open. For you are dearer to me than anything alive.”

“You would go,” he said. “Infadoos, my uncle and my Induna, shall take you by the hand and lead you with a regiment. There is another way across the mountains—he shall show you. Farewell, my brothers, brave white men. Do not come back. I cannot bear to see you go.”

He rose, looked at us earnestly for a moment, then threw the corner of his karross over his head, hiding his face from us. We left in silence.

At dawn the next day, we departed from Loo, escorted by Infadoos—our old friend, broken-hearted at our leaving—and the regiment of Buffaloes. Though early, the main street was packed with people. As we passed at the head of the regiment, they gave us the royal salute. Women threw flowers at our feet, blessing us for freeing their land from Twala. It was deeply moving—an honor rarely given by natives.

One incident broke the solemnity, and we welcomed it. Just before we left the town, a young girl ran forward, holding lovely lilies, and offered them to Good—everyone seemed to favor him, perhaps because of his eye-glass and solitary whisker, which gave him a curious charm. Then she asked for a favor.

“Let my lord show his servant his beautiful white legs,” she said. “So that I may remember them always, and tell my children of them. I have walked four days to see them—for their fame has spread across the land.”

Good recoiled. “I’ll be hanged if I do!” he exclaimed.

“Come now,” said Sir Henry, “you can’t refuse a lady.”

“I won’t,” insisted Good. “It’s positively indecent.”

But eventually, he relented. With the crowd watching, he rolled up his trousers to the knee. Gasps of admiration rose from the women—especially the young girl—and he had to walk like that until we cleared the town. I fear Good’s legs will never be admired again. His teeth, his “transparent eye”—these the Kukuanas grew tired of. But his legs? Never.

As we traveled, Infadoou told us of a second pass north of Solomon’s Great Road—a path where one could descend the cliff wall separating Kukuanaland from the desert, broken by the towering shapes of Sheba’s Breasts. He also spoke of hunters who, two years prior, had descended this route in search of ostriches. Thirst drove them far from the mountains, until they saw trees on the horizon. Following them, they discovered a vast oasis—fertile, well-watered, miles across. Infadoos suggested we take this route, and it seemed wise: it would spare us the harsh mountain pass. Moreover, several of the hunters were with us to guide us, and they claimed they could see other fertile patches far off in the desert.

It puzzled us all—how Ignosi’s mother, carrying a child, could have crossed these dangers and survived, when we nearly died. But I’ve since wondered: perhaps she took this very path, wandering into the wilderness like Hagar. If so, everything makes sense. She might have been found by ostrich hunters, brought to the oasis, then slowly guided southward to Zululand—just as Ignosi himself described.

We journeyed easily, and on the fourth night, we stood once more atop the mountain ridge overlooking the desert, stretching endlessly below, twenty-five miles north of Sheba’s Breasts. At dawn the next day, we reached the edge of a deep chasm, where we would descend to the plain two thousand feet below.

There, we bid farewell to Infadoos, our steadfast friend and warrior. He blessed us solemnly, nearly weeping.

“Never,” he said, “shall my old eyes see the like of you again. Ah! the way Incubu cut down his men in battle! Ah! the stroke that severed Twala’s head! It was beautiful—beautiful! I may never see such a sight again—unless in dreams.”

We were deeply saddened. Good was so overcome that he gave Infadoos a souvenir—what do you think? An *eye-glass*. Later we realized it was a spare one. Infadoos was thrilled, convinced it would greatly enhance his status. After several attempts, he actually managed to screw it into his own eye. The sight of the old warrior in a leopard-skin cloak, black ostrich plumes, and a European eye-glass was absurd—yet somehow fitting.

After ensuring our guides were well-supplied, and receiving a thunderous farewell salute from the Buffaloes, we shook Infadoos’s hand and began our descent. It was grueling, but by evening we reached the bottom safely.

That night, as we sat by the fire and looked up at the towering cliffs above, Sir Henry said:

“I think there are worse places than Kukuanaland in the world. I’ve known unhappier times than these past weeks—though never stranger ones. Eh, you fellows?”

“I almost wish I were back,” sighed Good.

For my part, I reflected that all’s well that ends well. But in a lifetime of narrow escapes, none had matched the ones we’d just survived. Thinking of the battle sent a chill through me. And the treasure chamber—!

Next morning, we set out across the desert, with our five guides carrying ample water. We camped in the open, marching again at dawn. By noon on the third day, we could see the trees of the oasis. By sunset, we walked once more upon grass, hearing the gentle sound of running water.

I come now to what may well be the most astonishing chapter of our entire journey—a moment so improbable, so full of wonder, that it seems almost beyond belief. Yet it happened, and I record it here as truthfully as I can.

I was walking ahead of Sir Henry and Good, along the banks of the stream that flows from the oasis into the vast, hungry sands of the desert. The sun beat down, the air shimmered, and everything seemed still and quiet—until suddenly I stopped in my tracks, rubbing my eyes as if to clear a dream.

There, not twenty yards before me, stood a hut. It was nestled beneath the shade of a fig tree, facing the water, built with grass and woven branches in the style of the Kafir people, but with a proper door—no mere hole like a bee’s nest. “What in the world,” I muttered to myself, “could a hut be doing here?”

Even as I spoke, the door creaked open—and out limped a man. A white man. Clad in animal skins, with a long, thick black beard, and one leg clearly injured. I blinked hard. I must have been hallucinating. No hunter would ever come this far. And certainly none would settle here. Yet there he stood, real as life.

I stared. So did Good when he caught sight of him. Then Sir Henry and Good came up, breathless from the heat.

“Look at that!” I called. “Is that a white man—or am I losing my mind?”

Sir Henry looked. Good looked. Then, with a cry that rang across the silence, the bearded man hobbled forward, arms raised, and collapsed at Sir Henry’s feet as if struck by a sudden blow.

In an instant, Sir Henry was beside him. “Great Powers!” he cried. “It’s George! My brother George!”

At that moment, another figure emerged from the hut—also dressed in skins, carrying a rifle. He ran toward us, then froze upon seeing me.

“Macumazahn!” he shouted. “Don’t you know me? It’s Jim—the hunter! I lost the note you gave me for the Baas, and we’ve been here nearly two years!”

He dropped to his knees, rolling over in tears of joy. “You careless scoundrel!” I said, laughing. “You should be well sjambocked”—that is, skinned alive.

Meanwhile, George Curtis had regained his senses. He rose slowly, and the two brothers embraced, gripping each other’s hands so tightly they could scarcely speak. Whatever past quarrels they’d had—perhaps over a woman, though I never asked—it all melted away in that moment.

“My dear old fellow,” burst out Sir Henry, “I thought you were dead. I crossed Solomon’s Mountains looking for you. I’d given up hope. And now—here you are, stranded in the desert like an old vulture.”

“I tried to cross those mountains nearly two years ago,” George replied, voice weak from disuse. “But a boulder fell on my leg and crushed it. I couldn’t go forward or back. I stayed here, hoping someone might come.”

Then I stepped forward. “How do you do, Mr. Neville?” I said.

He looked at me, stunned. “Why—aren’t you Hunter Quatermain? And Good too? Hold on a minute—I’m getting dizzy again. This is too strange, too wonderful. I thought you’d long since forgotten me, and that you were settled comfortably in England.”

That evening, by the campfire, George told us his story—every bit as dramatic as our own.

Less than two years before, he had set out from Sitanda’s Kraal, determined to reach Suliman’s Berg. He’d heard from the natives about a descent down the mountains shaped like a ladder—which turned out to be a better route than the one marked in Dom Silvestra’s old map. But Jim had lost the note I’d sent him, so he never knew of our plans.

They endured terrible hardships in the desert, but finally reached the oasis. On the day they arrived, George sat by the stream while Jim gathered honey from a nest of stingless bees atop a bank above him. In doing so, Jim disturbed a massive rock, which tumbled down and crushed George’s right leg.

From that day on, he was crippled. He couldn’t move forward or return. He chose to stay, rather than risk dying in the wasteland. But food was not lacking. They had ammunition, and the oasis drew game at night—antelope, deer, even lions. They hunted, trapped, and used the hides for clothing when their clothes wore out.

“We lived like Robinson Crusoe and Friday,” George said, “hoping someone would come. Only last night, we decided Jim would leave me and try to reach Sitanda’s Kraal for help. He was to go tomorrow—but I didn’t expect to see him again. And now—*you*, of all people—appear, just when I least expected it. It’s the most miraculous thing I’ve ever known.”

Sir Henry then recounted our adventures, telling the tale late into the night.

When I showed George some of the diamonds, he gasped. “Well, at least you’ve got something for your troubles—besides me.”

Sir Henry laughed. “The diamonds belong to Quatermain and Good. That was part of the agreement.”

That made me think. After speaking with Good, I told Sir Henry that both of us wished him to take a third share of the treasure—or, if he refused, that it go to his brother, who had suffered more than anyone else in pursuit of it.

Eventually, he agreed. Though George didn’t learn of this until later.

Here, I believe, I shall end my account. Our journey back across the desert was grueling, especially with George’s weakened leg constantly breaking apart, sending splinters of bone through the flesh. But we managed it, though repeating the details would only echo what we’d already endured.

Six months after returning to Sitanda’s Kraal—where we found our guns and supplies untouched, though the old caretaker was shocked we’d survived—we were once more safe at my home near Durban on the Berea, where I write these words.

And so I bid farewell to all who have followed me through this most extraordinary adventure.

P.S.—Just as I finished writing, a Kafir approached along my orange tree avenue, carrying a letter in a split stick. It was from Sir Henry. Since it speaks for itself, I reproduce it in full:

October 1, 1884
Brayley Hall, Yorkshire

My Dear Quatermain,

I send this a few mails back to tell you we’ve all arrived safely in England. We landed at Southampton and went straight to London. You should have seen Good the very next day—perfectly shaved, wearing a frock coat that fit like a glove, brand-new spectacles, and all the trimmings. I took him for a walk in the park, where I met some acquaintances, and immediately told them about his “beautiful white legs.” He’s furious—especially since some malicious soul printed it in a society paper.

Now, business: Good and I took the diamonds to Streeter’s for valuation, as we agreed. I’m afraid to tell you what they said—they’re worth more than I can bear to think of. They admit it’s largely guesswork, since stones of such quantity have never been offered on the market before. But they say (except for a few of the largest) they’re flawless, equal to the finest Brazilian gems. When I asked if they’d buy them, they said no—too much for their capacity. Instead, they suggest selling them gradually over many years, to avoid flooding the market. Still, they’ve offered £180,000 for just a small portion.

You must come home, Quatermain. We need you to handle things—especially if you insist on giving George that third share, which doesn’t belong to me. As for Good—he’s *no good*. His time is taken up with shaving and other vain pursuits. But he’s still haunted by Foulata. He told me he hasn’t seen a woman since returning—either in beauty or expression.

Come home, my old friend. Buy a house near here. You’ve earned it. There’s a lovely property nearby—perfect for you. Come soon. If you leave on receipt of this, you’ll arrive by Christmas. I’ve booked you a room with me. Good and George will be here, and so—by the way—will your boy Harry. I’ve had him down for a week’s shooting. He’s a cool young man. Shot me in the leg, removed the pellets himself, then remarked how useful it is to have a medical student on a hunting party!

Good-bye, old boy. I can’t say more. But I know you’ll come—not just for the diamonds, but for the sake of friendship.

Your sincere friend,

P.S.—The tusks of the great bull that killed Khiva now hang in the hall, above the buffalo horns you gave me. They look magnificent. And the axe I used to chop off Twala’s head is mounted above my desk. I wish we’d brought the chainmail coats. Don’t lose Foulata’s basket—the one you used to carry the diamonds. H.C.

Today is Tuesday. A steamer sails Friday. I think I must accept Curtis’s invitation. I’ll sail for England—just to see you, Harry, and to oversee the printing of this story. It’s a task I won’t entrust to anyone else.
