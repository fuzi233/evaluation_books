St Bertrand de Comminges lies on the Pyrenean spurs like a loaf gone stale—its crust of grey stone hard, its inner streets hollowed by time. The place had once been a bishop’s table, with its own library and chapter and discipline; now it lived on a thin broth of pilgrims and a few summer travellers who came to look up at the cathedral and then descend again to Toulouse or Auch as if nothing here could cling to them.

In the spring of 1883 an Englishman arrived alone for the day: Dennistoun, a Cambridge man with the neat, hungry patience of a scholar who does not so much “see” old things as taste them—testing their age the way one tests wine on the tongue, by scent and after-notes and the faint rasp of dust. He had left two friends in Toulouse, promising to rejoin them next morning. They would give St Bertrand half an hour, they said, like men swallowing a dry crust; but Dennistoun meant to spend the entire short day in the cathedral, filling a notebook and exposing plate after plate until every stall and screen had yielded its shape and story.

To do that he needed the sacristan with him. The landlady of the Chapeau Rouge—a brisk woman with a voice like iron ladles on copper pots—sent for the man. When he came, Dennistoun found not the expected harmless guardian of keys, but a small, dried creature whose very shoulders looked as if they were listening behind him. He stood as one stands in a wind that might suddenly turn. Again and again his head twitched, half glancing over his shoulder; his back was hunched and tense, the muscles in a continual recoil.

Dennistoun, watching with the cool curiosity of a collector, tried to settle the matter in his mind. Was the fellow mad with a fixed delusion? Or guilty? Or merely mastered by a wife? The last seemed likeliest, and yet the air about him was not of domestic nagging. It had the weight of something older and less nameable—something that did not speak in ordinary words.

But the church drew Dennistoun down its long stone throat. The day was a jar of chill air scented with damp mortar, wax, and the faint sourness of old woodwork. Every sound had a long tail. His pencil scratched like a small animal in the silence; his camera click seemed too loud; and now and then, from far above, the building answered with noises as if it were remembering other occupiers: muffled footfalls where no feet walked, a murmur as of distant talk swallowed by arches.

Once—Dennistoun told me later—he would have sworn he heard a thin metallic laugh high in the tower, the kind of laugh that has no chest behind it, only hard edges like coin struck on stone. He looked sharply at the sacristan. The little man had gone white to the lips.

“It is he—that is—it is no one,” he stammered. “The door is locked.”

They stared at each other for a full minute, as if both had heard the same note and neither dared to name it.

Another time Dennistoun paused before a large, dark, almost unreadable painting behind the altar—one of the series of St Bertrand’s miracles. The figures were a swamp of browns and blacks; the varnish had yellowed and the centuries had smoked it. Only the Latin legend below stood clear:

_Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare._

He turned, with a half-smile forming—ready with some light remark about devils and stranglings—then stopped short. The sacristan was on his knees. He stared at the picture not as at painted wood but as at a living threat, hands clasped till the knuckles shone, tears running over his cheeks with the helplessness of pain. Dennistoun, embarrassed, pretended he had not seen. Yet the question clung to him: why should a bad daub strike a grown man into such supplication? It was as if the man’s fear had a flavour, sharp and unmistakable, and the picture had just touched it.

By late afternoon the short day was dimming. Shadows pooled beneath the stalls and along the choir-screen; the dilapidated organ crouched in darkness like a broken animal. As the light thinned, the ear grew greedy. Those small disturbances—the scuff of something on stone, the suggestion of whispering—became more frequent, more insistently placed, as if the building were trying to draw Dennistoun’s attention away from his notes and toward the unseen.

The sacristan began at last to show hurry. When camera and notebook were packed, he beckoned Dennistoun quickly to the western door under the tower. It was time for the Angelus. He took the rope in both hands and pulled. The bell resisted, then yielded with a vast reluctant swing, and Bertrande’s voice climbed into the pines and dropped down to the valleys, where streams ran loud. The sound was a kind of blessing on the air: it cleared the day’s uneasy murmurs, and for the first time there seemed to be quiet, not merely absence of noise but the proper, living quiet of a place that hears its own rhythms.

They stepped out. The sacristan, breathing easier with the bell’s speaking behind him, ventured into talk.

“Monsieur seemed to interest himself in the old choir-books.”

“Indeed. Is there a library in the town?”

“No, monsieur. Perhaps there used to be one for the Chapter, but now—so small a place—” He hesitated, his face working as though he were chewing something bitter. Then, with a sudden plunge, as of a man stepping off a bank into cold water, he said: “But if monsieur is _amateur des vieux livres_, I have at home something that might interest him. Not a hundred yards.”

For a moment Dennistoun’s mind leapt with those collector’s dreams that are almost physical—hands on vellum, eyes on uncial strokes, the scent of old glue and dust like a spice one should not crave. Then scepticism cooled him: some printed missal, surely, perhaps Plantin, perhaps nothing. Yet to refuse would be to starve one’s own curiosity. He agreed at once.

As they walked, Dennistoun felt again the sacristan’s earlier hesitation and resolve, and, shamefacedly, the thought occurred to him that he might be being drawn into some crooked alley to be robbed as a rich Englishman. He began talking to make his presence known to the air, and contrived to mention—clumsily, but with intent—that two friends would join him early next morning. To his surprise, the sacristan seemed relieved.

“That is well,” he said, almost brightly. “That is very well. Monsieur will travel in company. It is a good thing to travel thus in company—sometimes.”

The last word fell like a stone into water. The brief light in him dimmed again.

They came to a house slightly larger than its neighbours, stone-built, with the shield of Alberic de Mauléon carved over the door: a canon of Comminges, 1680 to 1701, collateral kin, Dennistoun later learned, to Bishop John de Mauléon. The upper windows were boarded. The building had that shut, unused look of things not lived in but merely endured.

On the doorstep the sacristan paused.

“Perhaps,” he said, “perhaps, after all, monsieur has not the time?”

“Plenty of time,” Dennistoun answered. “Nothing to do till tomorrow. Let us see it.”

The door opened, and a young face looked out—his daughter’s. She was handsome enough, but the same distress marked her as her father: not quite fear for herself, rather a strained anxiety on behalf of someone else, like a person listening for a child’s cough in the next room. She brightened when she saw her father with an able-bodied stranger. A few quick words passed between them; Dennistoun caught the sacristan saying, “He was laughing in the church,” and the girl answered with a look of terror that seemed to put out the little warmth in the room before they had even entered it.

The sitting-room was small and high, its stone floor cold underfoot. A wood-fire flickered on a great hearth, sending up a smell of resin and smoke that mixed with the house’s older scent of damp stone and stored cloth. Moving shadows ran up the walls. A tall crucifix stood on one side, nearly reaching the ceiling: the figure painted in natural colours, the cross black, severe as a nail. Beneath it stood an old chest, heavy and solid as if it had been made to keep things not merely safe but shut away.

A lamp was brought. Chairs were set. The sacristan went to the chest and, with a nervous excitement that seemed to rise from his stomach to his hands, drew out a large book wrapped in a white cloth. On the cloth a cross had been crudely embroidered in red thread, the sort of red that suggests dried berries—or dried blood. Even before the wrapping came away Dennistoun felt the size and shape and knew it was no common service-book.

The cloth fell back. He looked down into a folio, bound, he guessed, late seventeenth century, with Canon Alberic de Mauléon’s arms stamped in gold. The paper leaves were thick, and on almost every one was pasted a leaf from an illuminated manuscript. The sight struck Dennistoun like strong food after fasting. The mind, greedy by nature, began at once to count and catalogue.

Here were ten leaves from Genesis, with pictures that could not be later than A.D. 700—colour laid on like ground spices, still bright in places, the tiny strokes fresh as if the hand that set them had only just lifted its brush. Further on, a complete set of pictures from a Psalter, English work, the finest thirteenth century could do: saints and beasts and vines curling with a life that did not belong to paper at all. And best—perhaps best—twenty leaves of uncial Latin from some very early unknown patristic treatise. A few words were enough to give Dennistoun a jolt of recognition and longing. Could it be Papias, “On the Words of Our Lord,” known to have existed at Nîmes as late as the twelfth century? Whatever it was, his mind made its decision with the cold, quick certainty of appetite: this book must go back to Cambridge, even if he drained his account and waited here for the money to arrive.

He glanced up, trying to read whether the treasure was offered or merely shown. The sacristan’s lips worked soundlessly; his face was drawn pale.

“If monsieur will turn on to the end,” he said.

Dennistoun turned, meeting marvel after marvel as each leaf rose. Then, at the end, two sheets of more recent paper lay like a bad aftertaste among sweet things. He took them for contemporary with the canon himself—Alberic, the unprincipled, who had surely plundered the Chapter library to make this scrap-book.

The first sheet bore a plan—carefully drawn—of the south aisle and cloisters of St Bertrand’s. In the corners were curious signs like planetary symbols, and a few Hebrew words. In the north-west angle of the cloister a cross had been drawn in gold paint. Beneath were lines of Latin:

_Responsa 12mi Dec. 1694. Interrogatum est: Inveniamne? Responsum est: Invenies. Fiamne dives? Fies. Vivamne invidendus? Vives. Moriarne in lecto meo? Ita._

Dennistoun smiled, the scholar’s smile that tries to domesticate the uncanny by naming it familiar. “A good specimen of the treasure-hunter’s record,” he said—thinking of romances and minor-canons and buried pots of coin. He turned the leaf.

What he saw then—he told me often—was beyond what any drawing ought to do to a man. The original is gone now, but I possess a photograph, and it bears out his horror.

It was a sepia drawing, late seventeenth century, an interior with semi-classical architecture, as artists of that time gave to Biblical scenes. On the right sat a king on a throne raised by twelve steps, canopy overhead, soldiers flanking him: King Solomon, one would say at first glance. He bent forward, sceptre outstretched, in an attitude of command. His face held horror and disgust and yet that confident imperiousness of a man who believes his word is law even in nightmare.

But the left side dragged the eye as a wound drags the tongue. On the pavement before the throne four soldiers stood grouped around a crouching figure—such a figure as makes the mind recoil and yet, horribly, insist on looking again. A fifth soldier lay dead, neck twisted, eyes bulging from his head with the obscene surprise of sudden death. The four living guards stared toward the king. Their terror was sharpened by discipline: they looked ready to flee but held by trust in their master, as if kingship were a charm.

And in their midst crouched the thing.

At first one saw only a mass of coarse matted black hair. Then the hair resolved into covering for a body of fearful thinness, almost skeletal, but with muscles standing out like wire. The hands—dusky, pale in a way that suggested skin stretched too tight over bone—were covered with long coarse hairs; the fingers ended in nails that rose and curved sharply down and forward, grey and horny and wrinkled. The eyes were touched with burning yellow; the pupils black and intense; and the gaze fixed on the king was beast-hate, intelligent and deliberate. There was an impression—common to all who saw it—that the artist had not invented. It looked drawn from life.

Dennistoun, his stomach turned as if by rancid fat, stole a glance at his hosts. The sacristan had his hands pressed over his eyes. His daughter looked up at the crucifix and told her beads with frantic speed, as if the clicking prayers might fill the air and leave no room for other sounds.

At last Dennistoun asked, steadying his voice as one steadies a knife hand, “Is this book for sale?”

Again the sacristan hesitated; again that plunge of resolve.

“If monsieur pleases.”

“How much?”

“I will take two hundred and fifty francs.”

Dennistoun was struck dumb. Even a collector’s conscience can wake; his was tenderer than most.

“My good man—your book is worth far more than that. Far more, I assure you.”

The sacristan did not move from his price. “Two hundred and fifty francs. Not more.”

There was no refusing such a thing. Dennistoun paid. A receipt was signed. They drank a glass of wine over the transaction—ordinary country wine with a scent of cask and sour cherries; the kind that warms the throat but does not cheer the mind. And as soon as the money was in the sacristan’s hands, a remarkable change came over him. He straightened. The hunted backward glances ceased. He even laughed, or tried to; the sound was thin, like a pot tapped with a spoon.

Dennistoun rose to go.

“I shall have the honour of accompanying monsieur to his hotel?” the sacristan asked quickly.

“Oh no, thanks. It isn’t a hundred yards. I know the way. There’s a moon.”

The offer was pressed, refused, pressed again, refused again, as if the older man were trying to place his body between Dennistoun and something that might leap from a doorway. At last he said, “Then monsieur will summon me if—if he finds occasion. He will keep the middle of the road; the sides are so rough.”

“Certainly,” Dennistoun answered, impatient now to be alone with his prize, and stepped into the passage with the book under his arm.

There the daughter met him. Her face held that same strained earnestness, and she spoke as one offering not a gift but a remedy.

“A silver crucifix and chain for the neck,” she said. “Monsieur would perhaps be good enough to accept it?”

Dennistoun protested he had no use for it; what did she ask?

“Nothing—nothing in the world. Monsieur is more than welcome.”

The sincerity in her tone left him with nowhere to stand but gratitude. He thanked her profusely. She placed the chain around his neck, the metal cool against his skin, its weight oddly insistent, as if it wished to be noticed. It seemed as though he had done them some great service without knowing it. When he set off with his book, father and daughter stood in the doorway watching him as if they were counting his steps. They were still watching when he turned on the inn steps and waved good night.

Dinner at the Chapeau Rouge was done. Dennistoun went upstairs to his room and shut the door. He set the folio on the table as carefully as one sets a rising loaf away from drafts. The landlady had been unusually attentive since hearing he had bought a book from the sacristan; her laugh, too, had grated on him—an uncheerful sound, as though someone were dead in the house and she were determined not to speak of it. He thought he had caught a hurried dialogue in the passage between her and the sacristan: words like “Pierre and Bertrand will be sleeping in the house,” and then a silence that thickened.

A discomfort had been creeping over Dennistoun all evening—perhaps mere reaction from excitement, perhaps the building’s late-hour noises still vibrating in him. It settled into a conviction that someone stood behind him. He found himself more comfortable with his back to the wall, like a man eating in company he does not trust. Still, the book’s richness overruled all. He opened it, leaf by leaf, and the room filled with the dry fragrance of old paper and paste, the faint sweet rot of centuries, and the ghost of pigments ground long ago. His eyes fed on the little scenes; his fingers itched to trace the strokes he dared not touch.

“Bless Canon Alberic,” he muttered—Dennistoun had the habit of speaking to himself as some men hum while they work. “I wonder where he is now? Dear me, I wish that landlady would learn to laugh in a more cheering manner; it makes one feel as if there was someone dead in the house. Half a pipe more, did you say? Perhaps you’re right. And that crucifix the girl gave me—last century, I suppose. It’s rather a nuisance, just too heavy. I might clean it up before I put it away.”

He lifted the chain over his head and laid the crucifix on the table. The metal made a small, dull sound on the cloth. As he did so his attention snagged on an object on the red table-cover near his left elbow. For a fraction of time his mind offered harmless names, as it does when it cannot yet accept what the senses have delivered.

“A penwiper? No—there’s no such thing here. A rat? No—too black. A large spider? God forbid—no. Good God—a hand like the hand in that picture!”

In the next instant he saw it fully: pale dusky skin stretched over bone and tendon of appalling strength; long coarse black hairs; nails rising and curving down, grey, horny, wrinkled. A hand—resting as casually as a guest’s hand might rest—on his table.

He sprang up with a terror so violent it seemed to seize his heart in a fist. Behind his chair something was rising to a standing posture. Its left hand lay on the table; its right hand crooked above his scalp as if testing the air for the place to strike. Black tattered drapery hung about it. Coarse hair covered it as in the drawing. The lower jaw was thin—shallow, almost bestial; teeth showed behind black lips; there was no nose. The eyes were fiery yellow, the pupils black and intense, and in them burned an exulting hate and thirst for life’s destruction that no animal could hold. Yet there was intelligence in the gaze—intelligence above a beast and below a man, as if it had learned enough of us to despise us.

Dennistoun’s fear was physical, like nausea; his loathing was mental, like a stain spreading across thought. What could he do? Later he could not be certain what words he spoke—only that he spoke, that his hand went blindly to the silver crucifix, that the thing moved toward him, and that he screamed with the voice of an animal in hideous pain.

Pierre and Bertrand—the two sturdy little serving-men—burst in. They saw nothing. Yet something passed out between them, thrusting them aside as if by a body that had weight and purpose. They found Dennistoun on the floor in a swoon.

They sat up with him that night. His friends arrived by nine next morning. He was shaken and nervous still, but near himself enough to tell them what had happened. At first they listened as friends do, half believing because they must, half resisting because they fear what belief would mean. Then they saw the drawing at the end of the folio, and their faces changed. After that, they spoke with the sacristan.

The little man came to the inn almost at dawn on some pretence and listened with intense interest to the landlady’s account of the night’s alarm. He showed no surprise.

“It is he—it is he! I have seen him myself,” was all he said; and to every question he returned only the same words, stubborn as a refrain: “_Deux fois je l’ai vu; mille fois je l’ai senti._” Twice I have seen him; a thousand times I have felt him.

He would say nothing of where the book had come from, nor of what had happened in his own house, nor of what Canon Alberic had done to earn such attendance. Only once, when pressed, did he answer with a weary gentleness that made his daughter begin to sob in the next room.

“I shall soon sleep,” he said, “and my rest will be sweet. Why should you trouble me?”

He died that summer. His daughter married and settled at St Papoul, never understanding the true circumstances of her father’s obsession.

What the canon suffered, or what bargains he struck in a language he could not wholly speak, we shall never know. Yet even in Dennistoun’s hands, the scrap-book kept a final hint of its own history: on the back of that fateful drawing were lines of writing—lines that might have explained the power and the price, had they not seemed to retreat from meaning the more one stared, like a dialect one has almost forgotten, whose sounds stir memory but refuse translation.
