CHAPTER XXIII. Though Elinor had never placed much trust in Lucy’s truthfulness, she could no longer doubt her now—especially when the weight of evidence was so overwhelming and the motive for lying so utterly absent. Lucy’s claim, supported by undeniable facts—their early acquaintance at Mr. Pratt’s house, Edward’s melancholy visit near Plymouth, his restless uncertainty, his inconsistent behavior toward her, the intimate knowledge the Miss Steeles had of Norland and their family, not to mention the portrait, the letter, and the ring—formed a chain of proof too strong to ignore. It was not mere suspicion; it was certainty. Edward had deceived her. And though the sting of betrayal burned fiercely at first, Elinor soon found herself caring more for him than for herself.

Had he meant to mislead her? Had he pretended affection he did not feel? Was his promise to Lucy one born of love? She doubted it. Whatever it had once been, it was no longer real. His heart belonged to her—she knew that with absolute conviction. Their shared history, the quiet glances, the unspoken understanding at Norland, the way even Fanny Dashwood had noticed his regard—none of it was illusion. He loved her. That belief softened the wound, making forgiveness almost possible.

Yet how deeply he had wronged himself! While she might heal, what future awaited him? Could he truly be happy with Lucy Steele? A woman whose mind was narrow, whose charm was superficial, whose character lacked depth? The boyish infatuation of nineteen might have blinded him to her flaws, but four years of maturity should have revealed them. Meanwhile, Lucy, removed from better company and immersed in trivial pursuits, may have lost the gentle simplicity that once made her appealing. Her beauty remained, but without grace or substance, it would soon fade into emptiness.

And now, instead of marrying a woman of equal standing and spirit, Edward faced an engagement to someone far beneath him in fortune and connection. If before, his mother’s opposition had seemed daunting, it was nothing compared to the resistance such a match would provoke. Yet if Edward’s heart was already turned away from Lucy, perhaps even the thought of familial disapproval brought him some comfort—a strange relief in his sorrow.

As these thoughts followed one another like shadows across her mind, Elinor wept—not for herself, but for him. She clung to the certainty that she had done nothing to deserve this pain, and that Edward, despite his fault, had not forfeited her respect. With that inner strength, she managed to conceal her anguish from her family. When she joined them at dinner just two hours after learning the truth, no trace of her suffering showed. To all eyes, she appeared calm, composed—while within, her soul was breaking.

She had chosen to keep the secret. Not because she feared their judgment, but because she knew they would grieve too deeply, and worse, condemn Edward out of love for her. Their sorrow would only deepen her own. She needed no counsel, no praise, no pity. Alone, she was stronger. Her reason held firm, her composure unwavering—though the wound was fresh and deep.

Still, she longed to speak with Lucy again. Not out of bitterness, but to understand. She wanted to hear the full story once more, to gauge the sincerity behind Lucy’s claims of devotion. And above all, she wished to show Lucy—through her calmness, her restraint—that she was not wounded, not jealous, not consumed by passion. She feared that her earlier agitation had made Lucy think otherwise. And indeed, Lucy’s jealousy was likely. Edward had always spoken of Elinor with admiration—not just in words, but in actions: trusting her with a secret so intimate, revealing it so soon. Even Sir John’s teasing remark carried weight.

But Elinor understood Lucy’s motives. Her confession was not kindness—it was strategy. To warn Elinor off, to assert her own claim. And Elinor saw through it. Yet she resolved to act honorably. She would avoid Edward, suppress her feelings, and remain true to her principles. But she would also prove to Lucy that her heart was not broken.

She was ready to face the conversation again. There was no new pain left to endure. So when the opportunity arose, she seized it.

The weather kept them indoors, and meetings at the park or cottage offered little privacy. They gathered for meals, laughter, games—no room for serious talk. Then came Sir John’s invitation: Lady Middleton, lonely without him, begged them to dine. Elinor saw a chance. Without her husband’s boisterous energy, the gathering might be quieter, more suited to quiet conversation. She accepted. Margaret followed, Marianne reluctantly agreed under maternal pressure, and they all went.

The evening was as dull as expected. No spark of thought or feeling stirred. The children joined them in the drawing room, and Elinor realized any attempt to speak privately would fail. Only when tea was cleared and cards were laid out did hope return.

Lady Middleton, noticing Lucy’s embroidery, said, “I’m glad you’re not finishing Annamaria’s basket tonight. Candlelight must strain your eyes. We’ll make up for it tomorrow.”

Lucy replied quickly, “You’re mistaken, Lady Middleton. I’ve been waiting to see if you need me at the table. I’d never disappoint the dear child. If you do want me now, I’ll finish it after supper.”

“Ah, how kind,” said Lady Middleton. “Would you ring for working candles? Poor Annamaria would be heartbroken if it weren’t done.”

Lucy moved her worktable at once, smiling brightly. The others drew cards for Casino. Marianne, ever impervious to social norms, declared, “Your Ladyship will excuse me—I detest cards. I shall go to the piano-forte. I haven’t played since it was tuned.” And she walked away without a glance back.

Lady Middleton looked relieved. “Marianne can never stay away from that instrument,” Elinor said gently. “And I don’t blame her—it’s the finest piano I’ve ever heard.”

Then, as the game began, Elinor added, “If I cut out, perhaps I could help Miss Steele with her papers. There’s so much still to do on the basket—surely she can’t finish alone. I’d enjoy helping.”

“Oh, I’d be most grateful!” Lucy exclaimed. “There’s more work than I thought. It would be dreadful to let poor Annamaria down.”

“Oh, indeed!” said Miss Steele. “How I adore that little angel!”

Elinor smiled. “Then I’ll wait until the next rubber,” she said, choosing to delay her turn.

Lady Middleton approved. “Very kind. You may take your chance later.”

Thus, Elinor gained her seat beside Lucy, both focused on the delicate filigree work. The piano nearby played its soft, distracting melody. And as Marianne lost herself in music and memory, Elinor judged the moment right. She could speak—without fear of being overheard—and begin the conversation she had waited for.
