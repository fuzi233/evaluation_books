CHAPTER IV. “_Gorgibus._— * * * Je te dis que le mariage est une chose sainte et sacrée: et que c’est faire en honnêtes gens, que de débuter par là.”
“_Madelon._—Mon Dieu! que si tout le monde vous ressemblait, un roman serait bientôt fini! La belle chose que ce serait, si d’abord Cyrus épousait Mandane, et qu’Aronce de plain-pied fût marié à Clélie! * * * Laissez-nous faire à loisir le tissu de notre roman, et n’en pressez pas tant la conclusion.”
MOLIÈRE. _Les Précieuses Ridicules._

It was not surprising that the rector of Pennicote, in his thoughtful reflections on life and character, should see in Gwendolen Harleth a girl destined for something extraordinary—a brilliant marriage, perhaps, such as would bring honor to her family and grace to any social circle. Why should he be expected to think otherwise? In an age where a woman’s future was often measured by the rank and fortune of her husband, it was natural enough for him to hope that his niece would rise above the ordinary, especially when she already stood out so clearly from those around her. And yet, though he entertained such expectations, his feelings were rooted in kindness rather than ambition. He did not wish her to suffer or sacrifice; he simply wanted her to shine where she belonged—among the best company, in the most favorable light.

His intentions matched perfectly with Gwendolen’s own desires. But let no one imagine that she saw marriage as her ultimate goal, the grand climax of her existence. While she accepted that she would marry someday, she had no particular longing for it. The idea of becoming a wife, bound by duties and children and domestic routine, held little appeal. To her, marriage seemed more like a quiet surrender—something necessary, yes, but hardly glorious. She had watched others settle into it, and what she saw was not enchantment, but weariness. Women became pale, preoccupied, their spirits dulled by repetition and constraint. Marriage might elevate one socially, but it could never fulfill the deeper hunger within her—the need to lead, to command attention, to shape her world rather than merely inhabit it.

She meant to be admired, certainly, and to be desired—not for herself alone, but as a symbol of power. To be pursued, to be envied, to be spoken of with breathless admiration—that was a kind of triumph. But to be confined by a man’s will, to lose her independence behind closed doors, that was another matter entirely. She would not become a mere ornament in someone else’s home. No, she intended to live vividly, to act decisively, to make every moment count. Her ambitions were not grand in the way of political influence or intellectual reform, but they were no less fierce. She wanted to do what pleased her—and to do it in a way that made others feel the force of her presence.

“Gwendolen will not rest until she has the world at her feet,” said Miss Merry, the gentle governess, using words that had long since lost their edge through overuse. Yet even in their exaggeration, there was truth. For who among us does not speak of having “the world at one’s feet” when meaning only a few flattering attentions, a place at a fashionable dinner, or a nod from a titled neighbor? Still, for Gwendolen, such phrases carried real weight. They hinted at a destiny beyond the ordinary, a life lived on a higher plane.

Others drifted through life, shaped by circumstance, pulled this way and that by forces they could not control. Not she. She refused to be sacrificed to lesser people, to be treated as a passive object of affection or duty. She would seize her chances, use her talents, and master the world around her—not through force, but through charm, intelligence, and sheer self-assurance.

Her situation at Offendene offered little in the way of dramatic opportunity. Lady Brackenshaw’s notice, the archery club, dinners with the Arrowpoints—these were modest pleasures, nothing more. Yet Gwendolen did not rely on her surroundings. Her confidence came from within. She believed herself well prepared for whatever life might bring. Her education had been thorough enough, though not profound. In school, her quick mind absorbed facts and rules without question, shielding her from any sense of ignorance. Beyond that, novels, plays, and poetry had filled in the gaps, giving her a broad understanding of human nature and society.

Her French and music were polished, her manners impeccable. And then there was that rare gift—what some call instinct, others intuition—the ability to judge any subject with ease and authority, simply because she chose to engage with it. It was this innate certainty, this feeling of being naturally equipped, that made her believe she could handle anything.

Of course, there were many things she found dull—subjects too dry, too slow, too irrelevant to her spirit. But she wouldn’t have felt helpless if they arose in conversation. She knew how to listen, how to respond, how to turn even the most tedious topic into a moment of elegance.

No one had ever challenged her superiority. From the moment she arrived at Offendene, everyone asked, *What will Gwendolen think?* If the footman clumped loudly down the hall, it was because “This will never do for Miss Harleth.” If the laundry was imperfect, the maid fretted: “This won’t suit Miss Harleth.” Even when the fireplace smoked, Mrs. Davilow apologized, fearing Gwendolen’s discomfort.

And when she finally appeared at breakfast—late, always late, her golden-brown hair tumbling freely over her shoulders, her eyes bright and sharp beneath long lashes—no one dared complain. Instead, the staff scrambled to serve her coffee piping hot, toast crisp, everything perfect. She was the princess, the guest of honor, the one whose comfort mattered above all.

Why was she treated so? Was it only her beauty? Her grace? Her position as the eldest daughter, born into a family burdened by misfortune, whose mother still bore guilt over the harshness of her second husband?

Perhaps. But consider this: I have seen similar deference paid to men and women far less attractive, far less remarkable in manner or birth. Some were common, even unremarkable. Yet they commanded the same attention—because they demanded it. Because they had a quiet, relentless will, a refusal to accept anything less than what they wanted. And if they didn’t get it? Then they threatened consequences—real or imagined. Fear, after all, is a powerful motivator.

So maybe Gwendolen’s power wasn’t just in her looks or her lineage. Maybe it was in her spirit—the unyielding belief in her own worth, the courage to assert it, and the subtle ability to inspire both fear and affection. People loved her, yes—but they also respected her, even feared her. And that fear was not cold or cruel, but tinged with admiration. For she was not simply one thing. She could be tender and fierce, playful and commanding, generous and demanding—all at once. Like light refracted through a prism, her character shimmered with contradictions.

Macbeth once declared that no man could be both loyal and treacherous in the same instant. But that was about action, not emotion. A moment can hold many truths. One can desire loyalty while secretly planning betrayal. One can feel remorse even as the knife rises. And so, too, Gwendolen could be beloved and feared, admired and challenged, all at once—because she was not merely a woman, but a force.
