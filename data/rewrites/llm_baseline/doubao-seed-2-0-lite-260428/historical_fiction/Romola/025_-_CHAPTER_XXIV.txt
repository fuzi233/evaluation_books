The heavy velvet curtain strung across the Duomo’s side doorway swished shut behind Baldassarre, his wrists raw where the hemp cord binding them rubbed against his skin, a second length of the same rough rope looped tight around his throat and torso. He had stumbled there half-expecting the vast marble nave to be all empty stillness: unlit candles guttering on half-shrouded side altars, pale statues rigid as corpses, a handful of doddering old worshippers far off in the choir chanting in droning, monotonous voices. That was the only sort of church he had ever known, the kind a man with no religious purpose entered only to hide.

But what met his gaze was no dead, silent space. Thousands of warm, breathing faces packed every aisle and cranny, all tilted upward in breathless, dead silence toward the pulpit set at the angle where the nave curved to meet the choir. Every rank of Florentine life was crammed shoulder to shoulder: crimson-robed magistrates near the front, silk-gowned noblewomen with pearl hairpins glinting in the candlelight, dye-streaked artisans still in their work tunics at the back, sun-browned peasants tramping in from the hill villages standing huddled in the side aisles. Up in the pulpit, a Dominican friar with sharp, strong features and dark hair held a plain wooden crucifix clamped tight in his fist as he preached.

For the first long minute, Baldassarre did not hear a single word he said. Even quiet as his entrance had been, a handful of eyes near the door flicked to him, sharp with surprise and suspicion. The rope made plain he was an escaped prisoner – but every man on the run knew the Duomo was a sanctuary he had every right to claim. His grey streaked hair and the wild, haggard misery carved deep into his face stirred pity more than fear, and as he stood motionless, his gaze drifting from the vast throng down to the worn marble pavement under his bare feet, the onlookers who had marked his presence soon turned back, drawn again to the far greater pull of the sermon.

One of those pairs of eyes that had lingered on him was Romola’s. She had slipped in late through a far side door, positioned so she had a full view of the main entryway, and the old man’s grey hair and the raw red line the rope left on his neck tugged at that long-nurtured softness she had always carried for the unspoken sorrows of old age. She thought for a moment their gazes had locked in that first wandering glance of his, but Baldassarre had never noticed her at all. His was only a fleeting, startled awareness of the scene around him, a flicker that did nothing to break the red, raging tumult of emotion left by his brutal run-in with Tito. Old half-mad memories of the man he had raised swirled through his head, tangled with the burning thirst in his throat and the ache of his broken body. No clear plan for the future had yet formed amid the fire of his hatred; the closest he could come to any thought of what came next was the bitter, searing knowledge that his strength was failing him, and a vague, ironclad resolution to trust no living soul ever again.

Then a sound rolled over the crowd, deep enough to vibrate in his very bones: a voice ringing with such absolute, triumphant certainty it felt like the echo of his own rage given flesh. “The day of vengeance is at hand!”

Baldassarre quivered so hard his teeth chattered, and wrenched his gaze upward. He was too far back to make out more than the broad shape of the preacher, one arm outstretched, the crucifix held high above his head – and he found himself panting to hear that threatening voice again, as if it was the promise of water to a man dying of thirst in the desert.

There was a pause. Slowly the preacher lowered his arm, set the crucifix down on the edge of the pulpit, and crossed his arms over his chest, sweeping his gaze over the multitude as if he meant to lock eyes with every single man and woman there.

“All of you in Florence are my witnesses – I did not speak my words in some hidden corner. You saw, four years ago, when no sign of war or tribulation had yet touched our land, that I preached of the scourge that would come. I lifted my voice like a trumpet to the prelates, the princes, the people of all Italy, and said: The cup of your iniquity is full. Behold, the thunder of the Lord is gathering, and it shall fall and shatter that cup, and the sin you have sipped like sweet wine shall pour out over you as molten lead. To you, O priests, who laugh and say ‘There is no Presence in the sanctuary – the Shechinah is nothing, the Mercy-seat is bare: we may sin behind the veil and none shall punish us,’ I said then: the presence of God shall reveal itself in His temple as a consuming fire. Your sacred vestments shall become winding sheets of flame, your sweet choirs shall turn to shrieks and hissing, your soft couches shall sprout thorns, and the breath of the wantons you harbor shall give birth to pestilence.

“Put no trust in your gold and silver, no trust in your high walled fortresses. Though your walls were forged of iron, your towers hewn from adamant, the Most High shall pour terror into your hearts and confusion into your councils, till you turn and flee like frightened women. He shall shatter mighty men beyond number, and raise up others in their place. For God will no longer suffer the pollution of His sanctuary; He will purge His Church entirely clean.

“As it is written, God does nothing without revealing His purpose to His servants the prophets. He has chosen me, His unworthy servant, and made His will burn in my soul through the living word of Scripture and the unmistakeable work of His providence. Through the ministry of angels He has shown me His purpose in visions, so that His word possesses me as the wind of heaven possesses a dry branch in the forest. I cannot keep silent, even when the scorner mocks me. For four years I have preached in obedience to His will, through all the scoffing: that in these times, God will regenerate His Church, that before that regeneration a scourge shall sweep across all Italy, and that these things shall come swiftly upon you.

“But the hypocrites who cloak their hatred of truth in a show of piety say to me, ‘Come now, Frate, lay down your prophesyings. It is enough for you to teach virtue.’ To them I answer: You whisper in your hearts that God lives far away, that His word is no more than parchment written by dead men, that He no longer walks among us to rebuke nations, punish oppressors, and strike down unholy priests as He struck down the sons of Eli. But I cry it again in your ears: God is near, not far off. His judgments never change. He is the God of armies. The strong men who march to battle are His ministers, no less than the storm, the fire, the pestilence. He drives them on by the breath of His angels, and they come upon the chosen land that has forsaken His covenant. And thou, O Italy, art that chosen land. Did not God set His sanctuary within your borders, and have you not polluted it? Behold, the ministers of His wrath are upon you – they are at your very doors!”

Savonarola’s voice had risen, growing more fiery and forceful with every word, until he fell suddenly silent, his hands dropping to clasp loosely before him. His hush was no signal for small stirrings or whispers in the crowd; it wrapped around them like a spell as strong as his words. Men and women across the whole vast cathedral sat motionless, faces upturned like breathing statues, until his voice sounded again, clear and low.

“Yet there is a pause – even as there was a pause before Jerusalem fell, so that the children of God might flee from the coming ruin. There is a stillness before the storm: see the black clouds roiling overhead, and not a single leaf trembles. The winds are stayed, so that the voice of God’s warning may be heard. Hear it now, O Florence, chosen city in the chosen land! Repent, turn away from evil. Do justice. Love mercy. Cast every unclean thing out from among you, that the spirit of truth and holiness may fill your souls and breathe through every street and home, and then the pestilence shall not cross your threshold, and the sword shall pass over you and leave you unhurt.

“For the sword hangs from the sky even now, quivering, ready to fall! The sword of God upon the earth, swift and sudden! Did I not tell you years ago that I had seen the vision, heard the voice? Behold, it is being fulfilled! Is there not a king with his army at your gates? Does not the very earth shake with the thunder of their horses’ hooves and the wheels of their swift cannon? Is there not a fierce multitude that can lay the land bare as a sharp razor? I tell you the French king and his army are the ministers of God. God shall guide him as a man guides a sharp sickle, and the joints of the wicked shall melt before him, and they shall be mown down like stubble. None who flee shall escape, none who run shall be delivered. The tyrants who built their thrones out of the vices of the crowd, the unbelieving priests who traffic in men’s souls and fill the sanctuary itself with fornication, shall be hurled from their soft couches into burning hell. Even the pagans and those who sinned under the old covenant shall stand afar off and say, ‘See these men, who brought the stench of a new wickedness into the everlasting fire.’

“But thou, O Florence, take the mercy that is offered you. See! The Cross is held out to you: come, and be healed. What other nation in Italy has been given a sign such as yours? The tyrant has been driven out from among you. The men who held a bribe in their left hand and a rod in their right are gone, and not a single drop of blood was spilled. Now cast every other abomination from among you, and you shall be strong in the strength of the living God. Wash yourselves clean from the black pitch of your vices, that have made you no better than the heathen. Cast out the envy and hatred that have turned your city into a nest of wolves. No harm shall come to you. The passing of armies shall feel no more threatening than a flock of birds flying overhead. Rebellious Pisa shall be returned to you, famine and pestilence shall be kept far from your gates, and you shall stand as a beacon among all nations. But mark this: so long as you suffer the accursed thing to remain in your camp, you shall be tormented and afflicted, even if a small remnant among you is spared.”

His tone had been sharp, authoritative for those warnings and promises, but now his voice softened, melting into raw, tender entreaty.

“Listen, O people, over whom my heart yearns as a mother’s heart yearns for the children she has travailed to bring into the world! God is my witness: if not for your sakes, I would happily live as a turtledove deep in the forest, singing low to my Beloved, who is mine and I am His. For you I toil, for you I languish, for you my nights are spent in watching, and my soul melts away with heaviness. O Lord, Thou knowest I am willing, I am ready. Take me, stretch me on Thy cross. Let the wicked who delight in blood, rob the poor, defile the temple of their own bodies, and harden their hearts against Thy mercy – let them wag their heads and shoot out their lips at me. Let the thorns press into my brow, let my sweat turn to anguish. I long to be made like Thee in that great love. But let me see the fruit of my travail. Let this people be saved. Let me see them clothed in purity. Let me hear their voices rise in concord, like the voices of angels. Let them know no wisdom but in Thy eternal law, no beauty but in holiness. Then they shall lead the way before all nations, and peoples from the four winds shall follow them, and be gathered into the fold of the blessed. For it is Thy will, O God, that the earth shall be converted to Thy law. It is Thy will that wickedness shall cease, and love shall reign. Come, O blessed promise. Behold, I am willing. Lay me on the altar. Let my blood flow, let the fire consume me, so that my witness shall be remembered among men forever: that iniquity shall never prosper for all time.”

As he spoke that last plea, Savonarola stretched his arms wide and lifted his eyes to the vaulted ceiling high above. His strong voice trembled with emotion one moment, then rose again in renewed, blazing energy, until the passion of his vow to offer himself as a victim grew too great for speech, and he broke off in a sob.

Every shifting tone rippled through the crowd, stirring them to answering emotion. Many there had only a lukewarm faith in the Frate’s prophetic mission, and hated him in their cooler, calmer moments. But even they were swept up by the great wave of feeling, rooted in sympathies that ran far deeper than any doctrine. A loud, collective sob rose up from the multitude at once, as Savonarola dropped to his knees and buried his face in the folds of his Dominican mantle. In that moment, he felt the rapture and glory of martyrdom, without the pain.

Baldassarre’s sob tangled right into that great roar of sound. Of all the thousands of souls packed into the Duomo, there was scarcely one whose frame vibrated more fiercely to the preacher’s words than his – but it vibrated like a harp from which every string had been torn away save one. That threat of fiery, unrelenting vengeance, of a future where the hated sinner could be pursued forever, held fast by the avenger in an eternal grapple, had come to him like the promise of an unquenchable spring to a man burning with unquenchable thirst. All his old learning, his old learned contempt for priestly superstitions, fell away from his soul like a forgotten tongue. What use could they ever be to his great, ravenous need, beside the answer this voice of absolute conviction gave him? The thunder of the preacher’s denunciation struck his passion-wrought nerves with all the force of self-evident truth. He never stopped to question it. He was possessed by it, as a war-horse is possessed by the sound of clashing weapons. No word that was not a threat touched his consciousness; he had no fibre left in him to thrill to anything else.

But the fierce, exultant delight that flooded him at the thought of endless vengeance found its climax, its release, in the preacher’s words of self-sacrifice. To Baldassarre, those words brought a vague, triumphant certainty that he too was devoting himself – signing his name in his own blood to the deed that gave himself over to an unending fire, a fire that would feel no hotter than cool water against his burning hatred.

“I rescued him. I cherished him. Let me clutch his heartstrings forever! Come, O blessed promise. Let my blood flow. Let the fire consume me!”

That last remaining string in his soul hummed at its utmost pitch. Baldassarre drove his long, sharp nails deep into the palms of his hands, till blood beaded up under his fingers, and burst into a sob right alongside the rest of the weeping multitude.
