The ordeal could not fail to sharpen Mr. Gryce's focus, hardening his resolve to unravel the labyrinthine secrets of the case. Upon returning to Headquarters, where his prolonged absence had undoubtedly sparked anxiety among his subordinates, his first inquiry concerned Butler Bartow. Had the man recovered his faculties during the night?

The reply offered no solace. Bartow remained unchanged, and the lunacy specialist consulted regarding his condition had delivered a verdict grim for immediate recovery. Mr. Gryce's countenance grew somber. Summoning the officer who had overseen Bartow's arrest, he asked how the mute servant had behaved upon realizing his confinement.

The officer's answer was brief but telling.

"Surprised, sir," he reported. "Shook his head and performed some peculiar gestures, then launched into his pantomime. It is quite a spectacle, sir. Poor fellow, he constantly holds his hand back."

Gryce observed the gesture closely; it mirrored exactly the motion Bartow had made upon first noticing an audience. Its intent remained obscure. The officer had used his right hand—there was no indication of left-handedness—and maintained the posture as if attempting to signal a fact that might justify his release.

"Do you observe any mood swings? Does his expression shift between fear and rage?"

"It fluctuates, sir. One moment he appears ready to drop asleep; the next, he erupts in fury, shaking his head and battering the walls. It is not a pleasant sight, sir. He requires constant vigilance."

"Leave him be, but monitor every alteration in his demeanor. His testimony may lack validity, yet there is significance in every movement he makes. Tomorrow, I shall visit him personally."

As the officer departed, Gryce sat alone for a brief interval, engaging in solitary reflection. From his pocket, he produced a small packet and emptied five tiny spangles onto his desk. He scrutinized them intently. It was a habit of his; gazing at trivial objects served to concentrate his scattered thoughts. After a moment of doubtful contemplation, he swept the sequins back into the envelope, glanced at his watch, and moved swiftly into the outer office. Pausing before a line of waiting citizens, he beckoned to a young man who had tracked his movements with an intensity that had not escaped the veteran detective's notice. Leading the youth back to his private room, Gryce closed the door.

"You require a hand in this matter?" he asked, his tone interrogative.

"Oh, sir—" the young man began, his face glowing with an enthusiasm that rendered his plain features striking. "I do not presume..."

"Enough!" interrupted Gryce. "You have been here six months without demonstrating any exceptional adaptability. Now, I intend to test your capabilities with something genuinely difficult. Can you rise to the occasion, Sweetwater? Do you know the city well enough to locate a needle in this vast haystack?"

"I would at least like to try," Sweetwater responded eagerly. "Success would be a greater feather in my cap than living in New York forever. I have been longing for such an opportunity. I assure you I will approach this judiciously, if only out of gratitude."

"We shall see," remarked the older detective. "If you crave difficulty, you will have ample supply. Indeed, I am asking for the impossible. We know of a woman only because she was last seen wearing a dress heavily bespangled with black, and because she carried a parasol during her visit to Mr. Adams prior to the murder—a parasol from which I can procure a glimpse before you depart. Her origins and destination remain unknown; that is your mission. You are not the sole operative assigned to this task, which, as you perceive, borders on hopelessness unless she voluntarily presents herself. I would despair entirely of your success were it not for one specific fact, which I shall now share as my confidential instruction. Just as she vanished from the single eye watching her, she approached the curbstone before Hudson's fruit store on Fourteenth Street and raised her right hand, *thus*. It is a slender clue, but it is all I possess aside from these five spangles retrieved from her gown and my conviction that she does not belong to the class of questionable women frequented by the police. However, do not let this conviction constrain you. Conventions are generally impediments rather than inspirations."

Sweetwater, whose heart might have fluttered at the song of the sirens, listened with delight, responding with a frank smile and a buoyant tone:

"I shall do my utmost, sir. But please, do not show me the parasol; merely describe it. I would not wish the others to mock me if I fail; I prefer to proceed quietly without raising false hopes."

"Well, then," Gryce replied, "it is one of those dainty, whimsical trifles crafted from gray chiffon, featuring a pearl handle and bows of pink ribbon. I suspect it was never used before. Given the value women place on such frivolities, it could only have been abandoned under the duress of extraordinary emotion or terror. There was no name inscribed upon it."

"Nor the manufacturer's mark?"

Gryce had anticipated this query and was relieved not to be thwarted.

"No; that would have assisted us excessively."

"And the hour at which this lady was observed at Hudson's?"

"Quarter past four—the precise moment the telephone message arrived."

"Precisely, sir. This is the most arduous undertaking of my career, though I accept the challenge. When shall I report back?"

"When you have information to convey. Ah, wait a moment. My suspicion is strong that this woman's first name is Evelyn. But remember, it remains nothing more than a hunch."

"As you direct, sir," Sweetwater said, his air of confidence masking underlying tension, before disappearing.

Gryce showed no trace of sharing Sweetwater's optimism. The fog enveloping the affair remained impervious to his scrutiny. Yet, he was not a young man of twenty-three, burdened only by triumphant memories.

His next avenue of hope lay in the published accounts of the crime, now circulating widely across the nation. A man of Mr. Adams's wealth and refinement must inevitably have possessed numerous acquaintances who would naturally surface following the shocking news of his sudden demise, particularly as his substantial assets and valuable effects were publicly acknowledged. Yet, as if fate conspired to frustrate the sagacity of this elderly investigator, the entire day passed without a single claimant emerging for Mr. Adams's fortune or a friend arriving to lift the veil shrouding the life of this enigmatic individual. While his banker and lawyer eventually appeared, they revealed little beyond the fact that his financial affairs were sound and that, to their knowledge, he stood without family or kin.

Even his landlord contributed scant new information. He had first heard of Mr. Adams through a deceased Philadelphia attorney, who had attested to his client's respectability and solvency. Upon their introduction, the landlord had been struck first by the tenant's ascetic appearance and secondly by his reserved manners and quiet intelligence. Though admirable, the tenant remained elusive. Rent payments were exact and punctual, yet the landlord's attempts at acquaintance were met with nothing but the cold politeness of a polished, indifferent stranger. The landlord viewed his tenant as a recluse, a bookworm consumed by study and scientific experiments conducted with the aid of his deaf-and-dumb servant.

When questioned about the servant, the landlord admitted his acquaintance was limited to two instances where he was ushered into his master's presence. He knew nothing of the servant's character or disposition, nor could he determine whether the servant's attitude toward his master stemmed from allegiance or antagonism. Thus, this path was effectively blocked.

Gryce was then taken into the room where Mr. Adams had perished. Surveying the massive steel plate barring the entrance and the high windows admitting only straggling sunbeams, he pointed to the latter.

"These were sealed at Mr. Adams's request. Originally, they extended down to the wainscoting."

He was shown where lath and plaster had been inserted and how the plate was constructed as a barrier. Yet, he could offer no explanation for its placement or divine its purpose, considering the immense expense involved.

The lamp presented another curiosity, its shifting lights fueling further astonishment. Gryce, having been received in the parlor on his previous visit, had been unaware of these arrangements, which served to accentuate the well-known oddities of his tenant.

The starling was conspicuously absent; that talkative bird had been transported to police headquarters specifically for Gryce's entertainment.

Further inquiries yielded little. No lead emerged regarding the owner of the insignia found on the wall from the Pension Office or any Grand Army of the Republic post within the city. Furthermore, the name of the artist who had painted the large portrait adorning the walls was not recognized in New York; otherwise, a clue concerning Mr. Adams's background might have been secured. Every drawer and receptacle in Mr. Adams's study had been ransacked, revealing no will or business documents. It appeared as though this strange man had sought to suppress his identity, or perhaps had transcended all interest in humanity, choosing instead to confine himself within the walls of his sanctuary.

Late in the afternoon, reports began filtering in from various tradesmen with whom Mr. Adams had dealt. They all commented on the peculiarity of his habits and the eccentricities of his mute servant. Both were described as hermits, distinct from their peers only in that they denied themselves no reasonable luxuries and seemed to embrace a secluded existence born of a pure love for isolation.
