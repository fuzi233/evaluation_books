Tito returned from Siena quickly, only to depart again on another journey, from which he did not come back until the seventeenth of August. Nearly two weeks had passed since the arrest of the accused, and still they remained in prison, their fate undecided. For Romola, all other concerns had faded into insignificance during this time—her world reduced to watching the shifting tides of possibility that might determine their outcome. At times, hope seemed strong: delay worked in their favor, and those in power hesitated to act, fearing the backlash of a hasty decision. On one side, voices clamored that the Republic was under threat, and mercy toward the prisoners would invite attack from its enemies. On the other, there was a clear understanding that condemning five prominent citizens to death and confiscating their property would ignite bitter enmity among their families, turning every influential household against those who signed the sentence.

The final judgment rested with the Eight—the body responsible for criminal justice—and required six votes to pass. But the Eight shrank from responsibility, asking instead that the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and eight Priors—share the burden. The Signoria, in turn, declined, proposing an appeal to the Great Council. This was possible thanks to a law enacted nearly three years earlier at Savonarola’s urging: any citizen condemned by the “six beans” (the fatal six votes) had the right to appeal to the Great Council. Yet now, the accused’s allies resisted this appeal, chiefly because it would hasten a verdict; legally, the sentence must be passed before an appeal could be filed. Their resistance succeeded, and a compromise was reached: the case would be referred to a large assembly scheduled for the seventeenth, composed of all higher magistrates, the Senate of Eighty, and a select group of citizens.

On that day, Romola had been granted permission to see her godfather for the second time, though only in the presence of witnesses. She returned to the Via de’ Bardi with her cousin Brigida, still unaware whether the council had reached a conclusion. Monna Brigida left immediately to await news at the home of a friend connected to one of the magistracies, hoping to bring back word as soon as it came. Romola sank into the first seat in the bright saloon, too overwhelmed to care about her surroundings or her position. Her back faced the door, her head resting in her hands. Time stretched long. She waited for her cousin’s return.

But when the door opened, it was not Monna Brigida who entered.

Since the night she had last seen Tito, Romola had no tangible proof that he had betrayed his oath—no evidence to confirm her worst fears. On the contrary, she had heard that he was still trusted by the Medici, believed to be carrying out secret missions in Romagna under the guise of fulfilling official duties. The confusion surrounding the conspirators' case allowed him to remain unimplicated. Yet her suspicion refused to fade. The horror she felt at his treatment of Baldassarre haunted her, coloring every thought of him. It was as if she had witnessed a murder—her imagination forever stained by the image of blood upon his hands.

As she heard his footsteps on the stone floor, a cold tremor ran through her. She could not turn. She could not rise. He said nothing at first. After a brief pause, he sat across the table from her. Then she lifted her eyes and looked at him—but she remained silent.

He did not seem irritated. Calmly, he spoke.
“This meeting mirrors our parting, Romola. But I know you are in great distress. I have come—if you will listen—to bring you hope.”

She started, shifted her posture, but her gaze remained wary.
“You speak even though it is you,” she murmured, “and yet I do not reject the news.”

“It is true,” he said. “The council has been postponed until the twenty-first. The Eight have finally issued a sentence of condemnation—but now the condemned have formally requested the Appeal to the Great Council.”

Her expression softened. Hope surged.
“And when will it be granted?”

“It has not yet been decided. But it may be. The Special Council will meet again on the twenty-first to decide whether the appeal should proceed. In the meantime, three days remain—a window in which things might change, in which influence might be brought to bear.”

Romola rose abruptly. Color flushed her face, her hands shook. In that moment, everything else—her anger, her doubt—was forgotten.

“Perhaps,” Tito said, rising too, “you already know what I am about to suggest. You are thinking of the Frate.”

“I am,” she said, looking at him in surprise. “Has he done something? Is there news?”

“Only this. Messer Francesco Valori’s fury and harshness were decisive today. Half those who voted against the prisoners did so only out of fear. And there are many supporters of Fra Girolamo here—Piero Guicciardini, for instance, who resisted strongly within the Signoria; and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, though he is a Piagnone, will not easily forgive the death of his brother Niccolò.”

“But how can they deny the appeal?” Romola asked, indignant. “It is the law. It was one of the greatest triumphs of the popular government!”

“They call it an exceptional case,” Tito replied. “There are arguments, yes—but more bluster about the danger to the Republic. Still, the council agreed to postpone. That means the next three days matter. A powerful voice, well placed, could sway the uncertain minds who wish to allow the appeal—and perhaps even check Valori’s rage. I’ve learned that the Frate himself has sent a message to Valori in favor of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. You know you sometimes have access to him. It might be worth trying now.”

“Yes,” Romola said quietly, as if speaking to herself. “I cannot believe the Frate would support denying the appeal.”

“I heard several men in the Palazzo say that it would be a lasting disgrace to Fra Girolamo if he allowed a government made up mostly of his own party to block the appeal—without protesting—when he himself has boasted in sermons and writings that he secured the law.” He paused. “And between us, Romola, though I respect your Frate deeply, he has grown accustomed to preaching about human sacrifice—killing tyrants, rooting out wicked malcontents. Some of his followers may find such rhetoric hard to reconcile with mercy now.”

“I know,” Romola said, her voice heavy with pain. “But he is driven to extremes. Once, it was different. I will ask for an audience. I cannot rest without it. I trust in the greatness of his heart.”

She did not look at Tito. Her eyes wandered downward, lost in thought, unaware that the words she spoke were addressed to her husband.

“Then waste no time,” Tito said smoothly, adjusting his hat as if preparing to leave. “And now, Romola—you may begin to see, beyond prejudice, that my wishes align with yours. You need not see my safety as a fault.”

A jolt ran through her—like lightning. The full awareness of his presence returned. She looked at him, silent.

“At least,” he continued, his tone slightly harder, “you will try to base our relationship on something more than the assumption that because evil is possible, I must have committed it. Am I alone to be excluded from your mercy?”

The emotion that had been suppressed for two weeks surged forward, unstoppable. She spoke with fierce clarity, indifferent to consequences.
“It is too late, Tito. Once suspicion takes root, it cannot be killed. Now I know everything. I know who that old man was—he was your father, the man to whom you owe your life, your standing, more than if you had been born of his blood. Compared to that, the betrayal of my trust, of my father’s trust, is small. As long as you refuse to admit the truth about him, a horror grows between us. The law that binds us cannot be obeyed. I am not a machine. I have a soul that recoils from your actions. Our marriage is a lie—a farce built on perpetual deception.”

Tito did not answer at once. When he did, his voice was careful, edged with alarm.
“So you mean to leave me, then? To abandon what the law gives you a claim to?”

“I want to leave you,” Romola said sharply.

“And suppose I refuse?” he challenged. “Suppose I insist on keeping what is mine by law? You will then, of course, make your accusations public. You will summon your mad assassin—no doubt ready to obey your command—and tell Florence that you believe his testimony because he desires to kill you. First, you’ll accuse me of calling you a Medicean conspirator. Then you’ll accuse the Medici of betraying you. And in both cases, you’ll offer the perfect proof: that you believe I am capable of anything. What a spectacle—what a role for a wife! And if you succeed in making yourself a laughingstock, you will surpass every heroine of ancient tragedy.”

He paused. She said nothing.

He pressed on, feeling the advantage.
“I think you have no real grievance except that I failed to live up to some vague, lofty ideals you set for me when you gave me your love. By withdrawing that love, you’ve reduced me to merely supplying your needs—as a respectable Piagnone, generous and pious. I doubt you’ll succeed in destroying me. But perhaps you’ll start by gaining the ear of Messer Bernardo del Nero?”

“Why do I speak of myself?” Romola cried, overcome with anguish, sinking back into her chair. “It is hateful to think of me at all.”

She did not notice when Tito left the room. She did not know how long passed before the door opened again, admitting Monna Brigida. But at that moment, she rose abruptly.
“Cousin—we must go to San Marco at once. I must see my confessor, Fra Salvestro.”
