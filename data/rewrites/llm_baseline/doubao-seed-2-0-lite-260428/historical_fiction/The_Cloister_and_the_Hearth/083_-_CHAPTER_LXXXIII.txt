Waiting for a letter freighted with all your dearest hopes never leaves the mind still, not for long. Margaret, in the letter she had sent off with young Luke, had poured out every last scrap of her energy and hard-won faith in her dying father’s final vision, the strange sight he had sworn to see of Gerard alive and well. But once Luke had vanished down the road, she quickly stared at her own impulsiveness, wondering how she could have been so foolishly credulous, and her conscience pricked her for dragging the kind-hearted boy on such a long, fruitless errand. Old Catherine fussed and scolded her for chasing wild fancies, and the sharp come-down from her brief, bright surge of hope dragged her into a far deeper despondency than any she had known before.

That was the state in which Joan Ketel, the loyal new friend she had made only weeks prior, found her. Joan bustled in, face bright with a new idea.
“Margaret, lass, I know the cure for this black mood of yours. The hermit of Gouda! A wondrous holy man, he is, and when the spirit moves him, he can tell you exactly what the future holds.”
“Ay, I’ve heard the tales,” Margaret replied listlessly, with no spark of hope left.
Joan wore her down at last, persuading her to walk the miles to Gouda to consult the hermit, and they tucked a pat of fresh butter and a clutch of new-laid eggs into a wicker basket to bring as an offering.

The two women had grown so fast and firm a friend over Jorian Ketel’s near-death illness six weeks prior. A brutal bowel pain had seized him first, and when that ebbed, an awful symptom settled in: no food or drink, not even a sip of water, would stay in his stomach for more than a minute. The village doctor had declared he would be dead before sundown, and ordered Joan to boil a chicken with a golden angel coin steeped in the broth to try to draw the sickness out. That gilded chicken broth came right back up, same as all the humble porridge and water that had come before it. Next the local curate steeped the relic of St. Sergius’s thumb in beef tea, to no better effect. At last Joan had run to Margaret sobbing, to beg for a scrap of good linen to sew her husband’s shroud.
“Let me see him first,” Margaret had said, and she walked straight to the sickbed, felt Jorian’s racing pulse, and ordered the stifling room’s windows thrown wide open, every scrap of damp, overheated linen stripped off him and changed. Joan had protested that it was waste to use good linen on a dying man, but Margaret had insisted, and Joan had found herself obeying. Margaret returned moments later with her apron heaped with flowering feverfew she had spotted growing wild right below the sickroom window. She brewed a strong decoction, sipped a spoonful first to prove it was harmless, and coaxed the half-dead man into swallowing the rest, bit by bit, slipping a single drop of schiedam gin into the third spoonful and a little beaten egg yolk into the seventh. By slow, steady degrees, she dragged him out of Death’s grip, until his body settled and he began to heal. She made the pair of them swear never to tell a soul who had cured him, for the village would accuse her of witchcraft and lock her away, far from her little boy.

When Margaret and Joan reached the hermit’s cave, they set their basket of offerings on the rough stone ledge at its mouth. Margaret leaned close to the speaking hole carved into the rock, and called out, “Holy hermit, we bring thee the best butter and eggs from our larders, and I, a poor deserted girl who is a wife yet no wife, and mother to the sweetest babe alive, beg thee tell me if my Gerard is quick or dead, true to his vows or false.”
A faint, rattling voice drifted out of the dark cave: “Trouble me not with the trivial things of earth. Send me a holy friar. I am dying.”
“Alas, poor soul!” Margaret cried. “Is it so? Let us come in and tend to thee.”
“Saints forbid!” the voice gasped. “Thine is a woman’s voice. Send me a holy friar, and do not draw near.”

They trundled home heavy-hearted. Joan huffed all the way that the notion women were imps of darkness forbidden a dying man’s bed was utter nonsense, but Margaret was too sunk in gloom to speak. Joan’s rough consolation fell on deaf ears until she mentioned Jorian’s unspoken secret.
“Jorian’s half ready to spill everything, you know. He’s that grateful to you he can scarce keep it in. Though he grumbles he can’t believe a man of his inches was cured of death’s door by no more than common feverfew — he was half minded to pull all the feverfew out of his own garden before he fell ill! He says he doesn’t want to stir up strife between Gerard’s kin and your friends for no good, that spilling what little he knows won’t bring Gerard back. He’s all twisted up about it, doesn’t know what to do.”
Margaret only shook her head softly. “Ay, we are wading in very deep waters, my little babe and me.”

Saturday came, and there was no sign of Luke. “Poor Luke,” Margaret murmured. “It was so good of him to go on such a fool’s errand for me.”
“Luke’s one in a hundred,” Catherine said warmly. “He’d never be anything but kind to your little Gerard.”
Margaret smiled. “Then I shall be extra kind to him, for all the trouble I’ve put him through, when he comes home.”

Brother Clement, once known to the world as Gerard Eliassoen, followed the directions the nuns had given him to skip a sharp, dangerous bend in the river, and strode fast and steady until he reached a staging post far closer to the coast than the spot where unsuspecting Luke lay waiting for him on the road. He set out before dawn the next day, and stepped onto the Rotterdam quay by noon, hurrying to meet Jerome so he would not keep his fellow Dominican waiting. He spotted a cluster of his order’s friars gathered at the water’s edge, but Jerome was not among them. When he asked after his companion, one of the monks pointed to a billowing white sail far out on the North Sea, already shrinking toward the horizon.
“Jerome? He sailed for England on that very ship, an hour past. He said he could not bear to lose a single day.”
Clement cried out in sharp, wordless anguish, and the rest of the monks turned, recognized him as the famous young preacher Jerome had spoken of, greeted him with brotherly warmth, and pressed a crumpled, hasty note Jerome had left behind into his hand. The note was cold, almost dismissive. Jerome wrote that he had not wished to waste the rare chance of a fair wind to England, that Clement was far too soft and tender a soul to win over the haughty islanders, that he would do far better to stay in his native Holland and preach to his own countryfolk, where his gift for speaking to Dutch hearts would shine. He warned Clement that a stranger had already asked after him by his old worldly name of Gerard, and bade him be on his guard, never to let the world snare him again with silken nets. Solitude, fasting, and prayer, he wrote, were the sword, spear, and shield of the soul. Farewell.

Clement was cut to the quick. They had been shipwrecked together, clinging to the same splinter of wreckage to stay alive. He had been Jerome’s pupil, his penitent, his spiritual son, and they had traveled four hundred miles together in the service of the Church — and Jerome had shaken him off like a bit of unwanted dirt at the first chance, with no kindness, no second thought. Even worldly hearts, he thought bitterly, had never been so cold and untrustworthy. The only soul that had ever truly loved him lay buried in the churchyard at Sevenbergen. He bade the monks farewell, promising he would stay at their convent and preach wherever their prior appointed, and turned his feet toward Sevenbergen.

Three hours later he passed Peter’s old cottage. It had been fully repaired, a new outhouse built on, and a crowd of noisy, rosy-cheeked children chased each other around the door. He turned his head hastily away, not to mar the soft, tender memory he carried of the place, and walked straight to the little village churchyard. He searched every grave for Margaret Brandt’s headstone, and could not find it anywhere. He could not believe they would grudge her a marker, and his heart ached at the thought that grim poverty had left no one alive who cared enough to put a name to her resting place. He stepped into the empty church, knelt, and prayed long and earnestly for the soul of the woman he had loved better than life itself.

When he came back out, he spotted an ancient man of eighty-two leaning on the churchyard gate, looking out over the graves. Clement asked if he knew where Margaret Brandt lay buried.
“Margaret Brandt? As if I didn’t know her,” the old man said, a little testy at first, then softening. “She doesn’t lie here, lad. Her whole family packed up and left ages ago, moved to Rotterdam, so I heard. I’d wager she died there, poor lass. She was a May rose of a thing, that one, blooming and sweet, with the softest smile you ever saw. No one expected to see her cut down so young, leaving old weeds like me standing here still.”

Clement bade the old man farewell, and hurried back to the Rotterdam convent as the sun dipped below the horizon. He spent the rest of the night in the chapel, keeping vigil, praying for the soul of Jerome who had abandoned him, and for the soul of Margaret he thought was buried in some unknown grave far away, until the small hours of the morning, and his frayed, aching soul settled back into the cold, calm stillness of his monastic life.
