The next afternoon, with the day already paling towards an early winter dusk, they left the main line some twenty miles from Milton-Northern, and were carried on a little branch railway that slipped away toward Heston. The carriage shook and sang on the rails with a thinner, more domestic sound than the great line—less roar, more rhythm—as if even the iron had manners here. Margaret, who had been holding herself stiff through so many changes, found that measured clatter loosening something within her, like a lid lifted from a pot to let the first steam escape.

Heston proved a single, straggling street set along the seashore, and it wore its difference openly. There was none of the painted gaiety Margaret remembered in southern bathing-places—no bright parasols, no languid lounging at shop-doors as if air itself were entertainment. Everything here had a use. The carts seemed shod and braced with more iron than wood; the harness was contrived for endurance rather than show. Even in the faces of people who had come, nominally, to take their pleasure, there was that look of minds still ticking over accounts and errands. The very colours of the town felt sober—greys that would not rub off.

She noticed, too, what was absent: the old country smock-frocks she had seen in other places, those roomy garments that carried a whole rural history in their folds. Here they had vanished, as if some word in an ancient dialect had dropped out of use; they hindered movement, they caught on machinery, they were not “fit” any longer, and so they were gone. It struck Margaret, with a little pang she could not have translated for her mother, that fashions could die the way languages did—by being made inconvenient under a louder, more dominant tongue.

The next morning she and Mrs. Hale went out to look for lodgings. Two nights at hotels had cost more than Mr. Hale had reckoned, and they were glad—almost too glad—to take the first clean, cheerful rooms that were willing to receive them. When they were fairly within them, with the door shut upon the public passages, Margaret felt, for the first time in many days, something like rest.

It came with a curious dreaminess, as if her mind, having been whipped onward too long, would now float. The sea lay at a little distance, and its sound—lap, withdraw, lap—was as steady as a clock that did not nag. Nearer, the donkey-boys cried and bickered, their voices carrying on the salt air; and even that rough piping felt harmless beside the hard metallic clamour she feared in the North. The scenes that passed before her—odd faces, purpose-driven steps, carts going by—seemed to move like pictures hung for an instant and then taken down again. She went down to the beach, breathing in air that tasted faintly of kelp and wet sand, soft and warm even at the end of November. A long misty sea-line touched a tender-coloured sky; a distant sail caught a pale sunbeam and turned briefly to silver. It all tempted her to believe she might dream her life away in that luxurious pensiveness, making the present enough because she dared not unpack the past, nor look too long at the future.

But the future, however stern and iron, had its summons. One evening it was settled that on the morrow Margaret and her father should go to Milton-Northern and seek a house. Mr. Hale had received several letters from Mr. Bell, and one or two from Mr. Thornton, and he was anxious to learn plainly what might be learned only by an interview—his position in Milton, his chances, the shape of life they were stepping into. Margaret knew removal could not be postponed. Yet her whole being shrank from a manufacturing town; and she clung to the belief—perhaps too eagerly—that the Heston air was doing her mother good, and that anything that hurried them away from this sea-breath would be a kind of cruelty.

They went.

For several miles before Milton they could see it: a deep, lead-coloured cloud hanging over the horizon in the direction where the town lay. It looked the darker because Heston had already shown the earliest signs of frost; the sky above them was a pale grey-blue, thin and cold, and the cloud ahead seemed like a bruise upon it. As they drew nearer, the air took on a faint taste and smell of smoke—perhaps less a positive odour than a stealing-away of all green fragrance, as if grass and herbage had been cooked out of the very wind. It sat on the tongue with a dry bitterness, as tea does when it has stood too long and lost its fine scent.

Then they were whirled into long, straight streets that seemed to have been ruled off by some severe hand—hopeless in their regularity, built all of small brick houses, each one like the next. Here and there a great oblong factory rose up, many-windowed, dominating its little domestic neighbours; and from it poured out black smoke—unapologetic, insolent, “unparliamentary”—which sufficiently explained the cloud Margaret had mistaken for weather. The sound changed too. In Heston the sea had given a measured music; here the air was full of harsh, hurried noise: wheels grinding, hooves striking stone, men calling sharp directions, and, beneath all, a continual throb, as if some giant heart beat in the walls. It was not merely loud; it was restless, the kind of sound that does not allow thought to settle. Margaret felt, without having words for it, that such noise could shape the mind as surely as diet does—that a town might feed its people on clangour until they knew no other flavour of peace.

From the station to the hotel they had to stop constantly; great loaded lurries blocked the not over-wide streets. In London, when Margaret had driven with her aunt, the heavy vehicles seemed varied in purpose—wood here, stone there, provisions somewhere else. Here every van, waggon, and truck bore cotton: in raw bags that had a dull, greasy look, or in bales of calico packed tight as if the cloth inside were a secret to be guarded. People thronged the footpaths. They were well-dressed as to material, but there was a loose slovenliness in the wearing of it that struck Margaret; it was not the threadbare effort at smartness she had seen in London, but something careless, as if clothes were only another tool to be flung on and off.

“New Street,” said Mr. Hale, leaning forward to read a corner sign as they turned. “This, I believe, is the principal street in Milton. Bell has often spoken to me about it. It was the opening of this street—once only a lane—into a great thoroughfare, thirty years ago, that has caused his property to rise so much in value. Mr. Thornton’s mill must be somewhere not far off, for he is Mr. Bell’s tenant. But I fancy he dates from his warehouse.”

“Where is our hotel, papa?”

“Close to the end of this street, I believe.” He paused, considering their hours, their list, the train they must take back. “Shall we have lunch before or after we have looked at the houses we marked in the Milton Times?”

“Oh, let us get our work done first,” said Margaret; her impatience was less of zeal than dread—if she delayed, she might lose her courage.

“Very well. Then I will only see if there is any note or letter for me from Mr. Thornton—he said he would let me know anything he might hear about these houses—and then we will set off. We will keep the cab; it will be safer than losing ourselves, and being too late for the train this afternoon.”

There were no letters waiting. So they began their house-hunting.

Thirty pounds a year was all they could afford. In Hampshire, for that sum, they might have found a roomy house with a pleasant garden—walls warm with fruit, perhaps, and a kitchen where one could smell the year turning through stored apples, pickled walnuts, hanging herbs. Here, even the necessary accommodation of two sitting-rooms and four bedrooms seemed unattainable. The houses were cramped and over-ornamented, or damp and narrow, or else beyond their reach. They went through their list, rejecting each as they visited it, and at last found themselves looking at each other in dismay, as if the very streets had shut like a mouth against them.

“We must go back to the second, I think,” said Margaret at length, taking the reins of the problem because someone must. “That one—in Crampton, don’t they call the suburb? There were three sitting-rooms—do you remember how we laughed at the number compared with the three bedrooms? But I have planned it all.”

Her mind, once engaged, moved quickly, arranging rooms as she might have arranged the courses of a meal, making each serve a purpose and conceal a lack. “The front room downstairs is to be your study and our dining-room—poor papa!—for you know we settled mamma is to have as cheerful a sitting-room as we can get. And that front room upstairs, with the atrocious blue and pink paper and the heavy cornice—oh, papa, what taste!—had really a pretty view over the plain, with a great bend of river, or canal, or whatever it is, down below. Then I could have the little bedroom behind, in that projection at the head of the first flight of stairs over the kitchen—you know—and you and mamma the room behind the drawing-room; and that closet in the roof will make you a splendid dressing-room.”

Mr. Hale listened with the half-smile of a man grateful to be saved from details. “But Dixon, and the girl we are to have to help?”

“Oh, wait a minute,” said Margaret, a flash of humour cutting through her weariness. “I am overpowered by the discovery of my own genius for management. Dixon is to have—let me see—I had it once—the back sitting-room. I think she will like that. She grumbles so much about the stairs at Heston; and the girl is to have that sloping attic over your room and mamma’s. Won’t that do?”

“I dare say it will,” said he. Then his brow knit, as his eye returned to what offended him most: “But the papers. What taste! And the over-loading such a house with colour and such heavy cornices!”

“Never mind, papa!” Margaret answered, forcing cheerfulness as one forces a sauce to thicken when it threatens to break. “Surely you can charm the landlord into re-papering one or two of the rooms—the drawing-room and your bedroom—for mamma will come most in contact with them; and your bookshelves will hide a great deal of that gaudy pattern in the dining-room.”

“Then you think it the best? If so, I had better go at once and call on this Mr. Donkin, to whom the advertisement refers me. I will take you back to the hotel, where you can order lunch, and rest; and by the time it is ready, I shall be with you. I hope I shall be able to get new papers.”

Margaret hoped so too, though she said nothing. She had never come fairly into contact with the taste that loves ornament—however bad—more than plainness and simplicity, those quiet frameworks within which elegance can breathe. It felt to her not merely ugly, but as if some loud, dominant vocabulary had been pasted over the walls, drowning out a more delicate speech.

Her father brought her through the entrance of the hotel and left her at the foot of the staircase, then went out to find Mr. Donkin, the landlord of the house they had fixed upon. Just as Margaret put her hand on the door of their sitting-room, a quick-stepping waiter followed her.

“I beg your pardon, ma’am. The gentleman was gone so quickly, I had no time to tell him. Mr. Thornton called almost directly after you left; and as I understood, from what the gentleman said, you would be back in an hour, I told him so; and he came again about five minutes ago, and said he would wait for Mr. Hale. He is in your room now, ma’am.”

“Thank you,” said Margaret. “My father will return soon, and then you can tell him.”

She opened the door and went in with that straight, fearless, dignified presence that belonged to her as naturally as breathing. She felt no awkwardness; her habits of society served her as armour. Here was someone come on business to her father—someone who had been obliging—and she was disposed to treat him with full civility.

Mr. Thornton, however, was more surprised—and, in his way, discomfited—than she. Instead of the quiet, middle-aged clergyman he expected, a young lady came forward with frank dignity—a kind of woman different from most he was in the habit of seeing. Her dress was very plain: a close straw bonnet of the best material and shape, trimmed with white ribbon; a large Indian shawl hanging in long, heavy folds about her, worn with the unconscious majesty of drapery. He had heard Mr. Hale had a daughter, but had imagined a little girl. He did not understand who she was at once, and his words, for an instant, would not come.

“Mr. Thornton, I believe!” said Margaret, after a half-moment’s pause that seemed to settle the air. “Will you sit down. My father brought me to the door not a minute ago; but unfortunately he was not told that you were here, and he has gone away on some business. He will come back almost directly. I am sorry you have had the trouble of calling twice.”

He was a man used to authority, used to giving directions that were obeyed; yet she seemed to assume some quiet rule over him at once. A moment before, he had been getting impatient at the waste of his time on a market-day; now, he found himself taking the offered seat as if it were the only natural thing.

“Do you know where it is that Mr. Hale has gone to?” he asked. “Perhaps I might be able to find him.”

“He has gone to a Mr. Donkin in Canute Street. He is the landlord of the house my father wishes to take in Crampton.”

Mr. Thornton knew the house. He had seen the advertisement, and, at Mr. Bell’s request, had gone to look at it, meaning to assist Mr. Hale to the best of his power; and, if truth were told, he was stirred also by his own interest in the case of a clergyman who had given up his living under such circumstances. He had thought the Crampton house really just the thing—until he saw Margaret. Now, with her before him, he began to feel ashamed of having imagined it would do very well, in spite of the vulgarity that had struck him even then. The rooms, the cornices, the gaudy papers: they seemed suddenly more offensive, as if he had offered coarse bread to someone accustomed to finer baking.

Margaret could not help her looks; but the short curled upper lip, the round, massive up-turned chin, the manner of carrying her head, her movements full of a soft feminine defiance, always gave strangers the impression of haughtiness. She was tired now, and would much rather have remained silent and taken the rest her father had planned; but she owed it to herself, she thought, to behave like a gentlewoman—to speak courteously from time to time to this stranger, though her courtesy was not over-brushed, and not over-polished, after her rough encounter with Milton’s streets and crowds.

She wished he would go, as he had once spoken of doing, instead of sitting there and answering with curt sentences whatever small remarks she made. She took off her shawl and hung it over the back of her chair, and sat facing him and the light. Her full beauty met his eye: the round white, flexible throat rising out of her full yet lithe figure; the lips moving so slightly as she spoke, hardly breaking the cold serene look of her face; the eyes, with their soft gloom, meeting his with quiet maiden freedom.

He almost said to himself that he did not like her before their brief, stunted conversation ended. He tried, in irritation, to compensate for the mortification he felt—that while he looked at her with an admiration he could not repress, she looked at him, he thought, with proud indifference, taking him for what he told himself in a kind of angry bitterness he was: a great rough fellow, without grace or refinement. Her quiet coldness he interpreted as contempt, and resented it until he was near to rising and leaving, determined to have no more to do with these Hales and their superciliousness.

Just as Margaret had exhausted her last subject—and “conversation” was hardly the word for that thin exchange of short speeches—Mr. Hale came in. With his pleasant, gentlemanly courtesy and apology, he somehow reinstated his name and family in Mr. Thornton’s good opinion, as if warmth had been put back into a room that had grown chill.

Mr. Hale and his visitor spoke at length of their mutual friend, Mr. Bell. Margaret, glad her duty of entertaining was over, went to the window and tried to make herself more familiar with the strange aspect of the street below. She became so absorbed in watching that she scarcely heard her father speak, and he had to repeat himself.

“Margaret! the landlord will persist in admiring that hideous paper, and I am afraid we must let it remain.”

“Oh dear! I am sorry!” she replied, and turned over in her mind the possibility of hiding part of it with some of her sketches—brightening a wall by laying art over ugliness, as one might mask a harsh taste in broth with herbs. But she gave it up as likely only to make bad worse: a poor remedy makes a worse illness.

Meanwhile her father, with a kindly country hospitality that had carried him through many a parish call, pressed Mr. Thornton to stay to luncheon with them. It would have been very inconvenient for Mr. Thornton to do so; yet he felt he might have yielded if Margaret, by word or look, had seconded her father’s invitation. He was glad she did not. And yet he was irritated at her for not doing so—an irritation that surprised even himself, as if he had expected her, without any right, to speak his name into welcome.

She gave him a low, grave bow when he left. He went out feeling more awkward and self-conscious in every limb than he had ever done in his life before, as though that quiet bow had struck some hidden string.

“Well, Margaret,” said Mr. Hale, turning back as soon as the door closed, “now to luncheon as fast as we can. Have you ordered it?”

“No, papa. That man was here when I came home, and I have never had an opportunity.”

“Then we must take anything we can get. He must have been waiting a long time, I’m afraid.”

“It seemed exceedingly long to me,” Margaret answered, letting out a breath as if she had held it too long. “I was just at the last gasp when you came in. He never went on with any subject, but gave little, short, abrupt answers.”

“Very much to the point though, I should think,” said Mr. Hale, who liked clearness as he liked honesty. “He is a clear-headed fellow. He said—did you hear?—that Crampton is on gravelly soil, and by far the most healthy suburb in the neighbourhood of Milton.”

When they returned to Heston, there was the day’s account to be given to Mrs. Hale, who lay with her tea-tray near her, and asked questions in the pauses between sips. The tea had a sea-air tang to it, as if the moisture of the place insisted upon flavouring even their domestic comfort.

“And what is your correspondent, Mr. Thornton, like?” asked Mrs. Hale at last.

“Ask Margaret,” said her husband. “She and he had a long attempt at conversation while I was away speaking to the landlord.”

“Oh! I hardly know what he is like,” said Margaret lazily, too tired to tax her powers of description. Then, rousing herself as if accuracy were a duty, she added, “He is a tall, broad-shouldered man, about—how old, papa?”

“I should guess about thirty.”

“About thirty—with a face that is neither exactly plain nor yet handsome—nothing remarkable—not quite a gentleman; but that was hardly to be expected.”

“Not vulgar or common though,” put in Mr. Hale, rather jealous of any disparagement of the sole friend he had in Milton.

“Oh, no!” said Margaret. “With such an expression of resolution and power, no face, however plain in feature, could be either vulgar or common. I should not like to have to bargain with him; he looks very inflexible. Altogether a man who seems made for his niche, mamma—sagacious, and strong, as becomes a great tradesman.”

“Don’t call the Milton manufacturers tradesmen, Margaret,” said her father. “They are very different.”

“Are they?” Margaret replied, the faintest touch of resistance in her tone. “I apply the word to all who have something tangible to sell; but if you think the term is not correct, papa, I won’t use it. But, oh mamma! speaking of vulgarity and commonness, you must prepare yourself for our drawing-room paper. Pink and blue roses, with yellow leaves! And such a heavy cornice round the room!”

Yet when at last they removed to their new house in Milton, the obnoxious papers were gone. The landlord received their thanks very composedly, letting them believe—if they liked—that he had relented from his expressed determination not to re-paper. There was no particular need to tell them that what he would not do for a Reverend Mr. Hale, unknown in Milton, he was only too glad to do at the one short, sharp remonstrance of Mr. Thornton, the wealthy manufacturer.
