Bartolommeo Scala, Secretary of the Florentine Republic — the very man Tito Melema had staked his most urgent hopes on winning over — lived in a grand palazzo steps from the Porta Pinti, the same house later known as the Casa Gherardesca. His coat of arms, emblazoned above the entrance, showed a transverse azure ladder against a gilded field, the word *Gradatim* (Step by Step) curling beneath it: a bold, unashamed proclamation that this former miller’s son had climbed every rung to high honours by his own effort, and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

The secretary was vain and pompous, yes, but he was also unfailingly honest: he sincerely believed in his own merit, and never bothered with performative modesty. By this May afternoon, he had already reached the topmost rung of his azure ladder. He had held his post as state secretary for twenty years, delivering the great public orations from the ringhiera platform outside the Old Palace as custom dictated, in front of visiting princes and dignitaries, while the republican lion Marzocco was crowned in gold for the occasion and crowds roared *“Viva Messer Bartolommeo!”* He had travelled on embassy to Rome, where he was named titular Senator, Apostolical Secretary, and Knight of the Golden Spur. Eight years prior he had even been elected Gonfaloniere, the highest office any Florentine citizen could hope to attain. As his honours grew, so did his fortune — and so did his gout, that familiar curse of successful, sedentary middle age. The Knight of the Golden Spur now often sat propped on cushions with his aching heel elevated, sheltered in the beautiful loggia he had built overlooking the wide lawns and blooming gardens at the back of his palace. That was exactly where he sat the day he had granted Tito Melema his long-awaited audience.

The golden afternoon sun spilled over the flowers and grass just beyond the loggia’s shade, and Scala had cast aside his heavy, formal silk lucco robe for a loose, light mantle draped over his plain tunic. His lovely daughter Alessandra and her husband, the Greek soldier-poet Marullo, sat on one side of his cushioned chair; two quiet, unpretentious friends sat on the other, the perfect unpressurised audience for his favourite stories. But for all the pleasant surroundings, Scala’s peace was soured by the stack of papers laid out on the table before him: the full, lengthy correspondence exchanged between himself and the great scholar Politian.

It was a common, if odd, human quirk of 15th century Florence to revisit old scholarly quarrels, and share every tedious letter of the back-and-forth with visiting learned friends. Scala had already asked for impartial judgment on this particular exchange half a dozen times before, all of it spurred by a handful of lines written in the most lighthearted, playful tone imaginable. It was a perfectly typical petty Renaissance intellectual feud, and one that mattered greatly right now: as the barber Nello had once joked, every slow-moving friendship needed that sharp little thistle of shared hatred to prod it into action.

The root of the fight was simple enough: Politian, widely hailed as the greatest classical scholar of his age, had once sought Alessandra’s hand in marriage, and been rejected. He had nursed a sharp, well-honed grudge ever since against the over-prosperous, presumptuous secretary who had turned him away as unworthy of his daughter. Scala was a dedicated public servant and a lucky man, the kind of success that always rankles with a snubbed scholar — but the fates had balanced the scales perfectly. He had an unacknowledged itch to be a celebrated author, and he was bad at it: one of those earnest men who penned little “poetical trifles” in their leisure hours purely for their own amusement, then posted them off to every friend they knew, letters being the 15th century’s version of shared literary periodicals. His close circle of friends — men like Marsilio Ficino and Cristoforo Landino, his fellow regulars in the Medici gardens — all praised his Latin prose as elegant and masculine, and the brutal, hyper-critical Joseph Scaliger who would later dismiss his Latin as entirely illiterate was still a full century away, across the Alps.

But no amount of praise from close friends could ever satisfy that quiet coquetry inherent to amateur writing. Scala was desperate for the critical, supercilious Politian — that fellow Medici garden regular who was far from amiable — to acknowledge that a busy statesman could turn out verses of such natural fertility that he would surely rival the best men of letters, if he did not have republic business to attend to.

That fatal moment came. The man you secretly loathe sends you a Latin epigram where he misgenders *culex* — the tiny gnat that buzzes everywhere along the banks of the Arno — and twists his hendecasyllable metre so badly one line has a beat too many, and his poetic conceits are obvious solecisms. Politian pounced the second Scala poked his soft amateur head out of his official state shell. What looked like a friendly correction, wrapped in a playful epigram mocking his grammatical error, turned sharp when Scala fired back a bad joke in Latin verse, referencing Politian’s rejected suit for his daughter.

Politian replied that the verses were delightfully facetious, a pity they were riddled with obvious metrical mistakes. Since Scala had claimed he had written the lines in imitation of a Greek epigram, Politian wrote, he would do him the favour of enclosing his own Greek epigram about the very same gnat — not out of any desire to humble the secretary, of course, purely to instruct him. The little verse contained the fashionable, whimsical conceit common to the era, drawing on the old myth that the gnat, like Venus, was born sprung fully formed from the waters.

Scala’s reply was masterfully passive-aggressive. He protested that his own silly verses were never meant for a man of Politian’s impossibly fastidious taste, the living scholar closest to the perfect grace of the ancient Romans, so refined even sturgeon must taste bland to him. He defended his own lines, of course — though they had been written hastily, no revision, as a silly summer distraction for himself and friends who were happy with mediocre verse, unlike other people who chased fame by pestering booksellers to publish their work. He claimed he barely had enough Greek to parse the literal meaning of Politian’s epigram, let alone appreciate its grace — but it was Politian’s work, so of course it was brilliant. Then, as a petty postscript, he added that the comparison of the gnat to Venus, born from the waters, was a very shaky conceit: Venus might well take offence, and besides, gnats loved warm air, not cold damp water, a fact that ran counter to the old pre-Socratic Thales’ theory that all life sprang from moisture. A fish would have made far more sense as a water-born comparison, he wrote, or an eagle if you referenced flight, or an owl or bat if you wanted a creature that moved in darkness.

Politian’s response was merciless. He wrote back that he had never expected to find frogs and minnows, not sturgeon, served up to him in verse from a high state official, tore every last thread of Scala’s defence of his bad Latin to shreds, and mocked every last one of his stupid, ill-informed criticisms of the Greek epigram as laughable cavils. Virgil himself, he snapped, had explicitly called the gnat the “nurseling of the waters”, and all his silly alternative comparisons were *nihil ad rem* — entirely irrelevant. Just because an eagle could fly higher did not mean a gnat could not fly at all, he jeered. He claimed he was ashamed to waste time on such trivialities, blowing a gnat up to the size of an elephant, but added a sharp parting shot that he never pretended to be a friend to men who flattered him to his face then slandered him behind his back, supporting the quip with a pithy Greek line from Homer that served as gunpowder for his final insult.

The quarrel only escalated from there. The petty logic got no more coherent, but the secretary grew ever more pompous and defensive, and the scholar’s temper grew more venomous. Politian protested that he had only ever tried to hold up a mirror to the overinflated secretary, to make him stop defending his bad Latin against centuries of accepted ancient authority — unless Scala deliberately intended to sink lower and lower in literary reputation the higher he rose in public honours, to give all men of letters a satisfying chance to feel his equal. Scala replied by begging Politian to read his full body of work: he was the most devout admirer of classical antiquity alive, he said, he was positively ashamed of his own century, blushed for its bad taste. It was other men, not him, who chased fame by praising their own trashy work as equal to the divine monuments of the ancients. And as for his public honours that made envious men seethe? He had earned every single one, every step of the way since he arrived in Florence as a penniless boy.

Politian’s final reply was brutal. He was not surprised that Scala found the entire age distasteful, he wrote, because the entire age found Scala distasteful. He had every right to call the ex-miller a branny monster, born in the filth and refuse of the mill-house, perfectly suited to spend his rest of his days turning the family millstones, not writing poetry.

Scala had brought this whole stack of letters out specifically for Tito’s visit. Here was a new young Greek scholar, untainted by any old loyalties to either man, whose opinion he could trust to be fully dispassionate — and there was nothing he wanted more than a neutral, expert verdict on Politian’s overpraised Greek gnat epigram.

After the usual pleasantries about Tito’s years of travelling the Mediterranean, a long shared look at his collection of ancient carved gems, and a natural drift from a discussion of the late Lorenzo de’ Medici’s famous obsession with collecting such ancient art to the general state of classical study in modern Florence, the talk of Politian was inevitable. Yes, the company agreed, he was a man of eminent ability — but absurdly arrogant. He styled himself a new Hercules, whose whole job was to go around smiting every silly literary “monstrosity” of the age, and sent unsigned letters to his elders as if they were miraculous divine revelations that could not possibly have any human source. For all his mockery of lesser men who thought they wrote like Cicero just because they ended every sentence with *esse videtur*, he had fallen straight into the opposite trap, chasing obscure, weird words and over-affected phrases. Even his much-celebrated *Miscellanea* did not hold up to close scrutiny.

Tito had flipped through the *Miscellanea* only days before, and he was happy to say exactly what the secretary wanted to hear, purely out of his natural instinct to please even before he considered what he stood to gain. He proved himself more than worthy of being named judge over the great gnat quarrel. He picked apart Politian’s vaunted Greek epigram line by line, found every weak point, every overstretched conceit, proving beyond a doubt that Politian had been lying when he pretended the “transmarini”, the native Greeks across the sea, would think nothing of it — he had accidentally been telling the truth in that false show of modesty.

Scala was delighted. This wise young judge, he was sure, could appreciate sharp satire even in the vulgar Florentine tongue, and Scala — that excellent man who never sought publicity through booksellers, who always kept a stock of “hasty uncorrected trifles” on hand to serve to visitors like iced sherbet on a hot day or spiced cordial on a cold one — pulled out a handful of his own private sonnets, gentle satires poking fun at Politian’s well-known foibles, that he did not want shared anywhere else, but which would certainly amuse the whole party.

By the time Tito took his leave, Scala had pressed him to come back again soon, urgently. His gems were fascinating: the agate with its natural *lusus naturae* marking that looked exactly like Cupid riding a lion, and the “Jew’s stone” carved with a lion-headed serpent, both of which the secretary agreed to buy immediately. The serpent stone, Scala explained, would make an excellent amulet to tuck beneath his tunic against his right breast, a proven protection against the savage gout pains that were growing worse and worse, making it obvious he would not survive the attacks long if he did not have a stock of magically potent rings and charms on hand. But Tito himself, he assured the young Greek, was far more interesting than any trinket. He had won his initial introduction through Bardo de’ Bardi, that old worthy scholar and long-time acquaintance of Scala’s (a little too fond of overestimating his own importance, as so many of the secretary’s friends were), but now he was invited back on the strength of his own obvious brilliance.

The interview could not have gone more perfectly for Tito. He wandered out through the Porta Pinti to laugh freely to himself at the sheer absurdity of the great gnat quarrel, and felt the sun warm on his shoulders. Fortune had taken him firmly by the hand, at last. There was no way she would turn her back on him now.
