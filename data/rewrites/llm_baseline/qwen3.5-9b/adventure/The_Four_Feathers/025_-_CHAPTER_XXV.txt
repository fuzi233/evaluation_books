As Calder stepped into a carriage at Assouan, disappointed by his failure to secure news of Feversham from the sole person who possessed it, Lieutenant Sutch was traversing a high, white road in Hampshire. The landscape was dotted with heather and gorse, and Sutch, too, was consumed by worry regarding Harry Feversham. Having spent much time in solitude, Sutch had developed the habit of voicing his thoughts aloud. As he guided his pony slowly and reluctantly forward, he muttered to himself repeatedly, "I foresaw there would be trouble. From the very beginning, I foresaw there would be trouble."

The ridge along which he traveled dipped abruptly into a hollow. Ahead, the road plunged steeply between dense forests of pine toward a small railway station. The sight of the gleaming rails stretching straight into the distance, where they seemed to huddle together under the afternoon sun, heightened Sutch's unease. He reined in his pony and stared with a furrowed brow at the red-tiled roof of the station.

"I promised Harry to say nothing," he murmured, finding a fragile comfort in the repetition. "I promised faithfully in the Criterion grill-room."

Suddenly, the shrill whistle of a distant engine broke his gloomy reverie. He spotted the white smoke of an approaching train trailing behind it like a ribbon.

"I wonder what brings him," he said doubtfully. Then, summoning a measure of courage, he declared, "Well, it's no use shirking." With a flick of his whip, he urged the pony down the hill. He arrived at the station just as the train pulled up. Only two passengers disembarked: Durrance and his servant, who emerged promptly onto the road. Sutch hailed Durrance, who walked to the side of the trap.

"You received my telegram in time, then?" Durrance asked.

"Luckily it found me at home," Sutch replied.

"I have brought a bag. May I trespass upon you for a night's lodging?"

"By all means," Sutch said, though the reluctance in his tone was unmistakable to Durrance's sensitive hearing. Durrance, however, had anticipated a hesitant welcome; he had intentionally delayed sending his telegram until the last moment. Had he provided an address earlier, he suspected he might have faced a flat refusal. While the telegram merely announced his arrival without stating his purpose, its dispatch served as a warning that something grave had transpired—something unsettling in the relationship between Ethne Eustace and Durrance. Durrance had undoubtedly come to renew his inquiries about Harry Feversham, questions Sutch was sworn never to answer. Yet, seeing Durrance feel for the step of the trap with his raised foot, Sutch realized his visitor was blind. Reaching out, he caught Durrance by the arm and helped him up. After all, he reasoned, it would not be difficult to deceive a blind man. Ethne herself had harbored similar thoughts, feeling a profound relief akin to what Sutch now experienced. In fact, the lieutenant was so relieved that an impulse of pity arose within him.

"I was very sorry, Durrance, to hear of your bad luck," he said as they began the ascent up the hill. "I know what it is to be suddenly halted and set aside just when things are moving forward and the world is smoothing itself out, though my leg wound is nothing compared to your blindness. I won't speak to you of compensations and patience; that is the chatter of those who are comfortable and have never suffered. *We* know that for a young, active man thriving in a career that demands energy, there are no compensations if his path is suddenly severed through no fault of his own."

"Through no fault of his," Durrance repeated thoughtfully. "I agree. It is only the man whose career ends due to his own fault who receives compensation."

Sutch glanced sharply at his companion. Was Durrance referring to Harry Feversham? Did he possess enough knowledge to make such a specific reference, or was it merely coincidence that his words struck so deeply?

"Compensations of what kind?" Sutch asked, uneasy.

"For one thing, the chance of knowing oneself. Above all, that. He is brought up short, stopped in his career, perhaps disgraced." Sutch flinched slightly at the word. "Perhaps... disgraced," Durrance echoed. "Well, the shock of disgrace is, after all, his opportunity. Do you see? It is his opportunity to truly know himself. Until the moment of disgrace, his life has been a sham and an illusion; the man he believed himself to be never existed, and now, finally, he knows the truth. Once he understands this, he can begin to repair his disgrace. Yes, there are compensations for such a man. You and I know a case in point."

Sutch no longer doubted that Durrance was deliberately discussing Harry Feversham. Though he could not guess how Durrance had acquired this knowledge, it was not entirely accurate in Sutch's estimation, and thus it did Harry some injustice. Primarily for this reason, Sutch chose not to feign ignorance regarding Durrance's allusion. Years had not dimmed his deep affection for Harry; he loved him with a concentrated intensity reminiscent of a woman's devotion, and he could not bear for Harry's memory to be slighted.

"The case you and I know of is not quite in point," Sutch argued. "You are speaking of Harry Feversham."

"Who believed himself a coward and was not one. He commits the fault that halts his career, discovers his error, and dedicates himself to retrieving his honor. Surely, it is a case perfectly in point."

"Yes, I see," Sutch conceded. "There is another perspective—a mistaken one, as I know—but for a moment, I thought it was yours—that Harry fancied himself brave and was suddenly confronted with the discovery that he was a coward. But how did you find out? No one knew the whole truth except myself."

"I am engaged to Miss Eustace," Durrance stated simply.

"She didn't know everything. She knew of the disgrace, but not of the determination to overcome it."

"She knows now," Durrance said sharply. "And you are glad of that—very glad."

Sutch was unaware that he had betrayed his delight through any gesture or exclamation. Perhaps his face revealed it clearly, but Durrance could not see it. Puzzled, Sutch nonetheless did not deny the implication.

"It is true," he asserted firmly. "I am very glad she knows. I can certainly see why, from your viewpoint, it would be preferable if she remained ignorant. But I cannot help it. I am very glad."

Durrance laughed, pleasantly enough. "I like you the better for being glad," he remarked.

"But how does Miss Eustace know?" Sutch asked. "Who told her? I did not, and there is no one else who could."

"You are wrong. There is Captain Willoughby. He arrived in Devonshire six weeks ago. He brought with him a white feather, which he gave to Miss Eustace as proof that he had withdrawn his charge of cowardice against Harry Feversham."

Sutch halted the pony in the middle of the road. He no longer bothered to conceal the joy this news inspired. Indeed, he momentarily forgot Durrance's presence beside him. Sitting silently and still, he radiated a happiness he had never known in his life. An elderly man now, well past sixty, Sutch usually experienced pleasures that were gray and sober, for joy had lost its feverish edge. Yet, a buoyant gladness surged through his heart, a sensation reserved only for the young. Five years prior, on the pier at Dover, he had watched a mail packet vanish into darkness and rain, praying he might live to witness this great moment. He had survived, and it had come. His heart swelled with gratitude. It seemed to him that a brilliant burst of sunlight had illuminated the entire world, transforming it into a kaleidoscope of color and joy. Since the night he stood outside the War Office in Pall Mall, watching Harry Feversham touch his arm and voice his despair, Sutch had been burdened by a crushing sense of guilt. Harry was Muriel Feversham's son, and Sutch, for that reason alone, should have watched over him in childhood after his mother's death and fathered him in his youth, since his own father failed to understand. But he had failed. He had neglected a sacred trust, imagining Muriel's reproachful gaze from the heavens above. He had heard her gentle voice in his dreams pleading, "Since I was dead, since I was taken away to where I could only see and not help, surely you might have helped. Just for my sake you might have helped—you whose work in the world was at an end." The long narrative of his idle years had stood as an accuser. Now, however, the guilt was lifted from his shoulders, removed by Harry Feversham's own actions. The news was not entirely unexpected, but the lightness of spirit he felt revealed how much he had awaited its arrival.

"I knew," he exclaimed. "I knew he wouldn't fail. Oh, I am glad you came today, Colonel Durrance. It was partly my fault that Harry Feversham ever incurred that charge of cowardice.
