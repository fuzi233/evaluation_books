There were hours when Shirley Keeldar slipped into such soft, unhurried indolence that moving even a single finger felt like a waste of perfect, sun-drenched joy. On bright afternoons, following a morning spent galloping her favourite mare or poring over her estate ledgers, she would stretch motionless on thick clover turf beneath the gnarled old oak at Fieldhead’s edge, perfectly content to do nothing at all. She needed no grand company save Caroline Helstone somewhere within earshot, no spectacle but the endless blue arch of sky streaked with wispy cloudlets, no sound but the drowsy hum of foraging bees and the soft rustle of leaves overhead. In those quiet moments she never reached for a book: her reading was the gentle drift of old happy memories, and the sweet, unspoken premonitions of what might lie ahead—some secret dream that pulled a soft private smile to her lips, never sad, never shadowed. Fate had always been kind to her, and she trusted it would not fail her now.

One golden afternoon, Caroline came trotting over to rouse her, thinking she had lain napping long enough, only to stop short when she looked down. Shirley’s cheeks were damp with tears, her dark eyes glistening and brimming over like dewdrops on a wild rose petal.
“Shirley, why are *you* crying?” Caroline asked, the word slipping out soft and stunned, for Shirley was the last person she would ever have imagined weeping unbidden in the sun.

Shirley laughed, turning her lovely wild-haired face up to her, tears still clinging to her lashes. “Because it feels so good to cry,” she said. “My heart is so full it aches, half with joy, half with tender sadness. But you, good patient thing—why don’t you join me? My tears are the sweet, fleeting kind that dry in the sun. Yours would be the bitter sort, I think, if you let them fall.”

“Why would I ever weep bitter tears?”
“Because you are a little mateless, solitary bird,” Shirley said softly, the only answer she would give.

“And aren’t you mateless too, Shirley?”
“At heart? No,” Shirley said, that giddy secret smile tugging at her mouth again.
“Oh—who has nested there, then?” Caroline teased, but Shirley only laughed louder, springing nimbly to her feet, curls tumbling loose over her shoulders.
“It was just a daydream,” she said, cheeks pink. “Bright as sunlight, and probably nothing but air.”

By this point, Caroline had few illusions left about her own quiet unrequited love for Robert Moore. She saw clearly how her future was likely to unfold, and yet old habits and old tender ties still drew her to the familiar field stile and the gnarled thorn tree overlooking the Hollow every evening, where she used to wait for the faint lamp glow that used to shine from Robert’s cottage window.

The night after he had sent her the short sharp business note, she waited there for hours, watching in vain: no lamp lit that night. She stayed until the constellations climbed high enough to warn her the hour was far too late, and turned for home. Passing Fieldhead, the full moon’s silver glow over its walls stopped her in her tracks. The great old hall rose peaceful under the sky, its stone gilded pale as pearl, wrapped in soft mellow brown shadow, the oak boughs over its roof draped in deep dark green. The wide pavement before the door gleamed too, as if some fairy spell had turned dark granite to glimmering white marble. Two sharp dark shadows stretched across that silver space, cast by two figures walking slowly side by side. They were still and silent at first, then began to move in perfect easy step, their voices low and soft. Caroline leaned forward, squinting as they stepped out from behind the thick cedar trunk. Was that Mrs. Pryor with Shirley?

Of course it was Shirley. No one else had that lithe, proud, graceful stride. Her face was turned up to the moon, soft and unguarded, half pensive, half mirthful, half teasing, half tender. She hadn’t bothered to cover her head against the night dew, her dark curls tumbling free, brushing her neck and caressing her shoulders. A gold chain glinted through the soft folds of the silk scarf wrapped across her chest, and a large bright sapphire glittered on the white hand that held the scarf closed. It was unquestionably Shirley.

Her companion then couldn’t possibly be Mrs. Pryor—unless Mrs. Pryor had suddenly grown to six feet tall, and traded her neat widow’s weeds for a tall young man’s coat. It was Robert Moore himself, her tenant, the master of the Hollow mill.

They spoke too softly for their words to carry at first, and Caroline could not bring herself to slip away. There had been a time not so long ago when Robert had walked these same paths with her, after the sun went down, when they would wander up the Hollow together to smell the sweet wild herbs that grew on the narrow terrace at the edge of the deep ravine, listening to the little watercourse gurgle over wet stones under the dark boughs of the alders.

“But I used to be closer to him,” Caroline thought, a sharp quiet ache winding through her chest. “He never felt like he had to treat me with distant homage. He only had to be kind. He used to hold my hand, and now he doesn’t even brush Shirley’s fingers. But Shirley isn’t proud with the person she loves—her stance has that natural noble grace she was born with, but there’s no haughtiness in her face right now. Robert must be looking at her and seeing exactly what I see: that bright soft fire in her eyes, that smile sweeter than any I’ve ever seen on any other woman. I can tell he feels its beauty, deep in a man’s heart, the kind of feeling I never dared let myself hope he would have for me. They look like two happy souls, walking that silver pavement like it’s the white shore we all dream of, beyond the river of death. And here I am, hidden in the dark, my own heart heavier than the shadows I’m shrinking into. I’m just a poor mortal, standing alone, wondering why I was ever born at all.”

She took a shaky breath, steadying herself. “I saw this coming, the very first day Shirley arrived. Rich, young, lovely—of course Robert would fall for her. She’s his now. He’s her lover. They’ll be married, and he’ll grow to love her more and more every day. I don’t begrudge them their happiness, truly I don’t. But my own pain burns so sharp sometimes, I can hardly breathe. I almost wish I’d never been born at all.”

Just then, Shirley stepped aside to pluck a dewy wild rose, and the pair turned toward the gate, their voices suddenly loud enough to hear. Caroline would not stay to eavesdrop. She slipped away silently, the moonlight falling soft on the stone wall where her shadow had just stood.

“You have all the tenacity of a bulldog,” Shirley was saying, half teasing. “I’ve never met anyone so stubborn, so quiet in his schemes, never giving warning, just coming up noiselessly behind a problem and holding on until it’s solved.”
“That’s hardly a compliment,” Robert laughed. “Am I really so unrefined?”
“Far-seeing, calculating, unshakable,” she went on. “That’s you.”
“I’ve gathered word that the men who rioted at the mill are plotting vengeance now that Barraclough has been sentenced to transportation,” Robert said, turning serious. “I’ve drawn up plans to defend the mill, to make sure no one gets hurt, and no one destroys the livelihood of the workers who depend on me. I wanted your approval before I act. Will you stand with me, as long as I stay on the defensive?”
“Of course I will,” Shirley said at once.
“Then I am satisfied,” he said, his voice warm, softer than she had ever heard it. “I would have gone through with it anyway, but now it doesn’t feel like a burden at all. It feels like a pleasure.”
“I can tell you’re enjoying yourself far more than you ever would have taking a government order for army cloth,” Shirley teased.
“Frankly, I do. Would you talk to old Mr. Helstone for me? I know he’s still cross with me over our old disagreements, but I’d like the rector on my side. He’s made of true, solid metal.”
“Leave it to me,” Shirley said, grinning. “He’ll be on your side before the week is out. He can’t resist a good noble cause, no matter how cross he is with you.”
“Whom couldn’t you win over to your side?” Robert said, and there was a low unspoken adoration in his tone that hung thick in the silver moonlight.
“Watch me win him, then,” Shirley laughed. “It’ll cost me a whole afternoon of tea, toast, cake, and a lot of gentle persuading. Oh, it’s growing chill,” she added, shivering a little.
“I’m so sorry to keep you out here,” he said, at once concerned. “It’s just that evenings like this, and your company, are so rare for me. I never want it to end.”
“I should go in,” Shirley said gently. “Mrs. Pryor will be cross if I stay out too late. You ought not to wander the Hollow alone tonight, either—there’s a mad weaver named Michael Hartley roaming the paths with a gun, the sort of man who’s sworn he’ll do you harm. Promise me you’ll go straight home, and stay out of the glen tonight.”
“If you wish it,” he said, pausing, his voice so low it was almost a murmur. “Life used to feel aimless to me, three months ago. I was drowning, and I didn’t much care if I sank. Then a hand reached out to me. A delicate, bright hand, strong enough to pull me all the way back from ruin. My life’s invaluable now. I have something worth living for.”
“Then live for it,” Shirley said, her cheeks pink. “Don’t go throwing yourself in harm’s way. Good night, Robert.”

The next evening, Caroline kept her promise to spend the night at Fieldhead. She had spent the whole prior shut up in her little bedroom, sewing and sewing at a summer muslin dress, her brain working faster than her fingers. She ached for some hard absorbing work, something to occupy her mind so thoroughly she would not have to think of the two people she loved most in the world, happy without her. She decided she would beg her uncle to let her take a post as a governess, and first she would ask Mrs. Pryor to speak for her. Tears fell on her busy hands every now and then, but she wiped them away quickly, re-threaded her needle, and kept sewing.

When she arrived at Fieldhead’s oak parlour, tea was just being set on the table. Shirley looked her over, frowning a little at her pale cheeks. “You look lovely in that new dress,” she said. “But you’ve gone too thin, Caroline. What’s weighing on you?”

When Caroline told them she wanted to leave the rectory and work as a governess, Shirley was immediately horrified. “Nonsense!” she said. “You will not go be a servant amongst strangers, while I am here. What sort of friend do you think I am, to let you waste away all alone, unhappy somewhere far away? My greatest joy these days is looking out my window to see you walking up the lane, coming to spend the day with me. I won’t let you leave me. Instead, I have a far better plan. Every summer I take a long holiday. This year, I’m going to the English Lake District for two months. And you are coming with me. If you say no, I won’t go a single step without you.”

Caroline’s throat tightened, and she smiled through the ache in her chest. “You are so very good to me, Shirley.”
