The air in the Lambert household was thick with the static of a continent at war, yet inside, the atmosphere remained suspended in a peculiar, suffocating stillness. Harry Warrington existed in this space as a monument to potential energy that refused to discharge. While Europe burned under the hammer of Frederick the Great, and the world held its breath awaiting the thunder of Rossbach or the slaughter of Leuthen, Harry remained anchored to the floorboards of London. It was a mercy, perhaps, that he stayed away; for if he had marched alongside the Prussian hero, the narrative would have shifted from domestic comedy to the grim chronicle of death, a genre reserved for other pens and far more terrible realities.

Instead, Harry lived a life of deliberate stagnation. He drifted through the military coffee-houses, a ghost among men who spoke of glory he would not claim. His interests were narrow, almost pathological: newspapers, and nothing else. Literature bored him; the sentimental weeping of society over Richardson's novels seemed a performance of exquisite stupidity. He possessed a capacity for laughter, but it was always late, arriving after the joke had already expired, a reflex rather than a response. Why should a gentleman possess a literary soul? Was the worth of a man measured by the couplets he could spin? He was ruined, idle, dependent on his brother's charity, sleeping over books he despised. Yet, in this paralysis, there was a strange safety. To be idle was to be safe. To be active, as Achilles once was, was to invite catastrophe. Had he been sent to the court of some distant king, surrounded by women and distaffs, what fate awaited him? Where would the mighty Pyrrhus have emerged from such softening? A young man of his mettle, dawdling by the hearth, was a contradiction waiting to explode.

Two women, the elder sisters of the Lambert family, offered him the only comfort available: a pity that bordered on the maternal. They were the gentle physicians of his suffering, soothing his wrath, amplifying his joys, and bandaging the invisible wounds inflicted by Fortune. But Theo and her mother were not alone in their concern. Hetty, however, viewed Harry differently. Her heart was harder, edged with a savage impatience. The world was in arms; harvest was underway with its thousand reapers; why could he not wield a musket or a sickle? To her, his dependence was not a tragedy but a crime against nature.

"Why, how savage the little thing is!" her father observed later, watching Hetty fire verbal volleys at Harry, who sat like a quivering target in the drawing-room.

"Abominable," her mother cried, envisioning a whip and a bed.

Theo, observing the scene, offered a softer interpretation. Perhaps Hetty's anger stemmed from affection. If one loved someone, one desired their perfection, their constant elevation above the rest. "My father is so much better than the world," Mrs. Lambert admitted, her voice tinged with pride. "He has spoiled us."

"He is good-tempered," Theo conceded. "Brave?"

"Mr. Wolfe, Harry, George—they are all brave."

"Witty?"

"George is learned, in his way. His manners are fine. Madame de Bernstein confirms it. He possesses a loftiness, a choice for noble things even amidst his satire. He treats the wicked with bitterness, but his heart remains tender. Yesterday, he bought apples for a crowd of children. The day before, he gave coin to a beggar. He reads Moliere beautifully, though I dare not recite the verses myself."

"And you know them by heart?"

"Oh yes, mamma! Nonsense!"

Theo fled the room, her cheeks flushed, leaving her mother staring after her with an odd, searching intensity. Why did she watch for George? Why did her cheeks burn? Time moved on, indifferent to these small dramas, cycling through seasons of bloom and decay, while the characters remained trapped in the amber of their own perceptions.

When George finally arrived, Theo descended with a fluttering heart, carrying a bouquet culled specifically for his honor. She watched from the window, noting his generosity to the sweep, his purchases from the fruit-seller. But if it was Harry who knocked, she retreated to her sanctuary of work and books, delegating the reception to her sister or her brothers. Theo's eyes were sharp instruments, distinguishing the golden hair of Harry from the dark locks of George, despite their striking resemblance. Hetty, conversely, showed no excitement, no rudeness, when the black-haired gentleman entered.

The visitors were welcome to stay as long as they pleased. One afternoon, Harry sat on a couch, radiating a despondency deeper than usual. Hetty appeared.

"What makes your honour look so glum this morning?" she asked.

"Nothing but looking glum," Harry replied. "Do you recall when we were boys? I was always asking for holidays, passing them swinging on gates or making ducks and drakes. Those days were the most melancholy. What have I to do now? Breakfast, walk, dinner, walk, tea, supper, and a pipe of Virginia."

"When I returned to the Chartreux last night," Harry continued, "I wanted to tell the master: 'Teach me. This boy knows more Latin and Greek at thirteen than I do at twenty. I have nothing to do, and I might as well try to repair my childhood's negligence.'"

"I laugh to imagine you at the head of a class," Hetty said.

"I wouldn't be," Harry admitted humbly. "George could lead any class. I am not a bookman. Neglect in youth, idleness—it might have been cured by a cane. Your account of Tom's schooling makes me realize I wasted my prime. For what am I good? Spending my patrimony abroad, lounging in coffee-houses, racing dogs. I am good for nothing."

"Such a great, brave, strong fellow, good for nothing?" Hetty exclaimed. "I would not confess that to any woman, even if she were twice as useless!"

"What am I to do? I seek leave for the army, but Madam Esmond ignores me. It is the only path for me. I have no money. I have spent mine and my brother's. If my mother would send me, I would jump."

"A gentleman of spirit does not need a woman to buckle his sword or clean his firelock," Hetty declared. "Recall Sir John Armytage, the noble young lord from the North, engaged to Miss Howe. Is marriage an obstacle to glory?"

"To what?"

"To glory!" Hetty shouted, pacing the room with a sudden, fierce energy. "No woman of spirit says 'Stay!' when her country says 'Go!' Sir John volunteered. The King asked when he would depart. 'Tomorrow,' he replied. That is a soldier's answer. Even my father longs to go, leaving us brats behind. Oh, why wasn't I a man? Both my brothers are destined for the Church. As for me, I would have made a famous little soldier!"

She strode about, adopting a military posture, looking as bold as Joan of Arc. Harry watched her with a tender, protective admiration. "I think," he said softly, "I would hardly like to see a musket on that little shoulder, nor a wound on that pretty face, Hetty."

"Wounds! Who fears wounds?" she cried. "If I could carry one, I would use it. Men think we are only for puddings and samplers. Why wasn't I a man? George read Tasso to us yesterday. Look, here is the book, marked where we left off."

"With the mark?" Harry asked dutifully.

"Yes! About a woman disappointed because her brother does not go to war. She says: 'Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail / With lively force and vigour strengthen, so / That I this silken gown...'"

"Silken gown?" Harry queried.

"Well, it is Calimanco, but the book says 'silken gown': '...this silken gown and slender veil / Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo; / Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail, / Nor storms that fall, nor blust'ring winds that blow, / Withhold me; but I would, both day and night, / In pitched field or private combat, fight—'"

"Fight? Yes, that I would! Why are both my brothers to be parsons? One of my papa's children ought to be a soldier!"

Harry laughed, a gentle, kind sound. He looked at her, realizing he would not wish to strike such a tender little warrior.

"Why," he said, extending a finger, "here is a digit nearly as large as your arm. How would you stand before a great, strong man? I should like to see a man try to injure you. Just to see him! You little, delicate, tender creature! Do you suppose any scoundrel would dare be unkind to you?"

Excited by this fantasy of protection, Harry began to pace the room himself, chafing at the mere possibility of a rogue Frenchman daring to insult Miss Hester Lambert.
