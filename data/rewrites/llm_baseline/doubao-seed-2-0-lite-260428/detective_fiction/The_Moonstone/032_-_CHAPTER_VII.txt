The stack of letters we’ve all waded through earlier explains perfectly well why I had no choice but to pass over Lady Verinder’s death with nothing more than the bare announcement at the end of my fifth chapter. I intend from this point forward to keep strictly to what I myself saw and heard with my own two eyes and ears, so let me tell you first that a full month passed between my aunt’s funeral and the next time I crossed paths with Rachel Verinder. That meeting came about when I was invited to spend a few days under her roof, and during that visit, a turn of events tied straight to her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite unfolded that is too important to leave unrecorded here. Once I set that last painful family circumstance down on paper, my work will be done. I will have told every last thing I know, as a most unwilling actual witness to all that came of the Moonstone’s theft.

My aunt’s remains were brought back to Yorkshire and buried in the little churchyard on her own park grounds. The whole family was invited to the service, but I could not, with my religious convictions, rouse myself from the shock of her death in only a few short days to make the journey. Worse still, I was told the rector of Frizinghall would be leading the ceremony — a clerical castaway I’d seen myself, time and again, camped at Lady Verinder’s whist table gambling away the hours he was meant to devote to his parish. Even if I’d been well enough to travel, I doubt I could have in good conscience knelt for a service led by that man.

Lady Verinder’s will left Rachel in the care of her brother-in-law, old Mr. Ablewhite, as her guardian until she came of age or married. It was a foregone conclusion he’d learn from his son Godfrey that he was soon to be the father-in-law of the wealthiest young heiress in the county, and ten days after my aunt’s death, the secret engagement was no secret at all inside the family circle. That old rascal, another confirmed worldly castaway, spent every waking hour thinking of how to make himself and his authority as agreeable as possible to the girl who was about to hand his son a fortune.

Rachel gave him no small amount of trouble at the start, trying to pick somewhere to live. The Montagu Square house reeked of the horror of her mother’s final days. The Yorkshire estate was tangled up in the disgrace of the stolen Moonstone. Her guardian’s house at Frizinghall had neither stain on it, but Rachel’s presence put a damper on the endless parties of the giddy Miss Ablewhite cousins, and she herself asked to delay any visit there to a later date. In the end, old Mr. Ablewhite proposed they take a furnished house in Brighton: his wife, his invalid daughter, and Rachel would move in together, he’d join them later in the season, they’d see no one but a handful of old friends, and Godfrey would trot back and forth from London on the train whenever they needed him.

This aimless flitting from house to house, this restlessness of the body matched by a terrible stagnation of the soul, only matters because it is what brought Rachel Verinder and me back together again.

My Aunt Ablewhite is a large, silent, fair woman with one defining trait: from the day she was born, no one has ever seen her do a single useful thing for herself. She drifts through life accepting every favor and adopting every opinion handed to her. I have never met a more hopeless soul from a spiritual point of view — there is no obstruction to work on at all. She would listen to the Grand Lama of Thibet with the same placid attention she gives me, and parrot his views back as readily as she parrot mine. She found the Brighton furnished house by holing up in a London hotel, flopping on a sofa, and sending for her son to arrange it. She found she needed servants one morning, still in that hotel, eating breakfast in bed, and gave her maid a holiday on condition the girl “start enjoying herself by fetching Miss Clack.”

I found her fanning herself placidly in her dressing gown at eleven o’clock. “Drusilla dear, I need some servants. You’re so clever, please get them for me.” I looked around the messy room, heard the weekday church bells ringing for service, and could not help but utter a word of affectionate remonstrance. “Oh aunt!” I said sadly. “Is this worthy of a Christian Englishwoman? To drift through life so unconcerned for your soul that you can’t even rouse yourself to dress for weekday worship?” My aunt only said, “I’ll put on my gown, Drusilla, if you’ll be kind enough to help me.” What was there to say after that? I have worked miracles on even the most hardened murderesses, but I have never advanced an inch with Aunt Ablewhite. I asked for the list of required servants. She shook her head. “Rachel has it, dear, in the next room.”

I walked through that door, and saw Rachel for the first time since we’d parted in Montagu Square. She looked pitiably small and thin swathed in deep mourning, and if I wasted thought on such perishable trifles as personal appearance, I might note her complexion always suffered for lack of a white border at her cuffs and collar to set it off. But what are complexions, what are pretty faces? Nothing but pitfalls set in our path to higher things. To my utter shock, she stood up when I came in, walked over to me, and held out her hand. “I am glad to see you,” she said. “Drusilla, I’ve spoken very foolishly and rudely to you so many times before. I beg your pardon. I hope you’ll forgive me.”

My face must have shown my astonishment, for she colored a little, and went on to explain. “When my poor mother was alive, her friends weren’t always my friends. Now that I’ve lost her, I turn for comfort to the people she loved. She liked you. Try to be friends with me, Drusilla, if you can.”

To any rightly constituted mind, that motive was simply shocking. Here was a bereaved young woman in Christian England, so utterly untaught in where to find true spiritual comfort that she actually looked for it among her dead mother’s mere acquaintances! Here was my relative, apologizing for her sharp words out of sentiment and impulse, not out of duty or true conviction! It was deplorable, of course, but full of hope for a woman of my experience in doing the Lord’s work. I decided to test how deep that change in her ran, and the very first thing to probe, of course, was her reckless engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

I accepted her hand with every show of cordiality, sat beside her on the sofa, and we talked of family matters and future plans — all except that one plan that was to end in her marriage. Try as I might to steer the conversation that way, she resolutely avoided the subject. I didn’t dare bring it up outright so soon after our reconciliation, but I already had all I needed to see. The reckless, defiant girl who’d screamed at me that day in Montagu Square was gone. That was more than enough to encourage me to set about saving her soul: start with a few earnest warnings against hasty, ill-considered marriage, and move straight on to higher things. I felt the solemn duty to interfere burn bright in my chest, certain I would achieve no small result here.

I steered the conversation back to the servants. “Where is that list, dear?” She handed it over. “Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, footman,” I read. “My dear Rachel, these are only temporary positions for the few months you’ll be renting that house. We’ll never find good, honest, properly devout people to take short term work in London. Has anyone even found the Brighton house yet?”

“Godfrey took it already,” she said. “The people who lived in it wanted us to hire their old staff, but he thought they wouldn’t suit us, and came back without settling anything.”

“You’ve no experience hiring servants yourself, Rachel?”
“None at all.”
“And Aunt Ablewhite won’t exert herself?”
“No, poor dear. Don’t blame her, Drusilla. I think she’s the only truly happy woman I’ve ever met.”

“There are degrees in happiness, darling. We’ll have a long talk about that some day soon,” I said. “In the meantime, I’ll sort the servant problem for you. Your aunt will write a letter to the house people —”
“She’ll sign a letter if I write it for her, which is the same thing.”
“Exactly the same. I’ll take that letter, and go down to Brighton tomorrow.”

Rachel lit up. “How kind you are! We’ll join you as soon as you’re ready for us. And do stay, please, as my guest. Brighton is so lively, you’re sure to enjoy it.”

The invitation was given, and the glorious prospect of doing good work opened up before me. It was mid-week. By Saturday afternoon, the house would be ready for them. In that short time I sifted every available out-of-work servant in Brighton, vetting not just their references but their religious views, until I had a selection my conscience could fully approve. I called on two of my most serious, devout friends living in the town, and told them of my pious purpose for being there: one, a clergyman I knew, kindly reserved our party sittings in his church. The other, a single lady like myself, gave me full run of her entire library of improving, precious publications. I borrowed half a dozen volumes, all carefully chosen for Rachel’s edification, and tucked them on the tables in every room she was likely to use. My preparations were perfect: sound doctrine in the servants who waited on her, sound doctrine in the minister who preached to her, sound doctrine in the books laying on every table. That was the treble welcome my zeal had prepared for the motherless girl. I sat at the window that Saturday afternoon, basking in that heavenly sense of a duty well done, watching the giddy worldly crowd wander past outside, and sighed to think how few of them knew the quiet joy I felt in that moment.

Between six and seven, the carriage pulled up. To my utter shock, the people climbing out were not escorted by Godfrey, as I’d expected — but by that old worldling lawyer, Mr. Bruff. “How do you do, Miss Clack?” he said, grinning. “I mean to stay this time.” The reference to that day in Montagu Square when I’d forced him to put his own business aside to listen to my tracts told me instantly he had some private scheme of his own afoot. I had built a little devout Paradise for my beloved Rachel, and here the Serpent was already slithering through the door.

“Godfrey was so vexed he couldn’t come with us,” Aunt Ablewhite explained. “Something came up in town that kept him tied there. Mr. Bruff volunteered to take his place, and make a holiday of it until Monday morning.” She pointed out the window at an invalid being pushed past in a wheeled bath chair. “The doctors say I must take exercise. That’s the only exercise I care for. You get all the air you want sitting in the chair, and the poor man pulling it is the one who does all the work.”

Rachel stood silent by the far window, staring out at the sea. “Tired, love?” I asked. “No,” she said softly. “Just a little out of spirits. I’ve seen the Yorkshire coast sea look just like this, with that same silver light on it. I was thinking of the days that can never come again.”

Mr. Bruff stayed for dinner, and lingered all evening. The longer I watched him, the surer I was he had some hidden agenda. He chatted the whole time about the most godless, trivial gossip, but when he shook Rachel’s hand to say goodbye, I caught his hard, cunning old eyes resting on her for a long moment with a very peculiar, sharp interest. She was clearly at the center of whatever scheme he was running. He invited himself to luncheon the next day, then trundled off to his hotel.

Next morning, it was impossible to get Aunt Ablewhite out of her dressing gown in time for morning service. Her “invalid” daughter — who in my view suffered from nothing but inherited, incurable laziness — announced she was staying in bed all day. So Rachel and I went to church alone. My gifted clerical friend preached a magnificent sermon, thundering for more than an hour about the heathen world’s total indifference to the sinfulness of small, everyday lapses. When we walked out, I turned to her eagerly. “Did it find its way straight to your heart, dear?” “No,” she said bluntly. “It only made my head ache.” That might have discouraged a lesser woman, but once I’ve set my hand to a good work, nothing can deter me.

We found Aunt Ablewhite and Mr. Bruff already waiting for luncheon. When Rachel said she couldn’t eat a bite because of her headache, that horrible old lawyer saw his chance instantly. “There’s only one cure for a headache,” he said. “A long walk, Miss Rachel. I am entirely at your service, if you’ll do me the honor of taking my arm.” “I’d love that,” she said at once. “A walk is exactly what I was craving.” “It’s past two,” I put in gently. “The afternoon service starts at three, Rachel.” “How can you possibly expect me to sit through another whole sermon with a headache this bad?” she snapped. Mr. Bruff held the door open for her, and a minute later the two of them were gone. I have never in my life felt the burning, solemn duty to interfere more strongly than I did that second — but there was nothing at all I could do. I would have to wait for the first chance later that day.

When I got back from afternoon service, they had just returned. One look at them told me Bruff had said every word he’d come to say. I had never seen Rachel so quiet, so lost in her own thoughts. I had never seen Mr. Bruff treat her with such marked, almost reverent respect. He claimed he had a dinner engagement, and said he would be taking the first train back to London the next morning. “Are you quite sure of your own resolution?” he asked her at the front door. “Quite sure,” she answered. And they parted.

The second he was gone, Rachel vanished into her own room, and never came down to dinner. Her maid was sent down to say her headache had come back. I knocked at the door, offering every sort of sisterly comfort, but it was locked. Plenty of obstinate material to work on here! I felt positively cheered, stimulated, to find her so determined to shut the world out.

The next morning, I followed her tea tray up to her bedroom, sat by her bedside, and said a few earnest, encouraging words. She listened with languid civility. I noticed my carefully chosen improving books were all huddled together in a pile in the corner. Had she looked into them at all? I asked. Yes, she said, and none of them had interested her. Would she let me read a few specially selected passages of the deepest spiritual interest that she must have missed? No, not now, she said, she had other things to think about. Her fingers were busy folding and refolding the frill on her nightgown, her attention elsewhere. I knew I had to rouse her by touching on the worldly matters she still cared for.

“Do you know, love,” I said, “I had the oddest fancy yesterday about Mr. Bruff. I thought, when I saw you after your walk, that he must have told you some terrible bad news.” Her fingers froze on the frill, and her sharp black eyes flashed up at me. “Quite the contrary!” she said. “It was news I was very glad to hear. I owe Mr. Bruff a deep debt of gratitude for telling it to me.” Her fingers went back to the frill, and she turned her face sullenly away. That sort of resistance was nothing new to me after years of doing good work. It only made me try harder. I took the plunge, and spoke openly of her engagement.

“News you were interested to hear?” I said. “My dear Rachel, I suppose that must have been news of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite?”

She shot bolt upright in the bed, her face as white as a sheet. I could see the old, unbridled insolence of her younger self on the tip of her tongue, but she held it back, laid her head back on the pillow, thought for a long minute, and then spoke two words that took my breath away:

“I shall never marry Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.”

I stared at her, stunned. “What can you possibly mean? The whole family considers that marriage a settled thing!”

“Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite is expected here today,” she said stubbornly. “Wait till he comes — and you will see.”

“But my dear Rachel —”

She rang the bell beside her bed. Her maid, the one with the ridiculous bright cap ribbons, appeared in the doorway. “Penelope! My bath.” I give her full credit: that was the only possible way to make me leave the room, and she knew it.

To a mere worldly mind, my position might have seemed full of impossible difficulties. I had planned to lead Rachel to higher things by exhorting her on the sin of rushing into an ill-considered marriage — and now, if she was to be believed, there would be no marriage at all. But oh, my friends! A working Christian of my experience, with an evangelizing prospect before her, takes far broader views than that. If Rachel really broke off the engagement the Ablewhites, father and son, had counted on as good as signed and sealed? The result could only be a storm of harsh words, bitter accusations, and hurt feelings on both sides. When that stormy scene was over, what would follow? A salutary moral depression, of course. Her pride would be worn out, her stubbornness exhausted, after putting up all that fierce resistance. She would turn for comfort to the nearest person who had any comfort to offer — and that nearest person was me, brimful of consolation, overflowing with exactly the right words she would need to hear. Never had the prospect of saving a soul looked brighter than it did in that moment.

She came down to breakfast, but ate nothing, and barely spoke a word. After breakfast she wandered listlessly from room to room, then suddenly sat down at the piano. The music she played was the most shockingly profane stuff I had ever heard — tunes taken straight from stage performances so lewd they curdle my blood to think of them.
