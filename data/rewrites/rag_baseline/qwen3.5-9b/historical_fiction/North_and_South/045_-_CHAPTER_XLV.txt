The echo of Landor's verse hung heavy in the air, a ghostly refrain that haunted Mr. Bell's waking consciousness and tore through the fabric of his sleep. For hours, his mind had wandered the verdant, impossible landscapes of Helstone, re-enacting the role of a tutor in a college where he once held the rank of Fellow. In this fever-dream, time and space dissolved into a fluid suspension; he leaped across babbling brooks that defied gravity, soaring through an autumnal haze where the air smelled of sweet herbs and blooming flowers. Here, amidst the gorgeous decay of falling leaves, stood his newly married friend, the proud Vicar, and beside him, the young wife whose expression was a complex tapestry of resentment toward her new wealth and fierce devotion to her husband—a dynamic Mr. Bell had observed with keen clarity forty years prior. The dream was so vividly textured with the sensory richness of life that upon awakening, the stark reality of his current existence felt like a cold, distant nightmare.

He found himself in the stifling, ornately furnished chamber of a London hotel, the silence pressing against his ears. Those who had spoken moments before, who had brushed against him, were gone—dead, buried, erased from the continuum of earth's fleeting forever. He was an old man, stripped of the exultant vitality of his youth, consumed by an utter loneliness that rendered thought unbearable. With frantic haste, he dressed, attempting to drown the memory of loss in the mundane rituals of preparing for breakfast in Harley Street.

Across the room, the atmosphere was equally fraught. Margaret watched as the lawyer's documents unfolded a grim narrative. Her eyes widened, pupils dilating until the irises seemed swallowed by darkness, while her lips grew pale as white wax. Each detail Mr. Lennox read acted as a stone dropped into a well, extinguishing the last flickers of hope regarding Frederick's exoneration. Evidence that should have cleared his name crumbled away, vanishing beneath the weight of fate's decree. Even Mr. Lennox, usually a man of precise, regulated tones, softened his voice to a tender murmur as he approached the precipice of finality.

"It was a foolish proposal of mine," Lennox admitted, his concern etched into every syllable. "Lieutenant Hale..." He paused, granting the title of the service from which the young man had been so cruelly cast aside, a small act of kindness that momentarily soothed Margaret's anguish. "Lieutenant Hale is happy now; more secure in fortune and future prospects than he could ever have been in the navy; and has, doubtless, adopted his wife's country as his own."

"That is it," Margaret whispered, forcing a brittle smile. "It seems so selfish in me to regret it... and yet he is lost to me, and I am so lonely."

Lennox turned his page, silently wishing for the prosperity he believed awaited him someday, while Mr. Bell blew his nose, maintaining a stoic silence. Within minutes, Margaret composed herself, masking her devastation behind a veneer of polite gratitude. She was acutely aware that her visible grief might seem ungrateful to the man who had spared her pain, yet she knew she would not have endured the ordeal without it.

Mr. Bell rose to bid her farewell, his movements stiff with age. "Margaret!" he called out, fumbling with his gloves. "I am going down to Helstone tomorrow to inspect the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it give you too much pain? Speak out, don't be afraid."

"Oh, Mr. Bell," she began, her voice breaking, unable to find the words beyond the initial plea. Instead, she reached out, grasping his gnarled, gout-ridden hand and pressing a kiss to it.

"Come, come; that's enough," he said, flushing with awkward embarrassment. "I suppose your aunt Shaw will trust you with me. We'll start tomorrow morning, arriving around two. We'll take a snack, order dinner at the Lennard Arms—that's where it used to be—and walk into the forest to whet our appetites. Can you manage it, Margaret? I know it will be a trial for both of us, but it will be a pleasure to me, at least. We'll dine on doe-venison, if we can procure any, and then I'll retire for a nap while you visit old friends. I'll return you safely, barring railway accidents, and I'll insure your life for a thousand pounds before we depart, which may offer some solace to your relations. Otherwise, I promise to bring you back to Mrs. Shaw by lunchtime on Friday. So, if you consent, I shall ascend to propose the terms formally."

"It's no use my trying to explain how much I shall like it," Margaret replied, her voice trembling through fresh tears.

"Well, then, prove your gratitude by keeping those fountains of yours dry for the next two days," Mr. Bell countered gently. "If you don't, I shall feel quite queasy about my own lachrymal ducts, and I dislike that sensation."

"I won't cry a drop," she vowed, winking rapidly to shed the moisture from her lashes and forcing a radiant, if strained, smile onto her face.

"There's my good girl," he said, satisfied. "Then we shall go upstairs and finalize everything."

Margaret stood in a state of almost trembling eagerness as Mr. Bell conferred with Aunt Shaw. Initially startled, the older woman became doubtful and perplexed, her mind racing through the proprieties and dangers of the journey. Yet, eventually, she yielded—not necessarily to her own conviction, but to the rough, persuasive force of Mr. Bell's confidence. Until Margaret's safe return confirmed the success of the endeavor, she could not fully reconcile the morality of the trip. Finally, the prospect of the young woman's happiness settled her doubts. "She was sure it had been a very kind thought of Mr. Bell's," she declared, her voice steady. "And just what she herself had been wishing for Margaret, providing the very change of scenery she required after all this anxious waiting."
