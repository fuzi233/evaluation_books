The morning light filtered weakly through the windowpanes, casting a pallid, unwarming glow that seemed to silver the flimsy wings of Margaret's hopes. Yet, even in this dim atmosphere, a ray of sunshine broke through—a letter from Edith. It arrived with the chaotic, affectionate energy characteristic of its writer, a stream of consciousness that flowed from one trivial observation to another, utterly unconcerned with linear logic. But to Margaret, whose own heart was woven from similar threads of tenderness, the disjointedness was not a flaw but a feature; she absorbed the inconsequence effortlessly, finding charm in the sheer effusion of the sentiment.

"Oh, Margaret, it is worth a journey from England to see my boy!" the letter declared, launching immediately into a celebration of infantile adoration. "He is a superb little fellow, especially in his caps, and most especially in the one you sent him, you good, dainty-fingered, persevering little lady!" Edith's prose danced with envy and joy, declaring that she had made all the mothers in the region jealous and now sought to display her treasure to a fresh audience. There was a hint of cousinly love mingled with maternal pride, a desire simply to hear new voices praise what she already cherished.

"But I do want you so much to come here, Margaret!" the plea intensified, shifting abruptly to the health of Aunt Hale. "It would be the very best thing for her health! Everybody here is young and well, and our skies are always blue, and our sun always shines, and the band plays deliciously from morning till night." The narrative spiraled into a ditty of perpetual summer, where the baby smiled constantly and the air tasted of grapes as common as blackberries. Edith confessed a fleeting moment of pettiness, comparing her husband's growing stoutness and grumpiness unfavorably to her son, only to retract the insult instantly upon hearing news of a charming picnic organized by the officers of the *Hazard*. In the fluid economy of Edith's mind, a pleasant anecdote could erase a grievance faster than fire could consume wood.

"I retract all I said just now," she wrote, admitting she couldn't literally burn her tongue for her rash words, but promising to retract them with equal speed. "Cosmo is quite as great a darling as baby... only, sometimes he is very, very busy." The letter then pivoted to a serious matter, a request born of desperation and love. "Dearest Margaret!—you must come and see me; it would do Aunt Hale good... Get the doctor to order it for her. Tell him that it's the smoke of Milton that does her harm. I have no doubt it is that, really. Three months... of this delicious climate... would quite cure her."

Edith's tone shifted mid-sentence, becoming more constrained, the handwriting tightening as she navigated the delicate politics of her father-in-law, Mr. Hale. She feared his disapproval of war, soldiers, and military bands, knowing his Dissenting principles aligned him with the Peace Society. Yet, she offered a compromise, a theatrical performance of submission: hiding Cosmo's red coat and sword, commanding the band to play only grave, solemn airs, or slowing their pompous marches to a double tempo. She pleaded for Margaret's visit, asking her to assure Mr. Hale that Cosmo and she would do their utmost to make him happy, even if he accompanied them. "If he would like to accompany you and Aunt Hale, we will try and make it pleasant, though I'm rather afraid of any one who has done anything for conscience' sake."

The letter concluded with a frantic urgency regarding clothing and timing. "Tell Aunt Hale not to bring any warm clothes... I tried to wear my great beauty Indian shawl at a picnic... I was like mamma's little dog Tiny with an elephant's trappings on; smothered, hidden, killed with my finery." Edith ended with a threat wrapped in humor: if Margaret did not pack immediately upon receiving the note, she would suspect her of being descended from King Herod.

Margaret read the letter twice. First, she felt a profound longing, a yearning to trade her current confinement for Edith's boundless freedom, to breathe the same air and feel the vitality of a life unburdened by hard pressure. She wished she could teleport there, just for a single day, to reclaim the youth she felt slipping away beneath the weight of responsibility. When she read it a second time, however, the emotion shifted to amusement. She laughed merrily at the letter's resemblance to Edith herself, the erratic jumps in logic and the sudden reversals of fortune.

Her laughter ceased abruptly as Mrs. Hale entered the drawing-room, leaning heavily on Dixon's arm. Margaret rushed forward to adjust the pillows, her movements sharp with concern. Her mother appeared more frail than usual, her skin translucent against the white bedclothes. "What were you laughing at, Margaret?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice thin, recovering only after Margaret settled her onto the sofa.

"A letter I received this morning from Edith," Margaret replied softly. She read it aloud, her voice steady despite the tremor in her chest. For a moment, the letter captivated her mother, who drifted into a reverie, guessing at the name Edith had chosen for her son and debating the merits of various possibilities.

Into this domestic tableau stepped Mr. Thornton, carrying a tray of fruit for Mrs. Hale. His entrance was marked by a strange intensity; he could not deny himself the pleasure of seeing Margaret, nor could he resist the opportunity to observe her. Yet, his demeanor was a study in contradiction. After a brief, cold bow, his eyes refused to linger on her. He remained only to deliver the fruit and exchange a few polite, kindly words before departing. As he left, his gaze met hers with a gravity that felt like a closed door, a silent farewell that carried the weight of deep offense.

Margaret sat frozen, pale and silent. "Do you know, Margaret, I really begin to like Mr. Thornton," Mrs. Hale observed gently.

"Do you?" Margaret managed to force out, her voice icy.

"Yes! I think he is really getting quite polished in his manners."

"He is very kind and attentive—there is no doubt of that," Margaret corrected, her composure returning.

"I wonder Mrs. Thornton never calls. She must know I am ill, because of the water-bed."

"I dare say, she hears how you are from her son."

"Still, I should like to see her. You have so few friends here, Margaret." Mrs. Hale's words revealed a tender craving, a desperate hope that some woman might extend kindness to the daughter who would soon be left motherless. But Margaret could not speak.

"Do you think," Mrs. Hale ventured after a pause, "that you could go and ask Mrs. Thornton to come and see me? Only once—I don't want to be troublesome."

"I will do anything, if you wish it, mamma—but if—but when Frederick comes—"

"Ah, to be sure! We must keep our doors shut—we must let no one in. I hardly know whether I dare wish him to come or not. Sometimes I think I would rather not. Sometimes I have such frightful dreams about him."

"Oh, mamma! We'll take good care. I will put my arm in the bolt sooner than he should come to the slightest harm. Trust the care of him to me, mamma. I will watch over him like a lioness over her young."

The conversation turned to practicalities: sending Martha away, hiring Mary Higgins, and the propriety of language. "Don't get to use these horrid Milton words," Mrs. Hale chided. "'Slack of work': it is a provincialism. What will your aunt Shaw say, if she hears you use it on her return?"

"Oh, mamma! Don't try and make a bugbear of aunt Shaw," Margaret laughed lightly. "Edith picked up all sorts of military slang from Captain Lennox, and aunt Shaw never took any notice of it."

"But yours is factory slang."

"And if I live in a factory town, I must speak factory language when I want it. Why, mamma, I could astonish you with a great many words you never heard in your life. I don't believe you know what a knobstick is."

"Not I, child. I only know it has a very vulgar sound; and I don't want to hear you using it."

"Very well, dearest mother, I won't. Only I shall have to use a whole explanatory sentence instead."

"I don't like this Milton," Mrs. Hale insisted. "Edith is right enough in saying it's the smoke that has made me so ill."

At these words, Margaret sprang to her feet. Her father had just entered the room, and she was terrified that the faint suspicion in his mind—that the industrial air of Milton was harming her mother's health—might crystallize into certainty. She began speaking hurriedly of other matters, oblivious to the fact that Mr. Thornton was following her father.

"Mamma is accusing me of having picked up a great deal of vulgarity since we came to Milton," she announced, referring specifically to the local dialect they had just discussed.

Mr. Thornton's brow darkened visibly. Margaret felt a sudden flush of shame, realizing how easily her speech could be misinterpreted by him. In a desperate attempt to avoid causing further pain, she moved forward, forcing a greeting and addressing him directly.
