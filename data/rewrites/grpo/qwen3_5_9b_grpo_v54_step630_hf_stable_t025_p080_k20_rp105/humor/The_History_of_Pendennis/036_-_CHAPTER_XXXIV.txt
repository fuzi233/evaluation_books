Captain Shandon, usually a man of action rather than administration, had been gently steered by his formidable wife into a new venture: appointing John Finucane, Esquire, as the sub-editor of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. This honor fell to Finucane, a man of the Upper Temple who harbored a deep, almost theatrical affection for the Captain and his family. Their bond was forged in the fires of financial ruin; Finucane's chambers had once served as Shandon's sanctuary when bailiffs were abroad, a refuge so well-known that the sheriff's officers eventually began waiting for the Captain on Finucane's staircase as regularly as at his own door. It was here, amidst the scent of old paper and tea, that Mrs. Shandon often came to pour out her sorrows, seeking rescue plans for her adored husband. Finucane, proud to host such a distinguished lady, would watch her descend the stairs, veil drawn low, with a mixture of genuine concern and a secret, boyish thrill. He hoped, perhaps foolishly, that some rogue might waylay her, giving him the glorious opportunity to rush forward and break the villain's bones—a fantasy that brought her sincere delight.

With the arrangement made, Finucane became the Captain's aide-de-camp in the newspaper world. He spent his evenings tucking little Mary into her crib, offering a sympathetic "Amen" to her prayers for her father, despite his own Roman Catholic faith. But duty called. At six o'clock, he departed for a dinner with Mr. Bungay, the publisher, dressed in his finest, albeit cheaply bought, garb of brilliant colors. In his pocket sat four pounds and two shillings—his weekly salary minus the cost of a new pair of gloves.

At Dick's Coffee-house, Finucane executed a masterclass in salesmanship. He "cut his mutton" with Mr. Bungay and his reader, Mr. Trotter, outlining a vision for the *Pall Mall Gazette* that dazzled the slow-witted Bungay. Finucane spoke with the authority of a man who knew the weekly earnings of every press worker and possessed a thousand economic dodges to save capital. He assigned roles for markets, turf, church intelligence, and chit-chat, presenting himself as the finest sub-editor London had to offer. His rapid calculations on paper left Bungay mystified and impressed.

Yet, beneath the professional veneer, Finucane's true agenda burned bright: the liberation of Captain Shandon. He knew the exact debt against the Captain at Fleet Prison, though no man in England truly knew their own debts. He argued that Shandon worked better in freedom, a claim Bungay initially dismissed, noting that a locked-up Captain was easier to find than a free one. But Finucane played the emotional card, describing Mrs. Shandon's pining and the child's sickness. Moved by this tableau, Bungay promised to consider the matter the next morning. The evening ended with rounds of brandy-and-water; Bungay refused to let Finucane pay the bill, handing the waiter the funds himself. Thus, Finucane retired to his Temple chambers with his full week's salary intact.

That night, he winked a knowing, joyful signal to Mrs. Shandon. She hurriedly gathered her bonnet and shawl, and Finucane offered to take them for a walk. Little Mary, ever delighted by Finucane's gifts and shows, skipped with joy.

"May I go, Charley?" Mrs. Shandon asked hesitantly. "He's got a headache, Mr. Finucane. I persuaded him to stay in bed."

"Go along with you, and Polly," Shandon replied cheerfully from his desk, where he was quoting Greek and Latin from his *Burton's Anatomy*. "Hand me over the book and leave me to my abominable devices."

Finucane escorted the mother and child through the prison gates into the fresh air. Their destination was Paternoster Row, specifically the shop of Mr. Bungay. There, Mrs. Bungay was arriving, dressed in a flamboyant shot-silk gown of red and purple, a yellow shawl, and a parasol of brilliant blue. She carried a parcel and a manuscript bound in red.

Mrs. Shandon, clad in an old black watered silk, looked every bit the lady despite her faded attire. The two women curtsied. Mrs. Bungay, with a tragic look, asked if Mrs. Shandon could leave little Mary with her while she attended to business in the counting-house.

"I want to stay with mamma," Mary cried, burying her face in her mother's dress.

"Go with this lady, Mary, my dear," Mrs. Shandon said gently.

Mrs. Bungay, adopting the voice of an ogress, promised to show Mary "pretty pictures" and opened her parcel to reveal choice sweet biscuits. The treat charmed the child, and she followed Mrs. Bungay into the private entrance. However, when the moment of separation approached, Mary's courage faltered. Seeing Mrs. Bungay's disappointed expression, Mrs. Shandon kindly offered to accompany them upstairs for a few minutes. A second biscuit sealed the deal, and Mary soon prattled away with perfect confidence.

Meanwhile, in the counting-house, Finucane faced a sterner reality. The previous night's generosity had evaporated. Mrs. Bungay had rebuked her husband, declaring Shandon a good-for-nothing who needed no money and criticizing the entire plan of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. She insisted Shandon remain in prison, the best place for him. Finucane's pleas fell on deaf ears; the publisher was inexorable.

But upstairs, the dynamic shifted. Mrs. Shandon's artless sweetness and winning frankness began to melt the fierce Mrs. Bungay. Describing her husband's virtues and the child's failing health, she painted a picture of a clergyman's daughter from the West of England. Mrs. Bungay, though as grim as Lady Macbeth, softened. She recalled her own daughter, Bessy, who had died eighteen years prior, and saw in little Mary a reflection of her lost angel.

"The Capting is an Irishman," Mrs. Bungay admitted, her eyes twinkling. "I couldn't abide them. But his wife is a lady... and a West of England woman, like myself."

She summoned her husband. "What's in the wind now, my dear?" Mr. Bungay asked, surprised by her change of heart.

"The little 'un said, 'Why don't you take Par out of prizn?' I did feel it, Flora, upon my honour I did," Mr. Bungay confessed, admitting he had felt the weight of the child's question.

The result was a heavy, clumsy speech from the publisher. Hearing that sixty-five pounds would secure the Captain's release, he offered to advance the sum, deducting it from future salaries, on the condition that Mrs. Shandon personally settled with the creditors.

"It was the happiest day Mrs. Shandon and Mr. Finucane had seen in a long time," the narrative notes. Finucane roared in a powerful brogue, "Bedad, Bungay, you're a trump!" He tossed Mary into the air with frantic joy.

Mrs. Bungay, looking out at a one-horse carriage, offered to drive them. With Mary nestled between the two women and Finucane on the back seat, they drove triumphantly away from Paternoster Row, while Mr. Bungay, returning to his desk, wondered how women remembered such things. He noted that the Captain's debt was only one hundred and thirty pounds; half would free him, and they would pay the rest later.

Mrs. Shandon's mission was a resounding success. Armed with the promise of payment, she mollified the creditors with a bribe of ten shillings in the pound. The following Sunday marked the last time, for some time, that the Captain spent his days behind bars.
