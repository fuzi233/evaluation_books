I left Bretton a few short weeks after little Paulina’s departure, never once guessing as I turned my back on its sun-warmed cobblestone lanes and the familiar glow of Mrs. Bretton’s parlour that I would never set foot there again. Six months away from my childhood home had stretched thin, and by the time I walked up the path to my family’s doorstep, the world I returned to was hollowed out, all the old certainties already washed away.

Readers might well picture me drifting through the next eight years wrapped in soft, unbroken calm: a small boat bobbing on glass-still harbour water, the steersman asleep on the deck, no sharp edges or storms to break the peace. So many young women are supposed to glide through life like that, untroubled, sheltered – why not me? I could let you imagine me lazing in the sun, warm, well-fed, safe, and no one would have any reason to contradict that pretty picture. But I cannot hide the truth forever. That quiet harbour was never mine. I still wake from nightmares gasping, choking on the briny salt of waves that drag me under, the cold, heavy pressure of water filling my lungs. The storm that wrecked my life raged for days and nights on end: no sun, no stars to steer by, every last bit of tackle thrown overboard to keep the ship afloat, until every shred of hope of rescue was gone. The ship sank. Everyone I loved went down with it.

I never spoke of that ruin to anyone. There was no one left to tell. I’d lost touch with Mrs. Bretton years before, obstacles other people had put between us cutting our correspondence clean off. I’d heard rumours, too: the fortune she’d held in trust for Graham, invested in a joint-stock venture, had all but vanished. Graham had qualified for his profession, and both he and his mother had left Bretton for London, no one knew exactly where. There was no one left I could lean on. Circumstances forced self-reliance out of me, same as it does thousands of people who have no other choice. So when Miss Marchmont, the wealthy, famously eccentric maiden lady who lived in the big house on the edge of our village, sent for me, I went at once, hoping she might have some work I could do.

Miss Marchmont had been a cripple from rheumatoid arthritis for twenty long years, her hands and feet all but useless, confined entirely to the upstairs floor where her sitting room opened straight onto her bedroom. She was furrowed-faced, grey-haired, sharp-edged from decades of solitude and constant unyielding pain, known far and wide for being irritable and exacting. Her lady’s companion of many years was about to marry, and she’d heard I was now orphaned and adrift, and thought I might fill that empty spot. She laid the offer out plain to me after tea, as we sat by her crackling fire, alone.
“It won’t be easy,” she said, no softening of the truth. “I need constant attention, you’ll hardly ever get to leave the house. But it might be better than the aimless life you’ve been leading lately.”
I hesitated. Logically, of course it should have been more than acceptable – but the thought of shutting myself up in those two small rooms, watching over a suffering woman for what felt like the rest of my youth, the years I’d already lost to grief weighing heavy on my chest for a heartbeat. Then I steadied myself. I’d never been one to romanticize hardship, to blow it all out of proportion, no matter how much it hurt.
“I’m only not sure I’m strong enough for it,” I told her.
“That’s my worry too,” she said bluntly. “You look half-dead, girl.”
She was right. I glanced in the mirror hung over her mantel, saw my own reflection in black mourning dress: pale, hollow-eyed, faded. But the blight was only on the surface, I thought. Deep down, I still felt the spark of life. When I told her I had no other clear plans yet, but might find work somewhere else, she was kinder than I expected. “Go try what you want,” she said. “I’ll keep this spot open for you for three months, just in case.”
Before I could thank her, a paroxysm of pain wracked her. I moved fast, following her directions to apply the right liniments, adjust her pillows, until the worst of it ebbed. In that small, urgent moment, a quiet sort of intimacy bloomed between us. I saw how firm and patient she was, even through agony, and she saw that my care for her was not just a paid duty, but genuine sympathy. She sent for me again the next morning, and the one after that, every day for a week. The more I knew her, the more her sharp edges softened into something I could respect: her outbursts of temper were never cruel, never humiliating, more like a grumpy, overprotective mother scolding a daughter than a harsh mistress lording over a servant. Even at her fiercest, she was always fair, always logical. By the end of that week, I’d agreed to stay.

My whole world narrowed down to those two warm, steam-fogged rooms. My days revolved entirely around her: her pain was my worry, her relief my small daily joy, her rare sharp scoldings my only small punishment, her quiet praise my greatest reward. I almost forgot there were fields outside the frosted windows, rivers, wide stretches of sea, a sky that changed colour with every dawn. I was almost happy to forget. I’d already been tamed by fate, softened by loss, and I found so much to love in her: the stubborn strength of her virtues, the depth of the passions she’d kept locked inside for thirty years, the raw honesty of every word she spoke. I would have stayed by her side, caring for her, for twenty more years if fate had let me. But fate had other plans. It wouldn’t let me hide away in that small, safe duty forever. The small, precious scrap of affection I held onto, that I valued like a solid pearl, was going to slip out of my fingers like melting hail.

It was a cold February night. After a winter that had been strangely still and mild, storms were howling to usher in spring. I’d helped Miss Marchmont into bed, and sat by the fire sewing, when the wind picked up outside, wailing at the windowpanes. All day it had moaned, but as midnight drew near, it gained a new, sharp, almost human voice – a plaint so raw and grief-stricken it made my nerves ache. I dropped my sewing, pressing my hands over my ears uselessly, because I’d heard that exact wind before. Three times in my life, that same keening east wind had blown before an outbreak of epidemic, before distant earthquakes, before rivers burst their banks and high tides swallowed the coastlines far away. I trembled, but Miss Marchmont slept peacefully.

Half an hour before midnight, the storm fell completely silent. The fire that had been smouldering flared bright, the air turned sharp and frosty. I pulled back the curtain, saw the stars glinting hard and bright over a world crusted with frost. When I turned back, Miss Marchmont was awake, propping herself up on one elbow, looking at me with a light I’d never seen in her eyes before.
“Is it clear out?” she asked. I nodded.
“I thought so,” she said, her voice soft, almost giddy. “I feel so strong tonight. So well. Raise me up, will you? I feel young again. Young, light, happy. What if this illness of mine is finally breaking? What if I get my health back? It would be a miracle.”
I said nothing. Miracles didn’t happen to people like us, I thought. She didn’t notice, though, she was already drifting back through her memories, her face soft with a glow I’d never seen there.
“Memory is my best friend tonight,” she said. “She’s bringing everything back to me, not as faded ghosts, but as real as they were thirty years ago. I can feel the joy of that one perfect year I had with Frank, the only love of my life. He was good, Lucy – noble, faithful, so much better than I was. We had one whole year of perfect, unclouded happiness. I loved him more than I’ve loved anything else in my life. Why did it get taken away? Why was I sentenced to thirty years of grief, after just twelve months of bliss?” She paused, her voice softening. “I don’t know why. But tonight, for the first time in thirty years, I can say: Thy will be done, God. And I truly believe I’ll see Frank again when I die. I never dared believe that before.”
“Frank is dead?” I whispered.
“It happened on Christmas Eve,” she said, and her voice was gentle, no sharp edge of pain left to it. “I was all dressed up, waiting for him to come visit me before our wedding. I sat by the fire, watching the snow fall outside the window, waiting to see him ride up the path. The moon was full, silver over the shrubs, over the snow on the lawn. I waited, a little impatient, but never a single doubt in my heart. I thought I heard his horse’s hooves thudding fast on the drive, and I laughed to myself, thinking you reckless thing, Frank, riding that hard in the snow, you’ll hurt yourself. Then I saw it: not Frank on his horse, but the horse itself, trembling, covered in sweat, being led up to the door. I ran out into the snow, and there he was, lying on the ground. He’d fallen, hit his head. He wasn’t dead yet. I dragged him into the house, wouldn’t let the servants push me away from him. The surgeon did everything he could, but there was nothing to be done. He had just enough strength to hold me, whisper my name, say he was dying in Paradise, with me. He breathed his last when the first pink light of Christmas morning broke over the snow. That was thirty years ago.”
She wiped a tear away, smiling a little. “I’ve been a bitter, selfish woman ever since. The pain just ate at me. But tonight, I think I’m finally ready. Tomorrow, I’ll start making things better for you, Lucy. I’ll leave something for you, so you’ll be taken care of long after I’m gone. My head aches now, talking so much. Go to bed, child. The clock’s striking two. Don’t worry about me. I’m going to sleep well tonight.”

I went to my small cot in the little closet connected to her bedroom. The night passed completely quiet. When I woke in the morning, she was still, no breath left in her, almost cold, her face soft and calm, no trace of the pain that had gnawed at her for thirty long years. That sudden flood of joy, that clear, soft peace, had been the last flicker of her life. One gentle stroke had cut the thread that had been fraying for decades, setting her free at last, to go meet her Frank.
