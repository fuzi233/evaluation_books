The Mother of Sorrows pushed herself to her feet, drew her veil tight over her face, and glided off to rouse the Little Ones. They slept so still they might have lain unmoved through a hundred cycles of the long twilight, but the second her voice brushed the edges of their dreams, they sprang upright, bright and sharp as if they had been newly spun out of starlight. Chattering soft, merry nonsense, they pattered down the stairs after her, until she led them to the chamber where Princess Lilith lay, tears still leaking slow down her cheeks as she slept.
Their bright, laughing faces smoothed over into quiet wonder. They glanced from the princess’s face out at the streaking rain lashing the window, then back to her glistening cheeks.
“The sky is falling apart!” one piped up.
“The white juice is running out of the princess!” another breathed, awed.
“Are they rivers?” Odu asked, leaning in to stare at the tiny, glimmering streams carving paths down her hollow cheekbones.
“Aye,” Mara answered. “The most wonderful rivers in all the worlds.”
“But I thought rivers were bigger! They rush and roar like a whole crowd of Little Ones yelling games!” Odu protested, glancing over at me, the only one who’d ever told him tales of waterways far from the desert.
“Look out at the rivers falling from the sky,” Mara said, nodding to the downpour. “They’re coming down to wake all the hidden waters under the earth. Soon rivers will run everywhere, loud and giddy as a hundred thousand happy children. How glad they’ll make you, little ones! You’ve never seen them, you don’t know how alive water is.”
“The sky is glad the princess is finally good,” Odu said. “That’s what the rain is.”
“Are her tears the princess’s gladness?” Luva asked. “They’re not juice, they’re not red.”
“They’re the juice hidden deep inside the juice,” Mara told her.
Odu poked his own eye with one finger, stared at the wet tip of it, and shook his head.
“The princess won’t bite anyone now, will she?” Luva said.
“No,” Mara said. “She never will again. Now we have to take her closer to her real home.”
“Is that place a nest?” Sozo asked.
“A very big nest, yes. But we stop somewhere first before we get there.”
“What place?”
“It’s the largest room in all this world. But I think it’s filling up, soon it’ll be too full of little nests to hold anything else. Go get your clumsies—your riding packs.”
“Are there cats there?”
“Not one. The nests are stuffed so full of lovely dreams no cat could ever squeeze through the doors.”
“We’ll be ready in one minute!” Odu yelped, and darted out the door, the rest of the boys tumbling after him, all except Luva.
Lilith was awake now, listening, a sad, faint smile on her face.
“Her rivers are running so fast,” Luva said, rooted at her side, unable to look away from the princess’s face. “Her whole robe is soaked, the clumsies won’t like sitting on it!”
“They won’t mind,” Mara said. “Those tears are so clean they scrub the whole world bright.”
I’d fallen asleep by the hearth, but I’d been awake and listening for some time, and now I stood up.
“Time to mount, Mr. Vane,” our hostess said.
“Tell me—isn’t there a way to avoid the monster channels and their den entirely?” I asked.
“There’s an easy, safe path across the dried riverbed I can show you,” she answered. “But you will have to pass through the horde of the monsters once more.”
“I fear for the children,” I said.
“Fear won’t come within a mile of them,” she promised.
We stepped out of the cottage. The great elephants and our horses were waiting, huddled quiet by the door. Odu was already perched on the neck of one of the two elephants that would carry Lilith between them. I swung up onto Lona’s horse, and Mara handed Lona’s soft, sleeping body up to me to cradle in my arms. When Mara came back out with Lilith, a little cry of delight burst from the children: the princess wasn’t wrapped in any shroud, her face was bare, and she was more beautiful than any of them had ever seen. They stared, entranced, and forgot all about taking her from Mara, but the elephants lifted her gently with their trunks—one curled around her waist, one around her knees—and between Mara’s guiding hands, laid her stretched out across the padded platform strung between their backs.
“Why does the princess have to leave?” a small boy asked. “She could stay here and keep being good!”
“She both wants to go and doesn’t want to go. We’re helping her,” Mara said. “She couldn’t stay good here. We’re taking her where she’ll get the help she needs to pry open that hand she’s kept clenched shut for a thousand years.”
“That long? Then she’s learned to do without it, why open it now?”
“Because her fist is closed tight around something that was never hers to keep.”
“Lady Mara, can we have some of your extra-dry bread before we leave?” Luva asked.
Mara smiled, and brought out four whole loaves and a massive jug of cold water.
“We’ll eat it while we walk,” they said, but they drank the water first, gurgling it down with glee.
“I think this must be elephant-juice,” one of them announced. “It’s making me so strong I could run all the way to the sky rivers.”
We set out, the Lady of Sorrows walking at our head, brighter than the sun, her white leopardess padding close at her heels. I thought she was only going to lead us to the cross-path across the channels, but soon it was clear she was coming all the way with us. I would have dismounted to offer her my horse, but she waved me off.
“I carry no burden,” she said. “I’ll walk with the children.”
It was the softest, fairest morning I’d ever seen. The sun blazed bright, the wind blew sweet as honey, but they couldn’t wake the dead desert to life—there was not a drop of water anywhere in the sand. We crossed the rutted channels without a single misstep, the Little Ones gamboling around Mara the whole way, and we didn’t reach the crest of the ridge overlooking the monster burrow until the sun was already slipping below the horizon. I made the Little Ones climb up onto their elephants’ necks then, because the moon might rise late, and I couldn’t quite shake my worry for them. The Lady of Sorrows led the way at my side, the two elephants carrying Lilith at the center of our little procession, the leopardess bringing up the rear. Just as we reached the edge of that cursed shallow basin, the moon peeked over the horizon, lighting up the flat, still ground in front of us like polished glass.
Mara stepped first into the basin. Her footsteps, and the hooves of my horse, made no ripple, drew no stir from the earth below. But the second the two elephants carrying Lilith stepped onto that seemingly solid ground, the whole surface began to heave and boil. The entire hellish brood of that accursed nest woke screaming. Monsters burst up from every side: long snaking necks, jaws full of needle teeth, writhing knotty tentacles by the hundreds, all surging straight for Lilith. She lay rigid, frozen in a paroxysm of terror, not daring to twitch a single finger. I doubt the horde even saw the Little Ones. Not one of the abominations touched a child. Not one filthy claw or slavering maw crossed the living rampart Mara had drawn around the princess.
“Little Ones, keep your elephants pressed tight around the princess!” I called. “Be brave, they won’t hurt you.”
“What won’t hurt us? We don’t know what we’re supposed to be brave about!” they called back, and I realized with a jolt that they couldn’t see a single one of the horrors writhing all around us.
“Never mind that, then,” I told them. “Just stay close.”
They were armored in their innocent blindness. What they could never perceive could never harm them. The hideous things I saw that night no words could ever describe.
Mara was a few paces ahead of me when a bodiless, bouncing head popped out of the dirt right between us. The white leopardess lunged out from under the elephants to tear it apart, but it twisted its face into a loathsome grimace, howled, spun in a full circle, and dove back into the dirt before she could reach it. With Lona’s soft, sleeping weight warm in my arms, the taint of death clinging to me kept me from fear, and I watched the whole horde unflinching. Mara walked on before me, the leopardess now padding close at her heels, shivering, for the air had turned bitter cold, when the ground to my left began to heave. A low wave of living earth slithered toward us, rising higher and higher, until a horrific head with fleshy tubes wriggling where its hair should be slid out of it, gaped a great oval maw, and snapped at me. The leopardess lunged, but bit nothing but empty air. A second later, a giant snake head shot up right under our feet, eyes blazing with a sick, watery light. The leopardess attacked it, and came away with nothing in her jaws. She charged a third monster with the same ferocity, and failed again, then fell back sullen, no longer wasting her fury on the phantoms.
I understood the peril we were in, and kicked my horse to speed the crossing—all the more so when I saw the moon acting strange. It would rise a little, then dip back down a full breadth of its own size, the arc of its path so low it looked like it was going to fall right out of the sky. We were almost across, when a long neck rose up between us and the far bank, topped with what looked like the bloom of some Stygian lily: the head of a rotting corpse, mouth half open and crammed full of sharp canine teeth. I rode on. It retreated, then slid aside. Mara stepped out onto the solid far shore, but the leopardess between us, roused one last time, turned and lunged straight for the monster’s throat.
I stayed where I was, making sure the elephants, the children, and Lilith all made it safe to the bank. When I turned back to look for the leopardess, the moon dropped entirely. For one split second I saw the great white cat and the snakemonster thrashing tangled in a cloud of dust, then absolute darkness swallowed them. My horse reared, trembling, and bounded after the elephants in three long leaps. As I caught up with them, a shapeless, quivering mass of jelly dropped right onto Lilith’s chest. A white dove fell out of the dark right after it, stabbing the jelly over and over with its beak. The thing gave a squelching, sucking whimper, and fell off her onto the ground.
Then I heard a woman’s voice talking to Mara, and I recognized it instantly.
“I fear she is dead,” Mara said.
“I will send to find her,” the Mother answered. “But why would you fear for her, or any soul? Death cannot harm one who dies doing the work they were sent to do.”
“I will miss her sorely. She is good, and wise. But I would not have her live past her appointed hour.”
“She went down with the wicked, and she will rise with the righteous. We will see her again before long.”
“Mother,” I called, though I could not see her in the dark, “we have come to you many, and most of us are Little Ones. Will you have room for all of us?”
“Every one of you is welcome,” she answered. “Sooner or later all will be Little Ones, for every living thing must sleep in my house. It goes well for those who come to sleep young, and willing. My husband is even now making up Lilith’s couch. She is not young, nor quite willing, but it is a good thing indeed that she has come at last.”
Their voices faded as Mara and her mother walked off together into the dark. But we saw a faint glimmer of light bobbing far ahead, and we stumbled across the moor toward it. Adam stood in the cottage door holding a candle, waving to guide us in, talking to Eve, who was behind him setting bread and wine out on the table.
“Happy children,” I heard her say, “to have already looked on the face of my daughter! Surely her face is the fairest in all the great worlds.”
When we reached the door, Adam welcomed us almost gaily. He set the candle down on the threshold and walked over to the elephants, meaning to carry Lilith inside himself, but she pushed him away, shoved the two elephants apart, and stood straight up between them. The elephants stepped back, leaving her face to face with the husband she had once abandoned. She looked gaunt, unlovely, ashamed—but not submissive. He looked at her, and a welcome shone so bright in his eyes it cut right through their usual severity.
“We have waited a very long time for you, Lilith,” he said.
She said nothing. Eve and her daughter stepped to the door.
“The mortal enemy of my children!” Eve murmured, radiant in her soft beauty.
“Your children are no longer in any danger from her,” Mara said. “She has turned away from evil.”
“Do not trust her too quickly, Mara,” her mother said. “She has deceived thousands.”
“But you will show her the mirror of the Law of Liberty, mother, so she can step inside it and stay there? She agrees to open her hand and give back what she stole. Will not the great Father welcome her home to inheritance with all his other children?”
“I do not know Him,” Lilith whispered, her voice thin with fear and doubt.
“That is why you are so miserable,” Adam said.
“I will go back where I came from!” she cried, wringing her hands, and turned to run.
“That is exactly where I want you to go—back to Him you came from,” Adam said. “When you were in your agony, didn’t you cry out for Him?”
“I cried out for Death! To escape Him, and you!”
“Death is already on his way to lead you to Him. You know nothing of Death, or the Life that lives inside Death. Both are your friends. I am dead, and I want you to die too, because I live, and I love you. You are tired, and heavy laden. Are you not ashamed at last? Has the thing you corrupted become a loathsome weight to you? Would you go on living in that disgrace forever? You can never stop existing. Will you not be restored, and at last *be* fully alive?”
She stood silent, her head bowed.
“Father,” Mara said, “pick her up in your arms, carry her to her couch. There she will open her hand, and die into life.”
“I will walk,” the princess said.
Adam turned and led the way. Lilith stumbled slowly after him into the cottage. Eve came to me where I sat on my horse, Lona still in my arms, reached up, and took the little girl from me to carry her inside. I dismounted, and the Little Ones clambered down off their elephants. The horse and the great elephants stood shivering, until Mara went to each of them, patting their necks, stroking their flanks. One by one they sank to the floor and fell asleep. Mara led us into the cottage, and gave the Little Ones bread and wine from the table. Adam and Lilith stood silent by the far wall. Eve came back out of the chamber of death where she had laid Lona down, and offered bread and wine to the princess.
“Your beauty slays me. I do not want food. I want death,” Lilith said, and turned away from her.
“This food will help you die,” Eve said. But Lilith would not taste a bite.
“If you will not eat or drink, Lilith,” Adam said, “come see the place I have prepared for you to lie in peace.”
He led her through the door that opened into the House of Death. She followed, quiet at last—until her foot crossed the threshold, and she wrenched it back, pressing a hand to her chest, as if some cold immortal thing had stabbed right through her. A wild blast howled down on the roof, then died away to a soft moan. She was ghastly pale, shaking with terror.
“It is him,” her lips formed, no sound coming out. I read the words.
“Who, princess?” I whispered.
“The great Shadow,” she breathed.
“He cannot come in here,” Adam said. “He can hurt no one here. Even he is under my authority.”
“Are all the children in the house safe?” Lilith asked, and at that one word, Eve’s heart softened completely to her.
“He has never dared touch a child,” Eve said. “And you never hurt a child, either. Your own daughter you only sent to a beautiful sleep—she was already long dead when you struck her. Now Death will be your atonement. You will sleep side by side, and wake together.”
“Wife,” Adam said, “let us tuck all the children into their beds first, so she can see they are safe.” He turned back to fetch them.
The second he was gone, the princess dropped to her knees before Eve, clinging to her knees. “Beautiful Eve, please make your husband kill me. He will listen to you. I want to, but I cannot pry my hand open. I cannot do it.”
“You cannot truly die until you open it. Killing you won’t help you,” Eve said. “No one but the Shadow can kill you, and anyone he kills never knows they are dead. They live to do his will, and think they are acting for themselves.”
“Then show me my grave. I am so tired I cannot live another minute. I have to go to the Shadow—even though I do not want to!” She could not understand. She struggled to stand, and collapsed at Eve’s feet. Eve lifted her up, and carried her deeper into the dark chamber.
