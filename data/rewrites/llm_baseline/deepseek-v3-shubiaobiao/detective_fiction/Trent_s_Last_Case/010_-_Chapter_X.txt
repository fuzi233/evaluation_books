The rain whispered against the windows of White Gables, a steady, mournful drizzle that blurred the world beyond the glass into a watercolor of grays and greens. Mrs. Manderson stood at the window, her reflection a ghostly outline against the gloom. The weather had turned unseasonably bitter—a rarity for June—and the oppressive chill seemed to seep into the room, into her bones.

A knock sounded at the door.

“Come in,” she said, straightening almost imperceptibly, as though bracing herself against an unseen weight.

The maid announced Mr. Trent. He had come at an early hour, apologetic but insistent—a matter of urgency.

*Of course it was urgent*, she thought. Everything was urgent now.

She turned from the window, caught her own gaze in the mirror—the dark eyes, the pale, composed face—and gave a faint, humorless twist of her lips before facing the door.

Trent entered, and she noted the change in him at once. The usual easy confidence was gone, replaced by a weariness that spoke of sleepless nights. His expression was shuttered, deliberate.

“May I come to the point at once?” he asked, dispensing with pleasantries. “There’s a train I ought to catch, but I can’t leave until this is settled.”

She gestured to a chair. “You look exhausted. Sit, please. If you’ve come, it must be necessary.”

He remained standing. “Mrs. Manderson, what I have to say won’t be easy. I’ve uncovered things about your husband’s death—things no one else suspects. What I *believe* I’ve proven will be a shock, possibly worse. But if you give me reason to think publication would harm you beyond bearing, I’ll suppress it.” He tapped a long envelope on the table. “This contains my report. The choice is yours.”

Her hands tightened slightly, but her face betrayed nothing. “I understand. Please go on.”

“First,” he said, his voice cool, methodical, “I must ask: was it true, what you testified at the inquest? That you had no idea why your husband had grown distant in those last months?”

Her eyes flashed. “You’re asking if I perjured myself.”

“I am.” His gaze didn’t waver. “We both know the truth isn’t always spoken under oath.”

For a moment, she was silent. Then she turned to the window, her voice low but clear.

“I was twenty when I married him. I admired his strength, his certainty. But I learned quickly that his world was his business, and I was… an accessory. A social asset, or so he thought.” She exhaled sharply. “I failed him in that. The society he wanted me to thrive in was hollow—money and pretension, nothing more. I tried to play the part, but he saw through it eventually. He *always* saw through things.”

Her words came faster now, as if a dam had broken. “He never understood how I loathed it—the endless parties, the same vapid faces. I stole moments of freedom, trips with old friends, just to breathe. But he knew. And once he did, everything between us became… polite. Distant.”

She sank into a chair, her energy spent. “That was how things stood when he died.”

Trent watched her, his mind racing. There was more here—something she hadn’t said. He steeled himself.

“Mrs. Manderson,” he said slowly, “was your husband’s coldness connected to John Marlowe?”

The effect was instant. A choked cry, her hands flying to her face as she crumpled forward, her whole body shaking with sobs.

Trent stood frozen. Every instinct screamed to go to her, to offer comfort—or worse, to confess the reckless, unthinkable words clawing at his throat.

Instead, he placed the envelope neatly on the table and walked out, shutting the door softly behind him.

Outside, the rain fell harder. He strode blindly, his jaw clenched, his heart a battlefield of duty and desire.

He had his answer.

And it shattered everything.
