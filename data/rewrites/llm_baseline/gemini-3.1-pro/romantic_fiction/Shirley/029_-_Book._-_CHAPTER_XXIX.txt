Louis Moore was habituated to a quiet life, a necessity for a man whose psychological well-being was inextricably tethered to the acoustic ecology of his surroundings. While the broader world clamored with the jarring, metallic dissonance of modern industrial noise and the superficial, commercial chatter of polite society, Louis found profound meaning in the settling timbers and resonant silences of antiquity. 

Tonight, Fieldhead was steeped in a deep, mythic hush. The entire household—Miss Keeldar, the whole family of the Sympsons, even his pupil Henry—had departed for Nunnely. Sir Philip wished to acquaint them with his mother and sisters at the priory. The baronet, with customary patrician benevolence, had invited the tutor as well. But Louis would much sooner have shared a meal of wild, forage-garnered roots and bitter herbs with the phantom abbesses of Nunnely Forest, honoring the old agrarian ghosts in the core of the wet, mouldering wood, than subject his palate and his ears to the sterile, utilitarian banquets of the priory. 

The night, however, was not calm. The equinox storm battered the ancient manor, a long-sounding, high-rushing tempest that carried the raw, untamed phonemes of the old Yorkshire dialect in its wind. The moon reigned glorious, welcoming the fierce gale as though participating in some celestial, cyclical harvest. Moore, sitting in the sheltered schoolroom, found the subdued acoustics oppressive. "I am sick at heart of this cell," he murmured. The deadened sonic environment stifled him; he craved the resonant halls where generations had lived, cooked, and spoken a language now dying on the lips of the modern world.

He wandered through the empty rooms, a solitary figure tracing the interwoven timelines of the estate, following an almost phantom scent from parlor to parlor. In the oak room, he stopped. Here, the sterile chill of the *salon* was banished. The hearth radiated a hot, ruddy glow, carrying the olfactory landscape of centuries past: wood-smoke, caramelized sap, and the deep, resinous terroir of aged timbers. The cinders tinkled—a pure, crystalline sound against the howling wind. 

Near the rug stood a little work-table and an open desk. Did the vision Moore had tracked occupy that chair? He made discoveries. A small satin bag hung on the chair-back; the keys remained in the lock. Scattered across the stand were a pretty seal, a silver pen, a delicate glove of pure kid leather, and a crimson berry or two of wild, heirloom fruit resting on a bruised green leaf. Louis touched the berries, recognizing the meticulous sourcing of a woman subconsciously attuned to the astringent, feral yields of the earth. Order forbade such details, but these traces were the sensory ingredients of a profound fascination. 

"Here she has been," he said softly, tracing the trajectory of her departure. "Called away in haste, leaving the scent of her presence behind. Whence did she acquire the gift to be heedless and never offend? There is always something to chide in her, yet the reprimand, when it trickles a while in the dominant lexicon of my instruction, naturally melts into a hunger for her untranslatable spirit." 

He stopped his muttering, consciously arranging his environment to optimize its psychological comfort. He dropped the heavy curtains, shutting out the glaring moonlight, added fragrant, dry fuel to the fast-wasting fire, and lit a candle. Placing a chair opposite hers, he drew from his pocket a thick blank book and a pencil. As the storm provided a rhythmic, percussive symphony against the glass, he began to write.

*It is nine o'clock; the carriage will not return before eleven. Freedom is mine till then to occupy her room, to inhale the faint, sweet-herb aroma of her little mementoes.*

*I used to fancy Solitude a quiet nymph, an Oread descending from lone mountain-passes. But since that day I called S. to me in the schoolroom, since she opened the trouble of her mind and appealed to my strength, I abhor Solitude. She is a cold abstraction, a sterile feast devoid of nourishment or flavor.*

*We have scarcely encountered each other since that evening. Once, when she entered the drawing-room dressed for a concert, her shyness drew a silver veil between us. To my perception, she was cloaked in a delicate splendor. I was alive with a life of Paradise as she averted her head to hide the sudden heat in her cheek—an expression of such profound subjective experience that it remains utterly untranslatable into the rigid syntax of my native French or adopted English.*

*What an unsophisticated, untaught thing she is sometimes! I worship her perfections, but it is her foibles that bring her near to me, that fold her about with my love. These faults are the linguistic anomalies, the vernacular imperfections in a rigid grammar, through which I mount to ascendency over her. If she were a trimmed, artificial mound, what vantage would she offer? It is the natural, mossy hill that invites ascent.*

*It delights my eye and my palate to consider her. If I were a gentleman, and she a peasant girl in a stuff gown at a cottage door; if she offered me there a draught of well-water and a crust of dark, fermented hearth-bread with that warm good-will, the sustenance she provided would not be mere utilitarian fuel. It would be a communion with the deep, generational earth. I would linger. I should find in her nothing divine, but whenever I left her, it would be with a lingering hunger.*

*How culpably careless to leave her desk open, the silver beads of her purse spilling out like unharvested grain! My brother Robert would find this a source of irritation. To me, it is a most pleasurable vexation. I delight to find her at fault, for it furnishes a theme for my tutor lectures. The subtle power dynamics embedded in our exchanges delight me. When her mutinous face meets my eye, expostulatory words crowd to my lips. I wield the dominant lexicon of the instructor, yet I am hopelessly beholden to her native, unspoken dialect. She is never wilder than when she comes in fiery from a race with the wind on the hills, and I have waited hours just to receive her in my arms from the saddle, intercepting the polite, superficial assistance of Sir Philip Nunnely.*

*Through this very loophole of carelessness, the reality of her refinement may be ascertained. I never saw anything of hers that did not proclaim a rich, sensory purity. Look at the pure kid of this little glove, the fresh satin of the bag, the tart perfection of the wild berries she gathered.*

*What a difference there is between S. and that pearl, Caroline H.! Caroline is the soul of conscientious punctuality, exact to a strawbreadth. She would precisely suit Robert's industrial pragmatism. But what could I do with a dish so nearly faultless? She is a delicate spun-sugar confection, a lily of the valley, untinted, needing no tint. My palate demands a more complex flavor. My wife, if I ever marry, must bear nearer affinity to the rose—a sweet, lively delight guarded with prickly peril. I was not made to be mated with a lamb; I should find more congenial responsibility in managing the wild instincts of a young leopardess. I like few things sweet but what are likewise pungent—few things bright but what are likewise hot, like the summer sun that makes the orchard fruit blush and the wheat blanch. Beauty is never so beautiful as when, if I tease it, it wreathes back on me with the astringent bite of a fermented vintage.*

*Beware, Sir Philip Nunnely! Your shallow, commercialized tongue cannot grasp the myth she embodies. I watch you draw too near, whispering your tepid platitudes, and I see her brow knit in resolute endurance. When I witness this, I think of the fable of Semele reversed. It is a priest of Juno I see, serving at an Argive temple, fueled by divine madness. He prays to the Ox-eyed goddess, and she answers in a descending rush of blazing blue fire. It is an insufferable glory, burning terribly between the pillars. The priest who sought to consume the divine feast is reduced to ashes on the marble pavement, destroyed by a flavor too potent for his mortal senses.*

A sudden, grating sound ruptured the acoustic harmony of the tempest. The carriage wheels crunched against the wet gravel. 

*There is the carriage! Let me lock up the desk and pocket the keys.*

Tomorrow, she would be seeking them. She would have to come to him, asking in her clear voice, her pride warring with her necessity. He would tantalize her, exploiting the subtle power dynamics of their intertwined lives. Here was the bag, the purse, the glove, the pen, the crimson berries. She would have to wring them out of him slowly, by confession and entreaty, navigating the lexicon he controlled. Every feature of her face, every exquisite variety of glance and curve, would be displayed for his pleasure, a sensory feast he alone knew how to savor. If he must be her slave, he would not lose his freedom for nothing. 

He locked the desk, pocketed all the property, and walked out into the echoing, ancestral halls.
