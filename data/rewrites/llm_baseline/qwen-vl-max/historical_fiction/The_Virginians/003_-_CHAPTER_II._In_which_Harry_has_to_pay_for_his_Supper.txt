Colonel Esmond’s grandson rang the bell of Castlewood, the ancestral home, but for a long while no one seemed to heed his call. When at last a servant emerged, he showed little interest in the fact that the visitor was a relation. The family was away, and John, the servant, cared little for distant kin—his only concern was returning to his card game with Thomas in the window seat. The housekeeper was busy preparing for the arrival of my lord and my lady, expected that evening. Only after persistent pleading did Harry gain permission to view my lady’s sitting room and the gallery of portraits. There, sure enough, hung a likeness of his grandfather—dressed in periwig and breastplate, identical to the portrait in Virginia—and a painting of his grandmother as Lady Castlewood, clothed in the fashion of Charles II’s reign: bare-necked, her golden hair falling in ringlets over her shoulders, the same curls Harry remembered once silvered by age. But before he could linger long, the housekeeper curtly dismissed him. Her own family was arriving soon—the Countess, my lord and his brother, the young ladies, and the Baroness Bernstein, who would occupy the state bedroom.

Who was the Baroness? She was the aunt of the young ladies, and Harry jotted down her name on a slip of paper from his pocketbook and placed it on a table in the hall: “Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, Virginia, arrived yesterday—staying at the Three Castles.” The lackeys rose hastily from their cards to open the door, eager for their “wails” (tips), and Gumbo left his bench at the gate, where he’d been chatting with old Lockwood, the porter, who took Harry’s guinea without understanding its significance.

During his brief visit to the ancestral seat, Harry had seen only little Polly’s face as showing any warmth or kindness. He walked away, hiding his disappointment, wounded by the coldness of the place. They ought to have known him. Had any of them ridden to his house in Virginia—whether the master were present or not—they would have been welcomed with open arms. Yet here, beneath the shadow of his ancestors’ hall, he found himself begging for a simple meal of bacon and eggs at a country alehouse.

After dinner, he went to the bridge and sat upon it, gazing toward the old house, where the sun was sinking behind the trees and rooks flew home to their nests in the elms. His imagination conjured visions of the past—knights crossing the ford, cavaliers from King Charles’s time, my Lord Castlewood, his grandmother’s first husband, riding forth with hawk and hound. Memories of his beloved brother stirred within him, bringing a wave of tenderness so sharp it made him lower his head, sorrow renewed for the companion with whom he had shared every joy and grief until recently.

Lost in thought, punctuated by the clink of the blacksmith’s forge nearby, the cawing of rooks, and birds calling through the twilight, two young men on horseback thundered across the bridge. One cursed loudly, calling him a fool and ordering him out of the way. The other, perhaps fearing he’d knocked the foot traveler off balance, spurred ahead, shouting to his companion, “Come on, Tom!” Before Harry could recover from the shock or anger at their rudeness, they were already climbing the hill toward Castlewood.

Moments later, two liveried servants on horseback followed, scowling at the stranger on the bridge—a true British welcome: “Curse you, who are you?” Then came a coach-and-six, a heavy vehicle drawn by six horses, carrying three ladies, two maids, and an armed man seated behind. Three pale, handsome faces peered out at Harry as the carriage passed. He recognized the family crest and offered a salute, but none returned it. The gentleman behind glared at him haughtily. Harry felt utterly alone. He longed to return to Captain Franks. The Rachel, with her small, restless cabin, seemed suddenly more inviting than this cold, indifferent world.

The landlord of the Three Castles had shown Harry a chamber, but he refused to unpack his trunk, certain the family would invite him to stay. Hour after hour passed—no invitation came. Finally, he allowed his luggage to be opened and called for slippers and gown. Just before dark, a second carriage with four horses crossed the bridge, and a stout, high-cheeked woman with dark eyes stared sharply at Mr. Warrington. That was the Baroness Bernstein, the landlord said—my lord’s aunt. Harry recalled that the first Lady Castlewood had been of German descent.

Earl, countess, baroness, postillions, gentlemen, and horses all vanished into the castle gates. Harry, defeated and lonely, finally retired to bed, his heart heavy with neglect. He could not sleep, and soon the sounds of revelry erupted from the inn’s bar—shouts, curses, giggles, screams—enough to keep any man awake.

Then came Gumbo’s voice outside: “You cannot go in, sar—my master asleep, sar!” But a shrill, oath-filled voice—Harry recognized it immediately—screamed back, calling Gumbo a “stupid negro woolly-pate,” and shoved him aside. A flood of oaths poured into the room, followed by a young man.

“Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington,” the young man cried, “are you asleep? I beg your pardon for nearly riding you into the river. Didn’t know you—course shouldn’t have done it—thought it was a lawyer with a writ—dressed in black, you know. Gad! Thought it was Nathan come to nab me.” Mr. William laughed wildly. Clearly, he was drunk.

“You did me great honor to mistake me for a sheriff’s officer, cousin,” Harry replied gravely, sitting up in his nightcap.

“Gad! I thought it was Nathan, and was going to send you souse into the river. But I ask your pardon. You see, I’d been drinking at the Bell at Hexton, and the punch is good there. Hullo! You, Davis! A bowl of punch; d’you hear?”

“I’ve had my share for tonight, cousin, and I should think you have,” Harry continued, maintaining his dignity.

“You want me to go, Cousin What’s-your-name, I see,” Mr. William said, sobering slightly. “You want me to go, and they want me to come, and I didn’t want to come. I said, I’d see him hanged first—that’s what I said. Why should I trouble myself to come down all alone of an evening, and look after a fellow I don’t care a pin for? Zackly what I said. Zackly what Castlewood said. Why the devil should he go down? Castlewood says, and so said my lady, but the Baroness would have you. It’s all the Baroness’s doing, and if she says a thing, it must be done; so you must just get up and come.”

Mr. Esmond spoke with rapid, indistinct energy, pacing the room. But Harry was furious. “I tell you what, cousin,” he exclaimed, “I won’t move for the Countess, or for the Baroness, or for all the cousins in Castlewood.”

When the landlord entered with the punch, the young man in bed bellowed at him to turn the “sot” out.

“Sot, you little tobacconist! Sot, you Cherokee!” shrieked Mr. William. “Jump out of bed, and I’ll drive my sword through your body. Why didn’t I do it today when I took you for a bailiff—a confounded pettifogging bum-bailiff!” He ranted on in drunken frenzy until the landlord, drawer, hostler, and kitchen staff arrived to drag him away. Afterward, Harry pulled the covers tightly around him, seething with anger, and likely fell into a deep, bitter sleep.

The next morning, the landlord was far more attentive, now knowing Harry’s name and status. Messengers had come from Castlewood during the night to fetch both young gentlemen home. Poor Mr. William, it turned out, had returned in a wheelbarrow—something he was not unfamiliar with. “He never remembers nothing about it the next day,” the landlord assured Harry. “He’s of a real kind nature, Mr. William. The men get crowns and half-crowns from him by saying he beat them overnight when he was in liquor. He’s the devil when he’s tipsy, Mr. William, but when he’s sober, he’s the very kindest of young gentlemen.”

As nothing is unknown to biographers of such tales, it may be well to reveal what transpired inside Castlewood House while Harry waited outside, hoping for some sign of recognition.

Upon arrival, the family discovered the note Harry had left. His appearance sparked a brief domestic debate. My Lord Castlewood believed it must have been the young man seen on the bridge. Since they hadn’t drowned him, they ought to invite him. A servant should carry a message. Lady Fanny suggested it would be more courteous for one of the brothers to go. Lord Castlewood had no objection—William should go.

But Mr. William declared (with even greater force) that he’d rather be hanged than go. Lady Maria remarked the young man at the bridge was quite attractive. “Castlewood is dreadfully dull,” she said. “Neither of my brothers does anything to make it amusing. He may be vulgar—no doubt he is—but let us see the American.”

Lady Castlewood remained neutral, favoring delay. “Wait till your aunt arrives,” she advised. “Perhaps the Baroness won’t like to see the young man. At least, let us consult her before we invite him.”

So the hospitality due to poor Harry Warrington remained suspended.

At length, the Baroness Bernstein’s carriage appeared. Though uncertain about the Virginian’s reception, the family greeted her with fervent enthusiasm. The state chamber had been readied. The cook had arrived the day before with instructions to prepare a supper exactly to her taste. The dining room sparkled with silver, set amid family portraits: the late Viscount, his parents, his sister—two exquisite paintings. There was Vandyck’s portrait of the former Viscount and his wife. And Colonel Esmond, the Virginia relative, whose grandson drew only mild interest from the household.

The supper was excellent, and the Baroness enjoyed it thoroughly. For an hour or two, the entire family hovered around her, attentive and respectful. The Countess pressed the best dishes upon her, partaking freely herself. The butler refilled her champagne glass the moment it was empty. The younger members listened more than they spoke, awaiting her amusement. She was lively, witty, and seemingly acquainted with everyone in Europe—and knew scandalous stories about each. Even the usually severe Countess smiled at the most outrageous tales. The girls exchanged glances and laughed at their mother’s cue. The boys giggled and roared, delighted by their sisters’ embarrassment. Wine flowed freely, and later, a smoking bowl of punch was brought to the table.

The Baroness reminisced about many nights spent at this very table beside her father. “That was his place,” she pointed to where the Countess now sat. “I saw none of the old plate. It was all melted to pay his gambling debts.” She raised her voice. “Young gentlemen, I hope you don’t play.”

“Never, on my word,” said Castlewood.

“Never, ’pon honour,” said Will, winking at his brother.

The Baroness was pleased. “Good boys,” she declared. Her cheeks flushed with punch, she grew animated, perhaps coarse—but times were different, and her audience inclined to leniency.

She spoke to the boys about their father, grandfather, and others in the family. “The only man of the family was that one,” she said, pointing with a still-graceful arm toward the portrait of the military man in red coat and cuirass. “The Virginian? What is he good for? I always thought he was good for nothing but cultivating tobacco and my grandmother,” my lord chuckled.

She struck the table so hard the glasses danced. “I say he was the best of you all. There never was one of the male Esmonds with more brains than a goose—except him. He wasn’t fit for this wicked, selfish world, and he was right to leave it. Where would your father have been, young people, but for him?”

“Was he particularly kind to our papa?” asked Lady Maria.

“Old stories, my dear Maria!” the Countess snapped. “I’m sure my dear Earl was very kind to him in giving him that great estate in Virginia.”

“Since his brother’s death, the lad who was here today is heir to that property,” said the Baroness. “Mr. Draper told me so! Peste! I don’t know why my father gave up such a property.”

“Who has been here today?” the Baroness asked, alarmed.

“Harry Esmond Warrington, of Virginia,” my lord replied. “A lad whom Will nearly pitched into the river, and whom I pressed my lady to invite.”

“You mean that one of the Virginian boys has been to Castlewood and hasn’t been asked to stay?”

“There is but one of them, my dear creature,” the Earl interjected. “The other—you know—has just been…”

“For shame, for shame!”

“Oh! It ain’t pleasant, I confess, to be se——”

“Do you mean that a grandson of Henry Esmond, the master of this house, has been here, and none of you have offered him hospitality?”

“Since we didn’t know it, and he is staying at the Inn?” Will added.

“That he is staying at the Inn, and you are sitting there!” the Baroness cried. “This is too bad—call somebody to me. Get me my hood—I’ll go to the boy myself. Come with me this instant, my Lord Castlewood.”

The young man rose, clearly angered. “Madame the Baroness of Bernstein,” he said, “your ladyship is welcome to go. But as for me, I don’t choose to have such words as ‘shameful’ applied to my conduct. I won’t go fetch the young gentleman from Virginia, and I propose to sit here and finish this bowl of punch. Eugene! Don’t Eugene me, madam. I know her ladyship has a great deal of money, which you are desirous should remain in our amiable family. You want it more than I do. Cringe for it—I won’t.” He sank back into his chair.

The Baroness looked at the family, heads lowered, then at my lord, but without malice. She leaned close and whispered rapidly in German: “I was wrong when I said the Colonel was the only man of the family. Thou canst, if thou willest, Eugene.”

To which my lord merely bowed.

“If you do not wish an old woman to go out at this hour, let William, at least, go and fetch his cousin,” she said.

“The very thing I proposed to him.”

“And so did we—and so did we!” the daughters chimed in.

“I am sure I only wanted the dear Baroness’s consent!” their mother added. “And shall be charmed to welcome our young relative.”

“Will! Put on thy pattens and get a lantern, and go fetch the Virginian,” my lord ordered.

“And we will have another bowl of punch when he comes,” William said, already overindulged. And he went forth—how we have seen; how he drank more punch; how ill he succeeded.

The noble lady of Castlewood, catching sight of young Harry by the riverside, must have seen a handsome, engaging youth—perhaps with reasons of her own for wanting him absent from her household. Not all mothers welcome interesting young men of nineteen when virgins of twenty are present. If Harry’s lands had lain in Norfolk or Devon instead of Virginia, the Countess might have been more eager. Had she desired him, she would have given him her hand willingly. Our fashionable folk may be selfish, but at least they show it plainly. Cold-hearted, yes—but not hypocritical.

Why should Lady Castlewood go to any trouble to welcome the young stranger? Because he was friendless? Only a fool would believe that. People of fashion are friendly to those with many friends. A poor lad, alone, from a distant land, with modest means not yet his own, uncouth manners, provincial ways—should a great lady go out of her way for such a youth? Allons donc! He was better off at the alehouse than at the castle.

This, no doubt, was her opinion—and the Baroness Bernstein, who knew her perfectly, understood it well. The Baroness was also a woman of the world, capable of selfishness like any other. She understood precisely why the Castlewood family deferred to her—mother, daughters, sons alike—and amused herself with their greed, their humiliations, their artless reverence for her purse. They were not rich; Lady Castlewood’s fortune was settled on her children. The elder siblings had inherited only flaxen hair from their German mother and a pedigree of immense distinction. But rich or poor, all craved the Baroness’s wealth. In this, the wealthy are no less greedy than the poor.

So when Madam Bernstein struck the table, making glasses and people tremble, it was not from genuine fury, but from the warmth of punch and champagne, and a generous impulse stirred by wine and pity for the lonely youth waiting outside his ancestors’ door. She was not truly angry with her relatives—she knew they would act exactly as they had.

Their selfishness and humility alike amused her, as did Castlewood’s defiance. He was selfish like the rest, but not as petty. And as he frankly admitted, he could afford a little independence, having a decent estate to fall back upon.

Madam Bernstein was an energetic woman, restless, resolute, extraordinarily active for her age. She rose long before the languid Castlewood ladies (just returned from London’s balls) had left their feather beds, or jolly Will had slept off his punch. She paced the dew-dappled terraces, where flowers bloomed in neat parterres, box hedges stood crisp and dark, marble fauns and dryads cooled beneath them, birds sang, fountains splashed, and rooks called from the great wood.

Did the familiar scene stir memories? Did it recall childhood days of innocence, or awaken remorse? Her manner was unusually gentle when, after half an hour, the young Virginian appeared—sent by a morning billet from one of the Lockwoods. The note, signed B. Bernstein, informed Mr. Esmond Warrington that his relatives at Castlewood, including a dear friend of his grandfather, were anxious for him to come to “Colonel Esmond’s house in England.”

Now, accordingly, Harry approached, passing under the Gothic archway, stepping down from terrace to terrace, hat in hand, fair hair fluttering, slim figure clad in mourning. The Baroness found him handsome and modest. He bowed low—a gesture worthy of Versailles. She held out her hand, and when his closed over it, she laid her other gently on his ruffle. She gazed warmly into his honest, blushing face.

“I knew your grandfather very well, Harry,” she said. “So you came yesterday to see his portrait, and they turned you away—though you know the house was his by right?”

Harry blushed deeply. “The servants did not know me. A young gentleman came to me last night,” he said, “when I was peevish, and he, I fear, was tipsy. I spoke rudely to my cousin and would ask his pardon. Your ladyship knows that in Virginia, our manners toward strangers are different. I had expected another kind of welcome. Was it you, madam, who sent my cousin to me last night?”

“I sent him. But you will find your cousins most friendly today. You must stay here. Lord Castlewood would have come to you this morning, but I was so eager to see you. Breakfast in an hour. Meantime, talk to me. We will send for your servant and baggage. Give me your arm. Stop—I dropped my cane when you came. You shall be my cane.”

“My grandfather used to call us his crutches,” said Harry.

“You are like him, though you are fair.”
