The following day, a Sunday consecrated to commerce rather than contemplation, transformed Catherine's dwelling in the Hoog Straet into a hive of industrious anticipation. The shop stood unveiled and fully prepared, a testament to the relentless energy of Cornelis and Sybrandt, whose names were already etched above the threshold alongside their heraldic emblem: a white lamb suckling a gilded sheep. Eli had arrived bearing fresh provisions from his store, eager to fortify their enterprise before the official opening. Within the domestic sphere, the atmosphere shimmered with a feverish optimism; the parents' hearts burned with the heat of reinvention. They strolled through the garden, their steps synchronized by a shared resolve to abandon the drudgery of their agrarian past. "We are weary of the soil," Sybrandt declared, his voice ringing with the clarity of conversion. "It is far sweeter to mint coin than to squander it upon wild oats. Let us bind ourselves to business as tightly as wax to the seal."

Catherine's mother, her ears perpetually attuned to the currents of household fortune, intercepted this vow through an open lattice. She relayed the news to Eli: the evening supper would serve as an inaugural banquet, welcoming Margaret and her son. It was to be a rite of passage where seasoned merchants would dispense wisdom upon the converts fleeing the unremunerative fields of agriculture, and where vintage wine would toast the triumph of commerce over the earth. Margaret descended to assist her mother-in-law, driven by a dual necessity to aid the household and to shed the heavy languor that had settled upon her spirit. Both women emerged flushed, their complexions glowing with the vibrant red of embers, signaling a vitality long dormant.

Suddenly, the front door swung open, admitting Joan carrying a crisp salad harvested from Jorian's garden. "He cut it specifically for you, Margaret," Joan announced, her tone laced with playful rivalry. "You are his sole conversation; I fear I shall become jealous. I reminded him of today's feast, but alas, what a thunderous sermon echoed from the new church! Preaching? I have never witnessed such a spectacle until this hour."

"I wish I had been present," Margaret murmured, her voice tinged with a spiritual thirst. "I am parched, deprived of the dew from heaven."

"Why, he preaches again this afternoon," Joan countered, her eyes darting toward the street. "Yet, perhaps your duties demand you remain here."

"Not she," Catherine interjected firmly. "Go, if your heart inclines you."

"Indeed," Margaret hesitated, "but methinks I should not dampen the spirits at the table if I could attend, warmed by the fervor of a divine discourse."

"Then you must move with alacrity," Joan advised, scanning the horizon. "Observe the congregation gathering; look, even the holy friar approaches. Oh, blessed be our salvation, Margaret—the hermit! We have forgotten him entirely." With a burst of kinetic energy, the active woman launched herself from the doorway, crossed the cobblestones, and halted the friar mid-stride. She returned moments later, breathless. "Behold, I was determined to view him in person."

"What did he impart to thee?"

"He spoke thus: 'My daughter, I shall visit him ere sunset, God willing,'" Joan recounted, her voice softening. "His voice was the sweetest melody. Yet, oh, my mistresses, observe his gaunt cheeks, those hollow features of a young man, and his great eyes, remarkably akin to yours, Margaret."

"I possess a strong desire to hear him," Margaret confessed. "However, my cap lacks cleanliness, and the assembly will surely wear their pristine white mutches."

"There," Catherine instructed, reaching into a woven basket, "take my handkerchief. I cannot spare the child; I require him for my poor Kate, who suffers from one of her grievous days."

Margaret ascended the stairs, leaving the boy behind. She found Kate confined to bed, her body wracked with agony. "How fares thou, sweetheart?" Margaret asked gently. "No inquiry is necessary; thou art in profound pain. Yet, thou smilest. Behold, I have brought thee one thou lovest."

"Two, by my reckoning," Kate replied, her countenance radiant with an angelic serenity despite the torment. Suddenly, a violent spasm seized her, a convulsion that might have caused onlookers to roar like bulls in their distress.

"In your lap?" Margaret queried, responding to the suffering girl's gesture.

"Nay, he is too heavy, and thou art in such anguish," Kate protested weakly.

"I love him too dearly to feel his weight," was the tender rebuttal. Seizing this brief respite, Margaret attended to her own preparations.

"I am bound for the church," she announced, "to listen to a magnificent preacher."

Kate sighed, a sound of resigned longing. "A moment ago, I was eager to depart; such is my condition for the past month. Up and down, up and down, like the restless waves of the Zuyder Zee. I would sooner remain beside thee; simply say the word!"

"Nay," Kate pleaded, her eyes filling with tears. "Pray, go. Bring me back every syllable. Alas, that I cannot journey myself."

This plea resolved Margaret's hesitation. She kissed Kate, glanced beneath her eyelashes at the sleeping infant, and let out a small, sorrowful sigh. "I deem I must not," she whispered. "I could never bear to kiss him lightly; nor could my father endure waking a child by day or night. Speak to Aunt Kate the two new words thou hast acquired when it is thy pleasure to rise." With a final, loving glance over her shoulder, she departed, shutting the door with an inaudible click.

Downstairs, Catherine turned to Joan. "Will you lend me a hand to peel these vegetables?"

"That I will, dame," Joan responded promptly.

Cooking resumed with a silent, vigorous intensity. "Listen well, Joan," Catherine commanded, chopping rhythmically. "Those who assist me in preparing and serving the meat shall share in eating it; that is the law of our house."

"There exist harsher statutes in Holland than that," Joan retorted cheerfully. "Your will is my delight, mistress. My Luke awaits his supper in the air; fortunately, he is digging today."

Margaret descended the stairs. "Eh, woman, that is a grim trade indeed."

She had merely washed her face and arranged her hair, and suddenly, she appeared transformed. "Rotterdam, that for you!" Catherine exclaimed, snapping her fingers in admiration of the capital city's influence on beauty. "Give us a buss, hussy! Mind now, Eli will not wait for the Duke to join us. Loiter not after your church service concludes."

Joan and Catherine accompanied Margaret to the threshold, standing sentinel as she ventured down the street. Among the mundane routines of housewives, the act of departing home holds half the drama of any event. Catherine paused to comment on the procession. "There, Joan, it feels as though I had just dispatched my own daughter to church, standing guard to ensure her safety—a vigilance I have performed countless times. Joan, lass, she will not tolerate a single word spoken against our Gerard. If he remains alive, he has treated her cruelly; hence, my bowels ache for the poor wretch. Being older and wiser, I intend to wed her to that simple Luke, and there shall be an end to her suffering. What is one grandchild more?"
