Paul Oleron found himself striding over the splintered plank he had hastily laid across the fractured step, descending the stairs into the gloom. Hatless and unsteady, he moved with a momentum that belied his confusion. It was only later, as the fog of the moment receded, that a hazy memory surfaced: he remembered leaving the candle burning on the table, having wedged the door open just wide enough for his bulk to pass, and slipping out into the corridor with a soft click of the latch. But the house held other shocks. At the foot of the stairs, a furry shape darted upward from the disused cellars, vanishing through the doorway with a flurry of claws. It was merely a cat, yet the sight triggered a childish sob from Oleron, a raw, unguarded sound that echoed in the empty hallway.

He pushed through the gate and paused beneath the flimsy "To Let" boards, nervously picking at his lip while staring up at the faint glow filtering through one of the red blinds. Still casting anxious glances over his shoulder, he stumbled up the square. Around the corner stood a small public house—a place he had never dared enter before—but tonight, driven by a compulsion he could not name, he crossed the threshold. On the counter, he dropped a shilling that skittered inches past the edge before settling. "Brandy," he stammered, his voice cracking, "brand-y." Immediately, he bent to retrieve the coin.

Alone in the immediate vicinity, save for the dust motes dancing in the lamplight, he sat upon a hard wooden settee with a perforated seat. He drained half the measure, then, fearing waste, finished the rest in one swallow. Now, amidst the murmur of voices drifting from the far compartment—where carters, laborers, and local tradesmen congregated—he began to formulate a plan. Which of these men would handle the removal of his effects tomorrow? In the interim, he ordered another glass. He refused to return to that room where the candle burned alone, to that pith-white, mesmerizing chamber where the broken step waited. No, he would retreat to his old arrangement: the workroom and the separate sleeping quarters. He would visit his old landlady shortly, once he had consumed his liquor, to ask for shelter for the night.

His glass emptied. He signaled for a refill and settled back. If anyone questioned his sudden departure, he had ample justification. Nails that retracted into the wood to gash fingers, stairs that shattered underfoot, and women who invaded a man's sanctuary to groom their hair in the darkness—these were sufficient causes for flight. He felt querulous, wounded. He had leased the premises for himself, not for invisible specters to coiffure their tresses in the dark. The solicitor from Lincoln's Inn ought to hear such grievances before sundown; it was outrageous to permit such intrusions under the guise of agreement.

A cut-glass partition separated his corner from the main bar, rising only seven or eight inches above the counter, offering no true concealment. Yet, as Oleron raised his eyes, he sensed faces watching him through the gap. When he turned to meet their gaze, they vanished. Seeking refuge, he shifted to a corner blind to the other side of the bar, only to find himself directly in the line of sight of the white-haired landlady. She recognized him, likely from countless passes, and eventually remarked on the weather. Oleron's response was lost in the haze, but her reply—that the winter had been cruel to influenza victims while spring seemed imminent—anchored him slightly. An idle, nascent curiosity arose: did she brush her hair nightly? Did it emit those tiny electric crackles? The thought snapped shut with a mental click, and Oleron felt steadier.

With the next glass, the urge to return to his flat surged. *Not go back?* Indeed, he would return! Soon they would learn if he was to be evicted like a common vagrant. He wondered why he behaved thus—hatless, intoxicated, in a tavern. What would the landlady think if he confessed the scratch of the nail, the collapse of the stair, the primal terror of hearing a whisper in an old house filled with squeaks and creaks? Surely madness. *Pshaw!* The truth was simpler: he had lacked occupation, drowning his days in dreams of a new *Romilly* as if the old one were insufficient. Now, startled that the devil might inhabit an empty head, he resolved to reclaim his sanity. He would walk the air, clear his mind, settle the fate of the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly*—a folly to destroy fifteen—and thereafter honor his obligations to mankind.

He finished the brandy and stepped out. The fresh air initially amplified the liquor's potency, but soon his mind cleared, surpassing its morning lucidity. As clarity returned, his boastful certainties wavered, replaced by a firm conviction: even after all explanations, some truths remained inexplicable. The hysteria of the hour faded, replaced by a growing calm, yet a disquieting certainty persisted. A deep fear seized him—not for himself, but for Elsie. Something in his dwelling was hostile to her safety. Her two accidents alone might not have convinced him, but her own words had: *"I'm not wanted here."* She had perceived the rot before he had. Therefore, she must be kept away for reasons far deeper than the humiliation Oleron had suffered. Fortunately, she intended to stay away; he must ensure she adhered to that vow. His initial impulse to flee forever was absurd. People did not abandon homes to shadows unless compelled. With Elsie secured, he could not, with any regard for his own dignity, allow himself to be displaced by a mere danger. He had to live there. He must return.

He conquered the faint chill of dread accompanying this decision and turned sharply in his stride. Should fear resurface, perhaps one more glass of brandy would suffice. But by the time he reached the short street leading to the square, the pub was closed, its lights dimmed, with a few men lingering on the curb. A sudden silence fell over them as Oleron passed. Lower down, the long-nosed Barrett ignored his greeting entirely.

Oleron turned into the broken gate, hesitated briefly in the alley, and ascended the stairs once more. Only an inch of wax remained in the Sheffield stick, and he refrained from lighting another. Deliberately, he forced himself to carry the stub up and inspect each of his five rooms before retiring. Returning from the kitchen across the small hall, he spotted a letter lying on the floor. He carried it to the sitting-room, examining the envelope before breaking the seal. Unstamped and inserted by hand, the handwriting was clumsy, running on without commas or periods. He read the first line, checked the signature, and finished. It was from Barrett, demanding that Oleron arrange alternative provisions for breakfasts and cleaning. The sting lay in the postscript, a biblical text containing an allusion that could only refer to Elsie Bengough.

A rare, deep frown carved lines into Oleron's brow. *So! That was it.* Very well; they would see about that tomorrow. For the rest, this was merely another reason for Elsie to remain absent. Then, his suppressed rage erupted. *The foul-minded lot!* The devil himself could not have sneered more bitterly at the intimacy between Paul Oleron and Elsie Bengough, yet this nosing rascal prided himself on prying and gossiping. Oleron crumpled the paper, held it to the candle flame until it curled and blackened, and ground the ashes under his heel.

One utility the letter provided: it ignited a wrathful blaze that effectively banished the pale shadows haunting his thoughts. Yet, one final puzzle marred the evening. As he undressed, he glanced at his bed. The coverlets bore an impression, as if someone had lain upon them. Oleron could not recall doing so during the day; offhand, he was certain he had not. But certainty eluded him. His indignation for Elsie, mingled with the residual warmth of the brandy, silenced all other considerations. He extinguished the candle, lay down, and drifted immediately into a deep, dreamless sleep that lasted nearly twenty-four hours, uninterrupted by Mrs. Barrett's morning summons.
