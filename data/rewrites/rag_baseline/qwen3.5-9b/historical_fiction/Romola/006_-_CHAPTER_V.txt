The Via de' Bardi cuts through the Oltrarno district, tracing the southern flank of Florence where the Arno bends. Stretching from the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de' Mozzi, the street is hemmed in by the precipitous rise of the hill of Bogoli—a former quarry that provided the city's paving stones, now rendered treacherous by rain—and the riverbank itself. On the north, the façades are irregular and ancient, their reflections softened in the water as the sun dips toward the west. Yet, to the historian's eye, these structures feel like a clumsy, modern replacement for the great Bardi palaces, obliterated by popular fury in the mid-fourteenth century.

Those Bardi were a formidable lineage, masters of the sword during Florence's earliest internecine wars when noble towers darkened the narrow thoroughfares and the gutters ran red. While the city worshipped Jupiter, the Bardi understood the utility of Mars. But their strength lay not merely in violence; they possessed the Florentine acumen to recognize that power is secured as effectively through minted coin as through steel. By the mid-century, they had transcended their status as *popolani*, acquiring vast estates and the feudal title of Counts of Vernio, a move that inflamed the republicans. Their ambition extended to European commerce; they supplied specie for King Edward III's campaigns and controlled immense revenues in wool, becoming the financial backbone of the era. However, their hubris invited catastrophe. A sudden demand from Sicilian creditors regarding unpaid deposits triggered a collapse that sent shockwaves across the Mediterranean. Like modern bankruptcies, they refused to bow in shame. Instead, they doubled down on arrogance, positioning themselves defiantly between the two bridges until the people's gonfalons forced them back. Twenty-two of their homes were burned, and many leaders fled, yet the family proved resilient, resurfacing repeatedly in Florentine affairs, though never reclaiming their ancestral riverfront properties, which passed to the Neri faction.

Within one of these Neri houses, however, dwelt a descendant of the Bardi, inheritor of the branch that had once ruled as Counts of Vernio. He retained the family's pride and drive, yet the arena had shifted. This man was no longer a warrior or a merchant prince; he was a blind scholar, Bardo de' Bardi. His hands, once fit for command, were now cramped from decades of copying manuscripts. Dressed in threadbare garments born of necessity rather than choice, he spent his days amidst books and marble fragments, illuminated only by the fading memories of a brighter age. He was a destitute old man, the very subject of the promise Nello the barber had made to introduce the Greek youth, Tito Melema.

His residence occupied the hillside side of the street, a somber mass of stone punctuated by small, barred windows and crowned by a loggia. Heavy doors with scrolled hinges opened onto a vaulted courtyard dominated by a single, massive iron lamp. A secondary door led to a stone staircase and the ground floor, which served as a warehouse. Both levels were packed with precious goods destined for the Scheldt, African shores, or the Aegean islands. Maso, the elderly servant, navigated this labyrinth daily, carrying provisions up to the second floor where his master awaited.

Maso pushed open the door to the antechamber and stepped into a long, cavernous room lined with shelves holding books and antiquities arranged with military precision. Statues stood sentinel: a perfect female torso, a headless warrior brandishing a bladeless sword, severed limbs of infants preserved in cold marble, and several Roman busts. Two vases from Magna Grecia completed the collection. A central table displayed bronze lamps and dark pottery vessels. The palette was monochromatic—pale vellum bindings, the livid grey of marble, the tarnished green of bronze—all bathed in insufficient light filtering through narrow windows facing the Via de' Bardi.

The sole injection of color belonged to a young woman of seventeen or eighteen standing before a carved reading desk (*leggio*). Her hair, a reddish-gold streaked with the warmth of sunset clouds, fell in an unbroken ripple, pinned back by a black fillet that framed her square-cut gown of black serge. She was absorbed in a large volume, one hand resting on the desk, the other gripping the back of her father's chair.

Her blind father sat beside her, head tilted slightly upward as if visually tracking her presence. His pallor, accentuated by a black velvet cap atop his white hair, mirrored hers; neither cheek held a trace of rose. They shared identical refined brows and noses, balanced by full, firm mouths and powerful chins that spoke of stubborn tenacity. The girl's posture echoed this defiance, her head poised high, her neck and shoulders projecting a grand, commanding line. One might hesitate to classify such a visage as purely lovely; it inspired a mixture of awe and apprehension, a quality ultimately defined by the eyes. The father's were silent, permanently veiled, while the daughter's remained fixed on the Latin text of Politian's *Miscellanea*.

"There was a certain nymph of Thebes named Chariclo," she read aloud, her voice steady at the eighty-first chapter, "especially dear to Pallas... Teiresias, coming as a hunter to quench his thirst, inadvertently beheld Minerva unveiled, and immediately became blind. For it is declared in the Saturnian laws that he who beholds the gods against their will shall atone by a heavy penalty..."

She paused briefly, recounting how Pallas, moved by Chariclo's tears, endowed the blinded prophet with prophecy and longevity, granting him a staff to navigate the world. "Hence, Nonnus," she continued, shifting to the Greek quotation, "introduces Actreon exclaiming that he calls Teiresias happy, since, without dying, and with the loss of his eyesight merely, he had beheld Minerva unveiled, and thus, though blind, could for evermore carry her image in his soul."

At this juncture, her hand slipped from the chair to meet her father's, which he had instinctively lifted. She did not look away, continuing into the Greek verse, though a subtle tension tightened her throat.

"Stop, Romola," Bardo commanded softly. "Bring me my own copy of Nonnus. It is superior to any in Poliziano's possession; I made emendations in it which remain unpublished. I completed it in 1477, when my sight began to fail."

Romola rose with a regal stride, characteristic of her tall, finely constructed frame, requiring no conscious adjustment of her bearing. "Is it in the proper location?" Bardo asked, seeking confirmation that reality aligned with his mental map.

"Yes, Father," she replied. "West end of the room, third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, and below Lucan and Silius Italicus."

A trained ear would have caught a faint tremor of exhaustion beneath her habitual patience in her clear voice. As she approached, witnessing his arms extend with nervous urgency to grasp the volume, her hazel eyes flooded with pity. She knelt beside him, placing the book in his lap and gazing up, convinced that the love radiating from her face could penetrate the darkness sealing his world. In that moment, the complex interplay of pride and passion in her features transmuted into pure, lovable tenderness. The depths of her emotion had not yet reshaped her static countenance, finding release solely through her gaze.

Unaware of this transformation, Bardo appeared flushed and agitated as his fingers traced the book's spine. "The vellum has yellowed in these thirteen years, Romola."

"Yes, Father," she said gently. "But your marginalia remain dark and legible—fine Roman script. And the Greek text," she added, opening the book on his knees, "is more beautiful than any purchased manuscript."

"Indeed, child," Bardo murmured, running his finger across the page as if trying to distinguish lines through touch alone. "What hired scribe can match the writer who loves the words growing under his hand, for whom an error is more painful than sudden blindness? Even these mechanical printers, threatening to degrade learning into vulgarity, must rely on the manuscript. Only through it can scholars access the poet's meaning, that insight akin to the *mens divinior* of the author himself. Without it, they would flood the world with grammatical falsities, turning the fountain of Parnassus into a deluge of poisonous mud. Locate the passage in Book Five referenced by Poliziano—I know it intimately."

Seating herself on a low stool beside his knee, Romola took the book onto her lap and prepared to recite the four verses containing Actreon's exclamation.
