Mrs. John Dashwood had fully settled into her role as mistress of Norland Park, her silk gown swishing past hallways that for years had belonged to her husband’s stepmother and half-sisters, who were now no more than tolerated guests in the home they had once loved as their own. Fanny treated them, for the most part, with a cool, unruffled civility that never strayed into warmth; John, for his part, showed them as much kindness as he was capable of feeling for anyone who was not himself, his adored wife, or their chubby, golden-haired little son Harry. He even urged them, with a show of earnest concern, to treat Norland as their own home for as long as they pleased — and since Mrs. Dashwood could not bear the thought of rushing away from the place where every tree, every window seat, every rose bush in the garden held memories of her late husband and years of unshadowed joy, she accepted the invitation.

It was exactly the kind of soft, lingering space her heart craved. In times of happiness, no temper could be brighter than hers, no one could hold that bright, sanguine expectation of coming joy that feels like happiness all on its own. But in sorrow, she was equally swept away by the power of her own affections, as far past all consolation now as she had once been past every possible hint of bitterness when her days were good.

Fanny Dashwood did not remotely approve of the promise her husband had made his father on his deathbed: to gift his three half-sisters three thousand pounds from the family fortune. To take that vast sum away from their dear little Harry would be to impoverish him beyond all repair, she insisted, curling herself close to John by their private parlour fire, little Harry playing with a wooden soldier at her feet. She begged him to reconsider. How could he live with himself to rob his own only child of so much wealth? What possible claim could the Miss Dashwoods — related to him only by half blood, which Fanny dismissed as barely counting as relationship at all — have on such extravagant generosity? Everyone knew children of different marriages never felt any real affection for one another, why on earth would he ruin himself, and their precious little boy, by throwing away their money on girls who were barely more than strangers?

“It was my father’s last request to me,” John protested. “That I look after his widow and daughters, make their situation easier.”
“He had no idea what he was saying, I’d wager,” Fanny sniffed. “Nine times out of ten he was light-headed from fever at the time. If he’d been in his right mind, he never would have dreamed of begging you to give away half of Harry’s inheritance.”
“He never named a specific sum, my dear Fanny,” he said. “He only told me, in general terms, to assist them, to make their lives more comfortable than he could. I daresay it would have been wiser if he’d left the whole matter to my own discretion. He could never have thought I would neglect them. But he asked me for the promise, and I could not refuse it — not then, at least. The promise is made, and I must keep it. I must do something for them when they leave Norland and move to a new home nearby.”

“Very well, do something,” Fanny said smoothly. “But that something does not need to be three thousand pounds. Think: once that money is gone, it never comes back. Your sisters will marry, and it will vanish into the hands of strangers forever. If only it could be returned to our poor little Harry, one day —”
“Why, to be sure,” John said, his face growing grave. “That would make all the difference. The day may come when Harry regrets that so large a sum was given away. If he has a large family of his own one day, that money would be a great comfort to him.”
“Exactly so!”
“Then perhaps it would be better for everyone if the sum was cut in half. Five hundred pounds apiece would be a wonderful addition to their fortunes!”
“Oh, that is beyond generous!” Fanny cried, patting his arm. “What brother on earth would do half as much even for full blood sisters — and these are only half related! You have such a noble, generous spirit, my love.”
“I would never want to do anything unkind or mean,” he said. “One would rather do too much than too little on such occasions. No one can say I did not do right by them. Even they can hardly expect more.”
“There is no telling what they might expect,” Fanny said, “but we need not care about their expectations. The question is what you can afford to give.”
“Quite right. I think I can easily afford five hundred pounds a piece. Even without a penny from me, each of them will come into three thousand pounds when their mother passes. That is a perfectly respectable, comfortable fortune for any young woman.”
“Of course it is! All told, the four of them will have ten thousand pounds between them. If they marry, they will do perfectly well for themselves. If they never marry, they can live very comfortably together on the interest of that sum. They want for nothing more.”
“True enough,” John said, nodding slowly. “Perhaps it would be wiser, then, to do something for their mother while she lives, rather than give the girls lump sums. An annuity, I mean — a hundred pounds a year. That would make all four of their lives far easier.”

Fanny hesitated a moment before she answered. “That is better than giving away fifteen hundred pounds all at once, to be sure. But what if Mrs. Dashwood lives another fifteen years? We would be trapped paying it all that time, completely swindled!”
“Fifteen years, my dear Fanny? She is hardly forty, but her health cannot be worth half that purchase.”
“Have you not noticed? People always live forever when there is an annuity to be paid them,” Fanny insisted. “An annuity is a terrible burden: it comes round every single year, over and over, and there is no getting rid of it. You do not know what you are agreeing to. I know all the trouble they cause — my father left my mother paying three annuities to superannuated old servants, and it was dreadful. Twice a year she had to trek across the county to deliver the money, there was endless hassle when one of them was rumoured dead only to turn up very much alive a month later. My mother was sick of the whole business. She said her fortune was never her own, not with those constant, unescapable demands on it. It is so unfair — if it were not for those annuities, all that money would be hers to spend as she pleased. It has given me such a horror of annuities, I would never tie myself down to paying one, not for anything in the world.”
“It is very unpleasant, to be sure,” John agreed. “To have those constant drains on your income, to be forced to set aside the same sum every rent day, it robs you of your independence entirely, just as your mother says.”
“Exactly! And you get no thanks for it, either. They think it is their right, they never feel grateful. If I were you, every penny I gave them would be at my own full discretion. I would never bind myself to pay them a yearly sum. Some years we may be hard pressed to spare a hundred, or even fifty, pounds from our own household expenses.”

“I believe you are right, my love,” John said, convinced. “An annuity is not the way to go. Occasional gifts of money will help them far more than a fixed allowance — if they knew a hundred pounds a year was coming in, they would just start spending more, living above their means, and end up no richer at the end of the twelve months. A fifty pound gift now and then will keep them from ever being short of money, and that will be more than enough to fulfill my promise to my father.”
“Of course it will!” Fanny said, smiling. “I am utterly convinced your father never meant for you to give them any actual money at all. The assistance he had in mind was only the kind of help that is naturally expected of you: help them hunt out a nice small cottage nearby, lend a hand moving their furniture over, send over extra hare or game from the estate larder when it is in season, a basket of apples from our orchard, that sort of thing. I will wager my life he never meant anything more than that — it would be absurd and unreasonable if he did. Think, my dear John! Between the interest of their seven thousand pounds, plus the thousand pounds each girl already has of her own, their income comes out to fifty pounds a year per girl, and of course they will pay their mother for their board out of that. All told, they will have five hundred pounds a year between four of them. On earth, what could four quiet gentlewomen possibly need more than that? They will live so cheaply! No carriage, no horses, barely two servants, no parties or company to entertain. Their housekeeping costs will be next to nothing. You cannot imagine how comfortably they will live! Five hundred a year? I cannot see how they will ever spend half of it. The idea of giving them more is completely ridiculous. They will be far better off than we are, in many ways — they will probably end up being able to give us gifts, not the other way round.”

“Upon my word,” John said, fully persuaded now, “I believe you are perfectly right. My father could never have meant anything more than that by his request. I see that now. I will keep my promise perfectly, by offering them that small acts of kindness and support you described. When my mother moves to her new cottage, I will gladly help her settle in as much as I can. A few small pieces of leftover furniture, that will be all they could possibly need.”
“Of course,” Fanny agreed. “And there is one more thing to consider: when your father and mother first moved to Norland, they sold all the furniture from their old home at Stanhill, but they kept all their china, silver plate, and linen, and all of that is left to your mother in the will. Her new cottage will be almost fully furnished the day she steps through the door.”
“That is a very important point, a very fine legacy indeed! Though some of that silver plate would have made a lovely addition to our own, here at Norland.”
“Yes, and their breakfast china set is twice as fine as the one we use in this house. Far too grand, in my opinion, for any small cottage they could ever afford to rent. But there it is — your father thought of no one but them. And I will say this: you owe him no particular gratitude, no obligation to follow his wishes blindly. We all know if he could have had his way, he would have left every last penny he owned to them, and left our poor Harry with nothing at all.”

That argument was impossible to push back against. It gave John the last little bit of firm resolve he had been missing, and he finally decided that it would be utterly unnecessary, if not outright ungentlemanly and indecorous, to do anything more for his father’s widow and his half-sisters than the small, neighbourly little favours his wife had outlined.
