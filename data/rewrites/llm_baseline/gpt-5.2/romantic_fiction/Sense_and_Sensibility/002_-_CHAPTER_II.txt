Mrs. John Dashwood took Norland the way a new cook takes a kitchen: not with loud command, but with a quiet, complete possession—keys that clicked in locks, inventories taken in a low voice, cupboards opened and shut as if the house itself had been a larder newly assigned to her care. The older mistress of that ample place—Mrs. Dashwood—found herself, with her daughters, reduced to guests in rooms that still smelled of their former life: beeswax on the stair rail, lavender in the linen press, yesterday’s woodsmoke caught in the curtains. They were treated with an even politeness by Fanny, and with as much kindness by Mr. John Dashwood as he could spare for any creature outside the small circle of himself, his wife, and their little boy.

He did press them, with what he believed to be real earnestness, to consider Norland their home. And because no plan seemed so tolerable to Mrs. Dashwood as remaining where every path and hedge-row spoke a remembered happiness—until she could find a smaller house close by—she accepted his invitation. To stay was to keep her grief from breaking apart into the open air; to stay was to fold it into familiar routine. When she was cheerful, no one could be more bright, more quick to taste pleasure and expect more of it, as if happiness were a broth that, once set to simmer, must surely deepen. But in sorrow she was equally carried away—her imagination, like a pot left untended, boiling over beyond consolation.

Fanny did not at all approve of what her husband had once, in the first flush of duty, intended to do for his sisters. Three thousand pounds, taken from the fortune of their dear little boy, seemed to her not generosity but theft done with a tender name. She spoke of it as if he meant to ladle away Harry’s portion—spoon by spoon—until the child’s future sat thin and pale in the bowl.

She begged him to think again. How could he answer to himself, she asked, for robbing his only child of so large a sum? And what possible claim had the Miss Dashwoods—connected only by half blood, which she treated as scarcely blood at all—on his liberality to such an extent? It was, she said, very well known that affection was never supposed to exist between children of different marriages; why should he ruin himself, and their poor little Harry, by giving away his money to half sisters?

“It was my father’s last request,” her husband replied, “that I should assist his widow and daughters.”

“He did not know what he was talking of, I dare say,” returned Fanny, with the brisk certainty of a person who hears only what suits her. “Ten to one he was light-headed at the time. Had he been in his right senses, he could not have thought of begging you to give away half your fortune from your own child.”

“He did not stipulate for any particular sum,” said Mr. Dashwood. “He only requested me, in general terms, to assist them, to make their situation more comfortable than it was in his power to do. Perhaps it would have been as well if he had left it wholly to myself. He could hardly suppose I should neglect them. But as he required the promise, I could not do less than give it; at least I thought so at the time. The promise, therefore, was given, and must be performed. Something must be done for them whenever they leave Norland and settle in a new home.”

“Well, then, let something be done,” said Fanny. “But that something need not be three thousand pounds. Consider—when the money is once parted with, it never can return. Your sisters will marry, and it will be gone for ever. If, indeed, it could be restored to our poor little boy—”

“Why, to be sure,” he answered, very gravely, as if she had brought him a sensible account-book truth. “That would make great difference. The time may come when Harry will regret that so large a sum was parted with. If he should have a numerous family, for instance, it would be a very convenient addition.”

“To be sure it would.”

“Perhaps, then, it would be better for all parties if the sum were diminished one half. Five hundred pounds would be a prodigious increase to their fortunes.”

“Oh! beyond anything great!” cried Fanny. “What brother on earth would do half so much for his sisters, even if really his sisters! And as it is—only half blood!—But you have such a generous spirit!”

“I would not wish to do any thing mean,” he replied, pleased to be thought liberal even while shrinking from liberality. “One had rather, on such occasions, do too much than too little. No one, at least, can think I have not done enough for them: even themselves, they can hardly expect more.”

“There is no knowing what they may expect,” said Fanny, “but we are not to think of their expectations. The question is, what you can afford to do.”

“Certainly—and I think I may afford to give them five hundred pounds a-piece. As it is, without any addition of mine, they will each have about three thousand pounds on their mother’s death—a very comfortable fortune for any young woman.”

“To be sure it is; and indeed it strikes me they can want no addition at all. They will have ten thousand pounds divided amongst them. If they marry, they will be sure of doing well, and if they do not, they may all live very comfortably together on the interest of ten thousand pounds.”

“That is very true,” said he, feeling his conscience soften under her arithmetic, “and therefore I do not know whether, upon the whole, it would not be more advisable to do something for their mother while she lives, rather than for them—something of the annuity kind I mean. A hundred a year would make them all perfectly comfortable.”

Fanny hesitated—only a little, but enough to show that even a hundred pounds, paid steadily, struck her as a regular draught upon the household, like a leaky tap you cannot stop.

“To be sure,” she said at last, “it is better than parting with fifteen hundred pounds at once. But then, if Mrs. Dashwood should live fifteen years, we shall be completely taken in.”

“Fifteen years! my dear Fanny—her life cannot be worth half that purchase.”

“Certainly not; but if you observe, people always live for ever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it. You are not aware of what you are doing. I have known a great deal of the trouble of annuities; for my mother was clogged with the payment of three to old superannuated servants by my father’s will, and it is amazing how disagreeable she found it. Twice every year these annuities were to be paid; and then there was the trouble of getting it to them; and then one of them was said to have died, and afterwards it turned out to be no such thing. My mother was quite sick of it. Her income was not her own, she said, with such perpetual claims on it; and it was the more unkind in my father, because otherwise the money would have been entirely at my mother’s disposal.”

Mr. Dashwood listened, and the image of money leaving his hands with the steady, unavoidable rhythm of rent days—like a kitchen dripping, drip after drip, into a bowl you must empty and fill again—grew more disagreeable the longer she spoke.

“It has given me such an abhorrence of annuities,” Fanny concluded, “that I am sure I would not pin myself down to the payment of one for all the world.”

“It is certainly an unpleasant thing,” he admitted, “to have those kind of yearly drains on one’s income. One’s fortune is not one’s own. To be tied down to the regular payment of such a sum, on every rent day, is by no means desirable: it takes away one’s independence.”

“Undoubtedly; and after all you have no thanks for it. They think themselves secure, you do no more than what is expected, and it raises no gratitude at all. If I were you, whatever I did should be done at my own discretion entirely. I would not bind myself to allow them any thing yearly. It may be very inconvenient some years to spare a hundred, or even fifty pounds from our own expenses.”

He yielded as he always did when she framed reluctance as prudence.

“I believe you are right, my love,” said he. “It will be better that there should be no annuity in the case. Whatever I may give them occasionally will be of far greater assistance than a yearly allowance; because they would only enlarge their style of living if they felt sure of a larger income, and would not be sixpence the richer at the end of the year. It will certainly be much the best way. A present of fifty pounds, now and then, will prevent their ever being distressed for money, and will, I think, be amply discharging my promise to my father.”

“To be sure it will,” said Fanny, and her tone was that of a person smoothing dough, pressing out the air until it lies obedient and flat. “Indeed, to say the truth, I am convinced within myself that your father had no idea of your giving them any money at all. The assistance he thought of was only such as might reasonably be expected of you: looking out for a comfortable small house for them, helping them to move their things, and sending them presents of fish and game, and so forth, whenever they are in season. I’ll lay my life he meant nothing farther.”

And then she spoke of incomes and interests, of sums that sounded large when spoken quickly—seven thousand pounds here, a thousand there—until they became, in her telling, a plentiful pantry for four women who, she insisted, would live “so cheap.” Their housekeeping would be “nothing at all.” No carriage, no horses, hardly any servants; they would keep no company and have no expenses of any kind. Five hundred a year among them! She could not imagine how they would spend half of it; and as to his giving them more, it was absurd—indeed, they would be much more able to give him something.

“Upon my word,” said Mr. Dashwood, his mind settling comfortably into the chair she had placed for it, “I believe you are perfectly right. My father certainly could mean nothing more by his request than what you say. I clearly understand it now, and I will strictly fulfil my engagement by such acts of assistance and kindness as you have described. When my mother removes into another house, my services shall be readily given to accommodate her as far as I can. Some little present of furniture too may be acceptable then.”

“Certainly,” returned Fanny—until she remembered another store-room to keep. “But one thing must be considered. When your father and mother moved to Norland, though the furniture of Stanhill was sold, all the china, plate, and linen was saved, and is now left to your mother. Her house will therefore be almost completely fitted up as soon as she takes it.”

“That is a material consideration,” he allowed. “A valuable legacy indeed! And yet some of the plate would have been a very pleasant addition to our own stock here.”

“Yes; and the set of breakfast china is twice as handsome as what belongs to this house,” said Fanny, with a small bitterness, as if the very glaze affronted her. “A great deal too handsome, in my opinion, for any place they can ever afford to live in. But, however, so it is. Your father thought only of them. And I must say this: that you owe no particular gratitude to him, nor attention to his wishes; for we very well know that if he could, he would have left almost everything in the world to them.”

This, delivered like a final seasoning—sharp, decisive—ended the matter. The argument was irresistible because it gave Mr. Dashwood what he most desired: the feeling of acting rightly while doing little. Whatever tenderness had been in his first intention was pared away, slice by slice, until only a thin offering remained. He finally resolved that it would be absolutely unnecessary, if not highly indecorous, to do more for the widow and daughters of his father than such neighbourly acts of assistance and kindness as his own wife had pointed out.
