“And therewith he blent, and cried ha! As though he had been stricken to the harte.” — *Palamon and Arcite*

That panic-stricken, heart-stopping shock that struck Chaucer’s knight in his prison cell struck Don Guzman de Soto too, and it came about in this wise.

The Spanish captive settled easily enough at Bideford on his parole, lodged in far finer quarters than he had known for years, and went with the flow like any seasoned soldier of fortune. Barely a month into his stay with Sir Richard Grenville, old Mayor Salterne invited himself to supper. At first Don Guzman had blinked in confusion at the strange English custom of welcoming plain burghers, men who kept ledgers and took on apprentices, to sit above the salt on equal footing with noblemen — but he knew full well that Richard Grenville was too proud a gentleman to break local custom, so he set his Spanish pride aside. Before the evening was out, he found the mayor neither foolish nor vulgar, and found himself drawn into easy conversation. Salterne was courteous enough to show deference to his guest’s high rank, but held his own opinions with a quiet dignity the Spaniard had only ever seen among the merchant princes of Genoa and Venice.

As supper ended, Salterne begged Grenville to grace his humble home for a meal the next evening, then turned frankly to Don Guzman. He knew, he said, that a Spanish grandee would stoop far to sit at a simple tradesman’s table, but if the Don would deign to come, he would find cheer fit for any rank, no matter the company. Bored and looking for any amusement to pass the slow parole days, Don Guzman accepted the invitation, and was treated to an excellent feast and a cellar of fine wine, had he chosen to drink it.

Shrewd as any Elizabethan merchant with a nose for new trade — as he would later prove when he poured his wealth into the Virginia settlement project — Salterne was determined to pry every secret he could about the Indies out of his unusual guest. He first tried to loosen the Spaniard’s tongue with copious draughts of sack, a plan that failed entirely: Don Guzman was as abstemious as a Trappist monk, drinking little but water. His second gambit, a stream of carefully crafted questions about the Spanish Main, Peru, the Moluccas, China and all the far flung trade routes, fared little better, for the Don was as wily as a fox and answered only with empty, vague pleasantries.

Midway through this gentle war of words, the Rose of Torridge stepped into the room, as radiant as ever. She added her own soft, artless questions to her father’s, and Don Guzman could not find it in him to dodge them. He revealed no critical commercial secrets, but spun tales of distant lands that entertained the two of them through the rest of the evening.

The old scholar Burton wrote in his *Anatomy of Melancholy* that Eros, for all the grand tales of him being Jove and Venus’ eldest son, is often little more than the child of idleness and full bellies. Don Guzman’s circumstances certainly lent that old slander credence: idle as all captives are forced to be, and feasted well on Sir Richard Grenville’s generous table, he had already half-heartedly looked for a casual romance to while away the weeks. He had fleetingly, shamefully, thought of Grenville’s wife first — but he was a man of honor, and his host’s guest, so he prided himself on his self-denial to banish that thrilling, dangerous fancy from his mind.

With Lady Grenville out of reach, Rose Salterne slipped into his thoughts instead, with no distinct intent to wrong her, just for amusement at first, and then because he could not help himself. The Rose of Torridge loved to be admired by all, but there was an innocent, unworldly freshness to her he had never encountered before. “A peacock’s poise and a dove’s gentle heart,” he thought to himself, the combination so enchanting that if he could persuade her to love no one but him, he might well fool himself into loving her back. He resolved prudently to keep out of her way — but the days dragged, Lady Grenville left for Stow and left him alone rattling around Grenville’s great house, and there was nothing for it but to wander down to the archery butts gardens, show off his fine black cloak and feathered hat, play rackets with the town’s young men, bowls with the elders, and inevitably get himself invited back to supper at the mayor’s house.

There he was treated like royalty, not just for his rank, but for the rare stories he could tell as a man who had travelled the whole world. This was the dawn of Elizabethan English commerce: every merchant in Bideford, and across the whole country, had their imagination fired by dreams of new discoveries, trade companies, royal patents, and far settlements. The Ottoman sultan had just granted unprecedented trading privileges to the English, Roger Bodenham had sailed the Levant aboard the *Aucher* to great profit, Captain Hare had just set sail for Brazil aboard the *Minion* with Devonshire kersies and Manchester cottages to trade for sugar at Santos, Drake had proven you could plunder the Spanish American coasts and carve out an East Indian trade route, Frobisher and Davis were pushing through the northern ice in search of a Northwest Passage to China, and Anthony Jenkinson of the Muscovy Company had travelled as far as Bokhara and Siberia, where the brave Sir Hugh Willoughby and his entire crew had frozen to death three decades prior.

Don Guzman watched these sharp Devon merchants talk broad, nasal Devon dialect, and he saw exactly the threat they posed. Spain claimed the entirety of the New World by right of the Pope’s grant, she had crushed the Dutch, turned Venice into her bank and Genoa into her mercenary, absorbed all of Portugal’s vast East Indian trade — but none of that would ever make her mistress of the world while these stubborn islanders insisted on free seas and free trade for all, backed up first with Scripture and policy, then, if that failed, with sharp shot and cold steel. He knew then, as every other Spaniard of his day knew, that the great clash that would spawn the Armada was inevitable.

One evening, after he had casually hinted that any Englishman who strayed into Spanish claimed waters would be sent home in chains, the table erupted in snorts and shouts:
“We’ll see about that, sir!”
“Depends on who says there’s no right to go there!”
“You claimed the Indian seas by might, we’ll claim our right there with might when we choose!”
“You are insolent burghers!” Don Guzman snapped, and stormed out of the house, fuming at his own foolishness for wasting his dignity on common tradesmen.

The next day, Salterne — his head full of the profits waiting to be made from a few leaked trade secrets — sought him out to beg pardon, promising none of the rude townsmen who had spoken out would ever be invited back if the Don would only grace his home again. The Don, to his own disgust, agreed to return, sneering at himself the whole walk there. “Fool that I am, that girl has bewitched me,” he muttered. “I’ll go, and eat my pride for her sake.”

He began dropping hints to the mayor, saying he had taken such a liking to him that he would share secrets he would never reveal to any other Englishman, and that there were easy fortunes to be made for a single shrewd trader who did not meddle with Spain’s general claim to monopoly. Salterne’s greedy weak spot was struck, and the mayor, blind to all but the dream of riches, soon began deliberately sending Rose to draw stories and secrets out of the Spaniard. He was utterly convinced no harm could come of it: Rose was a staunch Protestant, so she would never dream of marrying a Papist Spaniard any more than she would marry a man on the moon, and Don Guzman’s grand Spanish pride would never stoop to wedding a mere merchant’s daughter. He trusted her virtue absolutely, and assumed the Don was far too fond of his own neck to ever dare make untoward advances to the mayor’s only child.

For months after that, Salterne’s parlor was Don Guzman’s favourite haunt, and he saw Rose every day. No man she had ever known was like him: he had all the easy grace of Frank Leigh, but a bolder, more travelled edge. His pride never curdled into the boasting every other visitor complained of from Spaniards; he had more tales of wonders than even Amyas Leigh, and he told them with a soft, passionate eloquence the plain, straightforward Amyas could never muster. Almost all the local young gallants were gone, off fighting in the Netherlands or the Irish wars, so Don Guzman was the only interesting man within thirty miles, and Rose fell slowly, irrevocably, in love.

Don Guzman, for his part, carefully cultivated casual friendships with every young lady of rank within miles, to make Salterne think he had no special preference for Rose, but he still drifted back to the mayor’s house every chance he got, spinning tales of cannibals, headless tribes, active volcano craters, the horrors of the siege of Famagusta where he had escaped flaying alive by the Turks by the narrowest of margins, the Battle of the Three Kings where he had held the dying King of Portugal’s head in his lap. One evening he laid his tragedy out to her, all smooth and deliberately designed to win pity: his sisters were dead to him in a convent, his brother was a Jesuit, his father had been killed by Indians in Mexico, his mother worked as a companion to a Sevillian princess, he had lost his sword, his fortune, his rank, and there was no one left in the world who loved him. Rose cried for him, and listened wide-eyed to his retellings of Italian romances long before they ever reached the English stage.

Before a year was out, Rose loved him far more deeply than he loved her, and neither spoke of the truth: she was too afraid, he too proud of his blood, knowing he would have to sacrifice his family’s noble name to marry a heretic tradesman’s daughter.

One day, taking advantage of the old English custom of greeting ladies with a soft kiss on the cheek, he leaned in to salute her when no one else was in the room, and she drew back.
“No, sir. You must know my cheek is not for you.”
“Every counter-jumper in Bideford seems to help themselves to it,” he snarled, smothering his anger.
“They are my equals,” she said apologetically.
“And I?”
“You are a nobleman, sir. You should remember that.”
“It is a strange taste to prefer the shopkeepers,” he sneered.
“Prefer? It is nothing but a silly formality. They are nothing to me.”
“And am I less than nothing?” he demanded.

Rose blushed bright red, but she stood her ground. “Why should you be anything to me? You have done us far too much honor with these pleasant evenings already. Do not mistake that honor for anything more. If you take one step closer, I will call my father.”
“Do so! I care no more for his anger than you care for my misery,” he cried, and threw himself at her feet. “I adore you! I have forgotten all the foolish differences of rank, forgotten everything but you. My light, my lodestar, my princess! I was Lucifer himself to everyone else, but to you I am a beggar grovelling for mercy — for I am so lonely, so homeless, so lost. Look, the tears are in my eyes! Are there tears in yours too?”

“Go!” Rose shrieked, suddenly breaking free of her trance. “Leave me at once, and never come back!” She darted out of the room and locked herself in the upper chamber.

Don Guzman bowed with perfect, serene grace, and walked out of the house, leaving Rose peeping down at him through the curtains, her heart sinking as she saw him stroll off as if nothing had happened. He never set foot in the mayor’s house again. Rose spent weeks reproaching herself as the cruelest woman alive, convinced she had broken the heart of the lonely nobleman, and hid from the window the moment she saw him walk past, half hoping he would look up, half terrified he would.

Outside the small, tight world of Bideford, greater tides were turning. The fate of all Europe hung on the question of Queen Elizabeth’s proposed marriage to the Duke of Anjou. Sir Humphrey Gilbert was moving heaven and earth, and all of Devonshire most of all, to push through his royal patent to plant new settlements across the Atlantic — a patent that would one day give England Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Canada, and the whole of the northern American colonies, and Gilbert himself a far better, everlasting crown of glory in the world to come.
