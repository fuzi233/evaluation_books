On the night of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never failed to make her appearance at the Opera House. Indeed, she strategically scheduled her grandest event on an evening of operatic performance, thereby signaling to the city her total immunity from domestic drudgery and her command of a staff capable of orchestrating every detail of her celebration in her absence. The Beaufort residence stood as one of the rare estates in New York boasting a dedicated ballroom—a luxury that predated even the mansions of Mrs. Manson Mingott or the Headly Chiverses. At a time when it began to seem provincial to cover a drawing-room floor with a crash mat and shift the furniture upstairs, the Beauforts maintained a space reserved solely for dancing, left to shuttered darkness for three hundred and sixty-four days of the year. With gilt chairs stacked in corners and chandeliers stored in bags, this architectural excess served as a potent compensation for any regrets regarding the Beaufort legacy.

Mrs. Archer, prone to distilling social observations into axioms, once remarked, "We all have our pet common people." While the phrase was bold, its truth was quietly acknowledged within many an exclusive circle. Yet, the Beauforts occupied a peculiar stratum; some whispered they were merely common, others claimed they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort hailed from one of America's most revered families; she was formerly the radiant Regina Dallas of the South Carolina branch, a penniless beauty introduced to New York society by her cousin, the perpetually imprudent Medora Manson, who consistently acted wrongly from noble motives. Possession of connections to the Mansons and Rushworths granted a *droit de cité*—a right of entry—as Mr. Sillerton Jackson, a frequent visitor to the Tuileries, termed it. However, did one forfeit this privilege upon marrying Julius Beaufort? Who, precisely, was Beaufort? He passed as English, possessing charm, handsomeness, wit, and hospitality, though plagued by an ill temper. Having arrived in America with recommendations from Old Mrs. Manson Mingott's English son-in-law, a banker, he quickly secured a prominent position in business. Yet, rumors persisted of dissipated habits, a bitter tongue, and mysterious origins. When Medora announced her cousin's engagement to him, it was viewed as another lapse in judgment in her long history of indiscretions.

Yet, folly often bears fruit as surely as wisdom. Two years after the wedding, it was conceded that Mrs. Beaufort presided over the most distinguished house in New York. The mechanics of this miracle remained obscure. Indolent, passive, and occasionally dismissed as dull by the caustic, she nonetheless grew younger, blonder, and more breathtaking annually. Dressed like an idol and draped in pearls, she reigned supreme in Mr. Beaufort's heavy brownstone palace, commanding the attention of the entire city without raising a jeweled finger. The cognoscenti speculated that Beaufort himself managed the household: training the servants, instructing the chef, directing the gardeners, selecting guests, brewing punch, and dictating the notes his wife sent to friends. If true, these duties were executed in private, allowing him to present himself publicly as a carefree millionaire strolling into his drawing room with the detached ease of a guest, remarking, "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel, aren't they? I believe she imports them from Kew."

New York agreed that Beaufort's secret lay in his ability to carry things off. Whispers that he had been aided in leaving England by an international banking house were shrugged off as lightly as the rest. Though New York's business conscience was as sharp as its moral standards, Beaufort swept everything before him, dragging the city into his drawing rooms. For over two decades, invitations to the Beauforts were received with the same sense of security as visits to the Mingotts, accompanied by the added assurance of hot canvas-back ducks and vintage wines, rather than tepid champagne and reheated croquettes from Philadelphia.

As usual, Mrs. Beaufort appeared in her box just prior to the Jewel Song. And as customary, when she rose at the conclusion of the third act, wrapped her opera cloak around her lovely shoulders, and vanished, New York understood that the ball would commence within the hour. The Beaufort house was a destination New Yorkers proudly displayed to foreigners, particularly on ball nights. They were pioneers in owning their own red velvet carpets, having them unrolled by footmen beneath their own awnings, rather than renting them with supper and chairs. They also initiated the custom of removing cloaks in the hall, sparing ladies the indignity of retreating to the hostess's bedroom to re-curl their hair with gas burners; Beaufort was rumored to have quipped that he presumed all his wife's friends had maids to ensure proper coiffure before departure. Furthermore, the house was boldly designed with a ballroom, eliminating the need to squeeze through narrow passages as at the Chiverses'. Instead, guests marched solemnly down a vista of enfiladed drawing rooms—the sea-green, the crimson, and the bouton d'or—seeing from afar the reflection of multi-candled chandeliers in polished parquet floors, leading eventually to a conservatory where camellias and tree-ferns arched their costly foliage over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer, fitting his station as a young man of consequence, arrived somewhat late. He had left his overcoat with the silk-stockinged footmen—a vanity of Beaufort's—dawdled briefly in the library adorned with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite tables, where a few men conversed while donning dancing gloves, before joining the line of guests awaiting reception on the threshold of the crimson drawing room. Archer felt distinctly nervous. Unlike the typical young bloods who retired to their clubs after the opera, he had walked several blocks up Fifth Avenue on the fine night before returning toward the Beauforts. He harbored a definite fear that the Mingotts might be pushing boundaries, perhaps acting on Granny Mingott's orders to escort the Countess Olenska to the ball. Recalling the grave tone of the conversation in the club box, he realized the potential magnitude of such a breach. Although more determined than ever to "see the thing through," his chivalric eagerness to champion his betrothed's cousin had waned since their brief exchange at the opera.

Wandering into the bouton d'or drawing room, where Beaufort had presumptuously hung "Love Victorious," the controversial nude by Bouguereau, Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter near the entrance to the ballroom. Couples were already gliding across the floor beyond; candlelight illuminated swirling tulle skirts, girlish heads crowned with modest blossoms, the dashing plumes and ornaments of married women's hairstyles, and the gleam of starched shirt fronts and fresh *glacé* gloves. Miss Welland, seemingly ready to join the dancers, lingered on the threshold holding her lilies-of-the-valley as her sole bouquet. Her face was faintly pale, her eyes alight with candid excitement. A cluster of young men and girls surrounded her, exchanging handshakes, laughter, and pleasantries, while Mrs. Welland, standing slightly apart, offered a beam of qualified approval. It was clear that Miss Welland was about to announce her engagement, while her mother adopted the air of parental reluctance deemed appropriate for the occasion.

Archer paused. This announcement had been his express wish, yet he would have preferred a different manner of revealing his happiness. Proclaiming it amidst the clamor of a crowded ballroom seemed to strip the event of the delicate bloom of privacy due to matters closest to the heart. His joy ran so deep that this superficial blurring left its core untouched, yet he desired the surface remain pure as well. He found solace in realizing that May Welland shared this sentiment. Her eyes fled to his with a beseeching look, communicating silently: *"Remember, we are doing this because it is right."* No appeal could have resonated more immediately within Archer's chest. Still, he wished the necessity of their action stemmed from some higher ideal rather than the unfortunate circumstances surrounding poor Ellen Olenska.

The group around Miss Welland parted to make way for him with significant smiles. After accepting his share of congratulations, he drew his betrothed into the center of the ballroom floor and placed his arm around her waist. "Now we shan't have to talk," he smiled into her open eyes as they drifted away on the soft waves of the Blue Danube. She offered no verbal reply. Her lips trembled into a smile, but her gaze remained distant and serious, fixed on some ineffable vision. "Dear," Archer whispered, pulling her closer. It struck him then that the initial hours of engagement, even spent in a ballroom, held something grave and sacramental. What a new life awaited, with this whiteness, radiance, and goodness at his side!

Once the dance concluded, the pair, becoming an affianced couple, wandered into the conservatory. Sitting behind a high screen of tree-ferns and camellias, Newland pressed her gloved hand to his lips. "You see, I did as you asked," she said. "Yes; I couldn't wait," he replied, smiling. After a pause, he added, "Only I wish it hadn't had to be at a ball." "Yes, I know," she responded, meeting his glance with understanding. "But after all—even here we are alone together, aren't we?" "Oh, dearest—always!" Archer exclaimed.
