Gerard, now a novice in the monastery, bore his penances with quiet resolve. Among them was the effort to receive Lodovico with kindness, though the man’s presence had once filled him with revulsion. Yet this act of discipline, begun as self-denial, soon bore unexpected fruit.

Lodovico, the assassin whose blade had been meant for Gerard’s heart, had instead saved him—and in doing so, found himself altered. There was a strange pride in his eyes whenever he looked upon the life he had spared, as if he could scarcely believe he had done such a thing. At first, it might have seemed almost comical, this newfound tenderness in a man of blood. But the heart, once opened to mercy, cannot easily close again. The sweetness of sparing a life turned Lodovico’s thoughts away from killing. Remorse crept in, and then something like repentance.

Teresa, ever watchful, seized the moment. She had threatened before to leave him if he did not abandon his dark trade, but now her words carried weight. The friars, too, saw the change in him and lent their guidance. Soon, Lodovico and Teresa were sent away—not in flight, but in hope. They boarded a ship bound for Leghorn, where Teresa had kin, and there the former assassin took up the humble work of a porter.

At first, he grumbled. The labor was dull, the days long. But dullness was better than the alternative—a swift and bloody end at the hands of Princess Claelia’s hired blades. His exile spared him that fate, though he never knew it.

The princess, robbed of her vengeance, fell into brooding silence. Her old confessor was dismissed; in his place, she sought one famed for piety—none other than Fra Jerome. When she poured out her grievances to him, expecting sympathy, she received none.

He listened, grim-faced, then shattered her illusions with the force of a thunderclap.

*"You are the true criminal,"* he told her, his voice like steel. *"You tempted a man to murder with your gold, and now, blind with pride, you thirst for more blood—as if those you hired were guiltier than you."*

She protested, haughty even in confession. *"I am not accustomed to such reproof from my spiritual guides."*

But Fra Jerome did not waver. He stripped her of every excuse, threatened her with divine wrath, and left her trembling at his feet.

*"Oh, holy father,"* she wept, *"help me save my soul!"*

*"Cast off your jewels,"* he commanded. *"Your silks and velvets. Go to Loretto in the garb of a beggar, barefoot but for sandals. Wash the feet of pilgrims, and confess your sin to the humblest friars you meet."*

She begged to keep her mask—her beauty was too well known. With a grunt, he relented, but only just. *"Cover your eyes, then. No more."*

When she reached for his hand in gratitude, he pulled away. *"This hand holds the Eucharist. It is not for the likes of you to touch."*

Broken, she whispered, *"At least bless me before you go."*

*"Ask for that blessing when you return from Loretto,"* he said, and left her there.

So it was that the strange events by the Tiber left their mark on all who had played a part. Lodovico, the killer turned laborer. The princess, humbled to penance. And Gerard—once dragged half-dead into the monastery, now regarded within its walls as something like a saint.

His probation was brief. Soon he took priestly vows, then the full mantle of a Dominican friar. With his new life came a new name: Brother Clement. The old Gerard was gone, buried with the world he had left behind.

And so the story of Gerard ended. The story of Brother Clement had begun.
