Gerard awoke to consciousness—and to despair.

The fever had raged within him, a tempest in the veins, a fire in the brain. His auburn hair, long as a woman’s, lay shorn upon the table. Day by day, death’s shadow crept closer. The leech withdrew, abandoning hope. But then, as the sun dipped below the horizon on the fifth day, Gerard fell into a sleep so deep that some whispered he would never wake from it.

An old nurse, wise in the ways of suffering, declared that if his youth could sustain him for twelve hours of such slumber, he might yet live. The landlady, devout and desperate, vowed candles to the Virgin for every hour he slept.

Twelve hours passed. She rejoiced.

Twenty-four. She faltered.

Thirty. The nurse was summoned.

"Shall we wake him?"

The woman studied him—his steady breath, the dampness of his palm. "Learned men might rouse him to peer at his tongue and learn nothing. But we know better. Let nature mend what man cannot."

By the forty-eighth hour, curiosity swarmed like flies at the door. Only two were permitted entry: Fra Colonna, his eyes wet with grief, and the towering Fra Jerome, calm as ever, beads slipping through his fingers as if counting the moments between life and death.

Then, at last—sixty hours in that fathomless repose—Gerard stirred. His eyelids lifted gently, as though from a brief nap. He blinked, confused. His fingers found his shorn head. He saw Fra Colonna and smiled—then memory struck like a blade, and his face twisted in agony. A cry tore from his throat, and he turned his face to the wall.

The landlady wept. She knew the torment of waking to loss.

Fra Jerome murmured prayers. Gerard writhed beneath the bedclothes.

Fra Colonna, trembling, spoke of the immortal Muses—steadfast where mortal love had failed. But his voice broke, and he fled.

Fra Jerome lingered. "The Muses are phantoms," he intoned. "The Church alone offers peace. Earth has betrayed you; the Church stands open. Consecrate yourself to her."

Then he, too, was gone.

"The *Church*?" Gerard snarled, lurching upright, his fist clenched. "A curse upon it! Were it not for the Church, I would not lie here broken—and she would not lie cold in Holland! Margaret! My love! They let you die—blind, selfish wretches! I would have seen the signs. I would have saved you!"

Tears came, hot and furious. Then rage again, black as pitch.

"The Church! For its sake, I was torn from her. Damn the Church! Damn the world! Thieves and murderers live on, while angels perish. Curse life! Curse death! Curse the hand that made them so!"

Had Fra Jerome heard, he would have seen Gerard burned without a second thought. But the words were lost to the walls, and only the landlady returned, finding him dressing with feverish haste.

"Why rise so soon?" she pleaded.

"What use is lying here? I seek oblivion."

"Oblivion? Child, you are too young for such words!"

"Then I am old in grief," he said bitterly. He donned his finest clothes, though it was not Sunday.

The woman paled. "What new master do you serve now?"

A hollow laugh. "Satan."

And with that, Gerard stepped into the street—his cap rakish, his limbs unsteady, his face a death-mask twisted with defiance. A man already half-lost, walking into the dark.
