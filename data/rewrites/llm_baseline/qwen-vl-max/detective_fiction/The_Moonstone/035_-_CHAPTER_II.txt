The next matter I must address is the additional information I possess concerning the Moonstone—or more precisely, the Indian conspiracy to steal it. Though what I have to say may seem slight, it bears a significant weight in relation to events yet to unfold.

Approximately a week or ten days after Miss Verinder had departed from our household, one of my clerks entered my private office, holding a visiting card. He informed me that a gentleman awaited me downstairs, wishing to speak with me. I took the card and examined it. The name inscribed was foreign—now lost to memory—but beneath it, written in English, was a line I recalled perfectly: “Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker.”

The audacity of such a recommendation struck me with immediate surprise. Mr. Luker, though a man of some standing, held no authority to introduce anyone to me. I sat silent for a moment, stunned by the implication. My clerk, noting my bewilderment, offered his own observation: “He’s rather an unusual-looking man, sir. So dark-skinned that we all thought him Indian—or something similar.”

Connecting this description with the note on the card, I suspected the Moonstone might be behind this visit. Without hesitation—and much to my clerk’s astonishment—I granted the gentleman an audience.

Let me justify this unprofessional act of curiosity: no one in England, perhaps nowhere else, has been so closely entwined with the history of the Indian Diamond as I. I knew Colonel Herncastle’s secret plan to escape assassination. I received his periodic letters confirming he still lived. I drafted his will, leaving the Moonstone to Miss Verinder. I persuaded his executor to accept the jewel, believing it might enrich the family. And finally, I overcame Franklin Blake’s misgivings, urging him to deliver the stone to Lady Verinder’s home.

If any person can claim a rightful interest in the Moonstone and all its mysteries, surely it is I.

As soon as the visitor entered, I felt certain he was one of the three Indians—likely their leader. Though dressed in European attire, his swarthy complexion, tall and lean frame, and composed demeanor betrayed his Eastern origin. I gestured to a chair and asked the nature of his business.

After apologizing with careful English phrasing for disturbing me, he produced a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Beneath that lay a silken covering, which he removed to reveal a tiny casket—exquisitely inlaid with jewels upon an ebony base.

“I come, sir,” he said, “to request a loan. I leave this as assurance that I will repay you.”

I glanced at the card again. “And you seek me through Mr. Luker’s recommendation?”

He bowed slightly. “Yes, sir.”

“Then I must ask—why did Mr. Luker not lend you the money himself?”

“He told me, sir, that he had none to offer.”

“And so he sent you to me?”

He nodded, pointing to the card. “It is written there.”

A concise reply, and perfectly to the point. If the Moonstone had been in my possession, I would not have doubted for a second that this man would have killed me without hesitation. Yet, apart from that alarming possibility, he was the most courteous client I had ever encountered. He respected my time—something no Englishman had ever done in my experience.

“I regret,” I said, “that you’ve taken the trouble to come. Mr. Luker is mistaken. While I do handle loans like others in my profession, I never lend to strangers, nor on such collateral.”

Rather than press me, as others might have done, he simply bowed again and carefully rewound the casket in its wrappings. Without a word of protest, he rose—this remarkable man—to leave.

“Before I go,” he said, “might your kindness allow one question?”

I inclined my head. One question? A rarity among clients.

“Suppose,” he continued, “it had been possible—and customary—for you to lend me the sum. In what time could I reasonably expect to repay it?”

“In this country,” I replied, “you’d typically have one year from the date of the loan to repay it.”

He bowed once more—this time, deeply—and then slipped silently from the room. His departure was swift, soundless, almost feline. It startled me, though only briefly.

Once I regained my composure, I formed a clear conclusion about the stranger. During our conversation, his face, voice, and manner remained under perfect control—impossible to read. Yet, he gave me one clue. Until I mentioned the repayment timeline, he had shown no sign of absorbing anything I said. But when I spoke of the year-long period, he looked directly into my eyes for the first time.

This suggested a purpose behind his final question—an interest in that specific detail. I began to suspect the entire visit—the casket, the loan request—was merely a cover for that single inquiry. The formality was a ruse, designed to lead to that precise moment.

Just as I was probing deeper into his motives, a letter arrived—from Mr. Septimus Luker himself.

He apologized in the most groveling terms, claiming he could explain everything if I would grant him a personal meeting. Again, I yielded to curiosity. I arranged to see him the following day.

Mr. Luker was a man far below the dignity of the Indian who had visited me—vulgar, repulsive, cringing, and tedious. He deserves no extended account here. But the gist of his story was this:

The day before I saw the Indian, Luker had received a visit from the same man. Despite the European disguise, Luker immediately recognized him as the chief of the trio who had once loitered near his house, forcing him to call a magistrate. The realization that this was likely the man who had blindfolded and robbed him sent Luker into a state of terror. He believed he was moments from death.

Yet the Indian behaved like a complete stranger. He presented the casket and made the same loan request he later made to me. To get rid of him quickly, Luker claimed he had no money. The Indian then asked whom he should approach instead. Luker, panicked, answered that a respectable solicitor would be best. When pressed for a name, he blurted out mine—the first one that came to mind in his fear.

“The sweat was pouring off me like rain, sir,” he confessed. “I didn’t know what I was saying. I beg you, Mr. Bruff, forgive me—I was truly frightened out of my wits.”

I forgave him readily. It was the quickest way to end the encounter.

Before he left, I asked: Had the Indian said anything notable when departing?

“Yes,” Luker replied. “He asked exactly the same question he asked you—about the repayment period—and received the same answer.”

What did it mean?

Luker offered no insight. My own reasoning failed to unlock the mystery. That evening, I had a dinner engagement. I went upstairs, mood sour, unaware that the path to my dressing-room would also lead me to a discovery.
