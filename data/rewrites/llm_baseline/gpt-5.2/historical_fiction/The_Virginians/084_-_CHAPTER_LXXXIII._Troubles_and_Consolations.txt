When Harry and I were boys and thought it manly to be refractory, and showed our learning by dodging it, who could have foretold that I should live to become a bearleader in sober earnest, and to one of the most tractable cubs in London? My mother—when at last Fate and time brought us within one roof again—could never be persuaded to name that season plainly. She would touch it as you touch a bruise through linen: “that terrible time, my love, which I can’t bear to think of,” “those dreadful years when there was difference between us,” and then would turn the discourse, or call for the child, or bid Gumbo see whether the kettle sang. And though my pupil, a worthy and grateful man, sent me out to Jamestown, in due course, several barrels of the liquor by which his great fortune was brewed, Madam Esmond would speak of him as “your friend in England,” “your wealthy Lambeth friend,” and never once by his name; nor would she taste a drop of his beer. We brewed our own at Warrington Manor later, as becomes a house that would not have its daily bread beholden to another man’s oven; yet our good Mr. Foker never failed, in the years of our need, to ship to Ipswich every year a couple of butts of his entire—brown as walnut peel, with a head as firm as beaten whites, and a bitterness that clung to the palate like a remembered wrong. His son is now a young sprig of fashion, and has married an earl’s daughter; the father remains what he ever was—shrewd, kind, and plain in his beneficence—and it is to the luck of making his acquaintance that I owe some of the most welcome guineas that ever clinked in my hand.

It was not only the sum, though we counted every shilling as a cook counts eggs when the market is dear; it was the occupation and the hope the office gave me—governor to Master Foker—that were precious then. A man may endure a scant table if he is not made to sit idle before an empty trencher. Mr. F.’s brewery (the site has since changed) stood by Pedlar’s Acre in Lambeth, and the surgeon who attended my wife in her confinement tended the brewer’s household too. He was a Bavarian by birth, Voelker in his own tongue, but called Lance here, as if England must file every foreign name to her own measure. Many a time the Doctor would sit in my little garden with a pipe of Virginia—rich leaf, rank and comforting, smelling of earth and smoke and a far-off tidewater—while within doors Theo moved about the room with that quiet step of hers that never startled the baby. Through him, my name and story came to Mr. Foker.

The Doctor brought his patron to our house; and when Mr. Foker found I had a smattering of his language, and could sing “Prinz Eugen der edle Ritter”—a marching song my grandfather had brought home from the wars with Marlborough, the very consonants clacking like sabres—the German conceived a sudden liking for me, as if an old melody had found its way back to him through London soot. His lady put her chair and chariot at Mrs. Warrington’s service; his little daughter took a prodigious fancy to our baby (and though the Captain is as ugly a fellow now as ever wore a queue, he was beautiful as an infant; the very image of the Squire at thirty, everybody says); and his son and heir, Master Foker, being much maltreated at Westminster because of his father’s calling, the parents asked if I would take charge of him, and paid me no insufficient sum for superintending his education.

Mr. Foker was shrewd in business, and because he and his family truly interested themselves in me and mine, I laid my pecuniary affairs pretty unreservedly before him, as one lays open a pantry to a neighbour in a bad season, saying, See—this is what there is, and this is what is not. My statement, he said, increased his respect for me. He laughed at our stories of the aid my noble relatives had given—my aunt’s coverlid, my Lady Castlewood’s mouldy jelly, Lady Warrington’s contemptuous civility. But he wept outright over little Miles’s moidore; and as for Sampson and Hagan, “I wow,” says he, “dey shall have so much beer als ever dey can drink.” He sent his wife to call upon Lady Maria, and treated her with the utmost respect whenever she came to visit him. Lady Maria stayed with Mr. Foker when Hagan went to Dublin to complete his college terms; and the good brewer’s purse ministered to our friend’s wants and supplied his outfit.

When Mr. Foker came fully to know my affairs and position, he spoke of me with a kind of enthusiasm, as if my plain refusals of temptation were heroic. I have told how my mother saved money for Harry, and how the two were in my debt; but when Harry spent money he spent it fancying it to be his, and Madam Esmond never could be made to see that she dealt hardly with me—the money was paid and gone, and there was an end. Now, at the end of ’62, Harry sent over a considerable remittance to purchase his promotion, begging me at the same time to remember he was in my debt, and to draw on his agents if I had need. He did not know how great the need was, nor how my little capital had been swallowed as quietly as broth disappears from a hungry child’s spoon.

To take my brother’s money would delay his advancement; and I did not draw upon him, though I own I was tempted. Nor, knowing my dear General Lambert’s small means, did I choose to impoverish him by asking supplies. These simple acts of forbearance my worthy brewer must needs count as exalted virtue. He wrote privately to my brother in America, praising me and my wife as the most admirable of human beings; and he called upon Madame de Bernstein—who never told me of his visit indeed, but whom I perceived about this time to treat us with a singular respect and gentleness, surprising in one I could not but account selfish and worldly. In after days I asked him how he gained admission to the Baroness.

He laughed. “De Baroness!” says he. “I knew de Baron ven he vas a vallet at Munich, and I vas a brewer-apprentice.” I think our family had best not be too curious about our uncle the Baron.

Thus the part of my life which ought to have been most melancholy was made pleasant by many friends, happy accidents, and strokes of lucky fortune. The bear I led was a docile little cub, and danced to my piping readily. Better to lead him about—to hear the scrape of his pen, the thump of his schoolbooks on the table, the honest London chorus of carts and hawkers outside our window—than to hang round booksellers’ doors, or wait on the caprice of managers. My wife and I, during our exile as we may call it, spent many pleasant evenings with these kind friends. Nor were we without intellectual enjoyments: Mrs. Foker and Mrs. Warrington sang finely together, their voices twining like two scents rising from the same pot; and sometimes, when I was in the humour, I read my play of Pocahontas to this friendly audience in a manner better than Hagan’s own, Mr. Foker was pleased to say.

After that little escapade of Miles Warrington, junior, I saw nothing of him, and heard of my paternal relatives but rarely. Sir Miles was assiduous at court (as I believe he would have been at Nero’s), and I laughed one day when Mr. Foker told me he had heard on ’Change “dat dey vas going to make your uncle a Beer.”

“A beer?” says I.

“Can’t you understand de vort, ven I say it?” cries the testy old gentleman. “Vell, veil—a Lort!”

Sir Miles indeed was the obedient servant of the Minister, whoever he might be; I am surprised he did not speak English with a Scotch accent during the first favourite’s brief reign. I saw him and his wife coming from court when Mrs. Claypool was presented to her Majesty on her marriage. I had my little boy on my shoulder. My uncle and aunt stared resolutely at me from their gilt coach window. The footmen looked blank above their nosegays. Had I worn the Fairy’s cap and been invisible, my father’s brother could not have passed me with less notice.

We did not avail ourselves often of Lady Castlewood’s queer invitation—to come drink tea and sup with her ladyship when there was no other company. Old Van den Bosch, shrewd though he was in getting a fortune, was not amusing in talk, except to his daughter, who spoke of household and City matters—bulling and bearing, raising and selling stock, the price of hay, the cost of sauces and servants—with as much keenness as any broker. Nor was my Lord Castlewood often at home, nor much missed by his wife when absent, nor very much at ease in the old father’s company. The Countess told all this to my wife with her simple candour.

“Guess,” says she, “my lord and father don’t pull well together nohow. Guess my lord’s always wanting money, and father keeps the key of the box—and quite right, too. If he could have the fingering of all our money, my lord would soon make away with it, and then what’s to become of our noble family? We pay everything, my dear—except play-debts, and them we won’t have nohow. We pay cooks, horses, wine-merchants, tailors, and everybody—and lucky for them too—reckon my lord wouldn’t pay ’em! And we always take care he has a guinea in his pocket, and goes out like a real nobleman. What that man do owe to us—what he did before we come—gracious goodness only knows! Me and father does our best to make him respectable, but it’s no easy job, my dear. Law! he’d melt the plate, only father keeps the key of the strong-room; and when we go to Castlewood, my father travels with me, and papa is armed too, as well as the people.”

“Gracious heavens!” cries my wife. “Your ladyship does not mean to say you suspect your own husband of a desire to—”

“To what? Oh no, nothing, of course! And I would trust our brother Will with untold money, wouldn’t I? As much as I’d trust the cat with the cream-pan! I tell you, my dear, it’s not all pleasure being a woman of rank and fashion: and if I have bought a countess’s coronet, I have paid a good price for it—that I have!”

And so had my Lord Castlewood paid a large price to have his estate freed, his houses and stables furnished, his debts discharged. He was slave to his little wife and her father; no wonder the old man’s society was not pleasant to the poor victim, and that he gladly slunk away from his own fine house, to feast at the club when he had money, or at any board where knives rang cheerfully and no one kept the key of the cupboard against him.

To lead a bear, as I did, is no very pleasant business, to be sure. To wait in a bookseller’s anteroom until it should please his honour to finish his dinner and give me audience—hearing the clatter of plates behind the door, the loud laugh, the scraping of chairs, the soft tyranny of a man’s leisure—was sometimes hard for a man of my name and pride. But would I have exchanged my poverty for Castlewood’s ignominy? preferred his dependence to my own? At least I earned my wage, such as it was; and no man can say I ever flattered my patrons or was servile to them, or indeed was other than sulky, overbearing, and intolerable.

Now there was a certain person with whom Fate had thrown me into life-partnership, who bore her poverty with such smiling sweetness and easy grace, that niggard Fortune relented before her, like some savage ogre in a tale that softens at a child’s song. However poor she was, all who knew her saw that here was a fine lady. The little tradesmen and humble folk round about treated her with as much respect as the richest of our neighbours. She could go into a chandler’s shop and ask for a farthing’s worth of candles, and the man would hand them as if they were wax tapers for a chapel; she could order a bone for broth, and the butcher would choose it as carefully as if it were for a duke. She knew how to make a thin joint seem plentiful: how to coax savour from a scrap of bacon and an onion; how to keep the bread warm at the hearth while the water came to the simmer; how to hush the baby with the very sound of her spoon stirring in a pot. In those little domestic noises—kettle beginning its song, crust crackling as it toasted, the faint scrape of a knife on board—there was more comfort than in any trumpets of court.

“I think, my dear,” says good-natured Mrs. Foker when they rode out in the latter’s chariot, “you look like the mistress of the carriage, and I only as your maid.” Our landladies adored her; the tradesfolk executed her little orders as eagerly as if a duchess gave them. I have often thought of the lady in Comus, how through all the rout and rabble she moves serene and pure.

Several times—as often as we chose—the good-natured parents of my young bear lent us their chariot. If I must tell truth, we drove once to the Protestant Hero and had a syllabub in the garden—cream beaten to a froth with wine and lemon, a little nutmeg grated over, the scent rising as light as gossip; and the hostess would insist on calling my wife her ladyship the whole afternoon. We visited Mr. Johnson too and took tea with him (the ingenious Mr. Goldsmith was of the company); the Doctor waited upon my wife to her coach with as much ceremony as if she had been presented at court.

But our most frequent visits were to Aunt Bernstein; and I promise you I was not jealous because my aunt presently professed a wonderful liking for Theo.

This liking grew so that she would have her most days in the week, or would keep her altogether, and think Theo’s child and husband only plagues, and hate us in the most amusing way for keeping her favourite from her. Not that my wife was unworthy of any favour; but her forced absences and the constant difficulty of intercourse raised my aunt’s liking for a while into something like passion. She poured in notes like love-letters; her people were ever about our kitchen, with messages, parcels, hints, commands. If my wife did not go, she wrote heartrending appeals, and scolded me when I saw her; and once when the child was ill (it hath pleased Fate to spare our Captain to be a prodigious trouble to us, and a wholesome trial for our tempers), Madame Bernstein came three days running to Lambeth, vowed there was nothing the matter with the baby—nothing at all—and that we only pretended his illness in order to vex her.

The reigning Countess of Castlewood was as easy and affable with her old aunt as with other folk. “What air you all about, scraping and bowing to that old woman, I can’t tell, noways!” her ladyship would say. “She a fine lady! Nonsense! She ain’t no more fine than any other lady; and I guess I’m as good as any of ’em with their high heels and grand airs! She a beauty once! Take away her wig, and her rouge, and her teeth; and what becomes of your beauty, I’d like to know? Guess you’d put it all in a bandbox, and there would be nothing left but a shrivelled old woman!”

And indeed she spoke too truly. All beauty must come to that complexion at last; decay on the tree or under ground. Here was old age without reverence: grey hairs hid or painted; the world still loud about her—wheels on stones, boys crying oysters, fiddles in distant rooms—and she tottering on it, clinging with her crutch. For fourscore years she had moved on it, and eaten of the tree forbidden and permitted. She had had beauty, pleasure, flattery; but what secret rages, disappointments, defeats, humiliations—what thorns under roses, what stinging bees in fruit!

“You are not a beauty, my dear,” she would say to my wife, “and may thank your stars you are not.” (If she contradicted herself, I suppose the rest of us do likewise.) “Don’t tell me your husband is pleased with your face, and you want no one else’s admiration! We all do. Every woman would rather be beautiful than be anything else in the world—ever so rich, or ever so good, or have all the gifts of the fairies! Look at that picture—though I know ’tis a bad one, and that stupid vapouring Kneller could not paint my eyes, nor my hair, nor my complexion. What a shape I had then—and look at me now, and this wrinkled old neck! Why have we such a short time of our beauty? I remember Mademoiselle de l’Enclos at a much greater age than mine, quite fresh and well-conserved. We can’t hide our ages. They are wrote in Mr. Collins’s books for us. I was born in the last year of King James’s reign. I am not old yet. I am but seventy-six. But what a wreck, my dear: and isn’t it cruel that our time should be so short?”

Here my wife would state, softly, the incontrovertible proposition that the time of all of us is short here below.

“Ha!” cries the Baroness. “Did not Adam live near a thousand years, and was not Eve beautiful all the time? I used to perplex Mr. Tusher with that—poor creature! What have we done since, that our lives are so much lessened, I say?”

“Has your life been so happy that you would prolong it ever so much more?” asks the Baroness’s auditor. “Have you, who love wit, never read Dean Swift’s description of the deathless people in Gulliver? My papa and my husband say ’tis one of the finest and most awful sermons ever wrote. It were better not to live at all, than to live without love; and I’m sure,” says my wife, putting her handkerchief to her eyes, “should anything happen to my dearest George, I would wish to go to Heaven that moment.”

“Who loves me in Heaven? I am quite alone, child—that is why I had rather stay here,” says the Baroness, in a frightened, piteous tone. “You are kind to me, God bless your sweet face! Though I scold, and have a frightful temper, my servants will do anything to make me comfortable—get up at any hour of night, and never say a cross word. I like my cards still. Indeed, life would be a blank without ’em. Almost everything is gone except that. I can’t eat my dinner now, since I lost those last two teeth.” And she spoke it with a sort of outrage, as if the very act of eating—of biting crust, of cracking a nut, of holding a morsel in the mouth and making it yield—were a country stolen from her by time. “Everything goes away from us in old age. But I still have my cards—thank Heaven, I still have my cards!”

And here she would begin to doze, waking up if my wife stirred, imagining Theo was about to leave her. “Don’t go away. I can’t bear to be alone. I don’t want you to talk. But I like to see your face, my dear! It is much pleasanter than that horrid old Brett’s, that I have had scowling about my bedroom these ever so long years.”

“Well, Baroness! still at your cribbage?” (We may fancy a noble Countess interrupting a game between Theo and Aunt Bernstein.) “Me and my Lord Esmond have come to see you! Go and shake hands with grandaunt, Esmond! and tell her ladyship that your lordship’s a good boy!”

“My lordship’s a good boy,” says the child.

“And if he is, I guess he don’t take after his father,” shrieks out Lady Castlewood; she chose to fancy Aunt Bernstein deaf, and always bawled in her face.

“Your ladyship chose my nephew for better or for worse,” says Aunt Bernstein, now always flurried in the young Countess’s presence.

“But he is a precious deal worse than ever I thought he was. I am speaking of your Pa, Ezzy. If it wasn’t for your mother, my son, Lord knows what would become of you! We are a-going to see his little Royal Highness. Sorry to see your ladyship not looking quite so well to-day. We can’t always remain young, and law! how we do change as we grow old! Go up and kiss that lady, Ezzy. She has got a little boy too. Why, bless us! have you got the child downstairs?”

Indeed Master Miles was down below—for special reasons—sometimes accompanying his mother on her visits; and our aunt desired the mother’s company so much that she put up with the child.

“So you have got the child here? Oh, you slyboots!” says the Countess. “Guess you come after the old lady’s money! Law bless you! Don’t look so frightened. She can’t hear a single word I say. Come, Ezzy. Good-bye, aunt!” And my lady rustled out.

Did Aunt Bernstein hear her or not? Where was the wit for which the old lady had been long famous? Was that fire put out as well as the brilliancy of her eyes? With other people she was still ready enough, unsparing of sarcasm. When the Dowager of Castlewood and Lady Fanny visited her (those exalted ladies treated my wife with perfect indifference and charming good breeding), the Baroness in their society was stately, easy, even commanding. She would mischievously caress Mrs. Warrington before them; in her absence, vaunt my wife’s good breeding; say I had made a foolish match perhaps, but certainly taken a charming wife. “In a word, I praise you so to them, my dear,” says she, “that I think they would like to tear your eyes out.”

But before the little American, ’tis certain, she trembled. She was so afraid that she did not dare deny her door; and with the Countess’s back turned, did not even abuse her.

However much they might dislike her, my ladies did not tear out Theo’s eyes. Once they drove to our cottage at Lambeth, where my wife happened to be sitting at the open window holding her child on her knee, full in view. A gigantic footman strutted through our little garden and delivered their ladyships’ visiting tickets. Their hatred hurt us no more than their visit pleased us. When next we had the loan of the brewer’s carriage, Mrs. Warrington drove to Kensington, and Gumbo handed over to the giant our cards in return for those his noble mistresses had bestowed.

The Baroness had a coach, but seldom thought of giving it to us; and would let Theo, her maid, and baby start from Clarges Street in the rain, with a faint excuse that she was afraid to ask her coachman to take out the horses. Twice on her return my wife was frightened by rude fellows on the other side of Westminster Bridge; and I told my aunt plainly I should forbid Mrs. Warrington to go to her unless she could be brought home in safety; so grumbling Jehu had to drive through the darkness. He grumbled at my shillings; he did not know how few I had. Our poverty wore a decent face. My relatives never thought of relieving it, nor I of complaining before them.

I don’t know how Sampson got a windfall of guineas; but I remember he brought me six once, and they were more welcome than any money I ever had. He had been looking into Mr. Miles’s crib as the child lay asleep—breath making its small soft sound, like a moth’s wing against candlelight—and when the parson went away, I found the money in the baby’s little rosy hand. Yes: Love is best of all. I have many such benefactions registered in my heart—welcome fountains springing in desert places, kind lights in a smoky London dusk, small human mercies that made the city’s harsh clang and rattle bearable.

This worthy divine was willing enough to give as much of his company as Madame de Bernstein chose, whether for cards or theology. Having known her ladyship many years, Sampson could see—and averred to us—that she was breaking fast; and as he spoke of her increasing infirmities, and the probability of their fatal termination, Mr. S. would discourse in a feeling manner of the necessity of preparing for a future world; the vanities of this; and the hope that in another there might be happiness for all repentant sinners.

“I have been a sinner for one,” says the chaplain, bowing his head. “God knoweth, and I pray Him to pardon me. I fear, sir, your aunt, the Lady Baroness, is not in such a state of mind as will fit her very well for the change which is imminent. I am but a poor weak wretch, and no prisoner in Newgate could confess that more humbly and heartily. Once or twice of late, I have sought to speak on this matter with her ladyship, but she has received me very roughly. ‘Parson,’ says she, ‘if you come for cards, ’tis mighty well, but I will thank you to spare me your sermons.’ What can I do, sir? I have called more than once of late, and Mr. Case hath told me his lady was unable to see me.”

In fact Madame Bernstein told my wife—whom she never refused—that the poor chaplain’s tone was unendurable; and as for his theology, “Haven’t I been a Bishop’s wife?” says she. “And do I want this creature to teach me?”
