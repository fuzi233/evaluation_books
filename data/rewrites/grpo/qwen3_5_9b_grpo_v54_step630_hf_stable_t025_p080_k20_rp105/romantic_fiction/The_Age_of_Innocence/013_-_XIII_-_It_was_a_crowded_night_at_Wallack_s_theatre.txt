It was a crowded night at Wallack's theatre, the air thick with the scent of lilies and the rustle of silk. The stage was alive with "The Shaughraun," a production that had swept the city, featuring the formidable Dion Boucicault and the beloved English pair, Harry Montague and Ada Dyas. While the gallery audiences roared with unbridled enthusiasm for every hackneyed sentiment and clap-trap situation, those seated in the plush boxes offered only polite smiles, finding delight in the familiar rhythms of the play.

Yet, there was one moment that held the entire house in a suspended breath. It was the final scene: Harry Montague, having delivered a parting so sad and monosyllabic that it seemed to drain the color from the room, turned to leave. Miss Dyas stood near the mantelpiece, her tall figure draped in a simple gray cashmere dress devoid of fashionable trimmings, flowing elegantly to the floor. A narrow black velvet ribbon framed her neck, its ends trailing down her back. As her wooer stepped away, she leaned her arms against the shelf, burying her face in her hands. On the threshold, Montague paused, stealing a glance back. He reached out, lifted one end of the velvet ribbon, pressed a kiss to it, and vanished into the darkness without a sound. The curtain fell on this silent, aching goodbye.

For Newland Archer, this scene was the sole reason for his attendance. He found in Montague and Dyas's reticent sorrow a depth that surpassed even the finest performances he had witnessed in Paris or London. That evening, however, the scene carried a weight far heavier than usual. It triggered a memory he could not quite name: his own leave-taking from Madame Olenska, a conversation from a week prior that had left him unsettled.

The parallels between the two situations were as elusive as the resemblance between the actors and the people involved. Archer was no romantic hero like Montague, nor was Miss Dyas the vivid, striking Ellen Olenska. Furthermore, they were not lovers parting in heartbreak; they were client and lawyer separating after a discussion that had painted a grim picture of the client's precarious position. So, what stirred the young man's heart with such retrospective excitement? It was Madame Olenska's unique ability to suggest tragic possibilities that lay beyond the mundane flow of daily life. She possessed a mysterious quality, perhaps rooted in her exotic background, that made her seem inherently dramatic. Archer had always believed that fate played a smaller role than innate tendency in shaping lives, and he saw this clearly in her. She appeared to be a woman destined for drama, someone to whom things would inevitably happen regardless of her efforts to avoid them. The startling fact was her lack of surprise; she seemed plucked from a maelstrom, taking for granted the very storms she had once rebelled against.

Leaving her that afternoon, Archer had been convinced that Count Olenski's accusations were justified. The mysterious "secretary" from her past had likely been rewarded for his role in her escape. The conditions she had fled were intolerable; she was young, frightened, and desperate. Naturally, she would be grateful to her rescuer. The tragedy, as Archer had explained to her, was that in the eyes of the law and society, her gratitude placed her on equal footing with her abominable husband. He had made this clear, as he was bound to do, and had also shown her that simple-hearted New York was the least forgiving place for such a situation.

Witnessing her resigned acceptance had been excruciating. He felt drawn to her by a tangled mix of jealousy and pity, as if her confessed error had placed her at his mercy, simultaneously humbling and endearing her. He was relieved she had confided in him rather than facing the cold scrutiny of Mr. Letterblair or the awkward gazes of her family. Immediately, he took it upon himself to assure them that she had abandoned her pursuit of a divorce, basing her decision on the futility of the legal battle. With immense relief, the family turned their eyes away from the "unpleasantness" she had spared them.

"I was sure Newland would manage it," Mrs. Welland had said proudly of her future son-in-law. Old Mrs. Mingott, summoning him for a private interview, had congratulated him on his cleverness, adding impatiently, "Silly goose! I told her myself what nonsense it was. Wanting to pass herself off as Ellen Mingott and an old maid, when she has the luck to be a married woman and a Countess!"

These interactions had rendered the memory of his last talk with Madame Olenska so vivid that as the curtain fell, Archer's eyes filled with tears, and he rose to leave the theatre.

Turning toward the side of the house, he spotted the lady occupying his thoughts seated in a box with the Beauforts, Lawrence Lefferts, and a few others. He had avoided being alone with her since their evening together, yet now their eyes met. As Mrs. Beaufort recognized him and made her languid gesture of invitation, there was no choice but to enter the box.

Beaufort and Lefferts made way for him. After exchanging a few words with Mrs. Beaufort, who preferred to look beautiful rather than speak, Archer settled behind Madame Olenska. The only other occupant was Mr. Sillerton Jackson, whispering confidentially to Mrs. Beaufort about Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's recent reception. Under the cover of this narrative, Madame Olenska turned and spoke in a low voice.

"Do you think," she asked, glancing toward the stage, "he will send her a bunch of yellow roses tomorrow morning?"

Archer flushed, his heart leaping with surprise. He had visited her only twice, sending a box of yellow roses each time without a card. She had never acknowledged the sender, assuming perhaps she didn't know. Now, her sudden recognition of the gift, linking it to the tender farewell on stage, filled him with an agitated pleasure.

"I was thinking of that too," he admitted. "I was going to leave the theatre to take the picture away with me."

To his surprise, her cheeks flushed, reluctantly and dusky. She looked down at the mother-of-pearl opera-glass in her gloved hands and, after a pause, asked, "What do you do while May is away?"

"I stick to my work," he replied, faintly annoyed by the question.

Indeed, the Wellands had departed the previous week for St. Augustine, a tradition born of Mr. Welland's bronchial susceptibility. He was a mild, silent man with many habits and no opinions, requiring his wife and daughter to accompany him to preserve the domestic order essential to his peace of mind. His sons, bound by their winter practices, joined him for Easter, but Archer could not discuss the necessity of May's departure. The Mingott physician's reputation relied on the fact that Mr. Welland had never suffered pneumonia, making his insistence on the southern trip inflexible. Archer would have loved to join the travelers for sunshine and boating with his betrothed, but custom and convention bound him. To suggest a holiday in mid-winter would be deemed frivolous by the entire Mingott clan, so he accepted May's departure with the resignation he knew was necessary for married life.

He was aware that Madame Olenska watched him through lowered lids. "I have done what you wished—what you advised," she said abruptly.

"Ah—I'm glad," he returned, embarrassed by her bringing up the subject at such a moment.

"I understand—that you were right," she continued, breathless. "But sometimes life is difficult... perplexing..."

"I know."

"And I wanted to tell you that I *do* feel you were right; and that I'm grateful to you," she concluded, lifting her opera-glass quickly to her eyes just as the box door opened and Beaufort's resonant voice interrupted them.

Archer stood, left the box, and exited the theatre.

Only the day before, he had received a letter from May Welland, written with characteristic candor. She asked him to "be kind to Ellen" in their absence, noting that she liked and admired him, though she remained lonely and unhappy. May wrote that her grandmother and uncle Lovell Mingott misunderstood her, thinking her worldly and fond of society, whereas May saw that New York seemed dull to her, accustomed to wonders like great music, picture shows, and celebrities. "I think she's been used to lots of things we haven't got," May had written. "And I can quite see that New York must seem dull to her... but I can see that you're almost the only person in New York who can talk to her about what she really cares for."

His wise May—he had loved her for that letter! Yet, he had not meant to act on it. He was too busy, and as an engaged man, he did not wish to play too conspicuously the part of Madame Olenska's champion. He suspected she knew how to care for herself better than the ingenuous May imagined. She had Beaufort at her feet, Mr. van der Luyden hovering protectively, and candidates like Lawrence Lefferts waiting in the wings. Still, every time he saw her or spoke with her, he felt that May's innocence was almost a gift of divination. Ellen Olenska was lonely, and she was unhappy.
