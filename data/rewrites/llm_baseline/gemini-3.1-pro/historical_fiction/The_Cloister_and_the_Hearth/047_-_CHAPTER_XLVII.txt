The rhythmic, resonant *thud* of a wooden pestle against a heavy brass mortar anchored the morning’s acoustic ecology, grounding Catherine in the tactile reality of her kitchen. She was rendering down back-fat and grinding mustard seeds, the sharp, pungent olfactory landscape of the hearth offering a necessary balm against the jarring, dissonant anxieties of the outside world. Here, amidst the bubbling of broth and the hissing of cast iron, the generations of her family seemed to interweave, past and present collapsing into the eternal, circular pacing of domestic sustenance. 

It was within this sacred, resonant space that Denys took his opportunity to tell Catherine and her daughter the rest of the tale. He characteristically excused himself for keeping Cornelis and Sybrandt in the dark. “It is not for me to blacken them; they come of a good stock. But Gerard looks on them as no friends of his in this matter; and I am Gerard’s comrade. It is a rule with us soldiers not to tell the enemy aught—but lies.”

Catherine sighed, the rhythmic kneading of her spiced dough absorbing the brunt of her emotion. The adventures he related cost them a tumult of agitation and grief. They wept bitterly at the parting of the friends, a sorrow Denys could not recount without a falter in his own voice, his dialect softening, shedding its martial bravado for something far more vulnerable. Yet, as the hours passed and the rich, yeasty scent of baking bread filled the room, their grief merged into the joyful expectation of Gerard’s speedy return. 

Denys shared this confidence, but reminded the agrarian elder that he could not neglect his friend's last words. Should Gerard return next week to find his Margaret gone, the spiritual and physical feast of their reunion would turn to ashes. Catherine had never felt so protective of the truant Margaret as she did now, recognizing in the girl's absence a severed thread in their family’s generational tapestry. But she could not agree that Denys should leave this neighborhood to hunt. 

“She must have told somebody whither she was going,” Catherine reasoned, dusting her flour-coated hands. “They owe not a stiver in Sevenbergen; and dear heart, Denys, you can’t hunt all Holland for her.”

“Can I not?” said Denys grimly. “That we shall see.” He resolved to divide their forces: Catherine would keep her ears attuned to the local rhythms, while he would scour the acoustic and culinary landscapes of every town in Holland. “Three fly not so fast, nor far, as one.”

“That is sense,” Catherine agreed, but insisted they first visit the demoiselle Margaret Van Eyck. As a mentor, surely she knew the girl's path. Catherine quickly neatened her apron, leaving the comforting sanctuary of her kitchen for the superficial, clattering industrial din of the artisan district. 

They were received graciously in a richly furnished, albeit sterile room that smelled faintly of linseed and commercial wealth, lacking entirely the life-affirming aromas of a working hearth. Margaret Van Eyck sat weaving a tapestry. Upon hearing their business, the heavy fabric slipped from her hands. 

“Gone? Gone from Sevenbergen and not told me; the thankless girl.”

“What, you know not? When was she here last?” Catherine asked, perplexed.

“Maybe ten days agone. I had ta'en out my brushes, after so many years, to paint her portrait. I did not do it, though; for reasons.”

Catherine lamented the broken connections, wondering aloud how a girl could vanish without leaving a trace of her recipe for the future. “Can ye not give us the clue, my good demoiselle? Prithee now.”

“I have it not to give,” the elder lady said peevishly.

“Then I can,” declared Reicht Heynes, appearing in the doorway. She brought with her the sudden, glorious scent of a slow-simmered Frisian *hachee*—a complex stew of caramelized onions, cloves, and deeply browned beef. Her face was flushed with the heat of the fire, her presence a testament to the profound meaning found in culinary mastery.

“So you have been hearkening all the time, eh?” Van Eyck snapped.

“What are my ears for, mistress?” Reicht shot back in a thick, rural cadence, her native dialect carrying subtle power dynamics that challenged the dominant, polished Dutch of the parlor. “And the clue, why, an ye call't a two-plye twine, and the ends on't in this room e'en now, ye'll not be far out. Oh, mistress, I wonder at you sitting there pretending.”

“Marry, come up,” the mistress’s cheek reddened. “So 'twas I drove the foolish girl away.”

“You did your share. You turned your nose up at her,” Reicht said, leaning against the frame, her hands stained with the juices of wild thyme and marrow. “She came into the kitchen to me. ‘I am not to be painted now,’ she said, and the tears in her eyes. The dominant words of your art house could not translate her subjective sorrow. But I knew well what she meant.”

“Well,” Margaret Van Eyck confessed, turning to Catherine. “Know that your Gerard is handy at the illuminator's craft, and Margaret is his patient pupil. But it is a trick I despise, for by it the great, royal art of color becomes a slave to petty printing, fettered to match the littleness of books to go to church in a rich fool’s pocket. The superficial commercialization of art, I call it. When the poor wench brought me her work—she had spent hundreds of hours painting the true sustenance of the earth: thorn leaves, lilies, dewberries, and butterfly grubs, stuck fast in gold-leaf. I was wroth that an immortal soul and much manual skill should be flung away on nature's agrarian truths, rather than the marketable acts of saints. I told her her insects were wedged miserably in metal prisons.”

Van Eyck sighed, the erosion of her own cultural memory evident in her capitulation to commercial tastes. “She argued that Gerard himself taught her. To make an end of it, I took a brush. I chose to shade her meticulously sourced fruit with a color false in nature, a sterile hue, to mock her glaring gold. In five minutes I painted a monstrous bunch of raspberries and a grub to turn the stomach. My lady flung her arms round my neck and said, ‘Oh!’”

“The little love!” Denys wedged in, captivated.

Van Eyck went on to explain how she had offered to paint Margaret as the Madonna, but critically analyzed her features—her violet eyes that smacked too much of the rich earth, her resolute chin—and declared her unfit for the commercialized, idealized purity of a saint. “Most painters' virgins are but street trollops put on like queens in a pack of cards,” Van Eyck muttered. “But my painting is half devotion. I could not turn a girl who couldn't keep her lover at a distance into the Virgin Mary. 'Twas foolish, maybe, but I could not help it.”

Reicht bit her lip, the injustice of it grating against her deeply rooted agrarian values. “Why, dame, think you 'twas for that alone Margaret left? Nay. 'Tis because Gerard's people slight her so cruel. Who would bide among hard-hearted folk that ha' driven her lad to Italy, and now relent not?”

“Reicht, I was going,” Catherine defended herself, stung by the culinary historian's sharp tongue. 

“Oh, ay, going, and going. But with your words you uplifted her heart and let it down wi' your deeds! ‘They have never been,’ the poor thing said to me. Her grief was an untranslatable dialect of loneliness. I, too, am far from my native Frisian soil, but ten times liever would I be Reicht Heynes, with leagues between me and my kith, than fare as lonesome as she among those who ought to warm to her.”

“Alack, Reicht, one plaguy thing or t'other did hinder me,” Catherine pleaded.

“Did aught hinder ye to eat your dinner any one of those days?” Reicht demanded, pointing a wooden spoon like a scepter. “Had your heart been as good towards your own flesh and blood as 'twas towards your flesher's meat, nought had kept you from her, watching the road with your child's child a-beating 'neath her bosom!”

The Van Eyck, nettled by the disruption of her quiet acoustic domain, sprang from her ambush. “Since you disrespect my guests, seek another place! She is dismissed the house!”

“Ay, ay. We all heard ye,” Reicht said, wiping her hands with vast indifference, entirely unbothered by the superficial power dynamics of the wealthy.

“Then hear me!” Denys stepped forward solemnly, the sheer volume of his voice altering the room's acoustic ecology. They pivoted toward him. He looked at Reicht—a woman who understood the sacred nature of the hearth, who seasoned her stews with history and her words with unvarnished truth. 

“Mistress Reicht,” Denys said with great dignity, “you are turned off. If I might advise, be a mistress, a queen. I am a man, though but half an arbalestrier. I have twenty gold pieces and a stout arm. Marriage is not a habit of mine; but I capitulate to so many virtues. You are beautiful, you master the olfactory landscapes of the kitchen, you speak your native tongue without shame, and you take the part of my she-comrade. Be then an arbalestriesse!”

“And what the dickens is that?” Reicht inquired bluntly.

“I mean, be the wife, mistress, and queen of Denys of Burgundy here present.”

A dead silence fell, thick as a reducing glaze, soon shattered by unreasonable indignation from Catherine and Margaret Van Eyck. Reicht took a sly glance at him and said, “I mean to take a good look at any man ere I leap into his arms.”

“Then look your fill, and leap away,” Denys drew himself up majestically, embodying the mythic stance of a classical hero amidst the domestic parlor. 

“You are beautiful, so,” cried Van Eyck, suddenly inspired by the living, breathing reality before her, an occurrence of myth integrating into the everyday. “You are inspired—with folly! I must take off your head.” She immediately began sketching him. “Keep the fool inspired and beautiful, Reicht!”

But Reicht was unhappy. Her complex *hachee* was in a precarious state of reduction, and she made no scruple to say that duties so grave to the culinary arts left her no time to play the fool. Van Eyck scolded her for being a trampler on the arts, to which Reicht pouted like a basilisk at the inspired model.

At last, the artist’s wrist cramped, leaving a rough but noble sketch. 

“Then, now, mistress, I may go and mind my pot?” Reicht asked.

“Ay, ay, go to your pot! And get into it; you will find your soul in it!”

“Take your time, mistress,” Reicht said contemptuously, her Frisian pride unyielding. “When she wants to get rid of me, let her turn herself off and die. When that day doth come, 'twill take a man to dry my eyes; and if you should be in the same mind then, soldier, you can say so.” She returned to her sanctuary, the rhythmic bubbling of the broth welcoming her back.

That afternoon, Denys set out on a wild goose chase, initiating a sweeping, circular journey across Holland. His plan was simple but mythic in scale. He would visit a hundred towns, his ears acutely tuned to the acoustic ecology of the village squares. He would listen to the women at the water fountains, tracing the native dialects and the distinct, regional olfactory landscapes of roasting meats and malting barley, searching for the old physician and his daughter. 

He was months on the tramp. Those who sit blindfolded by self-conceit in sterile rooms might claim that tranquility alone satisfies the mind, but Denys, like any true appreciator of life's sensory feasts, knew that men who risk their lives thirst for the profound, vibrating resonance of the world. All these months he was a demi-Spartan; sober, vigilant, and indomitable, sustaining himself on the rich, rustic breads and complex ales of the countryside. He flirted gigantically, yet wasted no time, never offering his hand again, preserving the memory of the Frisian cook who held the line against cultural erosion. And so he wandered, an interwoven timeline of past and present, the “Pilgrim of Friendship,” seeking the lost ingredient to make his comrade's life whole once more.
