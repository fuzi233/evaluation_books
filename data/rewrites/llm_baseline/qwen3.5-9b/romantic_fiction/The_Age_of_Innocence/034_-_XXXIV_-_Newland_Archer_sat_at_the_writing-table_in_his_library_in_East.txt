Newland Archer sat at the writing-desk in his library on East Thirty-Ninth Street.

He had just returned from a grand official reception celebrating the opening of the new galleries at the Metropolitan Museum. The sight of those vast halls, teeming with the spoils of centuries, where fashionable crowds drifted through rows of scientifically cataloged treasures, had suddenly triggered a dormant spring in his memory.

"Why, this used to be one of the old Cesnola rooms," someone had remarked nearby. Instantly, the present dissolved. He found himself seated alone on a stiff leather divan against a radiator, watching a slender figure in a long sealskin coat walk away down the sparsely furnished corridor of the old museum.

That vision unleashed a flood of other associations. He stared anew at the library, a room that for over thirty years had been the theater of his solitary reflections and all his family gatherings. It was the sanctuary where the true events of his life unfolded. Here, nearly twenty-six years prior, his wife had broken the news of her pregnancy to him—a blush-inducing circumlocution that would have amused the women of the younger generation. Here, their eldest son, Dallas—too frail for winter church services—had been christened by their old friend, the Bishop of New York, the ample, magnificent, and irreplaceable clergyman who had long been the pride of his diocese. Here Dallas had first stumbled across the floor shouting "Dad," while May and the nurse laughed behind the door; here their second child, Mary—who bore her mother's likeness—had announced her engagement to the most dull and dependable of Reggie Chivers's many sons; and here Archer had kissed her through her wedding veil before descending to the motor carriage bound for Grace Church—for in a world where everything else swayed on unstable foundations, the "Grace Church wedding" remained an immutable institution.

It was in this library that he and May had always debated the future of their children: Dallas's and his younger brother Bill's studies, Mary's incurable disdain for "accomplishments" and her fervor for sports and charity, and the vague inclination toward "art" that eventually guided the restless, curious Dallas into the office of a rising New York architect.

Young men today were liberating themselves from the rigid paths of law and commerce, embracing a myriad of new pursuits. Unless absorbed in state politics or municipal reform, they were likely venturing into Central American archaeology, architecture, or landscape engineering; developing a keen, scholarly interest in the pre-revolutionary structures of their own nation, studying and adapting Georgian styles, and protesting the meaningless application of the term "Colonial." Nowadays, only suburban millionaire grocers possessed "Colonial" homes.

But above all—and sometimes Archer placed it above all—it was in that library that the Governor of New York, descending from Albany one evening to dine and stay overnight, had turned to his host, slamming his clenched fist upon the table and grinding his eyeglasses. "Hang the professional politician!" he had roared. "You are the kind of man the country needs, Archer. If the stable is ever to be cleaned out, men like you must lend a hand in the scrubbing."

"Men like you..." How Archer had burned with pride at those words! How eagerly he had risen to the call! It echoed Ned Winsett's ancient plea to roll up his sleeves and dive into the mud; but spoken by a man who set the example, whose summons to follow was irresistible.

Looking back, Archer was uncertain whether men like himself truly constituted what his country needed, particularly in the active service Theodore Roosevelt had championed. Indeed, there was cause for doubt; after a single term in the State Assembly, he had failed to secure re-election and retreated gratefully into obscure, albeit useful, municipal work, and thence to writing occasional articles for one of the reformist weeklies striving to shake the nation from its apathy. It was little to boast of; yet, remembering the narrow groove of money-making, sport, and society to which the young men of his generation and circle had looked forward, even his modest contribution to this new era seemed significant, like each brick in a well-constructed wall. He had achieved little in public life; by nature, he would remain a contemplator and a dilettante. Yet he had contemplated high things, delighted in great things, and possessed one great man's friendship as his strength and pride.

In short, he had become what people were beginning to term "a good citizen." In New York, for many years, every new movement—philanthropic, municipal, or artistic—had sought his counsel and desired his name attached. People said, "Ask Archer," when questions arose regarding the establishment of the first school for crippled children, the reorganization of the Museum of Art, the founding of the Grolier Club, the inauguration of the new Library, or the creation of a new chamber music society. His days were full, filled decently. He presumed this was all a man ought to aspire to.

Yet, he knew he had missed something: the flower of life. However, he viewed it now as so unattainable and improbable that to lament it would be akin to despairing because one had not drawn the first prize in a lottery. There were a hundred million tickets in *his* lottery, and only one prize; the odds had been decisively stacked against him. When he thought of Ellen Olenska, he did so abstractly, serenely, as one might regard an imaginary beloved in a book or painting: she had become the composite vision of everything he had missed. That faint, tenuous image had prevented him from entertaining thoughts of other women. He had been what was termed a faithful husband; and when May had suddenly passed away—swept off by the infectious pneumonia through which she had cared for their youngest child—he had genuinely mourned her. Their long years together had taught him that it mattered less if marriage was a dull duty, provided it maintained the dignity of a duty; lapse from that, and it devolved into a mere contest of ugly appetites. Surveying his surroundings, he honored his own past and mourned for it. After all, there was goodness in the old ways.

His eyes swept the room—refurbished by Dallas with English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, fragments of selected blue-and-white porcelain, and softly shaded electric lamps—before returning to the old Eastlake writing-desk he had never been willing to discard, and to his first photograph of May, which still held its place beside his inkwell.

There she was: tall, round-bosomed, and willowy, clad in starched muslin and a flapping Leghorn hat, just as he had seen her beneath the orange trees in the Mission garden. As he had seen her that day, so she had remained: never quite at the exact same height, yet never far below it; generous, faithful, tireless. Yet so lacking in imagination, so incapable of growth, that the world of her youth had crumbled and reconstructed itself without her ever perceiving the shift. This hard, bright blindness had kept her immediate horizon seemingly unchanged. Her inability to recognize change compelled her children to conceal their views from her, just as Archer concealed his; from the outset, there existed a joint pretense of sameness, a sort of innocent family hypocrisy in which father and children had unconsciously colluded. She died believing the world to be a good place, filled with loving, harmonious households like her own, resigned to depart because she was convinced that, regardless of what transpired, Newland would continue to instill in Dallas the same principles and prejudices that had shaped his parents' lives, and that Dallas, in turn (following her lead), would transmit this sacred trust to little Bill. Regarding Mary, she was as certain as she was of herself. Thus, having rescued little Bill from the grave and dedicated her life to the effort, she departed contentedly to her resting place in the Archer vault at St. Mark's, where Mrs. Archer already lay safely beyond the terrifying "trend" her daughter-in-law had never even perceived.

Opposite May's portrait stood one of her daughter. Mary Chivers was as tall and fair as her mother, yet large-waisted, flat-chested, and slightly slouching, as the altered fashions demanded. Mary Chivers's monumental athletic feats could not have been executed with the twenty-inch waist that May Archer's azure sash so effortlessly encircled. The disparity seemed symbolic; the mother's life had been as tightly cinched as her figure. Mary, though no less conventional and no more intelligent, lived a broader life and held more tolerant views. There was goodness in the new order, too.

The telephone clicked, and Archer, turning from the photographs, unhooked the receiver at his side. How distant they were from the days when the legs of the brass-buttoned messenger boy served as New York's sole means of rapid communication!

"Chicago wants you."

Ah—it must be a long-distance call from Dallas, who had been dispatched to Chicago by his firm to discuss plans for the Lakeside palace they intended to build for a young millionaire with ambitious ideas. The firm invariably sent Dallas on such missions.

"Hallo, Dad—Yes: Dallas. I say—how do you feel about sailing on Wednesday? Mauretania: Yes, next Wednesday as ever is. Our client wishes me to inspect some Italian gardens before finalizing anything, and has requested I nip over on the next boat. I must be back by the first of June—" the voice fractured into a joyful, self-conscious laugh— "so we must look lively. I say, Dad, I need your help: please come."
