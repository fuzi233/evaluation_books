Captain Shandon, being urged—gently, firmly, and with that quiet domestic omnipotence which belongs to wives who “never meddle”—had made one stipulation with the new Pall Mall Gazette: that John Finucane, Esquire, of the Upper Temple, should be named sub-editor. The spirited proprietor assented; for if gratitude can ever be entered in a publisher’s ledger without being immediately crossed out as “bad debt,” it was due here.

Finucane had earned every farthing of favour by services rendered in those old romantic days when Captain Shandon, hunted by bailiffs as a hare is by hounds, used to take refuge in Fin’s chambers. So often had the Captain been “not at home” there, that at last the sheriff’s officers grew social upon the staircase, calling as regularly at the Temple as at Shandon’s own door, as if Fin’s landing had been a club and they members.

And it was to Finucane’s rooms, too, that poor Mrs. Shandon came—slipping in with her veil down, as if modesty were a disguise and sorrow a crime—bringing her griefs, her stratagems, her little child; bringing, in short, the whole kitchen of misfortune, where there is always a pot boiling and never anything to put in it. Finucane would furnish meals there—honest, hot, consolatory things: a chop that hissed on the plate like a friendly rebuke to despair; bread whose very crust seemed to say, “Courage!”; and ale that made a small brave weather in the stomach. Little Mary, installed in her crib, would say her prayers at bedtime—“God bless papa”—and Finucane, though Romish, would add an “Amen” so feeling that it might have passed for a second petition.

On the evening before all our great events, however, Fin had an appointment with Mr. Bungay upon the affairs of the new paper—an appointment which was to be discussed, as such affairs always are, over a quiet dinner; quiet, that is, until the brandy-and-water begins to speak its mind. Fin went away from Mrs. Shandon at six, but next morning he appeared at the Fleet as punctual as the turnkey’s keys, arrayed in his best bravery: cheap splendours, loud as a drum, bright as a trumpet—ornaments that did not so much glitter as announce themselves. In his pocket he carried four pounds two shillings—his week’s salary from the Daily Journal, less two shillings that had melted away, like a sacrificial offering, in the purchase of a pair of gloves on the road to the prison.

The dinner had been at Dick’s Coffee-house, with Bungay and Mr. Trotter, Bungay’s reader and literary man of business. Finucane there “cut his mutton,” as Bungay called it; and it must be owned the mutton was cut with the air of a man carving destiny. He unfolded, with a pencil that flew like a swallow, the whole anatomy of the Pall Mall Gazette: the precise type for each kind of wisdom; who should chronicle the markets and weigh butter as solemnly as empire; who should attend the turf and ring and make vice look like sport; who should report Church intelligence with suitable gravity; and who should sift fashionable chit-chat like flour, leaving nothing but the finest, whitest nonsense for the public table.

He knew the weekly earnings of every man in the Press as a cook knows the price of every joint; he was up to a thousand economical contrivances by which a spirited capitalist might save money without appearing to do so; he set down figures that danced and coupled into totals before Bungay’s slow comprehension could seize them. Bungay, dazzled and mystified in equal parts, afterwards confided to Trotter that “that Irishman seemed a clever fellow.”

Having thus roasted Bungay in admiration and basted him with wonder, Finucane worked round to the dish nearest his heart: the liberation of Captain Shandon. He knew, to a shilling, the detainers at the porter’s lodge of the Fleet; he professed to know all Shandon’s debts—an impossible claim, for no man in England, certainly not the Captain himself, was acquainted with them. Fin represented, with a tenderness which might have softened a stone—and did soften something much harder, namely a publisher—that Shandon would work better out of confinement.

“Begging your pardon,” Bungay had said, with that stubborn common-sense which is the genius of dull men, “when the Captain’s locked up we are sure to find him at home; whereas, when he’s free, you can never catch hold of him.”

But Fin played his trumps: Mrs. Shandon pining in the prison; the child sickening there; the poor lady’s life, as it were, reduced to the prison’s daily gruel—thin, grey, and always ladled out at the wrong hour. Bungay, whose soul could be reached by a proper mixture of sentiment and spirits, promised that if Mrs. Shandon would come to him in the morning he would see what could be done. And when the reckoning came—Finucane, with four guineas in his pocket, longing to discharge it in a burst of gratitude—Bungay, warmed into magnificence, struck in: “No, sir,—this is my affair, sir, if you please. James, take the bill, and eighteenpence for yourself,” and thus paid, like a man who enjoys being virtuous on other people’s opportunities.

So Fin went to bed in the Temple that night with his week’s salary intact; and on Saturday morning at the Fleet, he gave Mrs. Shandon a wink so knowing and so jubilant that it was like the pop of a cork to a thirsty heart. She, good soul, guessed the bottle held hope, and hastened for bonnet and shawl when Fin begged the honour of walking her out for a breath of fresh air.

Little Mary jumped at the word “holiday.” Finucane never came empty-handed to that child: a toy, a ticket, a promise of some London marvel—all his pockets were lined with diversions, as if he carried a sweet-shop in one waistcoat and a theatre in the other. Mrs. Shandon hesitated, looking back to the Captain.

“May I go, Charley? or shall I stay with you, for you’re poorly, dear, this morning? He’s got a headache, Mr. Finucane. He suffers from headaches, and I persuaded him to stay in bed.”

“Go along with you, and Polly. Jack, take care of ’em. Hand me over Burton’s Anatomy, and leave me to my abominable devices,” Shandon said, with perfect good-humour; and indeed he lay there mining quotations—Greek and Latin ready-dressed for print—from that wonderful repertory of learning, as a cook might pluck herbs from a well-stocked shelf.

So Fin gave his arm to Mrs. Shandon. Mary skipped down the prison passages and out through the gate into the free air, which tasted, after the Fleet, like bread fresh from the oven. Fleet Street roared as it always does—wheels, cries, the metallic clack of commerce. Yet even that din, common as a kettle’s boil to Londoners, struck Mrs. Shandon with the strange relief of honest noise: not the locked, echoing hush of stone walls and keys, but the open clamour of people living.

From Fleet Street to Paternoster Row is but a short pilgrimage. As they reached Mr. Bungay’s premises, Mrs. Bungay herself was entering by the private door, carrying a brown-paper parcel and a manuscript volume bound in red—her accounts with the butcher, set down with the seriousness of a family Bible. It was a domestic chronicle of chops and suet, of shoulders and sirloins—history written in dripping.

Mrs. Bungay’s dress flashed with shot-silk red and purple; she wore a yellow shawl, red flowers in her bonnet, and carried a parasol of brilliant light blue—colours so loud one might have expected them to make a noise of their own. Mrs. Shandon was in old black watered silk; her bonnet had known no bright prosperity, yet she could not help looking like a lady, as some women cannot help breathing. They curtseyed, each in her manner: one with the weight of property, the other with the grace of breeding.

“I hope you’re pretty well, mum?” said Mrs. Bungay.

“It’s a very fine day,” said Mrs. Shandon.

“Won’t you step in, mum?” said Mrs. Bungay—staring at little Mary with such tragic intensity that the child half fancied herself about to be weighed and priced like a goose.

“I—I came about business with Mr. Bungay—I—I hope he’s pretty well?” Mrs. Shandon faltered.

“If you go to see him in the counting-house, couldn’t you—couldn’t you leave your little gurl with me?” Mrs. Bungay asked, in a deep voice, holding out one finger towards the child as if summoning a reluctant spirit.

“I want to stay with mamma,” cried Mary, burying her face in her mother’s dress.

“Go with this lady, Mary, my dear,” said the mother, gently forcing courage into her own voice.

“I’ll show you some pretty pictures,” Mrs. Bungay said, with the tone of an ogress bribing a captive, “and some nice things besides; look here”—and she opened her parcel to reveal sweet biscuits of the choicest sort, those rich little rounds of butter and spice which Bungay loved after his wine, crisp as new paper and perfumed like a bakery door at dawn.

The biscuits worked magic. Mary, who understood the theology of sugar better than most doctors understand the human heart, followed at once; and the party entered by the private way, a side door leading into Bungay’s commercial apartments. Yet at the moment of parting from her mother, Mary’s courage failed again; she flew back to the maternal skirt. Mrs. Shandon, seeing disappointment settle on Mrs. Bungay’s face like a lid on a pot, kindly said, “If you will let me, I will come up too, and sit for a few minutes,” and so the three females ascended together. A second biscuit established full confidence; and presently Mary prattled as freely as if the house were her own.

Meanwhile, faithful Finucane found Mr. Bungay in a far severer mood than on the previous evening. Port and brandy had then warmed Bungay into that benevolence which is the tipsy cousin of virtue; but morning, that cold water thrown upon every generous impulse, had brought him back to calculation. Mrs. Bungay had rebuked him at home. He was to give no relief to Captain Shandon: a good-for-nothing fellow, whom no money would help. She disapproved of the Pall Mall Gazette altogether, and prophesied Bungay would lose money in it as they were losing “over the way” (she always called her brother’s establishment by that phrase, as if even naming it would make the loss more contagious) at the Whitehall Journal. Let Shandon stop in prison and do his work; it was the best place for him.

Finucane pleaded, promised, implored. Bungay—fresh from an hour’s domestic lecture—was inexorable.

But what Fin could not do below-stairs amid ledgers and ink, Mrs. Shandon was doing above amid biscuits and the small, soft noises of a child: the rustle of a frock, the bright click of a biscuit breaking, the quick run of Mary’s talk—little domestic sounds that, in a world of carts and printing-presses, had power to soothe. There was an artless sweetness in Mrs. Shandon’s voice, and a winning frankness of manner, that made people fond of her in spite of themselves. Taking courage from the rugged kindness with which Mrs. Bungay received her, the Captain’s wife told her story: her husband’s goodness and virtues (which she believed in as simply as she believed in bread), the child’s failing health, the misery of that place; how she had been obliged to part with two children and send them to school, because she could not keep them in that horrid prison.

Mrs. Bungay, grim as Lady Macbeth in a bonnet, melted. Domestic sorrow has its own dialect, and though Mrs. Bungay spoke in imperious monosyllables, she understood this language perfectly. “I’ll go down and speak to Bungay,” she said at last.

In that household, Mrs. Bungay’s “speaking” was command; and Bungay’s “hearing” was obedience. She descended upon her spouse with majesty, politely requested Mr. Finucane to step upstairs to his friends in her drawing-room, and when she had Bungay alone, informed him of her intentions.

“What’s in the wind now, my dear?” asked Maecenas, surprised at her altered tone. “You wouldn’t hear of my doing anything for the Captain this morning. I wonder what has been a changing of you.”

“The Capting is an Irishman,” Mrs. Bungay replied; “and those Irish I have always said I couldn’t abide. But his wife is a lady, as any one can see; and a good woman, and a clergyman’s daughter, and a West of England woman, B., which I am myself, by my mother’s side—and, O Marmaduke! didn’t you remark the little gurl?”

“Yes, Mrs. B., I saw the little girl.”

“And didn’t you see how like she was to our angel, Bessy, Mr. B.?” Mrs. Bungay’s thoughts flew back eighteen years—to the little country town where Bacon and Bungay had once been small booksellers, where hopes were cheap and love dear, and where a child named Bessy had lived only long enough to make memory a permanent ache.

“Well, well, my dear,” Bungay said, seeing his wife’s little eyes begin to twinkle and grow red; “the Captain ain’t in for much. There’s only a hundred and thirty pound against him. Half the money will take him out of the Fleet, Finucane says, and we’ll pay him half salaries till he has made the account square. When the little ’un said, ‘Why don’t you take Par out of prizn?’ I did feel it, Flora, upon my honour I did, now.”

So the two of them went upstairs together, and Bungay made a heavy, clumsy speech—like a joint badly trussed, excellent in substance but awkward in presentation—announcing that, as sixty-five pounds would set Captain Shandon free, he was ready to advance that sum, to be deducted from the Captain’s salary; and that he would give it to Mrs. Shandon on condition that she herself would settle with the creditors for her husband’s liberation.

If there is a flavour to happiness, it is not always sugar. Sometimes it is simply the warm, sudden taste of possibility. That day was the happiest Mrs. Shandon and Mr. Finucane had known for a long while.

“Bedad, Bungay, you’re a trump!” roared Fin, his brogue bursting out with his emotion. “Give us your fist, old boy: and won’t we send the Pall Mall Gazette up to ten thousand a week, that’s all!” He jumped about the room, tossed up little Mary, and performed a hundred frantic antics, as if joy were a pan on the fire and he the cook appointed to keep it from boiling over.

“If I could drive you anywhere in my carriage, Mrs. Shandon—I’m sure it’s quite at your service,” Mrs. Bungay said, glancing out at the one-horse vehicle which had just drawn up, and in which she took the air considerably, as if fresh air were a luxury to be administered by wheels.

So the two ladies drove away from Paternoster Row with little Mary between them—Mary’s tiny hand fixed fast in Mrs. Bungay’s great grasp—and Finucane on the back seat, delighted as a man carrying home a new oven and already smelling the bread. Mrs. Bungay threw triumphant glances at the opposite windows at Bacon’s, as though her charity were also a competitive advertisement.

“It won’t do the Captain any good,” Bungay thought, going back to his desk and accounts; “but Mrs. B. becomes reglar upset when she thinks about her misfortune. The child would have been of age yesterday, if she’d lived. Flora told me so;” and he wondered, with that puzzled reverence dull men sometimes feel for women, how they contrive to remember things.

Mrs. Shandon sped very successfully upon her errand. She who had so often mollified creditors with no money at all—only tears and entreaties, those thin broths of persuasion—found no difficulty now in making them relent when she could offer a bribe of ten shillings in the pound. And the next Sunday was the last, for some time at least, which Captain Shandon spent in prison.
