We set out while the dew still clung to every blade, and the plain lay like a table newly laid—blue vault above, green cloth below, our little procession moving between them as if some old household had taken wings and decided the day’s work must be done elsewhere. The plan was as homely as it was warlike: travel through the morning, sleep away the afternoon, then go on again under the cold stars; rest the next day; start again in the brief twilight. Toward the end we would portion the hours more closely, so that the city should meet us at dawn, and we be within its gates before the place had properly rubbed sleep from its eyes.

It was as if the whole forest insisted on coming too, not as an army, but as a migrating pantry and larder—feathers, fur, and flutter, a moving store of life. Birds flew ahead in bright companies, calling to one another as if they meant to announce our approach like town-criers. Butterflies—spilled petals with purpose—played about our heads; wasps and dragonflies stitched the air with their thin, glinting thread. Four-footed things followed behind, padding in and out of the grass like quiet servants. At night most of the ground-creatures dropped away, each to its own den and customary darkness; but the birds and the bright insects kept with us stubbornly, all the way to the city’s very mouth.

We halted and slept through the afternoon. It was our first true march, yet none were tired—these children had bodies trained by wandering and weather, and spirits that took hardship as easily as warm bread. When night came we went faster, for the cold sharpened every step and made even the elephants eager to be done with standing. Many of the Little Ones fell asleep on the backs of their beasts and woke at morning as if they had been laid down in clean straw in some familiar loft. None tumbled. Some rode bears—shaggy, shambling fellows that looked as though they had been built for pondering, not for speed—yet they kept pace well enough, going as fast as the elephants in their long, steady way. Others were on deer, and would have turned the road into a racecourse had I not checked them; for the road was not ours alone, and a stumble in the dark would have broken more than pride.

Those perched atop the hay on the elephants—unable to see the great grey backs below, feeling only the slow sway and the warm animal-breath rising through the straw—kept talking to their mounts as long as they were awake, as if the elephants were hearth-stone kin. Once, when we had halted to feed, I heard a small voice burrowing down through the hay, and the child speaking as one speaks to dough one hopes will rise:

“Nosy dear, I am digging you out of the mountain, and shall soon get down to you: be patient; I’m a coming! Very soon now you’ll send up your nose to look for me, and then we’ll kiss like good elephants, we will!”

It was the kind of talk that makes the world feel older than any written record: the speech of people whose affection is practical, whose love is proven by feeding, brushing, keeping close.

But that same night there burst out a tumult—elephant-trumpeting, horse-neighing, and the children’s bold imitations of both—ringing far over the silent levels. The very air seemed to recoil and throw the sound back at us. It was not the wholesome chorus of a living place—the soft layers of frog, wind, leaf, and distant wing that heal the mind by their variety—but a sudden blare, a crude fanfare like metal struck in a narrow alley. I did not know how near the city might be; and if there is one thing a wicked town lives on, it is hearing. So I hurried along the line, hushed the little throats, stroked muzzles, spoke low, and at last stilled the uproar, lest we give warning of our approach.

Then, suddenly, one morning the sun and the city rose together, as if the same hand lifted them from behind the horizon. To the children the walls looked like a great mass of rock, as if a cliff had been persuaded into a square. But when I told them what waited within—nests of stone, piled close, one on another—their faces altered. Apprehension, then dislike, moved over them like a chill shadow. For the first time in their lives, I believe—many of them long little lives—they knew fear. The place looked wrong to them. How were they to find mothers in such a place? Where, among hard angles and shut doors, could the smell of milk and warm bread live?

Yet they went on bravely, because they trusted Lona—and, to my shame, me as well. We rode through the sounding archway. Never had that old pavement heard such drumming of hoof, such padding of paw and bare foot. The echo did not behave like a meadow, where sound has room to soften into wind; it came back sharp-edged, a hard copy of itself. The horses started at the noise of their own steps; some halted; some plunged and wheeled as if the stones had bitten them. But they were quieted and went on.

Some of the Little Ones shivered. All were still as death. The three girls drew the infants closer against their breasts, as if to keep out not only chill but the city’s very voice. All except the bears and the butterflies showed fear. On the countenance of the woman among us lay a dark anxiety; nor was I myself untouched by the general dread, for the whole army was on my hands and on my conscience. I had brought it up to a danger whose shadow now pressed upon us.

And still, foolishly, I was propped up by a dream: the coming kingdom of the Little Ones, the bad giants made slaves, the animals loving, obedient friends. It was an easy hope—like imagining you can sweeten rancid broth by adding honey. I, who dreamed of obedience in others, had not learned it myself. Untrusting, unfaithful obstinacy had set me at the head of that army of innocents. In the world I had left, I had seen kings who were slaves to their own appetite, doing only what pleased them; and I was not above them. But Lona rode beside me a child indeed, therefore a free woman—calm, silent, watchful, not one whit afraid.

We were near the heart of the city before any of its inhabitants truly knew we were there. Then windows began to open; sleepy heads leaned out. Every face wore at first a dull stare, the wonderless astonishment of people to whom even marvels have become a kind of inconvenience. But when they saw the animals, that stare shifted to consternation. Still, when they perceived their invaders were almost all children, the women came running into the streets, and the men followed. For a time they kept close to the houses, leaving the middle of the way open, for they dared not approach the beasts.

At last a little boy—five years old perhaps—full of the idea of his mother as a hungry child is full of the idea of food, spied in the crowd a woman whose face drew him like a remembered smell. He flung himself from his antelope, clung about her neck, and she was not slow to return his embrace and kisses.

But a man’s hand came over her shoulder and seized the child by the neck.

Instantly a girl ran her sharp spear into the fellow’s arm. He let out a savage howl, and before he could gather himself, two or three more spears found him, and he fled yelling.

“They are just bad giants!” cried Lona, her eyes flashing. She drove her horse straight at one of unusual height who stood barring her way with a club, as if his size were an argument. He did not dare abide the shock; he slunk aside, and the next moment went down, struck by several stones.

Another huge fellow, avoiding my charger, stepped suddenly—with a speech whose rudeness alone was intelligible—between me and the boy who rode behind me. The boy told him to address the king. The giant answered by striking the little horse on the head with a hammer. The poor creature fell like a loaf knocked from the board before it is baked—helpless, finished.

Before the brute could strike again, one of the elephants behind us reached him with its weight and will. It laid him prostrate and trampled on him so that he did not attempt to rise until hundreds of feet had gone over him and the army was already past.

But at sight of the women—at their faces, their mouths, their eyes—what dismay clouded Lona’s countenance. Hardly one of them was pleasant to look upon; there was something pinched and sour in them, as if their lives had been fed on bad grain and worse words. Were her darlings to find mothers among such as these?

We halted in the central square. Hardly had we settled when two girls rode up in anxious haste with tidings: two of the boys had been hurried away by some women. We turned at once, and then first discovered that the woman we had befriended—the one who had clasped the child—had disappeared with her baby.

At the same moment we saw, bounding toward us down a narrow lane that led from the square toward the palace, a white leopardess.

The Little Ones had not forgotten the fight of the two leopardesses in the forest. Some looked terrified; their ranks wavered. But they remembered the order I had just given them, and they stood fast. We stopped to see what would come, when suddenly a small boy named Odu—remarkable for speed and courage—who had heard me speak of the goodness of the white leopardess, leaped from the back of his bear. The bear went shambling after him as faithfully as a slow old dog. Odu ran to meet the leopardess.

She pulled herself up so sharply, to avoid knocking him down, that she went rolling over and over like a spilled sack. When she found her feet again, she found the child upon her back.

Who could doubt what such a sight would do to a people who lived in daily terror of that white shape? An enemy urchin bestriding what they feared like famine. Confident of the effect, we rode on.

Our guides brought us to a house. As we stopped there came a scream—thin, sudden, the kind that cuts through stone and makes the hairs along the neck listen. I sprang down and thundered at the door. My horse came and pushed me aside with his nose, turned, and began to batter the door with his heels, a patient, angry pounding like a pestle on grain.

Up came little Odu on the leopardess. At sight of her the horse stood still, trembling. But the leopardess too had heard the cry, and forgetting the child on her back, threw herself at the door. The boy was dashed against it and fell senseless.

Before I could reach him, Lona had him in her arms; and as soon as he came to himself, she set him again on the back of his bear, which had never ceased following him, as if loyalty were its natural gait.

When the leopardess threw herself the third time, the door gave way. She darted in. We followed, but she had already vanished. We sprang up a stair, searched the rooms—nothing. Then, darting down again, we spied a door under the stair and got into a labyrinth of excavations beneath the house, tunnels like cellars built not for storing apples or curing meat, but for hiding deeds that cannot bear daylight.

We had not gone far when we met the leopardess returning, the child we sought slung across her back. He told us—his words small, shaken—that the woman he had taken for his mother had thrown him into a hole, saying she would give him to the leopardess. As if the beast were the eater of children, and the woman merely the cook. But the leopardess was a good one; she had taken him out.

We followed to search for the other boy, and entered the next house more easily. There—too late. One of the savages had just killed the little captive.

Lona’s face hardened, and yet it was a strange comfort to her to learn which child it was; she had been expecting him to grow a bad giant, and from that worst of fates death had saved him. The city had murdered him, but in murdering had also barred the door on what it would have made of him. Such are the crooked economies of evil.

The leopardess sprang upon the murderer, took him by the throat, dragged him into the street, and followed Lona with him like a cat carrying a great rat in her jaws.

“Let us leave the horrible place,” said Lona. “There are no mothers here! This people is not worth delivering.”

The leopardess dropped her burden and charged into the crowd, this way and that, wherever it was thickest. The slaves cried out and ran, tumbling over each other in heaps. Their cries filled the square—raw, frantic, a noise without pattern, the opposite of the forest’s layered music. It was the sound of a place that has forgotten how to listen.

When we got back to the army we found it as we had left it, standing in order, ready. But I was far from easy. The princess gave no sign; what she might be plotting we did not know. Watch and ward must be kept through the night.

The Little Ones were such hardy creatures they could repose anywhere. We told them to lie down with their animals where they were and sleep until they were called. In one moment they were down; in another they were lapt in the music of their sleep—a sound as of water over grass, or a soft wind among leaves. It was the one pure thing the city could not counterfeit: the hush of living breath, the small, steady cadence of trust.

Their animals slept more lightly, always on the edge of waking, ears turning to the least change in the hard city air. The bigger boys and girls walked softly hither and thither among the dreaming multitude. All was still. The whole wicked place appeared at rest. But under the stones, and behind the shut mouths of houses, I felt the city listening.
