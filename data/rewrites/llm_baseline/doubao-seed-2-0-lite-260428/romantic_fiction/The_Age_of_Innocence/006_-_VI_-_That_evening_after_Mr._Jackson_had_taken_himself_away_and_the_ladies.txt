VI. That evening, after Mr. Jackson had taken his leave and the ladies had slipped off to their bedroom draped in soft chintz curtains, Newland Archer climbed slowly up the stairs to his private study, lost in thought. As usual, a careful, attentive hand had kept the hearth crackling and trimmed the lamp wick to a warm, steady glow; the room, lined from floor to ceiling with books, with its two little bronze statuettes of fencers posed on the mantel and its rows of cherished prints of famous old master paintings, felt impossibly soft, familiar, and welcoming.
When he sank into his worn armchair pulled close to the fire, his gaze fell straight to the large framed photograph of May Welland, the one she’d pressed into his hands in the giddy first weeks of their courtship, now set dead center on his side table, no other portraits crowding it out. He stared at it with a new, quiet awe: that bright unguarded forehead, those earnest, serious eyes, that laughing, innocent mouth. He was to be the custodian of that girl’s entire soul, the keeper of every part of her soft untested life. That strange, almost terrifying product of the world he’d always loved and trusted—the young woman who knew nothing of life’s sharper edges and expected only good things—looked back at him like a stranger, even behind May’s beloved, familiar features. It hit him all at once that marriage was never the safe, still harbor he’d been raised to expect. It was a voyage out across completely uncharted seas.
The case of Countess Olenska had stirred up every settled old conviction he’d ever held, sending them drifting wildly, dangerously through his mind. That line he’d blurted out earlier—*“Women should be free, just as free as we are”—*struck at the root of a problem his entire social circle had agreed to pretend did not exist. Good, respectable women, no matter how wronged, would never dare to claim that kind of freedom, and generous-minded men like himself were therefore free to play the chivalrous hero and grandstand about granting it to them, even as all those pretty words did nothing but hide the iron, unyielding conventions that bound everyone tight to the same old patterns. Now he was pledged to defend his betrothed’s cousin for choices that would have him calling down every thunder of Church and State on his own wife, had she made them. Of course, the dilemma was purely hypothetical: he was no cruel, blackguard Polish nobleman, and it was absurd to wonder what his wife’s rights would be if he *were*. But Archer was too imaginative not to see that the bond between him and May could chafe for far quieter, subtler, far less obvious reasons. What did the two of them really know of each other, anyway? It was his duty as a “decent fellow” to hide every scrap of his own past missteps from her, just as it was her lot, as a properly raised marriageable girl, to have no past of any kind to conceal. What if, for some small, quiet reason neither of them could name, they grew tired of each other? Misunderstood each other? Irritated each other in ways they could never speak of?
He ran through his friends’ marriages, the ones everyone called perfectly happy, and saw not a single one that came even close to the tender, passionate comradeship he’d dreamed of sharing with May for the rest of their lives. He realized that dream assumed she had the experience, the flexibility, the freedom of judgment that every one of her trainers—her mother, her aunts, her governesses—had carefully taught her to never, ever possess. A cold shiver of foreboding ran through him: his own marriage could easily turn out to be just like every other one around him: a dull, convenient union of shared social and material interests, held together by her total ignorance of the world and his own careful hypocrisy.
Lawrence Lefferts popped into his head, the high priest of social form who had perfected that exact “ideal” of a marriage. He’d shaped his wife Gertrude so completely to his own convenience that even during the most public, obvious moments of his many affairs with other men’s wives, she wandered around smiling, completely unknowing, telling everyone “Lawrence is so frightfully strict!” She’d even been known to blush indignantly and turn her face away when anyone hinted that Julius Beaufort, that foreigner of murky origins, kept a second household across town.
Archer tried to comfort himself with the thought that he was not nearly such a fool as Larry Lefferts, and May was not nearly such a simpleton as poor Gertrude. But the difference was only a matter of intelligence, not of the unspoken rules they both were raised to follow. In reality, they all lived in a kind of coded, hieroglyphic world, where the real truth was never said, never done, never even thought—only represented by a set of arbitrary, agreed-upon signs. Mrs. Welland, for example, knew perfectly well why Archer had begged her to announce May’s engagement at the Beaufort ball (and had fully expected him to do no less), yet she’d still felt obliged to pretend to hesitate, to act as if Archer had forced her hand—exactly like the stories of primitive brides being dragged shrieking from their parents’ tents in the books advanced cultured people were starting to read that year.
The end result of all this elaborate, collective trickery was that the young girl at its center only grew more inscrutable the more frank and unguarded she was. May was frank, his poor darling, because she had nothing to hide. She was confident, because she had no idea what to be on guard against. And with no better preparation than that, she was about to be plunged overnight into what people euphemistically called “the facts of life.”
Archer was sincerely, placidly in love. He delighted in May’s radiant good looks, her bright health, her easy grace on horseback, the quickness of her hand at lawn tennis and croquet, and the shy new interest in books and ideas she was just starting to develop under his gentle guidance. (She’d advanced far enough to join him in mocking the *Idylls of the King*, but not far enough yet to feel the strange, soft magic of Tennyson’s *Ulysses* and *The Lotus Eaters*.) She was straightforward, loyal, brave, and had a sharp sense of humor—mostly proven by the way she laughed so hard at his bad jokes. Deep in her innocent, wide-open soul, he suspected there was a warm, glowing passion that would be the greatest joy of his life to wake. But after he’d traced every wonderful part of her, he’d always circle back to that discouraged thought: all this frankness and innocence was an artificial product. Untrained human nature was not frank or innocent. It was full of twists, defenses, small instinctive guiles. He felt oppressed by this carefully manufactured thing of factitious purity, stitched together by a whole conspiracy of mothers, aunts, grandmothers, and long-dead ancestresses, made exactly to fit what society told him he wanted, what he had a “right” to—so he could exercise his lordly little pleasure of smashing it to pieces like an image carved out of snow.
These thoughts were nothing new; every young man had them in the weeks leading up to his wedding. But they usually came hand in hand with a sense of guilt and self-abasement that Archer felt no trace of. He could not bring himself to mope like Thackeray’s heroes (who always exasperated him so much) and mourn that he did not have a blank page to offer his bride in exchange for the unblemished one she was handing him. He could not escape the fact that if he’d been raised the exact way she had, the two of them would be no more fit to navigate the world than the Babes in the Wood. And for all his anxious turning over of the problem, he could not find a single honest reason—unconnected to his own fleeting momentary pleasure and silly masculine vanity—why his bride should not be allowed the same freedom to experience life he’d been granted.
These questions were bound to drift through his head at that hour, but he knew their uncomfortable, persistent sharpness was entirely the fault of Ellen Olenska turning up at the worst possible time. Right at the height of his betrothal, a moment that was supposed to be full of pure, cloudless hope, he’d been tossed headfirst into a mess of scandal that dragged up every difficult problem he’d been happy to leave buried. “Hang Ellen Olenska,” he grumbled under his breath, as he slid the fire screen over the embers and started to undress. He could not for the life of him see why her fate should have any bearing on his own quiet, happy future, yet he had a dim, uneasy feeling that he’d only just started to measure the risks of the defense his engagement had forced him to take up.
A few days later, the first bolt fell.
The Lovell Mingotts had sent out cards for what was known as a “formal dinner”: three extra footmen on staff, two dishes for every course, a frothy Roman punch served mid-meal to refresh the guests, and “To meet the Countess Olenska” printed at the very top of every invitation, in that warm hospitable American custom that treats strangers as if they were royalty, or at least royal ambassadors.
The guest list had been chosen with a boldness and shrewd discrimination everyone in on the joke immediately recognized as the firm hand of old Catherine the Great, Mrs. Manson Mingott. Alongside the perennial invites—the Selfridge Merrys, who got asked everywhere simply because they always had been, the Beauforts, who were related to the family, and Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy, who went wherever her brother told her to go—they’d invited some of the most fashionable, irreproachable members of the dominant young married set: the Lawrence Leffertses, the lovely widow Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth, the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, young Morris Dagonet and his wife, who had been a van der Luyden. The whole company was perfectly assorted, all belonging to that tight little inner group that ran around together every single night and day of the New York season, apparently with undiminished energy.
Forty-eight hours later, the unbelievable happened: every single guest sent their regrets, except the Beauforts, old Mr. Jackson, and his sister. Even the Reggie Chiverses, who were part of the Mingott clan, had turned them down. The intended slight was made even crueler by the fact that not a single one of the notes included the ordinary, polite courtesy lie of a “previously existing engagement.” New York society was far too small in those days, every part of it—even the livery stable keepers, butlers, and cooks—knew exactly which evenings everyone was free on, so every recipient of Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s note could make their determination not to meet the Countess Olenska perfectly, unmistakeably clear.
The blow was completely unexpected, but the Mingotts met it gallantly, the way they always did. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the whole mess to Mrs. Welland, who confided it to Newland Archer. Furious at the unprovoked outrage, he argued passionately to his own mother, who went through a short, painful stretch of inward resistance and outward temporizing before she caved to his pleading, the way she always did. She immediately embraced his cause with an energy doubled by her earlier hesitation, pulled on her grey velvet bonnet, and said: “I’m going to go see Louisa van der Luyden.”
The New York of Newland Archer’s day was a small, slippery pyramid, hardly a single fissure or foothold for outsiders carved into its smooth sides. At its base was a firm foundation of what Mrs. Archer called “plain people”: an honorable, obscure majority of respectable families, many of whom (the Spicers, the Leffertses, the Jacksons, for example) had climbed above their original station by marrying into one of the ruling clans. Mrs. Archer always said people weren’t as particular as they used to be, and with old Catherine Spicer ruling one end of Fifth Avenue and Julius Beaufort the other, the old traditions weren’t going to last much longer.
Narrowing steadily upward from that wealthy, unremarkable base was the compact, dominant group the Mingotts, Newlands, Chiverses, and Mansons all represented. Most people thought that group was the very apex of the pyramid, but they themselves—at least the members of Mrs. Archer’s generation—knew better. In the eyes of a proper professional genealogist, only a tiny handful of families could lay claim to that topmost eminence.
“Don’t tell me all that modern newspaper rubbish about a New York aristocracy,” Mrs. Archer would say to her children. “If there even is one, neither the Mingotts nor the Mansons belong to it—no, nor the Newlands or the Chiverses either. Our grandfathers and great-grandfathers were just respectable English or Dutch merchants, who came to the colonies to make their fortune, and stayed here because they did so well. One of your great-grandfathers signed the Declaration, and another was a general on Washington’s staff, who received General Burgoyne’s sword after the Battle of Saratoga. Those are things to be proud of, but they have nothing to do with real rank or class. New York has always been a commercial community, and there are not more than three families in it who can claim an aristocratic origin in the real sense of the word.”
Mrs. Archer, her son, and her daughter, like everyone else in their circle, knew exactly who those privileged families were: the Dagonets of Washington Square, descended from an old English county family allied with the Pitts and Foxes; the Lannings, who had intermarried with the descendants of Count de Grasse; and the van der Luydens, direct descendants of the first Dutch governor of Manhattan, tied by pre-revolutionary marriages to half a dozen branches of French and British aristocracy.
The Lannings were only represented by two very old, lively Miss Lannings who lived cheerfully surrounded by family portraits and Chippendale furniture. The Dagonets were a large, respected clan, tied to all the best old families in Baltimore and Philadelphia. But the van der Luydens, who stood above all of them, had faded into a kind of super-terrestrial twilight, from which only two figures emerged with quiet, imposing weight: Mr. and Mrs. Henry van der Luyden.
Mrs. Henry van der Luyden had been Louisa Dagonet, and her mother had been the granddaughter of Colonel du Lac, of an old Channel Island family that had fought under Cornwallis and settled in Maryland after the war with his bride, Lady Angelica Trevenna, fifth daughter of the Earl of St. Austrey. The tie between the Dagonets, the du Lacs of Maryland, and their aristocratic Cornish kinsfolk the Trevennas had always remained close and warm. Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had more than once paid long visits to the current head of the Trevenna house, the Duke of St. Austrey, at his country seat in Cornwall and his home at St. Austrey in Gloucestershire, and the Duke often talked of returning their visit one day (without the Duchess, who was terrified of crossing the Atlantic).
The van der Luydens split their time between Trevenna, their estate in Maryland, and Skuytercliff, the massive grand property on the Hudson that had been one of the original Dutch colonial grants to the first governor, making Mr. van der Luyden the last living Patroon. Their large, solemn house on Madison Avenue was almost never opened, and when they did come into town, they only invited their most intimate, cherished friends inside.
“I wish you’d come with me, Newland,” his mother said, pausing suddenly at the door of their Brown coupe. “Louisa is very fond of you. And of course, I’m taking this step for dear May—but also because if we don’t all stand together right now, there won’t be a single scrap of real Society left for any of us.”
