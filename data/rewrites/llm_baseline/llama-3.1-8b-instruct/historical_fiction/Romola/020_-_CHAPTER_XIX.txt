As the sun set over the Tuscan hills, casting a warm orange glow over the city of Florence, Messer Bernardo del Nero pondered the counsel of his trusted friend, Bernardo. The elder statesman's words echoed in his mind: the marriage between Romola and Tito should be postponed until Easter, a decision that would give the young couple time to solidify their bond and secure a stable future. Bernardo, ever the pragmatist, had taken a firm stance, but beneath his resolute exterior, a glimmer of hope flickered to life.

Tito's meteoric rise in the social circles of Florence was undeniable. His effortless charm and quick wit had won over the hearts of the city's elite, including the young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, who had even invited him to join his retinue on a forthcoming journey to Rome. As Tito's star continued to shine brightly, his reputation as a gracious companion at lavish gatherings in the Via Larga and at Florentine games only grew. His learning and accomplishments, though not necessarily exceptional, shone all the brighter against the backdrop of his affable nature, rendering him an attractive prospect for any family seeking to elevate their social standing.

Bernardo, a man of old-fashioned values and prejudices, found himself gradually won over by Tito's charm. The old scholar's attachment to the traditional ways of Florence was beginning to yield to the younger man's adaptability and charisma. As Tito became increasingly accepted into the inner circle of Florence's finest families, Bernardo's initial reservations gave way to a grudging respect. It was only rational, he thought, to acknowledge that Tito's presence had brought a new lease on life for both himself and Romola.

The blind old scholar, once a pillar of the community, had long been relegated to the shadows, his name whispered in hushed tones, often accompanied by criticism of his stubborn refusal to conform to the expectations of his peers. However, with Tito's arrival, a subtle shift occurred. The old man's name was no longer synonymous with bitterness and discontent, but rather with a sense of renewal and possibility. Romola, once trapped in a life of dowerless pride, now had a chance to break free, thanks to the charming young Greek who was poised to become both her son and husband.
