Two hours after we collapsed into our exhausted slumber, at roughly four in the morning, I jolted awake. The first brutal edge of bone-deep fatigue had worn off, and the torturous, crawling thirst we’d all ignored in our collapse crashed over me full force. I couldn’t sleep another minute. I’d been dreaming I was waist-deep in a rushing stream, green willows arching over the banks, the water so clear I could see pebbles glinting on the bed—and I woke to the scorch of arid desert air searing my throat, and the sharp, unshakable memory of Umbopa’s warning: if we didn’t find water that day, we’d die slow, ugly deaths. No human could survive long in that furnace heat without a drop to drink.

I sat up, rubbing my caked, gritty face with hands so dry the skin felt like cracked leather. My lips were stuck fast to my teeth, my eyelids glued shut, and it took a good minute of hard rubbing before I could pry them open. Dawn was close, but there was no soft, fresh pre-light in the air—just thick, hot, stagnant murk I can’t even find words to describe. The rest of the crew was still out cold. When it grew light enough to make out print, I fished the tattered little pocket copy of the *Ingoldsby Legends* I’d stuffed in my pack out, and read *The Jackdaw of Rheims*. When I hit the line about the nice little boy holding a golden ewer full of pure water, “as any that flows between Rheims and Namur,” I actually tried to smack my cracking, split lips. Just the thought of that clean, cool water drove me half mad. If the Cardinal had been standing there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have dived straight for that ewer and chugged every last drop—even if it was full of the soap he’d claimed was “worthy of washing the hands of the Pope,” and even if the full weight of the Catholic Church’s curse was waiting to land on my head for it. Thirst, weariness, and almost 24 hours without food must have addled my wits a little, because I started cackling at the mental image: a grizzled, sunburnt little elephant hunter bursting between the cardinal, the page boy, and the thieving jackdaw, shoving his dirty face straight into that basin and draining it dry. My harsh, croaking laugh woke the rest of the crew, who stumbled awake, rubbing their own caked faces and prying their own glued-shut lips open.

The second we were all fully lucid, we sized up the situation, and it was as bad as it could get. Not a single drop of water was left in any of our bottles. We turned every last one upside down, licking their necks and their caps, but they were bone dry. Good, who’d been carrying the flask of brandy, pulled it out and stared at it like it was a miracle, but Sir Henry snatched it right out of his hand—drinking raw spirits in that state would have only dehydrated us faster, and killed us far quicker.
“If we don’t find water, we’re dead,” Sir Henry said, blunt as always.
“If old da Silvestra’s map is worth anything, there should be some nearby,” I offered, but no one looked the least bit reassured. None of us had much faith left in that scribbled old map drawn on a tattered shirt tail.

As we sat staring blankly at each other, I noticed the Hottentot Ventvögel stand up, head down, eyes fixed on the sand beneath his feet. He stopped short a few paces away, grunted a guttural exclamation, and pointed at the ground. We all scrambled up to crowd around him.
“Looks like fresh springbok spoor,” I said. “What of it?”
“Springbok never wander far from water,” he answered in rough Dutch.
I smacked my forehead. I’d forgotten that. “You’re right. Thank God.”

That tiny scrap of hope breathed new life into all of us. When you’re staring death right in the face, even the flimsiest glimmer of hope feels like a lifeline—on a pitch black night, even a single pinprick star is better than total dark. Ventvögel lifted his snub nose, sniffing the hot air hard like an old impala ram catching the scent of a lion.
“I smell water,” he said.

We all whooped. We knew wild-born men like him had senses no European could match. Just then the sun crested the horizon, flooding the desert with golden light, and the sight that hit us made us forget our burning thirst entirely for whole minutes. Not 50 miles away, glinting like polished silver in the dawn, rose Sheba’s Breasts. Stretching hundreds of miles to either side of the twin peaks ran the vast, jagged Suliman Berg range. Two massive mountains, each at least 15 thousand feet tall, stood no more than a dozen miles apart, linked by a sheer, precipitous cliff, their peaks swathed in permanent snow, perfectly shaped like the breasts of a slumbering woman. At their bases, the slopes curved soft and smooth out of the plain, and the snow-capped “nipple” on top of each peak glowed white and solemn against the pale blue sky. The cliffs flanking them stretched as far as the eye could see, broken only by flat, table-top peaks identical to Table Mountain back at Cape Town. For a minute we just stood there, awestruck—these were clearly extinct volcanoes, so huge they felt almost sacred. Then thin, gauzy mist curled up around their bases, wrapping them in a soft veil till only their giant, ghostly outlines were visible. No wonder we hadn’t seen them clearly earlier.

The second the peaks vanished behind cloud, our thirst roared back twice as bad. Ventvögel could say he smelled water all he wanted, but there was no sign of it anywhere—nothing for miles but scorching sand and scrawny karoo scrub. We circled the little sand kopje we’d camped on, scanning the horizon, but there was no pan, no spring, no pool anywhere.
“You’re a fool,” I snapped at Ventvögel. “There’s no water for miles.”
He just kept sniffing the air. “I smell it, Baas. It’s close.”
“Sure,” I retorted. “It’s in the clouds, right? It’ll rain in two months, and wash our bones clean when we’re dead.”
Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard thoughtfully. “Maybe it’s on top of this kopje?”
“Rot,” Good scoffed. “No one finds water on the top of a hill.”
“Let’s check anyway,” I said, and we all stumbled up the sandy slope, Umbopa leading the way. He froze mid-step a few feet from the summit.
*“Nanzia manzie!”* he yelled at the top of his voice. “Here is water!”

We all bolted up to him, and sure enough, tucked into a deep indent right at the top of the kopje, was a murky, dark pool of water. We didn’t stop to wonder how on earth it had gotten there, didn’t care that it looked black and muddy. It was water. We hurled ourselves face down on the ground, sucking up that tepid, brackish muck like it was nectar fit for the gods. Heavens, how we drank! When we couldn’t sip another drop, we tore off our clothes and wallowed in the pool, letting the moisture sink into our scorched, parched skin. If you’ve never been three days from death by thirst, you can’t imagine the luxury of that dirty, muddy soak. We climbed out refreshed, tore into our biltong, which we hadn’t been able to even taste for 24 hours, ate our fill, smoked a pipe, and slept in the shade of the pool’s bank till noon.

We spent the rest of the day resting there, thanking our lucky stars—and old da Silvestra’s long-dead ghost—for marking that pool’s position perfectly on the tail of his shirt. We filled every water bottle we had to bursting, and set off again by the light of the moon, covering almost 25 miles that night. We found no more water, but the next day we got lucky, huddling in the shade behind giant termite mounds when the sun was at its hottest. When the mist cleared again at dawn, Sheba’s Breasts were only 20 miles away, looming right over our heads, grander than ever. By the next morning, we’d reached the lowest slopes of the left Breast, our target the whole time.

Our water was already exhausted again, and our thirst was torturing us once more. The lava slopes were sharp and jagged under our boots, made of cinder and rock spewed out of the earth’s crust eons ago, and by 11 a.m. we were all completely done in, our feet raw and bleeding. We spotted a cluster of large lava boulders a few hundred feet above us, and dragged ourselves there to huddle in their shade. Next to the rocks, we saw a patch of dense, bright green growth, sprouting from decomposed lava soil that birds must have dropped seeds into. We didn’t care about grass—we couldn’t live on it like Nebuchadnezzar. We sank down against the rocks and groaned, and I swore under my breath I’d never have signed on for this fool’s errand if I’d known how bad it would get.

Then Umbopa, quiet and dignified as ever, stood up and hobbled over to the green patch. A minute later I stared, shocked, as he started dancing and yelling like a maniac, waving a big green object over his head. We all stumbled after him as fast as our weak legs would carry us, hoping against hope he’d found water.
“It’s food and water, Macumazahn!” he yelled, waving the green thing. It was a wild melon. We’d stumbled on a whole patch of thousands of ripe wild melons, bursting with juice. I yelled to Good, and he sank his false teeth straight into the nearest one before I even finished talking. I think we each ate at least six that day. I’ve never tasted anything sweeter. We cut the rest of the melons we found and stood them upright in the sun to cool by evaporation, packing as many as we could carry. Then a flock of ten large pauw bustards flew straight towards us. We all dropped flat on the sand, I lifted my repeating Winchester, and fired two shots into the middle of the flock as they flew overhead. I brought down a 20-pound bird. Half an hour later we had a fire going of dry melon stalks, and we roasted that bustard whole. We ate every last bite of it, nothing left but leg bones and a beak, and it was the best meal we’d had in a week.

We marched on by moonlight, the air growing colder the higher we climbed. By dawn we were only a dozen miles from the snow line, and we found more melons growing further up, so we no longer worried about water—we knew we’d have all the melting snow we could want soon. The ascent grew so steep we could barely make a mile an hour, and we ate the last of our biltong that night. We saw no game, no springs, no streams anywhere, which struck us as odd with all that snow up above—we later learned every single waterway on those mountains ran down the north face, hidden from us. Now we only feared starvation. We’d escaped death by thirst, but it looked like we’d just starve to death on the snow fields.

I copied the entries from my notebook to tell those next three miserable days:
> 21st May. Started 11 a.m., air cool enough to travel by day, carrying armfuls of melons. Struggled all day, no more melons, no game. Halted at sundown, no food for hours. Bitter cold at night.
> 22nd May. Started at sunrise, faint and weak. Only made five miles all day. Ate patches of snow, nothing else. Camped under a plateau edge. Freezing. Shared a tiny sip of brandy each, huddled in our blankets to stay alive. Starving, exhausted. Ventvögel almost dead by sundown.
> 23rd May. Started at sunrise as soon as the sun thawed our frozen limbs. We’re in a terrible state. If we don’t find food today, we won’t make it through the night. Barely any brandy left. Good, Sir Henry, and Umbopa are holding on, but Ventvögel can’t handle the cold, not for long. We’re level with the lava cliff linking the two Breasts now. Behind us the desert glows all the way to the horizon, in front of us miles of smooth, level snow, the giant snow “nipple” of the peak rising four thousand feet over our heads. Not a living thing in sight. God help us. I think our time is up.

We struggled on up the snow all day, resting every few steps, the whole crew gaunt, ragged, half-starved, dragging our feet through the dazzling white. We only made seven miles total. Just before sunset, we stood directly beneath the snow nipple of Sheba’s left Breast, the setting sun painting the whole peak blood red and gilding the top of the dome like a crown.
“We ought to be almost at that cave the old Dom wrote about,” Good gasped.
“If the cave even exists,” I said.
“Quit talking like that, Quatermain,” Sir Henry groaned. “The map got us the water, didn’t it? It’ll get us the cave.”
“If we don’t find it before dark, we’re dead anyway,” I said.

We tramped in silence for ten more minutes, when Umbopa, who had a leather belt cinched so tight around his waist to “make the hunger small” that his waist looked like a girl’s, grabbed my arm. He pointed at the slope of the nipple, and 200 yards away we spotted a dark hole cut into the snow. It was the cave.

We stumbled there as fast as we could, and found it exactly as da Silvestra had described it. We got to the mouth of the cave just as the sun vanished over the horizon, so fast it went dark in seconds—no twilight in those latitudes. We crawled inside, a tiny cramped cave barely 20 feet deep, huddled together for warmth, and sipped the last dregs of our brandy, barely a mouthful each. The cold was so intense I’m sure the temperature was 15 degrees below freezing, and after days in the scorching desert, starved and exhausted, we could barely keep from freezing to death. We huddled closer and closer, but there was no warmth left in our emaciated bodies. We could only sleep for a few minutes at a time, and we all knew if we slipped into a deep sleep we’d never wake up.

An hour or two before dawn, I heard Ventvögel, whose teeth had been chattering like castanets all night, heave a deep, final sigh. His teeth went quiet. I thought he’d finally fallen asleep, till his back, pressed against mine, grew colder and colder, until it was hard as ice.
Dawn broke, golden light spilling across the snow, and the sun peeked over the lava wall, shining straight into the cave mouth. Ventvögel was dead, frozen stiff, his arms hugged around his knees. Shocked, we scrambled away from his corpse, revolted as all people are by the company of a dead body. Then one of the men gasped. I turned my head.
At the far end of the tiny cave, slumped against the rock, head resting on his chest, arms hanging loose at his sides, sat another dead man. A white man.

Our shattered, half-frozen nerves couldn’t take it. We all scrambled for the cave mouth, tumbling over each other to get out into the open snow, none of us able to speak a single word.
