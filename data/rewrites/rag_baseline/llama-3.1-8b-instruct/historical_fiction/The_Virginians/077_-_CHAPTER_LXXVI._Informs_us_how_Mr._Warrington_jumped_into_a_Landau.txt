The morning sunlight filtering through the grimy windowpane cast an eerie glow on the worn wooden floorboards of the boarding house. In the corner, a young woman sat hunched over a lute, her fingers deftly plucking the strings to produce a mournful melody. Her eyes, red-rimmed from lack of sleep, gazed out the window at the deserted streets. The notes of the lute seemed to capture the desolation of her thoughts.

Outside, a carriage rattled down the cobblestone street, its occupants hidden behind closed curtains. George Warrington, a young man from Virginia, sat stiffly upright, his face pale and drawn. He had been away from home for years, studying music and philosophy, but the memories of his past lingered, like the echoes of a forgotten song. As the carriage turned a corner, he caught sight of a small, unassuming house, its windows boarded up, a testament to the neglect of the family within.

Miss Hetty, a petite, fiery spirit, flung open the door and descended the steps, her eyes blazing with a mixture of anger and sorrow. She approached the carriage, her hands clenched into fists. "So you're finally here, Mr. Warrington," she said, her voice low and even. "And you've come to ask after Theo, and you're sorry that your neglect has made her ill?" Her words dripped with venom, each note piercing the air like a sharp arrow.

George's face contorted in a mixture of guilt and frustration. "Hetty, please—"

But she cut him off, her words tumbling forth like a torrent. "You've been here for a year, and all you've done is sit with her, day and night, and then, without a word, you leave her, because your mother doesn't approve of her. What has she ever done to you, but be a fool and too fond of you? You've taken her away from all of us, and now you're leaving her behind, because some old woman in America doesn't like her. She was happy with us before you came, loved her sister, till she saw you. And now, because your mother thinks she can find someone richer for you, you must leave her forsooth!"
