After Bratti had joined the cluster of talkers, the young stranger, weary of waiting and indifferent to the cause of the city’s stir—matters that seemed to concern only native Florentines—decided to wander through the piazza. He sought not news, but food: a simple vendor who might sell without prying into politics or gossip. Yet as he walked, his hand dipped into a worn leather pouch at his waist, rummaging with growing frustration. “Not an obolus, by Jupiter!” he muttered in a tongue foreign to Tuscany, perhaps even to Italy itself. “I thought I had one last coin left. Then I must buy my breakfast with kindness.”

He did not go far before spotting a quiet corner where such a bargain might be struck. There, apart from the throng, stood two mules adorned with red tassels and collars. One bore wooden milk jugs; the other carried panniers full of fresh herbs and greens. Leaning against the milk-laden mule was a girl no older than sixteen, her face framed by a red hood that hid her hair entirely. Her features were soft and childlike, almost delicate in their innocence. She appeared to have fallen asleep mid-task, her body half-slumped, half-supported by the beast.

But the stranger saw no reason to hesitate. With gentle intent, he touched her lightly—so softly it seemed to her, still dreaming, like a sprig of thyme brushing her lips as she bent to gather herbs. The spell broke. She blinked open blue eyes wide with surprise, startled to find a tall, dark-haired youth standing before her.

He spoke to her in a voice low and strange, yet sweet—a tone so unfamiliar that even if she’d been alert, she would have taken it for nonsense. Instinctively, she turned her head slightly and lifted a corner of her green serge mantle as a shield.

He repeated himself, this time choosing words she knew well: “Forgive me, pretty one, for waking you. I’m dying with hunger, and the scent of milk makes breakfast seem more tempting than ever.”

The phrase *muoio di fame* stirred recognition. The mantle dropped. Moments later, a large cup of fragrant milk was offered to him.

He said no more, lifting the cup to drink. As he sipped, the girl dared to look up. His clothes were torn and travel-worn, yet there was something noble in his bearing—unlike any beggar she had known. And though his voice had first sounded eerie, almost unnatural, now it softened, becoming strangely clear.

While he drank, her hand slipped into a bag beside the mule. When he set down the cup, she held out a thick piece of bread, her eyes meeting his—blue, shy, yet encouraging.

“But perhaps that is your own meal,” he said gently. “No, I’ve eaten enough already. A thousand thanks, my gentle one.”

She did not answer. But the bread moved closer, as if urging him to take it. He looked into her face, then smiled.

“Ah, then, if I must take the bread,” he said, laying his hand upon it, “I shall grow bolder still—and ask for another kiss to make it sweeter.”

His words grew clearer despite the odd cadence of his speech. The strange tone, once alarming, now seemed playful. She blushed deeply, raising her mantle again to cover her mouth.

Just as he leaned forward, fingers brushing the arm that held the veil, a harsh voice cut through the air.

“Who are you? With a murrain on you! No honest buyer, I’ll wager—you’re some follower of dice-men, or worse. Go on, dance off, or I’ll give you a tune faster than you’ll care for!”

The stranger stepped back, unafraid, his gaze steady. A smile flickered across his lips—bright, teasing—as he took in the speaker.

It was a stout woman, dressed in a man’s jerkin over a rough green gown, her face wrapped in a faded hood. Though coarse and weathered, her features bore a startling resemblance to the girl’s—same shape of brow, same curve of lip—a maternal likeness both tender and grim, like a shadow foretelling the future.

Her voice cracked with anger, but the stranger’s smile remained. It was oddly soothing, yet Monna Ghita showed no mercy.

“Yes, yes,” she snapped, “you can grin like any monkey in cap and jerkin. You’re a minstrel, or a mountebank—I know it. You look as foolish as a tumbler just turned upside down. And what tricks have you been up to, Tessa?” she demanded, turning on her daughter, whose frightened face made her rage sharper. “Giving away milk and bread? Ay, ay, you’d carry water in your ears for any idle vagabond who didn’t want to stoop! Turn your back and lift the herbs—unless you want to say ten Aves without counting!”

“Nay, Madonna,” the stranger replied, his voice softening with a pleading smile, “do not punish your lovely Tessa for showing pity to a hungry traveler who found himself without a quattrino. Your face looks so fine when it frowns—but I long to see it lit by a smile.”

“_Va via_!” she spat. “I know your kind. You may tickle me with that straw until doomsday, but I won’t laugh. Get gone, with a bad Easter! Or I’ll leave a few beauty spots on that face that’ll ruin your kissing until Advent.”

As she raised her hands, sharp and fierce, Bratti arrived—having followed the stranger earlier. “What think you of this parrot, Nello?” he asked his companion. “Doesn’t his tongue smell of Venice?”

“Nay, Bratti,” the barber whispered, “your wisdom has much of the ass in it—especially about ears. This man is Greek, else I’m not the barber who shaved Demetrio and pulled teeth from his learned jaw. This youth could pass for a godling straight from Olympus—if he’d had a touch of my razor.”

“_Orsù_, Monna Ghita!” Nello chimed in, amused. “What storm has come upon you? Has this young man misbehaved?”

“By San Giovanni!” Bratti interjected, still wary of the stranger’s shabby clothes and hidden wealth. “He did right to run from _me_, if he meant trouble. I found him under the Loggia de’ Cerchi, wearing a ring like Bernardo Rucellai’s own. Not a penny’s worth more do I know about him.”

“The truth is,” Nello said, grinning, “this _bello giovane_ has been too bold with your charms, Monna Ghita. He tried to kiss you while Tessa turned her back. I see she’s busy—no time to look this way. Was it not so, Messer?”

“You have guessed the offense like a prophet,” the stranger laughed. “Only I did not find Monna Ghita here at first. I begged milk from her daughter, accepted this bread as a gift, and was offering thanks when I had the honor of seeing these more mature beauties—though perhaps I was too bold in admiring them.”

“_Va, va_! All of you, off to purgatory! Stay there till I pay to bring you out!” Monna Ghita roared, shoving Nello aside and leading her mule forward, forcing the stranger to step aside. “Tessa, you fool, move your mule—here comes the cart!”

As Tessa reached for the bridle, she glanced back—just long enough to catch the stranger’s hand beckoning her farewell. He was walking away with Nello, heading toward the narrow street that led to the Piazza del Duomo.

“_Ebbene_,” Bratti called out, loud enough to carry over the cart, “I leave you with Nello, young man. I cannot carry my basket farther. I have business at home. But remember our bargain—because if you found Tessa without me, it was not my fault. Nello will show you my shop in the Ferravecchi, and I’ll not turn my back on you.”

“A thousand thanks, friend!” the stranger replied, laughing, and vanished into the alley with Nello, leaving behind the scent of milk and the echo of laughter.
