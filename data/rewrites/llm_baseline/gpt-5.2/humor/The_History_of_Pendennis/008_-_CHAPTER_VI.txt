After that business which had lately knocked all the sense out of Arthur Pendennis, Cicero and Euripides might as well have packed their trunks and gone into lodgings elsewhere. Honest Mr. Smirke, who had been engaged to cultivate a young gentleman’s mind, found himself chiefly employed in cultivating an amiable ignorance, and doing it with remarkable success.

The creature that bore the worst of it was Rebecca, the mare. For whenever Arthur could publicly proclaim, with a candour that would have done credit to a tax-gatherer, that he was “only riding over to Chatteris for a fencing-lesson,” he rode; and when he could not proclaim it, he rode all the same, only faster and with more mystery. Prior’s Lane—where a certain set of lodgings lay like a well-buttered trap for youth—became the true university to which he hurried, leaving Greek plays to Smirke and good intentions to Providence.

So, when Rebecca went lame, Arthur took it as a personal affront from Heaven. He raged over her with the tragical air of a monarch whose favourite charger has been slain at Bosworth, and then, like any sensible young man in love, proceeded to do the most practical thing possible: he plunged into the accounts of the hunting-stables, read up “doctoring” as if it were theology, hired another horse at ruinous rates, and paid for nostrums with the solemnity of a man endowing a church.

Once—sometimes twice—each week, under the pretext of reading a Greek play with Smirke, this young reprobate contrived his campaign with military precision: off he set early enough to catch the Competitor down coach, spent a couple of hours in Chatteris, and came back by the Rival which fled homeward at ten at night, as if it too had been compromised. It was nearly all over one day, through the mere innocence of virtue. Mrs. Pendennis, in her soft way, asked Mr. Smirke whether they had “read a great deal the night before.” Smirke, being born to tell the truth and to look painfully surprised by it, opened his mouth to say he had never seen Mr. Pen at all—when Arthur’s boot-heel descended on the curate’s toe under the table with the weight and distinctness of a moral lesson. Smirke’s countenance became that of a martyr; his words became those of a liar; and Greek, naturally, suffered.

They had, of course, those conversations which young men in love must have with somebody, as cooks must have salt. Smirke was the unfortunate cruet. Under vows of secrecy delivered with all the thunder of a miniature Jupiter, Arthur confessed his state. Smirke trembled, and with the air of a man asked to smuggle gunpowder into a nursery, ventured to hope that it was “no unworthy object—no unlawful attachment”—for if so, duty would force him to break his vow and inform Mrs. Pendennis, and then there would be quarrels, and then—most dreadful prospect—he might never again have the privilege of sitting in the same room with what he liked best in the world.

“Unlawful! Unworthy!” Arthur cried, bounding as if the very adjectives had slapped him. “She is as pure as she is beautiful. I would give my heart to no other woman. I keep it secret in my family because—because—there are reasons of a weighty nature which I am not at liberty to disclose. But any man who breathes a word against her purity insults both her honour and mine; and—dammy—I won’t stand it!”

Smirke, who could have fainted with relief at finding himself not compelled to denounce anybody, gave a small, frightened laugh. “Well, well—don’t call me out, Arthur; you know I can’t fight.” But by this compact the curate fell more completely into the boy’s power; and Greek and mathematics went, like untended broth, thin and tasteless.

Had the reverend gentleman possessed a malicious curiosity, and opened the Poet’s Corner of the County Chronicle when it arrived in Wednesday’s bag, he might have found the air thick with ardour: “Mrs. Haller,” “Passion and Genius,” “Lines to Miss Fotheringay of the Theatre Royal,” and other compositions which smelt of midnight ink and theatrically broken hearts. But these verses were no longer signed NEP; the author had changed his apron and styled himself EROS, as if a new signature could disguise the same hand that peppered the paper. Helen, good soul, who clipped every one of her son’s poems as if they were recipes for family preservation, never suspected that Nep and Eros were one and the same boiling pot.

At last Mrs. Pendennis, with that gentle, domestic interest by which mothers track all the world’s romances as if they were household accounts, asked, “Who is the lady whom your rival is always singing in the County Chronicle? He writes something like you, dear Pen, but yours is much the best. Have you seen Miss Fotheringay?”

Arthur said yes: he had seen her—that very night, indeed, in “The Stranger,” where she played Mrs. Haller. And by the way, she was to have a benefit; she was to appear as Ophelia—suppose they were to go? Shakspeare, you know, mother. They could get horses from the Clavering Arms. Little Laura sprang up as if the word Play had been sugar to a child.

Arthur introduced “Shakspeare, you know,” because his dead father—who respected the bard in the abstract with the fidelity of a man who does not read him in the concrete—had often declared there was more poetry in Shakspeare than in all Johnson’s Poets put together, and had promised what happiness it would be, when Pen was of a proper age, to take mother and boy to see some good plays. The widow’s eyes filled at the remembered speeches of the man that was gone. She kissed her son and said she would go. Laura capered with delight.

Was Arthur happy? Yes. Was he ashamed? Also yes: which is one of the common seasonings in youthful stew. As he held his mother to him he longed to tell her all, but kept his counsel, meaning to try her by the play, as Hamlet tried his uncle—only with less murder and more applause.

In her good humour Helen asked Mr. Smirke to join the party. Mr. Smirke, who had been bred at Clapham by a parent who regarded the theatre as a moral kitchen where the devil stirred the pots, had never yet seen a play. But Shakspeare!—and to sit the whole night by Mrs. Pendennis in her carriage! He spoke feebly of temptation and gratitude, and accepted. As he spoke he gave her a look—half longing, half sanctimonious—at which the widow felt more uncomfortable than if she had seen a mouse in her pantry. She had caught that look before lately. It pursued her like a tune one dislikes and cannot stop humming. Mr. Smirke became more positively odious every day in her eyes.

As for Arthur’s courtship, we shall not report a great deal of what he poured out, because the world contains enough nonsense already, and youth can always manufacture more. He sat with Miss Fotheringay hour after hour and emptied his soul upon her with the honest vehemence of a boy who believes eloquence is the same thing as sincerity. Everything he knew, hoped, felt, read, fancied—Byron, Moore, truth, eternity—came tumbling out as if his heart were a pantry door kicked open.

Miss Fotheringay’s part was not to talk, but to look as if she understood; and to look exceedingly handsome while doing it. Indeed, while Arthur was declaiming for twenty minutes at a stretch—delighted with his own thunder—Emily, who could not comprehend a tenth part of it, had leisure to attend to affairs far nearer the centre of her universe: how the cold mutton should be dressed next day (hashed with a sharp little bite of mustard and a respectful onion? or sliced and warmed in gravy, so it might pretend to be new?), how the black satin should be turned, how to coax a bonnet out of a scarf like Miss Thackthwaite’s. Arthur served up passion and poetry; Emily, with her calm fine eyes and rich voice, supplied the proper exclamations—“Oh, ’tis beautiful! Ah, how exquisite! Repeat those lines again”—as one sprinkles sugar over anything to make it go down. Off the boy went again, and she returned serenely to her mutton and millinery.

His passion was no secret from Emily or from her father. On Arthur’s second visit, both saw the hook was in; and when he had galloped away, Captain Costigan remarked, winking over his grog, “Faith, Milly darling, I think ye’ve hooked that chap.”

“Pooh—’tis only a boy, papa dear,” said Milly. “Sure he’s but a child.” Arthur, had he heard that, would have been transported; for on the ride home he shouted her name at hedges and milestones with the confidence of a conqueror.

“Ye’ve hooked ’um any how,” the Captain continued, “and he’s not a bad fish. I asked Tom at the George, and Flint the grocer where his mother dales—fine fortune—drives in her chariot—splendid park and grounds—Fairoaks Park—only son—property all his own at twenty-one—ye might go further and not fare so well, Miss Fotheringay.”

“Them boys are mostly talk,” said Milly, gravely. “Ye know at Dublin how ye went on about young Poldoody, and I’ve a whole desk full of verses he wrote me when he was in Trinity College; but he went abroad, and his mother married him to an Englishwoman.”

“Lord Poldoody was a young nobleman; and in them it’s natural,” said the Captain; “and ye weren’t in the position ye are now. But ye mustn’t encourage this young chap too much—for, bedad, Jack Costigan won’t have any thrifling with his daughter.”

“No more will his daughter, papa—you may be sure of that,” said Milly. “A little sip more of the punch—sure ’tis beautiful. Ye needn’t be afraid about the young chap—I think I’m old enough to take care of myself, Captain Costigan.”

So Arthur came day after day, rushing in and galloping away, growing wilder with each visit. Sometimes the Captain remained, sometimes—having, as he said, perfect confidence—he cocked his hat over his eye, strutted out on an errand, and left them to that most perilous of entertainments, conversation.

The Captain’s drawing-room was low and wainscoted, with a great window looking into the Dean’s garden. There Arthur sat and talked—talked—and Emily sat calm and beautiful at her work, the sunshine laying a warm glaze over her superb face and form like butter over hot bread. And the world, with a quaint mercy, kept interrupting him by sound. The great cathedral bell would begin to boom, and he would pause and smile, and be silent until the last vibration died away into the summer air. Toward sunset the rooks in the elms would quarrel like old women over a market-stall. Sometimes the organ and choristers would come drifting over the close, a soft discipline of harmony that hushed his noisy passion for a moment, as if the very air insisted on a quieter language than his.

It must be said, too, that Miss Fotheringay went to church every Sunday of her life, in a plain shawl and close bonnet and veil, accompanied by her indefatigable father, who gave the responses in a rich brogue, joined psalms and chanting with exemplary fervour, and behaved as if religion were a performance in which he had a respectable part.

Little Bows, the family’s house-friend, was furious at the notion of Emily marrying a stripling seven or eight years younger. Bows—crippled, and even a trifle more deformed than Bingley the manager, so he could not appear on the stage—was a wild singular man, with talent and humour in him like pepper in a stew. Attracted first by Emily’s beauty, he had taken to teaching her acting. He shrieked the parts in his cracked voice; she learned them by rote from his lips and repeated them in full rich tones. He indicated attitudes, set and moved those beautiful arms, drilled her eyes, her pauses, her very tears. Those who remember her afterwards can recall the precision: the same plank of the stage, the same instant of eye-roll, the same degree of weeping over the same syllable—acting as punctual as a kitchen clock.

And then, when she had come out trembling before the audience, looking so exhausted and tearful you fancied she might faint with sensibility, she would be behind the curtain in a moment gathering up her hair, and go home to a mutton-chop and a glass of brown stout; and the day’s harrowing labours over, she went to bed and snored as regularly as a porter. Bows, who had screamed her into greatness, was indignant that she should throw away her chances by marrying a little country squire. A London manager, seeing her, prophesied an engagement and success. The misfortune was: London managers had seen her. Three years before she had played in London and failed from sheer stupidity. Since then Bows had worked and screamed and twisted and repeated lines until he had made a machine of her; and she—patient and dull—let herself be made. She was not grateful or ungrateful, kind or ill-humoured: she was only stupid. And Arthur was madly in love with her.

The post-horses from the Clavering Arms arrived in due time and drew the party to the theatre at Chatteris. Arthur was gratified to see a tolerably large audience. The young gentlemen from Baymouth had a box; in the front sat Mr. Foker and his friend Mr. Spavin, blazing in full evening costume, as if they had been lit for the occasion. They saluted Arthur cordially and examined his party with approval. Little Laura, rosy-cheeked, with a quantity of shining brown ringlets, looked fit for any respectable comedy. Mrs. Pendennis, in black velvet with the diamond cross she wore on great occasions, looked handsome and majestic enough to be a duchess in exile. Behind sat Mr. Arthur and gentle Smirke, curl composed on his fair forehead, white tie in perfect order. He blushed to find himself there—yet was never so happy in his life. He and Mrs. Pendennis had brought books of Hamlet to follow the tragedy, as honest country-folks do when they go to a play in state, lest Shakspeare should slip a word past them unnoticed.

Samuel, coachman, groom, and gardener to Mr. Pendennis, took his place in the pit, where Mr. Foker’s man was also visible. The pit was dotted with non-commissioned officers of the Dragoons; their band, by permission of Colonel Swallowtail, sat in the orchestra; and the corpulent warrior himself, with his Waterloo medal, and a number of his young men, made a handsome show in the boxes.

“Who is that odd-looking person bowing to you, Arthur?” Mrs. Pendennis asked.

Arthur blushed. “His name is Captain Costigan, ma’am—a Peninsular officer.” It was the Captain indeed, in a new “shoot of clothes,” with large white kid gloves; one he waved to Arthur, the other he laid sprawling over his heart and coat-buttons. Arthur said no more. And how was Helen to know that this fine Peninsular officer was the father of the very lady whose praise was humming through the County Chronicle?

Mr. Hornbull, from London, played Hamlet; Mr. Bingley contented himself with Horatio and reserved his chief strength for William in Black-Eyed Susan, which was the second piece.

We have nothing to do with the play, except that Ophelia looked lovely and performed with admirable wild pathos: laughing, weeping, gazing, waving her beautiful white arms, flinging snatches of flowers and songs with charming madness. What a brave use she made of her splendid black hair, tossing it over her shoulders! She made the most charming corpse ever seen; and while Hamlet and Laertes battled in her grave, she was peeping from the back scenes with some curiosity towards Arthur’s box and the family party gathered there.

In that box there was but one voice about her: rapture. Mrs. Pendennis went into ecstasies over her beauty. Little Laura was bewildered by Ghost and murder and play-within-play; during which, as Hamlet lay at Ophelia’s knee, Arthur felt he could gladly strangle Mr. Hornbull for the convenience of the drama. Yet Laura cried praises of that beautiful young creature. Arthur was charmed by the effect Emily produced on his mother; and Smirke, for his part, grew enthusiastic.

When the curtain fell on that heap of slaughtered persons who are dispatched so suddenly at the end of Hamlet—poor Laura being not a little astonished at the quantity of death—there broke out an immense shouting and applause. The intrepid Smirke, violently excited, clapped and cried “Bravo! Bravo!” as loud as the Dragoon officers. The officers were greatly moved, and cheered as if courage were contagious; led by portly Swallowtail, who waved his cap, the non-commissioned officers in the pit following their chiefs as gallantly as if it were a charge. Bravos roared through the house. Arthur bellowed loudest, “Fotheringay! Fotheringay!” Messrs. Spavin and Foker gave the view-halloo from their box. Even Mrs. Pendennis began to wave her pocket-handkerchief; Laura danced, laughed, clapped, and looked up at Arthur with wonder.

Hornbull led the beneficiaire forward amid bursts of enthusiasm. She looked so handsome and radiant, hair still loose over her shoulders, that Arthur could hardly contain himself. He leaned over his mother’s chair and shouted, hurrahed, waved his hat, disguising the desire to cry, “Look—there! That’s the woman!” under a hurricane of applause.

As for Miss Fotheringay’s behaviour, she went through precisely the same business as always: surveyed the house with grateful glances, trembled, almost sank with emotion over her favourite trap-door, seized flowers and pressed them to her swelling heart, and so forth. Mr. Foker discharged a prodigious bouquet at her; even Smirke, blushing dreadfully, made a feeble shy with a rose, which fell into the pit and caused him an agony as if he had thrown it into sin. Twinkling at Emily’s breast, Arthur spied the locket he had bought at Mr. Nathan’s in High Street with the last shilling he was worth and a sovereign borrowed from Smirke.

Black-Eyed Susan followed; and at that sweet story the gentle-hearted party were charmed and affected. Susan in russet gown and pink ribbon looked as lovely as Ophelia, only with more hope of supper. Bingley was great in William. Goll as the Admiral resembled the figure-head of a seventy-four. Garbetts as Captain Boldweather—a miscreant who planned to carry off Susan—waved an immense cocked hat and proclaimed “Come what may, he will be the ruin of her,” with such relish you might have fancied he meant to be the ruin of the audience’s composure as well. It was with sincere regret that the friends saw the curtain fall and end that pretty tender tale.

If Arthur had been alone with his mother in the carriage on the way home, he would have told her all that night. But he sat on the box in the moonshine smoking a cigar by Smirke, who warmed himself with a comforter and the glow of having said “Bravo” in public. Mr. Foker’s tandem and lamps whirled by the sober Clavering posters a couple of miles on the road, and Mr. Spavin saluted Mrs. Pendennis’s carriage with variations of Rule Britannia on the key-bugle, making the night ring with heroic noise enough to scare the hedgerows into patriotism.

Two days after these gaieties, the Dean of Chatteris entertained select clerical friends at dinner at his Deanery. That they drank uncommonly good port, and abused the Bishop over dessert, is likely enough; but it is not our affair. Dr. Portman of Clavering was among the guests; and being a gallant man, and seeing from his place at the mahogany the Dean’s lady walking on the grass with her children sporting and her pink parasol over her head, he stepped out through the French windows into the lawn, leaving the other white neckcloths to gird at my lord Bishop. He offered Mrs. Dean his arm, and they sauntered over the ancient velvet lawn—mowed and rolled for immemorial Deans—in that quiet comfortable manner with which middle-aged good-tempered people walk after a good dinner in a calm golden summer evening, when the sun has just sunk behind the enormous cathedral towers and the sickle-shaped moon grows brighter every minute in the heaven.

Now at the end of the Dean’s garden stood Mrs. Creed’s house; and the windows of the first-floor room were open to admit the pleasant summer air. In that chamber, a young lady of six-and-twenty whose eyes were perfectly wide open sat with a luckless boy of eighteen whose eyes were shut by love. We have seen them there before; the reader will have no difficulty recognising Mr. Arthur Pendennis and Miss Costigan.

The poor boy had taken the plunge. Trembling, heart beating and throbbing, tears forcing themselves out in spite of him, voice choking with feeling, he had at last said the words he could withhold no more; and flung his whole store of love, admiration, ardour—his whole small treasure of life—at the feet of this mature beauty, as savages stake land and possessions for a draught of fire-water, or as civilised youth stakes peace and pride for a pair of fine eyes.

“Does your mother know of this, Arthur?” said Miss Fotheringay slowly.

He seized her hand and kissed it again and again. She did not withdraw it. “Does the old lady know it?” thought Miss Costigan; “well, perhaps she may.” And she remembered the handsome diamond cross Mrs. Pendennis wore at the play, and thought, with a practical tenderness all her own, “Sure ’twill go in the family.”

“Calm yourself, dear Arthur,” she said in her low rich voice, and sniffled sweetly and gravely upon him. With her disengaged hand she put the hair lightly off his throbbing forehead. He was in such a whirl of happiness he could hardly speak. At last he gasped, “My mother has seen you, and admires you beyond measure. She will learn to love you soon—who can do otherwise? She will love you because I do.”

“’Deed then, I think you do,” said Miss Costigan—perhaps with a sort of pity for him.

Think he did? Of course he went off into a rhapsody—love, truth, eternity, and the rest of the household gods of youth. Words were tried and found too small for the tremendous depth of his affection. This speech is no business of ours. It was most likely not very wise; but what right have we to pry into the pantry where a boy empties out his heart? Let him fling it down and be gentle with him. It is best to love wisely, no doubt; but to love foolishly is better than not to be able to love at all. Some of us can’t, and are proud of our impotence too, which is like being proud one cannot taste.

At the end of his speech Arthur again kissed the imperial hand with rapture. And it was at this very moment—while Mrs. Dean and Dr. Portman were talking comfortably together—that young Master Ridley Roset tugged his mother by the back of her capacious dress and said, waggling his innocent head,

“I say, ma! look up there.”

Indeed it was a view from the Dean’s garden such as seldom is seen by Deans—or written in chapters. There was poor Arthur performing a salute upon the rosy fingers of his charmer, who received the embrace with perfect calmness and good humour. Master Ridley looked up and grinned; little Miss Rosa looked at her brother and opened her mouth of astonishment. Mrs. Dean’s face defied expression. And as for Dr. Portman—when he beheld the scene, and saw his prime favourite and dear pupil Arthur—he stood mute with rage and wonder.
