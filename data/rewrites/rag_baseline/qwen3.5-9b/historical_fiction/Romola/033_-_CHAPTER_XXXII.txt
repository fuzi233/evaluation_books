The following day, the entire city of Florence vibrated with the electric anticipation of the French departure. For Romola, amidst the general rejoicing, there lingered a faint, superstitious hope that the specific anxieties plaguing her regarding Tito—burdens that had arrived alongside the foreign guests—might evaporate with them. The French occupation had lasted barely eleven days, yet within that brief span, Romola had endured a depth of acute unhappiness unknown to her prior existence. Since their arrival, Tito had donned a hateful armor of defensiveness; while she could not logically discern why their exit should dismantle the source of his terror—though the visceral image of a prisoner seizing him, captured in Piero's sketch, haunted her thoughts—she knew that with the French gone, she would finally be rid of the tangible associations of her pain.

Clad in her mantle, Romola stationed herself beneath the loggia atop the house, scanning the horizon for glimpses of the retreating troops and the royal entourage crossing the bridges toward the Porta San Piero, the gateway leading to Siena and Rome. Even after the gates sealed shut, she remained, seeking to resonate with the thunderous toll of the bells. Dusk had fallen by the time she descended into the library. With steely resolve, she kindled her lamp, vowing to quell the agitation that had paralyzed her throughout the day and resume her transcription of the catalogue. Tito had departed early; she anticipated his return later. Her intention was to vacate the study once he arrived, moving to the adjacent salon adorned with dancing nymphs and birds—a space he had previously deemed too chilly and somber for her comfort.

To her profound astonishment, Tito appeared before she had even commenced her work. Her initial reaction was a pang of concern: how desolate he must feel, navigating the vast darkness of the room illuminated only by a solitary oil lamp at the far end, with the embers of the fire nearly extinguished. She moved toward him with urgency.

"Tito, dearest," she breathed, raising her pale arms to loosen the *becchetto* from his neck, "I had no idea you would return so soon."

"Am I unwelcome?" he countered, flashing one of his brightest smiles. He embraced her, yet playfully tilted his head back, creating a playful distance.

"Tito!" The word escaped her lips in a tone of affectionate rebuke. Immediately, he kissed her, stroking her hair in his customary manner, seemingly unconcerned with removing his cloak. Romola quivered with delight. The cumulative emotions of the day had sharpened her sensitivity to the restoration of his familiar demeanor. "It will return," she murmured to herself, "the old happiness. He is like himself again."

Yet, Tito was exerting immense effort to project this normalcy; his heart hammered with underlying anxiety.

"If I had anticipated your return so quickly," Romola said, assisting him in shedding his wrappings, "I would have staged a small celebration for this joyous ringing of the bells. I did not intend to remain in the library upon your arrival."

"Never mind, sweet," he dismissed casually. "Cease worrying about the fire. Come, sit."

A low stool stood beside his chair, Romola's usual perch during their conversations. She rested her arm upon his knee, mimicking the gesture she once shared with her father, gazing upward as he spoke. He had failed to notice the portrait looming nearby, and she held her tongue, contemplating it with renewed intensity.

"I have experienced the clamor of the bells for the first time, Tito," she began. "I relished being shaken and deafened by them; I felt akin to a Bacchante possessed by divine fury. Do the citizens appear truly joyful tonight?"

"Joyful in a sour, pious manner," Tito shrugged. "Truth be told, those remaining in Florence have scant cause for jubilation. In my view, the most rational grounds for gladness lie in fleeing Florence entirely."

Tito struck the desired note effortlessly, devoid of pretense. His delivery was unemphatic, yet his gravity prompted an anxious query from Romola.

"Why, Tito? Have fresh troubles arisen?"

"No need for new ones, my Romola. Three powerful factions currently exist in the city, poised to tear each other apart. Should the Frate's faction prove dominant enough to silence the others—as seems probable—life will resemble a funeral procession. They are already simmering with plans for a Great Council; should they succeed, the individual who chants sacred Lauds the loudest shall be deemed most fit for office. Furthermore, the city will be financially hemorrhaged by the subsidy paid to the French King and the war to reclaim Pisa. The outlook is dismal enough without the tyranny of fanatics. Florence will undoubtedly be a paradise for those who spend their evenings in crypts whipping themselves, but for everyone else, the exiles hold the advantage. Personally, I have concluded that wisdom dictates we depart Florence, my Romola."

She recoiled. "Tito, how could we abandon Florence? Surely you do not think I could leave—at least not yet, certainly not for some time." Her voice faltered, turning cold and trembling. Tito must perceive the depths of her hesitation.

"That is merely a fabrication of your own imagination, my sweet one. Your isolation has led you to place undue weight on trivialities. Recall what I told you before our marriage: I wished we resided elsewhere than in Florence. Had you traversed more lands and encountered diverse peoples, you would understand my sentiment. There is something in the Florentines that reminds me of their biting spring winds. I prefer individuals who approach life with less fervor. It would benefit your Romola greatly to witness a new existence. I wish to immerse her slightly in the soft waters of forgetfulness."

He leaned forward, kissing her forehead and resting his hand once more upon her fair hair. Yet, she felt his caress no more than if he had touched a mask. Agitated by the widening chasm between their minds, she was oblivious to the contact of his lips.

"Tito, it is not because I deem Florence the most pleasant place in the world that I refuse to depart. It is because—and because—we must fulfill my father's wish. My godfather is elderly; he is seventy-one. We cannot abandon him."

"It is precisely those superstitions clinging to your mind like obscuring clouds that constitute a primary reason for my desire to relocate us two hundred leagues from Florence. I am compelled to act contrary to your will: if those tender eyes perceive falsely, I must see for them and rescue my wife from squandering her life on impracticable dreams."

Romola sat rigid and silent. She could not ignore the trajectory of Tito's argument: he sought to convince her that the library could be deposited in a monastery or otherwise disposed of to sever their ties to Florence. She resolved never to submit her conscience to his judgment on matters of filial duty, preparing internally to endure any pain required for resistance. However, this determination remained dormant in these opening moments, crushed by the heart-wrenching realization that she and Tito were now openly divided in their aspirations. He welcomed her silence; despite fearing the strength of her feelings, he could not help but overestimate the persuasive power of his own logic, trapped as he was in the narrow confines of a merely clever, unimpassioned mind. His actions did not appear sinister to himself, nor did his imagination allow him to visualize their impact on Romola. Proceeding in the same gentle, admonishing tone, he continued.

"You know, dearest—you whose clear judgment has always guided you—that the concept of isolating a collection of books and antiquities, attaching a single name to them eternally, offers no valid, substantial benefit to its object. Moreover, it is susceptible to defeat in myriad ways. Witness the fate of the Medici collections! In my opinion, it is even blameworthy to harbor such petty notions of appropriation. Why should anyone reasonably rejoice that Florence possesses the fruits of scholarly research and taste more than any other city? I comprehend your reverence for the wishes of the deceased; however, wisdom imposes limits on such sentiments, lest lives be perpetually wasted in futile devotion—much like praising deaf gods forever. You dedicated your life to your father while he lived; why demand more of yourself?"

"Because it was a trust," Romola replied, her voice low but distinct. "He trusted me, he trusted you, Tito. I did not anticipate you would feel differently, but I did expect you to recognize that."

"Yes, dearest, naturally I would feel so regarding points concerning your father's actual welfare or happiness. But there is no question of that now. If we believed in purgatory, I would be as anxious as you to ensure masses were said. If I believed it would pain your father to see his library preserved and utilized in a manner differing from his intent, I would share your rigidity. Yet, a touch of philosophy should teach us to cast aside these air-woven fetters that mortals bind themselves with, spending their lives in misery under the mere imagination of burden. Your mind, which seizes ideas so readily, my Romola, is capable of distinguishing between substantial good and these brain-wrought fantasies. Consider, dearest, what possible utility these books and antiquities serve, stored together under your father's name in Florence, compared to if they were dispersed or transported elsewhere? Nay, is not the very scattering of such treasures among hands that know how to value them a means of extending their usefulness? This rivalry among Italian cities is petty and illiberal. The loss of Constantinople was the gain of the entire civilized world."
