Lona’s revulsion for the city’s hollow, half-feral denizens—its women most of all—curdled to the point she begged we flee the blighted place at once. I, though, burned with the certainty that retreat would be a willful failure when victory hung within reach: far worse, running would shatter the fragile courage of the Little Ones, leaving them exposed to far greater peril. If we turned tail, the princess would never let us escape unassailed. If we marched to face her, the old prophecy we’d carried across the wastes hummed with new, living hope. The ancient law of the realm said mother and daughter must meet. It was possible Lona’s soft, radiant goodness might crack Lilith’s stony, cruel heart of its own accord. If she moved to strike violence, I would stand between them. If no other power could reach her, I was ready, for Lona’s sake, to drive my blow straight into that fateful closed palm the tales spoke of, the one sealed to bring about her doom. I knew her end was written. It seemed all but certain her fate would unfold through us, here and now. I did not breathe a word to Lona of the blood tie between her and the tyrant, only laid out my reasoning plainly. She agreed at once to walk with me to the obsidian palace.

From the peak of its tallest spire at dawn, while the whole city still slept, Lilith had spotted the approach of the Little Ones’ camp across the plain. The sight woke a wild, overpowering terror in her: she had failed to destroy them, and they were coming for her. The prophecy was about to come true. When she shook off her paralysis, she stumbled down to the Black Hall, the elliptical chamber hewn from pure, absolute dark marble, and seated herself at its northern focal point, directly beneath the oculus cut into the domed roof above. She had to think. For Lilith, “thinking” meant wrapping herself in the delusion of her own perfection, not as she was, but as she chose to see herself. To feed that illusion, she had hung a polished mirror in the shadows just above her head, invisible in the hall’s perpetual dim, positioned to catch the full light of the meridian sun and reflect its rays straight onto her own form. She waited for the sun to climb high enough to show her the glorious vision of her own beauty she craved. A dozen skittering shadow-things drifted through the dark around her, but she turned her inner eye away from every last one of them. Her personal shadow, the being that followed her every footstep through the realm, hovered right beneath the mirror, but wrapped in self-adoration, she did not see him at all.

By the time the city fell, its inhabitants cowering in their huts too afraid to even scream, the Little Ones and their great war horses and tiny riding elephants were encamped in the central square. The midday sun spilled over her, and for a few short minutes she drank in the sight of herself, radiant and unblemished, in the mirror. The vision faded, but she did not move. Night fell, and the mirror filled with inky darkness, yet she still sat motionless. Shadows swarmed thick through the halls of the palace, her servants shivering so hard their teeth chattered, too afraid of the Little Ones’ war beasts to dare flee. All night long the princess sat fixed to her throne. She had to see her beauty again. She had to force her delusions to hold. But her courage and her will had tired of her, and would stay no longer.

At dawn, we chose twelve of the tallest, bravest of the boy Little Ones to ride with us to the palace. We mounted our shaggy, great war horses, and the smaller boys rode their little ponies and young elephants. The princess still sat waiting for the sun to grant her the joy of her own glorified reflection. The tide of light crept up the shore of the sky, but not a single ray could pierce the Black Hall’s gloom until the sun stood directly overhead. It rose to meet our eyes as we climbed the palace’s steep marble steps, climbing higher still to the top of the great hall’s dome. It peered through the oculus, and sudden, blinding radiance flooded the room. Lilith caught sight of herself in the mirror, and sprang to her feet with a shriek of despair. The cursed brand, the great black spot that covered half her side, blazed as dark as the marble around her, impossible to hide. She clutched her silk robe tight to her chest and fell back against her throne. Her loyal shadow glided out of the hall, and she watched him go.

We found the palace gates hanging open as always, passed through the grove of petrified paved trees, up to the palace door, and stepped into the vestibule. There, locked in an iron cage, lay the spotted leopardess, her guard beast, still as a corpse, eyes closed. The Little Ones paused a moment to stare. She reared up with a roar, slamming against the cage bars. The horses reared and plunged; the elephants stumbled back a step. The next instant she collapsed flat on her back, writhed in short, quivering spasms, and went motionless.

We rode straight into the great hall. The princess still leaned back in her shaft of sunlight, and the echo of hoofs on stone drifted to her ears from the courtyard. She started, listened, and shook—no sound like that had ever echoed in her palace before. She pressed a hand to her branded side and gasped. The trampling drew closer, crossed the threshold of the hall itself, and shapes that were not her skittering shadows advanced through the dark. For us, there was no gloom where she sat: a single, blazing point of unearthly light, a glorious woman centered in all the blackness. Lona threw herself off her horse before it had even stopped moving, and bounded toward her. I sprang down right after her. “Mother! Mother!” she cried, and her clear, lovely voice bounced off the dome’s high walls.

The princess shivered. Her face twisted almost black with hate, her eyebrows knitting tight together over her forehead. She rose to her feet. “Mother! Mother!” Lona cried again, leaping up onto the dais and flinging her arms around the princess’s waist. One step more and I would have reached them. In that split, terrible second, I saw Lona lifted high over Lilith’s head, and dashed hard against the cold marble floor. The sound of her fall was wet, and horrible. She landed limp at my feet.

Lilith sank back into her throne with the smile of a demoness. I dropped to my knees beside Lona, lifted her from the stones, and pressed her tight to my chest. Burning with indignant hate, I glanced up at the princess, and she answered me with her sweetest, most innocent smile. I would have lunged for her, wrapped my hands around her throat and strangled the life out of her, but my love for the child in my arms was stronger than any hate I could feel for the mother. I held my precious burden tighter. Her arms hung limp and helpless. Her blood seeped between my fingers, dripping onto the floor in soft, slow little plashes.

The scent of blood hit the horses first: mine reared, wild-eyed and shivering, and bolted blindly down the dark hall, the rest of the horses after it. They never saw the far black wall, and crashed against it to shatter themselves to pieces. The elephants lumbered up to the foot of the dais and stopped, trumpeting wildly. The Little Ones clambered up onto the raised platform, frozen in horror. The princess lay back in her seat, her face as gray as a corpse’s, only her eyes alive, burning wickedly bright. She had withered back to the gaunt, wasted thing I had first found wandering in the woods, the brand on her side blazed dark as if a great hot hand had been pressed straight through her flesh.

But Lona saw nothing. I saw nothing but Lona. “Mother! Mother!” she sighed, and her breathing stopped.

I carried her out into the courtyard, where the sun shone down on her pale white face, and the faint, pitiful ghost of a smile curved her lips. Her head lolled back. She was dead as the dry dust of the realm. I forgot the Little Ones, forgot the murdering princess, even forgot the body I held in my arms, and wandered away, searching for the Lona that was no longer there. The doors and windows of the city were crowded with the half-brute denizens, jeering at me but too afraid to speak a word, for they saw the white leopardess padding right at my heel, head hung low. I kicked her away hard. She hesitated a moment, then followed me again.

I reached the square. The Little Ones’ army was gone. The empty space jolted me back to my senses. Where were the Little Ones? Her Little Ones? I had lost them. I staggered, helpless, over to the great stone pillar at the square’s center, and sank down on its base. As I sat staring at Lona’s still face, for a split second she smiled a living, warm little smile. I never once thought it was an illusion. I knew what it meant. I would see her alive again. It was not her that was lost. It was me, and she would find me.

I stood up to go after the Little Ones, and turned instinctively toward the gate we had entered through. I glanced over my shoulder, and the leopardess was gone. The street was filling fast with a fierce, rabid crowd of the city’s inhabitants. They saw me burdened with my dead, and for a while dared not attack. But by the time I neared the gate, their courage had returned. The women began shoving at me, and I held on, heedless. A man lunged and pushed hard against my sacred burden, and I kicked him so hard he went flying, howling. The crowd surged closer, and fearing they would defile the girl who could no longer feel harm, I clasped my treasure tighter and freed my right arm to fight.

At that exact moment, a commotion burst out behind me. The crowd split apart, and through the gap came the Little Ones I had thought lost in the palace. Ten of them rode four elephants, and on the other two elephants lay the princess, bound hand and foot, completely still save for her eyes rolling wildly in their ghastly sockets. The last two Little Ones rode behind her on Lona’s own horse. Every few steps, the wise great beasts bearing her curled their trunks behind them to test that her binding cords held fast. I walked on ahead of the small procession, and carried Lona out of the city.

What a bitter end to all the bright hopes I had carried when I stepped foot in that evil place. We had captured the tyrant princess at last, but we had lost our all-beloved queen. My life was bare. My heart was empty.
