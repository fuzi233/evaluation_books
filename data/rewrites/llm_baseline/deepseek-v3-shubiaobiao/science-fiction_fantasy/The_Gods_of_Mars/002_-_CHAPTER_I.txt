The night was cold and clear as I stood on the bluff overlooking the Hudson, its waters flowing like a silent ghost beneath me. Ten years had passed since I last felt the pull of Mars—ten years of waiting, hoping, pleading with outstretched arms toward the red eye of that distant world. And now, as I raised my hands once more beneath the stars, the old longing surged through me with unbearable force.

A wave of nausea struck without warning. My vision blurred; my knees buckled. I collapsed at the edge of the bluff, my mind reeling—until suddenly, clarity returned. Memories of that Arizona cave, of my lifeless body lying within, flooded back. The same paralyzing dread gripped me, as if the unseen horrors of that place had followed me across the void. With a desperate effort, I wrenched free from the invisible bonds.

Then came the familiar snap—the severing of an unseen tether—and I stood beside my own empty shell, freed once more. Without hesitation, I turned my gaze to Mars, arms raised, and waited.

The transition was instant. The same biting cold, the same abyssal darkness—and then light. Blinding, searing light. I found myself sprawled on a carpet of crimson grass beneath a towering forest canopy, the air thick with the perfume of alien blooms. Strange trees, their trunks smooth as polished metal, stretched skyward, their leaves shimmering in hues no earthly palette could replicate.

But it was the sea that seized my attention—an endless expanse of blue stretching beyond the trees, its waves glittering beneath a bronze sun. Mars? It had to be. Yet nothing here resembled the barren wastes I knew. This was a world of cultivated beauty, of deliberate design.

Then I saw them.

Creeping across the meadow were creatures out of nightmare. Ten feet tall, their ghastly blue skin stretched over grotesquely humanoid frames. A single, lidless eye stared from their foreheads, white as death. Where a mouth should have been gaped only a ragged hole, raw and inflamed. Their hands writhed like serpents, tipped with razor talons—and from their armpits dangled smaller versions of themselves, twitching like malignant growths.

They moved with eerie purpose, their tendril-like fingers combing the grass. I tensed, torn between flight and fascination—until a shriek split the air.

The creatures froze. Their hair—if it could be called hair—rose like a nest of worms, quivering. Then, as one, they bounded toward the cliffs, their kangaroo-like leaps covering yards in a single stride. Against all caution, I followed.

What I found was a slaughter.

A handful of green warriors—two males, four females—stood back-to-back, their swords flashing as the plant men descended. The beasts fought like nothing I’d ever seen. One leaped high, its whip-like tail crushing a warrior’s skull midair. Another’s strike felled two females in a single blow. The last green warrior fought like a demon, carving a path toward the trees—but the horde closed in.

I didn’t think. I moved.

Snatching a fallen blade, I charged. The plant men hissed as my sword cleaved through them, their viscous green blood spraying like sap. The warrior turned, eyes widening—then grinned, his own blade joining mine in a lethal dance.

We fought shoulder-to-shoulder, the tide turning—until another shriek echoed above us.

On a ledge high on the cliffs stood a figure, screaming, pointing—not at us, but at the horizon. My stomach dropped. Across the meadow, the forest, the river, more were coming. Hundreds. And with them, new horrors loped on all fours, their speed terrifying.

“A fine death,” I muttered.

The warrior laughed, teeth gleaming. “Then let it be worthy of *John Carter*.”

I stared.

Before me stood Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark—my oldest friend, and the greatest warrior of Barsoom.
