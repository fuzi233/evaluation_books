“A lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe,” someone once said—perhaps it was Sybrandt. And truly, that is so. A blow from a weapon may be felt, its damage measured, its reach foreseen. But a lie? It has no limits. It spreads unseen, twisting through lives like roots beneath the soil, breaking what cannot be mended, and often taking whole futures with it. In this tale, one such falsehood—uttered in cold calculation—set off consequences far beyond the minds of those who spoke it. Yet they reaped little reward for their deceit.

The brothers, who had invented the story merely to remove one rival from their father’s inheritance, saw instead their own brother Gerard rise in their mother’s heart. Not only that, but Eli, in a moment of open honesty, declared that since Gerard was presumed lost—likely dead—he had made provision in his will for little Gerard, and also for Margaret, his son’s grieving widow. At this, a look passed between the two brothers—the kind that speaks volumes without a word. It was a warning to all betrayers: even silence can carry judgment.

Cornelis, on the verge of speaking, saw his mother’s eyes upon him and held back. He could have said, “Gerard is probably alive.” But he did not.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the other architect of the lie, now stood near the end of his days. His wealth was fading, his body failing, and his conscience growing louder. Though intelligent enough to know the truth, he had never been free from remorse. When he took Gerard’s letter to Margaret, he had done so not out of kindness, but as a form of penance—a bargain with himself. “I cannot give up land or money,” whispered his inner Avarice. “But I will not cause her unnecessary pain,” murmured his dwarf Conscience. So he broke the seal, found no mention of the deed, and delivered it himself—taking credit for mercy, though it was mere self-deception.

Then came the birth of Margaret’s child. It stunned him. The weight of his act shifted, becoming something more terrible than before. If his letter were believed, it would bring disgrace upon a woman whose father had saved him from plague, whose grandfather had helped him in poverty, and whose family had long loved him. These thoughts gnawed at him, especially now, when memory stirred old friendships and the body weakened. He was filled with dread—but still he dared not confess. For confession meant honesty, and honesty meant surrendering everything. To him, greed was habit; repentance, only sentiment.

It was on such a day, returning from the town hall, that he found a woman waiting in his vestibule—veiled, holding a child. Assuming she was ashamed, he addressed her with stiff authority. But then she lowered her veil—and there stood Margaret Brandt.

Her sudden presence struck him like a thunderclap. He faltered, unable to hide his alarm.

“Where is my Gerard?” she demanded, her chest rising and falling rapidly. “Is he alive?”

“For all I know,” he stammered, “he might be. I hope so—for your sake. Come into this room. The servants—”

“Not a step,” she said firmly, seizing his shoulder with both hands. “You know the secret that is tearing my heart apart. Why does Gerard not come? Why has he sent no word these many months? Answer me—or the whole town shall hear, let alone your servants. My sorrow is too deep to be mocked.”

He denied knowing anything. She replied that those who had sent her to him told her otherwise.

“You do know why he neither comes nor writes,” she insisted.

At that, Ghysbrecht turned deathly pale. But he rallied his pride. “Would you believe two rogues over a man of honor?”

“What two rogues?” she asked sharply.

He hesitated. “Said ye not—?” Then he broke off. “Here I am, a poor broken man, my memory failing. You come, and confuse me so I know not what I say.”

“Ay, sir,” she answered quietly, “your memory is shaken—or else you would not be my enemy. My father saved you from the plague when no one else would approach you. He was your friend. My grandfather Floris helped you when you were poor, and loved you, man and boy. Three generations of us you have known—and here is the fourth. This child is Peter’s grandson, and Floris’s great-grandchild. Look down on his face. See the innocence in his eyes. Think of theirs!”

“Woman,” he groaned, “you torture me.” He sank onto a bench.

But she saw her chance. Kneeling before him, she placed the child upon his knees.

“This fatherless babe is poor Margaret Brandt’s—she who has done you no harm, born of a line that loved you. Look at his face. It will move you more than any words of mine. Saints above—what have a helpless girl and her child done to erase the memory of your youth, when you were guiltless as he is now, looking up at you with pleading eyes, begging you to return his father?”

She held the child aloft, cradling him gently under her arms, smiling into the old man’s eyes.

He stared—first at the child, then at her—then rose suddenly, shrieking, “Avaunt! Ye pair of adders!”

The pain tore through him, giving his aging frame a brief surge of strength. He paced wildly, clutching his hair, wringing his hands.

“Forget those days? I forget nothing. Oh, woman, woman—sleeping or waking, I see only the faces of the dead, hear only their voices. Soon I shall join them. There, there—what is done is done. I am in hell. I am in hell.”

His voice cracked. The effort collapsed him. He staggered, and only Margaret’s arm kept him from falling. She supported him as best she could, calling for help. Servants rushed in and carried him away, half-unconscious. The last she saw was his pale, furrowed face, white as snow, hanging limply over the servant’s elbow.

“Heaven forgive me,” she whispered. “I fear I have killed the poor old man.”

And so, her search for truth brought only deeper darkness. As she reflected on every word, she became certain: Ghysbrecht *did* know something about Gerard.

“And who were the two knaves he spoke of?” she thought. “Who called their lie a good deed? Oh, my Gerard, my poor deserted child—we are drowning in deep waters.”

The journey to Tergou had cost more than she could afford. A customer fled with unpaid debts. Again, she was forced to open Catherine’s angel—her last resort. But as she descended the stairs with it, she found loose silver on the table, along with a note:

She gasped. Then laughed—then wept with joy.

“He is alive!” she cried. “He sends this by some kind hand.”

She kissed the paper again and again, tucking it into her bosom.

Time passed. No news came. But every two months, a small sum of silver appeared—sometimes on the table, once flung through the bedroom window in a purse, once hidden in Luke’s basket after he stopped at the tavern to speak with a friend. The giver remained unknown. Catherine denied it. Margaret Van Eyck swore she had no part. Eli denied it too.

Each time, Margaret would whisper to little Gerard, “Oh, my poor deserted child, you and I are wading in deep waters.”

She spent half the money dressing the boy better than his station warranted. “If it comes from Gerard,” she said, “he must see his son neat.”

Soon, the mothers in the street began to sneer—especially those whose children wore ragged clothes. Their scorn stung.

Months stretched into years. The sharp pains of longing gave way to a dull, unrelenting ache. She returned to her first thought: *Gerard must be dead.* She would never see her husband again. Her marriage lines—her future—would remain unfulfilled.

This grief, once eased by Eli and Catherine’s acceptance of her betrothal, now returned full force. Others would not be so kind. And often, she wept over her son’s illegitimacy.

“Is it not enough that we are bereaved?” she moaned. “Must we also be dishonored? Oh, that we had never been born.”

Meanwhile, Peter Brandt changed. After nearly two years of confusion, his mind seemed to clear. Moments of lucidity returned. Then he and Margaret would talk of Gerard—until he drifted back into silence.

One day, returning after an absence, she found him talking with Catherine—something he had not done since his stroke.

“Eh, girl,” she said, “why must you be out?”

“I’ve told him all,” Catherine replied. “We’ve been crying together over your troubles.”

Margaret stood silent, watching them, her heart swelling with quiet joy.

Peter smiled at her. “Come, let me bless thee.”

She knelt. He blessed her—eloquently, tenderly.

“You have been the most loving, true, and obedient daughter Heaven ever sent to a poor widower,” he said. “May your son be to you what you have been to me.”

Then he dozed.

They whispered together. Catherine said, “We spoke just now of Gerard. It weighs heavily on his mind. His poor head must have listened to us even when it seemed dark. He’s a very understanding man. He thought of many things: ‘He may be in prison,’ says he, ‘or forced to fight for a king, or sent to Constantinople to copy books, or perhaps gone into the Church.’ He always had a bent that way.”

“Oh, mother,” Margaret whispered, “he only deceives himself—as we do.”

Before she could finish, a sudden cry rang out.

The old man’s eyes widened. He seemed to stare through the wall.

His voice dropped. “Sore disfigured—but I knew him. Gone! Gone! All dark.”

He slumped back, weakly asking, “Where is Margaret?”

“Dear father,” she said, kneeling beside him, “I am here. Oh, mother! Mother, what is this?”

“I cannot see you,” he murmured. “But a moment ago, I saw all around the world. Ay, ay. Well, I am ready. Is this your hand? Bless thee, my child, bless thee! Weep not! The tree is ripe.”

The physician understood. These were his final words.

Then, gently, peacefully, he lowered his head and slipped away—like a child sinking into sleep. The torch flared once, brightly, before going out.
