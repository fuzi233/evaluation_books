Sergeant Cuff said nothing more while we kept to the shelter of the fir-plantation. The needles underfoot muffled our tread with a dry, brushed whisper, and the wind moved high in the dark boughs like a hand passing over harp-strings. It was the only gentle sound in a countryside that, nearer the sea, always seemed to hold its breath.

When the last trees thinned and the sand-hummocks began, he roused, as if a decision had finished baking in him and was now ready to be served.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he said, “you’ve done me the honour of taking an oar in my boat; and you may do me another service before the evening is over. So we’ll have done with mystifying. You mean to tell me nothing that might hurt Rosanna Spearman. She has been a good girl to you; you pity her; and you credit your tenderness for humanity. Admirable—only wasted. Rosanna Spearman is in no danger of trouble. No—though I fixed her with a hand in the Diamond’s disappearance, on evidence as plain as the nose on your face.”

“Then my lady won’t prosecute?” I asked.

“Your lady can’t prosecute,” the Sergeant said. “Rosanna Spearman is an instrument in another person’s hands; and Rosanna Spearman will be held harmless—for that other person’s sake.”

He meant it. His voice had the settled weight of a man who has sifted a thing down to its last grain. And yet something in me—some loyalty that had been salted into my bones by long service—scraped uneasily against him.

“Can you give that other person a name?” I said.

“Can you, Mr. Betteredge?”

“No.”

He stopped dead and looked me over with a melancholy patience, as if my mind were a cupboard he had opened and found ill-stocked.

“I’m always tender towards human infirmity,” he said. “I’m particularly tender, at this moment, towards you. And you—out of the same excellent motive—are tender towards Rosanna Spearman. Has she had a new outfit of linen lately?”

The question came on me like a spoonful of cold water dashed into a hot pan. I couldn’t imagine what he was seasoning his talk with; but seeing no harm in the truth, I owned that Rosanna had come sparely provided, and that my lady—because of her good conduct (I laid stress on that as if it were yeast in bread)—had given her new linen not a fortnight before.

“This is a miserable world,” said the Sergeant. “Human life is a sort of target—misfortune fires at it and hits. But for that outfit, we should have found a new nightgown or petticoat among Rosanna’s things, and nailed her that way. You follow? You examined the servants. You know what two of them found outside her door. You know what she was about yesterday, after she was taken ill.”

I hesitated.

“Oh dear me,” he went on, as if the whole matter were a recipe and I the slowest kitchen-boy in England, “it’s as plain as that strip of light at the end of the trees. Thursday morning, at eleven, Superintendent Seegrave—who is a mass of human infirmity—shows the women-servants the smear on the door. Rosanna has her reasons for suspecting her own things. She gets to her room, finds the paint-stain on her nightgown or petticoat or what not, shams ill, slips away to the town, buys stuff to make a new petticoat or nightgown, makes it alone in her room on Thursday night—lights a fire, not to burn the old thing (two servants are prying at her door; she knows better than to make a smell of burning), but to dry and iron the substitute after wringing it out. Keeps the stained thing hidden—probably on her. And at this moment she is making away with it, in some convenient place, on that lonely beach ahead. I traced her this evening to your fishing village and to one cottage, which we may have to visit before we go back. She stayed there some time and came out with—so I believe—something under her cloak. A cloak on a woman’s back is an emblem of charity; it covers a multitude of sins. I saw her set off northward along the coast. Is your sea-shore considered a fine specimen of marine landscape, Mr. Betteredge?”

“Yes,” I said, as short as could be.

“Tastes differ,” he answered. “From my point of view, I never admired a marine landscape less. If you are following a person along this shore and that person looks round, there isn’t a scrap of cover to hide you. I had to choose: take Rosanna in custody on suspicion, or leave her, for the present, with her little game in her own hands. For reasons I won’t trouble you with, I’d make any sacrifice rather than give the alarm tonight to a certain person who shall be nameless between us. So I came back to the house to ask you to bring me to the north end of the beach by another way. Sand, in the matter of printing footsteps, is one of the best detective officers I know. If we don’t meet Rosanna by coming round on her, the sand may tell us what she has been at—if the light holds. Here is the sand. And if you’ll excuse my suggesting it—hold your tongue, and let me go first.”

If doctors keep a name for catching the detective-fever, then I was seized and burning. He went on among the hillocks, down to the open beach. I followed with my heart up in my throat, keeping a little behind, as if the quiet itself might crack under too near a foot.

I found myself almost on the very spot where Rosanna and I had spoken together when Mr. Franklin first came briskly upon us, arriving from London. My eyes watched the Sergeant; my mind, like a pot left too long unattended, boiled over into old remembrance. I could almost feel Rosanna’s hand again—cold and grateful—squeezing mine because I had spoken kindly. I could almost hear her voice telling me of the Shivering Sand and how it drew her against her will, the way some people are drawn by a tune they can’t stop hearing. I saw her face brighten, as it brightened when she first caught sight of Mr. Franklin stepping lively between the hummocks.

My spirits sank lower with every recollection. And the place itself—when I made myself look at it—did the rest.

The last of the evening light was failing. An awful calm lay over the little bay like a lid put down. The long heave of the ocean on the far sandbank made no sound; the inner water lay dim and breathless. Yellow-white patches of ooze floated on the dead surface, and scum and slime shone faintly where the last light struck the two great spits of rock that jutted out north and south. It was the turn of the tide; and even as I watched, the broad brown face of the quicksand began to dimple and quiver—the only moving thing in that horrid stillness.

The Sergeant started when he saw it. After watching a minute, he turned and came back to me.

“A treacherous place, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “And no signs of Rosanna Spearman anywhere on the beach.”

He led me lower, and I saw it with my own eyes: his footsteps and mine were the only marks on the sand.

“How does the fishing village bear from here?” he asked.

“Cobb’s Hole,” I said. “Due south, as near as may be.”

“I saw the girl walking northward from Cobb’s Hole,” he replied. “So she must have been coming towards this place. Is Cobb’s Hole on the other side of that point? And can we get to it now, at low water, by the beach?”

“Yes,” I said, to both.

“If you’ll excuse my suggesting it, we’ll step out briskly,” he answered. “I want to find where she left the shore before it’s dark.”

We had gone, I should say, two hundred yards, when Sergeant Cuff dropped to his knees on the sand as if seized with a sudden piety.

“There’s something to be said for your marine landscape after all,” he remarked. “Here are a woman’s footsteps, Mr. Betteredge. Let’s call them Rosanna’s until we can’t. Confused footsteps—purposely confused, I should say. Ah, poor soul, she knows the detective virtues of sand as well as I do! But hasn’t she been in too great a hurry to tread them out thoroughly? Here’s one step going from Cobb’s Hole; and here’s another going back. See that toe pointing to the water’s edge? And don’t you see two heel-marks further down, close to the water too? I don’t want to hurt your feelings, but I’m afraid Rosanna is sly. It looks as if she meant to get to that place you and I have just come from, without leaving marks. Shall we say she walked through the water from here to that ledge of rocks behind us, and came back the same way, and then took to the beach again where those two heel-marks are? Yes, we’ll say that. It fits with my notion of something under her cloak. Not something to destroy—if she meant destruction, why all these precautions? Something to hide is the better guess. Perhaps, if we go on to the cottage, we may find out what that something is.”

At the word cottage my fever cooled as suddenly as if cold sea-mist had blown through my mind.

“You don’t want me,” I said. “What good can I do?”

“The longer I know you, Mr. Betteredge,” he returned, “the more virtues I find. Modesty—oh dear me, how rare; and how much of that rarity you possess! If I go alone, tongues will be tied at my first question. If I go with you, I go introduced by a respected neighbour, and conversation flows of necessity. That is how it strikes me. How does it strike you?”

I tried to gain time by asking what cottage he meant.

When he described it, I knew it directly: the fisherman Yolland’s, with his wife and their grown son and daughter. Rosanna, I had said before, sometimes varied her walks to the Shivering Sand by calling on friends at Cobb’s Hole—the Yollands. She had made their acquaintance through the daughter, a poor afflicted girl with a misshapen foot, known among us as Limping Lucy. The two deformed girls, I suppose, had a fellow-feeling, as people do who share the same silent hunger. In any case, Rosanna and the Yollands always got on in a friendly, homely way.

The Sergeant tracing Rosanna to their cottage changed the flavour of the matter. If she had been with those worthy people, it looked like innocence, so far. I told myself—eagerly—that in helping the Sergeant I might be serving Rosanna instead of harming her. So I allowed myself to be convinced and went on with him.

We kept to the beach towards Cobb’s Hole, reading the sand as long as the light would let us.

At the cottage we found the fisherman and his son out in the boat; and Limping Lucy, always weak and weary, was resting upstairs. Mrs. Yolland received us alone in her kitchen. The place smelt of sea-salt dried into clothing, of old smoke in the rafters, and of spirits kept for company rather than cheer. When she heard Sergeant Cuff was a celebrated London man, she clapped a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes on the table, and stared at him as if he were a rare fish hauled up alive.

I sat quiet in a corner, waiting for the Sergeant to come to Rosanna. His roundabout way was roundabout beyond belief. How he steered from one thing to the next was a mystery then and is a mystery now; but I know where he began and where he landed. He began with the Royal Family, the Primitive Methodists, and the price of fish; and he got—by his dismal underground burrowing—to the loss of the Moonstone, the spitefulness of our first house-maid, and the hard usage of the women-servants towards Rosanna Spearman. Having reached that, he represented himself as inquiring partly to find the Diamond and partly to clear Rosanna from the unjust suspicions of her enemies. In a quarter of an hour, good Mrs. Yolland was persuaded she was speaking to Rosanna’s best friend, and she pressed the Sergeant to comfort his stomach and revive his spirits out of the Dutch bottle.

Thinking he was wasting breath on her, I sat and listened much as I have listened to a play—amused in spite of myself by his patience and his dreary little shots fired this way and that on the chance of striking the mark. But it ended always the same: everything to Rosanna’s credit, nothing to her prejudice. Mrs. Yolland talked nineteen to the dozen and trusted him completely. His last shot came when we looked at our watches and got up to go.

“I shall wish you good-night, ma’am,” said the Sergeant. “And I shall only add that Rosanna Spearman has a sincere well-wisher in me. But, oh dear me, she will never get on in her present place; and my advice is—leave it.”

“Bless your heart alive! she is going to leave it!” cried Mrs. Yolland.

Rosanna going to leave us—without a word first to my lady or to me? The statement pricked me like a pin through cloth. A doubt rose—whether the Sergeant’s random shot had hit home at last. I began to suspect my part in this business was not so harmless as I had comforted myself it was. I didn’t like lies in any shape; and though the Sergeant might think it all fair in his trade to net an honest woman with falsehoods, my Protestant conscience remembered that the father of lies is the Devil, and that mischief and the Devil seldom eat at separate tables.

Smelling mischief, I tried to draw the Sergeant out. He sat down again at once and asked for a little drop of comfort. Mrs. Yolland poured it. I went to the door, thoroughly uncomfortable, said I must bid them good-night—and yet stood there, unable to go.

“So she means to leave?” said the Sergeant. “What is she to do when she does? Sad, sad! The poor creature has no friends in the world except you and me.”

“Ah, but she has though!” said Mrs. Yolland. “She came in this evening, as I told you; and after talking a bit with my girl Lucy and me, she asked to go upstairs by herself into Lucy’s room. It’s the only room with pen and ink. ‘I want to write to a friend,’ she says, ‘and I can’t do it for the prying and peeping of the servants up at the house.’ Who the letter was to I can’t tell you. It must have been mortal long, by the time she stayed. I offered her a postage-stamp when she came down. She hadn’t the letter in her hand, and she wouldn’t take the stamp. Close, poor soul, about herself and her doings. But a friend she’s got somewhere, I’ll answer for that; and to that friend she’ll go.”

“Soon?” asked the Sergeant.

“As soon as she can,” said Mrs. Yolland.

That was enough. As chief of my lady’s household I couldn’t sit quiet and hear a servant’s going and coming treated as gossip.

“You must be mistaken,” I said. “If Rosanna meant to leave, she would have mentioned it first to me.”

“Mistaken?” cried Mrs. Yolland. “Why, only an hour ago she bought things for travelling—of my own self, Mr. Betteredge, in this very room. And that reminds me,” she added, fumbling in her pocket, “of something I’ve got on my mind about Rosanna and her money. Are you either likely to see her when you go back?”

“I’ll take a message with the greatest pleasure,” said Sergeant Cuff, before I could stop him.

Mrs. Yolland drew out a little heap of shillings and sixpences and counted them in her palm with a carefulness that would have suited a miser weighing flour by the grain. She offered the money to the Sergeant, looking sorry to part with it and yet determined.

“Might I ask you to give this back to Rosanna, with my love and respects?” she said. “She would pay me for what she took a fancy to—money’s welcome enough, I don’t deny. Still, I’m not easy taking the poor thing’s little savings. And to tell you truth, I don’t think my man would like to hear I took Rosanna’s money when he comes in tomorrow. Say she’s welcome to the things—as a gift. And don’t leave the money on the table,” she added, putting it down as if it burnt her fingers. “Times are hard, flesh is weak, and I might be tempted to put it back in my pocket.”

“Come along,” I said. “I can’t wait. I must go back.”

“I’ll follow you directly,” said the Sergeant.

For the second time I went to the door; for the second time I couldn’t bring myself to cross it.

“It’s a delicate matter, ma’am,” I heard the Sergeant say, “giving money back. You charged her cheap, I’m sure?”

“Cheap!” cried Mrs. Yolland. “Come and judge.”

She took up the candle and led him to a corner. Against my will I followed—drawn like a man drawn by the smell of something he shouldn’t taste. In the corner lay a heap of odds and ends—old metal mostly—salvage from wrecks that the fisherman had gathered but not yet sold to his liking. Mrs. Yolland plunged her hands into it and brought up an old japanned tin case with a lid and a hasp—the sort sailors use to keep maps and charts from wet.

“There!” she said. “Rosanna bought the fellow to that. ‘It will just do,’ she says, ‘to put my cuffs and collars in, and keep them from being crumpled in my box.’ One and ninepence, Mr. Cuff. As I live by bread, not a halfpenny more.”

“Dirt cheap!” said the Sergeant, with one of his heavy sighs.

He weighed the case in his hand. I thought I caught, faint and odd, a note or two of “The Last Rose of Summer” in him—as if the tune were a private way he had of tasting a discovery.

Then I knew. In the one place I had thought Rosanna safest, he had found something against her—and through me. My shame and anger were bitter; they lay in my mouth like burnt crust.

“That will do,” I said. “We really must go.”

Mrs. Yolland ignored me and plunged into the rubbish again. This time she came up with a dog-chain.

“Weigh it in your hand, sir,” she said to the Sergeant. “We had three of these, and Rosanna took two. ‘What can you want with a couple of dog’s chains?’ says I. ‘If I join them they’ll go round my box nicely,’ says she. ‘Rope’s cheapest,’ says I. ‘Chain’s surest,’ says she. ‘Who ever heard of a box corded with chain?’ says I. ‘Oh, Mrs. Yolland, don’t make objections!’ says she; ‘let me have my chains!’ A strange girl, Mr. Cuff—good as gold, and kinder than a sister to my Lucy—but always a little strange. There! I humoured her. Three and sixpence. On the word of an honest woman, three and sixpence.”

“Each?” asked the Sergeant.

“Both together,” said Mrs. Yolland. “Three and sixpence for the two.”

“Given away, ma’am,” said the Sergeant, shaking his head. “Clean given away.”

“There’s the money,” Mrs. Yolland said, edging back towards the silver on the table as if it had a pull on her like the tide. “The tin case and the dog-chains were all. One and ninepence and three and sixpence—five and three. With my love and respects—and I can’t find it in my conscience to take a poor girl’s savings.”

“I can’t find it in my conscience to give it back,” said Sergeant Cuff. “You’ve as good as made her a present—you have indeed.”

“Is that your sincere opinion?” Mrs. Yolland brightened at once.

“There can’t be a doubt,” he said. “Ask Mr. Betteredge.”

It was no use asking me. All they got out of me was, “Good-night.”

“Bother the money!” cried Mrs. Yolland; and with that she snatched the heap up and pocketed it in one motion. “It upsets one’s temper to see it lying there and nobody taking it!” She sat down with a thump and looked at the Sergeant as much as to say, It’s in my pocket now—get it out if you can.

This time I went out—fairly out—into the road back. Explain it as you may, I felt as if one or both had offended me past bearing. The cottage behind me held the clink of coins and the thick scent of gin; ahead lay the open air, salted and clean, with the far, steady breathing of the sea. Yet even that natural music seemed troubled in my ears—because the shore, with its treacherous hush and its whispering sand, had begun to feel like a place where human speech itself could be twisted and lost.

I had not taken three steps down the village when I heard the Sergeant behind me.

“Thank you for your introduction, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “I am indebted to the fisherman’s wife for an entirely new sensation. Mrs. Yolland has puzzled me.”
