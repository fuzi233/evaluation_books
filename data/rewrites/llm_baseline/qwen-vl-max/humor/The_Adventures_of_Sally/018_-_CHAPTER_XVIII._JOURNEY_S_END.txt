The sun, having done its best to be a decent fellow and stay put for just one more minute, finally gave up the ghost with a sigh that could almost be heard over the rustling of leaves. Darkness crept in like a shy guest who’d been invited but wasn’t quite sure he was wanted—though once inside, it made itself at home with impressive efficiency. To the west, beyond the trees, a lingering blush of orange clung stubbornly to the sky, as if refusing to admit defeat. Above the barn, a new moon hung like a silver spoon someone had forgotten to clean.

Sally stepped out onto the gravel, bowed three times with solemn precision (just in case luck was feeling particularly picky that evening), and took a deep breath. The air smelled of hay, damp earth, and the faint, sweet perfume of something that might have been a gardenia or possibly just nostalgia. Life, she decided, was rather excellent.

The darkness, now fully committed to its role, turned the sky into a velvet curtain studded with quiet stars. The state road—the great highway to Patchogue, Babylon, and other places of questionable importance—vanished from sight, leaving only silence and the occasional flicker of light from distant windows across the meadows. From the kennels came a single, drowsy bark, followed by Toto’s characteristic squeak—a sound somewhere between a challenge and a complaint.

Then, from the direction of the village, came the unmistakable thud-thud-thud of Ginger’s footsteps. He was returning from his errands: provisions, evening papers, and wool for the sweater Sally was knitting (a project she had abandoned after two rows because she couldn’t decide whether to make it blue or “not blue”).

Sally couldn’t see him yet, but she knew exactly when he’d arrive. She felt that familiar warmth spreading through her chest—the kind of cozy contentment that had become her nightly ritual.

“Ginger!” she called. “What ho!”

Toto, ever vigilant, scampered down the drive with another dramatic squeak, only to be met with a cold stare. Ginger, despite being fond of dogs in general, had never developed any particular affection for Toto. He’d protested when Sally, finding Mrs. Meecher distraught over her pet’s sudden lethargy, begged him to take the dog in.

“It’s wonderful what you’ve done for Toto, angel,” Sally said as Ginger approached, dodging Toto’s enthusiastic leaps with the grace of a man avoiding a charging badger.

“He’s a different dog.”

“Bit of luck for him,” Ginger replied. “In all the years I was at Mrs. Meecher’s, I never saw him move faster than a dignified stroll. Now he runs about like a lunatic.”

“The blighter had been overeating since birth,” Ginger declared. “That was all that was wrong with him. A little judicious dieting put him right. We’ll be able to ship him back next week.”

“I shall quite miss him.”

“I nearly missed him this morning—with a shoe,” Ginger added. “He was up on the kitchen table, devouring bacon like it was his life’s mission. I took steps.”

“My cave-man!” Sally murmured. “I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak. Ginger, what an evening!”

“Good Lord!” Ginger exclaimed as they stepped into the kitchen light. “Now what?” He stopped, stared at her, and blinked. “Do you know you’re looking prettier than when I left for the village?”

Sally hugged his arm. “Beloved!” she said. “Did you get the chops?”

Ginger froze. “Oh, my aunt! I clean forgot them!”

“Oh, Ginger, you are an old chump.” She patted his shoulder. “Well, you’ll have to go in for a little judicious dieting, like Toto.”

“I say, I’m most awfully sorry. I got the wool.”

“If you think I’m going to eat wool…”

“Isn’t there anything in the house?”

“Vegetables and fruit.”

“Fine! But, of course, if you want chops…”

“Not at all. I’m spiritual. Besides, people say vegetables are good for blood pressure or something. Of course you forgot the mail, too?”

“Absolutely not! I was on to it like a knife. Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies.”

“No! Ginger, we are getting on!”

“Pretty bloated,” Ginger agreed, puffing out his chest. “Pretty bloated. We’ll be able to get that two-seater if things keep buzzing along like this. There was a letter for you. Here it is.”

“It’s from Fillmore,” Sally said, examining the envelope. “And about time, too. I haven’t had a word from him for months.”

She sat down and opened it. Ginger, climbing onto the table like a man who’d already decided he was going to live here permanently, settled into a comfortable position and unfolded his evening paper. After a quick glance at the sporting page, he lowered it and let his gaze rest on Sally’s bent head, filled with a serene, almost holy satisfaction.

Though married for nearly a year, Ginger still wandered through life like a man who’d stumbled into a dream and hadn’t quite convinced himself it wasn’t a hallucination. He’d seen many things that looked good from afar—women, opportunities, fine hats—but none had survived closer inspection. Except this. Marriage. With Sally. It was the only thing in the world that seemed to come without a catch. No hidden clauses, no unexpected fees, no sudden collapse into absurdity. Just pure, unadulterated bliss.

His eyes softened as he watched her.

Then Sally burst into laughter. “Ginger, look at this!”

He reached down and took the slip of paper she handed him. Printed in bold, cheerful letters, it read:

Ginger frowned. “What is it?”

“It’s Fillmore.”

“How do you mean?”

Sally giggled. “Fillmore and Gladys have started a restaurant in Pittsburgh.”

“A restaurant!” Ginger gasped. Though he knew Fillmore’s managerial career had ended in a spectacular crash—something involving a stock market prediction and a goat—he still held a certain reverence for his brother-in-law. To think that Fillmore Nicholas, the Man of Destiny, should descend to running a *restaurant*—and a *small* one at that—seemed almost indecent.

Sally, however, was thrilled. “It’s the most splendid idea!” she declared. “It really does look as if Fillmore’s going to amount to something at last. They started small—just making pork-pies…”

“Why Popp?” Ginger interrupted, baffled.

“Just a trade name, silly. Gladys is a wonderful cook, and she made the pies while Fillmore toddled around selling them. They did so well they opened a proper restaurant—and it’s a success! Listen to this.”

She giggled again and turned the page. “Where is it? Oh yes! ‘…sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a really big scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast extension of this venture of ours, and in a very short time I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the big cities, each in charge of a manager and each offering as a special feature, in addition to the usual restaurant cuisine, these Popp’s Outstanding Pork-pies of ours. That done, and having established all these branches as going concerns, I shall sail for England and introduce Popp’s Pork-pies there…’”

“Isn’t he a little wonder!”

“Dashed brainy chap,” Ginger said, nodding slowly. “Always said so.”

“I must say I was rather uneasy when I read that,” Sally admitted. “I’ve seen so many of Fillmore’s Big Ideas. That’s always the way with him. He gets something good, then goes and overdoes it until it bursts. But now that he’s got Gladys to look after him, it’s all right. She’s added a postscript. Just four words, but oh! how comforting to a sister’s heart. ‘Yes, I don’t think!’ is what she says—and I don’t know when I’ve read anything more cheering. Thank heaven, she’s got poor dear Fillmore well in hand.”

“Pork-pies,” Ginger mused, rubbing his stomach. “I wish he’d sent us one of the outstanding little chaps. I could do with it.”

Sally stood up, ruffled his red hair, and smiled. “Poor old Ginger! I knew you’d never be able to stick it. Come on—it’s a lovely night. Let’s walk to the village and revel at the inn. We’re going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can afford it.”

They walked off together, hand in hand, leaving Toto to guard the gate with a look of profound disappointment.
