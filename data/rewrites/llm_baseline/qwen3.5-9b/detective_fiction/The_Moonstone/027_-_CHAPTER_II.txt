Mr. Godfrey followed the announcement of his name—with characteristic precision—at exactly the right moment. He arrived neither so promptly as to startle us nor so tardily as to force an awkward pause and leave the door standing open. One finds the true mark of a complete Christian in the seamless integration of their daily routine; this dear gentleman was thoroughly complete.

"Go to Miss Verinder," instructed my Aunt, turning to the servant, "and inform her that Mr. Ablewhite has arrived."

We both immediately inquired after his health, asking in unison if he had recovered his composure following the terrifying ordeal of the previous week. With impeccable timing, he managed to respond to us simultaneously. Lady Verinder received his verbal reply; I was treated to his radiant smile.

"What," he cried with boundless tenderness, "have I done to merit such overwhelming sympathy? My dear Aunt! My dear Miss Clack! I have simply been mistaken for someone else. I was merely blindfolded, merely strangled, and merely tossed flat upon my back onto a thin carpet covering a particularly unforgiving floor. Consider how much worse it could have been! I might have been murdered; I might have been robbed. What have I lost? Only 'Nervous Force'—a concept the law does not recognize as property. Strictly speaking, I have lost absolutely nothing. Had it been my choice, I would have kept this adventure to myself; I detest all this fuss and publicity. However, since Mr. Luker made *his* injuries public, mine have inevitably been proclaimed in turn. I have become the exclusive property of the newspapers until the gentle reader grows weary of the subject. I am profoundly weary of it myself. May the gentle reader soon find solace in my exhaustion! And how fares dear Rachel? Still reveling in the gaieties of London? I am delighted to hear it! Miss Clack, I require all your indulgence. I am dreadfully behind schedule regarding my Committee Work and my beloved Ladies. Yet, I sincerely hope to drop by the Mothers'-Small-Clothes next week. Did you achieve encouraging progress at Monday's meeting? Was the Board optimistic about future prospects? And are we adequately supplied with trousers?"

The celestial gentleness of his smile rendered his apologies irresistible, while the richness of his deep voice added an indescribable charm to the practical inquiry he had just posed to me. Truth be told, we were nearly *too* well-supplied with trousers; we were completely overwhelmed by them. I was on the verge of expressing this sentiment when the door swung open again, introducing an element of worldly disruption in the form of Miss Verinder.

She approached Mr. Godfrey with a speed that bordered on unladylike, her hair shockingly disheveled and her face flushed in a manner I deemed unbecoming.

"I am charmed to see you, Godfrey," she said, addressing him with the casual familiarity of one young man speaking to another. "I wish you had brought Mr. Luker along. You and he (given our current state of excitement) are the two most fascinating men in all London. It is morbid to say so; it is unhealthy; it is precisely the sort of thing that causes a well-regulated mind like Miss Clack's to instinctively shudder. Never mind that. Tell me the entire Northumberland Street story immediately. I suspect the newspapers have omitted several details."

Even dear Mr. Godfrey participates in the fallen nature inherited from Adam—it is a minuscule fraction of our human legacy, but alas, he possesses it. I confess it pained me to witness him take Rachel's hand in both of his own and lay it softly against the left side of his waistcoat. It served as a direct encouragement to her reckless speech and her insolent disregard for me.

"Dearest Rachel," he replied, using the same voice that had previously moved me when discussing our prospects and our trousers, "the newspapers have informed you of everything—and they have done so far more effectively than I could."

"Godfrey believes we all exaggerate the matter," my Aunt interjected. "He has just stated he prefers not to discuss it."

"Why?" She demanded, her eyes flashing suddenly as she lifted her gaze to meet Mr. Godfrey's.

On his part, he looked down at her with an indulgence that was both injudicious and undeserved, compelling me to intervene.

"Rachel, darling!" I remonstrated gently, "true greatness and true courage are invariably modest."

"You are a very good fellow in your own way, Godfrey," she retorted, ignoring me entirely and continuing to address her cousin as if they were peers. "However, I am convinced you are not great. I do not believe you possess any extraordinary courage, and I am firmly persuaded that if you ever did possess modesty, your numerous admirers relieved you of that virtue many years ago. You have a private reason for avoiding discussion of your Northumberland Street adventure, and I intend to discover it."

"My reason is the simplest imaginable and the easiest to acknowledge," he answered, enduring her with patience. "I am tired of the subject."

"You are tired of the subject? My dear Godfrey, I am about to make a remark."

"What is it?"

"You live far too much in the company of women. Consequently, you have cultivated two very detrimental habits. You have learned to speak nonsense with seriousness, and you have developed a penchant for telling fibs solely for the pleasure of deception. You cannot walk straight among your lady-worshippers. I intend to make you walk straight with *me*. Come, sit down. I am overflowing with direct questions, and I expect you to be equally brimming with direct answers."

She physically dragged him across the room to a chair by the window, ensuring the light would illuminate his face. I deeply regret having to report such language and describe such conduct. Yet, hemmed in as I am between Mr. Franklin Blake's financial demands on one side and my own sacred commitment to truth on the other, what am I to do? I glanced at my Aunt. She sat motionless, seemingly devoid of any inclination to intervene. I had never witnessed such torpor in her before. Perhaps it was a reaction to the trying ordeal she endured in the countryside. Regardless, it was an unpleasant symptom to note, especially considering her age and her autumnal exuberance of figure.

Meanwhile, Rachel had settled herself at the window with our amiable, forbearing—and *too* forbearing—Mr. Godfrey. She launched into the string of threats she had earlier made, paying no attention whatsoever to her mother or myself, as though we were invisible.

"Have the police taken any action, Godfrey?"

"Absolutely nothing."

"It is certain, I suppose, that the three men who trapped you were the same three who later trapped Mr. Luker?"

"Humanly speaking, my dear Rachel, there can be no doubt of it."

"And not a single trace of them has been discovered?"

"Not a trace."

"It is rumored—is it not?—that these three men are the three Indians who visited our estate in the country."

"Some people speculate so."

"Do you believe so?"

"My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me before I could discern their faces. I know absolutely nothing of the matter. How can I offer an opinion?"

Even the angelic gentleness of Mr. Godfrey was beginning to crack under the relentless interrogation. Whether unbridled curiosity or ungovernable dread drove Miss Verinder's questions, I refrain from speculation. I merely report that when Mr. Godfrey attempted to rise after providing his answer, she seized him by the shoulders and shoved him back into his chair. Oh, do not claim this was immodest! Do not even suggest that the recklessness of guilty terror explains such behavior! We must not judge others. My Christian friends, indeed, indeed, indeed, we must not judge others!

She continued her questioning, unabashed. Earnest biblical scholars might recall, as I did, the blinded children of the devil who proceeded with their orgies, shameless, in the days before the Flood.

"I require information regarding Mr. Luker, Godfrey."

"I am again unfortunate, Rachel. No man knows less about Mr. Luker than I do."

"You never encountered him prior to your accidental meeting at the bank?"

"Never."

"Have you seen him since?"

"Yes. We have been examined together, as well as separately, to assist the police."

"Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt obtained from his banker—was he not? What did the receipt cover?"

"For a valuable gem he had entrusted to the bank's safekeeping."

"That is what the newspapers claim. It suffices for the general reader, but it is insufficient for me. Must the banker's receipt have specified the identity of the gem?"

"The banker's receipt, Rachel—as I have heard it described—contained no such detail. It stated: 'A valuable gem, belonging to Mr. Luker; deposited by Mr. Luker; sealed with Mr. Luker's seal; and to be released only upon Mr. Luker's personal application.' That was the form, and that is all I know."

She paused briefly after his response. She glanced at her mother, sighed, then returned her gaze to Mr. Godfrey.

"Certain private matters of ours at home," she noted, "appear to have leaked into the newspapers?"

"I grieve to say, it is so."
