The boat ride offered a peculiar tranquility, a stillness that surprised Archer as much as it steadied him. By any conventional measure, the day had been a ludicrous failure; he had not kissed Madame Olenska's hand, nor drawn a single word from her promising future contact. Yet, for a man consumed by unrequited desire and facing an indefinite separation from his passion, he felt a humbling calm. It was the equilibrium she maintained between duty to others and honesty to herself that had stirred and settled him—a balance not calculated, but born of raw sincerity. This filled him with a tender awe, now that the immediate peril had passed, leading him to thank fate that no vanity, no urge to perform before sophisticated witnesses, had tempted him to push her further. Even after their final handshake at the Fall River station and his solitary departure, the conviction remained: he had salvaged far more from the encounter than he had lost.

Back at the club, Archer retreated to the empty library, replaying every second of their time together. Clarity sharpened with scrutiny. If she chose to return to Europe—to her husband—it would not be because her old life tempted her, even on new terms. She would leave only if she sensed she had become a temptation to Archer, a lure pulling him away from the standards they had jointly erected. Her strategy was to remain near him until he asked her closer; it fell to him to keep her precisely there, safe but secluded.

These thoughts accompanied him onto the train, enclosing him in a golden haze where fellow passengers appeared remote and indistinct. He felt a profound isolation, as if speech would render him unintelligible to strangers. Waking the next morning to a stifling September day in New York, the heat-withered faces streaming past the window remained blurred. Suddenly, however, one face detached itself, forcing its way into his consciousness. It was the young man he had noticed the previous day exiting the Parker House, a figure who did not fit the mold of the typical American traveler.

The realization struck him anew. The young man stood with the dazed air of a foreigner cast adrift in the harsh mercies of American transit, then approached Archer, lifting his hat. "Surely, Monsieur, we met in London?"

"To be sure! In London!" Archer grasped his hand, curiosity mixing with sympathy. "Did you actually make it here, after all?" he exclaimed, regarding the sharp, haggard little face of young Carfry's French tutor.

"Oh, I got here—yes," M. Rivière smiled, his lips drawn tight. "But not for long; I return the day after tomorrow." He clutched his light valise in one neatly gloved hand, gazing into Archer's face with an anxiety that bordered on appeal.

"I wonder, Monsieur, since I've had the good luck to run across you, if I might—"

"I was just going to suggest it: come to luncheon. Downtown, I mean. If you look me up in my office, I'll take you to a very decent restaurant in that quarter."

M. Rivière was visibly touched. "You're too kind. But I was only going to ask if you could tell me how to reach some sort of conveyance. There are no porters, and no one here seems to listen—"

"I know: our American stations must surprise you. When you ask for a porter, they offer chewing gum. But if you'll come along, I'll extricate you; and you must really lunch with me, you know."

After a barely perceptible hesitation, the young man replied, with profuse thanks but lacking complete conviction, that he was already engaged. Yet once they reached the comparative reassurance of the street, he asked if he might call that afternoon.

Archer, comfortable in the midsummer leisure of his office, fixed an hour and scribbled his address. The Frenchman pocketed it with reiterated thanks and a wide flourish of his hat before boarding a horse-car. Archer watched him depart and walked away.

Punctual at the appointed hour, M. Rivière appeared—shaved, smoothed, yet unmistakably drawn and serious. Archer sat alone in his office. Before accepting the seat offered, the young man began abruptly, "I believe I saw you, sir, yesterday in Boston."

The statement was trivial, and Archer prepared to assent when his words were halted by something mysterious yet illuminating in the visitor's insistent gaze.

"It is extraordinary, very extraordinary," M. Rivière continued, "that we should have met in the circumstances in which I find myself."

"What circumstances?" Archer asked, wondering crudely if the young man sought funds.

M. Rivière studied him with tentative eyes. "I have come, not to seek employment as I mentioned previously, but on a special mission—"

"Ah—" Archer exclaimed. In a flash, the two encounters coalesced in his mind. He paused to absorb the situation suddenly lit up for him, while M. Rivière remained silent, aware that his admission sufficed.

"A special mission," Archer repeated eventually.

The young Frenchman opened his palms, raising them slightly. They stared across the office desk until Archer roused himself to say, "Do sit down." M. Rivière bowed, took a distant chair, and waited.

"It was about this mission that you wished to consult me?" Archer finally asked.

M. Rivière bent his head. "Not in my own behalf: on that score, I—I have fully dealt with myself. I should like—if I may—to speak to you about the Countess Olenska."

Archer had known the words were coming; when they arrived, they sent blood rushing to his temples as if he had been caught by a bent branch in a thicket.

"And on whose behalf," he said, "do you wish to act?"

M. Rivière met the question steadily. "Well—I might say *hers*, if it did not sound like a liberty. Shall I say instead: on behalf of abstract justice?"

Archer regarded him ironically. "In other words: you are Count Olenski's messenger?"

He saw the darker reflection of his blush in M. Rivière's sallow countenance. "Not to *you*, Monsieur. If I come to you, it is on quite other grounds."

"What right have you, in the circumstances, to *be* on any other ground?" Archer retorted. "If you're an emissary, you're an emissary."

The young man considered. "My mission is over: as far as the Countess Olenska goes, it has failed."

"I can't help that," Archer rejoined on the same ironic note.

"No: but you can help—" M. Rivière paused, turning his hat in his still carefully gloved hands, peering into its lining before looking back at Archer. "You can help, Monsieur, I am convinced, to make it equally a failure with her family."

Archer pushed back his chair and stood. "Well—and by God I will!" he exclaimed. He stood with hands in pockets, staring down wrathfully at the little Frenchman, whose face, though he too had risen, remained an inch or two below Archer's eyes.

M. Rivière paled to his normal hue; paler than that, his complexion could hardly turn.

"Why the devil," Archer explosively continued, "should you have thought—since I suppose you're appealing to me on the ground of my relationship to Madame Olenska—that I should take a view contrary to the rest of her family?"

The change in M. Rivière's expression served as his only answer for a moment. His look shifted from timidity to absolute distress; for a young man of his usually resourceful demeanor, it would have been difficult to appear more disarmed. "Oh, Monsieur—"

"I can't imagine," Archer continued, "why you should have come to me when there are others so much nearer to the Countess; still less why you thought I should be more accessible to the arguments I suppose you were sent over with."

M. Rivière absorbed this onslaught with disconcerting humility. "The arguments I want to present to you, Monsieur, are my own and not those I was sent over with."

"Then I see still less reason for listening to them."

M. Rivière looked into his hat again, as if weighing whether these words constituted a sufficient hint to don his hat and depart. Then he spoke with sudden decision. "Monsieur—will you tell me one thing? Is it my right to be here that you question? Or do you perhaps believe the whole matter to be already closed?"

His quiet insistence made Archer feel the clumsiness of his own bluster. M. Rivière had succeeded in imposing himself; Archer, reddening slightly, dropped back into his chair and signaled for the young man to sit.

"I beg your pardon: but why isn't the matter closed?"

M. Rivière gazed back with anguish. "You do, then, agree with the rest of the family that, in face of the new proposals I have brought, it is hardly possible for Madame Olenska not to return to her husband?"

"Good God!" Archer exclaimed; his visitor emitted a low murmur of confirmation.

"Before seeing her, I saw—at Count Olenski's request—Mr. Lovell Mingott, with whom I had several talks before going to Boston. I understand that he represents his mother's view; and that Mrs. Manson Mingott's influence is great throughout her family."
