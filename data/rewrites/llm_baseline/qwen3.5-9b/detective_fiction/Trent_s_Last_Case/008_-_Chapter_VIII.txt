The coroner, acutely aware that for a single day he stepped out of the shadows of a provincial solicitor and into the blinding glare of public scrutiny, had resolved to meet this fleeting eminence with distinction. He was a portly man of jovial disposition, possessing a profound fascination for the theatrical elements of his office. News of Manderson's mysterious demise within his jurisdiction had elevated him to the status of the happiest coroner in England. Possessing a commendable aptitude for organizing facts, he bolstered this skill with a copiousness of impressive rhetoric that allowed him to mold juries like soft clay, occasionally obscuring dubious interpretations of evidentiary rules with sheer volume.

The proceedings took place in a long, unfurnished chamber recently annexed to the hotel, originally designed as a ballroom or concert hall. A regiment of reporters was entrenched in the front rows, while witnesses awaited their turn in chairs set to one side of the table where the coroner presided. Flanking him on the opposite side sat the jury, arranged in a double row; their hair was plastered flat, and they maintained a spurious air of casual ease. The remainder of the space was filled by an undistinguished public, listening in awed silence to the opening solemnities. Among the press, seasoned veterans murmured amongst themselves. Those who recognized Trent by sight quietly informed the others that he was absent from the courtroom.

The identity of the deceased was established by his wife, the first witness called. After probing briefly into the health and circumstances of the departed, the coroner guided her through an account of the last time she had seen her husband alive. Mrs. Manderson was led through her testimony with the sympathy every man feels for such a figure of profound grief. Before speaking, she raised her thick veil, revealing a face of extreme pallor and unbroken composure that struck a singular chord. This was not an impression of hardness. Rather, the first quality perceived was an interesting femininity. She was not enigmatic; it was evident only that the force of a powerful character was laboring to master the emotions of her situation. Occasionally, she dabbed her eyes with a handkerchief, yet her voice remained low and clear to the very end.

Her husband, she stated, had ascended to his bedroom around his customary hour for retiring on Sunday night. His quarters were effectively a dressing-annex to her own suite, connected by a door typically left ajar throughout the night. Both rooms opened onto a central passage via separate doors. Manderson had always favored the utmost simplicity in his sleeping arrangements, preferring a small chamber. She had not been fully awake when he arrived but had been half-roused, as often occurred, when the light was switched on in his room. She had spoken to him, though her memory of the exact words was hazy due to her heavy drowsiness. She recalled, however, that he had mentioned taking a moonlight drive in the car. She believed she had asked if he had enjoyed the run and what time it was, the latter query stemming from a feeling that she had slept only briefly and an expectation that he would be out late.

"In response to your question regarding the time," she recounted, "he told me it was half-past eleven. He then remarked that he had changed his mind about going for a run."

"Did he offer a reason?" the coroner inquired.

"Yes," the lady replied. "He did explain. I remember distinctly what he said because—" she paused, a flicker of confusion crossing her features.

"Because—" the coroner prompted gently.

"Because my husband was not, as a rule, communicative concerning his business affairs," the witness answered, lifting her chin with a faint, defiant touch. "He rarely deemed them worth my interest and seldom referred to them. That is precisely why I was surprised when he informed me he had dispatched Mr. Marlowe to Southampton to retrieve crucial information from someone departing for Paris on the following day's boat. He noted that Mr. Marlowe could accomplish this easily provided he met with no accident. He further stated that he had initially started the car but had walked back home a mile or so, feeling significantly better for the exertion."

"Did he mention anything else?"

"Nothing, as far as I recall," the witness said. "I was extremely sleepy and drifted off again shortly thereafter. My sole remaining memory is of my husband extinguishing his light. That is all. I never saw him alive again."

"And did you hear nothing during the night?"

"No," she affirmed. "I did not wake until my maid brought my tea at seven o'clock in the morning. As per her custom, she closed the door leading to my husband's room, and I assumed he was still inside. He required considerable sleep; he frequently slumbered until late in the morning. I ate breakfast in my sitting-room. It was approximately ten o'clock when I learned that my husband's body had been discovered."

The witness lowered her head, waiting silently for dismissal. Yet, the coroner's voice cut through the silence.

"Mrs. Manderson." His tone was sympathetic but carried a distinct undercurrent of firmness. "Given these tragic circumstances, the question I am about to pose must undoubtedly be painful; nevertheless, it is my duty to ask it. Is it true that your relationship with your late husband had, for some time past, ceased to be one of mutual affection and confidence? Is it a fact that an estrangement existed between you?"

The lady straightened, facing her interrogator as color rose in her cheeks.

"If this inquiry is necessary," she declared with cold clarity, "I shall answer it so that there can be no misunderstanding. During the final months of my husband's life, his attitude toward me caused me great anxiety and sorrow. He had altered; he became exceedingly reserved and seemed mistrustful. I saw him far less than before; he appeared to prefer solitude. I cannot offer any explanation for this change. I attempted to counteract it, doing everything within my power to uphold my own dignity, as I believed appropriate. Something lay between us, unknown to me, and he never revealed it. My own stubborn pride prevented me from asking directly what it was; I merely strove to treat him exactly as I always had, within the limits he permitted. I suppose I shall never know now what it was."

Having spoken these final words, her voice trembling despite her rigid self-control, she drew down her veil and stood erect and quiet. One member of the jury ventured a question, hesitating noticeably.

"Was there never anything of the nature of... 'words' between you and your husband, ma'am?"

"Never." The word was spoken colorlessly, yet everyone sensed that a crude misjudgment of the complexities involved in a woman of Mrs. Manderson's stature had incurred a severe penalty.

The coroner then asked if she knew of any other matters that might have been troubling her husband's mind recently. Mrs. Manderson knew of none. The coroner signaled the end of her ordeal, and the veiled lady made her way to the door.

The collective attention, which lingered on her for a few moments, swiftly shifted to Martin, whom the coroner had summoned next. It was at this precise juncture that Trent emerged from the doorway, edging his way into the vast room. However, his gaze did not rest on Martin. Instead, he observed the well-proportioned figure approaching rapidly along a cleared path through the crowd, his expression gloomy. He started slightly as he stepped aside from the entrance, offering a slight bow, only to hear Mrs. Manderson address him by name in a low whisper.

He followed her a step or two into the hallway.

"I wished to ask you," she said, her voice now weak and strangely fractured, "if you would lend me your arm for part of the way to the house. I could not locate my uncle near the door, and suddenly I felt rather faint... I shall feel better in the fresh air..."

"No, no; I cannot stay here—please, Mr. Trent!" she cried as he began to make an obvious suggestion to remain. "I must return to the house."

Her hand tightened momentarily on his arm, as if, despite her frailty, she possessed the strength to drag him from the scene; then, leaning heavily upon him, she walked slowly from the hotel, beneath the shade of the oaks, toward White Gables.

Trent moved in silence, his thoughts whirling and dancing insanely to a chaotic chorus of "Fool! Fool!" Everything he alone knew, everything he guessed and suspected regarding this affair, surged through his brain in a rout. Yet, the touch of her unnerved hand upon his arm never left his consciousness, filling him with an exaltation that both enraged and bewildered him. Still cursing himself furiously behind the mask of conventional solicitude he presented to the lady, he escorted her to the house and watched her sink onto a couch in the morning-room.

Raising her veil, she thanked him gravely and frankly, her eyes holding a look of sincere gratitude. She was much improved now, she said, adding that a cup of tea would perform a miracle. She hoped she had not detained him from something important. She expressed shame at her behavior, noting that while she thought she could endure the process, she had not anticipated the sting of those final questions.

"I am glad you did not hear me," she said when he explained the situation. "But of course, you will read it all in the reports. It shook me so terribly to have to speak of that," she added simply.
