The desert sprawled before them like a canvas of gold and crimson, the fading light of day casting long shadows across the dunes as they made their way towards the distant silhouette of the Suliman Berg. Our party had been on the move for days, the weight of the tusks from the elephants we'd killed weighing heavily on our minds, yet it was a prize worth the trouble – a magnificent haul of ivory, averaging forty pounds per tusk, with the great bull's tusks scaling an astonishing one hundred and seventy pounds the pair. We'd buried Khiva beneath an ancient acacia tree, his assegai planted beside him, a token of respect for the brave warrior who'd fallen in our service.

As we made camp, the scent of smoke and roasting meat wafted through the air, mingling with the sweet tang of the desert blooms. Good busied himself with the arrangements, while I took Sir Henry aside, climbing to the crest of the slope opposite our camp to survey the vast expanse of the desert. The air was alive with the songs of crickets and the distant call of a jackal, the silence broken only by the soft crunch of sand beneath our feet. Far off, the blue outlines of the Suliman Berg rose like giants from the earth, their peaks still capped with a wispy veil of cloud.

"There," I said, my voice barely above a whisper, "there lies the gateway to Solomon's Mines, but I fear we'll never find the entrance."

Sir Henry's eyes narrowed, his jaw set in a determined line. "My brother should be within those walls, and I'll find a way to reach him," he said, his words carrying an unwavering conviction that stirred something deep within me.

I nodded, my thoughts racing with the possibilities, and turned to make my way back to camp, but it was then that I noticed Umbopa standing behind us, his eyes fixed intently on the distant mountains. He spoke when he saw I'd noticed him, his voice low and smooth as he addressed Sir Henry, using the native word "Incubu" to address his master.

The Zulu's words were laced with a quiet confidence that bordered on familiarity, and I felt a spark of irritation at his boldness. It was one thing for the natives to have a name for us among themselves, but to use it to our faces was another matter altogether. Yet, there was something about Umbopa that commanded respect – a quiet authority that seemed to emanate from him
