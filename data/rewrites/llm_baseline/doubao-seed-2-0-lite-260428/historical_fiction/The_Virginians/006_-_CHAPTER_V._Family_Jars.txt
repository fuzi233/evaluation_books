As Harry Warrington unfolded the plain, unvarnished story of his life at the Virginian plantation to his newly discovered kinswoman Madam Bernstein, the sharp-witted, world-travelled lady of the court formed her own quiet judgment of the people and events he described. If her opinion was not always entirely favourable, one can scarce blame her: no life, after all, is free of imperfection, and the city-bred woman recoiled a little from the thought of her half-sister Rachel’s rough, unpolished country existence.

But Madam Rachel Warrington knew no other life, and was perfectly content with hers. She was a brisk, sharp little matriarch who ruled every inch of her vast Castlewood estate like a minor queen. No pie was baked in the great house without her oversight, she set the maids to their spinning, checked the kitchen maids’ work, trotted the fields on her pony to monitor overseers and enslaved hands tending the tobacco and corn crops. When a slave fell ill, she would trudge out to their quarters in any weather to tend them, armed with an ancient book of home remedies, a closet full of distilled elixirs, and a medicine chest that was the terror of every neighbour for miles: no one wanted to fall sick and face her bitter, pungent decoctions and unyielding pills.

A hundred years before this story, there were barely any proper towns in Virginia. Every gentry plantation was its own tiny, self-sufficient village, housing the family, their workers, and dependants. The neighbouring squires were mostly cash-poor, free-spending potentates who lived rough, kept ragged liveried servants, threw open their doors to every stranger, and were fiercely proud of their good lineage and addicted to field sports. Many a one of these gentlemen would have been only too happy to take the widowed Madam Warrington as his wife, to claim a share of her estate for his own – but Rachel held her Esmond birth as far too exalted for any of them, and gossiping local ladies joked she was holding out even for the Duke of Cumberland, rumoured to be coming over as Viceroy of the Americas. She would respond with great dignity that men of as high rank as the Duke had already sued for the hand of an Esmond.

Her right-hand companion was Mrs. Mountain, widow of an ensign who had been the late Colonel Warrington’s old regimental friend. The two girls had met at Kensington Academy, where Fanny Parker – later Mountain – had defended the young, haughty little Rachel from teasing English schoolmates. They had both married ensigns in the same regiment, and remained the dearest of friends. When Mr. Mountain died penniless, leaving his wife and infant daughter with nothing, Captain Franks brought her across the ocean on his ship the *Young Rachel* to Castlewood.

There was more than enough room at the big plantation house, and Mrs. Mountain made it far livelier. She played cards with her mistress, gave the boys music lessons, saw to the guest chambers and the household linen presses, and was a kind, jolly widow who turned down every local suitor that asked for her hand. One husband who had squandered both his fortune and hers before dying, she said, was more than enough for a lifetime. The two women quarrelled constantly, but loved each other dearly, making up their spats almost as quickly as they started them, and both had adored the old Colonel, who had prized Mrs. Mountain’s cheerfulness during his final illness.

Rachel was endlessly amused to tease her many suitors, telling half of them she was sure they had really come to court Mrs. Mountain first, and offering to put in a good word for them. If any gentleman insisted his addresses were for her alone, she would drop him her gravest curtsy and inform him that the daughter of the Marquis of Esmond had dedicated herself to her people and her sons, and had no intention of remarrying. She was a little Virginian Queen Elizabeth in her own small domain, lapping up flattery as her due, much to the amusement of local wags: Jack Firebrace of Henrico County won months of free board at Castlewood by reciting stolen verses in her honour, and Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania won a fifty-hogshead bet when he tricked her into declaring a silly, mock order of chivalry for her favourites.

The twin boys reacted very differently to their mother’s foibles. Oldest and quieter George seethed at the sight of these sycophants, and used his sharp juvenile satire to try to expose them, much to his mother’s amusement, who would sigh and say the Esmonds had always had a jealous, proud streak. Easy-going Harry, meanwhile, was only too happy to run off hunting, fishing, and cock-fighting with the lot of them, enjoying every bit of the rough country fun.

One winter, after their first tutor was dismissed, Madam Warrington took the boys to Williamsburg for a proper college education, and there the whole family fell under the sway of the famous travelling preacher Mr. Whitefield. The Anglican church in Virginia was staffed by imported parsons of very poor calibre – mostly disgraced, insolvent men who had fled debts or trouble at home – and none of the sleepy local clergy had ever stirred the colonists half as much as Whitefield’s thundering, fiery sermons. For a while, the twins were so caught up in the excitement they sang hymns day and night, waiting for the famous “holy call” to the ministry that every pious Protestant in England was talking about at the time, and would likely have ended up wearing black clerical coats instead of military epaulettes if Whitefield had stayed. But the preacher soon moved on to carry his mission across the colonies, and promised Madam Warrington he would send her a special tutor from England, one who could teach her sons both secular learning and far more precious spiritual truth.

In due course, the chosen “vessel” arrived. Young Mr. Ward had a voice almost as loud as Whitefield’s, and could preach for hours on end. The great hall of Castlewood echoed night and day with his exhortations, and the enslaved household workers crowded the doors and porches to listen, until they were so deeply moved they sang thunderous hymns that could be heard all the way across the Potomac. The old Colonel, who had always been deeply suspicious of clergymen and said he would only ever argue with a parson over a game of backgammon, would have never stood for such noise in his house.

Ward was a handsome, glib young man, and Madam Warrington soon came to adore his sermons far more than she had even Whitefield’s. She pressed her young favourite, Major George Washington, again and again to come stay at Castlewood and benefit from Ward’s spiritual guidance – but the major always contrived to find urgent business that called him away before the evening sermon hour. The twins, however, quickly saw through Ward’s facade: he was a poor scholar, dull, and ill-bred to boot. George already knew far more Latin and Greek than his supposed tutor, and caught him making constant blunders and false quantities. Harry, meanwhile, took to mimicking Ward’s greedy way of eating and his drawling preaching tone, so well that even Mrs. Mountain and little Fanny Mountain would cackle with laughter, much to their mother’s fury.

Rachel refused to see that the man was a vulgar fraud, determined to take her sons’ open dislike of him as nothing more than the infamous proud Esmond family pride. “What does trivial profane learning matter?” she would scold them. “Ward is our master in the things that truly count. His rough manners are no flaw – Heaven does not choose its elect from the ranks of the wealthy and well-bred. It is your wicked arrogance that makes you refuse to listen.” Ward, meanwhile, preached a whole sermon on Naaman the Syrian, too proud to bathe in the humble Jordan to be healed, and implied that the two stubborn boys would be damned forever if they did not submit to his guidance.

George, who had inherited his grandfather’s sharp, sarcastic tongue, began to pick apart Ward’s pompous speeches for fun, making the tutor choke with rage over his meals and turning the whole house against him. Madam Warrington’s authority was openly defied, she thought, by her stubborn older son, and she decided to restore order through force. The birch cane was a common enough method of discipline in those days, used freely by schoolmasters, sergeants, and overseers alike, and the boys had been caned many times by their old Scottish tutor as children. But no one had laid a hand on them since their grandfather’s death, and Rachel was determined to make an example of George.

Mrs. Mountain, the boys’ quiet ally, warned them of the plot being hatched against them. “Ward fawns over your mother more than ever now, it turns my stomach to see him gobble his food and flatter her so shamelessly!” she said. “I heard her talking to Major Washington the other day, and he told her: ‘Discipline is discipline. There can only be one commander in a house, ma’am, and you must be master of yours.’” Harry admitted that Washington had given him the very same warning, telling him his mother was “dangerously angry” and he had best obey Ward. But George only grew more haughty and refractory.

The storm broke the next morning. During lessons, George openly defied Ward, who brought the dispute to Madam Warrington at dinner. Poor Harry was forced to confirm that George had been in the wrong, and even George himself, standing under his grandfather’s portrait by the chimney, admitted the accusation was perfectly true. But his mother would accept no apology.

“Spare the rod and spoil the child,” she declared stamping her little foot, and ordered Ward to punish the disobedient boy immediately.

George went pale, and his eyes fell on the precious china cup perched on the mantel under the Colonel’s portrait – his grandfather’s favourite, which Rachel treasured above almost any other possession in the house. “Mother,” he said slowly, “if I broke this cup, it could never be put back together, could it? No tinker’s rivets would ever make it whole again.” When Rachel scoffed that an Esmond ought to be braver than to cower over a trinket, George let the cup slip from his fingers. It shattered on the marble hearth into a hundred pieces.

Harry lunged to catch it a second too late.

“Now, mother,” George said, white-faced, “I am quite ready. And when next I see Mr. Washington, I will thank him for the advice you have chosen to act on.” He bowed stiffly to Ward, and bade him go first to the study to receive his punishment.

Harry followed close behind, his face dark with rage. A few minutes later, a terrible yell rang out that made Madam Warrington leap in her chair. Ward came staggering out of the study, blood pouring from a deep gash on his scalp, and behind him was Harry, his eyes blazing, brandishing his grandfather’s old hunting hanger torn from the library wall.

“I don’t care, I did it!” Harry roared. “I saw him lift his hand to strike my brother, so I hurled the big oak ruler straight at his head. No man – no man – shall ever raise a hand to hurt George while I am alive. If any try, I will kill them.”
