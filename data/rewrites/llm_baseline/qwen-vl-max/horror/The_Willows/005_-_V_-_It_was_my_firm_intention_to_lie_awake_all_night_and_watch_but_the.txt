V. I had resolved to stay awake all night, to keep watch, to guard against whatever might come creeping from the dark—yet my body, worn thin by fear and fatigue, betrayed me. Sleep crept in like a thief, stealing over me with the quiet embrace of oblivion. That my companion also succumbed only hastened its arrival. At first he was restless, sitting up every few minutes, his voice hushed but urgent: “Did you hear that?” or “Something’s moving outside.” He tossed on his cork mattress, muttering about the tent shifting, the river rising, the island sinking beneath us. Each time, I rose to check—peering into the blackness, listening intently—and returned with reassurance: nothing stirred. Eventually, his movements slowed. His breathing deepened, then settled into a steady rhythm. Then came the snoring—low, rhythmic, almost soothing. It was the first time in my life I’d found such a sound comforting. That, I remember, was the last thought before I slipped into unconsciousness.

I woke abruptly, gasping. The blanket was pulled over my face, but something heavier pressed down upon me—not just fabric. For a moment, I thought my friend had rolled onto my bed in his sleep. I called out, sat up, and instantly felt it—the tent was _surrounded_. Outside, the soft, countless pattering returned, filling the night with an eerie, living dread. I shouted again, louder this time. No answer. And then I noticed: the snoring had stopped. The flap of the tent hung open. A cold certainty seized me—the Swede was gone.

I crawled out into the darkness, fumbling for the flap, pulling it shut with trembling hands. As I did, I realized with a jolt that he wasn’t inside. Not there. Not anywhere. Panic surged through me. I burst from the tent, stumbling into a wave of sound—a thick, humming drone that seemed to rise from the ground, the air, the sky itself. It was the same hum I’d heard before, but now it was wild, uncontrolled, as if a swarm of unseen insects filled the atmosphere, pressing in from every direction. My breath caught. The air felt thick, heavy, resisting my lungs. But my friend was out there. In danger. I could not hesitate.

Dawn was breaking—just a pale strip of light along the horizon, faint and ghostly. The sky was still, no wind stirring. I could barely make out the shapes of the willows, the river, the sandbars. I ran wildly across the island, calling his name, screaming anything that came to mind. But the trees swallowed my voice. The hum muffled everything. My shouts died within a few feet. I crashed through the underbrush, tripping, falling, scraping my face on thorns, tearing through branches like a madman.

Then, suddenly, I broke free at the island’s edge—and saw him.

A dark silhouette stood between the water and the sky. One foot already dipped into the river. He was about to jump.

I lunged forward, grabbing him around the waist, dragging him back with all my strength. He fought like a beast, thrashing, shouting words that twisted into the same cursed hum, ranting about “going _inside_ to Them,” “taking the way of the water and the wind”—phrases that made no sense, yet chilled me to the bone. I held on, refusing to let go, until finally, exhausted, he collapsed.

I dragged him into the tent, threw him onto the mattress, and held him down until the frenzy passed. Then, as suddenly as it began, it ended. The humming ceased. The pattering stopped. The silence was absolute.

He opened his eyes, turned his head toward me, and said—softly, like a child who’d just been saved—“My life, old man—it’s my life I owe you. But it’s all over now. They’ve found a victim in our place.”

And then he fell back, sank into sleep, snoring deeply, peacefully—as though nothing had happened. As though he hadn’t tried to drown himself moments before.

When the sun rose three hours later, I watched him wake naturally, without fear or memory. He rose, lit the fire, prepared breakfast. I followed him to the riverbank, anxious, watching closely. He dipped his head into the water, muttered something about how cold it was. “River’s falling at last,” he said. “Good.”

“The humming has stopped too,” I said.

He looked at me calmly, as though nothing unusual had occurred. “Everything has stopped,” he replied. “Because—” He paused, then smiled faintly. “Because they’ve found another victim.”

I forced a laugh. “Exactly,” I said. “That’s what I thought.”

He nodded. “I feel safe now. Truly.”

Then he turned and walked toward the bank, poking among the sandy hollows with a stick. I followed. He moved slowly, deliberately. Suddenly, he stopped. “Ah!” he exclaimed. The tone sent a shiver through me—familiar, terrible.

He pointed. Half in the water, half on the sand, lay a large dark shape. Twisted roots held it fast. A body. A man. Drowned. Recently. The current had carried it here—just as the fit had passed.

“We must give it a decent burial,” I said.

“I suppose so,” I replied, though I shivered. There was something about the corpse—its stillness, its emptiness—that made my blood run cold.

The Swede glanced at me sharply, his expression unreadable, then climbed down the bank. I followed more slowly. The river had stripped much of the clothing from the body. The neck and chest were bare.

Halfway down, he froze, raising a hand to stop me. But I stumbled, losing balance. We collided—me into him, him into the body.

At that instant, a sound erupted from the corpse—loud, sharp, pulsing. A chorus of humming, rising from the very skin, spreading outward like wings beating through the air. It swirled above us, rising into the sky, growing fainter, vanishing into the distance. It was as if we’d disturbed some invisible creatures, alive and hidden beneath the flesh.

We clutched each other, stunned. Then, the current shifted. The body turned. Slowly, it rotated, revealing the face—pale, dead, staring upward.

The Swede dropped to his knees, covering his eyes.

I rushed to his side.

And I saw.

The skin of the man’s chest and face—deeply indented with small, perfect hollows. Shaped exactly like the sand-funnels we’d found scattered across the island. Beautiful. Unnatural.

“Their mark,” the Swede whispered, voice broken. “Their awful mark.”

And then, the river took it.

The body turned once more, spun into the current, and was swept away—over and over, like an otter in the water—until it vanished into the distance, beyond sight, beyond reach.
