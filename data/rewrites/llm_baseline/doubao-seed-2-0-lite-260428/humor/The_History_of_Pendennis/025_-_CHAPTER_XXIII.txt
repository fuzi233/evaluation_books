The residents of Fairoaks were dozing their way through the dullest possible humdrum routine, while the great manor perched on the hill across the River Brawl finally shook off the two generations of deep slumber it had been stuck in, and started making enough noise to wake the very rooks nesting in its chimneys.

Right around the time Arthur “Pen” Pendennis was moping over his own tiny personal mishap, so wrapped up in his own suffering he failed to notice anything happening to people less important than himself, a little announcement popped up in the provincial papers that sent the entire county’s gossip network into a tailspin. From Clavering Market to Cackleby Fair, at Chatteris court sessions, on Gooseberry Green where the local squire and vicar would stop their respective carriages mid-road just to chat for 45 minutes, even at Tinkleton Church gate as folks trundled in for Sunday service in their white smocks and scarlet cloaks, everyone was saying the same thing: Clavering Park was finally going to have people living in it again.

Five years prior, the county papers had run a blurb about Francis Clavering, only son of Sir Francis Clavering the baronet, marrying Jemima Augusta, the widowed daughter of Calcutta’s very own Samuel Snell, at the British Legation in Florence. The county rumor mill immediately decreed that Clavering, who’d been flat broke for decades, had landed himself a rich Indian widow. A handful of locals spotted the newlyweds gallivanting about Europe: the Kickleburys ran into them at their rented Florentine palace, throwing fancy parties, though never once daring to come back to England. A year later, young Peregrine of Cackleby stumbled on them staying at Schloss Schinkenstein on the Mummel See. For years, whispers about them would blow back to Clavering’s ancestral home from random gambling dens and bath towns across France, Germany, and Italy, like stray confetti someone had accidentally mailed across the continent.

Last anyone heard, they’d set up shop in Paris, living it up in maximum splendor right after news arrived that Samuel Snell of Calcutta had kicked the bucket, leaving his only daughter a fortune big enough to make every indebted English aristocrat drool.

Old Sir Francis Clavering, meanwhile, didn’t have a single good anecdote attached to his name. The son of an outlaw hiding out in a moldy old chateau near Bruges, he’d tried and failed to make a decent go of it as a dragoon: he gambled away every penny he owned before two years were up, was forced to sell his commission, did a stint in the Fleet prison, then hightailed it to Ostend to move in with his gouty exiled dad. For years after that, you could spot the sad, washed-up would-be prodigal loitering in billiard halls, hanging around watering hole casinos, dancing at boarding house balls, and entering steeplechases on horses that belonged to other people.

It was at a Lausanne boarding house that he pulled off what he called the best lucky strike of his life: marrying the very newly widowed Mrs. Amory, fresh off the boat from Calcutta. His dad died not long after, and the second his wife got to call herself Lady Clavering, old Snell in Calcutta was so overjoyed by the fancy title he doubled her allowance. He popped his own clogs shortly after, leaving her a fortune so big the gossips kept inflating it by ten thousand pounds every single time they retold the story.

Before all that, the posh expat English community abroad had side-eyed Lady Clavering something fierce. Her manners weren’t exactly salon-ready, her origins were notoriously low, and the retired East India crowd in every continental English resort town loved to sneer at her dad, the disreputable old indigo smuggler and shyster lawyer, and her first husband, the ship’s mate who’d captained the vessel she’d taken out to Calcutta to join her father. Nobody in Calcutta high society knew them, they’d never set foot in Government House, and old Sir Jasper Rogers, former Chief Justice of Calcutta, once told his wife he knew a “real doozy of a story” about her first husband that he absolutely refused to spill, no matter how much his wife and daughters begged.

But once Lady Clavering was installed at Paris’s Hotel Bouilli, throwing parties and lighting up the social scene in 183-, every last stuffy moral English lady in the city came crawling to her guest list. The Faubourg St. Germain embraced her, the British ambassador paid her special attention, even the famously rigid, morally priggish Lady Elderbury, Lady Rockminster, and the venerable Countess of Southdown — women renowned for turning away anyone with even a hint of a scandal — all queued up to call on her. Turns out a fortune of ten thousand a year (some gossips swore it was twenty) does wonders for your reputation, no questions asked.

Her generosity was endless, too. Any charity within walking distance of her Paris home got a handout: she gave cash to the Armenian patriarch, a priest from Mount Athos begging for monastery funds, the Baptist missionaries off to convert the cannibals on Quashyboo island, and on the very same day she gave five Napoleons to a French nun supporting persecuted Jesuits, she wrote a check to the Reverend J. Ramshorn, who’d had a divine vision ordering him to convert the Pope. On top of all that, she threw the best dinners, balls, and late-night suppers in the entire city that season.

Somewhere in all that fun, the good-natured lady must have sorted out her husband’s mountain of English debts, because Sir Francis popped back up in London without a single arrest warrant hanging over his head, got his name printed in the Morning Post and the county papers as a resident of Mivart’s Hotel, and one fine afternoon, the elderly housekeeper at long-shuttered Clavering House watched a carriage drawn by four horses roll up the overgrown long drive, and stop right in front of the moss-covered, gloomy front steps.

Three men were riding in the open carriage. On the back seat sat our old acquaintance Mr. Tatham the Chatteris lawyer, while up front were a portly, handsome man dripping with mustache, side-whiskers, fur collars, and gold braid, and next to him a pale, listless fellow who climbed out of the carriage slowly once the other two had hopped out nimbly.

They trundled up the overgrown steps, and a foreign servant with gold earrings and a gold-laced cap yanked the mansion’s giant front bell so hard the clang echoed through every dark empty room. Seconds later, scuffling footsteps sounded on the marble entryway tiles, the great doors creaked open, and out trotted Mrs. Blenkinsop the housekeeper, her assistant Polly, and Smart the gamekeeper, all bowing so low their necks ached.

Smart tugged at the wisp of hay-colored hair on his sunburnt forehead, kicked out a leg like he was warding off a nipping farm dog, and bowed so far his nose almost touched his boots. Mrs. Blenkinsop dropped a deep curtsey, little Polly did three rapid-fire bows on top of her own curtsey, and the old housekeeper choked out tearfully, “Welcome home to Clavering, Sir Francis! It does my old eyes good to see one of the family back at last.”

She was talking straight to the dashing, fur-clad man who had his hat cocked rakishly to one side and was twirling his giant mustache like a royal. He burst out cackling immediately. “Wrong bloke, old lady! I’m not the lord of the manor come back to revisit his ancestral halls. Subjects and vassals — meet your actual master!”

He pointed at the pale, languid man, who sighed and said, “Don’t be an ass, Ned.”

“Right, Mrs. Blenkinsop, it’s me, Sir Francis,” the baronet said, taking the old lady’s trembling hand. “I remember you perfectly, though I expect you’ve forgotten me. How do you do?” He nodded at her in a surprisingly kind, if vacant, way.

Mrs. Blenkinsop swore on her life she’d recognize him anywhere — he was the spitting image of his father Sir Francis, and his grandfather Sir John before him, back to the days of yore.

“Right, yes, thank you, very much obliged,” Sir Francis mumbled, staring blankly around the giant entry hall. “Gloomy old dump, isn’t it, Ned? I only saw this place once before, when my old man had a screaming fight with grandad back in 1823.”

“Gloomy? It’s glorious! It’s Castle of Otranto, it’s The Mysteries of Udolpho, by Jove!” the man called Ned yelped. “Look at that fireplace! You could roast an entire elephant in there. That carved gallery? That’s Inigo Jones work, I’d put five to two on it.”

“The top half is Inigo Jones, the bottom was redone by Dutch architect Vanderputty under George the First, for the fourth baronet Sir Richard,” the housekeeper corrected him primly.

“Oh right,” Sir Francis yawned. “Gad, Ned, you know absolutely everything.”

“I know a few things, Frank,” Ned grinned. “I know that painting over the mantel definitely isn’t a Snyders — three to one it’s a copy. A little coat of varnish, mate, and this whole place will shine brighter than any manor in the shire. That old bloke in the red gown there is Sir Richard, yeah?”

“County sheriff, sat in Parliament under Queen Anne,” the housekeeper said, shocked he knew so much. “The portrait to his right is Theodosia, second baronet’s wife, painted by Lely as Venus, with her son Gregory the third baronet next to her as Cupid with his bow and arrows. The next panel is Sir Rupert, knighted by Charles the First and had all his property seized by Oliver Cromwell.”

“Thanks, no need to go on, Mrs. Blenkinsop,” Sir Francis said. “We’ll wander around ourselves. Frosch, hand me a cigar. You want one, Mr. Tatham?”

Little Mr. Tatham tried the cigar the courier handed him, and immediately started spluttering so hard he nearly choked. “You don’t need to follow us,” Sir Francis told the housekeeper. “Smart, get the horses fed and watered. We won’t be long. Come on, Strong — I know the way, I was here once in 1823 right before grandad croaked.” And so Sir Francis and Captain Edward Strong, for that was the grand friend’s name, wandered off into the empty reception rooms, leaving a confused Mrs. Blenkinsop to scuttle back to her tiny lodgings — the only heated, habitable rooms in the whole miles-wide mansion.

The place was so enormous no normal tenant could ever afford to run it, and the two men trundled through room after room, gaping at their vast, dusty, deserted grandeur. To the right of the entry hall were the saloons and drawing rooms, to the left the oak parlor, the grand dining room, and the library where Pen had snuck in to read books as a kid. A gallery ran around three sides of the hall leading off to dozens of stately, forgotten bedrooms, and the top floor was a warren of tiny drafty garrets built for servants back when the house was first constructed — a sobering reminder that our ancestors were perfectly happy sleeping in gilded canopies while their staff froze and breathed moldy air in attics worse than most modern stables.

While Sir Francis drifted along looking half-asleep and utterly indifferent to owning the giant manor, Captain Strong was buzzing with so much excitement you’d have thought he was the owner, and Sir Francis the random guest. “This place has so much potential, sir, huge potential!” he whooped. “Leave it to me, we’ll make it the pride of the whole shire for peanuts. We can turn the library into a private theater, hang curtains between the columns to divide the space! That grand ballroom? We can fit the entire county in there for a galop. Hang the morning parlor with that tapestry you’ve got stored from the Rue de Grenelle apartment, deck out the oak room with medieval cabinets and armor — armor looks chef’s kiss against black oak — and I saw a perfect Venice glass vase on the Quai Voltaire that’ll fit that high mantelpiece to an inch. The long saloon? White and crimson, obviously. Yellow satin for the main drawing room, pale blue with lace trim for the little one.”

“I remember my old dad caning me in that little blue room,” Sir Francis said glumly. “He always hated me, the old sod.”

“Chintz will do for the lady’s suite of rooms on the south landing — bedroom, sitting room, dressing room, we’ll stick a conservatory out over the balcony. Where d’you want your own rooms?”

“Stick me in the north wing,” Sir Francis yawned. “As far away from Miss Amory’s bloody piano as humanly possible. I can’t stand the noise, the girl screeches from dawn till midnight.”

The captain cackled, and mapped out every last renovation for the rest of their walk. Once they’d finished wandering the house, they popped back to the steward’s room where Mr. Tatham was poring over the estate plans, and Mrs. Blenkinsop had laid out a little spread of snacks for the new lord and master. After that they checked the kitchens and stables, which Sir Francis actually cared about, and Captain Strong was all set to go inspect the gardens — until the baronet said “to hell with the gardens and all that rubbish,” and they all climbed back into the carriage and drove off as casually as they’d arrived. That night the whole of Clavering found out Sir Francis Clavering had visited his ancestral park, and was moving back to the county for good.

When the news hit Chatteris, every last soul in the town lost their minds. High church and low church folk, half-pay captains, gossipy old maids, sporty small squires, farmers, shopkeepers, factory workers — everyone had an opinion. The news made its way to Fairoaks, and Pen and the two ladies living there got a little giddy over it. “Mrs. Pybus says there’s a very pretty girl in the new family, Arthur,” Laura teased, as only women who love teasing boys can. “Miss Amory, Lady Clavering’s daughter from her first marriage. I’m sure you’ll fall head over heels in love with her the second she steps off the carriage.”

Helen huffed. “Don’t talk nonsense, Laura.” Pen laughed right back. “Oh yeah? Then the little four-year-old heir of Sir Francis is all yours, I suppose.”

“Sir Francis the kid is four,” Laura said. “But I’m saving my attention for that handsome officer, Sir Francis’s friend. I saw him in the Clavering pew at church last Sunday, his mustache was absolutely magnificent.”

Pretty soon every single detail of the new household was common knowledge around town. On summer evenings, the drive leading up to the park was full of locals trundling up to gawk at the renovations going on. Van after van of furniture rolled up from Chatteris and London, and Captain Glanders the local retired soldier knew exactly what was in every last one, and personally escorted them up the drive to the manor house.

He and Captain Ned Strong were already thick as thieves. Strong had rented the very same quiet lodgings the meek Mr. Smirke had lived in before, and he was fast on his way to being everyone’s favorite person in town. He was broad-shouldered, jolly, blue-eyed, dark-whiskered, a little plump around the waist in the most charming way possible, and when he sauntered down Clavering High Street with his hat cocked to one side, cane tapping along the pavement, laughing loud enough to be heard down the whole high street, he was as welcome as sunshine after three weeks of rain.

The very first market day, he knew every pretty girl at the stalls by name, joked with every farmer about their sheep and cows, and showed up to the Agricultural Ordinary dinner at the Clavering Arms where he made every last farmer snort beer out their nose laughing at his stories. “That there Ned Strong is a proper top-notch gent,” every last farmer in top boots agreed. He shook hands with a dozen of them as they rode out of the inn yard on their old nags, waving his hat from the inn doorway while he smoked his cigar. By the end of the night, he knew exactly what the innkeeper paid in rent, how many acres he farmed, how much malt he put in his strong beer, and whether he snuck unexcised brandy in from the fishing villages down the coast.

He’d tried living at the big manor house first, but it was so dead quiet he couldn’t stand it. “I’m a creature born for society, mate,” he told Captain Glanders. “Frank’s got no energy, none at all, he’s not built for running this whole estate. But I need company. Old Mrs. Blenkinsop goes to bed at seven sharp and takes her assistant Polly with her. The first two nights I spent there it was just me and the house ghost. I like company, what can I say? Most old soldiers do.”

When Glanders asked him where he’d served, Strong curled his mustache and laughed, said Glanders might as well ask where he hadn’t served. “I started as a Hungarian Uhlan cadet, quit after a fight with my commanding officer when the Greek war of independence broke out, was one of seven men who escaped Missolonghi, got blown up on one of Botzaris’s fireships when I was seventeen. I’ve got my Cross of the Redeemer back at my lodgings, come over tonight for a glass of grog and I’ll show you. I’ve got the White Eagle of Poland too — Skrzynecki gave it to me on the field at Ostrolenka, I was a lieutenant in the fourth regiment and we charged straight through Diebitsch’s entire line without firing a single shot. Got this scar at the king’s side outside Oporto, served in Spain with the king’s troops until my dear friend Zumalacarreguy died, and I realized the war was lost. Now you know Ned Strong, Chevalier Strong to all my mates abroad, the whole story.”
