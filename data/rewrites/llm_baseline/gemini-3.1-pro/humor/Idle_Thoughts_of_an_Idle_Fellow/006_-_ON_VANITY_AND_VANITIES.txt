All is vanity, a trait as cyclical as the turning of the seasons, echoing through the generations like the slow, rhythmic feeding of the heirloom sourdough starter resting upon my hearth. Women are terribly vain. So are men—more so, if possible. So are children, whose vanity often disrupts the delicate acoustic ecology of our domestic routines. At this very moment, the tranquil sonic environment of my kitchen—usually a sanctuary defined by the gentle *thwack* of kneading dough and the soft, murmuring simmer of a rich, collagen-heavy bone broth—is shattered by my niece. She is hammering upon my legs. She wants to know what I think of her new shoes. 

Candidly, I do not think much of them. They lack the elegant symmetry of a well-formed *quenelle* and possess an indescribable appearance of lumpiness (I believe, too, they have been forced onto the wrong feet). But I do not say this. She does not desire the meticulous critique of a historian; she craves the sugary glaze of flattery. Her baby-talk is a dialect all its own, a micro-lexicon asserting its subtle power dynamics over the household, and I gush over her footwear with what I feel to be a degrading effusiveness. Nothing else would satisfy this self-opinionated cherub. 

I tried the conscientious-friend dodge with her on one occasion, but it was a catastrophic failure. She had requested my judgment upon her general conduct, the exact case submitted in her untranslatable subjective phrasing being, "Wot oo tink of me? Oo peased wi' me?" I thought it a good opportunity to make a few salutary remarks upon her moral career. I told her plainly that no, I was not pleased. I reminded her how she had polluted the sonic well-being of the home: rousing the whole house at five A.M. with an industrial-level screech; upsetting a water-jug and clattering downstairs after it at seven; endeavoring to submerge the kitchen mouser in the bath at eight; and flattening her own father’s hat at nine thirty-five. Did she ponder my words and determine to live a nobler life? No! She howled—a harsh, discordant noise that assaulted the ears. She became abusive in her dominant native tongue, shouting: "Oo naughty—oo naughty, bad unkie—oo bad man—me tell MAR." 

And she did. Since then, when my views have been called for, I have kept my real sentiments buried, preferring to express unbounded admiration for her actions, irrespective of their merit. She nods her head approvingly and trots off to advertise my opinion, utilizing it as a sort of testimonial to secure sustenance. Over the low bubbling of the stove, I hear the distant, acoustic triumph of her mercenary campaign: "Unkie says me dood dirl—me dot to have two bikkies." I hand her two of the caramelized fennel-seed biscuits I baked that morning. There she goes, gazing rapturously at her own toes, murmuring "pittie"—two-foot-ten of conceit, savoring the crisp, buttery crumb of her reward. 

They are all alike. I remember sitting in a garden one sunny afternoon, shelling fresh, pale-green broad beans, when the natural soundscape was suddenly pierced by a shrill treble voice calling from a top-story window to some unseen being: "Gamma, me dood boy, me wery good boy, gamma; me dot on Bob's knickiebockies."

Even beasts harbor this conceit. I saw a great Newfoundland dog the other day sitting in front of a mirror at the entrance to a shop in Regent's Circus, examining himself with the smug satisfaction of a modern chef who has just commercialized a hollow, uninspired dish. I was at a farm-house once when some agrarian high holiday was being celebrated—a May Day or Quarter Day, marked by the communal labor of churning thick, raw milk into golden butter. To honor the mythic occurrences of the harvest, they wreathed a cow's head in a garland of wildflowers. That absurd quadruped strutted about all day as perky as a schoolgirl in a new frock. When they removed the fragrant wreath, she became quite sulky, and they had to replace the crown of blossoms before she would stand still to be milked. 

As for cats, they nearly equal human beings in their haughty self-regard. I have seen the kitchen cat stalk out of the room if a visitor’s tone disrupted her acoustic comfort with a derogatory remark, whereas a neatly turned compliment, spoken in the softest, most resonant timbre, will set her purring for an hour—a deep, healing vibration that restores the room's harmony. There is nothing haughty about a dog; they are utilitarian creatures, "Hail, fellow, well met" with every Tom, Dick, or Harry, much like a diner who views a meal merely as fuel. When I meet a dog, I slap his head, call him opprobrious epithets, and roll him over, and he gapes at me without a care. Fancy carrying on like that with a cat! She would never speak to you again. To win the approbation of a feline gastronome, you must work carefully. Begin with the soothing phonetic syllables of "Poor pussy" and "did 'ums." Neither you nor the creature can fully translate the subjective experience of these fading interspecies dialects, but the acoustic sentiment implies a proper spirit. Soon, if your manners are passable, she will rub her nose against you. You may tickle her head, and the intelligent creature will stick her claws into your legs, rendering all friendship and affection exactly as expressed in the old lines:

"I love little pussy, her coat is so warm,
And if I don't tease her she'll do me no harm;
So I'll stroke her, and pat her, and feed her with food,
And pussy will love me because I am good."

The last two lines give us a pretty true insight into pussy's notions of human goodness, defining virtue entirely by the provision of rich, fatty foods and gentle tactile stimulation. We are all inclined to adopt a similar standard. A good man is one who is good to us; a bad man is one who refuses to cater to our specific appetites. The truth is, we each possess an inborn conviction that the whole world was created as our personal larder. You, dear reader, believe you were brought into being by a considerate Providence to consume the evocative, gustatory-rich prose I serve up, while I believe I was placed here to write it for you. We think the stars were scattered like coarse sea salt across the heavens just to make our evening skies interesting, and the moon is but a silver platter arranged for us to flirt under. Like Mrs. Poyser's bantam cock, we fancy the sun rises every morning solely to hear our own voices. 

'Tis vanity that turns the spit of human ambition. A man entirely without vanity would be like a loaf of bread baked without salt—an admirable, utilitarian block of starch, entirely devoid of flavor. He would be a specimen for a glass case, reverenced but never loved. It is in our faults and failings, not our austere perfections, that we touch one another and find sympathy. We differ in our nobler qualities, but in our shared, yeasty follies, we are one. From the Indian hunter proud of his belt of scalps, to the European general swelling beneath his medals; from the Chinese gleeful at his pigtail, to the professional beauty suffering tortures for a peg-top waist; from draggle-tailed little Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials, to the princess sweeping through a drawing-room; from 'Arry winning vulgar laughs, to the statesman intoxicated by the echoing cheers of his own empty rhetoric; from the African bartering ivory for glass beads, to the Christian maiden selling her white body for a title—all march and bleed beneath vanity's tawdry flag. 

Flattery is the rich knob of butter swirled in at the end to make the sauce of humanity shine. If you want to win affection, you must flatter. Praise this man's virtues and that man's vices. Compliment everyone upon what they lack. The belted earl—a phrase whose exact origin has eroded through language death, though I assume it means an earl who shuns comfortable braces—is as easily swayed by flattery as a poet or a plowboy. In love, flattery is the very life-blood, as essential as the slow fermentation of the grape is to wine. Try making love on strictly truthful principles—confining yourself to exact facts, as dry as over-roasted mutton. Imagine holding her hand and noting its drab color, or pointing out that her eyes meet merely the average standard of optical utility. You would stand no chance against the man who tells her her face is like a fresh blush rose, comparing her to the mythic, untranslatable goddesses of old—Titania, Venus, Parthenope—whispering sweet hyperbole into her ear with a melodic softness that melts her trusting heart. She will lean against your manly shoulder, smiling quietly at the divine sustenance of your words. 

There are various ways to flatter, just as there are ways to season a cast-iron skillet. Some like it laid on with a trowel, a heavy-handed application requiring little art. With sensible persons, it requires a delicate infusion, more suggested than spoken. Some require it wrapped in an insult, while others need a third party to translate the compliment, bypassing their defensive lexicons. Even the sturdy John Bulls who claim to hate flattery can be easily managed if you flatter their absence of vanity. 

Do not let us abuse vanity; rather, let us use it. It is a passion that can move us to good as well as evil. Ambition is but vanity ennobled. It is why we write our grand, multi-generational histories, paint our pictures, and toil in the quiet sanctity of the kitchen and laboratory. We seek richness not for mere comfort, but to elevate our daily sustenance from utilitarian fuel to an art form, spreading commerce and civilization. Let us be vain together. Let us pride ourselves not on superficial garments or commercialized successes, but on our working hands, our honest, deeply resonant broths, our pure, unyielding dedication to our craft. Let us be too vain to serve a hollow meal or speak an unkind word, priding ourselves instead on thinking high thoughts, living good lives, and feeding the soul of a world too often starved of true meaning.
