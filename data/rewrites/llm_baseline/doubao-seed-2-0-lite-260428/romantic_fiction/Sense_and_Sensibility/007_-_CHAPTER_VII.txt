Barton Park stood scarcely half a mile from the Dashwoods’ cottage. The ladies had often wandered near its grounds while walking the valley paths, but a broad, jutting shoulder of hill hid the great stone house entirely from view of their home. It was a large, handsome property, and the Middletons ran their household with equal parts unbridled hospitality and polished elegance — the former tailored perfectly to Sir John’s tastes, the latter to his lady’s. Their doors were seldom without guests, and they kept up a more brisk, varied social schedule than any other family in the whole neighbourhood.

This constant bustle was essential to both their happiness, for different as their temperaments and public manners were, they were perfectly matched in their total lack of cultivated talent or refined taste, which left them with little to fill their days outside the company of others. Sir John was a sportsman through and through: he lived for the hunt and the shoot, while Lady Middleton lived only to indulge her four rambunctious children. Those were their sole true pleasures, with Lady Middleton having the advantage of spoiling her darlings twelve months of the year, while Sir John’s favourite pastimes only occupied half the calendar. Still, endless social engagements at home and abroad more than made up for any gaps in their natural wit or formal education: they kept Sir John’s good spirits high, and gave Lady Middleton endless opportunities to show off her graceful, polished breeding.

Lady Middleton prided herself above all on the elegance of her dining table and every small domestic arrangement, and that quiet vanity was the source of her greatest joy at any of their parties. Sir John’s delight in company, though, was far more unfeigned: he loved cramming more young people into his rooms than his house could reasonably hold, and the louder and more lighthearted they were, the happier he was. He was a beloved figure to every young person in the neighbourhood, hosting unending summer picnics out on the downs piled high with cold ham and spiced chicken, and enough private winter balls to satisfy even the most dance-obsessed fifteen-year-old with an insatiable appetite for fun.

The arrival of a new family in the area was always a cause for great excitement for him, and he was utterly charmed by the new occupants of his Barton cottage. The Miss Dashwoods were young, pretty, and entirely unpretentious — that was more than enough to win his full approval, for he held that a pretty girl who was free of affectation had as captivating a mind as she did a face. His naturally warm, generous disposition made him eager to be kind to a family who had fallen so far from the comfort of their old home, and he got double the pleasure from having them as tenants: the genuine satisfaction of doing a good turn for his unfortunate cousins, plus the quiet delight of a sportsman who’d secured a fine new addition to his manor, with no competitive fellow huntsman to intrude on his favourite grounds.

Sir John met the Dashwoods at the very threshold of Barton Park, welcoming them with unfeigned, hearty sincerity. As he escorted them to the drawing room, he repeated to the young ladies the same regret he’d expressed the day before: he was gutted he hadn’t managed to round up a passel of smart, lively young bachelors to meet them. The only other gentleman staying in the house, besides himself, was an old, dear friend who was far past the first flush of youth and not nearly as playful as he wished he was. He begged them to forgive the smallness of their party, and swore it would never happen again. He’d called at half a dozen neighbouring houses that very morning trying to coax more guests over, but it was a bright full moon, it seemed, and every single family in the shire had a prior engagement. Luckily, Lady Middleton’s mother had arrived at Barton barely an hour before their own party, and she was such a cheerful, agreeable soul that he was sure the young ladies wouldn’t find the evening half so dull as they might fear.

Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters were perfectly happy to meet just two new strangers, and wanted no larger crowd at all. Mrs. Jennings, Lady Middleton’s mother, was a plump, merry, good-humoured elderly woman who talked a mile a minute, laughed freely, and was delightfully unrefined. She was full of silly jokes and raillery, and before dinner was even over she’d teased the girls no end about sweethearts and suitors, asking if they’d left their hearts tucked away somewhere back in Sussex, and pretending to spot blushes rising on their cheeks whether they coloured or not. Marianne, vexed on her sister’s behalf, kept glancing over at Elinor to see how she bore the silly jabs, and that earnest, concerned gaze of hers stung Elinor far more than Mrs. Jennings’ trivial, commonplace teasing ever could.

Colonel Brandon, Sir John’s resident friend, seemed no better suited to be his companion in manner than Lady Middleton was to be his wife, or Mrs. Jennings to be Lady Middleton’s own mother. He was quiet, reserved, and grave. His looks were not striking, but they were far from unpleasant, even to Marianne and Margaret, who at once labelled him an absolute confirmed old bachelor for being well past thirty-five. His face was thoughtful and sensible, and every one of his manners was marked by perfect gentlemanly grace.

There was little in the group that the Dashwoods would have naturally chosen as companions, but the cold, flat insipidity of Lady Middleton was so deeply unappealing that, by comparison, Colonel Brandon’s quiet gravity, even Sir John and Mrs. Jennings’ loud, unruly merriment, felt almost interesting. Lady Middleton seemed to rouse to genuine enjoyment only when her four noisy children burst into the drawing room after dinner, yanking at her sleeves, tearing at the hem of her gown, and putting an end to every other topic of conversation save the endless, lively anecdotes of their own antics.

Later that evening, when it was discovered Marianne was a gifted musician, they pressed her to play. The piano was unlocked, and everyone made a show of settling in to be charmed. Marianne, who sang beautifully, readily agreed to play through most of the scores Lady Middleton had brought with her when she married into the family — scores that had sat untouched on the piano stand for years, no doubt, for her ladyship had celebrated her wedding day by giving up music entirely, even though her mother swore she had once played exquisitely, and she herself claimed to be very fond of the art.

Marianne’s performance was met with loud, over-the-top applause. Sir John cheered raucously after every song, then chatted just as loudly to his guests through the next one. Lady Middleton would frequently scold him for rudeness, wondering how anyone could tear their attention away from music for even a moment — then turn right around and ask Marianne to replay the exact song she had just finished singing. Of all the party, only Colonel Brandon listened to her without any exaggerated, performative rapture. He gave her the only compliment that mattered: his full, unbroken attention. Marianne found herself feeling a genuine respect for him in that moment, a respect the rest of the party had fully forfeited with their shameless lack of taste. His love of music was not the wild, ecstatic, all-consuming passion that Marianne believed was the only true form of appreciation for the art, but it was still estimable, especially set against the total, horrible insensibility of everyone else in the room. She was reasonable enough to acknowledge that a man of five and thirty had long outlived the sharp, acute sensitivities and exquisite capacity for joy that belonged only to the very young, and she was perfectly willing to extend to Colonel Brandon every kindness and allowance that his advanced years, and his quiet courtesy, deserved.
