"My dear Lord March," wrote Mr. Warrington, his quill scratching briskly across the page, "I am pleased to inform you that I have won all three of our wagers. My new horses proved excellent—I drove them myself, with the postilion beside me to guide the way, and my man inside with Mrs. Betty. We made Bromley in just under the hour, encountered no trouble on Blackheath (though two riders approached us, thought better of it, and turned tail), and reached Tunbridge Wells by a quarter to twelve. This settles our account after yesterday’s piquet, and I remain—Your obliged and faithful servant, H. Esmond Warrington."

Now, the reader may well wonder how Lady Maria Esmond came to burst so unexpectedly into her aunt’s bedchamber that Saturday morning, triumphantly free from her confinement. The truth was, she had scarcely hoped for Mrs. Betty’s return that very day. Indeed, she and the chaplain were deep in their midnight card game, fortified by a modest supper provided by the bailiff’s wife, when the sudden clatter of carriage wheels sent her heart racing. The vehicle halted at the door, voices exchanged at the gate—and then, radiant despite her tears, Mrs. Betty appeared, followed by a tall young gentleman.

Need I name him?

The chaplain, with a joyous shout, flung down his cards. Lady Maria, pale as linen, swayed to her feet, took one trembling step—then collapsed into her cousin’s arms with a cry that was half sob, half laughter. How many kisses passed between them? A hundred? A thousand? Let decorum draw a veil. He had come for her. She had known he would. He was her champion, her deliverer from humiliation and captivity. And as she wept freely against his shoulder, I dare say she had never looked more beautiful in her life.

No fainting this time. She leaned upon his arm as they departed, though a fit of hysterics might have overtaken her in the night. Not that Madame Bernstein heard—she slept soundly through it all.

"You are both free to go," Harry announced at once. "Fetch your mistress’s hat and cloak, Betty. And Chaplain—we’ll share a pipe at my lodgings. I’ve need of one after that ride."

The chaplain, a man of excessive sensibility, was overcome. He seized Harry’s hand, kissed it, and with tears streaming down his face, invoked heaven’s blessings upon his noble, generous patron. Harry himself swelled with pride. There is no purer joy than turning sorrow into gladness, lifting the weight from another’s shoulders. And so, with hat cocked jauntily, he escorted his rescued princess through the night, imagining trumpets heralding his triumph, flowers strewn in his path.

Vanity? Perhaps. But let him indulge it. He had earned the right.

Sampson, that night, was a study in contrasts—now weeping gratitude, now toasting Harry’s health with fervor. The chaplain’s emotions were as fluid as his principles. He loved easily, hated just as swiftly, and tonight, brimming with Burgundy and admiration, he exalted Harry as the paragon of men.

"The junior branch of our family has treated you ill," Harry declared magnanimously, "but by God, Sampson, I’ll stand by you!"

(It must be noted that when in his cups, Harry was prone to wax eloquent about the grandeur of the Esmond lineage.)

"I’m glad to have been of service," he continued. "Rely on me whenever you’re in need. Didn’t you mention a sister at school? Here—" He slid a banknote across the table. "For her tuition."

Sampson’s face crumpled. "I don’t deserve this," he choked out. "I’m a villain, sir."

"Nonsense," Harry scoffed.

"I am. That pocket-book you lost last week—I found it that night at Barbeau’s. When you were... indisposed."

Harry stiffened. "You read the letters?"

"They told me nothing new," Sampson said heavily. "You’ve had spies about you, sir—ones you’d never suspect. You’re too young to guard your secrets."

Harry’s pulse quickened. "Then the stories about Lady Fanny—and my cousin Will—they’re true?"

"Every word."

A pause. Then, low and dangerous: "And Maria? If you breathe a word against her—"

Sampson spread his hands. "I would never! Though I might think her... *unsuited* to your youth."

Harry drained his glass. "She has my word. That’s enough."

Later, when Sampson returned from fetching the letters—or rather, the empty pocket-book, his trunk having been rifled—Harry’s anger cooled. The chaplain swore oaths of eternal loyalty, and Harry, in his magnanimity, forgave him.

By noon, Madame Bernstein summoned her nephew for chocolate. She praised his gallantry, lamented her own losses at cards ("I had to send Case to London for funds!"), and pressed fifty pounds into his hand.

"But I cannot approve your entanglement with Maria," she sighed. "A boy of twenty, bound to a woman of forty-two? It is folly."

Harry flushed. "Who showed you my letter to her?"

The Baroness arched a brow. "An accident. When she fainted, her locket fell open. I burned the letter, child. For your own good."

For a moment, Harry wavered—between duty and desire, between pride and relief. Then he squared his shoulders. "My word is given. In Virginia, we do not break such promises."

The Baroness’s eyes glistened—perhaps the first genuine tears in decades. "Then go," she whispered. "But remember: I loved you best."

And Harry, torn, could only kiss her hand and vow silence.
