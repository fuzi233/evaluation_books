My godmother resided in a stately home within the pristine, ancient town of Bretton. Her husband's lineage had anchored itself there for countless generations, bearing the proud designation of "Bretton of Bretton"—a name that suggested either a fortunate coincidence or perhaps the legacy of a distant ancestor significant enough to imprint his identity upon the neighborhood. Though I cannot verify the truth behind the title, I recall visiting Bretton as a child roughly twice a year, and I cherished those trips dearly. The house and its inhabitants were perfectly suited to my nature. I adored the spacious, tranquil rooms, the tastefully arranged furnishings, the expansive windows that flooded the space with light, and the balcony overlooking a magnificent, antique street. There, Sundays and holidays seemed to dwell permanently; the air was so serene, the cobblestones so immaculate, that everything brought me profound comfort.

In a household of adults, a single child is invariably doted upon, and in a quiet, understated manner, I was similarly cherished by Mrs. Bretton. She had become a widow, leaving behind one son, before I had even known her. Her husband, a physician, had passed away while she was still youthful and striking. As I remembered her, she was no longer a girl, yet she remained handsome—tall, well-proportioned, and possessing a brunette complexion that, though dark for an Englishwoman, radiated a clear vitality. Her cheeks held a healthy glow, animated by a pair of fine, cheerful black eyes. It was often lamented by others that she had not bestowed this radiant hue upon her son. His eyes were blue—piercing even in boyhood—and his long hair, described by cautious friends only as "golden" when the sunlight touched it, lacked her warmth. Yet, he inherited the contours of her face, her perfect teeth, her promising height, and, most importantly, her flawless health and a spirit marked by a steady, equal temperament—a quality far superior to wealth.

It was during the autumn of a certain year that I stayed at Bretton. My godmother had traveled personally to retrieve me from the relatives where I was then permanently residing. I suspect she foresaw approaching events, shadows of trouble that I barely perceived, yet whose faint hint was enough to instill in me a restless melancholy, making the prospect of changing scenes and company a welcome relief. Time moved with gentle ease in her presence—not with turbulent speed, but with the bland, soothing flow of a broad river gliding across a plain. My stays there reminded me of the biblical figures Christian and Hopeful resting beside a pleasant stream, surrounded by green trees and meadows blooming with lilies year-round. There was no variety to excite, no incidents to thrill; indeed, I loved peace so deeply and craved stimulation so little that when it finally arrived, I felt it merely as a disturbance, wishing it had remained at bay.

One day, a letter arrived that clearly startled and concerned Mrs. Bretton. At first, I assumed it was from home and trembled, fearing some dire communication. However, no mention was made of it, and the cloud of worry seemed to dissipate quickly. The following day, returning from a lengthy walk, I discovered an unexpected transformation in my bedroom. Beside my own French bed, nestled in its shaded alcove, stood a small crib draped in white linen. Next to my mahogany chest of drawers sat a tiny chest carved from rosewood. I paused, staring, pondering the meaning of these new objects. The answer was self-evident: *A second guest is arriving; Mrs. Bretton expects visitors.*

Descending for dinner, explanations unfolded. A little girl was to join me as a companion—the daughter of a friend and distant relative of the late Dr. Bretton. It was revealed that this child had recently lost her mother. Although Mrs. Bretton noted that the loss was not as devastating as it initially appeared, the circumstances were tragic. The mother, Mrs. Home, had been beautiful but giddy and careless, neglecting her child and disappointing her husband. Their marriage had proven uncongenial, leading eventually to a separation by mutual consent, avoiding legal entanglement. Shortly thereafter, overwhelmed by exertion at a ball, the lady contracted a cold, developed a fever, and died after a brief illness. Her husband, a man of highly sensitive feelings, was shattered by the sudden news. Consumed by guilt, believing his own lack of patience may have hastened her death, he fell into a deep depression. Medical advice prescribed travel as a remedy, and thus, Mrs. Bretton offered to care for his little daughter until he returned.

"And I trust," my godmother concluded warmly, "that the child will not resemble her mother—that silly, frivolous flirt who led every sensible man astray." She explained that Mr. Home was, in his own way, a sensible man, albeit impractical. Fond of science, he spent half his life in a laboratory conducting experiments—a pursuit his "butterfly wife" could neither understand nor tolerate. Indeed, she admitted, she would not have enjoyed it herself. When I asked further, she shared that her late husband had attributed Mr. Home's scientific inclination to a maternal uncle, a French savant. Of mixed French and Scottish descent, the father-in-law had connections abroad, including nobility who styled themselves with the prefix "_de_" before their names.

John Graham Bretton was away visiting a schoolfriend in the countryside. My godmother read the evening newspaper while I stitched. Outside, the night was wet and stormy; rain lashed against the windowpanes, and the wind roared angrily and restlessly.

"Poor child!" Mrs. Bretton murmured periodically. "Such terrible weather for her journey! I pray she arrives safely."

Several days passed, and it became evident that she was unlikely to develop much affection for anyone in the household. She was not precisely naughty or willful; far from disobedient, yet no sight was more detrimental to comfort—or even tranquility—than the one she presented. She moped. No grown person could perform that cheerless duty with greater skill; no weather-beaten face of an adult exiled to the antipodes of Europe ever displayed the marks of homesickness more clearly than did her infant visage. She seemed to be aging prematurely, becoming unearthly in her demeanor.

As for me, Lucy Snowe, I claim innocence regarding that curse of an overheated, discursive imagination. Yet, whenever I opened a door to find her seated alone in a corner, her chin resting in her tiny hand, the room did not seem inhabited so much as haunted. Again, on moonlit nights when I woke to behold her figure, stark and conspicuous in her nightdress, kneeling upright in bed and praying with the fervor of some Catholic or Methodist enthusiast—a precocious fanatic or untimely saint—I confess I knew not what thoughts occupied my mind. They were hardly more rational or healthy than the child's own must have been. I rarely caught a word of her prayers, for they were whispered so low; sometimes, indeed, they were not spoken aloud at all, but offered silently within her heart. The few fragments that reached my ears still carried the single, crushing burden: "Papa; my dear Papa!"

I perceived this to be a one-idea'd nature, betraying that monomaniacal tendency I have always considered the most unfortunate affliction a human soul can bear. What might have been the outcome of this fretting, had it continued unchecked, remains mere conjecture. However, it received a sudden turn.

One afternoon, Mrs. Bretton, coaxing her from her usual station in the corner, lifted her onto the window-seat. To occupy her attention, she instructed the child to watch the passersby and count how many ladies would walk down the street within a set time. She sat listlessly, hardly looking, and counted nothing. Then—while my eye was fixed upon hers—I witnessed a startling transfiguration within her iris and pupil.

These sudden, dangerous natures, often labeled *sensitive*, offer many curious spectacles to those whose cooler tempers have spared them from participating in their angular vagaries. The fixed, heavy gaze swam, trembled, and then glittered with fire; the small, overcast brow cleared; the trivial, dejected features lit up; the sad countenance vanished, replaced instantly by eager anticipation. "It _is_!" were her words. Like a bird or a flying arrow, she was gone from the room.

How she managed to open the house door I could not tell; perhaps it stood ajar, or perhaps Warren was nearby and obeyed her impetuous command. I, watching calmly from the window, saw her—in her black frock and tiny braided apron (she harbored a distinct antipathy toward pinafores)—dart halfway down the street. Just as I was about to turn away and quietly inform Mrs. Bretton that the child had run out mad and required immediate pursuit, I saw her snatched up and whisked away from my cool observation and the wondering stares of the passersby. A gentleman had performed this good deed, covering her with his cloak and advancing to return her to the house from which he had seen her emerge.

I assumed he would leave her in a servant's care and withdraw, but instead, he ascended the stairs. His reception immediately clarified that he was known to Mrs. Bretton. He asked this as he sat down and placed her gently on the floor before him. "How is Polly's Papa?" was the reply, as she leaned against his knee and gazed up into his face.

It was not a noisy, wordy scene; for that, I was thankful. Yet it was a scene of feeling so brimful that, because the cup did not foam high or overflow furiously, it oppressed the observer all the more. On occasions of vehement, unrestrained emotional expansion, a sense of disdain or ridicule often provides relief to the weary spectator; whereas I have always found most burdensome that sort of sensibility which bends of its own free will, a giant slave under the sway of common sense.

Mr. Home was a stern-featured man—or perhaps I should say, a hard-featured one. His forehead was knotty, and his cheekbones were marked and prominent. The character of his face was distinctly Scotch, yet there was feeling in his eye and emotion in his now agitated countenance. His northern accent harmonized perfectly with his physiognomy. I wished she would utter some hysterical cry so that I might find relief and feel at ease. She made wonderfully little noise; she seemed to have obtained everything she desired—all she wanted—and to be in a trance of content. In either mien or feature, this creature was unlike her sire, yet she was of his strain. Her mind had been filled from his, as a cup is filled from a flagon. Indisputably, Mr. Home possessed manly self-control, however he might secretly feel on certain matters.

"Polly," he said, looking down at his little girl, "go into the hall. You will see Papa's great-coat lying on a chair. Put your hand into the pockets; you will find a pocket-handkerchief there. Bring it to me."

It was a picture, in its own way, to see her, with her tiny stature and trim, neat shape, standing at his knee. Candidly speaking, I thought her a little busy-body; but her father, blind like other parents, seemed perfectly content to let her wait on him, and even wonderfully soothed by her offices.

"She is my comfort!" he could not help saying to Mrs. Bretton. That lady had her own "comfort" and nonpareil on a much larger scale, and, for the moment, absent; so she sympathized with his foible.

Leaving Bretton, a journey I undertook merely weeks after Paulina's departure—little suspecting then that I would never again set foot upon those tranquil, ancient streets—I returned home, having been away for six months. It is natural to assume I rejoiced in returning to the embrace of my family. Very well; let this pleasant supposition stand unchallenged, for it causes no harm. Nay, I grant the reader license to envision me, for the ensuing eight years, as a vessel drifting lazily through glassy seas in a harbor of perfect stillness. The helmsman lies sprawled upon the deck, face turned upward, eyes shut tight, buried in a prolonged prayer. Many women and girls are presumed to spend their lives exactly thus; why not I among them? Picture me then, idle, basking, round, and content, reclining on a plush deck, bathed in perpetual sunlight, swayed by winds of lazy gentleness.

However, the truth cannot be hidden: in such a scenario, I must inevitably have capsized, or disaster must eventually have struck. I recall clearly a season—a long, drawn-out one—marked by cold, peril, and strife. To this very day, when nightmares seize me, I taste the salt spray choking my throat and feel the freezing grip of the ocean pressing against my lungs. I knew there was a storm, and not a fleeting squall, but a tempest of enduring fury. For countless days and nights, neither sun nor star dared show itself; we threw our own rigging overboard; a crushing gale battered us until every shred of hope for survival vanished. In short, the ship was lost, and the crew perished. As far as I remember, I voiced no complaints to anyone regarding these tribulations. Indeed, to whom could I complain? I had long lost sight of Mrs. Bretton. Obstacles erected by others had severed our path years prior. Furthermore, time had wrought changes for her as well: the substantial estate she managed for her son, largely invested in speculative ventures, had reportedly dwindled to a mere fraction of its former value. Graham, I gathered from scattered rumors, had chosen a profession; both he and his mother had departed Bretton, rumored to be residing now in London. Thus, there remained no avenue for reliance upon others; I could look only to myself.

I confess I possessed neither a naturally self-reliant nor an active disposition; yet necessity forced these qualities upon me, as it does upon countless others. When Miss Marchmont, a spinster of our neighborhood, summoned me, I obeyed her summons, hoping she might entrust me with some task. Miss Marchmont was a woman of means, dwelling in a stately mansion, yet she was a rheumatic cripple, unable to move her feet or hands, a condition she had endured for twenty years. She resided permanently upstairs; her drawing-room adjoined her bedroom. Though I had often heard of Miss Marchmont and her peculiarities—she was known as highly eccentric—I had never seen her until now. I found her a woman of furrowed brow and grey hair, grave in her solitude, hardened by long affliction, and prone to irritability, perhaps even exacting demands.

It appeared that a maid, or rather a companion, who had attended her for several years, was preparing to marry. Hearing of my recent bereavement, she had sent for me, intending that I might fill this vacancy. She extended the offer after tea, as we sat alone by her fireplace. "It will not be an easy life," she stated frankly, "for I require considerable attention, and you will be greatly confined; yet, perhaps, compared to the existence you have recently led, it may seem tolerable."

I pondered this. Naturally, it ought to seem tolerable, I reasoned internally; yet, by some strange fatality, it did not. To reside here, in this cramped room, watching suffering—and perhaps becoming the target of temper—for all that remained of my youth, while everything that had passed behind me had not ended in bliss! My heart sank momentarily, then revived. Although I forced myself to *recognize* the evils ahead, I believe I was too pragmatic to *idealize* them, and thus to exaggerate their horror.

"My hesitation concerns whether I possess the strength for this undertaking," I remarked.

"That is my own concern," she replied, "for you appear a worn-out creature."

Indeed, I did. Gazing into the mirror, clad in mourning, I saw a faded, hollow-eyed specter. Yet, I paid little heed to this wan appearance. I believed the blight was merely external; I still felt vitality pulsing at life's core.

"What other prospects hold for you?" she asked.

"Nothing clear as yet: but I may discover something."

"So you imagine; perhaps you are correct. Follow your own method first, and if it fails, try mine. The opportunity I have offered remains open to you for three months."

This was generous. I thanked her profusely. As I spoke, a sudden paroxysm of pain seized her. I tended to her, applying remedies as directed. By the time she was relieved, a certain intimacy had already blossomed between us. From her bearing during this attack, I learned she was a woman of firm resolve and patience (though perhaps excitable under the weight of chronic mental anguish); conversely, from the goodwill with which I cared for her, she sensed she could sway my sympathies.

She invited me the following day, claiming my company for five or six consecutive days. Closer acquaintance revealed both her faults and eccentricities, yet simultaneously unveiled a character worthy of respect. Stern and even morose as she could be, I could attend to her and sit beside her with a calm that always brings peace when one realizes their presence soothes those they serve. Even when she chided me—as she did occasionally, quite sharply—it lacked humiliation; it carried no sting. It resembled an irascible mother scolding her daughter rather than a harsh mistress lecturing a dependent. Indeed, she could not truly lecture, though she could occasionally storm. Moreover, a vein of reason invariably ran through her passion; she remained logical even in her fiercest moments.

Soon, a growing sense of attachment transformed my perspective on remaining with her as a companion. Within another week, I had agreed to stay. These two stifling, overheated rooms became my entire world; a crippled elderly woman was my mistress, my friend, my everything. Her service was my duty; her pain, my suffering; her relief, my hope; her anger, my punishment; her regard, my reward. I forgot the existence of fields, woods, rivers, seas, and the ever-changing sky beyond the fogged lattice of this sick chamber; I was almost content to forget it. Everything within me contracted to this narrow lot. Tamed and still by habit, disciplined by fate, I required no walks in the fresh air; my appetite needed nothing more than the meager meals served to the invalid.

Furthermore, she offered me the unique privilege of studying the originality of her character: the steadfastness of her virtues, the potency of her passions, and the authenticity of her feelings. I possessed all these things, and for them, I clung to her. For them, I would have crawled alongside her for twenty more years, had her life of endurance been prolonged. But another decree was written. It seemed I must be spurred into action. I had to be goaded, driven, stung, compelled to energy. My small fragment of human affection, which I treasured like a solid pearl, must melt in my grasp and slip away like dissolving hail. My modest adopted duty had to be snatched from my easily satisfied conscience. I had sought to bargain with Fate: to evade occasional great agonies by submitting to a lifetime of privation and minor pains. Fate would not be so appeased; nor would Providence sanction this shrinking sloth and cowardly indolence.

One February night—I recall it distinctly—a voice drifted near Miss Marchmont's house, audible to every inhabitant, yet interpreted, perhaps, by only one. Following a calm winter, storms were ushering in the spring. Having settled Miss Marchmont into bed, I sat by the fireside sewing. The wind wailed at the windows throughout the day, but as night deepened, it assumed a new tone—an accent keen, piercing, almost articulate to the ear; a lament, pitiful and despondent, trilling through every gust.

"Oh, hush! Hush!" I murmured in my troubled mind, dropping my needlework and making a futile attempt to block my ears against that subtle, searching cry. I had heard that very voice before; compulsory observation had forced upon me a theory regarding its omen. Three times in my life, events had taught me that these strange accents in the storm—this restless, hopeless cry—forebode atmospheric conditions unfavorable to life. Epidemic diseases, I believed, were often heralded by a gasping, sobbing, tormented, long-lamenting east wind. Hence, I inferred, arose the legend of the Banshee. I also fancied I had noticed—though lacking the philosophy to confirm any connection—that we often simultaneously hear reports of disturbed volcanic activity in distant lands, of rivers bursting their banks, and of strange high tides surging furiously onto low coastlines.

"Our globe," I had mused to myself, "appears torn and disordered at such periods; the weak among us wither in its feverish breath, rushing hot from steaming volcanoes."

With my mistress gone and I once more standing entirely alone, I was forced to begin the search for a new situation. Around this time, I must admit, my nerves were somewhat shaken—not violently, perhaps, but enough to leave me feeling distinctly unwell. I looked pale, thin, and haggard, with hollow eyes that betrayed sleeplessness. I resembled someone who has stayed up too late, an overworked servant, or a person burdened by debt. Yet, I was not in debt, nor was I destitute. Although Miss Marchmont had not lived to fulfill her promise of providing for me on that final night, her duties were honorably settled afterward. Her second cousin, the heir, paid my wages. He was a man of sharp features, with a pinched nose and narrow temples, possessing an appearance that suggested avarice. Indeed, I learned later that he was a confirmed miser, a stark and painful contrast to his generous kinswoman, whose memory remains a blessing to the poor and needy to this day.

Possessing fifteen pounds, decent health despite its weariness, and a spirit similarly resilient, I could arguably claim an enviable position compared to many others. Yet, it was an embarrassing predicament. On a specific day—one that marked the end of my tenure at the current household—I faced the reality of having nowhere else to go, while simultaneously lacking funds for the next stage of my journey. In this tight spot, I sought counsel from an old servant of our family, formerly my nurse and now the housekeeper of a grand estate nearby. I spent several hours with her; she offered comfort but lacked practical advice. Leaving her as twilight fell, I faced a two-mile walk across a clear, frosty night. Despite my solitude, perceived poverty, and confusion, my heart beat with the vigor of a youth who had not yet turned twenty-three. It did not tremble. I walked through silent fields, passing no villages or cottages, guided only by the stars and the faint glow of the Aurora Borealis in the north. Rather than frightening me, this celestial phenomenon seemed to impart a new strength. Drawing energy from the crisp breeze, a bold idea took root in my mind: "Leave this wilderness and go forth."

"Where?" I asked myself. Looking out from this rural parish toward the flat, fertile lands of central England, I visualized something I had never truly seen: London. The following day, I returned to the hall to inform the housekeeper, Mrs. Barrett, of my plans. She was a serious, sensible woman, though her knowledge of the wider world matched my own. Nevertheless, she did not deem me mad. My composed demeanor had previously shielded me from suspicion, allowing me to undertake actions that might have labeled me a dreamer if performed with excitement. As Mrs. Barrett pondered potential obstacles while preparing orange rinds for marmalade, a child bounded into the room. It was a lovely boy, dancing and laughing until he reached me. We were not strangers; his mother, a young married daughter of the house, had been a schoolmate of mine when I was ten and she sixteen. I recalled her as good-looking but dull, belonging to a lower station than ours. As I admired the boy's dark eyes, his mother, now Mrs. Leigh, entered. What a transformation marriage and motherhood had wrought upon her! She was now a beautiful, kind, and comely woman, though seemingly devoid of intellect. She had completely forgotten me, and I feared I had not improved in her estimation. She arrived to take her son for a walk, accompanied by a nurse carrying an infant. I noted the incident primarily because, when addressing the nurse, Mrs. Leigh spoke French—with a terribly bad accent that instantly transported me back to our school days. The nurse was clearly a foreigner, and the little boy chattered volubly in the same tongue.

After the family departed, Mrs. Barrett revealed that the nurse had been brought home two years prior during a continental trip. She was treated almost as a governess, tasked merely with walking with the baby and conversing in French with Master Charles. "She claims," Mrs. Barrett added, "that there are many English women in foreign households placed even higher than she." I filed this observation away, knowing that seemingly trivial details often prove useful later. Before leaving, she provided me with the address of a respectable, traditional inn in the City, noting that my uncles had frequented it in the past.

My journey to London involved less risk and enterprise than one might assume. The distance was merely fifty miles. My resources were sufficient to travel there, sustain myself for a few days, and return if necessary. I viewed this not as a life-altering adventure, but as a brief respite for weary faculties. Taking things moderately keeps the mind and body calm; grandiose ambitions tend to induce fever. Fifty miles constituted a single day's journey in those days. Arriving in London around nine o'clock on a wet February night, I found myself in a Babylonian wilderness. The vastness and strangeness of the city tested my limited mental faculties to their limit. Upon exiting the coach, the dialect of the cabmen sounded like a foreign language to me, a jumbled version of English I had never heard before. Yet, I managed to communicate and secure passage to the inn.

How difficult, oppressive, and confusing my flight felt! For the first time in London, staying at an inn, exhausted by travel, blinded by darkness, frozen by cold, and lacking any experience or guidance, I was nonetheless compelled to act. I entrusted the matter to common sense. However, common sense was as chilled and bewildered as everything else, acting only under the pressure of necessity. Her speech carried a mincing accent that seemed to mock my own, and her fashionable attire flaunted a disdainful ease against my simple country clothes. By maintaining a quiet, dignified manner toward both the snobbish maid and the waiter in his black coat and white neckcloth, I eventually secured their civility. They initially mistook me for a servant but soon shifted to a tentative mix of patronage and politeness.

I held my ground until I had eaten, warmed myself by the fire, and retired to my room. Once seated by the bed, resting my head and arms on the pillow, a profound oppression seized me. Suddenly, my solitary position loomed before me like a ghost—anomalous, desolate, and devoid of hope. What was I doing alone in great London? What awaited me tomorrow? What prospects remained? Who were my friends? Where did I come from, and whither should I go? Tears rushed down my face, soaking my pillow, arms, and hair. This burst of grief was followed by a dark interval of bitter reflection, yet I did not regret the decision nor wish to turn back. A strong, vague conviction prevailed: that it was better to move forward than backward, and that I *could* move forward. That a path, however narrow, would eventually open. This belief quieted my despair enough for me to pray and prepare for sleep.

As I extinguished my candle and lay down, a deep, low, mighty sound resonated through the night. Initially unrecognized, it repeated twelve times. With the twelfth colossal hum and trembling toll, I whispered into the darkness: "I lie in the shadow of St. Paul's."

The following morning, the first of March, dawn broke with a struggle. As I rose and pulled back my curtain, the sun fought its way through a thick, clinging fog. Hovering above the rooftops, nearly level with the clouds themselves, loomed a solemn, orb-like mass of dark blue and dimness—THE DOME. As I gazed upon it, a stirring occurred within my soul; my spirit, usually bound by invisible chains, fluttered its wings free. For the first time, I felt a sudden conviction that I, who had never truly lived, was finally about to taste life. In that single morning, my spirit expanded as rapidly as Jonah's gourd.

"I acted wisely in coming," I told myself, dressing with hurried efficiency. "I possess a genuine affinity for the grand spirit of this great London. Who, save a coward, would spend a lifetime in petty hamlets, allowing their faculties to rot in the damp rust of obscurity?"

Dressed and composed, I descended. I was not weary or travel-worn, but tidy and refreshed. When the waiter brought my breakfast, I greeted him with a mixture of sedateness and cheerfulness. Within ten minutes, we established a useful rapport.

He was a grey-haired elderly man who had served in his current post for twenty years. Knowing this, I was confident he remembered my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, who had visited frequently fifteen years prior. Mentioning their names prompted a respectful recollection. This knowledge clarified my standing in his eyes; his previously doubtful demeanor vanished, replaced by ready, obliging courtesy. From this moment, I needed fear no longer regarding how to respond to sensible inquiries.

The street outside my small sitting-room was narrow, perfectly quiet, and spotless. The few pedestrians resembled those found in provincial towns; there was nothing formidable. I felt secure venturing out alone.

Breakfast concluded, and I stepped forth. Elation filled my heart; to walk alone in London felt like an adventure in itself. Soon, I found myself in Paternoster Row, classic territory. Entering a bookseller's shop kept by Mr. Jones, I purchased a small volume—a minor extravagance I could ill afford, yet one I hoped to gift to Mrs. Barrett someday. Mr. Jones, a dried-up man of business, stood behind his desk, appearing one of the gravest figures imaginable, while I felt among the happiest.

That morning, I experienced a prodigious amount of life. Standing before St. Paul's, I ascended to the dome. From there, I beheld London: its river, its bridges, its churches. I saw ancient Westminster and the verdant Temple Gardens bathed in sunlight, beneath a glad, blue sky of early spring, separated from the earth by a haze of clouds that was not too dense.

Descending, I wandered wherever chance led, intoxicated by a still ecstasy of freedom. Somehow, I plunged into the heart of city life. I traversed the Strand, climbed Cornhill, mingled with the rushing crowds, and dared the perils of crossing the streets. Doing this entirely alone yielded a peculiar, perhaps irrational, yet profound pleasure. Though I have since visited the West End, its parks, and its fine squares, I prefer the city. The city feels more earnest; its business, its rush, its roar are serious sights and sounds. The city is earning its living; the West End merely enjoys its pleasures. In the West End, one may be amused, but in the city, one is deeply excited.

Eventually, faint and hungry—a sensation I hadn't felt in years—I returned to my dark, old, quiet inn around two o'clock. I dined on two plain dishes: a joint and vegetables. Both tasted excellent, infinitely superior to the tiny, dainty meals Miss Marchmont's cook used to send to my late mistress and me, which we could barely muster an appetite to discuss. Delightedly tired, I lay across three chairs for an hour (for the room lacked a sofa). After sleeping, I spent two hours in thought.

My state of mind, combined with these circumstances, favored the adoption of a new, resolute, and daring—even desperate—course of action. I had nothing to lose. An unutterable loathing for my desolate past forbade returning. If I failed in my design, who, save myself, would suffer? If I died far from home—or rather, from England—who would weep?

I might suffer; I was accustomed to it. Death itself held no terrors for me that it possessed for the softly raised. Having viewed mortality with a quiet eye, I prepared for any consequence and formulated a plan.

That evening, I secured from my friendly waiter information regarding sailings to a specific Continental port, Boulogne-sur-Mer. There was no time to waste; I must take my berth that very night. Although I could have waited until morning, I refused to risk arriving too late.

"Better take your berth at once, ma'am," advised the waiter. Agreeing, I settled my bill and tipped him generously—a sum I now realize was princely, though to him it must have seemed absurd. As he pocketed the cash, he offered a faint smile hinting at my *savoir-faire*. He then summoned a coach. He instructed the driver to take me directly to the wharf and not leave me to the watermen. The driver promised compliance but betrayed his oath immediately. Instead of a dignified transfer, he deposited me as an offering into the hands of a throng of watermen.

This proved an uncomfortable crisis. It was a dark night. The coachman fled the moment he received his fare, and the watermen immediately engaged in a struggle over me and my trunk. Their oaths still ring in my ears today; they shook my philosophy more profoundly than the darkness, the isolation, or the strangeness of the scene. One grabbed my trunk. I watched calmly. But when another reached for me, I spoke firmly, shook off his grasp, and stepped straight into a boat. I commanded sternly that the trunk be placed beside me—"Just there"—an instruction instantly obeyed. The owner of the boat I chose became my ally, and I was rowed away.

The river was black as a torrent of ink, reflecting lights from the surrounding buildings. Ships rocked on its bosom. We rowed past several vessels—"The Ocean," "The Phoenix," "The Consort," "The Dolphin"—until we reached "The Vivid," my ship, lying further down.

Gliding down the sable flood, I thought of the Styx and Charon ferrying a solitary soul to the Land of Shades. Amidst this strange tableau, with a chilling wind biting my face and midnight rain falling, accompanied by two rude rowers whose insane oaths still tortured my ear, I questioned whether I was wretched or terrified. I was neither. Often in my life I have suffered far more under safer conditions. "How is this?" I wondered. "I feel animated and alert, not depressed or apprehensive." I could not explain why.

"The Vivid" emerged from the black night, white and glaring. "Here you are!" shouted the waterman, instantly demanding six shillings.

"You ask too much," I replied. He retreated from the vessel, swearing he would not embark me without payment. A young man, the steward, leaned over the ship's side, grinning in anticipation of the dispute. To disappoint him, I paid the money. That afternoon, I had paid crowns where shillings sufficed, but I comforted myself with the reflection: "It is the price of experience."

"They've cheated you!" exclaimed the steward triumphantly as I boarded. I answered phlegmatically, "I knew it," and went below.

In the ladies' cabin sat a stout, handsome, and showy woman. Requesting my berth, she scrutinized me closely, muttering about the unusual nature of passengers boarding at such an hour, and displayed a disposition less than civil. What a face she had—so comely, yet so insolent and selfish!

"Now that I am on board, I shall certainly stay here," I declared. "Please show me my berth."

She complied, albeit sullenly. Removing my bonnet and arranging my things, I lay down. Difficulties had been overcome; a sort of victory achieved. My homeless, anchorless, unsupported mind finally found leisure for brief repose. Until the *Vivid* docked, no further action was required, though... Oh! I could not look ahead. Harassed and exhausted, I drifted into a half-trance.

The stewardess talked incessantly all night—not to me, but to her young son, the steward, who was her very image. He moved constantly in and out of the cabin. They disputed, quarreled, and reconciled twenty times throughout the night. She claimed to be writing a letter home to her father, reading passages aloud while ignoring me as if I were a piece of furniture. Several passages revealed family secrets, specifically concerning a younger sister named Charlotte, seemingly on the verge of a romantic and imprudent match. The elder lady protested loudly against this distasteful union, while the dutiful son laughed her correspondence to scorn. She defended it and raged at him. They were a strange pair. She might have been thirty-nine or forty, yet bloomed as vibrantly as a girl of twenty. Hard, loud, vain, and vulgar, her mind and body seemed brazen and imperishable. I suspected she had lived in public stations since childhood, perhaps working as a barmaid in her youth.

As I slowly made my way down the broad staircase, pausing on each step because I had no real urge to hurry, I gazed upward at the soaring ceilings, admired the painted walls, and marveled at the vast windows flooding the space with light. I wondered at the keen insight displayed by the waiters and chambermaids in assessing their guests. How could they instantly discern that I was someone of no social standing, carrying little wealth? They certainly knew; within moments of seeing me, they seemed to calculate my worth at roughly the same negligible fraction. Finally reaching the great hall, bathed in the glare of skylights, I navigated my way to the coffee room. Entering, I trembled slightly, feeling uncertain, solitary, and wretched. I wished desperately to know if I was acting rightly or wrongly, convinced it was my final stand, yet powerless to stop myself. Adopting the calm demeanor of a fatalist, I seated myself at a small table. Soon, a waiter delivered my breakfast, which I consumed in a state of mind ill-suited for digestion.

Many others were eating at separate tables. I would have felt happier had I spotted a single woman among them, but there wasn't one; everyone present was a man. Yet, no one seemed to view my presence as strange. Occasionally, a gentleman glanced my way, but no one stared rudely. After breakfast, I faced another decision: where to go next? An inner voice urged, "Go to Villette." I knew nothing of who Madame Beck was or where she lived. I had asked, but my question went unheard as Miss Fanshawe hurried away. Assuming Villette was her residence, I decided to go there. The distance was forty miles. I knew I was grasping at straws, but lost in the vast, turbulent depths where I found myself, I would clutch at cobwebs if necessary.

Having arranged passage on a diligence, I departed with nothing but this faint outline—a shadow of a plan. Do not judge my rashness too quickly, dear reader. Consider where I began; consider the desert I had left behind. What little risk I undertook was akin to a game where one cannot lose and stands to gain everything. Though I lack an artistic temperament, I do possess the artist's ability to maximize present pleasure when it suits my taste. I enjoyed that day, despite the slow travel, the cold, and the rain. The landscape was barren, flat, and treeless, with slimy canals winding alongside the road like half-asleep green snakes, bordered by formal pollard willows lining fields tilled like garden plots. The sky was a monotonous gray, the air stagnant and humid. Yet, amidst these life-draining conditions, my imagination bloomed anew, and my heart warmed to the sunlight.

However, these feelings were constantly checked by a secret, relentless awareness of anxiety lurking nearby, waiting to devour my joy like a tiger coiled in the jungle. Its breath whispered in my ear; its fierce heart pounded against mine. It never shifted from its lair, yet I felt its presence; I knew it waited only for sunset to spring forth, ravenous.

I had hoped to reach Villette before nightfall, hoping to avoid the deeper confusion that often surrounds a lone traveler arriving at an unknown destination. I ask this question now, but I could not ask it then. I could say nothing. Possessing not a single phrase of *speaking* French, I was surrounded entirely by a babbling crowd speaking only French. *What* should I do?

Approaching the conductor, I placed my hand on his arm, pointed to a trunk, then to the roof of the carriage, and attempted to convey my query with my eyes. He misunderstood, seized the indicated trunk, and prepared to hoist it onto the vehicle.

Hearing the familiar accent of the Fatherland brought me immense relief. Turning to the stranger, I appealed to him without noting his appearance in my distress: "Sir, I cannot speak French. May I beg you to ask this man what he has done with my trunk?"

Without initially distinguishing the face to which I looked, I sensed a mixture of surprise at my plea and doubt regarding the wisdom of interfering. "*Do* ask him; I would do the same for you," I insisted.

Whether he smiled, I do not know, but he responded in a gentlemanly tone—neither harsh nor terrifying—"What sort of trunk was yours?"

I described it, mentioning the green ribbon. Immediately, he took hold of the conductor, and through the ensuing storm of French, I felt him thoroughly interrogating the man. Moments later, he returned to me.

"Thank you," I replied, but my heart sank.

Meanwhile, what should I do? Perhaps the English gentleman perceived the failure of courage in my expression. Kindly inquiring, "Do you have any friends in this city?"

"No, and I do not know where to go."

That night, my devotions were entirely acts of thanksgiving. My journey since morning had been strangely directed; my provisions unexpectedly generous. Hardly could I believe that less than forty-eight hours had passed since I left London, protected only by the vague guardianship afforded to a passenger bird, with nothing but the doubtful tracery of clouds to guide my hope.

I was a light sleeper. In the dead of night, I suddenly awoke. The room was hushed, yet a white figure stood within it—Madame, in her nightdress. Moving without a sound, she visited the three children in their beds, then approached me. Feigning sleep, I watched her study me for a long time. A small pantomime ensued, curious in its precision. She likely sat upon the edge of my bed for a quarter-hour, gazing at my face. Then, drawing near, she bent close, slightly raised my cap, and turned back the border to expose my hair. She examined my hand resting on the bedclothes.

Having completed this inspection, she turned to the chair at the foot of the bed where my clothes lay. Hearing her touch and lift them, I opened my eyes cautiously, driven by curiosity to see how far her thirst for research would lead. It led her far. Every article was inspected. I divined her motive: she wished to judge the wearer—my station, my means, my neatness—from the garments themselves. The intent was not malicious, but the methods were hardly fair or justifiable. Finding a pocket in my dress, she turned it inside out. She counted the coins in my purse. She opened a small memorandum book, coolly perusing its contents, and extracted a small, plaited lock of Miss Marchmont's grey hair hidden between the leaves.

She paid special attention to a bunch of three keys belonging to my trunk, desk, and work-box. Indeed, she withdrew briefly to her own room. Rising softly in bed, I followed her movements with my eyes. These keys, dear reader, were not returned until they had left the impression of their wards in the wax of the toilet in the adjoining room. Once everything was done decently and in order, my property was restored to its place, and my clothes carefully refolded. What conclusions did she draw from this scrutiny? Were they favorable? A vain question. Madame's face, in its nocturnal aspect, resembled stone. It had been human and motherly in the salon, but now it betrayed no response whatsoever.

Her duty fulfilled—she viewed this entire business strictly as a duty—she rose, noiseless as a shadow, and moved toward her own chamber. Mrs. Sweeny (I presume this was her name, whether Anglicized or Hibernian, Sweeny)—Mrs. Sweeny's fate was written in Madame Beck's eyes. An immutable purpose shone there. The following morning acquainted me further with Mrs. Sweeny. Apparently, she had introduced herself to her current employer as an English lady in reduced circumstances, a native of Middlesex claiming to speak the English tongue with the purest metropolitan accent. Madame, relying on her own infallible methods for uncovering the truth in time, displayed a singular intrepidity in hiring service immediately (as my own case seemed to prove abundantly). She accepted Mrs. Sweeny as nursery-governess to her three children.

Needless to explain to the reader that this lady was, in fact, a native of Ireland. Her social standing remains undefined to me; she boldly claimed to have "brought up the son and daughter of a marquis." I suspect she may have been a hanger-on, nurse, fosterer, or washerwoman in some Irish family. She spoke a smothered tongue, curiously overlaid with mincing Cockney inflections. Somehow, she had acquired and retained a wardrobe of suspicious splendor: gowns of stiff, costly silk that fitted her poorly, seemingly made for different proportions; caps with real lace borders; and, the chief item in her inventory, the spell by which she commanded awe throughout the household, quelling the otherwise scornful teachers and servants, and even influencing Madame herself—as long as her broad shoulders bore the folds of that majestic drapery—a **real Indian shawl**, *"un véritable cachemire,"* as Madame Beck said with unmixed reverence and amazement. I am quite certain that without this "cachemire" she would not have kept her footing in the pensionnat for two days; by virtue of it, and it alone, she maintained her position for a month.

However, when Mrs. Sweeny discovered that I had arrived to fill her shoes, she revealed herself fully. Then she rose against Madame Beck in her entirety, descending upon me with her concentrated weight. Madame bore this revelation and confrontation so well, so stoically, that I, overcome by shame, could offer no defense but composure. For one brief moment, Madame Beck absented herself from the room. Ten minutes later, a police officer stood amidst us. Mrs. Sweeny and her effects were removed. Madame's brow had not ruffled during the scene; not one sharply accented word fell from her lips.

This brisk little affair of dismissal was settled before breakfast. Order to march was given, the policeman called, the mutineer expelled; the "children's chamber" was fumigated, cleansed, and ventilated. Every trace of the accomplished Mrs. Sweeny—even the fine essence and spiritual fragrance that had served as such a subtle and fatal token of her offense—was annihilated from Rue Fossette. All this was accomplished between the moment Madame Beck issued forth like Aurora from her chamber and the moment she calmly sat down to pour her first cup of coffee.

Around noon, I was summoned to dress Madame. (It appeared my role was a hybrid between governess and lady's-maid.) Until noon, she haunted the house in her wrapping-gown, shawl, and soundless slippers. How would the headmistress of an English school approve of such customs?

Dressing her hair puzzled me. She had plenty of it: auburn, unmixed with grey, though she was forty years old. Seeing my embarrassment, she remarked, "You have not been a *femme-de-chambre* in your own country?" Taking the brush from my hand and setting me aside—not unkindly or disrespectfully—she arranged it herself. In performing other offices of the toilette, she half-directed, half-assisted me, displaying no temper or impatience. *Note:* That was the first and last time I was required to dress her. From then on, that duty devolved upon Rosine, the portress.

My days were suddenly filled with purposeful industry. Between instructing others and sharpening my own intellect, I possessed scarcely a moment of leisure. Yet, it was a delightful occupation. I felt myself advancing, no longer stagnating like prey rotting in the damp, but honing my faculties through constant exercise. Before me lay a vast field of experience. Villette is a cosmopolitan hub, and within Madame Beck's school gathered girls from nearly every European nation, hailing from ranks ranging from the highest nobility to the humblest bourgeoisie. At Labassecour, equality was practiced not merely in theory but in substance; though the republic remained a distant ideal, the reality at the desks was striking: the young countess sat shoulder-to-shoulder with the common citizen. One could rarely distinguish the noble from the plebeian by appearance alone. Indeed, the latter often displayed a frankness and courtesy that the former lacked. While the lower-born possessed a natural grace, the aristocracy frequently exhibited a delicate blend of insolence and deceit. Often, a dash of fiery French blood mingled with the local phlegm produced a glib, oily eloquence, where flattery and fabrication flowed effortlessly from the lips—light, lively, yet utterly heartless and insincere.

To be fair, the native daughters of Labassecour possessed their own brand of hypocrisy, albeit a coarse one capable of deceiving few. When a lie served their purposes, they delivered it with a casual breadth, entirely unburdened by conscience. No one in Madame Beck's house, from the scullery maid to the director herself, harbored shame regarding a falsehood. They deemed invention not a virtue, but the most forgivable of sins. "I have lied several times" was a standard item in every girl's monthly confession, met by the priest with no shock and granted absolution without reluctance. Conversely, missing Mass or reading a forbidden novel were treated as grave crimes, warranting stern rebuke and penance.

Though I was only half-aware of this moral landscape, I progressed well in my new role. After the initial perilous lessons—taught amidst the rumbling of a moral volcano sending sparks of anxiety into my eyes—the turbulent spirit eventually subsided. My mind was fixated on success; I could not tolerate the thought of failing due to mere indiscipline or stubbornness in my first attempt at independence. Sleepless nights were spent devising strategies to gain control over these mutinous spirits, to bring this stiff-necked tribe under permanent influence.

First and foremost, I realized that assistance from Madame was impossible. Her strategy was to maintain popularity with the pupils at any cost to justice or teacher comfort. Seeking her alliance during an uprising was tantamount to inviting one's own dismissal. Madame reserved for herself only the pleasant and agreeable interactions, leaving her lieutenants to handle crises with sufficient promptitude, regardless of the unpopularity involved. Consequently, I had to rely solely on myself.

It was evident that brute force would fail against this swinish multitude. They required patience, humor, and a courteous demeanor. A rare flash of wit worked wonders, whereas severe or relentless intellectual demands were rejected outright. Where an average English girl would quietly accept a challenge to master a subject, a Labassecourienne would laugh in one's face, retreating with the cry, "God, how difficult! I refuse. It bores me too much."

A skilled teacher would immediately withdraw the demand, smoothing every difficulty until it matched the student's understanding, only to return it modified. Then, with a hearty, unsparing sarcasm, she would expose their incapacity, ignorance, and laziness. Provided the sneer was bold and clear, not sour, the students would feel the sting but bear no malice. Their fragile self-respect, trained to be crushed, actually preferred the pressure of a firm heel.

Gradually, as I gained fluency in their language and mastered their nervous idioms, the older and smarter girls began to favor me. Whenever I succeeded in igniting a spark of emulation or honest shame within a pupil, she was won. If I could make their ears burn beneath their thick, glossy hair, victory was assured. Soon, bouquets began appearing on my desk. In exchange for this foreign attention, I occasionally walked with a select few during breaks. However, when I attempted to correct their distorted views of morality—particularly condemning the baseness of a lie—an awkward silence fell between us. The girls, taught to report everything a Protestant teacher said to their Catholic ears, interpreted my words differently. Bouquets continued, but conversation became impossible. As I strolled the alleys or sat in the pergola, a teacher would magically appear at my left flank, and Madame, silent as a zephyr, would loom behind me.

One day, a pensionnaire named Isabelle, to whom I had rendered a small kindness, exclaimed naively, "Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!"

"Why, Isabelle?"

She must possess excellent blood, for no duchess ever exuded nonchalance more perfectly or radically. A weak, fleeting amazement was the limit of her wonder. She was not proud. Being naturally fond of children, she instantly sought to make me a friend and confidante. I finally set her straight—a correction I should have administered sooner, as it always suited her.

Her demands for labor were even more burdensome. While her wardrobe of outerwear was elegant, her undergarments required constant repair. Hating needlework herself, she piled her stockings and other items onto my lap to mend. Threatened with weeks of intolerable drudgery, I firmly declared she must mend her own clothes. She burst into tears, accusing me of ending our friendship, but I stood my ground, letting the hysteria pass.

Despite these foibles—and others of a less refined nature—how beautiful she was! How charming she looked on a sunny Sunday morning, dressed in pale lilac silk, her fair long curls resting on her white shoulders. Sundays were holidays spent with friends in town, among whom she quickly revealed one who desired to become more than a friend. Through hints and glances, it became clear that ardent admiration, perhaps genuine love, was at her disposal. She called her suitor "Isidore," though she noted this was a pseudonym, as his real name was not "very pretty." Once, boasting of "Isidore's" fervor, I asked if she loved him in return.

"As that," she replied casually. "He is handsome, and he loves me to distraction, so I am well amused. That is enough."

Finding she prolonged this game longer than her fickle nature suggested, I inquired seriously whether her parents, and especially her uncle upon whom she depended, would approve. She admitted doubt, noting "Isidore" apparently lacked funds.

"Do you encourage him?" I asked.

"Furiously, sometimes," she answered.

"Without certainty that you will be permitted to marry him?"

"Oh, how dowdyish you are! I don't want to be married. I am too young."

"But if he loves you as much as you claim, and it ends in nothing, he will be devastated."

"Of course he will break his heart. I would be shocked and disappointed if he didn't."

"I wonder if this M. Isidore is a fool?"

"He is, regarding me; but he is wise in other things, as they say. Mrs. Cholmondeley considers him extremely clever; she believes he will advance through his talents. All I know is that he does little more than sigh in my presence, and I can wind him around my little finger."

Seeking a clearer picture of this love-stricken gentleman, whose position seemed precarious, I requested a physical description. She could provide none, lacking the vocabulary or ability to construct graphic phrases. She seemed not to have truly noticed him; the only assertion she could make was that he was "handsome, but rather a fine man than a pretty boy." My patience often faltered listening to her, save for one thing: every hint and detail she dropped unconsciously proved that M. Isidore's homage was offered with great delicacy and respect. I plainly informed her I believed him far too good for her and implied she was merely a vain coquette. She laughed, shook her curls from her eyes, and danced away as if I had paid her a compliment.

Madame Beck was a figure of remarkable consistency: forbearing toward everyone, yet tender toward no one. Even her own children failed to draw her from her stoic composure. While she was deeply solicitous regarding their futures and vigilant over their physical welfare, she possessed no desire to hold them close, to press their rosy lips against her own, or to envelop them in a warm embrace. I have watched her sit in the garden, observing the tiny bees wandering a distant path alongside Trinette, their *bonne*. In her demeanor, one read only care and prudence. She would sneak into their attics, rummage through their drawers and boxes, wantonly tear their finest caps and soil their best shawls. Whatever belonged to Madame's worktable or toilet that she could reach, she stole and concealed. Maintaining this hollow charade, the mother would calmly pretend to believe the denial, then relentlessly track the child until she found her hiding spots—some hole in the garden wall, some crack in the garret or outbuilding. Once located, Madame would send Désirée out for a walk with her *bonne*, using the child's absence to retrieve the stolen goods herself. The second child, Fifine, was said to resemble her deceased father. Certainly, though the mother had bestowed upon her a healthy frame, blue eyes, and ruddy cheeks, her moral character was not derived from her. Likewise, his overall appearance—his voice, demeanor, and manner—worked in his favor. As he passed me leaving the room, turning his face briefly in my direction—not to address me, but to speak to Madame, yet positioned so that I naturally looked up at him—a recollection that had been struggling to form in my memory since the first moment I heard his voice suddenly crystallized. Dr. Pillule had been summoned to treat a wealthy, elderly hypochondriac in the ancient university town of Bouquin-Moisi. Upon prescribing a change of air and travel as remedies, he was retained to accompany the timid patient on a tour lasting several weeks. Désirée, miraculously cured of her previous ailments, along with Fifine, had been hurried off to the countryside with Bonne-Maman as a precautionary measure against contagion. A few of the boarders began complaining of headaches and showed signs of sharing Georgette's malaise. Madame was indeed very prudent, yet she possessed a terrifying capacity for boldness. She actually introduced Dr. John into the school section of the building, establishing him as the attending physician for the proud and handsome Blanche de Melcy and her vain, flirtatious friend, Angélique.

Dr. John, I observed, seemed gratified by this mark of confidence. If discretion alone could justify the move, he would have done so abundantly. However, in a land dominated by convents and confessionals, such a presence could not be tolerated with impunity in a *pensionnat de demoiselles*. The school gossip mill churned, whispers flew from the kitchen, rumors spread through the town, and parents wrote angry letters and paid visits of remonstrance. Had Madame been weak, she would have been ruined instantly; a dozen rival educational institutions were poised to exploit this faux pas. But Madame was not weak. Though a little Jesuit in her methods, I clapped my hands and shouted "Brava!" watching her capable demeanor, her skilled management, and her unshakeable temper.

She met the alarmed parents with a good-humored, easy grace unmatched by anyone else—a certain *rondeur et franchise* (roundness and frankness) of a good woman that achieved results where severe gravity would have failed. "Poor Doctor Jean!" she would chuckle, rubbing her fat, white hands with joyful affection. "My dear young man! The best creature in the world!" She would explain how she employed him for her own children, who screamed at the thought of any other doctor; how, trusting him implicitly for her family, it was natural to extend that trust to others; and how, besides, it was merely a temporary expedient. The inhabitants of La Bassesse Couri must possess a vast organ of philoprogenitiveness; the indulgence of offspring seems to carry them to excessive lengths, with the children's will being the supreme law of the household. Madame now earned credit for acting with maternal partiality; she emerged victorious, and people liked her as a Directress better than ever. To this day, I never fully understood why she risked her reputation for the sake of Dr. John. Of course, everyone knew what they said: the entire house—pupils, teachers, and servants alike—affirmed she was going to marry him. They had decided it; the difference in age posed no obstacle in their eyes. It was simply to be so.

Appearances did not entirely contradict this notion. Madame seemed bent on retaining his services, seemingly oblivious to her former protégé, Pillule. She made a point of receiving his visits personally, appearing unfailingly cheerful, blithe, and benevolent toward him. Furthermore, around this time, she paid marked attention to her attire. The morning disarray, nightcaps, and shawls were discarded. Every morning visit found Dr. John greeted by Madame with neatly arranged auburn braids, a silk dress trimly fitted, and neat laced brodequins instead of slippers—in short, a complete toilette modeled on perfection, fresh as a flower. I scarcely think her intention went further than showing a very handsome man that she was not quite a plain woman; and plain she was not. Lacking conventional beauty of feature or elegance of form, she nonetheless pleased. Devoid of youthful gaiety, she cheered. One never grew tired of seeing her; she was never monotonous, insipid, colorless, or flat. Her unfaded hair, her eye with its temperate blue light, her cheek with its wholesome, fruit-like bloom—these qualities pleased in moderation but with constancy.

Did she harbor visions of adopting Dr. John as a husband, taking him to her well-furnished home, endowing him with her savings—which were rumored to amount to a moderate competency—and ensuring his comfort for the rest of his days? Did Dr. John suspect such dreams? I have seen him emerge from her presence with a mischievous half-smile playing about his lips and a look in his eyes suggesting masculine vanity, elated and tickled. With all his good looks and good nature, he was not perfect; he must have been very imperfect if he roguishly encouraged aims he never intended to succeed. But did he not intend them to succeed? People claimed he had no money and was wholly dependent on his profession. Madame, though perhaps fourteen years his senior, was the sort of woman who never aged, never withered, never broke down. They were certainly on excellent terms. Perhaps *he* was not in love; but how many people in this world truly love, or even marry for love? We waited for the outcome. For what *he* waited, I do not know, nor what he watched for; but the peculiarity of his manner—an expectant, vigilant, absorbed, eager gaze—never faded; it only intensified. He had never been quite within the reach of my understanding, and I felt he ranged farther and farther beyond it.

Not a living soul remained in the room except herself, aside from some goldfish in a glass globe, a few potted flowers, and a broad beam of July sunlight. Behind Madame Beck's house on the Rue Fossette lay a garden. Large, considering its location in the heart of a bustling city, and to my memory today, it appears a place of genuine charm. Yet, time, much like distance, possesses a softening influence over specific scenes. Where everything else is composed of cold stone, blank walls, and scorching pavements, how precious does a single shrub seem, and how delightful an enclosed, planted patch of earth!

There persisted a local tradition that Madame Beck's residence had once been a convent. In ages past—how many centuries exactly I cannot say, though I suspect several—before the city had swallowed this quarter, when it was still tilled fields and tree-lined avenues offering the deep, leafy seclusion fitting for a religious house, some event occurred on this very site. This incident, capable of arousing terror and inflicting horror, left the place inheriting a ghost story. A vague tale circulated of a black-and-white nun, occasionally spotted on certain nights of the year in parts of this neighborhood. The ghost must have been conjured eons ago, for now the area was surrounded by houses; yet, certain relics of the convent survived in the form of ancient, massive fruit trees. These consecrated the spot. At the base of one—a true Methuselah of a pear tree, dead save for a few stubborn boughs that still faithfully renewed their perfumed blossoms in spring and their honey-sweet fruits in autumn—one could see, by scraping away the mossy earth between the half-exposed roots, a glimpse of a slab: smooth, hard, and black.

The legend, unconfirmed and unaccredited yet widely propagated, claimed this was the entrance to a vault. Deep beneath that ground, where grass grew and flowers bloomed, lay imprisoned the bones of a girl whom a monkish council of the dreary Middle Ages had buried alive for some sin against her vows. It was her shadow that terrified trembling souls through generations after her poor frame turned to dust; it was her black robe and white veil that, for timid eyes, moonlight and shadows mocked as they danced in the night wind through the garden thickets.

Setting aside this romantic rubbish, however, the old garden possessed its own undeniable charms. On summer mornings, I used to rise early to enjoy them in solitude; on summer evenings, I would linger alone, keeping tryst with the rising moon, tasting a single kiss of the evening breeze, or imagining rather than feeling the fresh descent of dew. The turf was verdant, the gravel paths white; sun-bright nasturtiums clustered beautifully around the gnarled roots of the ancient orchard giants. There was a large *berceau* shaded by an acacia, and a smaller, more secluded bower nestled among vines running along a high, grey wall. These tendrils gathered in knots of beauty, hanging their clusters in loving profusion around the favored spot where jasmine and ivy intertwined.

Doubtless, at high noon, in the broad, vulgar middle of the day, when Madame Beck's large school turned out rampant, and externes and pensionnaires were spread abroad, vying with the boys' college nearby in the brazen exercise of their lungs and limbs, the garden was merely a trite, trodden-down place. But at sunset, or during the hour of *salut*, when the externes had gone home and the boarders were quiet at their studies, it was pleasant to stray down the peaceful alleys and hear the bells of St. Jean Baptiste peal out with their sweet, soft, exalted sound.

I was walking thus one evening, detained longer within the verge of twilight than usual by the still-deepening calm, the mellow coolness, and the fragrant breathing of flowers answering the persuasion of the dew, for no sunshine could win them now. I noticed a light in the oratory window; the Catholic household was gathered for evening prayer—a rite from which, as a Protestant, I occasionally exempted myself.

"One moment longer," whispered solitude and the summer moon. "Stay with us: all is truly quiet now. For another quarter of an hour, your presence will not be missed. The day's heat and bustle have tired you; enjoy these precious minutes."

The windowless backs of the houses bordering the garden, particularly the entire length of one side, were skirted by the rear of a long line of premises belonging to the neighboring college. This rear was all blank stone, save for certain attic loopholes high up, opening from the servants' sleeping rooms, and one casement in a lower story said to mark the chamber or study of a master. Though thus seemingly secure, an alley running parallel to this high wall was forbidden to the pupils. It was known as "l'allée défendue" (the forbidden alley); any girl stepping foot there would incur the severest penalty the mild rules of Madame Beck's establishment allowed. Teachers might enter with impunity, but since the walk was narrow and the neglected shrubs had grown thick and close on each side, weaving a roof of branch and leaf that let sunlight penetrate only in rare patches, the alley was seldom entered even during the day and carefully avoided after dusk.

From the start, I was tempted to break this rule of avoidance. The seclusion, the very gloom of the walk, attracted me. For a long time, the fear of appearing singular kept me away, but gradually, as people became accustomed to me and my habits—and to the peculiarities ingrained in my nature, shades certainly not striking enough to interest, perhaps not prominent enough to offend, but born with me and inseparable from my identity—I slowly became a frequent visitor to this strait and narrow path. I made myself the gardener of some colorless flowers growing between its closely ranked shrubs; I cleared away the relics of past autumns, choking up a rustic seat at the far end. Borrowing a pail of water and a scrubbing brush from Goton, the cook, I cleaned the seat thoroughly.

Madame saw me working and smiled with approval. Whether sincerely or not, I could not tell, but she *seemed* sincere. "Voyez-vous," she cried, "how neat this Miss Lucie is! You love this alley, do you not, mesdemoiselles?"

"Yes," I replied, "it is quiet and shady."

"C'est juste," she exclaimed with an air of kindness, recommending that I confine myself to it as much as I pleased. Since I was not charged with supervision, she noted, I need not trouble myself to walk with the pupils; I was free to allow her children to come there to speak English with me.

On that particular night, I sat on the reclaimed seat, listening to what seemed the far-off sounds of the city. They were not far off, in truth; this school was in the city center, mere five minutes' walk from the park, and less than ten from buildings of palatial splendor. Wide streets brightly lit teemed with life nearby; carriages rolled through them bound for balls or the opera. The same hour that tolled curfew for our convent, extinguishing lamps and dropping curtains around couches, rang for the gay city about us a summons to festal enjoyment. Of this contrast, however, I thought little. Gay instincts were few in my nature; I had never seen a ball or an opera. Though I had often heard them described and even wished to see them, it was not the yearning of one hoping to partake of a pleasure she could reach, nor the hunger to taste it. It was merely the calm desire to look upon a new thing.

A moon hung in the sky—not a full moon, but a young crescent. I saw her through a gap in the overhead boughs. She and the stars beside her were no strangers where all else was strange; my childhood knew them. I had seen that golden sign with the dark globe in its curve leaning back on azure, beside an old thorn at the top of an old field in Old England, in long-past days, just as it now leaned back beside a stately spire in this continental capital. Oh, my childhood! I had feelings. Passive as I lived, little as I spoke, cold as I appeared, when I thought of past days, I *could* feel. Regarding the present, it was better to be stoical; regarding the future—such a future as mine—it was better to be dead. And in a state of catalepsy and dead trance, I studiously held the quick of my nature.

At that time, I well remembered whatever could excite me. Certain accidents of the weather, for instance, were almost dreaded, for they woke the being I was always lulling and stirred up a craving cry I could not satisfy. One night a thunderstorm broke; a sort of hurricane shook us in our beds; the Catholics rose in panic and prayed to their saints. As for me, the tempest took hold of me with tyranny. I was roughly roused and forced to live. I got up, dressed, and crept outside the casement close to my bed, sitting on its ledge with my feet on the roof of a lower adjoining building. It was wet, wild, and pitch-black. Within the dormitory, they gathered round the night-lamp in consternation, praying aloud. I could not go in; too irresistible was the delight of staying with the wild hour, black and full of thunder, pealing out an ode that language had never delivered to man—too terribly glorious, the spectacle of clouds split and pierced by white, blinding bolts.

Villette possesses a climate as fickle as any English town, though perhaps less damp. A night of fierce winds succeeded a gentle sunset, leaving the following day as a dry tempest: skies dark and clouded, yet devoid of rain. The streets were obscured by swirling sands and dust kicked up from the boulevards. Even if the weather had been perfectly lovely, I doubted I would have wished to spend my evening hours studying and resting in the same location as yesterday. My alleyway, and indeed all the paths and shrubberies in the garden, had acquired a new, albeit unpleasant, significance. Their privacy was now precarious; their tranquility insecure. That casement which had previously showered me with notes had vulgarized the once cherished nook it overlooked. Elsewhere, the "eyes" of the flowers seemed to have gained sight, and the knots in the tree trunks listened like secret ears.

Some plants had indeed been trampled by Dr. John in his hurried search, and his careless progress—which I hoped to mend, water, and restore—had left marks on the flower beds. Yet, despite the strong wind, I managed to erase these footprints early in the morning, before anyone else could notice them. With a thoughtful sort of satisfaction, I sat down to my desk and my German studies while the pupils settled into their evening lessons and the other teachers picked up their embroidery.

The setting for our "evening study" (*étude du soir*) was invariably the refectory, a much smaller room than any of the three class rooms or schoolrooms. Only boarders were admitted here, numbering merely twenty. Two lamps hung from the ceiling over the two tables; their lighting at dusk signaled the end of schoolbooks, the assumption of grave demeanors, enforced silence, and the commencement of *la lecture pieuse* (pious reading). As I soon discovered, this "pious reading" was primarily designed as a wholesome mortification of the intellect, a useful humiliation of reason—a dose of common sense meant to be digested at leisure.

The book selected (which was never changed, but recommenced upon finishing) was a venerable volume, old as the hills and grey as the Hôtel de Ville. I would have given two francs for the chance to seize that book, turn over its sacred yellow pages, ascertain the title, and read with my own eyes the enormous fabrications that, as an unworthy heretic, I was only permitted to absorb through my bewildered ears. It contained legends of the saints. Good heavens! (I speak these words reverently) what legends they were! What boastful rogues those saints must have been if they first claimed these exploits or invented these miracles. These legends were nothing more than monkish exaggerations, over which one laughed inwardly. Furthermore, there were priestly matters, and the priestcraft of the book was far worse than its monkery. My ears burned on either side of my head as I listened, unwillingly, to tales of moral martyrdom inflicted by Rome; to the terrifying boasts of confessors who had wickedly abused their offices, trampling high-born ladies into deep degradation, reducing countesses and princesses to the most tormented slaves under the sun. Stories like that of Conrad and Elizabeth of Hungary recurred again and again, filled with dreadful vice, sickening tyranny, and black impiety—tales that were nightmares of oppression, privation, and agony.

I endured this "pious reading" for several nights as well as I could, remaining as quiet as possible, until once I accidentally snapped the points of my scissors by sticking them too deeply into the worm-eaten wood of the table before me. But finally, it became so unbearable; my temples, heart, and wrists throbbed with feverish heat, and my sleep afterward was shattered by excitement, that I could endure it no longer. Prudence dictated that I must clear the premises swiftly the moment that guilty old book was produced. No Mause Headrigg ever felt a stronger urge to testify against Sergeant Bothwell than I did regarding this papal "pious reading." Nevertheless, I managed to curb my impulses. Whenever Rosine lit the lamps, I slipped out of the room quickly yet quietly, seizing the brief moment of activity before the dead silence descended, and vanishing while the boarders stowed their books away.

When I disappeared, I entered darkness. Candles were forbidden, and the teacher leaving the refectory had only the unlit hall, schoolroom, or bedroom as refuge. In winter, I sought the long classrooms, pacing rapidly to keep warm—fortunate if the moon shone, or if there were only stars, soon reconciling myself to their dim glow or even their total absence. In summer, it was never quite dark; then I ascended to my quarters in the long dormitory, opened my casement (that chamber was lit by five windows as large as great doors), leaned out, and gazed upon the city beyond the garden, listening to band music drifting from the park or the palace square, lost in my own thoughts, living my own life in my own still, shadow-world.

That evening, fleeing as usual before the Pope and his works, I climbed the staircase, approached the dormitory, and quietly opened the door, which was always kept firmly shut and revolved noiselessly on well-oiled hinges. Before I saw anything, I *felt* that life was present in the great room, usually empty. Not that there was stir, breath, or rustle of sound, but vacuum was lacking; solitude was not at home. All the white beds—the *"lits d'ange"* (angel beds), as they were poetically termed—were visible at a glance; all were empty; no sleeper rested therein.

The sound of a drawer sliding out cautiously struck my ear. Stepping slightly to one side, my vision took a free range, unimpeded by falling curtains. I now commanded my own bed and my own toilet, with a locked workbox upon it and locked drawers beneath. Very well. A dumpy, motherly little figure, clad in a decent shawl and the cleanest possible nightcap, stood before this toilet, seemingly performing me the kindness of "tidying out" the furniture. The lid of the workbox was open, the top drawer ajar; dutifully and impartially, each succeeding drawer was opened in turn. Not a single item was left untouched; not a paper glanced over without inspection, not a small box unlidded. Beautiful was the adroitness, exemplary the care with which the search was conducted. Madame worked at it like a true star, "unhurried yet unremitting."

I will not deny that I watched her with secret glee. Had I been a gentleman, I believe Madame would have found favor in my eyes; she was so handy, neat, and thorough in everything she did. Some people's movements provoke the soul with their loose awkwardness, whereas hers satisfied it with their trim compactness. I stood, in short, fascinated. Yet, it was necessary to make an effort to break this spell; a retreat must be beaten. The searcher might have turned and caught me; there would have been nothing for it then but a scene, and she and I would have had to confront each other suddenly, stripped of conventions and disguises. We would have looked into each other's eyes and realized we could work together no more, destined to part forever. Where was the use of tempting such a catastrophe? I was not angry, nor did I wish to leave her. I could hardly find another employer whose yoke was so light and easy to bear; truly, I liked Madame for her capital sense, whatever I might think of her principles. As to her system, it harmed me not; she might work me with it to her heart's content; nothing would come of the operation. Loverless and unsuspecting of love, I was as safe from spies in my poverty of heart as the beggar is from thieves in his emptiness of purse.

I turned and fled, descending the stairs with progress as swift and silent as that of a spider running down the banister at the same instant. How I laughed when I reached the schoolroom! I knew then that she had certainly seen Dr. John in the garden; I knew what her thoughts were. The spectacle of a suspicious nature so thoroughly misled by its own inventions tickled me greatly. Yet, as the laugh died, a kind of wrath smote me, followed by bitterness. It was the rock struck, and Meribah's waters gushing out. I never felt such a strange and contradictory inner tumult as I did that evening: soreness and laughter, fire and grief shared my heart between them. I shed hot tears—not because Madame mistrusted me (for I cared twopence for her mistrust)—but for other reasons. Complicated, disquieting thoughts shattered the entire repose of my nature. However, that turmoil subsided; the next day I was once again Lucy Snowe.

Upon revisiting my drawers, I found them all securely locked; the closest subsequent examination could detect no change or apparent disturbance in the position of any object. My few dresses were folded exactly as I had left them; a certain little bunch of white violets that had once been silently presented to me by a stranger (a stranger to me, for we had never exchanged words), which I had dried and kept for its sweet perfume between the folds of my best dress, lay there undisturbed. Each of the teachers, in turn, made overtures of special intimacy, which I politely declined. One proved to be an honest woman, albeit a narrow-minded thinker, a coarse-feeling individual, and a profound egotist. The second was a Parisienne: externally refined, but internally corrupt. Devoid of creed, principle, or genuine affection, she had stripped away the outer crust of decorum only to reveal a slough beneath. Her defining passion was for gifts; in this regard, the third teacher—a person otherwise characterless and insignificant—closely mirrored her. This last woman possessed another distinct trait: avarice. In her domain reigned the love of money for its own sake. The sight of a single gold piece would bring a peculiar green gleam to her eyes, singular to witness. Once, as a mark of high favor, she lifted me upstairs and opened a secret door to show me a hoard—a mass of coarse, large coin totaling about fifteen guineas in five-franc pieces. She cherished this hoard with the fervor of a bird guarding its eggs. These were her savings, and she would come to talk to me about them with an infatuated, persevering dotage, strangely befitting someone not yet twenty-five.

Conversely, the Parisienne was prodigal and profligate (in disposition, at least; regarding action, I cannot say). That darker quality revealed its snake's head to me only once, peering out very cautiously. It appeared a curious kind of reptile; judging from that fleeting glimpse, its novelty whetted my curiosity. Had it emerged boldly, perhaps I might have philosophically stood my ground, surveying the creature from forked tongue to scaly tail-tip. Instead, it merely rustled in the leaves of a bad novel. Upon encountering a hasty, ill-advised demonstration of wrath, it recoiled and vanished, hissing. She hated me from that day forward.

This Parisienne was perpetually in debt; her salary was anticipated not only in dress but in perfumes, cosmetics, confectionery, and condiments. What a cold, callous epicure she was in all things! I see her now clearly: thin in face and figure, sallow in complexion, with regular features, perfect teeth, lips like a thread, a large prominent chin, and a well-opened yet frozen eye—one that craved everything and thanked nothing. She mortally hated work and loved what she termed "pleasure," leading an insipid, heartless, brainless dissipation of time.

Madame Beck knew this woman's character perfectly. On one occasion, she spoke to me about her with an odd mixture of discrimination, indifference, and antipathy. When I asked why she retained her in the establishment, Madame answered plainly, "Because it suits her interest to do so." She pointed out a fact I had already noticed: Mademoiselle St. Pierre possessed, in an almost unique degree, the power of maintaining order among her undisciplined ranks of scholars. A certain petrifying influence accompanied and surrounded her; without passion, noise, or violence, she held them in check as a breezeless frost-air might still a brawling stream. While of little use in communicating knowledge, she was invaluable for strict surveillance and the maintenance of rules. "I know well that she has no principles, nor, perhaps, morals," Madame admitted frankly, adding with philosophical resignation, "but her demeanor in class is always appropriate and even possesses a certain dignity: that is all that is needed. Neither the students nor the parents look further; consequently, neither do I."

It was a strange, frolicsome, noisy little world this school. Great pains were taken to hide chains with flowers; a subtle essence of Romanism pervaded every arrangement. Large sensual indulgence was permitted by way of counterpoise to jealous spiritual restraint. Each mind was being reared in slavery, but to prevent reflection on this fact, every pretext for physical recreation was seized and maximized. There, as elsewhere, the Church strove to raise her children robust in body but feeble in soul—fat, ruddy, hale, joyous, ignorant, unthinking, unquestioning. "Eat, drink, and live!" she seems to say. "Tend to your bodies; leave your souls to me. I hold their cure, guide their course, and guarantee their final fate." A bargain in which every true Catholic deems himself a gainer. Lucifer offers identical terms: "All this power will I give thee, and the glory of it... If thou therefore wilt worship me, all shall be thine!"

About this time, in the ripest glow of summer, Madame Beck's house became as merry a place as a school could possibly be. Broad folding-doors and casement windows stood wide open all day; settled sunshine seemed naturalized in the atmosphere. Clouds sailed far away beyond the sea, resting, no doubt, round islands such as England—that dear land of mists—but withdrawn wholly from the drier continent. We lived far more in the garden than under a roof; classes were held, and meals partaken of, in the "grand berceau." Furthermore, there was a note of holiday preparation that almost turned freedom into license. The autumnal long vacation was but two months distant, but before that, a great day awaited celebration: the *fête* of Madame.

The conduct of this *fête* devolved chiefly on Mademoiselle St. Pierre, while Madame herself was supposed to stand aloof, disinterestedly unconscious of what might be happening in her honor. Indeed, she never knew, never in the least suspected, that a subscription was annually levied on the whole school to purchase a handsome present. The polite tact of the reader will please to omit from the account a brief, secret consultation held in Madame's own chamber.

"What will you have this year?" asked her Parisian lieutenant.

"Oh, no matter! Let it alone. Let the poor children keep their francs," Madame replied, looking benign and modest.

St. Pierre would here protrude her chin. She knew Madame by heart; she always dismissed her airs of "kindness" as "grimaces." She never even professed to respect them for an instant.

"Hurry!" she would say coldly. "Name the article. Shall it be jewelry or porcelain, haberdashery or silver?"

"Eh bien! Two or three spoons, and as many forks in silver."

Thus, the result was a handsome case containing three hundred francs' worth of plate.

The program for the *fête*-day included the presentation of the plate, a collation in the garden, a dramatic performance (with pupils and teachers acting), a dance, and supper. The entire affair seemed very gorgeous to me, as I recall vividly. Zélie St. Pierre understood these matters and managed them ably.

The play was the main event, requiring a month of prior drilling. The selection of actors demanded knowledge and care, followed by lessons in elocution, posture, and the fatigue of countless rehearsals. As might be expected, St. Pierre alone was insufficient; other management and accomplishments were requisite. These were supplied by a master, M. Paul Emanuel, professor of literature. It was never my lot to attend his histrionic lessons, but I often saw him crossing the *carré* (a square hall between the dwelling-house and school-house). I also heard him, on warm evenings, lecturing with doors open, his name and anecdotes resonating in everyone's ears. Our former acquaintance, Miss Ginevra Fanshawe—who had been selected for a prominent role in the play—often bestowed upon me large portions of her leisure, larding her discourse with frequent allusions to his sayings and doings. She esteemed him hideously plain and used to profess herself frightened almost into hysterics at the sound of his step or voice.

He was certainly a dark little man: pungent and austere. Even to me, he seemed a harsh apparition, with his close-shorn black head, broad sallow brow, thin cheek, wide and quivering nostril, thorough glance, and hurried bearing. Irritable he was; one heard that he apostrophized with vehemence the awkward squad under his orders. Sometimes he would break out on these raw amateur actresses with a passion of impatience at their false conception, cold emotion, and feeble delivery.

"Listen!" he would cry, and then his voice rang through the premises like a trumpet. When, mimicking it, came the small pipe of a Ginevra, a Mathilde, or a Blanche, one understood why a hollow groan of scorn or a fierce hiss of rage rewarded the tame echo.

"You are nothing but dolls," I heard him thunder. "You have no passions—you others. Do you feel nothing? Your flesh is snow, your blood ice! I want all this to ignite, to have life, to have a soul!"

It was a vain resolve. When he finally realized it was vain, he abruptly dismantled the whole enterprise. Hitherto, he had taught them a grand tragedy; he tore the tragedy into morsels and returned the next day with a compact little comic trifle. To this, they took more kindly; he soon hammered it into their smooth, round heads.

Following Madame Beck's fête, preceded by three weeks of uneasy relaxation, a brief twelve-hour explosion of hilarity and dissipation, and succeeded by a single day of utter languor, came a period of sharp reaction. Two months of rigorous application, of close and hard study, descended upon the school. These final two months of the academic year were the only genuine working ones; into them, by the concerted effort of professors, mistresses, and pupils alike, was concentrated the entire burden of preparation for the examinations preceding the prize distribution. Those seeking rewards had to work in earnest; masters and teachers had to set their shoulders to the wheel, urging the backward students and diligently training the promising ones. A showy demonstration, a telling exhibition, had to be staged for public view, and all means were deemed fair to achieve this end.

I paid little heed to how the other teachers undertook their tasks; I had my own business to mind. My burden was not the least onerous: I was charged with imbuing ninety sets of young brains with a due tincture of what they considered a most complicated and difficult science—the English language—and drilling ninety tongues in what, for them, was an almost impossible pronunciation: the lisping and hissing dentals of the Isles.

The examination day finally arrived. An awful day! Prepared for with anxious care, dressed for with silent dispatch—nothing vaporous or fluttering now, no white gauze or azure streamers; the order was grave, close, and compact. It seemed to me that I was specially doomed this day, the main burden and trial falling solely upon me among all the female teachers. The others were not expected to examine in their own subjects; the Professor of Literature, M. Paul, had taken upon himself this duty. He, the school autocrat, gathered all and sundry reins into the hollow of his one hand; he angrily rejected any colleague; he refused help. Madame herself, who evidently wished to undertake the examination in geography—her favorite subject, which she taught well—was forced to submit and become subordinate to her despotic kinsman's direction. He set aside the whole staff, male and female, and stood alone on the examiner's estrade. It irked him that he was forced to make one exception to this rule. He could not manage English; he was obliged to leave that branch of education in the English teacher's hands, which he did not without a flash of naive jealousy.

A constant crusade against the "amour-propre" of every human being but himself was the peculiar obsession of this able, yet fiery and grasping little man. He had a strong relish for public representation in his own person, but an extreme abhorrence of such display in anyone else. He quelled and suppressed whenever he could; and when he could not, he fumed like a bottled storm.

On the evening preceding the examination day, I was walking in the garden, as were the other teachers and all the boarders. M. Emanuel joined me in the "defended alley"; his cigar was at his lips; his paletôt—a most characteristic garment of no particular shape—hung dark and menacing; the tassel of his Greek hat sternly shadowed his left temple; his black whiskers curled like those of a wrathful cat; his blue eye held a cloud in its glitter.

"Ainsi," he began abruptly, facing and arresting me, "you are going to reign like a queen; tomorrow—to reign at my side? Surely you are savoring beforehand the delights of authority. I see something radiant, a small ambitious one!"

Now, the fact was, he happened to be entirely mistaken. I did not—and could not—estimate the admiration or the good opinion of tomorrow's audience at the same rate he did. Had that audience numbered as many personal friends and acquaintances for me as for him, I know not how it might have been; I speak of the case as it stood. School triumphs shed but a cold lustre upon me. I had wondered—and I wondered now—how it was that for him they seemed to shine with hearth-warmth and glow. *He* cared for them perhaps too much; *I*, probably, too little. However, I had my own fancies as well as he. I liked, for instance, to see M. Emanuel jealous; it lit up his nature, woke his spirit, throwing all sorts of queer lights and shadows over his dun face and into his violet-azure eyes (he used to say that his black hair and blue eyes were "une de ses beautés"). There was a relish in his anger; it was artless, earnest, quite unreasonable, but never hypocritical. I uttered no disclaimer then of the complacency he attributed to me; I merely asked where the English examination came in—whether at the commencement or close of the day?

"I hesitate," he said, "whether at the very beginning, before many persons have come, and when your aspiring nature will not be gratified by a large audience, or quite at the close, when everybody is tired, and only a jaded, worn-out attention will be at your service."

"How hard you are, Monsieur!" I said, affecting dejection.

"One ought to be 'hard' with you. You are one of those beings who must be *kept down*. I know you! I know you! Other people in this house see you pass and think that a colorless shadow has gone by. As for me, I scrutinized your face once, and it sufficed."

"You are satisfied that you understand me?"

Without answering directly, he continued, "Were you not gratified when you succeeded in that vaudeville? I watched you and saw a passionate ardour for triumph in your physiognomy. What fire shot into the glance! Not mere light, but flame: *je me tiens pour averti*."

"What feeling I had on that occasion, Monsieur—and pardon me if I say, you immensely exaggerate both its quality and quantity—was quite abstract. I did not care for the vaudeville. I hated the part you assigned me. I had not the slightest sympathy with the audience below the stage. They are good people, doubtless, but do I know them? Are they anything to me? Can I care for being brought before their view again tomorrow? Will the examination be anything but a task to me—a task I wish well over?"

"Shall I take it out of your hands?"

"With all my heart; if you do not fear failure."

"But I should fail. I only know three phrases of English, and a few words: *par exemple, de sonne, de mone, de stare*—is that said correctly? My opinion is that it would be better to give up the thing altogether: to have no English examination, eh?"

"If Madame consents, I consent."

"Heartily?"

"Very heartily."

He smoked his cigar in silence. He turned suddenly.

"Give me your hand," he said, and the spite and jealousy melted out of his face, replaced by a generous kindliness.

"Come, we will not be rivals, we will be friends," he pursued. "The examination shall take place, and I will choose a good moment; and instead of vexing and hindering, as I felt half-inclined ten minutes ago—for I have my malevolent moods: I always had them from childhood—I will aid you sincerely. After all, you are solitary and a stranger, and have your way to make and your bread to earn; it may be well that you should become known. We will be friends: do you agree?"

"Out of my heart, Monsieur. I am glad of a friend. I like that better than a triumph."

"Pauvrette!" he said, and turned away, leaving the alley.

The examination passed over well; M. Paul was as good as his word, doing his best to make my part easy. The next day came the distribution of prizes; that also passed; the school broke up; the pupils went home, and now began the long vacation.

That vacation! Shall I ever forget it? I think not. Madame Beck went, on the first day of the holidays, to join her children at the seaside; all the three teachers had parents or friends with whom they took refuge; every professor quit the city; some went to Paris, some to Boulogne-sur-Mer; M. Paul set forth on a pilgrimage to Rome. The house was left quite empty, save for me, a servant, and a poor, deformed, and imbecile pupil, a sort of cretin, whom her stepmother in a distant province would not allow to return home.

My heart almost died within me; miserable longings strained its chords. How long were the September days! How silent, how lifeless! How vast and void seemed the desolate premises! How gloomy the forsaken garden—now grey with the dust of a departed town summer! Looking forward at the commencement of those eight weeks, I hardly knew how I was to live to the end. My spirits had long been gradually sinking; now that the prop of employment was withdrawn, they went down fast. To those who live in retreat, whose lives have settled into the quiet isolation of schools or secluded homes, there comes a sudden and prolonged erasure from the memories of friends living in the wider world. Without apparent cause, and often following a period of lively correspondence, a silent pause descends. Letters cease; visits stop; tokens of remembrance vanish. An unbroken, unexplained blank takes hold.

There are always excellent reasons for such lapses, if only the isolated one understood them. While the hermit stagnates in his cell, the world outside whirls in the vortex of life. For him, the intervening months drag endlessly, with clocks seeming frozen and hours moving like weary travelers resting at mile markers. Yet for his absent friends, that same interval teems with events, filled with urgency.

A sensible hermit learns to swallow his thoughts and lock away his emotions during these winters of solitude. He accepts that destiny may require him to mimic the dormouse: curling into a tight ball, retreating into a crevice of life's wall, and submitting quietly to the drifting snow that seals him in ice until spring returns. Perhaps the snow-cave opens, and the sun warms him again; perhaps not. The frost may take root in his heart forever, leaving only bones for crows to find when spring arrives. Even then, it is right. One must accept mortality and the eventual journey "as well soon as syne."

For me, the seven weeks following that eventful evening at the theatre were as blank as seven sheets of white paper. No words were written upon them; no visits, no tokens.

By the middle of this silence, I began to fancy that something terrible had happened to my friends at La Terrasse. The midpoint of a blank is always a clouded spot for the lonely; his nerves ache with the strain of long expectation. Doubts, previously repelled, gather into a heavy mass and roll back upon him with vindictive force. Night becomes unfriendly; sleep refuses him. Strange terrors and struggles harass his bed, led by a sinister band of nightmares promising calamity and the dread of total abandonment. Poor wretch! He tries his best to endure, but remains pale and wasting despite his efforts.

Towards the end of these seven weeks, I finally admitted what I had jealously denied for six: that these silences were inevitable. They were the result of circumstance, the decree of fate, a part of my lot. Above all, I resolved never to question their origin or utter a murmur against their painful sequence. Naturally, I did not blame myself for suffering; I thanked God for possessing a true sense of justice, avoiding the foolish extravagance of self-accusation. I knew logically that my friends were blameless, and my heart acknowledged it too. Still, it was a rough, heavy road to travel, and I longed for better days.

I tried various expedients to sustain existence: I began an elaborate piece of lacework, studied German diligently, and forced myself to read the driest, thickest books in the library. In all my efforts, I was as orthodox as I knew how to be. Was there error somewhere? Very likely. But the result was as if I had gnawed a file to satisfy hunger or drunk brine to quench thirst.

My hour of torment was the post-time. I knew it too well, trying vainly to cheat myself of that knowledge. I dreaded the rack of expectation and the sick collapse of disappointment that daily preceded and followed the well-recognized ring of the bell.

Throughout our walk, she rang the most fanciful changes on this theme, proving through her obstinate credulity or incredulity her inability to conceive how anyone, unsupported by birth or wealth, lacking any consciousness of name or connection, could maintain an attitude of reasonable integrity. As for me, it was sufficient for my mental tranquility that I was known where it mattered that I should be known. The rest sat lightly upon me: pedigree, social position, and obscure intellectual acquisitions occupied roughly the same space in my thoughts and interests; they were my third-class lodgers, entitled only to the small sitting-room and the little back bedroom. Even if the dining and drawing rooms stood empty, I never confessed this to them, considering minor accommodations better suited to their circumstances.

Soon, however, I learned that the world held a different estimate. And I make no doubt the world is very right in its view, yet I also believe I am not entirely wrong in mine. There are those whom a lowered position degrades morally, to whom the loss of connection entails the loss of self-respect. Are they not justified in placing the highest value on that station and association which serves as their safeguard against debasement? If a man feels that he would become contemptible in his own eyes were it generally known that his ancestry was simple rather than noble, poor rather than rich, a worker rather than a capitalist, would it be right to severely blame him for keeping these fatal facts out of sight—for starting, trembling, quailing at the chance of exposure? The longer we live, the wider our experience grows; the less prone are we to judge our neighbor's conduct or question the world's wisdom. Wherever an accumulation of small defenses is found, whether surrounding the prude's virtue or the man of the world's respectability, be assured, it is needed.

You may recall the description of Madame Beck's annual festival, where a generous gift was traditionally subscribed and presented by the school. This observance was a privilege reserved exclusively for Madame, and in a diminished capacity, for her relative and advisor, Mr. Emanuel. In his case, the honor was bestowed spontaneously, not premeditated, serving as further testament to the esteem in which the professor of literature was held by his students—despite his known partialities, prejudices, and irritabilities. Unlike others, no valuable item was offered him; he had explicitly stated he would accept neither silver nor jewelry. Yet, he appreciated a modest tribute. Mr. Paul's festival fell on the first of March, a Thursday. It was a fine, sunny day, coinciding with the morning Mass service and the half-holiday that allowed for afternoon walks, shopping, or visits. Always conscious of this basilisk-like attention, she would writhe beneath it, half-flattered, half-confused. Mr. Paul would follow her reactions, sometimes appearing terrifyingly acute. For in some instances, he possessed a terrible, unerring instinctual penetration, piercing through the hiding places of the heart to discern the bare, barren landscapes of the spirit beneath florid veils. He would triumphantly snatch the screen from poor, shrinking souls, passionately hurrying them to the summit of exposure, displaying them all naked, all false—poor living lies, the spawn of that horrible Truth which cannot be viewed unveiled. He believed he was dispensing justice; for my part, I doubt if man has the right to mete out such justice to another. He deserved it, yet it was difficult to shake his firm conviction that his work was righteous and necessary.

Yet, dear reader, do not be too hasty in drawing your charitable conclusions. Do not assume that from that single day onward, Mr. Paul underwent a miraculous transformation, becoming easy company and shedding his tendency to radiate discomfort and danger. Far from it. By nature, he was a small man prone to unreasonable moods. When overwrought—which happened frequently—he became acutely irritable. Furthermore, his veins ran dark with a livid tincture of belladonna, the very essence of jealousy. I do not refer merely to the tender, romantic jealousy of the heart, but to that sterner, narrower sentiment seated firmly in the head.

As I sat watching Mr. Paul knit his brow or protrude his lip over some exercise of mine—which invariably contained more faults than he desired, for he delighted in my blunders, finding a knot of errors sweeter to him than a cluster of nuts—I often thought he bore a striking resemblance to Napoleon Bonaparte. I maintain that comparison today. In his shameless disregard for magnanimity, he mirrored the Great Emperor. Mr. Paul would have quarreled with twenty learned women; he would have unashamedly engaged in a system of petty bickering and recrimination against an entire capital of social circles, never troubling himself about the loss of dignity. He would have exiled fifty Madame de Staëls if they had annoyed, offended, outrivaled, or opposed him.

I recall vividly a heated episode involving a certain Madame Panache, a lady temporarily employed by Madame Beck to teach history. She was clever—possessing considerable knowledge and, more importantly, the art of maximizing it. She held unlimited command over words and confidence. Her personal appearance was far from lacking advantages; many would have pronounced her "a fine woman." Yet, there were points in her robust and ample attractions, as well as her bustling, demonstrative presence, that clashed violently with the fastidious and capricious tastes of Mr. Paul. The sound of her voice echoing through the *carré* would send him into a strange trance; her long, free stride along the corridor would often cause him to snatch up his papers and flee instantly.

With malicious intent, one day he decided to intrude upon her class. Like lightning, he assessed her teaching method, noting it differed from his own pet plan. Without ceremony or courtesy, he pointed out what he termed her errors. Whether he expected submission, I know not; instead, he met acrid opposition and a round reprimand for his unjustifiable interference. Rather than withdraw with dignity, he threw down the gauntlet of defiance. Madame Panache, bellicose as Penthesilea, picked it up immediately. She snapped her fingers in the intermeddler's face and rushed upon him with a storm of words. Mr. Emanuel was eloquent, but Madame Panache was voluble. A fierce antagonism ensued.

Instead of laughing in his sleeve at his fair foe—with all her sore *amour-propre* and loud self-assertion—Mr. Paul detested her with intense seriousness. He honored her with his earnest fury, pursuing her vindictively and implacably. He refused to rest peacefully in his bed, to enjoy his meals, or to relish his cigar until she was fairly rooted out of the establishment. The Professor conquered, but I cannot say the laurels of this victory shaded his temples gracefully.

Once, I ventured to hint as much. To my great surprise, he conceded I might be right, but averred that when confronted with men or women of the coarse, self-complacent quality exemplified by Madame Panache, he had no control over his passions. An unspeakable, active aversion compelled him to a war of extermination. Three months later, hearing that his vanquished foe faced reverses and distress for want of employment, he forgot his hatred. Active in both good and evil, he moved heaven and earth to secure her another position. When she came to thank him and make up for former differences, the old voice—a little loud—the old manner—a little forward—acted upon him so strongly that in ten minutes he sprang up and bowed her, or rather himself, out of the room in a transport of nervous irritation.

To pursue a somewhat audacious parallel: in his love of power and eager grasp for supremacy, Mr. Emanuel was like Bonaparte. He was a man not always to be submitted to. Sometimes resistance was necessary; it was right to stand still, look up into his eyes, and declare that his requirements exceeded reason—that his absolutism verged on tyranny. The dawnings of peculiar talent within his range, struggling into life under his rule, curiously excited and disturbed him. He watched its birth with a scowl, holding back his hand, perhaps saying, "Come on if you have strength," but refusing to aid the process.

When the initial conflict ended, when the breath of life was drawn and the heart beating, he offered no fostering care. "Prove yourself true ere I cherish you" was his ordinance, and how difficult he made that proof! What thorns, briers, and flints he strewn in the path of feet not inured to rough travel! He watched tearlessly as ordeals were passed through fearlessly. He followed footprints that, as they approached the goal, were sometimes marked in blood, following them grimly while maintaining the austere watch of a police officer over a pain-pressed pilgrim. And when, finally, he allowed a rest, before sleep closed the eyelids, he opened those same lids wide with a pitiless finger and thumb, gazing deep through the pupil and iris into the brain and heart to search for Vanity, Pride, or Falsehood in their subtlest forms. If, at last, he let the neophyte sleep, it was but a moment; he woke him suddenly to apply new tests. He sent him on irksome errands while he staggered with weariness, trying the temper, the sense, and the health. Only when every severest test had been applied and endured, when the most corrosive acid had failed to tarnish the ore, did he admit it genuine, stamping it with his deep brand of approval in clouded silence.

The morning broke calm and summer-like, filled with the song of birds in the garden and a light dew-mist promising warmth. Everyone agreed it would be hot, and we all felt a genuine pleasure in discarding heavy garments for attire suitable to the sunny season. The uniform was a clean, fresh print dress and a light straw bonnet, crafted and trimmed by French workwomen alone capable of uniting the utterly unpretending with the perfectly becoming. No one flaunted faded silks; no one wore a second-hand best article. At six, the bell rang merrily, and we poured down the stairs, through the *carré*, along the corridor, and into the vestibule.

Had a young, ruddy-faced maid opened the door, I might have felt similarly out of place. Let us run so that we may obtain; let us endure hardship as good soldiers; let us finish our course and keep the faith, relying on an outcome where we shall come off more than conquerors: "Art thou not from everlasting mine Holy One? WE SHALL NOT DIE!"

On a Thursday morning, we were all assembled in the classroom, waiting for the literature lesson. The hour arrived, and we awaited the master.

The students of the first class sat perfectly still. Their compositions, written cleanly since the last lesson, lay ready before them, neatly tied with ribbons, awaiting the Professor's swift inspection as he made his rounds. It was July; the morning was fine, and the glass door stood ajar. A fresh breeze drifted through, causing the plants growing at the lintel to wave, bend, and peer inside, seemingly whispering tidings.

M. Emanuel was not always punctual; we scarcely wondered at his slight delay. However, we were startled when the door finally opened. Instead of him, with his characteristic swiftness and fire, the cautious Madame Beck quietly entered.

She approached M. Paul's desk, stood before it, and wrapped the light shawl around her shoulders more tightly. Speaking in low yet firm tones with a fixed gaze, she announced, "This morning, there will be no lesson of literature."

After a pause of about two minutes, she continued.

"It is probable that the lessons will be suspended for a week. I require at least that amount of time to find an efficient substitute for M. Emanuel. Meanwhile, it shall be our task to fill the gaps usefully."

"Your Professor, ladies," she went on, "intends, if possible, to take his leave of you properly. At the present moment, he lacks the leisure for such a ceremony. He is preparing for a long voyage. A very sudden and urgent summons of duty calls him to a great distance. He has decided to leave Europe for an indefinite period. Perhaps he can explain further himself. Ladies, instead of the usual lesson with M. Emanuel, you will, this morning, read English with Mademoiselle Lucy."

She bowed her head courteously, pulled the folds of her shawl tighter, and departed from the classroom.

A profound silence fell, followed by a murmuring ripple through the room. I believe some pupils wept.

Time passed. The noise, whispering, and occasional sobbing increased. I became aware of a relaxation of discipline, a growing disorder, as if the girls felt that vigilance had been withdrawn and surveillance had effectively ceased. Habit and a sense of duty allowed me to rally quickly. I rose in my usual manner, spoke in my usual tone, issued commands, and finally established quiet. I assigned extensive English reading and kept them occupied until the end of the morning. I remember feeling a sharp impatience toward the pupils who were sobbing. Indeed, their emotion held little value; it was merely hysterical agitation. I told them so unsparingly, half ridiculing them. I was severe. The truth was, I could not tolerate their tears or that gasping sound; I simply could not bear it.

One particularly weak-minded, low-spirited pupil persisted in her crying even after the others had stopped. Relentless necessity compelled me to confront her so harshly that she dared not continue the display and was forced to conquer her convulsions. That girl would have had every reason to hate me, except that when school ended and her companions departed, I ordered her to stay. Once they were gone, I did something I had never done before to any of them: I pressed her to my heart and kissed her cheek. However, this impulse yielded quickly. I promptly dismissed her from the classroom, for upon that poignant strain, she wept more bitterly than ever.

I filled every minute of that day with occupation and would have preferred to sit up all night if I could have kept a candle burning. Unfortunately, the night proved difficult, leaving me exhausted and poorly prepared for the next day's ordeal of insufferable gossip. Of course, this news sparked general discussion. While a small reserve accompanied the initial surprise, it soon wore off. Every mouth opened; every tongue wagged. Teachers, pupils, and even the servants whispered the name of "Emanuel." He, whose connection with the school dated back to its inception, was suddenly withdrawing! Everyone found it strange.

They talked so much, so long, and so often that, amidst the multitude of words and rumors, some intelligence eventually emerged. By the third day, I heard it said he was sailing in a week; later—that he was bound for the West Indies. I examined Madame Beck's face and eyes for disproof or confirmation of this report; I scrutinized her entirely for information, but no part of her demeanor disclosed anything more than what was unperturbed and commonplace.

"This secession is an immense loss to her," she claimed aloud. "She does not know how she will fill the vacancy. She was so accustomed to her kinsman; he had become her right hand. What should she do without him? She had opposed the step, but M. Paul convinced her it was his duty."

She said all this publicly, in the classroom, at the dinner table, speaking audibly to Zélie St. Pierre.

"Why was it his duty?" I could have asked her. I had impulses to seize her suddenly as she calmly passed me in the classroom, to grab her firmly, and say, "Stop. Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter. *Why* is it his duty to go into banishment?" But Madame always addressed some other teacher, never looking at me, never seeming conscious that I cared about the question.

The week wore on. Nothing more was said about M. Emanuel coming to bid us goodbye; no one seemed anxious for his arrival; no one questioned whether he would come; no one betrayed torment lest he depart silently and unseen. They talked incessantly, yet in all their chatter, they never touched on this vital point. As for Madame, she could naturally see him and say whatever she pleased. What should *she* care whether or not he appeared in the schoolroom?

The week concluded. We were informed that he was departing on a specific day, destined for "Basseterre in Guadeloupe." The business calling him abroad related to a friend's interests, not his own; I suspected as much.

"Basseterre in Guadeloupe." During this time, I slept little. Whenever I *did* manage to slumber, I was invariably roused with a start, while the words "Basseterre," "Guadeloupe," seemed pronounced over my pillow or ran athwart the darkness around and before me in zigzag characters of red or violet light.

For what I felt, there was no help, and how could I avoid feeling it? M. Emanuel had been very kind to me lately; he had been growing hourly better and kinder. It had now been a month since we settled our theological differences, and during that time, there had been no quarrel. Nor had our peace been the cold daughter of divorce; we had not lived apart. He had visited more often, spoken with me more frequently, and spent hours with me in a state of soothed temper, contented eye, and home-like mildness. Kind subjects of conversation had developed between us. He had inquired into my life plans, and I had communicated them. The school project pleased him; he made me repeat it more than once, though he called it an "Alnaschar dream." The friction was over; the mutual understanding was settling and fixing. Feelings of union and hope made themselves profoundly felt in the heart; affection, deep esteem, and dawning trust had each fastened its bond.

What quiet lessons I had during this time! No more taunts regarding my "intellect," no more threats of grating public displays! How sweetly the jealous gibes and the more jealous, half-passionate eulogies were replaced by a mute, indulgent help, a fond guidance, and a tender forbearance that forgave but never praised. There were times when he would sit for many minutes without speaking at all. When dusk or duty brought separation, he would leave with words like these: "Rest is sweet! Calm happiness is precious!"

One evening, less than ten days prior, he joined me while I was walking in my alley. He took my hand. I looked up into his face and thought he intended to arrest my attention.

I felt as if I were mesmerized by a three-headed basilisk; I could not tear myself away from this group. My feet seemed rooted to the ground beneath them. Above, the canopy of intertwined trees offered a cool shadow, while the night whispered a promise of safety. An officious lamp flashed a single beam to reveal a secluded, secure seat for me before vanishing into darkness.

Let me briefly inform the reader of what I have silently learned from rumor during this dark fortnight regarding the origin and purpose of Mr. Emanuel's departure. The story is short and familiar: its beginning is Mammon, and its end is Interest.

To these devotees of hers, Madame Walravens appeared as hideous as a Hindu idol, yet she possessed, in their eyes, the immense consequence of an idol. The truth was, she had been exceedingly wealthy. Although currently without funds, she was expected to regain her riches. In Basseterre, Guadeloupe, she owned a vast estate, inherited as a dowry sixty years prior, which had been seized following her husband's bankruptcy. However, it was now believed to be free of claims and, if managed by a trustworthy agent, capable of yielding significant returns within a few years.

Father Silas took a keen interest in this potential revival for the sake of the Church, of which Magliore Walravens was a fervent daughter. Madame Beck, a distant relative of the hunchback and aware that she had no family of her own, had long calculated possibilities with maternal foresight. Harshly treated by Madame Walravens, she never ceased courting her for financial gain. Thus, driven by money, both the priest and Madame Beck were equally and sincerely invested in preserving the West Indian property.

Yet, the distance was vast, and the climate perilous. A competent and upright agent was required—one who must be deeply devoted. Such a man Madame Walravens had kept in her service for twenty years, blighting his existence and then draining him like an ancient fungus. Father Silas had trained and bound such a man to him through gratitude, habit, and faith. Madame Beck knew of such a man and could exert some influence over him. "My pupil," Father Silas remarked, "if he stays in Europe, risks apostasy, for he has become entangled with a heretic." Madame Beck added her private commentary, harboring her own secret reason for desiring his removal abroad. Unable to secure the position herself, she refused to let anyone else win it; rather, she wished to destroy the opportunity. As for Madame Walravens, she simply wanted her money and land, knowing that Paul, if he chose, could be the most faithful steward. Consequently, these three self-interested individuals united to besiege the one unselfish man. They reasoned, appealed, and implored; casting themselves upon his mercy, they entrusted their interests to his hands. They asked for only two or three years of devotion, promising that thereafter he could live for himself. Perhaps one of them secretly hoped he might die in the interim.

No living creature ever placed an advantage so humbly at Mr. Emanuel's feet or trusted it so confidingly that he spurned the gift or rejected the repository. Whether he privately pained by leaving Europe or calculated his own future, none asked, knew, or reported. To me, this remained a blank. I could only guess at his conferences with his confessor and conjecture the role duty and religion played in the persuasions used. He was gone, having made no sign. There, my knowledge ended.

Bowing my head, my forehead resting on my hands, I sat among clustered tree stems and branching brushwood. Whatever conversation passed among my neighbors, I could hear if I wished; I was close enough. Yet for some time, there was little motive to pay attention. They gossiped about dresses, music, illuminations, and the fine night. I listened hoping to hear them say, "It is calm weather for *his* voyage; the *Antigua*" (his ship) "will sail prosperously." No such remark fell. Neither the *Antigua*, her course, nor her passenger were mentioned.

Perhaps the light chatter interested old Madame Walravens no more than it did me. She appeared restless, turning her head now this way, now that, peering through the trees and into the crowd as if expecting an arrival and impatient of delay. "Where are they? Why do they not come?" I heard her mutter more than once. Finally, as if determined to receive an answer to her question—which hitherto no one seemed to mind—she spoke aloud. The phrase was brief, simple, yet sent a shock through me: "Messieurs et mesdames," she said, "where, then, is Justine Marie?"

"Justine Marie!" What was this? Justine Marie—the dead nun—where was she? Why, in her grave, Madame Walravens—what could you possibly want with her? You shall go to her, but she shall not come to you.

Thus *I* would have answered, had the response been mine to give, but no one seemed to share my sentiment. No one appeared surprised, startled, or confused. The quietest, most commonplace answer met the strange, dead-disturbing, witch-like query of the hunchback.

"Justine Marie," said one, "is coming; she is in the kiosk; she will be here presently."

From this question and reply arose a shift in the conversation—it remained chat, easy, desultory, familiar gossip. Hints, allusions, and comments circulated the circle, but all so fragmented, so dependent on references to unnamed persons or undefined circumstances, that however intently I listened—and I *did* listen now with a fated interest—I could discern no more than that some scheme was underway involving this ghostly Justine Marie, whether dead or alive. This family junta seemed to be grasping at her for some reason; there seemed to be talk of a marriage, of a fortune—for whom I could not quite determine—perhaps for Victor Kint, perhaps for Josef Emanuel. Both were bachelors. Once, I thought the hints and jests rained upon a young, fair-haired foreigner of the party, whom they called Heinrich Mühler. Amidst all the banter, Madame Walravens still intruded occasionally with hoarse, cross-grained speeches; her impatience was diverted only by an implacable surveillance of Désirée, who could not move without the old woman threatening her with her staff.

"La voilà!" suddenly cried one of the gentlemen, "there is Justine Marie arriving!"

This moment was peculiar to me. I recalled the pictured nun on the panel; the sad love story presented itself to my mind; I saw in thought the vision of the garret, the apparition in the alley, the strange birth of the cradle; I underwent a presentiment of discovery, a strong conviction of imminent disclosure. Ah! When imagination once runs riot, where do we stop? What winter tree so bare and branchless—what humble, hedge-munching beast by the wayside—that Fancy, a passing cloud, and a struggling moonbeam, will not clothe in spirituality and make into a phantom?

With solemn force pressing on my heart, the expectation of mystery unraveling: hitherto I had seen this specter only through a dark glass; now was I to behold it face to face. I leaned forward; I looked.

"She comes!" cried Josef Emanuel.

The circle opened as if welcoming a new and beloved member. At this instant, a torch happened to be carried past; its blaze aided the pale moon in doing justice to the crisis, lighting the denouement perfectly. Surely those near me must have felt some of the anxiety I felt, in a degree so unmeasured. Of that group, the coolest must have "held his breath for a time!" As for me, my life stood still.

It is over. The moment and the nun have arrived. The crisis and the revelation have passed.

The flambeau glared still within a yard, held aloft by a park-keeper; its long, eager tongue of flame almost licked the figure of the Expected—there, standing fully in my sight. What was she like? What did she wear? How did she look? Who was she?

There were many masks in the park tonight, and as the hour grew late, such a feeling of revelry and mystery began to spread that you would scarce discredit me, reader, were I to say she resembled the nun of the attic, that she wore black skirts and white head-clothes, that she looked like the resurrection of the flesh, a risen ghost.

All falsehoods! All figments! We will not deal in such nonsense. Let us be honest and cut, as heretofore, from the homely web of truth.

"Homely," however, is an ill-chosen word. What I saw was not precisely homely. A girl of Villette stood there—a girl fresh from her pensionnat. She was very beautiful, possessing the beauty indigenous to this country. She looked well-nourished, fair, and plump. Her cheeks were round, her eyes good, her hair abundant. She was handsomely dressed. She was not alone; her escort consisted of three persons—two elderly, whom she addressed as "Uncle" and "Aunt." She laughed, she chatted; good-humored, buxom, and blooming, she looked, at every point, the bourgeois belle.

The day following that extraordinary Midsummer night proved to be no ordinary day. Yet, I do not speak of omens in the heavens or portents on the earth, nor of meteorological disasters like storms, floods, or whirlwinds. On the contrary, the sun rose jovial, wearing a July face. Morning adorned her beauty with rubies and filled her lap with roses until they fell in showers, blushing her path; the hours awakened fresh as nymphs, emptied their dew-vials onto the early hills, and stepped forth stripped of vapor. Shadowless, azure, and glorious, they led the sun's steeds along a burning, unclouded course. In short, it was a day as fine as the finest summer could boast. Yet, I suspect I was the sole inhabitant of the Rue Fossette who cared, or even remembered, to note this pleasant fact.

Another thought occupied all other minds—a thought that certainly shared space in my own meditations. But this master consideration, lacking for me the total novelty, the overwhelming suddenness, and the dense mystery it held for the majority of my fellow speculators, left me somewhat more open to collateral observations. Still, while strolling in the garden, feeling the sunshine, and watching the blooming plants, I pondered the very subject the entire house discussed. What was it? Simply this: when Matins were said, a seat remained vacant in the first row of boarders. At breakfast, a coffee cup sat unclaimed. When the maid made the beds, she found one bolster laid lengthwise, covered by a cap and nightgown. Furthermore, when Ginevra Fanshawe's music mistress arrived early, as usual, to give the morning lesson, her accomplished and promising pupil was nowhere to be found.

High and low, Miss Fanshawe was sought; the house was ransacked from end to end. Vainly. Not a trace, not an indication, not even a scrap of paper rewarded the search. The nymph had vanished, engulfed in the previous night like a shooting star swallowed by darkness.

Deep was the dismay of the supervising teachers; deeper still the horror of the defaulting Directress. Never before had I seen Madame Beck so pale or so appalled. This was a blow struck at her tenderest part, her weak side; damage inflicted upon her interests. How, moreover, had this untoward event occurred? By what exit had the fugitive taken flight? Not a window was found unlatched, not a pane of glass broken; all doors were bolted securely. To this day, Madame Beck has never obtained satisfaction regarding this point, nor indeed has anyone else involved, save and excepting one: Lucy Snowe. Only I could recall how, to facilitate a certain enterprise, a certain great door had been drawn softly to its lintel—closed, indeed, but neither bolted nor secured.

The thundering carriage-and-pair were now likewise recalled, as well as that puzzling signal: the waved handkerchief. From these premises, and one or two others inaccessible to any but myself, I drew but one inference: it was a case of elopement. Morally certain on this head, and witnessing Madame Beck's profound embarrassment, I finally communicated my conviction. Having alluded to M. de Hamal's suit, I found, as I expected, that Madame Beck was perfectly *au fait* with that affair. She had long since discussed it with Mrs. Cholmondeley and had placed her own responsibility for the business squarely on that lady's shoulders. To Mrs. Cholmondeley and M. de Bassompierre she now turned for help.

We learned that the Hôtel Crécy was already alive with news of the incident. Ginevra had written to her cousin Paulina, vaguely hinting at marital intentions; communications had been received from the de Hamal family; and M. de Bassompierre was tracking the fugitives. He overtook them, however, too late.

In the course of the week, the post delivered me a note. I shall transcribe it, for it contains explanations on more than one point:

"DEAR OLD TIM ('Timon'),—

'I am off, you see—gone like a shot. Alfred and I intended to be married in this way almost from the first; we never meant to be spliced in the humdrum manner of other people. Alfred has too much spirit for that, and so have I—*Dieu merci!* Do you know, Alfred, who used to call you 'the dragon,' has seen so much of you during the last few months that he begins to feel quite friendly toward you. He hopes you won't miss him now that he has gone; he begs to apologize for any little trouble he may have caused. He is afraid he rather inconvenienced you once when he came upon you in the *grenier*, just as you were reading a letter seemingly of the most special interest; but he could not resist the temptation to give you a start—you appeared so wonderfully taken up with your correspondent.

*En revanche*, he says you once frightened him by rushing in for a dress or a shawl, or some other *chiffon*, at the moment when he had struck a light and was going to take a quiet whiff of his cigar while waiting for me.

'Do you begin to comprehend by this time that M. le Comte de Hamal was the nun of the attic, and that he came to see your humble servant? I will tell you how he managed it. You know he has access to the Athénée, where two or three of his nephews, the sons of his eldest sister, Madame de Melcy, are students. You know the courtyard of the Athénée is on the other side of the high wall bounding your walk, the *allée défendue*. Alfred can climb as well as he can dance or fence; his amusement was to make the *escalade* of our pensionnat by mounting, first the wall; then—by the aid of that high tree overspreading the grand *berceau*, and resting some of its boughs on the roof of the lower buildings of our premises—he managed to scale the first class and the grand hall.

One night, by the way, he fell out of this tree, tore down several branches, nearly broke his own neck, and after all, in running away, got a terrible fright and was nearly caught by two people, Madame Beck and M. Emanuel, whom he thinks were walking in the alley. From the grand hall, the ascent is not difficult to the highest block of building, finishing in the great garret. The skylight, you know, is left half-open day and night for air; by the skylight he entered.

Nearly a year ago I chanced to tell him our legend of the nun; that suggested his romantic idea of the spectral disguise, which I think you must allow he has very cleverly carried out.

'But for the nun's black gown and white veil, he would have been caught again and again both by you and that tiger-Jesuit, M. Paul. He thinks you both capital ghost-seers, and very brave. What I wonder at is, rather your secretiveness than your courage. How could you endure the visitations of that long spectre, time after time, without crying out, telling everybody, and rousing the whole house and neighborhood?

"Oh, and how did you like the nun as a bed-fellow? *I* dressed her up; didn't I do it well? Did you shriek when you saw her? I should have gone mad; but then you have such nerves!—real iron and bend-leather! I believe you feel nothing. You haven't the same sensitiveness that a person of my constitution has. You seem to me insensible both to pain, fear, and grief. You are a real old Diogenes.

"Well, dear grandmother! and are you not mightily angry at my moonlight flitting and runaway match? I assure you it is excellent fun, and I did it partly to spite that minx, Paulina, and that bear, Dr. John—to show them that, with all their airs, I could get married as well as they. M. de Bassompierre was at first in a strange fume with Alfred; he threatened a prosecution for *détournement de mineur*, and I know not what; he was so abominably in earnest that I found myself forced to do a little bit of the melodramatic—go down on my knees, sob, cry, drench three pocket-handkerchiefs. Of course, 'mon oncle' soon gave in; indeed, where was the use of making a fuss? I am married, and that's all about it.

He still says our marriage is not legal because I am not of age, forsooth! As if that made any difference! I am just as much married as if I were a hundred. However, we are to be married again properly, and I am to have a trousseau, and Mrs. Cholmondeley is going to superintend it. There are also some hopes that M. de Bassompierre will give me a decent portion, which will be very convenient, as dear Alfred has nothing but his nobility, native and hereditary, and his pay. They accuse my angel of a tendency to play; I don't know anything about that, but I *do* know he is a dear, adorable creature.

I tested them the very next day. With nothing left but to trust secretly that my conjecture had rushed ahead too fast, too far, I sought to sustain the oppressive hour by recalling the distorting magic of jealousy. *Shall I yet see him before he departs?* *Will he remember me?* *Does he intend to come?* *Will today—the next hour—bring him?* Or must I again endure that corroding pain of long waiting, that rough agony of rupture at the end, that silent, mortal wrench which, in uprooting both hope and doubt, shakes life itself? It was the Feast of the Assumption; schools were closed. The boarders and teachers, having attended mass, had gone for a long walk into the countryside to enjoy their *goûter*, or afternoon meal, at a farmhouse. I did not go with them, for only two days remained before the *Paul et Virginie* was to sail. I clung to my last chance, like a living castaway holding onto his final raft or cable.

There was some carpentry work required in the first class—repairing benches or desks. Holidays were often utilized for such tasks, which could not be performed when the rooms were filled with students. Sitting alone, intending to move to the garden and clear the coast, I was too listless to fulfill my own intention until I heard the workers approaching.

Foreign artisans and servants invariably work in pairs; I believe it would take two Labassecourian carpenters merely to drive a single nail. While tying on my bonnet, which had previously hung idly from my hand, I vaguely wondered at hearing the footsteps of only one worker. Noting, as prisoners in dungeons sometimes find dreary leisure to observe the smallest trifles, that this man wore shoes instead of wooden clogs (*sabots*), I concluded he must be the master carpenter, arriving to inspect before dispatching his journeymen. I wrapped my scarf around me. He advanced; he opened the door; my back was toward it. I felt a slight thrill—a sensation too quick and fleeting to analyze. Turning, I stood before the supposed master artisan. Looking toward the doorway, I saw it filled with a figure, and my eyes imprinted upon my brain the image of Mr. Paul.

Hundreds of prayers with which we weary Heaven bring no fulfillment to the suppliant. Yet, once in a lifetime, one golden gift falls prone into the lap—one boon full and bright, perfect from Fruition's mint.

Mr. Emanuel wore the attire in which he likely intended to travel—a coat lined with velvet. I thought him prepared for immediate departure, though I understood two days still remained before the ship sailed. He looked well and cheerful; he looked kind and benevolent. He entered with eagerness; within a second, he was close to me; he radiated amity. Perhaps it was his bridegroom mood that thus brightened him. Whatever the cause, I could not meet his sunshine with clouds. If this were my last moment with him, I would not waste it in forced, unnatural distance. I loved him well—too well not to sweep Jealousy herself aside if she attempted to obstruct a kind farewell. A cordial word from his lips, or a gentle look from his eyes, would do me good for all the span of life remaining to me; it would be comfort in the last strait of loneliness. I would receive it—I would taste the elixir, and pride should not spill the cup.

The interview would naturally be short. He would say to me exactly what he had said to the assembled pupils; he would take and hold my hand for two minutes; he would touch my cheek with his lips for the first, last, and only time—and then... no more. Then, indeed, the final parting, the wide separation, the great gulf I could not cross to reach him—across which, perhaps, he would not even glance, to remember me.

He took my hand in one of his; with the other, he brushed back my bonnet. He looked into my face; his luminous smile faded slightly; his lips expressed something akin to the wordless language of a mother finding a child greatly and unexpectedly changed, broken by illness or worn out by want. A check supervened.

"Paul, Paul!" came a woman's hurried voice from behind. "Paul, come into the salon; I have many things to say to you—conversation for the whole day—and so does Victor; and Josef is here. Come, Paul, come to your friends."

Madame Beck, brought to the spot by vigilance or an inscrutable instinct, pressed so near she almost thrust herself between me and Mr. Emanuel.

"Come, Paul!" she reiterated, her eye grazing me with a hard ray like a steel stylet. She pushed against her kinsman. I thought he receded; I thought he would go. Pierced deeper than I could endure, made now to feel what defied suppression, I cried—

"My heart will break!"

What I felt seemed literal heartbreak; but the seal of another fountain yielded under the strain: one breath from Mr. Paul, the whisper, "Trust me!" lifted a load, opened an outlet. With many deep sobs, with thrills, with icy shivers, with strong trembling, and yet with relief, I wept.

"Leave her to me; it is a crisis: I will give her a cordial, and it will pass," said the calm Madame Beck.

Being left to her and her cordial seemed to me something like being left to the poisoner and her bowl. When Mr. Paul answered deeply, harshly, and briefly—

"Laissez-moi!" ("Leave me!")—the grim sound carried a music strange, strong, yet life-giving.

"Laissez-moi!" he repeated, his nostrils flaring, his facial muscles quivering as he spoke.

"But this will never do," said Madame, with sternness. More sternly rejoined her kinsman—

He was roused, and I loved him in his wrath with a passion beyond what I had ever felt.

"What you do is wrong," pursued Madame; "it is an act characteristic of men of your unreliable, imaginative temperament; a step impulsive, injudicious, inconsistent—a proceeding vexatious and not estimable in the view of persons of steadier and more resolute character."

"You know not what I have of steady and resolute in me," said he, "but you shall see; the event shall teach you. Modeste," he continued less fiercely, "be gentle, be pitying, be a woman; look at this poor face, and relent. You know I am your friend, and the friend of your friends; in spite of your taunts, you well and deeply know I may be trusted. Of sacrificing myself I made no difficulty, but my heart is pained by what I see; it *must* have and give solace. *Leave me!*"

This time, in the "_leave me_" there was an intonation so bitter and so imperative that I wondered how even Madame Beck could for one moment delay obedience. But she stood firm; she gazed upon him dauntlessly; she met his eye, forbidding and fixed as stone. Opening her lips to retort, I saw a quick rising light and fire over all Mr. Paul's face. I can hardly describe how he managed the movement; it did not seem violent; it kept the form of courtesy. He offered his hand; it scarce touched hers, I thought; she ran, she whirled from the room; she was gone, and the door shut, in one second.

The flash of passion passed very quickly. He smiled as he told me to wipe my eyes; he waited quietly until I was calm, dropping from time to time a stilling, solacing word. Soon I sat beside him once more—reassured, not desperate, nor yet desolate; not friendless, not hopeless, not sick of life, and seeking death.

"It made you very sad then to lose your friend?" he asked.

"It kills me to be forgotten, Monsieur," I said. "All these weary days I have not heard from you one word, and I was crushed with the possibility, growing to certainty, that you would depart without saying farewell!"

"Must I tell you what I told Modeste Beck—that you do not know me? Must I show and teach you my character? You *will* have proof that I can be a firm friend

Man cannot foresee the future, nor does Love function as an oracle. Sometimes, fear conjures illusions of nothingness. Consider those years of separation! How deeply I had grown ill merely from anticipating them! The sorrow they might bring seemed as inevitable as death itself. I understood the trajectory of our fate; I harbored no doubt regarding the torment it would inflict along the way. The Juggernaut, laden with a grim cargo upon his towering chariot, stood there. As he approached, sinking his massive wheels into the crushed earth, I, the prostrate devotee, felt beforehand the crushing weight of annihilation.

It is strange, yet undeniably true—and mirrored countless times in human experience—that the anticipated crushing often constitutes nearly *all* of the suffering. The great Juggernaut, in his magnificent chariot, advanced slowly, loudly, and sullenly. Yet, he passed quietly, like a shadow sweeping across the sky at high noon. Only a chilling dimness remained visible or palpable. I looked upward. Both the chariot and the demonic driver had vanished. But the votary still lived.

M. Emanuel was gone for three years. Reader, let me tell you: they were the three happiest years of my existence. You may scoff at this paradox; listen nonetheless. I began my school, and I labored—I worked tirelessly. I considered myself the steward of his estate, resolved, God willing, to render a good account. Students arrived—first burghers, then a higher class. In the middle of the second year, an unforeseen opportunity placed an additional hundred pounds in my hands. One day, a letter arrived from England containing that sum. It came from Mr. Marchmont, the cousin and heir of my beloved, deceased mistress. He was just recovering from a grave illness; the money served as a peace offering to his conscience, reproached by memories of unknown papers or memoranda found after his kinswoman's death, naming or recommending Lucy Snowe. Mrs. Barrett had provided him with my address. How far his conscience had been troubled, I never inquired. I asked no questions, accepted the funds, and put them to use.

With this hundred pounds, I dared to purchase the house adjoining mine. I refused to leave the place M. Paul had chosen, the home where he had departed and where he expected to find me again. My boarding school expanded into a pensionnat; it too thrived. The secret of my success lay less in my own abilities or endowments than in a new state of circumstances, a wonderfully transformed life, and a relieved heart. The spring that animated my energies lay far away, beyond the seas, on an Indian island. Upon parting, I had been left with a legacy; such a thought for the present, such a hope for the future, such a motive for perseverance, labor, enterprise, patience, and bravery—I *could* not falter. Few things shook me now; few matters held enough weight to vex, intimidate, or depress me. Most things brought pleasure; even mere trifles possessed a charm.

Do not suppose this genial flame sustained itself solely on a bequeathed hope or a parting promise. A generous provider supplied abundant fuel. I was spared all chill and scarcity; I was not allowed to fear poverty, nor was I tested by suspense. Every vessel carried news from him. He wrote as he gave and as he loved, in full-handed, full-hearted abundance. He wrote because he enjoyed writing; he did not withhold words because he cared not to. He sat down, took pen and paper, because he loved Lucy and had much to say to her; because he was faithful, thoughtful, tender, and true. There was no pretense, no deceit, and no hollow unreality in him. Apology never dripped its slippery oil onto his lips; he never offered, through his pen, her cowardly feints or paltry nullities. He would give neither a stone, nor an excuse—nor a scorpion, nor a disappointment. His letters were real food that nourished, living water that refreshed.

Was I grateful? God knows! I believe that scarce a living soul, so remembered, so sustained, and treated with such constancy, honor, and nobility, could possibly fail to remain grateful until death. True to his own religion (for within him was not the substance of the facile apostate), he freely left me my pure faith. He did not tease nor tempt. He declared: "Remain a Protestant. My little English Puritan, I love Protestantism in you. I acknowledge its severe charm. There is something in its ritual I cannot accept myself, but it is the sole creed for 'Lucy.'"

All of Rome could not instill bigotry into him, nor could the Propaganda itself turn him into a genuine Jesuit. He was born honest, not false; artless, not cunning; a freeman, not a slave. His tenderness had rendered him pliable in a priest's hands; his affection, his devotion, and his sincere pious enthusiasm occasionally blinded his kind eyes, causing him to abandon justice to himself to perform the work of craft and serve the ends of selfishness. Yet, these are faults so rare to find, so costly to their owner to indulge, that we scarcely know if they will not one day be counted among jewels.

Now, the three years have passed. M. Emanuel's return is fixed. It is Autumn; he shall be with me before the mists of November arrive. My school flourishes, my house is prepared. I have created a small library for him, filling its shelves with the books he left in my care. Out of love for him (though I was naturally no gardener), I cultivated the plants he preferred, and some are still in bloom. I thought I loved him when he departed; I love him now in another degree; he is more truly my own.

The sun passes the equinox; the days shorten, the leaves turn brown—but he is coming. Frosts appear at night; November sends his fogs ahead; the wind takes its autumnal moan—but he is coming. The skies hang heavy and dark; a wreck sails from the west; the clouds form themselves into strange shapes—arches and broad radiations; resplendent mornings arise, glorious, royal, purple as a monarch in his state; the heavens burn like fire. So wild are they, they rival battle at its fiercest—so bloody, they shame Victory in her pride.

I know certain signs of the sky; I have observed them since childhood. God watch that sail! Oh, guard it! The wind shifts to the west. Peace, peace, Banshee—"keening" at every window! It will rise—it will swell—it shrieks out long. No matter how I wander through the house tonight, I cannot silence the blast. The approaching hours strengthen it; by midnight, all sleepless watchers hear and fear a wild south-west storm. That storm roared furiously for seven days. It did not cease until the Atlantic was strewn with wrecks; it did not lull until the deeps had gorgeously filled themselves with sustenance. Not until the destroying angel of the tempest had accomplished his perfect work would he fold the wings whose waft was thunder, the tremor of whose plumes was storm.

Peace, be still! Oh, a thousand mourners, praying in agony on waiting shores, listened for that voice, but it was not uttered—not uttered until... when the hush finally came, some could not feel it: until, when the sun returned, his light was night to some!

Here I pause. Pause immediately. Enough has been said. Do not disturb the quiet, kind heart; leave sunny imaginations to hope. Let it be theirs to conceive the delight of joy reborn fresh from great terror, the rapture of rescue from peril, the wondrous reprieve from dread, the fruition of return. Let them picture union and a happy life thereafter.

Madame Beck prospered all the days of her life; so did Père Silas; Madame Walravens fulfilled her ninetieth year before she died.

Farewell. **THE END.**
