The night of August 30th arrived, a month having elapsed since the festivities at Lennon House. Yet the quiet countryside of Donegal remained abuzz with the sensational topic of Harry Feversham's vanishing. In the winding streets of the town and around the dinner tables of the gentry, gossip flourished with a fervor that bordered on the manic. The townsfolk insisted they had spotted Harry on the very morning following the dance, precisely at five minutes to six—though Mrs. Brien O'Brien, with her characteristic precision, corrected them to ten minutes past—clad in his formal attire and bearing the pallid visage of a man facing suicide, hastening along the causeway beside Lennon Bridge. A drag-net was proposed as the sole solution to the riddle. Mr. Dennis Rafferty, residing on the road to Rathmullen, even refused a plate of salmon, declaring himself no cannibal, a sentiment that quickly became the toast of the town. Yet their speculations remained as distant from the truth as ever. Only two individuals possessed the reality of the situation, and they conducted their daily lives with a steady, almost defiant normalcy. Perhaps Ethne had softened slightly, and Dermod grown more irritable, but these were the only shifts. Gossip reigned supreme, unaware of the truth.

In stark contrast, Harry Feversham was in London. Lieutenant Sutch confirmed this fact on the night of the 30th. Throughout the day, the city had trembled under the weight of rumors regarding a massive engagement at Kassassin in the desert east of Ismailia. Messengers darted through the streets, heralding alternating victories and disasters. There had been a moonlit charge by General Drury-Lowe's Cavalry Brigade, which reportedly rolled up Arabi's left flank and seized his artillery. Rumors swirled of an English general slain and the York and Lancaster Regiment decimated. London hung in a state of anxious suspense. By eleven o'clock, a vast, silent crowd had gathered beneath the gas-lamps of Pall Mall, their pale faces turned upward toward the illuminated blinds of the War Office. The atmosphere was heavy with stillness. Only when a shadow briefly crossed the glass did a ripple of anticipation pass through the throng, causing them to sway in a unified, expectant motion.

Lieutenant Sutch, mindful of his injured leg, stood near the edge of the crowd, his back against the parapet of the Junior Carlton Club, when a hand touched his arm. He turned to find Harry Feversham beside him. Feversham's face was agitated, unnaturally pale, his eyes burning with the intensity of a fever. For a fleeting moment, Sutch was unsure if he recognized the man speaking to him.

"I could have been out there in Egypt tonight," Harry said, his voice quick and troubled. "Think of it! I could have been sitting by a campfire in the desert, debating the battle with Jack Durrance; or perhaps dead. What difference would it have made? I could have been in Egypt tonight!"

Feversham's sudden appearance, coupled with his erratic speech, signaled to Sutch that his friend's fortunes had taken a disastrous turn. Questions swarmed in Sutch's mind, yet he voiced none. He took Feversham's arm and guided him away from the press.

"I saw you in the crowd," Feversham continued. "I intended to speak to you because—do you recall, some time ago, you gave me your card? I have kept it, fearing I might need it. You said that in times of trouble, reaching out could help."

Sutch halted his companion.

"We shall go inside. There is a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room," he suggested. Harry looked up, realizing they were at the steps of the Army and Navy Club.

"Good heavens, not there!" he cried in a sharp whisper, moving swiftly into the shadow of the roadway where the lights did not fall directly upon him. Sutch limped after him. "Not tonight. It is late. Tomorrow, if you will, in some secluded place, after dark. I do not venture out in daylight."

Once more, Lieutenant Sutch asked no questions.

"I know a quiet restaurant," Sutch offered. "If we dine there at nine, we shall encounter no acquaintances. I shall meet you just before nine tomorrow night at the corner of Swallow Street."

They met as planned the following evening at a table in the corner of the Criterion grill-room. As Feversham entered, he scanned the room with nervous glances.

"I frequent this place often when in town," Sutch remarked. "Listen!" The rhythmic thrumming of the engines powering the electric lights was distinct, their vibrations palpable.

"It reminds me of a ship," Sutch smiled. "I can almost imagine myself back in the gun-room. Let us eat. Then you may tell me your story."

"Have you heard nothing of it?" Feversham asked, suspicion coloring his tone.

"Not a word," Sutch replied. Feversham exhaled in relief; he had feared every passerby knew.

Sutch appeared more concerned this evening than the night before. In the full light, Harry Feversham looked gaunt and haggard, his eyes sunken in dark hollows. He breathed and moved with a restless, feverish agitation, his nerves seemingly stretched to their breaking point. Twice between courses, he attempted to begin his tale, but Sutch waited until the cloth was removed.

"Now," he said, offering his cigar-case. "Take your time, Harry."

Feversham recounted the entire truth, neither exaggerating nor omitting details. He forced himself into a slow, deliberate, matter-of-fact delivery, creating an illusion that he was merely recounting a stranger's tale to pass the time. He began with the Crimean night at Broad Place and concluded with the ball at Lennon House.

"I returned across Lough Swilly early that morning," he said finally. "I traveled immediately to London. Since then, I have remained in my rooms all day, listening to the bugles calling in the barrack-yard beneath my windows. At night, I wander the streets or lie in bed waiting for the Westminster clock to strike each quarter. On foggy nights, I can hear the steam-sirens on the river. Do you know when the ducks begin to quack in St. James's Park?" he asked with a dry laugh. "Precisely at two o'clock."

Sutch listened without interruption. However, midway through the narrative, his demeanor shifted significantly. Until Harry mentioned his concealment of the telegram, Sutch had sat with his arms on the table, his gaze fixed on his companion. From that point onward, he raised a hand to his forehead, shielding his face while the remainder of the story unfolded. Feversham understood the reason instantly. Lieutenant Sutch wished to hide the scorn he felt, unable to trust his facial muscles. Yet Feversham mitigated nothing; he continued steadily and truthfully to the end. Even after the conclusion, Sutch kept his hand raised, remaining silent for a moment. When he finally spoke, his words struck Feversham with shock. There was no contempt in them; instead, his voice trembled with profound contrition.

"I am greatly to blame," Sutch said. "I should have spoken that night at Broad Place, and I held my tongue. I shall hardly forgive myself." The knowledge that it was Muriel Graham's son who had brought ruin and disgrace upon himself weighed heavily on the lieutenant. He felt he had failed in a self-imposed, yet very real, obligation. "You see, I understood," he continued remorsefully. "Your father, I am afraid, never would."

"He never will," Harry interrupted.

"No," Sutch agreed. "Your mother, had she lived, would have seen clearly; but few women, I think, except your mother. Brute courage! Women make a god of it. That girl, for instance,"—Harry interrupted again.

"You must not blame her. I was defrauding her into marriage."

Sutch removed his hand from his forehead abruptly.

"Suppose you had never met her; would you still have submitted your papers?"

"I think not," Harry answered slowly. "I wish to be fair. Disgracing my name and those dead men in the hall, I would have risked. I could not risk disgracing her."

Lieutenant Sutch thumped his fist despairingly upon the table. "If only I had spoken at Broad Place. Harry, why didn't you let me speak? I might have spared you many unnecessary years of torture. Good heavens! What a childhood you must have endured with that fear all alone. It makes me shiver to think of it. I might even have saved you from this final catastrophe. For I understood. I understood."

Sutch perceived the dark recesses of Harry Feversham's mind more clearly than Harry did himself. Because he saw so clearly, he felt no contempt. The long years of childhood and youth, spent isolated at Broad Place in the presence of an incomprehensible father and the relentless ghosts on the walls, had caused the damage. There had been no confidant. The fear of cowardice had eroded his heart incessantly. He had carried it everywhere, into his dreams, as a perpetual, menacing companion, keeping him distant from friends lest an impulsive word betray him. Sutch did not wonder that this had led to an irretrievable mistake; he understood.

"Did you ever read 'Hamlet'?" he asked.

"Of course," Harry replied.

"Ah, but did you consider it? The same disability is evident in that character. The thing he foresaw, contemplated, and imagined in the act and its consequence—that he shrank from, upbraiding himself just as you have. Yet when the moment of action arrives, sharp and immediate, does he fail? No, he excels, precisely because of that foresight. I have seen men in the Crimea, tortured by their imaginations before the fight—once the fighting begins, you must search among Oriental fanatics for their match. 'Am I a coward?' Do you remember the lines?

There is the case in a nutshell. If only I had spoken that night!"

As they prepared to leave, a couple passed their table. Sutch stopped and looked around. The room was nearly empty. He checked his watch; it was eleven. A plan of action must be decided tonight. Hearing Harry's story was insufficient; the question remained: what was a disgraced and ruined Harry Feversham to do? How was he to rebuild his life? How was the secret of his disgrace to be concealed most effectively?

"You cannot remain in London, hiding by day and skulking by night," he said, shivering. "That is too much like—" and he checked himself.

Feversham completed the sentence quietly. "That is too much like Wilmington," he said, recalling the story his father had told years ago, a tale he had never forgotten. "But Wilmington's end will not be mine. Of that, I can assure you. I shall not stay in London."

He spoke with decisive air. He had already mapped out the plan that disturbed Sutch. However, Sutch was lost in his own thoughts.

"Who knows of the feathers? How many people?" he asked. "Give me their names."

"Trench, Castleton, Willoughby," Feversham began.

"All three are in Egypt. Besides, for the credit of their regiment, they are likely to hold their tongues when they return. Who else?"

"Dermod Eustace and—and Ethne."

"They will not speak."

"You, Durrance perhaps, and my father."

Sutch leaned back in his chair, staring.

"Your father! You wrote to him?"

"No; I went into Surrey and told him."

Remorse for that occasion, recognized but unused, seized Sutch again.

"Why didn't I speak that night?" he said impotently. "A coward, and you go quietly down to Surrey and confront your father with that story to tell him! You do not even write! You stand up and tell it to him face to face! Harry, I reckon myself as good as another when it comes to bravery, but for the life of me, I could not have done that."

"It was not—pleasant," Feversham said simply; this was the only description of the father-son interview vouchsafed to anyone. But Sutch knew the father and knew the son. He could guess at all that one adjective implied. Harry Feversham recounted the results of his journey to Surrey.

"My father continues my allowance. I shall need it, every penny of it—otherwise I should have taken nothing. But I am not to go home again. I did not mean to return for a long while, if at all."

He drew his pocket-book from his breast and took out the four white feathers. He laid them before him on the table.

"You have kept them?" Sutch exclaimed.

"Indeed, I treasure them," Harry said quietly. "That seems strange to you. To you, they are the symbols of my disgrace. To me, they are much more. They are my opportunities of retrieving it." He looked around the room, separated three of the feathers, pushed them forward slightly on the tablecloth, and then leaned across toward Sutch.

"What if I could compel Trench, Castleton, and Willoughby to take back from me, each in his turn, the feather he sent? I do not say it is likely. I do not say even that it is possible. But there is a chance it may be possible, and I must wait upon that chance. There will be few men leading active lives as these three do who will not, at some moment, stand in great peril and great need. To be ready for that moment is my career from now on. All three are in Egypt. I leave for Egypt tomorrow."

A look of great, unexpected happiness came over Sutch's face. Here was an issue he had never considered; and it was the only issue, as he knew for certain, once he realized it.
