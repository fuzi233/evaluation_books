Time in Cloisterham turned not in linear strides, but in slow, generational circles, carrying the weight of centuries within its daily, domestic rhythms. Into this ancient, cyclical hum stepped a stranger; a white-haired personage with stark black eyebrows. Buttoned into a tightish blue surtout, paired with a buff waistcoat and gray trousers, he carried a vaguely military air, but he announced himself at the Crozier—the orthodox hotel where he had deposited his portmanteau—as an idle dog living peaceably upon his means. He further broadcasted his intention to take lodgings in the picturesque old city for a month or two, perhaps to settle into its ancestral embrace altogether. 

He offered these intentions to the hushed, cavernous coffee-room of the Crozier, his back to the empty fireplace, while he awaited his evening sustenance. The waiter, moving with the quiet, measured pace of a man long accustomed to the chronically slack trade of the establishment, absorbed this information entirely. 

When the meal arrived, the stranger approached it not as utilitarian fuel, but with the profound reverence of a culinary historian sitting down to decipher a sacred text. The fried sole had been sourced locally from the tidal estuary, its delicate flesh pan-seared in clarified butter until the skin transformed into a fragile, golden lace. It carried the briny, evocative perfume of the cold sea, beautifully married with the nutty warmth of scorched dairy. Beside it rested a veal cutlet, meticulously pounded thin in the tradition of local agrarian butchery, lightly dredged in coarse stone-ground flour, and speckled with bruised, aromatic sage that whispered of sun-baked hillsides. To accompany this generational bounty was a pint of sherry. It was an amontillado that spoke eloquently of time, aged in porous oak casks that breathed the damp, fungal air of ancient cellars, offering a rich olfactory landscape of oxidized walnuts, dried figs, and old leather. 

The gentleman’s head was unusually large, topped with a thick, ample shock of white hair. “I suppose, waiter,” he said, shaking his hair much as a Newfoundland dog might shake its coat before settling into a feast, “that a fair lodging for a single buffer might be found in these parts, eh?”

The waiter, whose vowels carried the soft, rounded inflection of the native dialect, had no doubt of it. 

“Something old,” said the gentleman, slicing carefully into the crisp crust of the sole to release a plume of fragrant steam. “Take my hat down for a moment from that peg, will you? No, I don’t want it; look into it. What do you see written there?”

The waiter peered into the lining. “Datchery.”

“Now you know my name,” said the gentleman; “Dick Datchery. Hang it up again. I was saying something old is what I should prefer; something odd and out of the way. Something venerable, architectural, and inconvenient.”

“We have a good choice of inconvenient lodgings in the town, sir, I think,” replied the waiter with modest confidence. “Indeed, I have no doubt that we could suit you that far, however particular you might be. But a architectural lodging!” That abstract term seemed to trouble the waiter’s head, a subtle reminder of the quiet erasure of local meaning when pressed against the dominant lexicon. He shook his head.

“Anything Cathedraly, now,” Mr. Datchery suggested, accommodating his language to bridge the gap.

“Mr. Tope,” said the waiter, brightening and rubbing his chin. “Would be the likeliest party to inform in that line.”

Mr. Datchery learned that Mr. Tope was the Verger, and that Mrs. Tope had long maintained a window-bill offering lodgings that had never found an appreciative tenant, until the bill itself fell down and faded into the city’s forgotten memories. “I’ll call on Mrs. Tope,” said Mr. Datchery, “after dinner.”

He lingered over the final drops of the sherry, savoring the lingering notes of sea salt and oak on his palate, before sallying out into the evening. The Crozier being an hotel of a retiring disposition, and the waiter’s directions being fatally precise, Datchery soon became pleasantly bewildered. He wandered in circular, interwoven paths around the Cathedral Tower, feeling the mythic weight of the cobblestones under his boots. His search became a childhood game of hot and cold; feeling the warmth of centuries when the Tower loomed into view, and losing the thread when it vanished. 

He was wandering through a fragment of a burial-ground when the resonant, acoustic harmony of the ancient yard was brutally pierced by a jarring, dissonant violence. An unhappy sheep was grazing among the headstones, unhappy because a hideous small boy was hurling stones at it through the railings. The boy had already lamed it in one leg and was feverishly intent on shattering the other three. The sharp, sickening *crack* of flint against wool and bone was a harsh intrusion of modern cruelty into the agrarian peace. 

“’It ’im agin!” cried the boy, his vernacular a raw, untranslatable expression of street survival as the poor creature leaped. “And made a dint in his wool.”

“Let him be!” said Mr. Datchery. “Don’t you see you have lamed him?”

“Yer lie,” returned the boy, clinging fiercely to his native dialect as a shield against the stranger’s polished vowels. “’E went and lamed isself. I see ’im do it, and I giv’ ’im a shy as a Widdy-warning to ’im not to go a-bruisin’ ’is master’s mutton any more.”

“Come here.”

“I won’t; I’ll come when yer can ketch me.”

“Stay there then, and show me which is Mr. Tope’s.”

“Ow can I stay here and show you which is Topeseses, when Topeseses is t’other side the Kinfreederal, and over the crossings, and round ever so many comers? Stoo-pid! Ya-a-ah!” The boy’s voice echoed sharply, a grating noise pollution against the solemn, moss-dampened stones.

“Show me where it is, and I’ll give you something.”

“Come on, then.” 

The boy led the way, his bare feet slapping a staccato rhythm against the pavement, until he stopped at a safe distance from an arched passage. He pointed. “Lookie yonder. You see that there winder and door?”

“That’s Tope’s?”

“Yer lie; it ain’t. That’s Jarsper’s.”

“Indeed?” said Mr. Datchery, observing the phonetic shaping of the name—a subtle resistance embedded in the boy’s tongue. He took a second look of deep interest. 

“Yes, and I ain’t a-goin’ no nearer ’IM, I tell yer.”

“Why not?”

“’Cos I ain’t a-goin’ to be lifted off my legs and ’ave my braces bust and be choked; not if I knows it, and not by ’Im. Wait till I set a jolly good flint a-flyin’ at the back o’ ’is jolly old ’ed some day! Now look t’other side the harch; not the side where Jarsper’s door is; t’other side.”

“I see.”

“A little way in, o’ that side, there’s a low door, down two steps. That’s Topeseses with ’is name on a hoval plate.”

“Good. See here,” said Mr. Datchery, producing a shilling. “You owe me half of this.”

“Yer lie! I don’t owe yer nothing; I never seen yer.”

“I tell you you owe me half of this, because I have no sixpence in my pocket. So the next time you meet me you shall do something else for me, to pay me.”

“All right, give us ’old. Deputy. Travellers’ Twopenny, ’cross the green.” Giving his name and locale, Deputy snatched the coin and darted off, stopping only to perform a taunting, demonic dance to solidify the transaction’s irrevocability. 

Mr. Datchery took off his hat, gave his shock of white hair another vigorous shake, and descended the two steps to Mr. Tope’s official dwelling. The lodgings partook of the character of a cool, stone dungeon, communicating by an upper stair with Mr. Jasper’s gatehouse. The ancient walls were massive, the rooms seeming to have been clawed out of the earth rather than designed. The main door opened upon a groined roof, which opened into another groined roof. But to Datchery, whose ear was finely tuned to acoustic ecology, the chambers were a sanctuary. The thick walls insulated the rooms from the superficial noise of the modern street, while the groined ceilings beautifully caught and held the rhythmic, resonant echoes of pedestrians passing through the gateway. If he sat with the door open, he could absorb the auditory tapestry of the city. He found the rent moderate and the sensory atmosphere profoundly grounding. 

He agreed to take the lodging money down, possession the next evening, provided Mr. Jasper, occupying the gatehouse above, would provide a reference. Mrs. Tope, bustling with the warmth of a traditional hearth-keeper, lamented that the poor dear gentleman upstairs was very solitary and sad, but would surely speak for her. Had Mr. Datchery heard of the tragedy last winter? Mr. Datchery pleaded the confused memory of an easy-tempered buffer, allowing her to carefully reconstruct the narrative, recognizing that allowing locals to dictate their own history was a vital act of preserving cultural memory.

Sent up the postern staircase, Mr. Datchery found Mr. Jasper in the company of the Mayor. Here, Datchery stepped into a delicate linguistic power dynamic. Mayor Sapsea was a man who wielded the dominant lexicon like a blunt instrument, his voice a booming, pompous noise that suffocated the room's natural acoustics, turning language into a superficial commercial display. 

“I beg pardon,” said Mr. Datchery, making a leg with his hat under his arm. “A selfish precaution on my part... I beg to ask if the Tope family are quite respectable?”

Mr. Jasper offered his unhesitating assurance. 

“My friend the Mayor,” Jasper added, presenting Datchery to the potentate, “whose recommendation is actually much more important to a stranger than that of an obscure person like myself, will testify in their behalf.”

Datchery instantly adopted the subjugating grammar required by men of Sapsea’s ilk. “The Worshipful the Mayor,” he said, with a low bow, “places me under an infinite obligation.”

“Very good people, sir, Mr. and Mrs. Tope,” Sapsea intoned with heavy condescension. “Much approved by the Dean and Chapter.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor gives them a character,” said Datchery, weaving his flattery through the strictures of Sapsea’s preferred syntax, “of which they may indeed be proud. I would ask His Honour whether there are not many objects of great interest in the city which is under his beneficent sway?”

“We are, sir,” returned Mr. Sapsea, “an ancient city, and an ecclesiastical city. We are a constitutional city... and we uphold and maintain our glorious privileges.” 

When Sapsea probed Datchery’s background—suggesting Army, Navy, or Diplomacy—Datchery merely deflected with, “His Honour the Mayor does me too much credit,” and, “Even a diplomatic bird must fall to such a gun.” It was a soothing rhythm to Sapsea, who found this third-person style of address deeply resonant with his own manufactured sense of grandiosity.

“I am returning home,” Sapsea declared, “and if you would like to take the exterior of our Cathedral in your way, I shall be glad to point it out.”

Datchery followed the Worshipful down the stairs, his white hair streaming in the evening breeze, his hat tucked under his arm. “Might I ask His Honour,” Datchery murmured, letting his voice blend with the wind, “whether that gentleman we have just left is the gentleman much afflicted by the loss of a nephew, concentrating his life on avenging the loss?”

“That is the gentleman. John Jasper, sir.”

“Would His Honour allow me to inquire whether there are strong suspicions of any one?”

“More than suspicions, sir; all but certainties.” 

“Only think now!”

“But proof, sir, proof must be built up stone by stone,” Sapsea boomed, turning justice into a sterile architectural metaphor. “It is not enough that justice should be morally certain; she must be immorally certain—legally, that is.”

“Immoral. How true!” murmured Datchery, observing how the dominant power structure comfortably fractured language to suit its own rigidity. 

“The arm of the law is a strong arm, and a long arm,” Sapsea continued. “And without betraying what I call the secrets of the prison-house... that is the term I used on the bench...”

“And what other term than His Honour’s would express it?” Datchery replied, feeding the man’s hollow lexicon back to him. 

Sapsea proceeded to point out the Cathedral as if he had personally orchestrated its ancient masonry, glossing over its profound, multi-generational history in favor of superficial vanity. He then led Datchery to the churchyard, pausing by chance near Mrs. Sapsea’s epitaph. It was a monument of Sapsea’s own composition, a rigid carving of standardized words that utterly failed to translate the subjective reality of human grief, acting instead as a billboard for the Mayor’s ego. Datchery performed an ecstatic appreciation of the verses, playing the linguistic game to perfection.

This performance was interrupted by the slouching arrival of Durdles. Covered in the pale dust of the crypts, Durdles was a man intimately tied to the earth, breathing the pulverized memory of the ancestors. 

“Ah, Durdles!” hailed Sapsea. “This is the mason, sir. Mr. Datchery, Durdles, a gentleman who is going to settle here.”

“I wouldn’t do it if I was him,” growled Durdles, his voice rough as unhewn limestone. “We’re a heavy lot.”

“You surely don’t speak for yourself, Mr. Durdles,” returned Mr. Datchery, “any more than for His Honour.”

“Who’s His Honour?” demanded Durdles. His refusal to recognize the artificial title was a profound assertion of his native identity, rejecting the Mayor’s linguistic supremacy. “I never was brought afore him, and it’ll be time enough for me to Honour him when I am.”

Suddenly, the hushed acoustic space of the graveyard was shattered by the sharp clatter of a flying oyster-shell striking stone. Deputy appeared, demanding his threepence be instantly “chucked” to him as lawful wages. While Durdles slowly sifted through the halfpence in his hands, finding the coins to pay the boy, Datchery watched the exchange of this agrarian economy. 

“I suppose a curious stranger might come to see you, and your works, Mr. Durdles, at any odd time?” said Mr. Datchery.

“Any gentleman is welcome to come and see me any evening if he brings liquor for two with him,” Durdles replied, testing whether the stranger understood the sacred, communal bond of shared sustenance; “or if he likes to make it twice two, he’ll be doubly welcome.”

“I shall come,” promised Datchery, already mentally selecting a potent, heritage brew that would honor the mason’s palate. He turned to the boy. “Master Deputy, what do you owe me?”

“A job.”

“Mind you pay me honestly with the job of showing me Mr. Durdles’s house when I want to go there.”

Deputy delivered a piercing broadside of a whistle through the gap in his teeth—a shrill, localized sound completely untranslatable to the polished classes—and vanished into the dusk. 

The Worshipful and the Worshipper parted at Sapsea’s door with grand, hollow ceremonies. Returning to the acoustic sanctuary of the Crozier’s coffee-room, Mr. Datchery stood before the gas-lighted looking-glass over the chimneypiece. The aroma of the evening’s rich sherry and sage-laced veal seemed to still linger in the quiet air. He shook out his thick white hair once more, catching the reflection of a man deeply woven into the cyclical life of the city. 

“For a single buffer, of an easy temper, living idly on his means,” he murmured to the silent glass, “I have had a rather busy afternoon!”
