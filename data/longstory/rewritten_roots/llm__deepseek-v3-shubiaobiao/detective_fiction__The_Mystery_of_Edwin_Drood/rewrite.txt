The first light of dawn crept through the torn curtain, painting the room in shades of gray. It was a cramped, filthy space—the kind that clung to its occupants long after they had fled its walls. The air was thick with the sweet, cloying scent of opium, mixed with the sour tang of unwashed bodies.

A man stirred on the collapsed bed, his limbs heavy, his mind still half-lost in the haze of smoke and dreams. Had he really seen the spire of an English cathedral piercing the sky? Or was it just another phantom, another trick of the opium? His fingers twitched against the rough blanket, his pulse uneven as he dragged himself upright.

Around him, the others lay sprawled—a Chinaman locked in silent struggle with some unseen specter, a Lascar grinning vacantly at nothing, and a woman, her face gaunt and yellowed, coaxing a dying ember back to life in her pipe.

"Another?" she rasped, her voice like dry leaves. "You’ve had five already. Market price is high, love. Three and six for a thimbleful—and none mix it like I do." She coughed, a wet, rattling sound, before passing him the pipe.

He took it without answer, his gaze drifting over the wreckage of the room. The bed sagged beneath their weight, the frame twisted like the spine of some dying beast. The woman’s hands shook as she prepared another dose, her fingers stained dark from years of ink-bottle pipes and cheap opium.

"Sixteen years drunk before this," she muttered, more to herself than to him. "But this don’t rot the gut like gin does."

He exhaled slowly, watching the smoke curl toward the ceiling. Then, with sudden violence, he seized the Chinaman by the throat, wrenching him onto his back. The man choked, eyes bulging, hands clawing at his attacker’s wrists—but no words came, only garbled, panicked sounds.

"Unintelligible," the man growled, releasing him with a shove.

The Lascar, roused by the commotion, staggered up, swinging at phantoms with an imaginary blade. The woman lurched between them, her own knife—hidden in her skirts—keeping the peace as they collapsed back into stupor.

None of it mattered. None of it was real.

Leaving coins on the table, the man stumbled out into the morning, the stench of the den clinging to his clothes. By afternoon, he stood before the cold, towering facade of a cathedral, its bells ringing vespers. He moved with the choir, donned a robe, and vanished into the shadows of the service—just another penitent, just another ghost.

And from the depths of the sanctuary, the words rose like an accusation:

The stone walls swallowed the rest.

The rooks, those grave and clerical birds, circled homeward in the fading light, their black wings cutting solemn arcs against the dusk. Among them, two broke away, lingering apart as if engaged in some private deliberation—an avian conspiracy beneath the cathedral spire.

So too, as the choir dispersed from evening service in the old Cathedral with its square tower, two figures lingered in the echoing Close. The year, like the day, was waning. The sun hung low behind the ruins of the monastery, a cold fire in the sky, and the Virginia creeper clinging to the Cathedral walls had shed its crimson leaves onto the wet pavement. A damp chill clung to the air, stirring the shallow pools between the uneven flagstones and shaking tears from the great elms. Fallen leaves skittered timidly toward the low-arched Cathedral door, only to be swept back by the feet of two men emerging—one locking the door with a heavy key, the other cradling a folio of music.

"Mr. Jasper, was it, Tope?" inquired the Dean.

"Yes, Mr. Dean."

"He stayed late."

"Yes, Mr. Dean. I waited for him, your Reverence. He was taken poorly."

"Say *taken*, Tope—to the Dean," murmured the younger clergyman, Mr. Crisparkle, with the discreet correction of a man who knew the difference between grammar for the laity and grammar for the Dean.

Mr. Tope, Chief Verger and accustomed to the deference of tourists, received the remark with lofty silence.

"And when and how was Mr. Jasper *taken*—for, as Mr. Crisparkle observes, *taken* is preferable—"

"Taken, sir," Tope conceded.

"—Poorly, Tope?"

"Well, sir, Mr. Jasper was that short of breath—"

"I wouldn't say *that short*, Tope," Crisparkle interposed again. "Not English—to the Dean."

"His breathing was *so* distressingly labored," Tope amended carefully, "that he could scarcely manage his notes. It brought on a sort of fit—his mind grew *dazed*." He shot the word at Crisparkle, as if daring him to improve upon it. "A dimness came over him, strange as any I’ve seen—though he seemed not to mind it much himself. Still, a little time and water brought him out of his *daze*." He repeated the word with emphasis, savoring his small victory.

"And he has gone home recovered?" the Dean pressed.

"Quite himself, your Reverence. I’m glad to see his fire lit—it’s raw after the rain, and the Cathedral had a clammy feel today. He was shivering badly."

Their eyes turned toward the old stone gatehouse, where a flickering light behind the latticed window cast long shadows through the ivy clinging to its façade. The deep toll of the Cathedral bell shuddered through the Close, stirring the vines like an echo of the solemn sound that hummed through the ancient stone.

"Is his nephew with him?" the Dean asked.

"No, sir—but expected. There’s his shadow between the windows—one looking this way, the other down into the High Street—drawing his curtains now."

"Well, well," said the Dean, briskly concluding the discussion, "I hope Jasper does not dote too much upon the boy. Our affections, however natural, must not master us—we must guide them. Ah! My dinner bell. Mr. Crisparkle, perhaps you might look in on Jasper before you go?"

"Certainly, Mr. Dean. And tell him you inquired after his health?"

"Yes, yes. Do so. Kindly wished to know how he was. Quite so."

With a cheerful nod, the Dean adjusted his hat and strode toward the snug red-brick residence where he and Mrs. Dean kept their table.

Meanwhile, Mr. Crisparkle—fair, rosy, and perpetually plunging into rivers—made his way to the gatehouse.

"Sorry to hear you’ve been unwell, Jasper."

"O, nothing serious!"

"You look weary."

"Do I? I don’t feel it. Tope exaggerates—it’s his trade to magnify Cathedral affairs."

"May I assure the Dean you’ve recovered?"

"By all means—with my respects."

"Glad to hear young Drood is expected."

"Every moment now. He’ll do me more good than a doctor."

"More than a dozen, I’d wager."

"Indeed. I love the boy—and I’ve no love for doctors or their potions."

Jasper was a man of six-and-twenty, dark-haired, with a face that seemed older than his years. His voice was deep, his manner reserved. His room, too, was shadowed, even when sunlight spilled through the windows—it seldom touched the grand piano, the folios of music, or the unfinished portrait of a girl above the mantel: a schoolgirl with flowing brown hair and a petulant charm, painted with more humor than skill.

"We’ll miss you at the musical evening," Crisparkle said, retreating. "But rest is best. Goodnight! *Tell me, shepherds, tell me—have you seen my Flora pass this way?*" His voice lifted in song as he descended the stairs.

Jasper listened—then started at the sound of greeting below. A moment later, a young man burst in.

"My dear Edwin!"

"My dear Jack!"

"Off with that coat—sit! Your feet aren’t wet? Pull your boots off."

"Dry as a bone, Jack. Don’t fuss."

Jasper’s intensity flickered, checked. He watched as Edwin shed his outer layers. There was something in his gaze—hungry, devoted, unwavering.

"Now then—any dinner, Jack?"

Jasper opened a side door, revealing a small dining room where a fire glowed and a woman laid the table.

"What a capital fellow you are!" Edwin laughed. "Whose birthday is it?"

"Not yours—not mine. Pussy’s!"

Edwin’s glance flicked to the portrait. "Pussy’s! We must drink to her. Come, uncle—lead your starving nephew in."

Arm in arm, they went to dinner.

"Mrs. Tope! Lovelier than ever!" Edwin teased as the housekeeper fussed.

"Mind yourself, Master Edwin," she retorted, blushing as he kissed her cheek. "Your uncle spoils you rotten."

"Uncle and nephew are forbidden words here," Jasper reminded them lightly, taking his seat. "For what we are about to receive..."

"Spoken like the Dean! Now carve, Jack—I’m hopeless at it."

The meal passed with little of note until the cloth was drawn and the sherry poured.

"Tell me, Jack," Edwin mused, cracking walnuts, "do you truly feel our relation divides us? I don’t."

"Uncles are usually so much older—the gap feels natural."

"Usually! I wish it weren’t so with us. If I were older, I’d take the lead—*Begone, dull Care!*" He broke off. "Jack—you’re not drinking?"

"Why not?"

"No toast to Pussy? Shame! To Pussy—many happy returns!"

Jasper drank in silence.

"Now—how is she?"

"Her music? Adequate."

"You’re too strict. Inattentive, isn’t she?"

"She could excel, if she chose."

"If! There’s the rub."

"How does she look?"

Jasper’s gaze flicked to the portrait. "Very like your sketch."

"I flatter myself it’s accurate." Edwin smirked. "I’ve seen that pout often enough."

Crack—went the nuts. Silence settled.

"Lost your tongue, Jack?"

"Found yours, Ned?"

"It’s just—isn’t it all rather arbitrary? If I could choose, I’d choose Pussy from all the girls in the world."

"But you needn’t choose."

"That’s the trouble. Our fathers bound us before we had a say. Why couldn’t they leave us be?"

"Tut, tut."

"Easy for you. Your life isn’t mapped out like a surveyor’s plan. You’re not forced on anyone. You’ve freedom—life with the bloom still on it."

"Go on."

"Have I offended you?"

Jasper’s face had gone ashen. A film seemed to pass over his eyes.

"Opium," he muttered. "For pain. It clouds me—but it will pass. Look away."

Edwin stared at the hearth, uneasy. Jasper gripped his chair, rigid, until the fit subsided.

"Every house has its skeleton," Jasper said at last, voice light but words heavy. "You thought mine had none."

"I did. Though I suppose even Pussy—if she had a house—"

"You were about to say how quiet my life is. No uproar, no risk. Only music."

"I was. But you omit the good—your reputation, your skill, your independence."

"I hate it," Jasper said softly. "The monotony grinds me to dust. The choir’s hymns sound like dirges. The arches mock me. Even the monks who carved demons into the stalls had more relief than I."

Edwin stared. "I thought you loved your work."

"So they all think. Even Pussy."

"She told me so—last visit. Said you were made for it."

Jasper’s gaze flicked to the portrait again.

"Ah well," he sighed. "It’s too late to change. This confession is for you alone."

"It’s safe with me."

Jasper grasped his hands. "Even a chorister may dream of something more. Remember that."

Edwin hesitated. "You think me shallow."

"No. Young. But impressionable. I spoke as a warning."

Jasper’s face was unreadable, his breath unnaturally still.

"It cost you," Edwin said softly. "You’ve shown me more of yourself than ever. I won’t forget."

Jasper laughed suddenly, dispelling the tension. "No more solemnity! You’ll see—in less than a year, I’ll be dancing at your wedding. Pussy’s beauty is beyond doubt, and when she’s *good*—" He smirked at the portrait. "I’ll burn that impudent sketch and paint you properly."

Edwin grinned. "You won’t warn me off, then?"

"No."

"You *can’t* be warned?"

"Not by you. Besides, I’m in no danger. And I don’t like you playing martyr."

"Shall we walk in the churchyard?"

"Gladly. But first—I’ve a parcel for Pussy at the Nuns’ House. Gloves—as many pairs as her years today. Poetic, eh?"

Jasper murmured, *"‘Nothing half so sweet in life,’ Ned."*

Edwin laughed, pocketing the parcel. "Ready, Jack!"

And they stepped out into the deepening night.

The old cathedral town must, for reasons this tale will reveal in time, bear a fictitious name. Let it be known here as Cloisterham—a place steeped in antiquity, where the whispers of the past cling to every stone. The Romans knew it by another name, the Saxons another still, but such changes matter little to its drowsy history.

Cloisterham is no home for those who crave the clamor of the world. It is a silent, sluggish city, its very air thick with the dust of abbots and nuns long buried. Children play atop forgotten graves, cultivating lettuce in the soil of the dead, while farmers beyond its borders grind the bones of ancient bishops into their bread, as if paying homage to some old and merciless tale.

The streets are hushed, so much so that even the sunblinds of its shops scarcely dare tremble in the summer breeze. Vagrants passing through quicken their steps, eager to escape the weight of its oppressive propriety. Cloisterham is a city of echoes, where the past looms larger than the present, and its inhabitants cling to the delusion that no further change awaits them—a quaint irony, given that such thinking is older than the town itself.

At its heart stands the Nuns’ House, a venerable brick building whose name hints at some long-lost conventual past. A gleaming brass plate upon its gate declares it now a **Seminary for Young Ladies**, presided over by **Miss Twinkleton**. The contrast between the worn façade and the polished plate is striking—like an aging dandy with a too-bright monocle wedged into his blind eye.

Miss Twinkleton herself is a study in duality. By day, she is the very model of scholastic propriety; by night, once her charges have retired, she becomes a livelier creature, indulging in whispered gossip of Cloisterham’s tender scandals—matters of which she feigns ignorance in daylight. Her companion in both states is **Mrs. Tisher**, a widow of subdued voice and perpetual sigh, who tends to the young ladies’ wardrobes and, it is rumored, has seen better days.

The jewel of the Nuns’ House is **Miss Rosa Bud**—Rosebud, as she is inevitably called—a girl of enchanting prettiness and capricious whimsy. The other pupils regard her with a romantic fascination, for it is known that she is bound by her guardian’s will to marry a chosen husband when he comes of age. Miss Twinkleton affects to lament this fate, shaking her head over the poor doomed child—though the girls, unconvinced, whisper behind her back: *"O, what a pretending old thing Miss Twinkleton is!"*

The house is never in such a flutter as when **Edwin Drood**, Rosa’s betrothed, comes to call. At the sound of the gate-bell, every young lady who can contrive an excuse to peer from a window does so, while those at their lessons play out of time, and the French class dissolves into giggles.

On this particular afternoon, Edwin arrives, and Rosa is summoned. She descends, a vision of youth and reluctant obligation, to meet him in Miss Twinkleton’s parlor—a room adorned with globes, as if to remind visitors that even in private, the headmistress remains ever the scholar.

Their reunion is less than tender.

*"O, it’s so ridiculous!"* Rosa protests, hiding her face behind a little silk apron.

*"Don’t come nearer. It’s absurd—all of it. Being an engaged orphan, the girls spying, the servants scurrying about like mice—it’s all too absurd!"*

Edwin, half-amused, half-exasperated, endures her whims with dwindling patience. She teases him about his newly shorn hair, about imaginary rivals, about the prospect of their future in Egypt—where he, an engineer, dreams of grand projects, while she mocks the very thought of pyramids and Arabs.

Their walk through the Close is punctuated by bickering, by Rosa’s playful malice and Edwin’s mounting irritation. Yet beneath the sparring lies a shared unease—a sense that their betrothal is less a bond of affection than a duty neither quite knows how to escape.

*"We should both have done better,"* Rosa says suddenly, serious for once, *"if What is to be had been left What might have been."*

For a moment, the discord between them softens. But the cathedral organ swells, its solemn notes a reminder of deeper currents beneath their shallow quarrels. Edwin thinks of another voice—perhaps that of his friend Jack—while Rosa, uneasy, urges him to take her back before the choir emerges.

At the gate of the Nuns’ House, they part—Rosa sticky-fingered from sweets, refusing a kiss but blowing one into his palm instead.

*"Now, tell me,"* she asks, peering into his hand as if it were an oracle’s pool, *"what do you see?"*

*"A happy future?"* he ventures.

But neither of them, in truth, sees happiness in the present—only the weight of an obligation they do not yet know how to bear. The gate closes. One steps inside, the other walks away. And the old town of Cloisterham watches, silent as ever.

If one were to accept the jackass as the emblem of self-satisfied ignorance—a tradition perhaps more conventional than just—then the purest specimen in all of Cloisterham would be Mr. Thomas Sapsea, Auctioneer.

Mr. Sapsea affects the bearing of the Dean; has been mistaken for that dignitary in passing; has even been addressed as "My Lord" by those who supposed him the Bishop making an unannounced visit. This pleases him immensely, as does the sonorous quality of his voice and the grandeur of his manner. He has been known, while auctioning property, to adopt a faintly ecclesiastical intonation, as if bestowing a blessing upon the gathered bidders—an effect far surpassing the modest demeanor of the actual Dean.

He is a man of many admirers, widely regarded (even by his detractors) as a credit to Cloisterham. Portentous and dull, he moves with a deliberate roll in his gait and a measured cadence in his speech, his hands often drifting in a gesture suggestive of benediction. Nearing sixty, with a well-fed silhouette and a waistcoat strained by horizontal creases, he is reputed to be wealthy, votes respectably, and considers himself the pinnacle of human progress. How could such a man not be an ornament to society?

His premises stand on the High Street, opposite the Nuns’ House—a building of similar antiquity, though haphazardly modernized over generations. Above the door hangs a wooden effigy of his father, wigged and toga-clad, frozen in the act of auctioneering. The craftsmanship is admired, particularly the delicate rendering of the little finger gripping the gavel.

Within, Mr. Sapsea occupies a dim sitting-room overlooking a paved yard and a railed garden. A bottle of port rests before the fire—an indulgence on this crisp autumn evening—flanked by his portrait, his eight-day clock, and his barometer. These objects, he feels, assert his mastery over time, weather, and mortality.

A scrap of manuscript lies on the table. He reads it silently, paces, then recites from memory with solemn emphasis—though only the word *"Ethelinda"* escapes audibly. Three clean wineglasses stand ready.

His maid announces, *"Mr. Jasper is come, sir."*

Two glasses are claimed.

*"An honor to receive you,"* declares Sapsea, swelling with hospitality.

*"The honor is mine,"* Jasper replies.

*"You flatter me. Few merit such a compliment."* His tone implies: *You cannot fathom the privilege of my company.*

They drink. Sapsea toasts: *"When the French come over, may we meet them at Dover!"*—a relic of his youth, still dear to him.

*"You are a man of the world, Mr. Sapsea,"* Jasper observes.

*"I have some small acquaintance with it,"* Sapsea chuckles.

*"Foreign lands may not have known my feet,"* Sapsea proclaims, *"but they have known my eye. A French clock? ‘Paris!’ Chinese porcelain? ‘Canton!’ Sandalwood? ‘The Indies!’ I have identified artifacts from the Arctic to the Nile—once even an Esquimaux spear, which I catalogued over sherry."*

*"Remarkable,"* Jasper murmurs.

The conversation turns to the late Mrs. Sapsea.

*"Before seeking your opinion on this trifling composition,"* Sapsea says, lifting the manuscript, *"I must describe her character."*

Jasper feigns interest.

*"Six years ago, seeking a companion for my elevated mind, I observed Miss Brobity—proprietress of a rival establishment—frequenting my auctions. Gossip alleged she admired my oratory. In time, my phrasing seeped into her pupils’ dictation exercises. Some vulgarian even objected, but who would dare scorn *me*?"*

Jasper nods.

*"She revered intellect. When I proposed, she gasped, ‘O Thou!’—meaning myself—and could utter no more. We married. Yet she never found words equal to her admiration. To the last, she addressed me in fragments."*

Jasper murmurs sympathy.

*"Such is fate,"* Jasper sighs.

Sapsea presents his epitaph draft:

> Reverential Wife of
> Auctioneer, Valuer, Estate Agent, &c.
> Whose Knowledge of the World, Though Extensive,
> Never Revealed a Spirit More Devoted.
> Ask Thyself—**CANST THOU EQUAL THIS?**

Jasper applauds. *"Masterful."*

*"Durdles is come, sir!"* interrupts the maid.

The stonemason enters—a grizzled figure, dusted with the pallor of his trade. Known for his intimacy with crypts and catacombs, he inspects the epitaph with professional detachment.

*"It’ll fit,"* he declares, measuring with his two-foot rule.

Sapsea entrusts him with the monument key. Durdles pockets it among others, along with his bundled dinner.

*"You’re a walking vault,"* Jasper remarks, amused.

*"Keys to my own work,"* Durdles grunts. *"Not that they’re often needed."*

As the evening wanes, Sapsea prevails upon Jasper for backgammon and pontification. The visitor endures both with practiced courtesy before taking his leave—though not without a promise of future audiences, to Sapsea’s profound satisfaction.

Thus the chapter closes, leaving the auctioneer to bask in the afterglow of his own wisdom.

John Jasper cut through the Close, the cathedral’s shadow stretching long and skeletal in the moonlight. Then he stopped. There, propped against the iron railing of the burial ground like a misplaced tombstone, was Durdles—the stonemason, dinner-bundle under his arm—while a ragged, gap-toothed demon of a boy pelted him with rocks.

The stones found their mark more often than not, but Durdles didn’t flinch. The boy, however, celebrated each hit with a shrill whistle through his broken teeth, and each miss with a howl of *"Mulled agin!"* before adjusting his aim with vicious precision.

Jasper stepped into the light. "What are you doing to that man?"

"Making a cock-shy of him," the boy sneered.

"Give me those stones."

"I’ll give ’em to you—down your throat," the boy spat, twisting free. "Try takin’ me and I’ll smash yer eye in."

Jasper’s lip curled. "What’s he done to deserve this?"

"He won’t go home."

"And that’s your business?"

The boy grinned. "Gives me a ’apenny to pelt him home if I catch him out past ten." Then, with a grotesque little jig, he chanted:

The last word was a signal. Another stone flew.

Jasper beckoned the boy with a cold tilt of his head and crossed to Durdles, who stood like a monument among monuments.

"You know this creature?" Jasper asked, gesturing to the boy.

"Deputy," Durdles grunted.

"That’s his name?"

"All of us at the Travellers’ Twopenny are called Deputy," the boy cut in. "When the lodgers are snorin’, I come out for my ’elth." He retreated, took aim, and resumed his chant.

Jasper’s voice snapped like a whip. "Throw that stone and I’ll kill you." He turned to Durdles. "Walk with me. Need help with your bundle?"

"Never." Durdles adjusted his load. "Was just contemplatin’ my works. Like a poet among his verses." He gestured grandly at the graves. "Your brother-in-law—Mrs. Sapsea—late incumbent—departed taxes—all safe and sound under my hand. The common folk? Bundled quick and forgotten quicker."

Behind them, Deputy lurked like a shadow.

"He’s following," Jasper noted.

Durdles turned with drunken gravity. Deputy darted into the road, wary.

"You didn’t cry *Widdy Warning* first," Durdles accused.

"Liar, I did!" Deputy shot back.

Durdles, just as suddenly placated, muttered, "Own brother to Peter the Wild Boy, he was. But I gave him purpose."

"Purpose?" Jasper arched a brow.

"Before, he just broke things. Now he earns his keep—three penn’orth a week—stonin’ me home. Keeps competitors off, too."

Jasper chuckled. "No rivals?"

"Plenty. He stones ’em all away."

As they walked, Deputy trailed, pelting every wall and post with methodical malice.

"Found anything new in the crypt?" Jasper asked.

"Old things, you mean," Durdles corrected. "There’s a crook under the seventh pillar. Them old bishops must’ve clashed mitres regular in them narrow passages."

Jasper studied Durdles—mortar-dusted, stone-gritted—as if the man were a relic himself. "Yours is a curious life."

"Yours ain’t dull either," Durdles returned.

Jasper smiled faintly. "I’ve half a mind to apprentice under you. Learn the secrets of the crypt."

Durdles shrugged. "Find me when you want me."

Jasper pressed on. "What fascinates me is how you *know*—where each body lies."

Durdles halted, shifting his bundle. "Hand me my hammer."

Clink. The tool changed hands.

"Listen," Durdles said, tapping the pavement. "Solid. Solid. Then—hollow. Solid in hollow, hollow in solid. There’s your coffin, crumbled to dust."

"Astonishing."

Durdles unspooled his two-foot rule. Deputy crept closer, eyes gleaming at the prospect of treasure.

"Six foot inside this wall," Durdles intoned, "lies Mrs. Sapsea. Or near enough. Durdles never misses."

Jasper murmured, "A gift."

"Earned, not given," Durdles growled. He spun on Deputy. "Catch this ha’penny and vanish."

"*Warning!*" Deputy screeched, snatching the coin.

They crossed the skeletal remains of the monastery’s vineyard to the Travellers’ Twopenny—a sagging, warped husk of a building, its windows patched with greasy red rags. A lantern proclaimed its purpose.

A pack of boys—Deputy’s kin or rivals—materialized, stones already flying.

"Brutes!" Jasper snarled, shielding his face.

Durdles observed dryly, "They lack an object."

At the lane’s end, Jasper turned, furious—just in time for a stone to ricochet off his hat. A distant crow echoed: *"Wake-Cock! Warning!"*

He left the savagery behind.

Durdles stumbled into his yard, nearly plunging headfirst into an open grave. Jasper slipped back to his gatehouse, the fire still murmuring in the grate.

From a locked cupboard, he drew a peculiar pipe. Not tobacco—something darker. With delicate precision, he prepared it, then climbed the narrow stairs.

Two rooms. Two lights.

In one, his nephew slept, untroubled. Jasper watched him, pipe cold in hand, eyes unreadable. Then, silent as a shadow, he retreated to his own chamber.

The pipe flared to life.

And the midnight spectres came whispering.

The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle—so named because six fragile brothers before him had flickered out like brief candle flames—stood before his looking-glass, fists raised in a pugilist’s stance. Dawn had barely broken over Cloisterham Weir, where he had already invigorated himself by cracking the morning ice with his forehead. Now, he danced lightly on his toes, feinting and jabbing at his reflection with the precision of a seasoned boxer, his face alight with benevolent vigor.

Mrs. Crisparkle, his mother—not his wife, for the Minor Canon remained unwed—watched from the doorway, her sharp eyes tracking his movements. "One of these mornings, Sept," she remarked, "you’ll shatter the glass or your own skull."

"Neither, God willing," he laughed, ducking an imaginary blow. "Watch this, Ma—wind like a hurricane!" He concluded his routine with a flourish, narrowly avoiding her lace cap as if it were a prized trophy in the ring.

Breakfast was a quiet ritual. The urn hissed; the old lady murmured the Lord’s Prayer while her son bowed his head, as he had done since boyhood. She was as trim and bright as a porcelain figurine, her dress a delicate harmony of lavender and cherry ribbons. To Septimus, nothing was lovelier—save perhaps a young maiden—than his mother in her morning radiance.

Minor Canon Corner was a refuge of stillness, nestled in the cathedral’s shadow. The caw of rooks, the distant organ’s drone—these only deepened the hush. Centuries of tumult—warriors, serfs, monks—had faded, leaving behind red-brick walls softened by time, ivy-clad lattices, and orchards where monkish trees still bore fruit.

A letter arrived with the tea. Mrs. Crisparkle, proud of her keen sight, read it aloud while her son pretended to fumble with spectacles—a charade to humor her.

"From Mr. Honeythunder, of course," she said, lips pursed.

"Of course," Septimus sighed.

The missive announced the impending arrival of Neville and Helena Landless, wards of the bombastic philanthropist. Neville would lodge with the Crisparkles; Helena would enroll at the Nuns’ House. Honeythunder’s tone was as subtle as a hammer—*compliance assured, whether they like it or not.*

"Philanthopists," muttered Septimus, "always seizing folk by the scruff and *improving* them."

To ease the newcomers’ introduction, he proposed a dinner—Edwin Drood (Jasper’s affable nephew), Jasper himself, Miss Twinkleton, and the ethereal Rosa Bud. Eight place settings, no more—the table could bear no further strain.

When the omnibus from London lumbered into Cloisterham’s back lanes—where stray dogs and disreputable traffic lurked—it bore not only the Landless siblings but their self-appointed guardian. Mr. Honeythunder dominated the box seat, elbows squared, voice booming as if addressing a rally.

"Brotherhood!" he thundered at the driver, who recoiled as though struck.

Neville and Helena emerged—dark-eyed, lithe, with an air of untamed grace. They moved like creatures half-wild, half-hunted, their gazes flickering between defiance and wariness.

Honeythunder, meanwhile, bulldozed through the streets, preaching his grand design: jailing the unemployed until they embraced philanthropy. His presence at dinner was a calamity. Plates passed over his head; conversations drowned beneath his diatribes. He harangued Septimus as though the meek Minor Canon were a villainous opponent, reducing the gathering to a quivering silence.

*Unaccountable*, the night whispers. *Unaccountable indeed.*

The Nuns’ House was alive with the restless energy of impending holiday. The term’s end loomed, and with it, the usual unraveling of decorum. Bedrooms had transformed into clandestine supper clubs, where scissors carved tongues in place of knives, and curl-paper plates bore illicit marmalade. The air buzzed with whispers—of golden youths soon to call, of secrets traded for ribbons and worn-out shoes. Even Miss Ferdinand, ever the rebel, had met her muffled fate beneath a pillow after a spirited solo on comb-and-paper.

Yet amid the chaos, Rosa sat apart, untouched by the giddiness. She knew these rituals well—the sleepless vigils before departure, the dramatic farewells—but her mind was elsewhere. Helena’s silence on Edwin’s name had left a hollow space, a question unanswered. Why the avoidance, when reconciliation was promised?

Edwin, too, walked with heavier steps. Grewgious’s words had stirred a dormant conscience. The ring in his pocket weighed more than gold—it was a pledge, a responsibility. He could no longer drift toward matrimony as if carried by the tide.

They met on a crisp afternoon, frost glittering like shattered glass underfoot. Rosa, breathless with resolve, was the first to speak.

And so they were.

The words came haltingly at first, then in a rush—confessions of doubt, of quiet unhappiness, of a bond that had been more habit than heart. There were tears, but no bitterness. Something softer settled between them, a gentler understanding.

*"Let us be brother and sister,"* Rosa murmured. *"It’s kinder."*

Edwin agreed. The relief was palpable, yet beneath it lurked another unease—Jack. How would he take the news? The thought of his friend’s wounded devotion made Edwin hesitate.

*"Leave it to my guardian,"* Rosa suggested. *"He’ll know how to speak to him."*

Edwin seized the idea gratefully. Grewgious, steady as stone, would soften the blow.

They walked on, the river murmuring beside them, the sky bleeding into dusk. At the cathedral elms, where they had once dreamed childish dreams, they paused.

A kiss, not of lovers, but of friends parting. As she turned toward home, Rosa’s gaze lingered—a silent plea. *Do you understand?*

But Edwin was already stepping back, vanishing into the gathering dark.

Behind them, unseen, Jack watched. And the wind carried the first whispers of a storm yet to come.

The streets of Cloisterham wore a muted festivity on Christmas Eve. Strangers passed through, their faces unfamiliar, while others—once children of the city, now grown—returned from distant lives to find the place diminished, as though time had worn it thin. To them, the chime of the Cathedral clock and the cawing of rooks from its tower were echoes of a half-remembered childhood. Some, in their final hours far from home, would dream of autumn leaves scattered across the Close, the rustle and scent of youth returning as life’s circle closed.

Signs of the season were everywhere. Red berries glowed in the windows of Minor Canon Corner; Mr. and Mrs. Tope tucked sprigs of holly into the Cathedral’s carvings as if adorning the coats of the Dean and Chapter. The shops brimmed with excess—currants, raisins, spices, candied peel—while a greengrocer’s doorway sagged under the weight of a great bunch of mistletoe. A sad little Twelfth Cake, its Harlequin crumbling at the edges, sat in a pastry shop, awaiting raffle. Public amusements promised diversion: a waxwork exhibit, once the marvel of the Emperor of China, now occupied a bankrupt livery stable, and the Theatre boasted a new pantomime, heralded by a garish portrait of Signor Jacksonini the clown, grinning as if to mask his own misery.

Yet the High School stood empty, its scholars scattered, each nursing a secret infatuation with one of Miss Twinkleton’s young ladies (who remained blissfully unaware). The Nuns’ House, too, was quiet, its handmaidens flitting occasionally to the windows, bolder in their solitude than when under the watchful eyes of their betters.

Three were to meet at the gatehouse that night. How did each pass the day?

**Neville Landless** worked steadily in his room, though freed from study by Mr. Crisparkle’s holiday leniency. By afternoon, he had cleared his desk, burned stray papers, and packed a knapsack with spare clothes and sturdy shoes. A new walking stick, heavy and iron-shod, lay ready. When Mr. Crisparkle met him on the stairs and tested the stick’s weight, Neville admitted he knew little of such things—he had chosen it for heft, not finesse.

“Too heavy,” the Minor Canon declared, demonstrating its proper use. “You balance, not lean.”

Neville smiled. “I’ll learn. I’ve not lived in walking country.”

They parted with cheerful ease, though Neville’s mood darkened as evening fell. At the Nuns’ House, he waited at the gate—honoring his promise to avoid Rosa—and soon Helena joined him. As they walked, he confessed his unrest, the way his presence seemed to unsettle others. A fortnight’s solitary travel, he hoped, would steady him.

“Mr. Crisparkle approves?” Helena asked.

“Entirely.”

Yet as the city lights kindled around them, Neville’s spirits sank. “I wish I weren’t going to this dinner.”

“It will be over soon,” she reassured him.

“Soon,” he echoed, bleakly.

At the gatehouse, he hesitated twice before entering. Then, as the Cathedral clock chimed the quarter, he turned sharply and went inside.

**Edwin Drood** wandered the city alone, weighed by unspoken regrets. The ghost of what might have been with Rosa lingered, though pride still flickered at the memory of Helena’s striking figure. Rosa’s parting look—sharp, questioning—troubled him. Had she glimpsed beneath his careless surface?

He paused at a jeweler’s, where the man pressed a bridal bracelet upon him, then a signet ring—“gentlemen often choose these when changing their condition.” Edwin declined, but the jeweler’s mention of Jasper’s recent visit gave him pause. *Even my jewelry is known to him*, he mused. *Dear old Jack—he notices everything.*

Dusk found him in the Monks’ Vineyard, where a ragged woman crouched by a gate. Her sunken eyes fixed blankly ahead until he spoke.

“Lost?” he asked.

Her gaze sharpened, then a film seemed to pass over her. She coughed—a dry, rattling sound—and claimed to seek work, though her true trade was opium. When Edwin gave her coins, she clutched them greedily.

“What’s your name?” she croaked.

“Edwin.”

She repeated it drowsily, then asked, “Do sweethearts call you Eddy?”

He stiffened. “I’ve none to call me anything.”

As she shuffled away, she called back: “Be thankful it’s not Ned. A bad name now—a threatened name.”

The words clung to him, ominous as the rising wind. He resolved to tell Jasper—*only as coincidence*, of course.

**John Jasper** moved through the day with eerie serenity. Freed from lessons, he attended to the Cathedral service, his voice soaring with unearthly precision. The Anthem flowed flawlessly, each note measured, controlled. Even Mr. Crisparkle remarked on his composure.

“You seem wonderfully well,” the Minor Canon said.

“I am,” Jasper replied, a black silk scarf loose at his throat.

Later, as they walked, Jasper confessed: “I’ve been dwelling on trifles—exaggerating. When the year ends, I’ll burn my diary and start anew.”

Mr. Crisparkle beamed. “This is better than I hoped.”

And so, as night fell, each climbed the gatehouse stair—Neville reluctant, Edwin uneasy, Jasper untroubled—to a meeting that would bind their fates tighter than any of them knew.

Neville Landless had set out before dawn, walking briskly until the distant chime of Cloisterham’s church bells marked the morning service. By then, hunger gnawed at him, and he paused at a roadside tavern—The Tilted Wagon—where the promise of hospitality proved as crooked as its namesake. The fire smoked sullenly, the bread wept crumbs, and the landlady scolded a squalling child between slapping rashers onto a griddle. Neville ate without complaint, though the air clung damp to his bones.

Resuming his march, he hesitated at a fork: the high road, or a cart track veering across the heath? He chose the latter, its ruts gripping his boots like grudging hands. Soon, footsteps echoed behind him—too many, too deliberate. He stepped aside, letting four figures pass, only to find four more lagging at his heels. When he halted, they halted.

"Thieves?" he challenged, grip tightening on his stick.

"Quiet suits you better," one muttered.

Neville’s laugh was sharp. "Advice from shadows?" He lunged forward; the largest man intercepted him. They grappled, rolling through gorse until Neville’s stick cracked against flesh. Blood flecked both their faces before the man pinned him, knee to chest.

"Enough!" the victor panted, dragging Neville upright. "We’re no thieves—just hired to fetch you. Quiet now, or it goes harder."

Bound by grim escorts, Neville was paraded to the high road, where a cluster of men parted to reveal Jasper, wild-eyed, and Crisparkle, grave as a judge.

"Where is my nephew?" Jasper demanded.

Neville’s confusion curdled to dread. "Edwin? I left him at midnight—"

"Liar!" Jasper seized Neville’s stick, thrusting its bloodied tip at Crisparkle. "See his guilt!"

The crowd growled. Crisparkle intervened, steadying Neville. "Speak carefully. You walked with Edwin by the river?"

"Yes, for ten minutes. Then we parted at his door."

Jasper’s finger jabbed at Neville’s stained coat. "And this? His blood?"

"Mine—and his!" Neville pointed to his captor, whose split lip corroborated the brawl. Yet the mob’s murmurs thickened, their eyes black with accusation.

To Cloisterham they marched him, past gawping faces, straight to Mayor Sapsea’s parlour. There, Crisparkle pleaded reason while Jasper spun theories like spider’s silk—each thread a noose for Neville.

"Drag the river," Jasper urged. "Scour the banks. My boy lies somewhere, cold and forsaken!" His voice broke, but his gaze never wavered from Neville, whose protests drowned in the tide of suspicion.

By dusk, torches bobbed along the water’s edge. Nets trawled the depths; mud-slick men probed reeds and shallows. Jasper toiled among them, sleeves rolled to the elbow, his fervor chilling even the hardened searchers.

No corpse surfaced.

Exhausted, Jasper slumped homeward—only to find Grewgious waiting, lips pursed.

"Strange news," the lawyer began. "Your nephew and my ward… they dissolved their betrothal. By mutual consent."

Jasper’s hands clenched the chair. "When?"

"The very night he vanished. Edwin meant to tell you himself, but—"

A howl tore from Jasper’s throat. He collapsed, writhing, as Grewgious watched, unblinking. The fire crackled, indifferent to the ruin sprawled before it.

When John Jasper awoke from his unconscious state, he found himself attended by Mr. and Mrs. Tope. His visitor—stern and unyielding—sat rigidly in a chair, hands resting on his knees, observing Jasper’s slow return to awareness.

“There now, sir,” fretted Mrs. Tope, dabbing her eyes, “you’ve given us quite the fright! Exhaustion, plain as day.”

“A man,” intoned Mr. Grewgious with his usual detached precision, “cannot endure shattered rest, a tormented mind, and bodily strain without consequence.”

“I fear I’ve troubled you,” Jasper murmured weakly as they helped him into his chair.

“Not at all,” Grewgious replied stiffly. “Consideration is unnecessary.”

Mrs. Tope fussed over him. “You’ll take wine, sir, and the jelly you refused earlier—just as I warned! And a slice of the fowl that’s been kept warm for you.” She turned to Grewgious. “You’ll stay and see him eat?”

Grewgious responded with an ambiguous grunt.

Jasper ate ravenously, though without savor—fueling himself against despair rather than hunger. Grewgious remained statue-like, his expression unreadable, as if silently declining any conversation.

When the meal was done, Jasper leaned back, pensive. “Do you know,” he began slowly, “that I find a sliver of hope in your dreadful news?”

Grewgious arched a brow. “Do you?”

“If Edwin—knowing your message would reach me—anticipated my conclusions, might he have fled to spare himself the shame?” Jasper’s voice quickened. “It does not lessen my grief, but it absolves him of cruelty toward her.”

Grewgious conceded with a nod.

Jasper pressed on, feverish. “If he foresaw my reasoning, then his disappearance is not an act of malice but desperation! The very secrecy that wounded me now suggests he lives!”

Mr. Crisparkle entered then. Jasper repeated his theory eagerly.

Crisparkle, though wary, found logic in it. “The timing aligns. His strained relations could have driven him to vanish.”

“Precisely!” Jasper’s eyes burned with fragile hope. “I accused Neville Landless too hastily. My suspicions blinded me.”

Crisparkle shifted uncomfortably. He had withheld Neville’s volatile history, but Jasper’s candor shamed him. Steeling himself, he confessed Neville’s past fury and jealousy—details that cast darker shadows.

Jasper paled but clung to his newfound hope. “Even so, I will believe Edwin chose flight over disgrace.”

Yet fate intervened. Crisparkle, restless, walked to Cloisterham Weir. Under cold starlight, the water’s rush filled his ears—until a glint caught his eye. At dawn, he returned. There, wedged in the timbers, was Edwin’s gold watch.

Dread pooled in his chest. He dove into the frigid current, retrieving the watch and a sodden shirt-pin. The truth was inescapable: Edwin had not fled. He had been stripped of identifying marks and discarded.

The discoveries ignited a storm. Neville, already suspect, was arrested. The watch, last wound by Edwin himself, had stopped before being tossed into the river. Neville’s threats, his unexplained movements, his destroyed papers—all damned him.

But with no body, the law released him. The town’s wrath did not. Neville was exiled, his name blackened.

Jasper’s hope crumbled. That night, he scrawled in his diary:

*"My boy was murdered. The watch and pin are proof. I abandon false hope. I swear now: I will hunt his killer relentlessly, silently, until I hold justice in my hands. Let the guilty tremble."*

Then, hollow-eyed, he returned to the choir—a man possessed.

Half a year had slipped by like shadows at dusk when Mr. Crisparkle found himself waiting in the dim offices of the Haven of Philanthropy, biding his turn to speak with the formidable Mr. Honeythunder.

The place hummed with a peculiar energy—less like a sanctuary of benevolence and more like a training ground for moral pugilists. The men who strode through its corridors carried themselves with the swagger of prizefighters, their brows heavy with the certainty of their own righteousness. They moved as if spoiling for a fight, not with fists but with words, ready to pounce upon any dissent. Mr. Crisparkle, who had once frequented the boxing rings of his youth, could not help but note the resemblance. The philanthropists shared the same bullish necks, the same aggressive set of the jaw. Yet where the fighters had discipline, these men had only dogma; where the pugilists had honor, these had only self-importance.

When his name was called at last, a harried underling—poorly paid and poorly treated—led him to Mr. Honeythunder’s sanctum. The man sat like a judge, his massive frame dwarfing the desk before him, his voice booming before Crisparkle had even taken his seat.

Mr. Crisparkle did so, unruffled.

Honeythunder finished scrawling his signature across a stack of circulars—demands, not requests, for funds—before fixing him with a glower. **“We disagree, you and I, on the sacredness of human life.”**

**“Do we?”** Crisparkle replied mildly.

**“Then enlighten me,”** said the Minor Canon. **“What are your views?”**

Honeythunder’s face darkened. **“You tell me.”**

**“If we differ, you must have some notion of my stance,”** Crisparkle pressed. **“What is it?”**

**“A young man,”** Honeythunder thundered, **“struck down by violence. What do you call that?”**

**“Then we agree,”** sneered Honeythunder, though his tone suggested the opposite. **“Yet you defend Neville Landless.”**

**“Convenient,”** came the retort. **“And profitable, perhaps?”**

The implication hung in the air like a foul odor. Crisparkle’s patience, usually unshakable, thinned.

**“Mr. Honeythunder,”** he said, voice low but sharp, **“you mistake decency for complicity. You mistake doubt for guilt. And worst of all, you mistake bullying for righteousness.”**

The philanthropist bristled, but Crisparkle continued.

Honeythunder’s face purpled. **“You dare—”**

**“I dare,”** Crisparkle cut in, rising, **“to call hypocrisy by its name. Good day.”**

He left with the brisk step of a man unburdened by doubt, though his heart was heavy.

---

Later, in the dim attic rooms of Neville Landless, the air was thick with the scent of old paper and quiet determination. The young man, worn but resolute, looked up from his books as Crisparkle entered.

**“You’re working hard,”** the clergyman observed.

**“I must.”** Neville’s voice was steady, but his eyes burned with something between resolve and desperation.

Crisparkle studied him—the pallor of confinement, the feverish brightness of his gaze. **“You should walk in the sun.”**

Neville’s fingers tightened on the edge of his desk. **“The sun feels too harsh these days. The night is kinder.”**

**“Nor will strutting through the streets like a man without a stain.”** Neville exhaled. **“I am marked, Mr. Crisparkle. Every glance is a verdict.”**

The clergyman placed a hand on his shoulder. **“Then let them look. And let them see innocence, not shame.”**

For a moment, Neville wavered. Then, slowly, he nodded.

---

As dusk settled, Crisparkle made his way to Grewgious’s chambers, where the angular lawyer sat like a mantis lying in wait.

**“How fares our mutual friend?”** Grewgious inquired, gaze fixed somewhere beyond the window.

Grewgious’s eyes flicked toward the street below. **“Not for long, it seems.”**

A figure moved in the shadows—a familiar silhouette, stiff with purpose.

Jasper had come to London.

And he did not look pleased.

The quiet streets of Cloisterham were stirred by the arrival of a stranger—a man of striking appearance, with a shock of thick white hair and dark, expressive eyebrows. He carried himself with a certain military bearing, his blue surtout buttoned snugly over a buff waistcoat, his gray trousers neatly pressed. Yet his manner was anything but rigid; there was an air of leisure about him, as though time were a luxury he could afford to squander.

At the Crozier—the town’s most respectable inn—he announced himself as Mr. Dick Datchery, a gentleman of independent means with no particular occupation save the enjoyment of life. He made this declaration to the idle waiter (for business was seldom brisk at the Crozier) while awaiting his dinner—a fried sole, a veal cutlet, and a pint of sherry.

His hat, when examined at his request, bore the name *Datchery* inside the band. "Now you know me," he said cheerfully, hanging it back on its peg. "And what I’m after is lodging—something old, something with character. Preferably something inconvenient."

The waiter, though confident in Cloisterham’s abundance of inconvenient lodgings, hesitated at the word *architectural*. But upon Mr. Datchery’s clarification—"Anything cathedraly?"—he brightened. "Ah! Then you’ll want Mr. Tope, the Verger."

Thus, after his meal, Mr. Datchery set out in search of Mrs. Tope’s lodgings, guided only by vague directions and the occasional glimpse of the cathedral tower. His wanderings led him to a patch of burial ground, where a small tormentor was stoning a hapless sheep.

"Let it be!" Datchery called.

The boy—Deputy, as he later introduced himself—merely jeered before being bribed into service as an impromptu guide. He pointed out not only the Tope residence but also, with a wary glance, the dwelling of one *Jarsper*—a name that earned a second, sharper look from Mr. Datchery.

The lodgings, when found, were precisely as advertised: ancient, dim, and delightfully impractical. The low-ceilinged rooms, carved inelegantly from thick stone walls, were cool as crypts. Yet Mr. Datchery was charmed. The rent was modest, the location ideal, and the inconvenience thoroughly picturesque.

There was but one condition—a reference from Mr. Jasper, the cathedral’s choirmaster, who occupied the adjacent gatehouse. Mrs. Tope spoke of him in hushed tones—*a poor dear gentleman, very solitary, very sad*—and hinted at *last winter’s trouble*. Datchery professed only a vague recollection of the event, his memory, he claimed, being muddled by the general human tendency to dispose of one another with alarming frequency.

Jasper, when applied to, provided the necessary endorsement, as did the Mayor, Mr. Sapsea, who happened to be present. The latter, flattered by Datchery’s deferential manner—*His Honour the Mayor* this, *His Honour the Mayor* that—expanded under the attention, discoursing at length on Cloisterham’s virtues, its constitutional dignity, and the long, strong arm of the law.

"And that gentleman we left," Datchery ventured, "Mr. Jasper—am I right in thinking he suffered a grievous loss?"

"His nephew," Sapsea confirmed. "And though suspicion is near certainty, justice must be built stone by stone."

"Ah," murmured Datchery. "Immoral certainty—how true."

Their stroll concluded with an admiring tour of the cathedral (Sapsea pointing out its features as though he had laid each stone himself) and an accidental pause beside Mrs. Sapsea’s epitaph—a composition the Mayor admitted, with false modesty, to being his own.

They parted with ceremony, Datchery’s hat still tucked under his arm, his white hair streaming behind him like a banner.

That evening, regarding his reflection in the Crozier’s coffee-room mirror, he gave his hair a thoughtful shake. "For a man of leisure," he remarked to himself, "I’ve had a surprisingly busy day."

The Nuns’ House stands quiet now, the young ladies dispersed to their homes after Miss Twinkleton’s farewell speech, its halls emptied of laughter and chatter. Helena Landless has gone, her presence withdrawn like a cooling shadow in the summer heat, leaving Rosa Bud alone in the stifling stillness.

Cloisterham basks in the golden languor of late summer. The cathedral walls, usually so stern, seem to glow from within, as if the stone itself has softened under the weight of the sun. The gardens sag with ripe fruit, and the air hums with the drowsy murmur of insects. Dusty wayfarers, wanderers between haymaking and harvest, lounge on doorsteps, nursing battered shoes and weary feet. The city watches them with wary eyes, eager for them to move on, to take their restlessness elsewhere.

On such an afternoon, when the cathedral bells have fallen silent and the High Street slips into shade, Rosa is startled by the announcement that Mr. Jasper wishes to see her. The timing could not be worse—Helena is gone, Mrs. Tisher away, Miss Twinkleton picnicking with a veal pie in tow.

*Why did you say I was at home?* Rosa thinks desperately, but the maid only replies that Jasper did not ask—he *knew.*

Her breath catches. She cannot bear the thought of being shut indoors with him. The garden, then—open, visible, where she might scream if need be. With trembling hands, she sets her hat on her arm and steps out.

There he stands, leaning against the sun-dial, his mourning clothes stark against the green. The sight of him roots her to the spot, that old, dreadful pull tightening around her. She wants to flee, but her feet betray her, carrying her forward until she sits, stiff and small, beside the dial.

He does not touch her, though she feels the intention like a breath against her skin. His voice is smooth, rehearsed.

“I have waited,” he begins, “to resume my duty near you.”

*Duty.* The word curdles in the air.

“I no longer study music,” she says, voice thin.

“Discontinued, not abandoned, surely?”

“Never again.”

A flicker of something dark crosses his face. “You could have done no more if you had loved my nephew as you ought.”

“I *did* love him!” she flares, then regrets it instantly. Anger pleases him—she sees it in the way his gaze sharpens.

“Not as he deserved,” Jasper murmurs. “Not as *I* would have loved you.”

The admission hangs between them, poison in the honeyed air. Rosa shrinks back, but he does not move, his composure a mask over something ravenous.

“You are mistaken,” she says, rallying. “I want nothing from you—no lessons, no words, *nothing.*”

His smile is a knife. “Yet here you sit, listening. How curious.”

She stands, but his voice stops her—soft, lethal.

“If you leave now, harm will follow. Not to you. To others.”

*Helena.* The name flashes through her mind. She freezes.

“Ah,” he murmurs, seeing her fear. “You understand. Young Landless, perhaps? A rival, once. A suspect, still.”

“You lie,” she whispers.

“Do I? The noose tightens slowly, Rosa. A word from me, a nudge—circumstances can condemn even an innocent man.”

Her pulse hammers in her throat. “What do you want?”

“You.” The word is a caress, a threat. “Your hatred, your fear—I will take any part of you, so long as it is *mine.*”

She tries to rise again, but his voice pins her.

“A sign, Rosa. A flicker of your hand to show you hear me. Or the storm breaks.”

Trembling, she obeys.

He smiles. “Good. Remember—silence, or the blow falls. And Rosa?” He leans closer, his breath hot at her ear. “No matter where you run, I will follow. To the death.”

Then he is gone, tipping his hat as if this were any ordinary parting.

Rosa sways, the world tilting. The maids find her crumpled on the stairs, her skin cold as the coming thunder rumbles in the distance.

*The storm,* they murmur. *No wonder the poor dear fainted.*

But Rosa knows the truth.

The storm has already come.

Rosa’s senses returned to her with a jolt, and with them came the full, crushing weight of what had just transpired. The confrontation replayed in her mind with terrifying clarity, as though even her faint had not granted her a moment’s respite from his presence. One thought burned brighter than all others: she must flee. But where? And how?

She had confided her fear of Jasper to no one but Helena. If she sought refuge with her now, would it not invite the very catastrophe he had threatened? His power was real; his malice, undeniable. The more she dwelled on his words—his veiled threats, his obsession—the more certain she became that any misstep on her part could doom Neville.

For months, a dreadful suspicion had gnawed at her, rising and sinking like a specter in the depths of her mind. Jasper’s fervent devotion to Edwin in life, his relentless pursuit of justice (or vengeance?) after the disappearance—these were the actions of a grieving uncle, were they not? The town saw no darkness in him. Only she recoiled.

Had she conjured this monstrous thought from her own revulsion? Or was there something more?

She forced herself to recall his words by the sun-dial. He had insisted on murder, aligning himself with public opinion. Yet if he were guilty, would he not have encouraged the theory of voluntary flight? He had spoken of sacrifice—of laying six months of labor at her feet. Were these the words of a man feigning grief?

But then, his face as he leaned close—the hunger in his eyes—the way he had said, *"I could have swept even him from your side."*

A shudder wracked her. Reason warred with instinct, and instinct won. He was dangerous. She had to escape.

---

The note to Miss Twinkleton was brief: a sudden need to see her guardian, all was well. She stuffed a few necessities into a tiny bag, left the letter where it would be seen, and slipped out into the High Street—her first time alone in its winding lanes. Heart pounding, she hurried to the omnibus stop just as it was departing.

"Wait, Joe! To London, please!"

In moments, she was aboard, rattling toward the railway station under Joe’s bewildered but dutiful care. He handed her into the carriage with the gravity of a man delivering a royal treasure, her pitiful bag treated as though it were a trunk of gold.

"Tell Miss Twinkleton you saw me off safely?" she asked.

"Aye, Miss. And your love?"

"Yes—" She almost smiled at his earnestness, but the city loomed ahead, and doubt crept in. Was this madness? What would Mr. Grewgious say? What if he were not there? The further the train carried her, the more the enormity of her rashness pressed upon her.

---

London swallowed her whole—gritty, sweltering, alive with the shuffle of weary feet on scorching stone. The cab ride was a blur of shabby streets and stifling air until, at last, it halted before a grim, shuttered gate in Staple Inn.

"Mr. Grewgious?" she asked the watchman, who pointed her deeper into the gloom.

Upstairs, she found her guardian at an open window, lamp shaded, his angular frame silhouetted against the dim light. At her tentative knock, he turned—and gasped.

"Good Heaven!"

Rosa flung herself into his arms, and for a heartbeat, he stiffened—then clutched her tight. "My child! I thought you were—" He caught himself. "What has happened? Who brought you here?"

"No one. I came alone."

"Alone!" His shock melted into alarm. "Why did you not write? Why—"

"Eddy’s uncle," she choked out, trembling. "He—he made love to me. I cannot bear it. You must protect me—protect us all from him!"

For a breath, Mr. Grewgious stood frozen. Then, to her astonishment, he erupted.

"Damn him!" he roared, pacing like a man possessed. "Confound his politics! Frustrate his knavish tricks!" The outburst spent, he wiped his brow, sheepish. "Forgive me. Now—refreshments. What have you eaten? What shall you eat?"

His flustered kindness was a balm. He knelt to help remove her hat, his touch unexpectedly gentle for so awkward a man.

---

Over tea, he coaxed the full story from her, his frown deepening at every detail. When she finished, he stared into the middle distance, then guided her to the window.

"See there? Their rooms." His voice was grim. "Shall you go to Helena tomorrow?"

"May I?"

"Let me sleep on it." He escorted her to Furnival’s Inn with comical solemnity, surrendering her to the care of the "Unlimited head chambermaid." Her room was bright, comfortable—a haven.

As she thanked him, he waved it off, though his eyes gleamed with pleasure. "Stairs are fire-proof," he assured her, misreading her relief. "And I live across the way."

At the gate, he pressed a coin into the porter’s hand. "If she sends for me in the night, a crown for the messenger."

Long after, Rosa would learn he had paced outside the iron bars for an hour, peering up as if fearing his caged dove might yet fall into the jaws of lions.

The night passed peacefully, and Rosa awoke refreshed. At ten o'clock, Mr. Grewgious was joined by an unexpected visitor—Mr. Crisparkle, who had arrived straight from Cloisterham as if emerging from the river itself.

"Miss Twinkleton was deeply unsettled by your note," he explained to Rosa, "and came to me in such distress that I took the first morning train to see you. Though I wish you had sought me directly, perhaps it was wiser to come to your guardian instead."

"I did think of you," Rosa admitted, "but Minor Canon Corner was so near—"

"Naturally," Mr. Crisparkle agreed.

Mr. Grewgious interposed, "I've shared everything you told me last night with Mr. Crisparkle. Had he not come, I would have written to him at once. But his timing is fortuitous."

Rosa turned to them both. "Have you decided what's to be done for Helena and her brother?"

Mr. Crisparkle sighed. "I confess I'm at a loss. If even Mr. Grewgious, with his sharper mind and a night's head start, remains undecided, what hope have I?"

Just then, the Unlimited maid announced a gentleman asking for Mr. Crisparkle.

Rosa tensed. "Is he dark?"

"No, miss—more brown."

"Not black-haired?"

"Quite certain. Brown hair, blue eyes."

Mr. Grewgious, ever cautious, suggested, "Perhaps it's best to see him, reverend sir. In times of uncertainty, one shouldn't dismiss any path that might open."

With Rosa's permission, the gentleman entered—apologizing for the intrusion before posing an unexpected question:

"Who am I?"

Mr. Crisparkle studied the sunburnt face before him, and slowly, recognition dawned.

"Tartar!" he exclaimed.

The two clasped hands with startling warmth, then—uncharacteristically for Englishmen—clapped each other on the shoulders, grinning like boys.

"My old fag!" cried Crisparkle.

"My old master!" returned Tartar.

"You saved me from drowning!"

"And then you took to swimming like a fish!"

Their laughter filled the room as they recounted the tale: Tartar, the smallest junior, hauling Crisparkle—a hefty senior—from the water by his hair.

Mr. Grewgious, seizing the moment, extended a hand. "An honor, sir! I trust you took no chill from your heroics?" His words tumbled awkwardly, but his intent was clear.

Rosa marveled. If only such courage had been there for her mother...

Then Mr. Grewgious, pacing abruptly, declared, "I have an idea!" He turned to Tartar. "You reside in the top set beside Mr. Landless, correct?"

"Indeed."

"And you've spoken with him?"

"Briefly. I offered to share my rooftop garden."

Grewgious clapped his hands. "Then here's my scheme."

He outlined it carefully: Rosa, under the escort of Crisparkle and Tartar, would visit Tartar's chambers. From his garden, she could signal Helena without arousing suspicion from any spies lurking near Neville's rooms.

Tartar readily agreed. "My home is yours."

Rosa hesitated. "We impose too much—"

"Not at all," Tartar assured her. "I’d count it a privilege."

Blushing, she excused herself to fetch her hat while Crisparkle briefed Tartar on Neville's troubles.

Soon, they set out—Tartar offering his arm to Rosa, Crisparkle walking ahead.

*Poor Eddy,* Rosa thought, glancing at Tartar’s strong, sun-weathered hand. *Not so rugged when he saved Mr. Crisparkle, but steady all the same.*

Tartar spoke of his years at sea.

"When do you sail again?" Rosa asked.

"Never."

She wondered what her schoolmates would say, seeing her now. The passersby must think her a mere wisp beside him—a man who could lift her effortlessly from danger.

As they ascended to his rooftop garden, she felt as though she’d stepped into a fairy tale—a hidden world bursting into sudden bloom. And in that moment, she forgot everything else.

The chambers belonging to Lieutenant Tartar were a marvel of order—so immaculate that one might suspect London’s soot had surrendered entirely, banished from his domain forever. Every inch of brass gleamed like a looking-glass; not a speck of dust dared linger on any surface, large or small. His sitting-room resembled an admiral’s cabin, his bath-room was as spotless as a dairy, and his sleeping quarters—lined with lockers and drawers—might have been mistaken for a botanist’s curated collection. Even his cot swayed faintly, as if breathing in rhythm with the sea.

Every object in Tartar’s possession had its designated place: maps here, books there, brushes and boots each in their own precise nook. Nothing was out of reach; shelves, hooks, and drawers were arranged so meticulously that even a misplaced salt-spoon would stand out like a trespasser. His curiosities, gathered from distant voyages—stuffed birds, coral relics, polished shells—were displayed with the care of a museum curator. It was as though varnish lurked just out of sight, ready to erase any stray fingerprint that dared mar the perfection.

On this fine summer day, Tartar had rigged an awning over his small flower-garden with a sailor’s precision, lending the whole scene the air of a ship’s deck. One could almost imagine the entire setup sailing away, should its master raise the speaking-trumpet hanging nearby and bellow orders to weigh anchor.

Tartar himself was as harmonious with the scene as the tide with the moon. There was a cheerful earnestness in his manner, an unpretentious joy in his own fastidiousness that made even his quirks endearing. Rosa, who had been given a tour with all the ceremony befitting a visiting dignitary, found it impossible not to admire him—especially when, with a gallant wave, he ceded his admiral’s cabin to her as if she were its rightful sovereign.

"Helena! Helena Landless!"

A pause, then a second face appeared beside the flowers. "Rosa? Is that you?"

"Yes! Oh, how did you get here?"

"I—I’m not entirely sure," Rosa admitted, blushing. "Unless I’m dreaming!"

Helena laughed. "If this were a dream, I’d expect far stranger things. How have we managed to meet like this, of all places?"

Rosa quickly explained, her words tumbling out in a rush. "And Mr. Crisparkle is here too—can you believe he was once saved by Mr. Tartar?"

Helena’s expression warmed. "I’d believe any good of Mr. Crisparkle."

(Another blush—what mysterious fruit grew in this enchanted garden?)

"Not Crisparkle," Rosa corrected. "It was Tartar who saved him."

Helena’s dark eyes fixed on Rosa thoughtfully. "Is Mr. Tartar here now?"

"No, he’s given up his rooms to us. It’s like living inside a ship—or a dream."

Helena’s smile was faint, almost melancholy. After a pause, she said, "Neville’s reading in his room. The sun’s too bright on that side just now. Perhaps it’s best he doesn’t know you’re nearby."

"Oh, I agree!" Rosa said hastily.

Helena hesitated. "He’ll have to learn of it eventually, but... perhaps not yet. Would you ask Mr. Crisparkle what he thinks?"

Rosa vanished briefly into the cabin (now repurposed as her retreat) and returned with Crisparkle’s blessing: Helena should use her own judgment.

"If Neville is being watched," Helena mused, "and if the goal is to isolate him, then his enemy might try to warn others away from him. If Mr. Tartar were to visit openly and often, we might draw out some reaction—some clue."

Rosa’s eyes lit up. "Oh! So we could catch them in the act!"

She darted back to consult Crisparkle, who fetched Tartar himself. The lieutenant, earnest as ever, pledged to begin his friendly surveillance at once.

Helena thanked him warmly, then turned back to Rosa. "We should part now, before Neville notices. Are you returning to Miss Twinkleton’s?"

Rosa shuddered. "Never! After that awful scene? I couldn’t."

"Then where will you go?"

"I—I haven’t decided yet," Rosa admitted. "But my guardian will arrange something."

(It was, admittedly, a vague plan.)

"You’ll send word through Mr. Tartar?" Helena asked.

Rosa nodded, flustered again. But before they parted, she caught Helena’s hand. "Tell me you’re sure—sure I couldn’t have stopped him from becoming so cruel. I had to refuse him, didn’t I?"

Helena’s grip tightened. "I’d sooner see you dead at his feet than bound to him."

Relieved, Rosa watched her friend depart—until a sun-browned hand reached through the foliage to guide her back inside.

Later, in Mr. Grewgious’s chambers, the question loomed: What was to be done with Rosa?

"A furnished lodging," Grewgious declared. "And perhaps Miss Twinkleton could chaperone you for a month."

Rosa agreed, and they set out—though Grewgious’s method of inspecting lodgings involved much staring from across the street and very little actual entry. At last, he recalled a widowed cousin of his clerk’s: one Mrs. Billickin of Southampton Street.

Mrs. Billickin was a woman of theatrical faintness and brutal honesty. She disclosed, at length, every flaw in her property: the rattling slates, the suspect piping, the inevitable damp.

"The stairs," she warned gravely, "are not for the faint of heart."

Still, the rooms were clean, the price fair, and Grewgious—after exchanging a wary glance with Rosa—agreed to terms. Mrs. Billickin, however, refused to sign her full name.

"Billickin suffices," she said, clutching her shawl. "Let the riff-raff wonder if Billickin is man or woman, near or far. Mystery is my protection."

And so, with a baronial flourish, "BILLICKIN" was scrawled on the agreement.

As they departed, they encountered Tartar once more, who—with the tide in his favor—proposed a river excursion.

"I haven’t been on the Thames in years," Grewgious admitted.

"I’ve never been at all," said Rosa.

And with that, the matter was settled.

John Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle moved beneath the same vaulted arches daily, yet neither spoke of Edwin Drood—not since that silent exchange, half a year past, when Jasper had shown the Minor Canon his diary’s grim resolution. Their encounters were a dance of unspoken suspicion: the choirmaster, inscrutable as a tomb effigy; the clergyman, open as daylight yet barred from the truth by Jasper’s steeled reticence.

Jasper lived a paradox—harmonizing voices in the choir while his soul hummed a dissonant tune. He walked among men like a ghost, his purpose a lodestone pointing only to vengeance. Had Rosa’s flight shaken him? Did he fear she had whispered his threats to Crisparkle? The Minor Canon turned the questions like a key in a stubborn lock, but the mechanism would not yield.

*Crime* to love Rosa? No. *Crime* to plead love over vengeance? Not in any moral ledger Crisparkle kept. Yet the shadow clinging to Jasper—the one that had struck horror into Rosa—found no purchase in the clergyman’s mind. Neville and Helena, too, if they harbored doubts, gave them no voice. Even Grewgious, whose dislike for Jasper festered openly, never traced its roots to murder.

Cloisterham drowsed over the old scandal like a cat over stale milk. Was Edwin slain in treachery or fair struggle? Had he vanished by his own design? The town yawned, noted Jasper’s undiminished hunt, then dozed again.

---

The cathedral’s great doors groaned shut. Jasper, granted brief leave, turned toward London. The same soot-choked streets Rosa had fled through received him now. He carried little—a bag light as a sinner’s conscience—and took lodgings near Aldersgate Street, a place neither hotel nor hovel but something shuffling between.

He ate without tasting, then prowled eastward through narrowing alleys until the walls seemed to lean in, whispering. A broken stair led to a door; behind it, darkness and a voice like rusted hinges:

“Alone, deary! Worse for me, better for you.” A match flared. The woman’s face swam into view—a crumpled leaf of a face, eyes sharp as pins. “Ah! It’s *you*.”

“Surprised?”

“Thought you’d gone to Heaven. Or Hell.” She wheezed laughter. “Mourning clothes? Who died?”

“A relative.”

“Of what?”

“Death.”

Her grin faltered. “Short-tempered tonight, ain’t we? Needs a smoke to sweeten.” She prepared the pipe with ritual care—thimble, spoon, the slow dance of her hands.

Jasper lay back, the pipe’s stem between his teeth. “Is it as it was?”

“Same as ever.”

“No. Weaker.”

“You’re stronger, that’s all.”

He exhaled, eyes half-lidded. “Suppose… you had a thing to do. Not decided. Might do, might not. Would you rehearse it here?”

“Over and over,” she nodded, stirring the bowl.

“I did. A thousand times. A *million*.” His voice frayed. “A journey—dangerous. Look down!” He jerked upright, pointing at nothing. “See what waits below?”

She watched him, unblinking. “That’s the trip you’ve been on.”

His laugh was a blade on stone. “Yes. The *journey*.”

Silence. The candle guttered.

“Always the same way?” she prodded.

“Always.”

“Same pleasure in the telling?”

“Yes.”

She tilted her head. “Never tired of it?”

He surged up, teeth bared. “What else would I want?”

She pressed him back, coaxing. “Course, course. You came for the journey. I see now.”

“*It was one!*” he snarled—then slumped, spent.

The woman crouched closer, a spider testing threads. “Had a fellow traveler, didn’t ye?”

Jasper’s laugh was a shriek. “Oh, he walked with me—never knew it!”

The pipe slipped from his fingers. She caught it, watching as his breaths slowed to the drag of a dredged river.

---

Dawn crept in, gray as a jailer’s palm. Jasper woke stiff-necked, the taste of ash in his mouth. He paid the woman—her blessing chased him downstairs—but she was at his heels before the street swallowed him.

She tracked him to a house behind Aldersgate, waited hours, then slipped inside when he left.

“The gentleman from Cloisterham—when does he return?”

“By six.”

Her smile was a razor-cut.

---

That evening, Cloisterham’s High Street cradled her shadow. Nine o’clock chimed; the omnibus groaned in. Jasper stepped down, and she followed, a wraith with a predator’s patience.

He vanished under an archway. She darted after—found only a stair and a gray-haired man at a desk.

“Who d’you seek?”

“A gentleman just passed.”

“Mr. Jasper. Sings in the choir.”

Her eyes gleamed. “Where’s that?”

He pointed to the cathedral’s hulk. “There. Seven tomorrow.”

She thanked him, already turning.

The man—Datchery—ambled beside her, rattling coins. “Need lodging?”

Her fingers twitched at the sound. “A poor soul, I am. A little silver for my cough…”

He eyed her. “Opium, is it?”

Her grin was answer enough.

---

*Now*, she thought, watching Jasper’s window. *Now we dance.*
