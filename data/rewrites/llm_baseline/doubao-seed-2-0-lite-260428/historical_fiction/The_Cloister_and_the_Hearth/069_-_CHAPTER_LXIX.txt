Within the rough-hewn stone guest chamber of a Dominican convent lay a lone stranger, his frame wracked by successive, violent bouts of nausea that had finally ebbed, leaving him so weak he could barely lift his lids—just as Margaret had lain, helpless and fevered, that terrible night back in the Dutch lowlands. A massive oak fire blazed on the wide hearth, and beside its glowing embers hung the patient’s water-soaked clothing. A giant of a friar sat by his bedside, reading pious collects aloud from his breviary, the soft rolling Latin cutting through the crackle of flame. The stranger, Gerard, eyed him fitfully, seeming to listen at intervals, while at others he would squeeze his eyes shut and let out faint, ragged moans.

The monk knelt then, pressing his forehead flat to the cold stone floor, and prayed long and earnestly for the man on the cot. When he rose, he bade him farewell. “Day breaks over the city walls,” he said. “I must go prepare for matins.”
“Good Father Jerome, before you leave—how came I to be here?”
“By the hand of Heaven. You flung away God’s precious gift of life, and He reached out to give it back to you. Mark this: you have tested the world, and found only its bitter gall. Now test the Church. The Church is peace. Pax vobiscum.”
He was gone. Gerard lay back, drifting through confused, wondering thoughts, until his exhausted frame gave way and he slipped into a shallow doze.

When he woke, a new nurse sat beside his bed: a layman, whose small, restless eyes were a world away from Friar Jerome’s calm, majestic gaze. The man leaned in, eager to ask after his health.
“Very, very weak,” Gerard whispered. “Messer, have I not seen you somewhere before?”
“I hope you hold no grudge for my gauntlet blow!” the other replied, with no small measure of anxiety. “I had no choice but to strike you, or the both of us would be fathoms deep in the Tiber right now.”

Gerard stared at him, bewildered. “You are the one who saved me? How?”
“Ah, signor, I was on the Tiber banks that night on an errand I need not name. You came straight up to me, begging me drive my dagger through your heart. But before I could oblige you—ay, even as you spoke—I recognized you for the young gentleman who dived into a wild North Sea storm to save my wife Teresa and our baby from drowning.”
“Teresa’s husband?” Gerard breathed, his face draining of color. “And you are an assassin?”
“At your service,” the man shrugged. “Now, Ser Gerard, the next thing I knew you’d hurled yourself straight into the Tiber, screaming at me to keep back. Any other man on God’s earth, I’d have let him drown. I hold no love for the rest of the world, let them all hang on one rope or sink in the same river for all I care. But when you went under, crying out the name ‘Margaret’? My own heart screamed ‘Teresa’ right back. How could I ever look my wife in the face again if I let the man who saved her die the very same death she almost perished in? So I dived in after you, and lucky for both of us I swim as easy as a duck. You, determined to meet your end, grabbed at me to drag us both down, so I swam clear, and—forgive me for the rudeness—clouted you hard on the nape of the neck with my steel gauntlet till you lost your senses. The current was strong, and it was all I could do to drag your limp, water-heavy body to the bank. I slung you over my back, heading for my own hovel, thinking Teresa would nurse you back and be pleased I’d done a good turn for once. But not far from this monastery, that giant holy friar met us, asked what had befallen you, and recognized your face straight away. Said his brethren had heard tales of the young clerk from Holland, that the convent had far better warmth, food and care than my cottage could ever offer. We struck a deal: I get to come visit you once a day, no questions asked. That is how you came to be here.”

The man the world called a cutthroat fixed Gerard with a strange, soft look of sincere affection and interest. Gerard did not meet his gaze. He felt a cold prickle down his spine, as if a venomous snake had curled up at the foot of his bed. He shut his eyes tight.
“Ah, you would sleep,” the assassin said eagerly, taking the hint. He tiptoed for the door, promising to return every day.

Gerard lay still, eyes closed, not sleeping, his mind reeling. He had tried to throw his life away, and Heaven had sent a hired killer to fish him out of the river? He had cursed God, spat at the world, defied every holy truth he had ever been raised to believe, and instead of striking him down, the Almighty had snatched him back from the brink. He shuddered, thinking of all his recent blasphemies. He tried to pray, and found the words would form on his tongue, but they could not reach his heart. “I am doomed eternally,” he sobbed to himself. “Doomed, doomed.”

Then the rich, solemn harmony of the convent’s organ burst through the stone walls, followed by the voices of the choir lifting in full chant. One voice rose above all the rest, clear and pure, a boy’s tone so sweet and angelic it seemed to climb straight to the heavens. He closed his eyes and listened, and all the soft, unburdened days of his own boyhood came flooding back to him. No earthly rot there, no fierce, tearing passion that rends and corrupts the soul. Only peace: soft, balmy, gentle peace. “It is true,” he sighed, tears spilling down his cheeks. “The Church is peace of mind. Till I left her bosom I never knew sorrow, nor sin.”

And through the blur of his tears, a familiar face leaned over him: one he had never thought to see again in Rome. It was Father Anselm, the old friar who had tended his wounded body back to health in Juliers. The good father had only arrived at this convent two nights prior. Gerard cried out, reaching for him.
“Oh, Father Anselm! You cured my broken body in Juliers—now cure my hurt soul in Rome! Alas, I fear you cannot.”
Anselm sat beside the cot, laying a gentle hand on his fevered head, and calmed him with soft, soothing words. He already knew the full story of how Gerard had been pulled from the Tiber, and spoke to him with kind but steady gravity, helping him see the weight of his despairing act, urging him to turn to repentance, and give thanks to the Divine Power that had thwarted his suicidal will to save his soul.
“Come, my son,” he said. “Purge the load from your breast.”
“Ah father,” Gerard wept. “In Juliers I could confess, for then I was innocent. Now, I am an impious monster, I dare not speak my sins aloud to you.”
“Why not, my son? Do you think I have not sinned deeply against Heaven in my own youth? Oh, how deeply! Come, poor laden soul, pour out your grief, pour out all your faults, hold nothing back. Do not let hidden sins oppress and crush you.”

Before long, Gerard was kneeling at Father Anselm’s feet, confessing every last one of his sins, heaving with sobs and groans of unfeigned penitence.
“Your sins are great,” Anselm said gently, when he had finished. “But your temptation was far greater, terribly, cruelly great. I must go consult our good prior on a fitting penance for you.” He kissed Gerard’s forehead, and left the room.

And lo—Gerard could pray now. He prayed with all his heart. The phase his tormented mind now passed through could be summed in a single word: Penitence. He turned away from the thought of the world with terror and deep aversion, begging passionately to be allowed to stay within the convent walls. To him, raised in the shelter of the Church from boyhood, it felt like a wounded, worn bird flying back to its soft, safe nest. He entered his novitiate, and spent his days in prayer, quiet mortification, pious reading, and meditation.

Meanwhile, Princess Claelia’s spy returned to her palace, and reported that Gerard was most certainly dead, drowned in the Tiber, his body yet to be recovered. She went utterly still, stunned, as if the words had struck her a physical blow. When she finally found breath to speak, it was to bemoan her own cursed luck, to have been served by such overzealous brutes. “So soon,” she sighed. “See how swiftly these monsters rush to do ill deeds. They creep to us in our hot blood, tempt us with their venal daggers, then carry out the very mortal acts we would never have dreamed of on our own.”

Within hours, her pity for Gerard and her burning hatred for his killer Lodovico had risen to a fever pitch. “Poor soul, I cannot call you back to life. But the traitor who slew you shall not live another day.” She posted armed men hidden in the antechamber of her palace, ordering them to wait for Lodovico to arrive to collect his payment, then fall on him and hack him to pieces. “Strike for his head,” she commanded. “He wears a secret coat of mail beneath his tunic, and if he leaves that room alive, your own heads will answer for it.” And so she sat, weeping for the young man she had wronged, pulling the strings of her vengeance to shed a second man’s blood for having been the unwitting tool of her cruel, foolish whim.
