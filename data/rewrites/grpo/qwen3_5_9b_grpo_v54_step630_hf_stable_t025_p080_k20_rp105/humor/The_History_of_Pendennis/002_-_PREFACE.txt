If this collection of scribbles, representing two years of my rather chaotic output, fails to dazzle you with artistic brilliance—which it almost certainly does, given the laws of physics and literature alike—it at least boasts a certain rugged honesty that a polished masterpiece might sacrifice. By forcing myself into a constant, gossipy dialogue with you, the reader, I am compelled to speak my mind exactly as it rattles around in my skull. I see every typo, every hasty phrase, and every printer's error that I would dearly love to erase, but alas, the ink is dry. It is a sort of intimate, confessional chat between us, one that is liable to be dull, repetitive, and occasionally embarrassing. In my endless chatter, I inevitably expose my own pettiness, my vanity, and my peculiar quirks. Just as you judge a man's character not by a single outburst or a particularly sunny Tuesday, but by the general tenor of his life, so too should you judge a writer who throws himself at your feet without a filter. The question remains: Is he honest? Does he strive for the truth, or is he merely a charlatan mouthing empty sentiments for applause? Am I chasing popularity with cheap tricks? I cannot ignore the good fortune that has landed me thousands of readers far beyond my wildest dreams. I have no right to demand you stay awake or praise my craft, but I do beg you to believe that the person writing this strives to tell the truth. Without that, there is absolutely nothing here worth your time.

Perhaps those who crave "excitement" wish to know that this book originally began with a meticulously plotted scheme that was promptly discarded. Ladies and gentlemen, you were initially promised a thrilling saga of high-stakes horror. Imagine a ruffian (a man of surprising virtues, mind you) lurking in the slums of St. Giles, constantly pursued by a debutante from Belgravia! Think of the electric contrast between high society and the gutter, the clash of slang and polite parlance, the daring escapes, the fistfights, and the potential for murder! Indeed, as late as nine o'clock this morning, my poor friend Colonel Altamont was scheduled for execution, and I only spared him when he appeared at the window, looking rather undignified.

However, this "thriller" plan was abandoned—with the very noble restraint of my publishers—because, upon attempting it, I discovered I lacked the requisite expertise. Having never been close enough to a convict to smell his breath, and finding the manners of gaol-birds utterly alien to me, I decided that competing with the great M. Eugène Sue was a fool's errand. To describe a real scoundrel, one must paint him so horrifically that he becomes too ugly to display; unless an artist paints a villain fairly, I maintain they have no right to show him at all.

Even the gentlemen of our age—this is an attempt to sketch one such man, neither better nor worse than the average educated soul—we cannot reveal as they truly are, with their notorious selfishness and foibles. Since the burial of the author of *Tom Jones*, no fiction writer among us has been permitted to depict a MAN to his fullest power. We must drape him in fine clothes and force him to wear a conventional, smiling mask. Society simply will not tolerate the "Natural" in our art. Many ladies have chided me, and some subscribers have fled, simply because, in the course of the narrative, I dared to describe a young man resisting temptation and feeling its pull.

My true objective was to suggest that he possessed the passions to feel deeply, yet the manliness and generosity to overcome them. You will not hear—and it is best you know this—what actually moves in the real world, what transpires in clubs, colleges, and mess rooms; what is the genuine life and talk of your sons. A little more frankness than is customary has been attempted in this story. With no malicious intent on my part, and hoping for no ill consequences for any reader, I offer it to you. If truth is not always pleasant, at any rate, it is the best thing we have. From whatever chair a grave thinker argues, or from the humble seat where a storyteller concludes his labor and bids his kind reader farewell, the message remains the same: Speak the truth.

KENSINGTON, Nov. 26th, 1850.
