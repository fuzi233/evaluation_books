I awoke the next morning with a renewed sense of courage and refreshed spirits. The physical weakness that had previously clouded my judgment had vanished; my mind felt sharp, alert, and clear. As I finished dressing, a sharp knock echoed at the door. "Come in," I called out, expecting the chambermaid. Instead, a rough-looking man strode in. "Give me your keys, Meess," he demanded.

"Why?" I asked.

"Give!" he snapped impatiently. Snatching the keys from my hand, he added, "All right! Have your trunk soon."

Fortunately, everything turned out as he predicted. He was from the customs office. Unsure where to find breakfast, I hesitated before descending the stairs. Only now, stripped of my exhaustion, did I realize that this establishment was not merely an inn, but a grand hotel. As I slowly made my way down the broad staircase, pausing on each step because I had no real urge to hurry, I gazed upward at the soaring ceilings, admired the painted walls, and marveled at the vast windows flooding the space with light. I stepped on veined marble floors—uncarpeted and slightly dusty—which contrasted sharply with the tiny, sparsely furnished closet assigned to me as a bedroom. This disparity plunged me into a philosophical reverie.

I wondered at the keen insight displayed by the waiters and chambermaids in assessing their guests. How could they instantly discern that I was someone of no social standing, carrying little wealth? They certainly knew; within moments of seeing me, they seemed to calculate my worth at roughly the same negligible fraction. The observation was curious and heavy, yet I managed to maintain my composure despite the weight of it.

Finally reaching the great hall, bathed in the glare of skylights, I navigated my way to the coffee room. Entering, I trembled slightly, feeling uncertain, solitary, and wretched. I wished desperately to know if I was acting rightly or wrongly, convinced it was my final stand, yet powerless to stop myself. Adopting the calm demeanor of a fatalist, I seated myself at a small table. Soon, a waiter delivered my breakfast, which I consumed in a state of mind ill-suited for digestion.

Many others were eating at separate tables. I would have felt happier had I spotted a single woman among them, but there wasn't one; everyone present was a man. Yet, no one seemed to view my presence as strange. Occasionally, a gentleman glanced my way, but no one stared rudely. Presumably, if my situation appeared eccentric, they attributed it to my label as an "Anglaise."

After breakfast, I faced another decision: where to go next? An inner voice urged, "Go to Villette." This impulse likely stemmed from a careless remark made earlier by Miss Fanshawe as she bid me farewell: *"I wish you would come to Madame Beck's; she has some marmots whom you might look after; she wants an English governess, or was wanting one two months ago."*

I knew nothing of who Madame Beck was or where she lived. I had asked, but my question went unheard as Miss Fanshawe hurried away. Assuming Villette was her residence, I decided to go there. The distance was forty miles. I knew I was grasping at straws, but lost in the vast, turbulent depths where I found myself, I would clutch at cobwebs if necessary.

Having arranged passage on a diligence, I departed with nothing but this faint outline—a shadow of a plan. Do not judge my rashness too quickly, dear reader. Consider where I began; consider the desert I had left behind. What little risk I undertook was akin to a game where one cannot lose and stands to gain everything. Though I lack an artistic temperament, I do possess the artist's ability to maximize present pleasure when it suits my taste. I enjoyed that day, despite the slow travel, the cold, and the rain. The landscape was barren, flat, and treeless, with slimy canals winding alongside the road like half-asleep green snakes, bordered by formal pollard willows lining fields tilled like garden plots. The sky was a monotonous gray, the air stagnant and humid. Yet, amidst these life-draining conditions, my imagination bloomed anew, and my heart warmed to the sunlight.

However, these feelings were constantly checked by a secret, relentless awareness of anxiety lurking nearby, waiting to devour my joy like a tiger coiled in the jungle. Its breath whispered in my ear; its fierce heart pounded against mine. It never shifted from its lair, yet I felt its presence; I knew it waited only for sunset to spring forth, ravenous.

I had hoped to reach Villette before nightfall, hoping to avoid the deeper confusion that often surrounds a lone traveler arriving at an unknown destination. But due to our sluggish progress, frequent stops, a thick fog, and a heavy, dense rain, darkness—almost tangible—had already settled over the city by the time we reached its outskirts.

We passed through a gate guarded by soldiers, visible only by lamplight, then rattled over a pavement of strangely rough, flinty stones. The diligence halted at a bureau, and passengers disembarked. My immediate task was to retrieve my trunk. It was a minor matter, yet crucial to me. Knowing it was best not to appear eager or demanding regarding luggage, I stood aside, watching quietly as other boxes were unloaded until I spotted mine. Then, I intended to claim it promptly.

One by one, packages were removed, lowered, and grabbed. I was certain my trunk should have been visible by now; it was not. I had tied a direction card to it with a strip of green ribbon so I could identify it instantly. Not a shred of green was to be seen. Every package was cleared away; every tin case and brown-paper parcel disappeared. The oilcloth cover was lifted, revealing that not a single umbrella, cloak, cane, hatbox, or bandbox remained. And my portmanteau, containing my few clothes and my pocketbook holding the remainder of my fifteen pounds—where was it?

I ask this question now, but I could not ask it then. I could say nothing. Possessing not a single phrase of *speaking* French, I was surrounded entirely by a babbling crowd speaking only French. *What* should I do?

Approaching the conductor, I placed my hand on his arm, pointed to a trunk, then to the roof of the carriage, and attempted to convey my query with my eyes. He misunderstood, seized the indicated trunk, and prepared to hoist it onto the vehicle.

"Leave that alone, will you?" a voice commanded in excellent English. Correcting himself immediately, he added, "Qu'est-ce que vous faîtes donc? Cette malle est à moi." (*What are you doing? That trunk belongs to me.*)

Hearing the familiar accent of the Fatherland brought me immense relief. Turning to the stranger, I appealed to him without noting his appearance in my distress: "Sir, I cannot speak French. May I beg you to ask this man what he has done with my trunk?"

Without initially distinguishing the face to which I looked, I sensed a mixture of surprise at my plea and doubt regarding the wisdom of interfering. "*Do* ask him; I would do the same for you," I insisted.

Whether he smiled, I do not know, but he responded in a gentlemanly tone—neither harsh nor terrifying—"What sort of trunk was yours?"

I described it, mentioning the green ribbon. Immediately, he took hold of the conductor, and through the ensuing storm of French, I felt him thoroughly interrogating the man. Moments later, he returned to me.

"The fellow claims he was overloaded and admits that after you saw it loaded, he removed your trunk and left it behind at Boue-Marine with other parcels. However, he promises to send it tomorrow; consequently, you will find it safe at this bureau the day after tomorrow."

"Thank you," I replied, but my heart sank.

Meanwhile, what should I do? Perhaps the English gentleman perceived the failure of courage in my expression. Kindly inquiring, "Do you have any friends in this city?"

"No, and I do not know where to go."

There was a brief pause. As he turned more fully toward the light of a lamp above, I saw he was a young, distinguished, and handsome man. He might have been a lord; nature had crafted him worthy of royalty. His face was exceedingly pleasant; he appeared noble yet not arrogant, manly yet not overbearing.

Turning away, acutely aware that I had no claim to expect further assistance from someone of his stature, I paused. "Was all your money in your trunk?" he asked, stopping me.

How grateful I was to be able to answer truthfully: "No. I have enough in my purse"—for I possessed nearly twenty francs—"to keep me at a quiet inn until the day after tomorrow. But I am quite a stranger in Villette and do not know the streets or the inns."

"I can provide the address of an inn suitable for you," he said. "It is not far away; with my directions, you will easily find it." Tearing a leaf from his pocketbook, he wrote a few words and handed it to me.

*I truly* thought him kind. To distrust him, his advice, or his directions would have been as foolish as doubting the Bible. There was goodness in his countenance and honor in his bright eyes.
