Half a year had passed since the events of the previous chapters. Mr. Crisparkle found himself sitting in a waiting room within the London headquarters of the "Haven of Philanthropy," enduring the wait for an audience with Mr. Honeythunder.

During his university years, Mr. Crisparkle had studied the art of boxing and attended several matches. Now, observing the men who claimed to practice the "Noble Art" of philanthropy, he noted a striking physical similarity between them and professional pugilists. Both groups possessed prominent forehead ridges and developed muscles indicative of a propensity to strike others. Several of these philanthropic professors moved about with an aggressive readiness, eager to engage in verbal sparring with anyone foolish enough to challenge them. The organization was preparing for a series of moral lectures on a rural tour, and various professors were vying for prominence, much like sports promoters betting on heavyweight champions. Among the officials, Mr. Crisparkle recognized a man in a black suit who resembled a former benefactor known as "Frosty-Faced Fogo," a man famous for managing similar spectacles in the past.

However, Mr. Crisparkle noted significant differences. First, these philanthropists were poorly trained; they were overweight and lacked the lean conditioning of boxers. Second, unlike the disciplined temper of fighters, these men resorted to abusive language. Third, their ethical code was dangerously flawed, permitting them to overwhelm opponents verbally, attack them when they were vulnerable, and use cruel tactics without remorse. In these specific areas of brutality, the philosophers of charity were far less noble than the actual boxers.

Lost in these comparisons, Mr. Crisparkle watched the bustling crowd, noting that everyone seemed intent on seizing something from someone else, offering nothing in return. Before he could fully process his thoughts, his name was called. A shabbily dressed, underpaid clerk led him to Mr. Honeythunder's office.

Inside, Mr. Honeythunder commanded, "Sit down," in a voice reminiscent of a strict schoolmaster addressing a troublesome pupil. After quickly signing a stack of circulars demanding donations from the poor, a clerk collected them and departed. Once alone, Mr. Honeythunder turned toward Mr. Crisparkle, arms crossed and brows furrowed, declaring, "We hold different views regarding the sanctity of human life, you and I."

"Do we?" asked the Minor Canon calmly.

"We certainly do," Honeythunder insisted. When pressed to define his own views, the philanthropist stated simply, "Human life is sacred." When asked for Mr. Crisparkle's definition, Honeythunder feigned ignorance, claiming it was best known to the Canon himself.

"You started by saying our views differed," Mr. Crisparkle reasoned gently. "Therefore, you must have assumed a position for me. What assumption have you made?"

Honeythunder gestured dramatically. "Consider this young man," he said, referring to the missing Edwin Drood, "swept from the earth by violence. How do you classify that?"

"Murder," replied Mr. Crisparkle.

"And the perpetrator?"

"A murderer," the Canon confirmed.

"I am pleased you agree," Honeythunder sneered, lowering his voice menacingly. "But I must explain why I find your stance unacceptable."

"Please proceed with your explanation," Mr. Crisparkle interrupted politely.

"No terms with Cain!" Honeythunder roared, invoking biblical imagery. "I reject the bloodstained hand offered to me!" Instead of reacting with the typical applause of a political rally, Mr. Crisparkle merely shifted his posture and waited patiently.

"The Ten Commandments forbid murder!" Honeythunder continued, treating the concept as if Mr. Crisparkle had suggested exceptions were permissible.

"They also forbid bearing false witness," Mr. Crisparkle reminded him.

"E-nough!" Honeythunder bellowed, his theatricality reaching a crescendo. He announced that since his wards were now adults, he was relieved of his guardianship and demanded the financial accounts Mr. Crisparkle had agreed to manage. With a final nod, he dismissed the Canon, wishing he were "better employed."

Mr. Crisparkle rose, composed despite the heat rising in his cheeks. "Whether I am better or worse employed is a matter of opinion," he remarked dryly. "You might think joining your society would improve my situation."

"It would have been preferable if you had done so sooner!" Honeythunder snapped.

"Or," the philanthropist continued, "perhaps a clergyman should devote himself to uncovering guilt rather than leaving that to laymen."

"My profession dictates a duty to the needy and the oppressed," Mr. Crisparkle countered firmly. "However, I have determined that my role is not to make empty professions. Regarding Mr. Neville and his sister, I assure you: I understood Mr. Neville's state of mind perfectly at the time of the incident. Without hiding the flaws in his character, I am certain his story is true. As long as I hold this conviction, I will defend him. To compromise this would be a shame I could not endure, regardless of the praise I might gain."

His demeanor was so humble and steadfast that Mr. Crisparkle felt a surge of admiration. He was a man of integrity, loyal to his principles in both major and minor matters.

"Then, who did commit the act?" Honeythunder demanded abruptly.

"Heaven forbid," Mr. Crisparkle replied, "that in clearing one man I should implicate another. I accuse no one."

"Tcha!" Honeythunder spat in disgust, revealing the hypocrisy of the organization. "And remember, you are not a disinterested witness."

"How so?"

"There was a stipend paid to you for your pupil," Honeythunder accused bluntly. "That financial interest may have clouded your judgment."

"Perhaps I expect to keep receiving it?" Mr. Crisparkle asked with a faint smile.

"Well, sir," Honeythunder stood up, stuffing his hands into his pockets, "I don't measure people for hats. If you find a hat fits you, wear it. That is your lookout, not mine."

Mr. Crisparkle felt a surge of righteous indignation. "Mr. Honeythunder, I hoped to avoid criticizing the intrusion of stagecraft into private conversation. But your behavior today provides ample cause. These methods are detestable. They violate Christian justice and gentlemanly restraint. You assume a grave crime occurred against an innocent man. Because I disagree with your conclusion, your immediate response is to accuse me of lacking moral outrage and aiding the criminal! Next, you might claim I represent a ridiculous delusion; I refuse to believe it, and you declare I believe nothing. Then, you discover 'war' is terrible and propose absurd solutions, only to accuse me of reveling in battlefield horrors. Finally, you seek to punish the sober for the sins of the drunk, labeling me a monster for opposing your measures. In every instance, your followers attribute base motives recklessly and quote biased statistics. Consequently, I view the platform as a harmful institution in public life, and an unbearable nuisance when introduced into private discourse."

"These are strong words, sir!" Honeythunder protested.

"I hope so," Mr. Crisparkle replied coolly. "Good morning."

He exited the building swiftly, soon settling into a brisk walk. A smile played on his lips as he imagined the hypothetical reaction of a porcelain shepherdess witnessing his victory over Mr. Honeythunder. Possessed of a harmless vanity, he believed he had delivered a sharp rebuke to the philanthropist's pretensions.

Mr. Crisparkle headed to Staple Inn, bypassing the offices of Mr. Grewgious, and climbed the creaking stairs to an attic room belonging to Neville Landless. The space was sparse, filled with books, and exuded an atmosphere of isolation. The room looked like a prison, with rusted grates and heavy beams, yet sunlight streamed through a small window onto a cracked parapet where sparrows hopped listlessly. Despite the gloom, the presence of books and the warm light in Mr. Crisparkle's eyes transformed the room into a sanctuary of learning.

"How fares it, Neville?" the Canon asked.

"I am in good spirits, Mr. Crisparkle, and working diligently," Neville replied.

"I wish your eyes were not so wide and bright," the Canon observed softly.

"They brighten at the sight of you," Neville smiled. "If you were to leave, they would dim."

"Cheer up, Neville! Fight for it!"

"If I were dying, your words would revive me," Neville confessed. "But I have rallied, and I am recovering well."

Turning Neville slightly toward the light, the Canon noted his pallor. "I desire to see more color in your cheeks, Neville. More sunshine."

Neville slumped slightly. "I am not yet hardy enough for that. I cannot bear the daylight. Have you walked those streets of Cloisterham as I have? Have you seen the averted eyes and the silent avoidance? People give me too much room to pass, as if afraid to touch me. It is unreasonable to expect me to walk in the day."

"My poor fellow," the Canon sympathized, holding Neville's hand. "I never considered it unreasonable. But I urge you to try."
