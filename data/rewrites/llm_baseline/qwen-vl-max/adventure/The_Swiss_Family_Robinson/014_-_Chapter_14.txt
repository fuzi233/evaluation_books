The rainy season had arrived with a vengeance—dark clouds rolled in, thunder rumbled across the sky, and heavy downpours turned our usual outdoor adventures into impossible dreams. With the storms came silence and stillness, and even inside our spacious home, where we had so many projects to keep us busy, time seemed to drag. The mood of the family grew heavy; even brief rides during lulls in the rain failed to lift our spirits.

Fritz, ever restless and full of ideas, broke the quiet one afternoon. “Father,” he said, “why don’t we build a canoe? Something light and fast—faster than our pinnace or the old boat. I dream of gliding over the water like a bird on the breeze.”

His idea sparked excitement among us all. But my wife, who always worried when we ventured onto the sea, quickly voiced her concern. “We already have two boats,” she said firmly. “And each one carries enough danger. Why add another craft that might tempt us out into the unpredictable waves?”

I understood her fear, but I reassured her. “This won’t be some fragile shell,” I said. “I’ll build it strong—inspired by the Greenlander’s cajack. It will be tough, safe, and built to last. And while we’re at it, it’ll give the boys something meaningful to do.”

She hesitated, but seeing the determination in my eyes, she finally relented.

Work began at once. We crafted the frame from whalebone, reinforcing the sides with split bamboo canes. A deck ran the full length of the boat, leaving only a square opening for the rider to sit. When completed, we covered the entire structure with seal-skin, sealing every seam with caoutchouc to make it completely watertight. The result was a curious-looking vessel—light enough to carry with one hand, yet sturdy enough to brave the open waters.

When we launched it into the bay, it bounced across the surface like a rubber ball. Fritz was declared its rightful owner, though his mother insisted on one condition: before he could use it, she would make him a swimming dress—just in case.

“Even the strongest boat can fail,” she said. “If it sinks, he must still have a chance.”

So together we designed a strange-looking garment—a double waistcoat made of linen treated with a rubber solution. The seams were sealed with caoutchouc, and a small valve allowed air to be pumped between the layers, inflating the suit like a buoyant bubble.

One bright afternoon, Fritz stood on the shore, fully dressed and inflated, grinning as his brothers roared with laughter. He stepped into the water and paddled easily across the bay toward Shark Island, where we followed in one of our larger boats.

The test was a success. Ernest, Jack, and Franz, still chuckling, begged their mother to make them one too.

On the island, we visited the animals we’d sheltered the previous autumn—the antelopes had used our hut, as their footprints showed. We left behind handfuls of maize and salt, then wandered along the shore, collecting shells and coral for the boys’ museum. Near the water lay a thick layer of colorful seaweed, which the boys gathered under their mother’s guidance, knowing it had uses beyond decoration.

But I noticed something odd—my wife carefully selected certain leaves with serrated edges, washing and drying them in the oven. Curious, I asked if they were meant to replace tobacco.

She smiled. “Not at all. Do you think I’d miss that smelly stuff?” Then, with a twinkle, she added, “A mattress filled with these might be cool in summer.”

I didn’t press further—yet my curiosity only deepened.

Later, after a long hike, we rested on the verandah when my wife appeared with a porcelain dish holding a translucent, shimmering jelly. “Would anyone care for some jelly?” she asked.

We took spoons and tasted it—cool, sweet, and delicately flavored. “Ambrosia!” Fritz exclaimed.

“It’s not just delicious,” she said, “it’s made from those very seaweed leaves.” She explained how, during her time in Cape Town, she’d seen this dish prepared. The leaves are soaked daily for a week, then boiled with orange juice, citron, and sugar. The result is a clear, refreshing gelatin.

We all praised her skill. The boys immediately wanted to return to the island to gather more.

On the next trip, we checked on our plantings on Whale Island. The rabbits had multiplied, nibbling at young shoots—except for the coconut palms, which they left untouched. To protect the trees, we built thorny hedges around their trunks.

I also noticed that some seaweed had been gnawed by the rabbits. Intrigued, I collected a sample to study later.

Nearby, the skeleton of the great whale stood bleached white by sun and wind. I saw potential in its vertebrae—strong, rounded bones perfect for building machinery. I gathered ten or twelve joints and dragged them back to the boat.

Back home, I began planning a crushing machine. Stone would be too hard to shape, but these whale bones could serve as rollers. First, I needed timber. I set out alone, choosing Storm as my mount, since the boys had taken off on their own hunting expedition.

Crossing the bridge, I found the cassava field ruined—trampled and stripped. Examining the tracks, I recognized pig prints. Following the trail, I soon heard barking from Floss and Bruno. They had cornered a wild sow and her litter, standing defiantly in the clearing.

I raised my gun and fired twice. Two pigs fell. The rest fled, chased by the dogs. I retrieved the game and continued to the forest, where I marked a suitable tree for my project.

Ernest stayed behind and helped me skin the pigs. I gave the meat to my wife to prepare for supper, hoping the others would return soon.

That evening, the sound of hooves echoed through the trees. Jack burst in on his ostrich, followed by Fritz and Franz, laden with game. On the ground lay four birds, a kangaroo, twenty musk-rats, a monkey, two hares, half a dozen beaver rats—and a bundle of thistles.

Jack was breathless. “Father! You wouldn’t believe how fast an ostrich runs! It felt like flying. I had to close my eyes—the wind was too strong. I need a mask with glass eyes!”

I laughed. “No mask. You’re capable of making your own. But next time, let me know when you plan such trips. We worry here.”

They nodded, eager to share their tales. Fritz produced the thistles. “I thought this might be useful for wool-making,” he said.

I examined it closely. “You’re right—it’s fuller’s teazle. Its spines are used to raise the nap on cloth.”

We decided to rise early the next day to process the hunt. As the boys grew sleepy, I led the evening prayers.

Skinning so many animals was tedious work. I remembered a tool from the ship’s surgeon’s chest—a large syringe. With a few modifications, I turned it into an air pump. I attached valves and drilled a hole in the stopper.

The boys stared in disbelief when I approached the kangaroo with the device. “Are you going to skin it with a squirt?” they joked.

I ignored the laughter. I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs, made a small cut, and inserted the syringe. With force, I blew air between the skin and flesh. Slowly, the hide swelled, stretching until it separated entirely from the body.

With a few cuts, the skin slipped off.

“What a brilliant idea!” they shouted.

“It’s simple science,” I explained. “Animal skin is held by delicate fibers. Between them are tiny air pockets. By filling them with air, you stretch and break the fibers. The skin detaches naturally.”

“The Greenlanders use this method,” I added. “They inflate seal skins to tow them ashore, then peel them off easily.”

We applied the same technique to all the animals. Within days, every hide was removed and ready for use.

Now it was time to gather wood for the crushing machine. We returned to the marked tree. Fritz and Jack climbed high to cut large branches so the tree would fall safely. Then, using a pit saw, Fritz and I cut through the trunk.

We tied ropes to guide its fall. With a mighty effort, we pulled. The tree groaned, cracked, and crashed to the ground.

We chopped off the limbs and cut the trunk into blocks—four long ones and two smaller. Over the next few days, we transported them home. Using the whale vertebrae as rollers, I assembled the crushing machine in no time.

Meanwhile, I had neglected our grain fields. One evening, the chickens returned with full crops, ignoring their feed. I realized they’d come from the cornfield.

Rushing there, I found the grain perfectly ripe. My wife gasped when I told her. “What about the potatoes and manioc? How will we manage all this work?”

“Don’t fret,” I said. “Manioc can wait. Potatoes are easy to dig here. And we’ll harvest like the Italians—quick and efficient.”

I leveled a large patch of clay soil, watering and tamping it until it became a hard, smooth threshing floor. We slung our largest basket between Storm and Grumble. Armed with reaping hooks, we went to the field.

“Don’t worry about the straw,” I told the boys. “Just grab the stalks wherever convenient. Wind them together and throw them into the basket.”

They loved it. In no time, the basket was full. But my wife was horrified. “You’ve left ears on short stalks! And all that good straw wasted!”

“That’s not waste,” I said. “In Italy, farmers leave the stubble for cattle to graze. Then they let grass grow and mow everything together for winter feed. Now, for threshing—we’ll do it Italian style.”

We piled the sheaves in a circle. The boys mounted Storm, Grumble, Lightfoot, and Hurry, circling the pile at a brisk trot. Dust and chaff flew as the animals stamped out the grain.

My wife and I used hayforks to shift the sheaves, ensuring even coverage. The horses occasionally nibbled at the grain—fair enough, I thought. “Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn,” I quoted.

After threshing, we winnowed the grain by tossing it high into the air when the wind blew. The chaff floated away, and the clean grain fell to the ground.

Our poultry visited often, gobbling up stray kernels. “Let them eat,” I said. “We lose some grain, but gain rich poultry. Soon, we’ll have chicken pie, roast goose, and boiled turkey!”

When the harvest was stored, we counted our yield—sixty, eighty, even a hundred times what we’d sown. Our granaries overflowed.

We prepared the field again for planting—mowing the stubble, which attracted quails and partridges. We resolved to catch them next season with nets.

The straw was carried home and stacked. Maize stalks burned into ashes rich in alkali—useful for soap. We planted rye, barley, and oats, hoping they’d mature before the rains returned.

As we finished farming, schools of herring arrived. This year, we pickled only two barrels. But the seals came next—and we hunted them vigorously.

Their skins were essential. I planned to cover the deck of the cajack with seal-skin, creating a waterproof magazine for pistols, ammunition, water, and food. I also rigged two harpoons with seal bladders, hanging them along the side.

Our new boat was ready. Strong, swift, and built with care. And though the stormy season had passed, our adventure had only just begun.
