A heartbeat of paralyzed awe held Mr. Gryce rooted before he summoned the steel of his profession back to his gaze. The chamber was a tableau of dissonance: the gloom, the hushed silence, and the air of a forgotten romance clashing violently with the grim reality of the prostrate form. Lying there, stretched out like a saint awaiting interment, was the master of the house, the sacred emblem pinned to his chest serving as a grotesque counterpoint to the earthly fury that had driven the blade home. Such contradictions, however, were enough to stagger the mind of a veteran, yet they confused Mr. Gryce only for a fleeting second.

Before Styles could voice his own bewilderment, the older officer stepped forward, taking his station beside the fallen man. The evidence was plain: life had long departed the body. Yet, duty demanded verification. Gryce laid a heavy hand upon the wrist, feeling the final, fading warmth, and peered into the eyes, which seemed to hold a silent, reproachful judgment. It took every ounce of his practiced composure to meet that gaze without flinching.

The body was still warm, but undeniably cold in spirit. Conviction settled over him, and he refrained from removing the dagger, instead studying it with a craftsman's scrutiny. It was no common instrument, but a curiosity plucked from some Eastern bazaar—a slender, ornate thing. Its presence hinted at suicide, yet the angle of entry and the posture of the wound spoke of an external hand, a stranger's violence.

The scene itself told a story of sudden, ferocious attack, yet the apartment remained a sanctuary of order. No signs of struggle marred the well-appointed room. Save for a scattering of rose petals, the air was thick with the perfume of peace and quiet luxury. Even the massive central table, where the victim had likely stood moments before his fall, bore no marks of chaos. At first glance, the articles atop it—pipes, pens, portraits, weights, measures, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelains, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a scroll of music, a pot of blooming flowers—stood undisturbed, as if the shock of the tragedy had failed to ripple through their orderly arrangement.

But a closer inspection revealed the truth. Amidst these trappings of complex taste and attainments lay a row of electric buttons. Gryce reached out, and the moment his finger brushed one, the soft red glow of the lantern overhead flickered and died, replaced instantly by a sickly, greenish glare. The room was bathed in a ghastly tint, transforming the shadows into something spectral. In haste, Gryce pressed another button, grateful as the light shifted to a mild, white radiance, banishing the pallor that threatened to overwhelm the solemnity of the spot.

"Childish toys for a man of his station," Gryce muttered, his brow furrowing. Yet, as his eyes drifted to the upturned face beneath him, the doubt lingered. Could a man with features so sharply cut, so commanding, truly possess an instinct as frivolous as a switchboard? The face itself exerted a magnetic pull, disturbing the flow of his thoughts whenever it intruded upon his focus. To attribute madness or folly to a visage of such strength and purpose was to admit one's own poverty of judgment. Thus, the lamp, with its shifting hues and scientific connections, acquired a deeper meaning, one that seemed tied to the evidence surrounding the man—the books, the apparatus, the air of a scholar's mind.

Shifting his attention to the walls, draped in tapestries and curios, Gryce found little aid for his investigation. Then, his eye caught it: a bent nail, twisted from its socket. It was the nail that had held the wooden cross resting now upon the dead man's heart. The cord suspending the cross still clung to it, tangled with the crimson threads of the garment that had received the fatal wound. Who had torn it down? Not the victim; a man bleeding from the chest could not reach the wall, nor move across the circumscribed distance to where the nail lay. Another hand had pulled it free, placing it directly in the path of the dying man's breath. Was it an act of desperate religious hope, or mad remorse? Gryce dismissed the symbolism with a passing thought, too engrossed by the golden, isolated moments of the crime scene to dwell on it further.

Beyond the table, half-hidden against the wall, hung a portrait of striking power. It depicted a young girl of extreme, pathetic beauty, dressed in the fashion of the Civil War's twilight. Her face shared a haunting, intellectual charm with the man slumped on the floor. Though fair-haired she was, and dark-he haired he, the resemblance argued a close kinship. Beneath the canvas rested a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a medal awarded for valor.

"Mementoes," Gryce mused, tracing the line of the frame.

His search continued until he encountered a narrow curtain, its dark hue blending seamlessly with the drapery on the adjacent walls, rendering it invisible until now. It was not a window pane; the windows of this unique apartment sat high, nearly lost in the ceiling. Instead, this fabric veiled an entrance to another chamber. Pushing it aside, Gryce entered a narrow closet housing a bed, a dresser, and a small table. The bed was a narrow cot, the dresser a testament to luxurious habits, both immaculate save for a silver-backed comb missing from its surface. Gryce spotted the comb lying on the floor at the far end of the room. Nearby, on a small stand, rested a pearl-handled parasol. A woman had been here recently.

Confirmation came swiftly. On the threshold separating this room from the study lay a tiny black spangle, known to milliners as a sequin. As Gryce stooped to examine its sparkle, his eye caught others trailing along the floor, leading inevitably to the large center table. The dainty trail of these bright, falling drops affected him strangely. He knew the mechanics: the sequins were strung on a thread, and once broken, they fell one by one, allowing a detective to trace the path of a woman's steps by the debris she shed. Whether it was the delicacy of the clue or the irony of fate using vanity as a trap, Gryce smiled, a knowing, eloquent expression playing on his lips.

Before inspecting the table further, he sought the break in the string. Had it snagged on a chair leg or a doorframe? The chairs were cushioned; the floor was smooth. Then, his gaze landed on the cross again. Its base featured intricate fretwork of gold. Could the filigree have caught in the hem of her dress as she tore the symbol from the wall, severing the thread and releasing this exquisite trail?

Hastening to where the cross had hung, Gryce searched the floor, finding nothing until he reached the rug beneath the prostrate man. Amidst the long hairs of the bearskin lay a solitary spangle. He knew then that the woman in shiny attire had stooped there, right before him, leaving her mark.

Satisfied, he returned to the table for a thorough, minute examination. The result proved fruitful. After drawing his finger across a specific spot near the inkstand, a faint smile graced his lips. He lifted the inkstand, examined the underside, and replaced it with precise care. Had he expected to find something hidden? A detective's face rarely yields its secrets, but the satisfaction was evident.

Lost in contemplation before the table, Gryce turned sharply at the sound of a quick step. Styles, the officer, had returned from scouring the rest of the house and stood before him, poised to speak.

"What is it?" Gryce asked, moving toward him.

Styles pointed toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door. "Go up!" he indicated.

Gryce hesitated, glancing around the room, which held his interest deeply. Styles broke his silence, his tone urgent. "Come! I've found the guilty party. He's in a room upstairs."

"He?" Gryce was startled by the pronoun.

"Yes, no doubt about it. When you see him..." Styles trailed off, his eyes widening. "What is that? Is he coming down? I'm sure there's no one else in the house. Don't you hear footsteps, sir?"

Gryce nodded. Footsteps were indeed descending.

"Let us retreat," Styles suggested. "Not because he's dangerous, but because you must see him before he sees you. He's a strange-acting man. If he enters here, he'll surely do something to implicate himself. Where can we hide?"

Gryce recalled the small closet he had just vacated and signaled Styles to follow. They slipped inside, pulling the curtain shut until only a narrow loophole remained. The steps grew louder, advancing steadily. Soon, they heard the intruder's breathing, quick and labored.

"Does he know someone has entered?" Gryce whispered. "Did he see you when you arrived?"

Styles shook his head and pointed eagerly toward the opposite door. The awaited figure had just lifted the portière and stood fully revealed, just inside the threshold.

Both men stared. Was this the murderer? This pale, lean servant holding a tray with a single glass of water? Gryce looked to Styles for explanation, but the officer hid his own surprise.
