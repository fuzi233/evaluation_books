“The Kynge of Spayn is a foul paynim,
And lieveth on Mahound;
And pity it were that lady fayre
Should marry a heathen hound.”

Some six weeks after the duel on the sands outside Bideford, the miller of Stow trundled up to the Grenvilles’ great house, white-faced and shaking, to beg the loan of the estate’s prized bloodhounds. Rose Salterne had vanished from her bed in the dead of night, and no soul knew where she had gone.

Sir Richard was away on business in Bideford, but old steward Anthony took matters into his own hands, dispatching for the hound-keepers at once. A throng of idle parish lads trailed the serving-men down to the mill, chattering and delighted at the prospect of a maiden hunt for sport, all of them certain the poor girl had been cruelly wronged. They roundly reviled the miller and his wife as hard-hearted heathens, fully convinced their harshness had driven Rose to throw herself off the jagged north Devon cliffs or drown in the frigid Atlantic surf. The women of Stow, for their part, were of one single, unanimous mind: the hussy had run off with some rascally man, and pride was bound to have its fall, as it always did.

The facts of Rose’s disappearance spoke far more gently of her: all her trinkets and finery were left untouched in her chamber, so at the very least she had gone away honestly. The only items missing were a small bundle of her linen, which old Anthony broadly hinted would likely turn up in other people’s households soon enough. The sole clue the search party found was a tiny, delicate footprint under her bedroom window. They leashed the great bloodhound to the mark, and after a single soft whimper of recognition, the beast lifted his deep, thunderous bay and dragged the panting keeper behind him, out the garden gate, up the sunken lane, and across the high windswept downs, heading straight for Marslandmouth. The whole posse comitatus stumbled breathless to a halt at the door of Lucy Passmore’s cottage.

As noted earlier, Lucy was now a widow, and her state of widowhood had proven surprisingly profitable. The dark augury she had spoken over her drunken old husband had come true: he had never returned from that night he sailed out with Eustace Leigh and the rogue Jesuits. She shed a few showy, dutiful tears, dried them quickly enough to save whatever she might need for business, then made a bold play to avoid any suspicion of collusion: she traveled to Stow and told Lady Grenville a wildly dramatic tale, swearing her husband had gone out pollock fishing and never come back, that she had heard horsemen gallop past her cottage window after midnight, and that those Popish skulkers the Jesuits had dragged him off by force, no doubt murdering him and salting his body down for provisions on their voyage back to Rome and the Pope. She begged for Lady Grenville’s protection from any further Jesuit mischief, and hinted the Queen herself owed her a pension for losing the love of her heart to idolatrous traitors.

Lady Grenville, who swore by Lucy’s herbal remedies for the children’s “housty” – the local name for sore throats – pleaded her case to Sir Richard so effectively that Lucy was comfortably secure for the next two years. Now, though, the door to her cottage was locked fast. Her flock of goats huddled on the step bleating for milk, while her donkeys wandered the downs above, braying in raucous chorus at the bloodhound’s bay.
“They’m laughing at us, keeper, them neddies,” one local man grumbled. “Sure as fate we’ve wasted our time here.”

The hound nosed around the cottage perimeter for a few more minutes, then loped straight down the glen, never pausing until it reached the edge of the sea.
“They took to the water! Let’s go back and turn the old witch’s house inside out.”
The crowd swarmed back to the cottage, ransacking every corner half in indignation, half – if the truth be told – in the hope of finding plunder. But there was nothing to take. Lucy had fled with every last bit of her moveable wealth, leaving only a huge black cat curled in the embers of her hearth. At the sight of the bloodhound, the cat shot straight up the chimney (the villagers swore they caught a strong whiff of brimstone as it went) and vanished into the woods, no doubt living many long happy years plaguing every rabbit in the glen. The mob drove the goats and donkeys back to Stow, then slunk home, sheepish once their initial fury faded, and more than a little afraid of what Sir Richard would say when he returned. He sold the animals at market, gave all the proceeds to the village poor, and promised to refund the full sum if Lucy ever returned to turn herself in to justice. But Lucy never came back. Her cottage was avoided by neighbors as if it were haunted, and slowly crumbled away to four fern-choked walls, the little stream trickling past it the only sound to break the glen’s silence for years.

A few days later, Sir Richard stopped by Clovelly Court on his ride home from Bideford, and offhandedly mentioned a piece of news that made Will Cary leap so far out of his chair he nearly hit the ceiling. Someone patrolling the cliffs the night Rose vanished, he said, had spotted a pinnace sailing hard for Lundy Island.
Will was half out of his mind. Half an hour later, he and four armed servants were aboard a trawling skiff, sailing for Lundy. He did not return for three days, and when he did he brought grim news: an elderly foreign man, who looked for all the world like a mass-priest, had been living in the ruins of Moresco Castle on Lundy for months, renting the space from a local fisherman named John Braund. A few weeks prior, a younger foreign man had joined him off a Baltic trading ship, which came and went several times with the young man aboard. A few days before Will arrived, a lady and her stout maidservant had come ashore with the younger man, spoken at length in secret with the elder man, stayed the night, and then all three had sailed away at dawn. The local fishermen had heard the younger man call the older one “father” – and they knew better than to ask any more questions.

Old Cary and Sir Richard sent Will back to Lundy with the Hartland parish constable, and he returned with the hapless John Braund in chains. During endless questioning, Braund feigned extreme deafness and total loss of memory of any events, and was sent to Exeter Gaol on charges of harbouring Jesuits, gypsies, and other suspect traitorous persons. Poor Braund, who had only hidden the men to pay his overdue rent and feed his seven children, never survived the transition from Lundy’s crisp sea air to the gaol’s pestilent stench. He took the gaol fever within a week, and died raving in his noisome cell, his secret buried with him. Only vague, cruel rumour remained of Rose Salterne’s fate.

Nearly every dame in Bideford, who had long resented Rose for her unrivalled beauty, gossiped that she had run off with the Spanish heathen, and got exactly what she deserved. Local gentlewomen who had long claimed the girl was “getting above her station” shook their heads and said they had always expected she would end in ruin. Every young maiden in town was glad the fair rival was gone, and before long the bustle of daily life swept Rose’s name out of most people’s heads, leaving only a tender faded memory in a few hearts.

Will Cary, meanwhile, was half distracted with guilt. He cursed himself endlessly for disgracing Rose during the duel, and driving her straight into Don Guzman’s arms. St. Leger tried in vain to calm him, telling him the fault was not his – the two had clearly been drawn to each other long before the duel, and old Salterne’s harsh treatment of his daughter was far more to blame than Will’s anger. But Will refused to be comforted. He wrote to Frank Leigh at Whitehall, confessing every last detail, calling himself a fool and a villain, and begging for forgiveness.

Frank wrote back at once, telling Will he had no reason to blame himself at all. He said a “synastria” – a sympathy of the stars – had bound Rose and her chosen man together, and to fight that bond was to fight against heaven itself. As one of the Brotherhood of the Rose, he swore that Rose could never make anything but a worthy, virtuous choice, and that even if the man she loved was a Spaniard and a Papist, he was owed their full loyalty and respect. Will had no idea how much agony that letter had cost Frank, who was quietly wasting away of unspoken love for Rose, tormented by fears he dared not name. He showed the letter triumphantly to St. Leger, who agreed Frank was an angel even as he privately suspected Frank’s true feelings were far more turbulent.

The matter rested for a few days, until Jack Brimblecombe arrived. He was now curate of Hartland Town, “passing rich on forty pounds a year”, and he looked at Will Cary with steady, sober eyes.
“I hope no offence, Mr. William,” he said, the name Rose sticking tight in his throat. “But when are you and the rest going after – after her?”

Will tried to laugh it off, teasing him for being the “blood-drinking Catiline” from the night they had all gotten drunk and sworn their oaths as Brothers of the Rose. But Jack would not be swayed.
“I drank nothing worse than red wine that night, sir, as far as I can tell,” he said firmly. “And oaths are oaths. There’s no getting out of them.” He asked how far it was to Caracas – he had heard Don Guzman had been made governor there, and that was surely where they had taken Rose.

Will could not believe his ears. “You cannot mean to sail all the way to the Indies to look for her?”
“That depends on if I can scrape together the money, or get a berth on a westward-bound ship,” Jack said. “God’s will must be done, either way.”

Will stared at him, wondering if he was drunk or mad – but the little pig-like eyes behind his round spectacles were completely sane and serious. Jack explained that his mother was comfortably off if his father passed, his sisters were all strong, capable girls who could make good servants, and he begged Will to look after them if he never came home. He offered all his books as pawn if Will would lend him five pounds for travel fare.
“You’ll be seized by the Spanish Inquisition the moment you step ashore, and burnt alive!” Will protested.
“I know that,” Jack said, tears welling in his eyes. “I’m a great coward, Mr. Will, a dirty coward, as you well know. But I cannot bear this not knowing! Not knowing if that pure angel I have always revered is living in sin, or if those Papist devils are torturing her to make her renounce her faith. I am a deacon! I swore to tend Christ’s lost sheep, and no other soul will go for her – no one else loves her enough.” He said, if Amyas Leigh were home, he would not hesitate to set sail at once.

Will managed to persuade Jack to wait until Amyas returned, rather than rushing blindly to his death, and Jack agreed to sleep on it. He vanished for a week, then came back to Clovelly Court grinning, and said he had arranged for the parson of Welcombe to cover his services for two Sundays, and he planned to walk all the way to London to talk to Frank Leigh. Will could not dissuade him, and pressed a little extra money into his hand before he set off.

Three weeks later, Jack stumbled back into Clovelly Court thin and footsore, and told Will he had found Frank half dead with grief for Rose, wasting away for love. Frank agreed their oath was binding, and that they *must* go to find her – but he told Jack they must wait for the right time, and so Jack had come home to bide his time.

The thought of the voyage would not leave Will Cary’s head, and a few days later he ran into Mr. Salterne on Bideford Quay. The old ship merchant told him bluntly that all the young men of spirit ought to sail west to the Indies, to find new trade and new wealth for Devon, instead of moping around the shoreline. That was all the push Will needed. He went straight to Amyas, and told him he was ready.
“Westward-ho, with you,” he said. “Come foul weather or fair.”

Amyas agreed at once. Even if they did not find Rose in La Guayra, he said, the Spanish Plate fleets would be there, heavy with gold and silver from the mines. They would not come home empty handed. Will laughed and told him his old father had recently married a buxom young tenant’s daughter, who had already borne him a fat baby, so there was no shortage of heirs to Clovelly Court, and no one would miss Will if he left. He joked about his new stepmother Abishag Jewell, who endlessly bragged about her distant relation to the great Bishop Jewell, and he could no longer stand to see her occupy his own dead mother’s place at the head of the table.

They agreed at once that they could not leave Jack Brimblecombe behind – if he did not get to sail with them, he would no doubt hurl himself off Harty Point in despair, and his ghost would haunt them for the rest of their days. After a long consultation, they decided Amyas would travel up to London first, to talk to Frank and his mother, to get their blessing for the voyage. The rest of the Brothers of the Rose were scattered far and wide across the country at their posts, and it would take far too long to gather them all, as Amyas sagely noted: “Too many cooks spoil the broth, and half a dozen gentlemen aboard one small ship are as bad as two Kings of Brentford.”

The next morning, Amyas set off for London, leaving Yeo behind to help Will make all the final preparations for their westward voyage.
