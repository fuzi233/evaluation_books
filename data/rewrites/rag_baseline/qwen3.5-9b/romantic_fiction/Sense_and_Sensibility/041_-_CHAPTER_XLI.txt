Edward carried his thanks to Colonel Brandon, then proceeded with a buoyancy that bordered on manic glee to Lucy. By the time he reached Bartlett's Buildings, his spirits were so elevated that she could assure Mrs. Jennings, who called upon her the following day with her congratulations, that she had never witnessed him in such high spirits in her entire life. Her own happiness was equally certain, and she joined Mrs. Jennings wholeheartedly in their anticipation of settling comfortably at Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas. Yet, far from withholding the credit due to her, Lucy spoke of her friendship for both sisters with fervent gratitude, readily acknowledging their obligations to her and declaring that no exertion on Miss Dashwood's part, past or future, would ever surprise her, for she believed her capable of anything in the world for those she truly valued. Regarding Colonel Brandon, she was not only prepared to venerate him as a saint but was genuinely anxious that he be treated as such in all worldly matters; she desired his tithes raised to the maximum and secretly resolved to exploit his resources at Delaford—his servants, his carriage, his cattle, and his poultry—to the fullest extent possible.

It had been over a week since John Dashwood visited in Berkeley Street, and since that time, no attention had been paid to his wife's indisposition save for a single verbal inquiry. Elinor began to feel it imperative to pay her a visit. This obligation, however, clashed violently with her inclinations and lacked the support of her companions. Marianne, content merely to refuse attendance herself, became urgently insistent that her sister not go at all. Mrs. Jennings, whose carriage was perpetually at Elinor's disposal, harbored such a profound distaste for Mrs. John Dashwood that neither her curiosity regarding the recent revelations nor her strong desire to champion Edward's cause could overcome her reluctance to endure her company again. Consequently, Elinor departed alone to pay a visit she possessed little desire for, risking a tête-à-tête with a woman whom neither of the others held in such high regard.

Mrs. Dashwood was initially denied entry, but before the carriage could turn from the house, her husband emerged unexpectedly. He expressed great pleasure in meeting Elinor, claimed he had just intended to call in Berkeley Street, assured her that Fanny would be delighted to see her, and invited her inside. They ascended to the drawing-room, where nobody was present. "Fanny is in her own room, I suppose," he remarked. "I shall go to her presently; I am certain she will have no objection whatsoever to seeing you. Indeed, she will not. Especially now, there cannot be—but however, you and Marianne were always great favorites. Why would not Marianne come?" Elinor offered excuses for her sister. "I am not sorry to see you alone," he replied, "for I have much to say to you. This living of Colonel Brandon's—can it be true? Has he really given it to Edward? I heard it by chance yesterday and was coming specifically to inquire further."

"It is perfectly true," Elinor confirmed. "Colonel Brandon has given the living of Delaford to Edward."

"Really! Well, this is astonishing! No relationship! No connection between them! And now that livings command such prices! What was the value of this?"

"About two hundred a year."

"Very well. For the next presentation to a living of that value—supposing the late incumbent to be old and infirm, likely to vacate it soon—he might have secured, I dare say, four thousand pounds. And how did he fail to settle that matter before this person's death? Now, indeed, it would be too late to sell it, but a man of Colonel Brandon's sense! I wonder he should be so improvident in a point of such common, such natural concern! Well, I am convinced there is a vast deal of inconsistency in almost every human character. I suppose, however, on recollection, that the case may probably be this: Edward is only to hold the living until the person to whom the Colonel has actually sold the presentation reaches an age fit to take it. Yes, yes, that is the fact, depend upon it."

Elinor contradicted this assertion very positively, explaining that she had personally conveyed the offer from Colonel Brandon to Edward and therefore understood the exact terms under which it was granted, compelling him to submit to her authority.

"It is truly astonishing!" he exclaimed after hearing her explanation. "What could be the Colonel's motive?"

"A very simple one—to be of use to Mr. Ferrars."

"Well, well; whatever Colonel Brandon may be, Edward is a very lucky man. However, you will not mention the matter to Fanny. Although I have broken it to her and she bears it vastly well, she will not like to hear it discussed much. 'Mrs. Ferrars,' added he, lowering his voice to a tone befitting such an important subject, 'knows nothing about it at present, and I believe it will be best to keep it entirely concealed from her as long as possible. When the marriage takes place, I fear she must hear of it all.'"

"But why should such precaution be used? Though it is not to be supposed that Mrs. Ferrars can derive the slightest satisfaction from knowing her son has money enough to live upon—for that must be quite out of the question—yet why, upon her late behavior, is she supposed to feel at all? She has done with her son; she cast him off forever and caused all those over whom she had influence to cast him off likewise. Surely, after doing so, she cannot be imagined liable to any impression of sorrow or joy on his account; she cannot be interested in anything that befalls him. She would not be so weak as to throw away the comfort of a child and yet retain the anxiety of a parent!"

"Ah! Elinor," said John, "your reasoning is excellent, but it is founded on ignorance of human nature. When Edward's unhappy match occurs, depend upon it his mother will feel exactly as if she had never discarded him; and therefore, every circumstance that may accelerate that dreadful event must be concealed from her as much as possible. Mrs. Ferrars can never forget that Edward is her son."

"You surprise me; I should think it must nearly have escaped her memory by this time."

"You wrong her exceedingly. Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers in the world."

Elinor remained silent. "We think now," said Mr. Dashwood after a short pause, "of Robert's marrying Miss Morton."

Elinor, smiling at the grave and decisive importance of her brother's tone, calmly replied, "The lady, I suppose, has no choice in the affair."

"Choice! How do you mean?"

"I only mean that I suppose, from your manner of speaking, it must be the same to Miss Morton whether she marries Edward or Robert."

"Certainly, there can be no difference; for Robert will now, to all intents and purposes, be considered the eldest son. As to anything else, they are both very agreeable young men; I do not know that one is superior to the other."

Elinor said no more, and John fell silent for a short time. His reflections concluded thus: "Of one thing, my dear sister," he said, kindly taking her hand and speaking in an awful whisper, "I may assure you; and I will do it, because I know it must gratify you. I have good reason to think—in fact, I have it from the best authority, or I should not repeat it, for otherwise it would be very wrong to say anything about it—but I have it from the very best authority—not that I ever precisely heard Mrs. Ferrars say it herself, but her daughter did, and I have it from her—that in short, whatever objections there might be against a certain—a certain connection, you understand me—it would have been far preferable to her; it would not have given her half the vexation that this does. I was exceedingly pleased to hear that Mrs. Ferrars considered it in that light; a very gratifying circumstance you know to us all. 'It would have been beyond comparison,' she said, 'the least evil of the two, and she would be glad to compound now for nothing worse.' But however, all that is quite out of the question—not to be thought of or mentioned—as to any attachment you know, it never could be; all that is gone by. But I thought I would just tell you of this, because I knew how much it must please you. Not that you have any reason to regret, my dear Elinor. There is no doubt of your doing exceedingly well—quite as well, or better, perhaps, all things considered. Has Colonel Brandon been with you lately?"

Elinor had heard enough, if not to gratify her vanity and raise her self-importance, to agitate her nerves and fill her mind; she was therefore glad to be spared the necessity of replying extensively and the danger of hearing anything more from her brother by the entrance of Mr. Robert Ferrars. After a few moments' chat, John Dashwood, remembering that Fanny was yet uninformed of her sister's presence, quitted the room in search of her, leaving Elinor to deepen her acquaintance with Robert. He, by the gay unconcern and happy self-complacency of his manner while enjoying such an unfair division of his mother's love and liberality to the prejudice of his banished brother—an advantage earned solely by his own dissipated course of life and that brother's integrity—was confirming her most unfavorable opinion of his head and heart.
