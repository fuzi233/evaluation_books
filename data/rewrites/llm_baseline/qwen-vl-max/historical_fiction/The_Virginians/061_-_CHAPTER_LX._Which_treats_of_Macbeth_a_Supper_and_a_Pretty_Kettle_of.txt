When the play ended, our friends took a coach to Mr. Warrington’s lodgings, where the Virginian brothers had arranged an elegant supper. Mr. Warrington was eager to entertain them in the most generous manner, and General Lambert and his wife accepted with pleasure, delighted at the opportunity to repay their young hosts’ kindness. The gathering included the General and his wife, their son John from college, their two daughters—Miss Theo and Miss Hetty—and Mr. Spencer of the Temple, a recent acquaintance George had met at a coffeehouse. Together, they enjoyed the landlady’s fine fare with good spirits.

The seating arrangement remains uncertain, but it is clear that Miss Theo sat beside the chickens and Mr. George Warrington, while Miss Hetty shared her attention between a ham and Mr. Harry. Mrs. Lambert must have been seated to George’s right, leaving only the positions of the General, his son, and Mr. Spencer to be settled. Mr. Spencer had attended another performance earlier in the week, where he had introduced George to the greenroom.

Conversation soon turned back to the evening’s drama. Some guests professed great admiration for the play. “As for what our gentlemen say,” Mrs. Lambert declared to Mr. Spencer, “you must not believe a word of it. It was a delightful piece, and my husband and Mr. George behaved as ill as possible.”

“We laughed in the wrong places and cried when we shouldn’t have,” the General admitted with a smile. “That’s the truth.”

“You caused all the people in the boxes around us to turn and cry ‘Hush!’” Mrs. Lambert continued. “You made the pit shout, ‘Silence in the boxes, yonder!’ Such behavior I never saw, and I blushed for you, Mr. Lambert!”

“Mamma thought it was a tragedy,” the General said, “and we thought it was pure fun. George and I behaved perfectly well, didn’t we, Theo?”

“Not when I was looking your way, papa!” Theo replied.

At this, the General chuckled. “Was there ever such a saucy baggage seen?”

“I didn’t speak till I was bid,” Theo answered modestly. “I confess I was deeply moved by the play, especially by Mrs. Woffington’s performance. I was sorry the poor mother found her child and then lost him. And I’m sorry, too, papa, if I oughtn’t to have felt that way,” she added, smiling.

“Women aren’t as clever as men, you know, Theo,” Hetty remarked from the other end of the table, glancing slyly at Harry.

“The next time we go to the theatre, brother Jack,” she said, “please pinch us when we should cry, or nudge us when it’s time to laugh.”

“I wish we could have seen the fight,” General Lambert mused. “The battle between little Norval and the giant Norwegian—that would have been splendid! You should write to Mr. Rich, the manager, Jack, and suggest it.”

“I haven’t seen that,” Harry said gravely. “But I saw Slack and Broughton at Marybone Gardens.” He paused, wondering if he’d said something witty, for everyone burst into laughter.

“It wouldn’t take a giant to knock over that little fellow in red boots,” Harry added. “I, for one, could throw him over my shoulder.”

“Mr. Garrick is small,” Mr. Spencer observed. “But there are moments when he seems like a giant.”

“How grand he was in *Macbeth*, Mr. Warrington!” the Templar exclaimed. “How terrifying that dagger scene was! You should have seen our host, ladies! I introduced Mr. Warrington to Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard in the greenroom, and Lady Macbeth did him the honor of taking a pinch from his box.”

“Did the wife of the Thane of Cawdor sneeze?” the General asked, lowering his voice dramatically.

“She thanked Mr. Warrington in tones so hollow and tragic that he jumped back—and must have spilled some of his rappee, because Macbeth sneezed three times.”

“Macbeth, Macbeth, Macbeth!” the General thundered.

“And the great philosopher standing beside Mr. Johnson said, ‘You must mind, Davy, lest thy sneeze should awaken Duncan!’ who, by the way, was chatting with the three witches as they sat against the wall.”

“Oh, I’d give anything to go behind the scenes!” Theo sighed.

“To see the ropes pulled, smell the tallow candles, look at the pasteboard gold and tinsel jewels, and the painted old women, Theo? No—don’t look too close,” George warned, demurely sipping a glass of hock.

“You were angry with your father and me,” he added.

“Nay, George!” Theo protested.

“Nay? I say yes!” George countered. “You were angry because we laughed when you wanted to cry. If I may speak for both of us, sir,” he said, bowing to General Lambert, “I think we weren’t inclined to weep like the ladies, because we stood behind the author’s scenes. Looking closely at the young hero, we saw how much of him was mere rant and glitter. As for the pale, tragic mother—her pallor was white chalk, and her sorrow, her handkerchief. Now, Theo, admit you thought me unfeeling?”

“If you can find it out without my confessing,” she replied, “what’s the use of my owning it?”

“Suppose I were to die,” George went on, “and you saw Harry grieving—you’d see real sorrow, a true tragedy. But if you saw the undertaker in mourning clothes and a black cloak, you wouldn’t feel a thing.”

“Indeed, I would!” Mrs. Lambert insisted. “And so would any daughter of mine.”

“Perhaps we might find our own mourners, Mr. Warrington,” Theo said softly.

“Would you?” George asked, and both his cheeks and Theo’s flushed crimson—likely because they noticed Hetty’s bright eyes watching them.

“The older writers understood little of the pathetic,” Mr. Spencer remarked.

“What do you think of Sophocles and *Antigone*?” John Lambert called out.

“Faith, our wits don’t trouble themselves much about him—unless an Oxford man reminds us!” Spencer replied. “I meant only to go back as far as Mr. Shakespeare, whom we all agree doesn’t handle the elegant or the pathetic as well as modern playwrights. Has he ever approached Belvidera, Monimia, or Jane Shore? Can you find in his comic women the grace of Congreve?” He offered snuff to both sides.

“Do you suppose Mr. Spencer himself has tried his hand?” someone asked.

“Many gentlemen of leisure have,” Spencer replied. “Mr. Garrick, I confess, once read a piece of mine and returned it.”

“And I’ve four acts of a play tucked away in one of my boxes,” George confessed.

“I’ll bet it’s better than any of theirs,” Harry whispered to his neighbor.

“Is it a tragedy or a comedy?” Mrs. Lambert asked.

“Oh, a tragedy,” George replied. “With at least two or three dreadful murders!”

“Let us perform it,” the General declared. “And let the audience watch their eyes!”

“Then fetch the tragedy at once, Gumbo!” Mrs. Lambert called to the servant.

Gumbo bowed low. “Tragedy, madam? Yes.”

“In the great cowskin trunk, Gumbo,” George said solemnly.

Gumbo bowed again. “Yes, sir,” he replied, even more gravely.

“But my tragedy is buried under linen, packages, books, and boots, Hetty,” George added.

“Never mind,” Hetty said cheerfully. “Let’s have it and toss the linen out the window!”

“And the trunk is at our agent in Bristol,” George explained. “So Gumbo must get post-horses. We can keep the performance going until he returns the day after tomorrow.”

The ladies groaned in mock despair. “Oh!” they chorused.

The General, perhaps more seriously, said, “Let us be thankful for the escape. Let us think of going home. Our young gentlemen have treated us nobly. Let us all raise a final toast to Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, Virginia. Suppose, boys, you return home to find a tall, handsome stepfather waiting? Women as old as she have married before.”

“To Madam Esmond Warrington, my old schoolfellow!” Mrs. Lambert toasted. “I shall write and tell her how lovely her sons have treated us. And Mr. George, I won’t say how poorly you behaved at the play!”

With that, the company took their leave. The General’s coach and servant, bearing a flambeau, waited to carry his family home.

After such a gracious evening, what could be more natural than for Mr. Warrington to call upon his guests the next day to inquire how they had fared? Their coach might have taken the open road behind Montague House toward Oxford Road—where footpads lurked in the fields. The ladies might have caught cold or slept poorly after the emotional strain of the play. In short, there was no need for apology. So he closed his books early at the Sloane Museum and walked away, perhaps remembering little of what he had read.

Yet what was the meaning of his hesitation as he approached the door of the General’s lodgings in Dean Street? Why did he pause, knock with trembling hands, and study the footman who answered? Does any young man truly read? Does any old man remember? Does any weary, broken heart recall the days when it beat full and strong?

It has been many years since some of us were young; we forget—but not all. No, madam, we remember with advantages, as Shakespeare’s Harry promised his soldiers: if they survived Agincourt and St. Crispin’s Day, they would remember it well.

Old chargers turned out to grass may still kick up their heels and gallop once more when the trumpet sounds over the hedge. I no longer care for clowns or pantaloons; I find fairies ugly and their verses unbearable. Yet I enjoy seeing children at a pantomime. I no longer dance or eat supper, but I delight in watching Eugenio and Flirtilla whirl in a waltz, or Lucinda and Ardentio pull a cracker.

Burn your little fingers, children! Blaze out warm sparks from each other’s eyes! Then draw close and read the motto—yes, that old, sentimental motto, stale yet ever new—let her lips read it, and his interpret it. Divide the sweetmeat, young lovers, and crunch it together. I have no teeth. Bitter almonds and sugar disagree with me. But shall not bonbons melt on the tongue?

We follow John upstairs to the General’s rooms, entering with Mr. George Esmond Warrington, who bows with exaggerated politeness. Only one lady sits near the window—a place not often touched by sunshine in Dean Street. She is not especially beautiful, but spring is in the air, and she blooms like a young plant. A bouquet of fresh roses glows in her cheek. Her eyes, perhaps, are violets.

A hundred years ago, we would have told Mr. Sylvanus Urban that her neck was a lily, her form a nymph’s. We would have composed an acrostic and celebrated Lambertella in verse—between a neat engraving of Prague and the King of Prussia’s camp, and a map of Maryland.

Here is Miss Theo, blushing like a rose. What could her mother have meant an hour earlier, insisting she was pale and tired, best kept indoors? They had gone to thank Lord Wrotham’s ladies for their hospitality. The General was at the Horse Guards, likely expecting a new command—though his wife trembled at the thought of another posting abroad.

“My brother and yours have gone to visit our younger brother at his school at the Chartreux. My brothers are both destined for the clergy, I believe,” Theo said, busy hemming a boy’s shirt.

A century ago, young ladies stitched shirts without shame. I don’t claim they were better or worse for it. I have no desire, dear girl, that you make puddings—or that I black boots.

Harry has been visiting them often, almost daily.

“He likes us better than the fine folk,” Theo added, smiling. “They don’t care for him now—now that he’s no longer one of them.”

“Why don’t you embrace fashion?” she asked. “Why not frequent chocolate-houses and racecourses, Mr. Warrington?”

“Has my brother gained so much from his gay life that I should imitate him?”

“You might at least visit Sir Miles Warrington. His arms are wide open. Her ladyship came today in her chair, praising you! She says you’re on the path to preferment. The Duke spoke highly of you at court. When you become great, will you forget us?”

“Yes,” George said, “when I am great, I will.”

“Well, Mr. George, then—”

“—Mr. George!”

“When papa and mamma are here, I suppose we need no Mister,” Theo said, glancing out the window, slightly flustered.

“And what have you been doing, sir? Reading or writing more of your tragedy? Will it make us cry—as we like—or just frighten us—as you do?”

“There’s plenty of killing,” George replied, “but not much crying. I haven’t met many women. I’ve not been intimate with them. I daresay what I’ve written comes from books or poems I’ve read and imitated like other young men. Women don’t talk to me, generally. I’m told I have a sarcastic way that displeases them.”

“Perhaps you never cared to please them?” Theo ventured, blushing.

“I displeased you last night. You know I did.”

“Yes,” she said, “but it wasn’t really displeasure. Later, I realized I was wrong.”

“Did you think of me when I was away, Theo?”

“Yes, George—that is, Mr.—well, George. I thought you and papa were right about the play. As you said, it wasn’t real sorrow, only affectation. I wonder whether it’s fortune or misfortune to see so clearly. Hetty and I agreed we’d be careful in the future about enjoying tragedies. So be careful when yours comes. What’s its name?”

“He’s not christened yet. Will you be godmother? The lead character is—”

But at that moment, Mrs. Lambert and Miss Hetty returned from their walk.

“I never expected to see Mr. Warrington today,” the mother said quickly. “Well, I thought he might come. That is, it’s very kind of him. The play and supper were charming—except that Theo had a slight headache this morning.”

“I dare say it’s better now, mamma,” Hetty said.

“Indeed, my dear,” Theo replied, tossing her head, “it never mattered. I told mamma so.”

They began talking about Harry. He was downcast. He needed something to do. He was always at the Military Coffee-House, studying the King of Prussia’s campaigns. It was unfair to keep him in London after his fall from grace.

He said nothing, but one could see how he regretted his idle past and resented his current dependence. Passing the guard at St. James’s with John Lambert, he had whispered, “Why can’t I be a soldier too? I’m as tall as that fellow, and I can shoot as well as any man. But I earn less than sixpence a day. I’ve squandered my own bread and now eat half my brother’s. He’s the best brother, but it’s shameful I live off him. Don’t tell my brother, Jack Lambert.”

“And my boy promised he wouldn’t,” Mrs. Lambert said.

The girls were out of the room when she spoke.

George said he had written to his mother, offering half his inheritance—perhaps the whole, if needed—for Harry to buy a commission or pursue any venture that might bring him purpose.

“He has a good brother,” the lady sighed. “Let us hope for better days.”

“The Danes always come pour qui scait attendre,” George murmured.

“You heard that? Ah, George! My Theo is an—ah, never mind what she is, George Warrington,” the mother said, eyes brimming.

“Bah! I’m going to make a fool of myself, just as I did at the tragedy.”

But Mr. George had been planning a private scheme for his brother’s benefit. After his presentation to the Prince at Kensington, several people—including General Lambert—had mentioned that the Duke had inquired about him and wondered why he hadn’t come to his levee. Such royal interest could not be ignored.

A day was set, and George and the General visited the Prince at his house in Pall Mall. When it was George’s turn to bow, the Prince was especially gracious. He spoke at length about Braddock and the war, pleased by the young man’s modest and intelligent replies.

George attributed the expedition’s failure not only to panic and surprise, but more to delays caused by colonial greed and selfishness toward the King’s troops.

“If we had moved a month sooner,” George said, “the fort would have been ours, and the army never defeated.”

The Prince fully agreed.

“I’m told you saved yourself mainly through your knowledge of French,” the Duke remarked kindly.

George modestly explained his youth in the French colonies, where he had learned the language.

The Prince, known for his charm when pleased, asked who had taught him—and joked that French ladies were the best teachers.

George bowed low and said it was not for him to contradict his Royal Highness.

The Duke, laughing, called him “a sly dog.”

Still silent, George waited.

Then the Prince said, “I take the field immediately against the French, who threaten His Majesty’s Electoral dominions. If you wish to join me, your language skills may prove useful. I hope we’ll be more fortunate than poor Braddock!”

Every eye turned to the young man receiving such an offer from a prince.

And now George delivered his finest speech.

“Sir,” he said, “your Royal Highness’s proposal does me infinite honor, but—”

“But what, sir?” the Prince asked sharply.

“But I have enrolled at the Temple to study law and prepare for my duties at home. If my wound in service gives me any claim to your favor, I humbly ask that my brother—equally fluent in French, stronger, braver, and more gifted in military affairs—be allowed to serve in my place.”

“Enough, enough, sir!” the Prince snapped, visibly irritated. “I offer you a favor, and you hand it to your brother? Wait till I offer you another!”

And with that, he turned abruptly away—just as he would later turn on the French.

“Oh, George! Oh, George! Here’s a pretty kettle of fish!” General Lambert groaned as they walked home together.
