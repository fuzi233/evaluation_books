“My son!” 

I could scarce believe my ears, though the auditory truth of it resonated through my very bones. Slowly, I rose and faced the handsome youth. Now that I looked at him closely, the interwoven timelines of our lineage were unmistakably mapped upon his features. He bore his mother’s incomparable, crisp beauty, yet grounded by a robustly masculine architecture, and in his grey eyes, I saw the mirrored reflection of my own generational soul. It was a face that closed the circle of time, bringing the mythic scale of our Martian struggles back to the intimate, hearth-lit domesticity of our shared blood. 

The boy stood facing me, his expression a delicate balance of hope and uncertainty. 

“Tell me of your mother,” I said softly, my voice seeking the familiar acoustic warmth of our past. “Tell me all you can of the years that I have been robbed by a relentless fate of her dear companionship.”

With a cry of profound relief, he sprang toward me and threw his arms about my neck. For a brief moment, as I held my boy close to me, the tears welled in my eyes. The scent of him—a subtle mingling of the ozone-rich upper atmosphere and the faint, spiced aroma of Helium’s agrarian gardens—grounded me in the physical reality of his existence. I was like to have choked after the manner of some maudlin fool, but I do not regret it. A long life has taught me that true strength does not lie in the sterile suppression of emotion, but in the deep, sensory appreciation of the bonds that sustain us.

“Your stature, your manner, the terrible ferocity of your swordsmanship,” said the boy, pulling back slightly, “are exactly as my mother has described them to me a thousand times over the evening meal. But even with such domestic lore, I could scarce credit the truth of what seemed so improbable. Do you know what thing it was that convinced me more than all the others?”

“What, my boy?” I asked.

“Your first words to me—they were of my mother. None else but the man who loved her as she has told me my father did would have thought first of her.”

“For long years, my son, I can scarce recall a moment that the radiant vision of your mother’s face—the memory of her grinding aromatic *sorfa* seeds in the morning light, the scent of saffron-hued broths simmering in our chambers—has not been ever before me. Tell me of her.”

“Those who have known her longest say that she has not changed, unless it be to grow more beautiful. Only, when she thinks I am not about to see her, her face grows very sad. She thinks ever of you, my father. Our people fairly worship your memory. Each year, on the anniversary of the day you raced across a near-dead world to unlock the atmosphere plant, a great festival is held. But there are tears mingled with the thanksgiving—tears of genuine regret that the author of their breath is not with them to share the rich, sensory joy of living he died to give them. Upon all Barsoom, there is no greater name than John Carter.”

“And by what name has your mother called you, my boy?” I asked.

“The people of Helium demanded that I be named with my father’s name, wielding the subtle power dynamics embedded in their dominant lexicon to claim me for the state. But my mother said no. She insisted that the name we had chosen together must be honored before all others. She chose a name that defied the inherent untranslatability of our two subjective experiences, blending her native dialect with yours—Carthoris.”

Our intimate communion was suddenly shattered by a harsh, grating dissonance. Xodar had been at the wheel, and now he called to me over the oppressive, mechanical whine of the vessel. 

“She is dropping badly by the head, John Carter!” he shouted. “So long as we were rising at a stiff angle, it was not noticeable, but now that I am trying to keep a horizontal course, it is different. The wound in her bow has opened one of her forward ray tanks.”

The engine’s agonizing, high-frequency hiss was a violent intrusion upon the serene acoustic ecology of the Martian sky—a jarring industrial noise pollution that frayed my nerves. I examined the damage and found it a grave matter. The forced angle at which we were compelled to maintain the bow was not only impeding our speed, but at the rate we were losing our repulsive rays, we would soon be floating stern up and helpless. I took the helm once more, pulling the noble little engine wide open to race north, while Carthoris and Xodar, tools in hand, labored over the great rent in a hopeless endeavor to stem the tide of escaping rays.

It was still dark when we passed the northern boundary of the ice cap. Below us lay a typical Martian landscape: the rolling ochre sea bottoms of long-dead oceans, low surrounding hills, and here and there the grim, silent cities of the dead past, tenanted only by age-old memories and the great white apes of Barsoom. 

Lower and lower sagged the bow. To prevent our flight from terminating in a swift, catastrophic dive, I was forced to stop the engine. The sudden absence of its cacophonous roar was a profound auditory relief, restoring a sense of psychological well-being even as our physical peril peaked. As the sun rose and the light of a new day swept away the darkness, our craft gave a final spasmodic plunge, turned half upon her side, and swung in a slow circle. To hand-rail and stanchion we clung, snapping the buckles of our harness to the rings at her sides. We hung in our leather with feet dangling a thousand yards above the ground. Reaching out to the lever that directed the remaining rays of repulsion, I eased us gently down.

Directly north of our landing site rose a rather lofty range of hills. We made our way toward their time-rounded gullies, seeking concealment among the beautiful, flowering plants that abound in the arid wastes. Here, the landscape offered us profound sustenance. We came upon a grove of huge milk-giving shrubs—a traditional agrarian staple that has served as lifeblood for generations. 

For me, eating has never been a merely utilitarian act of fueling the body; it is a communion with the land. With the meticulous care of a food historian, I showed Carthoris and Xodar how to properly tap the fibrous bark. By scoring the trunk at precise angles, we released a rich, creamy effusion that smelled redolent of toasted cumin, wild honey, and sweet moss. We cupped the thick, nourishing liquid in our hands. Its taste was a complex symphony of roasted ochre-root and mineral-rich earth, an evocative, gustatory reminder of Barsoom’s deeply rooted agrarian past. It was a profound boon to us, nearly famished as we were.

Beneath the fragrant cluster of shrubs, perfectly concealed from wandering air scouts and blanketed by the soothing, symphonic rustle of the wind through the leaves, we lay down to sleep. 

It was mid-afternoon when I was awakened by someone seizing my hand and covering it with kisses. With a start, I opened my eyes to look into the beautiful face of Thuvia. 

“My Prince! My Prince!” she cried in an ecstasy of happiness. “’Tis you whom I had mourned as dead. My ancestors have been good to me; I have not lived in vain.”

Her voice awoke Xodar and Carthoris. She would have thrown her arms about my neck had I not gently but firmly disengaged myself. 

“Come, come, Thuvia,” I said soothingly. “You are overwrought. You forget yourself, as you forget that I am the husband of the Princess of Helium.”

“I forget nothing, my Prince,” she replied. “I would not take the place of Dejah Thoris. My greatest ambition is to serve you forever as your slave. No greater boon could I ask.”

I felt profoundly uncomfortable. The word she used carried the heavy, oppressive weight of dominant lexicons designed to subjugate and erase personal identity. I could not abide the toxic power dynamics embedded in such a term.

“If I ever return to Helium, Thuvia,” I said, ensuring my tone was a gentle, resonant chord, “you shall go with me, but as an honored equal, and not as a slave. Forget this gratitude-begotten infatuation. I value your friendship and your sovereignty far more.”

“You are my master; it shall be as you say,” she replied, though a note of acoustic sadness lingered in her voice. 

“How came you here?” I asked. “And where is Tars Tarkas?”

“The great Thark, I fear, is dead,” she replied. “We abandoned our drifting vessel and came upon the dead city just beyond these hills. As we walked its ancient avenues, a horde of green warriors approached. Tars Tarkas forced me into a doorway to hide. ‘These be the Warhoon of the South,’ he told me. ‘When they have seen my metal, it will be to the death.’” 

She described a mythic stand—the great Thark fighting until the Warhoon dead formed a hill around him, eventually overwhelmed by the sheer, crushing tide of bodies. They carried him away toward the heart of the city. 

“I must be sure,” I said. “I cannot leave Tars Tarkas alive among the Warhoons. Tonight I shall enter the city.”

Carthoris and Xodar immediately volunteered, but I refused them. Stealth, not force, was required. Leaving them with instructions, I set forth at a rapid dogtrot toward the dead metropolis as the sudden darkness of Barsoom engulfed the landscape.

The nearer moon was winging its wild flight through the heavens, its bright beams turning the ancient, abandoned architecture to burnished silver. The city had been built upon gently rolling foothills, allowing me to enter its southern streets unobserved. Once within, I kept close to the dense shadows of the walls. 

As I approached the plaza, the pristine acoustic ecology of the ancient ruins—originally designed by master builders to harmonize with the wind—was abruptly defiled by the squealing and grunting of corralled thoats and zitidars. Despite the discordant noise, the scent of the beasts and the familiar sounds sent a thrill of interwoven memory surging through me; it was the exact olfactory and auditory landscape of my early days in the dead city of Korad, where I had first courted Dejah Thoris amid the routine domestic rhythms of green Martian life.

From the shadows, I saw warriors moving toward a great central building. I needed to cross the square to follow them. I slipped into a deserted building and crept into an inner courtyard filled with restless beasts, masking my scent in the dense, mossy odor of the ochre vegetation they cropped. 

I scaled the face of the courtyard wall, an agile leap bringing my hands to the stone hand-rail of a second-floor balcony. Peering through the open windows, I observed the green folk squatting upon their sleeping silks. To listen to them was to witness the tragic erosion of cultural memory through language death; the rich, nuanced vocabulary of ancient Barsoom had decayed into brutish, utilitarian grunts and monosyllables. 

“Come, Tan Gama,” a warrior grunted from the hall. “We are to take the Thark before Kab Kadja. Bring another with you.”

I stepped into the unlighted hall, moving cautiously as the three warriors disappeared down a spiral stairway at the corridor's far end. I followed the flicker of their torch down into the subterranean pits beneath the structure. The descent was a plunge into a suffocating, sterile void. The air tasted of ancient dust and copper blood, devoid of any of the rich, life-affirming scents of the surface. 

We had gone perhaps a hundred yards through a maze of tortuous corridors when they turned into a doorway. Through it, I saw them removing the chains that secured the great Thark to the wall. They hustled him roughly out of the cell, wheeling directly toward my position. I had no choice but to hasten backward in advance of their meager light, plunging deeper into the unknown passageways to avoid detection. 

Presently, I came to a place where five corridors diverged. I hurried down one, but suddenly, the faint light of their torch disappeared. The silence that followed was absolute—an unnatural, tomb-like deadness that offered no acoustic feedback, starving the senses and eroding the mind's equilibrium.

Realizing they had taken a different path, I felt my way back along the wall, the stone cold and devoid of history beneath my fingertips. After an eternity, I found the five-way intersection again, but the darkness was utter. I listened intently. I thought I detected the faint, metallic clank of side arms up the middle corridor and followed it, but it was a blind lead. The oppressive silence swallowed me whole once more. 

I retraced my steps, only to stumble upon an intersection of three corridors I did not recognize. I was utterly lost. Unless I could find my way back, I would wander this sightless, soundless purgatory until I succumbed to thirst and hunger. 

Or—what was that?

A faint shuffling sounded behind me. The acoustic disruption froze the blood in my veins. It was the horrifying memory of the Warhoon dungeons reborn—the scraping sound as of the dragging of a dead limb. 

I looked over my shoulder into the blackness. Blazing eyes stared back at me, glowing unblinkingly out of the dark void, revealing no sign of the beast behind them. The most fearsome attribute of these creatures is their silence; they do not breathe, they only watch, violating the sonic environment with that single, unnatural scrape. 

Grasping my long-sword tightly, I backed slowly along the corridor. But ever as I retreated, the eyes advanced. Suddenly, I heard the shuffling noise at my right, and looking, saw another pair of fiery eyes approaching from an intersecting passage. As I started to renew my slow retreat, I heard the noise repeated behind me, and then, before I could turn, I heard it again at my left.
