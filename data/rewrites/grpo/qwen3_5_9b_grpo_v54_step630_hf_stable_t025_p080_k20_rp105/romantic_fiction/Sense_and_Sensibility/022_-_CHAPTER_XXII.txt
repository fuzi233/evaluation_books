The Miss Steeles, with their boundless energy and lack of refinement, found Marianne increasingly unbearable. Her spirits, already low, had curdled into a sharp intolerance for their vulgarity and inferiority of taste. She met their advances with a chilling indifference that acted as a wall, effectively shutting them out. Yet, it was this very coldness that seemed to spark a peculiar affection in the sisters' hearts, particularly in Lucy. Where Marianne turned away, Lucy stepped forward, seizing every opportunity to engage Elinor in conversation, striving to deepen their acquaintance with an easy, frank manner that masked her true intentions.

Lucy possessed a natural cleverness; her remarks were often just and amusing, making her a delightful companion for a fleeting half-hour. However, Elinor quickly discerned that these powers were unaided by education. Lucy was ignorant and illiterate, her mind lacking the cultivation that would have elevated her talents into something respectable. Elinor pitied this neglect of ability, but she viewed Lucy's lack of delicacy, rectitude, and integrity with far less tenderness. The attentions, assiduous flattery, and calculated efforts Lucy displayed at the Park revealed a thorough want of principle. To Elinor, a woman who joined insincerity with ignorance, whose lack of instruction prevented equality in discourse, and whose conduct toward others rendered her deference valueless, offered no lasting satisfaction.

One afternoon, as they strolled from the park to the cottage, Lucy broke the silence with a question that struck Elinor as oddly specific. "You will think my question an odd one, I dare say," Lucy began, her eyes fixed on Elinor. "But pray, are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law's mother, Mrs. Ferrars?"

Elinor felt the oddity immediately, her countenance betraying her surprise. "I have never seen Mrs. Ferrars," she replied.

"Indeed!" Lucy exclaimed, feigning wonder. "I thought you must have seen her at Norland sometimes. Then, perhaps, you cannot tell me what sort of a woman she is?"

"No," Elinor returned, carefully shielding her true opinion of Edward's mother and resisting what appeared to be impertinent curiosity. "I know nothing of her."

Lucy watched her closely, her expression shifting to one of wounded innocence. "I am sure you think me very strange for enquiring about her in such a way. Perhaps there are reasons... I wish I might venture, but I hope you will do me the justice of believing I do not mean to be impertinent."

After a civil reply and a few minutes of silence, Lucy resumed the topic with hesitant urgency. "I cannot bear to have you think me impertinently curious. I would rather do anything in the world than be thought so by a person whose good opinion is so well worth having as yours. I should not fear trusting *you*; indeed, I should be glad of your advice on how to manage in such an uncomfortable situation as I am. But since you do not know Mrs. Ferrars, there is no occasion to trouble you further."

"I am sorry I do *not*," Elinor said, astonishment coloring her voice. "If it could be of any use to *you* to know my opinion of her. But I never understood that you were at all connected with that family, and I am surprised at such a serious inquiry into her character."

"You are right to be surprised," Lucy admitted, lowering her gaze with an amiably bashful air. "Mrs. Ferrars is certainly nothing to me at present—but the time *may* come. How soon it will come depends on her, but when we may be very intimately connected."

"Good heavens!" cried Elinor, the shock nearly overwhelming her composure. "What do you mean? Are you acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars? Can you be?" She recoiled slightly at the thought of such a sister-in-law.

"No," Lucy corrected, fixing her eyes on Elinor with intense focus. "Not to Mr. *Robert* Ferrars—I never saw him in my life; but to his eldest brother."

Astonishment, sharp and painful, seized Elinor, though an immediate disbelief fought against it. She stared at Lucy in silent amazement, unable to fathom the reason or object of such a declaration. Her complexion flushed, yet she stood firm in her incredulity, refusing to succumb to hysteria.

"You may well be surprised," Lucy continued, sensing Elinor's turmoil. "He never dropped the smallest hint of it to you or your family, for it was always meant to be a great secret, faithfully kept by me to this hour. Not a soul of my relations knows but Anne. I never should have mentioned it if I had not felt the greatest dependence on your secrecy. I thought my inquiries about Mrs. Ferrars seemed odd, so I felt compelled to explain. I do not think Mr. Ferrars can be displeased when he knows I have trusted you; he holds your family in the highest esteem, regarding you and the other Miss Dashwoods as his own sisters."

Elinor remained silent for a moment, her mind reeling. Forcing herself to speak with a calmness that barely concealed her rising solicitude, she asked, "May I ask if your engagement is of long standing?"

"We have been engaged these four years."

"Four years!" Elinor gasped.

"Yes."

Though greatly shocked, Elinor still struggled to believe it. "I did not know," she said, her voice trembling slightly, "that you were even acquainted until the other day."

"Our acquaintance is of many years' date," Lucy replied smoothly. "He was under my uncle's care for a considerable while."

"My uncle?"

"Yes; Mr. Pratt. Did you never hear him talk of Mr. Pratt?"

"I think I have," Elinor managed, exerting her spirits to maintain control.

"He was four years with my uncle, who lives at Longstaple, near Plymouth. It was there our acquaintance began. My sister and I stayed often with him, and it was there our engagement was formed, though not until a year after he had left as a pupil. He was almost always with us afterwards. I was very unwilling to enter into it without my mother's knowledge and approbation, but I was too young and loved him too well to be prudent. You must have seen enough of him to know he is very capable of making a woman sincerely attached to him."

"Certainly," Elinor answered, though her mind was a chaotic mix of confusion and dread. After a moment's reflection, she added, with a desperate attempt to cling to her belief in Edward's honor, "Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars! I confess I am so totally surprised that I must assume there is some mistake of person or name. We cannot mean the same Mr. Ferrars."

"We can mean no other," Lucy cried, smiling triumphantly. "Mr. Edward Ferrars, the eldest son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street, and brother of your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood. You must allow that I am not likely to be deceived as to the name of the man on whom all my happiness depends."

"It is strange," Elinor murmured, in a state of painful perplexity, "that I should never have heard him mention your name."

"No; considering our situation, it was not strange. Our first care has been secrecy. You knew nothing of me or my family, so there was no occasion to mention my name. And since he was always particularly afraid of his sister suspecting anything, that was reason enough for his silence."

She fell silent. Elinor's sense of security crumbled, though her self-command held firm. "Four years you have been engaged," she said, her voice steady despite the storm within.

"Yes; and heaven knows how much longer we may have to wait. Poor Edward! It puts him quite out of heart." Lucy reached into her pocket and produced a small miniature. "To prevent the possibility of mistake, please look at this face. It does not do him justice, to be sure, but I think you cannot be deceived as to the person it drew for. I have had it for over three years."

She pressed the painting into Elinor's hands. Whatever doubts lingered in Elinor's mind—fear of a hasty decision, a desire to detect falsehood—vanished instantly upon seeing the portrait. It was unmistakably Edward's face. She returned it almost immediately, acknowledging the likeness.

"I have never been able," Lucy continued, "to give him my picture in return, which vexes me greatly, for he has always been so anxious to get it! But I am determined to send it the very first opportunity."

"You are quite in the right," Elinor replied calmly. They walked a few steps in silence before Lucy spoke again.

"I am sure," she said, "I have no doubt in the world of your faithfully keeping this secret. You must know how important it is that it not reach his mother; she would never approve of it, I dare say. I shall have no fortune, and I fancy she is an exceedingly proud woman."

"I certainly did not seek your confidence," Elinor said, "but you do me no more than justice in imagining I may be depended on. Your secret is safe with me; but pardon me if I express some surprise at such an unnecessary communication. You must feel that my knowing of it could not add to its safety."

As she spoke, Elinor looked earnestly at Lucy, hoping to find a crack in her facade, perhaps the falsehood underlying most of her story. But Lucy's countenance remained unchanged, smooth and untroubled.

"I was afraid you would think I was taking a great liberty," Lucy said, her voice dropping to a whisper. "I have not known you long personally, but I have known your family by description for a while. As soon as I saw you, I felt almost as if you were an old acquaintance. In this case, I felt some explanation was due to you after my inquiries about Edward's mother. I am so unfortunate that I have no one whose advice I can ask. Anne is the only one who knows, and she has no judgment at all; indeed, she does me more harm than good. I am in constant fear of her betraying me. She does not know how to hold her tongue, and I was in the greatest fright the other day when Edward's name was mentioned by Sir John, lest she should blurt it all out. You can't think how much I suffer in my mind from it. I only wonder I am alive after what I have suffered for Edward's sake these last four years. Everything in such suspense and uncertainty; seeing him so seldom—we can hardly meet above twice a year. I am sure I wonder my heart is not quite broke."

She pulled out her handkerchief, dabbing at her eyes, but Elinor felt little compassion.

"Sometimes," Lucy continued, after wiping her eyes, "I think it would be better for us both to break off the matter entirely. But then at other times I have not the resolution for it. I cannot bear the thought of making him so miserable, as I know the mere mention of such a thing would do. And on my own account, so dear as he is to me, I don't think I could be equal to it. What would you advise me to do, Miss Dashwood? What would you do yourself?"

"Pardon me," Elinor replied, startled by the directness of the question. "But I can give you no advice under such circumstances. Your own judgment must direct you."

After a few minutes of silence, Lucy resumed. "Of course, his mother must provide for him sometime or other; but poor Edward is so cast down by it! Did you not think him dreadful low-spirited when he was at Barton? He was so miserable when he left us at Longstaple to go to you that I feared you would think him quite ill."

"Did he come from your uncle's, then, when he visited us?"

"Oh, yes; he had been staying a fortnight with us. Did you think he came directly from town?"

"No," Elinor replied, feeling a strange sense of validation at the recollection. "I remember he told us he had been staying a fortnight with some friends near Plymouth." She recalled her own surprise at the time, at his total silence regarding even the names of those friends.

"Did not you think him sadly out of spirits?" Lucy repeated.

"We did, indeed, particularly so when he first arrived."

"I begged him to exert himself for fear you should suspect what was the matter; but it made him so melancholy, not being able to stay more than a fortnight with us, and seeing me so much affected. Poor fellow! I am afraid it is just the same with him now; for he writes in wretched spirits. I heard from him just before I left Exeter." She reached into her pocket again, pulling out a letter and casually showing the direction to Elinor. "You know his hand, I dare say—a charming one it is; but that is not written so well as usual. He was tired, I dare say, for he had just filled the sheet to me as full as possible."

Elinor examined the letter. It was undeniably his hand. Doubt vanished completely. This picture, she had allowed herself to believe, might have been accidentally obtained; it might not have been Edward's gift. But a correspondence by letter could exist only under a positive engagement, authorized by nothing else. For a few moments, she was almost overcome; her heart sank within her, and she could hardly stand. Yet, exertion was indispensable. She struggled resolutely against the oppression of her feelings, and her success was speedy and complete.

"Writing to each other," Lucy said, returning the letter to her pocket, "is the only comfort we have in such long separations. Yes, *I* have one other comfort in his picture, but poor Edward has not even *that*. If he had but my picture, he says he should be easy. I gave him a lock of my hair set in a ring when he was at Longstaple last, and that was some comfort to him, he said, but not equal to a picture. Perhaps you might notice the ring when you saw him?"
