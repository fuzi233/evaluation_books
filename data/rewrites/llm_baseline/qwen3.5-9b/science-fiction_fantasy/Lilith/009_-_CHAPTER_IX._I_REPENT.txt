I set the manuscript aside, comforted to discover that my father had glimpsed that mysterious realm and recognized Mr. Raven. Yet, a sudden memory struck me: I had never learned the circumstances of his passing. A dark suspicion took root—that he must have followed Mr. Raven, crossing over without return. At the thought, shame washed over me for my own cowardice. How many wonders lay hidden within their domain, truths about life and death and vast territories beyond human perception! Surely the Ravens were benevolent souls; a single night in their company would have caused me no harm. They were certainly strange, yet theirs was a peculiarity of function, while mine was a marvel of form. Beauty and utility—why had I distrusted them? Why had I deemed them unworthy of my trust, suspecting designs against me? The more I reflected on my behavior, the more repulsed I felt by my own actions. Why fear the dead? To share their sacred rest was an honor I had proven myself unfit to receive. What harm could that sleeping king, that lady with the wounded palm, possibly inflict?

A profound longing seized me for the serene stillness of their faces, and tears streamed down my cheeks. Sobbing, I collapsed onto a couch and instantly drifted into sleep.

I awoke abruptly, sensing a presence calling me. The house was silent, quiet as an empty church. Outside, a blackbird sang on the lawn. Resolving to seek forgiveness, I told myself, "I shall go and tell them of my shame; I will do whatever they wish." Rising, I climbed the stairs directly to the attic. The wooden chamber remained unchanged, the mirror faintly reflecting the room before it. It was nearly noon; the sun would be slightly higher than when I arrived. I needed to lift the hood a fraction and realign the mirrors. If only I had witnessed Mr. Raven doing it himself!

I pulled the chains, allowing light to strike the first mirror. Turning to the second, I saw the shapes of my previous vision—they were distinct yet trembling, like a landscape distorted in a pool disturbed by a gentle breeze. Touching the glass revealed it to be impenetrable. Suspecting polarization was key, I shifted the mirrors repeatedly, altering their angles until, by sheer chance, the alignment finally resolved. Suddenly, the mountains appeared blue, steady, and crystal clear.

Stepping forward, my feet touched the heather. All I recalled of the path to the cottage was a journey through a pine forest. I navigated thickets and small fir woods, constantly imagining I recognized fragments of the landscape, but no forest met my eyes. Now the sun hung low on the horizon, and the air had grown chilly with the onset of winter. To my relief, I spotted a small black figure approaching—a raven.

I hurried to greet him. "Sir, I beg your pardon for my rudeness last night," I said. "Will you take me with you now? I heartily confess I do not deserve it."

"Ah!" he replied, looking up. After a brief pause, he added, "My wife does not expect you tonight. She regrets that we ever encouraged your stay last week."

"Please, take me to her so I may express my sorrow," I pleaded humbly.

"It is of no use," he answered. "Your night had not yet come then, or you would not have left us. It is not come now, and I cannot show you the way. The dead were rejoicing beneath their daisies—all lying among the roots of the flowers of heaven—at the thought of your joy when winter passes and spring brings its birds. Before you departed, they shivered in their beds. When the spring of the universe arrives... well, that cannot happen for ages yet. How many, I do not know, and I do not care to know."

"Tell me one thing, I beg of you, Mr. Raven: Is my father with you? Have you seen him since he left the world?"

"Yes; he is with us, fast asleep. That was the man you saw with his arm on the coverlet, his hand half-closed."

"Why did you not tell me? I should have been so close to him, unaware!"

"And turn your back on him!" the raven corrected sharply. "I would have lain down immediately had I known!"

"I doubt it. Had you been ready to lie down, you would have known him—" he continued, addressing me as "Old Sir Up'ard"—"along with your twice-great-grandfather, both departed long ago. Your great-grandfather has been with us for many years; I believe he will soon begin to stir. You saw him last night, though of course you did not recognize him."

"Because he is so much closer to waking than you. No one who refuses to sleep can ever wake."

"I do not understand you at all!"

"You turned away, and chose not to understand!"

Silence stretched between us. If I spoke nothing, he would depart.

"And my grandfather—is he also with you?" I asked.

"No; he remains in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead."

"Where is the Evil Wood? Let me find him."

"You will not find it, but you will hardly fail to encounter it. It is the place where those who refuse to sleep rise at night to kill their dead and bury them."

"I cannot comprehend you!"

"Naturally not. Nor do I understand you; I cannot read your heart or your face. When my wife and I fail to understand our children, it is simply because there is not enough of them to be understood. Only God can comprehend foolishness."

"Then," I said, feeling exposed and utterly worthless, "will you kindly show me the nearest way home? I know there are many paths, for I have taken two already."

"There are indeed many ways."

"Please tell me how to identify the nearest."

"I cannot," the raven replied. "You and I use the same words with entirely different meanings. We often cannot tell people what they *need* to know because they *want* to know something else, thereby misunderstanding everything we say. Home is always so far away in the palm of your hand, and telling you how to reach it serves no purpose. But you will get there; you must get there; you have to get there. Everyone who is not at home must go home. You thought you were at home where I found you; if that truly were your home, you could not have left it. Nobody can leave home. And nobody is, or ever will be, at home without having gone there."

"An enigma stepping on another enigma!" I exclaimed. "I did not come here to solve riddles."

"No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting for you! Indeed, you are the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, appearing so only because you are not true."

"Worse and worse!" I cried.

"And you *must* answer the riddles!" he insisted. "They will keep asking themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle striving to escape, and you are holding your door tightly shut against it."

"Will you not, in pity, tell me what I must do—where I must go?"

"How should I dictate *your* duty, or the path to it?"

"If I am not to go home, at least direct me toward my own kind."

"I know of none. The beings most like you lie in that direction." He pointed his beak. I saw nothing but the blinding setting sun.

"Well," I said bitterly, "I cannot help feeling ill-treated—snatched from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and denied instruction on where to go or what to do!"

"You forget," the raven said calmly, "that when I brought you and you rejected my hospitality, you reached what you call 'home' safely. Now you have come of your own accord. Good night."

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak lowered to the ground. I stood stunned. True, I had come of my own volition, but had I not intended to make amends? My heart ached, and in my mind, there was no quest, no purpose, no hope, no desire. Gazing after the raven, I wanted to follow, but felt it futile.

Suddenly, he lunged at a specific spot, driving the full weight of his body into his bill. For several moments, he dug vigorously. Then, with a flutter of his wings, he flung back his head, and something shot from his bill, soaring high into the air. Just then, the sun dipped below the horizon, and the air instantly grew dusky. Yet, the object transformed, opening into a soft radiance and drifting toward me like a firefly, though vastly larger and glowing with a brilliant yellow light. It flew overhead.

I turned and pursued it.

Here, I must interrupt my narrative to note the constant struggle involved in describing what cannot be stated with even approximate precision. The events recorded, along with the nature of the creatures involved, differ so profoundly from any conceivable occurrence in this world that I can only present them by translating their effects upon me into the forms and languages of earthly life—not the things themselves, but the feelings they awaken. Even this attempt is made with a persistent sense of failure, for I find it impossible to capture more than one facet of a multitude of complex significances, or one concentric layer of a graduated embodiment.
