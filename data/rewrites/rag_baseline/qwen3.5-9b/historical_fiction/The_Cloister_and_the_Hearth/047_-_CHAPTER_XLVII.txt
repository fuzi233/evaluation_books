To reconstruct the fractured geography of their sorrow, Denys seized the morrow's quiet hour to unveil the remainder of his tale, carefully shielding the names of Cornelis and Sybrandt from the stain of accusation. "It is not for me to blacken them," he declared, his voice carrying the weight of a soldier's code, "for they descend from noble stock. Yet Gerard views them as enemies in this affair. Since I am Gerard's comrade, and since it is the iron law of war to withhold truth from the foe—even if that truth is merely a lie—I cannot speak ill of them."

Catherine exhaled a sigh that seemed to carry the dust of their shared grief, offering no rebuttal.

The adventures Denys recounted ignited a tumult of agitation and profound mourning within them. They wept bitterly at the memory of the friends' separation, a wound Denys himself could barely articulate without stumbling. Yet, eventually, the sharp edges of despair smoothed into a radiant, trembling hope: the expectation of Gerard's swift return. Denys shared this confidence wholeheartedly, yet he cautioned them that hope must not blind them to the final instructions of the departed. "If Gerard returns next week and finds no Margaret," he warned, his tone sharpening, "what figure shall he cut? How shall he explain the absence of the woman he loved?"

In this moment, Catherine felt a kinship toward the absent Margaret stronger than ever before. Her anxiety mirrored Denys's; she yearned to find the girl and offer kindness before Gerard's arrival. However, she could not align with his strategy of abandoning their neighborhood to scour the land. "She must have confided in someone regarding her destination," she reasoned gently. "These are not dishonest souls fleeing the country; they owe not a single stiver in Sevenbergen. Dearest heart, Denys, you cannot hunt the entirety of Holland for one girl."

"Can I not?" Denys retorted, his expression grim. "We shall see." After a pause for reflection, he proposed a division of labor: she would remain here, her senses alert and wide-open, while he would ransack every town across Holland if necessity demanded. "But she will not stray many leagues from here. There are three of them. Three do not fly as swiftly, nor travel as far, as one."

"That is sense," Catherine conceded, though she pressed him immediately to visit the Demoiselle Van Eyck. "She and our Margaret were bosom friends. She knows where the girl has gone, provided she chooses to speak." Eager to depart, Denys moved to leave, but Catherine, with a deft turn of her hand, adjusted her dress to appear slightly neater and compelled him to accompany her.

They were ushered into a richly furnished chamber where the elderly lady sat in gracious reception. Upon hearing their purpose, the tapestry slipped from Margaret Van Eyck's hands. "Gone? Departed from Sevenbergen without telling me? The ungrateful girl."

This reaction startled the visitors. "What? You know nothing? When was she last here?"

"Perhaps ten days ago," the old lady replied, her voice tinged with irritation. "I had taken out my brushes, after so many years, intending to paint her portrait. I did not finish it, though; for certain reasons."

Catherine lamented the strangeness of such a departure—"bag and baggage"—without permission or explanation. "Was there ever anything so untoward? Just when our hearts were warmest toward her, and here comes Gerard's companion from the ends of the earth bearing comfort, only to find her missing, while Gerard himself awaited her return. What am I to do? Surely she did not vanish thus without cause. Can you not provide us with a clue, my good demoiselle? Pray, tell us."

"I possess no clue to give," the elder lady snapped, her patience fraying.

"Then I can," announced Reicht Heynes, emerging from the doorway with cheeks flushed with indignation.

"So you have been listening all along, have you?"

"What are my ears for, mistress?"

"True enough. Very well, then, shed the light of your wisdom upon this dark matter."

"There is no darkness here that I perceive," Reicht declared. "And the clue, if you call it a double-stranded cord, has both ends lying right here in this room. Oh, mistress, I wonder at you sitting there feigning ignorance."

"Marry, come up," the mistress blushed, her complexion rivaling the servant's. "So it was I who drove the foolish girl away."

"You played your part, mistress. What manner of greeting did you afford her on her last visit? Could she fail to notice it, feeling so friendless? And you said, 'I have changed my mind about painting you,' turning your nose up at her."

"I did not turn up my nose. It is not shaped like yours for gazing heavenward."

"Oh, all noses can follow the heart's inclination, for that matter. Poor soul. She did come to my kitchen. 'I am not to be painted now,' she said, tears filling her eyes. She spoke no further. But I understood perfectly what she meant. I had seen you."

"Well," Margaret Van Eyck admitted, her defiance softening into confession, "I do confess as much, and I make you the judge, madam. Know that these young girls can achieve nothing of their own volition; they are most adept at mimicking their sweethearts. Your Gerard is reasonably skilled in many arts, including the illuminator's craft. Margaret was his pupil, a patient one. What marvel? Having a woman's eye for color and a lover to emulate. 'Tis a trick I despise in my heart: for thereby the great art of color, which should be royal, aspiring, and free, becomes a poor slave to the petty crafts of writing and printing, fettered and imprisoned, reduced to match the littleness of books, destined to go to church in a rich fool's pocket. Nevertheless, affection rules us all. When the poor wench brought me her thorn leaves, lilies, ivy, dewberries, ladybirds, and butterfly grubs—all the scum of Nature stuck fast in gold-leaf like wasps in a honey-pot—and alongside her diurnal book, showing she had pored over each singular page for a hundred, or one hundred and fifty, or two hundred hours, certainly I was wroth. That an immortal soul, countless hours of labor, and much manual skill should be flung away on Nature's trash—leaves, insects, grubs—and on barren letters. Yet, having bowels, I was forced to restrain my anger, damming my better feelings, and looked kindly at the work to suggest improvements. I said, 'Since Heaven, for our sins, has doomed us to spend time, soul, and color on great letters and little beetles, omitting such small fry as saints and heroes, their acts and passions, why not present the scum naturally?' I told her, 'The grapes I saw walking abroad hung in the air, not stuck in a wall; and even these insects, and Nature's slime in general, do not pass their noxious lives wedged miserably in metal prisons like flies in honey-pots and glue-pots, but crawl or hover at large, infesting the air.' 'Ah, my dear friend,' she replied, 'I see now whither you drive; but this ground is gold; whereon we may not shade.' 'Who said so?' I asked. 'All teachers of this craft,' she answered; and (to make an end of me at once, I trow) 'Gerard himself!' 'That for Gerard himself,' I retorted, 'and all the gang; give me a brush!'

"Then I chose to shade her fruit and reptiles with a color false in nature, but true relative to that monstrous ground of glaring gold. In five minutes, out came a bunch of raspberries, stalk and all, that almost flew into your mouth; likewise a butterfly grub she had so truly rendered it might turn the stoutest stomach. My lady threw her arms around my neck and exclaimed, 'Oh!'"

"Did she now?"

"The little love!" observed Denys, managing to wedge in a word at last.

Margaret Van Eyck stared at him, then smiled. She continued to recount how, step by step, she had been led to promise to resume the art she had abandoned with a sigh upon her brothers' deaths—to paint the Madonna once more, with Margaret as the model. Incidentally, she even revealed the alchemy by which girls are turned into saints. "'Thy hair is adorable,'" I said. "'Why, 'tis red,' she replied. 'Ay,' I said, 'but what a red! How brown! How glossy! Most hair is not worth a straw to us painters; thine is the artist's very hue. But thy violet eyes, which smack of earth, being now languid for lack of one Gerard, now full of fire in hopes of the same Gerard, these will I lift to heaven in fixed and holy meditation. And thy nose, which does already somewhat aspire that way (though not so piously as Reicht's), will I debase a trifle, and somewhat enfeeble thy chin.'"

"Enfeeble her chin? Alack! What may that mean? You go beyond me, mistress."

"'Tis a resolute chin. Not a jot too resolute for this wicked world; but when you come to a Madonna? No thank you."

"Well, I never. A resolute chin."
