The entire affair of Rockmetteller Todd concluded with a rather satisfying sense of closure, though I must confess that midway through the proceedings, I harbored a genuine suspicion that Jeeves might finally let me down. For the first time, the man possessed an air of bafflement that I had rarely witnessed. Usually, Jeeves operates on the principle of infinite competence; officially, he is employed to press my shirts and manage my laundry, but in reality, he functions more like the "bird" described by the poet Johnnie—a loyal companion who rallies around his master in times of dire necessity. He serves as a guide, a philosopher, and, I am inclined to believe, a true friend. Consequently, I relied on him implicitly.

Therefore, when Rocky Todd informed me of his aunt's peculiar predicament, I did not hesitate. Jeeves was privy to the scheme from the outset.

The crisis erupted one crisp spring morning. I was in bed, attempting to restore the "good old tissues" following approximately nine hours of dreamless slumber, when the door burst open. Some unseen force prodded me in the lower ribs and vigorously shook the bedclothes. After blinking and composing myself, I identified Rocky. My initial reaction was that I was suffering from some horrific nightmare.

Rocky, you see, resided on Long Island, miles from New York. Furthermore, he had repeatedly assured me that he rose no earlier than noon, and more often not until one o'clock. Constitutionally the laziest youth in America, he had devised a lifestyle that allowed him to maximize his idleness. He was a poet, or at least, he wrote verses when compelled to act; otherwise, he existed in a state of perpetual trance. He once told me he could sit on a fence, observing a worm and pondering its motives for hours without a twitch.

His schedule was meticulously calculated. Approximately once a month, he would dedicate three days to composing a handful of poems; the remaining 329 days of the year were reserved for repose. I never understood how poetry could sustain a gentleman, even one living as frugally as Rocky, yet it appears American editors will fight tooth and nail for rhyming verse if you avoid preaching about the "strenuous life." Rocky had shown me a piece once, titled *Be!* It exhorted the reader to live fully, to engage every nerve and muscle, and it was printed alongside a frontispiece of a semi-nude figure flexing his biceps at the rising sun. For this effort, he received a hundred dollars and remained in bed until four p.m. for over a month.

Regarding his financial future, he was surprisingly secure due to a wealthy aunt residing in Illinois. Having been named Rockmetteller in her honor and being her sole heir, his position was sound. He had promised me that upon inheriting her fortune, he would perform no labor whatsoever, save perhaps for occasional poems recommending the joys of life to a pipe-smoking gentleman lounging by the fireplace. And yet, it was this man who was poking me in the ribs at the grey dawn!

"Read this, Bertie!" I could envision him brandishing a letter or some equally abominable document in my face. "Wake up and read this!"

"I cannot read before my morning tea and a cigarette," I mumbled, reaching for the bell.

Jeeves entered, looking as fresh as a dew-covered violet. It remains a mystery to me how he sustains such vitality.

"Tea, Jeeves."

"Very good, sir."

He glided silently from the room—always imparting the sensation of a liquid substance flowing over mossy stones—and I discovered that Rocky was once again pacing the room, clutching his dreadful letter.

"What is it?" I asked. "What on earth is the matter?"

"Read it!"

"I can't. I haven't had my tea."

"Well, listen then."

"Who sent it?"

"My aunt."

At this precise moment, I drifted back to sleep. I awoke to hear him muttering, "So what on earth am I to do?"

Jeeves trickled back with the tea tray, like a silent stream meandering through a forest floor. Light returned to the room.

"Read it again, Rocky, old top," I said. "I want Jeeves to hear it. Mr. Todd's aunt has written him a rather puzzling letter, Jeeves, and we require your counsel."

"Very good, sir."

He stood in the center of the room, radiating unwavering devotion to the cause, as Rocky launched into his recitation:

"MY DEAR ROCKMETTELLER.—I have been contemplating matters for a considerable duration, and I have arrived at the conclusion that I have been unduly negligent in delaying what I have resolved to accomplish immediately."

"What do you make of that, Jeeves?"

"It appears somewhat obscure at present, sir, but it likely clarifies as the correspondence progresses."

"It becomes as clear as mud!" declared Rocky.

"Proceed, old scout," I said, chewing my bread and butter.

"You know how, throughout my existence, I have yearned to visit New York and witness firsthand the marvelous, vibrant life of which I have read so much. I dread that fulfilling this dream will now prove impossible. I am aged and worn out; I seem to possess no strength remaining."

"Sad, Jeeves, what?"

"Extremely, sir."

"Sad nothing!" snapped Rocky. "It is sheer laziness. I visited her last Christmas, and she was bursting with vigor. Her physician personally assured me that she suffered from no ailments whatsoever. Yet, she insists on portraying herself as a hopeless invalid, forcing the doctor to concur. She holds a fixed delusion that a trip to New York would kill her; consequently, despite her lifelong ambition to travel, she remains stationary."

"A bit like the chap whose heart was 'in the Highlands a-chasing of the deer,' Jeeves?"

"The cases share certain parallels, sir."

"Continue, Rocky, dear boy."

"Therefore, I have determined that if I cannot personally enjoy the wonders of the city, I may at least experience them vicariously through you. Yesterday, after reading a poignant poem in the Sunday paper concerning a young man who desired something for his entire life and only obtained it when he was too elderly to enjoy it, I was deeply moved. It was a sad, touching tale."

"A thing," Rocky interjected bitterly, "that I have not been able to achieve in ten years."

"As you are aware, you will receive my inheritance upon my demise; however, I have never previously deemed it feasible to provide you with an allowance. I have now resolved to do so—one condition attached. I have instructed a firm of lawyers in New York to remit you a substantial monthly sum. My sole stipulation is that you reside in New York and indulge yourself as I have always wished. I require you to be my representative, to expend this capital as I would have done myself. I demand that you immerse yourself in the gay, kaleidoscopic life of New York. I expect you to be the life and soul of brilliant supper parties. Above all, indeed, I insist upon this, you must write me letters at least once a week providing a comprehensive account of your activities and the events unfolding in the city, allowing me to enjoy these things at second-hand, as my wretched health precludes my direct enjoyment. Remember, I anticipate full details, and no detail is too trivial to interest.—Your affectionate Aunt, ISABEL ROCKMETTELLER."

"What about it?" asked Rocky.

"What about it?" I echoed.

"Yes. What on earth am I supposed to do?"

Only then did I truly grasp the peculiar attitude of this gentleman, considering the sudden descent of a generous windfall from a clear blue sky. To my mind, this was an occasion for a beaming smile and a joyous shout; yet here the man sat, appearing and speaking as if Fate had struck his solar plexus. It astounded me.

"Aren't you excited?" I asked.

"Bucked?"

"If I were in your shoes, I would be thoroughly braced. I consider this arrangement quite lenient for you."

He emitted a yelp, stared at me for a moment, and then began discussing New York in a manner reminiscent of Jimmy Mundy, the reformer. Jimmy had recently arrived in New York on a crusade, and I had attended a brief engagement at the Garden a few days prior. While Jimmy had certainly delivered some blunt truths about the city, seemingly disliking it, by Jove, dear old Rocky made him look like a publicity agent for the metropolis!

"Precisely lenient!" he cried. "To have to relocate to New York! To abandon my cozy cottage and occupy a stifling, malodorous, overheated apartment in this Heaven-forsaken, festering Gehenna! To mingle night after night with a mob that believes life is a form of St. Vitus's dance, imagining they are having a good time merely because they generate sufficient noise for six and consume excessive alcohol for ten! I loathe New York, Bertie. I would not approach the place if I needed to consult editors. There is a blight upon it. It suffers from moral delirium tremens. It is the limit. Merely contemplating staying there for more than a day induces nausea. And you call this arrangement lenient for me!"
