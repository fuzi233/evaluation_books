The next morning’s post brought Margaret a letter with the faint, far-off scent of sea-air clinging to it, as if the paper had lain for a moment on some sun-warmed table near an open window, where salt and orange-blossom drifted in and out with the tide-breath. Edith’s hand—always hurrying, always turning corners too quickly—had dashed across the page with affectionate extravagance, as inconsequent as birdsong.

It began with rapture, and ran on without pausing to take breath.

“Oh, Margaret, it is worth a journey from England to see my boy! He is a superb little fellow, especially in his caps, and most especially in the one you sent him, you good, dainty-fingered, persevering little lady! Having made all the mothers here envious, I want to show him to somebody new, and hear a fresh set of admiring expressions; perhaps, that’s all the reason; perhaps it is not—nay, possibly, there is just a little cousinly love mixed with it; but I do want you so much to come here, Margaret! I’m sure it would be the very best thing for Aunt Hale’s health; everybody here is young and well, and our skies are always blue, and our sun always shines, and the band plays deliciously from morning till night; and, to come back to the burden of my ditty, my baby always smiles. I am constantly wanting you to draw him from me, Margaret. It does not signify what he is doing; that very thing is prettiest, gracefullest, best. I think I love him a great deal better than my husband, who is getting stout, and grumpy—what he calls ‘busy.’ No! he is not. He has just come in with news of such a charming picnic, given by the officers of the Hazard, at anchor in the bay below. Because he has brought in such a pleasant piece of news, I retract all I said just now. Did not somebody burn his hand for having said or done something he was sorry for? Well, I can’t burn mine, because it would hurt me, and the scar would be ugly; but I’ll retract all I said as fast as I can. Cosmo is quite as great a darling as baby, and not a bit stout, and as ungrumpy as ever husband was; only, sometimes he is very, very busy. I may say that without love—wifely duty— where was I?—I had something very particular to say, I know, once. Oh, it is this—Dearest Margaret!—you must come and see me; it would do Aunt Hale good, as I said before. Get the doctor to order it for her. Tell him that it’s the smoke of Milton that does her harm. I have no doubt it is that, really. Three months (you must not come for less) of this delicious climate—all sunshine, and grapes as common as blackberries, would quite cure her. I don’t ask my uncle—”

Here the lines tightened, as if Edith had felt her pen checked by a presence in the room; the handwriting grew more careful, the froth subsiding into decorum. Margaret could see it without being told—her father somewhere near, a quiet gravity pulling Edith’s words into better order.

“—because, I dare say, he disapproves of war, soldiers, and bands of music; at least, I know that many Dissenters are members of the Peace Society, and I am afraid he would not like to come; but, if he would, dear, pray say that Cosmo and I will do our best to make him happy; and I’ll hide up Cosmo’s red coat and sword, and make the band play all sorts of grave, solemn things; or, if they do play pomps and vanities, it shall be in double slow time. Dear Margaret, if he would like to accompany you and Aunt Hale, we will try and make it pleasant, though I’m rather afraid of any one who has done anything for conscience’ sake. You never did, I hope. Tell Aunt Hale not to bring any warm clothes, though I’m afraid it will be late in the year before you can come. But you have no idea of the heat here! I tried to wear my great beauty Indian shawl at a picnic. I kept myself up with proverbs as long as I could; ‘Pride must abide,’—and such wholesome pieces of pith; but it was no use. I was like mamma’s little dog Tiny with an elephant’s trappings on; smothered, hidden, killed with my finery; so I made it into a capital carpet for us all to sit down upon. Here’s this boy of mine, Margaret—if you don’t pack up your things as soon as you get this letter, and come straight off to see him, I shall think you’re descended from King Herod!”

Margaret let the sheet fall softly into her lap and sat quite still. The house around her seemed to contract into its familiar, muffled Milton quiet: a thin clink somewhere below of a fire-irons’ adjustment; Dixon’s tread on the stair—careful, yet too heavy ever to be thought light; and under everything, at a distance, the town’s incessant iron breath, a far grinding and throb that no closed curtain could quite deny. Edith’s world had sounded, in contrast, like brightness made audible—the “delicious” band-music, the laughter on a picnic cloth, the sea’s larger hush behind it all. Margaret longed, for one day only, to exchange her mother’s sick-room air for that easy sunshine, to feel youth return as simply as appetite returns when one steps from smoke into a clean wind.

Not yet twenty—and yet, in these last weeks, care had pressed on her so heavily that she felt old.

Then she read the letter again, and Edith’s very self rose before her—fluttering, affectionate, absurd, endearing—and Margaret laughed out, merrily and helplessly, as if the sound might shake off, for an instant, the weight upon her chest.

Mrs. Hale came in at that moment, leaning on Dixon’s arm. Margaret sprang up, her hands practised now in all the small ministries of illness—straightening a pillow, arranging the shawl so it would not slip, easing her mother down upon the sofa as if laying a tired child into bed. Mrs. Hale’s face had the worn translucence of long suffering; even her breath seemed a labour.

“What were you laughing at, Margaret?” she asked, when she had recovered from the exertion of settling herself.

“A letter I have had this morning from Edith. Shall I read it to you, mamma?”

Mrs. Hale’s eyes brightened a little—news from the child who had escaped all this—and Margaret read aloud. For a while the letter’s airy inconsequence did its work. Mrs. Hale smiled faintly, and kept stopping Margaret with questions—wondering what name Edith had given the boy, suggesting one after another, and reasons for each: a father’s name, a grandfather’s name, a saint’s, a fashionable one, a romantic one—each possibility turning her mind, for a moment, from her own weakness.

In the midst of these musings Mr. Thornton was announced.

He came in with fruit—peaches, their skins like soft bloom, as if they held the memory of summer and southern walls. He offered them with that grave gentleness Margaret had seen him use with her mother, the same steady kindness that seemed to him almost a point of honour. He could not—perhaps he would not—deny himself the chance of being in the same room as Margaret. Yet, as he entered, and his eyes took in everything at a glance—Mrs. Hale propped among cushions, Dixon hovering, Margaret near—he bowed to Margaret with a cold, distant formality, and afterwards behaved as though she had ceased to exist.

He spoke to Mrs. Hale, and to Mr. Hale when he came in, in tones softened from his usual strong decisiveness; he placed the fruit as if he were setting something precious within reach; he stayed a little, only long enough to offer this small attention and to say a few kindly words. Then, as he left, his grave offended eyes met Margaret’s for an instant in a farewell that had no warmth in it.

Margaret sat down again, silent, her colour gone. The room felt altered by his presence and his withdrawal—like a hearth when a log has been shifted, leaving the warmth uneven and the air suddenly full of draughts.

“Do you know, Margaret,” said Mrs. Hale, after a moment, “I really begin to like Mr. Thornton.”

Margaret did not answer at first.

“Well?” her mother persisted, with the fragile obstinacy of the sick.

Margaret forced out an icy little “Do you?”

“Yes! I think he is really getting quite polished in his manners.”

The word polished struck Margaret oddly—like the shine on something hard that has been rubbed to a brilliance. Her voice steadied.

“He is very kind and attentive—there is no doubt of that.”

“I wonder Mrs. Thornton never calls. She must know I am ill, because of the water-bed.”

“I dare say she hears how you are from her son.”

“Still, I should like to see her. You have so few friends here, Margaret.”

Margaret felt, with a sudden painful clearness, what lay beneath that simple sentence. It was not vanity in Mrs. Hale, nor mere loneliness; it was the instinct of a mother, already half withdrawing from the world, trying—tenderly, anxiously—to secure some other woman’s kindness around her daughter, as one might tuck extra blankets over a child before leaving the room for the last time.

But Margaret could not speak.

“Do you think,” Mrs. Hale went on, after a pause, “that you could go and ask Mrs. Thornton to come and see me? Only once—I don’t want to be troublesome.”

“I will do anything, if you wish it, mamma—but if—but when Frederick comes—”

The name fell into the room like a stone into still water.

“Ah, to be sure!” said Mrs. Hale; her hand tightened a little on the shawl at her throat. “We must keep our doors shut—we must let no one in. I hardly know whether I dare wish him to come or not. Sometimes I think I would rather not. Sometimes I have such frightful dreams about him.”

“Oh, mamma! we’ll take good care. I will put my arm in the bolt sooner than he should come to the slightest harm. Trust the care of him to me, mamma. I will watch over him like a lioness over her young.”

Mrs. Hale’s eyes filled, not with tears, but with that look of clinging dependence which pain and fear make in the helpless.

“When can we hear from him?”

“Not for a week yet, certainly—perhaps more.”

“We must send Martha away in good time. It would never do to have her here when he comes, and then send her off in a hurry.”

“Dixon is sure to remind us of that,” Margaret said. “I was thinking that, if we wanted any help in the house while he is here, we could perhaps get Mary Higgins. She is very slack of work, and is a good girl, and would take pains to do her best, I am sure, and would sleep at home, and need never come upstairs, so as to know who is in the house.”

“As you please. As Dixon pleases.” Then Mrs. Hale’s faint animation returned in a different direction, seizing on a small grievance as if it were safer than fear. “But, Margaret, don’t get to use these horrid Milton words. ‘Slack of work:’ it is a provincialism. What will your aunt Shaw say, if she hears you use it on her return?”

“Oh, mamma! don’t try and make a bugbear of aunt Shaw,” Margaret answered, and her laugh, though light, had an edge, like a spoon tapping thin china. “Edith picked up all sorts of military slang from Captain Lennox, and aunt Shaw never took any notice of it.”

“But yours is factory slang.”

“And if I live in a factory town, I must speak factory language when I want it. Why, mamma, I could astonish you with a great many words you never heard in your life. I don’t believe you know what a knobstick is.”

“Not I, child. I only know it has a very vulgar sound; and I don’t want to hear you using it.”

“Very well, dearest mother, I won’t. Only I shall have to use a whole explanatory sentence instead.”

“I don’t like this Milton,” Mrs. Hale said, sinking back. The familiar undertone of the town seemed to swell in Margaret’s ears at the words, as if the very walls were listening. “Edith is right enough in saying it’s the smoke that has made me so ill.”

Margaret started up. At that instant Mr. Hale entered. She had seen, of late, an uncertain impression forming in his mind—that perhaps the air of Milton had injured his wife’s health—and Margaret, with a quick fear, was desperate that nothing should deepen it into certainty. She could not tell whether he had heard Mrs. Hale’s last sentence. To forestall it, to cover it, she began speaking hurriedly, almost at random, catching at the nearest harmless thread.

“Mamma is accusing me of having picked up a great deal of vulgarity since we came to Milton.”

The word vulgarity, in Margaret’s meaning, had been no more than local phrases, the homely dialect of a place that had its own life and names. But behind Mr. Hale, just then entering, came Mr. Thornton.

Margaret felt, rather than saw, the change in him. His brow darkened, and something in his set face hardened at the very sound. She realised, with a hot rush of shame, how her careless sentence might strike him—how it could be heard as condemnation of the people and work that made Milton what it was, and of him as their representative. The sweetness in her—the instinct that hated to give pain where none was meant—urged her forward. She forced herself into a little greeting, and went on speaking, turning deliberately towards him, trying to make her meaning clear.

“Now, Mr. Thornton, though ‘knobstick’ has not a very pretty sound, is it not expressive? Could I do without it, in speaking of the thing it represents? If using local words is vulgar, I was very vulgar in the Forest,—was I not, mamma?”

It was unlike Margaret to thrust her conversation upon another; it came from anxiety, not vanity. And the moment she had finished speaking she blushed all over, conscious of herself, conscious of the effort, conscious—too—that he did not in the least take up her appeal. He seemed hardly to understand why she had said it at all. With a cold reserve of ceremonious movement, he passed her by and went straight to Mrs. Hale, as though Margaret’s voice were merely part of the room’s general sound, like the ticking of a clock.

The sight of him brought back to Margaret, with sudden sharpness, her mother’s wish: to see Mrs. Thornton—to draw, if possible, some thread of feminine kindness from that stern woman towards their house. Margaret sat in burning silence, vexed with herself, ashamed of her own awkwardness and of the strange difficulty she always had in keeping a steady, natural place when Mr. Thornton was present.

She heard her mother, slowly and timidly, ask him if Mrs. Thornton would come and see her—only once—soon; to-morrow, if it were possible. Mrs. Hale’s voice had that frail pleading note that illness brings, a note which, even to unwilling ears, is hard to resist.

Mr. Thornton promised that she should. He spoke a little longer—quietly, properly—and then he took his leave.

And when the door closed behind him, Margaret felt as if invisible chains had fallen away. She drew breath more freely; her voice, when she spoke to her parents, came back to its natural tone; even her hands seemed less clumsy.

He had never looked at her; and yet, in the very care with which he kept his eyes away, she read a knowledge of her—of where she sat, of where she moved—as exact as if he had been watching her all the while. If she spoke, he gave no sign of hearing; yet his next words to some one else would be shaped by what she had said, as if her speech had entered him in spite of himself. Sometimes he even answered her remark—but turned it, perversely, into an answer addressed to another person, as though her voice had not been the source.

It was not ignorance; it was wilfulness—wilful bad manners born of a deep offence.

And Margaret—who had believed herself merely indifferent—found her mind returning to him again and again. Not with what people called love; rather with regret that she had wounded him so deeply, and with a patient desire to come back to their former footing of antagonistic friendship. She understood now that, before all this, he had held a friend’s place in her regard, as he had in her father’s and mother’s.

There was, in her manner towards him in these days, a pretty humility, as if she were mutely apologising for the over-strong words that had sprung, in reaction, from the terrible scene of the riot. But he resented those words bitterly; they rang in his ears like a harsh phrase shouted over and over amid the din of looms. Proud of his own sense of justice, he compelled himself to every kindness he could offer to her parents; he even exulted in the power he showed in facing Margaret whenever he could think of any act that might give Mr. or Mrs. Hale pleasure. He told himself he disliked seeing her—told himself it was a duty, and nothing more.

He was mistaken.

To be in the room with her, and feel her presence—silent, vivid, inescapable—was a stinging pleasure. Yet he did not analyse himself; he mistook the very pulse of his own heart, and called it merely pride and offence.
