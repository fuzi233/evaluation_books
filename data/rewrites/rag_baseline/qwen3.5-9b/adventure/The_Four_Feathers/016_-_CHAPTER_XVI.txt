To reconstruct the architecture of Ethne's present moment, one must first dismantle the illusion of her past five years. Since the instant Captain Willoughby placed that soiled, yellowed feather—the relic of a shattered honor—into her palm, Colonel Durrance ceased to exist in her consciousness. He was erased, overwritten by the singular, consuming gravity of Harry Feversham. She had dragged Willoughby into the sanctuary of the garden enclosure, allowing his narrative to flood her mind, filling the hollow spaces of her own recollection with vivid details of Harry's gestures, the cadence of his voice, the specific geometry of his looks. In doing so, she had been swept violently from that August garden of sunlight and blooming color. Those five weary years, during which she had forced a smile of courage upon a world that demanded her submission, were scrubbed clean from her experience, as if they had never occurred. Perhaps she never truly understood the depth of her exhaustion until she lifted her head and found Colonel Durrance standing at the hedge's threshold, a living accusation in the greenery.

"Hush!" she commanded Willoughby, her voice trembling. Her face drained of blood, her eyes squeezing shut in a spasmodic recoil of pain. Yet, there was no luxury for grief. Their brief conversation with Willoughby had been a fleeting holiday, and the respite had ended abruptly. She was thrust back immediately into the crushing weight of responsibility those five years had imposed. If she could not perform the impossible task of forgetting—and she now knew with crystalline certainty that she never would—she must execute the next best strategy: the performance of oblivion. She must offer Durrance no sign that the debt of her heart remained unpaid. She must convince him that she bore no lingering affection for the man whose name lay buried beneath the soil of her soul.

He stood precisely at the entrance to the enclosure, then stepped inside. His speed in guessing her location rendered it unwise for him to encounter Willoughby, or even acknowledge his presence. Ethne scanned the perimeter for an exit, knowing instinctively that none existed. The creek blocked their front; three walls of dense, towering hedge imprisoned them behind and to the sides. There was only one gate, and Durrance himself had barred the path.

"Stay still," she whispered, her voice barely audible. "Do you recognize him?"

"Of course," Durrance replied, his tone casual, betraying nothing of the tension coiling in the air. "We served together for three years at Suakin. I heard rumors he had gone blind. I am relieved to find that false." He noted the ease of Durrance's stride, a physical testament to his claimed sight.

"Lower your voice," Ethne urged, her composure cracking slightly. "It is true. He *is* blind."

"One would never have guessed it," Durrance murmured, a note of pity coloring his words. "Consolation feels so futile in the face of such loss. What words could possibly comfort him?"

"None," Ethne snapped, her resolve hardening like iron.

Durrance remained rooted just within the enclosure, his gaze seemingly fixed directly upon the two figures seated on the bench.

"Ethne," he said—not a shout, but a quiet, commanding invocation—that single syllable constructed an illusion of perfect vision so complete it bordered on the supernatural.

"It is impossible that he is blind," Willoughby interjected, his skepticism rising. "He sees us."

"He sees nothing," Ethne corrected, her voice steel.

Once more, Durrance called out, "Ethne!" but this time the volume increased, laced with uncertainty.

"Do you hear?" Ethne whispered urgently to Willoughby. "He is unsure. Remain absolutely motionless."

"Why?"

"Because he must not know you are here," she explained, seizing Willoughby's arm in a vice-like grip to prevent any involuntary shift. Willoughby, sensing the gravity of her demeanor, fell silent. She sat rigid, mimicking the stillness she demanded of him, adopting a peculiar, huddled posture. She held her breath, her chest expanding and contracting with suppressed terror. Her eyes were locked on Durrance, wide with a profound, primal fear. Her face was drawn taut, every muscle frozen in a mask of anticipation, so that Willoughby, observing her, felt a strange, irrational excitement rise within him—a fear born solely of her remarkable apprehension. Unaccountably, he began to dread the discovery of their presence.

"He is advancing," he whispered.

"Not a word. Not a movement."

"Ethne!" Durrance cried again. He pushed further into the enclosure, moving steadily toward the bench. Ethne and Willoughby sat like statues, their eyes tracking his progress. He passed directly in front of the seat and halted, facing them squarely. Surely, Willoughby reasoned, he sees. His eyes were fixed upon them; he stood with the easy confidence of a man ready to speak. Even Ethne, who knew with absolute certainty that he was blind, began to harbor a flicker of doubt, her faith wavering under the weight of his apparent perception.

"Ethne!" he said again, returning to that initial, quiet tone. But receiving no response, he shrugged his shoulders and turned toward the creek. His back was now to them, yet Ethne's intimate knowledge of him allowed her to decipher nearly imperceptible signals in his bearing. Nowadays, his face revealed little, but the tilt of his head, the poised stance of his body, and the careless swing of his walking stick told her he was listening—with every ounce of his being. Her grip on Willoughby's arm tightened. They remained thus for a full minute, suspended in the heavy silence, before Durrance turned suddenly and took a brisk step toward the seat. Ethne, familiar with the man's deceptions, was prepared for such unpredictability. She did not stir; not a rustle disturbed the fabric of her skirt, and her hold on Willoughby remained unbroken.

"I wonder where in the world she could be," Durrance muttered aloud, breaking the spell. He walked backward, exiting the enclosure. Ethne did not release Willoughby's arm until Durrance vanished completely from sight.

"That was a narrow escape," Willoughby admitted once he was permitted to speak. "Imagine if Durrance had sat right atop us."

"Why speculate, since he did not?" Ethne asked, her calmness restored. "Did you tell me everything?"

"As far as I recall."

"And all of this transpired in the spring?"

"The spring of last year," Willoughby confirmed.

"Yes. I have a question. Why did you not bring this feather to me last summer?"

"Last year my leave was brief. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin."

"I see," Ethne said softly. "I trust you had good sport."

"It was satisfactory."

Last summer, Ethne had been free. Had Willoughby returned with news of victory instead of tales of mountain hunting, the trajectory of her life would have shifted irrevocably. A cry echoed loudly within her heart: *Why didn't you come?* Yet outwardly, she displayed no sign of the irreparable damage caused by his delay. Possessing the self-command of a woman forged in trial, she spoke with such feigned indifference that Willoughby assumed her inquiry stemmed from mere curiosity.

"You might have written," she suggested gently.

"Feversham implied there was no urgency," Willoughby replied. "Explaining the situation via correspondence would have been arduous. He instructed me to deliver the feather when I had the chance, and I lacked that opportunity until now. Truthfully, I feared finding Feversham had already returned before me."

"Oh, no," Ethne countered smoothly. "There was no possibility of that. Two feathers remain unredeemed before he dares ask me to reclaim mine."

Willoughby shook his head. "Feversham could never persuade Castleton and Trench to retract their accusations as he managed to sway me."

"Why not?"

"Major Castleton was killed when the square broke at Tamai."

"Killed?" Ethne exclaimed, laughing briefly and with satisfaction. Willoughby turned to stare at her, disbelief warring with the evidence of his ears. Her face, however, betrayed her clearly; she was thoroughly pleased. Ethne possessed a Celtic spirit that viewed death as a trivial interruption. She could hate with ferocity, becoming harder than iron toward those she despised. These three men were objects of her intense hatred. While she had technically agreed with them, surrendering the fourth feather to Harry Feversham merely to demonstrate compliance, she cared little for the gesture. She was delighted to learn that Major Castleton was removed from the world, finished with forever.

"And Colonel Trench?" she asked.

"No," Willoughby answered gravely. "Are you disappointed? He fares even worse. He was captured during a reconnaissance mission. He is currently a prisoner in Omdurman."

"Ah!" Ethne acknowledged.

"I suspect you lack comprehension of what captivity in Omdurman entails," Willoughby stated sternly. "If you disliked the captive, you would nonetheless feel pity."

"Not I," Ethne declared stubbornly.

"I will elucidate what it implies."

"No. I refuse to hear of Colonel Trench. Moreover, you must depart. Before you go, I require one answer," she said, rising from the bench. "What became of Mr. Feversham after he handed you that feather?"
