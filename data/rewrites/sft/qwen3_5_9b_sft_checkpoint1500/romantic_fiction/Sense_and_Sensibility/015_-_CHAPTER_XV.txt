The following day, Mrs. Dashwood visited Lady Middleton, accompanied by two of her daughters. Marianne declined to join them, citing a trivial preoccupation, a decision her mother readily accepted, having already received a letter from Willoughby promising to call upon them during their absence. Upon returning to Barton Cottage, they discovered Willoughby's carriage and servant waiting, confirming Mrs. Dashwood's hopes that he intended to pay his promised visit. However, as soon as they crossed the threshold, the scene inside shattered any sense of normalcy. Marianne emerged from the drawing-room in a state of profound distress, her handkerchief pressed to her face, and fled upstairs without a word. Alarmed, Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed her into the room she had vacated, finding only Willoughby standing by the fireplace, his back turned. When he turned around, his expression mirrored the turmoil that had clearly overwhelmed his sister.

"Is anything the matter?" Mrs. Dashwood cried, rushing forward. "Is she ill?"

"I hope not," Willoughby replied, attempting to project cheerfulness, though a strained smile quickly gave way. "If anyone is to be ill, it should be me. I am suffering from a heavy disappointment."

"A disappointment?"

"Yes. I find it impossible to keep my engagement. Mrs. Smith has, this morning, exercised the privilege of wealth upon a poor cousin by sending me on urgent business to London. I have just received my dispatches and bid farewell to Allenthorpe; and, seeking some sort of exhilaration, I have come to bid farewell to you."

"To London!" Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed, her voice trembling slightly. "And you depart this morning?"

"Almost at this moment."

"This is terribly unfortunate," Mrs. Dashwood stammered. "But Mrs. Smith must be grateful, and surely her business will not keep you away from us long."

Willoughby flushed deeply. "You are too kind, madam, but I have no intention of returning to Devonshire immediately. My visits to Mrs. Smith are rarely repeated within a year."

"And is Mrs. Smith your sole friend? Is Allenthorpe the only house in the neighborhood where you are welcome? For shame, Willoughby, could you not wait for an invitation here?"

His color deepened further, and he stared fixedly at the floor. "You are too generous."

Mrs. Dashwood exchanged a look of surprise with Elinor, who shared her astonishment. A heavy silence fell over the room until Mrs. Dashwood spoke again. "My dear Willoughby, let me assure you that Barton Cottage will always remain open to you. I need not urge you to return immediately, for you alone can judge whether doing so might offend Mrs. Smith, and I trust your judgment as fully as I trust your inclinations."

"My current engagements," Willoughby murmured, looking confused and troubled, "are of such a nature that I dare not flatter myself..."

He trailed off. Mrs. Dashwood, too stunned to speak, allowed another silence to stretch before Willoughby broke it with a faint, hollow smile. "It is folly to linger thus. I shall not torment myself any longer by remaining among friends whose company I can no longer enjoy."

With hurried farewells, he stepped out into the hall. They watched him mount his carriage, and within moments, it disappeared down the lane.

Mrs. Dashwood, overcome with emotion, retired to her own room to process the shock and alarm caused by this sudden departure. Elinor, however, remained in the drawing-room, her mind racing with anxiety and suspicion. She scrutinized every detail: Willoughby's abrupt exit, his affected cheerfulness, and most painfully, his refusal to accept her mother's offer of hospitality—a reluctance so contrary to his usual self and to the behavior of a devoted lover, it disturbed her deeply. Did he harbor no serious intent at all? Or had some terrible quarrel erupted between him and Marianne? The depth of Marianne's grief suggested a serious falling-out, yet given how utterly she adored him, a rupture seemed almost inconceivable.

Whatever the specifics, Marianne's anguish was undeniable. Elinor pondered with tender compassion how violently she must feel, fearing that this sorrow was not merely a release from pain but a duty she was feeding and encouraging.

An hour later, her mother returned. Though her eyes were red, her countenance retained a semblance of composure. "Our dear Willoughby is now several miles from Barton, Elinor," she said, sitting down to her work. "And with how heavy a heart does he travel?"

"It is all so strange," Elinor observed. "So abruptly gone! It seemed like the work of a moment. Last night he was with us so happy, so affectionate... and now, after barely ten minutes' notice—he is gone, with no intention of returning. Something far worse than what he told us must have occurred. He did not speak, he did not act like himself. You must have noticed the difference as keenly as I. What could it be? Have they quarreled? That is the only explanation for his refusal to stay."

"It was not a lack of inclination, Elinor," her mother answered firmly. "I saw plainly that he lacked the *power* to accept. I have thought everything through, and I can account for every aspect that initially seemed strange."

"You can?"

"Yes. I have explained it to myself in the most satisfactory way. Perhaps you, Elinor, who delight in doubting where others cannot, will not find it satisfying, but you shall not talk me out of my conviction. I am persuaded that Mrs. Smith suspects Willoughby's regard for Marianne, disapproves of it—perhaps because she has designs of her own—and is eager to send him away. The 'business' sent to London is likely an invention, an excuse to dismiss him. I believe this is what has happened. Furthermore, he knows she disapproves; he dares not, in his dependent position, confess his engagement to her, so he feels compelled to acquiesce to her schemes and abscond from Devonshire temporarily. You may argue that this may or may not be true, but I will hear no cavils unless you can offer a more satisfactory interpretation. Now, what have you to say?"

"Nothing," Elinor admitted softly. "For you have anticipated my answer."

"Then you would have said it might or might not be true. Oh, Elinor, how incomprehensible your feelings are! You prefer to take evil on credit rather than good. You look out for misery for Marianne and guilt for Willoughby rather than an apology. You are resolved to deem him blameworthy simply because he departed with less affection than usual. Do you make no allowance for inadvertence, or for spirits crushed by recent disappointment? Do you reject probabilities merely because they are not certainties? Is nothing owed to the man whom we all hold in such high esteem, to the possibility of motives that are unanswerable in themselves, though unavoidably secret for a time? After all, what exactly do you suspect him of?"

"I can hardly tell you myself," Elinor confessed. "But the inevitable consequence of such an alteration in his manners is a suspicion of something unpleasant. Yet, there is great truth in what you have urged regarding allowances for his conduct. Willoughby undoubtedly has sufficient reasons, and I wish to be candid. He may have valid grounds, and I will hope so. But it would have been more like Willoughby to acknowledge them at once. While secrecy may be advisable, I cannot help wondering at its practice here."

"Do not blame him for deviating from his character where necessity demands it. But you truly admit the justice of my defense?—I am happy, and he is acquitted."

"Not entirely," Elinor corrected gently. "It may be proper to conceal their engagement from Mrs. Smith—if they are engaged—and if so, it is highly expedient for Willoughby to avoid Devonshire for now. But this offers no excuse for hiding it from us."

"Concealing it from us! My dear child, do you accuse Willoughby and Marianne of deception? This is strange indeed, considering your eyes have been reproaching them daily for their incaution."

"I require no proof of their affection," Elinor insisted, "but of their engagement, I do."

"I am perfectly satisfied of both."

"Yet not a syllable has been uttered by either of them on the subject."

"I have not needed words where actions have spoken so plainly. Has not his behavior toward Marianne and toward all of us for the last fortnight declared that he loves and considers her his future wife, feeling for us the attachment of closest kin? Have we not perfectly understood each other? Has not my consent been daily sought through his looks, his manner, his attentive respect? My Elinor, is it possible to doubt their engagement? How could such a thought occur to you? How is it to be supposed that Willoughby, persuaded as he must be of your sister's love, would leave her, perhaps for months, without telling her of his affection—that they should part without a mutual exchange of confidence?"
