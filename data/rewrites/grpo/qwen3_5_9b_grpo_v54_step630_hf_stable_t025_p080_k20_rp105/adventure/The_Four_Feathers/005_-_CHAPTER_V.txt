Habit was a heavy cloak they both slipped on without thinking. The careless chatter of the ballroom spilled from their lips automatically; the mask of enjoyment plastered itself onto their faces so perfectly that no soul in Lennon House that night suspected a swift, violent severance had torn them apart. Harry Feversham watched Ethne laugh and talk as if the world owed her nothing, perpetually surprised by her own performance, failing to notice he wore the same gaunt disguise. When she swung past him, the light, rhythmic thud of her feet nearly convinced him her heart was still in the dance. It seemed she could command the color on her cheeks with a mere thought. Thus, they both wore brave faces, exactly as she had commanded. They even danced together.

Yet, all the while, Ethne carried a great, crushing load of pain and humiliation that threatened to collapse her at any moment, while Feversham felt those four feathers burning hot against his breast. It was a marvel to him that the entire company remained ignorant of their disgrace. He never approached a partner without the terrifying notion that she would turn upon him, spitting the contemptuous name that was now his on her tongue. Yet, strangely, he felt no fear. He would not have cared if the word had been spoken aloud. He had already lost Ethne. He watched her, scanning her circle of guests in vain, searching for a fit comparison to measure himself against. There were women—pretty, graceful, even beautiful—but Ethne stood apart, defined by the particular architecture of her beauty. The broad forehead, the perfect arch of her eyebrows, the great steady, clear grey eyes, the full red lips capable of dimpling into tenderness or shutting level with resolution, and the royal grace of her carriage marked her out to Feversham's thinking, a beacon in any crowd. He watched her with a despairing amazement that he had ever possessed a chance of owning her.

Only once did her endurance crack, and then only for a fleeting second. She was dancing with Feversham, and as she glanced toward the windows, she saw the daylight beginning to bleed through the blinds, pale and cold.

"Look!" she said, and suddenly Feversham felt her entire weight press against his arms. Her face drained of color, turning tired and very grey. Her eyes squeezed shut tight, then fluttered open again. He feared she would faint.

"The morning at last!" she exclaimed, and then, in a voice worn as thin as her complexion, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain."

"Hush!" whispered Feversham. "Courage! A few minutes more—only a very few!" He stopped, standing firm before her until her strength returned.

"Thank you!" she said gratefully, and the bright wheel of the dance caught them in its spokes again.

It was strange that he should be exhorting her to courage while she thanked him for support; but the irony of this queer, momentary reversal of their positions escaped both of them. Ethne was too exhausted by the strain of those final hours, and Feversham, having learned from that single failure of her endurance, from the drawn aspect of her face and the depths of agony in her eyes, understood how deeply he had wounded her. He no longer thought, "I have lost her"; he no longer considered his loss at all. He heard her words, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain," ringing down the world with him, persistent in his ears, spoken with the very accent of her voice. He was certain he would hear them at the end, above the voices of any who should stand around him when he died, hearing in them his condemnation. For it was not right.

The ball finished shortly thereafter. The last carriage drove away, and those staying in the house sought the smoking-room or retired upstairs to bed, segregated by sex. Feversham, however, lingered in the hall with Ethne. She understood why.

"There is no need," she said, standing with her back to him as she lit a candle, "I have told my father. I told him everything."

Feversham bowed his head in acquiescence.

"Still, I must wait and see him," he said.

Ethne did not object, but she turned and looked at him quickly, her brows drawn in a frown of perplexity. To wait for her father under such circumstances seemed to argue a certain courage. Indeed, she felt a flicker of apprehension as she heard the door of the study open and Dermod's footsteps echo on the floor. Dermod walked straight up to Harry Feversham, looking for once truly what he was—a very old man—and stood there staring into Feversham's face with a muddled, bewildered expression. Twice he opened his mouth to speak, but no words came. In the end, he turned to the table, lit his candle and Harry Feversham's, and then turned back toward Feversham. He moved rather quickly, so that Ethne took a step forward as if to interpose herself, but he did nothing more than stare at Feversham again for a long, agonizing time. Finally, he took up his candle.

"Well—" he said, and stopped. He snuffed the wick with the scissors and began again. "Well—" he said, and stopped once more. Apparently, his candle had offered no suitable expressions. He stared into the flame now instead of into Feversham's face, for an equal length of time. He could think of nothing whatever to say, and yet he was conscious that something must be said. In the end, he said lamely:

"If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

Thereupon, he walked heavily up the stairs. The old man's forbearance was perhaps not the least part of Harry Feversham's punishment.

It was broad daylight when Ethne was finally alone within her room. She drew up the blinds and threw the windows wide. The cool, fresh air of the morning struck her like a draught of spring water. She looked out upon a world as yet unillumined by colors, finding therein an image of her days to come. The dark, tall trees looked black; the winding paths, a singular dead white; the very lawns were dull and grey, though the dew lay upon them like a network of frost. It was a noisy world, however, for all its aspect of quiet. For the blackbirds were calling from the branches and the grass, and down beneath the overhanging trees, the Lennon flowed in music between its banks. Ethne drew back from the window. She had much to do that morning before she slept. For she designed, with her natural thoroughness, to make an end at once of all her associations with Harry Feversham. She wished that from the moment she next woke, she might never encounter a single thing that could recall him to her memory. And with a sort of stubborn persistence, she went about the work.

But she changed her mind. In the very process of collecting together the gifts he had given her, she altered her course. For each gift she looked upon held its history, and the days before this miserable night, which had darkened her happiness, came back to her one by one slowly as she gazed at them. She determined to keep one thing that had belonged to Harry Feversham—a small thing, a thing of no value. At first, she chose a penknife he had once lent her and she had forgotten to return. But the next instant she dropped it and hurried away. For she was after all an Irish girl, and though she did not believe in superstitions, where superstitions were concerned, she preferred to be on the safe side. She selected his photograph in the end and locked it away in a drawer.

She gathered the rest of his presents together, packed them carefully in a box, fastened it, addressed it, and carried it down to the hall so the servants might dispatch it in the morning. Then, returning to her room, she took his letters, made a little pile of them on the hearth, and set them alight. They took some time to consume, but she waited, sitting upright in her armchair while the flame crept from sheet to sheet, discolouring the paper, blackening the writing like a stream of ink, and leaving in the end only flakes of ashes like feathers, and white flakes like white feathers. The last sparks were barely extinguished when she heard a cautious step on the gravel beneath her window.

It was broad daylight, but her candle was still burning on the table at her side, and with a quick, instinctive movement, she reached out her arm and put the light out. Then she sat very still and rigid, listening. For a while, she heard only the blackbirds calling from the trees in the garden and the throbbing music of the river. Afterward, she heard the footsteps again, cautiously retreating; and in spite of her will, in spite of her formal disposal of the letters and the presents, she was mastered all at once—not by pain or humiliation, but by an overpowering sense of loneliness. She seemed to be seated high on an empty world of ruins. She rose quickly from her chair, and her eyes fell upon a violin case. With a sigh of relief, she opened it, and a little while after, one or two of the guests sleeping in the house chanced to wake up and heard floating down the corridors the music of a violin played very lovingly and low. Ethne was not aware that the violin she held was the Guarnerius that Durrance had sent to her. She only understood that she had a companion to share her loneliness.
