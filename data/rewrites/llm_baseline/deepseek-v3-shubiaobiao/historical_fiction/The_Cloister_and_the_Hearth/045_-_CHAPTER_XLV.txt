The family sat gathered around the table in the hosier’s house, the air thick with unease, when their mother returned. Her sharp eyes swept over them, lingering longest on Cornelis and Sybrandt, before she placed Gerard’s letter flat upon the wood. Though she could not read, she traced the lines with her finger, reciting each word from memory, as if to prove—if only to herself—that she knew its contents well.

Then she lifted her head, and her gaze hardened. The storm that had simmered within her now broke.

Catherine’s voice was a torrent, swift and relentless, a flood of anger and grief that no man could hope to match. Her words lashed like a whip, each syllable striking with precision.

*"I’ve long suspected you of poisoning your father against Gerard, of whispering in Ghysbrecht’s ear like serpents. And now—here it is in his own hand! You’ve driven your own brother from his home, sent him into exile, and torn the heart from my breast. Do you think I’ve forgotten what you were as boys? The rest were a comfort—but you? Mischief trailed behind you like shadows. A woman’s labor could scarce keep your clothes whole, for where honest work wears cloth, wicked play slashes it to ribbons!"*

Her breath came sharp, her chest heaving.

*"And now—where does this sudden wealth come from? Since that night you slunk home late, Cornelis, your pockets heavy, your head swollen with secrets. Sloth and greed make poor bedfellows. If you didn’t earn it, then you stole it—and from whom? Some poor soul weaker than you, no doubt, for a man would have broken you with his bare hands!"*

She scoffed, shaking her head.

*"But your coin has shown me one truth—how you’d squander our name if it ever fell to you. I’ve watched you these weeks, spending like lords while I scrape and save to keep you fed. No more. This house, this trade—they will never be yours. Gerard is our heir. My Gerard, whom you cast out and left for dead!"*

Her voice cracked, but her resolve did not waver.

*"You thought to cut him from our lives, but you failed. You severed the stalk, yet left the seed. Margaret Brandt carries his child—my grandchild—and I pray it is a son. He will be our heir. He will outlive you both, for a rotten heart turns the body to dust before its time."*

She turned to Kate, her tone softening only slightly.

*"Fetch Gerard’s things from the drawer. We’ll take them to Sevenbergen soon—Peter Buyskens’ cart will serve. Margaret is his wife, no matter what lies Ghysbrecht spins. She is my daughter now, as much as you are, Kate."*

Then her gaze returned to Cornelis and Sybrandt, cold as winter.

The brothers shrank beneath her words, their faces dark with shame and resentment. They slunk away, avoiding her stare, and for many days after, she spoke not a word to them—neither kindness nor rebuke.

*Liberaverat animum suum.* She had freed her soul.
