"Of course, he’ll marry Shirley," Caroline murmured to herself as she rose that morning. "And he should—she can help him in ways I never could." But the thought that followed was sharp and cold: "Yet when they are married… I will be forgotten. Truly forgotten. And what then? What will become of me when Robert is no longer mine? Where shall I turn? _My_ Robert—I wish I could say those words without guilt. But I am nothing but poverty and weakness; she has wealth, power, beauty, and love. I cannot deny it. This isn’t a petty rivalry. She loves him—not with lesser feelings, but with a depth that makes him proud to be loved. There’s no fault to find. Let them wed. But after that… I shall mean nothing to him. To pretend to be his sister? That would be a lie I could not bear. I want either everything or nothing from a man like Robert. No half-measures, no false kindness. Once they are united, I will leave them. I cannot stay, playing at friendship while my heart bleeds. I couldn’t be their friend any more than their enemy. I couldn’t stand between them—or crush them beneath me. Robert is a great man, in my eyes. I have loved him, I do love him, I must love him. If I could be his wife, I would. Since I cannot, I must go where I will never see him again. There is only one choice—to cling to him as if I were part of him, or to be torn from him as far as the poles are apart. So tear us apart, Providence. Do it quickly."

These thoughts swirled through her mind late in the afternoon when she saw a familiar figure pass by the parlour window. Miss Keeldar strolled past, her walk slow, her expression a blend of quiet longing and careless ease—the look she wore when still, but which, when stirred, gave way to warmth and charm, so that her laughter and smile carried a tender sentiment that made mirth feel like something pure and alive, not hollow or mocking.

"What do you mean by not coming to see me this afternoon, as you promised?" Shirley asked briskly upon entering.

"I wasn’t in the mood," Caroline replied honestly.

Shirley had already fixed her with an unnerving gaze.

"No," she said, "I can see you’re not in the mood for liking me. You’re in one of your shadowed, stormy moods—when even another person’s presence feels like an intrusion. You have such moods. Do you know it?"

"Do you intend to stay long, Shirley?"

"Yes. I’ve come for tea—and I’ll have it before I leave. So I’m going to take off my bonnet without asking."

She did so, standing on the rug with her hands behind her back.

"You’ve a peculiar expression on your face," Shirley continued, watching her closely, though not harshly—more with pity than judgment. "You look wonderfully self-contained, like a deer wounded in solitude, afraid I’ll discover you’re hurt and bleeding."

"I’ve never feared you."

"But sometimes you dislike me. Often you avoid me. Shirley can feel when she’s being slighted. If you hadn’t walked home with him last night, you’d be a different woman today. What time did you arrive at the rectory?"

"By ten."

"Humph. It took you three-quarters of an hour to walk a mile. Was it you, or Moore, who lingered?"

"Shirley, you’re talking nonsense."

"_He_ talked nonsense—though I doubt it. Or he looked it, which is worse. I can see his reflection in your eyes right now. I’m tempted to challenge him—if only I could find a trustworthy second. I’m furious. I was last night, and I’ve been all day."

"You don’t ask why," Shirley went on after a pause, "you little silent, overly modest creature. And you don’t deserve that I pour out my secrets without invitation. Honestly, I could have followed Moore yesterday evening with deadly intent. I have pistols—and I know how to use them."

"Nonsense, Shirley! Who would you shoot—me or Robert?"

"Neither, perhaps. Maybe myself. More likely a bat or a branch. Your cousin is a puppy—a quiet, serious, sensible, ambitious puppy. I see him now, standing before me, speaking his half-stern, half-gentle words, pressing his purpose down on me, as I know he does. And yet—I have no patience with him!"

Miss Keeldar began pacing the room, repeating with fervor that she had no patience with men—especially her tenant.

"You’re wrong," Caroline insisted, anxious. "Robert isn’t a puppy or a flirt. I can vouch for that."

"_You_ vouch for it? Do you think I’ll accept your word on this? There’s no testimony I’d trust less than yours. For Moore’s sake, you’d cut off your own hand."

"But not lie. And if I speak truth, I must say he was merely civil to me last night—that was all."

"I never asked what he was. I can guess. I saw him from the window take your hand in his long fingers just as he left my gate."

"That means nothing. I’m not a stranger. I’m an old acquaintance—his cousin."

"I’m indignant," Shirley declared. "That’s all there is to it. My peace is shattered by his meddling. He keeps inserting himself between you and me. Without him, we’d be friends. But this six-foot puppy casts a constant eclipse over our friendship. Again and again, he crosses my path, blinding my light. He turns me into a bore, a nuisance in your eyes."

"No, Shirley, no."

"He does. You didn’t want me this afternoon, and I feel it deeply. You’re naturally reserved, but I’m social—unable to live alone. If we were left undisturbed, I’d cherish you enough to sit beside you forever, never wishing to be rid of you. But you can’t say the same about me."

"Shirley, I can say anything you wish. I like you."

"You’ll miss me tomorrow, Lina."

"I won’t. I grow more fond of you every day. I’m too English to rush into deep friendships overnight—but you’re so much better than most young women. You’re unlike anyone else. I value you, I respect you—you’re never a burden. Do you believe me?"

"Partly," Shirley replied, smiling slightly, unconvinced. "But you’re unusual. Quiet as you seem, there’s strength and depth inside you—hard to reach, hard to understand. Still, you’re clearly unhappy."

"And unhappy people are rarely good," Caroline ventured.

"Not at all. I mean that unhappy people are often distracted, not in the mood for conversation with someone like me. Worse, some unhappiness doesn’t just weigh down—it eats away. And I fear that’s yours. Will pity help, Lina? If it will, take some from me. I offer plenty—and it’s genuine."

"Shirley, I never had a sister—you never had one. But it strikes me now how sisters feel—affection woven into life, unshaken by shocks, crushed only briefly by quarrels, springing back stronger when the pressure lifts. Affection that no passion can truly surpass—even love itself can only compete. Love hurts, Shirley. It tears us apart, burns our strength. But affection gives comfort, not fire. When you’re near—only you—my soul is calm, soothed. Do you believe me now?"

"I always believe when the idea pleases me. Then we really are friends, Lina, despite the black eclipse?"

"We really are," Caroline answered, drawing Shirley toward her and making her sit. "No matter what happens."

"Then let’s talk about something else than the Troubler." But at that moment the rector entered, and the subject was dropped until Shirley’s departure. As she lingered in the hall, she turned to Caroline.

"Caroline, I need to tell you—I carry a heavy weight. My conscience troubles me, as if I’ve committed or am about to commit a crime. Not my private conscience—but my duty as landowner, as lord of the manor. I’ve fallen under the grip of an eagle with iron claws. A stern influence I don’t fully approve, but cannot resist. Something will happen soon—something I deeply regret. To ease my mind and prevent harm, I mean to begin a series of good works. Don’t be surprised if I suddenly become wildly charitable. I don’t know how to start—but you must advise me. We’ll talk more tomorrow. And please invite Miss Ainley up to Fieldhead. I have an idea of placing myself under her guidance. Won’t she have a precious pupil? Drop her a hint, Lina, that though well-meaning, I’m rather neglected—so she won’t be shocked by my ignorance of clothing societies and such things."

The next day, Caroline found Shirley seated solemnly at her desk, surrounded by an account book, banknotes, and a full purse. She looked grave, yet puzzled.

"I’ve been reviewing our weekly household expenses," Shirley explained. "Trying to find where I might cut back. I also just spoke with Mrs. Gill, the cook. I sent her away convinced I’m losing my mind. I lectured her on thrift in a way entirely new to her. I was so eloquent on economy that I surprised myself—for this is a fresh idea. I’ve never thought or spoken on it before. But it’s all theory. In practice, I couldn’t reduce a single pound of butter, nor investigate the fate of dripping, lard, bread, cold meat, or any kitchen item. We don’t hold illuminations at Fieldhead, but I couldn’t ask about unexplained pounds of candles. We don’t wash for the parish, yet I silently noted entries of soap and bleaching powder that would satisfy any curious investigator. None of us are carnivores—Mrs. Pryor, Mrs. Gill, or I—but I only blinked and widened my eyes when I saw butcher bills whose numbers seemed to prove otherwise—falsehood, I mean. Caroline, you may laugh, but you can’t change me. I’m weak in certain areas. I feel it. There’s a base alloy of moral cowardice in me. I blushed and lowered my head before Mrs. Gill—when she should have been confessing to me. I couldn’t summon the courage even to suggest, let alone prove, that she was cheating. I lack calm dignity, true courage."

"Shirley, what is this sudden self-criticism? My uncle, who rarely speaks kindly of women, says there aren’t ten thousand men in England as genuinely fearless as you."

"I’m fearless physically. I’m never nervous about danger. I wasn’t shaken when Mr. Wynne’s great red bull charged at me, bellowing, head lowered, across the cowslip field. But I was afraid of seeing Mrs. Gill humiliated. You, whom no argument can persuade to pass a bull, however quiet, would have firmly shown my housekeeper her mistake, gently corrected her, and forgiven her if she repented. I couldn’t do that. Yet, despite my flaws, I still live within our means. I have money—and I must use it wisely. The poor of Briarfield are suffering. They must be helped. What should I do, Lina? Should I distribute the cash at once?"

"No, Shirley. You’ll mismanage it. I’ve noticed your charity is usually random—giving shillings and half-crowns carelessly, which invites abuse. You need a guide. You mentioned Miss Ainley yourself. I’ll speak to her. Until then, promise not to spend a penny. How much you have, Shirley! You must feel very rich."

"Yes. I feel important. It’s not a fortune, but I feel responsible for it—and that responsibility weighs more than I expected. They say families in Briarfield are nearly starving. Some of my own tenants are in terrible straits. I must help them."

"Some say we shouldn’t give alms to the poor, Shirley."

"They’re fools. For those who aren’t hungry, it’s easy to talk about the shame of charity. But they forget how short and bitter life is. None of us lives long. Let’s help each other through hardship, without caring for the empty philosophy of others."

"But you already help, Shirley. You give generously."

"Not enough. I must give more—or one day, my brother’s blood will cry out against me. After all, if political agitators come here and attack my property, I’ll defend it like a tigress. I know I will. Let me listen to Mercy while she’s near. Her voice drowned by shouts of defiance, and I’ll be filled with rage to resist. If the poor rise in mob form, I’ll turn against them as an aristocrat. If they bully me, I’ll defy them. If they attack, I’ll fight—and win."

"You speak like Robert."

"I feel like Robert—only fiercer. Let them touch Robert, his mill, his interests, and I’ll hate them. Right now, I’m not a noblewoman, nor do I see the poor as inferiors. But if they violently wrong me or mine—and then demand to dictate to us—I’ll forget pity and respect, lost in scorn for their ignorance and fury at their insolence."

"Shirley, how your eyes blaze!"

"Because my soul burns. Would you, any more than I, let Robert be overwhelmed by numbers?"

"If I had your power to aid Robert, I would use it as you plan. If I could be such a friend to him as you can, I’d stand by him—until death."

"And now, Lina, though your eyes don’t flash, they glow. You lower your lashes, but I saw a spark. Still, it’s not time for conflict. What I want is to _prevent_ it. I cannot forget—day or night—that these bitter feelings stem from suffering. The poor wouldn’t hate or envy us if they didn’t believe we’re happier. To ease their pain, and thus lessen their hatred, I’ll give freely from my abundance. And to make it count, I’ll give wisely. We must bring clear, calm sense to our efforts. Go fetch Miss Ainley."

Without a word, Caroline put on her bonnet and left. It may seem odd that neither she nor Shirley consulted Mrs. Pryor—yet they knew instinctively it would only cause her distress. Mrs. Pryor was wiser, better read, deeper-thinking than Miss Ainley. But she lacked administrative drive. She’d gladly give her small share in secret—but not lead public initiatives. She couldn’t originate plans. Shirley knew this. So she spared her the discomfort of useless discussions that would only highlight her limitations.

It was a bright day for Miss Ainley when summoned to Fieldhead to shape a plan so close to her heart. Seated with honor at a table, pen, paper, ink, and—most thrilling—actual funds before her, she was asked to design a practical scheme to relieve Briarfield’s destitute. She knew them all, studied their needs, and had long known how best to help—had the means existed. Now, she felt capable, and a quiet joy warmed her kind heart as she answered the girls’ questions with clarity and confidence, showing how deeply she understood her neighbors.

Shirley placed £300 at her disposal. At sight of the money, Miss Ainley’s eyes filled with tears—she already saw the hungry fed, the naked clothed, the sick comforted. She swiftly drafted a simple, thoughtful plan. She assured them brighter days would follow, for she believed the lady of Fieldhead’s example would inspire others. She’d seek additional donations and build a fund—but first, she must consult the clergy. On this point, she was firm: Mr. Helstone, Dr. Boultby, Mr. Hall—must be consulted. Not only Briarfield, but Whinbury and Nunnely must be included. It would be presumption, she said, to act without their approval.

To Miss Ainley, the clergy were sacred. No matter how insignificant the individual, his office made him holy. Even the curates—whom she privately considered too trivial to tie her patten-strings—she saw as budding saints. No matter how obvious their faults, she remained blind to them. The white surplice covered a multitude of sins.

Shirley, aware of this innocent devotion, insisted that the curates have no say in the funds. Their interference must be prevented. The rectors, of course, were paramount—they had experience, wisdom, and Mr. Hall, at least, had compassion. But the younger men? They must be kept in place, taught silence and subordination.

Miss Ainley heard this with horror. But Caroline stepped in, gently praising Mr. Sweeting, calming her. Sweeting was indeed her favorite. She tried to respect Messrs. Malone and Donne, but the sponge cake and cowslip wine she offered Sweeting during visits came with maternal affection. She once served the same to Malone—he scorned it, and she never repeated it. To Donne, she always offered it—and was delighted when he ate two pieces and tucked a third into his pocket.

Eager to act, Miss Ainley wanted to walk ten miles to visit the rectors immediately. But Shirley stopped her, suggesting instead a gathering that evening at Fieldhead. The clergy would meet, discuss the plan in private council.

Shirley managed to assemble the senior priests, and before Miss Ainley arrived, she had charmed them into excellent spirits. She handled Dr. Boultby—a stubborn Welshman, hot-tempered but effective—and Mr. Helstone with kindness. She took them through the garden, gathered flowers, treated them like beloved elders. Mr. Hall, she left to Caroline—though in truth, he had chosen Caroline himself.

And so, under Shirley’s careful guidance, the wheels of mercy began to turn.
