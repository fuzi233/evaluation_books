Not long after, word reached Rotterdam that Margaret Van Eyck had died and been buried. By a will made the year before, she had bequeathed all her possessions—after funeral costs and small gifts to Reicht Heynes—to her beloved daughter, Margaret Brandt, with a heartfelt request that Reicht remain in her service until she married.

Through this legacy, Margaret inherited a well-furnished house, along with paintings and sketches that would today be considered priceless. Among them was one she cherished above all others: a painting titled *A Betrothal*. The solemnity of the ceremony was captured in the man’s grave expression and the woman’s quiet contentment. Though nearly the entire composition was painted by Margaret Van Eyck, Jan van Eyck had completed the rest. The details were so finely rendered they still astonish viewers today. Margaret sent word to Reicht to stay in the house until she could summon the courage to return there—until she could bear to face the silence where her mother’s voice once rang out—and to guard especially the picture “in the little cubboord,” meaning the diptych.

Soon after, Luke Peterson returned home and learned that Gerard had become a monk.

He nearly lost his mind with joy. He rushed to Margaret and said, “Heed, mistress. If he cannot marry you, I can.”

“You?” Margaret replied. “Why, I have seen him.”

“But he is a friar now.”

“He was my husband, and my son’s father long before he ever took vows. And I have seen him—I’ve seen him.”

Luke was utterly bewildered. “I’ll tell you what,” he said. “I have a cousin who is a lawyer. I’ll go ask him whether you are legally married or free.”

“Nay,” Margaret answered firmly. “I shall ask my own heart, not a lawyer. Is this how you regard me? To go making me the talk of the town? Oh, fie!”

“That’s already done, without a word from me.”

“But not by those who hold me in respect. And if you do it, never come near me again.”

“Ay,” Luke sighed, “you’re like a dove to everyone else—but to me, you’re a hard-hearted tyrant.”

“It’s your own fault, dear Luke, for loving me. That’s what keeps me from being as kind to you as I wish. Listen, Luke, my bonny lad. I am rich now. I can make my friends happy—even if not myself. Look around the street, look through the parish. There are many women fairer than I, and none so worn down by sorrow. Look high, and choose your own. Speak to the girl yourself, and I’ll speak to her mother; they shall not refuse you. Take my word for it.”

“I see what you mean,” Luke said, turning red. “But if I can’t have your love, I want none of your money. I was your servant when you were poorer than I—much poorer. No. If you’d rather be a friar’s leman than an honest man’s wife, you’re not the woman I thought you were. So let us part, without malice. Seek your comfort on that road—where no woman has ever found it. As for me, I’ll live and die a bachelor. Good evening, mistress.”

“Farewell, dear Luke,” she said softly. “And God forgive you for speaking thus to me.”

For days, Margaret dreaded—and yet longed for—the moment she might meet Gerard again. She told herself, “I wonder not that he stays away. I would too.” Yet he must know he was a father. The desire to see their child would surely overcome everything. “And,” she whispered to herself, “if meeting him does not kill me, I feel I shall be better afterward than I am now.”

But day after day passed, and no word came. A cold dread began to seep into her soul. What if his absence was deliberate? What if he had gone to other monks, men of colder hearts, who had told him never to return? The cloister had already shown itself as merciless to true lovers as death itself.

At that thought, life seemed to drain from her.

Now, for the first time, deep anger mingled with her grief and fear. “Could he ever have loved me? To flee from me and our child without a word? Why, even poor Luke thinks more of me than he does.”

While her heart wrestled with these thoughts, Giles burst in, roaring with excitement. “I’ve hit the mark! Our Gerard is Vicar of Gouda!”

To understand this triumph, a brief glimpse of Giles’s life at court is needed. Some months before, his mind had suddenly awakened. He himself marked the change on June 8th, when, swinging by one hand along a rope strung with washing in the drying yard, something cracked inside his head—and lo! his intellect was unleashed. At court, his sharp wit, blunt speech, booming voice, and tiny frame made him a force to be reckoned with. Without the last, he might have ended up at the gallows. The young Duchess of Burgundy and Marie, the heir apparent, both doted on him, as noblewomen have always doted on dwarfs. The court poet wrote verses in his honor, buttering his back enough to stew Goliath. Even Giles’s own rough sayings were turned into elaborate poetry by the poet.

The result was a letter sent to Eli, titled:

*“The High and Puissant Princess Marie of Bourgogne Her Lytel Jantilman Hys Complaynt of Y’ Coort, and Praise of a Rusticall Lyfe, Versificated, and Empapyred by Me the Lytel Jantilman’s Right Lovynge and Obsequious Servitor, etc.”*

But Giles reached his peak with a clever mix of brains and brawn. On the eve of a grand joust, he challenged the Duke’s giant to a strength contest. The challenge sparked laughter and anticipation.

Giles had a tall pole planted ready. At the appointed hour, he climbed it like a squirrel, then bent his body at a right angle and held it—before sliding down so fast the princess shrieked and hid her face, fearing her little Hercules had perished.

The giant managed only ten feet, then looked up and down in despair, sweating profusely, before descending to argue.

“It wasn’t greater strength,” he claimed. “It was smaller size.”

The crowd laughed loudly. The truth was clear: Giles excelled at climbing poles; the giant excelled only at excuses. In short, Giles had measured their minds—with his own body.

“Come,” said Giles, “if you insist, I’ll wrestle you, my lad—if you’ll let me blindfold your eyes.”

The giant, stung by defeat and confident he could win this way, agreed.

“Madam,” said Giles, “see yon blind Samson? At my signal, he shall bow low and remove his hat to me.”

“How may that be, if he’s blind?” asked a maid of honour.

“I’ll wager on Giles for one,” said the princess.

“That’s my affair.”

When several wagers were placed, Giles struck the giant in the groin. He doubled over (the bow), and his hat fell off.

The company roared with delight at this clever jest. Giles fled. The giant pursued, but too late—Giles had prepared a hidden door in the wall, just wide enough for him, not a giant, and painted it to blend perfectly. He tore it open and vanished, leaving behind a message written large on the back of the door:

> *Long limbs, big body, panting wit,*
> *By wee and wise is bet and bit.*

After this, Giles became a power.

Now, let him speak.

Finding Margaret skeptical about the news and doubtful that a dwarf could influence Church affairs, he recounted:

“When the princess summoned me to her bedroom, as usual, I did not come full of mirth or country sayings, as I often do, but dull as lead.

‘What ails you?’ she asked. ‘Are you sick?’
‘In heart,’ I replied.
‘Alas,’ she said, ‘he is in love.’
Five brazen maids of honour giggled.
‘Not so mad as that,’ I said, ‘seeing what I see at court of women folk.’

‘There, ladies,’ she said, ‘let him be. He’s a liberal mannikin, and still gives more than he takes in saucy words.’

‘In all seriousness,’ she said, ‘what troubles you?’

I told her I was thinking, and what troubled me was that common folk sometimes kept their word—but princes never.

‘Heyday!’ she said. ‘Your shafts fly high this morning.’
I said, ‘Ay, because they hit the truth.’

She said I was as keen as keen, but it became neither me nor her to play riddles. ‘Stand aside, mesdames,’ she said, ‘and speak freely.’ For she saw I was in earnest.

I began to tremble—mind you, she could shed freedom and don dignity faster than slipping from dressing gown to kirtle of state. But I softened my voice like honey (why do you smile?), and said: ‘Madam, one evening five years ago, as you sat with your mother, the Countess of Charolois—now in heaven, worse luck—you with your lute, she with her tapestry—do you remember a fair youth entering with a letter from a painter named Margaret Van Eyck?’

She said she thought she did. ‘Was it not a tall youth, exceeding comely?’

‘Ay, madam,’ I said. ‘He was my brother.’

‘Your brother?’ she said, staring at me.
(What are you smiling at?)

So I told her all that passed between her and Gerard—how she wished to give him a bishopric, but the good countess said, ‘Gently, Marie—he is too young.’ Then both promised him a living. ‘Yet,’ I said, ‘he has been a priest for years, and no living. Hence my bile.’

‘Alas!’ she said. ‘It is not my doing. All you’ve said is true—and more. I remember my dear mother saying to me, “See thou to it if I be not here.”’ Then she cried, ‘Ay, dear mother, no word of thine shall fall to the ground.’

Seeing her moved, I said quickly, ‘Madam, the Vicar of Gouda died last week.’ (For when you seek favors from the great, you must know exactly what you aim at.)

‘Then thy brother is vicar of Gouda,’ she said, ‘as sure as I am heiress of Burgundy and the Netherlands. Nay, thank me not, good Giles,’ she added, ‘but my good mother. And I thank thee for giving me something to do for her memory. And doesn’t she weep for her mother? And doesn’t that make me weep for my dear brother whom I love so much? To think that a poor little elf like me could speak in the ear of princes and make my beautiful brother vicar of Gouda—eh, lass, it is a bonny place, and a bonny manse, and hawthorn in every bush at spring-tide, and dog-roses and eglantine in every summer hedge. I know what the poor fool loves—leave that to me.’

Giles began his tale strutting before Margaret, but ended it in her arms. She could not contain herself, and embraced him tightly. “Oh, Giles,” she said, blushing, kissing him, “I cannot keep my hands off you—your body is so small, and your heart so great. You are his true friend. Bless you! Bless you! Now we shall see him again. We haven’t seen him since that terrible day.”

“Gramercy, but that is strange,” said Giles. “Perhaps he’s ashamed of having cursed those two vagabonds—our own flesh and blood, worse luck.”

“Do you think that’s why he hides?” asked Margaret eagerly.

“Ay, if he hides at all. But I’ll have him announced by the bellman.”

“No,” she said. “That might offend him.”

“What care I? Shall Gouda go without a vicar, and the manse grow wild with nettles?”

To Margaret’s secret satisfaction, Giles had the new vicar proclaimed in Rotterdam and nearby towns. He easily convinced her that Gerard would hear of it soon and come to his post. She visited his manse and imagined how comfortable it could be made—how dearly she would love to do it.

But days passed. Gerard came neither to Rotterdam nor Gouda. Giles was mortified. Margaret was furious and heartbroken. She said to herself, “He thought me dead, so he came home. Now that I’m alive, he’s gone back to Italy—that’s where he’s gone.”

Joan advised her to consult the hermit of Gouda.

“Oh, he’s surely dead by now.”

“Yon one, perhaps. But the cave is never empty long—Goudا never lacks a hermit.”

But Margaret refused to go again to Gouda for such a purpose. “What can he know, shut up in a cave? Less than I, likely. Gerard has gone back to Italy. He hates me for not being dead.”

Soon, a man from Tergou arrived with news from Catherine: Ghysbrecht Van Swieten had seen Gerard later than anyone else. Margaret decided to visit the property left to her and bring Reicht Heynes back to Rotterdam. Naturally, her first step was Ghysbrecht’s house.

She found him in his garden, seated in a wheeled chair. He greeted her weakly but warmly. When she asked if it was true he had seen Gerard after August fifth, he replied, “Gerard no more—but Friar Clement. Ay, I saw him—and blessed be the day he entered my house.”

Then he told her, in his own words, of his meeting with Clement.

He also revealed that the friar had confessed he came to Tergou with the missing deed in his bosom, intending to force Ghysbrecht to surrender the land. But finding him repentant, he changed course.

“Was not this a saint—come to right you, yet must save your soul in the doing?”

When she asked if he recognized him, he said, “I never suspected it. It was only after three days that he revealed himself. Listen while I speak my shame and his praise.

I said to him, ‘The land is restored, and my stomach feels lighter—but another fault clings to me.’ Then I told him of the letter I wrote at the request of his brethren, whose duty it was to restrain them. I said, ‘That letter was meant to separate two lovers—and with the devil’s help, it did the foul work. Land and houses I can return, but that mischief is done forever.’

‘Nay,’ he said, ‘not forever—but for life. Repent it while you live.’

‘I shall,’ I said, ‘but how can God forgive it? I would not,’ I added, ‘were I He.’

‘Yet He will forgive it,’ he replied, ‘for He is ten times more forgiving than I am—and I forgive you.’

I stared. Then he said softly, quavering, ‘Ghysbrecht, look closer at me. I am Gerard, son of Eli.’

I looked—and looked—and at last, lo! it was Gerard. Verily, I would have fallen at his feet in shame and contrition—but he would not allow it. ‘That becomes not mine years and his, for a particular fault. I say not I forgive you without struggle—not being a saint. But these three days you have spent in penitence, I have spent under your roof in prayer. I do forgive you.’ Those were his very words.”

Margaret wept. The old man spoke with brokenness, and the story touched her deeply. He continued: “And even then, he bid me farewell.

‘My work here is done,’ he said. I could not stop him. For though he forgave me, the sight of me must be wormwood to him. He left me in peace—and may a dying man’s blessing follow him wherever he goes. Oh, girl, when I think of his wrongs, and yours, and how he avenged himself by saving this stained soul of mine, my heart is broken with remorse, and these old eyes weep night and day.”

“Ghysbrecht,” said Margaret, weeping, “since he forgave you, I forgive you too. What is done, is done. And you have let me hear what I would have walked the world to learn. But oh, burgomaster, you are a wise man—help a poor woman who has forgiven you her misery.”

She then told him all that had happened. “And,” she said, “they won’t keep the living for him forever. He may lose it—as well as break all our hearts.”

“Call my servant!” the burgomaster cried, suddenly energized.

He sent for a table and writing materials, dictated letters to the burgomasters of all major towns in Holland, and one to a Prussian official, his friend. His clerk and Margaret copied them, and he signed them. “There,” he said, “the matter shall be dispatched throughout Holland by trusted couriers—and as far as Basel in Switzerland. Fear not—we shall soon have the vicar of Gouda in his village.”

She returned home filled with renewed hope, blaming herself for ingratitude toward Gerard. “I value my wealth now,” she said.

She also resolved never to judge his actions until she heard his own explanation.

Not long after, disaster struck again. Catherine, as it happened, had secretly met the brothers the very day after their expulsion. Cornelis followed her to Tergou, living on secret funds, while Sybrandt chose to stay in Rotterdam. Before leaving, Catherine asked Margaret to lend her two gold angels. “For,” she said, “all mine are spent.” Margaret was eager to give them. But the moment the words left her lips, she saw a look of regret on Catherine’s face—and instantly understood where the money would go. It was meant to sustain Sybrandt until Catherine could find a reason to visit Rotterdam again—and then bring him some of her own savings.

But Sybrandt, now with gold in his pocket, believed it endless. Free from restraint, he lived like a complete drunkard—until one afternoon, in a drunken spree, he climbed onto the roof of the stable at the inn where he was carousing and began walking along it—a trick he had done many times sober. But now, his unsteady mind made his legs fail. He rolled down the roof and crashed onto a horizontal paling, hanging there for a moment in a semicircle—then toppled and lay silent on the ground, amid roars of laughter from his companions.

When they tried to lift him, he couldn’t stand—only fell giggling each time.

They staggered down the street with him, carrying him to the shop in the Hoog Straet. He had babbled his disgrace everywhere.

As soon as he saw Margaret, he hiccupped, “Here’s the doctor who cures all hurts—a bonny lass.” He also declared he bore her no malice, for he was visiting against his will. “Wherefore, prithee send away these drunkards, and let you and me have t’other glass, to drown all unkindness.”

All the while, Margaret flushed pale and red at seeing her enemy—and his insolence. But one man whispered what had happened, and a strange mark on Sybrandt’s face caught her eye.

“And he cannot stand up, you say?”

“He couldn’t just now. Try, comrade! Be a man now!”

“I am a better man than you!” Sybrandt roared. “I’ll stand and fight you all for a crown!”

He rose—and instantly collapsed into his attendant’s arms with a piteous groan. Then he began cursing his companions, claiming they had stolen his legs. “I can feel nothing below the waist.”

“Alas, poor wretch,” said Margaret. She turned gravely to the men and said, “Leave him here. And if you brought him to this, go on your knees—for you have ruined him for life. He will never walk again. His back is broken.”

The drunken man heard these words. The foolish look of intoxication vanished, replaced by agony. “The curse,” he groaned. “The curse!”

Margaret and Reicht carried him gently and laid him on the softest bed.

“I must do as he would do,” she whispered. “He was kind to Ghysbrecht.”

Her fears were confirmed: Sybrandt’s spine was fatally injured. He lay helpless, fed and cared for by the man who had once deeply harmed him.

The news reached Tergou. Catherine came over.

It shattered her. She blamed herself. “Oh, false wife—oh, weak mother,” she cried. “I am rightly punished for my treason to my poor Eli.”

She sat by his bedside for hours, rocking silently. She never recovered fully. From that day, the first grey hairs appeared in her hair.

As for Sybrandt, all he begged for was Gerard. He whined to Margaret like a wounded hound: “Oh, sweet Margaret, oh, bonny Margaret, for our Lady’s sake, find Gerard and bid him take his curse off me. Thou art gentle, thou art good—thou wilt entreat for me, and he will refuse thee nought.”

Catherine shared his belief that Gerard could heal him—and joined his pleas. Margaret hardly needed urging. After the burgomaster’s efforts failed, she sent her own agents—and spent money lavishly. Among them, poor Luke enrolled himself.

She met him one day, looking thin and weary. She spoke kindly. He broke down, sobbing, saying he was more miserable than ever, and would gladly be friends again—on almost any terms.

“Dear heart,” she said sadly, “why can’t you say to yourself, now I am your little sister, and you are my older brother, worn down by care? Say so, and I will indulge you, pet you, make you happier than a prince.”

“Well, I will,” Luke said fiercely, “sooner than stay away altogether. But above all, give me something to do. Perhaps I may have better luck this time.”

“Get me my marriage lines,” said Margaret, suddenly somber.

“That’s as much as to say, get me him! For where they are, he is.”

“Not necessarily. He may refuse to come near me—but he will not deny a poor woman, who once loved him, her lines of betrothal. How can she enter any honest man’s house without them?”

“I’ll get them for you—if they’re in Holland,” said Luke.

“They’re as likely to be in Rome,” Margaret replied.

“Let us begin with Holland,” Luke suggested wisely.

The slave of love was given money by his tender tyrant and wandered far and wide—coopering, carpentering, searching for Gerard. “I can’t be worse if I find the vagabond,” he muttered, “and I may be a hantle better.”

Months passed. Sybrandt improved in spirit, but not in body. He became Margaret’s lifelong charge. Another sorrow fell upon Catherine, bending her further. She lost her lively nature, no longer singing in the house. Her nerves were frayed, and she lived in terror of misfortune befalling Cornelis. The curse weighed on him too. She prayed Eli, if she had been a faithful partner all these years, to take Cornelis back into his house and let her live in Rotterdam awhile.

“I have good daughters here,” she said, “but Margaret is so tender, so thoughtful. And the little Gerard—he is my joy. He grows more like his father every day, and his chatter cheers my heavy heart. I do love children.”

Eli, strong but kind, consented reluctantly.

And the people of Gouda petitioned the duke for a real vicar. “Ours never comes near us,” they said, “these six months past. Our children die unchristened, our folk unburied—except by chance visitors.” Giles’s influence blocked the complaint once—but a second petition was drawn up, and he gave Margaret little hope the current situation could last another day.
