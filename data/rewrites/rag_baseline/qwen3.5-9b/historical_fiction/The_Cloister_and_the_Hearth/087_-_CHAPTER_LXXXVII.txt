While Jorian adjusted the buttons of his doublet and tightened the lacing of his jerkin, preparing to descend to Peter's tomb, his tongue refused to remain silent. "They used to call him a magician out in Sevenbergen," he began, his voice carrying the weight of gossip sharpened by grief. "And they say he bestowed a final touch of his craft upon them before departing. He claimed to have seen Margaret's son descending the Rhine clad in noble attire, laden with wealth, yet his visage marred by a foreign blade, lacking the full complement of limbs he departed with. But, dear heart, nothing came of it. Margaret still wastes away waiting for her boy; and Peter lies as still as his neighbors, save that she has placed a stone slab over him to keep him fixed in his grave—as you shall witness."

Jorian pressed both palms against the rough edge of the fresh mound, rising slightly as if to peer deeper into the earth, when the friar's hand, trembling with suppressed emotion, settled upon his shoulder. "How long since Peter Brandt passed?" the monk whispered, his voice strained.

"About two months. Why ask?"

"And his daughter interred him, you say?"

"Nay, I performed the burial rites, though she bore the cost and commissioned the stone."

"Then—but he acknowledged only one daughter, Margaret?"

"No more, at least, that he ever admitted."

"Then you believe Margaret is... is she alive?"

"Believe? Why, I should be dead without such faith. Riddle me that."

"Alas, how can I doubt? You love her!"

"Not more than reason demands, being a husband and father to four sturdy knaves like myself. Nay, the truth is simpler: she saved my life scarcely six weeks past. Had she perished, I could not have endured. Blessed be your heart, I could barely keep food upon my stomach; physicians declared me lost. My Joan remarked, 'Tis time to buy thee a shroud.' 'I dare say, so 'tis,' I replied, 'but try borrowing one first.' Then in came my lady, this Margaret—who, by your reckoning, died three years ago—she opened the casements, shifted me from my prone position, and cured me in the blink of an eye. But with what? Herein lies the pinch; with the most despicable herb, plucked from my own garden, sweet feverfew. An herb, quotha, 'tis a weed; leastways, it was a weed until it restored me. Yet now, whenever I pass my hunch, I doff my cap and declare, 'Fly service to ye!' Why, how now, Father? You wax pale, then flush red, then turn ashen. Pray, what is the matter? What, in Heaven's name, troubles you?"

"The surprise—the joy—the wonder—the terror," Clement gasped, his composure fracturing.

"Why does it concern thee? Are you of kin to Margaret Brandt?"

"Nay; but I knew one who loved her with such ferocity that her death nearly extinguished his soul, body and spirit. And yet you claim she lives. And I believe you."

Jorian stared, the silence stretching taut between them before he spoke gravely. "Father, you have interrogated me thoroughly, and I have answered with truth. Now, for our Lady's sake, answer me but two questions. Did you, in very sooth, know one who cherished this poor girl? Where?"

Clement hovered on the brink of confession, yet the memory of Jerome's letter caused him to recoil, shrinking from the name he had once worn in the world. "I knew him in Italy," he murmured.

"If you knew him, you can reveal his name," Jorian urged cautiously.

"His name was Gerard Eliassoen."

"Oh, but this is strange. Stay, what prompted you to assert Margaret Brandt was dead?"

"I stood beside Gerard when a missive arrived from Margaret Van Eyck. It informed him that the woman he loved lay buried. Let me sit, for my strength fails me. Foul play! Foul play!"

"Father," Jorian said, his tone shifting from inquiry to resolve, "I thank Heaven for sending you to me. Sit; you resemble a specter, fasting yourself to weakness. My mind misgives me; I feel I hold the key to this riddle. If I do, there are two knaves in this town whose heads I would fain batter to pieces as I crush this mold beneath my boot." He clenched his teeth, hoisting his long spade high above his head and bringing it down furiously upon the earth several times.

"Foul play? You have never spoken a truer word in your life. If you know where Gerard hides now, lose no time in showing him the trap they have set. Mine is but a dull head, but sometimes the slow hound puzzles out the scent. Go on. I believe you and I have seized two ends of the same stick, and a dreadfully foul one at that."

After dispensing with the usual preliminaries of his station, Jorian cut straight to the core of the narrative. Employed by the burgomaster of Tergou to repair the flooring of an upper chamber, he had grown curious during a sudden visit to retrieve his tools. Hearing loud arguments below, he had propped himself upon his stomach and listened. The burgomaster spoke of a letter Cornelis and Sybrandt intended to deliver to Gerard, replacing one carried by Hans Memling. "'It seems their will was good, but their stomach was small,'" the official had boasted, producing a drawer brimming with silver to embolden the thieves. "If they succeeded, each should fill their hand with as much as it could hold."

"Well, Father," Jorian continued, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "I paid little heed then, save for the bargain itself, which haunted my dreams all night. Consider this: next dawn, who should I behold emerging from their homes but Cornelis and Sybrandt, each sporting a blackened eye? 'Oho,' I muttered, 'what mark has Hans Memling left upon you? Well now, I hope that is all you have suffered for your pains.' Did they not hasten to the burgomaster's residence? I retreated to my hiding place."

At this juncture, the monk's nostrils flared, his restless eyes betraying the tension coiling within him.

"Well, Father," Jorian pressed on, "the burgomaster summoned them to that very room. He held a letter in his grasp. Though I am no scholar, I possess as many eyes as the Pope, and I witnessed the drawer open. Those two knaves thrust in a hand each and drew it out overflowing. Saints in glory, how they struggled to cram more in, greed gnawing at them! And Sybrandt—he had daubed his palm in something viscous, I suspect glue, managing to drag one or two pieces away stuck fast to his skin. He! He! He! 'Tis a sin to laugh. Thus, fortune favored the wrong side as custom dictates; they completed the trick, yet how they executed it remains unknown until Judgment Day. Go on, they left their immortal treasures in that drawer. Well, they secured a handful of silver; the Devil won the worst of that bargain. There, Father, that weighs light on my mind. Often I yearned to confide in someone, but dared not speak to the women; otherwise, Margaret would have been left friendless in the world. For those two black-hearted villains are the favorites. Is it not always so? Have they not recently acquired a splendid new establishment in this very town, on the Hoog Straet? There you may view their sign: a gilt sheep and a lambkin. A pair of wolves suckling their dam would be nearer the mark. And there the entire family feasts today; oh, 'tis a fine world. What, not a word, holy father? You sit like stone, offering not even a curse upon them, the stony-hearted miscreants. Was it not enough that the poor lad stood alone in a strange land? Must his own flesh and blood conspire to bury the sole blessing his enemies spared him? And consider her, pining and wasting these years, seated at the window gazing down the street for Gerard! So constant, so tender, so true: my wife insists no woman ever loved a man more faithfully than she loves the boy those villains have torn from her side. Not a single day passes without her weeping salt tears for him. And when I reflect that, but for those two greedy, lying knaves, that winsome lad—whose life I saved—might walk beside her today, the happiest soul in Holland; and that sweet lass, who saved my life, might rest her cheek upon her sweetheart's shoulder, the blissest in Holland instead of the saddest; oh, I thirst for their blood, the nasty, sneaking, lying, cogging, cowardly, heartless, bowelless—how now?"

The monk leaped wildly to his feet, his complexion draining to a livid hue of fury and despair. With both hands clenched and raised high, he charged headlong from the spot. Such was the magnitude of this inarticulate eruption of rage that Jorian's petty anger evaporated at the sight, leaving him standing in stunned dismay after the human tempest he had unleashed.
