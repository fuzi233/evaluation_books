The moment I crossed the threshold, a chill like that of a frozen vault seized me—cold so deep it seemed to freeze the breath in my lungs. The door clicked shut behind us, sealing us into silence. The sexton murmured something to his wife, and she turned toward us with a change so profound it startled me. Her eyes—once ordinary—now blazed with an inner light, as though they had held too much radiance and overflowed, flooding her face with a luminous beauty. She looked like a vision from beyond time, a being touched by eternity. Her hands, pale and translucent, shimmered faintly, each fingernail glowing like moonstone beneath the candle’s flicker. It was overwhelming; I felt relieved when she turned away.

But the candlelight barely reached beyond our feet. At first, the room was swallowed in shadow. Then, slowly, it caught on something just above the floor—a glimmering shape raised slightly from the ground. A bed? Could any living thing endure such cold and rest here? And if so, why would it ever wake?

Beyond that, other faint reflections danced in the darkness. As we stepped forward, the glow revealed more: one after another, figures lay upon white-draped couches, arranged in long, silent aisles. Each form was still, head turned toward the path we walked. I could not tell gender or age—only that they were all motionless, wrapped in sheets like snowfall.

My heart slowed. Dread settled over me like frost. We moved through row after row, endless corridors of slumbering forms stretching into infinity. They vanished into the shadows, disappearing into the depths of what might have been a cathedral, a mausoleum, or some vast temple of repose. Was this where I was meant to lie? To sleep among those who did not stir, never to be roused? Was this the sexton’s library? Were these his books—living volumes bound in flesh and memory?

“Just one of the cellars I’m assigned to watch,” Mr. Raven whispered, as if afraid to disturb the silence. “Much wine is stored here to age… But it’s dark for a stranger.”

“The moon is rising,” his wife said softly, her voice clear and sweet, yet carrying the weight of ancient sorrow, long buried but not forgotten.

As she spoke, the moon broke through a gap in the wall. Instantly, a thousand points of silver light flared across the chamber—each sheet, each face, each still figure catching its glow. Yet even then, I could not see the edges of the place. The aisles stretched endlessly, narrowing into the distance, lined with solitary sleepers, each alone in their eternal repose.

At first, I thought them dead. But soon I realized—this was deeper than death. This was not cessation. It was suspension. A state beyond understanding.

The moon climbed higher, casting blue-white beams through scattered openings. Where it fell, the sheets gleamed like ice, and the faces beneath appeared almost spectral—pale, serene, untouched by time. Yet none were alike. Some bore the marks of pain, though faint now, as if grief had once burned there but was now cooled into peace. Others wore no trace of suffering at all—just quiet acceptance.

One man lay with dark beard flowing like water from a glacier, his brow smooth as marble, lips parted only by the ghost of anguish. Next to him, a young woman—so beautiful it hurt to look—her features calm, eyes closed, yet her expression still held the echo of farewell, not despair. No sign of illness, no mark of sorrow. Pain had slept here, and never awakened.

Children rested beside adults, their small forms peaceful. But not one infant—no newborns, no infants whose lives had barely begun. Only those who had lived, loved, suffered, and then… paused.

The most striking of all was a woman with silver hair, her face noble and composed—not submissive, but certain. There was no fear, no regret, only a deep, unshakable belief that all had come to pass as it should. On others, the scars remained—faint lines of war, loss, sorrow—but even these bore a plea: *Pardon me—I died yesterday.* Or: *I died only a century ago.*

Some, I knew, had lain here longer than centuries. Not by count of years, but by the stillness in their bones, the depth of their silence. Something older than memory resided in them. Something I could not name.

We came at last to three empty beds. Beyond them, a woman lay—still beautiful, past her prime. One arm rested outside the sheet, palm upward. In its center, a dark stain. Beside her, a man of middle age, strong and broad-shouldered, his hand clenched as if gripping a sword. He looked like a warrior fallen in battle—perhaps defending truth itself.

“Would you hold the candle closer, wife?” the sexton whispered, bending low.

“It heals well,” he murmured, “the nail found nothing to harm her.”

I finally dared to speak. “Are they… dead?”

“I cannot answer that,” he replied, voice low. “I’ve nearly forgotten what ‘dead’ means in your world. If I said someone was dead, my wife would understand one thing—you another. This is merely one of my treasure vaults. Not all are buried underground. Out there on the moor, they lie thick as autumn leaves after the first storm—thick as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals across the land. All night, the moon reads their faces—and smiles.”

“But why leave them exposed to the moonlight? Won’t it rot them?”

“Our moon,” he said, “is not like yours—the burnt-out cinder of a dead world. Hers does not decay. She preserves. You see, we do not bury deeply. Our sexton lays the dead upon the earth, not beneath it. Your people pile stones atop them, as if to keep them down. I wait for the hour to ring the resurrection bell—and wake those still asleep. Your sexton watches the clock to call the dead to church. I listen for the cock on the spire to crow: *‘Awake, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead!’*”

For a moment, I thought him mad—an insane parson lost in delusion. But how could I escape? I was trapped in a realm larger than thought. Here, space bent. Two things could occupy the same place. Why not twenty? A thousand? I dared not follow that line further.

“You seem to perceive differences in your dead that I cannot,” I ventured.

“None of these,” he said, “are truly dead. Some have only just begun to die—to awaken. Others had already begun to live before they came to us. When they are fully dead—when they truly cease—then they will rise and go. Every night, some wake and leave. But I won’t say more. My words only confuse.”

Then he pointed to one of the empty couches. “This one has waited for you.”

“Why this one?” I asked, trembling. “Why not another?”

“For reasons you’ll be glad to know one day.”

“And when?”

“When you wake.”

“But these are all dead,” I protested. “And I am alive!”

“Not nearly enough,” he replied with a smile. “Blessed be the true life—where the pauses between beats are not death.”

“The place is too cold to sleep,” I said.

“Do they feel it?” he asked.

“They sleep well—or will soon. Cold heals them. Their wounds close under its touch. Do not be afraid, Mr. Vane. Turn your back on fear. Face whatever comes. Surrender to the night—and you shall rest. Harm will not touch you. But a good you cannot foresee.”

He stood beside the couch. His wife stood at the foot, candle in hand. Her eyes still shone, but her face had returned to stillness—no longer radiant, only pure, white, and quiet.

“Would they make my bedroom a charnel house?” I cried. “I will not! I’ll sleep out on the heath—it can’t be colder there!”

“Ah,” said the librarian, “but the dead are there too—*thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks in Vallombrosa.*”

“I WILL NOT!” I shouted, and in the suffocating dark, the two figures glowed like spirits guarding the sleeping.

They did not answer. Just stood—silent, sorrowful—watching each other.

“Be at ease,” the sexton said gently to his wife. “We watch the flock of the great shepherd.”

Then he turned to me. “Did you not find the air pure when you entered?”

“Yes,” I said, “but oh, so cold.”

“Then know,” he said, voice firm, “that you—who call yourself alive—have brought the scent of death into this chamber. Its air will not heal the sleepers until you are gone.”

They walked deeper into the hall. I was left alone—bathed in moonlight, surrounded by the silent dead.

I turned to flee.

How far it seemed—back through the endless rows. At first, anger burned hotter than fear. But as I calmed, the shapes grew more terrible. I began to run—wildly, recklessly—bursting through the door and slamming it shut behind me.

It closed without sound.

Darkness.

I fumbled along the wall, found another door, opened it—and saw the dim glow of a lamp.

I stood in my library. The masked door was still open. Had I dreamed it all? Or had I escaped into a dream?

Which was real—the world I’d just left, or this one?

Could both exist—separate, yet overlapping?

I collapsed onto a couch and fell into sleep.

In the east, a small window let in the first rays of dawn. By this season, the sun struck a mirror, which reflected the light onto the masked door.

When I woke, the golden beam was there—drawing my gaze.

I sprang up, knowing what lay beyond.

I threw open the door.

Light—like a hunting hound—darted into the closet and leapt onto the gilded edges of a large book.

“What fool,” I muttered, “has placed this book backward?”

But the reflection bounced again—onto a nest of drawers in the corner. One was half-open.

“More meddling,” I sighed, stepping to close it.

Inside: old papers, piled high. The drawer refused to shut.

I pulled out the topmost. My father’s handwriting. Long. Important.

The first few words made my blood quicken.

I carried it to the library, sat in the western window, and read what followed.
