Martin craved a second dose of that intoxicating thrill, a renewed grip on the dignity of power. For days, Miss Helstone—the girl he had once dismissed as plain, whose visage now haunted his waking and sleeping hours alike—had slipped within his orbit. The thought that their encounters might cease forever gnawed at him. Though he possessed the restless spirit of an original thinker, Martin knew that at sixteen, the world demanded conformity; yet he had never truly molded himself to its pattern. An indelible, unique stamp remained upon him. Sitting idly at his grammar school desk, he spun threads for a new chapter in his unfinished romance, unaware that many stories never survive past their opening pages. That same Saturday, his sanctuary was the woods, where fairy tales and the wild landscapes of his imagination mingled.

Yet, Martin dreaded Sunday. While his parents, though critical of the Establishment, dutifully filled the large pew at Briarfield Church with their entire brood, Martin found the long services and dry sermons tedious. However, a heavy snowstorm altered the atmosphere entirely. By breakfast, Mrs. Yorke had declared that the children would be safer indoors, proposing they spend two hours in silence in the back parlor while Rose and Martin read from John Wesley’s *Sermons*. Wesley, a reformer and agitator, held favor in both their hearts.

"I shall do as I please," Martin stated, not lifting his gaze from the volume he studied over his bread and milk—a habit he retained throughout his life.

"Rose will do as she pleases," Martin confirmed. "And so shall Martin."

"Rose will do as she is told, and so will Martin," corrected his mother.

"I am going to church."

Her son replied with the quiet, unyielding assurance of a true Yorke. One who knows his will and the means to enforce it, he would endure crushing pressure rather than capitulate, unless trapped with no exit.

"The weather is unfit," his father interjected.

Silence answered, broken only by the sound of Martin breaking bread and sipping milk.

"Martin hates church, but he hates obedience even more," Mrs. Yorke observed.

"Pure perverseness influences me?"

"Yes."

"Mother, I am not."

"Influenced by what, then?"

"A tangle of motives so complex I'd sooner explain them to you than turn myself inside out to show you my machinery."

"Hear Martin!" cried Mr. Yorke. "I must see this boy raised to the Bar. Nature intended him for the tongue. Hester, your third son must be a lawyer; he possesses the stock-in-trade: brass, self-conceit, and words—endless words."

"Some bread, Rose, if you please," Martin requested, his voice low and plaintive, softening in his stubborn moods to a whisper. He rang the bell, gently asking for his walking shoes.

"But Martin," his sire urged, "there is drift everywhere. A man could hardly wade through it."

Seeing the boy rise as the church bell tolled, the father relented. "In a case like this, I would not balk the obstinate lad of his will. Go, by all means. The wind is pitiless, the sleet sharp, and the ground deep with snow. Go forth into it if you prefer it to a warm fireside."

Martin donned his cloak, comforter, and cap, stepping deliberately into the storm.

"My father has more sense than my mother," Martin murmured, reflecting. "Women drive nails into flesh, believing they are striking stone."

Reaching the church early, Martin resolved himself. If the tempest frightened Miss Helstone away, or if Mrs. Pryor objected, he would be vexed. Yet, whatever the gale, she *ought* to come. If she possessed a soul worthy of her eyes, she *would* come. He sought her presence as passionately as she sought his; he desired a word regarding her "confounded sweetheart" as fervently as he craved another flavor of existence itself—an adventure to counteract stagnation.

Inside, the air was cold, the silence profound. Only one old woman sat initially, joined gradually by others, mostly the frail, aged, and poor who braved the worst elements to prove their constancy to Mother Church. Not a carriage, not an affluent family; only gray-haired elders and feeble paupers occupied the bare oak benches.

*I'll scorn her if she does not come,* Martin muttered savagely. The rector's shovel-hat passed the porch; Mr. Helstone and his clerk were in the vestry. The doors closed, the service began. The rectory pew stood void. She was not there.

Martin scorned her internally. *Worthless! Vapid! Commonplace humbug!* Like all girls, weak, selfish, shallow. Her eyes lacked the large expressiveness, her nose the straight, delicate Hellenic line, and her mouth lost the charm he had imagined could beguile his sullen moods. In his mind, she was merely paper, a doll, a toy, a *girl*.

So absorbed in his cynicism that he forgot to rise at the proper moment, remaining kneeling until the Litany ended and the first hymn began. Startled, he jumped up, his cheeks flushing red. Worse still, the church door reopened. A flood of tiny feet trooped in—the Sunday School children, kept warm by the stove until the final moments. Finally, trailing the procession, a tall group of young women entered, seating themselves quietly. Their teacher followed to the rectory pew.

There, amidst the storm-wracked air, was the French-gray cloak and the small beaver bonnet. Miss Helstone had not succumbed to the impediments of weather. She was there. Martin likely whispered a sigh of relief into his hymn book, hiding his face for two minutes.

But satisfaction turned swiftly to irritation. She had not glanced his way. Had she not noticed him? If she showed no sign that he occupied her thoughts, his opinion of her would plummet further. Why did she come merely for the sake of those sheep-faced children, ignoring him and the memory of long-skeleton Moore?

The sermon concluded, the benediction was spoken, and the congregation dispersed. She had stayed far from him.

As Martin walked homeward, the sleet biting his face and the east wind chilling his bones, he chose a dangerous, untrodden path through the fields. Near the second stile, a clump of trees sheltered an umbrella, struggling valiantly against the blast. Behind it fluttered the familiar French-gray cloak.

A grin spread across Martin's face as he climbed the steep, treacherous slope, resembling a trek up the slopes of Etna. Upon reaching the stile, he sat down calmly, initiating a conference he was prepared to prolong indefinitely.

"I think you ought to strike a bargain. Exchange me for Mrs. Pryor."

"I wasn't certain you'd choose this path, Martin, but I decided to risk it. There is no quiet corner in the church or yard."

"Will you agree? Transfer Mrs. Pryor to my mother and step into her skirts?"

"As if I understood you! What brings Mrs. Pryor to mind?"

"You call her 'Mamma,' don't you?"

"She *is* my Mamma."

"Not possible—not so inefficient, so careless a Mamma. I'd make a five-times-better one."

"Martin, what now? I thought the Yorkes never paid compliments?"

"They haven't done so until this generation. I feel it is my vocation to produce a new variety of Yorke. I'm tired of my own ancestors. Traditions stretch back four ages—Hiram, Samuel, John, Zerubbabel Yorke. From Zerubbabel down to the last Hiram, they were like my father. Before that, there was Godfrey. His portrait hangs in Moore's room; it resembles me. We know little of his character, but I suspect it differed vastly from his descendants'. He had long, curling dark hair, dressed with careful cavalier flair. Having said he looks like me, I need not add that he was handsome."

"You are not handsome, Martin."

"No; but wait. Take your time. I intend to begin cultivating and polishing today, and we shall see."

"You are a strange, unaccountable boy, Martin. But don't imagine you'll ever be handsome; you cannot."

"I mean to try. But we were discussing Mrs. Pryor. She must be the most unnatural Mamma alive, letting her daughter venture out in this weather. Mine was furious when I insisted on church; she was fit to throw a kitchen brush at me."

"Mamma was deeply concerned. I fear I was obstinate. I *wanted* to go."

"To see me?"

"Exactly. I thought of nothing else. I greatly feared the snow would stop you. You don't know how pleased I was to find you alone in the pew."

*"I"* came to fulfill my duty and set the parish a good example. And so you were obstinate? I'd like to see you obstinate. I wonder if I'd keep you in good discipline if I owned you? Let me take the umbrella."

"I can't stay two minutes; dinner will be ready."

"And so will ours. We always have a hot dinner on Sundays. Roast goose today, with apple pie and rice pudding. I always contrive to know the menu. I like these things uncommonly, but I'll make the sacrifice if you will."
