One of Novice Gerard's self-imposed penances was to treat Lodovico with kindness, though inwardly he regarded the man as nothing less than a slimy serpent. Yet, there is a peculiar virtue in such self-denial; like many rational acts of contrition, it quickly ceases to feel like a burden. Initially, the pride and complacency with which the former assassin gazed upon the single life he had spared were almost comical in their absurdity, yet undeniably pathetic. However, opening a good door in a human heart often invites a cascade of goodness. Finding the act of preserving life strangely sweet, Lodovico gradually grew averse to taking it. From this aversion sprang remorse, and from remorse, something resembling true penitence.

Teresa played her part in this transformation, repeatedly threatening to withdraw her protection unless he vowed to live honestly. The good fathers of the convent supported her efforts, and eventually, Lodovico and Teresa were dispatched by sea to Leghorn, where Teresa possessed friends. There, the assassin settled down, trading his blade for the heavy load of a porter. At first, he found the labor miserably dull and complained openly. Yet, I believe this quiet, plodding existence suited him far better than the fleeting, violent excitement of being hacked to pieces by the Princess Claelia's mercenaries. His exile had saved the unconscious penitent from that gruesome fate. Meanwhile, the Princess, thwarted in her quest for revenge, descended into a profound melancholy. She dismissed her old confessor and sought out a new one renowned for his piety—a man named Fra Jerome—to whom she poured out what she termed her "griefs."

Fra Jerome proved to be the perfect instrument for her redemption. He listened to her grievances with grim patience before seizing her by the shoulders and shaking the delusions from her mind with the roughness one might reserve for a kitchen maid. For this hard monk, the path of duty meant fearing neither the wrath of princes nor the fury of the sea. In a few sharp words, crackling with thunder and lightning, he stripped away her pretenses. "Thou art the devil," he declared, "who, with thy gold, tempted one man to slay his fellow, and then, blinded by self-love, instead of condemning and punishing thyself, art now thirsting for the blood of others—men perhaps not so guilty as thou."

At first, she resisted, claiming she was unaccustomed to such harsh scrutiny from her confessors. But Fra Jerome overpowered her objections. He threatened her with the Church's eternal curses, tore the scales from her eyes, and thundered against her until she sank to the floor, groveling in remorse and terror at the feet of this giant Boanerges. "Oh, holy father," she cried, "have pity on this poor, weak woman and help me save my guilty soul! I was lost in darkness, lacking guidance like yours, good father. I wake now as from a nightmare."

"Doff thy jewels," commanded Fra Jerome sternly.
"I will. I will," she replied.
"Remove thy silk and velvet; clad yourself in garments humbler than those worn by thy meanest servant, and hasten instantly to Loretto."
"I will," said the princess, her voice faint.
"No shoes; but a bare sandal."
"No, Father!"
"Wash the feet of every pilgrim, both arriving and departing. To any who are holy friars, reveal thy sin and await their admonition."
"Oh, holy father, let me wear my mask!"
"Humph!"
"Oh, mercy! Consider! My features are known throughout Italy."
"Aye. Beauty is a curse to most of you. Very well, thou mayst mask thine eyes, but no further."

On this concession, she reached out to seize his hand, intending to kiss it, but he snatched it away rudely. "What would you do?" he asked sharply. "That hand touched the Eucharist but an hour ago; is it fit for one such as thou to handle it?"
"Ah, no. But oh, do not depart without granting your penitent daughter your blessing."
"There is ample time to ask it when you return from Loretto."

Thus, that marvelous event by the banks of the Tiber left its indelible mark upon all involved, much as legends say prodigies do. The assassin, softened by the miracle of having saved a life rather than taken one, turned from the stiletto to the company of porters. The Princess went barefoot to Loretto, weeping over her crimes while washing the feet of low-born men. As for Gerard, carried from the Tiber into the convent as a near-suicide, he was now hailed as a young saint within its walls. Eyes loving yet experienced watched over him. Upon a probation shorter than usual, he was admitted to the priesthood. Soon thereafter, he took the monastic vows and became a friar of St. Dominic. Dying to the world, the monk renounced even the name by which he had once lived, thereby severing the final link to his earthly past. Here ended the tale of Gerard, and the story of Brother Clement began.
