CHAPTER LXV. “O, welcome, pure-eyed Faith, white-handed Hope, Thou hovering angel, girt with golden wings!” —MILTON.

Deronda hesitated before answering Gwendolen’s summons, not from pride, but from a deep sensitivity to the weight of what she might ask of him. He felt keenly how much her heart had come to depend on him—more than he could bear without pain. It was no longer a question for debate; it was a truth that lived in his bones: Gwendolen’s soul clung to him with an ache so fierce, it seemed to threaten her very survival. We do not argue when we hear anger or scorn in another’s voice—we feel it, and there is no room for doubt. So Deronda knew, with a quiet certainty, that her fate hung by a thread, and he was the one who held it.

Had this moment come just over a year earlier, he would have acted without hesitation. The impulse then would have been clear: to save her, to shield her from sorrow, to fulfill the promise he had begun when he returned her necklace—a gesture that had become more than a mere act of kindness. Then, love would have dictated his course. But now, other bonds bound him—love, duty, purpose—each pulling him in directions beyond her reach. Yet even as he stood apart, he carried within him a quiet longing, a trembling at the thought of seeing her eyes again, hearing her voice, feeling the silent plea that never left her lips.

He waited in the back drawing-room—the same space where they had once sat together at the musical gathering, where she had first confessed that her life depended on his staying near her, and where her words had melted into Mirah’s song: *Per pietà non dirmi addio*. But the melody had come from Mirah’s voice, not his own heart.

The room, familiar to him for years, now felt transformed. Every object—the gentle smile of Lady Mallinger’s portrait, the dignified lions flanking the fireplace—seemed to belong to a past life, one he was visiting only in memory. His world had changed. The house, once a home, now felt like a stage upon which a new drama unfolded—one in which he was both actor and witness. And here he stood, hat in hand, awaiting the woman whose life was unraveling before him, caught in a transformation too painful to name.

When Gwendolen entered, she looked altered—not only in mourning dress, but in spirit. There was a stillness about her, a quiet resolve that hadn’t been there in Genoa. She was satisfied, simply because he was there. Yet no smile passed between them. They met, hands clasped, each lost in memory, each burdened by what lay ahead.

“It was kind of you to come,” she said softly, seating herself. “Let us sit down.”

He took the chair opposite, silence settling between them like a veil.

“I asked you here because I need to know what I should do,” she began at once. “Don’t hold back if it’s hard. I’ve made up my mind to do whatever you think right. Once I feared poverty—I couldn’t bear being dependent on others. That’s why I married. But I’ve endured worse. Now I think I could endure poverty—if you say I must. Do you know about my husband’s will?”

“Yes,” Deronda replied, already sensing her question. “Sir Hugo told me.”

“Then you understand what I’m asking,” she said, her voice growing urgent. “I want to know if I ought to accept anything he left me. I’ve thought about this endlessly. You may not realize how much I considered my mother when I married. I was selfish—but I loved her. I cared about her suffering. When I was unhappy, the only comfort was knowing she was better off because of me. Now, the hardest thing I can imagine is seeing her poor again. I’ve been thinking—what if I take only enough to provide for her? Nothing for myself. Would that be wrong? After all, I was precious to her—he took me from her—and if she had known—”

She stopped, breath catching. She had prepared for this conversation for days, rehearsing her thoughts in solitude, but now the emotions surged forward, too strong to contain. Her words faltered, her hands—bare except for her wedding ring—resting limply in her lap.

“Do not hurt yourself by speaking of that,” Deronda said gently. “There’s no need. The matter is simple. You’ve come to me because I am the only one who knows the deepest part of your pain—and I understand your scruples.”

He paused, giving her time to gather herself. The silence stretched, filled with unspoken tenderness.

“You feel something terrible,” he said finally. “A guilt toward someone who is gone. You believe you’ve lost your claim as a wife. You shrink from taking what was his. You wish to punish yourself—for disobeying the better part of you, the part that resisted temptation. I’ve known that feeling too. Am I right?”

“Yes,” she whispered. “I want to be good—not like I’ve been. I’ll bear whatever you think I must. I’ve told you everything. What should I do?”

“If only you were the one involved,” Deronda said slowly, “I wouldn’t dare oppose any remorseful instinct. But I see your concern for Mrs. Davilow—and that changes things. I don’t believe your husband’s rights are canceled by your actions. He chose to enter your life, and shaped its path in the most profound way. Even aside from that, it was his duty, in his position, to care for your mother. He understood that if this will took effect, she would share in what he provided for you.”

“She has had eight hundred a year,” Gwendolen said. “I thought—maybe I should take only that, and leave the rest.”

Her mind had clung to this idea for so long it was hard to let go.

“But that would be a mistake,” Deronda said quietly. “You’d be creating a painful mystery for your mother—an income from which you exclude yourself would only cause her bitterness. And your own path would grow harder. We agreed in Genoa that your burden is one no one else should know. Your future kindness will be best served by keeping this secret from all. In my view, you should follow your husband’s will exactly. Let your remorse guide how you use your independence—not whether you accept it.”

As he spoke, he picked up his hat, unconsciously, from the floor beside him.

Gwendolen felt her heart leap, as though it sensed his intention to leave. In that instant, she rose—without thinking—unable to stop herself. Deronda, of course, stood too, stepping forward slightly.

“I’ll do what you say,” she murmured, hurriedly. “But what else should I do?”

That was all she could manage. Even these few words brought her to the edge. Her pride, long guarded, had crumbled. The childlike simplicity of her speech struck her like a mirror, revealing her helplessness. A sob rose, and great tears filled her eyes.

Deronda felt a deep pang, but he pressed on. Duty called louder than emotion.

“You’ll likely be going to the country with Mrs. Davilow soon,” he said gently.

“Yes—in a week or ten days.”

She paused, staring out the window, as if searching for a distant horizon.

“I want to be kind to them all—they can be happier than I can. Is that the best I can do?”

“It is,” Deronda said firmly. “It’s a duty beyond question.” He paused, each word weighed with care. “Other duties will follow. To see your life as a debt may seem bleak from afar—but it won’t truly be so. What makes life bleak is the absence of purpose. But once you begin acting with this penitent, loving intent, unexpected joys will arise—new needs will appear, guiding you day by day. Your life will grow—like a plant.”

She turned to him, her eyes wide with thirst, as if listening to the sound of water in a dry land.

Deronda felt her gaze as if she reached across a vast distance, arms outstretched from a lonely shore. His voice softened, pleading.

“This sorrow—so deep, so sudden—has come while you’re still young. Don’t see it as a ruin. See it as preparation. Let it be a preparation—”

Any listener would have thought he was begging for his own happiness.

“Look! You’ve been spared the worst horrors of a marriage you knew was wrong. You’ve seen what selfishness can become—what degradation lies ahead. Imagine a stern guardian, watching you walk a dangerous path, seizing your wrist and showing you the abyss. And it came to you in your youth. Think of it as preparation. You can—will—become one of the best women, the kind who make others glad they were born.”

The words touched her like a miracle. Emotions swirled through her—hope, shame, strength—mingling into something new, powerful, alive. The divine hope of renewal stirred within her, fueling a quiet energy she had never known. And yet, this rebirth felt inseparable from Deronda. His presence was the breath of her new life.

She did not speak. But a flush rose across her cheek, brow, neck—deepened for a moment, then faded slowly.

Deronda stepped forward, holding out his hand. “I mustn’t weary you.”

She startled—realizing he meant to leave—and placed her hand in his, silent still.

“You look unwell,” he said softly, holding her hand. “Unlike yourself.”

“I can’t sleep,” she whispered, her old weariness returning. “Things keep repeating. They come back… they’ll all come back,” she finished, shuddering, fear creeping into her bones.

“They’ll grow quieter with time,” he said.

He could not let go. Not yet.

“Sir Hugo says he’ll come to stay at Diplow,” Gwendolen said suddenly, grasping at words that had slipped away. “You’ll come too.”

“Probably,” he answered—and then, sensing the coldness of the word, corrected himself. “Yes, I shall come.” Then, with a final, gentle pressure, he released her hand.

“And not again here, before I leave town?” she asked, pale, timid.

What could he say?

“If I can help—if you wish it—I will,” he replied.

“I must wish it,” she said fiercely. “You know I must. What strength do I have? Who else is there?”

Another sob threatened.

Deronda’s face darkened with pain. He looked distressed as he said, “I will certainly come.”

She saw the change in him—yet relief flooded her. The thought of his return gave her courage. The fragile hope returned, glowing in her eyes.

“Don’t be sad for me,” she said, her voice warm with affection. “I’ll remember every word you said. I’ll remember what you believe about me. I’ll try.”

She looked at him steadily, reaching out again as if forgetting all that had passed since those words.

But no smile touched her lips. She had not smiled since her husband’s death.

Standing still, silent, she resembled a melancholy statue—once full of laughter, now carved in sorrow.

Only by understanding the depth of her anguish can we grasp her behavior—her openness, her desperate appeal. Thoughts that would haunt others didn’t touch her. She was consumed by fire, and had thrown herself into his arms, clinging as if he alone could carry her to safety.

She saw in him not just a friend, but the force of her rebirth. He had saved her, not with words, but with action. And so she believed he would remain—because he was the only one who could.

And yet, he could not stay.

He visited Park Lane again before she left—twice—but always in Mrs. Davilow’s presence. Their meetings were less intense, more guarded.

Since deciding to accept her inheritance, Gwendolen had formed a plan she spoke of often: to bring her mother and sisters back to Offendene, to rebuild their lives as if time had turned backward. She wanted to piece her life back together—to return to the days when joy was real, though she hadn’t known it then.

She had spoken to Sir Hugo, who would work to lease Gadsmere at a rent that would cover Offendene’s cost.

Deronda listened willingly. Any subject that gave her peace was welcome.

He said nothing of his own concerns. She asked nothing of his life.

Her mind was fixed on his coming to Diplow before autumn ended. The Lapidoths—the little Jewish girl and her brother—meant nothing to her. Nor did the shifting tides of politics or society. Her world had narrowed to the path of conscience, the struggle to rise from guilt.

For Gwendolen, memory had been shattered. Everything outside her inner turmoil lay in dim silence.
