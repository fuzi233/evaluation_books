More than three weeks elapsed before the final contents of the library were packed and removed. During this time, Romola refused to shut her eyes or ears to the proceedings. The exhaustion born of violent emotion often breeds a dreamlike disbelief in the reality of its cause; thus, in the evenings, once the workmen had departed, Romola would take her hand-lamp and wander slowly through the chaos of straw and wooden crates. She paused at every empty pedestal, every familiar object laid low, driven by a bitter urge to confirm that there was a sufficient reason for her love's departure and the world's newfound barrenness. Yet, as the nights wore on, her visits became less about seeking confirmation and more about sustaining herself. This vivification of pain and despair regarding her father's memory remained the sole vitality left in her affections.

On the twenty-third of December, she knew the last packages were destined for removal. She rushed to the loggia atop the house, unwilling to miss the final pang of watching the slow wheels traverse the bridge.

It was a cloudy day, approaching dusk. The Arno flowed dark and shivering; the hills stood mournful. Florence, encircled by its stone towers, possessed a silent, tomb-like appearance, cast by unbroken shadows upon the city viewed from above. Santa Croce, where her father lay interred, was swallowed by this darkness. Slowly crawling across the bridge and vanishing up the narrow street was the white load, resembling a cruel, deliberate fate carrying away her father's lifelong hope to bury it in an unmarked grave. Romola felt less that she was witnessing this event herself than that her father, helpless beneath the imprisoning stones where her hand could not reach to comfort him, was conscious of it.

She remained motionless even after the load had disappeared, indifferent to the cold, soothed by the gloom that seemed to cloak her like a mourning garment, shutting out the discord of joy. Suddenly, the great bell in the palace tower rang out a mighty peal—not the hammer-sound of alarm, but an agitated cry of triumph. One by one, every other bell in every other tower caught the vibration, joining the chorus. As the swell grew until the air seemed composed of sound alone, little flames, vibrating as if ignited by the noise, burst forth between the palace turrets and the girdling towers.

That sudden clangor and leaping light struck Romola like sharp wounds. These were the triumphs of demons celebrating the success of her husband's treachery and the desolation of her life. Less than three weeks prior, she had been intoxicated by the sound of those very bells; in the gladness of Florence, she had heard a prophecy of her own joy. Now, however, the general jubilation seemed cruel to her. She stood aloof from that common life—from that Florence flinging out its loud exultation to deafen the ears of sorrow and loneliness. She realized she could never join hands with gladness again, save with those whom the hard nature of joy was designed to forget. In her bitterness, she felt all rejoicing to be mockery. Men shouted paeans with souls heavy with grief, then glanced at their neighbors' faces to verify if such a thing as true joy existed. Romola had lost her belief in the happiness she once thirsted for; it appeared to her now as a hateful, smiling, soft-handed entity with a narrow, selfish heart.

Covering her ears, she ran down from the loggia and hurried across the antechamber, only to be startled by the unexpected arrival of her husband, who came seeking her.

His step was elastic, bearing a radiance of satisfaction somewhat unusual. "What! The noise was a little too much for you?" he asked, noting how she clutched her hands tighter against her ears at the sight of him. He took her gently by the wrist, drawing her arm within his, leading her into the salon adorned with dancing nymphs and fauns. "Florence has gone quite mad with acquiring its Great Council, which promises to end all evils under the sun, particularly the vice of merriment. You may well look stunned, my Romola, and you are cold. Do not stay so late under that windy loggia without proper wrappings."

He continued, "I was coming to tell you that I am suddenly summoned to Rome regarding some learned business for Bernardo Rucellai. I depart immediately to join my party at San Gaggio tonight, so we may start early tomorrow. I need give you no trouble; my packages are already prepared. It will not be long before I return."

Tito knew he could expect nothing from her but quiet endurance. He dared not even venture to kiss her brow that evening, merely pressing her hand to his lips before leaving. Tito sensed that Romola was a more unforgiving woman than he had imagined; her love was not that sweet, clinging instinct, stronger than all judgment, which he now recognized as the great charm of a wife. Still, this petrified coldness was preferable to a passionate, futile opposition. Her pride and her ability to recognize the futility of resistance held a certain convenience for him.

Once the door closed behind Tito, Romola shed the look of cold immobility that descended upon her like an inevitable frost whenever he approached. Inwardly, she was far from a state of quiet endurance. The days following the scene that had severed her from Tito had been filled with active planning and preparation for the fulfillment of a purpose.

Her first action was to summon old Maso. "Maso," she said in a decided tone, "we take our journey tomorrow morning. We shall now be able to overtake the first convoy of cloth while they await us at San Piero. Arrange the two mules tonight; be ready to set off with them at dawn and meet me at Trespiano."

She intended to travel with Maso as far as Bologna, then send him back with letters to her godfather and Tito, informing them she had departed and never intended to return. She had orchestrated her departure so perfectly that its secrecy would be absolute, hiding her broken love and shattered life from vulgar scrutiny. Bernardo del Nero had been absent at his villa, eager to evade political suspicion by tending to his lands. She had settled his debt without a personal interview. He did not even know the library had been sold, left to conjecture that some sudden stroke of fortune had enabled Tito to raise the necessary funds. Maso had been informed only that her journey was a secret; doing precisely as she commanded was the thing he cherished most in his withered, wintry age.

Romola did not intend to sleep that night. Having fastened the door, she took her taper to the carved and painted chest containing her wedding clothes. The white silk and gold lay there, along with the long white veil and the pearl circlet. A great sob rose within her as she beheld them; they seemed the shroud of her dead happiness. In a tiny gold loop of the circlet, a sugar-plum had lodged—a pink hailstone from a shower of sweets. Tito had discovered it first and declared it should always remain there. At certain moments—and this was one of them—Romola was swept, by a sudden wave of memory, back into the time of perfect trust. She felt again the presence of the husband whose love made the world as fresh and wonderful to her as to a child sitting in stillness among sunny flowers. She heard his gentle tones, saw his soft, truthful eyes, and breathed again that vast freedom of the soul granted by the faith that the being nearest to us is greater than ourselves. In those brief instants, tears invariably rose: the woman's lovingness felt something akin to what a bereaved mother feels when tiny fingers seem to lie warm on her bosom, yet feel like marble to her lips as she leans over the silent bed.

However, something else lay in the chest alongside the wedding garments: a dark, coarse bundle wrapped tightly. Turning her eyes from the white and gold to the dark mass, her tears began to subside as her hands touched the serge. That coarse roughness anchored her firmly in the present, from which love and delight had vanished. Unfastening the thick white cord, she spread the bundle on the table. It was the grey serge dress of a sister belonging to the Third Order of Saint Francis, a member of the *Pinzochere*—a personage Florentines knew well, living in the world but devoted to deeds of piety. Romola intended to wear this dress as a disguise and resolved to don it immediately, ensuring that if she required sleep before morning, she would awaken in perfect readiness to leave.

She removed her black garment. As she thrust her soft white arms into the harsh sleeves of the serge mantle and felt the hard girdle of rope pinch her fingers as she tied it, she welcomed those rude sensations. They aligned with her new scorn for that thing called pleasure, which rendered men base—that dexterous contrivance for selfish ease, that shrinking from endurance and strain when others bowed beneath burdens too heavy for them, a trait that now united her with her husband. Gathering her long hair, she pulled it tight from her face, binding it in a great hard knot at the back of her head. Taking a square piece of black silk, she tied it close across her forehead and under her chin, then drew the cowl over it. Lifting the candle to the mirror, she verified that her disguise would be complete to anyone who had not lived intimately near her. To herself, she looked strangely like her brother Dino; the full oval of the cheek needed only to be wasted, and the eyes, already sad, only to sink further.
