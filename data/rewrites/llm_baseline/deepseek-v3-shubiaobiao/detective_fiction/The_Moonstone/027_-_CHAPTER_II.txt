Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite entered with the precision that marked all his actions—neither too swiftly to startle nor too tardily to leave us waiting. A model of Christian virtue, he moved through life with an effortless grace that spoke of perfect harmony.

“Inform Miss Verinder that Mr. Ablewhite has arrived,” my aunt instructed the servant.

We greeted him warmly, inquiring after his health, still shaken from his ordeal the week prior. With characteristic tact, he answered us simultaneously—my aunt received his words, while I was blessed with his radiant smile.

“What have I done to deserve such kindness?” he said, his voice rich with warmth. “A mere case of mistaken identity—blindfolded, throttled, and deposited upon a rather unforgiving floor. It might have been far worse! I could have been robbed. Murdered. Yet I emerge with nothing lost but a little Nervous Force—hardly a matter for the courts.” He sighed. “I would have preferred to keep the affair private, but Mr. Luker’s public outcry dragged me into the spotlight. Now the newspapers feast upon it, and I, like the gentle reader, grow weary of the tale.”

His gaze softened further. “And dear Rachel—does she still delight in London’s diversions? I must beg your forgiveness, Miss Clack, for my neglect of our charitable endeavors. But I fully intend to attend the Mothers’-Small-Clothes meeting next week. How fares the Committee? Are we sufficiently stocked in Trousers?”

His smile could have disarmed the sternest critic. Indeed, we were nearly *overwhelmed* by Trousers, but before I could say so, the door burst open, and in swept Miss Verinder—hair disheveled, cheeks flushed, her manner more befitting a boy in a tavern than a lady of refinement.

“Godfrey!” she exclaimed, seizing his hands. “You should have brought Mr. Luker. The two of you are the most intriguing men in London at present—though I know Miss Clack despairs of my morbid curiosity. No matter. Tell me everything the newspapers omitted about Northumberland Street.”

Even a man of Mr. Godfrey’s virtue is not immune to the temptations of our fallen nature. I watched, dismayed, as he clasped Rachel’s hand and rested it against his waistcoat—an unseemly encouragement to her forwardness.

“The papers have said all there is to say, and more elegantly than I could,” he demurred.

“He finds the subject tedious,” my aunt remarked.

Rachel’s eyes flashed. “Why?”

“My dear,” I interjected, “true courage is ever modest.”

She ignored me entirely. “You’re too accustomed to the flattery of women, Godfrey. It’s made you dishonest. Sit. I have questions, and I will have answers.”

With shocking impropriety, she dragged him to the window, where the light fell stark upon his face. My pen trembles to recount such behavior, but truth compels me.

“Have the police made progress?” she demanded.

“None.”

“And the men who attacked you—they’re the same who targeted Mr. Luker?”

“Almost certainly.”

“And no trace of them?”

“None.”

“Some believe they’re the Indians who came to our house in Yorkshire.”

“Some do.”

“Do *you*?”

“Rachel, I was blindfolded. I know nothing.”

Still, she pressed—until at last, she struck upon the heart of the matter.

“These rumors linking Mr. Luker’s gem to the Moonstone—what do they say of *you*?”

Mr. Godfrey paled. “Leave it be.”

“Tell me!”

“Scandal claims,” he said at last, his voice strained, “that the Moonstone was pawned to Mr. Luker… and that I am the man who did it.”

Rachel recoiled as if struck. “This is *my* fault!” she cried, wild-eyed. “I will not let an innocent man be ruined for my silence!”

She raged, she wept, she demanded a written declaration of his innocence—which he, ever indulgent, provided. She signed it feverishly.

“Show it to the world,” she said. “I misjudged you, Godfrey. You are better than I thought.”

Yet when the knock came—the summons to the flower-show—she turned brittle once more, donning gaiety like a mask. “Don’t pity me, Miss Clack,” she hissed as she left. “See how happy I am?”

The moment she was gone, Mr. Godfrey burned the declaration with a match. “Let her believe I accepted her sacrifice,” he said gently. “But I will not have her honor stained for my sake.”

I was moved beyond words.

Yet the afternoon’s trials were not over. My aunt, her face ashen, confided in me at last—her health was failing. She had a Will to sign.

And I was to bear witness.
