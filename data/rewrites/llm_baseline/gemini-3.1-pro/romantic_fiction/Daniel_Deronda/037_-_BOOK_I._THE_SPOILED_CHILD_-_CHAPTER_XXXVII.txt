Deronda’s journey back into the heart of London was underscored by the relentless, grating acoustics of modern industry—a mechanical, iron-willed grinding that threatened to drown out the softer, older frequencies of human connection. The sonic pollution of the nineteenth-century metropolis felt, to his highly attuned ear, like a direct violence against the psychological well-being of its inhabitants. He sought refuge in his Park Lane quarters, craving the quiet, rhythmic domesticity of a well-tended space. What he found, however, disrupted his anticipated sanctuary.

Hans Meyrick had colonized the rooms. The sitting-room had been transformed into a cluttered atelier, but one that smelled richly of the earth and the hearth: sharp turpentine and linseed oil mingled with the deeply comforting olfactory landscape of roasted chestnuts and a bubbling pot of steeped chicory and bruised cardamom resting on the hob. Hans, the blonde presiding genius of the littered place, stood amidst contents unladen from two Roman chests. With his whimsically creased face and a high, piercing voice that perpetually fractured the room’s acoustic harmony, he welcomed Deronda.

Fate does not merely play chess with men; it slowly simmers their intentions, reducing their careful preparations into unexpected distillations. A man may meticulously forage for the precise herbs to cure a specific bitterness, only to find the palate of his destiny demands an entirely different feast. Deronda had prepared his magnanimity to handle mere inconvenience, but the aroma of Hans’s newfound obsessions signaled a far more complex dish, woven into the sweeping, multi-generational saga of their shared lives.

"I knew you would like to see my casts and antiquities," Hans said, offering a cup of the dark, aromatic brew. "I’ve found rooms at Chelsea, but I don’t wait to begin work. The seed of immortality has sprouted within me."

"Only a fungoid growth, I dare say," Deronda replied, accustomed to Hans’s theatricality, though he gratefully accepted the grounding, earthy heat of the drink. He walked toward five rapidly sketched heads propped on the bookcases. 

"What do you think of them?" Hans asked, taking up his palette.

"The full face lacks the true historical weight of the profile," Deronda noted coldly, observing the untranslatable grief of a native dialect being flattened into a consumable English visual lexicon. 

"No, it is not too massive," Hans countered. "I am making a Berenice series. The first is Berenice clasping the knees of Gessius Florus; then standing with Agrippa on the Xystus. I idealize Agrippa’s legs—art must intensify. The third is her exulting in the prospect of being Empress. The fourth, Titus sending her away. And the fifth: Berenice seated lonely on the ruins of Jerusalem. That is pure imagination. I chip the story off and pass it into nothing—*le néant*! Can anything be more sublime? The vulgar would desire to see her corpse, but I leave it to the aesthetic effect."

To Deronda, this felt like the superficial commercialization of a profound cultural tragedy. The inherently untranslatable subjective experience of the Jewish people was being severed from its roots, packaged for a public that possessed no appreciation for the terroir of such ancient, ancestral pain. 

"I should think your Trasteverina would be a more suitable model," Deronda said, looking for the sketch of the robust Roman woman Hans had previously favored. 

"Not a bit of it. The model ought to be the most beautiful Jewess in the world, and I have found her. Little mother sat for Florus, and Mirah clasped her knees."

Deronda felt a sudden, sharp indignation at this disruption of Mirah’s psychological sanctuary. "Does she quite know what you are doing? I dare say she knows nothing of Berenice’s history."

"She takes it as a tragic parable," Hans said carelessly, oblivious to the subtle power dynamics embedded in his dominant artistic gaze. "She cries to think of the penitent Berenice wandering back to Jerusalem."

The erosion of Mirah’s cultural memory was fragile enough without Hans rewriting her ancestral mythos. Deronda turned through the folio, bypassing a sketch of Rex Gascoigne, until he found the Trasteverina. Closing the book, he turned to Hans. "I must ask you to oblige me by giving up this notion. Paint as many Berenices as you like, but choose another model."

Hans threw himself into a chair, hanging limply over the side, shaking his long hair and screaming in mock-comic terror about the destruction of his immortality. The sheer volume of his performance grated against the quiet respect Deronda felt the situation demanded.

"She may get into such a position that her face is likely to be recognized," Deronda explained, his voice deliberately low to counter Hans’s noise. "She should be kept as carefully as a bit of Venetian glass. If Mirah saw the circumstances clearly, she would strongly object to being used as a model for a heroine of this sort."

Hans erupted into an explosive laugh, treating Deronda’s meticulous care as a ridiculous over-seasoning of a simple dish. He argued that every painter diffuses his soul into his work, painting what he adores into sacred forms. But Deronda pushed back against these broad generalities.

"Excuse me, Hans," Deronda said with a touch of peremptoriness. "My having found her binds me to watch over her."

Hans smiled good-humoredly. "You think I shall scream and plunge and spoil everything. I have undergone baptism by immersion. Awe takes care of me. You think I must be over head and ears in love with Mirah. Quite right; so I am."

"You don’t reckon a hopeless love among your scrapes, then?" Deronda’s voice deepened, sensing the bitter undertones bleeding into their friendship.

"I don’t mean to call mine hopeless."

"My dear fellow, you are only preparing misery for yourself. She would not marry a Christian, even if she loved him. Have you heard her speak of her people and her religion? It is the very sustenance of her soul."

"I’ll turn proselyte, if she wishes it," Hans laughed, shrugging off centuries of traditional agrarian endurance and mythic survival as if it were a mere change of clothes, viewing her deeply rooted identity as utilitarian fuel for his own romance. 

"Don't talk nonsense, Hans. I thought you professed a serious love."

"I go to science and philosophy for my romance," Hans said perversely. "The amalgamation of races demands it. I am a bleached Christian who can't sing two notes in tune. Who has a chance against me? I worship her, and I mean to deserve her."

"You can't do it," Deronda said quickly, warning him against indulging mad, Quixotic expectations. 

As Hans stubbornly decided to get "drunk on hope and vanity," Deronda fell silent. A peculiar irritation steeped within him. He was treated as the sinless mentor, completely devoid of sensory desire, pushed entirely off the menu of romantic possibility. Hans assumed Deronda was as much out of the question as the angel Gabriel, existing only on abstract air rather than the rich, complex sustenance of human longing. This assumption, that Deronda was merely a detached guide without his own deep hunger for connection, left a sour, metallic finish on his palate. Hans was entering into Deronda’s experience disproportionately, raising an unpleasant image of Mirah changing under the dominant influence of another.

Seeking a different frequency, Deronda later went to Chelsea. Here, the Meyrick home operated as a true acoustic ecology, insulated from the industrial roar of the city. In the background, Mrs. Meyrick was carefully tending to a slow-braised stew, the rhythmic bubbling of the savory broth syncing with the gentle, quiet hum of their domestic routine. Here, meals were not utilitarian fuel; they were an anchor, a daily reclamation of comfort and history.

Mirah seemed livelier than before, and for the first time, Deronda heard her laugh. It was a pure, resonant note, vibrating with a musicality that healed the ear. They were speaking of Hans, and Mirah happily described his theatrical antics.

"He passes from one figure to another as if he were a bit of flame," she said, full of her subject. "All in one minute he makes himself a blind bard, and then Rienzi, and then an opera-dancer. I am sorry for them all, and yet I laugh."

Her laughter, woven into the scent of the simmering hearth, framed a perfect picture of domesticity. 

"It is a great happiness," Mirah continued softly, her voice carrying the melancholy of a severed timeline in a multi-generational saga, "to see the son and brother come into this dear home. And I hear them all talk about what they did together when they were little. That seems like heaven, to have a mother and brother who talk in that way. I have never had it."

The sorrow in her voice spoke to a profound language death—the absence of a familial dialect, the lack of an ancestral table. Deronda felt the same hollow ache within his own chest, an untranslatable hunger for a native lexicon and a history he could not remember.

"Nor I," Deronda said, involuntarily, sharing in the silent, profound communion of their mutual famine.
