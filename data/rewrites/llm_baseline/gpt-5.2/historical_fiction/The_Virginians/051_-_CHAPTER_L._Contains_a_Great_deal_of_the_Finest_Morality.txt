When first I had the honour to be presented to Sir Miles Warrington at the King’s drawing-room in St. James’s, I confess my senses—those foolish, hopeful sentinels—were taken in at once. His face was as round and coloured as an October pippin; his waistcoat broad as a harvest moon; his manner that of a man who has spent his life in fields and coverts and believes the world was created chiefly that a good horse might carry him over it. I thought I had at last stumbled upon that celebrated creature, trumpeted in ballad and sermon—the good old English country gentleman: the landlord who smiles upon his tenants as if they were sheep he has named, the squire whose very sneeze is supposed to be a blessing to the parish.

And indeed, if felicity had a savour, it would be tasted at such a man’s table: the roast sent up brown and crackling, the gravy clear, the beer of his own brewing with a head like whipped cream, and the air in his hall loud with the wholesome noises of domestic plenty—the click of knives, the clink of glass, the crackle of logs, and the deep, satisfied hum with which Englishmen praise their own cookery. To ride to church in health, to drop into the pew under the sound of a village chorus—coughs, shufflings, the clerk’s nasal leading—while the vicar looks down with dutiful reverence; to say, in a voice of proper gravity, “Have mercy upon us, miserable sinners,” as if misery were a customary condiment sprinkled over prosperity—why, it is a phrase served like grace before meat: uttered because printed, not because tasted.

Give any man five thousand a year, an appetite that never fails him, and a parish trained to touch forelocks at the mere creak of his saddle, and you will not make him feel “miserable” except when the claret is stopped and the doctor puts him upon arrowroot. Misery, in such weather, is a word for other people: for Colin Ploughman with his ague and his seven children and his eight shillings a week; for the widow whose broth is as thin as the parish’s pity. The rich man’s penitence is commonly as light as the foam on his ale.

Yet there is a flavour to hypocrisy too, and it is rancid. There are country houses in which charity is dealt out as one deals out small beer—sour, watered, and only to those who bow low enough; and where the broth for the poor has been skimmed until it will hardly grease a spoon. There are excellent Christians who cry “Amen!” loud as the clerk, who would as soon give their last guinea away as give up their opinion of themselves; who patronise the miserable without relieving them, and buy virtue as they cheapen a goose—proud of the bargain, and persuaded that the seller ought to be grateful.

Poor Harry Warrington, in his extremity, had appealed to his relations. His aunt answered with a tract and her blessing, as if paper could be stewed into nourishment; his uncle had business out of town and could not, of course, be troubled with a petition that smelt of debt. How often, in respectable life, does this same cold cookery go on—refusals dressed up as duties, stinginess spiced with Scripture? One can imagine a Macbeth with blood on his hands shrinking from his own wife’s look; but a Lord and Lady Skinflint can refuse a starving kinsman, go straight down to family prayers, discourse of Providence, and afterward sit nose to nose as serenely as if they had fed him rather than turned him away. They are indignant with the prodigal for being foolish; they are never ashamed of themselves for being hard.

It was in this spirit that Lady Warrington, in her drawing-room, was at that moment preparing one of her moral ragouts—an excellent dish in appearance, made up of much piety cut into neat slices, seasoned with indignation, and served cold.

“Very sad, very sad, my dear!” said Sir Miles, wagging his head with a genial gravity, as if sorrow were a thing to be performed like carving.

“To think of so much extravagance in one so young!” cried Lady Warrington. “Cards, bets, feasts at taverns of the most wicked profusion—carriages and riding-horses—the company of the wealthy and profligate of his own sex, and, I fear, of the most iniquitous persons of ours.”

“Hush, my Lady Warrington!” her husband interposed, with a glance towards Dora and Flora, whose downcast eyes and carefully arranged blushes seemed to say they knew perfectly well what their mamma meant, though they would faint sooner than name it.

“They can’t go to an opera or the Park without seeing ’em, to be sure,” Sir Miles remarked, with that easy tolerance men often keep for sins they do not pay for.

“To think we should have introduced such a young serpent into the bosom of our family,” pursued my lady, raising her voice as if she were in the pulpit, “and have left him in the company of that guileless darling!” She indicated Master Miles, the cherished Innocent, who sat stoutly planted, a small, rosy tyrant, with a top-string in his fingers.

“Who’s a serpent, mamma?” inquired that youth. “First you said cousin Harry was bad: then he was good: now he is bad again. Which is he, Sir Miles?”

“He has faults, like all of us, Miley, my dear,” said Sir Miles, who could season a sermon with humour. “Your cousin has been wild, and you must take warning by him.”

“Was not my elder brother—who died—my naughty brother—was not he wild too?” the child persisted, in that fearless way in which the young speak truth, having not yet learned the parish custom of silence. “He was not kind to me when I was quite a little boy. He never gave me money, nor toys, nor rode with me, nor—why do you cry, mamma? Sure I remember how Hugh and you were always fight—”

“Silence, sir!” cried papa and the girls together, as if the very name were a draught of poison.

“I know I love Harry, and I didn’t love Hugh,” said little Miles sturdily. “And if cousin Harry is in prison, I’ll give him my half-guinea that my godpapa gave me, and anything I have—yes, anything, except—except my little horse—and my silver waistcoat—and—and Snowball and Sweetlips at home—and—and, yes, my custard after dinner.”

This last exception was prompted by a sisterly whisper; yet even then the boy’s generosity fought with his appetite as if both were born warriors.

“But I’d give him some of it,” he added, after a pause—meaning, no doubt, the custard, for there are limits to saintship when it comes in a glass dish and smells of nutmeg.

“Shut thy mouth with it, child, and then go about thy business,” said Sir Miles, laughing. He had a considerable fund of easy humour, and liked the honest noise of a family squabble almost as much as the honest noise of knives on trenchers.

“Who would have thought he should ever be so wild?” Lady Warrington resumed, for she could not let her dish cool. “That we should have been so misled in him!”

“And that he should kiss us both!” cried Sir Miles, with a rogue’s twinkle.

“Sir Miles Warrington, I have no patience with that sort of vulgarity!” said the majestic matron, as if kissing were a crime and her own coldness a virtue.

“Which of you was the favourite yesterday, girls?” Sir Miles continued, delighted to prick their vanity.

“Favourite, indeed!” cried Flora, handsome and quick as a flame. “I told him over and over again of my engagement to dear Tom—I did, Dora—why do you sneer, if you please?”

“Nay, to do her justice, so did Dora too,” said papa.

“Because Flora seemed to wish to forget her engagement with dear Tom sometimes,” Dora remarked, with that precise malice which can be cut as thin as lemon-peel and yet flavour the whole room.

“I never, never, never wished to break with Tom!” Flora exclaimed, tossing her head and turning, by instinct, towards the glass, as if her own reflection were her truest confidante. “It is wicked of you to say so, Dora! It is you who are for ever sneering at him: it is you who are always envious because I happen—at least, because gentlemen imagine that I am not ill-looking, and prefer me to some folks, in spite of all their learning and wit!”

“Why are you always looking there, sister?” asked little Miles, with blunt innocence. “Sure, you must know your face well enough!”

“Some people look at it just as often, child, who haven’t near such good reason,” said Sir Miles gallantly, tossing his compliment as a cook tosses herbs—lightly, and to make the dish smell better.

“If you mean me, Sir Miles, I thank you,” cried Dora, accepting the seasoning gravely. “My face is as Heaven made it, and my father and mother gave it me. ’Tis not my fault if I resemble my papa’s family. If my head is homely, at least I have got some brains in it. I envious of Flora, indeed, because she has found favour in the sight of poor Tom Claypool! I should as soon be proud of captivating a ploughboy!”

“Pray, miss, was your Mr. Harry of Virginia much wiser than Tom Claypool? You would have had him for the asking!” Flora flung back.

“And so would you, miss, and have dropped Tom Claypool into the sea!” Dora returned.

“I wouldn’t.”

“You would.”

“I wouldn’t.”

And thus the shuttlecock of wrath went briskly from hand to hand—an old domestic sport, accompanied by the little sounds that make a house alive: a chair creaking, a silk sleeve whispering, Master Miles’s top-string rasping between his teeth. Lady Warrington laid down her embroidery with an air of martyrdom.

“Oh, my children! Is this the way you dwell together in unity?” she cried, as if unity were a pudding liable to be spoiled by over-stirring. “What an example you set to this Innocent!”

“Like to see ’em fight, my lady!” roared the Innocent, rubbing his hands with glee. “At her, Flora! Worry her, Dora! To it again, you little rogues!” Sir Miles encouraged, for he loved the honest sound of life when it did not threaten his dignity.

But Lady Warrington, who could never allow noise to be merely noise, must turn it into edification.

“These disputes are unseemly,” she said, pressing a hand to her bosom as if her heart were being carved. “They tear a fond mother’s heart. What cause for thankfulness ought we to have that watchful parents have prevented any idle engagements between you and your misguided cousin! If either of you had any preference for him, your excellent good sense will teach you to overcome—to eradicate—the vain feeling. That we cherished and were kind to him can never be a source of regret. ’Tis a proof of our good-nature. What we have to regret is, that your cousin should have proved unworthy of our kindness, and, coming away from the society of gamblers, play-actors, and the like, should have brought contamination—pollution, I had almost said—into this pure family!”

“Oh, bother mamma’s sermons!” Flora muttered, under her breath; and Sir Miles, whistling softly, edged away on tiptoe as if escaping a draught. Meanwhile the artless Miles junior wound up his top, pegged it under his sisters’ robes, and sent it humming across the carpet—its little wooden music—a brief, brave buzzing—staggering, falling, and dying in its usual tipsy manner long before Lady Warrington’s discourse had run itself out.

“Were you listening to me, my child?” she asked, laying a hand upon his head.

“Yes, mother,” he answered, with the whipcord in his mouth, already winding again.

“You was a-saying that Harry was very poor now, and that we oughtn’t to help him. That’s what you was saying; wasn’t it, madam?”

“My poor child,” she replied, turning her eyes upward to the ceiling—her usual refuge, as if Heaven were painted there—“thou wilt understand me better when thou art older!”

At that moment Dora, stung by the top at her toes, cried, “Get out, you little wretch!” and the boy laughed in pure delight at having made a noise and a commotion—those small domestic earthquakes by which children learn they have power.

But suddenly a different sound entered the room, and with it a change of air, as when a door opens upon a colder passage: brisk footsteps; the scrape of a boot; a voice too eager to be ceremonious.

Sir Miles returned, and after him came Tom Claypool, striding as if pursued, his face heated, his breath puffing, his waistcoat straining like a soldier’s. The very hush that followed them had its own note—an expectant silence, sharp as a knife poised over a joint.

“Here’s a pretty business, my Lady Warrington!” Sir Miles cried. “Here’s a wonderful wonder of wonders, girls!”

“For goodness’ sake, gentlemen, what is your intelligence?” asked the virtuous matron, lifting her hands as if for prayer and scandal together.

“The whole town’s talking about it, my lady!” Tom gasped. “I seen him.”

“Seen both of them,” Sir Miles continued. “At Ranelagh last night, with a regular mob after ’em. And so like, that but for their different ribbons you would hardly have told one from the other. One was in blue, the other in brown; but I’m certain he has worn both the suits here.”

“What suits?” cried the girls together.

“Why, your fortunate youth, to be sure,” said Sir Miles, swelling with the pleasure of a discovery. “Our precious Virginian, and heir to the principality!”

“Is my nephew, then, released from his incarceration?” Lady Warrington asked, already arranging the face with which she would receive a penitent.

“Confound him!” Sir Miles roared, with an oath too hearty for her taste. “What should you think, my Lady Warrington, if this precious nephew of mine should turn out to be an impostor—by George! no better than an adventurer?”

“An inward monitor whispered me as much!” cried the lady, instantly promoting her suspicion into prophecy. “But I dashed from me the unworthy thought. Speak, Sir Miles, we burn with impatience.”

“I’ll speak, my love, when you’ve done,” Sir Miles returned, his humour not quite vanquished. “Well, what do you think of my gentleman, who comes into my house, dines at my table, is treated as one of this family, kisses my—”

“What?” Tom Claypool barked, turning suddenly as red as his waistcoat.

“Hem! Kisses my wife’s hand,” Sir Miles amended, “and is treated in the fondest manner. What do you think of this fellow, who talks of his property and his principality—turning out to be a beggarly SECOND SON! A beggar, my Lady Warrington, by—”

“Sir Miles Warrington,” she cut in, “no violence of language before these dear ones! I sink to the earth, confounded by this unutterable hypocrisy. And did I entrust thee to a pretender, my blessed boy? Did I leave thee with an impostor, my innocent one?” She clutched at Master Miles as if the child were a silver spoon in danger of being stolen.

“Who’s an impostor, my lady?” the boy asked.

“That confounded young scamp of a Harry Warrington!” Sir Miles bawled; whereupon little Miles, after a puzzled pause, seemed struck by some hidden grief and burst out crying—great honest sobs that made more truth in the room than all Lady Warrington’s morals. She tried to gather him up, but he kicked and bawled and flung off the caress as if affection, when it is too late, hurts like rough wool.

And then the butler’s voice—steady as if announcing dinner—rang out: “Mr. George Warrington, Mr. Henry Warrington!”

Lady Warrington dropped Master Miles from her lap as if he had suddenly become inconvenient. Sir Miles’s face grew as hot as Claypool’s waistcoat. Dora and Flora rose with the chillest of curtseys; their mother with a third, majestic and frozen. The air itself seemed to tighten, the domestic sounds silenced, as when a kitchen stops clattering because the master has entered.

George Warrington and Harry came in.

Little Miles, wiping his face with the back of his hand, darted forward. “Oh, Harry! No! which is Harry? You’re my Harry!”—and he chose rightly, flinging his small arms about the younger brother with the fierce fidelity of childhood. “Oh, you dear Harry! I’m so glad you are come! and they’ve been abusing you so!”

“I am come to pay my duty to my uncle,” said George, the dark-haired gentleman, his voice measured and grave, “and to thank him for his hospitalities to my brother Henry.”

“What, nephew George? My brother’s face and eyes!” cried Sir Miles; and in an instant the honest delight broke through his anger as sun through cloud. He seized a hand of each young man and held them fast, as if touch itself could make amends. “Boys both, I am delighted to see you!”

“This indeed hath been a most mysterious and most providential resuscitation,” Lady Warrington said, as if she were reading the caption to a monument. “Only I wonder that my nephew Henry concealed the circumstance until now,” she added, letting suspicion peep sidelong from beneath her propriety.

“He knew it no more than your ladyship,” George answered quietly.

The young ladies looked down, and their silence had its own language, like a dish refused without a word.

“Indeed, sir! a most singular circumstance,” Lady Warrington continued. “We had heard of it, sir; and Mr. Claypool—our county neighbour—had just brought us the intelligence, and it even now formed the subject of my conversation with my daughters.”

“Yes,” cried a small voice—too truthful to be safe—“and do you know, Harry, father and mother said you was a—a imp—”

“Silence, my child! Screwby, convey Master Warrington to his own apartment!” Lady Warrington commanded, as if she could banish the sound itself. “These, Mr. Warrington—or I suppose I should say nephew George—are your cousins.”

Two frigid curtseys followed—two cheeses made of young faces; two hands held out as if charity were being given. George made a profound bow that included all three ladies, but embraced none. He laid his hat to his heart.

“It is my duty, madam, to pay my respects to my uncle and cousins,” he said, “and to thank your ladyship for such hospitality as you have been enabled to show to my brother.”

“It was not much, nephew, but it was our best. Ods bobs! it was our best!” Sir Miles cried, eager to pour warmth over the frost.

“And I appreciate it, sir,” George said, and looked gravely round the family as if taking inventory of what had been given and what withheld.

“Give us thy hand. Not a word more,” Sir Miles insisted, smacking George’s hand into his with a hearty sound, like a cook clapping dough. “Do you think I’m a cannibal and won’t extend the hand of hospitality to my dear brother’s son? What say you, lads? Will you eat our mutton at three? This is my neighbour, Tom Claypool—son to Sir Thomas Claypool, Baronet—and my very good friend. Hey, Tom! Thou’lt be of the party, Tom? Thou knowest our brew, hey, my boy?”

“Yes, I know it, Sir Miles,” Tom answered, without any rapture, his eyes still smouldering.

“And thou shalt taste it, my boy!” Sir Miles persisted. “What is there for dinner, my Lady Warrington? Our food is plain, but plenty, lads—plain, but plenty!”

“We cannot partake of it to-day, sir,” George replied. “We dine with a friend who occupies my Lord Wrotham’s house—your neighbour. Colonel Lambert—Major-General Lambert he has just been made.”

“With two daughters, I think—countrified-looking girls—are they not?” Flora asked, in that tone with which fine ladies speak of rustic poultry.

“I think I have remarked two little rather dowdy things,” Dora added, sharpening the point.

“They are as good girls as any in England!” Harry broke out suddenly—Harry, who had sat there burning, dismissed by everybody’s eyes as if his reign were over and he had become invisible. The words escaped him hot and unguarded, as a hungry man defends bread.

“Oh, indeed, cousin!” Dora said, turning at last to look at him, and letting the glance do what words might deny. “You are very charitable—or very lucky, I’m sure. You see angels where we only see ordinary little persons. I’m sure I could not imagine who were those odd-looking people in Lord Wrotham’s coach, with his handsome liveries. But if they were three angels, I have nothing to say.”

“My brother is an enthusiast,” George interposed smoothly. “He is often mistaken about women.”

“Oh, really!” Dora returned, and for an instant looked uneasy, as if she felt the ground shift under her well-planted superiority.

“I fear my nephew Henry has indeed met with some unfavourable specimens of our sex,” Lady Warrington remarked, with a sigh manufactured for the occasion. “We are so easily taken in.”

“We are both very young yet, madam,” George said. “We shall grow older and learn better.”

“Most sincerely, nephew George, I trust you may. You have my best wishes, my prayers, for your brother’s welfare and your own. No efforts of ours have been wanting. At a painful moment, to which I will not further allude—” She paused, enjoying the pause like a moralist who knows the power of silence.

“And when my uncle Sir Miles was out of town,” George added, looking towards Sir Miles, who smiled at him with affectionate approval.

“—I sent your brother a work which I thought might comfort him, and I know might improve him. Nay, do not thank me; I claim no credit; I did but my duty—a humble woman’s duty. For what are this world’s goods compared to the welfare of a soul? If I did good, I am thankful; if I was useful, I rejoice. If, through my means, you have been brought, Harry, to consider—”

“Oh! the sermon, is it?” Harry broke in, plain as a kitchen-boy and twice as sincere. “I hadn’t time to read a single syllable of it, aunt—thank you. You see I don’t care much about that kind of thing—but thank you all the same.”

“The intention is everything,” George said, with a quiet courtesy that did not concede a line. “And we are both grateful. Our dear friend General Lambert intended to give bail for Harry; but, happily, I had funds of Harry’s with me to meet any demands upon us. But the kindness is the same, and I am grateful to the friend who hastened to my brother’s rescue when he had most need of aid, and when his own relations happened—so unfortunately—to be out of town.”

Sir Miles’s face changed—shame and affection wrestling there like two dogs. “Anything I could do, my dear boy—my brother’s son—my own nephew—ods bobs! you know—that is, anything—anything, you know!” He struck George’s hand again with that generous smack.

“You can’t stay and dine with us? Put off the Colonel—the General—do, now! Or name a day. My Lady Warrington, make my nephew name a day when he will sit under his grandfather’s picture, and drink some of his wine!”

But the young men, having paid duty, must go. They took their leave; and with them went a certain honest warmth, leaving behind the faint chill of virtue congratulating itself.

“His intellectual faculties seem more developed than those of his unlucky younger brother,” Lady Warrington observed as soon as the door closed, as if a nephew were a text to be anatomised. “The younger must be reckless and extravagant about money indeed; for did you remark—the loss of his reversion in Virginia, the amount of which has no doubt been grossly exaggerated, but nevertheless must be something considerable—did you remark that the ruin of Harry’s prospects scarcely seemed to affect him?”

“I shouldn’t be at all surprised that the elder turns out to be as poor as the young one,” Dora tossed out, for she could not bear admiration to go unpunished.

“He! he!” Flora tittered. “Did you see that cousin George had one of cousin Harry’s suits of clothes on—the brown and gold—that one he wore when he went with you to the oratorio, Dora?”

“Did he take Flora to an oratorio?” Tom Claypool demanded, fiercer than a man whose roast has been stolen.

“I was ill and couldn’t go, and my cousin went with her,” Dora answered coolly, and the lie of emphasis was that she seemed to take pleasure in Tom’s smart.

“Far be it from me to object to any innocent amusement, much less to the music of Mr. Handel, dear Mr. Claypool,” Lady Warrington began, rallying her morality once more like a cook bringing out a second course. “Music refines the soul, elevates the understanding, is heard in our churches, and ’tis well known was practised by King David. Your operas I shun as deleterious; your ballets I would forbid to my children as most immoral; but music, my dears! May we enjoy it, like everything else in reason—may we—”

“There’s the music of the dinner-bell,” Sir Miles said, rubbing his hands; and indeed, from below came that metallic summons, a sound that ruled the house more surely than sermons. In that clang lay the promise of steam and savour: the hiss of gravy upon hot metal, the sweet animal smell of mutton, the yeasty breath of ale drawn fresh, and the blessed domestic chorus of plates and appetite.

“Come, girls. Screwby, go and fetch Master Miley. Tom, take down my lady.”

“Nay, dear Thomas, I walk but slowly,” Lady Warrington said, arranging herself as if moving were a virtue. “Go you with dearest Flora downstairs.”

And Dora—who had resolved, by some spiteful instinct, that if she could not choose the menu she would at least choose the noise—made the evening pleasant by talking of Handel and oratorios constantly during dinner, until even the clink of knives seemed to keep time to her discourse.
