Elinor watched with quiet concern as her friend’s spirits remained low throughout his visit. His presence brought her only partial comfort, for it was clear that he was not truly at ease. Though he had come to Barton Cottage, his joy in being there seemed incomplete. She longed to believe that the affection he once clearly held for her still burned bright—but his manner was uncertain, shifting between warmth and reserve, leaving her unsure of where his heart truly lay.

The next morning, Edward joined Elinor and Marianne in the breakfast room before the rest of the household descended. Marianne, ever eager to foster closeness between them, soon excused herself and retreated upstairs—only to stop short when she heard the parlour door open. Turning around, she was startled to see Edward stepping out.

“I’m going into the village to check on my horses,” he said, “since you’re not quite ready for breakfast. I’ll be back shortly.”

When he returned, he spoke with renewed appreciation for the surrounding countryside. His walk had taken him through various parts of the valley, and from the higher ground of the village, he’d been treated to a sweeping view that delighted him. This sparked Marianne’s interest instantly. She began to describe her own deep admiration for the scenery, eager to know what details had caught Edward’s eye.

But he cut her off gently. “Don’t ask too closely, Marianne,” he said. “I confess—I have no real understanding of the picturesque. I’ll offend you if we go into specifics. I’ll call hills steep when they should be bold, surfaces odd when they ought to be rugged, and distant views invisible when they’re merely softened by mist. You must settle for the honesty of my opinion. To me, this is a fine country—the hills are steep, the woods thick with good timber, and the valley looks warm and inviting, dotted with rich meadows and tidy farmhouses. It fits perfectly with my idea of beauty: nature combined with usefulness. And since you find it beautiful, I can easily believe it’s picturesque too—even if I don’t see the rocks or moss or tangled brushwood that make it so. I simply don’t understand those things.”

“It’s true enough,” Marianne replied, “but why boast of it?”

Elinor smiled faintly. “I think,” she said, “Edward avoids one kind of pretension by falling into another. He dislikes people who feign deep emotion over landscapes, so he chooses instead to pretend indifference. He’s fastidious—he must have an affectation of his own.”

“Quite right,” Marianne agreed. “Admiration of scenery has become nothing more than empty talk. Everyone pretends to feel deeply, quoting the same old phrases about ‘picturesque beauty’ as if it were a language of its own. I hate such jargon. Sometimes I’ve kept my real feelings silent because I couldn’t express them without sounding stale and worn-out.”

“I’m certain,” Edward said, “that you feel every bit of delight you claim to feel. But in return, your sister must allow me to feel only what I say. I enjoy a fine view—but not for the reasons others do. I don’t admire twisted, dying trees. I prefer tall, straight ones that thrive. I don’t care for crumbling cottages or wild thistles. A neat farmhouse pleases me far more than a ruined tower—and a group of cheerful villagers brings me more joy than any band of romantic outlaws.”

Marianne stared at him, astonished. Then she glanced at Elinor with pity. Elinor only laughed softly. The conversation ended there, and Marianne fell into thoughtful silence—until something unexpected drew her attention.

She was seated beside Edward when Mrs. Dashwood handed him his tea. As he reached for the cup, his hand passed directly in front of her, revealing a ring on one finger—a ring with a plait of hair set in the center.

“I never saw you wear a ring before, Edward,” she exclaimed. “Is that Fanny’s hair? I remember her saying she’d give you some. But I would have thought hers darker.”

Her words slipped out without thought, but the moment she saw the pain in Edward’s face, she regretted them fiercely. His cheeks flushed deeply, and he glanced briefly at Elinor before replying, “Yes—it’s my sister’s hair. The setting changes how it looks, you know.”

Elinor met his gaze—and felt a sudden certainty. That hair was hers. Not a gift, but something taken, hidden, perhaps even stolen from her own possession. Yet she did not feel offended. Instead, she chose to ignore the moment, turning immediately to another subject, while silently resolving to examine the ring closely whenever she could, to confirm beyond doubt that the hair matched her own shade.

Edward remained visibly unsettled for hours, his thoughts drifting inward. He was unusually quiet all morning. Marianne reproached herself bitterly for her careless words—but she didn’t realize how little hurt she had caused Elinor.

By midday, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings arrived, drawn by news of a gentleman guest at the cottage. With Margaret’s earlier hints, they quickly deduced that the name Ferrars began with an F—and thus, their future teasing was already planned. For now, though, they contented themselves with meaningful glances and knowing smiles directed at Elinor.

Sir John, as always, came bearing invitations. “You *must* come drink tea with us tonight,” he declared. “We’ll be alone—perfect for a quiet evening. And tomorrow, you absolutely must dine with us—we’ll have a large gathering.”

Mrs. Jennings added her encouragement. “And who knows? You might even raise a dance!” she teased. “That would tempt *you*, Miss Marianne.”

“A dance?” Marianne gasped. “Impossible! Who would dance?”

“Why, yourselves, the Careys, the Whitakers—naturally!” Sir John replied. “What, you thought no one could dance now that *certain someone* has gone?”

“I wish with all my heart,” he continued, “that Willoughby were here again.”

His words, and Marianne’s sudden flush, struck Edward sharply. He leaned toward Elinor, whispering, “Who is Willoughby?”

She gave a brief answer. But Marianne’s expression said far more. Edward read her face now—understanding not just the meaning of others’ remarks, but also the mystery behind her earlier emotions.

As their visitors left, he moved quietly beside her and whispered, “I’ve been guessing. May I tell you what I think?”

“What do you mean?”

“Shall I?”

“Of course.”

“Well then—I guess Mr. Willoughby hunts.”

Marianne was both surprised and flustered, yet she couldn’t help smiling at his quiet wit. After a pause, she murmured, “Oh, Edward! How can you? But someday—perhaps you’ll like him.”

He looked at her, puzzled by her earnest tone. Had he not assumed it was a joke, a light-hearted tease meant to amuse their circle, born of mere acquaintance? He would never have dared speak of it otherwise.
