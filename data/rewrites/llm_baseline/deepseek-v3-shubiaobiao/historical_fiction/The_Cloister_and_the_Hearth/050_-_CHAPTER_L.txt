Margaret Brandt found a measure of peace among strangers. Yet her trials were far from over. When a prominent townsman fell ill—a man as fanciful as he was wealthy—he exhausted every physician in Rotterdam before sending for Peter. The old man arrived to find the patient bled and purged to the brink of death. With a scoff, Peter hurled the apothecary’s bottles out the window, flung open the casement, and set to work with simpler, kinder remedies: yolks beaten in good Schiedam, meat stewed in wine and water with mild herbs. The man recovered, weak as a wrung cloth, but alive—and voluble. He sang Peter’s praises through every street, and the old physician, long overlooked, found himself suddenly famous.

For a time, fortune smiled. Peter’s pride swelled; his purse grew heavier. Yet he poured much of his earnings back into rare herbs and costly drugs—until Margaret, with a mother’s foresight, wrested a portion from his grasp. But the triumph came too late. The excitement proved fatal.

One evening, his farewell to her slurred oddly. By morning, he lay senseless, his speech stolen. Margaret, her father’s pupil for years, recognized the stroke at once. Still, she fetched another physician—one whom Peter had once thwarted—who confirmed her fears and eagerly reached for his lancet. She refused. "He despised bloodletting," she said, "especially for the aged." Peter lived, but his mind was no longer his own. Memory frayed; his prescriptions became unreliable. Patients still came, a few stubbornly waiting for his return. But Margaret saw the truth: ruin loomed.

Pride and poverty had kept her from her cousin, William Johnson. Now, desperate, she sought him—only to find him deep in drink, his servant lolling familiarly beside him. When Margaret hinted at private family matters, the woman bristled.

Margaret flushed. "I beg your pardon. I did not know my cousin had adopted the customs of this town. Another time, then." She turned to leave.

*"What d’you mean by ‘customs’?"* the woman snapped. *"A faithful servant keeps her master safe from beggarly kin!"*

Margaret whirled. "Modesty becomes you—for you are no servant. Your tongue betrays you. The ape only shows her tail once she’s climbed the tree. No, *there* sits the servant—God help him! And while that’s so, fear not—his kin will never be so poor in spirit as to seek charity where the likes of *you* may sneer at it." With one burning glance at her silent, shamefaced cousin, she left.

Tears waited until she was home. Now she faced the stark truth: two helpless men to feed, a child on the way, and only five gold pieces left. Yet that unborn child, helpless as it seemed, became her salvation.

Nature, cunning in all creatures preparing for their young, sharpened her wits. She brooded, schemed—then acted. She moved Peter to another room, scrubbed her own chamber spotless, polished the glass globe, hung it from the ceiling, dusted the crocodile, and nailed it outside. Then she schooled Martin in his new role: loitering sentinel, murmuring to passersby of the great alchemist within—a sage who, in rare moments of leisure, deigned to cure mortal ills.

Patients came. Margaret received them, barred from the "learned master" (lost in search of the grand elixir), and bade them return in two hours with symptoms. For the worshipful, she mimicked a physician’s scrutiny—pulse, skin, a grave frown—then vanished to "consult the sacred texts," emerging with illegible scrawls and mysterious vials. If she had the ingredients, she’d compound them before wide eyes, lips moving over the "ancient script."

*Populus vult decipi.* The people wished to be fooled. And fooled they were—relieved of coin, stuffed with faith and physic, and sent away satisfied.

Each evening, she knelt, begging God’s forgiveness *"for the sake of my child."* Yet as cures mounted, her conscience calloused. Martin, stiffening with age but strong as an ox, proved a willing accomplice—until drink loosened his tongue. Boasting of how *"me and my General bite the burghers,"* he drew the law’s eye.

Constables came. Margaret, trembling, was hauled before the burgomaster, flanked by scowling physicians in square caps, accusing her of unlawful practice. At first, fear choked her. Then, rallying, she spoke plain: she had been her father’s pupil, followed his methods, and *healed* where they had failed.

*"It is not for myself, sirs, but for two helpless men who depend on me. Forbid me this, and how shall I feed them? You are fathers—would you have me choose between starvation and shame?"*

The appeal faltered as her own words struck her. She hid her face, tears slipping between her fingers. The burgomaster remained unmoved.

She vowed, weeping, never to meddle again. Then came the worst blow: to pay the fine, she must break open Gerard’s *"To-morrow box,"* where she’d hidden their savings. The nails resisted. The constables grew impatient.

*"No!"* She seized a hammer, struck weakly, then with a cry, shattered it. The fine took all her gains and two gold pieces more. When they left, she slumped over the wreckage, loose hair veiling her as she sobbed, *"My love’s box is broken—and my heart with it."*

Martin found her thus. Furious, he vowed vengeance on whatever gossip had betrayed her. Margaret groaned. *"When you’ve slain every tongue in Rotterdam, shall we feast on corpses? Find me a way to feed us, or be still!"*

By evening, she rose, washed her face, and set Martin to work. The crocodile came down; a laundry basket took its place.

Even as they spoke, a patient arrived. Margaret refused medicine but offered washing. The man stormed off. *"Stupid creature,"* she sighed. Then a woman came—for a sick child. Again, Margaret proposed laundry.

So *"Doctor Margaret"* became a washerwoman. And though Martin balked at suds, she drilled him: *"A mule could wash with his hooves if driven to it!"* Laughing, he rolled up his sleeves. They worked fiercely, keeping the wolf from the door—but no more. The mended *"To-morrow box"* stood empty.

Then fate twisted anew. Among her clients was the mayor. Returning his linen, she heard his daughter was deathly ill. By careful questioning, she pieced together the symptoms: listlessness, no appetite, a shunning of all company. The girl was fading like a plucked flower.

Margaret waylaid the mayor. *"I was your unlicensed doctor. Now I’m your washerwoman. Yet let me see your daughter—not for pay, but because no healer in Rotterdam has helped her."*

Reluctantly, he agreed.

The girl, pale and sullen, turned from her. Margaret sat beside her, gentle but probing.

*"I didn’t—till you told me."* She spun a tale of two star-crossed lovers—her own story, thinly veiled. The girl, despite herself, was drawn in.

The girl did, and wept. *"You’ve done me good. Come again."*

Margaret returned, watching what stirred her. Only one topic sparked life: love. She confronted the mayor.

At her urging, the mayor paid his household in his daughter’s presence. Margaret watched, fingers on the girl’s wrist. When Ulrich, the dark-haired clerk, entered, the pulse leapt.

*"Him,"* she told the mayor. *"And he loves her in turn."*

The dignitary balked. *"A street brat I took in!"*

At last, he yielded. Margaret returned to the girl with a smile.

The girl flushed head to toe.

*"Cruel!"* the girl whispered.

Margaret grinned. *"Your father says if he loves your hair so, he may as well love the rest. So—will you wed Ulrich, or waste to your grave?"*

And the marble girl melted.
