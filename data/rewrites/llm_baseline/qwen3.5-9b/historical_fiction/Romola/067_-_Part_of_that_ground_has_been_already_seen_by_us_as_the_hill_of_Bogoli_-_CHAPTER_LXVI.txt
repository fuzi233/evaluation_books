The following day was Palm Sunday, known locally in the olive-rich Valdarno simply as "Olive Sunday." The morning sun broke forth with a clarity and sweetness born of the previous night's rain. Once again, Savonarola ascended the pulpit in San Marco. Before him gathered a flock whose faith remained unshaken, and in a tone of calm, sorrowful sincerity, he proclaimed his readiness to die. Amidst all visions, he beheld only his own impending doom. He offered the benediction, watching the faces of men and women rise toward him in veneration and love. Then, he descended the steps of the pulpit and turned forever away from that scene.

Before the sun had set, Florence erupted into chaos. The passions ignited the day prior had smoldered through the quiet morning, now bursting forth with a fury aided by design and tacitly encouraged by official complicity. The disturbance began at the Duomo, where certain members of the *Compagnacci* attempted to disrupt the evening sermon that the *Piagnoni* had assembled to hear. But once blood rose in the veins of the crowd and the gathering swelled into an affray, the cry rang out: "To San Marco! Fire to San Marco!"

Long before darkness fully claimed the sky, both the church and the convent were besieged by an enraged, swelling multitude. Resistance was fierce. The monks, long aware of the mounting hostility outside, kept arms within their walls. Some fought with such vigor in their long white tunics that they resembled Knights Templar, defying even Savonarola's commands to stand down. Their muscles, still strong beneath the rough Dominican serge, refused to yield to the impulse of surrender. Laymen who had chosen to remain also fought desperately; gunfire echoed near the high altar beside the great crucifix, stones and burning embers rained from the convent roofs, and swords clashed violently within the cloisters.

Despite the overwhelming force of the attackers, the assault raged until deep night. Meanwhile, the government's demonstrations had targeted the convent directly. Early in the conflict, guards were summoned—not to disperse the rioters, but to order everyone inside the convent to lay down their arms, to command all laymen to depart, and to demand that Savonarola himself leave Florentine territory within twelve hours. Had Savonarola obeyed then, he likely would have been torn apart immediately; he was willing to go, but his friends obstructed him. Many considered it a grave risk even for prominent laymen to exit via the garden wall, yet among those who chose to flee was Francesco Valori, hoping to rally aid from without.

By the time the struggle reached its deepest hour, the fight could scarcely last much longer. Soon, the *Compagnacci* might carry their swords into the library, where Savonarola prayed with the brethren who had either not taken up arms or had laid them down at his command. Just then, a second body of guards arrived, commissioned by the Signoria to seize Fra Girolamo and his two coadjutors, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

When the lantern-light revealed the Friar emerging from the convent door accompanied by a guard offering no protection save that of the prison, a roar of triumphant hatred swept through the crowd. The struggle became a frantic race: who would reach the prophet first in the rushing stream ascending the narrow street? Who would secure the best position to hurl insults, or perhaps strike and kick him? This was easy for many armed *Compagnacci*, who mixed freely with the guards. As Savonarola felt himself dragged and shoved through the jeering multitude, as lanterns were raised to reveal mocking faces, and as he endured spittle, blows, and kicks accompanied by grossest insults, it seemed to him that life's bitterest cup was finally drained. If men deemed him guilty and thirsted for his blood, death alone awaited him. Yet, the deepest bitterness cannot always be forced from without; the lowest depth of resignation is not found in martyrdom, but only when one covers one's head in silence and feels, "I am not worthy to be a martyr; the Truth shall prosper, but not by me."

That brief, imperfect triumph of humiliating the Friar, who soon vanished beneath the doorway of the Old Palace, proved merely like the taste of blood to a tiger. Why stop there? Were not the houses of the hypocrites' friends waiting to be sacked? Already, half the armed multitude, positioned too far in the rear to participate significantly in the siege of the convent, engaged in the more profitable task of attacking wealthy homes. They did not plunder with mindless frenzy, but with discriminating precision, targeting residences belonging to leading *Piagnoni*. This selectivity indicated that the riot was guided, the rabble with clubs and staves well-commanded by sword-girt *Compagnacci*.

Next in line for criminality stood the ambitious Francesco Valori, suspected of plotting with the Friar to establish himself as a lifelong Doge or Gonfaloniere. Then came the grey-haired man who, eight months prior, had raised his arm and voice in a ferocious demand for justice against five fellow citizens. Having narrowly escaped San Marco to experience what others termed "justice," he now watched his house surrounded by an angry, greedy mob. His wife was slain by an arrow, and he himself was murdered on his way to answer a summons to the Palazzo, struck down by the swords of men named Ridolfi and Tornabuoni.

Thus, this "Masque of the Furies," labeled Riot, was enacted throughout Florence during the hours of night and early dawn. However, the chief director remained invisible, issuing orders from a private retreat due to his high standing, unwilling to let his red plume wave amidst the work before sunrise. This sanctuary was the same house and room in a quiet street between Santa Croce and San Marco where we previously witnessed Tito's secret visit to Dolfo Spini.

Here, the Captain of the *Compagnacci* sat through this memorable night, receiving a revolving cast of visitors—some disguised as armed *Compagnacci*, others dressed obscurely without visible weapons. Abundant wine flowed on the table, cups available for any chance comer. Though Spini guarded against excess, he drank enough to heighten the excitement generated by the ceaseless flow of bad news. Among the shadowy guests, Ser Ceccone was a frequent presence. As the hours advanced toward the morning twilight, he remained Spini's constant companion alongside Francesco Cei, who hid carelessly in Florence, awaiting the revocation of his banishment once the Friar fell.

The tapers burned down into low, shapeless masses, and holes in the shutters glowed with a somber outer light when Spini suddenly sprang from his seat. Walking up and down with an angry flush on his face, reacting to recent conversation with his two unmilitary associates, he exploded: "The devil spit him! He shall pay for it, though. Ha, ha! The claws shall come down on him when he least expects it. So he was to be the great man after all! He pretended to throw everything into my cap, as if I were a blind beggar, all the while winking and filling his own purse. I'd like to hang skins about him and set my hounds on him! And he stole that fine ruby of mine—I was a fool to give it to him yesterday. Malediction! He laughed at me in his sleeve two years ago and spoiled the finest plan ever laid. I was a fool to trust myself with a rascal whose twisted contrivances no one could see to the end except himself."

"A Greek, too," interjected Francesco Cei, a slight smile of amusement playing on his face at Spini's rage, "who dropped into Florence with gems packed about him."

"You did not choose your confidant very wisely, my Dolfo," Cei remarked.

"He is a cursed deal cleverer than you, Francesco, and handsomer too," Spini retorted, turning on his associate with a general desire to torment whatever presented itself.

"I humbly conceive," said Ser Ceccone, "that Messer Francesco's poetic genius will outweigh—"

"Yes, yes, rub your hands! I hate that notary's trick of yours," Spini interrupted, noting that his patronage relied largely on such frankness. "But here comes Taddeo, or someone else: now is the time! What news, eh?" he demanded as two *Compagnacci* entered with heated expressions.

"Bad!" replied one. "The people have decided they want to sack Soderini's house. Now that we have blocked them, they will turn on us unless we act carefully. I suspect some Mediceans are buzzing among them; we may see them attacking your palace over the bridge shortly unless we find another bait."

"I have it!" cried Spini. Seizing Taddeo by the belt, he drew him aside to give instructions while the other continued informing Cei of the Signoria's interference regarding Soderini's house. Presently, Spini exclaimed, pushing Taddeo slightly toward the door, "Go, and make quick work."
