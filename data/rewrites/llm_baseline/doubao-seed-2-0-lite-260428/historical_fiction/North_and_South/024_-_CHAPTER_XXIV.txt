Margaret dragged herself out of bed the next morning, breathless with relief that the long, haunted night was over. She had not slept deeply, not refreshed, but the mere passing of the riot’s chaos had granted her a kind of quiet rest. All in the Hale household had stayed peaceful through the dark hours: her mother had woken only once, and the doctor said her fever had dipped at last. A faint, welcome breeze stirred in Milton’s usual heavy, smoke-thick air. No leafy trees lined the town’s cobblestone streets to show the wind’s playful toss, but Margaret could picture it clear as day, far beyond the factory walls, in the hedgerow lanes, the little bramble copses, the thick green woods south of the town—could hear that soft, murmuring rustle, that rushing, dancing sound that sent a little echo of unlooked-for gladness thrumming through her tired chest.

She settled to her sewing in her mother’s bedroom, resolving that once Mrs. Hale’s usual forenoon nap ended, she would help her dress, and after dinner she would walk to the Higgins’ cottage to see Bessy. She would banish every thought of the Thornton family, she told herself. There was no need to waste a moment’s consideration on them until they stood before her in the flesh. But of course, the effort of trying not to think of them only dragged every memory sharper to the front of her mind. Hot, unbidden flushes swept over her pale face, staining her cheeks bright as a sudden sunbeam skittering across a grey, choppy sea.

Dixon opened the door so softly the hinges did not creak, and tiptoed across the carpet to Margaret’s side by the shaded window.
“Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret. He’s waiting in the drawing-room.”

Margaret’s sewing slipped from her fingers and fell to her lap. “Did he ask for me? Isn’t papa come back from his call on Mr. Bell?”
“He asked for you, miss. Master’s out still.”

“Very well. I will come,” Margaret said, steadying her voice. But she lingered strangely, smoothing down her gown, tucking the stray strands of dark hair that covered the faint bruise on her temple tighter against her skin.

Downstairs, Mr. Thornton stood stiff at the drawing-room window, his back turned to the door, apparently fixed on watching some commotion in the street. In truth, he was fighting a war against himself. His heart thudded heavy and thick at the thought of her stepping into the room. He could not forget the press of her arms around his neck, the day before—at the time he had been too furious, too caught up in the threat of the rioters to feel anything but impatient to get her out of harm’s way, but now the memory of her throwing herself between him and the stones sent a thrill shooting through every limb, melting every iron resolve he had built up over years of running his mill, all his hard-won self-control, like wax held to a flame. He dreaded the moment he would turn and see her, afraid he would stride forward and hold his arms out, wordlessly begging her to nestle there the way she had clung to him unthinking the day before—afraid that this time, he would never let her go. His pulse hammered loud and fast. Strong, steady man that he was, he trembled at the thought of the speech he had rehearsed all night, at the way she might receive it. Half his heart burned with a wild, impatient hope that she would soften, that she would flush and falter and fall into his arms like she was coming home. The other half flinched from the thought of cold, angry rejection, a fate that would wither every bright prospect of his future so thoroughly he refused to even name it to himself.

He only startled to awareness when he felt her presence in the room, quiet as a shadow. He turned, and realized he had not heard her step at all: the clatter of the street carts and factory whistles had drowned out the soft rustle of her muslin gown.

She stood by the table, making no move to sit. Her eyelids were half-drooped over her eyes, her teeth set firm but not clenched, her lips just barely parted to show a faint white line between their curves. Slow, deep breaths only moved her chin and the fine curve of her nostrils; every other line of her face was still and marble-pale. The fine grain of her skin, the soft oval of her cheeks, the rich full outline of her mouth with its corners tucked into deep dimples—all were drained of their usual warm, healthy colour, made paler still by the dark fall of her hair pulled low over her temples to hide the bruise the rioter’s stone had left. Her head tilted just a little back, in that old proud, unyielding stance he had admired from the first day she stepped into his Milton drawing-room. Her long arms hung perfectly still at her sides. She looked exactly like some noble prisoner, falsely accused of a crime she loathed and despised, too indignant to even waste breath justifying herself.

Mr. Thornton took one hasty step forward, then reined himself in, walked quiet and firm to the open door, and shut it. He came back to stand opposite her, pausing for a long moment just to drink in the sight of her, beautiful and fierce, before he dared to break the spell with the words he had to say.

“Miss Hale. I was very ungrateful yesterday—”
“You had nothing to be grateful for,” she cut in, lifting her eyes to look him full and straight in the face. “You mean, I suppose, that you think you owe me thanks for what I did.” Against every effort of her will, a deep hot blush spread all over her face, burning so bright it stung the corners of her eyes—but her gaze never wavered from his, steady and grave. “It was only a natural instinct. Any woman would have done exactly the same. We all hold the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege, to stand between danger and a man when the crowd runs mad. I ought rather to apologise to you,” she added hastily, “for speaking those thoughtless words that sent you down into the thick of the danger in the first place.”
“It was not your words that did it—it was the truth they carried, sharp as it was,” he said. “But you will not turn me aside from this, and let me skip over saying how deeply grateful I am—how—” He was teetering on the edge of the confession he had held back for months. He forced himself to slow, to weigh every word, to master his hot, rushing passion. For a second his will held, and he stopped mid-sentence.
“I do not try to escape anything,” she said. “I only tell you, you owe me no gratitude. And I will add that any expression of it will be painful to me, because I do not feel I deserve it. Still, if speaking will relieve you of even a fancied obligation, you may go on.”
“I do not want to be relieved of that obligation,” he said, stung sharp by her calm. “Fancied or real, I do not stop to question it. I choose to believe I owe you my very life—ay, smile if you will, and call it exaggeration. I believe it, because it makes that life worth more to think that every bit of joy I ever feel, every honest pride I take in my work at the mill, every sharp, bright moment of being alive from this day forward, I owe it all to you.” His voice dropped so low, thick with tender, burning passion, that Margaret shivered where she stood. “To owe all of that to you—you must hear me out,” he said, stepping forward, his tone stern and determined, his hand closing tight around hers. “To the one woman I love, as I do not believe any man ever loved a woman before.”

He panted, waiting to hear her answer. The first icy, faltering words that fell from her lips made him fling her hand away as if it burned him.
“Your way of speaking shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot help that being my first feeling. I dare say it might not be, if I understood the kind of feeling you describe. I do not want to vex you, and we must speak softly, for mamma is still asleep—but your whole manner offends me—”
“Offends you!” he exclaimed, raw with hurt. “I am the most unfortunate man alive.”
“Yes,” she said, regaining all her quiet dignity. “I do feel offended, and I think justly. You seem to fancy my conduct yesterday”—the deep carnation blush flooded her cheeks again, but this time her eyes flashed with indignation, not shame—“was some personal act between you and me. You come to thank me for it, instead of seeing, as any gentleman would—yes, a gentleman,” she repeated, echoing their old argument on the very meaning of the word, “that any woman worthy of the name would step forward to shield a man in danger from a violent crowd, with all the revered helplessness of her sex.”
“And the man you rescued is forbidden the small relief of saying thank you!” he broke in, his tone sharp with contempt. “I am a man. I claim the right to speak my feelings plainly.”
“And I yield you that right,” she replied, proud and steady. “I only tell you, insisting on it causes me pain. But you seem to have imagined I was not just following a natural womanly instinct, but”—the tears she had held down for hours, fighting against them fiercely, rose thick in her eyes and choked her voice—“that I was prompted by some particular feeling for you—you! Why, there was not a man in that crowd, not a poor desperate rioter driven to rage by hunger, for whom I did not have more sympathy—for whom I would not have done every small thing in my power far more heartily.”
“Speak on, Miss Hale. I am well aware of these misplaced sympathies of yours,” he said, his voice hard. “I know now that what made you act so nobly was only your innate hatred of oppression—yes, even I, a mill master, may be oppressed, too. I know you despise me. Let me say it is only because you do not understand me.”
“I do not care to understand you,” she said, catching hold of the edge of the table to steady herself. She thought him cruel then, and she was weak from all the day before’s shocks, indignant to the bone.

Margaret pressed her lips tight together. She would not deign to answer such unfair accusations. But for all his savage, sharp words, he longed to throw himself at her feet, to press a kiss to the hem of her gown. She said nothing, did not move. Hot tears of wounded pride spilled fast down her cheeks. He waited, for what felt like an hour, hoping she would say something—even a taunt, any line he could reply to. But she was silent. He picked up his hat.
“One word more. You look as if you thought being loved by me somehow taints you. You cannot avoid it. Nay, I, even if I wished, could not take that feeling back, and cleanse you from it. And I would not if I could. I have never loved any woman before. My life has been too busy, my thoughts too swallowed up by work at the mill to leave any room for it. Now I love, and I will love. But do not fear that I will ever embarrass you with any more unasked-for displays of feeling.”
“I am not afraid,” she said, lifting herself straight, tall and proud. “No one has ever dared to be impertinent to me, and no one ever shall. But Mr. Thornton, you have been so very kind to my father,” she said, her whole tone softening into something gentle and womanly. “Do not let us go on making each other angry. Pray, do not.”

He paid no mind to her plea. He stood smoothing the nap of his hat with his coat sleeve for half a minute, his face set hard, then deliberately ignored the hand she held out to him, turned away before he could let her see the look of raw, breaking grief on his face, and stormed out of the room.

Margaret caught one last glance of his face as he left. When the door clicked shut behind him, she realized she had seen the bright, glistening track of unshed tears in his eyes. In a second, all her hot, proud dislike melted into something softer, and far more painful: sharp, biting self-reproach, for having caused such deep, public mortification to any living soul.
“But how could I help it?” she whispered to herself, leaning against the table. “I never liked him. I was always civil, but I never made any effort to hide my indifference. I never even thought of how he might take my manners, so of course they showed the truth of it. That act yesterday was just an act of decency, nothing more. He mistook it. But that is his fault, not mine. I would do it all again, if there was need—even if it leads me to all this shame and trouble.”
