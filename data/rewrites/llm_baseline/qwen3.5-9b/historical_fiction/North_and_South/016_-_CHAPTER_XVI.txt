The following afternoon, Dr. Donaldson arrived to pay his first visit to Mrs. Hale. The delicate mystery that Margaret had hoped their recent intimacy might have dissolved was immediately reinstated. She was barred from the room, while Dixon was ushered inside. Margaret was not naturally prone to jealousy, yet where her affection burned, it consumed her with a fierce, possessive intensity. Retreating to her mother's bedroom, situated directly behind the drawing-room, she paced the floor, waiting for the physician to emerge. Periodically, she halted, straining her ears. She imagined she heard a moan—a faint, ragged sound. Her fingers curled tightly, her breath hitched in her throat. She was certain she had heard it. Then, silence reigned for several moments. Finally, the rustle of chairs, the murmur of raised voices, and the subtle noises of departure filled the air. As the door opened, Margaret slipped out swiftly.

"My father is away, Doctor," she announced, her tone crisp and authoritative. "He must attend to a pupil at this hour. Would you kindly follow me to his study downstairs?"

She observed Dixon's startled reaction and felt a surge of triumph. She had successfully navigated every obstacle the old servant erected; adopting the demeanor of the eldest daughter, she silenced Dixon's officiousness with effortless authority. This sudden assumption of dignity amused her momentarily, cutting through the sharp edge of her anxiety. She watched Dixon's bewildered face and allowed herself a brief respite from the crushing weight of the situation. But the reality crashed back instantly, stealing the air from her lungs.

It took a moment for her voice to return, but when it did, it carried an air of absolute command. "What is the matter with my mother? You will oblige me by stating the plain truth."

Seeing a flicker of hesitation in the doctor's eyes, she pressed on. "I am her only child—at least, here in this house. I fear my father is not sufficiently alarmed. Therefore, if there is grave cause for concern, it must be delivered to him gently. I can handle this. I can care for my mother. Please, speak, sir. To see your face unreadable is a terror worse than whatever words you might deliver."

"My dear young lady," Dr. Donaldson replied, "your mother appears to have a most attentive and efficient servant, someone who acts more like a friend—"

"I am her daughter, sir."

"But when I tell you she explicitly requested that you remain unaware—"

"I am neither virtuous nor patient enough to submit to such a prohibition. Furthermore, I am convinced you are too wise, too experienced, to have sworn to secrecy."

"Well," he said, offering a half-smile tinged with sadness, "in that, you are correct. I made no promise. Indeed, I fear the truth will surface soon enough without my revealing it."

He paused. Margaret turned pale, pressing her lips together tighter. Not a muscle elsewhere betrayed her emotion. Possessing the intuitive grasp of character essential to a physician of his stature, Dr. Donaldson realized she would demand the complete truth; she would detect even the slightest omission, and the torment of suspicion would be far sharper than the knowledge itself. Speaking in a low voice, he watched her intently until her pupils dilated into pools of black horror and her pallor deepened to a livid shade. He stopped. He waited for the shock to pass, for her gasping breath to steady.

Then she spoke. "I thank you most sincerely, sir, for your confidence. That dread has haunted me for weeks. It is a genuine, excruciating agony. My poor, poor mother!"

Her lips trembled, and he permitted her the release of tears, confident in her capacity for self-command to restrain them. A few tears flowed, and then she remembered the flood of questions burning within her. "Will there be much suffering?"

He shook his head. "We cannot predict that. It depends on constitution, on countless variables. However, recent advancements in medical science have granted us considerable power to alleviate pain."

"My father!" Margaret whispered, trembling violently. "I do not know Mr. Hale. I mean... it is difficult to offer counsel. Yet, I would say: endure, armed with the knowledge I have forced upon you so abruptly, until the fact I could not conceal becomes somewhat familiar to you. Only then will you be able, without excessive strain, to offer comfort to your father. Until then—my visits, which I shall undoubtedly repeat, though I fear I can do little but ease symptoms—a thousand minor incidents will arise to heighten his alarm, preparing him gradually. Nay, my dear young lady—nay, my dear—I saw Mr. Thornton, and I honor your father for the sacrifice he makes, however mistaken I may deem his judgment. Very well, this once, if it pleases you, my dear. Remember, when I return, I come as a friend. You must learn to view me thus, for meeting in such critical times is worth years of polite morning calls."

Margaret could not speak; instead, she squeezed his hand tightly at parting.

"That is what I call a fine girl," Dr. Donaldson mused as he settled into his carriage, examining the ringed hand that had suffered from her grip. "Who would have thought such a small hand possessed such strength? The bones are well-set, granting immense power. What a queen she is! First, throwing her head back to compel me to speak the truth, then leaning forward so eagerly to listen. Poor creature! I must ensure she does not overexert herself. It is astonishing what these thoroughbred souls can endure. That girl is spine-backed. Another, who had drained to that color, would have collapsed into fainting or hysteria. But she refused both—not she! The sheer force of her will brought her back. A girl like that would win my heart if I were thirty years younger. Too late now. Ah, here we are at the Archers'."

Leaping from the vehicle, he descended with thought, wisdom, experience, and sympathy, ready to serve the family as if they were the sole occupants of the world.

Meanwhile, Margaret retreated briefly to her father's study to regain her composure before ascending to her mother's chamber. "Oh, my God, my God! This is terrible. How shall I endure it? Such a fatal illness! No hope! Oh, Mamma, Mamma, I wish I had never gone to Aunt Shaw's, never spent those precious years away from you! Poor Mamma! How much she must have suffered! Oh, pray, my God, that her pain may not be too acute, too dreadful. How can I bear to witness it? How can I withstand Papa's agony? He must not be told yet—not all at once. It would kill him. But I cannot waste another second of my beloved, precious mother's time."

She ran upstairs. Dixon was absent. Mrs. Hale reclined in an easy chair, draped in a soft white shawl, wearing a modest cap, anticipating the doctor's arrival. Her face held a faint flush, and the exhaustion following the examination lent her features a serene quality. Margaret was taken aback by her mother's apparent calm.

"Why, Margaret, how strangely you look! What is the matter?" Mrs. Hale asked. Then, as the realization of the true state of affairs crept into her mind, she added, with a touch of displeasure, "You haven't seen Dr. Donaldson, have you? Haven't you asked him any questions, child?"

Margaret remained silent, gazing wistfully toward her. Mrs. Hale grew more annoyed. "He would surely not break his word to me, and—"

"Oh, yes, Mamma, he did. I compelled him. It was I—blame me."

Kneeling beside her mother, Margaret seized her hand, refusing to let go despite Mrs. Hale's attempts to withdraw. She kissed it fervently, her hot tears soaking the fabric.

"Margaret, it was very wrong of you. You knew I did not wish you to know." Yet, worn down by the struggle, she left her hand in Margaret's grasp, eventually returning the pressure feebly. This encouragement prompted Margaret to speak.

"Oh, Mamma! Let me be your nurse. I will learn whatever Dixon can teach me. But you know I am your child, and I believe I have a right to do everything for you."

"You do not realize what you are asking," Mrs. Hale shuddered.

"Yes, I do. I understand far more than you suspect. Let me be your nurse. Let me try, at any rate. No one has ever, nor shall ever, strive harder than I will. It will be such a comfort, Mamma."

"My poor child! Well, you shall try. Did you know, Margaret, that Dixon and I feared you would recoil in horror upon learning the truth?"

"Dixon thought!" Margaret retorted, her lip curling. "Dixon could not credit me with enough true love—not even as much as she possesses herself! She likely assumed I was one of those frail women content to lounge on rose leaves and be fanned all day. Do not let Dixon's fantasies intervene between us, Mamma. Do not, I beg you!"

"Do not be angry with Dixon," Mrs. Hale pleaded anxiously.
