The three or four TO LET boards had crouched inside the low paling for so many years that the little triangular Square seemed to have grown around them, and no one living could have sworn they had ever stood straight. Each leaned at its own decided slant, overhanging the paling like a row of blunt cleavers arrested in the instant before they fell—wooden, splintered, weather-grayed, their painted letters scabbed and flaked until the words were less an invitation than a threat. They made a kind of silent promise to anyone who walked beneath: something could come down on you at any hour, not enough to kill, perhaps, but enough to mark you.

Yet there was scarcely ever a passer-by to menace. No stream of feet ran through that Square. The real stream of the city—its traffic, its shouts, the iron-scrape of carts, the clatter and thin music from public-houses—flowed a furlong away, beyond the maze of tenements and alleys that had thickened since the old house was built, until it stood completely hemmed in, like a grandparent shut into a back room while the younger voices take over the kitchen. The Square itself had an air that suggested postponement: the old place was suffered to stand only until a lease fell in, only until some papers were signed, only until the neighbourhood could be cleared with a sweep of official ink.

The house was of bloomy, old red brick, and in its walls, like stale decorations from a forgotten feast, were set crowns and clasped hands and other insignia of insurance companies long extinct. The symbols looked cheerful from a distance, but close up they had the dead confidence of names no one spoke anymore. The low gate at the end of the entrance-alley had been swung on by generations of children—swung on until little remained but the solid top bar. The alley ran between boarded basement windows that tramps had chalked with marks as private as recipes in a dialect no one else could read. Water spilled from the eaves of the encroaching next house and had washed the path into an uneven trough. Cats and dogs owned the approach, not in any sentimental way, but by right of smell and habit: warm fur, damp brick, the sourness of refuse, and that quiet, patient stink of old timber that has held too many winters.

The boards, at least, were not maintained with any belief in their own usefulness. Their letters had once been confident; now they were mere survivals—like a few last words remembered from a grandmother’s speech, repeated because one cannot bear to lose them, not because they still command an answer.

For six months Oleron had passed the Square twice a day or oftener, going from his lodgings to the room, ten minutes’ walk away, that he had taken to work in. For six months no hatchet-board had dropped across his path. That may have been only because he generally kept to the other side of the Square; he was the sort of man who avoids grime without thinking about it, by instinctive angles and distances, as if his skin itself disliked contact.

Then one morning he happened to take the side that ran past the broken gate and the rain-worn entrance-alley. The air was close, and in it there was the faintest tang of something that might have been last night’s fried fish drifting from a back kitchen, already turning old; under that, brick-dust, wet wood, and the metallic rain-smell that cities get when the sky has just threatened and held back. Oleron paused before one of the boards.

Beneath the agent’s name, in handwriting that looked as if it had been applied around the time of his own early youth, was the note: KEY TO BE HAD AT NO. SIX.

Oleron stood with his thumb along his lean, shaven jaw, and looked through the scant privet at the boarded basement windows. He looked up at the blank, grimy panes of the first floor, and then higher, to the second, and to the flat stone coping of the leads. The windows did not reflect; they absorbed. The whole front of the house seemed to listen rather than watch.

He had reasons for stopping that were entirely practical and as domestic as an irritation at a misplaced spoon. He was already paying for two separate rooms—bedroom and workroom—more than an author without private means, and with a stubborn habit of disregarding his public, could afford. Besides that he paid a small rent to store the greater part of his grandmother’s furniture. And the division of his life had begun to tease him into little torments: the book he wanted in bed lay half a mile away at his work-quarters; the note or letter he suddenly required during the day was as likely as not in the pocket of another coat hanging behind his bedroom door. He was tired of living like a man with his pantry in one house and his hearth in another.

He glanced once more at the board and crossed the Square to Number Six.

He knocked. The door stood open, but no one answered. He waited two or three minutes, hearing only the small noises of the Square: a pigeon’s complaining croon, a distant cough, and somewhere behind the walls of Number Six the muffled clink of crockery—the intimate, homely sound of a plate set down, which made the silence of the old house opposite seem all the more deliberate. He knocked again.

A long-nosed man in shirt-sleeves appeared at last, looking as though he had been disturbed in something solemn. “I was arsking a blessing on our food,” he said, with severe explanation, as if Oleron had interrupted a sacrament.

It struck Oleron—absurdly, and yet with a pinch of unease—that the phrase “our food” carried more weight in the man’s mouth than any mention of rent or property could have done. It was as if the meal, whatever it was—boiled scraps, bread, dripping—was the true centre of the household, and the world outside merely a nuisance.

Oleron asked if he might have the key of the old house.

The long-nosed man withdrew without further ceremony. Oleron waited on the step. From within came the chew and pause of someone eating; the rhythm of it set itself against the Square’s quiet like a slow, private clock. Five minutes passed.

The man returned, still masticating. “The key’s lost,” he announced.

“But you won’t want it,” he added, with a mouthful’s conviction. “The entrance door isn’t closed, and a push’ll open any of the others. I’m a agent for it, if you’re thinking of taking it—”

Oleron did not ask how a man could be agent to a house he could not lock. He recrossed the Square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, passed along the alley where the damp boards over the basement windows held the coldness of the last rain, and turned in at the old, wide doorway.

Immediately within, to the right, steps descended into roomy cellars. The air drifting up from them had the faint sweet rot of long-shut spaces, as if barrels had once stood there and their ghostly wine had seeped into the stones. Before him rose the staircase: broad, handsome, and filthy, its carved rail obscured by layers of grime that had the dull sheen of long-handled touch. Oleron went up without letting his palm meet either rail or wall, as though he could preserve himself by a few inches of distance.

At the first landing a door facing him had been boarded up; the boards were nailed with a decisiveness that looked less like repair than prevention. He pushed at the door to his right. An insecure bolt or staple yielded with an easy, shamefaced give, like something that has been pretending to guard a secret and is relieved to be relieved of it.

He entered the empty first floor.

The rooms were bare and dust-filmmed, but they were not small. The air had the smell of old paint and cold soot, and underneath it a faint, persistent odour like dried herbs kept too long in a tin—ghost of kitchen thrift, of some careful hand that once tried to preserve summer in jars. The sound in those rooms was peculiar: his shoes on the boards did not answer him cleanly. There was a soft thickening to every step, as if the house held its breath and swallowed the noise. From beyond—above? below?—came at times a remote, uncertain rustle, a movement too slight for certainty, like a page turned in another room.

He spent a quarter of an hour there, going from room to room, taking in proportions, panelling, the deep window-recesses, the way the light fell. The place was very dirty, very neglected, and yet the bones of it were made with a gaiety and rest that builders no longer knew. Even in the dust he could feel the intentions of the men who had shaped it: they had made rooms for living, not merely for shelter.

He came out again without going higher, and went back across the Square to Number Six.

“Can you tell me how much the rent is?” he asked.

The long-nosed man named a figure. Its comparative lowness made sense, given the neighbourhood and the abominable disrepair; it sounded like the price of something one ought to distrust.

“Would it be possible to rent a single floor?”

The agent did not know; they might—

“Who are they?” Oleron asked.

The man gave him the name of a firm of lawyers in Lincoln’s Inn. “You might mention my name—Barrett,” he added, as if that name were a seasoning to be sprinkled into the conversation to make it go down.

Work pressed on Oleron and kept him from Lincoln’s Inn that afternoon. He went the next day, however, and found himself instantly offered the whole house as a purchase: fifty pounds down, the remainder to remain on mortgage. The readiness of the offer had something indecent in it, as though they were eager to be rid of the place and would have thrown in the Square itself if they could.

It took him half an hour to disabuse the lawyer of the idea that he wanted anything more than to rent a single floor. There were hums and haws; the lawyer was by no means certain it lay within his power to do as Oleron suggested; the language of ownership and letting tangled itself into little power-phrases that were not meant to describe so much as to control. Oleron listened, feeling as one feels when a familiar word in one’s native dialect has no equal in the dominant tongue: the thing is clear in the mind and stubbornly refuses to be carried over.

In the end it was extracted that, provided the notice-boards were allowed to remain up, and provided it was agreed that if the whole house should let, the arrangement should terminate automatically without further notice, something might be done.

That the old place should suddenly let over his head seemed to Oleron the slightest of risks. He promised a decision within a week.

On the morrow he visited the house again and went through it from top to bottom.

Higher up the stairs the dust lay thicker, and the air thinned into a colder flavour. Doors yielded to a push as Barrett had said, opening on rooms that stared back with blank floors and stained ceilings. Sound behaved differently on each level: on one floor his footsteps rang thin and sharp, as if the boards resented being reminded they still existed; on another they were muffled again, as if the house drew his noise into itself. At the top, near the leads, the wind made a faint piping in some unseen gap, a high note that faltered and returned, and it irritated him the way a badly tuned instrument does—because it implied disorder in the very atmosphere.

He went down into the cellars too, where the walls sweated, and where the darkness had a taste—earthy, metallic, like a damp knife. He was glad to come out of them. He left the house and went home to his lodgings and took a bath with the thoroughness of a man rinsing away more than dust.

He was immensely taken with the portion of the house he had already decided should be his. Scraped clean and repainted, and with his grandmother’s old furniture in it, it ought to be entirely charming. There came over him, with the suddenness of hunger, the vision of those familiar pieces set where they belonged: not in a warehouse, not in the exile of storage, but returned to walls that could hold their silhouettes.

He went to the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of his half-forgotten belongings and to take measurements. The warehouse smelled of rope, pine-crate, stale wool, and furniture polish gone cold. He ran his fingers along the edge of a tall lattice-paned china cupboard, and the motion brought back his grandmother’s room as sharply as any taste can: the faint beeswax scent of well-tended wood, the clean mineral smell of china, the way the cupboard’s door used to catch slightly before closing, with a click that had been a kind of punctuation in her days.

There was Derby and Mason and Spode—patterns like family stories that do not translate well outside the household. He remembered her naming things in phrases he had not heard anywhere else, little turns of speech that belonged to her generation and had died with it, and he found himself murmuring them without quite knowing he did so, as if the objects demanded their old names and would not be satisfied with the flatter, more modern word.

He measured the large folding Sheraton table. He paced out where the long, low bookshelves would run; two of them he had already had “copied,” with the pedant’s delight in exactness. He looked at chairs, Sheffield candlesticks, riveted rose-bowls. Each thing had its own weight and promise, as ingredients do when laid out before cooking: you can see the meal in them before the fire is lit.

From there he went to a decorator’s, and afterwards his days became crowded. He was very busy with his regular work and could have wished the notice-board had caught his attention a few months earlier, or later in the year; but the quickest way, he decided, would be to suspend work entirely until after his removal, and to push the change through like a cook clearing the kitchen for a great undertaking—better one fierce day of heat and disorder than weeks of half-done meals.

A fortnight later the first floor was painted throughout in a tender elderflower white. The paint was dry. The scent of it—sharp, sweetish, and new—filled the rooms, mixing with the older smells that would not be driven out at once: a trace of soot, a trace of damp, a trace of that herb-dryness he could not name. Oleron was in the middle of his installation.

He was animated, delighted; and he rubbed his hands as he polished and made disposals of his grandmother’s effects. The tall lattice-paned cupboard stood like a dignified ghost made solid again, its glass panes catching the light and giving it back softened. He set the Derby, the Mason, the Spode in their places, each plate and cup making a small, satisfying ring as it met the shelf—an acoustic you feel in the teeth as well as hear. The Sheraton table opened and shut with a precise, intelligent resistance, like a good knife meeting the grain of a vegetable. The bookshelves took their lines along the wall as though the room had been waiting for them.

He set the chairs and the Sheffield candlesticks and the riveted rose-bowls against the newly painted elder-white walls—walls of wood, panelled in the happiest proportions, moulded and coffered to the low-seated window-recesses in a mood of gaiety and rest. The ceilings were lofty and faintly painted with an old pattern of stars, so pale they seemed more like a remembered sky than a decoration. Even the tapering mouldings of his iron fireplace were delicately designed, as if for jewellery rather than heat. He walked about rubbing his hands, frequently stopping for the mere pleasure of glimpses from white room to white room, the way one pauses between courses to let a flavour settle.

“Charming, charming!” he said to himself. “I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!”

The Square outside remained poor and tucked away, but within his floor there was, already, a domestic ritual beginning: the placing, the wiping, the adjustment by inches until everything fell into its proper silence. And silence mattered. He had lived long enough amid the city’s grind to know what relentless noise does to the mind—how it flattens thought into irritability, how it makes art into a cheap commodity for distraction. Here, at least, the air seemed to accept quieter sounds: the soft scrape of a chair-leg, the clink of china, the whisper of a cloth over polished wood. Those sounds felt like sanity.

He bought a bolt and a Yale lock for his door and shut off his quarters from the rest of the house, making a boundary as clear as a larder door between clean food and the street. If he now wanted to read in bed, his book could be had by stepping into the next room. He thought, continually, how exceedingly lucky he was to get the place.

He put up a hat-rack in the little square hall and hung up his hats and caps and coats. Late at night, passers through the small triangular Square—few enough—looking up past the serried row of wooden TO LET hatchets, could see the light within Oleron’s red blinds, or else the sudden darkening of one blind and the illumination of another, as Oleron, candlestick in hand, passed from room to room making final settlings of his furniture, or preparing to resume the work his removal had interrupted.

And all the while the old house stood around his bright, elder-white rooms as a shell stands around the living creature that has moved into it: apparently inert, but full of its own history, its own trapped air, its own strange acoustics—full, too, of words and meanings that had once been used here, and were no longer understood, and might not be pleased to be replaced.

As far as the chief business of his life—his writing—was concerned, Paul Oleron treated the world with a consideration it did not return. He had long since ceased to keep accounts of injury and recompense. To do so would not have changed the arithmetic, and it might have soured what was left of his temper. At forty-four, he had chosen his way in the years when generosity still looked like a kind of strength, and he had committed himself to it beyond any decent thought of retreat. If he had ever paused to cross-examine his own motives, he would still have answered—stubbornly, almost piously—that a life without some nobility in it was no life at all. Only of late, and only in odd flashes, had he suspected there might be a cost hidden in that answer—some day, perhaps, when his powers reached their crest and began their inevitable fall, he would be left with the question whether less exacting ideals might have bought him more ease. But to anticipate that day was useless.

For the present, his removal into the old house—its brickwork still tattooed here and there with the faint, stamped scars of long-ago insurance marks—had only interrupted *Romilly Bishop* at the fifteenth chapter.

He moved about his new rooms with a tall, lean exactitude, his ascetic face set in a kind of quiet fastidiousness that might, in another sex and another century, have been called spinsterish. Twenty years of lodgings and garrets had trained him to do most things for himself, and he had learned that method saves both time and irritation. He had arranged with the long-nosed Barrett’s wife—a stout Welshwoman with a high, almost piping voice, her Merionethshire accent surviving London as if it were a stubborn seed—to come each morning across the square to prepare his breakfast, and on Saturday to “turn the place out.” For the rest he found, not without surprise, that a little housework made a tolerable counterweight to the strain of writing: the mind, after all, does not float well in a room that smells of stale dust and old paint.

The kitchen, with the narrow strip of adjoining space where a modern bath had been fitted, looked down upon the alley at the side of the house. There, at one end, was a large closet with a door, and in the upper part of that door a square sliding hatch. Oleron stood before it longer than he meant to. The shape of it pricked some half-remembered image—then the use came to him, and his mouth softened into a faint smile that carried, inexplicably, a little tenderness. It had been a powder-closet, once; through that hatch an elaborately dressed head had been thrust to receive, with practiced resignation, the click and puff of the powder-pistol.

He would put it to a different purpose now. It would, he supposed, serve as his larder.

He opened it and rummaged. The back was shelved, the upper shelf running deep into the wall like a recess made for secrets. Pushing his hand into the shadowed reach, he drew out two mushroom-shaped wooden wig-stands, old, smooth, and oddly patient-looking under the grime. He could not imagine how they had come to be there. Perhaps the painters had found them and stuffed them away. But cupboard-space was scarce in his five rooms, and he had already been forced into little feats of ingenuity to house linen, boxes, and those accumulations of papers that were seldom used yet somehow could not be destroyed. He set the wig-stands aside with a brief shrug, as one notes a curiosity and postpones its meaning.

It was early spring when he took up the tenancy, and he was anxious to have *Romilly* ready for publication in the coming autumn. Even so, he did not intend to whip it into premature completion. If it demanded longer, then longer it must have. He knew—more sharply than he liked to admit—its importance in his development; it had to be allowed its own length and time. In the rooms he had just left he had made excellent progress; *Romilly* had begun, as people say, to speak and act of herself, the characters moving with their own weight. He had no doubt she would continue, once the fuss of removal quieted.

The fuss was nearly done. He told himself it was time to pull his mind into harness again. On a March morning he went out and returned with two great bunches of yellow daffodils—their cut stems bleeding cold sap, their trumpets smelling of green water and roadside earth. He set one bunch on the mantelpiece between the Sheffield candlesticks, and the other on the table directly before him, so that the sharp, clean scent rose into his face as he sat. Then he drew out the half-completed manuscript of *Romilly Bishop*.

But before he began he went to a small rosewood cabinet, took out his cheque-book and pass-book, and totted up his figures. His monk-like face grew thoughtful as the sum diminished under his pencil. Installation had cost more than he meant, and he had rather less than fifty pounds left, with no immediate prospect of more.

“Hm. I’d forgotten rugs and chintz curtains and so forth mounted up so,” he said aloud, as if speaking made the fact less humiliating. “But it would have been a pity to spoil the place for the want of ten pounds or so…. Well. *Romilly* simply *must* be out for the autumn. So here goes—”

He drew his papers toward him.

But he did not work. Or rather, his hand made the motions of work while his mind was tugged sideways by the world outside and, more subtly, by the house itself. The square had its own noises—frequent, various, newly intimate. Hawkers came first, the rattle of their carts and the practiced cries that cut through the air like knife-strokes. At midday, children returning from school poured into the square in a rush of voices and boots; they swung on his gate until the hinges complained, and their laughter ricocheted off the opposite brickwork. When they went back for afternoon school, an itinerant musician stationed himself under Oleron’s window and began to strum a mandolin.

The sound was not unpleasant—thin, metallic, but at least it was human. Oleron pushed up the sash and threw the man a penny. The coin rang faintly as it hit; the mandolin answered with a livelier phrase, and then settled back into its repetitive, commercial sweetness. Oleron returned to his table.

No. It was no good.

He came to himself at long intervals to find he had been staring about the room, not at his own furniture but at ghosts of other arrangements: a settee in buttercup satin, perhaps, or petunia; a glimmering crystal chandelier suspended from the centre moulding of the lofty ceiling; a tambour-frame in the light; a picquet-table near the window where someone’s rings might tap against a glass. He grew tired without having produced anything but a kind of mental dust.

Better, he decided, to be frankly idle than to wear himself out on futile effort. He would take a walk. Yet, chancing to sit for a moment, he fell asleep in his chair.

“This won’t do,” he yawned when he woke at half-past four, the late light slanting changedly across the floor. “I must do better than this tomorrow—”

He felt so luxuriously lazy that for a few minutes he even considered breaking an appointment he had for the evening, as if he could buy rest by a small act of disloyalty.

The next morning he sat down to work without allowing himself even the delay of answering his three letters—two tradesmen’s accounts and a note from Miss Bengough forwarded from his old address. It was a bright day of white and blue, a gay, noisy wind sweeping the square, and a subtle shift in the colour of growing things—buds tightening, grass darkening, the first hint of sap rising. Over and over, once or twice a minute, the room brightened suddenly and then subdued again as shining clouds rolled north-eastward. The soft, fitful illumination winked in the polished table-top and even in the worn old boards of the floor. The morning sounds had begun again—the cartwheels, the distant knocks, the small clink of milk bottles, the too-bright cries.

Oleron made a pattern of dots on the paper before him, then broke off to move the jar of daffodils precisely opposite the centre of a creamy panel, adjusting it as carefully as one might adjust a garnish to make the plate look balanced. He wrote a sentence that ran cleanly for a couple of lines; then it broke down into notes and jottings like a recipe abandoned halfway when the cook loses the thread of heat and timing. For a time he managed to persuade himself that the memoranda were work. Then he rose and began to pace.

As he moved, an idea struck him with the odd brightness of a practical solution. The place, he thought, might be improved by more positive colour. It was pale—mild and sweet as a kind old face—yet a little devitalised, even wan. It would bear a warmer note: richer flowers, perhaps; and some gay stuff for cushions on the window-seats, something that would not look frightened of light.

“Of course I really can’t afford it,” he muttered, and fetched a two-foot rule to measure the width of the recesses, as if numbers would discipline the desire.

He stooped to measure. Then his whole attitude changed. He paused, peering; his fingers went to the painted wood with a new delicacy. Presently he straightened, rubbing his hands together with gentle glee.

“Oho, oho!” he said. “These look to me very much like window-boxes, nailed up. We must look into this! Yes—those are boxes, or I’m… oho, this is an adventure!”

On that wall of his sitting-room were two windows; the third sat in another corner, and beyond the open bedroom door on the same wall was yet another. The seats of all had been painted, repainted, and painted again until the wood seemed sealed under a skin of history. His investigating finger had barely found the old nailheads beneath the layers. Under the ledge, an old keyhole had been puttied over.

He took out his penknife and worked carefully for five minutes, prising, scoring, lifting paint in neat curls. Then he went into the kitchen for a hammer and chisel. Driving the chisel cautiously under the seat, he started the lid slightly. He used the penknife again, cutting along the hinged edge, outward along the ends. Then he fetched a wedge and a wooden mallet.

“Now for our little mystery—” he said.

The first blows fell.

In that sweet, pale apartment the sound seemed brutal—almost indecent. The panelling rang and rattled and vibrated like the body of some large instrument struck in the wrong place. The house answered from within itself. From cellarage to garrets a flock of echoes woke and ran, overlapping, multiplying, as if every hollow space had been waiting to be made vocal. The noise got on his nerves in a way that was not simply annoyance; it was as if the vibration made his teeth and thoughts buzz together.

All at once he stopped, fetched a duster, and muffled the mallet, ashamed—absurdly—of the violence of his own hands.

When the edge was raised enough, he slid his fingers under and lifted. The paint flaked and starred; rusty nails squeaked and grunted like reluctant mouths; the lid came up and laid open the box beneath.

Oleron looked in.

It was empty, except for a couple of inches of scurf and mould and old cobwebs—matter that had once been soft and now looked like the desiccated remains of softness. No letters, no heirloom, no hoard.

“No treasure there,” he said, amused with himself for having half expected one. “*Romilly* will still have to be out by the autumn. Let’s have a look at the others.”

He turned to the second window. The raising of the remaining seats occupied him until well into the afternoon, his hands cramped with careful leverage, his ears still prickling with the remembered resonance of the house. The bedroom seat, like the first, was empty. But from the second seat in his sitting-room he drew out something yielding and folded, furred over with an inch of dust. It came away reluctantly, as if it had been pressed there for safety and did not like the light.

He carried it into the kitchen and swept it over a bucket; a small cloud of dry powder rose, smelling of old plaster, cold wood, and a faint, stale sweetness like cloth stored too long with dried flowers. Then he took a duster and began to uncover it.

It was some sort of large bag made of an ancient, frieze-like material. When he unfolded it, it occupied most of the little kitchen floor, its breadth forcing him to step back against the sink. In shape it was an irregular—very irregular—triangle, with a couple of wide flaps and the remains of straps and buckles. The patch that had been uppermost in the folding was faded to a yellowish brown, as if sun had worried it for years; the rest was in shades of crimson that shifted according to how long each portion had been exposed, like meat darkening where air has reached it.

“Now whatever can that have been?” Oleron mused, standing over it as if over an unknown cut of something once familiar. “I give it up. Whatever it is, it’s settled my work for today, I’m afraid—”

He folded it up carelessly and thrust it into a corner of the kitchen, where it slumped like a thing too tired to hold its shape. Then, taking pans and brushes and an old knife, he returned to the sitting-room and began to scrape and wash and line with paper his newly discovered receptacles. The work steadied him. The wet rag made a small, controlled sound on the wood; the paper crackled as it smoothed down; the house’s earlier ringing seemed to withdraw, listening with sullen patience.

When he had finished, he stowed spare boots and books and papers into the boxes and closed the lids again. He was amused by his little adventure—yet not quite at ease. The day had been eaten up by the house, and he was anxious, more anxious than he admitted, for the hour to come when he should sit down and *Romilly* would speak again as she had spoken before, without the square’s cries, without the house’s answering echoes, without these concealed spaces that seemed made to keep what could not be easily named.

It nettled Oleron a little that Miss Bengough should dismiss, with one broad glance and no more, the rooms he had found so oddly, so dangerously engaging. She scarcely lifted her eyes to the pale walls, the high mouldings, the way the light lay—like sifted flour—along the boards. Yet she had always been like that: indifferent to the small courtesies of beauty, careless of appearances, more honestly herself with a paper bag of biscuits on her knee than at any table laid with ceremony.

Elsie Bengough was thirty-four, an unattached journalist, large and showy, fair as churned butter, pink as a hedgerow rose, as if she had been cut fresh from a florist’s display and set—too boldly—in the world. She moved in sudden, ample sweeps, leaving little whirlwinds behind her, and she spoke with a moist explosiveness that made the very air seem to slap and ring. She “pulled a better living out of the pool” than Oleron did; and by certain cunningly disguised puffs of drapers and haberdashers she “pulled” as well the better part of her various wardrobe. When she came into a room she brought the outside in with her: a gust of street-air in the folds of her veil, the stale tang of omnibus smoke clinging to cloth, the metallic click of her hatpins like small knives.

He had been a month in the new flat when he heard her on the staircase—the flurry of skirts rising and falling, the quickened rhythm of her steps, and then a single loud knock that made the panels answer.

When he opened the door she was already half inside, a bundle of ladies’ journals under one arm as if she meant to barricade herself, and a paper parcel in the other hand.

“Don’t knock off for me,” she said across a mouthful of those large-headed hatpins as she set to work at her hat and veil. “I didn’t know whether you were straight yet, so I’ve brought some sandwiches for lunch. You’ve got coffee, I suppose?—No, don’t get up—I’ll find the kitchen—”

“Oh, that’s all right, I’ll clear these things away. To tell the truth, I’m rather glad to be interrupted,” Oleron said, and he gathered his papers as though the very act of stacking them were a relief, as if he could pack away with them a certain pressure in his skull.

She was already in the kitchen. He heard the tap run, the kettle filled—sound as intimate as breath in a house so empty it seemed to listen to itself. He joined her and watched her brisk competence: a hand at the matchbox, the rasp and flare, the steadying of the kettle on the flame. She moved as if she had always owned every room she entered, regardless of how little she seemed to notice it.

Ten minutes later he followed her back into the sitting-room with coffee and sandwiches on a tray. The coffee was ordinary enough, ground too long ago and kept too close to other smells; yet as the hot water struck it the room took on that familiar dark bitterness that promises comfort even when it cannot deliver it. The sandwiches, unwrapped, had that slightly compressed look of things made in haste: pale bread, a conservative ration of filling, corners softened by their journey in the paper.

They sat with the tray between them on a small table.

“Well,” Oleron asked, as she poured out the coffee in a generous stream that hissed faintly against the cup, “what do you think of the new place?”

“Hm!… Anybody’d think you were going to get married, Paul.”

He laughed, though the sound came out thin. “Oh no. But it’s an improvement on some of them, isn’t it?”

“Is it? I suppose it is; I don’t know. I liked the last place, in spite of the black ceiling and no watertap. How’s Romilly?”

Oleron thumbed his chin, dry-shaving with two fingers the way he did when he was thinking too hard to keep still. “Hm! I’m rather ashamed to tell you. The fact is, I’ve not got on very well with it. But it will be all right on the night, as you used to say.”

“Stuck?”

“Rather stuck.”

“Got any of it you care to read to me?…”

It had long been his habit to read bits of his work to Elsie now and then. Her comments were quick, practical, sometimes directly useful, sometimes suggestive in a way he could not have explained to her. She, in return for his confidence, kept all mention of her own work carefully from him. His, she said, was “real work”; hers merely filled space, not always even grammatically.

“I’m afraid there isn’t,” Oleron replied, still at his chin, still listening to the soft scrape of his own fingertips on skin, the little domestic sound made loud by the quiet of the rooms.

Then, with a suddenness that surprised even him, he added: “The fact is, Elsie, I’ve not written—not actually written—very much more of it—any more of it, in fact. But, of course, that doesn’t mean I haven’t progressed. I’ve progressed, in one sense, rather alarmingly. I’m now thinking of reconstructing the whole thing.”

Miss Bengough gave a gasp as if he had proposed to throw the coffee on the floor. “Reconstructing!”

“Making Romilly herself a different type of woman. Somehow, I’ve begun to feel that I’m not getting the most out of her. As she stands, I’ve certainly lost interest in her to some extent.”

“But—but—” Miss Bengough protested, “you had her so real, so living, Paul!”

Oleron smiled faintly. He had been prepared for her disapproval. He wasn’t surprised she liked Romilly as she at present existed; she would. Whether she admitted it to herself or not, there was much of Elsie in his fictitious creation. Naturally Romilly would seem “real,” “living,” to her—like a familiar voice overheard in another room.

“But are you really serious, Paul?” she asked at last, staring at him with a round-eyed steadiness over the rim of her cup.

“Quite serious.”

“You’re really going to scrap those fifteen chapters?”

“I didn’t exactly say that.”

“That fine, rich love-scene?”

“I should only do it reluctantly, and for the sake of something I thought better.”

“And that beautiful, beau-tiful description of Romilly on the shore?”

“It wouldn’t necessarily be wasted,” he said, a little uneasily, as if the words had a weight he didn’t want to feel.

Miss Bengough made one of her large windy gestures—an arm cutting the air, bracelets giving a small bright clatter—and then she let him have it.

“Really, you are too trying!” she broke out. “I do wish sometimes you’d remember you’re human, and live in a world! You know I’d be the last to wish you to lower your standard one inch, but it wouldn’t be lowering it to bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you’re sometimes altogether too godlike!… Why, it would be a wicked, criminal waste of your powers to destroy those fifteen chapters! Look at it reasonably, now. You’ve been working for nearly twenty years; you’ve now got what you’ve been working for almost within your grasp; your affairs are at a most critical stage (oh, don’t tell me; I know you’re about at the end of your money); and here you are, deliberately proposing to withdraw a thing that will probably make your name, and to substitute for it something that ten to one nobody on earth will ever want to read—and small blame to them! Really, you try my patience!”

As she talked, Oleron shook his head slowly. It was an old story between them. Elsie—noisy, able, practical—was an admirable friend up to a certain point; beyond that… well, each of us knows that point beyond which we stand alone. She sometimes said that if she had one-tenth part of Oleron’s genius there were few things she could not have done—as if genius were a measurable ingredient, a pinch of spice one could add to a dish to make it palatable. That it was not an additive but a nature, essential and indivisible, informing everything it touched, passed her comprehension. Their spirits parted company there. Oleron knew it. She did not appear to know it.

“Yes, yes, yes,” he said at last, a little wearily, “practically you’re quite right, entirely right, and I haven’t a word to say. If I could only turn Romilly over to you you’d make an enormous success of her. But that can’t be, and I, for my part, am seriously doubting whether she’s worth my while. You know what that means.”

“What does it mean?” she demanded bluntly.

“Well,” he said, smiling wanly, “what does it mean when you’re convinced a thing isn’t worth doing? You simply don’t do it.”

Miss Bengough’s eyes swept the ceiling as if seeking help from the plaster against this impossible man. “What utter rubbish!” she broke out at last. “Why, when I saw you last you were simply oozing Romilly; you were turning her off at the rate of four chapters a week; if you hadn’t moved you’d have had her three-parts done by now. What on earth possessed you to move right in the middle of your most important work?”

Oleron tried to put her off with a recital of inconveniences—the last place too cramped, too grimy, too hungry for daylight—but she wouldn’t have it. Perhaps in her heart she suspected the reason, and feared it. He was mortally weary of narrow circumstances: twenty years of garrets and roof-chambers and dingy flats and shabby lodgings. He was tired of the way rooms could smell of other people’s boiled cabbage and paraffin, tired of ceilings that pressed down as if they had weight, tired of hearing, night after night, the same thin noises through walls that made a man feel he had no skin.

The reward was as far off as ever—or if it was not, he no longer cared as he once would have cared to put out his hand and take it. It is all very well to tell a man at the point of exhaustion that only another effort is required; if he cannot make it, he is as far off as ever.

“Anyway,” Oleron said, summing it up with a forced briskness, “I’m happier here than I’ve been for a long time. That’s some sort of a justification.”

“And doing no work,” Miss Bengough returned pointedly.

At that, a trifling petulance that had been gathering in him came to a head, sharp as grounds between the teeth.

“And why should I do nothing but work?” he demanded. “How much happier am I for it? I don’t say I don’t love my work—when it’s done; but I hate doing it. Sometimes it’s an intolerable burden that I simply long to be rid of. Once in many weeks it has a moment, one moment, of glow and thrill for me; I remember the days when it was all glow and thrill; and now I’m forty-four, and it’s becoming drudgery. Nobody wants it; I’m ceasing to want it myself; and if any ordinary sensible man were to ask me whether I didn’t think I was a fool to go on, I think I should agree that I was.”

Miss Bengough’s comely pink face grew serious, the colour deepening as if the words had struck blood nearer the surface. “But you knew all that, many, many years ago, Paul—and still you chose it,” she said in a low voice.

“Well, and how should I have known?” he demanded. “I didn’t know. I was told so. My heart, if you like, told me so, and I thought I knew. Youth always thinks it knows; then one day it discovers that it is nearly fifty—”

“Forty-four, Paul—”

“—forty-four, then—and it finds that the glamour isn’t in front, but behind. Yes, I knew and chose, if that’s knowing and choosing… but it’s a costly choice we’re called on to make when we’re young!”

Miss Bengough’s eyes dropped to the floor. Without moving them she said, “You’re not regretting it, Paul?”

“Am I not?” he said, catching at her words as if they were something he could shake. “Upon my word, I’ve lately thought I am! What do I get in return for it all?”

“You know what you get,” she replied.

He might have known, from her tone, what else he could have had for the holding up of a finger—herself. She knew, and could not tell him, that he could have done no better thing for himself. Had he, any time these ten years, asked her to marry him, she would have replied quietly, “Very well; when?” He had never thought of it; or if he had, the thought had passed like a sound in the street, unheard because another sound filled his head.

“Yours is the real work,” she continued quietly. “Without you we jackals couldn’t exist. You and a few like you hold everything upon your shoulders.”

For a minute there was silence. In it, the house seemed to settle and breathe. There were small noises that in other places would be swallowed—some distant creak in the building’s bones, the faint tick of a cooling kettle, the soft shifting of fabric as Elsie adjusted in her chair. Oleron became aware of how different these sounds were from the old districts where carts rattled, pipes hammered, and cheap music ground its way through walls. Here the quiet was not empty; it was attentive. It put weight on words, and made even a pause feel like something spoken in a language you only half understood.

Then it occurred to Oleron that this had been common vulgar grumbling, and it was not his habit. Abruptly he rose and began to stack cups and plates on the tray as if he could tidy the mood away.

“Sorry you catch me like this, Elsie,” he said, with a little laugh that tried to be light. “No, I’ll take them out; then we’ll go for a walk, if you like….”

He carried out the tray and came back with his manner smoothed. To turn her thoughts—and his—he began to show her round the flat.

She made few comments. She moved through the rooms with those sudden ample motions, scarves fluttering, veil-tails stirring. The place, which had seemed to Oleron to have a singular grace, took on, under her indifference, a kind of stern plainness. Only the silence clung to them both, and the way their footsteps made different notes in different corners, as if the floorboards kept their own memory of pressure and release.

In the kitchen she stopped short and pointed.

“What’s that?” she asked, nodding at an old faded square of reddish frieze that Mrs. Barrett used as a cushion for her wooden chair.

“That? I should be glad if you could tell me what it is,” Oleron answered. He unfolded it from the bag he had kept it in and told her again how he had found it crammed into the window-seat, as if someone had shoved it away in haste and forgotten it.

Miss Bengough took it between finger and thumb, rubbing the worn fabric as though testing its history by touch. The stuff had a smell of dust long shut from air, and of something faintly sweet and stale beneath that—like old varnish, or dried flowers pressed in a book.

“I think I know what it is,” she said. “It’s been used to wrap up a harp before putting it into its case.”

“By Jove, that’s probably just what it was,” Oleron said, honestly relieved. “I could make neither head nor tail of it….”

They finished the tour and returned to the sitting-room.

“And who lives in the rest of the house?” Miss Bengough asked, glancing—not up at the cornice or the pictures, but sideways, as if listening for the answer in the building itself.

“I dare say a tramp sleeps in the cellar occasionally. Nobody else.”

“Hm!…” She set her mouth a little, thinking. Then: “Well, I’ll tell you what I think about it, if you like.”

“I should like.”

“You’ll never work here.”

“Oh?” Oleron said quickly. “Why not?”

“You’ll never finish Romilly here. Why, I don’t know, but you won’t. I know it. You’ll have to leave before you get on with that book.”

He mused for a moment, his gaze going to the quiet corner where the light lay, and then back to her. “Isn’t that a little—prejudiced, Elsie?”

“Perfectly ridiculous. As an argument it hasn’t a leg to stand on. But there it is,” she replied, her mouth once more full of the large-headed hatpins, which clicked softly as she gathered them like weapons.

Oleron reached down his hat and coat. He laughed, but the sound didn’t quite break the stillness; it merely made it sharper.

“I can only hope you’re entirely wrong,” he said, “for I shall be in a serious mess if Romilly isn’t out in the autumn.”

As Oleron sat by his fire that evening, turning over Miss Bengough’s prophecy as one turns a bruised apple in the hand to find the rotten spot, he decided it would have been kinder if she had kept her certainties to herself. A man does not set a fine sauce the better for being told beforehand it will split; the warning itself becomes the heat under the pan. Speak of difficulties, and you season the air with them until the tongue has made them almost inevitable.

He heartily confounded her.

Some hostile influence to the finishing of *Romilly* had, by her mere utterance, been brought to birth; and in that brisk, illogical manner women sometimes possess, she had fastened the thing upon his new abode as though she had pinned an evil charm to the curtains.

“You’ll never finish *Romilly* here.”

Why not? Was this her notion—that comfort, like over-rich pastry, cloys the appetite for work and leaves a man slack, dropped out of the race? The place was well enough—entirely charming, for that matter—but not so enervating as all that. No; Elsie had missed the mark.

He shifted his chair to take the room in again, and the room, in firelight, looked as though it smiled back—positively smiled, as a hearth-smile answers a hand held out to it. He smiled too, as if pity were due to a maligned apartment. In the soft glow even that slight lack of robust colour he had noticed by day was gone; the chintz curtains, flowered and trellised with baskets and oaten pipes, fell in long quiet folds to the window-seats; the old bookcases showed their rows of bindings as richly as jars on a pantry shelf catch lamplight; and whatever sallowness the daylight had insisted upon seemed to have been cooked away by the fire’s kindly heat.

If the truth must be told, it had been Elsie herself who had seemed a little out of the picture.

That thought, once tasted, returned with an after-flavour he could not at first name. The room had, quite accidentally, done Miss Bengough a disservice that afternoon; it had placed her—set her down—so that a contrast declared itself. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the room in which he sat were indeed characterised by a certain spareness, a lack of vigour; then so much the worse for Elsie, who certainly erred on the side of redundancy, of general muchness. If one must set qualities side by side, Oleron knew his taste ran to the austere.

It came on him with the sharpness of discovering too much spice in a dish: he wondered he had not found it before. He saw her again as she had appeared—large, showy, moistly pink, with that prize-bloom quality exuding from her as if she were perpetually just out of the hothouse—and instantly she suffered in his thought. He even recognised now that, at the time, he had noticed something odd, and that unconsciously his attitude, even while she had been there, had been one of criticism.

The mechanism of her was a little obvious. Her melting humidity seemed the outcome of processes that could be analysed, like a glaze that shines because of sugar and heat, not because of any miracle; and behind her—behind the florid, healthy surface—there had lurked, dimly, some emblem of mortality, as though the very abundance foretold the slackening and collapse that follow it. In ten years of intimacy he had never once dreamed of asking her to marry him; none the less, for the first time he felt a distinct thankfulness that he had not.

Then, suddenly and swiftly, his face flamed at the meanness of his own mind. What was he doing—dissecting Elsie Bengough, his friend? Elsie, with whom he had spent weeks and weeks of afternoons; the good chum on whose help he would have counted if the rest of the world failed him; the woman whose loyalty he knew would not swerve while she drew breath. To turn her, even in thought, into a thing to be judged and sliced open—he was an ingrate, a cad.

Had she been there at that moment he would have humbled himself before her.

For ten minutes and more he sat staring into the fire while that humiliating red ebbed slowly from his cheeks. All was still within and without—save for a tiny musical tinkling from the kitchen, the drip of water from a tap not quite turned off into the vessel beneath it. It was so domestic, so small, so like a neglected detail after washing up, that it might have belonged to any house in any street; yet in this quiet it was as present as a clock. Mechanically he began to beat with his finger to the faint falling of the drops, as though keeping time for a tune he did not know he knew; and the little regular motion seemed to hasten the retreat of shame. He cooled again.

And when he took up his meditation, he did it without any consciousness that he returned precisely to the place he had left.

It was not only Elsie’s florid superfluity he had approached in a critic’s attitude; there were also the wide differences between her mind and his. Looking back, he felt no gratitude that, up to a certain point, their natures had run companionably side by side; he was full of questions beyond that point. Their intellects diverged. Had there been any real coincidence at all? He had read his work to her; she had seemed to understand, to speak to the point—yet what can a man do who has assumed another sees as he does, until a single phrase falsifies and discredits everything that has gone before? He doubted all now, and the doubt spread like a stain in hot water.

It did occur to him, briefly, that the man who demands more of a friend than can be given risks losing that friend; but he put the thought aside. Again he ceased to think, and again his finger moved in time to the distant dripping—one small sound in an otherwise merciful hush, a little bright pulse against the room’s calm.

And now, if these things were true of Elsie Bengough, they were also true of the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop.

Since he could say of Romilly what decency would not let him say of Elsie, he let his mind go. He did so there in that smiling, fire-lighted room, with the faint tap-music for accompaniment. There was no longer any doubt about it.

He hated the central character of his novel.

Even as he had described her physically, she overpowered the senses: coarse-fibred, over-coloured, rank—like a fruit that has been forced too quickly and sweats its sweetness out into cloy. The moment he named the thought, it became true beyond argument. Gulliver had spoken of Brobdingnagian maids-of-honour so; and mentally and spiritually Romilly corresponded—unsensitive, limited, common. The model—the model stuck out through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters so plainly that, without knowing why, he had been unable to begin the sixteenth.

He marvelled that it had only just dawned upon him.

And this—this was to have been his Beatrice, his vision. As Elsie she was to have been fed into the furnace of his art and to have come out the Woman all men desire: her thoughts culled from his own finest; her form from his dearest dreams; her setting wherever he could find one fit for her worth. He had brooded long before making the attempt; then, one day, he had felt her stir within him as a mother feels a quickening, and he had begun to write, and so had added chapter to chapter—

And those fifteen sodden chapters were what he had produced.

Again he sat, his finger softly moving to the drip.

Then he bestirred himself, decisively, as a cook will when he has tasted and knows the whole pot is spoiled. She must go. All fifteen chapters of her. That was settled. For what was to take her place his mind was blank; but one thing at a time. A man is not excused from taking the wrong course because the right one is not immediately revealed. Better would come if it was to come; in the meantime—

He rose, fetched the fifteen chapters, and read them over before he should drop them into the fire.

But instead of putting them into the fire, he let them fall from his hand.

The drip asserted itself again. It had, he realised, a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes; it was foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like, as though some small, bright instrument were being plucked in the kitchen by invisible fingers. In his mind he could see each drop gathering, swelling, trembling on the lip of the tap, and then the tiny percussion of its fall—plink, plunk—minimised almost to nothing. Following the lowest note there seemed to be a brief phrase, irregularly repeated; and presently he found himself waiting for the recurrence of that phrase as one waits for a refrain.

It was quite pretty.

It was also, in its way, unnerving—not by loudness, but by how it insinuated itself into attention, like a remembered air creeping in under the door. It did not conduce to wakefulness. Sitting there with the papers in a heap, with the fire lowering, Oleron dozed.

When he awoke again the fire had burned low, and the flames of the candles were licking at the rims of the Sheffield sticks. Sluggishly he rose, yawned, went his nightly round of locks and fastenings—door, window, latch by latch—and passed into his bedroom. Soon he slept soundly.

A curious little sequel followed on the morrow.

Mrs. Barrett usually tapped, not at his door, but at the wooden wall beyond which lay his bed; and then Oleron rose, put on his dressing-gown, and admitted her. He was not conscious that, as he did so that morning, he hummed an air; but Mrs. Barrett lingered with her hand on the door-knob, her face a little averted, smiling as though at some old intimacy.

“De-ar me!” her soft falsetto rose. “But that will be a very o-ald tune, Mr. Oleron! I will not have heard it this for-ty years!”

“What tune?” Oleron asked.

“The tune, indeed, that you was humming, sir.”

His thumb was in the flap of a letter. It stayed there, arrested.

“I was humming?… Sing it, Mrs. Barrett.”

Mrs. Barrett prut-prutted with modesty. “I have no voice for singing, Mr. Oleron; it was Ann Pugh was the singer of our family; but the tune will be very o-ald, and it is called ‘The Beckoning Fair One.’”

“Try to sing it,” said Oleron, his thumb still holding the envelope as though the paper had suddenly grown heavy; and Mrs. Barrett, with dimpling confusion, hummed the air.

“They do say it was sung to a harp, Mr. Oleron, and it will be very o-ald,” she concluded.

“And I was singing that?”

“Indeed you wass. I would not be likely to tell you lies.”

“Very well—let me have breakfast,” Oleron said, and opened his letter; but the trifling circumstance struck him as odder than he would have admitted. For the phrase he had hummed—this ancient scrap with its title like a hand held out in the dark—had been that which, the evening before, he had associated with the falling from the tap.

Even more curious than the way an ordinary tap could dribble in the very cadence of a tune he knew—an air that had no right to be there at all—was what that little sound did to him afterward. It keyed his hearing as a cook’s ear is keyed when the kitchen is finally quiet enough to catch the first warning hiss of a pan before the fat smokes. Once the tap had taught him to listen, the rest of the house would not be unheard.

Silence, he discovered, was not the absence of noise but a kind of broth: you only notice its depth when a single grain of sound drops in and blooms. And this place was never truly still. The spring mildness, seeping in under warped sashes, seemed to loosen the old boards the way warmth loosens stiff dough; his fires, banked and raked and coaxed, made the timbers extend and settle back again with tired acquiescence. Underneath, in the unseen courses of lath and joist, there was a whole culinary industry of small lives—borers and beetles, mites and mice—gnawing and tapping as steadily as a mortar’s pestle.

He had only to sit and wait, as one waits for a stock to come to its first simmer, and his ear would adjust. At first there was the obvious: a mouse’s quick scamper like a spoon skittering on a plate; a flake of plaster dropping behind the paneling with the dry snap of old sugar; the pop from the hearth as some trapped bubble in the coal or a hidden knot in the wood released itself. Then, underneath those recognitions, the subtler conversation began: beams easing deeper into mortises with a long, weary exhale; tiny tickings in the walls; joints that cracked as if complaining of their age; window-sashes shifting, without any stirring of air, and giving a soft knock in their frames like a cautious knuckle on a door.

It made him smile—involuntarily, as if at an impudent thought—how arbitrary the line was between what men called living and not living. The house had a language. If he had known the dialect, he felt it could have told him things: not in words, but in pressures, in small contractions and relaxations, in the particular accent of each creak. And whether or not it was “alive” in any reasonable sense, it had a personality that insinuated itself into his days as surely as a smell clings to clothing after you’ve fried onions.

An hour of musing would take him farther still. As his own body stood in some intimate, serviceable relation to his mind—hands obeying, lungs filling, heart keeping its stubborn rhythm—so, by some attenuated extension, his habitation might stand in a relation to him. He even entertained, half amused, half uneasy, the fancy that he might identify with it so thoroughly that a future tenant would say the place was haunted. What an absurd joke, if he—a harmless author with nothing darker on his conscience than a novel he had discovered he must begin again—should be laying, without meaning it, the foundation of a ghost.

Yet as his attachment to the fabric of the house deepened, Elsie Bengough’s dislike sharpened from cool disinterest into something like active distaste. She did not trouble to hide it.

“It doesn’t belong to to-day at all,” she said, with her brisk decision, “and for you especially it’s bad. You’re only too ready to loosen your hold on actual things and slip into apathy. You ought to be in a place with concrete floors and a patent gas-meter and a tradesmen’s lift. And it would do you all the good in the world if you had a job that made you scramble and rub elbows with your fellow-men.” Her eyes held his as she added, “If I could get you something—two or three days a week—leaving you heaps of time for your proper work—would you take it?”

He felt, absurdly, as if she had pulled his chair out from under him. Diagnosed. Labeled. Spoken over, as though he were a patient refusing broth.

“Thank you,” he said, and could not manage a smile. “But I don’t think so. After all, each of us has his own life to live.”

“His own life to live!” She took the phrase up sharply, as if it were a tasteless seasoning. “How long is it since you were out, Paul?”

“About two hours.”

“I don’t mean to buy stamps or post a letter. How long since you had anything like a stretch?”

“Oh—some little time perhaps. I don’t know.”

“Since I was here last?”

“I haven’t been out much.”

“And has Romilly progressed so wonderfully for your being cooped up?”

“I think she has. I’m laying her foundations. I shall begin the actual writing presently.”

It seemed she had forgotten their earlier tussle over the first Romilly—forgotten it, or laid it by like a knife kept for a later cut. She frowned, turned half away, then swung back with sudden heat.

“Ah! So you’ve still got that ridiculous idea in your head?”

“If you mean,” he said slowly, feeling the stiffness in his own voice, “that I’ve discarded the old Romilly and am at work on a new one, you’re right. I have still got that idea in my head.”

Something uncordial in his tone struck her, and it lit her fighting temper rather than softened it. She made a sound of impatience.

“Where is the old one?” she demanded.

“Why?” he asked.

“I want to see it. I want to show some of it to you. I want—if you’re not wool-gathering entirely—to bring you back to your senses.”

This time he turned away, his back to her as if to the draught from a door. When he faced her again he tried to speak more gently, as though temper were a kind of noise he must not add to the house’s.

“It’s no good, Elsie. I’m responsible for the way I go, and you must let me go it—even if it seems wrong to you. Believe me, I am giving thought to it.” He hesitated, then yielded. “The manuscript? I was on the point of burning it, but I didn’t. It’s in that window-seat, if you must see it.”

She crossed quickly and lifted the lid.

At once she gave a small exclamation, startled and sharp, and pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. Over her shoulder she said, with that odd domestic practicality people cling to in fright, “You ought to knock those nails in, Paul.”

He was at her side in two strides. “What? What is it? What’s the matter?”

“I did knock them in—or rather, pulled them out.”

“You left enough to scratch with,” she said, and held up her hand.

From the upper wrist to the knuckle of her little finger a raw red gash welled up, bright and immediate. It was not a scratch; it was a slice, as if something had been waiting there with a point and a purpose.

“Good—gracious!” he ejaculated.

“Here—come to the bathroom and bathe it quickly—”

He hurried her down the passage to the small bathroom. He turned on the warm water and held her hand under it, the water running over the cut and turning faintly pink before it found the drain. He washed it carefully, as one rinses grit from herbs, his fingers gentle but swift. Then he turned on cold and kept her hand beneath it, talking in broken astonished fragments.

“Good Lord—how did that happen? As far as I knew I’d—Is this too cold? Does it hurt? I can’t imagine how on earth—there; that’ll do—”

“No—one moment longer—I can bear it,” she murmured, eyes closed, her face drawn and pale.

When he led her back to the sitting-room he bound the hand with his own handkerchief, knotting it with clumsy care. But his face would not lose its perplexed look. He had spent half a day opening those three window-boxes, making them serviceable, doing the work with the thoroughness of a man who likes his tools in order and his surfaces safe. He could not conceive how an inch and a half of rusty nail could have been left standing, ready like a trap. He himself had lifted those lids again and again and noticed nothing—no snag, no point, no danger. Yet there it was.

“It shall come out now, at all events,” he muttered, and fetched pincers.

This time there was no mistake. The nail came out with a reluctant little squeal against the wood—a sound too like a tiny cry for comfort. He threw the thing down as if it were a worm pulled from fruit.

Elsie had sunk into a chair. Her face stayed white, and she cradled her injured hand; yet in the other she held the manuscript of Romilly, as if clinging to what she had come for. Pain did not dislodge her purpose. When she spoke again her voice had that earnestness that makes even foolish things sound like prophecy.

“Oh, Paul, it will be the greatest mistake you ever, ever made if you do not publish this!”

He hung his head. He was genuinely distressed; but the nail—its presence, its impossibility—kept pushing itself between him and her words. For the moment Romilly had fallen into second place behind that inch and a half of rust that should not have existed.

Still, she insisted, and when he answered it was almost apologetic, as though he were begging forgiveness for refusing a dish she had cooked with care.

“What can I say, Elsie? I can only hope that when you see the new version you’ll see how right I am. And if in spite of all you don’t like her—well—” He made a helpless gesture, palm up, empty. “Don’t you see that I must be guided by my own lights?”

She said nothing.

“Come, Elsie,” he added gently, wanting peace, wanting the air to settle. “We’ve got along well so far; don’t let us split on this.”

The words had hardly left him before he wished them unsaid. She had been nursing her injured hand with her eyes closed, as if concentrating on keeping her composure together. Now her lids and lips quivered at once. When she spoke, her voice shook.

“I can’t help saying it, Paul, but you are so greatly changed.”

“Hush, Elsie,” he murmured, soothing as he would have soothed a child who’d cut a finger in the kitchen. “You’ve had a shock. Rest for a while. How could I change?”

“I don’t know, but you are. You’ve not been yourself ever since you came here. I wish you’d never seen the place. It’s stopped your work, it’s making you into a person I hardly know, and it’s made me horribly anxious about you.” She drew a breath that hitched. “Oh—how my hand is beginning to throb!”

“Poor child,” he said.

“Will you let me take you to a doctor and have it properly dressed?”

“No—I shall be all right presently—I’ll keep it raised—”

She lifted her elbow to the back of her chair and let the bandaged hand rest lightly on his shoulder.

The touch, simple as it was, landed in him like a sudden change in the house’s acoustic scale—the way a room sounds different the instant someone begins to breathe in it beside you. Hundreds of times on their excursions she had slipped her hand within his arm with the easy affection of a sister, and he had accepted it as a brother might. But now, with her eyes closed and her head fallen back and a faint, lost smile parting her lips, a clarity rushed in on him with shocking force.

Good God.

He had never divined it. And strangest of all, now that he did see—now that he could not unsee—that she was lost in love of him, he did not feel humbled or sorrowful or tender. Something else rose, something raw and ungenerous: petulance, irritation, resentment, as if her feeling were an intrusion, another claim laid upon him by a world he was trying to keep at bay.

The selfish prompting mastered him before he was aware, and he almost gave it words. What was she doing here at all? Why was she not at her own work? Why was she interfering with his? Who had appointed her his guardian? Changed? It was she—she—who had changed.

By the time she opened her eyes he had beaten the thought back far enough to speak with restraint.

“I wish you would let me take you to a doctor.”

She rose carefully, holding herself together. “No, thank you, Paul. I’ll go now. If I need a dressing I’ll get one; take the other hand, please. Good-bye—”

He did not try to detain her. He walked with her to the foot of the stairs. Her steps went down into the narrow alley between the old walls, her figure moving away into the outdoor noise of the street—a world of carts and footsteps and ordinary human clatter that suddenly seemed, to his newly tuned hearing, both harsh and thin.

Half-way along she turned back.

“It would be a long way to come if you happened not to be in,” she said. “I’ll send you a postcard the next time.”

At the gate she turned again. Her face looked mournful, and the bandage made her seem oddly vulnerable, as if the house had marked her.

“Leave here, Paul,” she said. “Everything’s wrong with this house.”

Then she was gone.

Oleron returned to his room. He crossed straight to the window-seat as though drawn by a scent. He opened the lid and stood long looking down into the space where the manuscript lay and where the nail had waited. The wood showed the small torn place where it had been, dark with old rust.

He closed the lid again and turned away.

“That’s rather frightening,” he muttered, and the house, in its minute shifting and ticking, seemed to listen. “It’s simply not possible that I should not have removed that nail.”

Oleron understood perfectly what Elsie had meant when she said her next call would be heralded by a postcard. She had understood too—understood that, at last, he knew, and that knowing had not turned into want. The knowledge should have brought him relief; instead it lodged in him like a fish-bone, and when, within a week, she knocked at his door without warning, it gave him a wretched, almost shameful pang.

She did not even cross the threshold at first. Her voice came from the landing, careful and thin, as if she were trying not to disturb the house. She said she would not stay; she had only stopped in passing; and he had to press her before she would step inside at all, as though the air of his rooms were a draught that might chill her.

Her excuse was domestic in its way—practical, almost nourishing: she had heard of an inquiry for short stories, a market he might do well to follow, she said, and she had thought of him. It was the sort of thing a woman brings like a small basket of provisions—an errand dressed up as kindness—so as not to bring the hungrier thing she truly carried. He thanked her. The moment the words were spoken she looked again toward the stairs, already in flight.

He did not try to keep her. Even he could see the falsehood in the pretext, as one smells a sweetness laid over a taint. He accompanied her down, because it was the least he could do, and because the stairwell of that house had a way of swallowing sound, so that any parting felt too much like abandonment.

Halfway down came the sharp report of splintering wood, like a cleaver striking a dry bone. Elsie checked a cry, but it escaped her in a broken breath. Oleron knew the staircase was old—grain raised and worn as a chopping-board that has taken a thousand knives—yet he had gone up and down often enough without mishap.

Her foot had gone through the tread.

He sprang to her, the dust of old timber already in his nostrils, that stale, cellar-sweet scent that rises when you disturb long-shut boards. “Oh, I say! My poor girl!”

She laughed—a frantic, airless laugh—and it turned at once to sobbing. “It’s my weight—I know I’m getting fat—”

“Keep still—let me clear these splinters away,” he muttered, and his voice sounded strange to him in that narrow place, swallowed and thrown back in small, hard echoes. He thrust down at the broken boards. The wood gave grudgingly, barbed and resentful. It was not easy to free her. When he saw the torn leather of her boot, he knew what the foot and ankle inside must be like—scraped raw, scored and peppered with little bites of wood. The sight of it made his stomach turn.

“Good God—good God!” he kept saying, as though repetition might make it less true.

“I shall be too heavy for anything soon,” she sobbed and laughed again, and the sound of her laugh, against the house’s little creaks and ticks, was like someone striking a cracked glass.

He begged her to go back up, to sit, to look at the hurt, to let him do the smallest human thing—fetch water, tear linen, bind the wound as neatly as a careful cook trusses a bird so it will not fall apart in the pot. But she shook her head, breathing fast.

“No—let me go quickly—let me go quickly,” she repeated, as if speed were the only bandage she trusted.

“But it’s a frightful gash!”

“No—not so bad—let me get away quickly—I’m—I’m not wanted.”

At that phrase his head dropped, as though she had struck him. “Elsie!” he choked, not with anger but with shock, as if the words had slapped him with a wet hand.

She made a quick gesture, as if pushing something away from her—something invisible but heavy. “Oh, Paul, not that—not you—of course I do mean that too in a sense—oh, you know what I mean!… But if the other can’t be, spare me this now! I—I wouldn’t have come, but—but—oh, I did, I did try to keep away!”

It was intolerable. He stood there with his hands half raised, as helpless as if he had been asked to translate a cry into a language that had no words for it. What could he do? What could he say? He did not love her. Not in the way she meant, not in the way she had starved herself to be meant.

“Let me go—I’m not wanted—let me take away what’s left of me—”

“Dear Elsie—you are very dear to me—” he began, and even as he said it he heard how thin it sounded beside the thick need in her voice.

Again that violent gesture. “No, not that—not anything less—don’t offer me anything less—leave me a little pride—”

He could at least insist on the ordinary mercies. “Let me get my hat and coat—let me take you to a doctor,” he muttered.

She refused. She refused even the support of his arm, wobbling away from him with a sudden, unsteady laugh as if to make light of her own bleeding. “I’m sorry I broke your stairs, Paul…. You will go and see about the short stories, won’t you?”

It wrung a groan from him—her clinging to the pretense like a wrapper held tight around an empty lunch. “Then if you won’t see a doctor, will you go across the square and let Mrs. Barrett look at you? Look, there’s Barrett passing now—”

Barrett—the long-nosed man—was peering down the alley as if the house itself had called him with some faint, unpleasant noise. Oleron was about to hail him when Barrett, without a word, turned away and made off, as though he had heard something he preferred not to hear.

Elsie wanted nothing so much as to be clear of the place. At last she promised she would go straight to a doctor, but insisted on going alone, dragging the injured foot with a stubborn, almost punitive determination.

“Good-bye,” she said.

Oleron watched her until she was past the hatchet-like boards that cried TO LET in black letters, watching as if he feared those boards might loosen and fall upon her, as if everything the house held—nail, plank, sign—had grown eager to hurt her.

That night he did not dine. Hunger had no voice in him beside the noise of what had happened. He walked from room to room, restlessly, as if pacing could carry him away from Elsie Bengough’s words, which kept sounding in his head with the flat insistence of a phrase learned too late: I’m not wanted—don’t offer me anything less—let me take away what’s left of me—

If only he could have persuaded himself he loved her. If only he had watched better, listened better, understood earlier. Those walks, those sisterly offerings of an arm—how foolish he had been, how modern and inattentive, as though affection were a thing that could be handled with gloves and no one would notice the lack of warmth. But it was too late now. It was she, not he, who must act by staying away. He would help her all he could. He would not sit with her. If she came, he would hurry her out again as fast as he could.

The room grew dark. He did not light candles. The fire sank; he stirred it up and flung himself into a chair, listening to the house the way one listens for milk to boil over—too late, too late, the scorch already there. The quiet was never quiet in that place. It was full of little domestic sounds, the shy creaks of settling wood, the small dry tickings, the delicate rappings that came from within the panelling as if the walls had teeth and were clicking them.

From time to time he winced as some fresh fragment of Elsie’s speech rose and stabbed him. Poor, poor Elsie. And yet it was out of the question. Her face came back to him as he had seen it on the stairs—drawn, swollen, ugly with pain and tears. Ugly—yes, blubbered. If tears were women’s weapons, as people said, then these were turned against herself. And with that came the morbid picture again: a dark river below an embankment; a hook on a door; an inquest; his own name spoken in a public room; Barrett saying he had seen her leave Oleron’s house; questions about a badly healed wound on a hand and an abrasion on an ankle.

He recognized the sickness of those thoughts and forced them aside. He sat and listened, trying to be rational, trying to do what he had always done—identify each sound, give it a material cause, file it away as harmless. The house offered him its acoustic ecology: the soft breathing of the chimney, the faint click of cooling metal, the slow complaining of beams. Modern streets had their grinding wagons and the tinny shouting of commerce; here there was instead an older soundscape, intimate and close, as if the building were pressed cheek to cheek with him in the dark.

And yet there was one noise he had not accounted for—not consciously. It had come some minutes ago, and then again: a soft sweeping rustle, carrying within it the finest crackling, like dry strands drawn through fingers.

For half a minute it caught his attention; then his mind slid back to Elsie. He found himself nearer to loving her in that moment than he ever had been—nearer in the bleak, human way that comes when one sees how frail bodies are, how quickly the world abrades them. Some men loved the more for blemish and mortal failure, for the reminders that we are only here briefly and that nothing is worth much except tenderness. In that sense—only in that sense—he did love her. Her happiness had never moved him; her sorrow almost did.

Then the rustling came again.

Again and again, with a curious insistence, a domestic urgency. It quickened a little as he attended; or perhaps his attention made it louder, the way a quiet room makes a whisper enormous. He leaned forward, tense, listening, trying to fit it to something—curtain, paper, mouse, draft.

All at once he started bolt upright. Terror went through him as abruptly as cold water.

He leapt to his feet. His chair hung for a moment and then toppled, setting the fire-irons clattering as it fell, a harsh metallic chatter that seemed indecently loud, like a factory noise dropped into a chapel.

There was only one sound in the world like that soft sweep with its tiny crackle.

The next time it came he reached behind him into empty air, backing until his shoulders found the wall. “God in Heaven!” broke from him.

The sound ceased.

The next moment he gave a high cry. “What is it? What’s there? Who’s there?”

Something scuttled; his knees buckled for an instant, and he knew it was only a mouse. That was not what had turned his stomach sick and made his mind reel. That other sound—the like of which was not in the world—had entirely ceased.

He called again and again, and then a new terror seized him: the terror of his own voice in the house, of the way it filled the rooms and came back altered, as if the walls were tasting it. He did not dare call again.

His hand, shaking, went to his pocket for a match and found none. He thought of the mantelpiece. Keeping his back to the wall, he worked his way around a small recess. His fingers found the mantel, groped along it, and struck a matchbox, which fell to the hearth. He could just see it in the fire’s dying light, but his fingers could not catch it until he had cornered it against the fender like a small animal.

He rose, struck a match. The flare made the air smell of sulphur, sharp and kitchen-like, like the first spark under damp kindling. He struck a second. A candle stood on the table. He lit it; the flame sank for a moment as though the room breathed on it, then steadied and burned clear.

He looked around.

The room was as usual. There was nothing.

There was nothing—and yet there had been something, and might still be something. Once he had smiled at the fancy that, by living here and loving his beautiful rooms, he might be preparing a ghost for the future, a coalescence of self and place that would persist like a scent in a closed cupboard. It had never occurred to him that a similar merging might have happened before him—that the house might already hold, folded into its timbers like a recipe written in a dialect no one spoke any more, the untranslatable residue of another life.

With that staggering impossibility he found himself face to face. Something did persist in the house; it had a tenant other than himself. And that tenant—whatsoever, whosoever—had appalled his soul by making, in the dark, the unmistakable sound of a woman brushing her hair.

Without quite knowing how he came to be there, Oleron found himself stepping over the loose board he had laid across the broken stair—Miss Bengough’s stair—his foot placing itself with a practised caution that did not reach as far as his mind. He was hatless and already on the descent, and only afterwards did a blurred remembrance come back: the candle left burning on the table as if to keep watch, the door opened no more than the breadth of his shoulders, the sideways slip through the crack like a thief in his own house, the soft, guilty click as he drew it to again.

At the foot of the stairs the house threw him a last spite. Something came in a rush from the disused cellars, a low streak with claws skittering on stone and a wild flutter of fur, and shot through the outer door into the night. Only a cat—only that—yet the sound of it, the sudden scrawl of life where no life should be, tore a childish sob out of him.

He passed under the “TO LET” boards at the gate and stood a moment as if the square itself had turned into an audience. His fingers worried his lip. He stared up at his own windows, at the faint glimmer behind one of the red blinds, that thin domestic glow which should have meant kettles and lamplight and ordinary human noises—knife on board, spoon in cup, a chair creaking beneath a living weight. Instead it looked like a signal agreed upon by someone else.

Still glancing over his shoulder, he moved unsteadily up the square. The air was cold with that late-winter damp that smelled of soot-wet brick and old cabbage leaves. Round the corner was a small public-house he had never entered. He entered it now as though pushed.

A shilling slid from his fingers and missed the counter by inches, clinking against the wood and dropping into sawdust. “B—b—bran—brandy,” he managed, and then bent down, absurdly intent on finding the coin as if the act of searching could hold the world steady.

The little bar, floored and sprinkled to keep down the spit and mud, was empty save for him. The carters and labourers and small tradesmen of the neighbourhood kept to the farther compartment, where talk came and went in thick murmurs and occasional bursts of laughter, like pots boiling over. Between Oleron and the white-haired landlady was a cut-glass partition that ended an uneven hand’s breadth above the counter, so that the smells—beer-sour, gin-sharp, damp wool, tobacco—floated freely. The landlady moved among taps and bottles with the calm economy of someone who knows the weight of every jug by the swing of her arm.

Oleron sat on a hard wooden settee whose perforated seat pinched through his trousers. He drank half the brandy and then, with a sudden resentful thrift, swallowed the rest because he might as well drink it as spill it. The raw warmth ran down him; his mouth filled with that harsh, sweet burn, like scorched sugar on a pan scraped too late, and it made his eyes water. He ordered more at once.

He would not go back to that room with the candle still burning. No. Not now. Not to the entry and the stairs and the broken step, certainly not to that pith-white room that held the eye the way a bone-white fish belly holds it on a slab—fascinating, obscene in its cleanliness. He would return to his old arrangement, workroom and separate sleeping-quarters. He would go to his old landlady—presently, when he had finished this glass—and ask if she could put him up for the night. He could hear, in imagination, the safe little domestic sounds of her house: a kettle singing, a grate settling, a drawer shut with a familiar thump. Sounds that belonged to human lives and not to empty spaces.

His glass was empty again. He rose, had it refilled, sat.

And if anybody asked why he was removing? He had reason enough—reason enough! Nails that gash a hand and then, like some cunning thing, set themselves back into the wood as if they had never been disturbed; steps that give way under a foot; and women—women that have no right—coming into a man’s place and brushing their hair in the dark, those faint cracklings like tiny sparks in a dry comb, a sound so intimate it ought to belong only to a bedchamber, to breath and skin and lamplight. He was querulous, injured. He had taken the place for himself, not for an unseen presence to practise the nightly rites of a toilette. That lawyer fellow in Lincoln’s Inn ought to be told so before many hours were out. It was outrageous, letting a man sign for a lease as though he were taking rooms, when in truth he was taking—what?—a share.

Presently, lifting his eyes, he saw faces watching him through the gap above the partition: pale ovals hovering, then withdrawing the instant he looked directly at them. Their attention had the furtive savour of people pausing with their mouths full. Oleron shifted to a corner where he could not be seen from the farther bar; but that placed him squarely in the landlady’s line of sight.

She knew him by sight, of course—had seen him passing and repassing the square at all hours with his manuscript-bag and his set jaw. After a time she made a remark about the weather. He did not know what he replied, only that it led to influenza and then to the spring coming at last.

The commonplace steadied him as a slice of bread can steady a stomach against spirits. For a moment an idle, shameful wonder intruded—whether she, too, brushed her hair every night, and if so whether it crackled in the dark with that little electric speech. He snapped the thought shut like a trap and felt better for having done it. Here, at least, there were honest noises: a glass set down; a tap run; a man coughing behind the partition; the subdued thunder of boots as someone crossed the farther room. Sound had its proper causes.

With his next glass he became, perversely, all for going back. Not go back? He would go back. They should very soon see whether he was to be turned out of his place like that. He began to wonder why he was doing this unusual thing—unusual for him—sitting hatless and drinking brandy in a public-house like a clerk in trouble.

Suppose he told the landlady the whole business: that a caller had scratched her hand on a nail, had later put her foot through a rotten stair, and that he himself, in an old house full of squeaks and creaks and whispers, had heard a minute noise and bolted from it in fright. She would think him mad. Of course she would.

Pshaw. The truth was simpler. He had not been doing enough work. He had been dreaming his days away, letting the hours go stale like a pot left uncovered, filling his head with moonshine about a new Romilly—as if the old one were not good enough—and then he was surprised that the devil should enter an empty head. That was it. He would go back. He would take a walk first; he hadn’t walked enough lately. He would take himself in hand and settle the hash of that sixteenth chapter—fancy, he had actually entertained the folly of destroying fifteen chapters!—and thenceforward remember obligations, remember there was work to do, words to put in their places as carefully as ingredients weighed and sifted. There it was in a nutshell.

He finished the brandy and went out.

For some time he walked with nothing in his mind but himself: the sting of humiliation, the flare of pride. The fresh air at first made the drink rise, quickening his heart and loosening his steps; then, as the brandy thinned in his veins, his mind grew clearer than it had been since morning. And the clearer it grew, the less final his loud assurances became, the more solid became his conviction that, after every explanation had been made, something would remain that could not be explained.

The hysteria of an hour before had ebbed away. He grew steadily calmer; but the disquieting certainty remained, heavy as a stone in the pocket.

A deeper fear took him—fear for Elsie.

There was something in his place inimical to her safety. Her two accidents alone might not have persuaded him, but she herself had said it in that quiet, stubborn way of hers: “I’m not wanted here.” She had declared there was something wrong with the place. She had seen it before he had. Very well. One thing stood out. If that was so, she must be kept away for quite another reason than the one that had confounded and humiliated him earlier. Luckily she had said she meant to stay away; she must be held to it. He must see to it.

And all the more must he see to it because his first impulse—never to set foot in the place again—was absurd. People did not do that. With Elsie secure, he could not, with any respect for himself, allow himself to be turned out by a shadow, nor even by a danger merely because it was a danger. He had to live somewhere, and he would live there. He must return.

He mastered the faint chill that came with the decision and turned abruptly. If fear grew on him again, perhaps he would take one more glass of brandy.

But by the time he reached the short street that led back to the square he was too late. The little public-house was still lit within, but closed; one or two men stood talking on the kerb, their voices low. As Oleron passed, a sudden silence fell—as if someone had clapped a hand over a mouth. Lower down he passed long-nosed Barrett, who did not return his good-night.

Oleron turned in at the broken gate, hesitated one instant in the alley where the house’s smell gathered—damp plaster, old smoke, the sourness of disuse—and then mounted the stairs.

Only an inch of candle remained in the Sheffield stick. He did not light another. Deliberately he forced himself to take the stub up and make the tour of his five rooms before retiring, as if making rounds in a sickhouse. The little flame hissed faintly as it ate the last of the wick, and his own footsteps sounded too loud in the hollows—an intrusion into a silence that seemed to have its own listening quality.

Crossing back from the kitchen through his little hall, he noticed a letter on the floor.

He carried it into the sitting-room and glanced at the envelope before opening it. It was unstamped and had been put through the door by hand. The handwriting was clumsy, and when he unfolded it he saw that the lines ran on without comma or period, the words jammed together like a mouth that could not stop itself.

He read the first line, turned at once to the signature, and then went back and finished.

It was from Barrett. Barrett would be obliged if Mr. Oleron would make other arrangements for the preparing of his breakfasts and the cleaning-out of his place.

There was a sting in the tail: a postscript, a text of Scripture, and in it an allusion that could only be to Elsie Bengough.

A frown, seldom seen on Oleron’s face, cut deep into his brow. So. That was it. Very well. They would see about that on the morrow.

For the rest, it was merely another reason why Elsie should keep away.

Then his suppressed rage broke out, hot and sudden, like spirits set alight.

The foul-minded lot. The devil himself could not have put a leer on anything that had ever passed between Paul Oleron and Elsie Bengough; yet this nosing rascal must pry and talk, must drag his own coarse dialect of insinuation over what had been plain and decent, changing the very flavour of it by naming it crookedly. Oleron crumpled the paper, held it into the candle flame, watched it blacken and curl, and then ground the ashes under his heel until there was nothing left but grey smears.

One useful purpose the letter served: it raised in him a wrathful blaze that banished pale shadows. Anger made honest noise in him—blood in the ears, breath in the throat—drowning those minute, treacherous sounds that had unseated him.

Nevertheless, one other puzzling circumstance closed the day.

As he undressed, he happened to glance at the bed. The coverlets bore the impress as though someone had lain on them, a long shallow hollow, the fabric smoothed and pressed where a body might have been, not the casual rumple of a hand, but the patient weight of time. He could not remember lying down during the day—off-hand he would have said certainly not—yet he could not be positive.

His indignation for Elsie, perhaps aided by what brandy still lingered, excluded all other considerations. He put out his candle, lay down, and passed at once into a deep, dreamless sleep; and in the absence of Mrs. Barrett’s morning call it lasted almost once round the clock.

Terror, when it first alights, is a clean thing—sharp as vinegar on the tongue, unmistakable as smoke in a sleeping-room. It warns. It insists on doors being shut, fires banked, the kettle set on, the table laid in the old, agreed way. A man who listens to the inward caution—the small voice that says twilight is pooling where it ought not—does what generations have always done: he builds little ramparts of habit. He counts spoons. He sweeps the hearth. He measures out tea leaves as his mother measured them. He keeps names for things, and distinctions between them, because to break down terms is to break down the world.

In his own person, Oleron began to commit that last treason: not by marching into darkness, but by letting it come close enough to be handled, turned, weighed—like a dubious mushroom brought in from the hedge and laid on the kitchen board “just to see.” Two months earlier, had anyone said of his charming, bemusing house, “It is haunted,” the words would have iced his marrow and driven him, with every instinct of self-preservation, out into the ordinary street where a milk-cart rattled and voices rose from shops. Now—his scale of sensation dulled, his palate for dread blunted by repeated sips—he could hear “haunted” and respond, almost with a critic’s curiosity: haunted by what? And he did not perceive that when horror can be made relative, argued over, itemized, it loses its proper quality and becomes merely another subject for the mind to gnaw.

Landmarks were being put aside. Mists—fine, insinuating—wrapped him. And what he felt most strongly, more than fear even, was a ravenous inquisitiveness. He wanted to know. He was resolved to know. Nothing but knowledge would satisfy him; and, with a kind of sly industriousness, he began to cast about for methods.

He might have spared himself the craft. The way opened as easily as a door that has been waiting, oiled, for a touch. As once, in his best days of writing, phrases had risen of themselves, not to be altered afterward—complete, inevitable, as if dictated—so now the very questions he put to himself were answered in the instant of asking. There was exhilaration in it, a swift clean motion like the sure stroke of a knife through an onion, the pieces falling into order. He had known no such joy in his own power since the days when work had tasted fresh in his mouth and his mind had been a daily delight. It was almost as if the course he must pursue were being set for him, line by line.

And first—of course—he must define the problem.

He defined it as if it were mathematics, as if figures might keep the thing from seeping into him. Granted that he did not have the place to himself; granted that the old house had caught and engaged his spirit past any reason; granted that, by virtue of the common denominator of the place, this unknown co-tenant stood in some relation to himself: what next? Clearly the nature of the other numerator must be ascertained. And how?

Ordinarily it would not have seemed simple. To Oleron it was pellucidly clear. The key lay—of course—in his half-written novel, or rather in both Romillys: the old one and the proposed new. A little while before, he would have thought himself mad to embrace such an idea. Now he accepted the dizzying hypothesis without so much as a tremor of protest, the way a man accepts a strange flavor on the tongue once he has eaten it three nights running.

He began to examine the first and second Romillys, and from the moment he did so the thing advanced by leaps and bounds. Swiftly he reviewed the history of the Romilly of the fifteen chapters. It rose before him not in abstract paragraphs, but as a succession of mornings and nights in that house, each with its odor, its temperature, its noises—or their lack.

He remembered clearly now that he had found her insufficient on the very first morning he had sat down to work there: something in her—flat, obedient—had resisted the air of the rooms. Other instances of his aversion sprang up to confirm the obscure inquiry. There had been the night when he had hardly forborne to fling the whole manuscript into the fire and watch it curl like thin rind. And the next morning he had begun to plan the new Romilly.

It had been on that morning, too, that Mrs. Barrett, overhearing him humming a brief phrase—suggested, he supposed, by the maddening drip of a tap the night before—had told him, with her blunt housewife’s certainty, that he was singing an air he had never heard in his life, called “The Beckoning Fair One.”

The Beckoning Fair One.

The words, once more, had their own taste: old metal, watered milk, something sweet gone wrong.

With scarcely a pause he went on. The first Romilly, once thrown over, had been instantly supplanted; the second had fastened herself upon him, clamoring for birth in his brain. And looking back now, he fancied—no, he felt—that there had been passion in the supplanting, hate almost; that more than once a stray thought given to the discarded creation had—astonishing how credible the almost unthinkable had become—had offended the supplanter.

Yet that a malignancy nearly homicidal should be extended to his fiction’s poor mortal prototype—

“Good God!” broke from him, aloud.

At that, his inquiry—so cleanly mathematical a moment before—collapsed into the personal, the mortal, the unmanageably warm. The intrusion of Elsie Bengough into his thought ended abstraction at once. The mere idea of her was fatal to calm analysis. And there was, besides, the memory of Barrett’s letter, and the small scene that had followed it, which still brought heat to his face and a quick contraction to his brow.

For, wisely or not, he had had the argument out at once.

The morning after the letter, striding across the square with his nerves rasped raw, he had bearded Barrett on his own doorstep. Coming back again a few minutes later he had been strongly of opinion that he had only made matters worse. Barrett had been vagueness itself—impossible to challenge into clarity, impossible to browbeat into definition; he had muttered a farrago in which “Certain things… Mrs. Barrett… respectable house… if the cap fits… proceedings that shall be nameless,” recurred like a sour refrain. “Not that I make any charge—” he had ended.

“Charge!” Oleron had cried, the word snapping.

“I ’ave my idears of things, as I don’t doubt you ’ave yours—”

“Ideas—mine!” Oleron had burst out, then dropped his voice as heads appeared at windows like pale faces behind gauze. “Look you here, my man; you’ve an unwholesome mind, which probably you can’t help, but a tongue which you can help—and shall. If there’s a breath of this repeated…”

“I’ll not be talked to on my own doorstep like this by anybody,” Barrett had blustered, swelling himself up with borrowed righteousness.

“You shall, and I’m doing it—”

“Don’t you forget there’s a Gawd above all, who ’as said—”

“You’re a low scandalmonger—”

And so forth, continuing badly what was already badly begun, until Oleron, shaking, had left him there with his sanctimonious mouth working.

After that, looking out of his windows, Oleron had seen Barrett’s face at odd times lifting blinds or peering round curtains, as if he were laying up evidence against him—evidence of what? of who?—for some future day when his mutterings might need a scaffold.

The occurrence made certain minor differences in his domestic arrangements. Barrett’s tongue, he gathered, had already been busy. He was looked at askance by the dwellers of the square—by women pausing with parcels, by men lingering at corners, by children whose quick eyes learn adult judgments before they learn adult words. And he judged it better, until he could find other help, to make his purchases farther afield than the small shops of the neighborhood. He would return, he told himself, to old bachelor habits: a chop, a loaf, tea; a little butter; plain things he could cook without calling in anyone else’s gaze.

Yet even in those errands, the world had changed its sound. The nearer streets had their own steady domestic music—brooms on steps, a door-latch clicked, a butcher’s cleaver, a canary’s thin insistence in an upstairs cage. But the farther he went, the more he heard the harsher edges of the city: carts jolting over stones, iron on iron, a hooter somewhere, the commercial roar that flattened thought. He found himself craving the square’s softer acoustics again, even while dreading what waited inside his own walls.

Meanwhile he was deep in certain rather abstruse investigations, in which it was better he should not be disturbed. He spent hours with the pages of his manuscripts as if they were old recipes, comparing versions, noting substitutions, seeing where a word had been changed as a cook changes herb for herb and alters the entire savor. He watched for what he had not written, what had written through him.

One midday he stood at his window, tired, not very well, and glad that it was not likely he would have to stir out. The weather had broken. The day looked as if it tasted of tin: raw, gusty, damp without the mercy of rain. And then he saw Elsie Bengough crossing the square toward his house.

She fought the wind that set her ample skirts bellying about her opulent figure; her veil spun and streamed behind her like a black ribbon torn loose. In her progress there was something steady, the determination of a woman who has walked to offices and stations in all weathers, carrying her own life in her own hands.

Oleron acted swiftly and instinctively. A sort of panic seized him. She must not set foot in the place.

He snatched up his hat, sprang to the door, and ran down the stairs. As he ran along the alley his eyes went up to the eaves as if something drew them—an absurd, involuntary fear, as if a slate might choose that moment to fall, as if the house itself might fling something down upon her. He reached the gate and met her there before she could lift the latch.

He spoke with curious volubility, words spilling out as if he could drown the moment in them.

“This is really too bad, Elsie! Just as I’m urgently called away. I’m afraid it can’t be helped, and you’ll have to think me an inhospitable beast.”

She asked if he was going to town.

“Yes—yes, to town,” he said too quickly. “I’ve got to call on—on Chambers. You know Chambers, don’t you? No, I remember you don’t; a big man you once saw me with… I ought to have gone yesterday, and—” He grasped at invention as if it were a railing. “And he’s going out of town this afternoon. To Brighton. I had a letter from him this morning.”

He took her arm and led her up the square, talking on, urging her along with his body as much as with his voice. She had to remind him, gently, that his way to town lay in the other direction.

“Of course—how stupid of me!” he laughed a little too loudly. “I’m so used to going the other way with you—of course. It’s the other way to the bus. Will you come along with me? I’m so awfully sorry it’s happened like this…”

They took the street to the bus terminus. This time Elsie bore no signs of those interior struggles he had once watched with such uncomfortable interest. If she detected anything unusual in his manner she made no comment. Her composure, instead of easing him, made him newly uneasy; and as they walked, through silences that were not quarrels and yet not peace, his recklessness ebbed.

By the time they reached the bus terminus, no one seeing the pallid-faced man without an overcoat and the large ample-skirted woman at his side would have supposed that one of them was near to sinking to his knees with thankfulness—thankfulness that he had, as he believed, saved the other from a danger too wildly unthinkable to name aloud.

They mounted to the top of the bus, Oleron protesting that he should not miss his overcoat and that he found the day, if anything, rather oppressively hot. The lie sat on him like greasy food. They took a front seat where the air cut cleanly and the city’s noises came up in layers: hoofs, wheels, shouted bargains, the hiss of breath and steam. Above it, the wind worried at Elsie’s veil; it made a thin, continuous sound like paper being slowly torn.

Now that the meeting had forced itself upon him, he had another thing to say—something he had rehearsed and postponed, because it made demands upon tact and because tact had been leaving him in small, daily increments. He turned it over for several minutes, and then, remembering the success of his sudden call to town, cut his knot with another lie.

“I’m thinking of going away for a little while, Elsie,” he said.

She merely said, “Oh?”

“Somewhere for a change. I need a change. I think I shall go to-morrow, or the day after. Yes—to-morrow, I think.”

“Yes,” she replied.

“I don’t quite know how long I shall be,” he went on. “I shall have to let you know when I am back.”

“Yes, let me know,” she answered, evenly.

The evenness was—for her—suspicious. He felt as if he had bitten on something hard in bread.

“You don’t ask me where I’m going,” he said, attempting, clumsily, to rally her.

She looked straight ahead, past the bus-driver, as if she were listening to something beyond the clatter.

“I know,” she said.

He started. “How—you know?”

“You’re not going anywhere,” she replied.

The words fell between them and lay there, heavy. He found not a word to answer.

It was a minute or so before she continued, in the same controlled voice she had used from the beginning.

“You’re not going anywhere. You weren’t going out this morning. You only came out because I appeared; don’t behave as if we were strangers, Paul.”

A flush rose to his cheeks. He noticed absurdly, irrelevantly, that the wind had put color in her face too—a brisk, vegetative pink like the first cut of rhubarb. It made her look more alive than he felt capable of being. He still said nothing.

“Of course, you ought to go away,” she went on. “I don’t know whether you look at yourself often in the glass, but you’re rather noticeable. Several people have turned to look at you this morning. So, of course, you ought to go away. But you won’t, and I know why.”

He shivered, coughed a little, and then, to stop her, broke into speech with forced hardness.

“Then if you know, there’s no use in continuing this discussion,” he said curtly.

“Not for me, perhaps, but there is for you,” she replied. “Shall I tell you what I know?”

“No,” he said, and his voice rose in spite of himself.

“No?” Her round eyes came earnestly to him.

“No.”

Impatience, old and ugly, stirred. The strain had been too long. Her devotion and fidelity and love—those steady domestic virtues, like bread and salt on a table—plagued him now; they humiliated him and, he thought, humiliated her. It would have been bad enough if he had ever given her cause by word or deed for such a fastening of herself upon him. But that, he told himself with bitterness, was the worst of this kind of life for a woman. Women like Elsie—business women, in and out of offices, always among men—made comradeship into a cover for something else, whether they knew it or not. They accepted an unconventional status, came and went as men did, were honestly taken at their own valuation—and then, after all, it turned into “the other thing” and they fell in love. No wonder there was gossip in shops and squares and public houses; no wonder tongues worked like grinders. In a sense the gossipers were right. Independent, yet not efficient; some graces forgone, yet with all the hunger and need; half sophisticated, yet not wise—he was tired of it all.

And it was time, he thought, he told her so.

“I suppose,” he said tremblingly, looking down between his knees at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else, “I suppose the real trouble is in the life women who earn their own living are obliged to lead.”

He could not tell in what sense she took the lame generality. She merely replied, “I suppose so.”

“It can’t be helped,” he continued, “but you do sacrifice a good deal.”

“A good deal,” she agreed, and after a moment added, “What, for instance?”

“You may or may not be gradually attaining a new status,” he said, pressing on as if the argument were dough he could knead into a shape that would excuse him, “but you’re in a false position to-day.”

“Very likely,” she said. “I hadn’t thought of it much in that light—”

“And,” he went on desperately, “you’re bound to suffer. Your most innocent acts are misunderstood; motives you never dreamed of are attributed to you; and in the end it comes to—” He hesitated, then drove the knife in, “—to the sidelong look and the leer.”

She took his meaning with perfect ease. She shivered a little, and said the name as if it were something tasteless that had got into her mouth.

“Barrett?”

He did not answer; he did not need to.

The bus stopped at a stage, and fresh passengers clambered up the stairs with their parcels and their damp coats, bringing with them gusts of cold air and the reek of wet wool and street-mud. Their voices, loud in the cramped upper deck, made a temporary wall between Oleron and Elsie. When they had settled, she spoke again.

“You’d better get down here and go back, Paul,” she said.

He stared at her.

“I understand perfectly—perfectly,” she continued. “It isn’t Barrett. You’d be able to deal with Barrett. It’s merely convenient for you to say it’s Barrett. I know what it is… but you said I wasn’t to tell you that. Very well. But before you go let me tell you why I came up this morning.”

In a dull tone, as if he were already far away, he asked her why.

She kept her gaze straight before her as she replied, and her voice, though low, had the steadiness of something decided long ago.

“I came to force your hand. Things couldn’t go on as they have been going, you know; and now that’s all over.”

“All over,” he repeated stupidly, as if he had not heard the words in English but in some other dialect whose sense slipped away as soon as it was spoken.

“All over. I want you now to consider yourself, as far as I’m concerned, perfectly free. I make only one reservation.”

He had hardly the spirit to ask what it was.

“If I merely need you,” she said, “please don’t give that a thought; that’s nothing; I shan’t come near for that.” Then she dropped her voice, as if the sentence itself had to be sheltered from the bus’s coarse public sound. “But if you’re in need of me, Paul—I shall know if you are, and you will be—then I shall come at no matter what cost. You understand that?”

He could only groan, and the groan seemed to him like an animal sound.

“So that’s understood,” she concluded. “And I think that’s all. Now go back. I should advise you to walk back, for you’re shivering—good-bye—”

She gave him her hand. It was cold—cold as a spoon left on a stone sill in winter. He took it and let it go. He descended.

On the edge of the kerb, as the bus started again, he turned to look up at her. For the first time in all the years he had known her, she parted from him with no smile and no wave of her long arm. The bus rolled on, carrying her away into the city’s layered roar; and Oleron stood a moment in the street, hearing, beneath all the public noise, the thin private insistence of something he would not name, as unmistakable as a drip in the night.

He stood on the kerb, struck through with misery, and watched her until the last shred of her dress was swallowed by the turning of the street. Yet the instant she was gone the weight in him shifted, as if some hand had slackened a strap round his ribs. Liberty—she had given him that. He had never quite been without it, perhaps, but hair-splitting was for men with leisure. He had it now in the only sense that mattered: he could act.

By the time he had crossed the square the lightness had mounted from relief to a kind of rejoicing, sharp as the first clean breath after smoke. Before he reached his own door he knew what he must do next.

The vicar of the parish lived within ten minutes. Oleron went there with the decision of a man who has chosen his knife and now goes to the pantry to see what meat there is. It was information he wanted—about the old house with the insurance marks, the slanting “TO LET” boards, the air of long-shut cupboards—and the vicar, with his fingers always in the parish’s old registers, seemed the likeliest larder.

But the vicar gave him less than he had hoped. The house was old—no clergyman was needed to taste that in the damp of its very name. It was reputed haunted—Oleron’s attention rose like a dog’s ears at a whistle—but there were few old houses, the vicar said, without some rumour in the mouths of the ignorant, and the deplorable lack of Faith in the modern world did nothing to scald such superstitions away.

For the rest, the vicar spoke with that careful, smoothing manner of a man ladling soup he has not tasted, anxious not to offend whichever palate sits opposite him. Oleron caught it at once and smiled.

“You may leave my nerves out of the question,” he said. “How long has the place been empty?”

“A dozen years, I should say.”

“And the last tenant—did you know him—or her?” Oleron felt a tingling, absurdly physical, as he offered the choice of sex, as if even that syllable might turn a key.

“Him,” said the vicar. “A man. If I remember rightly, his name was Madley; an artist. A great recluse; seldom went out of the place, and—”

He stopped, then pushed on with a small, sudden candour, like someone finally naming an ingredient everyone has been smelling but no one has dared to say aloud.

“And since you appear to have come for this information, and since it is better that the truth should be told than that garbled versions should get about, I don’t mind saying that this man Madley died there, under somewhat unusual circumstances. It was ascertained at the post-mortem that there was not a particle of food in his stomach, although he was found to be not without money. And his frame was simply worn out. Suicide was spoken of, but you’ll agree with me that deliberate starvation is, to say the least, an uncommon form of suicide. An open verdict was returned.”

“Ah!” said Oleron; and the syllable came out as if he had tested something on his tongue and found it bitter, promising. “Does there happen to be any comprehensive history of this parish?”

“No; partial ones only. I myself am not guiltless of having made a number of notes on its purely ecclesiastical history, its registers and so forth, which I shall be happy to show you if you would care to see them; but it is a large parish, I have only one curate, and my leisure, as you will readily understand…”

The parish expanded; the curate diminished; the vicar’s leisure shrank to a bone. The rest of the interview was that, and Oleron, having thanked him, left and walked slowly home.

He walked slowly for a reason. Twice he came within a stone’s throw of his own gate and turned away, taking another circuit of twenty minutes, as if to let some flavour dissipate from his mind. He had a ticklish piece of work before him: to bring his thoughts into such an unpreoccupied, receptive state that he might see the place as he had seen it on that morning when, his removal accomplished, he had sat down to begin the sixteenth chapter of the first Romilly.

If he could recapture that first impression—clean, fresh, unhandled—he now expected far more from it. Formerly he had dragged a whole kitchen of mental lumber about with him: those dreary chapters, those boiled-down pages of stale invention, had sat in him like heavy bread. Before the influence of the place could find him, it had had that inertia to overcome. No results had shown. The process had been slow saturation, charging, filling up to a brim.

But now he was light, unburdened—rid at last both of that Romilly and of her prototype. Now for the new unknown: coy, jealous, bewitching, Beckoning Fair.

At half-past two he put his key into the Yale lock, entered, and closed the door behind him.

His fantastic attempt was instantly, astonishingly successful. He could have shouted with triumph. It was as if he had escaped into the room—as if the outer world with its clangour, its tram-grind and door-slam and advertisement-loud chatter had been a harsh tin basin from which he had stepped into a deeper, kinder vessel. Here the air itself seemed to hold more oxygen. His own specific gravity felt altered; his tread was less ponderable, as though the boards received him with a softer, older knowledge of feet.

The flowers in the bowls, the fair proportions of the meadowsweet-coloured panels and mouldings, the polished floor, and the lofty, faintly starred ceiling fairly laughed their welcome. He laughed back—out loud, shamelessly.

“Oh, you’re pretty, pretty!” he said, flattering it as a man might flatter a host’s table laid with quiet profusion.

Then he lay down on his couch.

He spent that afternoon the way a convalescent might spend it who expects a dear visitor: in delicious vacancy, smiling now and then as if in sleep, and lifting drowsy, contented eyes to those alluring surroundings. The place had its own hush, not the dead hush of a stopped clock, but the living hush of a room that remembers other voices and chooses not to spend them yet.

He lay so until darkness came; and with darkness, the nocturnal noises of the old house—the delicate creaks like old joints, the faint settling of timbers, the far-off shiver of pipes as if the building sighed in its sleep. Those sounds arranged themselves about his mind like cutlery placed in its proper order, each small note taking its place in the acoustic ecology of the place. Outside, modern life made its coarse, indifferent roar; inside, the house spoke in a language of minute rustles and ticks that no street could translate.

But if he waited for any specific happening, he waited in vain.

He waited similarly in vain on the morrow, keeping up, though with less ease, that sensitised-plate condition of mind, ready for the least impression. Nothing came. Whatever it was that he wooed so patiently seemed shy—and exacting.

On the third day he thought he understood. A look of gentle drollery and cunning entered his eyes; he chuckled under his breath, tasting the idea as one tastes a sauce and finds the missing sharpness.

“Oho, oho! Well, if the wind sits in that quarter we must see what else there is to be done. What is there, now?… No, I won’t send for Elsie; we don’t need a wheel to break the butterfly on; we won’t go to those lengths, my butterfly…”

He stood, musing, thumbing his lean jaw, looking aslant; then crossed to the hall, took down his hat, and went out.

“My lady is coquettish, is she?” he chuckled as he went down the stairs. “Well, we’ll see what a little neglect will do.”

He sought a railway station, got into a train, and spent the rest of the day in the country. Hedgerows, earth, the honest clop and whisper of rural sound—wind worrying the grass, a bird’s single note clean as a bell—were a relief after the city’s iron chorus. He let those simpler noises wash the film from his mind. Oh yes: Oleron thought he was the man to deal with Fair Ones who beckoned and invited and then took refuge in shyness and hanging back.

He did not return until after eleven.

“Now, my Fair Beckoner,” he murmured as he walked along the alley and felt in his pocket for his keys.

Inside his flat he was perfectly composed, perfectly deliberate, exceedingly careful not to give himself away. As if to intimate that he intended to retire immediately, he lighted only a single candle; and as he set out with it on his nightly round he affected to yawn.

He went first into his kitchen. A full moon stood outside, and a lozenge of moonlight—almost peacock-blue by contrast with the candle’s warm frame—lay on the floor like a fallen tile. The window was uncurtained. In it he could see the reflection of the candle and, faintly, that of his own face as he moved about. The door of the powder-closet stood a little ajar; he closed it before sitting down to remove his boots on the chair with the cushion made of the folded harp-bag. Leather gave its small, intimate sigh as he pulled; the house answered with its own soft creak, as if marking his care.

From the kitchen he passed to the bathroom. There another slant of blue moonlight cut the windowsill and lay across the pipes on the wall, turning the metal into pale ribs. He visited his seldom-used study and stood for a moment gazing at the silvered roofs across the square, the city’s distant noise pressed flat by the hour.

Then, walking straight through his sitting-room—his stockinged feet making no noise—he entered his bedroom and put the candle on the chest of drawers.

His face all this time wore no expression save tiredness. He had never been wilier, nor more alert.

The small bedroom fireplace was opposite the chest of drawers on which the mirror stood; his bed and the window occupied the remaining sides. Oleron drew down his blind, took off his coat, and then stooped to get his slippers from under the bed.

He could have given no reason for the conviction, but that the manifestation withheld for two days was close at hand he did not doubt for an instant. Nor—though he could not form the faintest guess at the shape it might take—did he feel fear. It might startle; it might surprise; he was prepared for that. But his scale of sensation had been depressed, the way appetite can be dulled by long anticipation.

His hand moved this way and that under the bed.

Then his heart gave a leap and a pause—almost horrid in its sudden arrest. His fingers had found the slippers, but he was still on his knees; and save for that posture he would have fallen. The bed was low; the search accounted for the turned head; and he held the attitude until he had partly recovered himself. When he rose there was a drop of blood on his lower lip where he had bitten it, and his watch had jerked out of his waistcoat pocket and was dangling at the end of its short leather guard, swinging like a small, accusing pendulum.

Before the watch had ceased its oscillation he was himself again.

In the middle of the mantelpiece stood a picture—a portrait of his grandmother. He placed himself before it so that he could see in its glass the steady flame of the candle burning behind him on the chest of drawers. He could see also the little glancings of light from bevels and facets about the mirror and candle—small sparks hopping and keeping their places.

But he could see more.

The twinklings and reflections and re-reflections did not change position. Yet there was one gleam that moved. It was fainter than the rest, and it travelled up and down through the air. It was the reflection of the candle on his black vulcanite comb; and each downward movement was accompanied by a silky, crackling rustle—a sound like dry hair under careful fingers, intimate and domestic, yet made monstrous by the absence of the body it belonged to.

Watching in the glass of his grandmother’s portrait, Oleron continued his part. He felt for his dangling watch and began slowly to wind it. Then, for a moment ceasing to watch, he began to empty his trouser pockets and to place methodically in a little row on the mantelpiece the pennies and halfpennies he took from them. The coins clicked softly—a tame, human sound—while the sweeping, minutely electric noise filled the whole bedroom.

Had he altered his point of observation he could have brought the dim gleam so into position that it would almost have outlined his grandmother’s head. Any other head it might have been following was invisible.

He finished the emptying of his pockets. Then, under cover of another simulated yawn, not so much summoning resolution as overmastered by exorbitant curiosity, he swung suddenly round.

That which was being combed was still not to be seen.

But the comb did not stop.

It had altered its angle a little and moved a little to the left. It was passing, in fairly regular sweeps, from a point rather more than five feet from the ground in a direction roughly vertical, to another point a few inches below the level of the chest of drawers—down, up, down—like a patient hand grooming what could not be fed, could not be reasoned with, could only be attended.

Oleron continued to act to admiration. He walked to the little washstand in the corner, poured out water, and began to wash his hands. The water ran with a thin, cold music; the candle smelt of tallow and wick; the room held the faint odour of old plaster warmed by flame. He removed his waistcoat and continued his preparations for bed, his mind at once sharpened and dulled—sharpened by intent, dulled against shock.

The combing did not cease.

He stood for a moment in thought. Again his eyes twinkled. The next move was very cunning.

“Hm!… I think I’ll read for a quarter of an hour,” he said aloud, pitching his voice in that casual key a man uses when speaking to himself—yet aimed like bait cast into a dark pool.

He passed out of the room. He was away a couple of minutes.

When he returned the room was suddenly quiet.

He glanced at the chest of drawers. The comb lay still between the collar he had removed and a pair of gloves, as harmless as any cheap object bought without thought.

Without hesitation he put out his hand and picked it up. It was an ordinary eighteenpenny comb, taken from a card in a chemist’s shop—of a substance with a definite specific gravity, and no more capable of rebellion against the laws by which it existed than the worlds that keep their orbits through the void.

He set it down again.

Then he glanced at the bundle of papers he held.

What he had gone to fetch were the fifteen chapters of the original Romilly.

“Hm!” he muttered as he threw the manuscript into a chair. “As I thought…. She’s just blindly, ragingly, murderously jealous.”

On the night after that, and on the following night, and for many nights and days—so many that he began to be uncertain about the count—Oleron, courting, cajoling, neglecting, threatening, beseeching, eaten out with unappeased curiosity and careless that his life was becoming one consuming passion and desire, continued his search for the unknown co-numerator of his abode.

As the season wheeled on and the days lengthened, it came about that scarcely any foot but the postman’s found Oleron’s stairs worth the climbing. Even that thin, dutiful tread grew rare; for a man who writes no letters earns few in return, and once or twice a week became the measure of all commerce between his door and the street.

A letter arrived from his publishers, politely hungry for the manuscript of the new book. It lay on the table like an unopened tin whose seam one keeps meaning to prise. He let it wait. He meant to answer after tea, after the next page, after the next hour. Days slid over it; then his mind slipped off it altogether. A second letter came—firmer, still civil—and he failed it too. No third letter followed. The world, affronted, simply turned its face away.

Outside, brightness gathered, and warmth. Privet along the notice-boards burst into white froth; flower-women lined the kerbs in the streets where he still went, at first, to buy necessities. Buckets brimmed with narcissus, tulips like small torches, moon-daisies, lilac that carried its own dusk. Oleron bought flowers daily. His room clamoured for them, as if the very plaster were thirsty for scent; and he did not stint. He learned which stall kept its stems in the coldest water, which woman cut on a slant so the stalks drank better, which bunch had been gathered that morning and not yesterday. He trimmed ends with the care of a cook filleting fish—clean, swift, no bruising. He scalded the jars with kettle-water, scrubbed the green slime from their throats, changed the water before it clouded, and crushed a bit of charcoal for sweetness. Under his hands the bowls became altars of wet, breathing colour.

And yet the necessity of going out to buy them began to irk him more and more. The street, once merely a street, now had the feel of an open oven door: too much air, too much space, a heatless glare that struck at the nerves. Each time he returned home he felt a greater, almost childish relief, as if he had escaped some invisible hand that reached for the nape of the neck from behind.

He became conscious that his whole scale of sensation had subtly altered again—not restored to what it had been, but enlarged, extended, as a palate may grow so keen it begins to taste bitterness in water. Terror had returned, yet in a new guise. Lux orco, tenebrae Jovi. This time its name was agoraphobia: the dread of open air and breadth, of a gap at the back into which something might spring. He began to dread space itself—doorways, crossings, the easy stretch of a pavement without a friendly wall to graze his shoulder. Soon he contrived that food and flowers should be delivered to his door. The moment he struck upon the expedient he rubbed his hands in satisfaction. Better. Much better. Now the outside could be kept at a distance and yet his table need not be bare.

It was quickly confirmed. He took pleasure in remaining immured.

But he was not happy—or if he was, the happiness ran oddly, like a sauce that has split though the flavour remains rich. He fretted. Sometimes he wept quietly for no reason he could name, weak with a softness he despised; and yet, dimly, he knew he would not have traded his sadness for the loud, jangling mirth of the world beyond his windows.

Noise, especially, had become unendurable. It was not merely unpleasant; it pricked and bruised him, as if sound were a grit between skin and cloth. Cartwheels on cobbles, the rasp of a knife-grinder, the shrill commerce of children—each was an assault. He moved from room to room in slippers. He would stand for long seconds with a hand on a door, closing it so gradually that the latch made no click at all. The stillness itself delighted him; it was like the hush in a kitchen after the last pot has been set to soak, when only the faintest settling noises of the house remain—wood contracting, a distant pipe giving a sigh.

Sunday became intolerable. Since the fine weather, a small congregation gathered each Sunday morning in the square under his windows: members of the sect to which the long-nosed Barrett adhered. They came with a great drum and brass-bellied instruments that belched their sound into the air. Men and women uplifted strained, anguished voices as if wrestling the very sky, and Barrett himself, eyes closed, brows working, prayed that the sound of his voice might pierce unbelievers’ ears—as it certainly pierced Oleron’s. There was something obscene in it: not faith, but noise; not devotion, but display. Their hymn was not a song belonging to the place, grown from its speech and soil; it was a crude, imported din, the kind that tramples local cadence flat. It chewed the square’s quieter ecology into pulp—sparrows silenced, ordinary talk driven indoors, even the bell of a distant milk-cart made irrelevant.

One day, in the middle of one of these rhapsodies, Oleron sprang up, yanked down his blind, and heard, as he did so, his own name turned into a fresh torrent of intercession. The syllables of it—his name—sounded wrong in Barrett’s mouth, like a foreign spice used without understanding: harsh, mismeasured, a theft of identity, a dominating lexicon laying claim to him.

Sometimes, without expecting an answer, Oleron stood still and called softly into his rooms. Once or twice he whispered, “Romilly,” and waited; but more often his whisper took no shape of name at all. Names belonged to other things—printed things, spoken things, things of the street. What he waited for had not consented to any such handling.

There was a spot within his abode he began to haunt with increasing persistency: just inside the opening of his bedroom door. He had discovered, one day, that if he opened every door in the place—always excepting the outer one, which he opened only unwillingly—and placed himself precisely there, he could see, more or less, into each of his five rooms without moving. He could command the sitting-room, almost all the bedroom save the portion hidden by the open door itself, and glimpses of kitchen, bathroom, and the rarely used study. He stood there often, breathless, finger upon his lip. And once, standing there, he abruptly wondered whether Madley—of whom the vicar had spoken—had ever discovered the strategic importance of that entry.

Light, too, began to trouble him more than darkness. Direct sunlight, as the sun moved round the house and each room took its turn of glare, was like flame inside his brain; even diffused day was a dull ache, numbing and persistent. He began, hour by hour, to lower his crimson blinds. Each lowering was a short, daring excursion into a room as if into a risky street: he went quickly, backing out in good order, always careful to keep his retreat open. In time it became a daily, methodical exercise. When he had made his circuit, his rooms lay in the blood-red half-light of a photographer’s darkroom. The flowers—tulips, moon-daisies, narcissi—glimmered like pale tongues and eyes in the gloom, and the air grew thick with their breath: green sap, peppery stems, the faint sweetness that turns, if overfed, toward decay.

One day, as he drew down the blind of the little study and backed out again, he broke into a soft laugh.

“That bilks Mr. Barrett!” he murmured.

And the thought of baffling Barrett amused him for a full hour, as if by darkening his rooms he had in some way denied Barrett’s crude trumpetings any purchase upon him.

But soon after, on another day, he suffered a fright that left him trembling almost as long. He had seized the cord to darken the window over the seat where he had found the harp-bag, standing with his back protected by the embrasure, when he thought he saw the tail of a black-and-white checked skirt vanish round the corner of the house.

He could not be sure. Had he run to the other window—which was already blinded—the skirt would have been gone. Yet he was almost sure it was Elsie. He listened in an agony of suspense for her tread on the stairs.

No tread came.

After three or four minutes he drew a long breath, relief loosening him like a knot undone.

“By Jove, but that would have compromised me horribly,” he muttered, and kept muttering, as if the word itself must be chewed to be understood: “Horribly compromising… no woman would stand that… not any kind of woman… oh, compromising in the extreme!”

Yet he was not happy. The quiet weeping that took him sometimes had no assignable cause; it came and went like the intermittent brightening of clouds drifting across the square. Perhaps, after all, if he was not happy, he was not unhappy either. To be unhappy, something must have been withdrawn; and nothing had yet been withdrawn from him, because nothing had yet been granted. He was waiting for that granting in the flower-laden, frightfully enticing apartment of his, the pith-white walls subdued by the crimson blinds into a blood-like gloom.

He paid no heed to the fact that his money ran perilously low, nor to the other fact—that he had ceased to work.

Ceased to work? He had not ceased to work. They knew very little who supposed that Oleron had ceased to work. Only now was he beginning. He was preparing such a work—such a work—such a Mistress was forming in the gestation of his Art—only let this probation and poignant waiting be over and men would see. How should men know her, this Fair One of Oleron’s, until Oleron himself knew her? Lovely radiant creations are not thrown off like How-d’ye-do’s. The men committed to father them must weep wretched tears, as Oleron did; must swell with presumptuous hopes, as Oleron did; must pursue, as Oleron pursued, the capricious, fair, mocking, slippery, eager Spirit that, ever eluding, ever ensures the chase does not slacken. Let him hunt this Huntress a little longer; he would have her sparkling and panting in his arms yet.

If all else was falling away from him, gladly he let it go. So do we all when our Fair Ones beckon. At the beginning we wink and promise ourselves we will put Her Ladyship through her paces—neglect her for a day, turn her jealous wiles against her, flout her when she comes wheedling. Perhaps a heartless sprite in us is never fooled. In the end, all falls away. She beckons and beckons, and all goes.

And so Oleron kept his post within the frame of his bedroom door, watching, waiting, smiling, finger to lip. It was his service, his worship, his troth-plighting, all he had ever known of Love. And when he found himself hating the dead man Madley—wishing he had never lived—he felt that hatred, too, was acceptable service.

But as he prepared himself, as it were, for a Marriage, and moped and chafed more and more that the Bride made no sign, he made a discovery he ought to have made weeks before. The thought of Madley led him to it. Since that night when he had imagined, in his greenness, that a little studied neglect would bring the lovely Beckoner to her knees, and had made use of her jealousy to banish her, he had not set eyes on those fifteen discarded chapters of Romilly. He had tossed them into the window-seat and forgotten their existence.

Now, his jealousy of Madley reminded him of hers—of her jilted rival of flesh and blood—and he remembered.

Fool that he had been. Had he expected his Desire to manifest while evidence of divided allegiance remained? And she with a passion so fierce and centred that it had not hesitated at destruction—twice attempted—of her rival? Fool.

But if that was all the pledge and sacrifice she required, she should have it. Quickly.

He drew the manuscript from the window-seat and brought it to the fire. He kept the fire burning always now; the steady warmth coaxed out the last vestige of flower-odour from the crowded room, lifting it, thick and heady, like steam rising from a pot in which herbs have been bruised. He did not know the time; long since he had let the clock run down. The ticking had begun to seem an insult—a petty measurer of hours set against the stupendous things happening to Oleron. But he knew it was late.

He knelt before the fire with the Romilly manuscript. The sheets were fastened together; his fingers worked at the clasp. He had scarcely begun when he started, turned his head over his shoulder, and listened, spine tightening as if a cold spoon had touched it.

The sound was not loud—no more than a tap, twice or thrice repeated—yet it filled him with alarm. His face darkened as it came again. A voice sounded outside on his landing.

“Paul!… Paul!…”

Elsie.

“Paul!… I know you’re in… I want to see you….”

He cursed under his breath but kept perfectly still. He did not mean to admit her.

“Paul!… You’re in trouble. I believe you’re in danger—at least come to the door!…”

A low laugh rose in him and he smothered it. It amused him that she, in such danger herself, should speak to him of his. If she was in danger, serve her right; she knew—or said she knew—all about it.

“Paul!… Paul!…”

He mimicked her under his breath, a whisper shaped like her voice: “Paul… Paul…”

“Oh, Paul, it’s horrible!”

Horrible, was it? Then let her go away and live.

“I only want to help you, Paul… I didn’t promise not to come if you needed me….”

A pitiful sob broke the low cry. He remained impervious. The devil take the woman. Should he shout at her to go? No. Let her call and knock and sob. She had a gift for sobbing; she mustn’t imagine it could move him. It irritated him; he set his teeth and shook his fist in the direction of the door, as if she could see through wood.

“Paul!… Paul!…”

With his teeth hard set he dropped the first page of Romilly into the fire.

Then another.

Then another.

Sheet by sheet, he fed the flame as if he were salting something down for preservation—methodical, deliberate, each motion a vow. The paper curled, blackened, and flared; the smell was sharp and dry, like scorched flour, like over-toasted crust. For many minutes the calling continued behind the door. Then suddenly it ceased. He heard feet descending the stairs, slowly.

He listened for the noise of a fall, a cry, the crash of splintering rail. None came.

She was spared. Apparently her rival allowed her to crawl away abject and beaten. Oleron heard her steps pass under his window. Then she was gone.

He dropped the last page into the fire. With a low laugh he rose and looked fondly round the room—at the bowls, the jugs, the jars, their wet throats and glimmering heads of bloom under the bloody light.

“Lucky to get away like that,” he remarked softly. “She wouldn’t have got away if I’d given her as much as a word or a look. What devils these women are…! But no; I oughtn’t to say that; one of ’em showed forbearance.”

Who showed forbearance? And what was forborne?

Ah, he knew.

Contempt, perhaps, had been at the bottom of it; but contempt or mercy, it did not matter. The pestering creature had been allowed to go unharmed. Yes—she was lucky. He hoped she knew it, as one knows, in the mouth, the difference between a knife that cuts clean and one that saws.

And now—now—now for his reward.

He crossed the room. All the doors were open. His eyes shone as he placed himself within the frame of his bedroom doorway.

Fool that he had been not to destroy the manuscript sooner.

How, in a houseful of shadows, should he know his own Shadow? How, in a houseful of noises, distinguish the summons he felt to be at hand? Ah—trust him. He would know.

The place was full of jugglery of dim lights: the blind at his elbow that allowed the street-lamp to struggle through; the distant glimpse of greeny-blue moonlight through the kitchen doorway; the sulky glow of the fire under the black ashes of burnt pages; the pallid glimmer of tulips and moon-daisies and narcissi in their vessels. These would not trick his eyes so utterly that he would not know his Own. It was he, not she, who had delayed the shadowy Bridal. He bowed his head a moment in mute acknowledgment, then bent his gaze into the deceiving, puzzling gloom again. He would have called her name had he known it—but now he would not ask her to share even a name with the other. Names were bargains; names were what Barrett threw into the air until they meant nothing.

His own face, within the door’s frame, shone white as narcissus in darkness.

A shadow, light as fleece, seemed to take shape in the kitchen. The low illumination on the blind at his elbow grew dimmer. The fire settled, letting down blackened, charred papers. A flower fell from a bowl and lay indistinct on the floor, as if it had chosen to faint rather than watch. All was still.

Then a stray draught moved through the old house and passed before Oleron’s face, cool and intimate, smelling faintly of extinguished flame and bruised stems.

Suddenly, inclining his head, he withdrew a little from the jamb. The wandering draught moved the door slightly on its hinges. Oleron trembled violently. He stood a moment longer, then put out his hand to the knob and softly drew the bedroom door to. He sat down on the nearest chair and waited, as a man might await the calling of his name that should summon him to some weighty, high, and privy Audience.

No one knows what mercy can do for a hunger that is not in the flesh.

There are illnesses that make a body pale and thin and tremulous, and there are other illnesses—quieter, craftier—that drop the pitch of living itself, so that what was once ordinary becomes intolerable, and what was once sweet turns rancid on the tongue. When a man is altered in that way, the human bond thins to a thread. Friends turn their faces aside, not always from cruelty, but from a terrified thrift: they must keep their own minds from catching. They are not saints with hands full of cures. They do not know how to cast out what has nested. They only know how to bar their own doors.

So the letter slipped beneath the dark threshold and lay where it fell. The telegram never reached a hand. The messenger, whistling as he climbed the stairs—whistling because boys whistle, and because the living city teaches them to fill every quiet with cheap sound—went away again without understanding why, for one heart-stopping instant, a blind had been pinched aside by a single finger and then shoved back with a small, frantic violence.

It was better, the world would have said, that the miserable wrestle with his own shadowed bride in a house where no clean air entered and no honest sun found a seam. Better that he clasp his nightmare to his chest alone. Humanity had other errands, other dinners to cook, other children to tuck in. Let the lost remain lost.

And in that flat, Paul Oleron had already become a place people spoke of in lowered tones—if they spoke of him at all.

He had once been a man particular about sensations. Even in poverty he had taken small, stubborn pleasures: bread from the baker on the corner rather than the grey sliced stuff; coffee ground fresh because stale grounds tasted like old paper; a shaved curl of lemon peel over fish because the oil woke the tongue like a struck bell. Those preferences had been part of his vanity, perhaps, but they were also his tether to the day.

Now his outer landing was littered with parcels he had ordered and then feared to touch—as if the mere act of opening the door would let something rush in on the draught. Packets of provisions. Bundles of flowers. More flowers. Flowers he had wanted, insisted on, demanded, as if their color could feed him. The errand-boys had come, knocked, waited. In time their shoulders had begun to lift in that shrug that ends commerce: there must be some mistake about the order. After a week they stopped.

Inside, in the light that was never properly daylight—red-tinged as though the walls remembered a sunset that had died there long ago—the flowers turned brown. Heads slumped, petals grew papery, stems sweated and then stiffened. The air thickened with the sour-sweet of rot: the smell of a vase forgotten at the back of a room, multiplied until it became a weather, a climate. It was not only flowers. It was vegetable decay in general: green turned to slime, then to dark pulp; water in jars went cloudy; the faint, medicinal tang of wilting leaves rose and rose until it seemed to coat his palate and make him swallow against it.

Oleron lay in bed and let it happen.

His strength had been draining for days, perhaps weeks; time no longer behaved in him like time. Something fastened itself upon his power and sucked steadily, as if it had a mouth set to the very root of will. After the draining came long spells of prostration in which he could do nothing but gaze at the ceiling stained by that reddish twilight, letting whatever fancies drift up have their way with him. Even his strongest memories clung only precariously, like scraps of paper to a wet wall. They slid and fluttered away before he could pin them.

Sometimes a half-memory of a novel he must write teased him—a novel important to write, a promise made, a deadline—and then vanished as if blown out. Sometimes entire novels arrived, perfect and splendid, as if someone else had written them inside his skull while he slept: books established to endure, with sentences that shone like cut glass. And sometimes the memories were absurdly remote and trivial: garrets he had inhabited; landladies whose breath smelled of onions; the shape of a kettle he had once owned; the clatter of carts in a street where he had written his first pages.

He had known a good deal about such things in his time—streets, meals, the way a city’s sound changes from market-morning to nightfall. But all that was past. He had found, at last, a place he did not intend to leave until they fetched him out.

Some might have thought the rooms a little too green-sick: that damp, plantish smell that clings to old plaster and shaded corners. Others might have thought them too redolent of long-dead and morbid things for a living man to be mewed up in. Yet to Oleron it had become irresistible. It had an authority of its own, as if the building were a host and he the guest expected. It had an associate—no, more than an associate; a presence with a claim—and there were delights in it once a man stopped struggling against its inexorable will.

A novel? Somebody ought to write a novel about a place like this. There must be so much to write if one could only get to the bottom of it—if one could only get beneath its everyday crust of kettles, keys, deliveries, and blinds, and into the real pantry where it kept its secrets.

Had it been painted already, perhaps, by a man called Madley who had lived there… yes, Madley. Oleron had not known him; had a strong feeling he would not have liked him; would rather Madley had lived somewhere else; really could not stand the fellow. Hated him, Madley, in fact.

(Aha. That was a joke.)

He seriously doubted whether the man had lived as he ought. Sometimes Oleron imagined himself telling that long-nosed guardian of public morals across the way about him—though the fellow probably knew, and had already made his praying hullabaloos for Madley as well. That was his line. Why, Oleron himself had had a dust-up with him—about something… some girl… Elsie Bengough, her name was.

At that name a pulse of uneasiness went through him, like a sudden wrong note heard under music. Or rather, he was not uneasy about Elsie herself so much as restless about the things she did—chief among them the way she persisted in thrusting herself into his thoughts. Whenever he was quick enough, he sent her packing at once.

She had always been a bore. He had never denied that. But now it had come to the pitch where her very presence in his fancy was inimical to the full enjoyment of certain experiences.

She had no tact. She really ought to have understood that people are not at home to the thoughts of everybody all the time. In mere politeness she should have allowed him seasons wholly to himself. She was monstrously ignorant if she did not know—she appeared not to know—that there are certain special hours when a man’s veins run with fire and daring and power, when… well, when he has a reasonable right to treat folk as he had treated that prying Barrett: to shut them out completely.

But no. Up she popped—the thought of her—and ruined all.

The bright towering fabrics that sometimes rose before him—by whose side even the perfect magical novels were dun and grey—vanished at her intrusion. It was as if fog swallowed a fair-beaming star; as if, at the threshold of some golden portal prepared for him, a pit yawned suddenly; as if a bat-like shadow turned the growing dawn back into mirk.

Therefore he strove to smother even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless there came an occasion when this woman Bengough refused to be suppressed.

Oleron could not have told exactly when it happened. He only knew by the faint glimmer of the street lamp through his blind that it was some time during the night, and that for a long while she had not presented herself. He had no warning. None. She just came—was there.

Strive as he would, he could not shake off the thought of her nor the image of her face. She haunted him.

But for her to come at that moment of all moments…

It was past belief. How she could endure it, he could not conceive—actually to look on, as it were, at the triumph of a Rival.

Good God. It was monstrous.

Tact—reticence—he had never credited her with an overwhelming amount of either; but he had never attributed mere—there was no word for it. Monstrous. Monstrous. Did she intend thenceforward—Good God—to look on?

Anger rushed up to the roots of his hair, hot and sudden as alcohol flaring. “Damnation take her!” he choked.

And then, in the very next moment, heat and resentment turned to a cold sweat of cowering fear.

For something else had lit.

He did not know what he had done, but he knew he had done something—something fatal, irreparable, blasting. Anger he had felt, yes, but not this blaze that flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white infernal light. That appalling flash was not his—not his that open rift of bright and searing Hell.

His had been the hand of a child, preparing a puny blow. But what was this other horrific hand drawn back to strike in the same place? Had he set it in motion? Had he provided the spark that touched off the whole accumulated power of that formidable and relentless house?

He did not know. He only knew that his poor igniting particle was blown out at once, snuffed, and that—oh, impossible—a clinging kiss (how else could he express it?) changed on his very lips to a gnashing and a removal. And for sheer pity at the awful odds he must cry out to the one he had been raging against to guard herself—guard herself—

“Look out!” he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion was instant.

As if a cold slow billow broke over him, he came to with a jolt of miserable clarity. He was lying in his bed. The mist and horror that had enwrapped him for so long had departed like smoke pulled away by a draught. He was Paul Oleron.

And he was sick, naked, helpless—unutterably abandoned and alone.

His faculties, weak as they were, answered at last to his calls. It must have been a hideous nightmare. Yes. He was himself: Paul Oleron, tired novelist, already past the summit of his best work and slipping downhill again empty-handed.

It came to him not in a procession of thoughts but in a single word, enwrapped and complete: failure. He had tried too much. He had over-estimated his strength. He had struck short.

He had missed. And he had missed not one happiness but two: the ease of this world, which men love, and also that other shining prize for which men forgo ease—the snatching and holding and triumphant bearing up aloft that alone justifies the mad adventurer who hazards the enterprise.

And there was no second attempt. Fate has no morrow.

His morrow must be to sit down to profitless, ill-done, unrequired work again, and so on the morrow after that, and after that, for as many morrows as there might be. He lay there, weakly yet sanely considering it.

Since the whole attempt had failed, it was hardly worth considering whether anything could be saved from the wreck. No good would come of that half-finished novel. It had been meant for the autumn. He was under contract. No matter. Better to pay forfeit than to waste what days were left. He was spent. Age was not far off. Wisdom and sadness were the proper paths for what remained.

If only he had chosen the wife, the child, the faithful friend at the fireside, and let others chase their will-o’-the-wisps.

In the meantime, a practical puzzle began to worry him with a steady insistence: why was he so weak? Why did his room smell so overpoweringly of decaying vegetable matter? And why did his hand, straying to his face in the dark, encounter a beard?

“Most extraordinary,” he muttered. “Have I been ill? Am I ill now? And if so, why have they left me alone?… Extraordinary…”

He thought he heard a sound from the kitchen—or the bathroom. He lifted himself a little on his pillow and listened.

Yes. He was not alone then.

It certainly would have been extraordinary to leave him ill and alone. Alone? Oh no. Someone would look after him. He would not be left to shift for himself. If everyone else had forsaken him, he could trust Elsie Bengough—dearest chum—to do that much for him. Bless her faithful heart.

Then a short, stifled, spluttering cry rang sharply out:

“Paul!”

It came from the kitchen.

And in the same moment it flashed upon him—he did not know how—that minutes before another sound, unmarked at the time but now transfixing, had tried to reach his intelligence: the slight touch of metal on metal, exactly like the sound he made when he put his key into the lock.

“Hallo!… Who’s that?” he called sharply from the bed.

No answer.

He called again. “Hallo!… Who’s there?… Who is it?”

This time he was sure he heard noises in the kitchen—soft and heavy, as if someone shifted weight without wishing the floorboards to speak. But the house had its own voice, and it betrayed everything: a small click, a faint scuff, the hush of cloth brushing a chair.

“This is a queer thing altogether,” he muttered. “By Jove, I’m as weak as a kitten too.… Hallo, there! Somebody called, didn’t they?… Elsie! Is that you?”

Then he began to knock with his hand on the wall beside his bed.

“Elsie!… Elsie!… You called, didn’t you?… Please come here, whoever it is!”

A sound as of a closing door. Then silence—thick, listening silence, in which even the distant city seemed to hold its breath. The usual night-noises—pipes, carts, a far-off laugh—were dulled, as if wrapped in damp cloth. Only the street lamp’s faint electrical hum seeped through the blind, that thin modern vibration that never belonged to any natural night.

Oleron began to be alarmed.

“It may be a nurse,” he muttered. “Elsie’d have to get me a nurse, of course. She’d sit with me as long as she could spare the time, brave lass, and she’d get a nurse for the rest.… But it was awfully like her voice.… Elsie, or whoever it is! I can’t make this out at all. I must go and see what’s the matter…”

He put one leg out of bed. The weakness of it startled him; the joint felt as if it belonged to someone else. He reached for the wall to steady himself.

Before he put out the other leg, he stopped—his fingers worrying at the beard on his chin as if it were evidence in a case.

He wondered, suddenly, whether he dared go into the kitchen.

It was such a frightfully long way. In that corridor, in that strip of domestic space that ought to have been no more than lino and skirting-board and the familiar smell of soap, no man knew what might leap and huddle onto his shoulders if he went so far. When a man has an overmastering impulse to get back into bed, he ought to heed it. To ignore it was to invite the thing he feared most: not death, but contact.

Besides, why should he go? What was there to go for? If it was that Bengough creature again, let her look after herself. He was not going to have things cramp themselves on his defenceless back for the sake of such a spoilsport as she. If she was in, let her let herself out again, and the sooner the better for her.

He could not be bothered. He had work to do.

On the morrow he must set about writing a novel with a heroine so winsome, capricious, adorable, jealous, wicked, beautiful, inflaming—and altogether evil—that men should stand amazed.

She was coming over him now. He knew by the alteration of the very air when she was near: the smell of rot thinned, replaced by something faintly sweet and ruinously intimate, like warmed milk left too long in a closed room, like flowers bruised between fingers. The room seemed to listen. The blind hung as if it had weight. And in him rose that soft thrill of bliss that never came unless she was beckoning—beckoning.

He let go the wall and fell back into bed again as—oh, unthinkable—the other half of that kiss that the gnash had interrupted was placed, how else could one convey it, upon his lips, robbing him of very breath.

In the bright June sunlight the square was full, as if a market had been called without any stalls. People stood shoulder to shoulder and looked up at the old red-brick house with the antique insurance marks on its face, and the agents’ notice-boards hanging over the paling like blunt cleavers left out to dry. Even from across the open space one could taste the place in the mind—stale soot, damp lime, and that sickly-sweet rot that clings to bruised petals when they’ve been shut too long from air.

Two constables held the broken gate of the narrow entrance-alley, their boots planted where the cobbles pinched into shadow. The women kept to the outskirts, shifting as though a different angle might make the drawn red blinds tell a different story. Their voices stayed low, a thread of whispering that made its own small weather under the broader sound of the crowd. The children had been put away in houses behind shut doors, not for fear of what they might see so much as for what they might hear—the adult noises that carry into a life and spoil it.

A long-nosed man with a sharp chin had gathered a little ring about him and told his tale again and again until it had the rhythm of a chant. Beside him, a little fat man with wide, frightened eyes kept trying to wedge his own story in, one that hinged on a key, but the crowd’s attention clung to the long-nosed man the way flies cling to syrup.

“…and it was revealed to me that there’d been something that very afternoon,” the long-nosed man insisted, his voice carrying cleanly in the bright air. “I was standing there, where Constable Saunders is—or rather, I was passing about my business, when they came out. There was no deceiving me, oh, no deceiving me! I saw her face….”

“What was it like, Mr. Barrett?” someone asked, leaning in.

“It was like hers whom our Lord said to, ‘Woman, doth any man accuse thee?’—white as paper, and no mistake! Don’t tell me!” Barrett’s nostrils flared with the pleasure of having a horror to serve hot. “And so I walks straight across to Mrs. Barrett, and ‘Jane,’ I says, ‘this must stop, and stop at once; we are commanded to avoid evil,’ I says, ‘and it must come to an end now; let him get help elsewhere.’

“And she says to me, ‘John,’ she says, ‘it’s four-and-sixpence a week’—them was her words.

“‘Jane,’ I says, ‘if it was forty-six thousand pounds it should stop’… and from that day to this she hasn’t set foot inside that gate.”

A hush came, brief as a spoon poised over a mouth. Then somebody, vague and hungry for detail, asked, “Did Mrs. Barrett ever… see anythink, like?”

Barrett turned on him with a righteousness like a lid clamped down. “What Mrs. Barrett saw and Mrs. Barrett didn’t see shall not pass these lips; even as it is written, keep thy tongue from speaking evil.”

Another voice, coarser, pushed through. “He was pretty near canned up in the Waggon and Horses that night, weren’t he, Jim?”

“Yes, ’e ’adn’t ’alf copped it….”

“Not standing treat much, neither; he was in the bar, all on his own….”

“So ’e was; we talked about it….”

The little fat man bobbed forward again, sweating as if he’d run. “She got the key off of me—she ’ad the number of it—she come into my shop of a Tuesday evening….”

No one listened. The crowd liked its stories without responsibility. A heavy labourer, thick in the shoulders, growled, “Shut your heads. She hasn’t been found yet. ’Ere’s the inspectors; we shall know more in a bit.”

Two inspectors had come up, their black coats catching the sun. They spoke to the constables at the gate, low and clipped. The little fat man darted to them at once, trying to pour out his account as if it might wash him clean.

“I remember the number,” he said eagerly, “because of it’s being three one’s and three three’s—111333!”

An inspector put him aside with the back of his hand, not roughly, merely as one moves a chair that is in the way.

“Nobody’s been in?” he asked the constable.

“No, sir.”

“Then you, Brackley, come with us; you, Smith, keep the gate. There’s a squad on its way.”

They went down the alley where the light thinned and the sound changed. The square behind them was a great breathing thing—murmur, shuffle, the occasional sharp laugh—yet in the passage the noises flattened, as if the bricks drank them. Even the inspector’s boots seemed to tread more quietly there, and the constable’s belt-creak sounded loud, indecent.

Inside, the old house took them in without resistance. The wide carved staircase rose in a slow curve, its banister polished by generations of hands, each of them thinking of supper, of rent, of sickness—ordinary burdens carried up and down until they wore the wood smooth. On the landing outside Oleron’s door lay a litter of dead leaves and paper, a kind of dry, tasteless garnish to the threshold.

“This don’t look as if he’d been out much lately,” one inspector muttered, kicking the litter aside with his toe. “I don’t think we need knock—break a pane, Brackley.”

The door had two glazed panels. Glass shattered with a quick, bright note that made the house seem to flinch. Brackley thrust his hand through, his elbow widening the hole, and drew back the latch.

“Faugh!” one of the inspectors choked as the door swung inward. “Let some light and air in, quick. It stinks like a hearse—”

Down in the square, faces tipped up, the people saw the red blinds jerk upward and the windows of the old house flung open. The sudden spilling of daylight looked, from a distance, almost like an act of mercy. A draught moved out into the alley, bringing with it an odor that had been coddled in darkness—sweet and sour at once, like spoiled fruit mashed into old cloth, like flowers left to stew in their own water.

“That’s better,” said the inspector at the window, putting his head out and drawing a deep breath, as though the clean air were a ration. “That seems to be the bedroom in there; will you go in, Simms, while I go over the rest?…”

In the bedroom they had pulled up the blind, too, and the waxy-white man on the bed—emaciated, the skin on his face stretched as if it had been tightened too many times—raised a hand to shield his eyes from the brutal June brightness. He blinked and blinked, unable at first to accept what his senses reported. His ears, so long accustomed to the muffled hush of drawn blinds and closed windows, made the firm tread of policemen in his room sound unreal, like steps in a dream.

They bent over him, their voices loud in the brightened air.

“This woman Bengough… goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough… d’ye hear? Where is she?…”

He shook his head, slow, as if the movement were all he could manage.

“No good, Brackley; get him up; be careful with him; I’ll just shove my head out of the window, I think….”

While they fussed with the man on the bed, the other inspector went through Oleron’s study and found nothing that answered. Dust, paper, the sterile look of rooms abandoned by regular living. He moved on to the kitchen.

There the floor was cumbered ankle-deep with vegetable refuse, as if a whole season’s peelings had been left to sour: limp skins, stalks, bruised scraps, the dulled shine of rot. Dead flowers lay among it—petals blackening, stems collapsed, blossoms flattened like used herbs. The smell had a kitchen’s shape and yet no kitchen’s comfort: not the honest tang of onions browning, nor the yeasty warmth of bread, but an obscene imitation—sweetness turned rancid, greenness turned slime.

The kitchen window had no blind. It looked out into the narrow alley where the blank end of the opposite house pressed close, stealing light the way a larger body steals warmth. The room appeared empty. Still, the inspector’s boot disturbed the floor and he saw, in the rubbish, a shuffling track that had not been made by him—an uneven sweep, as though something had been dragged or had dragged itself. The track led to a cupboard in the corner.

In the upper part of the cupboard door was a square panel that looked as if it slid on runners. The door itself was closed.

He went nearer, put out his hand, slid the hatch along its groove.

And took an involuntary step back.

Framed in the aperture—falling forward a little before it jammed in its frame—was something that, in the dim kitchen light, resembled a large lumpy pudding done up in a pudding-bag of faded browny-red frieze. It had the heavy, sagging weight of a boiled thing taken too soon from its pot. It pressed toward him with a grotesque domestic familiarity, as if the house were offering up a course.

“Ah!” the inspector said, and his voice went thin.

To shove the hatch closed he would have had to thrust that “pudding” back with his hand, and his skin refused the thought. Instead he took the cupboard handle and pulled.

There was weight behind it—so much weight that the door yielded only three or four inches. He peered in, breathing through his mouth. Something inside resisted, packed tight as meat in a mold. He had to put his shoulder to the door to close it again.

When he did, he pinched a triangle of black-and-white check skirt in the gap near the floor, leaving it sticking out like a corner of a tablecloth caught in a drawer.

He went into the small hall.

“All right!” he called.

Upstairs, they had got Oleron into his clothes. He still held his hands as blinkers and his mind swam, slow and cottony. Things happened around him with a briskness he could not join. He could not understand the extraordinary mess of dead flowers that seemed to be everywhere—on sills, in corners, in heaps like spent offerings. He could not understand why there were police officers in his room. He could not understand why one of them was sent for a four-wheeler and a stretcher. And he could not understand what heavy article they seemed to be shifting about in the kitchen—his kitchen—where once, long ago, the ordinary sounds of daily feeding must have lived: a knife against board, a kettle’s breath, the clink of a spoon.

“What’s the matter?” he muttered, his voice thick with sleep, with illness, with days of dimness.

Then he heard the murmur in the square, like surf beyond a wall, and the stopping of a four-wheeler outside. A police officer leaned to him and whispered something; Oleron stared, trying to catch it, and the whisperer suddenly ran off a string of words—something about being “used in evidence against you.”

They lifted Oleron to his feet. His legs seemed not to belong to him. They assisted him toward the door, hands firm on his elbows, careful as if he might break like a brittle biscuit.

He could not understand it at all.

They got him down the stairs and along the alley. The sound of the crowd swelled, then sharpened into confused angry shoutings. Oleron, dragged through the noise, gathered only that a number of people wanted to lynch somebody or other. The air outside tasted of sunlight on stone, of privet, of bodies pressed too close. The voices struck him like thrown things.

His attention fixed, oddly, on the little fat frightened-eyed man who was making a statement while an officer wrote in a notebook. His words came out in jerks, as though he were coughing them up.

“I’d seen her with him… they was often together… she came into my shop and said it was for him… I thought it was all right… 111333 the number was,” the man said, and the numbers sounded, in the heat and uproar, like a charm that failed.

The people were very angry. Police kept them back, arms spread, shoulders braced. One inspector’s voice cut through with a kind of practiced kindness, as if he were soothing a skittish horse.

“Get him into the cab before—before that’s brought out,” he told someone.

Oleron saw the four-wheeler drawn up at the gate. It seemed it was he who was to be put into it. As they lifted him he caught a glimpse past the inspector who tried to stand between him and what waited behind the cab. The inspector was not quick enough.

There was a hooded stretcher.

The angry voices surged like a sea. Something hard—stone or brick—hit the back of the cab with a dull crack. The inspector climbed in after Oleron and stood with his back to the window on the crowd’s side, making his body a shield. The door remained open, as though they waited for the other inspector to come. Through the opening Oleron saw the “To Let” boards among the privet-trees, hatchet-shaped and pale, like a row of cheap gravemarkers. One of them said the key was at Number Six, and the phrase lodged in his mind without meaning, like a scrap of dialect heard in childhood and never translated.

Suddenly the rage of voices hushed.

Along the entrance-alley came shuffling steps. The other inspector appeared at the cab door.

“Right away,” he said to the driver.

He entered, fastened the door, and blocked the second window with his back. Between the two inspectors—between their bodies, their coats smelling of street dust and tobacco and ordinary days—Oleron slept peacefully, as if the noise and the light and the horror had been ladled out and he’d been given only the blank, soft middle of it.

The cab moved down the square. The other vehicle went up the hill.

The mortuary lay that way.
