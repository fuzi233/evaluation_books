Attached to the account that follows, Doctor Hesselius has penned a detailed note, referencing his own treatise on the unsettling phenomena explored within these pages. His essay dissects the subject with characteristic precision and scholarly depth, though he treats it with a chilling clarity that borders on the unnerving. This particular study will appear among the collected volumes of his life’s work—a series as enigmatic as the man himself.

I present this narrative not to dissect its mysteries for the learned, but to unsettle the curious, the unprepared. The woman who recounts these events does so with such exacting detail that I dare not intrude upon her testimony with my own conjectures. Nor will I attempt to condense the Doctor’s dense theorizing, for he himself warns that the matter at hand touches upon "the deepest and most shadowed truths of our twofold nature—and what may lurk between."

Upon discovering these papers, I sought to trace the correspondence once exchanged between Doctor Hesselius and his informant—a woman of sharp intellect and meticulous observation. But time, as it so often does, had claimed her. Whatever secrets she might have added to this tale are lost now, buried with her. What remains is her own account, set down with an almost unsettling thoroughness, as though she feared the story might slip away into the dark if not pinned firmly to the page.

And so, reader, I leave you to it.

In the quiet heart of Styria, where the woods stretch endlessly and the air hums with solitude, our castle rises—a relic of older, darker times. My father, an Englishman by blood though never by residence, found this place in his retirement: a Gothic schloss half-swallowed by forest, its towers clawing at the sky, its moat still as a grave. We are not rich, but here, where gold stretches thin as mist, we live as comfortably as lords.

The world beyond our walls is empty. To the left, the nearest village lies seven miles distant. To the right, the ruins of Karnstein brood in the trees, their dead nobility rotting in the aisles of a roofless church. The legend of their fall is a tale for another night—one better told in the shuddering dark.

Our household is small. My father, kind but aged; myself, then a girl of nineteen; and the two governesses who filled the silence where a mother ought to have been. Madame Perrodon, plump and gentle, had been with me since infancy. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, sharp as a quill, taught me French and the sharp grace of a lady’s posture. Together, we made a strange little chorus of tongues—French, German, and my father’s stubborn English, spoken out of duty to a homeland I’d never seen.

Visitors were rare. A handful of girls my age sometimes came, or neighbors from leagues away, but the schloss was a place of echoes. I was left to my own whims, indulged and unchecked—until the night that changed everything.

I was six years old. The nursery, vast beneath its steep oak beams, was mine alone. That night, I woke to emptiness. No nurse, no maid—only the cold bite of abandonment. I was not a fearful child; I had been spared ghost stories, fairy tales, the whispers that make shadows writhe. But loneliness prickled like a thorn. I drew breath to wail—

—and saw her.

A young woman knelt beside my bed, her hands beneath the covers. Her face was pale, solemn, beautiful. She smiled, and the fear melted. Her fingers brushed my cheek; she lay beside me, pulling me close. I sighed, comforted, and slept.

Then—pain. Twin needles in my breast, sharp as sin. I screamed.

The lady jerked away, her eyes wide, black, *hungry*. She slid from the bed, vanishing beneath it as my cries brought the household running. They soothed me, dismissed it—*a dream, only a dream*—but their faces were bloodless. The housekeeper pressed her palm to the hollow in the mattress. *Still warm.*

They searched. Under the bed, in the cupboards, as if something might still lurk. The doctor came, dosed me with bitter draughts. My father laughed, called it fancy. But I knew. The woman was real.

That same day, an old priest in a black cassock came to pray over me. He clasped my hands, whispered words I would repeat for years: *"Lord, hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus’ sake."* His voice trembled. The women knelt. The light was thin.

And though much of my childhood is lost to shadow, *that* night remains—clear as a wound that never heals.

The tale I am about to share is so strange that you may doubt its truth. Yet I swear upon my honor—every word is real, and I witnessed it all myself.

The evening was warm, the air thick with the scent of blooming lindens as my father invited me to stroll along the forest path before the schloss. The fading sun cast long shadows across the road, painting the world in hues of gold and deepening blue.

“General Spielsdorf will not arrive as soon as I had hoped,” my father said, his voice measured. The General had promised to visit us for several weeks, bringing with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt—a girl I had never met but had long imagined as a delightful companion. The news of his delay struck me like a physical blow.

“When *does* he come?” I asked, unable to hide my disappointment.

“Not until autumn. Two months, at least.” He hesitated. “And perhaps it is for the best. You never knew Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt.”

“Why do you say that?” I pressed, unease threading through me.

“Because she is dead.”

The words landed like a stone in still water. I had known she was unwell—the General had mentioned it weeks prior—but never had I imagined *this*. My father handed me his letter, its contents frantic, ink smudged as though written in haste.

*"I have lost my darling daughter—for as such I loved her. The fiend who entered my home under the guise of friendship has destroyed her. She died in innocence, never knowing the horror that befell her. I curse my blindness, my arrogance. Now, I live only to hunt the monster responsible. Pray for me, dear friend."*

The letter trembled in my hands. Though I had never met Bertha Rheinfeldt, grief tightened my throat.

The sun had set by the time we turned back toward the schloss, the moon rising in its place, bathing the landscape in silver. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us, their voices hushed as they marveled at the night’s eerie beauty.

Then—hooves.

A carriage thundered down the road, horses wild-eyed, their panic driving them toward us at a breakneck pace. The vehicle swayed dangerously, a woman’s scream piercing the air. Before we could react, the team swerved—too late. The carriage struck the roots of an ancient linden, wheels lifting, wood splintering.

I turned away, unable to watch.

When I looked again, chaos reigned. Horses thrashed, men shouted, and amidst it all stood a woman—tall, imperious, clad in black velvet. At her feet lay a girl, pale as the moonlight, unmoving.

My father rushed forward, pressing fingers to the girl’s wrist. “She lives,” he assured the woman, who clasped her hands in desperate relief.

“I cannot delay,” the woman declared, voice sharp with urgency. “I must reach Vienna. But my daughter—she cannot continue with me.”

My heart leapt. “Papa,” I whispered, tugging his sleeve. “Let her stay with us.”

He offered without hesitation. “Madame, entrust her to our care. We will guard her as our own.”

The woman’s gaze flickered—something cold, calculating—before she knelt beside the unconscious girl. A whispered word, a fleeting kiss, and then she was gone, vanishing into her carriage as swiftly as she had arrived.

The girl remained.

And with her, a creeping dread I could not name.

The carriage vanished into the mist-laden woods, the clatter of hooves fading into the thick night air. Only the young lady’s sudden stirring proved the encounter real. She lifted her head, eyes fluttering open, and in a voice soft as a whisper, asked, *“Where is mamma?”*

Madame Perrodon soothed her with gentle reassurances, but the girl’s confusion only deepened. *“Where am I? What is this place?”* Her gaze darted past us, searching. *“I don’t see the carriage—and Matska, where is she?”*

Madame answered as best she could, recounting the accident, the unharmed coachmen, the mother’s swift departure. At the mention of her abandonment, the girl’s face crumpled. Tears spilled silently down her cheeks.

I moved to comfort her, but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine caught my wrist. *“Not yet,”* she murmured. *“Too much attention may overwhelm her.”*

Servants led the girl inside, her steps unsteady. The castle loomed around us, its ancient stones steeped in shadow. Our usual sitting room—a cavern of carved oak and faded tapestries—felt colder than usual tonight. The firelight danced across scenes of forgotten hunts, the figures frozen in eternal revelry.

*“What do you think of our guest?”* I asked once we’d settled.

Madame Perrodon sighed dreamily. *“Exquisite. The most beautiful creature I’ve ever seen—your age, perhaps, and so very gentle.”*

*“And that voice,”* Mademoiselle added, eyes alight. *“Did you notice the woman in the carriage? The one who didn’t step out?”*

We hadn’t.

*“Black as midnight,”* she whispered, *“with a turban and teeth bared like a beast’s. She watched us—grinning, nodding—as if she knew something we didn’t.”*

My father strode in, dismissing the servants as *“hangdog rogues.”* But his smile was uneasy. He’d spoken with the girl’s mother—a woman in black velvet, who’d insisted her daughter was *“delicate but not prone to fits, nor illusions.”*

*“She emphasized *‘secret,’*”* he mused. *“Stared right through me as she said it.”*

By nightfall, the doctor pronounced the girl unharmed. Rest was all she needed.

I found her in the grandest guest chamber, bathed in candlelight, swathed in silk. She turned—and my breath died in my throat.

The one from my childhood nightmares, now smiling at me with eerie recognition.

*“Twelve years ago,”* she whispered, *“I dreamed of you.”*

Her words mirrored my own terror. We had seen each other before—*known* each other—long before this night.

She described my golden hair, my blue eyes, as if she’d touched them yesterday. I recounted my own vision: her dark beauty, the unsettling pull between us.

*“How strange,”* she murmured, fingers brushing mine. *“I feel as though we were always meant to meet.”*

I should have recoiled. Instead, I leaned closer.

*“Stay with me,”* she pleaded as I rose to leave. *“The night is so long.”*

But I left her, locking the door behind me—though I doubted locks would keep her out if she truly wished to enter.

Sleep didn’t come.

When dawn broke, we met again. The horror of our shared vision had faded, replaced by something far more dangerous: fascination.

She was beautiful.

And I was no longer afraid.

She enchanted me in most ways—yet some things unsettled me.

Carmilla stood taller than most women, slender, her movements languid as drifting smoke. No trace of illness marked her, save for that unnatural slowness. Her skin glowed with vitality, her features delicate as if carved by some devoted hand. But it was her eyes that arrested me—dark, depthless, gleaming like wet stones in moonlight. And her hair—God, her hair. Heavy as sin, thick as a curtain of night, spilling over her shoulders in waves of deep brown threaded with gold. I would let it cascade through my fingers, marveling at its weight, its silk, while she reclined in her chair, murmuring in that voice like a secret.

Yet for all her openness in these moments, she was a locked box in other ways. She spoke nothing of her family, her past, her home—only fragments, teasing and vague. A name: Carmilla. A lineage: ancient, noble. A direction: west. No more. No matter how I coaxed, how I swore secrecy, she would not yield. It maddened me. What harm in a name, a place?

Her refusals were never cruel, though. She’d draw me close, her breath at my ear, whispering riddles that curled like fog in my mind:

*"Dearest, your heart bleeds because I wound it. But mine bleeds with yours. This is the way of things—we draw near, we devour, we become. You will know in time. For now, let me keep my secrets, and love me still."*

Then her lips would press to my cheek, soft and burning, and I would feel myself slipping, my will dissolving like sugar in hot tea. A trance. A surrender. And beneath it all, a creeping dread—not of her, but of the thing inside me that thrilled to it.

She was a creature of contradictions. By day, languid, drifting through the halls like a ghost. By night, her eyes burned with a feverish light, her fingers tightening around mine with sudden, desperate strength. Once, as a funeral procession passed—a village girl, dead too young—Carmilla recoiled as if struck, her face twisting into something inhuman.

*"Don’t you hear how it scrapes?"* she hissed, clapping her hands over her ears. *"That hymn—it’s a knife. And why mourn? Death is kinder than they know."*

Then, just as swiftly, the storm passed. She laughed, light as a girl again, as if the darkness had never touched her.

But the shadows gathered. Strange deaths plagued the countryside—girls wasting away, whispering of phantoms at their throats. My father dismissed it as superstition. Carmilla only smiled, her teeth very white.

*"Nature ordains all things,"* she said. *"Life, death… hunger."*

And when the hunchbacked peddler came with his charms and his grinning insinuations—*"A needle’s tooth, my lady, sharp as sin!"*—she recoiled like a thing scalded. For a moment, her mask slipped entirely, fury flashing in her eyes before she smoothed it away.

That night, the doctor came. I caught only scraps of their talk—*hippogriffs, dragons*—but my father’s laughter was uneasy.

And Carmilla, watching from the shadows, said nothing at all.

The evening air was thick with anticipation when the somber-faced son of the portrait restorer arrived from Gratz, his cart laden with two massive crates. Each was filled with paintings, their surfaces dulled by centuries of neglect. The journey had been long—ten leagues through the winding roads—and his arrival stirred a rare excitement in our quiet schloss.

We gathered in the hall, drawn like moths to the flicker of news from the outside world. The crates loomed ominously by the entrance, their contents veiled in dust and shadow. The messenger, weary from his travels, was led away to sup while servants fetched tools—hammers, chisels, screws—all the instruments of revelation.

Carmilla watched with disinterest as the portraits emerged one by one, their faces resurrected from the grime of ages. Most were ancestors of my mother’s Hungarian line, their features softened by time, their stares vacant yet strangely watchful. My father consulted his list, calling out names as the restorer unearthed each relic.

Then, a pause.

“There’s one more,” my father murmured. “In the corner, the name reads ‘Marcia Karnstein,’ dated 1698. I’m curious to see how it has fared.”

I remembered it—a small, unframed panel, so darkened it was nearly illegible. The artist lifted it with reverence, wiping away the last film of decay.

And there she was.

Carmilla.

Not merely a likeness—no, it was her. The same delicate features, the same knowing smile, the same dark eyes that seemed to follow you. Even the faint mole upon her throat was there, as though the painter had captured not just her image, but her very essence.

“An absolute miracle,” I breathed. “Carmilla, it’s you! Look, even the mole—”

My father chuckled, though his gaze slid away too quickly. “A striking resemblance,” he conceded, but his voice lacked the wonder choking my own. He turned back to the restorer, discussing brushstrokes and varnish as though this were any other portrait.

But I could not tear my eyes away.

“May I hang it in my room?” I asked.

“Of course,” my father said, smiling indulgently.

Carmilla herself said nothing. She merely leaned back, her long lashes casting shadows over her cheeks, her lips curled in a slow, secretive smile.

Then I saw it—the inscription, now clear as day. Not *Marcia*.

Beneath it, a coronet. The year 1698.

“I’m descended from the Karnsteins,” I said. “Through my mother.”

Carmilla exhaled, her voice a languid whisper. “As am I. A very ancient line. Are there any Karnsteins left?”

“None,” I replied. “The family was ruined long ago. Their castle lies in ruins not far from here.”

“How… fascinating,” she murmured, though her gaze drifted past me, toward the moonlit courtyard. “Shall we take the air?”

Arm in arm, we stepped outside. The night was cool, the silver light painting everything in ghostly hues.

“You were thinking of the night I came,” she said, her voice barely audible.

“I was.”

“And you are glad?”

“Delighted.”

Her fingers tightened around my waist. “And you asked for the portrait—the one that looks like me—to hang in your chamber.” She sighed, resting her head against my shoulder. “How romantic you are.”

I laughed softly. “You say that as though you’ve known great romance yourself.”

Her breath hitched. “I have loved no one,” she whispered. “And never shall… unless it be you.”

Her lips brushed my neck, her grip suddenly feverish. Her body trembled against mine.

“I live in you,” she gasped. “And you would die for me, I love you so.”

I recoiled.

Her face was blank, her eyes hollow.

“A chill,” she murmured, listless once more. “Let us go inside.”

But as we turned, I caught the flicker of something—fear? Hunger?—in her gaze before it vanished.

That night, as I lay awake, the portrait of Mircalla watched me from the wall.

And I wondered if it had ever truly been *just* a painting.

The drawing room was warm, bathed in the flickering glow of candlelight as we settled with our coffee and chocolate. Carmilla, as usual, abstained, but her mood had lifted—her enigmatic smile returning as Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us for cards. Papa wandered in later, claiming his “dish of tea,” and once the game concluded, he took a seat beside Carmilla on the sofa.

“Have you heard from your mother?” he asked, a thread of unease in his voice.

“No,” she replied softly.

“Do you know where she might be reached?”

A shadow passed over her face. “I cannot say. But I’ve thought of leaving you—you’ve been too kind. I’ve imposed enough. Tomorrow, I should like to take a carriage and seek her out.”

My heart clenched at the thought.

“Nonsense!” Papa protested. “I won’t hear of it. Not without your mother’s express instruction. Besides—” He hesitated. “The reports of this illness plaguing the countryside grow worse by the hour. I wouldn’t dream of sending you away unprotected.”

Carmilla lowered her eyes, that faint, unsettling smile returning. “You are too generous. I’ve never been happier than here, in your home, with your dear daughter.”

Papa, ever the gentleman, kissed her hand. The gesture should have been charming, but something in her stillness made my skin prickle.

Later, in her chamber, I lingered as she readied for bed. The fire cast long, trembling shadows over the walls.

“Will you ever trust me completely?” I asked.

She turned, her smile widening, but silent.

“You won’t answer?” I pressed.

“You have every right to ask,” she murmured. “But I am bound by vows—tighter than any nun’s. Soon, you’ll know everything. And you’ll despise me for it.” Her voice dropped to a whisper. “Love is selfish, darling. The fiercer it burns, the more it consumes. You must come with me, loving me unto death—or hating me, and still coming. There is no middle ground.”

A coldness slithered down my spine. “You’re speaking madness again.”

“Am I?” She laughed, low and musical. “Have you ever been to a ball?”

“No. What does that matter?”

“I remember mine… dimly. Like watching through dark water. Something happened that night. Something that left me wounded.” Her fingers brushed her chest, just above her heart. “A cruel love. Love always demands blood.”

Her eyes gleamed in the half-light, tracking my every movement. I bid her goodnight and fled, my pulse unsteady.

Sleep did not come easily. I had taken to locking my door, infected by Carmilla’s paranoia. That night, I dreamed—or perhaps I did not.

I was in my bed, the candle still burning, yet the room darkened unnaturally. Something moved at the foot of the bed—a shape black as pitch, long and sinuous, like a monstrous cat. It paced, its eyes burning into me. The air thickened. I couldn’t scream.

Then it leaped.

Twin needles of pain pierced my chest. I woke with a gasp—and saw her.

A figure stood at the bedside, draped in shadow, hair spilling like ink over her shoulders. Motionless. Breathless.

As I stared, she drifted toward the door—then through it, though it never opened.

The room was empty. The door remained locked.

I curled into myself, trembling, until dawn crept through the curtains.

Something had visited me that night.

And I feared it was no dream.

Even now, the memory of that night claws at me with icy fingers. It was no fleeting terror, no phantom of a dream to be shaken off at dawn. The horror seeped into the walls, into the very air I breathed, thickening with each passing hour.

I could not bear solitude the next day. I longed to tell my father, but fear held my tongue. Would he laugh, dismissing it as girlish fancy? Or worse—would he see in my trembling the same cursed affliction creeping through our village? He was already unwell; I would not burden him.

Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle Lafontaine, ever kind, noticed my unease. Mademoiselle laughed when I confessed, but Madame’s face darkened.

"By the way," Mademoiselle said, grinning, "the lime tree walk behind Carmilla’s window is haunted!"

"Foolishness," Madame chided, though her voice lacked conviction.

"Martin swears he saw a woman walking there—twice—before sunrise," Mademoiselle insisted.

"Cows in the river fields, nothing more," Madame countered, but her fingers tightened around her shawl.

"Don’t speak of it to Carmilla," I pleaded. "She can see that path from her window. She’s frightened enough as it is."

Carmilla appeared late that morning, her pallor more pronounced. "I had such a fright last night," she murmured. "If not for my charm, I’d have seen something dreadful. A black shape hovered near my bed—I felt it. But the charm drove it away."

I told her of my own ordeal. Her eyes widened. "You didn’t have the charm with you?"

"No. But tonight, I will."

Somehow, I slept. The charm, pinned to my pillow, warded off the unseen. For two nights, my rest was deep, untroubled. Yet each morning, a heaviness clung to me, sweet and sorrowful. A languor draped itself over my limbs, a melancholy I welcomed like an old friend.

Carmilla watched me with feverish devotion. Her gaze lingered too long, her touch too tender. It unsettled me—a flicker of something ravenous beneath her affection.

Then the dreams began.

Cold fingers trailed my skin. Lips pressed to my throat, lingering, hungry. My heart hammered, breath hitching as invisible hands tightened. I woke gasping, drenched in sweat, the echo of a woman’s voice in my ears—*"Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin."*

A light flared.

Carmilla stood at the foot of my bed, her white nightgown drenched in blood.

I screamed.

The next I knew, I was in the hallway, shrieking for help. Madame and Mademoiselle rushed out, their faces pale in the lamplight. We pounded on Carmilla’s door. No answer.

The lock splintered under the servants’ hands.

The room stood pristine, untouched.

But Carmilla was gone.

The sight of the undisturbed room—save for the violence of our entrance—cooled our frantic energy. The chaos had left no mark, no sign of disturbance. Mademoiselle suggested, breathlessly, that Carmilla might have woken in terror at the clamor outside her door and hidden herself in some forgotten corner—a wardrobe, perhaps, or behind the heavy drapes—unable to emerge until the servants had withdrawn.

We called for her again, voices fraying with desperation. No answer came. The windows were fastened; the dressing room door, still locked from within, offered no escape. My pleas turned frantic. *Carmilla, if this is some cruel jest, end it now. Show yourself.* Silence swallowed every word.

She was not here. She could not be. And yet—where else? Had she found one of those lost passages the old housekeeper whispered of, the ones whose secrets had crumbled with time? The night offered no answers. As the clock neared four, I retreated to Madame’s room, the weight of dread pressing close.

Dawn brought no relief. The household stirred in frenzy, my father at its helm. Every stone of the château was turned; the grounds combed. No trace. The river would be dragged next. His distress was palpable—what would he tell her mother? My own grief was quieter, sharper. A different kind of mourning.

By noon, nothing. Then—

I found her.

Standing before her dressing table, still as a portrait. Her finger lifted, beckoning me in silence. Fear lived in her eyes. I rushed to her, laughing, weeping, clutching her to me as if she might dissolve like mist. The bell-rope burned under my frantic tugging—*let them come, let them see she is real.*

“Where *were* you?” My voice cracked. “We’ve been sick with fear—”

Carmilla’s smile was faint. “Last night was… strange.”

“Tell me.”

“I slept as always,” she murmured. “Doors locked—both of them. I woke just now on the dressing room sofa. The door between was open. The other—forced.” Her fingers twisted in her skirts. “How does one sleep through such violence? I wake at a breath. And yet…”

The room filled quickly—Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, servants crowding in with relief and questions. Carmilla offered only bewilderment, her story unchanging. When the others dispersed, my father lingered, thoughtful.

“May I hazard a guess?” he asked gently. She inclined her head.

“When you were young—did you walk in your sleep?”

A pause. “Yes. My nurse told me so.”

His smile was warm with relief. “Then here’s the truth: you rose in your sleep, unlocked the door, took the key. Wandered somewhere in this labyrinth of a house—so many rooms, so many shadows. You returned only after the search, still asleep, and woke where you did, as confused as we.” He laughed. “Would all mysteries unravel so neatly!”

Carmilla’s lashes lowered. My father’s gaze flickered between us.

“I only wish Laura looked half so well,” he sighed.

The terror was over. Carmilla was safe.

And yet—her eyes, when she thought no one saw, held something darker than relief.

Carmilla refused to allow any attendant to sleep inside her room, so my father arranged for a servant to keep watch outside her door—ensuring she could not slip away unnoticed again. The night passed without incident, but the next morning brought an unexpected visitor.

Early, before I had even stirred from bed, the doctor arrived. I had not been told of his summoning, and when Madame led me to the library, I found the elderly physician waiting—the same solemn little man with his white hair and spectacles. His expression darkened as I recounted my recent experiences, the nightmares, the spectral visitations, the inexplicable weakness. He listened intently, his gaze locked onto mine with a mixture of fascination and dread.

When I finished, he leaned against the window recess, silent for a long moment. Then, without a word to me, he requested an audience with my father.

Father entered with his usual cheer, jesting that the doctor would surely call him a fool for dragging him out on a trivial matter. But his smile died when he saw the physician’s grave face. They withdrew into the same recess where I had stood moments before, their voices hushed, their postures tense. Madame and I lingered at the far end of the room, straining uselessly to catch a whisper of their exchange. The thick stone walls swallowed their words whole.

When my father finally turned back to us, his face was ashen. He called me forward, his voice carefully controlled.

"Laura, come here. Madame, you needn’t stay—the doctor has instructions for now."

I approached, my pulse quickening. I did not feel ill, only drained, as though something had been leeching the life from me in slow, insidious drafts.

The doctor’s questions were precise, clinical. He asked about the needle-like pain in my neck, the choking sensation, the cold currents that had shuddered through me. Then, with unsettling focus, he directed my father to examine a spot just below my throat.

My collar was adjusted slightly. A breath of cold air brushed my skin.

"God above," my father whispered.

I twisted, trying to see. "What is it?"

"A mark," the doctor said, his voice low. "A small blue spot. No larger than the tip of your finger."

Fear coiled in my stomach. "Is it dangerous?"

The doctor hesitated. "Not if treated. But precautions must be taken."

Precautions. The word hung in the air, heavy with unspoken meaning. Madame was instructed never to leave me alone—not for a moment. The insistence of it prickled the back of my neck.

Father then mentioned another patient—Carmilla. Her symptoms, he said, were milder but similar. The doctor agreed to return that evening to examine her.

They left together, pacing the grounds in deep discussion. I watched from the window as the doctor rode away, his figure swallowed by the dark fringe of the forest.

Madame and I exchanged uneasy glances. She suspected a sudden fit might strike me, some violent seizure requiring immediate aid. I hoped it was merely an overabundance of caution—a fear of fainting spells or some girlish ailment.

Father returned later with a letter in hand, his mood altered. General Spielsdorf was coming, though the news brought no joy to my father’s face. If anything, he seemed to resent the timing.

"Papa," I pressed, "what did the doctor say is wrong with me?"

"Nothing you need fret over," he replied, sharper than I’d ever heard him. Then, softening, he added, "You’ll know soon enough. For now, trust me."

Before I could press further, he announced we would ride to Karnstein at noon—Madame and I accompanying him, while Carmilla would follow later with Mademoiselle for a picnic amidst the ruins.

The journey was beautiful, the road winding through untamed woods and rolling hills. But beauty could not distract me from the unease gnawing at my thoughts.

Then, rounding a bend, we met the General himself on horseback. After courteous greetings, he joined us in the carriage, his servant leading his horse ahead.

And though the sun shone bright above us, I could not shake the chill that had settled deep in my bones.

The General had aged a decade in ten months.

The robust, jovial man we once knew had withered—his frame gaunt, his face carved with deep lines of grief and something darker. His piercing blue eyes, once warm with camaraderie, now burned with a cold, relentless fire beneath his shaggy brows. This was not merely sorrow. This was fury, honed sharp by horrors unseen.

As our carriage rolled on, he spoke of his loss—his beloved niece, his ward—with a voice like gravel and venom. He cursed the "hellish arts" that had claimed her, his words trembling not with pious resignation, but with outrage at a heaven that allowed such evil to thrive.

My father, sensing the depth of his torment, pressed gently. "Tell me what happened. I would hear it, if you can bear to speak of it."

The General’s laugh was hollow. "You would not believe me."

"Why not?"

"Because you cling to your rational world," he snapped. "As I once did. But I have seen what cannot be explained. I have been *played* by forces beyond mortal understanding."

Though my father nodded, I caught the flicker in his gaze—doubt, perhaps, or the first chill of dread. The General did not notice. His eyes were fixed on the skeletal trees clawing at the horizon.

"You travel to Karnstein?" he asked abruptly.

"Yes," my father replied. "The ruins are fascinating. The tombs there—"

"I care nothing for titles or land," the General interrupted, his voice a growl. "I mean to dig up the dead. To purge the earth of what should not walk upon it."

A silence followed. My father’s face paled.

"The Karnsteins have been dust for a century," he said carefully. "The village is abandoned. There is nothing left."

The General’s smile was ghastly. "You are wrong. There is *everything* left." He leaned closer, the scent of gunpowder and sweat clinging to him. "Three months ago, my child was radiant. Alive. And now? She is gone—taken by things that hunger in the dark. And I will see them burn."

The carriage jolted as we reached the fork in the road—one path leading back to Drunstall, the other winding toward the ruins.

"How much farther?" the General demanded, his fingers tightening around the hilt of his sword.

"Half a league," my father murmured.

"Good." The General exhaled, his breath ragged. "Then listen closely. For what I have to tell you will haunt your nights long after I am gone."

“With all my heart,” said the General, his voice heavy with reluctance. After a weighted pause, he began his tale—one so strange it would haunt me long after the telling.

“My daughter had been eagerly anticipating her visit to your home, to keep company with your charming girl.” Here, he offered me a bow, gallant yet shadowed by grief. “But before that could come to pass, we received an invitation from my old friend, the Count Carlsfeld. His schloss lay six leagues beyond Karnstein, and he was hosting a series of lavish fetes in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles.”

“Ah, yes,” my father murmured. “I recall hearing of their grandeur.”

“Princely does not begin to describe it,” the General replied. “The Count wielded wealth like a sorcerer’s spell. The night that marked the beginning of my sorrow was given over to a masquerade of unparalleled extravagance. The grounds were transformed—trees strung with colored lamps, the sky ablaze with fireworks that would shame Paris herself. And the music…” He trailed off, a shudder passing through him. “Such music. The finest orchestra in Europe, voices plucked from the grand operas, singing as if to lure souls from their bodies. One moment, you wandered beneath the moonlit château, its windows bleeding rosy light onto the terraces; the next, those unearthly voices would rise from the darkness of a grove or drift across the lake like spirits. It was as though I had slipped back into some long-lost romance of my youth.”

When the fireworks faded and the ball began, we retreated to the grand salons thrown open for the dancers. A masked ball is always a spectacle, but this—this was something other. Aristocracy filled every corner; I was nearly the only man present without title or pedigree.

My daughter stood out among them, radiant, her face bare. Excitement had lent her an almost unearthly glow. Yet as I watched her, I noticed another—a young woman in sumptuous attire, her features hidden behind a mask, studying my ward with unsettling intensity. I had glimpsed her earlier in the evening, first in the hall, then lingering near us on the terrace. A stately older woman, also masked, accompanied her, every inch the noble chaperone. Had the girl’s face been uncovered, I might have been certain of her fixation. Now, I have no doubt.

We had moved into one of the salons. My daughter, having danced herself breathless, rested near the door while I stood close by. The two women approached. The younger took the seat beside my ward, while her companion positioned herself near me and spoke to her charge in hushed tones.

Then, with the boldness the mask permitted, she turned to me. Addressing me by name, as though we were old friends, she wove a conversation designed to ensnare my curiosity. She spoke of courtly gatherings, of long-forgotten encounters, resurrecting memories I had not stirred in years. Each allusion was precise, each reference unsettling in its accuracy.

I grew desperate to uncover her identity. She deflected with practiced ease, delighting in my frustration. Meanwhile, the younger woman—whom her companion called *Millarca*—had struck up a conversation with my daughter.

She introduced herself as the daughter of an old acquaintance of mine. The mask, she claimed, made audacity charming. She praised my child’s beauty, her wit, laughing with her over the absurdities of the other guests. Before long, they were fast friends, and the stranger—yielding to some unspoken impulse—lowered her mask.

Her face was exquisite. Neither I nor my daughter had ever seen her before, yet there was something mesmerizing in her features, an allure impossible to resist. My poor girl was enchanted. And the stranger—she seemed equally captivated, her gaze lingering with an intensity that bordered on hunger.

Taking advantage of the masquerade’s liberties, I pressed the elder woman further.

“You’ve baffled me completely,” I admitted with a strained laugh. “Will you not relent? Remove your mask and grant me the pleasure of knowing you properly?”

Her reply was coy. “What a request! Would you ask a lady to surrender her advantage? Besides, time alters us all. You might not recognize me.”

“Philosophically true,” I conceded. “But I would risk it.”

She laughed, evading again. “Millarca is my daughter. That alone should tell you I am no longer young.”

“Your figure betrays you,” I countered.

Before she could reply, a gentleman approached—dressed in black, his bearing aristocratic, his face a deathly white that chilled me. He wore no mask, only the severe elegance of evening attire. With a bow both graceful and hollow, he addressed her:

“Madame la Comtesse, might I have a word?”

Her reaction was immediate—a finger pressed to her lips in warning. Then, to me: “Hold my place, General. I shall return shortly.”

With that, she stepped aside with the pale stranger. Their conversation was hushed, urgent. After a moment, they vanished into the crowd.

I spent the interval wrestling with the mystery of her identity, turning over every half-remembered name. I considered joining my daughter and Millarca, hoping to extract some clue before the Countess’s return. But then she reappeared, the pale man at her side.

“I will inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage arrives,” he said, then withdrew like a shadow retreating from light.

The Countess’s voice was low, urgent, as she leaned toward me. “We must part, but only briefly—I hope no more than a few hours.” Her gloved fingers tightened around her fan. “Though it may stretch to weeks. His words to me just now were… unfortunate. You do not recognize me, do you?”

I confessed I did not.

A shadow of a smile flickered across her lips. “You shall know me in time, but not yet. We are older friends than you realize.” She glanced over her shoulder, then back at me. “In three weeks, I will pass by your estate—I have already inquired after it. I will visit you then, if only for an hour, to renew what was once dear to me. But now—” Her voice faltered. “Now I must leave at once. A matter of life and death calls me away, and I must travel swiftly, by obscure roads.”

She hesitated, then spoke carefully. “I would not burden you, but my daughter has not fully recovered from a riding accident. The physician insists she must not endure another journey so soon. We came here in easy stages—six leagues a day at most. But I cannot take her with me now. I must ride hard, through day and night.”

Her request, when it came, was delivered not as a plea, but as a command wrapped in velvet politeness. She asked—no, *instructed*—me to take charge of her daughter in her absence.

It was audacious. Absurd. Yet before I could refuse, my own child was at my side, whispering eagerly, “Oh, *please*, let Millarca stay with us!”

The Countess’s daughter—Millarca—stood silent, her dark eyes fixed on mine. There was something unsettling in her gaze, something that made my skin prickle even as I found myself agreeing.

The Countess kissed her daughter swiftly, murmured words I could not hear, and then—with a final, lingering look—she was gone, swept away by the pale gentleman in black.

We watched from the window as her carriage vanished into the night.

“She didn’t look back,” Millarca said softly.

I tried to reassure her. “She may not have known you were watching.”

But the girl only sighed, her fingers curling into fists at her sides.

The ball wore on. Dawn approached. And somewhere in the crush of dancers and the flicker of candlelight, Millarca slipped away.

We searched. Of course we searched. But she was nowhere to be found.

It was not until the next afternoon that a servant brought word—a young lady, distressed, asking after us.

Relief turned to unease as she reappeared, spinning a tale of exhaustion and misdirection. She had slept, she claimed, in a stranger’s room, lost in the labyrinth of the castle.

I should have sent her away then.

But I did not.

And so Millarca came home with us.

A decision I would soon regret.

The idyllic days with Millarca soon darkened. She claimed exhaustion—lingering weakness from her illness—and rarely left her chamber before late afternoon. Yet whispers followed her. Though she locked her door from within, servants swore she vanished at odd hours: dawn’s first light, twilight’s hush. Through the schloss windows, figures glimpsed her drifting eastward among the trees, pale as mist, eyes vacant as a sleeper’s. A somnambulist, I reasoned. But how? Doors barred, windows sealed.

Then, worse. My child withered. Nightmares first—visions of a specter flickering between woman and beast at her bedside. Then sensations: the chill of phantom water against her skin, the stab of invisible needles below her throat. Finally, the slow crush of an unseen hand at her neck, stealing breath, stealing consciousness.

The General’s words slithered into my bones as our carriage rolled over the overgrown path, the dead village rising ahead, its roofless cottages gaping like skulls. Every symptom he described—*my* symptoms. Every eerie habit—*Carmilla’s*. The forest peeled back, revealing the carcass of Karnstein Castle, its towers clawing at the sky, ancient trees choking its corpse.

“A cursed lineage,” the General muttered, staring through the broken windows at the sea of pines. “Their sins fester beyond the grave.” He pointed to a crumbling chapel below, half-swallowed by roots. “There. And listen—the woodman’s axe.” Its steady *thunk* punctuated his words. “Perhaps he knows where they buried *her*.”

My father stiffened. “Mircalla? She’s been dust for a hundred years.”

The General’s smile was a razor. “Not as dead as you’d hope.”

Beneath the chapel’s arch, his voice dropped to a snarl. “One purpose remains: to sever her head from her shoulders.” His fist clenched, trembling as though gripping an unseen blade. “Axe, spade—I’ll use my teeth if I must.”

The woodman, gnarled as the oaks he felled, could offer little—but spoke of an elder, a keeper of Karnstein secrets. As he left to fetch him, the old man’s tale unspooled: a village slaughtered by revenants, a Moravian hunter who lured a vampire to the chapel’s spire, split its skull, and burned its remains.

“And Mircalla’s tomb?” the General pressed.

The woodman shrugged. “Gone. Forgotten. If her bones were moved, none remember where.”

With that, he vanished into the trees, leaving us in the chapel’s throat, the General’s vengeance simmering in the silence.

My beloved child was slipping away from me. The physician who attended her could do nothing—his remedies were useless against the unseen force that drained her vitality. Desperate, I summoned another doctor from Gratz, a man renowned for his wisdom and piety.

When they met in my library, their voices rose in sharp debate. I listened from the next room, my heart pounding, until their argument grew so heated I could bear it no longer. I entered to find the old physician from Gratz standing firm, while the other laughed in derision.

*"Monsieur,"* said my first physician with a sneer, *"my learned colleague seems to think you require an exorcist, not a doctor."*

The old man’s face darkened. *"I shall explain my views in my own time. For now, I confess, my science is powerless."* He sat at the table and began to write. As I turned to leave, the other doctor tapped his temple, as if to say the man was mad.

The consultation left me in despair. I wandered the grounds, my thoughts a storm of dread, until the old physician found me again.

*"Forgive my intrusion,"* he said, *"but I cannot leave without warning you. Her death is near—perhaps a day, no more. If the attack is stopped now, she may yet recover. But delay, and all is lost."*

*"What attack?"* I demanded.

He pressed a letter into my hand. *"Read this only in the presence of a priest. If none can be found, then—and only then—read it alone."*

Before he left, he urged me to seek a man versed in the very horror I would soon face.

I waited for the priest, but he did not come. Alone, I broke the seal.

The words were madness. *A vampire.* The punctures at her throat—the teeth marks, the livid stain of the demon’s kiss—all matched the signs. I scoffed, yet what choice had I? I would try anything to save her.

That night, I hid in the dressing room, my sword ready. A candle burned in her chamber as she slept. Then, just past one, I saw it—a black mass creeping over the bed, swelling at her throat.

For a heartbeat, I was frozen. Then I lunged, sword in hand. The thing shrank back, slithering to the floor. And there, in the gloom, stood Millarca—her eyes burning with feral malice. I struck, but she vanished. My blade shattered against the door.

The house erupted in chaos. The specter was gone, but my poor girl was beyond saving. By dawn, she was dead.

The General’s voice trembled as he finished his tale. My father wandered among the tombstones, reading inscriptions, while the old man leaned against the wall, his face wet with tears.

Then I heard them—Carmilla and Madame, their voices floating toward us before fading again. The silence that followed was worse. The air thickened with dread, the gravestones looming like sentinels of the dead.

A shadow moved beneath the archway—Carmilla, her smile as enchanting as ever. I nearly called out to her when the General let out a strangled cry. He snatched up a hatchet and charged.

Her face twisted into something inhuman. She recoiled, but he swung with all his strength. She dodged, seized his wrist—and then she was gone. The hatchet clattered to the ground. The General swayed, his face ashen.

Madame’s voice cut through the silence. *"Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?"*

*"I—I don’t know,"* I stammered. *"She was just here—"*

But Madame had been waiting in the passage. Carmilla had never passed her.

The General turned to me, his voice hollow. *"Carmilla... that is Millarca. Once, she was Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Leave this cursed place. Go to the clergyman’s house and wait. Do not look for her. You will not find her here."*

A figure emerged from the chapel’s shadowed archway—a man so peculiar he seemed plucked from some fevered nightmare. Tall and stooped, his narrow frame draped in black, he moved with an unsteady shuffle, his grizzled hair hanging like cobwebs over his shoulders. A broad-brimmed hat cast his face into deeper furrows, and behind gold spectacles, his eyes flickered with an unsettling mirth, as though privy to some ghastly joke. His hands, swallowed by oversized gloves, twitched and gestured as if conducting unseen horrors.

“The very man!” The General strode forward, relief sharp in his voice. “Baron, you cannot imagine my gratitude.”

Introductions were made—this skeletal stranger was the Baron Vordenburg, a scholar of grim repute. From his coat, he produced a roll of parchment, its surface marked with frantic lines. A plan of the chapel, I realized, as his bony finger traced invisible paths. He spoke in hushed tones, consulting a tattered book whose yellowed pages whispered of forgotten terrors.

Together, they paced the aisle, their boots echoing like distant heartbeats. Then they halted before a section of the wall, clawing away ivy, rapping plaster with their canes. Beneath the grime, a slab of marble emerged, its inscription worn but legible: *Mircalla, Countess Karnstein*.

The General’s face twisted into something between triumph and dread. “Tomorrow,” he murmured, “the commissioner arrives. The Inquisition shall begin.”

My father drew the Baron aside. I watched their glances dart toward me, their words swallowed by the chapel’s hollow silence. When my father returned, his kisses were too fervent, his grip too tight. “We must fetch the priest,” he said. “He will accompany us home.”

Fatigue dragged at me as we departed, but my weariness curdled into ice when we reached the schloss. Carmilla was gone. No explanation was given—only the weight of my father’s secrecy, thick as a shroud.

That night, madness ruled. Two servants and Madame Perrodon kept vigil in my chamber, while the priest and my father stood guard in the dressing room. Candles guttered as the cleric muttered rites I did not understand. Sleep, when it came, was mercifully dreamless.

Days later, the truth unveiled itself in the chapel’s ghastly theater. Mircalla’s grave was pried open. There she lay—Carmilla, her lips flushed, her chest faintly rising. No stench of decay, only the obscene mimicry of life. The doctors confirmed it: breath, heartbeat, limbs pliant as a sleeper’s. And the coffin—ah, the coffin—swimming in blood, her body adrift in its crimson tide.

The stake came down. Her scream was human.

Then the blade. Then the fire.

The ashes were scattered, the curse broken.

My father keeps the Commission’s report, its ink and seals a testament to the nightmare. From those pages, I have drawn this account.

Believe it or not. The dead, it seems, do not always stay buried.

You might imagine I recount these horrors with steady hands and a calm heart. You would be wrong. Even now, the mere act of setting these words to paper sends a tremor through me, as if the past has sunk its teeth into my nerves and refuses to let go. Only your insistence—your relentless, pleading curiosity—could have compelled me to relive this nightmare. Years have passed since my deliverance, yet the shadow of those days clings to me, turning solitude into a waking dread and the dark into something unspeakable.

But before I lay down my pen, allow me to speak of Baron Vordenburg, that odd and learned man whose knowledge unearthed the truth of Countess Mircalla’s cursed grave. He lived in Gratz then, a pauper by necessity, the last remnant of his family’s once-great fortune. What little he had, he poured into his obsession: the study of vampirism. His shelves groaned under the weight of ancient tomes—*Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*—each a thread in the grotesque tapestry of the undead. From these, he distilled laws that govern their kind: some absolute, others flickering into force under certain conditions.

Do not believe the tales of pallid, bloodless specters. The vampire does not skulk like a corpse. No—it walks among the living, flushed with stolen vitality, its lips red, its gaze bright. Only in the grave does the truth reveal itself: the body pliant, the eyes wide and glistening, the mouth stained as if fresh from a feast. And yet, how they come and go without disturbing earth or coffin remains a mystery even Vordenburg could not unravel.

Their existence is a perverse duality—daylight hours spent in the false death of the tomb, nights animated by an insatiable hunger. They fixate upon their prey with a lover’s intensity, stalking with inhuman patience. Some they court, drawing out their torment like connoisseurs of suffering. Others they take swiftly, brutally, draining them in a single, ravenous night.

Carmilla—Mircalla—Millarca—she was bound by a singular rule: her name, an unbroken cipher. Alter a single letter, and the spell unravels. My father shared with Vordenburg the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at Karnstein, and in return, the Baron revealed his own grim discovery. His ancestor, another Vordenburg, had loved Mircalla in life. Her death, and the horror that followed, drove him to hide her remains, to spare her the final desecration of a stake through the heart. But guilt followed him into old age, and he left behind clues—notes, tracings—that led us, too late for so many, back to her resting place.

One last thing he told us: the vampire’s touch lingers. Its grip is iron, yes, but worse is the numbness it leaves behind, a cold that seeps into the bone and never fully fades.

The spring after Carmilla’s destruction, my father took me to Italy. For over a year, we wandered, as if distance could scour the terror from my mind. It did not. Even now, she visits me—sometimes the languid, laughing girl who leaned too close, sometimes the thing I saw writhing in the ruined church. And sometimes, in the quiet of evening, I startle at nothing, certain I hear her light step at the door.

She is gone.

But not gone enough.
