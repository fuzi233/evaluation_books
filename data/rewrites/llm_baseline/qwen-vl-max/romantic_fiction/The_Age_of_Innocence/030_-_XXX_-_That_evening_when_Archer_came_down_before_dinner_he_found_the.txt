That evening, when Archer descended the stairs before dinner, he found the drawing-room empty. He and May were dining alone—every family engagement having been postponed since Mrs. Manson Mingott’s illness—and though May was usually the more punctual of the two, he was surprised she hadn’t arrived first. He knew she was home; during his dressing, he had heard her moving quietly in her room. Yet something had detained her. He had grown accustomed to such small mysteries, using them as anchors to keep his thoughts grounded in reality. Sometimes, he felt he understood his father-in-law’s obsession with trifles: perhaps Mr. Welland, long ago, had known dreams too vivid to bear, and so built a fortress of routine to shield himself from them.

When May finally appeared, she looked weary. She wore the low-necked, tightly laced dress required by Mingott tradition—even for informal evenings—and her fair hair was coiled into its usual elaborate style. But her face was pale, almost washed out. Still, she greeted him with her familiar warmth, her eyes holding that same bright blue sparkle from the day before.

“What happened to you, dear?” she asked. “I was waiting at Granny’s. Ellen came alone and said she’d dropped you on the way because you had urgent business. Is everything all right?”

“Just some letters I forgot,” he replied. “Wanted to send them off before dinner.”

“Oh,” she said softly, then after a pause, “I’m sorry you didn’t come to Granny’s—unless they really were urgent.”

“They were,” he answered, startled by her concern. “Besides, I didn’t know you were there.”

She turned toward the mirror above the mantel, lifting one arm to adjust a loose puff in her hair. As she stood there, Archer noticed a stiffness in her posture, a kind of quiet fatigue. Was the endless rhythm of their life beginning to weigh on her too? Then he remembered: earlier that morning, as he left the house, she had called down the stairs that she would meet him at her grandmother’s so they could return together. He had cheerfully replied, “Yes!” but once absorbed in his own thoughts, he had forgotten entirely.

Now, guilt pricked him—but also irritation. How could such a small oversight be held against him after nearly two years of marriage? He was tired of living in a perpetual, tepid honeymoon—no fire, no passion, yet all the expectations of love. If May had voiced her frustrations (and he suspected she harbored many), he might have laughed them away. But she had been trained to hide pain behind a smile, to wear sorrow like a well-tailored dress.

To mask his annoyance, he asked about her grandmother.

“She’s still improving,” May said, “but she was quite upset by the latest news about the Beauforts.”

“The Beauforts?” he echoed.

“It seems they’re staying in New York. He’s going into insurance or something. They’re looking for a small house.”

The absurdity of it needed no comment. They went in to dinner.

Their conversation remained within its usual narrow circle. But Archer noticed May made no mention of Madame Olenska—or of old Catherine’s reception of her. He was grateful, yet oddly uneasy. After dinner, they moved to the library for coffee. Archer lit a cigar and pulled down a volume of Michelet. Since May had begun asking him to read aloud whenever he picked up poetry—something he now found stifling—he had taken to history instead. Not that he disliked his voice, but he could predict her reactions: thoughtful, sometimes critical, always dissecting what he read. During their engagement, she had simply reflected his opinions back to him. Now, without his guidance, she offered her own—often ruining the pleasure of the work itself.

Seeing he’d chosen history, she fetched her embroidery basket, pulled an armchair close to the green-shaded lamp, and uncovered a cushion she was stitching for his sofa. She wasn’t skilled with needlework—her hands were strong, meant for riding and rowing—but she did it anyway. Other wives embroidered cushions; she wouldn’t be left behind in the ritual of devotion.

As she sat, her head bent over the frame, her sleeves slipping back from her firm arms, the betrothal sapphire glinting beside her gold wedding band, Archer watched her in silence. The lamplight caught her clear brow, and suddenly he felt a pang of dread: he would always know what lay beneath it. Never again would she surprise him—with a new thought, a hidden emotion, a flicker of rebellion. Her romance had been spent in their brief courtship; now it was gone, used up. She was becoming her mother—quiet, dutiful, composed—and in doing so, somehow trying to turn him into Mr. Welland.

He set the book aside and rose abruptly. At once, she looked up.

“What’s wrong?”

“The room feels stifling. I need some air.”

He had insisted the library curtains slide open and closed on a rod—unlike the fixed, lace-draped ones in the drawing-room. Now he pulled them back and raised the sash, leaning out into the cold night. The simple act of not seeing her—of looking beyond the house, across rooftops and chimneys, into other lives, other cities—cleared his mind. For a moment, he breathed freely.

After a few minutes, she spoke.

“Newland! Shut the window. You’ll catch your death.”

He lowered the sash and turned back.

“Catch my death?” he repeated, half-laughing. And he almost added: *But I already have. I’m dead—been dead for months.*

Then, like lightning, a wild idea struck him: What if it were *she* who died? If she passed soon—suddenly—wouldn’t that free him?

The thought was shocking, intoxicating. To stand here, in this warm, familiar room, staring at her, wishing her gone—it felt both monstrous and thrilling. His soul, sick with longing, clung to the possibility. Yes, May could die. Young people died. Healthy people like her. She might die—and leave him unchained.

She glanced up, sensing something strange in his expression.

“Newland… are you ill?”

He shook his head and walked toward his chair. She returned to her embroidery, and as he passed, he touched her hair.

“Poor May,” he murmured.

“Poor? Why?” she asked, laughing faintly.

“Because I’ll never be able to open a window without worrying you.”

She smiled, but it was strained.

“I’ll never worry—if you’re happy.”

“And I’ll never be happy unless I can open the windows.”

“In *this* weather?” she protested.

He sighed and buried his face in his book.

Days passed. No word from Madame Olenska. Her name was avoided in conversation. Archer didn’t try to see her—not while she stayed at old Catherine’s side. In the uncertainty, he drifted, yet beneath it all, a decision had formed—the one he’d made leaning into the icy night.

It gave him strength. He waited. He did nothing.

Then May told him Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to see him.

It wasn’t unusual—Catherine was recovering, and she had always favored Archer above the others. May delivered the message with pride; she loved knowing her grandmother admired her husband.

There was a pause. Then Archer said, “All right. Shall we go together this afternoon?”

Her face lit up—but instantly she replied, “Oh, you’d better go alone. Granny gets bored seeing the same people too often.”

His heart pounded. He had wanted to go alone precisely for this reason: to speak privately with Ellen. He had waited for the chance—now it was here.

Behind the door, in the yellow damask room beside the hall, she must be waiting. In moments, he’d see her. Just one question—he needed only that. When would she return to Washington? A simple question, impossible to refuse.

But when he entered, it was the mulatto maid who met him. Her white teeth gleaming like piano keys, she slid open the doors and led him to the old woman.

Mrs. Mingott sat in a throne-like armchair near her bed. Beside her, a mahogany stand held a bronze lamp with a glass globe shaded in green paper. No books, no knitting—only conversation had ever mattered to her. She seemed unchanged, save for paleness and deeper shadows under her massive form. Her fluted mob-cap, tied with a starched bow between her chins, and her purple dressing-gown draped with muslin, made her look like a wise, indulgent ancestor.

She held out a small hand—delicate, resting in the hollow of her lap like a pet—and called to the maid: “Don’t let anyone else in. If my daughters come, say I’m asleep.”

The maid vanished. Mrs. Mingott turned to Archer.

“My dear, am I perfectly hideous?” she asked gaily, gesturing toward her chest. “My daughters say it doesn’t matter at my age—as if hideousness matters less the harder it is to hide!”

“You’re handsomer than ever,” he replied, matching her tone.

She threw back her head and laughed. “Ah, but not as handsome as Ellen!” she teased, twinkling at him. “Was she awfully beautiful the day you drove her up from the ferry?”

He laughed. She continued: “Was it because you told her so that she had to put you out on the way? In my youth, young men didn’t abandon pretty women unless they were forced!”

She chuckled, then added, almost plaintively: “It’s a pity she didn’t marry you. I always told her so. It would’ve spared me all this trouble. But who ever thought of sparing their grandmother worry?”

Archer wondered if illness had dulled her mind. Then she burst out:

“Well, it’s settled—she’s staying with me, whatever the rest say! She hadn’t been here five minutes before I’d have fallen to my knees to keep her—if only I’d known where the floor was these past twenty years!”

He listened in silence.

“She’d talked me over,” she went on. “Lovell, Letterblair, Augusta Welland—all of them. They said I must cut off her allowance until she saw her duty to return to Olenski. Even the secretary brought forth proposals—handsome ones, I admit. Marriage is marriage, and money is money—both useful things... and I didn’t know what to say—”

She paused, breathing deeply.

“But the minute I saw her, I said: ‘You sweet bird! Shut you up in that cage again? Never!’ And now it’s decided: she’ll stay and nurse her Granny as long as there’s a Granny to nurse. It’s not gay, but she doesn’t mind. And I’ve told Letterblair she’ll get her proper allowance.”

Archer felt his blood surge. But confusion clouded his joy. He had planned one path—now, the world shifted.

Yet slowly, a deep relief took hold. Difficulties delayed. Opportunities granted. If Ellen chose to stay, it must mean she couldn’t give him up. This was her answer—her refusal to take the final step, but a yielding to something in between.

He sank into the comfort of safety—dangerously sweet.

“She couldn’t have gone back,” he exclaimed. “It was impossible!”

“Oh, my dear,” she said, “I always knew you were on her side. That’s why I sent for you today—and why I told your lovely wife, when she offered to come: ‘No, my dear, I’m pining to see Newland. I don’t want anyone sharing our joy.’ Because you see—” she leaned back as far as her chins allowed, locking eyes with him—“you see, we’ll still have a fight. The family doesn’t want her here. They’ll say it’s because I’m weak, because I’m ill. But I’m not strong enough to face them yet. You must do it for me.”

“For me?” he stammered.

“You. Why not?” she snapped, her round eyes sharp as blades. Her hand darted from the armrest and gripped his—tiny, pale nails like bird claws. “Why not?”

Under her gaze, he regained composure.

“Oh, I don’t count. I’m too insignificant.”

“Well, you’re Letterblair’s partner, aren’t you? You’ve got to reach them through him. Unless you’ve got a reason,” she pressed.

“Oh, my dear,” he said gently, “I believe you can stand alone. But I’ll help if you need it.”

“Then we’re safe,” she sighed, smiling with ancient cunning. “I always knew you’d support us—because they never quote you when they talk about her duty to go home.”

He winced. He longed to ask: “And May—do they quote her?”

But he held back.

“And Madame Olenska?” he said instead. “When may I see her?”

The old lady chuckled, crinkling her eyes, playing the part of mischief.

“Not today. One at a time, please. Madame Olenska has gone out.”

He flushed with disappointment.

“She’s gone out,” she continued, “in my carriage to visit Regina Beaufort.”

She paused, letting the words sink in.

“That’s what she’s reduced me to already. The day after she arrived, she put on her best bonnet and told me, cool as a cucumber, that she was going to call on Regina Beaufort. ‘I don’t know her,’ I said. ‘Who is she?’ ‘She’s your grand-niece,’ she replied. ‘A most unhappy woman.’ ‘She’s the wife of a scoundrel,’ I said. ‘Well,’ she answered, ‘so am I—and yet all my family want me to go back to him.’”

She stopped, then added: “That floored me. I let her go. Then one day she said it was raining too hard to walk, and she wanted to borrow my carriage. ‘For what?’ I asked. ‘To see cousin Regina’—_cousin_! I looked out the window. It wasn’t raining a drop. But I understood. I gave her the carriage…”

She paused, then said softly: “After all, Regina’s a brave woman. And so is she. I’ve always liked courage above all.”

Archer bent down and kissed the small hand still resting on his.

“Eh-eh-eh!” the old lady snapped, mocking him. “Whose hand did you think you were kissing, young man? Your wife’s, I hope?”

As he rose to leave, she called after him: “Give her my love. But don’t tell her about our talk.”
