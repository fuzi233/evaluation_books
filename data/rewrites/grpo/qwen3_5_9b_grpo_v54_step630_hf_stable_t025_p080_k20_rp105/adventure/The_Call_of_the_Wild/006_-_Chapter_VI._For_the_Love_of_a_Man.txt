John Thornton had spent the previous winter freezing his feet, a hardship his partners had met with kindness, leaving him to recover while they ventured upstream to harvest saw-logs for Dawson. By the time Buck stumbled upon him, Thornton was still limping, though the warming spring had soon erased the ache. Lying by the riverbank, watching the water flow and listening to the birdsong, Buck slowly reclaimed his strength.

Rest is a profound healer after three thousand miles of travel. Buck grew lazy as his wounds knit, his muscles swelling and flesh returning to cover his bones. He was not alone in this idleness; Thornton, and his two dogs, Skeet and Nig, were all waiting for the raft that would carry them downstream. Skeet, a small Irish setter, had bonded with Buck during his weakest moments, unable to resent her early advances. Possessing a natural healing instinct, she washed and cleansed his wounds daily, a ritual Buck soon came to anticipate as much as Thornton's presence. Nig, a massive black hound-deerhound mix with laughing eyes and boundless goodwill, shared this affection, though with less overt display.

To Buck's astonishment, these dogs showed no jealousy. They seemed to radiate the same expansive kindness as John Thornton. As Buck recovered, they dragged him into ridiculous games, with Thornton joining in, and through this play, Buck emerged from convalescence into a new existence. For the first time, he knew genuine, passionate love. It was a feeling entirely absent from his days in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley. With the Judge's sons, it had been a working partnership; with the grandsons, a pompous guardianship; with the Judge himself, a stately friendship. But it was John Thornton who ignited a feverish, burning adoration, a madness of the heart.

This man had saved his life, certainly, but he was also the ideal master. While others tended their dogs out of duty or business necessity, Thornton cared for them as if they were his own children, compelled by an instinct he could not suppress. He remembered every kind word and cherished long conversations with them, which he called "gas." He had a habit of gripping Buck's head roughly between his hands, resting his own forehead against Buck's, shaking him back and forth while hurling insults that Buck interpreted as terms of endearment. There was no greater joy for Buck than this rough embrace and the sound of murmured oaths; with each jerk, his heart seemed to shake loose from his chest in ecstasy. When released, Buck would spring to his feet, mouth grinning, eyes eloquent, throat vibrating with unspoken sounds, standing motionless until Thornton would reverently cry, "God! you can all but speak!"

Buck had a unique way of expressing love, akin to pain. He would seize Thornton's hand in his mouth, biting down so fiercely that the flesh bore the mark of his teeth for hours. Just as Buck understood the oaths as love words, Thornton understood this feigned bite as a caress.

Most often, however, Buck's love was expressed through adoration. While he thrilled at Thornton's touch, he rarely sought it. Unlike Skeet, who nudged Thornton's hand incessantly, or Nig, who rested his great head on Thornton's knee, Buck preferred to adore from a distance. He would lie for hours at Thornton's feet, alert and eager, gazing up into his face, studying every fleeting expression and movement. Sometimes he would lie further back, watching the man's outline and the subtle shifts of his body. Often, the intensity of Buck's gaze would draw Thornton's head around, and they would lock eyes in silent communion, their hearts shining out of their gazes.

For a long time after his rescue, Buck could not bear to lose sight of Thornton. From the moment he left the tent to when he returned, Buck followed at his heels. His transient masters in the Northland had bred in him a fear that no master could remain permanent. He feared Thornton would fade away as Perrault, François, and the Scotch half-breed had done. Even in his dreams, this fear haunted him; he would shake off sleep and creep to the tent flap to listen to his master's breathing.

Yet, despite this overwhelming love, the primitive strain awakened in him by the Northland remained active. Faithfulness and devotion, born of fire and shelter, were his, but so too were his wildness and cunning. He was a creature of the wild, come to sit by Thornton's fire, rather than a dog of the soft Southland stamped by generations of civilization. Because of his great love for Thornton, he could not steal from him, but from any other man in any other camp, he hesitated not an instant. His cunning allowed him to evade detection.

His face and body were scarred by the teeth of many dogs, and he fought as fiercely as ever, perhaps more shrewdly. Skeet and Nig were too good-natured for quarrels, besides belonging to Thornton, but any strange dog, regardless of breed or valor, quickly acknowledged Buck's supremacy or found itself struggling for its life. Buck was merciless. He had learned the law of the club and the fang, never foregoing an advantage or drawing back from a foe he had set upon the path to death. He had learned from Spitz and the fighting dogs of the police and mail that there was no middle course. To show mercy was a weakness; mercy was often mistaken for fear, and such misunderstandings led to death. The mandate was kill or be killed, eat or be eaten, a law from the depths of time that he obeyed.

He was older than the days he had seen and the breaths he had drawn. He linked the past with the present, and the eternity behind him throbbed through him in a mighty rhythm, swaying with the tides and seasons. Sitting by Thornton's fire, a broad-breasted dog with white fangs and long fur, the shades of all manner of dogs—half-wolves and wild wolves—hovered behind him. They urged him, tasting the meat he ate, thirsting for the water he drank, scenting the wind with him, listening to the sounds of wild life in the forest, dictating his moods and directing his actions. They lay down to sleep with him, dreamed with him, and became the stuff of his dreams.

So peremptorily did these shades beckon him that each day, mankind and the claims of mankind slipped farther from him. Deep in the forest, a call sounded, mysteriously thrilling and luring. Whenever he heard it, he felt compelled to turn his back on the fire and the beaten earth, plunging into the forest, onward and onward, knowing not where or why. He did not wonder, for the call sounded imperiously. Yet, whenever he gained the soft, unbroken earth and the green shade, the love for John Thornton drew him back to the fire.

Thornton alone held him. The rest of mankind was nothing. Chance travelers might praise or pet him, but he remained cold beneath it all, walking away from anyone too demonstrative. When Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck refused to acknowledge them until he knew they were close to Thornton. After that, he tolerated them in a passive way, accepting their favors as if he were granting them in return. They were of the same sturdy type as Thornton, living close to the earth and thinking simply. Before they swung the raft into the big eddy by the saw-mill at Dawson, they understood Buck and his ways, refusing to demand the intimacy Skeet and Nig enjoyed.

For Thornton, however, his love seemed to grow endlessly. He was the only man who could put a pack upon Buck's back during summer travel. Nothing was too great for Buck when Thornton commanded. One day, having grub-staked themselves from the raft's proceeds and left Dawson for the headwaters of the Tanana, the men and dogs sat on the crest of a cliff dropping straight down to naked bedrock three hundred feet below. Thornton sat near the edge, Buck at his shoulder. A thoughtless whim seized Thornton, and he drew Hans and Pete's attention to the experiment he intended. "Jump, Buck!" he commanded, sweeping his arm over the chasm. The next instant, he was grappling with Buck on the extreme edge, while Hans and Pete dragged them back to safety.

"It's uncanny," Pete said after they caught their breath.

Thornton shook his head. "No, it is splendid, and it is terrible, too. Do you know, it sometimes makes me afraid."

"I'm not hankering to be the man that lays hands on you while he's around," Pete concluded, nodding toward Buck.

"Py Jingo!" Hans added. "Not mineself either."

It was at Circle City, before the year ended, that Pete's apprehensions were realized. "Black" Burton, a man of evil temper and malice, had been picking a quarrel with a tenderfoot at the bar when Thornton stepped in good-naturedly. Buck, as usual, lay in a corner, head on paws, watching his master's every move. Burton struck out without warning, straight from the shoulder. Thornton was spun backward, saving himself only by clutching the bar's rail.

Those watching heard neither bark nor yelp, but a roar. They saw Buck's body rise into the air as he leaped from the floor to Burton's throat. The man saved his life by instinctively throwing out his arm but was hurled backward to the floor with Buck on top. Buck loosened his teeth from the arm and drove in again for the throat. This time, the man only partly blocked, and his throat was torn open. Then the crowd surged upon Buck, driving him off. While a surgeon checked the bleeding, Buck prowled up and down, growling furiously, attempting to rush in and being forced back by an array of hostile clubs. A "miners' meeting" called on the spot decided the dog had sufficient provocation, and Buck was discharged. But his reputation was made, and from that day his name spread through every camp in Alaska.

Later, in the fall, he saved John Thornton's life in quite another fashion. The three partners were lining a long, narrow poling-boat down a treacherous stretch of rapids on the Forty-Mile Creek. Hans and Pete moved along the bank, snubbing with a thin Manila rope from tree to tree, while Thornton remained in the boat, poling its descent and shouting directions. Buck, on the bank, worried and anxious, kept abreast of the boat, his eyes never off his master.

At a particularly bad spot, where a ledge of barely submerged rocks jutted into the river, Hans cast off the rope. As Thornton poled the boat out into the stream, Hans ran down the bank with the rope's end to snub the boat once it cleared the ledge. The boat flew downstream in a current as swift as a mill-race, and Hans checked it with the rope, checking too suddenly. The boat flipped over, snubbing to the bank bottom-up, while Thornton, flung sheer out, was carried downstream toward the worst part of the rapids—a stretch of wild water where no swimmer could live.

Buck sprang in instantly. Three hundred yards later, amid a mad swirl of water, he overhauled Thornton. When he felt the man grasp his tail, Buck headed for the bank, swimming with all his splendid strength. But the progress shoreward was slow; the progress downstream was amazingly rapid. From below came the fatal roaring where the wild current went wilder, rent in shreds and spray by rocks thrusting through like the teeth of an enormous comb. The suck of the water as it took the beginning of the last steep pitch was frightful, and Thornton knew the shore was impossible. He scraped furiously over a rock, bruised across a second, and struck a third with crushing force. Clutching its slippery top with both hands, he released Buck and, above the roar of the churning water, shouted: "Go, Buck! Go!"

Buck could not hold his own and swept downstream, struggling desperately but unable to win back. When he heard Thornton's command repeated, he partly reared out of the water, throwing his head high as if for a last look, then turned obediently toward the bank. He swam powerfully and was dragged ashore by Pete and Hans at the very point where swimming ceased to be possible and destruction began.

They knew that the time a man could cling to a slippery rock in the face of that driving current was a matter of minutes. They ran as fast as they could up the bank to a point far above where Thornton hung on. They attached the line used for snubbing the boat to Buck's neck and shoulders, careful not to strangle him or impede his swimming, and launched him into the stream. He struck out boldly, but not straight enough. He discovered the mistake too late when Thornton was abreast of him, a mere half-dozen strokes away, while Buck was carried helplessly past.

Hans promptly snubbed with the rope as if Buck were a boat. The rope tightening on him in the sweep of the current, he was jerked under the surface and remained there until his body struck the bank and he was hauled out. Half-drowned, Hans and Pete threw themselves upon him, pounding breath into him and water out of him. He staggered to his feet and fell down. The faint sound of Thornton's voice came to them; though they could not make out the words, they knew he was in his extremity. His master's voice acted on Buck like an electric shock. He sprang to his feet and ran up the bank ahead of the men to the point of his previous departure.

Again the rope was attached, and he was launched. Again he struck out, but this time straight into the stream. He had miscalculated once; he would not be guilty a second time. Hans paid out the rope, permitting no slack, while Pete kept it clear of coils. Buck held on until he was on a line straight above Thornton, then turned, and with the speed of an express train headed down upon him. Thornton saw him coming, and as Buck struck him like a battering ram, with the whole force of the current behind him, he reached up and closed with both arms around the shaggy neck.
