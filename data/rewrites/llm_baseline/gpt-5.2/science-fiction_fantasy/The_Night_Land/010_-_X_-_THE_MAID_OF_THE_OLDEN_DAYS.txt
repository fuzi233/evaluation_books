In that instant when my eyes found the shape of a vast Pyramid rising up into the Night, all the despair that had sat like ash upon my heart turned, sudden, to a fierce, hot gladness—so swift that it seemed I had but to run a little way and I should be with mine Own.

Yet it was no warm, windowed beacon such as a refuge should be. It stood black upon black, a wedge of deeper dark against the far, low burnings of the Land. And even while my feet began to fly down from the shoulder of the great volcano, a cold thought slid in beside the joy: if this were truly the Lesser Refuge, why did it show no answering glow, no living pulse of the Earth-Current, no homely ring of light where folk would move and work?

But longing is a hunger, and I was starved of her. I ran.

For a few minutes I went blind with haste, and then the ground betrayed me. My foot took a hidden drop; the world turned over; and I fell headlong with such hardness that pain burst white through me, as though my neck were snapped clean. There I lay, moaning small, helpless as a thing set out for slaughter. In that time any Brute might have come out of the dark and ended me with a careless blow, and I should not have lifted an hand.

After a great while—how long I know not—I got to sitting, both hands clapped about my neck. The ache eased by degrees, as if the Night itself grew tired of pressing. At last I stood again, shaking, and went forward with more wit than before. My hope had not died, but it had learned fear.

I thought to send the Master-Word forthwith—aye, to call Naani and tell her I was come—yet the blackness of the Pyramid troubled me. It might be no refuge. It might be a shell taken by Evil, or some foul Power laying a pretence upon my sight to draw me in. Still, the thing showed plain now, a great bulk shouldering the distant fires. Whether it were refuge or trap, it was the only sure landmark in all that waste; and my feet would not be denied.

The climb up the volcano had taken me, mayhap, thirteen hours. I went down in ten, though sore from the fall and wary of every treacherous slope. At the end of that tenth hour the ground leveled; I was come again upon the wide Plain of the Night Land. The Pyramid was hidden now in the upper dark, yet the hill on which it stood made a heavier shadow between me and the far glowings, and I knew my course.

Four hours I went across the Plain, passing here and there fire-holes that bled a dull red into the black, like coals buried under ash. Those fire-holes made a kind of false hearth-light, enough that the darkness was never whole; yet there was no comfort in it, for the air about them carried a sour tang—sulphur and hot stone—and the Land was full of small, listening noises that were not wind.

When I had gone those four hours, the bulk of the hill-bottom hid the distant shinings altogether, and the world ahead became a wall of black. I judged I was near, yet another full hour passed ere my hand could have touched the rise of the ground. In those five hours since leaving the volcano, sound came to me often: three times, and again, the quick patter and rush of running things in the darkness; once a great roar far off, as if a giant beast proclaimed itself; and once a screaming, strange and horrid, that set my teeth to grinding and made the skin tighten upon my arms beneath the armour.

I began to go up the hill.

At first an excitement took me so strongly that I could scarce keep from shouting her name aloud into the Night, as a child shouts for a lost mother in a wood. But the higher I went, the more my joy chilled. A caution returned—sharp, inward—as though my spirit, wiser than my heart, smelled corruption where my eyes saw only stone.

Near three hours the ascent took me. And then, when I came so high that the Pyramid showed its full grimness—an enormous slope going up, desolate and silent into the starless dark—an utter shaking fear seized me. It was not guessed; it was known. There was no human life within that bulk. No dear, small motions of folk at labour; no guarded voices; no domestic clatter of a place held by the living. Only waiting—monstrous, patient, and evil.

I went down the hill again, very quiet, keeping to darkness, and so away from that place.

Four hours I walked before I felt I had put the hill safely behind me, and even then there was no safety in all that Land. I went a little blindly, my mind loose within me, as though my heart had died and I were only a body that continued because it had not yet fallen.

So it was that I came upon the shore of the olden sea, and had no knowing how I had reached it, for I thought it lay far aside. Yet afterward I considered that the dry bed might curve in that Country, or that there be more than one dead sea there in the Night.

I sat down, weak and bewildered, on the cold, hard earth. In me was a desolation so deep that it was like a silence in the blood. For my spirit told me there was Evil in that dark Pyramid, and I doubted not that destruction had come upon the Peoples of the Lesser Refuge, and that wicked creatures and Powers now kept house there. And if that were so, then I was come too late to save the Maid; and in that thought I found a terrible readiness: let any Brute come now. Let me fight and be done. Life had no savour left.

Yet even in that bleakness there was a thread of sense: I had no sure proof the black Pyramid was the Lesser Refuge. It might be some other structure, some older bulk. But the sureness in my spirit was like iron; it allowed no comfort.

After a while, a new thought rose: I would send the Master-Word through the Night. If Naani yet lived anywhere, she would answer; and if no answer came, or if some Evil Power replied to destroy me, then at least my heart-ache would end.

I stood and looked about into the blackness. The land lay open, with scattered bushes like low, thorny shadows and the dull red of a fire-hole not far off, breathing heat and stink into the cold. I sent the Master-Word with my brain-elements, and then I called Naani thrice, sending the call with all the strength that was left in me.

And lo! it seemed but a moment, and the Master-Word broke round me, low and solemn, beating in the Night like a great, slow drum heard through stone. With it came a voice—far, small, lonesome—so faint it was as if it sounded from the end of the world. Yet it was her voice: Naani’s, and Mirdath’s, calling me by mine olden love-name.

I near choked with the affright of joy. The despair vanished as if it had never been. She lived. She called. For an age of grim labour and dread I had heard nothing of mine Own, and now the sound of her—even so faint—made the world new.

But in the same beat of gladness there was fear: the voice was very far. She was not safe under my hand. Peril could reach her before I could.

And then—before I made answer—my spirit pricked me with another knowing: someone was near, a little way off, among the bushes by the fire-hole. I did not speak again into the aether. I went swift, without sound, into a great bush on the near side, and peered through into the open about the fire.

There, in the red, wavering light, knelt a small figure upon the ground, sobbing. It was a slim maid. She shook as she wept, yet even in her weeping she listened—harking, desperate—as though she waited for a sound that would not come.

Mine own soul knew her in one white moment. This was she, here in the very flesh, and not a far voice only. And I understood, sudden, a bitter domestic truth hidden inside all that mighty travel: she had been crying to me for a month of loneliness, as a child cries into a storm; and because her strength was spent, her call could not carry plain through the great spaces. The Night had swallowed it. Her hunger and cold had stolen the force from her brain-elements, so that what should have been a clear Word became only a faint ache in the aether—if it reached me at all.

I drew in my breath, set my teeth to steady my lips, and spoke with the human tongue, softly but clear, out of the bush:

“MIRDATH.”

She stopped as though struck. Her head lifted; her wet face turned this way and that; fear and hope shone together in her tears in the fire-light. I parted the bush and stepped through, standing before her in my grey armour.

My heart cried to seize her and gather her close, for it seemed I had come back to Mirdath after an utter lost Eternity. Yet I was held a moment, adrift within myself; for she was Naani and she was Mirdath, and yet in my eyes she was a stranger too—dainty, pretty, torn and shaken with woe, as one who had walked long among stones.

In the same moment that I came out, she screamed and fell back, striving weakly toward the nearer bushes as if to flee from a monster. Then she saw my shape was human. I spoke the Master-Word aloud, that she might know peace and help, and I told my name, saying I am That One.

She knew even as the sounds left my lips. She cried out in a broken voice and ran to me, thrusting both her small hands into my keeping, and then fell to sobbing and shaking so that I was troubled to ease her. I held her hands fast—bare, for I had no armoured gloves—saying nothing, for speech was too small for that moment.

She leaned against me, weak as a child. Her breath came in catches. When her sobbing lessened, I saw her trembling did not wholly cease, and I bethought me at once of hunger. A body may be brave a long while, but it cannot live upon terror and hope. Her hands in mine were so thin that bone seemed close under skin.

I set her gently down beside a hump of smooth rock, so her back might rest. I stripped off my cloak and wrapped it about her quick, for her own clothes were scarce any covering—torn among bushes until they hung in rags—and the bitter chill took her. Part of the shaking was cold; part was weakness, for she was nigh starved unto death, and broken with grief and loneliness.

From my back I took the scrip and pouch. I drew out a food-tablet and broke it into my cup. The water—flat and cold—took the crumbled meal, and I made a little broth swift upon a hot rock at the very edge of the fire-hole, where heat seeped constant from the earth. The rock hissed faint under the cup; a thin steam rose with the smell of warmed grain and salt, a comfort so small and yet so mighty in that place that it near made me weep.

Her hands shook too much to manage it, so I fed her the broth myself, holding the cup steady to her mouth. She drank as one half-asleep. When the warmth reached her belly, her face crumpled and she fell again to sobbing, quiet as a child trying not to wake a house.

I slid my hands under the cloak and took her hands again, holding them firm. That simple pressure—skin upon skin, human steadiness against the tremor—seemed to lend her peace. The trembling eased. The weeping lessened. The broth did its work, like a spark catching in damp tinder.

After a little, her fingers stirred. I loosened my grip, and she answered by clasping my hands with a weak, gentle grasp. She did not yet look up; she lay quiet, gathering strength, as though she must first make a hearth within herself before she could speak.

All that while, a new fearfulness kept me listening. Danger had always been near me in the Night Land, yet it had been my own life only. Now mine Own sat wrapped in my cloak, and the thought of any hurt to her was like a knife held at my heart. I harked about me, again and again, to every shift of sound—the low bubbling of the fire-hole, the far, thin running noises, the soft scrape of something small upon stone.

Suddenly the Maid made as though to rise. I moved to help, but she caught my hand and slipped down upon her knees and kissed it, and began again to weep.

I was so abashed I stood stupid and let it be for a moment. Then I drew my hand free, for it might not be. I knelt likewise before her, took her hands, and kissed them once—humbled anew—so she might know what honour and love were in me, and that I understood her. She sobbed the more, for she was weak, and moved beyond bearing because I had come to her through the Night of the world.

I let her hands go, thinking she would need them for her tears, but she laid them back into my palms, bowing her head over her weeping. In that small act she gave herself to me, utterly, to the very essence of her dear spirit.

I took her into mine arms, very gently, without any rough caress, and then, unable to help myself, I stroked her hair. It was soft, though dusted with the Land’s grit; it held the faint, sour smell of sulphur and smoke, and beneath that the human scent of skin—proof, intimate and untranslatable, that she was alive. I called her Naani and Mirdath, and spoke many things I scarce remember now, yet which she knew in the after time. She lay quiet in my arms, wondrous content, and sobbed on a long while, not asking comfort save only shelter—only the fact of being held where no monster could reach her.

Presently she grew calmer. I would have set her again against the rock, wrapped close, that I might make more broth; yet she nestled toward me with a sweet wistfulness that warmed my heart in a strange fashion, for surely she was mine Own. Still, she obeyed gentle when I asked, and rested quiet, watching me with her gaze as I worked.

I made more broth upon the hot rock, breaking another tablet, stirring it until it thickened, careful not to scorch it where the rock was fiercest. The thin steam rose again, carrying that small domestic scent of food—an odour that belongs to kitchens and guarded rooms, not to the killing dark. I gave it her, and this time she drank using her own two hands, steadier now.

Then I ate myself—three tablets—and drank some water; for it was a foolish great while since last I had taken any proper sustenance. The food lay heavy and plain in the mouth, yet I blessed it, because in the Night Land even the simplest meal is an act of holding on to being human.

After a while the warmth and broth brightened her eyes, and she began to talk. She spoke with pauses, for strength came and went in her like a tide, and there was more to tell than any human heart can carry easy. Twice she fell again to weeping, for her father was dead, and the Peoples of the Lesser Redoubt were all slain or scattered through that dreadful Land.

An Evil Force—so she told—had made action upon the Peoples within the Lesser Refuge. Some, grown utter weak because the Earth-Current failed, had opened the Great Door and gone out into the Night. Immediately great and horrid monsters poured into the Lesser Pyramid. There had been a brutish chase in the passages and chambers; many were slain; some escaped into the darkness.

Naani fled with three other maids after her father, the Master Monstruwacan, was slain by a shaggy man—brutal and monstrous. They hid among bushes to sleep, but certain creatures came upon them in the dark and stole two away. The remaining maid ran off one way and Naani another, and they never met again.

How long a time had passed since then she could not tell, for there is no counting in such a life—no marked days, no bells, no steady change of labour to measure hours. It had been dread long to her. And by my questions concerning my callings, I learned she had been lost through all the time of my journey; she had heard none of my calls since she escaped the Lost Refuge into that Land.

Yet oft had she cried to me with her brain-elements, until her heart grew sick of her own unanswered longing. Worse, those callings had drawn Evil things, for the Land listens. More than once beasts and searching horrors had come toward her, and because she had the gift of hearing she had known their approach and slipped away; but with piteous runnings and hidings among rocks and bushes, until she dared make no calling save odd whiles, lest she bring monsters upon her. Her weakness had been such that she could not throw the Word strongly afar, nor shape her spiritual cry plain through the thick aether.

Her garments had been torn from her little by little by thorn and stone; she had no thread, no tool, no quiet place to mend. For food she had eaten moss from rocks, and strange berries and growths whose taste she described with a grim face—bitter as old metal, sharp as smoke, sweet at first and then making the belly turn. She drank from hot springs because the cold drove her, and oft that water made her sick with sulphur. Yet, she thought, it might have saved her from worse poison in the plants; in that, she and I could only guess.

In that dreadful time she had heard a score of drear things: once, the slaying of a young maid near by, by some Brute out in the darkness; thrice and more, the feet of people running, and the heavier tread of giants pursuing. By that I understood the sounds mine ears had taken in during my own goings across the Land, and a new pity and horror rose in me.

She told how once she came upon some of the Peoples of the Lesser Redoubt hiding among bushes. She called to them that she was human as they, but they ran from her without heed, so sore was the panic upon them.

The bitter chill kept her always striving to be near fire-holes, which were plentiful; yet the heat and dull red glow drew the monstrous Brutes, as I had known elsewhere in the Night Land and in the Upward Gorge. So she was oft forced to go without warmth, crouching in utter cold, teeth chattering, listening to the Land’s hateful small sounds. And odd whiles she grew so desperate she would venture near the fire for a time of heat; and because of this she had been nigh slain in her sleep, twice and thrice. There were snakes about the fires, though not everywhere; and spider-crabs; and monstrous scorpions.

And even this very time—when she had lain by the fire-hole, weak and thinking death close—there had been creatures like crabs squatting all about, a silent circle of waiting, impudent and horrible, content to let her die and then feed. She had been afeard to sleep lest they destroy her in slumber.

So, when my Master-Word beat through the Night, strong as a low spiritual thunder, it had stirred her heart—but she had thought it came from far away, from the Mighty Pyramid itself, and it brought her not hope but a sharper despair, as if the world mocked her with a sound of me that she could not reach. Then she heard her name spoken with the human tongue—different from the name my spirit had used after the Word—and in a minute I came out of the bush. She had fallen back in fear of a monster; then seen a young man in grey armour; and known at once that I was the olden one of her memory-dreams, the one that had spoken to her across half the dead world.

So she spoke, with that wonder still upon her, as if she could not fit the thing into words. And indeed no words can fully carry such meeting; there is always something left—some taste of the moment, some sound in the air—known only to the two who lived it.

When she spoke of the spider-crabs, I looked about at once, and saw that they were not gone. Beyond the red-lit circle about the fire-hole, in the border where light fell off into black, they sat—low, many-legged shapes, motionless, watching with a steady assurance that was like hunger given patience.

Disgust and anger took me. I rose, went to the edge of the light, and spurned them one by one, kicking perhaps a dozen until they scuttled away into the darkness. Their calm was foolish, and their courage small; they made no attack. Yet I thought, grimly, that a true crab of my own day would have been eager enough to pinch my toe had I offered it.

Then I returned to the Maid, and sat close, listening outward into the Night and inward to her breathing—thankful for that soft human sound, the gentlest music left in all the world.
