Xodar listened with a mixture of shock and disbelief as I recounted the horrors of the arena during the rites of Issus. Even having previously doubted the divinity of the Great Mother, he struggled to comprehend how a mortal could draw a sword upon her without being obliterated into dust by her divine fury.

"It is the final proof," he declared finally, his voice heavy with conviction. "Nothing remains to shatter my superstitious awe. She is merely a wicked old crone, wielding terrible power through machinations that have kept her own people—and all Barsoom—in religious ignorance for ages."

"She retains absolute authority here, however," I countered. "Therefore, we must depart the instant conditions permit."

"I trust you'll find a propitious moment," Xodar remarked with a dry chuckle. "For in all my life, I have never witnessed a scenario wherein a prisoner of the First Born might escape."

"To-night will suffice," I replied.

"It will soon be dark," Xodar noted. "How may I assist?"

"Are you a swimmer?" I asked.

"No slimy Silian haunting the depths of Korus swims with greater ease than Xodar," he answered proudly.

"Excellent. The Red Men likely cannot swim," I observed. "Their domains lack sufficient water to float the smallest craft, and centuries of isolation have terrified their ancestors from lakes, rivers, and seas alike. One of us must support him through the water to our chosen vessel. I hoped to submerge completely, but the Red Youth would surely panic at depth.

"The Red One accompanies us?" Xodar asked.

"Yes."

"That is wise. Three swords outweigh two. Especially when the third blade belongs to a warrior as formidable as this lad. I have watched him duel in the arena many times. Until I fought alongside you, I had seen no one who appeared unconquerable amidst such odds. You two bear a striking resemblance—a master and pupil, or perhaps father and son. Observe the grim smile, the maddening contempt for the foe etched into every movement and expression."

"As it may be," I said, "he is a fierce fighter. Together, the three of us form a trio nearly impossible to vanquish. Had Jeddak of Thark, Tars Tarkas, joined us, we could carve a path from one end of Barsoom to the other, regardless of opposition."

"When they discern your origin," Xodar interjected, "your legend will grow. That is merely one of Issus's deceptions. She operates through the Holy Therns, who are as ignorant of her true nature as the outer worlds. Her edicts arrive inscribed in blood upon strange parchment, delivered to guarded altars where access is forbidden. The deluded fools believe they receive revelations from a goddess, unaware that I have borne these messages for Issus for decades.

"A secret tunnel links the Temple of Issus to the principal shrine of Matai Shang, excavated eons ago by the First Born in utter secrecy. Through it, the Therns transmit doctrines regarding the Mysterious River Iss, the Valley Dor, and the Lost Sea of Korus, persuading the gullible masses to undertake voluntary pilgrimages. These journeys swell the coffers of the Holy Therns and increase the ranks of their enslaved laborers.

"Occasionally, the First Born raid the outer world, capturing royal women from the Red Men and seizing skilled artisans to construct battleships they cannot create themselves. We pride ourselves on non-productivity; it is criminal for a First Born to toil or invent. Labor is reserved for the lower orders, existing solely to sustain our luxuries and endless idleness. Fighting is our sole purpose. Were it not for combat, the population of First Born would vastly outnumber all other creatures, for few of us know natural death. Our females would live eternally save for our weariness, leading us to discard them for fresh partners. Only Issus herself is immune to mortality, having survived countless ages."

"Do the other Barsoomians not live forever because of the pilgrimage doctrine?" I asked.

"Now I am convinced they are the same species as the First Born," Xodar admitted. "I pray to live long enough to fight for them, atoning for the sins I committed through generations of false teaching."

Just then, a strange, haunting call echoed across the waters of Omean. It signaled the end of the day; the men of Omean spread their silken cloaks upon the decks of their battleships and cruisers, sinking into the dreamless sleep characteristic of Mars.

Our guard entered to conduct his final inspection before dawn. His duty completed, the heavy doors of our prison barred us inside, leaving us alone for the night.

Allowing him time to retreat, I leaped to the grated window and scanned the adjacent waters. Half a mile away floated a monstrous battleship, flanked by smaller cruisers and scout craft. Only the battleship maintained a watch, visible clearly in the upper works. As I observed, the lone sentinel spread his sleeping silks upon a tiny platform, collapsing onto his couch. The discipline on Omean was remarkably lax, unsurprising given that no enemy suspected the existence of the First Born, their fleets, or the Sea of Omean itself.

Returning to the floor, I described the vessels to Xodar.

"There is one there," he pointed out, "my personal property, designed to carry five men. It is the swiftest of the swift. If we board her, we can make a memorable dash for liberty." He detailed the engine and equipment, revealing a trick of gearing I recognized from Kantos Kan, taught to me when we sailed under false flags in the navy of Zodanga beneath Prince Sab Than. I realized instantly that the First Born had stolen this technology from Helium, for only ships of that realm possessed such gear. Xodar's praise of her speed was justified; nothing on Mars could match the velocity of Helium's craft.

"We shall wait an hour," I decided, allowing the stragglers to settle into their silks. Meanwhile, I would retrieve the Red Youth to ensure our party was ready.

Climbing atop the partition wall, I traversed a narrow ledge until reaching the cell where the boy sat upon his bench. Leaning back, gazing at the glowing dome of Omean, he looked up in astonishment as I balanced above him. A grin of appreciation spread across his face.

As I crouched to descend, he motioned me to wait, whispering, "Catch my hand. I can nearly leap to the top. I try daily, growing stronger each time."

Lying prone across the wall, I extended my hand downward. With a burst of energy, he ran from the center of the cell, springing upward until I seized his outstretched grip, pulling him up beside me.

"You are the first jumper I've seen among the Red Men," I said.

"Not strange," he smiled. "I will explain when we have time."

Descending back into the cell, we rejoined Xodar. Descending to the floor, we spent the remaining hour finalizing our plans, swearing a solemn pact to fight to the death for one another against whatever foes awaited us. We knew that even if we escaped the First Born, the weight of religious superstition might still oppose us.

Agreed, I would navigate the craft once boarded, and if we reached the outer world safely, our destination would be Helium.

"Why Helium?" the boy asked.

"I am a prince of Helium," I replied.

He bestowed a peculiar look upon me, saying nothing further. I marveled at the expression then, wondering if it held hidden meaning, but pressing matters consumed my thoughts, and the question faded from my mind.

"Come," I urged, "now is as good a time as any."

Moments later, I was back atop the partition with the boy. Unbuckling my harness, I snapped it into a single long strap and lowered it to Xodar below. Grasping the end, he settled beside us.

"How simple," he laughed.

"The balance requires simplicity," I corrected. Rising to the top of the outer prison wall, I peered over to locate the patrolling sentry. Waiting five minutes, I watched him complete his slow, snail-like circuit around the structure. Once he rounded the corner, disappearing from view of the prison side, I grabbed Xodar and drew him up. Lowering the strap, I released it to the ground. The boy slid down to Xodar's side.

Following their lead, they walked slowly toward the water, passing the guardhouse filled with sleeping soldiers. After a dozen steps, I dropped down and followed them leisurely toward the shore. Passing the guardhouse, the sight of the array of fine blades paused me; if ever men needed swords, it was my companions and I on this perilous journey.

Glancing at Xodar and the boy, I saw they had slipped over the edge of the dock into the water. Clinging to metal rings studded into the concrete-like substance of the dock, they bobbed at the surface, waiting for me.

The temptation of the weapons within the guardhouse was strong. Hesitation, I recalled, leads to ruin. Another moment saw me creeping toward the door. Gently, I pressed it open a crack, revealing a dozen blacks stretched upon their silks in profound slumber. At the far side, a rack held swords and firearms. Warily, I widened the gap to admit my body. A hinge groaned in protest. One man stirred. My heart froze. I cursed my folly, yet there was no turning back.
