This man contrives a secret ’twixt us two,
That he may quell me with his meeting eyes—
Like one who quells a lioness at bay.

The letter lay on Gwendolen’s table when she returned from the springs, its edges crisp and unopened, yet already carrying within it the weight of a world turned upside down.

*DEAREST CHILD,* it began, *I have been expecting to hear from you for a week. In your last you said the Langens thought of leaving Leubronn and going to Baden. How could you be so thoughtless as to leave me in uncertainty about your address? I am in the greatest anxiety lest this should not reach you.*

Her breath caught. The words were not merely urgent—they were desperate.

*I must now entreat you to return as quickly as possible,* the letter continued. *If you spent all your money, it would be out of my power to send you any more, and you must not borrow from the Langens—for I could not repay them.*

She read on, her fingers tightening around the paper.

*This is the sad truth, my child—I wish I could prepare you for it better—but a dreadful calamity has befallen us all. Grapnell & Co. have failed for a million. We are totally ruined—your aunt Gascoigne as well as I. Only your uncle has his benefice, so that by putting down their carriage and getting interest for the boys, the family can go on. All the property our poor father saved for us goes to pay the liabilities. There is nothing I can call my own.*

Gwendolen stood frozen. The certainty of ease—the quiet assumption that life would always provide—had been her foundation. Now it was gone, like smoke. She had never known want; she had never even imagined it. And yet here it was, written plainly before her: poverty, dependence, the end of everything she had taken for granted.

She looked at herself in the mirror. Her hair was still coiled neatly, her face untouched by sleep or sorrow. But inside, something had cracked. Not grief—not yet—but a cold fury.

She sat down heavily on the red velvet sofa, picked up the letter again, and read it twice. Then let it fall to the floor. Her hands rested in her lap, clasped tightly. No tears came.

She did not weep for her mother. Fanny Davilow had lived a life of quiet constraint, of careful appearances and unspoken burdens. Gwendolen had never felt the need to pity her. If anything, she pitied herself. For wasn’t she the one meant for grandeur? Wasn’t she the one whose destiny should have been carved in gold and silk?

But instead—this.

A sharp anger flared. Not just at fate, but at chance. At the roulette table where she had lost her gains yesterday. If only luck had held—just one more spin—she might have won enough to carry them all through. She still had four napoleons. And jewelry—her Etruscan necklace, which she hadn’t worn since arriving. It could be sold. Easily. The Germans did it all the time. It was no shame.

She pictured it: ten louis in hand, a few more days at the table, a return of fortune. A triumph.

But then came the memory of Deronda—standing beside her, watching with those calm, knowing eyes. Not mocking, not laughing, but seeing. Seeing her pride, her recklessness, her fragile hope. And now, if she sold the necklace, the Langens would notice. They would ask questions. They would offer pity. She would be indebted. She could not bear it.

So she made her decision: sell the necklace early, tell them only that her mother wanted her home—no explanation—and leave that evening. No maid. No delays. Just a train to Brussels, and then straight to Offendene.

She didn’t go to bed. Instead, she lit every candle she could find and began to pack. Her movements were swift, precise. But in her mind, scenes unfolded: the farewell, the journey, the changed house, the silence of a home now stripped of comfort. And then—another scene: the roulette table again, the wheel spinning, the ball falling… and Deronda standing there, watching.

His presence haunted her. Not because he was handsome or clever—but because he seemed to know her too well. To see through her bravado. And now, if she stayed, she might lose everything again.

At midnight, she finished. By dawn, the room was empty except for a single pile of belongings. She dressed in her gray traveling gown, adjusted her felt hat, and stepped into the morning light.

She paused before the mirror. Her reflection stared back—pale, but composed. Her lips curved slightly, not in sadness, but in defiance. She took off her hat, leaned forward, and kissed the glass.

How could she believe in sorrow? She had strength. She had will. She would not break.

Madame von Langen never left her room before breakfast, so Gwendolen walked early, through the Obere Strasse, toward Mr. Wiener’s shop. She passed the *Czarina*, where Deronda stayed. She saw no one. But she knew—knew with a strange certainty—that someone watched.

She entered the shop with cool grace, offering the necklace without a word. Mr. Wiener examined it, nodded. “Fine stones,” he said. “Very good.”

She received nine louis. Only nine. The rest was lost to the dealer’s greed. But it was enough. Thirteen louis total—more than enough for the journey.

She turned homeward, entering the *salon* to wait for breakfast. She was tired. Hungry. But her mind was set. She would tell them she had heard from her mother. That was all.

Then—knock. A servant entered, holding a small packet addressed to Miss Harleth.

Gwendolen took it. Her pulse quickened. Before opening it, she *knew*.

Inside, wrapped in cambric, was her necklace. Beneath it, a scrap of torn paper:

> _A stranger who has found Miss Harleth’s necklace returns it to her with the hope that she will not again risk the loss of it._

She flushed. Not with gratitude—but with outrage.

It was him. Deronda. He had seen her. He had followed her. He had bought back the necklace and sent it back to her—like some moralizing guardian, pretending to do her a kindness while stripping her of dignity.

He had humiliated her. He had made her feel small.

Tears burned behind her eyes. But she blinked them away. She could not cry. Not now. Not in front of anyone.

She shoved the necklace, the note, the handkerchief—all—into her *nécessaire*. Pressed her handkerchief to her face. Waited. Breathed.

Then she walked into the *salon*, her composure restored.

“Mother wrote,” she said lightly. “She wants me home.”

They protested. Of course they did. But she refused help. Refused companionship. Would travel alone.

“I’ll be fine,” she said. “I’ve traveled before.”

And so, on Thursday evening, she left Leubronn.

On Saturday morning, she arrived at Offendene—home, for now. But soon, she would leave it too.

For the first time in her life, Gwendolen Harleth was not certain of what came next.
