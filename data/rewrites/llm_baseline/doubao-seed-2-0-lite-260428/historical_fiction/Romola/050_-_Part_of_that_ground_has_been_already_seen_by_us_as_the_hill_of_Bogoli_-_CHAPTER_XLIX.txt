The wintry weeks slipped past Romola the way white-sailed gliders drift by a lone figure standing on a shore—silent, nearly indistinguishable from one another, yet each carrying a hidden cargo of unspooling change. Tito’s offhand warning had tangled so much quiet dread into her curiosity over Florence’s shifting public fate that she had begun to avoid news rather than seek it. The menacing advance of the German Emperor had fizzled out, and other pressures weighing on the city had eased, but widespread hardship lingered, keeping Romola’s days full of charitable work that offered her the only refuge she knew from her tangled, unnameable doubts.

She dared not rejoice that the desperate last-minute relief that had seemingly vindicated Savonarola’s Piagnoni party had swept them to such sweeping power: hot-tempered Francesco Valori, the fiery chieftain of the Piagnoni, had been elected Gonfaloniere at the start of the year, and raced to push through his liberal reforms in the narrow two months of his term. For the moment it looked like a victory for the faction that defended republican freedom, a new bulwark to shield Savonarola from his enemies—but Romola was attuned to every faint undercurrent that fed her foreboding, that the present was nothing but a quiet brood of mixed seeds of change that could burst into tragedy at any moment. By Carnival, just after mid-February, her premonition proved right: the Medici sympathizers had stopped lying low, and were openly campaigning to secure Bernardo del Nero as the next Gonfaloniere.

On the final day of Carnival, between ten and eleven in the morning, Romola stepped out as promised, heading for the Corso degli Albizzi to collect her cousin Brigida. The pair planned to return to the Via de’ Bardi by early afternoon to claim the spot Tito had reserved for them at a window overlooking the Piazza della Signoria, where a spectacle so unprecedented was set to unfold that every last Florentine was desperate to see it. The Piagnoni had seized full control of Carnival celebrations, and Dolfo Spini and his rowdy crew had fought in vain to resurrect their beloved old lewd masques and crude, indecent pranks—there was no place for such things in a city that had formally declared Christ its king.

Romola’s mood was listless, the dull lassitude of someone dragging themselves through a full day of sightseeing only to please a dear friend. This reimagined Carnival was a historic turning point for the city, but it stirred no fire of enthusiasm in her. She had no idea that it would mark a turning point in her own life, tying a separate, hidden fate irrevocably to her own where no one could fail to see it. She chose to cut straight through the main Piazza first, to sneak a first look at the legendary spectacle while she was still alone. Entering from the south, she spotted it: a monstrous, riotously colored pyramid, more like a giant sixty-foot fir tree lined with tiered shelves that flared wider and wider down to its base, where its full circumference stretched eighty yards.

The Piazza thronged with life. Slim young boys in white linen, olive wreaths circling their heads, darted back and forth at the pyramid’s base carrying baskets of bright trinkets, while older men—some in monk’s frocks, some in the loose tunics and deep red caps of Florentine artists—helped arrange the displays, stepping back at intervals to admire the full wondrous structure. A small, tight group of onlookers huddled on the marble steps of Orcagna’s Loggia, keeping their distance in clear discontent and scorn, and Romola recognized the painter Piero di Cosimo among them.

Stepping closer, she paused to scan the objects stacked tier by tier from the base to the peak: indecent tapestries and brocades, paintings and sculptures deemed too likely to stir vice, game boards, playing cards and their printing blocks, dice and every gambling apparatus imaginable, secular songbooks, lutes, drums, cymbals and trumpets of every make, old Carnival masks and masquerade costumes, elegantly bound copies of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarch, Pulci and all other books labeled vain or impure, and all the tools of feminine vanity: rouge pots, false hair, mirrors, perfumes, face powders, sheer veils stitched to draw greedy, wandering gazes. At the very top, perched above it all, was the unflattering effigy of a mythical Venetian merchant rumored to have offered a vast sum to buy the whole collection of these “abominations,” and above him, even uglier, the grotesque symbolic figure of the old debauched Carnival itself.

This was the setup for the new Bonfire of the Vanities: dry tinder and gunpowder were hidden inside the pyramid’s frame, and that evening, as trumpets blared, the whole pile would be set alight, the old Carnival effigy tumbling into the flames amid hymns of reformist triumph. This strange new tradition could only exist thanks to the youth organization Savonarola had founded two years prior. No longer were Florence’s boys and young men left to their own devices, running wild in the streets with foul tongues and crude amusements. Trained by Fra Domenico, Savonarola’s trusted lieutenant, these lads—called the future hope of Florence—were taught to speak no impure word, to burn with a zeal for the unseen good that shamed their lukewarm elders, to take no joy but the angelic kind: singing sacred hymns, processing in white robes. Rows of raised seats had been built against the Duomo’s walls for them, and Savonarola constantly preached to them that they were the chosen future glory of a city anointed to do God’s work.

These bright-cheeked troops were the backbone of the new pious Carnival, a sacred parody of the old unruly one. The old Carnival had bonfires? This new one had a bonfire that burned all impurity off the face of the earth. Old Carnival had symbolic processions? This new one had processions of white robes, red crosses, olive wreaths, emblems of peace and innocent joy, with banners and icons held aloft to celebrate the triumph of goodness. Old Carnival had ring dancing in the open Piazza air to loose, bawdy songs? This new one had ring dancing too, monks and lay folk dancing side by side in fraternal divine joy, their voices lifted in hymns. The old Carnival collected alms from passersby for gluttonous excess and feasts? This new one collected far more, all for the hungry and destitute, alongside collecting the *Anathema*—the Vanities destined for the great pyramid bonfire.

Squads of these young self-appointed inquisitors went door to door, demanding households surrender their vanities. After the obvious public vanities were handed over, what if the matriarch of the house still had little red pellets from the Levant, meant to paint a sallow cheek with a false, deceptively innocent bloom? She was to bring them down and cast them into the basket of doom. What if she had coils of “dead hair” to hide the gray streaks of age under a ghastly mockery of lost youth? She was to bring those curls to her front door in her hands, not on her head, and publicly renounce the Anathema. As a reward, the fresh young voices of the boys would call down a blessing on her and her whole house.

These beardless little regiments threw themselves eagerly into the work. Coercing people, through shame and gentle spiritual harassment, to hand over trinkets they would grieve to lose, is a brand of piety that comes very naturally to boyhood. And if some stubborn, wicked man flew into a rage and threatened them with a whip or cudgel? That only made the work more exciting. Savonarola himself struggled with the flaws of this youth training, as all men who chase noble great ends do, when they lack a clear path and grasp at supernatural zeal as the only sure route. Florentine youth had once had terrible habits and foul tongues: at first, it seemed a pure unalloyed good to get them shouting *“Viva Gesù!”* But Savonarola was eventually forced to correct them from the pulpit: “You shout ‘Viva Gesù!’ far too often. This constant repetition of sacred words will make them trivial, make them an object of contempt. I will hear no more of this shouting until the next high feast day.”

Even so, that morning, when the long procession of white-robed youth, bearing their small red crosses and olive wreaths, filed into the Duomo at dawn to take communion from Savonarola’s own hands, it was a sight of unearthly beauty. Many of those young souls would carry memories of that awe and hope for the rest of their lives, saving them from ever settling for a trivial, vulgar view of their duty as men and citizens. Any deliberate, disciplined obedience is an improvement over unbridled lawlessness, and these very boys would grow to be the generation of men who fought and endured heroically through the Republic’s final, desperate struggle. Right now, between that early communion and their midday meal, they were making their last rounds to collect alms and remaining vanities—exactly why Romola saw the slim white figures flitting around the base of the great pyramid.

“What do you make of this folly, Madonna Romola?” a brusque voice cut in right at her ear. “Your Piagnoni will turn hell itself into a desirable prospect for us, if they keep running things this way on earth. It’s enough to make a man grab a cudgel and storm over the mountains, to see painters like Lorenzo di Credi and that young Baccio over there helping burn all the color out of life with this nonsense.”

“My good Piero,” Romola said, looking up to smile at the gruff old painter, “even you must be glad to see some of these things burned. Look at all those gewgaws, wigs, and rouge-pots: I’ve heard you rail against those things as indignantly as Fra Girolamo himself.”

“So what?” Piero snapped, spinning sharply on her. “I never said a woman should scrub herself plain to blend into the drab background. Pah! Madonna Antigone, it’s a crime for a woman with hair and shoulders like yours to buy into this nonsense—leave it to women who aren’t worth painting. The most Holy Virgin herself has always been properly dressed, that’s Church doctrine! And don’t talk to me about heresy! I’d like to know what your father, the excellent Messer Bardo, would have said to these friars burning the divine poets, men no more fit to imitate real humanity than an onion stuck root-down on its top. Look at that Petrarch perched right next to a rouge-pot: do these idiots seriously claim that the heavenly Laura was some painted harridan? And Boccaccio now—you, Madonna Romola, you who are fit to be the model for a wise Saint Catherine of Egypt—are you going to stand there and tell me you’ve never read the stories of the immortal Messer Giovanni?”

“It’s true I have read them, Piero,” Romola said softly. “I read many of them over and over when I was a little girl. I used to sneak the book down from the shelf when my father was napping, so I could read to myself.”

“Ebbene?” Piero’s tone was fiercely challenging.

“There are parts of them I never want to forget,” Romola admitted. “But you must confess, Piero, that so many of those stories center on cheap deceit for the cheapest, lowest ends. Men don’t need books that teach them to treat vice as a trivial joke, as if all life is nothing but a silly farce. I can’t blame Fra Girolamo for teaching us our time is meant for far better things.”

“Yes, of course it’s easy to say that now that you’ve gotten all the pleasure of reading them,” Piero said bitterly, turning on his heel to storm away.

Romola walked on, smiling at the painter’s petty anger, with soft fondness for his outrage—she knew her father would have felt much the same. For her own part, she felt no inner friction with the strict, sombre view of pleasure that sought to stamp out vice even if it must smother poetry in the process. Sorrow and joy each have their own narrow blind spots. A religious enthusiasm like Savonarola’s, one that would eventually bless all of humankind by driving the soul to feel deep sympathy for suffering, boil over with indignation at injustice, and cast off the chains of sensual greed, will always be accused of denying life’s small joys. Romola’s own life had taught her to lean into sadness, a bias that made her unconsciously dismiss lighthearted merriment. That subtle refinement of the mind we call Taste had been completely overruled by her hunger for deeper, meaningful purpose, the same way a finicky, delicate palate vanishes completely when a man is starving. She moved constantly through scenes of widespread suffering, and carried the heaviest disappointment a woman could bear in her heart—so this brand of severity, stitched to selfless, generous strength, felt no discord to her at all.
