“In prison, sir? Good lack—for what misdeed?”

“Well, she is a witness, and may be a necessary one.”

“Why, Messire Bailiff,” Denys broke in, smooth as warm butter on trenchers, “you lay not all your witnesses by the heels, I trow.”

The alderman—pleased to be titled bailiff, as a man is pleased when a fine spice is named in his honor—grew looser of tongue. “In a case of blood we detain all testimony that is like to give us leg-bail, and so defeat justice; and that is why we still keep the women folk. For a man at odd times hides a week in one mind; but a woman, if she do her duty to the realm o’ Friday, she shall undo it afore Sunday, or try. Could you see yon wench now, you should find her a-blubbering at having betrayed five males to the gallows. Had they been females, we might have trusted to a subpoena. For they despise one another. And there they show some sense. But now I think on’t, there were other reasons for laying this one by the heels. Hand me those depositions, young sir.”

He set his glasses upon his nose as carefully as a cook sets a pot to simmer, and began to read, lips moving to each line. “Ay! she was implicated; she was one of the band.”

A loud denial sprang from Denys and Gerard together, like two staves struck at once.

“No need to deave me,” said the alderman. “Here ’tis in black and white. ‘Jean Hardy (that is one of the thieves), being questioned, confessed that—humph? Ay, here ’tis—‘And that the girl Manon was the decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band; and hanged last month: and that she had been deject ever since, and had openly blamed the band for his death, saying if they had not been rank cowards, he had never been taken; and it is his opinion she did but betray them out of very spite—and—’”

“His opinion!” Gerard flamed, indignation ringing in his voice like a clapper in a bell. “What signifies the opinion of a cut-throat, burning to be revenged on her who has delivered him to justice? And an you go to that, what avails his testimony? Is a thief never a liar? Is he not aye a liar? and here a motive to lie—revenge—why, ’tis the strongest of all the passions. And oh, sir! what madness to question a detected felon and listen to him lying away an honest life—as if he were a true man swearing in open day, with his true hand on the Gospel laid!”

“Young man,” said the alderman, “restrain thy heat in presence of authority! I find by your tone you are a stranger. Know then that in this land we question all the world. We are not so weak as to hope to get at the truth by shutting either our left ear or our right.”

“And so you would listen to Satan belying the saints!”

“Ta! ta! The law meddles but with men and women, and these cannot utter a story all lies, let them try ever so. Wherefore we shut not the barn-door against any man’s grain. Only having taken it in, we do winnow and sift it. And who told you I had swallowed the thief’s story whole like fair water? Not so. I did but credit so much on’t as was borne out by better proof.”

“Better proof?” Gerard stared, the blood in his cheeks cooling. “Why, who but the thieves would breathe a word against her?”

“Marry, herself.”

“Herself, sir? What—did you question her too?”

“I tell you we question all the world. Here is her deposition; can you read? Read it yourself, then.”

Gerard took the paper, and, leaning so Denys could see, read the words that smelt of ink, fear, and stale stone walls:

“I am a native of Epinal. I left my native place two years ago because I was unfortunate: I could not like the man they bade me. So my father beat me. I ran away from my father. I went to service. I left service because the mistress was jealous of me. The reason that she gave for turning me off was, because I was saucy. Last year I stood in the marketplace to be hired with other girls. The landlord of ‘The Fair Star’ hired me. I was eleven months with him. A young man courted me. I loved him. I found out that travellers came and never went away again. I told my lover. He bade me hold my peace. He threatened me. I found my lover was one of a band of thieves. When travellers were to be robbed, the landlord went out and told the band to come. Then I wept and prayed for the travellers’ souls. I never told. A month ago my lover died.

“The soldier put me in mind of my lover. He was bearded like him I had lost. I cannot tell whether I should have interfered, if he had had no beard. I am sorry I told now.”

The sheet seemed to weigh more than parchment ought; it nearly slipped from Gerard’s fingers. Now, at last, he heard—beneath the alderman’s dry voice and the scrape of quills—the sentence humming like a rope under strain. Manon’s life stood in the very draft of death.

He dropped to his knees, not as a courtier kneels, but as a man flung down by a sudden blow. “Oh, sir! think of the difference between those cruel men and this poor weak woman! Could you have the heart to send her to the same death with them; could you have the heart to condemn us to look on and see her slaughtered, who, but that she risked her life for ours, had not now been in jeopardy? Alas, sir! show me and my comrade some pity, if you have none for her, poor soul. Denys and I be true men, and you will rend our hearts if you kill that poor simple girl. What can we do? What is left for us to do then but cut our throats at her gallows’ foot?”

The alderman was tough, yet mortal; Gerard’s shaking fervor first amazed him, then found a tender place like a knife finding a joint. He showed it oddly—by turning peevish, as if moved against his will. “There, get up, do,” said he. “I doubt whether anybody would say as many words for me. What ho, Daniel! go fetch the town clerk.”

The clerk entered from the next room, smelling of wax and ledger dust; a man built of refusals.

“Here is a foolish lad fretting about yon girl,” said the alderman. “Can we stretch a point? say we admit her to bear witness, and question her favourably.”

The clerk shook his head as steadily as a mill-wheel. “Nay, sir, we cannot do that: she was not concerned in this business. Had she been accessory, we might have offered her a pardon to bear witness.”

“But she did better,” Gerard cried. “Instead of being accessory, she stayed the crime; and she proffered herself as witness by running hither with the tale.”

“Tush, young man, ’tis a matter of law.”

They argued long: the alderman pushing, the clerk barring every opening with “cannot,” “may not,” “is not done.” And this, by a turn of temper as sudden as a gust in a chimney, proved Manon’s salvation; for the alderman, nettled by so much denial, struck the table and swore he would show the man he could do anything he chose.

So Manon was brought out. Her eyes were raw as if washed in brine; her face had the whiteness of flour before it is wetted, and the tremble of a small creature held too long. Upon the landlord of “The White Hart” becoming bondsman, and Denys laying down five gold pieces—bright, round suns fallen into a clerk’s palm—and upon Manon promising (after Denys coaxed her, voice low, almost like a lullaby) to attend as witness, the alderman released her.

Yet, to ease his conscience, he must season his mercy with a coarse jest, like a man sweetening sour wine with honey and calling it charity. “The town had to buy a new rope for everybody hanged, and present it to the bourreau, or compound with him in money: and she is not, in my opinion, worth this municipal expense—whereas decided characters, like her late confederates, are.”

Thus they carried her off: Gerard capering round her in sheer relief; Denys speaking briskly of a troublesome personage’s speedy demise, as if to prop her courage the way one props a failing fire with kindling. But Manon wept—soft, miserable drops that foretold nothing fair.

The street had its own breath and ear, as a village has: the clop of hooves, the rasp of wheels on cobbles, the shrill cries that pass from mouth to mouth faster than any runner. Word slid ahead of them. Before they reached “The White Hart,” folk had found her out; and, having heard the tale from the archers—who told it warmly in her favour—followed her with hurrahs and rough encouragement. The sound of it, swelling behind her like a tide, steadied her; being backed by numbers, she dared lift her head.

The landlord, too, measured the thing in his own scales: not of justice, but of custom. A heroine under his roof would draw drinkers like the scent of roasting meat draws hungry men. He received her politely, and gave her an upper chamber—high, safe, and yet a cage. There she shut herself away, and, alone, let tears run again, as water runs when no hand turns the sluice.

Her mind was but a little ripple: up and down, down and up—now bold with the street’s shouts in her ears, now sunk again in private dread. Gerard and Denys bade the landlord be kind to her, and keep her a prisoner without letting her feel it; then they went out.

And lo, as they stepped into the street, two processions came toward them from opposite ends, like two currents meeting where the channel narrows.

One was large, loud, thick with noise—howls, hoots, and those cries by which rude throats confess, now and then, their kinship to beasts of field and forest: a pack-sound, hungry for spectacle. The other was thin: a few nuns and friars, slow and silent, feet whispering, beads clicking faintly like small bones.

The prisoners were going to exposure in the market-place.

The gathered bones of the victims were coming to the churchyard.

The two met in a narrow street almost at the inn door, and could not pass. The bier that bore the relics of mortality wedged against the cart that carried the men who had made those bones what they were—and in a few hours must die for it themselves. For a space the crowd’s mind, dulled by habit, did not take the meaning. Then a woman—sharp-voiced as a jay—cried, “Look at your work, ye dogs!”

The cry flew. A horrible yell rose, a whole town’s throat turned into one. The culprits groaned and tried to hide their heads upon their bosoms, but could not, their hands being tied. There they stood—pale, hollow-eyed—despair made visible. How their eyes clung to the bier; how they envied those bones, already past fear, already beyond jeers, already out of the market’s noise and into silence.

Denys and Gerard, the cause of both processions, stood and looked. Even Manon, hearing the uproar, crept to her window; and, like women will, hid her face and peeped trembling through her fingers.

That meeting parted the two friends. Denys yielded to curiosity and revenge and went with the noisy crowd. Gerard doffed his bonnet and, with a piety that sat in him as naturally as hunger, followed the poor remains of those whose fate had so nearly been his own.

For a time he was the one lay mourner, solitary amid the thin file. But when they reached the suburbs, far from the louder lure of the market-place, others began to join: an artisan wiping his hands and dropping his tools, a shopman shutting his door, men drawn by pity, by common humanity, and perhaps by the example of Gerard—bareheaded now, and grave. Together they watched the bones laid, with the Church’s prayers, in hallowed ground. The air there was different: the coarse roar of town life dulled to a distant murmur; what you heard was footfall, prayer, and the soft scrape of earth—sounds that do not mock the dead.

When the rites were done, Gerard stepped to the curé and offered to buy a mass for their souls.

Gerard, son of Catherine, ever looked at two sides of a penny; so he tried, with the nimblest conscience, to purchase the mass a trifle under the usual terms, pleading the pitiable circumstances. But the good curé parried him gently and adroitly, like a man turning aside a small knife with the flat of a spoon, and screwed him up to the market price with a bland smile.

In the bargaining they found a likeness of spirit. Piety and worldly prudence do keep company, though not often do they walk so arm in arm as in these two. Their collision in the prayer-market bred esteem, as when knight meets knight worthy of his steel. Moreover, the curé loved a morsel of gossip; and finding his customer was one who had fought the thieves at Domfront, he drew him into the parlour to hear all from his own lips.

His heart warmed. “God was good to thee,” said he. “I thank Him for’t with all my soul. Thou art a good lad.” Then, dryly, as if pinching out a candle: “Shouldst have told me this tale in the churchyard. I doubt I had given thee the mass for love. However—’tis ill luck to go back upon a bargain. But I’ll broach a bottle of my old Médoc for thee: and few be the guests I would do that for.”

He went to a cupboard, and while he groped for the choice bottle he muttered, half to himself, “At their old tricks again!”

“Plaît-il?” said Gerard.

“I said nought. Ay, here ’tis.”

“Nay, your reverence. You surely spoke: you said, ‘At their old tricks again!’”

“Said I so in sooth?” The curé smiled, and set to work with practiced hands, drawing the cork so the sound was a soft, satisfying sigh, like a loaf cracking as it cools. He filled a cup for each. Then he put a log on the fire—for stoves were none in Burgundy—and the flames caught with a low crackle, a homely sound that made the room feel inhabited, safe.

“And so I said ‘At their old tricks!’ did I? Come, sip the good wine; and, whilst it lasts, story for story, I care not if I tell you a little tale.”

Gerard’s eyes brightened. “As my life.”

“Nay, but raise not thine expectations too high neither. ’Tis but a foolish trifle compared with thine adventures.”

And the curé told his tale: of a poor priest in a stingy parish who, having found most of his baptised babes later cheated the Church by ending as pendards—hanged and “buried in air”—demanded burial fees at the christening table, taking greed by the sleeve and turning it back upon itself. How parishioners plotted, how a churchwarden complained, how the bishop summoned him with a frown; how the curé confessed the fact, then laid out his reason: to keep them from the gallows by making them fear paying fees twice. How the bishop laughed till tears ran, approved the stratagem, and later moved the curé to a better parish, where he might offer old Médoc to worthy guests.

As the tale ended, a light broke in on Gerard’s face; he saw the man behind the story.

“Ay,” said his host, meeting his look, “I am that curé. So now thou canst guess why I said ‘At their old tricks.’ My life on’t they have wheedled my successor into remitting those funeral fees. You are well out of that parish. And so am I.”

At that moment the curé’s little niece burst in, breathless. “Uncle, the weighing—la! a stranger!” and fled again as quickly, the very sound of her slippers darting away like a small bird’s panic.

The curé rose at once, yet would not loose Gerard. “Wet thy beard once more, and come with me.”

In the church porch they found the sexton with a huge pair of scales and weights of all sizes. Several humble folk stood by, their talk subdued by the place, though the world’s noises still leaked in—wheels in the street, a vendor’s cry thinning with distance.

Soon a woman stepped forward with a sickly child. “Be it heavy be it light, I vow, in rye meal of the best, whate’er this child shall weigh, and the same will duly pay to Holy Church, an if he shall cast his trouble. Pray, good people, for this child, and for me his mother hither come in dole and care!”

The child was weighed and yelled as if the scale had been the font, his thin cry echoing off stone, a little creature protesting the world’s measures.

“Courage! dame,” cried Gerard. “This is a good sign. There is plenty of life here to battle its trouble.”

“Now, blest be the tongue that tells me so,” said she, and hushed him against her breast, rocking as if her arms were a cradle and a prayer. She stood aside while another mother brought her child.

Presently a loud dictatorial voice cut the air. “Way there—make way for the seigneur!”

The small folk parted like waves before a lordly galley, and in strode the lord of the manor, gorgeous as a painted saint: cap with feather and a topaz at its root, furred jerkin, satin doublet, red hose, shoes like skates, diamond-hilted sword in velvet scabbard, and a hawk on his wrist. He flung himself into the scales as if he were lord of the zodiac too. The hawk balanced, flapped, then settled and winked.

While the sexton heaved in the great weights, the curé told Gerard, “My lord had been sick unto death, and vowed his weight in bread and cheese to the poor, the Church taking her tenth.”

The seigneur, pressing his staff on the other scale, grinned at the trick of it.

“Permit me, my lord,” said the curé, “if your lordship continues to press your lordship’s staff on the other scale, you will disturb the balance.”

His lordship removed it—but then leaned on it. The curé objected again, politely and firmly.

“Mille diables! what am I to do with it, then?” cried the seigneur.

“Deign to hold it out so, my lord, wide of both scales.”

When the seigneur did, and so fell into the trap that Holy Church had laid for him, the curé whispered to Gerard, “Cretensis incidit in Cretensem!”—diamond cut diamond. Then, aloud, with an obsequious air, “If that your lordship grudges Heaven full weight, you might set the hawk on your lacquey, and so save a pound.”

“Gramercy for thy rede, cure,” cried the great man, reproachful. “Shall I for one sorry pound grudge my poor fowl the benefit of Holy Church? I’d as lieve the devil should have me and all my house as her, any day i’ the year.”

“Sweet is affection,” whispered the curé.

“Between a bird and a brute,” whispered Gerard.

“Tush!” The curé looked frightened, as if words themselves could summon peril in sacred stone.

The seigneur’s weight was booked. And men—then as now—did not bargain only over wheat or cloth; they bargained with Heaven too, striving always for the sunny side. In that age, body being master, all sentiments took material form: repentance by scourge, prayer by bead, bribery by candle, sanctity by fish, and gratitude weighed out in bread and cheese down to the last ounce.

While this went on, the curé had been soliciting the seigneur to step into the church and give order what should be done with his great-great-grandfather.

“Ods bodikins! what, have you dug him up?”

“Nay, my lord, he never was buried.”

“What, the old dict was true after all?”

“So true that the workmen this very day found a skeleton erect in the pillar they are repairing. I had sent to my lord at once, but I knew he would be here.”

“It is he! ’Tis he!” cried the descendant, quickening his pace. “Let us go see the old boy. This youth is a stranger, I think.”

Gerard bowed.

“Know then,” said the lord, as they entered the porch, “that my great-great-grandfather held his head high; and being on the point of death, revolted against lying under the aisle with his forbears for mean folk to pass over. So, as the tradition goes, he swore his son—my great-grandfather—to bury him erect in one of the pillars of the church. ‘For,’ quoth he, ‘NO BASE MAN SHALL PASS OVER MY STOMACH.’ Peste!”

Even as he spoke, his lordship parried with his stick a skull that came hopping at him, bowled by a boy in the middle of the aisle. The child took to his heels yelling the moment he saw what he had done. The seigneur, in a gust of temper, flung the skull after him. The curé cried out and put forth his arm to hinder him—but too late.

The curé groaned aloud. As if the groan had loosed a nest of devils, up started a whole pack of children from some ambuscade; unseen, but heard loud enough, they clattered out of the church like a covey rising in a thick wood, the racket of their flight turning holy hush to a barnyard din.

“Oh! these pernicious brats,” cried the curé. “The workmen cannot go to their nonemete but the church is rife with them. Pray Heaven they have not found his late lordship; nay, I mind, I hid his lordship under a workman’s jerkin, and—saints defend us! the jerkin has been moved.”

His worst fear was true. The rising generation of plebeians had played mischief with the haughty old noble: jockeyed for the bones, pocketed such pieces as suited their rude games, scattered the rest with the careless cruelty of children who have not yet learned what a body once held.

“I’ll excommunicate them,” roared the curé, “and all their race.”

“Never heed,” said the scapegrace seigneur, stroking his hawk as one strokes a beloved beast. “There is enough of him to swear by. Put him back! put him back!”

“Surely, my lord,” pleaded the curé, “’tis your will his bones be laid in hallowed earth, and masses said for his poor prideful soul?”

The noble stroked his hawk again, as if weighing devotion in feathers. “Are ye there, Master Cure? Nay, the business is too old: he is out of purgatory by this time, up or down. I shall not draw my purse-strings for him. Every dog his day. Adieu, messires—adieu, ancestor!” And he sauntered off, whistling to his hawk and caressing it, his whistle sharp and bright in the vaulted air.

His reverence watched him go, rueful as a man who has set bread to rise and finds the oven cold.
