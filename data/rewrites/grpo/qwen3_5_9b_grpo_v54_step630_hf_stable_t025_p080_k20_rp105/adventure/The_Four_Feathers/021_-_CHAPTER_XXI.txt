Mrs. Adair watched the scene with a growing knot of anxiety in her stomach. She had spilled secrets to Durrance, and now she waited, trembling, to see how he would handle them. Part of her feared he would strip away the illusion, telling Ethne plainly that he had seen through her feigned affection for him to the true depth of her love for Feversham. If he did, he would fail to hide her role in the discovery, and Ethne would be forced to confront him, her eyes searching his face for the truth. It was a terrifying prospect. So, initially, she breathed a sigh of relief when nothing changed between Ethne and Durrance. They moved through the days as if the day Willoughby arrived and the evening Ethne played the *Musoline Overture* had never happened. But as the initial panic faded and the futility of her interference settled in, confusion gave way to anger and disappointment.

Durrance, however, had already decided on a course of action. Ethne wanted him to remain ignorant; in her distress, she found comfort in believing she had brought happiness to the one man she truly respected. Durrance saw no reason to shatter this fragile peace yet. He needed absolute certainty regarding the rift between Ethne and Feversham, and he needed to know Harry's fate before acting. So, he played the part of the oblivious friend. He dropped his scrutiny, feigned contentment, and made light of his own blindness, claiming Ethne's company was a sufficient compensation.

"You see," he told her, "one gets used to blindness and accepts it as natural. But one never gets used to you, Ethne. Every meeting reveals something new and delightful. Besides, there is always the possibility of a cure."

The strategy worked. Ethne sensed his suspicions had vanished, allowing her to mask her misery with his boundless cheerfulness. Yet her grief was profound. For a brief moment, she had felt the lightness of heart she possessed before the three white feathers arrived at Ramelton; now, she felt the heavy, dull ache that followed. There was a difference, though. Her pride was restored, and Durrance's words sparked a faint hope that Harry might yet be saved. But she knew the reality: the sleepless nights, the burning heat in her head as she waited for dawn. She could no longer pretend Harry was merely a dead friend. He was alive, trapped in unknown straits, and she thirsted to know his condition, her patience fraying at the edges.

"I suppose escape is possible from Omdurman," she said one day, forcing her voice to sound indifferent.

"Possible? Yes, I think so," Durrance replied cheerfully. "It is difficult and takes time. Attempts have been made to get Trench and others out, but they haven't succeeded yet. The problem is the intermediary."

Ethne looked sharply at him. "The intermediary?" she asked, then stopped herself abruptly. "I think I begin to understand. You mean the Arab who can move between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier?"

"Yes," Durrance confirmed. "Usually a Dervish pedlar or merchant trading with the Sudanese tribes. He slips into Wadi Halfa or Assouan or Suakin to arrange the exchange. The risk is immense; he would face swift execution if caught. So, it's no wonder he shirks the danger at the last moment. Often, he is a rogue. You make arrangements in Egypt, pay him, and six months or a year later, he returns alone with excuses. 'Summer was unfavorable,' or 'the guards were tighter,' or 'I was suspected.' He asks for more money. His story may be true, you pay again, and he returns empty-handed once more."

Ethne nodded slowly. "Exactly."

In that moment, Durrance had explained a crucial detail she had missed. She realized Harry Feversham must have planned to use himself as the intermediary. She couldn't determine how his capture served that aim, but now she understood: he intended to be his own go-between. Her hopes surged. He had planned with care; he would be desperate for the second feather to return to her, and if he could rescue Trench, he wouldn't stay behind.

They sat on the terrace as the sunset painted the creek water red.

"Life would not be easy in the prison of Omdurman," Ethne murmured, striving for indifference.

"Easy!" Durrance exclaimed. "No, it would be terrible. A hovel crowded with Arabs, no light, no air, the roof perhaps two feet above your head. Locked from sundown to morning. Likely, the prisoners would stand all night in that foul den, packed so tightly. Imagine it here in England on a night like this! Think what it would be on an August night in the Sudan! Especially if you had memories of a place like this to make the torture worse."

Ethne gazed across the cool garden. Right now, Harry Feversham might be gasping for breath in that dark, stinking cell, dry-throated and feverish, with visions of Ramelton's grassy slopes and the liquid music of the Lennon River playing in his ears.

"One would pray for death," Ethne said slowly, "unless..." She paused, about to add, "unless one went there deliberately with a purpose," but cut herself off. Durrance finished the thought.

"Unless there was a chance of escape," he said. "And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman."

He feared he had revealed too much about the horrors of the prison, so he added, "Of course, what I've described is hearsay, not proven fact. Prisoners may not have such a hard time," and dropped the subject. Ethne didn't pursue it. She wondered how Durrance knew about her sudden disappearance from the drawing room that night, but he never mentioned it. The noticeable shift in his demeanor, the lack of his usual caution, soothed her fears. It seemed he had found a simple explanation for himself. She also questioned why he had told her about the meeting in Wadi Halfa and Feversham's journey south, but she found a strange, satisfying answer: she believed the news was a message delivered by a higher power, meant solely for her understanding.

For the month of September, they maintained the pretense. Every morning, while Durrance was in Devonshire, he would cross the fields to meet Ethne at The Pool. Mrs. Adair, watching them laugh and talk without a trace of estrangement, grew increasingly furious, finding it harder to maintain her silence. It was a month of strain for all three. Everyone felt a wave of relief when Durrance announced visits to his oculist in London. These trips increased in frequency and duration. Even Ethne was grateful; she could drop the mask, feel exhaustion, and find solitude to gather strength for her high spirits when Durrance returned. There were hours when despair seized her. "Shall I be able to keep up the pretense when we are married, when we are always together?" she asked herself. But she pushed the question away, daring not to look ahead lest her strength fail.

After the third visit, Durrance said, "Do you remember that famous oculist at Wiesbaden? It seems advisable I go to him."

"You are recommended to go?"

"Yes, and to go alone."

Ethne looked up with a sharp glance. "You think I'd be dull at Wiesbaden? No fear of that. I can find a relative to accompany me."

"No; it is for my own account," Durrance answered. "I may have to enter a home. It is better to be quiet and see no one for a time."

"You are sure?" Ethne asked. "It would hurt me if you proposed this because you felt I'd be happier at Glenalla."

"No, that isn't the reason," Durrance replied truthfully. He felt it necessary for both of them to separate. Like Ethne, he suffered under the tyranny of constant simulation. Only because he knew how deeply she valued keeping her resolve intact—that two lives shouldn't be ruined by her—could he hold back from shouting the truth.

"I am returning to London next week," he added. "When I come back, I'll be in a position to tell you if I'm going to Wiesbaden or not."

Durrance was glad he had mentioned this plan before Calder's telegram from Wadi Halfa arrived. Ethne couldn't link his departure to any news about Feversham. The telegram came one afternoon; Durrance took it to The Pool that evening and handed it to her. There were only four words:

"Feversham imprisoned at Omdurman."

With a newfound delicacy born of his suffering, Durrance stepped away as soon as he gave her the paper and joined Mrs. Adair in the drawing room. He folded the telegram so that by the time Ethne unfolded it, she was alone on the terrace. She recalled his words about the prison, and her imagination expanded upon them. The quiet of a September evening hung over the fields; a light mist rose from the creek and crept over the garden bank. Already, the prison doors were shut in that hot land where the Niles met. "He is to pay for his fault ten times over," she cried in revolt against the disproportion. "And the fault was his father's and mine too more than his own. For neither of us understood."

She blamed herself for giving him the fourth feather. Leaning on the stone balustrade with her eyes closed, she wondered if Harry would survive the night, if he was still alive to endure it. The coolness of the stones beneath her hands became the bitterest reproach.

"Something can now be done."

Durrance emerged from the drawing room window, warning her of his approach. "He was and is my friend; I cannot leave him there. I shall write tonight to Calder. Money will not be spared. He is my friend, Ethne. You will see. From Suakin or Assouan, something will be done."

He credited all potential aid to his own friendship, ensuring Ethne believed he had no further personal stake in Harry Feversham's fate.

She turned to him suddenly, interrupting him. "Major Castleton is dead?"

"Castleton?" he exclaimed. "There was a Castleton in Feversham's regiment. Is that the man?"

"Yes. He is dead?"

"He was killed at Tamai."

"You are sure—quite sure?"

"He was within the square of the Second Brigade on the edge of the great gulley when Osman Digna's men sprang out of the earth and broke through. I was in that square, too. I saw Castleton killed."

"I am glad," Ethne said.

She spoke simply and distinctly. The first feather had been brought by Captain Willoughby. It was possible Colonel Trench might bring back the second. Harry Feversham had succeeded once under great difficulty and peril. The peril was greater now, the obstacles more arduous, but she understood that. She took that one success as an omen that another might follow. Feversham had planned with care; he had the money to execute his plans. Besides, she was a woman of strong faith. She was relieved to know the sender of the third feather could never be approached. Moreover, she hated him, and that ended the matter.

Durrance was startled. He was a soldier of a type rarer than war stories suggest: Hector of Troy was his ancestor. He was neither hysterical nor vindictive, not an elderly schoolboy with a taste for loud talk, but a quiet man who did his work without noise, stern when needed, yet gentle and compassionate by nature. This barbaric utterance from Ethne Eustace baffled him.

"You disliked Major Castleton so much?" he exclaimed.

"I never knew him."

"Yet you are glad that he is dead?"

"I am quite glad," Ethne said stubbornly.

She made another slip speaking thus of Major Castleton, and Durrance did not miss it. He remembered it, pondering it in his gun-room at Guessens. It added weight to the explanation he was building regarding Harry Feversham's disgrace and disappearance. The story was becoming clear to his sharpened wits. Captain Willoughby's visit and the token he brought had given him the clue. A white feather meant nothing but an accusation of cowardice. Durrance couldn't recall detecting any sign of cowardice in Harry Feversham; the charge perpetually struck him as incredible.

But the fact remained. Something had happened on the night of the ball at Lennon House, and from that date, Harry had been an outcast. Suppose a white feather had been forwarded to Lennon House and opened in Ethne's presence? Or perhaps more than one? Ethne had returned from her long talk with Willoughby clutching that white feather as though it were the most precious thing in the world.

So much Mrs. Adair had told him.

It followed, then, that the cowardice had been atoned for, or at least in one particular instance. Ethne's recapture of her youth pointed inevitably to that conclusion. She treasured the feather because it was no longer a symbol of cowardice, but a symbol of cowardice atoned.

But Harry Feversham had not returned; he still slunk in the world's by-ways. Willoughby was not the only one who had brought the accusation; there were two others. Durrance had long since identified one of them. When Durrance suggested Harry might be taken to Omdurman, Ethne had immediately replied, "Colonel Trench is in Omdurman." She needed no explanation for Harry's disappearance from Wadi Halfa into the southern Sudan. It was deliberate; he had gone out to be captured, to be taken to Omdurman. Moreover, Ethne had spoken of the untrustworthiness of the go-between, which again aided Durrance's conjectures. There was some obligation upon Feversham to come to Trench's help. Suppose Feversham had laid his plans for rescue and ventured into the desert to be his own go-between? It followed that a second feather had been sent to Ramelton, and that Trench had sent it.
