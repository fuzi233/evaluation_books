A week had passed since Gerard and Pietro had begun their uneasy partnership. The Italian worked in fits and starts—flinging himself upon his task with feverish energy, only to cast it aside moments later with a curse, then snatching it up again in a burst of frustration. Gerard, ever steady, worked with quiet diligence, his Dutch patience a stark contrast to Pietro’s tempestuous spirit.

Their labor now was humble—painting playing cards to keep hunger at bay. When the last strokes were finished, Pietro gathered his own unfinished masterpiece, preparing to make his daily rounds in search of a buyer. Gerard pressed him to take the cards as well.

Pietro’s face darkened, but after a moment, he embraced Gerard roughly in the Italian fashion and took them, first laying the freshly painted cards in the sun to dry.

Left alone, Gerard turned to mending a small tear in his hose. His landlady found him thus occupied and scoffed.

"Are there no women in this house to do such work?" she teased. "When you’ve finished, come speak with Teresa—the friend I told you of, the one whose husband boasts of knowing great men but does little else."

Gerard obeyed, and to his surprise, Teresa was none other than the Roman matron whose child he had saved.

"Ah, madama!" he exclaimed. "The good dame did not say it was you. And the little boy—is he well? Unharmed by his strange voyage?"

"He is well," Teresa answered, though her voice trembled.

The landlady stared between them. "What is this? You know each other?"

Teresa composed herself. "He saved my child’s life. And never spoke a word of it."

The landlady turned on Gerard. "Shame on you! Keeping such a thing from me!"

But Teresa laid a gentle hand on her friend’s arm. "Do not scold him." Then she poured forth the tale of Gerard’s courage, her words painting his deed in colors so bright he scarcely recognized it as his own. He stood there, flushing like a maiden, unused to praise.

"And to think you asked me to help your lodger," Teresa said to the landlady. "I would have done so for your sake alone, but now—" She turned to Gerard. "In serving you, I serve my own heart."

The women then fell into eager discussion—how to breach the fortress of fees and flatterers that stood between an unknown artist and Rome’s wealthy patrons. Teresa shook her head at Gerard’s naive hope that leaving his work at noble doors would suffice.

"Without bribing the servants? Without promising them a share? You might as well have thrown your art into the Tiber!"

Gerard sighed. "Then how is a poor artist to find a patron?"

"By seeking a city less greedy than this," Teresa said bitterly. "The Roman court wants no sheep without wool." She paused, thoughtful, then promised to return the next day.

The landlady, once Teresa had gone, clapped her hands. "She has a plan—I can see it!"

No sooner had she spoken than Pietro stormed in, his face thunderous. Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady and followed him upstairs.

"They let you bring your masterpiece home again?" Gerard asked gently.

"As always."

"Fools."

"That is not the worst of it," Pietro snarled.

"What then?"

"They bought the *cards*." His hands clenched, his whole body vibrating with outrage.

Gerard laughed. "All the better! They must have admired them greatly."

Pietro’s eyes flashed. "They fell upon them like wolves! Tried to hide their greed, but I saw it. I played them, I fleeced them—curse the dull-witted oafs!" He hurled a handful of silver coins to the floor and stomped on them, sending them skittering in every direction.

Gerard dropped to his knees, gathering the scattered coins with care. "You should laugh at their ignorance—and profit by it."

Pietro’s fury cooled into something darker. "So I shall. We’ll paint a pack a day. Let all Rome gamble itself to ruin—we’ll grow fat on its vices." Then his voice softened. "Though there was one queen I hated to part with—the one you painted. Hair like autumn, eyes like sapphires… and soul. You put your heart into her."

Gerard smiled faintly. "I did."

Pietro studied him. "You love her so much, yet you left her?"

Gerard’s voice was barely a whisper. "It is because I love her that I walked this long road."

Before more could be said, the landlady’s voice rang up the stairs. "Come down! You’re wanted!"

Gerard descended to find Teresa waiting.

"Come with me, Ser Gerard," she said.
