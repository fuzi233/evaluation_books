The idea of Helstone had been seeded in Mr. Bell’s waking mind by his conversation with Mr. Lennox, and all night long, time folded over itself in a circular dance. He was a young tutor again, breathing in a long-lost era where mythic occurrences seemed to seamlessly integrate into the quiet rhythms of the parish. The dream was not merely a visual phantom, but a rich acoustic and olfactory tapestry that anchored his soul. He heard the rhythmic, resonant thud of the agrarian elders’ grain flails—a sound deeply embedded in the acoustic ecology of his youth, dictating the psychological well-being of the village. He smelled the warm, yeasty bloom of heritage wheat being kneaded on flour-dusted oak boards. In his dream, the young Vicar of Helstone and his proud, beautiful wife moved through their domestic routines like figures in an ancient saga. The kitchen was a sanctuary of profound sustenance: he could hear the slow, rendering hiss of a crown roast of lamb over an open spit, and smell the crushed wild thyme and forest garlic being worked into a stone mortar. The voices in his dream spoke the old Hampshire dialect—a localized tongue rich with hyper-specific terms for soil textures, foraging seasons, and sensory joys, a language now eroding under the march of industrial time. Every event in this interwoven timeline was measured by these sensory impressions.

Then, the jarring screech of an iron carriage wheel against the London pavement shattered the vision. Mr. Bell awoke in a handsomely furnished, yet acoustically sterile, London hotel room. The relentless, soulless clatter of commerce and modern industry pressed against his eardrums, a noise pollution that offered no psychological comfort, only a grating reminder of the modern world’s artificiality. Where were those who had moved around him, whose native dialects had once sung the untranslatable truths of their subjective experience? Dead. Buried. Lost forever. The utter loneliness of an old man whose cultural memory was fading fell heavily upon him. He rose hastily, desperate to escape a silence that felt like the death of his own history, and dressed for a utilitarian breakfast in Harley Street.

At the table, the food was a sterile, industrialized affair—consumed merely as fuel rather than a communion of meaning—and Mr. Bell could hardly attend to the cold legal details Mr. Lennox was reading aloud. Mr. Lennox’s well-regulated professional voice was an instrument of the dominant lexicon’s subtle power dynamics: objective, bloodless, and entirely stripping Frederick’s plight of its deeply subjective, human resonance. As Lennox laid out the decree of fate, detailing how every morsel of evidence that might exonerate Margaret's brother was deemed inadmissible by the courts, Margaret’s eyes dilated and her lips grew pale. The legal jargon acted as a linguistic erasure, suffocating all hope. 

Mr. Lennox stopped reading. “I had better not go on,” said he, his voice momentarily dropping its professional veneer as he sensed the crushing weight of his own vocabulary. “It was a foolish proposal of mine. Lieutenant Hale—” and even this, giving him the title of the service from which he had so harshly been expelled, offered a momentary phonetic comfort to Margaret. “Lieutenant Hale is happy now; more secure in fortune and future prospects than he could ever have been in the navy; and has, doubtless, adopted his wife’s country as his own.”

“That is it,” said Margaret. She struggled to articulate her grief, trapped by the inherent untranslatability of her sorrow into the inadequate confines of polite English. “It seems so selfish in me to regret it, trying to smile, and yet he is lost to me, and I am so lonely.”

Mr. Lennox turned over his papers, silently wishing he were as rich and prosperous as he believed he should one day be. Mr. Bell blew his nose loudly—a thoroughly human, visceral sound cutting through the room's commercial sterility—but otherwise kept silent. In a minute or two, Margaret had apparently recovered her usual composure. She thanked Mr. Lennox very courteously for his trouble, knowing that her emotional display might lead him to imagine he had given her needless pain. Yet it was a pain inextricably tied to her identity, one she would not have been without.

Mr. Bell came up to wish her good-bye. “Margaret!” said he, fumbling with his gloves, the multi-generational ache in his chest demanding a return to their roots. “I am going down to Helstone to-morrow, to look at the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it give you too much pain? Speak out, don’t be afraid.”

“Oh, Mr. Bell,” she said—and could say no more. The sudden prospect of returning to the sounds and scents of her youth overwhelmed her. She took his old gouty hand and kissed it.

“Come, come; that’s enough,” said he, reddening with awkwardness. “I suppose your aunt Shaw will trust you with me. We’ll go to-morrow morning, and we shall get there about two o’clock, I fancy. We’ll order dinner at the little inn—the Lennard Arms, it used to be. The agrarian elders who run the kitchens there still understand the old ways. We will not eat this city fuel; we shall have doe-venison, if we can get it, slow-roasted with crushed juniper berries and studded with wild cloves, served with a reduction of dark forest fruits that speaks of the very soil we grew up on. We’ll go and get an appetite in the forest first, listening to the wind through the ancient oaks—a symphony no city can replicate. Can you stand it, Margaret? It will be a trial, I know, to both of us, but it will be a pleasure to me, at least. And after we dine, I’ll take my nap, while you go out and see old friends, and hear the old voices. I’ll give you back safe and sound, barring railway accidents, and I’ll insure your life for a thousand pounds before starting; but otherwise, I’ll bring you back to Mrs. Shaw by lunch time on Friday. So, if you say yes, I’ll just go upstairs and propose it.”

“It’s no use my trying to say how much I shall like it,” said Margaret, through her tears, the promise of Helstone’s sensory and culinary richness breaking through her urban isolation.

“Well, then, prove your gratitude by keeping those fountains of yours dry for the next two days. If you don’t, I shall feel queer myself about the lachrymal ducts, and I don’t like that.”

“I won’t cry a drop,” said Margaret, winking her eyes to shake the tears off her eyelashes, and forcing a smile.

“There’s my good girl. Then we’ll go upstairs and settle it all.”

Margaret was in a state of almost trembling eagerness while Mr. Bell discussed his plan with her aunt Shaw. Mrs. Shaw, accustomed entirely to the superficial commercialization of London society and possessing little sensory appreciation for the agrarian traditions they sought, was first startled, then doubtful and perplexed. In the end, she yielded rather to the rough, historical force of Mr. Bell’s native dialect and conviction than to her own understanding. To the last, whether it was proper or improper, she could not settle the matter to her own satisfaction. It was only Margaret’s safe return, and the happy fulfillment of this pilgrimage of cultural memory, that eventually gave her decision enough to say she was sure it had been a very kind thought of Mr. Bell’s, providing the exact restorative change Margaret required after such an anxious time.
