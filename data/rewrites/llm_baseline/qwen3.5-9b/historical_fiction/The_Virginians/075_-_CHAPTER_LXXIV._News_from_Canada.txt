Our relations at Castlewood kept us in their household until the new year commenced. After an absence of a fortnight—a span that felt like an eternity to the absurd and infatuated young man—I returned to the side of my beloved. Madame de Bernstein was relieved to depart from her father's home. Once we were away, she began to speak more freely. Regarding the intense confrontation between her and her niece, she offered no details. However, the old lady ceased speaking of forming *cette petite*, and when she did mention her, it was with a nervous laugh, devoid of genuine hostility toward the young Countess.

She remarked that her nephew, Eugene, was destined to be henpecked for the remainder of his days, observing that a touch of domestic order would benefit him immensely. She deemed the little, somewhat vulgar American gentleman a shrewd individual who would serve excellently as a steward. Concerning the Countess's mother, she noted she was likely a convict transported from England, having presumably endured hard labor in various prisons; yet, she argued this news need not be broadcast to the town crier. Ultimately, she posited that for certain types of individuals, lineage mattered little. "The young woman will be honest for her own sake now; she is shrewd and will learn English quickly," she declared. "And the name to which she has a right is great enough to secure her entry into any society. Whether one is the daughter of a grocer, a smuggler, or a slave-dealer—what does Mr. Van den Bosch's past profession matter? The Countess of Castlewood can afford to be anyone's daughter. As soon as my nephew produces her, it is our duty to stand by her."

Madame de Bernstein was deeply bound to her nephew by blood ties. Mr. Warrington hoped she would extend similar affection to his niece and spoke of his visits to Mr. Hagan and his wife, seeking her aunt's favor. Yet, the old lady remained obdurate regarding Lady Maria, begging that her name never be mentioned, and proceeded for two hours discussing everyone else. She recounted a series of anecdotes concerning her niece; since this book is open to all, including the young ladies and gentlemen of the family, I shall refrain from recording them. However, I must note that while the kind creature may have sinned, she was not alone in doing so, and if she repented, others should follow her example.

Hagan, after leaving the stage, lived an exemplary life, renowned for his elegance and eloquence in the pulpit. His wife adopted extreme religious views but commanded great respect within her sect. When I last saw her, she spoke of possessing a peculiar spiritual illumination, which I strongly suspected at the time resulted from the excessive consumption of alcohol. Nevertheless, I recall when she and her husband were kind to me and mine during times of need, when many a Pharisee would have turned away.

I have described how easily one could rise and fall in my fickle aunt's favor, noting how each of us brothers was alternately embraced and neglected. My turn of glory came following the success of my play. I was introduced to the town wits, held my place among them reasonably well, and was judged to be fairly well-bred by the macaronis and people of fashion. Had my purse been deeper, and had I chosen that path, I might have pursued a career among them. Instead, I loved a pair of kind eyes more than the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or Chudleighs, or all the painted beauties of the Ranelagh ring. Because I was fond of your mother, children, it was believed my tastes were low and deplored by my genteel family. Indeed, my godly Lady Warrington and my worldly Madame Bernstein laid their elderly heads together to lament my lifestyle.

"Why, with his name, he might marry anybody," said meek Religion, keeping one eye on Heaven and the other on the main chance. "I meddle with no man's affairs and admire genius," replied my uncle. "But it is a pity you consort with those poets and authors, and that sort of people, especially when you might have secured a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, only to let her slip and pursue a country girl without a penny-piece."

"But if I had promised her, uncle?" I asked.

"Promise, promise! These things are matters of arrangement and prudence requiring a careful lookout," he retorted. "When you first committed yourself to little Miss Lambert, you had not yet seen the lovely American lady your mother wished you to marry, as a good mother naturally would. Your duty to your mother, nephew—your duty to the Fifth Commandment—would have warranted breaking with Miss L. and fulfilling your excellent mother's intentions regarding Miss... What was the Countess's Dutch name? Never mind. A name is nothing, but a plum, Master George, is something to look at! I have my dear little Miley at a dancing school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell's daughter, and I do not disguise my wish that the children may develop an attachment enduring through their lives! I tell the Nabob so. We went from the House of Commons one dancing day and saw them. 'Twas beautiful to see the young things performing a minuet together! It brought tears to my eyes, for I have a feeling heart, George, and I love my boy!"

"But if I prefer Miss Lambert, uncle, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess with her hundred thousand pounds?"

"Why then, sir, you have a singular taste, that's all," said the old gentleman, turning on his heel and leaving me.

I understood his vexation at my inability to view the world as he did. Neither did Aunt Bernstein approve of the engagement I had made or the family with whom I spent so much time. Their simple ways wore thin and perhaps annoyed the old woman of the world; she relished their company no more than a certain person (who is not quite as black as he is painted) enjoys holy water. The old lady chafed at my constant dallying at my sweetheart's lap. Having risen mightily in her favor, I began to fall again. Once more, Harry was the favorite, and his brother, heaven knows, was not jealous. He was now our family hero.

He wrote us brief letters from the seat of war where he was engaged. Madame Bernstein cared little initially about the letters or the writer, finding them simple and the facts narrated uninteresting. We had earlier learned in London of the action on the glorious first of August at Minden, where Wolfe's old regiment was one of the British six contributing to that famous victory. At that very hour, the young General lay in his bed, in sight of Quebec, struck down by fever, and perhaps also by rage and disappointment at the setback his troops had just suffered.

Arriving in the St. Lawrence in June, the fleet carrying Wolfe and his army landed them on the last day of the month on the Island of Orleans, opposite which rises the great cliff of Quebec. Following the great action in which his General fell, the dear brother who accompanied the chief wrote home one of his simple letters, describing his modest share in that glorious day. He added nothing to the many existing descriptions of the action of September 13th, except that, according to the testimony of a brother aide-de-camp standing beside him, the General never spoke at all after receiving his death wound. Thus, the phrase often attributed to the dying hero is no more authentic than an oration of Livy or Thucydides.

From his position on the island, lying in the great channel of the river north of the town, the General was ever hungrily watching for a chance to meet and attack his enemy. Above the city and below it, he landed—now here, now there; bent upon attacking wherever he saw an opening. 'Twas surely a prodigious fault on the part of the Marquis of Montcalm to accept a battle from Wolfe on equal terms, for the British General possessed no artillery. When we executed our famous scaling of the heights and reached the Plains of Abraham, we were certainly a little nearer the city, though as far removed from entering it as before. The game played between the brave chiefs of those two gallant little armies, lasting from July until Mr. Wolfe won the crowning hazard in September, must have been as fascinating a match as ever eager players engaged in.

On the very first night after landing (as my brother has narrated), the sport began. At midnight, the French sent a flaming squadron of fireships down upon the British vessels discharging their stores at Orleans. Our seamen thought it good sport to tow the fireships clear of the fleet and ground them on the shore, where they burned out. As soon as the French commander heard our ships had entered the river, he marched to Beauport, in advance of the city, and took up a strong position. When our stores and hospitals were established, our General crossed from his island to the left shore, drawing nearer to his enemy. He had the ships in the river behind him, but the entire countryside facing him was armed. The Indians in the forest seized our advanced parties as they tried to clear the area, murdering them with horrible tortures. The French were as savage as their Indian allies. The Montmorenci River rushed between Wolfe and the enemy. He could neither attack these nor the city behind them. Determined to find another point of vulnerability, the General passed around the town of Quebec and skirted the left shore beyond. Everywhere was guarded, as well as in his immediate front. Having run the gauntlet of the batteries up and down the river, he returned to his post at Montmorenci.
