For generations, the Dashwoods had called Sussex home, their substantial estate centered upon Norland Park standing as a testament to their enduring reputation among neighbors and friends alike. For decades, the estate had been the domain of a widower who lived to a ripe old age, his household anchored by his devoted sister, a companion who shared his life until her passing ten years prior. Her death left a void that the old gentleman filled by welcoming his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood—the rightful heir—and his family into his heart and home. There, amidst the warmth of his nephew, niece, and grandchildren, the elder gentleman found solace. Their genuine kindness, devoid of mere calculation, and the infectious joy of the children brought him profound contentment, making his twilight years a season of cherished companionship.

Mr. Henry Dashwood had been blessed with a son from a previous union and three daughters from his current marriage. The son, a steady and respectable youth, stood secure thanks to his mother's ample fortune, half of which passed to him upon reaching maturity, augmented further by his own marriage. Consequently, the inheritance of Norland Park held little weight for him compared to its sisters. Their futures, however, hung precariously in the balance; their mother possessed nothing, and their father, while holding seven thousand pounds, lacked full control over it, as the remainder of his first wife's dowry was legally bound to their younger sibling.

Tragedy struck swiftly. The old gentleman departed, leaving behind a will that offered a mixture of sorrow and relief. Though he acted without malice, the terms he set effectively stripped the value of his gift in half. Mr. Dashwood, who had coveted the estate primarily for the sake of his beloved wife and daughters, found his hopes dashed. The property was entailed for the benefit of his grandson—a spirited boy of four whose charming, if noisy, antics had captivated his uncle's affections, overshadowing the quiet devotion of the aunt and nieces. While the uncle's benevolence shone brightly in the form of a thousand pounds apiece for the three girls, the ironclad restrictions on the land itself left Mr. Dashwood powerless to provide for those who needed it most. He could not sell the timber nor burden the estate with necessary charges.

Mr. Dashwood's initial shock was deep, yet his naturally cheerful spirit allowed him to harbor hopes of managing the estate frugally and securing a future. Alas, fate intervened before these plans could take root. He succumbed to his illness just twelve months after the reading of the will. His estate, once vast, dissolved into a mere ten thousand pounds, divided between his widow and her three daughters.

In his final moments, knowing his end drew near, Mr. Dashwood summoned his son, Mr. John Dashwood. With the urgency born of impending death, he implored the young man to ensure the welfare of his mother-in-law and sisters.

John Dashwood, a man of mild temperament and conventional respectability, felt the weight of his dying father's plea. He readily promised to do whatever fell within his power to render them comfortable. This assurance soothed his father's anxious heart, granting John a brief respite to calculate the limits of his generosity.

John was not inherently wicked, though his nature leaned toward self-preservation and a lack of fervent emotion. A man of propriety, he maintained his station through careful adherence to duty. Perhaps a kinder wife might have softened his edges, perhaps even awakened a deeper capacity for love within him, for he had indeed loved his young bride. Yet, Mrs. John Dashwood proved to be a starker mirror of his own limitations, embodying a sharper, more relentless version of narrow-mindedness and selfishness.

Before his father's funeral concluded, Mrs. Dashwood arrived at Norland with her infant and retinue, having taken no counsel with her mother-in-law regarding her intentions. Though the house now belonged to her husband by right of inheritance, her sudden appearance was deemed by many a breach of courtesy, a stark contrast to the gentle hospitality she had previously enjoyed. To a woman of ordinary feeling, such an affront would have caused lasting bitterness. However, Mrs. Dashwood possessed a peculiar, almost theatrical sense of honor and a romanticized notion of generosity. To her, any slight, whether given or received, became a source of immutable disgust, rendering her incapable of acknowledging the rudeness of others' actions.

Though never a favorite of her husband's family, she had thus far lacked the opportunity to reveal how little regard she held for their comfort. Now, her true colors emerged.

Mrs. Dashwood felt the sting of her son's ingratitude acutely, her disdain for his conduct growing with every passing hour. So profound was her resentment that she considered fleeing the house forever. It took the earnest entreaties of her eldest daughter, Elinor, to persuade her to pause and reconsider the propriety of leaving. Furthermore, her tender love for her three children ultimately anchored her to the scene, compelling her to remain and avoid a rupture with her brother.

Elinor, the eldest, possessed a mind of remarkable strength and a judgment that was cool and clear. At nineteen, she served as her mother's counselor, often steering her away from the impulsiveness that threatened to lead them astray. Her heart was noble and affectionate, her feelings deep, yet she mastered the art of governing them. Where her mother struggled, Elinor prevailed; where her mother faltered, Elinor stood firm.

Her youngest sister, Marianne, was equally gifted in intellect and spirit, yet her nature differed vastly from Elinor's. Marianne was eager in everything she undertook, her emotions ranging from sorrow to joy without measure. Generous, amiable, and captivating, she lacked the prudence that guided her older sister. The resemblance between Marianne and her mother was striking, both in spirit and in their tendency toward emotional excess.

Watching her sister, Elinor observed the dangerous depth of Marianne's sensibility with concern. Yet, her mother, in her grief, viewed this same trait with pride. Together, the mother and Marianne indulged in the violence of their mourning, seeking out further anguish in their reflections, deliberately rejecting any solace offered. They dwelt entirely upon their sorrow, feeding it with thoughts that only deepened their wretchedness, vowing never to accept comfort again.

Elinor, though deeply afflicted, retained a reservoir of strength. She could summon the fortitude to consult with her brother, to welcome his wife with due politeness, and to strive to rouse her mother from her despair. She endeavored to encourage her mother toward the same restraint and forbearance that she practiced so diligently herself, hoping to guide her family back from the precipice of ruin.

Then there was Margaret, the third sister, a girl of thirteen with a good-natured and well-meaning disposition. Having absorbed much of Marianne's romanticism without acquiring her sister's sharp intellect, she seemed unlikely to match her elders' capabilities as she grew older.
