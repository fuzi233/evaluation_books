The information Major Pendennis had gathered about the Clavering family—courtesy of Strong and his own meddling as a self-appointed guardian—was enough to make the old gentleman pause in his tracks. The idea of marrying his nephew, Arthur, to a woman with two fathers-in-law like the current occupants of Clavering Hall seemed less like a blessing and more like a trap. One was a convict who had recently taken a hatchet to an officer; the other was a man who would be strung up if caught. No sensible guardian would hand his ward a wife whose parents were currently being hunted by the law. Yet, Major Pendennis reasoned that Altamont, with the gallows looming so large, would surely avoid recognition, while Clavering, terrified of exposure, would become a willing slave to anyone holding the secret.

But there was a catch. The Begum, who had paid Clavering's debts a dozen times over, would soon find her treasury empty, spent entirely on this irreclaimable reprobate. Her heirs would inherit nothing but an empty vault and a pedigree tarnished by the "sus. per coll." attached to the last male of her line. Miss Amory, instead of bringing a fortune and a seat in Parliament, would bring only her person and a scandalous footnote to Arthur's name.

However, the old schemer in Pendennis's mind had another card to play. To understand it, one must witness a conversation that soon took place between the Major and the Honourable Baronet, the Member for Clavering.

When a man falls on hard times and vanishes from his usual circle, he tends to resurface in the strangest places. I have known a dandy of Pall Mall vanish from the clubs only to be found laughing and drinking at a pennyworth in Billingsgate; a scholar of great wit, fleeing the constable, sending his address to a pub called "The Fox under the Hill" in a dark archway of the Strand. Such men, when misfortune strikes, are rarely in their own homes. Only the faithful know where to find them.

Since his quarrels with his wife and the subsequent financial ruin, finding Sir Francis Clavering at home was impossible. "Ever since I lent him my book for fourteen pounds, he doesn't come home till three," Mr. Lightfoot told his friend Morgan. "He pretends to sleep when I bring his water in the morning and dodges out when I'm downstairs." Lightfoot planned to move to the house and marry his old woman. Similarly, after his fallout with Strong, the Baronet fled to other haunts, escaping the Chevalier's reproaches and, perhaps, his own conscience.

Thus, despite the Major's ulterior motives, it took considerable effort to corner the shy Baronet. Since the Major had become the family friend, Clavering treated him like a lawyer or an agent: avoiding him unless money was involved. The Major made several futile attempts. Once, he invited the Baronet to dinner at Greenwich to meet "friends"; the Baronet accepted, suspected a trap, and didn't show, leaving the Major to eat his whitebait alone. Another time, the Major requested ten minutes of talk; the Baronet agreed to meet at four o'clock the next day at Bays's, carefully underlining the word "precisely." Four o'clock arrived, as destiny decreed, but no Clavering appeared. If he had borrowed twenty pounds from Pendennis, he couldn't have been more timid.

Just as Strong's patron had been showering the Chevalier with blessings in front of his face and curses behind his back, Sir Francis Clavering, having sworn to his wife's advisers to sign no more bills, managed to sign a piece of stamped paper carried off by his friend, Mr. Moss Abrams. Strong, overhearing the transaction in the back-parlour of Mr. Santiago's cigar shop near St. James's Street, learned the truth.

"He's at his old work again," Mr. Santiago told a customer. "He and Moss Abrams were in my parlour. Moss sent my boy for a stamp. It must have been a fifty-pound bill. He told Moss to date it two months back. He'll pretend it's an old bill he forgot about during his settlement with his wife. I dare say they'll give him more money now he's clear." A man who signs "promises to pay" at six months knows his signature is circulating among London's worst knaves.

As the Major walked daintily toward his lodgings, Strong, smelling of Havana tobacco and burning with wrath, strode past him. "Confound these young men," the Major thought, eyeing the cigar and mustache. "Every fellow who smokes and wears mustachios is a low fellow."

"Good evening, Mr. Strong?" the Major called out, making a dignified bow before entering his house.

"My news is bad, sir," Strong replied. "It concerns our friends at Tunbridge Wells. Clavering is at his old tricks again."

"Indeed! Come into my lodging," the Major cried, ushering the Chevalier into his drawing-room.

Strong unburdened himself of his indignation. "No promises bind him, sir," he said. "Remember when we met with the lawyer? He wouldn't be satisfied with his honour; he wanted to swear on his knees, ring for a Bible, and swear perdition on his soul if he gave another bill. He signed one today. He will sign as many as you please for ready money. He'll deceive his wife, his child, or any old friend who has backed him a hundred times. There's a bill of his and mine due next week."

"I thought we had paid all."

"Not that one," Strong blushed. "He asked me not to mention it. I had half the money for that, Major. They'll be down on me. But I don't care; I'm used to it. It's Lady Clavering that riles me. It's a shame that good-natured woman, who has paid him out of gaol a score of times, should be ruined by his heartlessness. A parcel of bill-stealing boxers get his money, and he doesn't scruple to throw an honest fellow over. Would you believe it, sir, he took money of Altamont?"

"Indeed? Of that singular man who came tipsy once to Sir Francis's house?" Pendennis asked, his face impenetrable. "Who is Altamont, Mr. Strong?"

"I don't know, if you don't," Strong answered, suspicious.

"To tell you frankly," said the Major, "I have my suspicions—mind, I only suppose—that in our friend Clavering's loose life, there are queer secrets he'd rather keep buried. Very likely this Altamont knows a story against Clavering and holds him to ransom. I know many men of the best families paying through the nose in that way. But their private affairs are no business of mine. And so with Clavering, I am most interested for his wife's sake and her daughter's, who is a charming creature. When her ladyship asked me, I looked into her affairs and tried to set them straight. And if I am called upon—and by the way, this Mr. Altamont? How is he? Are you acquainted with him? Is he in town?"

"I don't know that I am called upon to know where he is, Major Pendennis," Strong said, rising in dudgeon, offended by the Major's patronizing caution.

Pendennis's manner shifted instantly from hauteur to knowing good humor. "Ah, Captain Strong, you are cautious too, I see; and quite right. We don't know what ears walls may have. As a man of the world and an old soldier, you know there is no use throwing away your fire. You may have your ideas, and I may put two and two together. But there are things many men had better not know, eh, Captain? And I, for one, won't know them until I have reason. That seems to be your maxim too. Regarding the Baronet, I think it advisable he be checked. I strongly reprehend any departure from his word that causes pain to his family. That is my full and frank opinion, and I am sure it is yours."

"Certainly," Strong said dryly.

"I am delighted," Pendennis said, extending a hand. "Delighted that an old brother soldier agrees with me. Good evening. Thank you. Morgan, show the door to Captain Strong."

Strong, preceded by the valet, took his leave, puzzled by the Major's sudden reticence. Morgan, however, had stood close to the door, overhearing much of the talk.

"Who is that Altamont?" Morgan asked Lightfoot later at the Club.

"Strong's man of business," Lightfoot replied. "He draws the Governor's bills and indorses them. That kite-flying always takes two or three to set the paper going. Altamont put the pot on at the Derby and won a good bit. I wish the Governor could get some somewhere, and I could get my book paid up."

"Do you think my Lady would pay his debts again?" Morgan asked. "Find out for me, Lightfoot, and I'll make it worth your while."

Major Pendennis often joked that his valet Morgan was richer than himself. Indeed, through careful speculation, the silent attendant had amassed a considerable sum. When Arthur Pendennis finally inherited his property, Morgan surprised him by offering to lend him fifty or a hundred pounds for investment. Arthur, knowing nothing of finance, politely declined, but mentioned the offer to his uncle.

"It was then," the Major told Arthur, "that I believed Morgan had been getting devilish rich for a devilish long time. In fact, he bought the house in Bury Street where I lodge and made a considerable sum by buying up as many of the Baronet's acceptances as he could raise money to purchase. Of these transactions, I knew no more than most gentlemen know of their servants."

"So he offered to lend you money, did he?" the Major remarked to his nephew. "He's a devilish sly fellow and a devilish rich fellow. Many a nobleman would like such a valet. He ain't a bit changed. He does his work just as well as ever—he's always ready to my bell, steals about the room like a cat, and is so devilishly attached to me."

On the day of Strong's visit, the Major bethought himself of Pen's story and rallied the valet regarding his wealth.

"I hear you have got some money to invest, Morgan," the Major said.

"It's Mr. Arthur has been telling, hang him," the valet thought.

"I'm glad my place is such a good one."

"Thank you, sir—I've no reason to complain," Morgan replied demurely.

"You're a good fellow. I believe you are attached to me. I'm glad you get on well. And I hope you'll be prudent, not taking a public-house or that kind of thing."

A public-house? Me in a public-house! The old fool! Dammy, if I was ten years younger, I'd set in Parliament before I died.

"No, thank you kindly, sir. I don't think of the public line. And I've got my little savings pretty well put out, sir."

"You do a little in the discounting way, eh, Morgan?"

"Yes, sir, a very little—I—I beg your pardon, sir—might I be so free as to ask a question?"

"Speak on, my good fellow," the Major said graciously.

"About Sir Francis Clavering's paper, sir? Do you think he's any longer any good? Will my Lady pay on 'em any more, sir?"

"What, you've done something in that business already?"

"Yes, sir, a little," Morgan replied, dropping his eyes. "And I don't mind owning, sir, and I hope I may take the liberty of saying, sir, that a little more would make me very comfortable if it turned out as well as the last."

"Why, how much have you netted by him, in Gad's name?" asked the Major.

"I've done a good bit, sir, at it: that I own, sir. Having some information and made acquaintance with the family through your kindness, I put on the pot, sir."

"You did what?"

"I laid my money on, sir—I got all I could, and borrowed, and bought Sir Francis's bills. Many of 'em had his name, and the gentleman's as is just gone out, Edward Strong, Esquire, sir: and of course I know of the blow-hup and shindy as is took place in Grosvenor Place, sir: and as I may as well make my money as another, I'd be very much obleeged to you if you'd tell me whether my Lady will come down any more."

Although the Major was as surprised as if he'd heard Morgan was a disguised Marquis, and indignant at the audacity of the fellow growing rich under his nose, he felt a natural admiration for money and success. He found himself respecting Morgan, and rather afraid of that worthy.

"Well, Morgan," he said, "I mustn't ask how rich you are; and the richer the better for your sake, I'm sure. And if I could give you any information that could serve you, I would speedily help you. But frankly, if Lady Clavering asks me whether she shall pay any more of Sir Francis's debts, I shall advise and I hope she won't, though I fear she will—and that is all I know. And so you are aware that Sir Francis is beginning again in his—eh—reckless and imprudent course?"

"At his old games, sir—can't prevent that gentleman. He will do it."

"Mr. Strong was saying that a Mr. Moss Abrams was the holder of one of Sir Francis Clavering's notes. Do you know anything of this Mr. Abrams; or the amount of the bill?"

"Don't know the bill, know Abrams quite well, sir."

"I wish you would find out about it for me. And I wish you would find out where I can see Sir Francis Clavering, Morgan."

Morgan said, "Thank you, sir, yes, sir, I will, sir," and retired with his usual stealth
