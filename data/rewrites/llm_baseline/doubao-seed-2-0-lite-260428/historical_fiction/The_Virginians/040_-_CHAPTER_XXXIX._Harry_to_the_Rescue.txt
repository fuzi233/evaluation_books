“My dear Lord March” (wrote Mr. Warrington from Tunbridge Wells, on Saturday morning, the 25th August, 1756): “This is to inform you (with no small satisfaction) that I have won all three of our wagers. I pulled into Bromley two full minutes inside the agreed hour; my new pair of horses kept a capital pace the whole way. I drove them myself, with the postilion perched beside me to point out the turns, and my black servant inside the chaise with Mrs. Betty. I trust the pair found the journey no hardship. We were hailed by two highwaymen on Blackheath, but one look at our determined countenances sent them galloping off without daring to halt us, and we rolled into Tunbridge Wells, where I transacted all my urgent business, at forty-five minutes past eleven o’clock. This makes us quits, after yesterday’s piquet game, and I shall be delighted to give you your revenge whenever you please. I remain, Your most obliged and faithful servant, H. ESMOND WARRINGTON.”

That wild, breakneck dash across Kent is the very trick that freed Lady Maria Esmond from her unexpected debtors’ captivity. She had sent Mrs. Betty racing to London to beg Harry’s aid, never dreaming the woman could return the very same night of her departure. Midnight had fallen, and she and Parson Sampson were whiling away the hours over cards and a meagre supper the bailiff’s wife had scraped together, when the thunder of speeding carriage wheels came roaring up the lane. Maria dropped her trump cards mid-play, her heart hammering so loud she could scarce hear her own breath.

The chaise skidded to a halt right at the door. A few quick words passed at the gate, and in burst Mrs. Betty, cheeks streaming with happy tears, radiant as a sunrise. And behind her, tall, broad-shouldered, his riding boots caked with London mud, was Harry Warrington. The chaplain slapped his cards down with a wild huzzay, while Lady Maria turned white as a sheet, rose tottering to her feet, and with one hysterical little sob, flung herself straight into her cousin’s outstretched arms. What matter if he showered her with a thousand kisses, then a hundred, then a thousand more after the old Catullan fashion? No one shall call us to account for counting them. She knew he would come, her champion, to pluck her from the shame and ignominy of bondage. She wept so hard against his shoulder that her fine lace collar was soaked, and for once, every tear was genuine. She never looked more beautiful than she did in that moment, the author swears.

Harry was on cloud nine. “You are both free,” he told them, grinning. “Betty, fetch my lady’s hat and cardinal cloak. Chaplain, we’ll smoke a pipe back at my lodgings, that long ride has left me parched for refreshment.” Sampson, whose soft, impressionable nature was given to easy bursts of emotion, broke down entirely, seizing Harry’s hand to kiss it over and over, praying God to heap blessings on his generous young patron. Harry felt that warm, glowing thrill that comes only when you have the power to turn someone’s sorrow into joy, to lift the helpless out of trouble. He swaggered a little on the walk back to his lodgings, hat tilted jaunty over his eye, escorting his “rescued princess” like a conqueror fresh from a tournament, the world strung with laurel wreaths for him, trumpets blaring his praise. It was such a small thing he’d done, after all – only slipped the bailiff a handful of guineas to dispel the dragon guarding the door, send the tyrant of the house packing. A little vanity never hurt the good-natured lad; he bragged a little that night over their fine Burgundy, talked of London clubs, White’s and Almack’s, Lord March’s famous racehorses, like any fashionable young macaroni, and we can scarce hold it against him.

Sampson’s flattery knew no bounds that night. His whole life, as a wandering preaching parson, he’d trained his sensibilities to turn on a dime, to weep when a mourner wept, laugh when a tavern companion laughed, and this night his gratitude was so loud and fulsome it would have turned a wiser head than Harry’s. With every bumper he downed, he praised Harry as the noblest, finest gentleman alive, and Harry, simple soul, lapped every word up. “The younger branch of our family has used you scurvily,” he declared grandly, thumping the table. “But by Jove, Sampson, I’ll stand by you always! You’ve a little sister at boarding school, you said? Here’s a bank note to cover her fees, no thanks needed.”

Sampson froze, pushed the note back half an inch, and burst into fresh tears. “I don’t deserve your kindness, Mr. Warrington – no, by God, I don’t.”
“Pshaw,” Harry laughed. “I’ve plenty more. Only last week I lost that cursed pocket-book, had nary a farthing in it.”
“Ah – no, sir, there was no money in it,” Sampson mumbled, dropping his eyes.
Harry jolted upright. “How the devil would you know that, parson?”

It all spilled out then: Sampson had found the pocket-book the night Harry got too drunk at Barbeau’s tavern. He’d read all the letters inside, knew all the family secrets Harry had thought no soul but he and his brother George shared. He confirmed the ugly tales of Lady Fanny, and cousin Will’s reckless debts, all true. When Harry flew into a rage demanding to know if Sampson dared breathe a word against Lady Maria, the parson swore he never would, only added that in his humble opinion, Maria was far too old for a fine young gentleman like Harry, who deserved a wife his own age.

Harry ordered him to bring the pocket-book and remaining letters at once. But when Sampson returned from his chamber, he was as pale as a ghost. The pocket-book was there, he said – the one George had given Harry, with his name stitched on the cover – but the letters were gone. Someone had rummaged through his locked trunk while he was held at the bailiff’s house, and stolen them. The lodgings’ landlady swore no stranger had stepped foot in the rooms, Harry’s own things were untouched, no coin or clothing missing – only those few pieces of paper. Harry, still giddy from his successful wagers, his daring rescue, his fine vintage Burgundy, dismissed Sampson’s tearful pleas for forgiveness, waved off the whole mishap, pardoned the parson on the spot. By dawn they were both roaring drunk, toasting the Esmond family’s ancient glory, before they stumbled off to bed.

By noon the next day, a servant from Madame de Bernstein came to invite Harry over for chocolate. She greeted him with open arms, cooed over his new fine London-made clothes, thanked him profusely for rescuing dear Maria – then casually dropped that she’d had to send her steward Case galloping to London the very same day, to borrow three hundred pounds to cover her own card debts to Lady Yarmouth, she’d been stone broke. She laughed, and pressed a fifty-pound bank note on him, told him to go spend it on pleasure, that his mother the Virginian heiress would no doubt reimburse her. Then the mood shifted. She leaned across the table, sharp as a tack, and told him plainly he was a fool for getting himself betrothed to Maria – a woman of forty-two, as old as his own mother, who would gamble away every penny he had.

Harry’s face burned. “Who told you of my promise?”
The Baroness admitted the truth: when the bailiffs seized Maria, she fainted, and a little sachet holding Harry’s silly, flowery love letter slipped from her pocket. The Baroness had found it, read it – and burned it.

A jolt went through Harry. He ought to have been angry, but a wild, unplanned rush of relief flooded through him. His stupid letter, signed with oaths he’d written on his knees, gone. But his honour was too rooted in the Virginian code of chivalry to let him back out. “My word is given,” he said, standing firm. “Us gentlemen of Virginia do not break promises, not for all the gold in the Indies. If she holds me to it, I’ll marry her. If we end up wretched, I’ll take a musket and fight for the King of Prussia, and let a bullet end the trouble.”

The Baroness’s face crumpled. She curtsied stiffly, turned to leave – then her hand flew to her heart, and she sank back onto the sofa, weeping the first genuine, unselfish tears Beatrix Esmond had shed in more than fifty years. Harry knelt beside her, seized her cold hand, and kissed it, confessing how ashamed he was to admit he’d been glad when he heard she’d burned that foolish letter. The old worldly woman, whose hard heart had long been shielded from soft feelings, put her hands on his shoulders and kissed his forehead. “You will not tell Maria what I have done?” she whispered. He swore he never would, just as demure Mrs. Brett, her waiting woman, stepped through the door to find the two locked in that tender, sentimental moment.
