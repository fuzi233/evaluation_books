Gwendolen awoke the next morning radiant as a lily kissed by the dawn—yesterday’s doubts no more than ripples on a deep, swift current. The archery match in Cardell Chase promised freedom, laughter, the dappled shade of beech trees, and the thrill of sport. And if Grandcourt lingered near her, well, so much the better. He was no passionate shepherd, but passion was tiresome anyway. Today, she anticipated his slow, deliberate words circling toward a proposal, and she would welcome them—just as she had told her uncle she would.

At breakfast, a letter waited beside her plate. She read it with a secretive smile before passing it to her mother.

“They’ve left town,” Mrs. Davilow observed, returning the note. “Do you mean to reply?”

“Tomorrow will do. They’re bound for Dover.”

“Shall I write for you?”

Gwendolen sipped her coffee, then answered brusquely, “No, I’ll manage it.” But catching the flicker of hurt in her mother’s eyes, she softened. “Dear, beautiful mamma—*old* only because I adore you.”

“Old in truth, child.”

“Don’t say that!” Gwendolen’s voice sharpened. “Twenty-five years is nothing. When you speak like that, I feel my life shriveling before it’s begun.”

Mrs. Davilow sighed. “One can find joy in twenty-five years.”

“Then I’d best start at once,” Gwendolen countered, laughing. “The sooner I have my carriages, my palaces, the better.”

“And a husband who adores you.”

Gwendolen’s lips curved, but she said nothing.

---

The archery party gathered at Green Arbor, a sunlit hollow framed by ancient trees. The game was to be leisurely—strolling first, then a proper chase in the golden afternoon. Gwendolen moved through the dappled light with practiced grace, though her arrows went wide. Distracted, perhaps. Or perhaps she no longer cared to win.

Grandcourt remained at her side, his presence as unreadable as ever. No one could have guessed, from his manner, whether they were strangers or betrothed.

“Do you know how long it’s been since I first saw you in that dress?” he asked, as they wandered back toward the others.

Gwendolen laughed. “The meeting was the 25th. Today is the 13th. Even my poor arithmetic makes it nearly three weeks.”

“A great loss of time.”

“For you? Pray don’t insult me.”

“It’s the gain that makes me feel the loss.”

She left his words hanging, her silence more telling than any reply. He studied her, then murmured, “Do *you* like uncertainty?”

“I think I do,” she said, flashing him a smile. “There’s more amusement in it.”

His gaze fixed on hers, steady, unnerving. “More torment for me, you mean?”

The directness startled her. She flushed, turning away. “No. That would make me sorry.”

Had they been alone, he might have pressed further. But they were descending a steep slope now, and decorum demanded he offer his hand. She took it, pulse fluttering, and they rejoined the group in silence.

---

A letter found its way to her as the party dispersed—slipped into her palm as she retrieved her bow. A lady’s delicate script, a name unknown. She read it apart from the others, heart hammering.

*If Miss Harleth doubts, let her return to the Whispering Stones. She will hear what decides her. But she must tell no one.*

Gwendolen folded the note away, resolve hardening. A secret. An adventure. She would go.

---

The Whispering Stones loomed, two gray sentinels. Rounding them, she came face to face with a woman—dark-eyed, beautiful in a ravaged way. Two children played nearby.

“Miss Harleth?”

“Yes.”

“I am Lydia Glasher.” The name struck like a blade. “Grandcourt ought to marry no one but me. I left my husband for him. These are his children.”

Gwendolen’s gaze flicked to the boy—sunlit curls, a cherub’s face. Her stomach twisted.

“He should make that boy his heir,” Mrs. Glasher said, voice low and bitter. “It is not fair that he should be happy while I—”

“I will not interfere,” Gwendolen interrupted, cold as marble. Inside, she trembled.

A curt exchange, a parting. Gwendolen walked away, the world tilting beneath her feet.

---

She rejoined the party with brittle cheer, spinning a tale of wandering and lost bows. No one questioned her. But when Grandcourt appeared, unhurried as ever, her politeness was edged with frost.

“May I call tomorrow?” he asked.

“If you like,” she said, sweeping past him.

In the carriage, she turned to her mother. “I’m leaving for Dover. Tonight.”

“Gwendolen—!”

“I will not marry him. Don’t ask me why.”

Mrs. Davilow paled. “What will your uncle say?”

“I don’t care.” Gwendolen’s voice was flint. “I don’t care about anything.”

And for the first time, she almost believed it.
