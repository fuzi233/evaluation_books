When I awoke, the earth beneath me was damp, and the path leading back to the grave had turned to shifting quicksand. The ancient river was swelling in its bed, pushing against its banks with renewed force. Soon, it would roar over the precipice, splitting into two torrents—one to swallow the orchard valley whole, the other to potentially drown the monstrous horde—while leaving the Evil Wood isolated in the middle.

Without delay, I turned toward the place that had sent me. Reaching the edge of the cliff, I threaded my way through the hanging branches, determined to pass once more by Mara's cottage. I longed to catch a final glimpse of her before I surrendered to the sleep that awaited me, confident now that I could navigate the flooded channels even if the river had risen to meet me. Yet, when I arrived, the door stood ajar. Inside, the loaf of bread and pitcher of water remained untouched upon the table, bathed in a profound, heavy silence.

I paused, calling out loudly, but the response was nothing but the echo of my own voice. Disappointed, I moved on. A short distance further, I found a gray-haired man sitting on the sand, weeping openly.

"What troubles you, sir?" I asked gently. "Have you been abandoned?"

"I weep," he sobbed, "because they refuse to let me die. I have gone to the House of Death, and though my years are many, the Mistress denies me entry. If you know her, intercede for me!"

"Nay, sir," I replied softly. "I cannot. She refuses none whom it is lawful for her to receive."

"How do you know this?" he demanded, wiping his eyes. "You have never sought death yourself! You are far too young to desire it!"

"I fear your words suggest that, if you were young again, you would not desire it either."

"Indeed, young sir, I would not! And I am certain you cannot."

"I may not be old enough to *desire* to die, but I am young enough to *desire* to live! That is why I go now to learn if she will finally accept me. You wish to die because you no longer care to live; she will not open her door to you, for no one can die who has not first longed to live."

"It ill becomes your youth to mock a friendless old man. Please, stop your riddles!"

"Did not the Mother tell you something similar?"

"In truth, I believe she did, but I paid little heed to her explanations."

"Then, sir," I said, "it is clear you have not yet learned how to die, and I am deeply grieved for you. I was once just as you are, had it not been for the Lady of Sorrow. Though I am young, I have shed many tears. Pardon me if I dare offer counsel: Go to the Lady of Sorrow and 'take with both hands' whatever she gives you. Her cottage lies yonder. She is absent now, but her door is open, and bread and water sit upon her table. Enter, sit, eat, drink, and wait for her arrival. Then seek her counsel, for she is true, and her wisdom is vast."

He broke into fresh sobs, and I departed, fearing my words had fallen on deaf ears. Perhaps Mara would find him eventually.

The sun had dipped below the horizon, and the moon had not yet risen, when I reached the dwelling of the monsters. The place lay silent as a stone until I crossed the threshold, at which point the sound of rushing waters erupted, accompanied by a great cry behind me. I did not turn my head. By the time I approached the House of Death, the cold was biting and the darkness absolute; the two merged into a single entity that seeped into my very bones. Yet, the candle of Eve, glowing from the window, acted as a beacon, keeping frost and gloom at bay within my heart.

The door stood open, revealing an empty cottage. Sitting down in despair, I was struck by a loneliness unlike any I had felt during my countless wanderings. Thousands were near, yet not one was with me. Technically, I was the dead one among them, but whether by their living or my dying, we were severed. They were alive, while I was not dead enough to truly perceive them as such. Doubt crept in. They were distant, and I had no helper to lay me beside them. Never before had I known, or truly imagined, such desolation.

I chided myself, telling myself the solitude was merely an illusion, but I was awake while they slept—that was the crux of it. They lay so still, silent, yet they were present. Soon, very soon, I would join them. Dropping Adam's old spade, the dull thud against the clay floor seemed to reverberate from the chamber beyond. A childish terror seized me. I sat staring at the coffin door.

But Father Adam, Mother Eve, and Sister Mara would come soon. Then—welcome the cold world and the white neighbors! I pushed aside my fears, lived a fleeting moment, and loved my dead.

Something moved in the Chamber of the Dead. It emanated a sound like a dim, far-off vibration, yet it was not sound as I knew it. My soul leaped into my ears. Was it merely a tremor in the stagnant air, too faint for the ear but quivering in every spiritual sense? I *knew* without hearing, without feeling it! The presence was arriving; it drew nearer. Within the bosom of my abandonment, an infant hope stirred.

The noiseless thrill reached the coffin door, transformed into sound, and struck my ear. The door began to move, emitting a low, soft creak of its hinges. It was opening! I ceased listening, staring expectantly.

It cracked open slightly, and a face appeared in the frame. It was Lona's. Her eyes were closed, yet the face itself seemed fixed upon me, seeing me clearly. It was white as Eve's, white as Mara's, but it lacked their radiant glow. She spoke, her voice like a sleepy night wind rustling through the grass.

"Are you coming, King?" she whispered. "I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the great sea and the beautiful dream-land. The sleepiness is full of lovely things; come and see them."

"Oh, my darling!" I cried. "Had I but known! I thought you were dead!"

She collapsed onto my lap—cold as ice frozen to marble. She wrapped her arms, so white and feeble, around me and sighed.

"Carry me back to my bed, King. I want to sleep."

I bore her to the death-chamber, clutching her tightly lest she dissolve from my grasp. Unaware that I was watching, I carried her directly to her couch.

"Lay me down," she murmured, "and cover me from the warm air; it hurts—a little. Your bed is there, next to mine. I shall see you when I wake."

She was already asleep. I threw myself onto my own couch, blessed as no man has ever been on the eve of his wedding.

"Come, sweet cold," I whispered, "and still my heart speedily."

Instead, a glimmer of light appeared in the chamber, and I saw Father Adam approaching. He held no candle, yet I saw him clearly. Beside Lona's couch, he looked down upon her with a questioning smile, then greeted me across the space between us.

"We climbed to the top of the hill to hear the waters on their journey," he said. "They will reach the monsters' den tonight. But why did you not await our return?"

"My child could not sleep," I answered.

"She is fast asleep!" he rejoined.

"Yes, now!" I said. "But she was awake when I laid her down."

"She was asleep the entire time!" he insisted.

"She was perhaps dreaming of you—and came to you?"

"She did."

"And did you not see that her eyes were closed?"

"Now that I think of it, I did."

"If you had looked before laying her down, you would have seen her asleep on the couch."

"That would have been terrible!"

"You would only have found that she was no longer in your arms."

"That would have been worse!"

"It is something to consider, but seeing it would not have troubled you."

"Dear Father," I said, "why am I not sleepy? I thought I would fall asleep like the Little Ones the moment I laid my head down!"

"Your hour is not quite come. You must eat before you sleep."

"Oh, I ought not to have lain down without your permission, for I cannot sleep without your help! I will rise immediately!"

Yet I found my own body heavier than I could move.

"There is no need: we will serve you here," he answered. "--Do you not feel cold?"

"Not too cold to lie still, but perhaps too cold to eat!"

He stepped to the side of my couch, leaned over me, and breathed upon my heart. Instantly, warmth flooded me. As he withdrew, I heard a voice and recognized it as the Mother's. She was singing, a melody sweet, soft, and low.
