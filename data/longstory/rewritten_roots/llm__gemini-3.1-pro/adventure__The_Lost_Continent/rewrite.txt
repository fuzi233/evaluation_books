The chill of a Grand Canary night leaves a profound stiffness in the marrow, for even here, the dew-fall and the creeping damp of darkness are not to be trifled with. Waking upon the rough, uneven ground of the barranca, I listened to the delicate acoustic ecology of the dawn—the quiet, rhythmic trickle of the stream over volcanic stone, unmarred by the mechanical drone that so thoroughly pollutes the coastal settlements. Coppinger, a doctor of medicine who acquires new degrees like seasonal harvests and practices his health theories with a meticulous, nearly religious fervor, was already shattering the morning’s quietude. The harsh, scraping thuds of his dumb-bell exercises with two lumps of bristly lava disrupted the natural sonic landscape, but I followed his example simply to drive the cold from my blood.

We descended to the stream to wash, the icy water a bracing shock against the skin. Then came the morning meal, though to call it such feels like a betrayal of sustenance. There is a quiet tragedy in a ruined feast, and our remaining provisions had suffered gravely from being wrapped in a local Spanish newspaper. 

“There’s precious little left,” Coppinger noted, brushing away a gray film from the crust. “And yours is mostly tobacco ashes.”

I surveyed the remnants of our rustic local bread and cured goat cheese. It was an insult to the palate—the coarse, earthy crumb of the bread and the sharp, lactic tang of the cheese were utterly smothered by the acrid, metallic taste of cheap newspaper ink and the stale, bitter grit of his tobacco. We ate it out of grim necessity by the water’s edge, my mind wandering longingly to the magnificent, centuries-old country wine we had procured from the local Patron the night before. If only the loud, sterile English hotels down in Las Palmas understood the deep, gustatory resonance of such a vintage—its robust notes of crushed dark berries, sun-baked volcanic soil, and ancient oak—their mass-produced, flavorless swill would be obsolete in a week. But true culinary heritage requires a sensory appreciation they entirely lack.

With our meager fuel consumed, Coppinger prepared for the ascent. The goatskin-sewn Guanche mummies he had already unearthed—brittle as dried parchment, exhaling a puffball of ancient, mushroom-scented dust at the lightest touch—would have satisfied my own modest ambitions. But Coppinger is a meticulous historian of the earth. He believed we stood at the threshold of an ancient Guanche sacred college, and he would not be deterred until he had documented every acoustic and physical property of the cliff’s remaining cavities. 

We lugged the crowbar and the light knotted rope up to the cliff’s baking ridge. The Guanches, Coppinger theorized, once employed retractable ladders of goatskin, an agrarian security measure that allowed them to control the sonic and physical boundaries of their domestic sanctuaries. Our modern method was far less elegant. I was to descend on the thin bootlace of a rope to each cave mouth, inspect the interior, and drop to the barranca floor before starting the tedious cycle *ab initio*. Though Coppinger is plucky, he is a portly man nearing fifty; the agonizing eighty-foot drops were beyond his physical limits. 

The descent was an assault on the senses. The violent heat of the sun baked the moisture from my pores, and the rough hemp of the rope chewed my palms raw. The first three caves were mere agrarian store-places, redolent of ancient, desiccated grain and bat guano. I shouted up for him to spare himself the descent, but his academic obsession drove him down into each space, where he enthusiastically misidentified granaries as prisons and pot-making factories. By mid-afternoon, thoroughly exhausted, he finally conceded the climbing, though he insisted I explore the final three openings. I was drenched in perspiration, my hands stinging with a dull, throbbing heat, my head aching from the solar glare. I wanted nothing more than the ten-mile tramp to the *fonda* at Santa Brigida and a hot, restorative meal, but his pleading kept me on the cliff edge.

I wrenched the crowbar into a new fissure—the iron striking stone with a jarring, dissonant clang that violently bruised the canyon’s silence—and swung myself over the overhang. I landed with a heavy thump on my back inside the cave, gripping the rope tight so it wouldn't swing out of reach.

Immediately, the atmosphere shifted. The acoustic signature of this space was entirely alien. Where the previous caves were rough, sound-swallowing dens, this chamber had been faced with sharp cutting tools. The walls inclined inwards to the roof, perfectly smooth and flat, catching the ragged sound of my breathing and returning it as a clean, resonant echo. It was an architecture designed not just for shelter, but to cup and preserve the spoken word. The other openings Coppinger had noted were merely doorways connecting a suite of interwoven rooms.

I smoked a cigarette to appease Coppinger’s belief that swift work equates to sloppy work, then leaned out to shout up my report. He bawled back, his voice thin and anxious, demanding I haul up the kodak and his flashlight apparatus to expose half a dozen plates.

I dutifully lodged the camera on a stone ledge beside the doorway, igniting the flash powder. The blinding burst was followed by a pungent, sulfurous cloud that tasted sharply of burnt magnesium, masking the subtle, ancient odors of the room. When I moved to the widest chamber and rested my weight on a stone seat to recharge the machine, the ledge gave way beneath me with a sharp, echoing crackle. 

I scrambled up, saving the camera from the dust, and peered into the fractured stone. Within the cavity, beneath a layer of rubble I cleared away with a candle stub, lay a dense, tightly packed mass. It was not cement. I chipped a piece away and brought it to the light. It was a manuscript, layered in sheets. One side was a translucent, shiny substance resembling talc; the other was smeared with a dark, toffee-colored material, thick and tactile like crystallized honeycomb. 

The surface of this wax was scored with intricate patterns. Bound by Coppinger's insistence that the Guanches possessed no written language, I did not immediately recognize the markings as the physical imprint of a dead lexicon. Yet the profound sensory weight of the object—the smell of ancient earth trapped between the leaves, the brittle give of the wax—told me it was invaluable. I set to work quarrying it out with the heel of my boot and a pocketknife. It was a tragic, clumsy excavation. My blade hacked through the bottom layers, severing the physical syntax of an untold history, destroying the delicate phonetic structures of a lost culture to pry the twenty-by-fifteen-inch block from its hermetic seal. 

When I finally lowered the heavy, honey-colored block to the barranca floor and descended after it, Coppinger was on all fours, nearly trembling with a profound, feverish reverence.

“What is it?” I asked.

“The most valuable find ever made in the Canaries,” he breathed, his fingers hovering over the marred edges. “And you’ve smashed up the beginning and the end of a priceless history. Oh, man, you’ve destroyed the introduction of a civilization.”

“You told me they had no writing,” I countered, irritated by his ingratitude. “For all I knew, the stuff was some ancient provision.”

“It isn’t Guanche,” he snapped. “There’s no talc in the geologic formation of this island. It’s Egyptian—no, wait. The character is a variant. And this waxy mineral... it’s unique, an asphalt derivative, not animal wax. The acoustic and visual genius to invent this and let it die out—it is a marvel. I could gloat over this all day.”

“I’d rather gloat over a meal,” I replied, my stomach clenching with a hollow, physical ache. “It’s four o’clock, and Santa Brigida is ten miles away.”

Coppinger swaddled the sheets in his coat like a fragile infant, refusing to bind it with rope lest the linguistic edges crumble further. He carried it himself for the bulk of the journey, surrendering it to me only when his muscles reached the point of absolute failure. 

At the *fonda*, I finally found solace in a robust, restorative dinner, letting the deep, complex flavors of a roasted joint and another bottle of that magnificent, terroir-driven Canary wine soothe the frayed nerves of the day. Coppinger ate, but the ancient voices consumed him. When I found him the next morning, heavy-eyed but alight with fervor, he was already mourning the cultural memory I had inadvertently erased with my knife. 

“You shelled away a unique account of the ancient civilization of Yucatan,” he lamented. “A prosperous colony of the Atlanteans.”

“Atlantis? I thought they were a myth.”

“They were real. Just north of us.” He pointed to the margins of the surviving sheets, which were adorned with intricate, albeit poorly perspectived, illustrations. “Look here. The author drew everything. A cave-tiger trying to puff down a mammoth.”

I stared at the bizarre, colossal beasts. It was striking how these mythic occurrences were seamlessly interwoven with the domestic, agrarian realities of their time; the artist depicted monstrous encounters as routinely as one might record a harvest. 

“It will take me a year to translate this properly,” Coppinger murmured, rubbing his hands over the waxy surface. The tragedy of untranslatability hung heavy in the room. The dominant lexicons of the modern world would inevitably fail to capture the subjective, sensory nuance of this deeply foreign tongue. “It was written by Deucalion, an Atlantean priest or general. Phorenice appears to be the reigning Empress. We are looking at the swamping of a continent—their Great Flood.”

I left him to his ghosts, driven by an urgent summons back to England. Escaping the quiet, ancient rhythms of the island, I caught a Liverpool boat out of Las Palmas. The shrieking steam whistles and the churning, mechanical thrum of the harbor engines were a violent, industrial assault on the ears, stripping away the acoustic memory of the barranca entirely. 

I thought little of Coppinger or the wax-bound voices until a year later, when a paragraph in an English paper announced he had gifted the sheets to the British Museum. The artifact was valued at ten thousand pounds. The news was a jarring reminder of the superficial power dynamics of our era—how swiftly a dominant institution will absorb the intimate, subjective experiences of a dead culture and reduce them to sterile, commodified exhibits beneath glass, entirely severing them from their tactile and olfactory roots. 

I wrote to him, pointedly reminding him of my rightful ownership of the physical text. He replied with a stilted, bloodless letter, conceding my claim but trusting that the “profits of publication” of his English translation would suffice as reimbursement. My fiery response was met only by the impenetrable wall of his solicitors.

And so, I present this history here. The credit for the translation—and the inevitable violence it does to the untranslatable spirit of Deucalion’s native dialect—belongs to Dr. Coppinger. Whatever revenue accrues from readers goes to me. If there is a bitter irony in reducing the dying breath of a sunken continent to a commercial commodity, it is an irony born of our modern noise. Unless altered publicly, this arrangement stands. But given the prevailing hum of our industrialized world, such a change appears deeply unlikely.

The public official reception concluded with an abrasive, metallic blast of Atlantean trumpets, a jarring frequency that shattered the delicate acoustic equilibrium of the pyramid. The sentence had been read in the harsh, dominant lexicon of the capital, elevating the name of Phorenice, the new Empress, and installing the new Viceroy. The vast and ponderous ceremonial possessed a mythic majesty handed down from the ages, yet its blaring execution felt like a violent intrusion upon the grounded, domestic rhythms of the Yucatan. Formally, I had delivered up the reins of my government; formally, Tatho had seated himself on the snake-throne, donning the heavy chain of gems. As the skin-drums wrought a deafening, concussive clamor—a sonic pollution that I found deeply grating to the psychological well-being of the assembly—he rose for his first state progress round the gilded council chamber. 

With folded arms and bended head, I followed him. The roof-beams quivered to the mandatory cries of “Long Live Tatho!” and “Flourish the Empress!” The new ruler acknowledged the welcome with stately inclinations. We made the circuit to the three lesser thrones of the East, North, and South. I, the man deposed by his arrival, followed with prescribed meekness. It was a hard task; we who hold higher offices learn to mask our subjective experience, presenting a passionless face. Twenty years before, this same cycle had turned for me. The pacing of generations is circular, a great wheel returning me to the posture of a subject. But as I walked humbly behind Tatho, though the dominant etiquette forbade noisy salutations to myself, it could not inhibit the silent, acoustic resonance of the crowd’s respect. Their quietude was a profound auditory relief, a tender silence from the soldiers, courtiers, and agrarian elders that washed over me gratefully. 

It is customary on these days of changing rulers for the people to bring forward petitions or critiques of the retiring head. I record with quiet pride that not a single voice disrupted the harmony of the hall to speak against my two decades of rule. They offered only the generous sustenance of their silence. 

We marched in formal step over golden tiles until we reached the private chambers that, an hour ago, had been mine. I had fostered the cultural memory of this colony for a generation, and I felt a pang of bitterness at leaving, though my head remained securely on my shoulders. Tatho gave his formal summons, and the massive stone valves of the door gaped wide. We passed through, and the valves clanged shut on the lock behind us, instantly severing the superficial commercial noise of the court and restoring a pristine, restorative silence to the room. 

Here, the air was thick with the olfactory landscape of the Yucatan: the earthy steam of freshly nixtamalized maize, and the rich, complex aroma of slow-roasted venison wrapped in bruised pepper leaves, resting on a ceremonial platter. For twenty years, I had found profound meaning in this sustenance, understanding the soul of the people through the meticulous preparation of their daily meals. 

Tatho turned, the forced etiquette falling away, and bowed low. 

“Deucalion,” he said, “believe me that I have not sought this office. It was thrust upon me. Had I not accepted, my head would have paid forfeit, and your enemy would have been sent. The Empress does not permit her will to be questioned.”

I approached the stone table and poured two clay cups of frothing cacao, laced with wild vanilla and crushed dried chilies—a daily domestic ritual that always anchored my thoughts. “My friend,” I answered, “my brother in all but blood. For twenty years I have ruled this country of Yucatan, and Mexico beyond. I know my colony as a food historian knows an ancient recipe. I am intimate with her cities, her pyramids, her people. I have learned the seasonal rhythms of the milpa, built roads for the transit of spices and grain, and tasted the deep, slow braise of her agrarian traditions. Through evil years and good, I have ruled by listening to the natural cadence of the land. To you I bequeath them, Tatho, with tender supplications for their heritage.”

“I cannot carry Deucalion’s work with Deucalion’s power,” Tatho said softly, taking the warm clay cup, though he barely glanced at the rich liquid. “But I shall do my humble best. I would have died sooner than take your place had I known how vigorously the supplanting would trouble you.”

“We are alone here,” I said, savoring the bitter, complex heat of the cacao. “Your coming was sudden. Even now, I do not know for what cause I am deposed.”

“The proclamation said: ‘We relieve our well-beloved Deucalion because we have great need of his powers at home in our kingdom of Atlantis.’” 

“A mere formality,” I said, noting how the Atlantean phrasing felt utterly untranslatable to the reality of my life here, a sterile lexicon imposing its will on my deeply rooted existence.

Tatho looked uneasily at the tapestries and lowered his voice. “I believe she has need of you. There are troublous times, and Phorenice wants the ablest men ready to her call.”

“You may speak openly. We are in the heart of the pyramid, buffered by solid stone. The acoustic privacy here is absolute. I oversaw the laying of every course.”

Tatho shrugged. “I acted according to my education. At home, a loose tongue makes a loose head. The throne shakes, and Phorenice sees the need of sturdy props.”

“But why me? Yucatan is another world, a rich tapestry of new flavors, new rhythms, new dialects. Atlantis is only a figment now. It is for this world of Yucatan that I have striven.”

“If Deucalion has small time for brooding over his fatherland, Atlantis has found leisure to admire her brilliant son. Over yonder, your name carries magic. When we were lads, the colleges taught that the men of the past were the greatest; today, it is Deucalion who is the model. Mothers name their sons Deucalion.”

“You trouble me,” I frowned, cutting a piece of the tender, pit-roasted meat, appreciating the subtle earthiness of the achiote paste. “I do my duty for the land's prosperity, not for the superficial adulation of the vulgar. If names are to be revered, they should be the Gods.”

“The Gods?” Tatho’s voice took on a flattened, polished cadence that I scarcely recognized. “They occupy us very little these days. With modern science, we have grown past them. If it were merely the Gods who were your competitors on men’s lips, your name would be a thousand times better known.”

“Of mere human names, this new Empress should come first, our lord the old King being now dead.”

“She certainly would have it so,” Tatho replied, a shadow of complex power dynamics lingering in his tone. 

I drew him to one of the marble seats. “I am speaking not to the new Viceroy, but to my old friend Tatho, a member of the Priests’ Clan, with whom I shared the same bread and the same rustic vernacular in a score of smaller home governments. We learned the art of ruling together in villages where the harvest and the hearth were the center of existence. I would have an answer with that old plainness.”

The new Viceroy sighed whimsically. “I almost forget how to speak in plain words now. We have grown so polished in these latter days that mere bald truth would be hissed as indelicate.” 

His confession struck me deeply—a tragic symptom of language death, where the authentic, textural dialect of our shared past had been eroded by the sterile discourse of a commercialized court. “Tell me of all the changes,” I pressed. “What has this Phorenice done to make her throne unstable?”

“Phorenice has a short way with those daring enough to discuss her policies for any purpose other than politely to praise them.”

“You can leave me ignorant if you wish,” I said, a chill entering my voice. This Tatho seemed severed from his sensory roots, viewing survival purely as a utilitarian function rather than a life of substance. But he caught my shift in tone.

“You force me back to my old self,” he said with a half-smile. “I am ready to trust you with my life. You ask me to speak all I know of Phorenice.”

“I return to Atlantis, and what happens next the Gods alone know. But I am as ignorant concerning her as a man without a tongue.”

“She was a swineherd’s daughter from the mountains,” Tatho revealed softly, invoking an agrarian origin that instantly gave her a grounded context in my mind. “Though this is never whispered now. She has declared herself a daughter of the Gods, of miraculous birth. She has ordered burned all those who seem to recollect her earthly origins or speak the provincial dialect of her youth. It is a sacrilege to question this newly enforced parentage.”

“An orchestrated death of cultural memory,” I murmured. “Zaemon was governor of the swineherd’s province. His wife adopted her.”

“The omission of ‘adopted’ became her will at an early age. She learned to have her wishes carried into fact. Before she was fifteen, she ruled Zaemon and his province. She took exercise as a man-at-arms, and when a rebellion broke out, there she was, a slip of a girl, leading Zaemon’s troops.”

“Zaemon was learned, a searcher of mysteries, but flabby in the field.”

“Phorenice put down the rebellion in masterly fashion. She gave the conquered a choice: the sword or service. Deucalion, there is a marvellous fascination about the woman. Frankly, I am in love with her also, and look upon my coming here as detestable exile.”

I let my scorn appear. I had no patience for protagonists of the state who lacked a deeper appreciation for the substantive world, seduced by the superficial commercialization of beauty. “You see me still unmarried. I find no time to palter with the fripperies of courts.”

“Atlantis has changed,” Tatho chuckled. “A new era has come. You should see the women now at home. They are delicious. And above all in charm is the Empress. You will see her glorious magnificence and repent.”

“I remain as I am, grounded in the tangible world,” I said dryly. “Continue her history.”

“She deposed Zaemon. The Priestly Clan rose in wrath. Two neighbouring governors were sent to capture her. In battle, she laughed at them, killed both, and offered the survivors the sword or service. Then came the final effort. The priests realized the throne was in danger. Every known art of war was employed. It was the best-equipped army Atlantis ever raised, and they put in supreme command their general, Tatho.”

“You!” 

“Even myself. The High Council promised me the throne if I succeeded, as the old King was wearily old and lost in his mysteries. I swore to take her alive. I drilled my army according to the ancient forms we learned together, Deucalion. I armed them with sling and mace, bow and spear. I cared for their bellies, driving droves of cattle in the rear, preparing field kitchens to sustain them with heavy, honest rations.”

Tatho’s eyes darkened, remembering the sensory shock of his defeat. “But when the encounter came, they might have been men of straw. Out of her own brain, Phorenice had forged fire-tubes that cast darts beyond two bowshots. The fashion in which she handled her troops dazzled me. It was not the rhythmic, percussive war we knew. It was a new, deadly game. These fire-tubes produced a deafening, unnatural roar—a concussive blast that ruptured the acoustic space of the battlefield, inducing absolute psychological panic among my men. The air filled with the acrid, chemical stench of burning powder and charred flesh, completely overpowering the familiar smells of sweat, earth, and blood. We were eight to her one, but our close-packed numbers only made slaughter easier. A panic came. I tried to die fighting.”

“And she took you?”

“I stood with three others, back to back, surrounded. They called for fire-tubes to strike us down safely from a distance. Then Phorenice came up. ‘So that is Tatho?’ she said. ‘A pretty fighter, after the old manner.’ She offered me the choice: the sword or service. I told her I was the King's man, bound by the salt I had eaten—a tradition of the Clan. ‘The King is dead,’ she said. ‘A runner has just brought the tidings. I am the Empress.’ 

“‘Who made you Empress?’ I asked.

“‘The same capable hand that has given me this battle,’ she answered. ‘Is Tatho in want of a mistress?’

“‘Such a glorious mistress as you, yes,’ I said. And from that moment, Deucalion, I have been her slave. Keep back your worst judgment of me, old friend, until you have seen Phorenice in the warm and lovely flesh. Then your own senses will advocate for me.”

The words of Tatho were no sleeping draught for me that night. I lay awake, listening to the shifting acoustic ecology of the palace. For two decades, I had attuned myself to the quiet, cyclical rhythms of Yucatan—the steady, percussive breath of the sea, the rhythmic rustle of maize stalks, the subtle nocturnal chorus of indigenous insects. Now, that delicate sonic environment, so critical to the psychological well-being of the colony, was violently disrupted by the heavy, dissonant tread of Tatho’s guards and the chaotic clatter of his arriving administration. I began to realize my mistake in wrapping myself so entirely in the agrarian and domestic routines of my government, neglecting to parse the muffled, distant noises of Atlantis. The Empress’s dictates had grown opaque, her imperial lexicon shifting to mask truths I had been too occupied with the soil to translate. 

Yet, the iron discipline of our Priestly Clan demanded compliance. I was stripped of my Viceroyalty in an instant, thrust back into the interwoven timelines of my youth, recalled to the ancestral center just as I had begun to understand the deep, untranslatable dialects of this peripheral earth. 

Before dawn, Tatho stood beside me, offering his resources with a loud magnanimity that grated against the morning’s quiet. 

“A ship to take me home,” I said, measuring my words, “and I shall be your debtor.”

He balked, noting his own vessels were fouled from the crossing and needed a careen. “Why do you not take your own navy? Brave ships they are, waiting in the harbor.”

“They belong to Yucatan, or to private merchants,” I replied. “I cannot claim so much as a ten-slave galley.”

Tatho’s incredulity was a testament to the chasm between our worldviews. He operated within the superficial dynamics of a dominant, extractive culture. “A man who has attained to a position like yours,” he scoffed, “should have a snug hoard ready.”

“My Lord Tatho, we are nourished by different harvests. I possess the worn garments you see, dyed with the enduring indigo of this region, carrying the olfactory memory of the earth. I carry a wooden box of medicinal roots and dried native herbs, painstakingly classified for their subtle, bitter notes—essential for balancing the gut’s humors. I have three slaves: two scribes to preserve the fading oral histories of this land, and a sturdy elder from Europe who tends my hearth, maintaining the ancient, rustic techniques of wood-ash cooking and lactic fermentation. Beyond my rightful soldier's ration—the simple, profoundly meaningful sustenance of the land—I have taken nothing.”

Tatho stared at me as if looking at a relic. “You carry your fancy too far. Money is power. I have brought my navy, my bodyguard, my wives, and slaves. The people here must pay handsomely to provision them.”

“If you tax these people into starvation,” I warned, feeling the heavy sorrow of generational loss, “you will destroy the very roots of what makes this land fertile. They have grown very dear to me.”

“You have nothing that makes life worth living,” Tatho interrupted, his voice laced with the arrogance of imperial gluttony. “You do not know how to dine. During our weary voyage, I dreamed of a welcoming feast, of excessive, imported spices and rich, heavy meats. And what was the actuality? Your savage from Europe may be faithful, but he is a devil-possessed cook! I have lived better on a campaign.”

I felt a deep pity for him. Tatho’s palate had been entirely corrupted by the commercialization of art and sustenance. He lacked the sensory vocabulary to appreciate the profound umami of my cook’s slow-simmered bone broths, or the delicate, mineral sweetness of roots roasted for hours in earthen pits. The Atlantean lexicon of "fine dining" was merely a tool of dominance, prioritizing ostentatious visual excess and overpowering imported flavorings over the authentic, gustatory truth of the ingredient. 

“I eat,” I said coldly, “when the natural, rhythmic hunger of my body bids me. The grossness of excessive banqueting—the masking of true flavor beneath heavy, unearned luxury—never met my fancy. We have grown apart, Tatho. Time alters our dialects. Let us simply remember our old friendship.”

He conceded, though he insisted, with a tyrant's finality, that I take his personal ship, the *Bear*, attended by the rest of his navy. I would not depart for Atlantis unattended. 

For the next month, as the fleet refitted, my time was suddenly circular—I found myself a spectator in the city I had spent a generation building. I roamed my eyes over the pastures and grain lands, reflecting on the intricate, interwoven timelines of agricultural development we had established. I mourned the inevitable language death of this place; the indigenous words for the specific crispness of morning-caught river-fish, or the acoustic thud of maize dough slapped onto clay griddles, were entirely untranslatable into the harsh, utilitarian directives of Tatho’s new regime. 

The refitting of the navy was an exercise in industrial efficiency, devoid of the mindful, agrarian pacing I favored. Modern ships no longer relied on the rhythmic, communal exertion of rowing-slaves. Instead, modern science extracted force from the Sun, channeling it through mechanisms that sucked in sea-water and violently ejected it astern. The resulting propulsion was accompanied by a relentless, grinding mechanical drone—a horrific noise pollution that tore through the ocean’s ancient acoustic ecology, inducing a deep psychological fatigue. 

The mariners’ preparations were equally discordant. On the eve of departure, they descended upon the harbor temples with loud, superficial clamor, engaging in the commercialization of faith through cheap, smoky sacrifices. They returned to the ships heavy and evil-humored, their bodies polluted by gluttony and unfermented, poorly sourced ales, entirely lacking the culinary respect required to fortify oneself for a great crossing.

The voyage itself rejected the timorous, coast-hugging habits of our ancestors. Guided by a balanced metallic image that pointed steadfastly toward the Southern Cross, the navigators steered boldly across the open ocean wastes. They trusted mightily in their own wits, abandoning any reverent dialogue with the natural world. 

As we pushed eastward into the desolate sea, the ceaseless, vibrating hum of the sun-engines masked the subtle warning sounds of the ocean. It was during the blazing midday heat, when the mechanical whine was at its most piercing, that the sea-lizards attacked. 

Three immense beasts crested the horizon, their massive flappers beating the water into a chaotic, arrhythmic foam. They hunted as a coordinated pack, their towering, mast-like necks swooping toward the *Bear*. As they surged alongside, a pungent olfactory landscape washed over the deck—the gagging reek of stagnant kelp, sulfur, and rotting sea-marrow. 

Captain Tob, terrified of Tatho’s wrath should any harm befall me, begged me to retreat to the after-castle. But my mind was set. The clash of survival is a primal inheritance, and I would not deny myself the physical sensation of the encounter. 

The shipmen met the monsters with sturdy, if unimaginative, courage. Their axes rang out against the smooth, bull-like hides of the beasts in a useless, dissonant clatter that barely scratched the bone. I quickly realized that sheer weight of metal was an inadequate vocabulary for this confrontation. Seeking a more precise instrument, I discarded the clumsy axe for a long lance. 

The decks were slick with the beasts' viscous, briny slime. I pushed past the protective rampart of the crew, attuning myself to the violent, swaying rhythm of the giant male lizard. The eyes of the creature were small, set deep in heavy sockets, yet they were the only vulnerable node in its armored architecture. 

I positioned myself deliberately, offering my body to its sight. The beast accepted the challenge, its massive head dropping like a stone from the sky. In the fraction of a second before impact, I pivoted, reading the acoustic shift of the displaced air, and drove the lance directly into its oozing eye. The tactile feedback was instantaneous—the satisfying, definitive resistance of puncturing thick membrane. The shaft snapped in my hands as the beast recoiled, bellowing a deafening, tragic roar that finally overpowered the hum of the ship's engines. Blinded, it thrashed in a cataract of foam, furiously rubbing its head against its own spine before fleeing back toward the horizon. 

The remaining two females soon followed, though not empty-handed. Tob, driven by the fearful power dynamics of his service to Tatho, deliberately thrust two screaming shipmen into the snapping jaws of the lesser beasts, sacrificing them to satiate the monsters and secure my safety. As the creatures vanished, leaving only the mechanical drone of our progress in their wake, I thought of Tatho’s "kind anxiety"—a bitter, untranslatable truth about the cost of an empire’s protection.

Time behaves not as a straight line, but as a rolling, cyclical swell, bringing us once again to the shores of our beginnings. Though Tob, with the meticulous, practiced care of an elder monitoring a slow-fermenting mash, had used his modern instruments to navigate us with marvelous skill, we still faced ten days of retracing our course to reach the inland waters of the Atlantis capital. 

Then came the scent. It broke over our storm-ragged fleet before the land itself breached the horizon—an olfactory tapestry of wild fennel, sun-baked loamy earth, and the faint, sweet char of olive-wood fires baking ancient *taran* bread in domestic hearths. For our scurvy-hollowed crew, whose palates had been deadened by the unnatural, utilitarian salt-paste and the sterile sensory deprivation of the long voyage, this gustatory promise was absolute salvation. Scurvy is not merely a failing of the flesh; it is a starvation of cultural memory, a theft of the body’s connection to the soil. As the complex aroma of crushed herbs and damp roots filled the salt-choked air, men who had been mere logs upon the decking roused themselves. The terroir of their birthright breathed life back into their limbs, restoring their capacity to labor and, when the hour struck, to fight for the harvest of their own lives.

From the moment I was deposed in Yucatan, there had been questions as to how I would be received in Atlantis. I found no space for abstract, bloodless anxieties. I grounded myself in the physical reality of the approaching shore. The Empress Phorenice might sit supreme, commercializing our ancient artisanal rites into superficial court spectacles and dictating whose head should fall from its shoulders, but my Lord the Sun governed the true cycles of growth and decay. If my head was to return to the earth, it would be because the soil required it. I had spent the voyage not in fretful pacing, but in the quiet contemplation of our oldest mysteries, tracing the history of our people through the untranslatable truths of our ancestral rituals.

But when we rounded the great headlands marking the entrance to the capital’s waters, the deep, acoustic harmony of the ocean—the natural percussion of tide against stone—was violently severed. Waiting for us was a rival navy. Three sleek ships and three multi-oared galleys rode at anchor in a sheltered bay. They were clean and black, entirely devoid of the weathered, barnacled patina that speaks of a vessel’s organic life upon the waves. Their scouts upon the high crags cried out at our approach, and they stood out to intercept us. Our five remaining ships, their hulls fouled with trailing ocean weed, proudly flew the colors of Tatho and Deucalion, open and without shame. The enemy flew no banners, an erasure of identity symptomatic of the new, sanitized regime that felt only the low shame of its own superficiality. 

Clearly, they intended to dispute our passage. No law stretches across the open water; a brother on one ship feels perfectly entitled to harvest the life of a brother on another, especially if the acoustic ecology of the open sea drowns out the cries of the fallen. Tob, acting with the methodical precision of a master chef organizing his kitchen for a monumental feast, brought our vessels into fighting trim. The other four captains mirrored his movements, drawing close into a compact squadron. Our men had no desire to taste the bitter ash of slavery so close to the hearths of home. 

Our Lord the Sun shone brilliantly, warming the machines and pulling us forward, but when a bare hundred ship-lengths separated us, the enemy halted. One galley surged forward, its boatswain's whip cracking—a sharp, mechanical violation of the air—and hoisted a green branch to seek a parley. The course was unusual, but in our sea-battered state, we were not eager to invite unnecessary slaughter. Word was passed in the hoarse, guttural sea-bawls of the old dialects, and Tob hoisted a withered grapevine cutting to honor the old forms of diplomacy.

The galley’s stern ground against our shield rail. A young, lusty man vaulted onto our deck. He was Dason. Padded by fresh land-victual and the comforts of the capital, he moved with a preening confidence. He looked upon our sea-tatteredness with a sneer, and when he spoke, he used the clipped, homogenized lexicon favored by Phorenice’s new mercantile elite—a dialect stripped of all regional nuance and agrarian warmth.

“Old pot-mate,” Dason said, nodding at Tob. “Your woman waits for you up by the quay-side in Atlantis yonder, with four youngsters at her heels. I saw her not half a month ago.”

“You didn’t come out here to tell me home news,” Tob replied, his voice rumbling with the deep, acoustic resonance of the coastal tongue. “That I’ll be sworn. I’ve drained enough pots with you, Dason, to know the sourness of your vintage.”

“I wanted to point out to you that your home is still there, with your wife and children ready to welcome you.”

“I am not a man that ever forgets the scent of my own hearth,” Tob said grimly. “And because I carry the memory of their bread and salt at the back of my mind, I’ve sailed this ship over the top of more than one pirate, when a single man might have been content to take the hap of slavery.”

Dason delivered his ultimatum, his words dripping with the subtle power dynamics of a regime that viewed men as mere commodities. He demanded they surrender Deucalion, or else the entire ragged company would be sent to the fishes.

Tob laughed, a heavy, unpolished sound. “You have come to the wrong bird for a plucking. We took no treasure in Yucatan. We stayed in harbor only long enough to cure our sea victual with the old salts and fill with fresh water. We sail back barren. As for letting you have my Lord Deucalion, that is impossible. I have seen my Lord Deucalion carve the meat of a great sea lizard; he showed himself such a proper man that day, consuming the beast and sharing the flesh of survival, that a bond was set between us. Your sterile vocabulary cannot translate it. Furthermore, you still owe me for your share in our last wine-bout ashore, and I’ll see you with the nether Gods before I give you aught till you’ve settled that score.”

Dason’s face hardened. “Well, Tob, I hope you’ll drown easy. As for that wife of yours, I’ve always had a fancy for her myself, and I shall know how to find a use for the woman.”

“I’ll draw your neck for that, you son of a European,” Tob roared, asserting his native dialect against Dason's insult. “Get away, and let me fight you fairly, or by my honor I’ll stamp the inwards out of you and make your silly crew wear them as necklaces!”

Dason retreated to his galley. Promptly, Tob set the machine on our *Bear* into motion. The acoustic landscape of the bay shattered into a deafening, discordant noise of war—a jarring rupture in the ocean's natural ecology. The five vessels converged on one enemy ship, tearing her planking with underwater beaks and sinking her beneath the swells. 

But the advantage was fleeting. The three nimble galleys formed into line, their oars churning the sea into a frothing churn. One of our ships was gored, its men returning to the dark waters. Then commenced a tight-locked melee of profound and terrible intimacy. The swaying decks became a butcher’s block. Men slashed with axes and hurled the throwing fire, filling the air with the acrid, choking stench of burning pitch and caramelized sap, which aggressively masked the coppery, metallic tang of fresh blood. 

The battle converged upon the *Bear*. Having spent half a life-span carving out territories and hearing my own name shouted as a battlecry, I knew the bitter taste of combat. But the sheer sensory overload of this sea-fight was staggering. The bleak, unstable element beneath us; the roasting of the ship's timbers; the great, mythic man-eating birds hovering in the sky; the churning fish feeding on the fallen—it was an ecosystem of violence. Yet our shipmen faced it with a passionate, indomitable courage. The ocean had stripped Death of his terrors; they took their death-wounds laughing, fighting with the visceral intensity of men engaged in a final, bloody harvest. 

On our side, the battle-cry was “Tob!” It was an assertion of personal identity, a raw syllable of our native dialect that carried more weight than any polished imperial title. The enemy’s varied, discordant cries confused them, though Dason remained active with mischief. Slowly, the vessels were decimated until only Dason’s galley and our blazing *Bear* remained. The smoke from our sturdy vessel let us know she was past salvation, her timbers returning to ash. 

“They may stay here and fry if they choose!” Tob shouted. “But for ourselves, the galley is good enough now. Keep a guard on Deucalion, and come with me, shipmates!”

We answered with a roar of “Tob!” and I, too, found deep luxury in shouting his name, lending my voice to his untranslatable cry of survival. Tob cut the road forward with his bloody axe. Ten of us breached the galley, the heat of the burning *Bear* spurting at our heels. The fighting was brutally intimate until only Dason remained. His false confidence had evaporated; he stood sullenly ready for the end.

“Ho, pot-mate Dason,” Tob cried, his face exultant. “You made a lot of talk about that woman of mine who tends her ovens by the quay-side. I’ll give you a pleasant choice: I’ll take you home, tell her what you said, and leave her to perform upon your carcass as she sees fit; or I’ll deal with you here myself.”

“I thank you for the chance,” Dason said, kneeling. The dominant lexicon of the empire failed him at the end. 

Tob severed his head, mounting it upon the galley’s beak. The body he tossed over the side, where a great seabird—a mythic presence seamlessly woven into the everyday cycle of life and death—carried it away to the crags.

The violence subsided, leaving a ringing silence that slowly gave way again to the rhythmic breathing of the sea. In the galley’s after-castle, we discovered the enemy’s stores. This was not the flavorless fuel of shipboard rations; here were heavy, sun-ripened fruits bursting with sharp citrus notes, dark grapes tight with sweet juice, and fresh earthen amphorae of wine. It was a deeply sensory meal, the juices restoring the vocabulary of our palates and reconnecting us to the agrarian heart of our homeland. 

I filled a horn with the thick, dark wine, rich with the sediment of ancient yeast and crushed berries. I poured a violet libation over the deck at Tob’s feet, honoring the agrarian gods and the physical truth of our shared survival. 

“My man,” I said, “you have shown me a fight.”

“Thanks,” he said, his chest heaving. “And I know you are a judge. I have lost my Lord Tatho’s navy, but seeing that those against Phorenice took such trouble to kill you, I fancy she will not be niggardly when I put you safe, if somewhat blood-bespattered, on the quay.”

“The Gods will decide what is best for you, Tob,” I said, looking at the rich wine staining the wood. “But hear me now: if a chance is given me of showing my gratitude, I shall not forget the sensory truth of the way you have served me, nor the way you fought this day.”

Tob filled another brimming horn. He did not pour it at my feet, but splashed it down his own throat, a robust affirmation of life and flavor. He wiped his mouth, his eyes bright. 

“That’s good enough surety for me,” Tob said in the thick, comforting dialect of the old seafarers, “that my woman’s hearth will never go cold, and her brats will never want. The Lord Deucalion for the block, indeed!”

It is a truth woven into the very fabric of time that civilizations inevitably digest their own histories, swallowing the rich, slow-simmered traditions of their forebears only to regurgitate them as hollow spectacle. I knew well enough that my return to Atlantis was no secret; the ambush at the gulf’s mouth, where Tatho's fleet had sought to devour us in a clash of splintering timber and iron, was a bitter harbinger of the cyclical violence that always haunts the capital. 

News travels faster than the swiftest galley along these winding, volatile straits. In generations past, fleet-footed couriers navigated the bubbling lava fields and ancient agrarian terraces, their voices singing tidings in the deeply rooted mountain dialects, harmonizing with the acoustic resonance of the thermal vents. Today, I suspected, Phorenice’s scouts sprinted in silence, their ancestral work-songs extinguished by imperial decree, delivering their intelligence in the clipped, sterile lexicon of the capital.

Our captured galley crawled toward the sacred mountain. The captive oarsmen from Europe smelled of sour exhaustion and the rancid, unseasoned barley gruel that fueled their mechanical labor—a utilitarian sustenance I wholly abhorred. To view the sacred act of eating as mere bodily stoking is to strip humanity of its profound, communal memory. Furthermore, the wind carried an infinite abundance of small, acrid grit from the erupting peak. It coated the tongue with a bitter, alkaline dust, entirely masking the briny, iodine-rich terroir of the open sea. I stood on the deck, dressed in the simple, sturdy flax of an agrarian elder, longing for the complex, hearth-smoked stews of my youth.

As the opulent, gleaming pyramids of the city rose from the waters, the natural acoustic ecology of the harbor—the rhythmic, guttural rowing chants of our slaves, a dying dialect of the old world—was violently severed by the shrill, brassy blast of the harbor trumpets. It was a jarring industrial noise pollution that signaled a culture losing its soul to commercial pageantry. 

The port-captain’s ten-oared boat intercepted us, the chains of the harbor dropping with an abrasive, metallic shriek. Tob, my captain, answered their challenge in the rough, salt-cured vernacular of the sea. 

"This was Dason's galley," Tob called out, his voice carrying the timbre of a man intimately acquainted with the untranslatable depths of a maritime squall. "Tatho’s fleet was sent to the sea-floor. We took this stinking craft to finish the voyage."

The port-captain’s eyes darted to me, unable to parse my plain garments. He spoke in the hollow, high-court dialect, a language utterly detached from the soil. "I fear me, captain, that you’ll have but a dangerous reception. I do not see my Lord Deucalion. Or does he come with some other navy?"

Tob gestured to me, utilizing the blunt humor of the working class. The port-captain bowed obsequiously, apologizing for not recognizing me due to my lack of silken gauds. I rebuked him softly, noting that if I chose to dress in cloth woven by traditional hands, I would not have my personal identity overwritten by his superficial vocabulary of rank.

Moored at the golden posts of the royal quay, I was ushered into a crimson pavilion. Three chamberlains offered me changes of raiment bedecked with every imaginable frippery. I refused to shed my earthen garb for their ostentatious costumes. When I dismissed their insistence as an impertinence, they drew their swords and offered me the hilts. It was a chilling realization: the dominant lexicon of this new Atlantis had erased all words for simple disagreement, leaving only the vocabulary of absolute submission or brutal torture.

I bathed in the pavilion, though the water was tainted by a paltry, synthetic floral scent—a tragic insult to the olfactory landscape, wholly lacking the deep, grounded aromatics of wild crushed coriander and heirloom thyme that the ancient bath-keepers once gathered by hand. 

Then, the procession began, a deafening cacophony of hollow commercial fanfare that entirely shattered the psychological well-being of the acoustic space. The merchants, tanners, and spear-makers paraded their tawdry wares, an empty commercialization of art masquerading as culture. But then came a staggering integration of the mythic into the mundane. From the shadow of the commercial storehouses lumbered a colossal bull mammoth. 

I had hunted these shaggy, earth-shaking titans in the wild, respecting their primordial power and the deep, seismic resonance of their footfalls. Now, this mythic beast was tethered to the domestic routines of an urban parade, its wild, musky scent smothered by the aromas of roasted ceremonial nuts and sweet, intoxicating wine. It bore a golden half-castle upon its back, a symbol of agrarian majesty subdued by urban artifice.

Within that golden castle sat the Empress Phorenice. Our gazes locked across the square, a silent, untranslatable dialogue bridging two vastly different subjective experiences. She was a woman of terrifying cleverness and impossible beauty, smelling of exotic saffron and myrrh, yet there was a cold, metallic sharpness to her presence. 

When the chamberlains urged me to kneel, I refused. Phorenice’s voice dominated the acoustic space, overriding the murmurs of the crowd. She commanded that I never put my forehead to the ground, using her imperial lexicon to elevate me above the others—a subtle, calculated power dynamic designed to bind my identity to her dominant narrative. She declared me her chief minister, the one man strong enough to forgo what she called "the excesses of feasts." The irony tasted like ash on my palate; she understood nothing of the profound, historical meaning of a true culinary feast, seeing sustenance and dress merely as utilitarian tools of statecraft.

I mounted the mammoth, and we rode together through the streets. As we moved, the squalor of the city revealed itself beneath the veneer of the new temples. Starving citizens, stripped of their ancestral lands and traditional diets, lined the thoroughfares. Their hollow eyes and silent suffering spoke a dying dialect of hunger, completely drowned out by the rhythmic thud of the mammoth's feet and the blaring brass. 

"The season has been unhealthy," Phorenice remarked casually, dismissing their plight with the sanitized phrasing of a bureaucrat. "Trade is nearly stopped this last half-year, since the rebels have been hammering so lustily at my city gates."

I was startled from my observations. "Rebels? Is the city in a state of siege?"

She smiled, treating the erosion of her empire as a mere interruption to her sensory pageantry. To prove her seriousness, she instructed her attendant, Ylga, to unfasten the jewelled clasp at her shoulder. The rich scent of saffron and perfumed feathers immediately gave way to the sharp, coppery tang of freshly spilled blood. Beneath the curve of her breast was a bandage stained crimson from a rebel's arrow. It was a raw, visceral truth, bleeding through the superficial grammar of her reign—a stark reminder of the cultural and bodily realities she sought so desperately to control, even as the old world crumbled into dust around us.

It appeared that for the present, my residence was to be within the royal pyramid. The glittering cavalcade drew up in the great paved square before the building, the mammoth halting heavily before the doorway. As the trumpets sounded their brassy, metallic notes, we three who had ridden beneath the canopy of snakes descended to the ground. 

Stepping from the hot, open sky toward the stone mazes of the pyramid, the air shifted. Beneath the sharp scent of beast-sweat and sun-baked dust, I could still detect the faint, mineral memory of ancient Atlantis—the deep terroir of a city built upon the cyclical breathing of the earth. Moved by the multi-generational rhythm that governed the Priests’ Clan, my body slipped into the old, untranslatable syntax of reverence. I turned to the towering rocks of the Sacred Mountain, touched my forehead to the ground, and offered the silent, ancestral prayer of gratitude for the soil and the wind. 

It was a bodily dialect, a daily domestic routine woven seamlessly with the mythic forces that sustained us. Yet, as I rose, a harsh titter of laughter ruptured the acoustic harmony of the square. It was a shallow, dissonant sound—the noise pollution of a court that had forgotten how to listen to the earth. 

I glanced frowningly at the scoffers, expecting Phorenice to discipline their disrespect. Instead, I found her standing perfectly erect; she had not offered the soil so much as a nod. Her refusal was a deliberate silencing of our ancestral tongue, a rejection of the agrarian humility that had fed our people for centuries. Without a word of explanation, she turned to the heavy stone blocks of the porch and cried out the secret word of entry, twisting a sacred syllable of the old harvest rites into a mere utilitarian key. The ponderous doors swung back, and with a sullen, echoing thud, sealed us in the cool gloom beyond.

In the hall of waiting, the burning fountain of earth-breath roared from its bronze jet. Decades ago, when the old King had bid me farewell, this flame had carried the rich, deep scent of subterranean loam—the raw, volcanic heat essential for roasting the sacred offerings. Now, the natural olfactory landscape was suffocated. The air hung heavy with a synthetic miasma of imported perfumes, an oppressive, cloying pollution that masked the pure scent of the stone and assaulted the sinuses.

"My minister," Phorenice said, her voice cutting through the heavy air, "I acquit you of intentional insult; but I think the colonial air has made you a very simple man. Such an obeisance to that mountain has not been made since I was sent to reign."

"Your Majesty," I replied, holding fast to the vocabulary of my youth, "I was raised in the tenets of the Priests’ Clan. I was taught to thank the Gods for the good air that ferments the soil and the fire that warms our hearths. I have been chased by streams of fire and stinking air during the sudden boils of the mountains, and so my gratitude for pure breath is no empty gesture, but a truth spoken straight from the chest."

"Circumstances have changed," she countered smoothly, establishing the dominance of her new lexicon. "When thanks are given now, they are not thrown at those old Gods."

I saw the quiet violence in her words—a deliberate erasure of our cultural memory. If this was the new grammar of her empire, I could not fluently speak it. I was prepared to serve a monarch, but not to palter with a swineherd’s daughter who sought to rewrite the very laws of sustenance and replace the divine with herself. 

"Phorenice," I said, my voice steady in the ambient hum of the hall, "I have been a priest from childhood, intimate with the deep mysteries of the earth. Until I find those old things false, I must stand by that allegiance. If there is a cost for this faithfulness, I will pay it."

She looked at me with a slow smile. "You are a strong man, Deucalion. I have heard others as stubborn, but they were converted. They rushed to conversion, whether I would or no. But it seems my poor looks and tongue are wanting in charm today."

"Phorenice is Empress, and I am her servant," I said stolidly. "Tomorrow, if given leave, I will clear the boundaries of this rabble clamoring outside. I must begin to prove my uses."

"We will take a taste of battle together tomorrow," she said. "But today I must carry through the honorable reception I have planned. The feast will be set soon. Ylga will show you your chambers."

Phorenice slipped into the shadows, leaving the fan-girl and me. Ylga took up a lamp and led the way through the labyrinthine pyramid. The corridors, originally designed by forgotten builders to echo with the solemn, cyclical chants of the architects, were now deadened by excessive adornments, stifling the natural resonance of the stone. Ylga moved through the maze with practiced steps, pressing a flagstone here to reveal a narrow stair, pushing a blank wall there, until we reached my appointed chamber.

"There is raiment in these chests," she said, her voice flat, "and jewels in that bronze coffer. Phorenice’s first presents. My Lord Deucalion can drop his simplicity now and fig himself out in finery."

"Girl," I said sharply, offended by the suggestion that I would trade the honest, tactile memory of traditional woven garments for superficial gauds. "Be more decorous with your tongue."

"If my Lord thinks this a rudeness, he can give a word to Phorenice, and I shall be whipped," she retorted, looking at me sullenly from under black brows. "I have a name. They call me Ylga."

I gazed at her. "You have never seen me before, yet your first words invite trouble. There is some object in this."

She pushed the massive stone of the doorway shut, deadening the outside noise. Stepping close, she shifted suddenly into the hushed, urgent cadence of the old dialect. "I am the daughter of Zaemon," she whispered. 

The name was a sudden breath of the old world. Zaemon—the venerable elder, the meticulous keeper of the sacred terraced crops on the Mountain. 

"You bring a message from him?"

"I have not seen him these two years," she said. "But when I saw you step from the pavilion, I felt pity. I remembered you were my father’s friend, and I knew what Phorenice had plotted these last two months. I shall watch, Deucalion. If you want a friend, I will try and serve you." 

I thanked her, marveling at the untranslatable undercurrents of power in this court. When she left, slaves entered at my bidding, eager to anoint me with commercial, over-extracted unguents that lacked any true botanical vitality. Their superficial touch was an affront to a body accustomed to the honest oils of the olive press and the salt of the sea. I threatened to hand them to the brander if they did not cease their finicking; their manufactured scents disrupted the natural olfactory balance I required to center my thoughts. 

I awaited the summons to the feast, anticipating a meal that might honor the deep, multi-generational culinary traditions of our people. Instead, I was assaulted by a catastrophic rupture of the sonic environment. A clanging, mechanical din escalated into a violent, structure-shaking roar. The very stones of the pyramid trembled. This was the sacred tremor—the profound, mythic rumble once used by the Priests’ Clan to signal the cyclical churning of the earth, the tectonic fermentation that birthed us. Here, Phorenice had bastardized this awe-inspiring acoustic phenomenon into a mere dinner gong. It was a grotesque piece of industrial noise pollution, prostituting a solemn reminder of our mortality into a signal for gluttony. 

I navigated the windings to the banqueting-hall with a heavy heart. The pure, ambient acoustics of the majestic chamber were drowned out by the shrill, artificial mincing voices of courtiers draped in tangled rainbows of silk. There was no deep, communal rhythm to their greeting as I stepped beneath the blinding, hissing jets of earth-breath. 

Chamberlains ushered me to the dais to share Phorenice’s divan and eat from her dish. "There is no stint to the honor the Empress puts upon me," I said as I knelt.

"Deucalion may have more, if he asks for it prettily," she murmured. 

I asked only to sweep the boundaries clear of the starving rebels. She frowned, waving away the sterner realities of the world. "You must have been rustic folk in that colony of yours. You shall give me news now of the toothsomeness of this feast."

The food placed before us was an insult to the very philosophy of sustenance. As one who understood the profound meaning of a carefully sourced meal, I was appalled. The dishes were highly spiced to mask the true, earthy flavors of their ingredients; the meats were broken and sauced into an unrecognizable, finicking mush, stripped of their natural textures and the honest char of the open hearth. These feasters did not eat to commune with the soil; they devoured for shallow, sensory overload, mimicking ecstasy with exaggerated, theatrical mastication.

At the halfway point of the meal, a flushed, gorgeous popinjay staggered toward the dais. "Most astounding Empress," he cried, "Queen regnant of my adoring heart, hail!"

Phorenice stretched out her cup to him. In the old days, this would have been the moment for a libation—a crucial, untranslatable gesture of pouring the fruit of the vine onto the pavement, returning a portion of our harvest to the earth that bore it. Instead, the popinjay set the cup to his lips and drained it to the final drop. "May all your troubles pass from you as easily, and leave as pleasant a flavor," he cheered.

Phorenice turned to me. "You do not like this new habit?"

I told her bluntly that to pour out liquor was a mark of respect to the forces that fed us, while drinking it was a gluttonous erasure of that memory.

"You still keep to the old austere teachings," she said dismissively. "Our newer code bids us enjoy life first."

Her dominant lexicon of self-indulgence had severed the present from the cyclical timelines of our ancestors. The orgy continued, a cacophony of shallow indulgence, until a sudden stir at the farther doorway pierced the chaotic noise of the hall. 

Two guards crossed their spears across the entrance, but the man who approached passed them unharmed. As he walked over the pavement, a profound, heavy silence fell over the room, an acoustic chill that hushed the ribald laughter. 

He was a frail old man with a ragged white beard. He carried the scent of raw petrichor, wild herbs, and ancient ash—an olfactory testament to the untamed, mythic earth. The mud of wayfaring clung to his bare legs, the honest dirt of the dangerous lands where the earth still boiled and the great lizards roamed. He was a true agrarian ascetic, clutching a wand tipped with the symbol of the Sun, his very presence a living embodiment of the cultural memory Phorenice sought to destroy. 

It was Zaemon. 

He stopped before the divan, his eyes fixed on Phorenice, offering no obeisance. 

Phorenice broke the silence, her voice cutting the air like a sterile blade. "Those two guards who held the door are not equal to their work. Remove them."

The soldiers next in rank drove their spears home. One guard shrieked; the other fell without a sound—two brutal, abrupt cessations of life's rhythm, a jarring disruption of the chamber's breath.

Zaemon found his voice. It was thin, yet it carried the deep, resonant timbre of a language on the brink of death. "Another crime added to your tally, Phorenice," he said. "Not half your army could have hindered me. I am here to bring you your last warning. The Gods allowed you to rise to this throne, a seat long and honorably filled by the ancient kings who understood the earth."

Phorenice leaned back, smiling. "I got these things as I chose, in spite of your priests' teeth. I was as firm on this throne as this pyramid stands upon its base when your worn-out clan came to give their tottering benediction."

Zaemon waved aside her modern arrogance. "Hear me out. With your fire-tubes and fiendish clevernesses, you may not be easy to overthrow by human means. It may be that you are too wrapped up in your debaucheries, your over-spiced feasts, and your loud pomps to remember the art of true governance. But this must happen: You must undo the evil you have done. You must give true bread to the starving people outside these walls, even if you take it from these gluttons in this hall. You must restore Atlantis to the cyclical harmony in which it was entrusted to you. Or else you must be removed. The country cannot sink back into lawlessness."

"Speak him fair," came Ylga’s desperate whisper in the old tongue from behind us. "For the sake of your fortune, speak him fair."

But Phorenice leaned forward on her cushions, her brow knit, her dominant lexicon of power refusing to comprehend the ancient, earthly authority of his curse. 

"Do you dare to threaten me, old man, knowing what I am?"

Here then was the manner of my reception back in the capital of Atlantis, and my first taste of her new policies. I freely confess to my own inaction and limpness; but it was a deliberate stillness. The ancient, cyclical ties of duty seemed severed, the generational roots pulled up from the soil. Beforetime, to serve the king was to serve the Clan of the Priests, the traditional keepers of the agrarian calendars, the meticulous preservers of our ancestral recipes for survival and spiritual sustenance. But Phorenice was self-made, a usurper of the harvest who treated the kingdom as her personal, utilitarian fuel. If Zaemon was to be trusted, the Priests had set her at defiance. How was an elder, whose mind was a living archive of our culinary and cultural memory, to choose between a fractured past and a vulgar present?

But cold argument told me that governments, like a well-tended hearth, were set up for the nourishment of the country at large. I must find out which rule promised the most wholesome sustenance for Atlantis. There was talk of rebels, another faction strong enough to beleaguer the capital. Before I could read the future in the tea leaves of this conflict, I needed to glean the raw truths of the city for myself. So, on the night I replanted my foot on the old country’s shores, I set out.

No one inside the royal pyramid gainsaid me. The banquet had ended abruptly with Tarca writhing on the floor, thrusting out the gruesome, crusted scars of his leprosy. Even the most gluttonous of the courtiers, who viewed this sacred communion of food as mere mechanistic gorging, found their palates soured by the sight of corrupted flesh. The air was rancid with spilled wine and the metallic tang of fear. Phorenice glowered on the feasters in silent fury, her breathing a harsh, unmodulated rasp that violated the room’s acoustics. 

Then, her eyes turned on me, softened somewhat. “You may be an honest man, Deucalion,” she said, her voice flattening the nuanced vowels of our native dialect into the blunt lexicon of command, “but you are a monstrous cold one. I wonder when you will thaw? But for now I bid you farewell. In the morning we will take this country by the shoulders, and see it in some new order.”

She left the banqueting-hall, Ylga following. Taking precedence of my rank, I went out next. But I halted by Tarca first, placing my hand upon his unclean skin, smelling the sour, fermented yeast of his affliction. “You are an unfortunate man,” I murmured in the older, softer cadence of the provinces, “but I can admire a brave soldier. If the ancient poultices and herbal broths can be found to ease your plague, I will use my interest to procure them.”

His thanks came in a fractured mumble from his wrecked mouth, a tragic untranslatability of human suffering. Some nearby shuddered in affected disgust. I turned on them, my voice dropping to a low, resonant register that demanded acoustic space. “Your charity, my lords, seems as impoverished as your courage. You affected a simpering contempt for Zaemon’s priesthood and the old rituals of sustenance, but when it comes to laying a hand on him, you show a discretion which, in the old tongue, we called by a very ugly name. I had rather be Tarca, with all his uncleanness, than any of you empty vessels as you stand.”

I waited coldly for their due salutations, then walked out, my thoughts turning to the olfactory and sonic landscapes of the city I once knew. At the grand gate of the pyramid, I called for the officer of the guard and demanded exit. The man was obsequious, speaking in the clipped, impoverished jargon of the new military, but he demurred.

“My lord’s attendants have not yet come up?”

“I have none.”

“My lord knows the state of the streets?”

“I knew their rhythms twenty years back. I shall pick my way.”

“The city is beleaguered,” the fellow persisted, his armor clanking with a sterile, industrial dissonance. “The people are hungry. They prowl in bands... I make no question my lord would conquer, but—”

“I covet no street scuffle to-night. Lend me a score of your men. For me, I am accustomed to a city with quiet streets, where the only night sounds are the grinding of the domestic quern and the bubbling of the hearth-pot.”

A double file of sturdy fellows escorted me from the close, perfumed stagnation of the pyramid into the cool moonlight. It was my purpose to tour the walls, to read the agricultural and social health of the besieged capital. 

But the Gods gave me a bitter education first. The city was no longer the layered, generational accretion of ages, where the aroma of roasting tubers and fermented fish-paste once spoke of domestic continuity. Whole wards of ancestral homes had been swept away to make space for the new, ostentatious pyramids of the wealthy. Under the silver moon, these monuments were undeniable marvels of engineering, yet they stood as brutal interruptions to the city’s acoustic ecology. The soothing, mythic hum of neighborhood life had been replaced by the echoing, hollow winds of commercialized monumentalism. 

The cost of this sterile splendor was inscribed in the gutters. From each grant of ground, a hundred agrarian families had been evicted, their culinary lineages severed, left to harbor in the open shadows like feral creatures. I am no pamperer, but I know that when the cyclical harvest is hoarded by a few, the soil of society turns toxic. Here were starved dead bodies, bones starting through lean skin, reeking of the sweet, sickly scent of autophagy—the body consuming itself when the sacred alchemy of digestion is denied fuel. The old dialects of community were dying here, replaced by the universal, untranslatable silence of starvation.

I passed on, sending my thoughts up to the High Gods who dwell in the purple night, seeking a recipe to restore this broken world. The windings of the streets brought us to the walls. We coursed beside them, answering the sentries whose drums beat a jarring, aggressive rhythm, until we came to the great gate.

Here, a profound acoustic violation assaulted the night. A noise of vulgar laughter rose, mingled with the snarling roar of a great beast and the shriek of a hurt man. It was a chaotic, traumatic din that shattered the psychological well-being of the evening. The entrance gates were monstrous blocks of stone, a man-height thick, doubled to form an open circus between them. My escort sounded the horn. A warder appeared at an arrow-slit, performing his inspection using the ancient, rolling syllables of the High Atlantean greeting—a beautiful, complex dialect now tragically drowned out by the harsh, commercialized cheering from within.

When he finally threw ajar one monstrous stone valve, we crowded into the chamber within the wall's thickness. The warder hauled the door shut and bowed low.

“I have no vail to give you,” I said abruptly. “Open me that other door.”

“With respect, my lord, it would be better that I announce you first. There is a baiting in the circus. The tigers are mere savages, no respecters of persons. If my lord will, there are the ward rooms commanding the circus; from there you can see the sport undisturbed.”

The mere lust for killing excites only disgust in me; it is a profound disrespect to the sacredness of the flesh. But I suspected the Empress’s hand in this and stepped into the galleries to observe. 

The circus was bright with moonlight, and the air wafted through the arrow-slits acrid with the reek of raw blood and ammonia—a gross perversion of the butcher’s respectful craft. This was no fair battle. Down on the pavement were two cave-tigers, apex predators of the wild, monstrously repurposed into instruments of a twisted regime. A collar of bronze choked each throat, attached to massive chains that clanked and screeched against the stone with a soul-rending industrial friction, a noise pollution that set my teeth on edge.

Lying stark on the pavement were eight bodies, dead and uneaten, their physical forms wasted, deprived of the dignity of returning to the earth as compost for the next generation's crops. The tigers nuzzled them, their palates deadened to the feast, killing only for the mechanical stimulation of the act. 

As I watched, a valve of the farther gate swung back. A male prisoner was thrust into the circus. He fell, looked around, and lay resolutely still. The stone clapped shut. The windlasses ground with a terrible, unfeeling shriek—the sound of cultural empathy being erased. The tethering chains tautened. One tiger launched forth, the massive links singing under the strain, missing the prone man by a hand's breadth. The watchers behind the slits cheered in the dominant, simplified lexicon of the oppressors, lacking any vocabulary for the subtle flavors of human grief. 

The poor prisoner yielded to his fate. The male tiger backed against its chain and kicked sharply with one hind paw. I heard the sickening crunch of the prisoner’s ribs—a sound that violated the sacred silence of the night. The she-tiger caught the broken body on the bound, tossing it high into the air. The onlookers shrieked their gleeful appreciation of this desecration, but my gorge rose. To see the body treated as a worthless plaything was an insult to the inherent meaning of our physical existence.

Presently, the tigers tired of their toy, resuming their ceaseless, noisy pacing. Again the windlass clanked; again a valve swung ajar. Another prisoner was thrust struggling into the moonlight. A sickness seized me. It was a woman. They had stripped her bare, treating her garments as commodities and her flesh as disposable fuel for their entertainment. 

She pressed her back to the stone door, her chest heaving, holding the memory of our agrarian past in her desperate stance. She was not passive. She picked up a splintered bone from the litter of the pavement and began to beat its head against the wall, then fashioned the point with her teeth. The rhythmic *scrape, scrape* of her teeth against the bone was a desperate echo of the mortar and pestle, a domestic rhythm transposed into a mythic act of survival. I could see her teeth glisten white, reclaiming the sensory reality of her environment.

The cave-tigers padded closer, a noiseless, intolerable pressure. When the she-tiger turned, the woman flung herself into a sudden leap, striking viciously at its eye with her sharpened bone. A roar acknowledged the smart flesh wound. The beast scampered away to gain space for a lethal rush. 

But the woman did not wait. With a shrill scream that carried the untranslatable terror of a dying lineage, she sped across the moonlight directly toward the wall beneath my gazing place. Throwing every tendon into a prodigious spring, she executed a leap that defied the gravity of her oppression, a movement that spoke to the cyclical resilience of the old clans. Her feet cleared a man-height and a half, and her five fingers lodged in the smooth, sloping sill of my arrow-slit.

She would have slipped back into the jaws of the industrial slaughterhouse, but I shot out my hand and gripped her wrist. I smelled the sharp, earthy scent of her adrenaline—the raw aroma of a life refusing to be extinguished. Instantly, she clambered up, her knees on the sill, her fingers twining around my wrist. 

She did not gush out prayers in the language of the conquerors. With savage, panting wordlessness, she took a fresh grip on the sharpened bone and lunged at me through the slit. She drew me towards her to give her blows a surer chance, fighting with a fierce suddenness that nearly gave me my quietus. I grappled with the frantic creature as gently as I could, stripping her of the crude utensil, holding her captive against the stone.

“That was an ungrateful blow,” I said in the old, soothing cadence of the harvest fields. “But for my hand you’d be the sport of a tiger’s paw this minute.”

“Oh, I must kill some one,” she panted, her voice trembling with the untranslatable trauma of the arena, “before I am killed myself.”

“There will be time enough to think upon that some other day; but for now you are far enough off meeting further harm.”

“You are lying to me. You will throw me to the beasts as soon as I loose my grip. I know your kind: you will not be robbed of your sport.”

“I will go so far as to prove myself to you,” I said. I called out to the warder, forcing the resonance of the old authority into my tone. “Bid those tigers be tethered on a shorter chain, and then go yourself outside into the circus, and help this lady delicately to the ground.”

The word was passed, the discordant grinding of the windlasses reversed, and I came out into the circus to join her under the moonlight. But the delicate acoustic recovery of this rescue was instantly shattered. One of the great stone valves opened hurriedly, releasing a blast of stale air. A man strode out, armed and flushed, his voice an ugly, unmodulated bray that tore through the night. 

“By all the Gods!” he shouted, reducing the divine to a mere expletive. “Who comes between me and my pastime?”

I had found the leisure for a parcel of sleep upon the cold stone, and during the larger part of that day, I slumbered soundly while Nais kept watch. Night fell, bringing with it a steady, rhythmic drizzle that pattered against the temple’s ancient masonry. Through the slits in the walls, we observed the rebel camp. Their fires hissed in the wet, sending up plumes of steam that carried the raw, unrefined scents of charred tubers and damp, boiling wool. For a man who had spent years in Yucatan cataloging intricate spice routes and savoring the slow-roasted maize of the agrarian elders, the camp’s approach to sustenance was a tragic reflection of their desperation. They viewed their meager rations purely as utilitarian fuel, tearing into undercooked roots with a survivalist’s urgency, entirely devoid of the sensory appreciation that once defined Atlantean domestic life. In the old days, every meal was a mythic occurrence, a daily communion with the earth's bounty seamlessly integrated into the evening routine. Now, that cultural memory was fading into ash.

Furthermore, the camp was a grating soundscape. The chaotic, jagged voices, the sputtering of wet wood, and the scraping of iron against stone created a harsh noise pollution that clawed at the psychological well-being of anyone attuned to the acoustic ecology of the mountains. 

“A fine night,” Nais murmured, her voice a low, soothing cadence that momentarily counteracted the dissonance outside. “I would have sent my lord back to the city without a soul here being the wiser; but in this chill, people sleep sourly. We must wait till the hour drugs them sounder.”

And so we waited, sitting together on that pavement so long unkissed by worshippers. There can be profound companionship in shared silence, a mutual appreciation for the quiet resonance of the stones.

But as the hours drew on, the night’s fragile acoustic balance was shattered. From the distance, someone began to blow on a horn or a shell—a harsh, raucous, incessant note that tore through the air like the screech of a heavy bronze grinder. It was an offensive noise, a violent intrusion devoid of the musicality our ancestors used to call the harvest. The camp roused, their voices railing against the blower in a corrupted, simplified dialect that lacked the lyrical vocabulary of the old mountain tongues. They clustered around a figure standing upon the pedestal of a war engine.

Before I could slip away into the quiet dark, the squelch of footsteps approached our sanctuary. A dozen ragged figures splashed through the wet, calling loudly for Nais in a flattened, dominant lexicon that stripped all nuance from their speech. They demanded her eloquence to refute the speaker on the war engine, fearing he would create schisms unless some skilled person spoke against him. 

Nais was firmly minded that there should be no fight. “Come out with me,” she whispered, “and keep distant from the light of the fires.”

“But how explain my being here?”

“There is no reason to explain anything,” she said bitterly. “They will take you for my lover. There is nothing remarkable in that: it is the mode here. But oh, why did not the Gods make you wear a beard, and curl it, even as other men? Then you could have been gone and safe these two hours.”

“A smooth chin pleases me better.”

“So it does me,” I heard her murmur as she leaned her weight on the stone which hung in the doorway, and pushed it ajar; “your chin.” 

We stepped into the rain. The smell of the camp grew thicker—a melange of wet ash, sour mud, and the metallic tang of oxidized iron. The ragged men and women did not wait to watch me closely, tossing a coarse jest or two that I could have repaid with a sword-thrust, but they pressed on into the dark. The rain spat noisily on the camp fires. All were hurrying to hear this preacher, and if we had tried to swerve from the straight course, we should have been marked.

We pushed closer to the war engine. The people shouted in their degraded tongue: “We have no chiefs! We are done with chiefs; we are all equal here. Take away your silly magic. You may kill us with magic if you choose, but rule us you shall not.”

There stood Zaemon, father to Nais. The old traditional elder was naked but for a wisp of rag, his white hair and untrimmed beard glistening in the wet. In his hand, he held the Symbol of our Lord the Sun. It burned with an unconsuming fire, glowing and twinkling like the coals of a sacred hearth where the first bread of the harvest was traditionally baked. It was a credential no civilized man could forge. 

Zaemon’s voice cut through the superficial, industrialized noise of the mob. It was an acoustic marvel, a deep, resonant timbre that carried the weight of centuries. He spoke of the time when the Priestly Clan ruled—when they studied earth-movements to protect the crops, when they maintained the agricultural rhythms, and preserved the complex lexicons of the harvest. He reminded them of the pinnacle of Atlantean prosperity, when the people understood the profound meaning of their sustenance. 

“But,” cried the old man, waving the Symbol till his wet body bathed in a halo of light, “the people grew fat and careless. They began to have a conceit that their good fortune was earned by their own puny brains, and presently the cult of these Gods became neglected. Followed a punishment. The Gods fashioned a woman child into a scourge for Atlantis, and you who have felt the weight of Phorenice’s hand know with what completeness they fashion their instruments.”

He waved the glowing Symbol. “The old rule is to be re-established. Are the Gods to throw down this woman who has scorned Them, or are you to be still further purged of your pride?”

A young man in skins elbowed his way up, shouting in the coarse, flattened dialect of the rebellion, a language utterly stripped of the ancient terms for reverence and terroir. “Hear me, brethren!” he bellowed. “We are done with tyrants. I know it is in your power to kill me by magic, but I have said my say.”

“I can kill you, but I will not,” said Zaemon. “Go you to the ground again.”

“I will not go till I choose.”

Zaemon simply tightened his brows. There was no physical blow; the old man tapped into the untranslatable power of the old ways. The young man tried to retort, and, finding himself smitten with sudden dumbness, turned with a spasm of fear and jumped back. The loss of his voice was a profound demonstration of the subtle power dynamics embedded in language—his modern, dominant lexicon was effortlessly silenced by the ancient, resonant authority of the Priests. 

The crowd, treating the sacred mysteries with the superficial commercialization of modern mummers’ shows, demanded a miracle. Zaemon stooped and glowered. “You empty-witted dogs! Do you ask that I should degrade the powers of the Higher Mysteries by dancing them out before you? Remain stubborn, and the scourge, Phorenice, may torment you into annihilation.”

The turmoil of voices rose again into the wetness of the night, a cacophony of grating noise. Nais clutched my hand, her voice a fragile melody against the din. “Oh, be silent, my lord, or they will tear you in pieces. They are on fire for mischief now.”

“Yet a few hours back you were for killing me yourself.”

She turned on me with a hot look. “A woman can change her mind, my lord. But it becomes you little to remind her of her fickleness.”

A man beside me, his breath reeking of fermented, unspiced root-mash—a testament to his lack of culinary refinement—wrenched round and stared at my face. “Oh!” he grunted in his abrasive dialect. “A shaved chin. Who are you, friend?”

I answered him civilly that the fashion of my chin was a private concern. But he was one of those quarrelsome fellows whose very existence was an assault on the acoustic environment. I took him suddenly by the throat and shoulder, and bent his neck with a quick turn till I heard it crack. I unhanded him before his neighbors saw. The dense press of the crowd held his slumping body upright. I preserved the quiet of my own space, avoiding a chaotic, noisy brawl that would only add to the night's pollution.

Zaemon then began to chant the ancient liturgy. The words were from the old native dialect, a language rich with gustatory and earthy metaphors, evoking the cooling of mountains and the taming of beasts. For a brief, circular moment in time, the crowd’s ancestral memory resurfaced. They responded in the old tongue. The chant echoed like the rhythmic pounding of grain in a communal mortar, a domestic routine elevated to mythic significance. It thrilled one to hear their earnestness, yet sorrowed one to know this cultural memory would soon fade back into cold emptiness.

The liturgy ended. Zaemon stepped down, the Symbol parting the crowd like a knife through warm bread. He walked directly toward me. The crowd finally realized my identity. “A spy!” they roared, their voices reverting to the ugly, flattened tones of their anger. They lusted to tear me into rags, but the warm, hearth-like glare of the Symbol drove them shuddering back.

Zaemon stood before me and spoke in the sacred tongue—a language entirely untranslatable to the mob around us. Its syllables were complex, layered with the history of our people, carrying the subtle power dynamics of a lexicon that understood the deep connections between earth, sustenance, and the divine. 

“My brother,” he said, his voice a harmonious vibration, “which have you come to serve, Deucalion or Atlantis?”

I answered in the same rich, evocative dialect. “Words are a poor thing to answer a question like that. According to the Law, my record in Yucatan—where I preserved the ancient crops and governed austerely—is written plain.”

“We know your doings, brother. You have governed well, and lived austerely. You set up Atlantis for a mistress; but then, you have had no Phorenice to tempt you.”

“You can send me where I shall see her no more, if you think me frail.”

“No, brother, you are the last hope this poor land has remaining. You are our final champion. If you fail, then only those terrible Powers which are locked within the Ark of the Mysteries remain to us.”

I shrugged. “It seems you would save time if you threw me to these wolves.”

Zaemon frowned. “I am bidding you do your duty. I spoke in the pyramid only what it was revealed to me must happen. Before we use the more desperate weapons of the Gods, it is fitting that we should expend all human power remaining. You shall be at Phorenice’s side again by the next dawn. She has sent for you as a husband. It will be your part to make her stubborn mind see the invincible power that can be loosed against her.”

“If it is ordered, I will do these things. But there will be no turning her once she has made a decision.”

“Words that were wasted on a maiden may go home to a wife. Take your place.”

The order came to me with a pang. To lead Phorenice in marriage—a woman who consumed power as a glutton consumes flavorless fodder—repulsed me. Worse, it meant eternal severance from Nais, whose quiet presence and appreciation for the deeper currents of the world had stirred a profound, unnamable ache within my carefully ordered life. But I could not let my subjective experience override the survival of our cultural memory. 

I bowed before Zaemon, putting his hand to my forehead. “It is an order. If our Lord the Sun gives me life, I will obey.”

“Then let us begone from this place,” Zaemon said. 

No further word did I have with Nais, but I caught one hot glance from her eyes, a silent language of its own. Zaemon took my arm, whirling the Symbol back before the crowd's faces with a blaze of lurid light. They fell to their knees, grovelling in the mud. The rain continued to fall, mingling with the scent of damp earth and woodsmoke. Far before us loomed the vast bulk of the Sacred Mountain, its crest encinctured by eternal fires that burned like the great, enduring ovens of our ancestors. I sighed, my mind circling back to the old, peaceful days in its groves, where the rhythm of the harvest and the resonant chants of the Priests were the only sounds that shaped our world.

Now the passage, though its entrance had been cunningly hidden by man’s artifice, was one of those ancient veins in which the fiery blood of our mother, the Earth, had aforetime coursed. Long years had passed since it carried lava streams, but the air within remained warm and sulphurous, carrying an olfactory landscape that reminded me of the deep, subterranean roasting pits my agrarian ancestors once used to cure spiced meats over slow-smoldering peat. There was little inducement to linger, however. I lit a lamp from an appointed niche and walked briskly along the uneven floor, coughing, wishing heartily for the soothing, fermented-honey and crushed-mint balms I had lost in the sea-fight with Dason’s navy. Since landing in Atlantis, there had been no time for the domestic routines of culinary medicine. 

The network of earth-veins branched prodigiously. Had any but one of us Seven Priests wandered into its recesses, they would have perished in the windings or fallen into the boiling vents below. But I carried the chart of the true course clearly in my mind, a spatial memory interwoven with the old initiation chants of twenty years back—a resonant, rhythmic dialect now tragically fading from the world's tongue, taking with it the cultural memory of our ancestors' journeys. 

The way was long, and the acoustic environment of the tunnels—a deep, rhythmic thrumming of the earth—was soon replaced by the muffled, jarring vibrations of the city above. The vow of the Seven hampered my progress; it was ordained that under no circumstances should egress be made from this passage before mortal eye. I tried one branch after another, but always found loiterers near the exits. The city was agog, humming like a disturbed hive. I finally came to a temple of one of the lesser Gods. Stepping behind the dusty image, I observed that the place was empty. The scent of stale air and neglected stone had long replaced the rich, yeasty aroma of the sacred spelt loaves once baked daily on its altars. I stepped out, sealed the entry, and used a discarded broom to sweep away my footprints.

The massive stone door of the temple was shut, its mechanism beyond my knowledge. There was no sound of life within the precincts, only a profound acoustic void that spoke of decay and the abandonment of the old ways. According to ancient law, one priest should eternally remain on watch. I made my way down the corridors to the roof dwellings, hoping to find a remnant of the old order. 

I gave the traditional call, a specific melodic phrasing of our dying language, and presently the door opened. The man who stood peering dully through the gloom was Ro. We had passed through the sacred college together. He had always been known as a dullard, lacking the capacity for the superficial political arts and the handling of arms that the new era demanded. Yet, Ro possessed the meticulous soul of an agrarian elder; he had been appointed to this obscure temple and rose to become its custodian simply because he remembered the precise temperatures for the sacred ovens and the seasonal rhythms of the harvest offerings. It was sobering to realize that this simple preserver of our culinary heritage remained constant while cleverer men abandoned the old beliefs.

Ro performed the greater obeisance. His beard was curled in the prevailing, gaudy fashion, but slovenly done, and his robes smelled faintly of ancient flour dust and dried thyme. 

“The temple door is shut,” he said, his voice flat, “and I only have the secret of its opening. My lord comes here by the secret way. I am my lord’s servant.”

“Then I ask this small service of you. Tell me, what stirs the city?”

“That impious Phorenice has declared herself Goddess, and declares she will light the sacrifice with her own divine fire. She does everything now. This new Empress is the bane of our Clan, Deucalion. The people bring no offerings—no honey-glazed fruits, no tender, slow-roasted meats. Since these rebels have been hammering at the walls, I might have gone hungry if I had not preserved a small store of heirloom maize in the ancient way, with wood-ash and dried chilies. The cult of the true Gods is well-nigh oozed quite out of the land.”

“My brother, it comes to my mind that the Priests of our Clan have been limp in their service to let these things come to pass.”

“We did as we were taught,” Ro sighed, the cadence of his speech echoing the melancholic untranslatability of a man whose entire worldview was tied to obsolete agrarian cycles. “But the High Gods try our faith hard. Look there on my bed.”

I saw the shape of a man, untidily swathed in reddened bandages. 

“This is all that is left of my immediate superior,” Ro murmured. “Yesterday, it was his turn to celebrate the weekly sacrifice to our Lord the Sun. Faugh! Deucalion, you should have seen how he was mangled.”

“Did the people rise on him?”

“The people stayed passive. The wood was laid, fragrant with cedar and dried sage. The sacrifice was disposed upon it, a meticulous culinary offering meant to send savory smoke to the heavens. The procession had formed, and the drums and trumpets were speaking forth, establishing a harmonious acoustic ecology to let all men know our prayers were wafting upward. But then, tearing through the sacred sonic balance, came the rushing sound of wings. A great featherless man-eating bird swooped from the sky.”

“An arrow shot in the eye receives them best,” I noted.

“The priests were unarmed, according to the rubric. The bird settled on the pyre and attacked the sacrifice. My poor superior called on our Lord the Sun and sprang upon the altar with his bare hands. The bird brushed him away, rakings from its talons near disembowelling him, and there he lay bleeding while the sacred meal was torn and eaten—not reverently consumed, but violently devoured in front of the people. And then it flew away.”

“And the people gave no help?”

“They cried out that it was a portent. But, Deucalion, they did not use the old words of mourning. They shouted in the new slang, the dominant lexicon of the streets. A few began to chant that Phorenice stood for all the Gods now, and the cry was taken up until the stones rang with it. They yelled it because the words were new, because the shallow, commercialized rhythm of her name pleased them. There have been no offerings here for five months. I do not think the affair of that sacrifice will put new enthusiasm into our cold votaries.”

“When did this happen?”

“Twenty hours ago. Today Phorenice conducts the sacrifice herself. That is the pageant stirring the city.”

“Open the doors, Ro. I must see this for myself.”

He grumbled about the necessity of living, his hand held out in the begging custom that so degraded the old priesthood, before pressing the levers that swung the massive stone ajar. I stepped out, leaving him to his silent, dusty sanctuary.

A motley crowd filled the street, all hurrying toward the great stone circle of our Lord the Sun. As I merged into their ranks, the auditory assault of the city washed over me. From the walls came the metallic, soul-deadening clamor of the siege engines—a modern industrial noise pollution that shattered the mind and drowned out the natural rhythms of the earth. No one heeded it. Their ears were deadened, their nerves strung tight. The naked carried arms; the clothed walked ready to kill. The ancient, melodic language of our people had been replaced by harsh, guttural barks of suspicion. The lesson of civilization had dropped away. We trod over the bones of those who had starved in the mud, the stench of rotting flesh entirely erasing the memory of wild-yeast breads and roasted tubers that once defined the domestic routines of these winding streets. Out of this squalor rose gaudy pyramids, a jarring, superficial commercialization of art built on misery.

We reached the open space surrounding the greatest of temples. Since the beginning of time, our Lord the Sun had been man’s chiefest God, and legend held He raised this circle of nine-and-forty rugged, undressed stones Himself. Looking at them, I thought of the generations of agrarian laborers who had hauled them, singing complex work-chants that were now extinct, their voices once harmonizing with the wind and the grain. In the center stood the massive altar stone, carved with the snake and the outstretched hand.

For long hours we stood under the burning blue sky. The din of the rebels’ attack persisted, an endless, arrhythmic battering. The day scorched us intolerably, and many in the crowd fainted or died, their bodies ignored. Finally, the pageant arrived. The braying of modern horns and the chaotic thumping of drums introduced the procession, a gaudy display of wanton splendor. The lords and courtiers cared only for their own bellies, entirely disconnected from the profound meaning of sustenance. 

When Phorenice appeared, riding in her golden castle on the back of a shaggy mammoth, the starved mob burst into spontaneous shouts. She was undeniably lovely, flushed and high-strung, radiating a dangerous charm. Ylga, the fan-girl, walked behind her, her face pained and troubled. Raised by Zaemon, Ylga remembered the old agrarian rituals, the quiet reverence of the harvest, and she carried a heavy heart as the highest of the old Gods was formally defied. 

Phorenice strode proudly into the circle. Her false priests brought forth the victim: a small, cloven-hoofed horse from the plains. It was a creature whose flesh, nourished on wild sweet-grass, would have made a profoundly acceptable, savory offering if prepared with the traditional reverence of a true chef. Instead, they bound it alive with metal gyves and threw it on the pyre of wood.

The false priests stepped back. Phorenice stood alone before the altar. She lifted her voice, sweet and carrying, yet the words she chose were a weapon. She did not speak the sacred, multi-layered tongue of the ancestors—a language rich with untranslatable terms for the subjective experience of the divine. Instead, she spoke in the common speech of the people, flattening the profound mystery of the cosmos into the utilitarian vocabulary of the dominant lexicon. She utilized the very language of their subjugation to tell of her wondrous birth and her right to rule. 

As she spoke, a small mist was born in the cloudless sky, rapidly growing darker and more compact, casting a shadow over the sacred circle. 

She lifted her white arms to the blackness, asserting her power through the stolen tongue. “O my high Father, the Sun, acknowledge me as Your very daughter! Here is sacrifice unlit, where mortal priests with their puny fires had weekly sent savory smoke towards the sky. Send down the heavenly fire to burn this beast, in token that You have given me Atlantis to be my kingdom!”

“O mighty Father, hear me!” she cried, her face contorted with will.

Out of the black cloud came a blinding flash. The thunderclap was an act of supreme acoustic violence, a deafening rupture that shattered the sacred sonic balance of the ancient circle. The cloven-hoofed horse gave one shrill neigh and fell back dead. Flames crackled instantly. But there was no rich, meticulously tended aroma of a sacred roast; there was only the sharp, acrid, violent stench of scorched hair and flash-burned fat—a tragic waste of sustenance, an insult to the culinary reverence of the past.

The cloud melted into nothingness. The smoke went up in a thin, pale spiral. Phorenice stood flushed and exultant before the snake and the outstretched hand, while the knot of priests and the great mob of people collapsed to the ground, surrendering their cultural memory as they chanted her name in their hollowed-out dialect. 

“Phorenice, Goddess of all Atlantis!”

But for myself, I did not kneel. Amidst the deafening roar of their apostasy, I stood silent, holding fast to the untranslatable truth of my ancestors, and awaited fate.

A murmur sprang up around me, swiftly coalescing into a jagged, industrial dissonance that tore at the acoustic ecology of the plaza. “Kneel,” one whispered, the old melodic dialect of his province flattened by fear into the dominant, sterile lexicon of the capital. “Kneel, sir, or you will be seen.” 

Another voice grated like a poorly oiled millstone: “Kneel, you without beard, and do obeisance to the only Goddess, or by the old Gods I will make myself her priest and butcher you!” 

The shouts arose into a discordant roar, a violent noise pollution that battered the senses. Yet, as the syllables of my name—“Deucalion”—began to ripple through the mob, the aggressive frequencies dampened. They might know little of the man who had left Atlantis twenty years before to cultivate the rich, complex agrarian terroirs of Yucatan, but they remembered the Viceroy who had ridden beside the Empress beneath her canopy of snakes. When they weighed my capacity for vengeance, their shouts limply evaporated, leaving only the hollow echo of a people who had forgotten the resonant, untranslatable chants of their ancestors.

When the silence had organically reseeded itself, Phorenice turned and saw me standing alone amongst the prostrate worshippers. I stepped out, navigating the space between two megaliths, and approached her by the altar. The air here was oppressive; the sacrificial smoke carried none of the complex, slow-roasted marrow or wild-foraged sage of the old rites. It was a sterile, purely utilitarian burn of commercial resin, a fast-burning fuel meant only to dazzle, devoid of true sustenance. I did not prostrate myself. I offered the precise, formal salutation of my station and hailed her as Empress.

“Deucalion, man of ice,” she retorted.

“I still adhere to the old Gods, and the old harvests.”

“I was not referring to that,” she said, her voice a superficial, commercialized trill as she offered a sidelong smile.

Ylga rushed toward us, her face as pale as unkneaded dough, her hands trembling. She pleaded for my life, but in her terror, the rich, native inflections of her youth had been entirely eroded, replaced by the subservient grammar of the court. “If he will not leave the old Gods yet,” she begged, “surely you will pardon him? He is a strong man, not easily seasoned to a new palate. Think, Phorenice, he is Deucalion. If you slay him here, there is no other man who would so worthily serve—”

The Empress snatched the words from her, weaponizing the dominant language. “You slut,” she snapped, her tone entirely devoid of harmonic warmth. “I keep you near to appoint my wardrobe and carry my fan, and you dare meddle in my recipes of state? Back outside this circle, or I’ll have you whipped. Shall I point a finger at you, and smite your pretty skin with a sudden leprosy?”

The girl bowed her shoulders, her cultural memory momentarily crushed by the sheer weight of imperial power dynamics, and slunk away. Phorenice turned back to me. 

“My lord,” she said, “I am like a young bird that has suddenly found its wings. I am curious to taste every current of the air.”

“May each new flight yield a nourishing harvest for Atlantis.”

“Oh,” she flashed, “I know what ingredients you hold most dear. We will return to the pyramid and let this simmer at leisure. Conduct me back to my riding beast.”

It seemed my refusal to consume her artificial deification was to be pardoned, a pragmatic concession to the recipe Zaemon had tasked me to complete. When she offered her fingers—warm, delicate, yet so ruthlessly efficient at wielding the utensils of war—I took them with the gravity of a meticulous historian handling a fragile, ancient text. I led her away from the circle she had polluted with her synthetic trickeries, confident the old Gods would choose Their own hour to cleanse the palate of this realm.

We walked toward the huge red mammoth waiting tamely in the square. The heavy, earthy musk of the beast—a complex olfactory blend of fermented sweet-grass, wet coarse hair, and the deep, loamy soil of the savage grounds—was a sudden, welcome anchor of organic reality against the cloying, commercial perfumes of the Empress. She mounted the stair to the golden castle on its back and bade me take the seat beside her. The fan-girl’s place was empty; our acoustic bubble was private.

As we rode, she pressed me for the details of the gate attack. I relayed the events, carefully laying stress on the deep-rooted resilience of Nais, suggesting the girl possessed a rare, authentic essence that might yet be cultivated back into allegiance. I withheld only the sacred, untranslatable phonemes of Zaemon’s commands; such profound linguistic heritage belonged only before the Ark of the Mysteries, not in the superficial air of the streets.

“You seem to have an unusual kindliness for this rebel Nais,” Phorenice noted.

“She possesses a sensory appreciation for the world, far more thoughtful than the common herd who consume life without tasting it.”

“Ay,” she answered, with a sigh that briefly touched genuine resonance. “An Empress loses much that meaner women feast upon as their daily bread.”

“In what particular?”

“She misses the honest wooing of her equals.”

“If you set up for a Goddess—” I began.

“Pah! I wish to be no Goddess to you. That is for the common people; it is a cheap spice that hides the rot and helps my schemes. Can you not be generous and admit a woman might discover the natural laws of fire as expertly as your musty elder priests?”

“Remains that you are Empress.”

“Nor Empress either. Look upon the woman seated beside you, Deucalion. What words rise first to your tongue? Do you wish to wait until all your manhood withers like an unwatered vine? Who but a fool watches a fruit grow heavy and sweet, and then leaves it on the branch until it ferments into ruin?”

I looked at her undeniable beauty, yet it left my palate entirely cold. To me, profound connection was a slow-simmered reduction, requiring the finest ingredients of shared worldview and the patient tending of an ancestral hearth. Phorenice viewed sustenance—and love—solely as utilitarian fuel, a quick transaction to satisfy a fleeting pang. But I remembered my mandate. 

“I may have been fastidious with my tastes,” I said, forcing the words through the rigid structure of her courtly lexicon, “but I do not regret letting the vintage age.”

“Nor I. I am a woman ripe, full-blooded, and the day has come to feast upon more than I have.”

I let my hand close over hers. “Take me to husband then, and I will be a solid hearth to you. But I speak to the woman now. I will be no puppet.”

She observed me sidelong. “I have been the master chef so long, it may be a delicacy to be mastered sometimes. But it would take a lusty lung to play the pipes if you were to dance against your will.”

“Then we shall rule this country, sweeping aside these bitter differences. The rebels shall return to their agrarian roots, to hunt and harvest the provinces. We will reestablish the deep, cyclic rhythms of prosperity.”

Petulantly, she withdrew her hand. “Oh, your conditions and your Atlantis! You carry a crudeness in your colonial manners, Deucalion, that palls once the first blunt flavor has faded. Am I to do all the wooing? Is there no fiery spice beneath all your ice?”

“In truth, I do not know the grammar of this love you speak of. I have conversed little with women through all my years of stewardship.”

“We were a pair then, though I have sampled sighs and protestations from every bearded man in Atlantis. Some tickled the palate for a day, but none nourished me. If you continue with this coldness, my Deucalion, you must teach me soon.”

“From what I have observed, such flighty emotion is a thin broth, fit only for those lacking deeper substance.”

“Then I would rather be some ill-dressed creature of the gutter, dragged away by a strong man for sheer passion! How I could revel in the raw taste of it!” She laughed, a sharp, unmelodic sound. “But if I do not change your inwards, there will be bitter herbs brewed for this Atlantis you set such store upon.”

She would have had me fondle her right there, amid the noise and spectacle of the public thoroughfare, treating intimacy as yet another performance to be consumed by the masses. I was physically repelled. I understood the power dynamics embedded in her dominant lexicon of romance, but to concoct a rapid, flavorless confection of courtly praise was a culinary trick I had always neglected. My soul required the slow fermentation of genuine connection; to palter with a woman who tasted nothing but her own ambition was beyond my linguistic and emotional translation.

“I had heard the colonists in Yucatan were far ahead of Egypt in the arts and graces,” she mocked, interpreting my silence as sheer rusticity. “I’ve had gentry from the Nile who remembered their manners. For ten pleasant words, one gallant cut off his own right hand. We made the bargain, and the dried meat of it rests on my shelf today as a memento.”

I was saved from further baiting by a sudden, violent disruption of our acoustic environment. The rebels had ceased hurling stones and begun firing carcasses of wood lined with throwing-fire. The mechanical, industrial shriek of their war engines shattered the air, a vile noise pollution that set my teeth on edge. 

When the fire splashed upon the rude wooden booths, the olfactory landscape was instantly desecrated. The rich, historic scents of roasted cumin, dried sea-kelp, and ancient cedar were obliterated by the acrid, sterile, chemical stench of the unquenchable flames.

This destruction of the market archives might have been a trivial siege detail, but our mammoth had not been properly seasoned to such sensory assaults. As the first blue whiff of chemical smoke curled into its sensitive trunk, the beast’s deep, harmonic rumblings gave way to frantic, discordant trumpetings. Its native dread of fire—a primal memory rooted in the untranslatable cycles of the savage grounds—overrode the superficial training of the Empress’s handlers.

“Some one shall lose a head for this!” Phorenice snarled, shaking her red curls. “I ordered this beast trained to stand indifferent to drums and fire!”

I slipped my girdle. “Here,” I said. “Let me lower you to the ground.”

“Are you afraid for my neck, Deucalion? I will ride it out.”

“There is little chance of that now.”

The spikes of the metal collar tore the mammoth’s flesh, the coppery scent of its blood mixing with the toxic smoke. With ear-splitting screams that completely destroyed the local acoustic ecology, the beast spun and charged blindly down the avenue, mowing through the pageantry. The golden castle pitched violently. I braced my feet, locked one arm around a pillar, and pulled Phorenice tightly against my chest to cushion her from the kinetic shocks.

She lay there contentedly, her synthetic perfumes clashing with the raw, panicked sweat of the mammoth. The great beast held furiously on, deafening us, entirely beyond the control of imperial commands. To leap would mean being trampled to pulp—reduced to mere organic matter beneath its feet. I recognized the poetry in this: the High Gods answering Phorenice’s superficial sacrilege with the overwhelming, untamable force of the natural world.

We thundered past the artisans’ quarters, leaving the heart of the city behind, until we reached the open expanse of the harbor. I expected the beast to swerve, but mad with sensory overload, it plunged straight into the water.

Here, the harbor was a tragedy of urban decay. The great estuarine flows, once vibrant with the complex salinity of the sea, had been choked by the untreated, soured refuse of a bloated empire. The mammoth plowed into this impalpable, stinking sludge, the runoff of a thousand ruined kitchens and neglected sewers. Bogged irretrievably, the beast churned the toxic compost, lurching the golden castle so violently that we were catapulted from our sanctuary into the foul, briny muck.

Floating in the cold, decaying broth of the harbor, I felt the grim reality of Atlantis's decline seep into my pores. Phorenice merely laughed as she swam.

“You handle yourself like a sore man, Deucalion! How did you like the ride, sir?”

“I’d liefer have walked.”

“Pish! Ho, the boat there! Hold your arrows!” she commanded the approaching ten-slave galley, her voice cutting through the water’s chop. “Tell them if they hurt a hair of my mammoth, I’ll kill them all by torture! We’ll haul him out later and tame him afresh. I must have my red mammoth.”

The port-captain’s galley, driven by the rhythmic, unnatural crack of well-plied whips, swerved to collect us. We were hauled over the side, dripping with the odorous sludge. The captain, a mincing figure, spoke in the flattened, obsequious dialect of the court, fawning over the Empress and lamenting the danger to her life and wardrobe.

Phorenice waved him off, asserting her ownership over the beast with the arrogant vocabulary of sheer property. Desperate to offer reparation, the captain offered two prisoners captured the previous night, suggesting they be sacrificed to appease the mammoth's rage.

“How taken?” Phorenice demanded.

“Trying to pass the harbor chain, Your Majesty. The man took a death-wound, but the woman fought like a cat. She claims she tired of the savage life outside the city. More so, she claims an intense craving for the nearness of a specific man within these walls.”

“Is this a romance we have swum to?” Phorenice mocked, stepping onto the royal quay as the galley moored. “Produce your woman prisoner, master captain. Let us see this lovesick girl. I am in a mood for tender romance.”

The captain vanished into the hutch, returning with the prisoner. As she was hauled into the daylight, the intricate, unspoken rhythms of the earth seemed to settle around her. It was the girl from the cave-tigers—Nais. Even battered, she held within her stance the untranslatable memory of old Atlantis, a stark, nourishing contrast to the hollow noise of the empire. Phorenice recognized her instantly, addressing her by name, mockingly probing the menu of her affections and the bitter harvest of the rebellion.

So this mighty Empress chose to be jealous of a mere woman prisoner. 

Now, my mind has been trained by the ancient disciplines of the Sacred Mountain to work with a soldierly quickness in these moments of stress, and I decided upon my proper course the instant the words left her lips. I was sacrificing myself for Atlantis by the order of the High Council of the Priests, and, if needful, Nais must be sacrificed also—although in the same flash, a scheme came to me for preserving her.

I bowed gravely before the Empress. “In this, and in all other things where a mere human hand is potent, I will carry out your wishes, Phorenice,” I said. She patted my arm, fresh waves of feeling welling from the depths of her wondrous eyes. Surely the Gods won for her half her schemes when They bestowed upon her that shape, that voice, and the subtle, dominant lexicon embedded in her very features. 

By this time, the merchants and dwellers adjacent to the royal quay had come down, offering changes of raiment and houses for our retirement. Our garments were soaked, reeking of the mammoth’s marshy passage and the brackish, sulfurous taint of the churned harbor. I followed Phorenice’s example gladly enough, though I refrained from giving Nais even a glance of farewell. A small sop like this was no meal for Phorenice’s appetite; she issued strict orders in her piercing, imperial High Atlantean to the port-captain for the guarding of his prisoner before we departed.

At the house into which I was ushered, I was provided a bath infused with faint, residual oils of bitter almond and sea-fennel. I eased my host of the plainest garment in his store, and he was pleased enough to escape so cheaply. But I had an hour to spend outside on the pavement, and as I waited, I closed my eyes to map the fractured acoustic ecology of the city. The distant din of the bombardment was a rhythmic, industrial brutalism, a series of concussive thuds that vibrated against the teeth, violently disrupting the harmonic resonance of the tide against the harbor walls. It was a sound that poisoned the psychological well-being of the populace. 

When Phorenice emerged, I felt a grim amusement at the merchant trailing her. The fellow was clearly ruined. He possessed a store of jewels—rare amethysts and fire-opals—bought in partnership with other merchants, trading consortiums whose complex, cooperative dialects were already fading from cultural memory. Phorenice had appropriated them all, draping them over her person.

“Are they not pretty?” she asked, her voice a sharp treble that cut through the merchant’s silent, unvoiced despair. “See how they flash under the sun. I am quite glad, Deucalion, that the mammoth gave us that furious ride, since it has brought me such a bonny present. Ah! They have brought us litters. Send one away; we shall share mine. We must play at being lovers today.”

I dismissed the guard, waving away the throng of gaping sightseers whose collective murmurs formed a dissonant, anxious hum. The bearers put their heads to the pole, their sandals slapping the wide pavement in a steady, circular rhythm—a comforting, ancient cadence that recalled the domestic migrations of our ancestors. 

As we swung along the rim of the dimpling waters, I thought sadly of the harbor’s decline. Time was when the air here was an olfactory tapestry of imported cumin, star anise, slow-cured meats, and the rich, fermented fish pastes that were the lifeblood of our agrarian ancestors. In those days, trade roared with lusty, organic life. Your merchant was a meticulous food historian in his own right, a portly elder who understood the profound meaning of sustenance and gave generously to the Gods. But now, the world was in arms. Your modern courtier viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel, consuming sterile, flavorless gruels while adorning his coat and vapouring at banquets. The old trade routes were dead, and the rich, untranslatable dialects of the spice-islanders were no longer heard on these quays. Instead, there was only the superficial commercialization of war, and a devastating famine of both taste and acoustic harmony.

Phorenice did her best to interest me between her bursts of trifling. We arrived where the westernmost harbor wall joined the land, and the panting bearers set us down. She led me into a stone house built on a promontory where the tide ran fast, unbarring the door to reveal her new gold collectors. 

In the ancient groves, we knew that gold resided dissolved within the ocean’s briny broth. But to extract it was a mythic occurrence, a matter of deep elemental communion. Here, this upstart Empress had perverted a natural rhythm into an industrial machine. 

“I tell all others that the Gods gave me this secret,” she laughed, patting my arm. “Look. The sea-water sluices in through that culvert, passes over these rough metal plates, and flows out. The yellow metal is caught in the ridges.”

I listened to the mechanized, grating roar of the water being forced through the culvert—a sound of sheer acoustic violence that entirely obliterated the natural, soothing susurration of the tide. The olfactory landscape here was stripped of its fertile, iodine-rich complexity, replaced by a cold, metallic tang. It was a utilitarian reduction of the sea’s vast, untranslatable mystery into sterile currency. 

“My fellows here melt it with fire into bars,” she continued. “They thought to cheat me once, but I towed two of them behind a galley till the cannibal fish ate them. Since then, they give me credit for divinity.”

I stared at the plates. The extraction was a desecration of the ancient domestic routines of the coast, but her mind was undeniably supple. Only one plan seemed humanly possible to restore Atlantis to its ancient, cyclical harmony: a dagger-stroke to end her further mischievousness. Yet, the thought revolted me. If it were fitting, the High Gods would have placed a definite command upon me. 

Our emergence from the stone house brought us into sudden peril. We had traveled out beyond the merchants’ quarters to the mud and grass huts of the fishing population. The life of these barbarous fishers is a harsh, multi-generational saga of survival. Their sustenance relies on trained sea-fowl. To each fisher is a large bird, fitted with a bronze ring around its neck to prevent it from swallowing the spoil. For each catch, the bird is rewarded with the fish’s head, inwards, and gills—a meticulous, raw butchery that provides a dense, iron-rich, and coppery meal for the fowl, while the pristine flesh is kept for the fisher. This visceral, ancient culinary technique binds man and bird in a shared cycle of survival. But the beasts of the sea frequently prey upon these birds, and a fisher bereft of his fowl is left to starve, cast out by his guild. 

Desperation and the erosion of their traditional livelihoods had reduced these people to ravening poverty. As we mounted the litter, two clumsy boats paddled toward us over the ripples, and savage-faced men poured from the narrow lanes. 

“With permission,” I said, “I will step out and scatter this rabble.”

“Oh, the people always cluster round me,” Phorenice said, her tone dripping with the condescension of a dominant lexicon. “But scatter them. I am taking a holiday, Deucalion, am I not?”

I stepped to the ground. The fishers were shouting, their voices a staccato of glottal stops and sharp fricatives—a native dialect deeply tied to the rhythms of the tides, yet entirely untranslatable to the aristocratic ears of the city, and now dying out beneath the crushing weight of imperial poverty. 

“Put your houses in order,” I commanded in the High Tongue, “and clean the rotting fish offal from these lanes. To-morrow I will inspect. But for to-day, the Empress desires privacy.”

“Then give us money!” a shrill voice cried from the huts, the syllables thick with the guttural desperation of their eroding language. 

“I will send you a torch and rig a gallows if you give me more annoyance,” I said grimly. 

They might have obeyed, shrinking back into the shadows where the scent of drying nets and fermented fish paste hung thick in the air, but the boisterous cries from the hide-covered boats called them back. The visual feast of Phorenice’s stolen jewels—flashing with superficial commercial wealth—was too great a temptation for starving bellies. 

Phorenice ordered the bearers to stand. She stepped out, strapping a sword to her hip and sliding a gold target onto her forearm. “An unfair trick, Deucalion,” she laughed, subverting the grim reality of the ambush into a parlor game. “Are these fools truly going to stand against us?”

The fishers closed in. The olfactory assault of their unwashed bodies, coated in the brine and oxidized blood of their trade, was overpowering. A grimy hand launched out to seize the jewels on Phorenice’s breast. With a clean, precise strike, I lopped it off at the elbow. 

“Your back to mine, comrade!” she cried. 

Then came the atonal, jarring clatter of bronze and steel—a violent pollution of the acoustic environment. We kept a tight circle, our movements a dark parody of an ancient harvest dance. It was mere slaughter. Phorenice played a fine sword, touching life with every thrust. I could smell the sudden, hot copper of opened veins mingling with the stagnant, marshy rot of the shore. 

Despite their clumsiness, the fishers had the ferocity of starved wolves denied their sustenance. I plucked a shield from a fallen man, forced to engage fully to keep us from being dragged down by their sheer numbers. And always, rising above the discordant, burly uproar, came Phorenice’s voice, claiming my name as her battle shout: “Deucalion!” It was a linguistic assertion of power, an attempt to weave my identity into her own violent narrative. 

At last, the attack slackened. We stood in the center of a ring of twisted dead. The surviving fishers shrank back. It was the moment for a truce, a time to allow the survivors to retreat to their domestic hearths. But Phorenice would not let the silence hold. She sang a hymn to her sword and mine, gloating over our invulnerability. She demanded payment for her slain litter-bearers, her High Atlantean words falling like hammer blows as she threatened them with burnings, skinnings, and sawings asunder. 

“It makes your backs creep, does it?” she mocked, forcing her dominant lexicon over their terrified, untranslatable silence. “Come now, you nasty-smelling people. If you make a good fight of it, I swear by my face I will be lenient with those who do not fall.”

Our Lord the Sun was riding towards the end of His daily cycle, and the smoke from a burning mountain fanned black and forbidding before His face, carrying the sharp, charred olfactory bite of roasted flint and ancient ash across the water. Phorenice wrung the sea-brine from her clothes and shivered. 

“Work hard with those paddles, Deucalion, and take me in through the water-gate,” she commanded. Her voice possessed the flattened vowels and clipped consonants of the new imperial dialect, a dominant lexicon that was rapidly eroding the musical, resonant tongue of old Atlantis. “That merchant would rue if he saw how his pretty garments were spoiled. What think you of my wisdom in coming where we have come without an escort?”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I replied, quoting the ancient, cyclical formula with a polite smile, though the profound, multi-generational reverence embedded in the original phrasing was fundamentally untranslatable to her ears. 

“At least I have shown you that I can fight,” she said, tossing back the short red hair from her eyes. “You were a difficult man to thaw, Deucalion, but you warm perceptibly. We shall be a pair of rustic sweethearts yet.”

She had orchestrated this skirmish with the fishers merely to display her prowess, treating the lives of two score hard-working, net-weaving agrarians as expendable fodder for a superficial spectacle. As I rowed, I focused on the acoustic ecology of the moment—the rhythmic, measured *thrum-swish* of the wooden blades biting the water. This ancestral sonic environment was a restorative counterpoint to the chaotic, commercialized noise of her modern empire. Still, I could not help comparing her to Nais. Nais, who understood the deep, untranslatable sanctity of life and would have spared the fishers out of a profound kinship with the working people of the earth.

I steered toward the jagged reefs guarding the harbor, silently reciting a prayer in the dying native dialect of the Priestly Clan. I waited for the High Gods to provide an omen, listening to the ambient murmur of the sea. The answer came not in words, but in a mythic resonance woven seamlessly into the physical world. A sub-bass rumble vibrated through the hull—an earth tremor that fundamentally altered the sonic landscape. A massive wave swelled beneath us, tasting of churned kelp and deep-ocean salt, lifting our boat and hurling it safely over the jagged stones without a single scraping dissonance. The Gods had spoken through the acoustic geometry of the water.

At the harbor forts, the sentries communicated by beating heavy drums. The sound was a brutal, industrial noise pollution that battered the delicate acoustic ecology of the evening air, a stark degradation from the resonant, breath-driven shell-horns of my youth. Once recognized, the great iron chain was splashed down to give us passage. 

Inside the fort, we were given dry garments. To ward off the harbor fevers, I prepared a quick medicinal draught from my pouch. It was a coarse powder of crushed fennel seed and dried bitterwort, steeped in water. As I swallowed, the intense, earthy bitterness bloomed on my palate—a complex, gustatory anchor tying my physical sensation directly to the loamy, terraced gardens of our agrarian ancestors. Phorenice, claiming divinity, nevertheless feared the damp, yet she showed no interest in the profound sustenance of the herbs, viewing them merely as utilitarian fuel to preserve her comfort.

Out in the harbor, her tamed mammoth stood shoulder-deep in the water. The lantern light reflected off the ripples of its trembling form. I felt only disdain for the creature's presence. Taking a majestic beast from its ancestral ecosystem and turning it into a captive, commercialized spectacle was a jarring violation of nature's rhythm. The unnatural splashing it made ruined the harbor's quietude, and I longed only for the steady, honest feeling of my own two feet upon the ancient earth.

When the port-captain arrived, speaking the abrasive, truncated jargon of the modern guard, he informed Phorenice that Nais had been locked in a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid. 

“It is to be hoped he understands his work,” the Empress said viciously. “You would feel pleasure if the girl escaped, Deucalion?”

“Assuredly,” I answered, preserving the integrity of my own subjective truth. “I have no enmity against Nais.”

“But I have,” she snapped. “And I am still minded to lock your faith to me by that wedding gift you know of.”

We were borne to the royal banqueting-hall. Here, the subtle power dynamics embedded in food and language were laid bare. Phorenice proclaimed me her future husband to the jeweled throng. Yet, perhaps sensing my deep reverence for traditional foodways and my distaste for the over-spiced, sterile decadence of her usual court, the banqueters had instinctively altered their habits. When I called them to receive portions from my platter, they ate simply and thankfully. 

I inhaled the olfactory landscape of the hall. The earth-breath flared with a clean, mineral scent, unpolluted by the heavy, cloying perfumes Phorenice usually demanded. The chefs, perhaps tapping into an older cultural memory, had prepared a feast of profound, elemental meaning: tubers roasted in their own earthen skins until the starches caramelized into deep, nutty sweetness, and salted marsh-fowl cured over smoldering peat. It was a gustatory resurrection of our agrarian past.

Phorenice leaned close, her red curls brushing my face, and whispered a complaint that revealed her utter lack of sensory appreciation. “Oh, sir, do not make your rule over us too ascetic. I have given no orders for this change, but to-night there are no perfumes in the air; the food is so plain and I have half a mind to burn the cook... Why, look at the beards of the men! They have swung round to this change by sheer courtier instinct.”

“Why hinder them if they feel more cleanly shaven?” I asked, relishing the tender, smoke-infused meat that spoke volumes of a forgotten culinary technique.

“No, sir,” she said, articulating her philosophy of dominance. “If you and I chose to say that our sovereignty was marked only by our superior strength, they would hate us. Whereas, if we keep apart to ourselves a few mere personal decorations, these become just objects to admire and pleasantly envy.”

Her worldview was an endless, superficial commercialization of power, a manipulation of symbols completely detached from the authentic, sustaining rhythms of life. The banquet ended abruptly. The dancers she had hired postured to jarring, discordant music—a hollow spectacle devoid of the acoustic sanctity of the old ritual dances. Phorenice threw them a jewel and silenced a swelling poet whose long-winded, sycophantic verses epitomized the death of true, meaningful language. 

“To-morrow,” she said wearily, her body proving its mortality despite her claims of godhood. “My Lord Deucalion has given me much food for thought this day.”

I led her down the pyramid’s passages. The heavy stone doors swung on their hidden pivots with a deep, resonant, earthen grind—a sound that anchored me to the generations of masons who had built them. At her bedchamber, she pressed close, her breath warm. She operated on the assumption that passion must be taken by brute force, the dominant grammar of her reign. But my internal world—my adherence to the cyclical, measured pace of ancestral vows—was entirely untranslatable to her.

“It rests largely with you,” I told her, my fingers brushing the beeswax texture of the green botanical sphere in my pouch, “as to whether Nais dies to-morrow, or whether she is thrown into a sleep from which she may waken on some later and more happy day.”

"Hear me, O my people," Phorenice proclaimed. "I take this man Deucalion to be my husband... May all our enemies perish as she is now perishing above whom we sit." She kissed me hotly on the mouth. Her lips tasted of imported spices and the sterile hunger for power.

"In token of our merging," she announced, invoking a twisted version of the old forms, "we will make the symbolic joining together."

She pulled down the neck of her robe. I pricked the rounded whiteness of her shoulder. Knowing who lay entombed just inches beneath us, I could have driven the blade through to her heart. Instead, I put my lips to her skin and took the drop of yielded blood.

It was a sharp, coppery shock to the palate. The blood was hot and metallic on my tongue, a stark, violent flavor utterly devoid of the nourishing depth of the communal broths and shared hearth-loaves of my youth. It tasted of tyranny and industrial iron. My tongue was dry, my throat parched.

The passage across the great, wounded continent of Atlantis was a cyclical retracing of ancestral migrations, a journey where the mythic past and the bleeding present walked side by side. I shouldered my carefully bundled meat and strode on, grateful to have preserved the harvest.

But as the day waned, the rhythm of my own pulse became a deafening, irregular drumbeat in my ears. The leg swelled, the skin drawing tight as parchment, while a violent sickness disrupted my body’s equilibrium. The sensory world blurred into a cacophony of pain. By nightfall, I had dragged myself into the crux of a great tree, suspended in a feverish, acoustic void where the roars of nocturnal predators seemed to echo from both the deep past and an unimaginable future. With the dawn, I limped onward. The moss on the trees, which should have served as a reliable, tactile compass, seemed to grow in dizzying, impossible circles. My final memory before collapsing in the damp undergrowth was of a figure stepping from the shadows, murmuring in a dialect I had not heard since the days of my youth.

When my waking senses returned, I was cocooned in a cleft between two rocks, the entrance fortified with heavy stakes. My wound was bound in a cooling poultice of crushed fern and pungent medicinal barks. Across the cavern squatted an elder woman, a custodian of the old ways. The air was rich with a deeply evocative, gustatory aroma. Over a slow fire of sweet-smelling cedar sticks, she was caramelizing a skillet of plump wood-grubs, glazing them meticulously with wild-sourced, amber honeycomb. The scent of roasting proteins and bubbling sugars was profoundly grounding, tethering my drifting consciousness back to the physical world.

“How came I here?” I asked.

“I brought you,” she replied. 

“And who are you?”

“They call me a nymph,” she said, her voice carrying the lilting, rhythmic cadences of a dying agrarian dialect. “I am a gatherer of roots, a reader of the earth’s seasons, and a healer. I trade in the old wisdom, demanding only what each can afford. It is a lean living now, when wars and pestilence have stripped the land of its farmers, leaving the soil to forget the touch of the plow.”

“Do you visit Atlantis?”

“Not I. Phorenice would have me boiled in brine. Our Empress tolerates no magic, no traditions, and no words but her own. She enforces a sterile, imperial lexicon, crushing the old dialects beneath the noise of her decrees. They say she is preparing to pull the Priests off their Sacred Mountain, to silence the old songs forever.”

Recognizing my deep appreciation for the complex, savory-sweet aroma of her cooking, she spoke plainly. “You are no vulgar wanderer, my lord. Your speech carries the deep, resonant tones of the old generations. Phorenice still reigns, most vilely, not as a maiden, but as a mother to twin sons. Tatho is her husband now.”

“Tatho! And who took his place as viceroy of Yucatan?”

“There is no Yucatan,” she said, stirring her earthen pot, the wooden spoon scraping in a slow, mournful rhythm. “A vast swarm of little hairy men came from the West. They carried only stone-tipped clubs, but they possessed a terrifying, ravenous hunger. They did not conquer; they consumed. They devoured the colonial outposts, stripped the granaries, and ate the very custodians of the land. They ate clean every city, extinguishing centuries of culinary and cultural memory in a single, blind feast. They even ate Tatho’s wife. If they ever cross the seas to Atlantis, they will consume whatever heritage Phorenice has left intact.”

I grieved silently for Yucatan, mourning the loss of its vibrant markets, its terraced farms, and the specific, localized dialects of its people, which I had spent years helping to cultivate. 

“What of Egypt?” I asked, guiding the conversation away from my true identity.

“In its accustomed darkness,” she sighed, offering me a wooden bowl of the glazed grubs. The dish was a masterpiece of survival cuisine, the earthy, nutty flavor of the grubs perfectly balanced by the bright, floral sweetness of the raw honey. I ate with profound gratitude, recognizing the profound meaning embedded in this simple sustenance. 

“It is a poor life,” she continued. “The people no longer appreciate the nuances of the harvest. They believe only in the harsh, transactional reality Phorenice has built. She leaves her slain unburied, generating a pestilence of rot. She has replaced the ancient pantheon with her own image, demanding worship in a flat, monotonous language that lacks all spiritual resonance. Those who will not kneel have their hamstrings slit.”

I harbored with this passionate elder for six days, my strength returning through her meticulous care and restorative broths. In payment, I hunted the plains and brought her a fat, cloven-hoofed horse, ensuring her larder was full, before I resumed my cyclical journey toward the capital.

The lands I traversed were a haunting testament to the erosion of cultural memory. Once, this had been a robust agricultural heartland. Sturdy log homes, built with generational foresight, now stood abandoned, their intricate dovetail joints rotting. The forest’s greedy fingers had reclaimed the vegetable patches, dragging cultivated heirlooms back into wild, tangled obscurity. When I passed walled villages, the air, once alive with the rhythmic sounds of milling grain and communal singing, was now choked with the anxious, discordant noise of survival. Phorenice’s blight had silenced the acoustic harmony of the continent. 

I avoided these settlements, repelled by the sterile, utilitarian desperation of their inhabitants. I had no desire to shed the blood of a people who had already lost their language and their connection to the earth. I moved like a ghost through the periphery, driven by the burning fever of my mandate, until the grim walls of Atlantis loomed before me. Beside it rose the Sacred Mountain, its eternal ring of fires casting a mythic glow against the encroaching darkness.

The sonic environment here was deeply corrupted. An army invested the Mountain, the air polluted with the clanking of bronze, the harsh shouts of officers, and the grinding, industrial machinery of siege. Force was useless against such a cacophony. I sought covert in the outskirts of a wood, integrating myself into the quiet rhythm of the trees to strategize. 

Across the plain, the royal pyramid caught my eye, its ancient, weathered white stone now gaudily plated in sheets of shining yellow metal—a superficial commercialization of sacred architecture. But my focus lay deeper. Beneath the square’s granite throne rested Nais. For eight years she had slept in a suspended timeline, her life held in a delicate, drug-induced stasis. She was my anchor in this sweeping saga, waiting for my return to complete the circle.

The following morning, the oppressive silence of my covert was broken by a man running heavily through the brush. 

“You need not threaten me, Deucalion,” he panted, his voice carrying the frantic, shallow pitch of a man disconnected from his roots. It was Ro, a lower priest I had always held in little esteem. “I should not have known you in those skins, but Zaemon guided me.”

“You serve Phorenice now?” I asked, noting the scent of cheap, mass-produced incense clinging to his robes—a far cry from the complex, aged resins of the old temple.

“I serve the one who feeds me,” Ro whined, embodying the transactional emptiness of the new era. “We swept out the old Gods. The people are obedient, the offerings are plentiful, and the living is easy. But Zaemon came to me. He speaks in that old, terrifying tongue, full of words that burn. Look at my right hand.” He held up a withered, paralyzed appendage. “Zaemon gave me this to ensure my attention. He despises my apostasy, ignoring the fact that a man must eat. He commanded me to find you.”

“Zaemon’s dialect carries the weight of the old magic,” I said, recognizing the subtle power dynamics embedded in Ro’s punishment. Zaemon had stripped Ro of his ability to craft, to touch, leaving him only as a hollow vessel for a message. “What is the word?”

“You are to make a great blaze of the plains’ grasses. Tomorrow night, approach the sheer flank of the Sacred Mountain, away from the city’s noise. They will lower a rope. And Deucalion, I beg you, ask Zaemon to restore my hand. I am at great expense buying nourishment for my wife.” His eyes darted over my ragged form. “A small coin? A link of bronze?”

I gave him nothing, repulsed by his view of sustenance as mere financial transaction. He scurried away, and I immediately set about my task. I sparked a blaze in the sweet-grass, the smoke rising thick and fragrant, a visual and olfactory beacon bridging the gap between the exiled king and the high priests. 

The next night, under the cover of darkness, I navigated the perimeter of the siege. At the sheer cliff face, away from the harsh acoustic pollution of the army, a slender rope descended like a serpent from the heavens. I secured it, and the slow, rhythmic haul began, pulling me upward through the interwoven timelines of my exile and my return. 

The priests who hauled me up were elders, panting with the exertion, their faces lined with the profound histories of their order. They led me into the acoustic sanctuary of the Mountain’s crest. Here, the eternal earth-fires burned, breathing a deep, resonant bass-note that soothed the mind and drowned out the superficial noise of Phorenice’s empire below.

Zaemon greeted me, his voice rich with the untranslatable majesty of our native tongue. “All hail to Deucalion, King of Atlantis, called by the High Council.”

“Is Phorenice dead?” I asked, feeling the weight of the generations pressing upon us.

“It remains for you to slay her,” Zaemon decreed. “We have borne her infamies as she commodified our religion and destroyed the cyclical trade of the earth. But she has committed the ultimate sacrilege. She has discovered the Secret of Life.”

The words struck me like a physical blow. “Only the Three know that.”

“She overstepped the boundary of the natural order,” Zaemon continued, his tone echoing the tragedy of a disrupted harvest. “She seeks to live eternally, to share this sterile stasis with Tatho and her favorites. She seeks to arrest the beautiful, necessary cycle of decay and rebirth. She defies the High Gods, claiming her imperial magic is superior. She would freeze the world in a single, unending moment, destroying the circular pacing of generations.”

I understood the magnitude of her crime. To deny death was to deny the soil its nourishment, to rob the future of its inheritance. 

“You are King,” Zaemon said. “You must restore the balance.”

“Then, as King,” I answered, letting the ancient dialect shape my authority, “I must first have my Queen. My roots must be whole before I can heal the land.”

“She shall be brought here.”

“Then let us view the defenses,” I said. I stepped out into the eternal, sun-bright glow of the earth-breath fires, ready to weave the fractured timelines of Atlantis back into a single, resonant harmony.

From the crest of the Sacred Mountain, the sprawling expanse of Atlantis lay below us, trapped in a discordant cycle of its own making. The city’s nightly hum was a testament to the auditory violence of Phorenice’s reign—a relentless, grinding cacophony of industrial bellows, commercial looms, and the hollow, tinny clatter of superficial entertainments. It was a sonic environment that fractured the psychological well-being of the populace, drowning out the gentle, acoustic ecology of the wind and the estuary. 

Beside me stood old Zaemon, his hands rough like unpolished mortar, smelling faintly of dried sage and the deep terroir of agrarian earth. He was a custodian of old cycles, an elder who understood that history, like the seasons, always circles back to the harvest. 

“You look upon your center of gravity, Deucalion,” Zaemon murmured. He spoke in the Old Tongue, a native dialect whose complex, cyclical syntax and rolling vowels were rapidly eroding under the sharp, utilitarian lexicon enforced by the Empress. To hear it spoken was to taste the memory of a richer world. “Deucalion, the man who landed from Yucatan, was strong. But the cold viceroy they have chosen to lead us possesses a rigid weakness.”

“Strength is a matter of nourishment,” I replied, my voice dipping into the same ancient cadence. “Give me Nais—living, breathing, and infused with the warmth of the world—and I am far stronger than any sterile soldier. A man cannot fight on the hollow fuel of duty alone; he must be sustained by the profound meaning of what he loves.”

“You shall have her,” Zaemon said, the lines of his face deepening like plowed furrows. “But the yield of this night requires the turning of the soil. We must break this city to rid it of Phorenice’s blight. Though, I fear, she has learned the recipes of the earth’s deep tremors. She may slip the pot before it boils over.”

I knew her methods. I had seen her juggle the raw, subterranean frequencies of the world, warping mythic occurrences into cheap commercial spectacles. “I am ready. Tell me the preparation.”

Zaemon stepped back, his eyes mapping the shaggy, unkempt reality of my disguise. “You are well-seasoned for the dark, my brother. You look the part of a wild forager, stripped of the polished, dominant aesthetics of the court. In the chaos of our earth-shaking, your savage crust will be your armor. Sleep now. I must enter the Ark of Mysteries to blend the catalysts that will bring this city to a rolling boil. It is a dangerous alchemy we stir tonight.”

I slept with the cyclical patience of a farmer awaiting the frost. When the dark was absolute, unlit by Our Lady the Moon, I was lowered into the lightless void by rope. The descent was a plunge into the stagnant olfactory landscape of the lower cliffs—a suffocating draft of damp rot, oxidized copper, and the bitter ash of the city’s endless furnaces. 

I crept past the uneasy campfires of Phorenice’s soldiers. They shifted nervously, their ears subconsciously picking up the dissonant shift in the earth’s acoustic hum. I found the hidden fissure and slipped into the ancient network of Priests’ tunnels. 

Here, the tragedy of our lost cultural memory was written in stone. The modern architects, driven by the superficial power dynamics of Phorenice’s dominant lexicon, had sealed off the old passages. They had replaced the resonant, harmonious acoustics of the ancient builders with blind, dead-end masonry that stifled the breath and confused the ear. I was forced to navigate by touch and the subtle, changing drafts of stale air, emerging finally near the harbor.

The earth beneath my bare feet already felt altered, springing lightly like a tense membrane. The subterranean frequency was shifting, a deep, mythic bass note integrating itself seamlessly into the mundane silence of the sleeping city.

*Gods,* I thought, the untranslatable grief of nine years surging in my chest. Would I be too late to unearth my love? Would her silent form be laid bare to the sterile, utilitarian gaze of a mob?

I ran. I tore through the winding streets, my lungs burning with the sharp scent of pulverized limestone and sulfur as the stonework began to grit and groan. The earth’s tremors intensified, shifting from a simmer to a rolling, violent boil. The domestic routines of the city shattered. People poured from their doorways, their panic stripping away the Empress's mandated trade-language. In their terror, they cried out in the fragmented syllables of their ancestors’ dying dialects, a tragic regression to the cultural memory they had allowed to erode.

I reached the great square before the royal pyramid. The air was a choking emulsion of dust that tasted of stale flour and ancient decay. The ground undulated in massive, circular waves, the pacing of the destruction mimicking the grinding of a cosmic millstone.

Through the darkness, illuminated by the violent, flashing deglaze of lightning, I saw the pyramid’s dovetailed masonry begin to split. Yet, amidst the deafening roar of collapsing architecture, Phorenice stood in the center of a billowing flagstone. She was a monument to artificiality, swaying with the earth’s rhythm with contemptuous amusement. Beside her, Ylga crouched, overcome by the visceral nausea of the quake. Phorenice represented the ultimate commercialization of power—immune to the acoustic and physical collapse of the world because she possessed no true roots within it. 

My focus, however, was anchored to the granite throne. I scrambled up its steps, fighting off panicked citizens who sought refuge on its heights. I struck them down, my actions fueled by a fierce, proprietary love. They wailed, naming me a devil, their voices a discordant clamor against the deep, rhythmic thrum of the earth.

I crouched by the throne. With each mythic pulse of the tremor, the carved capstone shifted, grinding laterally like a mortar over a pestle. The wait was an agony of untranslatable subjective time. I threw my shoulder against the granite, my muscles knotting, trying to force the harvest before its season. But the High Gods work in their own circular time. Only when the city was numb with auditory and physical exhaustion did the final shock tilt the massive capstone past its balance. It crashed to the plaza floor with a resonant, final toll.

I clambered onto the flat of the stone. The lightning had ceased, leaving a stifling darkness thick with the taste of pulverized bedrock. My hands, trembling, traced the metal clamps I had sealed nine years prior. I unhasped them, reaching into the hollow depression. 

My fingers found the cold, delicate curves of her body. An overwhelming, sensory exultation flooded me. I drew Nais up from the stone, pressing my lips to her dust-coated face. She was cold, utterly devoid of the olfactory bloom of life, but she was mine.

To linger was to risk discovery, to let the dominant forces of Phorenice’s regime reclaim her. The mob was fleeing toward the city limits, crying out that the buildings were accursed. I hoisted Nais over my shoulder. The physical boundaries of the city—its towering walls and imposing gates—had been plowed flat by the earthquake, returned to the raw, defenceless earth from which they were built. It was a violent but necessary clearing of the field.

I navigated the jagged, ruined topography, avoiding the looters who would soon scavenge the broken remnants of commercial excess. We reached the base of the Sacred Mountain, and through the strength of the rope, ascended back to the sanctuary of the elders.

In the warmth of a lamplit room, I finally saw the toll of her long slumber. The Nais I had entombed was vibrant, a woman of deep, sensory presence. The Nais upon the bed was a fragile husk, her weight halved. Her hair had grown into a dense, tangled cushion, and the nails of her waxy, translucent fingers spiraled like neglected vines. She was starved, stripped of the very essence of worldly consumption.

I set to work, not merely as a savior, but as a meticulous restorer of life’s profound sustenance. I heated a great copper bath, preparing a restorative medium that transcended mere utilitarian healing. From the elders’ stores, I drew the deepest essences of the earth—rich, gelatinous bone marrow, rendered fats, wild medicinal roots, and the concentrated, umami-laden reductions of mountain beasts. I blended these into the steaming water, balancing the salinity and the heat with exacting precision, creating a deeply evocative, gustatory-rich broth of life.

The room filled with the complex, layered olfactory landscape of the preparation—earthy, maternal, and fiercely restorative. It was the antithesis of the sterile ash below. Reverently, I lowered her chin-deep into the heavy, simmering stock. I sat beside her, constantly monitoring the temperature, trusting that the ancient, untranslatable language of true nourishment would seep through her translucent skin and coax her spirit back into the cyclical rhythm of the living world.

It was Nais herself who sent me to attend to my sterner duties. She waked not to the gentle, rhythmic percussion of the mortar and pestle that usually governed the morning acoustics of the Sacred Mountain, but to the discordant, metallic shrieks of an industrial assault. The sheer din of the attack penetrated the thick, sound-dampening stone of the house where I was tending her, a brutal dissonance that shattered the acoustic ecology of our sanctuary. 

She asked the meaning of the dreadful noise, and as pithily as might be—for she was in no condition to digest tedious listening—I fed her the history of her nine years’ sleep, measuring out the narrative in small, easily absorbed increments.

The colour flushed more to her face, a returning vitality that pleased me deeply. “My lord is the properest man in all the world to be King,” she whispered, her voice a delicate, ancient melody against the roaring background of the siege.

“I refused to touch the trade till they had given me the Queen I desired, safe and alive, here upon the Mountain,” I replied.

“How we poor women are made the chattels of you men!” she said. “But, for myself, I seem to like the traffic well enough. You should not have let me stand in the way of Atlantis’ good, Deucalion. Still, it is very sweet to know you were weak there for once, and that I was the cause of your weakness. What is that bath over yonder? Ah! I remember; my wits seem none of the clearest just now.”

“You have made the beginning. Your strength will return to you by quick degrees. But it will not bear hurrying. You must have a patience, like the slow fermentation of our oldest, richest wines.”

“Your ear, sir, for one moment, and then I will rest in peace. My poor looks, are they all gone? You seem to have no mirror here. I had visions that I should wake up wrinkled and old.”

“You are as you were, dear, that first night I saw you—the most beautiful woman in all the world.”

“I am pleased you like me,” she said, and took the cup of broth I offered her. It was a painstaking extraction of restorative marrow, steeped with wild mountain fenugreek and bruised coriander, carrying the deep, earthy umami of the very soils she had slept beneath. I had tended the earthen pot for hours, skimming the impurities, ensuring the olfactory landscape of the steam spoke only of nourishing agrarian cycles. “My hair seems to have grown; but it needs combing sadly. I had a fancy, dear, once, that you liked ruddy hair best, and not a plain brown.” 

She closed her eyes then, lying back amongst the cushions where I had placed her, and dropped off into healthy sleep, with the smiles still playing upon her lips. I put the coverlet over her, and kissed her lightly, holding back my beard lest it should sweep her cheek. And then I went out of the chamber.

That beard had grown vastly disagreeable to me. I found instruments in an adjoining room and shaved it down to the bare chin, stripping away the matted overgrowth like peeling the fibrous husk from a sweet heart of palm to expose it to the air. I changed from my squalid rags into a clean robe. If a man is in truth a king, he owes these things to the dignity of his office; he must respect the sensory presentation of himself.

But, if the horrific noise of the fighting was any guide, mine was a narrowing kingdom. Every hour it seemed to grow fiercer and more near. The Sacred Mountain’s natural amphitheater, once a resonant chamber for polyphonic chants and the subtle rustle of terraced agriculture, was now polluted by the deafening roar of Phorenice’s war machine. It was clear that some of the gates in the passage up the cleft in the cliff had yielded to the vehemence of her attack. With her genius spurred to fury by the blow that had wrecked so fair a part of her city, the Empress was an adversary who commanded a relentless, industrialized slaughter. 

Our stone defences were cunningly planned, but they needed men to line them. Always in a storm of this kind, some desperate fellows get to hand grips, or slingers slip in their shot, or newfangled machines like Phorenice’s fire-tubes make one in a thousand of their wavering darts find the life. Thus, we were not without our dead.

The slaughter was prodigious. As fast as the stormers came up, the Priests holding the lowest remaining gate rained down great rocks upon them till the narrow alley of the stair was paved with writhing bodies. The air was thick with the acrid, unpalatable stench of pulverized granite, oxidized copper-blood, and the sour sweat of doomed men. Phorenice stood on a spur of rock below, urging on the charges. With insane valour, company after company marched up to hurl themselves hopelessly against the defences. They had no machines to batter the massive gates, and their attack was as pathetically useless as a child hammering a wall with an orange. Meanwhile, the terrible stones mowed them down.

It was no part of Phorenice’s mood to ever count the cost. She treated these men not as individuals with rich local dialects and ancestral memories, but as standardized ingredients—cheap fuel dumped into a boiling pot until they filled the passes with the slain, allowing her to march on a level bridge of their bodies.

Two gates had she carried in this dreadful fashion, slaughtering the Priests behind them. Here I came down to take my share. There was no trumpet to announce my coming, but the Priests picked up “Deucalion!” as a battle-cry. The name rippled through the ranks, a resurgence of the old tongue, carrying the mythic weight of our cultural memory into the everyday rhythm of our defense. 

The word carried to the other side. A company of doomed wretches halted. There were shouts from behind to advance, but they did not budge, until a glittering officer panted up, striking right and left with his sword. From the high rampart, I recognized him.

“It matters not what they use for their battle-cry!” he was shouting, his voice carrying the flat, utilitarian lexicon of Phorenice’s imperial court. “You have the orders of your divine Empress. You should be proud to die for her wish, you cowards. If you do not obey, you will die afterwards under the tormentors. As for Deucalion, he is dead any time these nine years.”

“There it seems you lie, my Lord Tatho,” I shouted down to him.

He started, and looked up. “So you are there in real truth? Well, old comrade, I am sorry. You are on the side of these mangy Priests, and the Empress has made an edict that they are to be rooted out.”

“You used to be skilful of fence,” I called back. “If it please you to stop this pitiful killing, make yourself the champion of your side, and I will stand for mine.”

“It would be a grand fight, old friend,” Tatho replied, “but I cannot pleasure you. I am general here under Phorenice, and she has given me orders not to peril myself. Besides, Deucalion, you are not chief. You are not even one of the Three.”

“I am King.”

Tatho laughed. “Few but yourself would say so, my lord.”

“Few truly, but what there are, they are powerful. I was given the name yesterday. I was in Atlantis and saw how you took the mischief done there.”

“You were in Atlantis!”

“I went for Nais. She is on the mountain now, and to-morrow will be my Queen. Tatho, as a priest to a priest, let me solemnly bring to your memory the infinite power you bite against. If you persist in this attack, you can bring about a wreck over which even the High Gods will shudder.”

“You cannot scare us back by words,” said Tatho doggedly, thoroughly entrenched in the dominant power dynamics of the Empress's regime. “Phorenice has found by her own cleverness as many powers as were ever stored up in the Ark of the Mysteries.”

“Yet she looked on helplessly when her royal pyramid was trundled into a rubbish heap. Zaemon had prophesied it, and I stood closer to her than we two stand now.”

“I do not understand these matters myself,” Tatho admitted. “Phorenice keeps the knowledge of her magic to herself.”

“Having a liking for Nais, it seems I am debarred from the highest Mysteries,” I said. “So I content myself with being a soldier, and when the appointed day comes, I shall fall and kiss my mother the Earth for the last time. You, so I am told, have ambition for longer life.”

He nodded. “Phorenice has found the Great Secret, and I am to be the first to share it with her. We shall be as Gods, and the twin sons she has borne me will be made immortal also.” It was a tragic ambition—an eternity without the natural decay, seasonal fermentation, and generational passing that gives mortal life its true, profound flavor. A sterile preservation.

“Her immortality will be snatched from her very rudely,” I warned him. “Save yourself before it is too late. You shall have again your vice-royalty in Yucatan.”

“But, man, there is no Yucatan,” he burst at me hotly. “A great horde of little hairy creatures swept down upon our cities and ate them out. You know what a train of driver ants is in the forests? They will march into fires and put them out with their bodies, and the rest march on to devour all in their path. Those little hairy creatures were like the ants. To-day, there is no Yucatan.” It was a chilling metaphor for the mindless, consuming maw of an imperialism that devoured diverse agrarian cultures, leaving nothing but bare rock behind.

“You shall have Egypt, then.”

“I would not take seven Egypts! In your place, would you throw your Nais away to save yourself?”

“That is different.”

“In no degree. She is my wife and the mother of my children. This parley is leading to nothing, and I do not wish these common soldiers to carry evil reports about me to whatever Gods they chance to come before.”

He saluted and drew back. The deafening noise resumed, an acoustic violence raining upon us. Scaling ladders were hopelessly pressed forward into the narrow way, and our Priests used crow and lever to send massive rocks crashing down. The horrid butchery was renewed. Ever and again, a fire-tube’s dart found its way up, and we lost another man—a terrible loss of irreplaceable skill and memory, unlike Phorenice’s endlessly conscripted, anonymous masses.

I calculated the chances and saw that if this continued, the uppermost gate would be reached with no Priest left alive. Resolving to plot coldly from a place of safety, I left those at the gate with some rough words of cheer and withdrew up the narrow stair of the pass. 

I decided to destroy the pathway. It was an agonizing choice, for to sever this bridge was to physically enact the linguistic isolation of our Sacred Clan, erasing the stone vocabulary carved by our ancestors. I sent men to fetch our tools of unmaking: iron rams, fulcrum levers, and jars of concentrated acid that bit the air with the sharp, astringent tang of over-fermented citrus. 

Every pair of hands that could be spared was put to the work. Presently, carved balustrades, wayside statues, lettered wall-stones, and foot-worn cobbles roared down into the gulf. Gods! It was a hateful task, smashing down the splendid handiwork of the past, rendering our shared cultural memory into silent rubble in the abyss. But it was better than letting Phorenice profane it.

We drove our rams and levers under the living rock, applying heat to expand the metal. A narrow ledge remained undestroyed, and over this I crossed. Below, the three massive gates were already deserted by our garrisons, who I passed through unchallenged until I reached the busy rampart where twelve Priests still worked, stripped to the waist, heaving down rocks. 

I stopped an occasional fire-tube dart on my shield, tasting the metallic, smoky air of the fierce attack. Our stones had less distance to fall now, the pass below being choked with the dead. I gave the word for one more furious avalanche of rock, and whilst the reinforcements scrambled up over the bodies, I sent my remaining nine men running up the stairway. I followed, shutting the gates and breaking their levers. Not till we were through the last did the roar from below tell us the besiegers had found the gate deserted.

One by one we balanced across the narrow ledge. One wounded Priest grew giddy and toppled into the abyss before a hand could steady him. Once we were all over, heat was put to the rams, and they tore the remaining ledges from the rock with resistless force. A pang went through us all as we watched the last connecting link cut away, severing our mountain dialect from the deeply estranged nation below. The acoustic reality of the cleft instantly shifted; the bridge was gone, leaving only a hollow, untranslatable gulf between us.

With this break in the fighting, I set a guard and gave the others word to rest. I sought Zaemon first, but he was engaged inside the Ark of the Mysteries, enveloped in workings beyond my rank. Free to follow my likings, I returned to the house that sheltered Nais.

She waked as I crossed the threshold. I knelt beside her, resting my face on her pillow. The quiet acoustics of her chamber were a profound balm after the industrial noise outside. She rallied me on the cleanness of my chin and the symbols on my new robe, professing a quaint awe for my kingship. But of my marriage to Phorenice, she spoke with less lightness.

“It gives me a pang to think that I shall not be the first to lie in your arms, Deucalion,” she murmured, her thin hand drawing my face to her lips.

“Then you may gaily throw your pang away,” I whispered back. “I was husband to Phorenice in mere word. She married me by a form she prescribed herself, ignoring all the old rites and slow-cooked ceremonies of our ancestors. She has completely annulled that commercial transaction. Tatho is her husband now.”

We shared small repetitions of endearments, exchanging those subjective, untranslatable nuances of experience that bind two souls, a private sustenance too delicate for the deep index of the historian. 

But Phorenice had more rage against our Clan, and more bright, cold genius, than I had credited. Her troops easily swarmed up to where the pathway was broken. In the fierceness of their rush, the foremost were thrust over the brink into the abyss. Realizing the position could not be taken by a lavish spending of men, all were drawn off save for some sentries.

The Empress herself reconnoitred the place. With extraordinary nimbleness, she must have planned an engine to suit her purposes, for soon, the sounds of her dark industry drifted up to us. All the woodwork and metalwork was cut, tongued, forged, and fitted by skilled craftsmen in the plain below. Piece by piece, the ponderous balks and crosspieces were dragged up the steep pass, ready instantly for fitting into a completed machine. It was a mechanized threat, the clatter of mass-produced uniformity rising up to crush the last sanctuary of our ancient tongue.

The harsh, arrhythmic clatter of Phorenice’s siege engines tore through the once-perfect acoustic ecology of the temple gates, a jagged industrial noise that set my teeth on edge. Amidst this auditory violence, a tottering old Priest came up and touched me on the shoulder. He carried with him the faint, comforting olfactory memory of our dying world—the scent of crushed coriander, dried thyme, and ancient parchment.

“Well?” I said sharply, the metallic tang of adrenaline dulling my palate for interruptions. 

“News has been carried to the Three, my King, of what is threatened.” He spoke in the old, melodic agrarian dialect, a language of rolling vowels shaped by generations of harvest songs, now nearly swallowed by the dominant, clipped lexicon of Phorenice’s empire.

“Then they will know that I stand here now, brother, to enjoy the finest fight of my life. When it is finished I shall go to the Gods, and be there standing behind the stars to welcome them when presently they also arrive. They have my regrets that they are too old and too feeble to die and look upon a fine killing themselves.” 

“I have commands from them, my King, to lay upon you, which I fear you will like but slenderly. You are forbidden to find your death here in the fighting. They have a further use for you yet.”

I turned on the old man angrily, my blood simmering like a dark, heavy broth. “I shall take no such order, my brother. I am not going to believe it was ever given. You must have misunderstood. If I am a man, if I am a Priest, if I am a soldier, if I am a King, then it stands to my honour that no enemy should pass this gate whilst yet I live. And you may go back and throw that message at their teeth.”

The old man smiled enviously, the crinkles around his eyes reminiscent of sun-baked melon rinds. He, too, had been a keen soldier in his day, one who knew the visceral, rhythmic pulse of combat. “I told them you would not easily believe such a message, and asked them for a sign, and they bore with me, and gave me one. I was to give you this jewel, my King.”

I looked down. “How came they by that? It is a bracelet from the elbow of Nais.”

“They must have stripped her of it. I did not know it came from Nais. The word I was to bring you said that the owner of the jewel was inside the Ark of the Mysteries, and waited you there. The use which the Three have for you further concerns her also.”

Even when I heard that, my obedience was sorely tried. The promise of the battle was a heady spice, rich and intoxicating. But in the end, my love for Nais, and the memory of her skin—scented of bitter almond and sea-salt—prevailed. I gave the salutation that was needful in token that I heard the order and obeyed it. 

I turned to the knot of Priests left for the defence, breathing in the sour, metallic odor of their drawn bronze. “You will have what I shall miss, my brothers,” I said. “I envy you that fight. But, though I am King of Atlantis, still I am only one of the Seven, and so am the servant of the Three and must obey their order. They speak in words the will of the most High Gods. You will stand behind the stars before I come, and I ask of you that you will commend me to Those you meet there.”

They heard my words with smiles, courteously saluting me with their weapons, the resonant *clack* of bronze against wood ringing pure in the ash-choked air. We parted.

I did not see the fight, but I know it was good, from the time which passed before Phorenice’s hordes broke out onto the crest of the Mountain. With a sour feeling, I went up to the head of the pass. My path took me through the sacred groves, weaving back through the timeline of my life. For generations, these terraced slopes had yielded the delicate golden-fleshed *maru* fruits and fragrant shade-spices. The daily domestic routine of the cultivators—the rhythmic sweeping of their scythes, the low humming of their agrarian work-chants—had once been a soothing acoustic backdrop to the city. Now, the earth was shaking beneath my boots, and those ancient linguistic rhythms were being violently erased.

In the temple which marked the boundary, old Zaemon was waiting. With the profound solemnity of an elder who understands that the soul of a culture resides in its rituals and its sustenance, he sought dispensation from the High Gods for my trespass. I chafed at the delay, my temper hot, knowing Phorenice’s tower and bridge were already in position, filling the air with the cacophony of shrieking iron and dying men. The roof of the Sacred Mountain would soon be at the open mercy of the invader. 

But Zaemon was a meticulous historian of the spirit; he would not let the ancient ritual be truncated by mere panic. He led me up to the Ark of the Mysteries, chiding my impatience, and waited till I had given it my reverential kiss. Then he called aloud, and another old man came out of the opening in the top of the Ark, climbing painfully down by the battens. He was a man I had never seen before, hoary, frail, and emaciated—yet he carried the quiet, undeniable dignity of a master provisioner, a keeper of the earth's most profound secrets. 

“Look!” cried Zaemon, his shrill old voice piercing the industrial din of the siege. He swept a trembling finger over the shattered city. 

A chill began to crawl through me. Our Lord the Sun burned high overhead in a sky of cloudless blue. The distant mountains smoked more than ordinary, spouting fires, while the sea boiled like a chaotic, unskimmed cauldron. Far out on the distant coast, it surged against the rocks in enormous rolls of surf; and up the great estuary, it gushed in successive waves of enormous height which never returned. The harbour walls were drowned, and the salty, sulfurous flood was creeping up into the lower wards of the great city, swallowing the open-air markets, the spice stalls, the grain silos.

“You have seen?” asked Zaemon.

“I have seen.”

“You understand?”

“In part.”

“Then let me tell you all. This is the beginning, and the end will follow swiftly. The city of Atlantis, the great continent that is beyond, and all that are in them are doomed to unutterable destruction. To this end, the Ark of the Mysteries was built. Inside is stored on sheets of the ancient writing all that is known in the world of learning that is not shared by the common people.” He gestured to the massive vessel, a triumph of acoustic and physical engineering. “Also, there is grain in a store, and sweet water in tanks sufficient for two persons for the space of four years, together with seeds, weapons, and all such other matters as were deemed fit.”

In that moment, the Ark transformed in my eyes from a mythic monolith to a domestic larder—a sanctuary holding heirloom spelt, dried stone-fruits, sealed amphorae of mineral-rich mountain water, and the seeds of wild herbs. It was the edible, living history of our people.

“Out of all this vast country,” Zaemon continued, “it has been decreed that two shall not perish. A man and a woman, who are fit and proper persons to carry away with them the ancient learning to dispose of it as they see best. The woman is within the Ark already. For the man, the choice has fallen upon yourself.”

I was half numb. “I do not know that I care to be a survivor.”

“You are not asked for your wishes,” said the old man. 

Habit rode strong upon me. I made salutation in the required form, and said that I heard and would obey. 

“Then it remains to raise you to the sublime degree of the Three, and if your learning is so small that you will not understand the keys to many of the Powers, and the highest of the Mysteries, when they are handed to you, that fault cannot be remedied now.”

The time remaining was terrifyingly short. The ocean rolled in upon the land in an ever-increasing flood, a boiling stew of destruction sending up the reek of cooked mud and sulfur. Yet, there was no hurrying Zaemon and his fellow sage. Without temple, without sacrifice or incense to decorate it, they used the sky for a roof to make their echoes, and the Gods themselves for witnesses. They went through the work of raising me to their own degree with all the grand and majestic form which has gathered dignity from the ages, reciting the ancient names of the earth—words that tasted of ozone and iron, words embodying subjective experiences that could never be translated into Phorenice’s flat, utilitarian trade-tongue. 

A burning mountain burst with a bellowing roar as the incoming waters met its fires, but gravely they went on. Phorenice’s troops broke down the last resistance, pouring in a frenzied stream amongst the groves, yet the priests quavered never in their ritual. I stood there bareheaded in the heat, trying to concentrate, striving to memorize these semantic seeds of Atlantis before the language died forever.

Phorenice’s conquering legions swarmed onto the crest of the Mountain, running about like distracted men. The sea below rolled deeper, and the fires ringed half the mountain, burning with an unceasing, deafening roar. But for all the distraction, we might have been in the quietness of some ancient kitchen, preparing a meal that required absolute, meditative focus. 

At last, an end came to the ceremony. “Kneel,” cried Zaemon, “and make obeisance to your mother the Earth, and swear by the High Gods that you will never make improper use of the powers over Her which this day you have been granted.”

When I had done that, he bade me rise as a fully installed member of the Three, telling me to climb into the Ark and be ready to shut the door. I was expressly forbidden to accept challenge or provocation to fight.

I went to the top of the Ark, climbing by the battens, descended by the interior stair, and found Nais in a little chamber. The domestic interior was a sensory haven against the apocalyptic noise outside, fragrant with the smell of raw cedar, dried pulses, and cured meats. 

“I was bidden stay here by Zaemon,” she said. “Is there no chance to save him from what is going to happen?”

“He will not come into this Ark,” I told her. “He has a message to be delivered to the Gods, and he grudges every minute that is lost in carrying it to them.”

I left her then, and went out again up the stair, standing once more on the roof of the Ark. The plains and the level lands were foaming lakes. The great city of Atlantis had vanished eternally, swallowed by a roaring, briny deluge that smothered the lower grounds and the beasts within them. The acoustic horror of a continent drowning was absolute—a crushing wall of sound as mountains spewed steam and boiling stone into the whirlpools.

Presently, a compact little body of people headed directly across the open ground towards the Ark. In the middle of her guards, borne in a litter of gold and ivory by her grotesque slaves, rode the Empress. She was the architect of our cultural erosion, a patron of superficial commercial art who treated the world as a banquet of hollow calories. Her two children lay at her feet. On her right marched Tatho; on the other side walked Ylga, plying her fan with unruffled defence. 

“Do not go nearer to those unclean old men,” Phorenice ordered, her voice a sharp, dissonant blade cutting through the heavy air. “They carry a rank odour with them, and for the moment we are short of essences to sweeten the air of their neighbourhood.” She looked up at me. “Why, there is Deucalion, that once I took the flavour of and threw aside when he cloyed me.”

“I have Nais here,” I said, “and presently we two will be all that are left alive of this nation.”

“Nais is quite welcome to my leavings,” she laughed, a sound devoid of true sustenance. “You are a very rustic person, Deucalion. Did you swell much with dignity? Come, sir, find your tongue.”

“Have your gibe. I do not gibe back at a woman who presently will die.”

“Bah! Deucalion, you will live behind the times. My arts can make life run on eternally.”

“Then the waters will presently test them hard,” I said. 

Before more could be spoken, Zaemon stepped forward. He held the Symbol of our Lord the Sun glowing in his hand, and burst into a flow of cursing. He spoke in the deep, resonant mother-tongue, his voice a pure acoustic force that overpowered the industrial screech of her guards and the roar of the swamped burning mountains. Phorenice tried to rise, to command her guards to kill him, but the power of that ancient, untranslatable lexicon mastered all her superficial arts. She lay back sullenly amongst her cushions, a beaten woman. 

Only Ylga broke from the knot, leaning up to the side of the Ark to cry a farewell through the gathering roar of the flood. 

“I would I might save you and take you with us,” I called down to her.

“As for that,” she said, “I would not come if you asked me. I am not a woman that will take anything less than all. But I shall meet what comes presently with the memory that you will have me always somewhere in your recollection. I know somewhat of men... and you will never forget that you came very near to loving me once.”

The bellowing rush of the waters drowned her further words. A great mist of steam, lit like gold by the volcanic fires, stopped all accurate view, save for a last sight of soldiery swept away by a scum of surge billowing at their heels. My eye fell on old Zaemon waving to me with the Symbol to shut down the door in the roof of the Ark. 

I obeyed his last command. I went down the stair and closed all ingress behind me, sliding the heavy bronze bolts into their sockets. Suddenly, we were plunged into the dark. The chaotic, deafening noise of the drowning continent was muted, replaced instantly by a new acoustic reality—the deep, womb-like resonance of the Ark’s interior. 

I went to the place where Nais lay among the provisions, breathing in the scent of the seeds and the dried herbs that would be the foundation of a new world, and put my arms tightly around her. Without, we heard men beating desperately on the Ark with their weapons, wrenching at the fastenings. The end was coming to them now.

Then there was a little surge that lifted the Ark a hand’s breadth in its cradle, setting it back with a jar. The blows from outside redoubled into frenzied batterings on the rounded roof. Some screams came to us dully through the ponderous sheathing. And then, another surge came, roaring and thundering, which picked up the great vessel as though it had been a feather, and spun it giddily. We touched earth or rock no more. 

We tossed about on the crest and troughs of delirious seas. Crouched there in the darkness, enveloped in the rich, earthy smells of the stored harvest, we were dully weighed with the burden of knowledge: we alone were saved out of what was yesterday a mighty nation, left to carry the profound, untranslatable memory of its vanished language and its lost, fertile shores.

The Ark was rudderless, oarless, and machineless, left to drift wherever the High Gods dictated, though its lack of mechanical propulsion was to my mind a profound relief. Inside, however, the acoustic environment was a traumatic assault. Bereft of the lamp’s comforting glow, we lay in the pitch black, swallowed by the heavy, fermented aroma of ancient, sealed wood and the sour dampness of the bilge. The noise was absolute. The grinding groan of the timbers and the deafening, chaotic roar of the ocean outside mirrored the unnatural, shrieking industrial noise pollution that had plagued Phorenice’s commercialized capital. It was a cacophony that battered our psychological well-being as fiercely as the waves battered the hull. I clung to the side of Nais, holding her to the bed in the darkness. We did not speak; the sheer untranslatability of our grief and fear rendered all words inadequate, leaving us to find companionship solely in the shared, silent endurance of the sonic terror.

When Atlantis finally sank to form the new ocean bed, her drowned fires sent up great whirlpools and spouting geysers, a fleeting, violent legacy. Following this came a vast hurricane, as if the Gods of the Sea meant to leave no note in this symphony of desolation unplayed. For seven nights and seven days, tracked only by the rhythmic, dripping cadence of the hour-reckoner swinging in the dark, the auditory assault continued. Then, the howling departed. 

The sonic landscape shifted instantly to a profound, resonant acoustic ecology—the gentle, rhythmic lapping of a long, still swell. The motion grew regular and oily. Recognizing this healing frequency in the water, I finally dared to climb the stair and throw open the door set into the Ark’s roof. 

Sweet, brine-laced air gushed down into the belly of the vessel, purging the stagnant olfactory landscape of our confinement. The Ark rode dryly over the swells. I descended to bring Nais up, drawing her into the curing rays of our Lord the Sun. We offered our adorations, weaving the mythic scale of our salvation seamlessly into the immediate, domestic necessity of our survival. Afterward, we lay upon the deck to seek the rest our bodies so urgently craved. 

Sleep evaded me for a long time. I stared wearily out over the oily, sunlit waters. No blur of land broke the endless ring of the horizon. The acoustic silence was matched only by the visual ruin; the bosom of the deep was littered with the flotsam of our drowned culture. Shattered timber, a smashed boat of hides, and the bloated corpses of beasts bobbed on the swells, a silent testament to the death of our world. Yet, the steady rocking eventually lulled me into unconsciousness, my hand tightly clasping that of Nais. 

When I woke, I found her open-eyed and watching me with a tender, grounding presence. The rest had fortified us, bringing a quiet complacency that allowed us to accept the station the High Gods had chosen for us. Our thoughts turned at once to sustenance, the foundational rhythm of all human continuity. We went below into the belly of the Ark to eat, drink, and maintain our strength. 

Now, with the hatch open, we could observe the Ark intimately. It was a masterpiece of ancient agrarian preservation. Though it swam upon the waters for the first time in its centuries of existence, there was no leak or suncrack. The floor was bone dry. It was built from an organic architecture lost to our modern age; living timbers had been grown together without a single suture or joint, creating an acoustic harmony and structural integrity as hard as metal, yet deeply tied to the earth. 

Within this gloomy, cavernous belly lay the ultimate larder. On either side of the central alley stood prodigious tanks storing the agricultural memory of Atlantis. I plunged my hands into the dry depths, bringing up handfuls of pristine grain—ancient strains of einkorn and robust barley that released a dense, malted scent of dormant life and sun-baked dust. At the far end were tanks of sweet water, infused with the complex, oaky tannins of the seasoned wood. In other compartments lay meticulously categorized drugs, botanical samples, and the distilled essences of beasts. 

We prepared our first meal, soaking the grains until they yielded a nutty, starchy sweetness on the tongue. In the slow, deliberate chewing of this food, we acted as the final food historians of our people, finding profound, stabilizing meaning in the utilitarian act of eating. Surrounding us, carved into the partitions and woodwork in the rude art of bygone times, were representations of all the beasts that had lived in Atlantis. I looked upon them with deep reverence; they were a visual menu and an ecological catalog of a vanished world, perpetuating the memory of creatures that had died alongside the artisans who carved them. 

For many weeks, our life fell into a circular, domestic rhythm as the Ark roamed the face of the waters. We possessed no governance over our direction and could not increase our speed by a hair’s breadth. We cheerfully resigned our fate to the High Gods. Of the land we eventually reached, where we established our hearth and where our children were born, I shall tell in due course. But to maintain the interwoven timelines of this exact chronicle, I must first record how I came to possess the very sheets upon which I write.

In a great coffer in the center of the Ark’s floor lay the whole of the Mysteries, meticulously inscribed in the dominant script of our fallen empire. As the days passed, I read through some of these texts. The awfulness of the Powers over which they granted control appalled me. I had seen the catastrophic results of these Powers set loose upon Atlantis. Here was the great Secret of Life and Death that Phorenice had twisted for her own ends—a lexicon of extraction, dominance, and unnatural force. 

The longer I studied them, the more I recognized the subtle power dynamics embedded within this imperial vocabulary. It was a sterile language that had systematically eroded the true cultural memory of our people, suffocating our native dialects and dismissing the sensory, agrarian realities of our ancestors. The subjective experience of a life well-lived—the scent of rain on dry soil, the shared warmth of a communal meal—was entirely untranslatable into these cold, tyrannical runes. 

The thought of remaining the custodian of this linguistic poison became an intolerable burden. At last, I went to the fire-box and heated the iron tools until they glowed. I brought the hot irons to the sheets, pressing the searing metal against the tablets. The enclosed space filled with the rich, caramelized scent of melting beeswax and the bitter smoke of scorching pigments. I obliterated every letter, erasing the dominant lexicon of the old world until nothing remained but a smooth, blank surface. 

If I did wrong, the High Gods in Their infinite justice will punish me. If it is well that these sterile secrets endure, They will dictate them afresh. But I destroyed them there as the Ark swayed over the swells. Later, when we finally came to land and established our new agrarian life, I took up my stylo to rewrite upon these very sheets a new chronicle—one of sustenance, survival, and the true death-throes of great Atlantis, recorded in my own words.
