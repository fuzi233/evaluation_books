Throughout the preceding pages, I have assumed—though perhaps this should have been established from the outset as a foundational principle—that every inhabitant of Flatland is a Regular Figure. By "Regular," I mean that each being is constructed with perfect symmetry: a Woman must be not merely a line, but a straight one; an Artisan or Soldier must possess two equal sides; a Tradesman, three; a Lawyer (to which humble rank I belong), four; and so on, with all Polygons having their sides uniformly matched in length. The size of these sides naturally varies with age—a newborn Female might measure an inch, while a mature Woman could stretch to a foot. Among males, the combined length of an adult’s sides typically amounts to about two feet. But it is not size that matters here—it is equality.

This uniformity of form is not mere convention; it is the very bedrock upon which our society stands. If sides were unequal, then angles would differ too. Instead of needing only a single glance or touch to identify a person's shape, we would be forced to examine every angle individually. Such a system would render social interaction impractical, if not impossible. The entire science of sight recognition—the art of identifying others at a glance—would collapse. Feeling, once a refined skill, would become a tedious chore. Communication would falter, trust would vanish, and daily life would descend into chaos. Civilization itself would crumble back into primal disorder.

Is this exaggeration? Consider a simple scene: you encounter several Tradesmen in the street, instantly recognizing them by their familiar angles and gently fading edges. You invite them to lunch in your home—an act performed with complete confidence, for everyone knows roughly how much space a grown Triangle occupies. Now imagine one of them dragging behind his noble vertex a distorted, lopsided appendage stretching twelve or thirteen inches across. What then? Could such a figure even pass through your doorway? Would your house become a trap?

I do not belabor this point lightly. It is clear to any rational mind that without regularity, existence in Flatland becomes untenable. Even now, navigating a crowd demands precision and care; if no one could predict the dimensions of another, collisions would be constant, panic inevitable, and harm widespread—especially if Women or Soldiers were involved, whose sharp angles could inflict real injury.

Thus, both nature and practical necessity favor regularity. Our laws reflect this truth. In Flatland, “Irregularity of Figure” is not simply a physical flaw—it carries moral weight, akin to criminality or moral decay in your world. Those who deviate are treated accordingly.

Some reformers argue that geometric irregularity does not imply moral corruption. They claim that Irregulars are victims of prejudice: scorned by family, ignored by servants, shunned by society, denied opportunity, and confined to menial roles under strict surveillance. Once they reach adulthood, they face inspection—if found beyond acceptable deviation, they are destroyed; otherwise, they are relegated to low-level government work, barred from marriage, forced into drudgery, and monitored even during rest. “No wonder,” say these voices, “such lives breed bitterness and vice.”

Yet such arguments fail to persuade me—or indeed, the wisest leaders among us. Our ancestors wisely decreed that tolerance of Irregularity endangers the state. Yes, the life of an Irregular is harsh—but the stability of the many demands sacrifice from the few. Suppose a creature existed with a triangular front and a polygonal rear. How would we build homes, churches, or public spaces? Should ticket clerks measure every citizen’s perimeter before allowing entry? Could Irregulars serve in the military? And if they did, how could we prevent them from disrupting ranks or causing disaster?

Moreover, what temptations would such beings face? Imagine a man entering a shop with his polygonal side forward, appearing respectable, then ordering goods freely—exploiting trust for personal gain. These are not hypothetical dangers; they are real risks.

Let those who champion misplaced compassion speak all they wish. I have never met an Irregular who was not also, in spirit, a hypocrite, a misanthrope, and a source of mischief within limits. Not that I support the harshest measures—some states destroy infants whose angles deviate by half a degree. That is extreme. We know that even great minds—men of genius—have shown early deviations as large as forty-five minutes. To eliminate them would be a tragedy for the nation.

Medicine, too, has made remarkable strides. Through careful compression, extension, trepanning, dietary control, and other techniques, many cases of Irregularity have been corrected—sometimes fully, sometimes partially. Therefore, I advocate a middle path. Let us not draw rigid lines. Instead, when a child’s frame begins to set, and medical experts conclude recovery is unlikely, let us mercifully and painlessly end their suffering. This is neither cruelty nor fanaticism—it is wisdom. For in preserving order, we preserve civilization.
