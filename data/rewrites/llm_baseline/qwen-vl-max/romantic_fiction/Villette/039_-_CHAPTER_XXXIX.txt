I stood transfixed, as if under the gaze of a serpent with three heads, unable to move from the circle of figures gathered beneath the trees. The ground seemed rooted beneath my feet. Above, the canopy of intertwined branches cast a deep shadow; the night murmured secrets of safety, and a single lamp flared briefly—just enough to reveal a hidden seat in the darkness—then vanished as if it had never been. Now, let me tell you what I have silently learned over these past two weeks of silence, through whispers and glances, about the reason for M. Emanuel’s sudden departure. The story is short, and not new: its beginning is money, its end—ambition.

Madame Walravens was as grotesque as a Hindu idol, yet to those who worshipped her, she carried the weight of one. She had once been immensely wealthy—very wealthy—and though now she lived without means, there was talk that she might soon regain her fortune. In Basseterre, Guadeloupe, she owned a vast estate, inherited at marriage sixty years before, long held in limbo after her husband’s financial ruin—but now, it was said, free of claims, and with proper management, capable of yielding great returns within a few years.

Père Silas cared for this potential revival not only for the sake of wealth, but for religion—for Magliore Walravens had been a devout daughter of the Church. Madame Beck, distantly related to the hunchback and aware that she had no family of her own, had long planned for every contingency, calculating like a mother would for her child. Though harshly treated by Madame Walravens, she never stopped seeking favor—not out of affection, but for gain. Thus, both the priest and the headmistress were united in their interest in securing the West Indian estate—each driven by self-interest, each sincere in their pursuit.

But distance was vast, and the climate dangerous. A man of integrity, devoted and trustworthy, was needed. Such a man existed—one whom Madame Walravens had kept in her service for twenty years, crushing his spirit and living off him like a parasite. This same man had been shaped by Père Silas—trained, bound by gratitude, habit, and faith. And Madame Beck knew him well, could influence him subtly. “My pupil,” said the priest, “if he remains in Europe, risks apostasy—he has become entangled with a heretic.” Madame Beck, too, had her private motive: what she could not possess, she did not wish others to claim. Better to destroy it than see it fall into another’s hands. As for Madame Walravens, she wanted her land and her money, and she knew Paul could be the most loyal steward—if he chose to be. So the three, each selfish in their own way, joined forces to press upon the one unselfish soul. They reasoned, pleaded, implored. They laid their hopes at his feet, placing their futures in his hands. They asked only a few years of sacrifice—after which, he could live for himself. One among them, perhaps, hoped he would not live at all.

No one ever offered M. Emanuel such a burden without him accepting it. He never refused a trust, nor turned away a duty. What sorrow he felt, what fears he carried—what plans he made for his own future—none knew, none questioned. His conversations with his confessor, I could guess at; the role of duty and piety in persuading him, I could imagine. But he left, quietly, without a word. That was all I knew.

Bent forward, my forehead resting on my hands, I sat among tree trunks and tangled brushwood. The conversation around me drifted faintly—I could hear it if I wished, but for a while, there was little reason to listen. They spoke of dresses, music, lights, the lovely night. I strained to hear someone say, “It’s calm weather for _his_ journey—the _Antigua_ will sail safely.” But no such words came. The ship, her course, her passenger—none were named.

Perhaps the idle chatter meant nothing to old Madame Walravens any more than it did to me. She seemed restless, turning her head from side to side, scanning the crowd, the trees—as if expecting someone, growing impatient with delay. “Où sont-ils? Pourquoi ne viennent-ils?” I heard her mutter more than once. At last, as if determined to demand an answer—since none had offered—she spoke aloud: “Messieurs et mesdames,” she said, “où donc est Justine Marie?”

“Justine Marie!” What did this mean? Justine Marie—the dead nun—where was she? Why, in her grave, Madame Walravens! You may go to her, but she shall not come to you.

That was what I would have said, had I dared. But no one else seemed surprised. No one blinked. The quietest reply met the strangest question—the kind that stirs spirits from tombs. “Justine Marie,” said one, “is coming. She is in the kiosk. She will be here soon.”

From that moment, the conversation changed—but remained light, casual, familiar. Hints, jokes, remarks passed between them, so fragmented, so dependent on unnamed people and unspoken events, that even when I listened intently—now, with a sense of fate—I could only grasp that some plan was in motion, involving this spectral Justine Marie—dead or alive. This gathering seemed to reach for her, clutching at her for some purpose. There was talk of marriage, of fortune—though I could not tell for whom. Perhaps Victor Kint. Perhaps Josef Emanuel. Both were unmarried. Once, I thought the teasing was aimed at a fair-haired young foreigner named Heinrich Mühler. Amidst the laughter, Madame Walravens still broke in with hoarse, irritable remarks—her impatience eased only by watching Désirée closely, threatening her with her cane whenever she moved.

“La voilà!” suddenly called one of the men. “Voilà Justine Marie qui arrive!”

That moment was mine. I recalled the painted nun on the panel—the sad tale of love, the vision in the garret, the ghost in the alley, the mysterious birth in the cradle. I felt a premonition, a certainty of revelation. Ah! When imagination runs wild, where can we stop? What bare winter tree, what humble hedge animal, cannot be transformed by fancy—a passing cloud, a flicker of moonlight—into something unearthly?

The weight of mystery pressed upon my heart. Until now, I had seen this specter only through a glass darkly. Now, I would see it face to face. I leaned forward. I looked.

“She comes!” exclaimed Josef Emanuel.

The circle parted, as if welcoming a new guest. At that instant, a torch passed nearby—its flame mingling with the pale moonlight, illuminating the scene perfectly. Surely those near me must have felt something of the tension I felt—though none showed it. Even the calmest must have held his breath. As for me—my life stood still.

It is over. The moment has passed. The nun has arrived. The revelation is complete.

The torch still blazes within a yard, held high by a park keeper. Its flame almost touches the figure of the Expected—there, standing full before my eyes. Who is she? What does she wear? How does she look?

There are many masks tonight in the park. As the hour grows late, a strange mood of revelry and mystery spreads—so much so that I scarcely blame you, reader, if you think she looks like the nun from the attic, dressed in black skirt and white veil, rising from the grave, a true ghost returned.

But no—those are lies. Let us be honest. Let us cut straight from the fabric of truth.

Though “homely” is a poor word to use. What I see is not plain. A girl from Villette stands there—fresh from her boarding school. She is very beautiful, with the natural grace of this country. She is well-fed, fair, plump. Her cheeks are round, her eyes bright, her hair thick. She is elegantly dressed. She is not alone—her escort consists of three people—two older ones, whom she calls “Mon Oncle” and “Ma Tante.” She laughs, she talks—cheerful, healthy, blooming—every inch the bourgeois beauty.

“So much for Justine Marie,” then—so much for ghosts and mystery. Not that the mystery is solved. This girl is certainly not my nun. The figure I saw in the garret and garden was taller by a span.

We’ve seen the city belle. We’ve glanced at the respectable uncle and aunt. Can we spare a glance for the third member of this group? Should we notice him? We ought to, reader—he has claims on us. We do not meet him for the first time.

I clenched my hands tightly. I drew in my breath deeply. I stifled the cry, swallowed the gasp, suppressed the start. I did not speak. I did not move. But I knew. Through the dimness left by nights of weeping, I recognized him. They said he was sailing on the _Antigua_. Madame Beck said so. She lied—or she once told the truth and failed to correct it when it became false. The _Antigua_ had sailed. And there stood Paul Emanuel.

Was I glad? A great weight lifted from me. Was it cause for joy? I cannot say. Ask first—what were the circumstances? How did this delay affect me? Were there not others who might care more?

Still—who is this girl, this Justine Marie? Not a stranger, reader. I know her by sight. She visits the Rue Fossette. She attends Madame Beck’s Sunday gatherings. She is related to both the Becks and the Walravens. Her name honors the sainted nun who would have been her aunt had she lived. Her surname is Sauveur. She is an heiress, an orphan, and M. Emanuel is her guardian—some say her godfather.

The family council wants this heiress married to one of their own. Which one? That is the vital question.

I felt glad now that the draught given me had filled me with a strength that made sleep and rest unbearable. I have always loved to uncover truth—to seek the goddess in her temple, to lift the veil, to dare the terrible gaze. O Titaness among gods! Your form, hidden behind shadows, often sickens us with uncertainty. But show us one line, one feature—clear, stark, undeniable—and though we gasp in terror, we drink in your divinity. Our hearts tremble, our blood surges like rivers shaken by earthquake—but we have gained strength. To see and know the worst is to rob Fear of her greatest power.

The Walravens’ party, now larger, grew lively. The men brought refreshments from the kiosk. All sat on the grass beneath the trees. They toasted, laughed, joked. M. Emanuel was teased—half kindly, half cruelly, especially by Madame Beck. Soon I understood: his voyage had been delayed—not by force, but by choice, against the advice of his friends. He had let the _Antigua_ sail. He had booked passage on the _Paul et Virginie_, set to depart two weeks later. They mocked him for it, demanding why he had chosen this. He gave only a vague answer: “a small matter of business I am determined to settle.” What was this business? No one knew. But one person seemed partly privy—there was a glance exchanged between him and Justine Marie. “La petite va m’aider—n’est-ce pas?” he said.

The reply came swiftly—God knows how fast!

“Mais oui, je vous aiderai de tout mon cœur. Vous ferez de moi tout ce que vous voudrez, mon parrain.”

And the dear “parrain” took her hand and kissed it reverently. At this, the fair-skinned young German, Heinrich Mühler, stirred uneasily—as if displeased. He muttered a few words. M. Emanuel actually laughed in his face—laughed with the cold triumph of a victor—and pulled his ward closer.

M. Emanuel was joyful that night. He seemed untouched by the change ahead. He was the life of the party—perhaps a little domineering, eager to lead in joy as in work—but at every turn, he proved his right to command. His wit sparkled, his stories delighted, his laughter rang clear. Restless as always, he moved among them, attending to everyone. Yet I saw whose favorite he was. I saw at whose feet he lay on the grass, whom he wrapped warmly, whom he watched with tender care—like a jewel, like a treasure.

Still, jests flew. Still, I gathered that while Paul was away, working for others, these others would guard the treasure he left behind. Bring back an Indian fortune, and they would give him a young bride and a rich inheritance. As for the sacred vow, the promise of constancy—forgotten. The vibrant present outweighed the past. And finally, his nun was truly buried.

So it must be. The revelation had come. My预感 had not deceived me. There is a kind of premonition that never errs. It was I who misread the sign—thinking she spoke of visions when, in truth, she foretold reality.

I might have lingered longer, pondered deeper. Some might say the evidence was weak, the proof uncertain. Some cautious doubters might demand more before believing a match between a poor, forty-year-old man and an eighteen-year-old heiress. But not I. No hesitation, no excuse, no cowardice in facing the inevitable. No paltering with the truth. No fleeing from the Fact—the swift, relentless, conquering Fact.

No. I embraced the whole plan. I seized it with urgency, wrapped it around me like a soldier clutches his banner. I summoned Conviction to fix it upon me—this truth, abhorrent yet accepted—with the strongest nails, the deepest blows. When the iron had entered my soul, I rose—renovated.

In my fervor, I said, “Truth, you are a good mistress to faithful servants! While a lie pressed on me, I suffered. Even when falsehood was sweet, flattering, warm to the heart, it consumed me with torment. Believing love was won, I feared its loss. Truth stripped away illusion, flattery, expectation. Here I stand—free!”

Now, all I had to do was carry this freedom to my room, to my bed, and see what I could make of it. The play was not quite over. I might have stayed, watched longer—the love scene beneath the trees, the woodland courtship. Had there been no love in it, my imagination—so generous, so creative—could have shaped it into passion, colored it with depth and fire. But I would not look. I had made my decision. I would not betray my nature. And then—something tore at my shawl, dug into my side, like a vulture with strong beak and talon. I must be alone to wrestle with it. I think I never truly felt jealousy until now.

This was not like enduring Dr. John and Paulina’s affection—when I closed my eyes, shut my ears, withdrew my thoughts, I still acknowledged its charm. This was an outrage. Love born of beauty was not mine. I had no claim to it. But another love—tentative, born of long acquaintance, forged in pain, marked by constancy, strengthened by pure affection, tested by intellect, perfected by his own hand—this love, which laughed at passion’s frenzy and fleeting heat—this love, I had a stake in. Anything that touched it, either to build or destroy, I could not witness indifferently.

I turned from the trees and the merry company beneath them. Midnight had long passed. The concert was over. The crowds thinned. I followed the tide. Leaving the radiant park and brightly lit Haute-Ville—still aglow, for this was to be a “nuit blanche” in Villette—I sought the dim lower quarter.

Dim, I should not say. For the beauty of moonlight—forgotten in the park—flowed here again, flooding the senses. High and calm, she rode, pure and unstained. The music, the mirth, the fire and color of lamps had drowned her for an hour. But now, her glory and silence triumphed. The rival lamps died. She moved on like a white fate. Drum, trumpet, bugle—once loud—were forgotten. With a pencil-light, she wrote records on earth and sky, destined for eternity. She and the stars seemed to me both symbols and witnesses of truth reigning supreme. The night-sky lit her reign. Like the slow progress of the heavens, her victory advanced—an eternal movement, past, present, and future.

These narrow streets are very still. I love them for their humility and peace. Occasionally, a homeward-bound citizen passes—but these are quiet walkers, gone quickly. I love Villette in this mood. I would not willingly return under a roof, but I must finish my strange adventure and quietly reclaim my bed in the dormitory before Madame Beck returns.

Only one street lies between me and the Rue Fossette. As I enter it for the first time, the sound of a carriage tears through the silence. It comes this way—fast. How loudly it rattles on the paved path! The street is narrow. I keep to the curb. The carriage thunders past—but what do I see—or think I see—through the window? Something white flutters—surely a hand waves a handkerchief. Was that signal meant for me? Am I known? Who could recognize me? It is not M. de Bassompierre’s carriage, nor Mrs. Bretton’s. Neither the Hôtel Crécy nor La Terrasse lies in that direction. Well, I have no time to wonder. I must hurry home.

Reaching the Rue Fossette, arriving at the pensionnat, all is quiet. No fiacre has yet brought Madame and Désirée. I left the great door slightly open. Will it still be so? Might the wind or some accident have slammed it shut, triggering the spring bolt? If so, entry would be impossible. My adventure would end in failure. I gently pushed the heavy door—would it yield?

Yes. As silent, as effortless, as if some helpful spirit had waited for a magic word, it opened into the vestibule. I stepped inside, breath held, quietly secured the door, climbed the stairs barefoot, reached the dormitory, and found my bed.

Ah! I reached it. I drew a free breath. Then—almost I shrieked—almost, but not quite. Thank Heaven.

Throughout the dormitory, throughout the house, reigned the stillness of death. All slept. So deep was the hush, it seemed no one dreamed. Nineteen beds held nineteen still forms, stretched out and unmoving. On mine—the twentieth—nothing should have lain. I had left it empty. It should have remained so. But what do I see between the half-drawn curtains? What dark, strange shape lies supine, long, and unnatural? Is it a thief who slipped through the open door? Does it wait in ambush? It looks very black. Not human. Could it be a stray dog, come from the street, curled up here? Will it leap at me if I approach? I must approach. Courage! One step!

My head spun. By the dim night-lamp, I saw stretched across my bed—the old phantom—the NUN.

A cry now might have undone me. Whatever the sight, I could afford neither panic, scream, nor faint. Besides, I was not overcome. Tempered by recent trials, my nerves rejected hysteria. Fresh from lights, music, and throngs, lashed by a new wound, I defied ghosts. In a moment, without a sound, I rushed to the haunted bed. Nothing leapt, nothing stirred. All motion was mine—so was all life, all substance, all strength. Instinct told me. I tore her up—the incubus! I held her aloft—the goblin! I shook her loose—the mystery! And down she fell—down in pieces—down in shreds—and I stamped upon her.

Again—behold the leafless tree, the unstable horse, the cloud, the moonbeam. The long nun was merely a long bolster wrapped in a black stole, artfully covered with a white veil. The garments—strange as it sounds—were genuine nun’s robes. Someone had arranged them to deceive. Where did they come from? Who devised this trick? These questions remained.

Tied to the headband was a slip of paper. In pencil, it read:

“The nun of the attic bequeaths to Lucy Snowe her wardrobe. She will be seen in the Rue Fossette no more.”

And who, then, was the woman who haunted me? I had seen her three times. No woman I knew matched her height. She was not tall for a woman. Nor could any man I knew have created this deception.

And yet—she was real. Or was she?
