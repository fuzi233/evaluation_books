The winter days drifted past Romola with the silent, relentless monotony of white ships passing a lone watcher on the shore—each vessel identical in appearance, yet carrying a hidden cargo of impending transformation. Tito's whispered warnings had infused her curiosity regarding public affairs with a paralyzing dread, driving her to court ignorance rather than seek truth. The menacing figure of the German Emperor had vanished once more; Florence's political posture had softened in other respects. Yet, the underlying distress persisted, leaving Romola's active duties undiminished. As always, she sought refuge within the complexity of these tasks, shielding her mind from its gnawing doubts. She refrained from celebrating the relief that had arrived in extremity—a reprieve that seemingly validated the strategy of the Frate's faction. Indeed, Francesco Valori, the fiery leader of the Piagnoni, had been elected Gonfaloniere at the start of the year, eager to imprint his brand of radical liberty upon the city during his brief tenure. Superficially, this appeared to fortify the party most devoted to freedom and to bolster Savonarola's protective shield. However, Romola perceived every nuance suggesting that this triumph was merely an unconscious incubation of mixed seeds of change, destined to sprout tragedy at any moment. By Carnival time, shortly after mid-February, her intuition crystallized into certainty. The Medici had abandoned their passivity, mobilizing openly to secure the election of Bernardo del Nero as the new Gonfaloniere.

On the final day of the Carnival, between ten and eleven in the morning, Romola departed toward the Corso degli Albizzi, fulfilling her promise to retrieve her cousin Brigida. Their shared goal was to depart from the Via de' Bardi early in the afternoon, securing positions at a window Tito had reserved in the Piazza della Signoria. A spectacle of unprecedented novelty awaited them there, demanding the gaze of every Florentine eye. The Piagnoni had seized total control of the Carnival's observance. Despite the desperate efforts of Dolfo Spini and his allies to revive traditional masques and practical jokes seasoned with indecency, such frivolity was deemed incompatible with a city where Christ had been proclaimed King. Romola ventured forth in a languid mental state, typical of those embarking on a long day of sightseeing solely to gratify a child or a cherished friend. While the day marked an epoch in Carnival reform, this specific phase failed to ignite her enthusiasm. Unaware of it, she stood at the precipice of a personal epoch wherein another destiny would become visibly, irrevocably entwined with her own.

She chose to traverse the vast Piazza first, conducting a solitary reconnaissance of the unparalleled sight unfolding below. Entering from the south, her vision was arrested by a monstrous, multi-colored edifice shaped like a pyramid—or, more accurately, a colossal fir-tree towering sixty feet high. Its shelves widened progressively toward the base until reaching a circumference of eighty yards. The Piazza teemed with life: slender youths clad in white garments, olive wreaths adorning their heads, moved to and fro around the pyramid's base, carrying baskets brimming with vibrant hues. Maturer figures, some in monastic robes, others in the loose tunics and dark-red caps of artists, assisted, examined, or retreated to distant vantage points to survey the wondrous totality. Among a significant group standing on the marble steps of Orgagna's Loggia, Romola recognized Piero di Cosimo, whose demeanor radiated discontent and scorn.

Approaching closer, she paused to inspect the multifarious objects arranged in gradation from the base to the summit. There were tapestries and brocades of immodest design; paintings and sculptures deemed too likely to incite vice. Boards and tables hosted every variety of game, alongside playing cards, printing blocks, dice, and other gambling apparatus. Worldly music books lined up with instruments of all varieties—lutes, drums, cymbals, and trumpets. Masks and costumes from the old Carnival shows cluttered the space, joined by handsome copies of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarch, Pulci, and other texts dedicated to vanity or impurity. Implements of feminine vanity abounded: rouge pots, false hair, mirrors, perfumes, powders, and transparent veils designed to provoke inquisitive glances. Finally, at the very apex, sat the unflattering effigy of a presumably mythical Venetian merchant, rumored to have offered a heavy sum for this collection of marketable abominations. Soaring above him in surpassing ugliness was the symbolic figure of the old, debauched Carnival.

This assembly served as the preparation for a novel bonfire—the Burning of Vanities. Concealed within the pyramid's interior was a plentiful stockpile of dry fuel and gunpowder. On this final evening, the pile was to be ignited to the sound of trumpets, sending the grotesque relic of the old Carnival tumbling into the flames amidst songs of reforming triumph. Such a crowning act required a peculiar organization initiated by Savonarola two years prior. The mass of Florentine boyhood and youth was no longer left to their natural inclinations toward street mischief and crude dissoluteness. Under the tutelage of Fra Domenico, Savonarola's lieutenant, lads and striplings—the hope of Florence—were trained to utter only pure words, cultivating a zeal for the Unseen Good that would shame the lukewarmness of their elders. Their sole pleasures were of an angelic sort: singing divine praises and walking in white robes.

For these troops, rows of seats had been erected high against the Duomo's walls, accustomed to hearing Savonarola address them as the future glory of a city divinely appointed to execute God's work. These fresh-cheeked soldiers were the primary agents of the regenerated merriment of the new Carnival, a sacred parody of the old. Had there been bonfires in ancient times? Now, a bonfire consumed impurity from the earth. Had there been symbolic processions? Now, symbols consisted of white robes, red crosses, and olive wreaths—emblems of peace and innocent gladness—with banners proclaiming the triumphs of goodness. Had there been dancing in rings under the open sky to the sound of choral voices chanting loose songs? Now, monks and laity danced in fraternal love and divine joy, accompanied by the music of hymns. Collections from street passengers exceeded previous records, not for gross suppers, but for the benefit of the hungry and needy. Furthermore, there was the collection of the *Anathema*, the Vanities destined for the great pyramidal bonfire.

Troops of young inquisitors patrolled the streets, urging households to surrender their *Anathema*. Perhaps, after yielding the obvious vanities, the Madonna of the home still possessed small reddened balls imported from the Levant, intended to force a sudden, insincere bloom onto a sallow cheek? If so, she was to deposit them into the basket of doom. Or perhaps she retained ringlets and coils of "dead hair"? Then she was to bring them to the street door, holding them in her hands rather than wearing them, publicly renouncing the *Anathema* that disguised the respectable signs of age with a ghastly mockery of youth. In reward, she would hear fresh, youthful voices pronounce a blessing upon her and her house.

These beardless inquisitors, organized into miniature regiments, undoubtedly embraced their duties with vigor. Coercing people through shame or spiritual pressure into relinquishing items they would otherwise cherish is a form of piety to which the boyish mind converts with remarkable ease. Even when obstinate wicked men erupted in threats of whips or cudgels, the excitement mounted. Savonarola himself evidently wrestled with the difficulty inherent in training these boys—noble yearnings for great ends clashing with an imperfect grasp of means, forcing reliance on supernatural constraint as the only sure hope. The Florentine youth had harbored vile habits and foul tongues; initially, it seemed an unmixed blessing when they shouted "*Viva Gesù!*". Yet, Savonarola was eventually compelled to declare from the pulpit that there was too much shouting. Constant repetition of sacred words bred contempt. He ordered a cessation of the clamor until the next Festa.

Nevertheless, as the long stream of white-robed youthfulness, adorned with little red crosses and olive wreaths, proceeded to the Duomo at dawn to receive communion from Savonarola's hands, it constituted a sight of profound beauty. Doubtless, many of these young souls stored memories of hope and awe that would protect them from viewing their civic duty through a merely vulgar lens. No conscious obedience represents a regression from lawlessness; these boys became the generation of men who fought and endured greatly in the Republic's final struggle.

In the interim hours between early communion and dinner, they conducted their final perambulations to collect alms and vanities, explaining Romola's observation of the slim white figures circling the pyramid's base.

"What think you of this folly, Madonna Romola?" a brusque voice intoned close to her ear.

"Your Piagnoni will make *l'inferno* a pleasant prospect for us if they continue to carry things their own way on earth," the speaker continued. "It takes a cudgel fetched from the mountains to witness painters like Lorenzo di Credi and young Baccio assisting in the burning of color out of life in this manner."

"My good Piero," Romola replied, lifting her gaze to smile at the grim man, "even you must find solace in seeing some of these things destroyed. Observe those gewgaws, wigs, and rouge pots. I have heard you speak with as much indignation against them as Fra Girolamo himself."
