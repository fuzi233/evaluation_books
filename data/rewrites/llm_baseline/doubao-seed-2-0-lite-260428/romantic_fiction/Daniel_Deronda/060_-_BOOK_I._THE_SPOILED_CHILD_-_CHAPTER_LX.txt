“But I shall say no more of this at this time; for this is to be felt and not to be talked of; and they who never touched it with their fingers may secretly perhaps laugh at it in their hearts and be never the wiser.”

Old legends tell of a Roman emperor who put ten learned Jewish sages to death to avenge Joseph’s sale at the hands of his brothers. There have always been men of his ilk, whose piety bends only to punishing what they perceive as a slight, who can see the justice of a grudge but never the grace of gratitude. You will never convince a man in the grip of a fierce feeling that his logic is flawed, nor persuade someone who has never loved that there is good, true reason for any heart to bend to affection. The very shape of our language proves this: the word love was spoken long before the word lovable ever came to be.

Daniel Deronda’s hand shook just a little as he handed his calling card across the counter of the bank on Mainz’s Schuster Strasse, asking to see Joseph Kalonymos. The clerk led him straight back to a sunlit inner office, and there behind a table stacked with open letters was the white-bearded man he had glimpsed a year prior in the Frankfurt synagogue. He still wore that same faded old felt hat, and a leather portmanteau sat packed at his elbow, a wool wrap and overcoat folded atop it. He rose when Deronda stepped over the threshold, but did not move forward or offer his hand at once. His small, sharp eyes glinted like polished black gems against the soft gold of his weathered face and the cloud of white hair framing it, and he spoke in warm, guttural German: “Good. Now at last, you are the one coming to seek me, young man.”

“I came to you full of gratitude, as a friend of my grandfather’s,” Deronda replied easily, his own German flowing soft and natural. “I owe you so much trouble taken on my behalf, for which I can never thank you enough.”

Kalonymos’s stern face softened at once, and he stretched out his hand to clasp Deronda’s firmly. “So you are no longer angry to learn you are more than just an Englishman?” he asked.

“Angry? Never.” Deronda’s smile was bright, unburdened at last. “I thank you from the bottom of my heart for saving me from a life of ignorance of where I came from, and for safeguarding the chest my grandfather left in trust for me all these years.”

“Sit, sit.” Kalonymos gestured to a chair beside his table, lowering his voice to an eager murmur as he sank back into his own seat. He took off his hat, revealing a thick, full head of silver hair, and stroked his long white beard slowly, his gaze roaming over Deronda’s face as if he was mapping every familiar line. That moment wrapped around Deronda like something sacred: here before him was the last living link to the grandfather who had dreamed of him, longed for him, long before he was ever born. He could almost feel the electric hum of the chain of his own ancestry, stretching back through generations he had never known existed, and he bore Kalonymos’s quiet scrutiny with a soft, awed delight, the same kind of hush you feel standing in a place where old, long-ago acts still shape every breath you take today.

His face must have shown all that quiet, tender feeling, for Kalonymos nodded, pleased, and spoke a line from an old beloved Hebrew hymn: “As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter.” He paused a beat, leaning forward. “I rejoice I had not yet set off on my travels again, that you got here in time for me to see my oldest friend’s face as it looked in his youth—no longer pulled away from the fellowship of his people, no longer shrinking in proud hurt from the hand that reached to claim him as a Jew. You come of your own free will to claim the heritage and kindred that cruel trickery would have stolen from you. You come with a willing heart to say: ‘I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.’ Is that not the truth?”

“It is,” Deronda said firmly. “And I must add, I would never have treated any Jew unkindly simply for who they are. Before I knew all this, it was only the shame of not knowing anything of my own mother that made me shy away from speaking of it to a stranger.”

Kalonymos shook his head, closing his eyes for a moment as if the injustice of it still stung. “A robbery. A sin. As wicked as when our young boys and girls were raised to serve the Roman tyrant Edom, torn from their people. But that plot is broken. I broke it. When Daniel Charisi—may his Rock and his Redeemer guard him!—was a stripling no taller than my shoulder, we made a solemn vow we would be friends for all our days. He said, ‘Let us bind ourselves to duty, as if we are sons of the same mother.’ That was always his way: he built his soul out of promises. He used to say, ‘Bind love fast with duty, for duty is the love of law, and law is the very nature of the Eternal.’ And though we were apart for decades in our later lives, that bond never snapped. When he died, those who would rob him tried to take the truth of his grandson too. But they could never rob him of me. I rescued the part of him he treasured most, the things he preserved for his unborn grandchild. And now I get to hand that grandson back to the legacy he was meant for.”

He stepped out of the room for a few minutes, returning with a clerk who carried a heavy, carved chest set down on the floor between them, drawing a soft leather cover off its surface. It was not large, but its gilded iron braces and handles made it feel heavy with weight, the wood intricately incised all over with flowing Arabic script.

Kalonymos drew a small leather pouch from his coat, pulling out an odd, ornate key. “Here it is. Guard it carefully. I hope you are methodic, and wary of the careless mistakes of youth.” He gave Deronda a look of gentle, age-old caution.

“I will guard this better than any other thing I own,” Deronda smiled, tucking the key into the inner breast pocket of his coat, right over his heart. “I have never owned anything that carries so much love, so much hope, so much effort behind it. I will never forget that half of that effort was yours. Do you have time to tell me more of my grandfather? I don’t want to trespass on your schedule.”

“Stay a little while longer. I leave for Trieste in an hour and eighteen minutes,” Kalonymos said, glancing at his pocket watch. “My sons will be here soon, and I want to introduce you to them, so they can welcome my oldest friend’s grandson into their home. They live here in Mainz, comfortable and well-settled, even if I prefer to wander the world.”

“I would be glad to make their acquaintance another time,” Deronda said gently. “There are people waiting for me back in England, friends who need me more than I can say. I’ve already stayed away longer than I meant to, thanks to all these unexpected turns. But the chance to come back to Mainz, to get to know you and your family better, is reason enough to travel across the continent again soon.”

“Good. You will not find me here, though. I am past seventy, and I wander always, carrying my winding sheet with me wherever I go. But my sons, and their children, live here, wealthy and united. The days are better for our people now, ever since Charlemagne brought my ancestors over from Italy, to bring a little light of learning to their rough German kin. My generation had to fight for every bit of that safety, when we were young in harder times. Now we grow our wealth in peace, and the scholarship of all Germany is fed by Jewish minds—though not all those minds still hold Jewish hearts. Were you raised to know nothing at all of the life of your people, young man?”

“Not at all,” Deronda said quickly. “Even before I had any hint of where I truly came from, I found myself drawn to study every part of Jewish history, more than any other subject in the world. It turns out I was already unconsciously preparing myself, to understand my grandfather a little before I ever learned he was mine.”

The line pleased Kalonymos, and he launched into stories of the two old boys’ decades of adventures: Daniel Charisi’s iron will, the deep upright line etched between his brows even when he was barely out of boyhood, the way he used to say it was better to have a steady will than no will at all, better a steadfast enemy than a wavering friend, better a sure belief than no belief at all. He scorned nothing more than lazy indifference. But he was no narrow man: he drank in learning as easily as a plant drinks rain, studied medicine, traveled every corner of the world, spent his fortune chasing knowledge. What he insisted on always was that the strength of all humanity lay in the balance between holding fast to your own identity and reaching out to the rest of the world. He hated the thought of his people melting into the Gentile population, losing all that made them distinct: “It is no different,” he would say, “than letting every kind of grain grow together until all the distinct, precious varieties blur into one dull sameness.”

The two men were as different as the inside and outside of a bowl, Kalonymos laughed. He loved to wander, to do business, to stare up at the great bright stars on the deck of a ship in the East, and be perfectly content with just looking. Charisi was never content to just look: he wove every sight he saw into plans for the future of their people, poured every part of his soul into that core of their faith. But their vow held fast, all their lives, and Kalonymos had kept his end of it to the very last.

“And you have done right by him, and by me,” Deronda said, his voice thick with feeling, as they both stood to say goodbye. “It would have been the greatest robbery of all, to never know the inheritance he spent his whole life preparing for me. I thank you with all my soul.”

“Be worthy of him.” Kalonymos’s hand rested heavy on Deronda’s shoulder, his eyes sharp but kind. “What is your life’s work, your vocation?”

Deronda hesitated, but he could not lie. He had been reading law, but it was never his true calling. “I do not have one, not yet.”

“Get one. The Jew must never be idle. Will you call yourself a Jew now, and hold fast to the faith of your fathers?”

“I will call myself a Jew,” Deronda said, his face flushing pale with the weight of the truth that settled over him in that moment. “I cannot say I will believe exactly as my fathers believed, for even they stretched the bounds of their understanding, learned from every people they met. But I will hold fast to my grandfather’s idea: separateness, and connection. My first duty is to my own people. If there is work to be done to restore, to build up our common life, that work will be my vocation.”

In that moment, the act of speaking the words out loud seared them into a resolve he had never been able to name before, a quiet new purpose thrumming in his bones. Kalonymos smiled, tears glistening in the corners of his old eyes, and spoke a soft Hebrew benediction over him. They parted then, and before Deronda had even settled back into London, the old wanderer was out at sea again, leaning on the ship’s rail to greet the bright familiar stars, no curious questions, no urgent striving, just peace.
