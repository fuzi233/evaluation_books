It was my iron-clad resolve to remain wide-eyed until the sun crested the eastern ridge, guarding against whatever shadows lurked in the dark. Yet, the sheer fatigue of both spirit and limb conspired against me. Before long, a heavy, suffocating blanket of oblivion wrapped around me. My companion's rapid descent into slumber acted as a catalyst, accelerating my own surrender to dreams. Initially, he was restless, twitching constantly on his cork mattress, waking me with frantic whispers about strange noises—"Did you hear that?" "Is the tent shifting?" "Has the river breached the island?" Each time I ventured outside to verify these terrors, I returned with the reassuring news that everything remained unchanged. Gradually, his agitation subsided. His breathing smoothed into a steady rhythm, culminating in the distinct, rhythmic roar of snoring—a sound so profound and soothing that it marked the first and only instance in my life where such a cacophony brought genuine comfort.

This was the final thought lingering in my mind as consciousness slipped away entirely.

A sudden constriction in my throat jolted me awake. The blanket had been pulled over my face, but beneath it, something else pressed heavily against me. My immediate, instinctive fear was that my companion had rolled off his mat onto mine during his rest. I cried out his name and sat upright, and in that same instant, a chilling realization struck me: the tent was *encircled*. Outside, a deafening, multitudinous soft pattering filled the air, a sound that transformed the night into a nightmare.

I shouted again, my voice cracking with terror. He gave no answer. Worse still, the comforting cadence of his snores had vanished, replaced by silence. Furthermore, the tent flap hung open—an unforgivable breach in our sanctuary. Driven by a primal dread, I crawled out into the suffocating darkness to secure the flap. It was then, with a sinking heart, that I realized with absolute certainty: the Swede was gone. He had fled.

Seized by a madness that bordered on hysteria, I dashed out, plunging instantly into a swirling torrent of a humming sound. It surrounded me from every direction, a chaotic, frenzied vibration that echoed the familiar hum of the insects but twisted it into something monstrous. It sounded as though a swarm of giant, invisible bees had descended upon me, thickening the air itself until breathing became a labored, desperate struggle.

Yet, my friend was in peril, and hesitation was not an option.

Dawn was breaking, casting a faint, milky pallor over the horizon above a thin strip of clear sky. The air was utterly still. Through the gloom, I could barely distinguish the dense willow thicket and the river beyond, along with the pale patches of sand. In a frenzy, I ran back and forth across the island, screaming his name, shouting the first words that sprang to my lips. But the willows swallowed my cries, and the oppressive hum muffled them further, confining my voice to a sphere of mere feet around me. I threw myself into the bushes, stumbling over gnarled roots, scraping my face raw as I tore blindly through the tangled, restraining branches.

Then, quite unexpectedly, I emerged onto the point of the island. There, silhouetted against the grey line where water met sky, stood a dark figure. It was the Swede. Already, one foot hovered over the river's edge. One moment longer, and he would have jumped.

I lunged at him, wrapping my arms tightly around his waist and hauling him toward the shore with every ounce of strength I possessed. Naturally, he fought back with furious resistance, emitting a guttural noise identical to that cursed, driving hum. As he thrashed, he blurted out bizarre, incoherent phrases about going "inside" to Them, taking "the path of water and wind," and other horrors I could not grasp even as I dragged him back. They left me sick with a mixture of horror and bewilderment, though I would try in vain to recall their exact meaning later. Finally, I managed to shove him into the relative safety of the tent, throwing him onto the mattress where I pinned him down until the fit subsided.

What struck me most profoundly was the suddenness of the entire episode. Just as abruptly as the humming and the pattering ceased outside, the Swede fell silent and grew calm. I believe this simultaneity was the strangest aspect of the ordeal. He had just opened his eyes, lifting his exhausted face to meet mine, illuminated by the pale light of the rising sun streaming through the doorway. With the innocent, trembling demeanor of a frightened child, he whispered, "My life, old man... it's my life I owe you. But it's all over now anyway. They've found a victim in our place!"

Then he collapsed backward onto his blankets, falling asleep literally within inches of me. He simply succumbed, beginning to snore again with a healthy vigor, as if nothing had transpired and as if he had never attempted to offer his soul to the waters in sacrifice. When the sunlight roused him three hours later—three hours of unceasing vigil for me—it became crystal clear that he retained absolutely no memory of his near-suicide. I judged it prudent to remain silent, sparing neither him nor myself any dangerous inquiries.

He awakened naturally and easily, as I had described, when the sun burned high in a windless, scorching sky. Immediately, he rose to prepare the fire for breakfast. I followed him anxiously as he bathed, noting that he did not dare plunge fully into the water, merely dipping his head and commenting on the peculiar extra chill of the stream.

"The river's falling at last," he remarked, "and I'm glad of it."

"The humming has stopped too," I replied.

He looked up at me with a quiet, composed expression. Evidently, he remembered everything save his own act of self-destruction.

"Everything has stopped," he said, pausing significantly, "because—"

He faltered. However, I sensed that a reference to his earlier ramblings lingered in his mind, and I was determined to draw it out.

"Because 'They've found another victim'?" I asked, forcing a brittle laugh.

"Exactly," he answered, his voice steady. "Exactly! I feel as positive of it as though—as though—I feel quite safe again, I mean," he concluded, though the relief in his tone was tinged with something else.

He began to scan the surroundings curiously. Hot patches of sunlight lay on the sand; there was no breeze. The willows stood motionless. Slowly, he rose to his feet.

"Come," he said, "I think if we look, we shall find it."

He broke into a run, and I hastened to follow. He stayed close to the banks, probing the sandy bays, caves, and shallow backwaters with a stick, while I trailed closely behind.

"Aha!" he exclaimed presently, "Aha!"

The tone of his voice transported me instantly back to the visceral horror of the last twenty-four hours, and I rushed to join him. He pointed his stick at a large, dark object lying half-submerged in the water, half-resting on the sand. Twisted willow roots seemed to entangle it, preventing the river from sweeping it away. Only a few hours prior, this spot must have been underwater.

"Look," he said quietly, "the victim that made our escape possible!"

As I peered over his shoulder, I saw that his stick rested upon the form of a man. He flipped it over. It was the corpse of a peasant, the face buried deep in the sand. Clearly, the man had been drowned, but only a few hours ago. His body must have drifted down upon our island sometime around dawn—at the precise moment the fit had ended.

"We must give it a decent burial, you know," I suggested.

"I suppose so," I replied, shuddering involuntarily. Something about the appearance of that drowned wretch sent a chill through me that I could not suppress.

The Swede glanced up sharply, an unreadable mask on his face, and began to scramble down the bank. I followed at a more leisurely pace. I noted that the current had stripped much of the clothing from the body, leaving the neck and part of the chest starkly exposed.

Halfway down the slope, my companion halted abruptly, holding up a hand in warning. Either my footing betrayed me, or I had gathered too much momentum to stop quickly, and I collided with him, sending him forward with a bounding leap to avoid a fall. Together, we tumbled onto the hard sand, our feet splashing violently into the water. And before we could react, we had crashed somewhat heavily against the corpse.

The Swede let out a sharp cry. I recoiled as if I had been fired upon.

In that split second, as we brushed against the body, a loud, resonant humming erupted from its surface—a chorus of many hums—that swelled with a vast commotion, like countless winged creatures beating the air around us. Then, with a disorienting rush, they ascended upward into the sky, fading gradually until they finally died away in the distance. It was as if we had inadvertently disturbed some living, invisible workers at their labor.
