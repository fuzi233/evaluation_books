—DANTE: *Il Purgatorio*

Gwendolen did not linger long in Genoa after her mother’s arrival. The desire to leave that glittering shore gave her a brittle strength, a resolve to move forward, though she could not yet name her destination. Every beautiful place now seemed to her a possible prison, its charm twisted into torment.

“I never want to see the Mediterranean again,” she said abruptly to her mother, who mistook the words for grief rather than revulsion. Mrs. Davilow, though obliged to treat this time as one of mourning, found an unexpected solace in her daughter’s dependence. For the first time since Gwendolen’s marriage, she felt needed—cherished not as a duty, but as a reprieved treasure.

In the quiet of their shared room, Gwendolen’s voice would pierce the dark:
“Mamma, are you there?”
“Yes, dear. Do you need anything?”
“No. Only I like knowing you are.” A pause. “Does it trouble you, my waking you?” (This hesitation would not have marked the old Gwendolen.)
“I wasn’t sleeping.”
“It didn’t feel real that you were near. I had to make sure.” A rustle of sheets. “I can endure things if you are with me. But you mustn’t lie awake worrying. You must let me make you happy now—what else is left to me?”

Mrs. Davilow’s heart ached with a bittersweet joy. Yet the next night, hearing Gwendolen’s restless sighs, she ventured: “Shall I give you your sleeping draught?”
“No. I don’t want to sleep.”
“It would do you good, darling.”
“Don’t tell me what’s good for me!” Gwendolen’s voice turned sharp. “You don’t know. You and Uncle mustn’t decide for me anymore.”

Then, suddenly, the storm broke. “I was always cruel to you, mamma.”
“No, dearest—”
“I was,” Gwendolen insisted, the words tearing loose. “It’s because I was wicked that I’m wretched now.” Sobs overtook her, the dammed-up guilt and fear flooding out.

The truth of Grandcourt’s will had reached her—not through gentle revelation, but in cold fragments. Sir Hugo, with uneasy kindness, had disclosed the facts to Mr. Gascoigne: the bulk of the estate entailed on the son of Lydia Glasher, Gwendolen left with a pittance. The rector, stung in his pride, masked his humiliation behind clerical stoicism. But Mrs. Davilow, piecing together hints, guessed her daughter had known more of her husband’s secrets than anyone suspected.

One evening, Gwendolen shattered the silence.
“You mustn’t expect me to be rich, mamma. I may have nothing.”
Mrs. Davilow, startled, reassured her: “Sir Hugo says you’re to have two thousand a year and Gadsmere.”
“What I keep depends on what I accept.” Gwendolen’s voice was steel. “You mustn’t question me. I’ll care for you—but about *him*, no one interferes. Would eight hundred a year suffice for you?”
“More than enough! You mustn’t give me so much.” A hesitation. “Do you know who inherits the rest?”
“Yes.” Gwendolen’s hand slashed the air. “I know everything. It’s settled. I forbid it being spoken of again.”

Mrs. Davilow flushed, turning away. The gulf between them yawned—her daughter’s suffering locked away, beyond reach. But Gwendolen, watching, softened.
“Come nearer, mamma. Don’t grieve.” She drew her close. “I mean to be wise now. Good to you—so good, you won’t know me. Only don’t cry.”

Beneath the vow lay a resolve: she would see Deronda. He alone could tell her how to navigate the wreckage. The thought of him was a lifeline—his judgment the compass by which she might steer clear of deeper shame.

Sir Hugo, oblivious to her desperation, cheerfully pressed her to stay in Park Lane. Gwendolen seized the chance. When he mentioned leaving for the Abbey, she spoke coolly:
“Sir Hugo, I must see Mr. Deronda. I don’t know his direction. Will you send for him?”

The baronet’s glance flickered, but his tone was smooth. “Certainly. I’ll have him come at once.” Privately, he wondered at her intensity—and at Deronda’s confounding reserve. Had the boy no heart? Here was beauty and sorrow pleading for him, and he lingered in shadows.

Yet Gwendolen cared nothing for appearances now. Pride had burned away. She would see Deronda, even if the world whispered. The need was elemental—as urgent as a prisoner’s cry for light.

Sir Hugo wrote the note. It found Deronda in his chambers, and he came.
