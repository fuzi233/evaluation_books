When my mother learned I had taken a paid post here in England rather than surrender to her demands that I bring my whole family back to Virginia to live dependent on her bounty, I do not think she was best pleased. It seems she meant to starve me into submission, cutting off my allowance entirely to force my hand. We never once, not on the very day of her death, spoke openly of that bitter pecuniary rift between us. She withdrew all support; I said not a word of complaint, and contrived to keep my household going without a penny from her. I never heard she owned her injustice outright, save for a private confession Harry passed on to me years later. When we met at last, long after our years of estrangement, Madam Esmond showed me unusual gentleness, and lavished uncustomary respect and affection on my wife, no doubt intending I should read in that behaviour her silent admission she had been in the wrong. But she spoke no formal apology, and I never asked for one.

Harry was well provided for, and I could not grudge his good fortune, so all my mother’s thrifty, carefully hoarded savings fell to me as her heir in time. Once, a few guineas would have served me far better than the hundreds that came to me long after I had any need for them, but by the time Madam Esmond and I met again, my days of scraping by were far behind. I no longer had to scheme over mean little economies, or haggle to pay the doctor’s bill: I had more than enough, and all her fortune could do was add to what I already possessed. I do not doubt her pride gnawed at her to think my children had gone hungry once, and she had turned away from their need to let strangers relieve them. Proud? But was I prouder still? A single soft word of explanation would have healed our rift years earlier, but I would not speak first, and no more would she. When I do a wrong and recognise it afterwards, I love to ask pardon not out of pure humility, but to salve my own pride, to prove to myself I have not fallen short of my own standards of honour. That stubborn regard for my own ego, I think, is what kept me from ever stooping to a mean or underhand act. What must it be like to live a whole life made up of lies, schemes, and petty subterfuges? What company do men keep when they are alone, every smile a ruse and every wink an act of hypocrisy? Do they wear their masks even in private, before their own consciences? When I choose to overlook an injury, I fear it is rarely out of Christian forgiveness: it is only because I can well afford to write the debt off, and disdain to hound the offender for settlement. I have known one or two rare, sweet souls for whom forgiveness comes as naturally as a flower blooms, but I myself only know how to remit a wrong – not truly forgive it. I wonder if we proud men are secretly proud of being proud?

For years I gave her no sign of submission at all, and we stayed estranged, only brief, cold notes passing between my wife and her, to announce a child’s birth or some other trivial piece of family news. Our troubles after the Stamp Act riots in America were soon followed by new misfortunes for me in London. Though I had stood on the Tory side of the colonial quarrel, fully believing the Imperial government held every right to levy taxes across the colonies, I could not resist publishing a sharp, public letter to a member of the Virginia House of Burgesses that roundly criticised the mother country’s habitual overbearing treatment of the colonies. The sentiments I voiced were so offensive to men in power that I was stripped of my post as hackney-coach licenser, to my uncle’s unutterable horror – he never could abide anyone who fell out of favour at court. He had grown very fond of my wife and little boy, of course, but to me he extended a sort of pitying, quiet contempt that never failed to amuse me. He had a natural scorn for poverty, and a corresponding devotion to luck and worldly success. Any opinion that strayed even an inch from the common accepted track shocked and terrified him, and any plain speaking of truth would turn him pale with fright. He must have had some genuine warmth of heart left in him, for all his snobbery: even after the shocks my imprudence gave him, he still called to see Theo and the child, and nodded a mournful greeting to me when we met, regarding me as a pitiable lost soul past all hope of worldly advancement. No matter. I set about casting about for new work, and the unexpected repayment of an old debt Harry owed me kept my household afloat for months, even years. Oh, the foolish fatuity of youth! I still marvel at how we dared to be so poor, and yet so little cast down by it.

It was at this time that His Majesty’s uncle, the Duke of Cumberland, dropped dead of a sudden fit. Strangely enough, his death was the turning point that changed all my fortunes. My poor uncle Sir Miles Warrington never missed a single court ceremony he could finagle his way into: he was present at every drawing-room, royal christening, court ball, and state funeral. If any prince or princess fell ill, his coach would be parked outside their door before most folk had heard the news. Now nothing would do but he must attend the stout duke’s funeral, standing bareheaded for hours in the pouring rain. He caught a savage cold that turned to inflammation of the throat, and before I had even heard he was taken ill, a lawyer turned up at my Bloomsbury lodgings, and saluted me by the name of Sir George Warrington.

The party quarrels, the fear of tomorrow, all were over now. We laid the poor old gentleman to rest beside his little son in the family churchyard, where so many generations of his kin slept. Little Miles and I walked as chief mourners behind the coffin. Obsequious tenants bowed and curtseyed before us, falling over themselves to win favour with the new master of the estate. The dowager Lady Warrington and her daughter soon withdrew to Bath, and my family and I took possession of the manor house that I have now called home for thirty years. Be not in too much haste, my son, I tell the boy who will one day inherit it: wait but a little while, and I too shall sleep under those yew trees at the churchyard edge, and the villagers will toss their caps in the air for you, Sir Miles.

The records of a quiet, prosperous country life are easily told. The steward’s ledgers note what rents were paid and what forgiven, what crops were sown and in what rotation. The visitor books record who came to stay, and how long they remained; what pensioners my wife supported, who tended their ills, and who passed peacefully away. I was made sheriff one year, the hounds met on our lands a dozen times over – all these trivial details are written down in our house journals, for any heir of mine who cares to take the trouble to read them. We could not afford the grand Hill Street mansion my uncle had once occupied, so we took a smaller London house, where we spent our money far more freely than he ever did. We kept none of the ostentatious show he was famous for, with liveried footmen, gilded equipages, and endless silver plate, but our home-brewed ale was stronger, and my wife’s charities cost far more than the dowager’s had ever done. I have no doubt the old lady thought there was no sin in fleecing the new owners: she charged us unconscionable prices for every stick of furniture she left behind at the country house, and I bore all her extortions with unending good humour. What a fancy price she put on the potted plants in her greenhouse! What a fortune she demanded for that dreadful old spinet she left gathering dust in the drawing room! And those framed bits of worsted work stitched by the accomplished Dora and lovely Flora – why, they could not have fetched higher prices even if they were masterpieces by Titian or Vandyck.

Yet for all our generosity, for all that our care for the local poor far outstripped the dowager’s, we had the worst reputation in the shire for years, all sorts of ugly stories circulated about my supposed dangerous opinions. I had hoped I might step into my uncle’s seat in Parliament alongside his landed property, but I soon found no such luck was coming my way. I would not bribe voters, and I would not force my own tenants to cast their ballots for me at the 1768 election. A gentleman came down from Whitehall with a pocketbook stuffed full of banknotes, and I stood no chance against my government-backed competitor.

Good heavens! Now that we were free of all worldly worry, now that tenants bowed and curtseyed as we walked to church, now that we drove to call on neighbours in the great old family coach pulled by four plump, well-fed horses – did we not often find ourselves longing for the days of our poverty, for that tiny dear cottage in Lambeth where Want was always prowling at the door? Did I not catch myself pining for the old days when I trundled foreign authors around London, swearing that translating books for publishers was not such terrible drudgery after all? When we went up to London, we made sentimental pilgrimages to every old haunt we had once known. I dare say my wife hugged every former landlady we ran across. We invited every old friend from our days of hardship to come and share the comfort of our new home. The Reverend Mr. Hagan and his wife visited us more than once, and when he preached in our local parish church his fine, fiery oratory caused a grand scandal through the whole neighbourhood. Sampson came too, that other unlucky clergyman, and we made him welcome for as long as he cared to stay. Mr. Johnson talked of coming down to see us, but put us off again and again. I dare say our country house was very dull. I know I myself would fall silent for days at a time, and I fear my moodiness tried the patience of the sweetest-tempered woman in the world who ever lived as my wife. I did not care for field sports. The act of killing one partridge was so like killing another that I never could understand how men could spend whole days chasing after that sort of pointless slaughter. Their fox-hunting stories would start the moment the dinner cloth was removed, and run right on till supper time. I would sit silent and listen, and doze off day after day. No wonder I was never popular with the local gentry who called on us.

What confession is this I am making? The storm had passed, I had avoided all the sharp rocks, the ship was safely in port – and the sailor was not even especially content? Was the Susan I had dreamed of all through the long voyage not half the beauty I had expected to find waiting for me on the shore? Well, Susan and all the family will read these pages, so I shall not set down all my secrets here. No, Susan, I never had any secrets from thee. I have seen far more beautiful women than you, but none who ever suited me half so well. I have met Mrs. Carter and Miss Mulso, Mrs. Thrale and Madame Kauffmann, the angelical Gunnings, the Duchess of Devonshire, and a whole host of great beauties who were not a bit angelic, and none of them ever dazzled me. Do not think, young folks, that though I may have led your mother a weary life, and been a great testy Bluebeard to her, that I ever hid another woman’s head in my closet. But once the first joy of taking possession of our new home wore off, I own I quickly tired of my crown. When you boys inherit it, you will be far better princes than I ever was. You can ride to hounds five days a week and find the sport endlessly diverting. I dare say you could sit through the same dull parish sermon fifty times and not yawn half as much as I did the first time I heard it. But my sweet Joan, my beloved Baucis! Thy faithful husband, thy true lover always, thy old Darby is ashamed of how often he was testy with you. Arrived at the very peak of supposed perfect happiness, thy Philemon begs pardon for dozing off so often after dinner. There came a stretch of my life where I had climbed to the very top of the mountain of felicity, and found the view from the summit terribly dull. I yawned in Eden, and said, “Is this all? What, no lions to bite? No rain to drench you? No thorns on the rose bush to prick your finger when you sit down? Nothing but Eve, for ever sweet and tender, and figs for breakfast, dinner, supper, week in week out?” Shall I make a clean breast of it? Listen then.

I know the theory and practice of the Roman Church, but raised as I was in another faith (and sceptical enough of my own creed at that), I cannot help doubting whether Catholics ever truly confess all their sins. Do we Protestants ever do so? Has education ever made those fellow men of ours so fundamentally different from ourselves? I do not think we ever imagine Catholic priests or laymen are any more frank and open than we are. Which brings me back to my question: does any man confess all? Does that dear woman who has shared my life for thirty years know every last corner of it? The one who has always been ready to soothe any sorrow I told her of, who has sat silent beside me when I would not speak, or worn a bright, easy smile out of that charming womanly hypocrisy, so that I would not feel pressed to share a secret I wished to keep private? Oh, that dear hypocrite! Have I not watched her hide the boys’ small misdeeds from my anger? Have I not known her scrape money out of the housekeeping accounts to pay off their little wild extravagances, then talk to me with the most artless face in the world, pretending she did not know that our eldest son, the captain, had been borrowing money from the moneylenders in Duke’s Place, and our younger son, the university student, had very pressing reasons to bar his door against tradesmen come for payment? From the very earliest days, that wise woman of mine was forever hiding little things from me: this one stealing jam from the larder, that one getting into trouble at school, that naughty rebel (put on the caps, young folks, whichever fits you best) flinging an inkstand at his mother in a rage, while I was told the stained gown and carpet had been ruined by a complete accident. We all hide little things from one another. We all have our secrets. We are all alone in the end. We sin alone, and let us hope we repent alone too. That dear woman would gladly break her own foot to spare me a twinge of the gout, but when the pain does come, the ache is only in my own slipper.

At the end of a novel or a play, the hero and heroine marry or die, and the poet cheers them on till their postchaise rounds the corner, or calls for the hearse and plumes, and shovels them underground, and the story is done. But when Mr. Random and Mr. Thomas Jones go home to their brides, is all the excitement over? Are there no quarrels behind closed doors? No Lady Bellastons waiting out in the world? No constables to outrun, no temptations that may conquer you or that you must conquer? The Sirens sang long after Ulysses was married, and suitors whispered in Penelope’s ear. They both had many a long, weary day of doubt and care, just as we all do. My inheritance freed me from all worry about money, but does not black care sit behind the baronet just as surely as it sits behind the poorest common knight? My friends in London all congratulated me on my perfect happiness. Who would not want to be master of a fine house and a fat estate? But can Gumbo, my butler, shut the front door on the blue devils, or drown them forever in a red sea of claret? Does a man sleep better for having twenty-four whole hours a day to doze in? Do his wits brighten after a sermon from the dull old local vicar, ten minutes of sycophantic chattering from the village apothecary, or the company of Sir John and Sir Thomas and their ladies, who ride ten miles through mud and moonlight to eat a haunch of venison and play a hand of whist? All those little indulgences are very well: tradesmen bowing to your carriage door, a place of honour reserved for you at quarter sessions, your wife being led in to dinner second or third in order of precedence. But those pleasures fade fast, and bring their own irritations. For seven years after we moved into Warrington Manor, our two best neighbours in the shire were my Lord Tutbury and Sir John Mudbrook. Our family was older than the Mudbrooks, so when we dined at their house, Lady Tutbury – who was deaf, and fell asleep half way through dinner – always fell to me as my dinner companion. If I was paired with Lady Mudbrook instead, she chattered with such unending, empty folly that even my wife, consummate hypocrite that she is, could barely keep her polite smile pasted on her face, even as she watched me fuming inwardly at the woman’s endless clatter.

I went up to London one season, showed my tongue to Dr. Heberden, and poured out my whole long list of complaints. “Pshaw, my dear Sir George!” the unfeeling physician said. “Headaches, languor, bad sleep, bad temper—” “Not bad temper,” my wife cut in. “Sir George has the sweetest temper in the world, only he is sometimes a little melancholy.” “Bad sleep, bad temper,” the doctor went on, ignoring her. “My dear lady, his inheritance has been his ruin. A little poverty and a great deal of hard work would do him more good than all the physic in my apothecary’s shop.”
