As the morning sun seeped through the grimy windows of the spunging-house, Harry Warrington lay on his narrow bed, the chill of the stone floor seeping into his bones. His thoughts were a jumble of anger and resentment, a tangled web of disappointment and disillusionment. He had once believed in the promises of his friends, in the kindness of strangers, but now those ideals seemed like nothing more than a distant melody, fading into the din of reality. The bills he had received earlier that morning had only added to his sense of desperation, a constant reminder of the debt he owed, the weight of his obligations.

Gumbo, his loyal servant, entered the room, a somber expression on his face. "I brought some of your clothes, Master Harry," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. "But the landlord, Mr. Ruff, he wouldn't let me bring much." Harry's gaze drifted towards Gumbo, a mix of frustration and gratitude etched on his face. He had grown accustomed to Gumbo's exaggerations, but there was something in the man's tone that suggested a deeper concern.

As he dressed, Harry's thoughts turned to the letters Gumbo had brought. He had expected more bills, but instead, he found a note from the landlord, threatening to sell off his aunt's belongings unless he paid his debt. The thought sent a pang of guilt through him, a sense of responsibility he couldn't shake. He pushed the feeling aside, focusing instead on the note Gumbo had spoken of, the one from Colonel Lambert. Harry's anger flared anew, a bitter taste in his mouth. He had trusted Lambert, had believed in his words, but the man had betrayed him, flinging his gifts back in his face like a cruel jest.

The memory of that moment still stung, a raw wound that refused to heal. Harry's jaw clenched, his teeth grinding together in a silent snarl. He wouldn't ask Lambert for help, not now, not ever. He would find a way to escape this place, to leave this city behind and its cruel, unforgiving people. He would go to Germany, or Virginia, anywhere but here. The thought of returning to his family's lands, of becoming a factor and land-steward, was almost appealing, a chance to escape the complexities of the world and find some measure of peace.
