"My cousins are at Diplow," Grandcourt announced, "shall you go there? Tomorrow? The carriage will come for Mrs. Davilow. You may dictate what arrangements you desire for the rooms. We must ensure everything is in proper order while we are away at Ryelands, and tomorrow is the only day available."

Grandcourt sat sideways on a sofa in the drawing-room at Offendene, one arm propped casually on the backrest, the other tucked between his crossed knees. He adopted the posture of a man deeply engrossed in observing the person beside him. Gwendolen, who had previously harbored a distaste for needlework, had recently embraced it with feigned enthusiasm since her engagement. Currently, she held a piece of white embroidery that, upon closer inspection, revealed numerous faulty stitches. Over the past eight or nine days, their time had been predominantly spent riding, yet they always reserved moments for this more demanding form of companionship, which Gwendolen had surprisingly found tolerable. She was entirely content with Grandcourt. His responses to her spirited inquiries about his travels and experiences were delivered in a slow, deliberate manner that suited her perfectly. From the outset, she had noted his uncanny ability to say exactly the right thing. She constantly sensed not only that he possessed none of the foolishness found in others but that, through some subtle alchemy, he projected the impression that all folly belonged to everyone else, save for those who acted as he did. A man who appears capable of commanding the best possesses a sovereign power to diminish all else. Furthermore, Grandcourt's conduct as a lover barely exceeded the bounds of affectionate homage; it was as inconspicuous as the scent of drifting roses, serving primarily to gratify his own vanity. On one occasion, he had kissed not her cheek but the skin beneath her ear. Startled, Gwendolen had risen with palpable agitation, prompting him to stand and ask, "I beg your pardon—did I offend you?" "Oh, it was nothing," Gwendolen replied, somewhat frightened of her own reaction, "only I cannot bear to be kissed under my ear." She sat down again with a playful laugh, yet her heart hammered with a vague terror. She was no longer free to mock him as she had mocked poor Rex. Her agitation seemed flattering to him, and he was content not to push further.

Today, a light rain prevented riding, but as compensation, a package arrived from London. Mrs. Davilow had just departed the room, presenting the exquisite items (arranged by Grandcourt's orders) scattered across the tables for admiration. Gwendolen was currently basking in the scenery of her life. Letting her hands drop onto her lap, she said with a prettily perverse air, "Why is tomorrow the only day?"

"Because the following day marks the first hunt," Grandcourt replied.

"And after that?"

"After that, I must depart for a couple of days—it is tedious—but I shall leave one day and return the next." Noticing a shift in her expression, Grandcourt withdrew his hand from beneath his knees and placed it over hers. "You object to my leaving?"

"It is useless to object," Gwendolen said coldly. She fought desperately against the urge to confess her suspicion regarding his destination, to speak freely without restraint.

"Yes, it is useful," Grandcourt said, enveloping her hand. "I will postpone my departure. I shall travel at night, ensuring I am away for only one day." He believed he understood the cause of what he internally termed this fleeting temper; she was particularly captivating to him at this moment.

"Then do not postpone it, but travel at night," Gwendolen insisted, feeling a surge of control and finding a small vent for her irritation in his peremptory tone.

"So, you will go to Diplow tomorrow?"

"Oh, yes, if you wish it," Gwendolen agreed in a high, careless register. Her preoccupation with other emotions had blinded her to the fact that her hand was being held.

"How you treat us poor devils of men!" Grandcourt murmured, lowering his voice. "We always end up with the worst of it."

"_Are_ you?" Gwendolen asked, her tone inquisitive, gazing at him with unusual naivety. She yearned to accept this banal jest as profound truth about her lover; if so, she too would be justified. If she knew everything, Mrs. Glasher would appear far more blameworthy than Grandcourt. "_Are_ you always receiving the worst?"

"Yes. Are you as kind to me as I am to you?" Grandcourt asked, fixing her with his narrow gaze.

Gwendolen felt struck dumb. Convinced of the immense favor she had received, her sense of command faltered and dissolved into the realization that, regardless of how she looked around, she could not retreat. It was as if she had agreed to board a chariot where another held the reins, and it was not in her nature to leap out before the world's eyes. She had not consented in ignorance; all she could offer now was a confession that she had not been unaware. Her right to demand explanations had vanished. All that remained was to adjust herself so that the sharp thorns of the penance imposed by conscience would not wound her. With a mental shudder, she resolutely shifted her mindset. After a brief pause during which she had not averted her eyes, she broke into a smile and said, "If I were as kind to you as you are to me, it would ruin your generosity: it would cease to be as magnificent as it is now."

"Then I am not to expect a single kiss," Grandcourt stated, pleased to pay a high price for this new style of courtship, which introduced marriage through the finest contrasts.

"Not one?" Gwendolen teased, nodding defiantly.

He raised her small left hand to his lips before releasing it with respectful deference. To call him merely "not disgusting" would be faint praise; he was almost charming. At this moment, she felt unlikely to ever love another man more deeply than this one. His reserve granted her an inexplicable, delightful awareness.

"Apropos," she said, picking up her embroidery again, "are there anyone else at Diplow besides Captain and Mrs. Torrington? Or do you leave them alone together? I suppose he talks of cigars, and she replies with her chignon."

"She has a sister with her," Grandcourt said, offering a shadow of a smile, "and there are two other men—one of whom, I believe, you know."

"Oh, then I hold a poor opinion of him," Gwendolen said, shaking her head.

"You met him at Leubronn—Young Deronda, a youth associated with the Mallingers."

Gwendolen felt as though her heart performed a sudden somersault, and her fingers, striving to maintain a firm grip on her work, grew icy.

"I never spoke to him," she said, fearing any detectable change in her demeanor. "Is he not disagreeable?"

"No, not particularly," Grandcourt replied in his most languid manner. "He thinks a little too highly of himself. I thought he had been introduced to you."

"No. Someone mentioned his name the evening before I left. That was all. Who is he?"

"A sort of ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger's. Nothing of any consequence."

"Oh, poor creature! How very unpleasant for him!" Gwendonen said, speaking lightly without intending sarcasm. "I wonder if the rain has stopped!" she added, rising to look out the window.

Fortunately, the next day was dry, and Gwendolen rode to Diplow on *Criterion*, as she had done on the previous occasion when returning with her mother in the carriage. She always felt bolder in her riding attire, bolstered by the pleasant belief that she looked impeccable—a comforting assurance in any encounter that seemed daunting. Her anger toward Deronda had transformed into a superstitious dread, perhaps due to the coercion he had exerted over her thoughts, lest his initial interference in her life presaged some future influence. Superstitions are often forged from such material: an intense self-feeling that makes the evening star shine with menace, or a beggar's blessing encourage us. Such beliefs carry consequences that frequently validate their hopes or fears.

Before luncheon, Gwendolen toured the rooms with Mrs. Torrington and Mrs. Davilow. She assumed that if she encountered Deronda, a mere bow would suffice. She intended to acknowledge him as little as possible.

Yet, ultimately, she found herself under an internal compulsion stronger than her pride. From the moment they entered the room together, she seemed to do nothing but observe him; everything else became an automatic performance of a habitual role.

When Deronda took his seat at lunch, Grandcourt remarked, "Deronda, Miss Harleth informed me you were not introduced to her at Leubronn?"

"Miss Harleth hardly remembers me, I imagine," Deronda said, looking at her with simple sincerity as they bowed. "She was intensely occupied when I saw her."

Did he suppose she had not suspected him as the man who had redeemed her necklace?

"On the contrary. I remember you very well," Gwendolen said, feeling nervous but mastering herself, returning his gaze with renewed scrutiny. "You did not approve of my playing at roulette."

"How did you arrive at that conclusion?" Deronda asked seriously.
