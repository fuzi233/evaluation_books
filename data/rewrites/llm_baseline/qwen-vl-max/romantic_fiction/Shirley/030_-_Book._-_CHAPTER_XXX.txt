Everyone agreed it was long past time for Mr. Moore to return home. Briarfield buzzed with curiosity over his prolonged absence, and even Whinbury and Nunnely added their own shares of astonishment.

Was there any explanation? Oh yes—there were at least forty plausible ones circulating through the village, each more convincing than the last. But one thing everyone did agree on: it wasn’t business that kept him away. That had been settled long before. He’d tracked down the four ringleaders, seen them tried, convicted, sentenced, and shipped off to transportation. The newspapers had reported every detail—the *Stilbro’ Courier* even embellished the story with dramatic flair.

Yet no one celebrated his success. Not openly, at least. The mill-owners were relieved, hoping the weight of justice would silence the unrest simmering beneath the surface. But the discontent still muttered in alehouses, swearing dark oaths over cheap beer and raising toast after toast in fiery gin.

Some said Moore dared not return to Yorkshire. They whispered he knew his life wouldn’t be safe if he stepped foot back into the county.

“I’ll tell him that,” Mr. Yorke declared when his foreman mentioned the rumor. “And if that doesn’t bring him galloping home, nothing will.”

Whatever it was—fear, duty, or something else—it finally brought him back. He told Joe Scott the exact day he’d arrive in Stilbro’, asking that a hackney be sent to the George for his lodging. Joe passed the word to Mr. Yorke, who made sure to be there to greet him.

It was market day. Moore arrived just in time for the customary dinner among the manufacturers. Though somewhat of an outsider, he was a man of consequence, and the gathering welcomed him with polite deference. Some men who would never have acknowledged him in public—afraid of being tainted by the hatred reserved for him—now spoke of him as a kind of champion behind closed doors. As wine flowed, their admiration warmed, but Moore’s calm indifference kept it from ever turning into fervor.

Mr. Yorke, the steady president of these dinners, watched his young friend with quiet satisfaction. Few things stirred his temper more than seeing a man flattered into pride; few things soothed him more than witnessing someone untouched by fame—someone who didn’t care what others thought.

Robert sat back in his chair, quiet, almost sullen, while clothiers and blanket-makers praised his courage, retold his exploits, and tossed in coarse insults against the working class. To Yorke, it was a delight to see Moore endure it all without a flicker of pleasure. Flattery could be laughed off. Abuse could be shrugged aside. But praise from those you despise? That cuts deep.

Yorke couldn’t help himself. “How do you like your supporters?” he asked. “Don’t you think they honor your cause?”

“But it’s a pity,” he added, “that you didn’t hang those four scoundrels. If you’d done that, the gentry here would’ve torn the horses from the coach, yoked twenty donkeys to it, and pulled you into Stilbro’ like a conquering hero.”

Moore soon left the table, abandoning the wine and the company. In less than five minutes, Mr. Yorke followed.

They rode out of Stilbro’ together.

The day was late, though not yet night. The last light of the sun had slipped from the clouds, and October’s chill was settling over the moors, cloaking the land in shadow.

Yorke, slightly warmed by his drink and pleased to have Moore back in Yorkshire, took the reins of conversation. He touched briefly, mockingly, on the trials and convictions, then drifted into local gossip—before turning directly to Moore.

“Bob,” he said, “I believe you’ve lost her—and you deserve it. Everything was smooth. Fortune herself seemed to fall for you. She’d handed you the grand prize—twenty thousand pounds. All you had to do was reach out and take it. And what did you do? You saddled up and rode off to Warwickshire, chasing foxes like a fool. Your sweetheart—Fortune, I mean—was patient. She said, ‘He’s young. I’ll forgive him.’ She waited, like Patience on a monument, until the hunt was done and the prey caught. She expected you’d come back, a good lad, and claim your reward. You might still have had her.”

“It stunned me—stunned me completely—to find that instead of riding home at breakneck speed, laying your laurels at her feet, you calmly took a coach to London. What you did there, only Satan knows. Probably just sat and sulked. Your face was never pale, but now it’s green with regret. You’re not half the man you used to be.”

“And who gets this prize you keep talking about?”

“Only a baronet—that’s all. I’m certain you’ve lost her. She’ll be Lady Nunnely by Christmas.”

“Hmm. Quite possible.”

“But she needn’t have been. Foolish boy! I swear you could’ve had her.”

“By what sign, Mr. Yorke?”

“Every sign—by the fire in her eyes, the flush on her cheeks. They turned red when your name came up, even though usually they’re pale.”

“My chance is gone, I suppose?”

“It should be. But try. It’s worth trying. I call Sir Philip weak tea. And he writes poetry, they say—trite rhymes. _You_ are above that, Robert, at least.”

“Would you advise me to propose, so late—this close to the end?”

“You can but try, Robert. If she cared for you—if, by my soul, I believe she did or once did—she’ll forgive much. But you’re laughing. At me? Better laugh at your own stubbornness. I see you’re grinning on the wrong side of your mouth. You look sourer than I’d wish to see anyone.”

“I’ve quarrelled with myself, Yorke. I’ve kicked against the pricks, struggled in a tight waistcoat, twisted my wrists in handcuffs, battered my head against a harder wall.”

“Ha! Good for you. Sharp exercise. I hope it’s helped—taken some of the arrogance out of you?”

“Arrogance? What is it? Self-respect? Even self-tolerance? Do you sell those? Know anyone who does? Point me to a shop. I’d buy them all—give my last guinea right now.”

“So it is with you, Robert? That’s spicy. I like honesty. What’s wrong?”

“The whole machine of my nature—every gear in this human mill. The boiler—my heart—is ready to burst.”

“That ought to be printed. It’s poetic. You’re almost writing verse already. If inspiration strikes, let it flow, Robert. Don’t mind me—I’ll bear it this time.”

“Hideous, terrible mistake! You can do something in a moment that you’ll regret for years—something life can’t erase.”

“Go on, lad. I call it pie, nuts, sugar-candy. I love the taste. Go on. Talking will help. The moor lies ahead, and we’re alone for miles.”

“I *will* talk. I’m not ashamed. There’s a wildcat in my chest, and I want you to hear its roar.”

“To me, it’s music. You and Louis both have powerful voices. When Louis sings—soft, deep tones like a bell—I’ve felt my bones tremble. The night is still. It listens. It leans toward you, like a black priest to a darker penitent. Confess, lad. Don’t hide anything. Be as honest as a Methody preacher at an experience meeting. Make yourself as wicked as Beelzebub. It’ll ease your mind.”

“As mean as Mammon, you’d say. Yorke, if I dismounted and laid myself across the road, would you ride over me—back and forth—about twenty times?”

“With the greatest pleasure—if there were no coroner’s inquest.”

“Hiram Yorke, I truly believed she loved me. I’ve seen her eyes spark with joy when she spotted me in a crowd. Her cheeks flushed crimson when she offered me her hand and said, ‘How do you do, Mr. Moore?’”

“My name had magic for her. When others said it, she changed color. I know she did. She said it herself, in the most musical tone of all her many voices. She was warm to me, interested, concerned, wished me well. She sought every chance to help me. I watched, studied, pondered. I could draw only one conclusion—this was love.”

“I looked at her, Yorke. Youthful, strikingly beautiful. Powerful. Her wealth meant redemption—for my honor, my standing. I owed her gratitude. She’d lent me five thousand pounds—substantial help. Could I forget that? Could I believe she loved me? Could I listen to reason urging me to marry her, and ignore every advantage, dismiss every flattering thought, reject every sound advice, and walk away? Young, graceful, generous—my benefactress, devoted to me, enamored of me. I used to say that to myself, repeat the words, swell with secret pride, admire myself entirely, unshaken even by respect for her. I smiled in private at her naïveté—her simplicity in loving first, in showing it.”

“That whip of yours looks heavy, Yorke. Swing it around your head. Knock me off my horse if you like. I’d welcome a good thrashing.”

“Hold on, Robert. Wait till the moon rises and I can see you. Speak plainly—did you love her or not? I want to know. I’m curious.”

“Sir—sir—I’ll say—she’s very pretty, in her way. Very captivating. At times, she seems made of fire and air—something I stand and marvel at, without wanting to hold her or kiss her. I felt her pull—strong on my interest, my vanity. But I never felt as if nature meant her to be my other half. When the question came, I pushed it aside. I told myself brutally: I’d be rich with her, ruined without her. I vowed to be practical—not romantic.”

“A sensible decision. What happened, Bob?”

“With that resolve, I went to Fieldhead one night last August. The very eve of my departure for Birmingham. I wanted to secure Fortune’s prize. I’d sent a note requesting a private meeting. I found her at home, alone.”

“She greeted me without hesitation—she thought I was there on business. I was nervous, but determined. I don’t remember how I managed it, but I pressed forward, cold and firm—frightening, I suppose. I offered myself—my person, my debts included—as a settlement.”

“It angered me—set my blood boiling—to see she didn’t blush, tremble, or lower her eyes. She replied, ‘I doubt whether I’ve understood you, Mr. Moore.’”

“I had to repeat the whole proposal twice, spell it out like ABC, before she grasped it. Then—what did she do? Instead of saying Yes with a soft voice, or keeping silent in confusion (which would’ve been sweet), she jumped up, walked twice quickly across the room—just the way *she* does, no one else—and gasped, ‘God bless me!’”

“Yorke, I stood by the fireplace, leaning against the mantel, bracing myself for anything. I knew my fate. I knew myself. Her expression, her voice—no misunderstanding. She stopped and looked at me.”

“‘God bless me!’ she repeated, in that shocked, indignant, yet sorrowful tone. ‘You’ve made a strange proposal—strange from *you*. And if you knew how strangely you said it, how you looked, you’d be horrified at yourself. You spoke like a brigand demanding my purse, not like a lover asking for my heart.’”

“A strange sentence, wasn’t it, Yorke? And as she said it, I knew it was true—strangely true. Her words were a mirror. I saw myself.”

“I stared at her, speechless, fierce. She enraged and shamed me.”

“‘Gérard Moore,’ she said, ‘you know you don’t love Shirley Keeldar.’”

“I could have lied—sworn I did—but I couldn’t lie in her clear face. I couldn’t perjure myself in her truth. Besides, such false oaths would’ve been meaningless. She wouldn’t have believed me any more than she’d believe Judas’s ghost if he rose from the night. Her heart sensed deeper than deception—she knew my admiration was half-coarse, half-cold, not true, throbbing love.”

“What happened next?” Yorke asked.

“She sat down in the window seat and wept. Passionately. Tears fell like rain, but also flashed—bright, dark, proud eyes fixed on me. They said, ‘You’ve hurt me. You’ve insulted me. You’ve deceived me.’”

“She spoke soon after.”

“‘I *did* respect you—I *did* admire you—I *did* like you,’ she said—‘yes, as much as if you were my brother. But *you—you* want to make a bargain of me. You’d sacrifice me to that mill, your Moloch!’”

“I stayed silent. I had the sense not to defend myself. I let her scorn me.”

“I was sold to the devil, for a time. I was infatuated. When I did speak, what do you think I said?”

“‘Whatever my feelings, I was convinced *you* loved *me*, Miss Keeldar.’”

“Beautiful, wasn’t it? She sat stunned. ‘Is it Robert Moore speaking?’ I heard her mutter. ‘Is it a man—or something lower?’”

“‘Do you mean,’ she asked aloud, ‘do you mean you thought I loved you as we love those we wish to marry?’”

“It *was* my meaning. I said so.”

“‘You held an idea offensive to a woman’s feelings,’ she answered. ‘You’ve expressed it in a way revolting to a woman’s soul. You suggest that every kind word I’ve given you was part of a bold, immodest scheme to trap a husband. You imply you come here now out of pity, offering your hand because I’ve pursued you. Let me say this: Your vision is jaundiced; you’ve seen wrongly. Your mind is twisted; you’ve judged wrongly. Your tongue betrays you; you now speak falsely. I never loved you. Be at peace on that. My heart is as pure of passion for you as yours is empty of affection for me.’”

“Did I get my answer, Yorke?”

“‘I seem to be a blind, foolish man,’ I said.”

“‘*Loved* you!’ she cried. ‘Why, I’ve been as open with you as a sister—never avoided you, never feared you. You cannot,’ she declared triumphantly, ‘make me tremble at your presence, nor quicken my pulse by your nearness.’”

“I argued that often, when she spoke to me, she blushed, and the sound of my name moved her.”

“‘Not for *your* sake!’ she said sharply. I pressed for explanation, but got none.”

“‘When I sat beside you at the school feast, did you think I loved you then? When I stopped you in Maythorn Lane, did you think I loved you then? When I visited you in the counting-house, walked with you on the pavement—did you think I loved you then?’”

“She questioned me. I said I did.”

“By God, Yorke, she rose—tall, radiant, almost glowing. A trembling ran through her, like live coal at its hottest.”

“‘That means you hold the worst opinion of me,’ she said. ‘You deny me everything I value. You accuse me of betraying all my sisters. You say I’ve acted as no woman can without dishonoring herself and her kind. You say I’ve sought where the pure of my sex naturally scorn and abhor to seek.’”

“We were silent for minutes. Then she continued: ‘Lucifer, Star of the Morning, thou art fallen! Once high in my esteem, you are cast down. Once close in friendship, you are cast out. Go!’”

“I didn’t go. I heard her voice shake, saw her lip quiver. I knew another storm of tears would come—and after that, calm. I waited.”

“The tears fell again—warm, fast, quieter than before. A softer sound mingled with them—regretful, sorrowful. I watched as her eyes lifted to me—more reproachful than proud, more mournful than angry.”

“‘O Moore!’ she said. Worse than ‘Et tu, Brute!’”

“I let out what should’ve been a sigh—but it became a groan. A feeling of Cain-like loneliness ached in my chest.”

“‘There has been error in what I’ve done,’ I said. ‘And it has earned me bitter wages. I’ll go and spend them far from her who gave them.’”

“I took my hat. I couldn’t bear to leave like that. I thought she wouldn’t let me go. And she wouldn’t—except for the wound I’d dealt to her pride, which silenced her compassion.”

“I had to turn back on my own, approach her, and say, ‘Forgive me.’”

“‘I could, if I weren’t also forgiving myself,’ she replied. ‘But to mislead a wise man so deeply, I must have erred.’”

“I burst out with some impassioned words I don’t recall. I know they were sincere—my aim was to absolve her in her own eyes. In truth, self-accusation was impossible in her case.”

“At last, she reached out her hand. For the first time, I wanted to hold her, to embrace her. I kissed her hand—many times.”

“‘One day,’ she said, ‘we may be friends again. When you’ve had time to understand my actions and motives rightly, not horribly misread them. Time may give you the key. Then, perhaps, you’ll understand me. Then we may be reconciled.’”

“Farewell tears rolled slowly down her cheeks. She wiped them away.”

“‘I am sorry,’ she sobbed. ‘Deeply sorry.’ So was I, God knows. And so we parted.”

“A strange tale,” said Mr. Yorke.

“I’ll never do it again,” Moore vowed. “Never will I mention marriage to a woman unless I feel love. From now on, credit and commerce can fend for themselves. Bankruptcy may come when it will. I’m done with fear of ruin. I’ll work hard, wait patiently, bear steadily. If the worst comes, I’ll take my axe and an emigrant’s passage, and go west with Louis—we’ve decided. No woman shall ever look at me as Miss Keeldar did, or feel toward me as she did. I’ll never again stand before a woman as both fool and knave, brute and pup.”

“Tut!” said Yorke, unmoved. “You’re making too much of it. Still—I’m impressed. First, that she didn’t love you. Second, that you didn’t love her. You’re both young, both handsome, both capable of wit and temper—if you’re seen properly. What went wrong that you couldn’t agree?”

“We never *have* been, never *could* be at ease with each other, Yorke. We admired each other from afar, but clashed up close. I’ve sat at one end of a room, watching her at the other—perhaps in a joyful moment, surrounded by her favorites—men like you and Helstone, with whom she’s playful, bright, eloquent. I’ve watched her when she was most natural, most lively, most lovely. My judgment said she was beautiful—beautiful when her mood and clothes matched her splendor. I’ve drawn closer, thinking our acquaintance gave me the right. I’ve joined her circle, caught her eye, held her attention. Others have withdrawn, leaving us alone. Were we happy then? For me, no. Always a tension. Always a tendency toward stiffness, distance. We talked politics, business. Never a soft intimacy, never warmth between us. Our confidences were of the counting-house, not the heart. She never made me better, gentler. She only sharpened my mind, heightened my wit. She never entered my heart, never touched its rhythm. And why? Because I never learned how to make her love me.”

“Well, lad, it’s a queer thing. I might laugh at you, mock your sensitivities. But since it’s dark and we’re alone, I’ll tell you—your words bring back a glimpse of my own past. Twenty-five years ago, I tried to win a beautiful woman’s love. She refused me. I couldn’t unlock her heart. She was a stone wall—doorless, windowless.”

“But you *loved* her, Yorke. You worshipped Mary Cave. Your behavior was that of a man—never a fortune-hunter.”

“Aye, I *did* love her. But she was as beautiful as the moon we don’t see tonight. Nothing like her exists now. Perhaps Miss Helstone has a hint of her—quiet, delicate—but no one else.”

“Who looks like her?”

“That black-coated tyrant’s niece—Miss Helstone. I’ve often put on my spectacles in church to watch her. Gentle blue eyes, long lashes. When she sits in shadow, pale, still, nearly asleep from the sermon’s length and the heat of the building—she looks like one of Canova’s marbles.”

“Was Mary Cave like that?”

“Far grander—less girl-like, less flesh-like. You wondered why she didn’t have wings and a crown. She was a stately, peaceful angel—my Mary.”

“And you couldn’t win her love?”

“Not with all I could do. I prayed Heaven, on my knees, many times, to help me.”

“Mary Cave wasn’t what you think, Yorke. I’ve seen her portrait at the rectory. She’s no angel. Just a fair, regular-faced, quiet woman—too white, too lifeless for my taste. But suppose she had been better than she was…”
