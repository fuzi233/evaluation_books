And Gwendolen? Her thoughts looped back to Daniel Deronda far more often than his ever drifted to her. She spent hours daydreaming what he made of the world, what quiet, secret parts of his life he never showed anyone else. To her, gilded and sheltered as she was, trying to guess at the shape of his days was like a lap-dog attempting to map the whole wide wild world of canines beyond its drawing room cushion: she could no more imagine Deronda’s quiet, lifelong tie to his people’s ancient historical destiny than she could picture him climbing onto a brass flying horse and vanishing over the treeline to turn into a twinkling star.

For all the moments of shame and inferiority he’d stirred in her, it never crossed her mind that she occupied only a small, far corner of his thoughts. Only the very old and very wise learn to stop assuming their own sharp, thrumming anxieties and joys are mirrored exactly in the minds of others—and Gwendolen, young, lonely, and used to being the center of every room she walked into, could not be blamed for clinging to every small sign of kindness he’d shown her, the first person who had ever made her want to be softer, better, than the sharp, spoiled girl she’d always been.
She kept turning his words over in her head: *Find more interest in other people. Read more. Care for things that matter.* But how was she ever supposed to start? She wondered what books he would tell her to bring up to her room, smirking to herself half the time at the stack of heavy, famous classics she’d dragged home—Descartes, Bacon, Locke, all those writers everyone said were geniuses, that she’d always found unbearably tedious—wishing she could tease him and ask if they were really just bitter medicine to force your mind to be good. Then she’d repent of her sauciness, and tuck the books onto her shelf, convinced if she skimmed them fast enough with her quick wits, she might scramble up to stand on the same level he occupied, just a little.

But she barely had a spare minute for those grand, heavy mental journeys. Every waking hour she was on stage, performing the part of Mrs. Grandcourt, and her husband’s exacting, cold eyes watched every move she made. He’d set his sharp, stubborn will to making their marriage deliver every single thing he wanted from it—and the more he sensed the quiet streak of rebellion thrumming under her perfect, polite mask, the harder he pushed to break it. No part of her wanted to fail at the performance, though. She could not bear to let the world see a single crack, a single hint that her grand, lavish marriage was not the triumph everyone assumed it was. The one person she could let all that bottled-up pressure spill out to, the one person she dared show how broken she was, was Deronda—her secret, quiet anchor, the only hand she could imagine pulling her back from the edge of herself when she was one breath away from screaming or breaking something.

All around Diplow, those early weeks of the new year, everyone agreed Mrs. Grandcourt wore her new rank like a queen. She hunted, rode, paid calls and hosted visitors with such effortless, cool poise that Mrs. Arrowpoint was heard to sniff, “She carries it like she condescended to marry him, not the other way up. I always knew there was something double-faced about her.”

Most of all, Gwendolen was determined to convince her own mother she was perfectly happy. Poor Mrs. Davilow was fooled enough that she blamed the unexpected distance between them on Gwendolen’s new married life, not Grandcourt’s icy dismissiveness. All she was allowed was an occasional invitation to lunch with the Gascoignes, a ride home at dawn the next morning, and rare brief calls where Gwendolen could only spend five minutes with her before Grandcourt called her back to their carriage or his horse, waiting impatiently outside.
The second time Gwendolen suggested inviting her mother and her aunt and uncle to stay for a week, Grandcourt had stayed silent for long enough to make her stomach twist, then drawled, “We can’t have those people underfoot all the time. Gascoigne talks too much. All country clergy are bores, with their confounded fuss over everything.”
Those words hung over Gwendolen like a bad omen. She couldn’t risk bringing her mother any closer, not when Grandcourt looked at her whole family like they were annoying pests, and she certainly couldn’t tell her mother the truth: that if she saw too much of her married life, she would immediately realize how miserable Gwendolen was. So she waved off her mother’s hints about renting a small house near Ryelands, telling her it would be far nicer for her to stay close to the rectory, that they would probably be travelling away for most of the year anyway.
Strangely, the more Grandcourt tried to shut her family out, the fonder Gwendolen grew of them. She found herself looking back on her uncle’s bumbling cheer, his silly over-eager efforts to keep the household running smoothly, even when he got things wrong, as a far warmer comfort than the cold, perfect neutral grandeur of Ryelands that chilled her a little more every single day. It was the strangest sort of growth, far more real than anything she could glean from skimming those heavy philosophy books that demanded she forget all her messy, roiling daily anxieties just to make sense of a single paragraph.

One bright day, to the whole family’s shock and delight, Gwendolen cantered up to Offendene alone, no husband beside her, only a trailing groom. Everyone at the lunch table, her four little sisters included, stared out the dining room window at her, radiant on her prancing chestnut horse that held its head like it was proud to carry her, half-convinced she was some storybook happy heroine, Harriet Byron or Miss Wardour, come back to visit the quiet home she’d left behind. Her uncle hurried to the door to help her dismount, and she sprang off the saddle with such bright, easy energy she could have convinced anyone she was perfectly, deliriously happy. She was determined to set her mother’s mind at ease that day, even for an hour, the rare taste of freedom riding over alone making her feel strong enough to mask all the pain gnawing at her. The seven family kisses they greeted her with didn’t even feel tedious, for once.

“Henleigh’s out riding, so I decided to slip away and come see you, mamma,” she said, dropping her hat on the table and plopping down next to her mother, teasing her playfully, “And I’ve come to scold you for wearing that terrible shoddy lace on your head. You never thought I’d turn up and catch you being so careless with yourself, did you?” She brushed a soft hand over her mother’s hair.
“Scold all you like, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, her worn, delicate face flushing pink with pure joy. “I wish we had something proper for you to eat after that ride, not these scraps. Jocosa will make you a cup of chocolate, the way you used to love it as a girl.”
Gwendolen tried to protest, but Miss Merry was already hurrying off to make it. “Oh, don’t bother, a biscuit is fine. I haven’t come to eat, I’ve come to say goodbye.”
“Goodbye? Are you going back to Ryelands already?” Mr. Gascoigne asked.
“No, we’re off to London,” Gwendolen said, picking at a piece of bread without taking a single bite.
“Rather early for London, isn’t it? Henleigh’s not in Parliament,” Mrs. Gascoigne said.
“There’s only one last hunt left this season, and he’s got some lawyer business to take care of in town,” Gwendolen lied easily. “I’m glad to go. There’s so much to see there.”
“You’ll get to see your new house in Grosvenor Square!” Mrs. Davilow said, her eyes shining, the girls all staring at Gwendolen like she was a goddess about to vanish back to a world of unimaginable luxury.
“You know what I hope, my dear Gwendolen?” her uncle said, leaning forward with earnest, paternal good intention. “I hope you use your influence with Henleigh to push him to run for Parliament. A man of his position owes it to the country to make his weight felt. Everyone says the government is going to call a new election over the Reform Bill soon, and he should be ready for it. Wives have so much quiet influence over their husbands, after all. Use yours.”
Gwendolen had to bite her tongue so hard to not laugh she almost tasted blood. Once, not long ago, she’d believed that too—believed she could wrap any man around her little finger, bend him to whatever will she wanted. Now she knew exactly how much influence she had, zero, in the grand scheme of things. “I’d be happy to try, uncle,” she said, “But I don’t think Henleigh would ever want to make speeches at election rallies.”
She jumped at the sight of Jocosa coming back with her chocolate, escaping having to promise anything she could never deliver. The good rector, for all his earnestness, never for a second imagined that Grandcourt would laugh in her face if she ever dared bring up the idea of running for office. Even Mrs. Gascoigne, for all her quiet irritation at Grandcourt’s haughty treatment of her husband, wouldn’t let herself believe the couple was anything less than perfectly happy.
They gushed about their son Rex, who was on track to graduate with top honors and get a fellowship, and how they planned to meet him in London at Easter, how Lord Brackenshaw had already invited them to stay at the Castle. Gwendolen told them they were more than welcome to visit her in Grosvenor Square, though privately she hoped she would never have to see any of her family under Grandcourt’s roof for the rest of her life.
Then her mother told her about little Mr. Jodson’s death, the tiny white cottage hidden by trees down the lane to the church, that would be free for them to move into, small and perfect, only a mile from the rectory. Gwendolen’s throat tightened. “You have no furniture for it, mamma,” she said, melancholy seeping into her voice.
“Oh, I’ve got money for that,” Mrs. Davilow said, laying a soft hand over hers. “You know who made me comfortable, dear.”
Gwendolen’s heart swelled so much she thought she might cry. “Come on, let’s go up to the bedroom and fix my hat before I leave, mamma,” she said, fumbling at her hair like a stray pin had come loose. As soon as the door to their old shared bedroom clicked shut behind them, she sank into the chair in front of the mirror, pale and breathless, holding back tears so hard a little groan escaped her like she was in physical pain.
“Are you unwell, darling?” Mrs. Davilow asked, alarmed.
“Just the chocolate made my stomach turn,” Gwendolen said, letting her mother take her hand and press it to her chest.
“I would come nurse you, if you were ill, wouldn’t I?” Mrs. Davilow said softly, and for the first time she was sure Gwendolen still needed her, more than ever.
“Of course you would,” Gwendolen said, leaning her head on her mother’s shoulder, forcing a smile. “But I never get ill. I’m as strong as a horse. Don’t you ever fret over me, alright? Be happy with the girls. They’re far better daughters to you than I ever was.”
“Nonsense, you’ve always been perfect,” Mrs. Davilow said.
Gwendolen laughed, forced and bright, jumping to her feet to grab her hat. “The only good thing I ever did for you was marry Henleigh Grandcourt, and I only did it because I wanted to, no other reason. You’ve got to be half happy now, alright? That’s an order.” She pulled a stack of thirty pounds’ worth of notes out of her pocket and pressed them into her mother’s hand. “Henleigh gives me piles of money I never know what to do with, and I don’t want to waste it on boring charity. Make the girls buy whatever silly, pretty little things they want for the new cottage, alright?” She hurried out before her mother could protest, the emotion bubbling over too hot to hold.
She said all her goodbyes with perfect, easy poise, and rode away from Offendene telling herself sarcastically, *I think I’m making a very fine Mrs. Grandcourt, after all.*
She knew perfectly well where Henleigh had gone that day, not to any hunting meet or any London lawyer. He’d gone to Gadsmere, that run-down ugly little house in the dark mining country, the “dog hutch” he’d once joked about, where Mrs. Glasher and his secret children lived. The thought burned her like a brand on her skin. She’d married him knowing all of it, to save her family from ruin, and now she was trapped. She couldn’t say a single word to anyone, couldn’t make a single move that would blow the secret wide open and turn her into a spectacle for every gossip in the county to tear apart. The only other person who knew the truth besides her and Mrs. Glasher was Deronda, the only person she dared confide in, the only person who might ever understand.
For his part, Grandcourt never fully guessed exactly how sharp and wild Gwendolen’s quiet rebellion was—but he didn’t need to. He had an instinct for every flicker of resistance in her, every little swallow of pride she had to force down, and it delighted him. Taming that bright, stubborn, untouchable girl had become his favorite game. He could not imagine any of the soft, shadowy, tender parts of her that loved her mother, that longed for Deronda, that ached to be free—but every time he won a small victory over her will, it only made him more satisfied that his prize, his perfect new wife, was exactly where he wanted her.
