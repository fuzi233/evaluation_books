Lady Clavering—our Begum, whose good heart had been stretched and stitched and stretched again like an old kitchen apron—began by declaring she would tie no more knots in the leaking money-bag of her husband’s honour. The last Derby business, that famous harvest of ruin, had brought home to her, as plainly as a scorched taste in the mouth, what manner of man Sir Francis was: not merely weak, but cunningly weak; not merely foolish, but industriously so. She vowed she would separate; she would keep what little of her fortune remained from being skimmed off like fat by a pack of gentlemen who lived on wagers and wine.

Sir Francis, after that fatal day’s transactions, was in the condition of a man who has swallowed an oyster-shell and insists it was a bonbon. He avoided everybody—his turf friends, because he owed them; his wife, because she had eyes and could count. When Lady Clavering asked next morning if he was in the house, the answer came back like a cold plate: he had not returned. He had sent a messenger to his valet, ordering clothes and letters to be forwarded.

Captain Strong—Chevalier Strong, as they called him, as if a title could season a character—knew the pattern of his principal’s cowardice. Sir Francis, when fortune tapped him on the shoulder, always began by bolting out the back door and hiding in the nearest hole that would take an “Mr. Francis.” And sure enough, a note arrived: Strong was implored to call at Short Hotel, Blackfriars, and ask for Mr. Francis there.

But London, like a well-trained kitchen, runs on whispers and the clatter of small errands. The boots at Mr. Short’s carried the message to Grosvenor Place and returned with a carpet-bag; the boots knew at once whose bag it was, in the same way a cook knows whose appetite has been in the pantry. He told the footman; the footman told the servants’ hall; the servants’ hall told Mrs. Bonner; and Mrs. Bonner told my lady. Thus, before dinner, every soul under that roof knew that Sir Francis Clavering, Baronet, was skulking at an inn on the Blackfriars Road, under a name as thin as watered broth. Sir Francis’s coachman, with that particular delight coachmen have in other men’s disasters, carried the news outward; it travelled by stables and street-corners to other households, and to Tattersall’s, where they began already to pack him off in conversation to the Levant, as if disgrace were a steamship.

Meanwhile the hall-table at Grosvenor Place, that altar of genteel correspondence, received offerings. Letters arrived in shoals, like fish drawn by a smell. Bills, too: the French cook’s account (which, had it been paid, might have perfumed a dozen dinners); tradesmen’s statements for meats and wines, and those other necessities of existence called ribbons and trimmings—Messrs. Finer and Gimcrack, and Madame Crinoline, who could make a bonnet look like a moral ruin. And in the same basket, like a private spice among public provisions, came Miss Amory’s personal little reckonings—no small pinch, those, but a liberal handful.

The afternoon after the Derby, Strong—having first visited his distracted friend at Short’s and found him performing tears into curacoa, like a child sweetening medicine—went, as was his custom, to Grosvenor Place to transact business. In Sir Francis’s study he found the documents laid out in rows: little white napkins of paper each covering some morsel of debt. Strong opened and examined them with a face fit for bad news and thin soup.

Mrs. Bonner descended upon him in the midst of it—Mrs. Bonner, her ladyship’s housekeeper and confidential maid, a fixture of the household like the tea-urn, and as necessary to her mistress as Strong was to Sir Francis. She was, by duty, even angrier than Lady Clavering; a servant’s indignation is often the family’s indignation boiled down to a sharper gravy.

“She won’t pay, if she takes my advice,” Mrs. Bonner said, with the decision of one who has salted many a joint and knows when enough is enough. “You’ll please to go back to Sir Francis, Captain—and he lurking about in a low public-house and don’t dare to face his wife like a man!—and say that we won’t pay his debts no longer. We made a man of him, we took him out of gaol (and other folks too perhaps), we’ve paid his debts over and over again—we set him up in Parliament and gave him a house in town and country, and where he don’t dare show his face, the shabby sneak! We’ve given him the horse he rides and the dinner he eats and the very clothes he has on his back; and we will give him no more. Our fortune, such as is left of it, is left to ourselves, and we won’t waste any more of it on this ungrateful man. We’ll give him enough to live upon and leave him, that’s what we’ll do: and that’s what you may tell him from Susan Bonner.”

As she spoke, you could almost hear the house itself agreeing: the muffled hush of carpets, the far clink of china, the restrained cough of a clock. A great London house is an instrument; when the master plays false, every room knows the discord.

Lady Clavering, hearing Strong had arrived, sent for him at this moment. The Chevalier went up with a faint hope—he had often coaxed her good nature back into service, as one coaxes a sauce that has begun to separate, warming and stirring and promising it will come together again. He tried once more. He painted in dismal colours the condition in which he had found Sir Francis: crying, drinking, despairing; and he would not answer for consequences if means could not be found to meet the engagements.

“Kill hisself,” laughed Mrs. Bonner, as if the notion were a joke told badly. “Kill hisself, will he? Dying’s the best thing he could do.”

Strong swore he had found razors on the table. At that, Lady Clavering laughed too—bitterly, as if she tasted something burnt and would not spit it out.

“He’ll do himself no harm, as long as there’s a shilling left of which he can rob a poor woman. His life’s quite safe, Captain: you may depend upon that. Ah! it was a bad day that ever I set eyes on him.”

“He’s worse than the first man,” cried Mrs. Bonner, remembering some older misery like an old recipe gone wrong but still remembered by the tongue. “He was a man, he was—a wild devil, but he had the courage of a man—whereas this fellow—what’s the use of my lady paying his bills, and selling her diamonds, and forgiving him? He’ll be as bad again next year. The very next chance he has he’ll be a-cheating of her, and robbing of her; and her money will go to keep a pack of rogues and swindlers—I don’t mean you, Captain—you’ve been a good friend to us enough, bating we wish we’d never set eyes on you.”

That slip—diamonds—fell into Strong’s ear like the faint chink of a key. He saw that Lady Clavering’s heart, bruised though it was, still retained its terrible elasticity. She was disposed to relent, at least once more; and where there is relenting, there is money.

He leaned in with real sympathy—he could feel for her, and admire her, even while serving her husband’s squalid interests. “Upon my word, ma’am,” he said, “anything you say against Clavering, or Mrs. Bonner here cries out against me, is no better than we deserve, both of us; and it was an unlucky day for you when you saw either. He has behaved cruelly to you; and if you were not the most generous and forgiving woman in the world, I know there would be no chance for him. But you can’t let the father of your son be a disgraced man, and send little Frank into the world with such a stain upon him. Tie him down; bind him by any promises you like: I vouch for him that he will subscribe them.”

“And break ’em,” said Mrs. Bonner, as promptly as she would have said “and scorch it” of a careless cook.

“And keep ’em this time,” Strong insisted, with a heat that sounded like sincerity and was partly made of it. “He must keep them. If you could have seen how he wept, ma’am! ‘Oh, Strong,’ he said to me, ‘it’s not for myself I feel now: it’s for my boy—it’s for the best woman in England, whom I have treated basely—I know I have.’ He didn’t intend to bet upon this race, ma’am—indeed he didn’t. He was cheated into it: all the ring was taken in. He thought he might make the bet quite safely, without the least risk. And it will be a lesson to him for all his life long. To see a man cry—oh, it’s dreadful.”

“He don’t think much of making my dear missus cry,” said Mrs. Bonner—“poor dear soul!—look if he does, Captain.”

When Strong carried the result of this kitchen-council of war back to his principal, he spoke more plainly than he had upstairs.

“If you’ve the soul of a man, Clavering,” he said, “you’ll keep your promise this time: and, so help me Heaven! if you break word with her, I’ll turn against you, and tell all.”

“What all?” cried Mr. Francis, at Short’s Hotel, where Strong found him again in the same posture of anguish—tears and curacoa, like sugar on sorrow.

“Psha! Do you suppose I am a fool?” Strong broke out. “Do you suppose I could have lived so long in the world, Frank Clavering, without having my eyes about me? You know I have but to speak and you are a beggar to-morrow. And I am not the only man who knows your secret.”

“Who else does?” gasped Clavering, turning a colour which in another man might have been shame, but in him was merely fear.

“Old Pendennis does, or I am very much mistaken. He recognised the man the first night he saw him, when he came drunk into your house.”

“He knows it, does he?” shrieked Clavering, the sound sharp enough to crack a glass. “Damn him—kill him.”

“You’d like to kill us all, wouldn’t you, old boy?” Strong said, sneering, and puffing his cigar as if moral reproof were best delivered through smoke.

Sir Francis flung his weak hand to his forehead. Perhaps the other had translated him too accurately. “Oh, Strong!” he cried, “if I dared, I’d put an end to myself, for I’m the d——est miserable dog in all England. It’s that that makes me so wild and reckless. It’s that which makes me take to drink”—and he drank, trembling, a bumper of his fortifier—“and to live about with these thieves. I know they’re thieves, every one of ’em, d——d thieves. And—and how can I help it?—and I didn’t know it, you know—and, by Gad, I’m innocent—and until I saw the d——d scoundrel first, I knew no more about it than the dead—and I’ll fly, and I’ll go abroad out of the reach of the confounded hells, and I’ll bury myself in a forest, by Gad! and hang myself up to a tree—and, oh—I’m the most miserable beggar in all England!”

And so he went on, seasoning his repentance with blasphemy, and his despair with oath after oath, like a cook who cannot leave off peppering a dish already ruined.

Yet the proverb held: an ill wind blows good to somebody. The day Sir Francis’s debts were settled, another tenant of Strong’s chambers in Shepherd’s Inn found himself, by lucky accident, “good” in a wager. Colonel Altamont had taken thirty to one in fifties against the winning horse; and when Captain Clinker paid Sir Francis’s creditors—Lady Clavering, by Major Pendennis’s advice, refusing to let the Baronet touch the business with his own fingers—Altamont received his odds with a satisfaction as audible as the snap of bank-notes.

Altamont’s friends swarmed round him at once, like flies round a fresh tart. His set, and the gentlemen of the convivial Wheeler at the Harlequin’s Head, came to congratulate and, with hearty generosity, to propose how they might spend his luck on their behalf. Tom Driver said now was the time to raise that specie ship sunk in the Gulf of Mexico with its three hundred and eighty thousand dollars and bars and doubloons. Mr. Keightley insinuated the Tredyddlums were low—never such a chance to buy shares for an old song. Jack Holt pressed a tobacco-smuggling scheme whose sheer audacity tickled the Colonel more than any sober honesty could have done. Then the Harlequin’s Head boys: Jack Rackstraw recommended a pair of horses; Tom Fleet begged two hundred pounds to float his satirical paper, The Swell, which would be worth a thousand a year to any man “with such a power and influence, Colonel, you rogue, and the entree of the green-rooms in London.” Little Moss Abrams, in a tone of injured virtue, implored him not to listen to these absurd fellows but to invest in some good bills that Moss could procure, returning fifty per cent “as safe as the Bank of England.”

They all made their pitches with the eager rhythm of a bad orchestra—voices overlapping, the scrape of chairs, the thump of hands on tables. It is one of the worst noises in the world: the sound of men hearing money and mistaking it for music.

But Altamont had courage enough to resist. He buttoned up his notes in his coat-pocket, went home to Strong, and “sported” the outer door. Strong had advised him about his acquaintances; and though, when pressed, he did not mind frankly taking twenty pounds himself out of the Colonel’s winnings, he was too upright to let the rest of the pack get their teeth in.

Altamont, in good fortune, was not an unkind fellow. He ordered a smart livery for Grady; he made poor old Costigan’s gratitude bubble up and spill over into tears that dried almost as soon as they came, by giving him a five-pound note after a snug dinner at the Back Kitchen—Irish stew thick with onions and pepper, a steam that clung to the clothes and made even poverty smell briefly like home. He bought a green shawl for Mrs. Bolton, and a yellow one for Fanny—two bright “sacrifices” from a Regent Street haberdasher’s window, as gaudy as sweetmeats in a jar.

Not long after, on Miss Amory’s birthday in June, a parcel arrived from “a friend”: an enormous brass-inlaid writing-desk, and in it a set of amethysts—great staring stones like the most hideous eyes ever looked upon—a musical snuff-box, and two Keepsakes of the year before last, and gown pieces of colours so astounding that the Sylphide laughed and wondered immoderately. It was known that about this time Altamont had bought cigars and French silks from some duffers in Fleet Street; and Strong found him in the open Auction Room in Cheapside investing in two desks, several pairs of richly plated candlesticks, a dinner epergne, and a bagatelle-board. The epergne remained at chambers and figured at their banquets, glittering in candlelight until Jack Holt said it looked as if it had been taken “in a bill.” Jack Holt knew.

The dinners at Shepherd’s Inn became pretty frequent, and Sir Francis Clavering condescended to partake of them constantly. His own house was shut up; the successor of Mirobolant, who had sent his bill in with such unseasonable promptitude, was dismissed by the indignant Lady Clavering; the luxuriance of the establishment was pruned as ruthlessly as an overgrown herb bed. One of the large footmen was cashiered; the other gave warning, not liking to serve without his mate, or in a family where “on’y one footman was kep’.” Economical reforms were practised with a severity that would have done credit to a hard winter. The Major as friend, Strong as advocate for the culprit, her lawyer, and the honest Begum herself executed them briskly.

After paying the Baronet’s debts—the settlement occasioning scandal enough to make the very street sound different under one’s boots—Lady Clavering quitted London for Tunbridge Wells in high dudgeon, refusing to see the reprobate husband whom nobody pitied. Clavering remained in London, patient enough, not eager to meet her just indignation. He sneaked in and out of the House of Commons, and from thence he and Captain Raff and Mr. Marker would go to billiards and cigars and sporting public-houses; or he might be seen lurking about Lincoln’s Inn and his lawyers’ offices, kept waiting for hours while clerks winked at each other over his bowed head.

No wonder he relished Shepherd’s Inn. There he was nowhere scorned; there he was chief guest—“Yes, Sir Francis,” “No, Sir Francis”—there he told his wretched jokes; there he quavered his dreary little French song after Strong had sung his jovial chorus and Costigan had piped Irish ditties. Such a jolly menage as Strong’s, with Grady’s Irish stew bubbling like a small domestic myth in the saucepan, and the Chevalier’s punch after dinner—hot lemon, sugar melting, the sharp fragrant sting of spirits rising with the steam—would have been welcome to many a better man than Clavering. The solitude of his own great house frightened him; there, attended only by an old woman and a valet who sneered, he heard too plainly the hollow sound of his life.

“Yes, dammit,” he said at Shepherd’s Inn, “that fellow of mine, I must turn him away, only I owe him two years’ wages, curse him, and can’t ask my lady. He brings me my tea cold of a morning, with a dem’d leaden teaspoon, and he says my lady’s sent all the plate to the banker’s because it ain’t safe.—Now ain’t it hard that she won’t trust me with a single teaspoon; ain’t it ungentlemanlike, Altamont? You know my lady’s of low birth—that is—I beg your pardon—hem—that is, it’s most cruel of her not to show more confidence in me. And the very servants begin to laugh—the damn scoundrels! I’ll break every bone in their great hulking bodies, curse ’em, I will.—They don’t answer my bell: and—and my man was at Vauxhall last night with one of my dress-shirts and my velvet waistcoat on, I know it was mine—the confounded impudent blackguard—and he went on dancing before my eyes confound him! I’m sure he’ll live to be hanged—he deserves to be hanged—all those infernal rascals of valets.”

Now he was very kind to Altamont. He listened to the Colonel’s loud stories—how he had worked his way home from New Zealand after a whaling expedition and had to slink on board at night to escape from their wives, by Jove; and how the poor devils paddled after the ship in canoes when she sailed; how he had been lost in the bush three months in New South Wales on a trading speculation; how he had seen Boney at Saint Helena and been presented to him with the rest of the officers of an Indiaman. Over cups Altamont told many such tales, and lied and bragged a great deal; Sir Francis listened with hungry attention, drinking wine with him and treating him with distinction.

“Leave him alone, I know what he’s a-coming to,” Altamont said, laughing to Strong, when Strong remonstrated. “And leave me alone; I know what I’m a-telling, very well. I was officer on board an Indiaman, so I was; I traded to New South Wales, so I did, in a ship of my own, and lost her. I became officer to the Nawaub, so I did; only me and my royal master have had a difference, Strong—that’s it. Who’s the better or the worse for what I tell? or knows anything about me? The other chap is dead—shot in the bush, and his body reckonised at Sydney. If I thought anybody would split, do you think I wouldn’t wring his neck? I’ve done as good before now, Strong—I told you how I did for the overseer before I took leave—but in fair fight, I mean—in fair fight; or, rayther, he had the best of it. He had his gun and bay’net, and I had only an axe. Fifty of ’em saw it—ay, and cheered me when I did it—and I’d do it again,—him, wouldn’t I? I ain’t afraid of anybody; and I’d have the life of the man who split upon me. That’s my maxim, and pass me the liquor.—You wouldn’t turn on a man. I know you. You’re an honest feller, and will stand by a feller, and have looked death in the face like a man. But as for that lily-livered sneak—that poor lyin’ swindlin’ cringin’ cur of a Clavering—who stands in my shoes—stands in my shoes, hang him! I’ll make him pull my boots off and clean ’em, I will. Ha, ha!”

He burst into a wild laugh. Strong rose and put away the brandy-bottle as if shutting a door against noise.

“You’re right, old boy,” Altamont said, still good-humoured. “You always keep your head cool, you do—and when I begin to talk too much—I say, when I begin to pitch, I authorise you, and order you, and command you, to put away the rum-bottle.”

“Take my counsel, Altamont,” Strong said gravely, “and mind how you deal with that man. Don’t make it too much his interest to get rid of you; or who knows what he may do?”

The very event Altamont had been awaiting with cynical relish came quickly. One day, Strong being absent on an errand for his principal, Sir Francis appeared at the chambers and found the envoy of the Nawaub alone. He began by abusing the world for being heartless; then his wife for being ungenerous; then Strong for being ungrateful. Hundreds of pounds, he said, had he given Ned Strong—been his friend for life and kept him out of gaol, by Jove—and now Ned was taking her ladyship’s side and abetting her infernal unkindness.

“They’ve entered into a conspiracy to keep me penniless, Altamont,” he whined. “They don’t give me as much pocket money as Frank has at school.”

“Why don’t you go down to Richmond and borrow of him, Clavering?” Altamont broke in with a savage laugh. “He wouldn’t see his poor old beggar of a father without pocket-money, would he?”

“I tell you, I’ve been obliged to humiliate myself cruelly,” Clavering said; and he produced pawn-tickets, as if they were a menu of shame. “Look here, sir—look here, at these pawn-tickets! Fancy a Member of Parliament and an old English Baronet, by Gad! obliged to put a drawing-room clock and a buhl inkstand up the spout; and a gold duck’s-head paper-holder, that I dare say cost my wife five pound, for which they’d only give me fifteen-and-six! Oh, it’s a humiliating thing, sir, poverty to a man of my habits; and it’s made me shed tears, sir,—tears; and that d——d valet of mine—curse him, I wish he was hanged!—he had the confounded impudence to threaten to tell my lady: as the things in my own house weren’t my own, to sell or to keep, or fling out of window if I chose—by Gad! the confounded scoundrel.”

“Cry a little; don’t mind cryin’ before me—it’ll relieve you, Clavering,” Altamont said. “Why, I say, old feller, what a happy feller I once thought you, and what a miserable son of a gun you really are!”

“It’s a shame that they treat me so, ain’t it?” Clavering went on—for though ordinarily silent and apathetic, about his own griefs he could whine like a kettle that will not boil. “And—and, by Gad, sir, I haven’t got the money to pay the very cab that’s waiting for me at the door; and the porteress, that Mrs. Bolton, lent me three shillin’s, and I don’t like to ask her for any more: and I asked that d——d old Costigan, the confounded old penniless Irish miscreant, and he hadn’t got a shillin’, the beggar; and Campion’s out of town, or else he’d do a little bill for me, I know he would.”

“I thought you swore on your honour to your wife that you wouldn’t put your name to paper,” Altamont said, puffing at his cigar.

“Why does she leave me without pocket-money, then? Damme, I must have money,” cried the Baronet. “Oh, Am——, oh, Altamont, I’m the most miserable beggar alive.”

“You’d like a chap to lend you a twenty-pound note, wouldn’t you now?” Altamont asked.

“If you would, I’d be grateful to you for ever—for ever, my dearest friend,” Clavering cried, leaning forward as if gratitude could be used as collateral.

“How much would you give? Will you give a fifty-pound bill, at six months, for half down and half in plate?” Altamont asked, with a grin that had more edge than mercy.

“Yes, I would, so help me——, and pay it on the day,” screamed Clavering. “I’ll make it payable at my banker’s: I’ll do anything you like.”

“Well, I was only chaffing you,” Altamont said. “I’ll give you twenty pound.”

“You said a pony,” Clavering interposed, greedy even in correction; “my dear fellow, you said a pony, and I’ll be eternally obliged to you; and I’ll not take it as a gift—only as a loan, and pay you back in six months. I take my oath, I will.”
