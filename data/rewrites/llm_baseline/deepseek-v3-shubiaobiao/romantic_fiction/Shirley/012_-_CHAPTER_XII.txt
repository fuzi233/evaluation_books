Shirley had meant what she said—she was glad of Caroline’s company. And so she sought it often, for Caroline herself was slow to seek new friendships, held back by the quiet belief that others would not want her, that she could not possibly amuse them. How could someone as bright and full of life as Shirley Keeldar, heiress of Fieldhead, ever truly find pleasure in her company?

Yet Shirley, for all her brilliance and spirit, was not immune to the need for kindred companionship. Though she had swiftly made the acquaintance of nearly every family in the neighborhood—exchanging easy words with the Misses Sykes, the Misses Pearson, and the stately Misses Wynne of Walden Hall—she confessed to Mrs. Pryor one morning that none among them stirred her heart.

"If I were truly Shirley Keeldar, Esquire," she mused, "lord of Briarfield Manor, there isn’t a lady in this parish or the next whom I’d be tempted to make Mrs. Keeldar."

Mrs. Pryor, ever composed, replied only, "My dear, you must not indulge in speaking of yourself as a gentleman. It is an odd habit, and those who do not know you might mistake it for affectation."

Shirley did not laugh. She respected Mrs. Pryor too deeply for that. Instead, she turned to the window, watching a bird perched on the cedar outside. Soon, she began to whistle—softly at first, then clearer, until the notes formed a melody.

"My dear!" Mrs. Pryor protested.

Shirley stopped at once. "Forgive me. I had resolved not to whistle before you."

"But where did you learn such a thing? You never did so before coming to Yorkshire."

"Oh, I picked it up long ago."

"From whom?"

"No one. I learned by listening. Though yesterday evening, as I walked up the lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that very tune beyond the hedge—and it reminded me."

"What gentleman?"

"There is only one gentleman here who isn’t gray-haired—Mr. Moore. My other favorites, Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, are fine old souls, but they belong to another generation."

Mrs. Pryor was silent.

"You do not like Mr. Helstone?" Shirley pressed.

"His office shields him from criticism."

"You always leave the room when he arrives."

"Shall you walk out this morning?" Mrs. Pryor deftly shifted the subject.

"Yes. I mean to go to the rectory and persuade Caroline to take some air. She needs a brisk walk over Nunnely Common."

"If you go that way, remind her to wrap well. The wind is fresh, and she seems delicate."

"I shall. Will you not join us?"

"No, my dear. I should only slow you down."

---

Shirley found Caroline easily persuaded. Once they were out on the quiet road, the vast sweep of Nunnely Common stretching before them, conversation flowed between them as naturally as the breeze.

"You like this place?" Shirley asked, gazing at the heath.

Caroline nodded. "It reminds me of moors I’ve read about—wild and endless."

"I’ve seen such moors," Shirley said. "Once, traveling near Scotland, we rode for hours through nothing but deep heath, the sky heavy and still. No sound but wild birds, no sight but sheep."

Caroline could picture it—the purple-black expanse beneath a brooding sky. "Did storms come?"

"Only distant thunder then, but by evening it broke in full fury."

They paused at the crest of the common, looking down into Nunnely Dale. The valley lay bathed in sunlight, the woods dark and ancient.

"Our England is beautiful," Shirley murmured. "And Yorkshire most of all."

"You’re Yorkshire-born?"

"Through and through. Generations of my blood lie in Briarfield churchyard."

Caroline offered her hand. "Then we are countrywomen."

Shirley clasped it solemnly. "Yes."

Caroline pointed toward the distant wood. "Nunnwood. Have you been?"

"Often."

"It’s like stepping into another time. The trees are ancient, the silence profound. There’s a hollow in the center—ruins of an old nunnery."

"Let us go," Shirley said suddenly. "Just the two of us. We’ll take books, sketching pencils, baskets of food. Spend the whole day there."

Caroline’s eyes lit. "I know every hidden glade, where strawberries grow wild, where the oldest oaks stand like guardians. I could guide you."

"You wouldn’t find me dull company?"

"Never. Who else could we bring who wouldn’t spoil it?"

Shirley laughed. "No one of our age—no lady, at least. And as for gentlemen..."

"The moment they join, it becomes something else entirely," Caroline finished.

"Exactly. If they’re the wrong sort, they irritate. If the right sort..." She hesitated.

"Then Nature herself seems to retreat," Caroline said softly. "We forget to watch the sky, the trees. The peace is gone."

"Our happiness depends much on ourselves, I think," Caroline reflected. "I’ve gone to Nunnwood in large parties and found it tedious. Gone alone and been perfectly content."

"Have you ever gone with your cousin—Robert Moore?"

"Once."

"What is he like as a companion?"

Caroline hesitated. "A cousin is different from a stranger."

"But if he were dull, it would be worse."

"He isn’t dull. Only... sometimes the company of clever men leaves its own ache. When you admire someone greatly, you wonder if you deserve to be near them at all."

Shirley scoffed. "I would never think so. If they are good, truly good, then they are the finest of creation. Worthy of reverence."

Caroline smiled. "You speak like someone already in love."

Shirley’s eyes sparkled. "With the idea, perhaps. But I’ll not marry unless I find one who proves himself above the common run—kind, constant, noble."

"Many would say such men don’t exist."

"Then I’ll stay unwed," Shirley declared. "I’ll not chain myself to someone who’ll grow weary of me. I’d rather keep my freedom."

Caroline sighed. "Sometimes I think we’d all be wiser to remain single."

"But then," Shirley countered, "we’d never know if the exception might be out there—the one who proves the rule wrong."

At the rectory gates, they parted, but not before Shirley impulsively kissed Caroline’s cheek. "We suit each other," she said. "I’ve never talked so freely with anyone before."

---

Mrs. Pryor, too, seemed eager to know Caroline better. She called at the rectory one afternoon, uncharacteristically flustered by the unfamiliar visit. Caroline found her trembling on the sofa, fanning herself as if overcome.

"Forgive me," Mrs. Pryor murmured. "The heat—the walk—"

Caroline gently helped her remove her bonnet and shawl, and soon Mrs. Pryor grew calm. They spoke then of books, of art—Mrs. Pryor proving an intelligent, measured conversationalist.

Her eyes drifted to the portraits on the wall. "That is a beautiful face," she remarked, gazing at the central painting.

"My aunt—Mrs. Helstone," Caroline said.

"Passive features," Mrs. Pryor observed. "Not a woman of spirit, I think."

"No. She was very quiet."

"A curious choice for your uncle, who enjoys lively company."

"He says he needs quiet at home."

Before leaving, Mrs. Pryor pressed Caroline to visit Fieldhead often. "You must be lonely here, with no female kin. If you ever need guidance in your studies, call on me."

Caroline, touched, saw her to the door, wondering at the unexpected warmth blooming between herself and these two women—Shirley, bright as the sun, and Mrs. Pryor, steady as the moon.
