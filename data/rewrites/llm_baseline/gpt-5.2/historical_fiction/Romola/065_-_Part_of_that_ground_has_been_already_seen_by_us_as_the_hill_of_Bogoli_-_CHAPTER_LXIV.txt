Tito’s coming to San Marco had been asked for and agreed to; no sudden knock in the night, no furtive entry as if he carried contraband. Fra Niccolò, the Prior’s secretary, met him and led him upward by the narrow spiral stair whose worn stone held the chill of centuries and the faint smell of damp wool and extinguished lamp-wick. Above, the long passages stretched, quiet as a cloistered stomach between meals: doors shut, cells emptied by the hour of relaxation, the house breathing in a hush broken only by sandals scuffing and a distant cough that fell and was swallowed.

Here and there Fra Angelico’s frescoes flashed on the whitewashed walls—Madonna and Child, crowns and lilies—so tender and sudden that they seemed like color seen after fasting, a sweetness on the tongue of the eye. But most of what the narrow windows admitted fell on bare things: a hard pallet, a crucifix, a shelf, a jug—objects plain as black bread and as necessary.

At the end of one corridor, beyond an antechamber where the Prior often wrote and received, a door stood half-open. The long blade of daylight from a high window cut across the floor and struck the back of a kneeling figure in the white Dominican tunic and scapulary. He might have been any friar, bending to confess trivial rebellions—an unchaste thought while chanting, a mean spark of pleasure at another man’s blotched illumination—any soul busy with the ordinary ladder of prayer and obedience.

But under that white cloth beat a consciousness that would have startled the most seasoned confessor. The man kneeling was Savonarola; and though Latin prayer moved on his lips, he was not praying. He had thrown himself down as one puts a hand to water when heat and dust burn—seeking calm by the accustomed act—yet the mind would not drink. While his palms pressed his face and his mouth shaped, “Cor mundum crea in me,” his inward ear was full of other sounds: the taunts of enemies, the coarse laughter of those who made a snare out of piety, the thunder of a Piazza waiting to be fed with spectacle.

Worse than his enemies were his friends. That morning he had felt anew how his own followers, as greedily as the Arrabbiati, urged the Trial by Fire. They wanted, and almost expected, that he—he himself—should at last take the challenge and draw down the long-promised miracle like rain on a thirsty harvest. Had he not said God would declare Himself at the fitting time? To plain Florentines hungry to settle party quarrels, no time could be more fitting than now. Fra Domenico’s ardor to walk through flame was a bright omen to them, a whiff of incense before the miracle; but Savonarola knew—felt like a knife at the roots of his tongue—that their secret longing for his own entry into the fire was not quieted by any reasons he gave for refusing.

And yet he could not satisfy them. He could not even fully satisfy himself.

He had not lied when he spoke of divine attestation; he could believe in a miracle that lay at a distance, veiled by the strong reasons his mind could marshal for its coming; he could believe more easily still in inward miracles—in prophetic heat, in sudden illuminations that struck his thought with a certainty like a bell-note. But this particular miracle—the passage of a human body through fire unhurt—pressed its details against his imagination until belief buckled. It was one thing to assent in doctrine; it was another to picture the stacked faggots, the heat shivering the air, the hair singeing, the flesh blistering. His nature was a fierce mixture: a sensibility that could flood an idea with ecstasy, and—alternating with it—a practical judgment sharpened to the hard edges of outward fact. Here the practical side was roused to cruelty by his physical quickness of nerve: he knew pain too well to let the mind treat it as a mere word.

Practical belief is not the same as professed opinion. A man may accept in theory that iron should not burn him and still draw back from it; the nerves argue faster than syllogisms. And with Savonarola, when the Trial by Fire was made immediate, he could not believe—could not bring himself, even for a moment, to believe—that any man would walk through those flames untouched. More bitter still: he could not believe that even if he resolved to offer himself, he would not shrink at the last step, when the heat struck the face like a door flung open into an oven.

But Florence did not love fine distinctions. It has always seemed to the common run of men a proof of vigor to find moral knots easy, and to judge conduct by blunt alternatives. What would seem plainer than that a man who spoke of a miracle, yet drew back from the test proposed, had spoken without belief? Were not Fra Domenico and Fra Mariano, and scores of Piagnoni, eager to enter the fire? If their courage was greater, was it not because their faith was greater?

Savonarola could not have explained himself even to friends who loved him, even if he had been able to set his own heart’s motions into clear speech. Naked feelings hasten to dress themselves in ready-made propositions. To tell the truth about what passes within, sincerity is not enough; one needs an understanding as merciless as a knife and as patient as a cook who keeps tasting until the seasoning is exact.

His lips moved; his ears no longer heard the Latin. Within him, arguments rose and arranged themselves as if for a tribunal.

“To appeal to Heaven by rashly taking a challenge set as a snare by ignoble foes would be to tempt God, and the appeal would not be answered. Let the Pope’s legate come; let the ambassadors of all the great powers come and pledge that a General Council, reform, the cleansing of the Church—let all this hang on the miracle, and I will enter the flames, trusting God will not withhold His seal from a work so vast. Until then I must not leap from the chariot because every loud braggart calls. Fra Domenico’s zeal may be a sign of vocation, a pledge that the miracle—”

But when he forced his mind close to the threatened scene in the Piazza, the imagination would not look on it from a safe distance. It felt it. The thought of fire ran along his nerves into his fingertips, and belief recoiled like skin from heat. The miracle could not be. No—the trial itself must be hindered; he was warranted in doing all he could to stop it.

And yet: the preparations might go on, the wood might be piled, the people might gather, the solemnities might be performed. He could no longer resist without bringing dishonor on himself—yes, and therefore, as he told himself with that swift sliding from “me” to “the cause of God,” dishonor on the work he led. But the Franciscan would not truly mean to enter; while he hung back there would be means to prevent Fra Domenico’s entrance. Or, if the worst came, Fra Domenico could carry the consecrated Host, the Mystery, in his hand; and with that, perhaps, the natural effects of fire might be stayed. Or—more likely—this demand would be refused, and refusal might be the final obstacle.

But such intentions could not be spoken aloud. Outwardly he must seem frank, waiting, trusting, as if his inward world matched his public posture. That cleft between reality and seeming was not the Christian simplicity he had hungered for in youth and preached as the fruit of divine life. Yet who, when cheeks burn and shouts ring, remembers the morning’s cool yearnings and knows he has not belied them?

“O God,” his mind cried, though his lips were now silent, “it is for the people—because they are blind—because their faith hangs on me. If I cast myself in ashes, who will lift the standard? Have I not been led by a way I knew not to the work that lies before me?”

The conflict did not end; it only shifted its weight. In place of the immediate dread, there rose again the intoxication of vast results—Europe listening, thrones moved, corruption judged. In that glow the sharpness of present danger blurred, and he remained kneeling, still as a statue, while within him the future sounded like distant trumpets.

Then the knock came—measured, expected.

Savonarola drew his mantle about him as was his custom when he received visitors, and stepped out from the inner cell. Whatever tempest had been beating inside, his face met the world with the command of one who is used to making others feel his will through speech. He looked at Tito with a self-possession that might have come from settled resolution rather than from wrestling.

Tito did not kneel. He made only a deep, perfectly calculated reverence—deference without abasement—which Savonarola received without blessing or sacerdotal phrase. He motioned Tito to sit, and spoke at once.

“Your business is something of weight, my son, that could not be conveyed through others?”

“Assuredly, father—else I should not have presumed to ask it. I will not waste your time with a proem. Messer Domenico Mazzinghi let fall a remark from which I gathered you might wish to use the next special courier the Ten send to France. I entreat you to pardon my officiousness; but Messer Domenico is away at his villa, and a courier carrying important letters is to depart for Lyons at daybreak to-morrow.”

Savonarola’s face, trained to obedience like a disciplined troop, did not alter in any gross way. Men of force learn to hold their features as they hold their tongue. But his eyes—under strong inward stimulus—betrayed him: they widened, brightened, took on a flash no will could wholly suppress. He looked steadily at Tito and did not answer at once, as if weighing whether this information served a purpose.

Tito’s glance was one of those that never seems to observe and misses little. He had expected that very dilation and light, having noted it before. The moment he saw it, he bent his attention to a small thing: he adjusted the gold fibula at his mantle as if it had slipped—an air of patient waiting, the modesty of a man who had no desire to pierce the Prior’s mind.

In truth Savonarola had expected this intimation from Domenico Mazzinghi, a disciple among the Ten whom he had already used to send a private letter to the Florentine ambassador in France—preparing the way for a letter to the French king, now lying ready in the desk near at hand. It called on the king to aid in summoning a General Council to reform the Church and begin by deposing Pope Alexander—no rightful Pope, but a vicious unbeliever, raised by corruption and ruling by simony. Tito did not know the contents; yet his subtle talent for construction, guided by hints and hopes, had led him to guess enough to set his desire on intercepting what was to go.

“It is true, my son,” Savonarola said at last, with guarded quiet. “I have letters I would gladly send by safe conveyance under cover to our ambassador. Our community has affairs in France—you know—we are responsible, among other things, for a debt to that singularly wise Frenchman, Signor Philippe de Comines, for the Medici library we purchased. But I think Messer Domenico may return before evening, and I shall have more time to prepare if I wait to deposit the letters in his hands.”

“Assuredly, reverend father,” Tito replied, smooth as poured oil, “that might be better on all grounds except one: if anything should hinder Messer Domenico’s return, the despatch of the letters would require either my coming to San Marco again at a late hour, or your sending them by Fra Niccolò. And I know you wish to guard against false inferences that might be drawn from too frequent communication between yourself and any officer of the government.”

He threw the difficulty out gently, like a man laying a cloth on a table to see if it would lie flat. And he felt that the more reluctance the Frate showed, the more likely his conjecture was right.

Savonarola did not answer. His mouth kept its firmness; but a faint color rose under the skin, the suppressed excitement mounting. This, then, would be the critical moment—the moment of letting that letter pass out of his hands.

“It is most probable Messer Domenico will return in time,” Tito said, as if the matter were settled. He rose. “With your permission, father, I will take my leave. I would not trespass on your time now that my errand is done. But as I may not be favored with another interview, I venture to confide to you—what is not yet known to others except to the magnificent Ten—that I contemplate resigning my secretaryship and leaving Florence shortly. Am I presuming too much on your interest in what relates chiefly to myself?”

“Speak on, my son,” Savonarola said. “I desire to know your prospects.”

“I find I have mistaken my vocation in forsaking pure letters, for which I was trained. The politics of Florence are worthy to occupy the greatest mind—to occupy yours—when a man can execute his own ideas. But when, like me, he can only be the instrument of changing schemes, he needs the minor attachments of a born Florentine. Also, my wife’s unhappy alienation from a Florentine residence since the painful events of August naturally influences me. I wish to join her.”

Savonarola inclined his head with approval; the domestic note, the mention of a wife estranged and sorrowing, touched his pastoral habit.

“I intend, then, soon to leave Florence, visit the chief courts of Europe, and widen my acquaintance with men of letters in the universities. I shall go first to Hungary, where scholars are welcome; and I shall probably start in a week or ten days. I have not concealed from you, father, that I am no religious enthusiast; I have not my wife’s ardor. But religious enthusiasm is not necessary, as I conceive, in order to appreciate the grandeur and justice of your views concerning the government of nations and the Church. If you condescend to entrust me with any commission that will further the relations you wish to establish, I shall feel honored. May I now take my leave?”

“Stay, my son,” said Savonarola. “When you depart I will send a letter to your wife, whose spiritual welfare I would fain be assured of—for she left me in anger. And as for the letters to France—such as I have ready—”

He rose and turned to the desk as he spoke. He drew out a letter addressed in his own minute, exquisite hand—handwriting that, like the delicate marginalia in his Bibles, seemed made for patient truth rather than for haste. He slipped it within a larger sheet and sealed it.

“Pardon me, father,” Tito said quickly, before the Frate could offer it, “unless it were your decided wish, I would rather not incur the responsibility of carrying the letter away. Messer Domenico Mazzinghi will doubtless return; or if not, Fra Niccolò can bring it to me at the second hour of the evening, when I shall place the other despatches in the courier’s hands.”

“At present, my son,” Savonarola answered, waving aside the point, “I wish you to address this packet to our ambassador in your own hand, which is preferable to my secretary’s.”

Tito sat again and wrote the address. While he wrote, Savonarola stood with folded arms; the color in his face rose higher, and at last his lip quivered—an involuntary betrayal, as if the sealed paper in his presence were still alive and resisting separation.

Tito rose, the packet in hand, and was moving away when Savonarola spoke abruptly, decision cutting through the hesitation that had made his silence heavy.

“Take it, my son. There is no use in waiting. It does not please me that Fra Niccolò should have needless errands to the Palazzo.”

As Tito received the letter, Savonarola remained standing in a suppressed excitement that seemed to forbid further words. Passionate natures like his cast a subtle influence—when they are absent-minded with inward strain, the very air grows still, as if sound itself were held back. Tito made a deep reverence and went out, the letter hidden under his mantle.

The packet was duly delivered to the courier, and so carried out of Florence. But before that happened, another messenger—quietly hired by Tito—had already borne information in cipher, sent by relays to the armed agents of Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, who watched the borders for this very purpose: to intercept such despatches as they could seize when they crossed into Milanese reach.
