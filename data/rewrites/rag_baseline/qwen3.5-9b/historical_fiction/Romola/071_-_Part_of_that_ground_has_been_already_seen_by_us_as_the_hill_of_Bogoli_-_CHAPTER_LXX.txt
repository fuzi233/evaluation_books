On the fourteenth of April, Romola returned to Florence, driven by a singular, overriding imperative. Having failed to find rest in Pistoia due to conflicting rumors regarding the Trial by Fire, she had pushed onward to Prato, intending to cross the threshold of the city regardless of her dread. Her resolve was tested immediately upon encountering the Augustinian monk from San Spirito, former confessor to her late father-in-law. From him, she extracted the brutal facts of Savonarola's arrest and the fate of her husband. The monk recounted how Tito had fled an enraged mob by plunging into the Arno, only to be slain on the riverbank by a lifelong enemy. Yet, Romola perceived the catastrophe with a clarity that eluded others. When the monk spoke of Savonarola's fall, adopting the typical bias of the Black Brothers toward a brother who had shown weakness under duress, claiming he had confessed to deceiving the people, Romola remained unmoved by such partisan rhetoric.

That evening, she sat in a state of agitated silence within Florence, surrounded by the cacophony of Monna Brigida's hysterical joy and wailing. The older woman, having recently reverted to wearing false hair, now pulled it off, declaring her willingness to embrace gray locks if Romola would remain with her. Romola, however, was too consumed by the weight of Tito's death and Fra Girolamo's coerced confession to absorb Brigida's trivial gossip. Her entire mental faculty, strained by awe-struck grief, focused exclusively on two objectives: locating Savonarola and discovering the whereabouts of Tessa and the children.

"Tell me, cousin," Romola interrupted Brigida's rambling proposals for cohabitation, "has anything been seen or heard of a young woman with two small children since Tito's passing?"

Brigida recoiled, her eyes widening in horror. "Cristo! No! Was he so terrible, my poor child? Ah, that explains your departure. You left me only a vague note of your own volition. Had I known... well, I suppose I blamed your father's temper. 'She's got her father's fire,' I muttered to myself. But if you had spoken the truth about the woman and the babes..."

"No, dear cousin," Romola insisted, cutting her off firmly. "Cease speaking thus. Above all, I must find that woman and her children. They are utterly helpless. Do not speak against it; securing their safety is my first commandment."

"If that makes me a Piagnone," Brigida muttered, lowering her voice with confused discomfort, "then I've been counting peas instead of prayers. Fra Girolamo preached that widows should not remarry. Stepping through the door is a sin; crawling through the chimney is grace. Two children—Santiddio!"

"Cousin, the poor soul committed no conscious wrong. She is ignorant of everything. I will explain later—not now."

Early the following morning, Romola proceeded to the house beyond San Ambrogio where she had previously located Tessa. As feared, the young woman was gone. Romola deduced that Tito had dispatched her ahead to a rendezvous point, unwilling to separate her from the children. The prospect was agonizing; if Tessa had fled Florence, recovery seemed impossible, leaving the child to wander in helpless misery at some roadside halt. Neighbors offered only that the elderly deaf Lisa had departed a week prior with her belongings, providing no lead on Tessa. With no tangible starting point for inquiry, and restrained by both natural reserve and a noble sensitivity that forbade exposing Tessa's connection to Tito, Romola fell into days of anxious inaction.

Even under the pressure of other concerns, her heart raced at the sight of round brown legs or a peasant woman in the traditional *contadina* dress. She never rationalized this vigilance as heroic charity; rather, she possessed a specific, binding obligation to protect these lives. She yearned to embrace the children and earn their affection, a sweet redemption for her own sorrows. While legal avenues existed to claim Tito's property, Romola distrusted the purity of such funds. She resolved to surrender all assets to the State, retaining only enough to purchase her father's library—a sum sufficient for Tessa and the children's modest sustenance.

Monna Brigida, however, drowned these plans in clamorous insistence that Romola reside with her until she reached Paradise, questioning why she had encouraged the conversion to the Piagnoni order otherwise. Brigida argued that if Romola wished to raise other people's children, she must do so alongside her. Romola recognized the subtext: a demand for submission. Yet, strong emotion fueled a superstition of hope. Somehow, she would find the mother and the children.

A new avenue of investigation emerged. She learned that Tito had arranged horses and mules at San Gallo, indicating his intention to flee via that gate. Determined, though lacking absolute confidence, she decided to query the gatekeepers regarding anyone matching Tessa's description who had passed before the morning of the ninth of April.

Walking along the Via San Gallo, her gaze sharp and watchful beneath her long widow's veil, she spotted Bratti haggling with a customer. This itinerant man might prove useful; she would not hesitate to discuss Tessa with him. As she removed her veil and stepped across the street, a glint of gold hanging from the corner of his basket caused her heart to leap.

"Bratti, my friend," she called out abruptly, "where did you acquire that necklace?"

"Your servant, madonna," Bratti replied, scanning the street with practiced deliberation, his demeanor immune to surprise. "It is a piece worth money, yet I shall gain little from selling it. My heart is too tender for trade; I have pledged it."

"Please, tell me where you obtained it—from a young woman named Tessa, is it not true?"

"Oh! If you know her," Bratti said, "and would redeem it for a small profit to return it to her, you'd be performing a great charity. She wept bitterly when parting with it—as if diving into a brook. A trifling fee, I'll grant you. One florin, for I dislike being hard-hearted."

"Where is she?" Romola asked, handing over the coin and retrieving the necklace from the basket with trembling hands.

"Just outside the gate, at the far end of the Borgo, at old Sibilla Manetti's. Anybody can show you the house."

With wings of purpose, Romola hastened toward the destination, grateful for the Carnival memory that allowed her to recognize the distinctive necklace instantly. Upon arrival, she found the young woman and the children confined to an inner room. They had been displaced weeks ago, possessing only their clothes to barter for food and lodging with a destitute widow. Since Romola knew them, the transaction ceased to matter. She entered without delay.

Tessa sat on a low bed, her earlier screaming replaced by silent, tearless sobs. Her eyes were fixed with a hollow sadness on the two children playing in the corner. Lillo covered his head with his skirt, roaring at Ninna to frighten her, then peeking out cautiously to gauge her reaction. The door stood slightly behind Tessa, and she did not turn, assuming it was the old woman; hope had long since extinguished itself.

Romola discarded her veil, holding the necklace aloft for a brief moment. Then, in the pure, resonant voice that once cheered her father, she spoke: "Tessa!"

Tessa sprang to her feet, turning in alarm. "See," Romola said, fastening the beads around Tessa's neck. "God has sent me to you again."

The poor woman screamed and sobbed, clutching the arms that secured the necklace, unable to form words. The children rushed from their corner, seizing their mother's skirts and gazing upward with wide, startled eyes at the stranger.

That afternoon, the group returned to Monna Brigida's residence in the Borgo degli Albizzi. Romola gently informed Tessa that Naldo could never return—not out of cruelty, but because he was dead. "Be comforted, my Tessa," Romola assured her. "I have come to care for you forever. We have Lillo and Ninna."

Monna Brigida's face twisted in a struggle between reverence for Romola and the urge to interject unseasonably. *Let it be for now,* she thought. *It feels like a millennium until I reveal to this simple country girl, who cannot count her fingers, exactly who Romola is. I shall tell her eventually, or she will never understand her station. It suits Romola perfectly; no one owns their soul when she is present. But if this cat-faced minx is to live in my house, she must submit to me.*

Nevertheless, Brigida's maternal instincts surfaced during supper, offering the children excessive sweets. Before retiring, she confessed to Romola that it would be shameful not to care for such cherubs. "However, you must yield to me somewhat regarding their feeding and such matters," she added, noting Romola's lack of infant experience. "I had twins, though they died shortly after birth."
