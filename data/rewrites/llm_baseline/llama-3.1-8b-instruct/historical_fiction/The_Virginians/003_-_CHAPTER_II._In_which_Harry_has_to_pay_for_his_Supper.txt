As the fading sunlight cast long shadows across the facade of Castlewood, Colonel Esmond's grandson, Harry, stood before the ancestral mansion, his hand raised to summon the attention of those within. The servant, summoned at last, emerged with a look of mild curiosity, rather than warmth, at the prospect of receiving a visitor from the family's distant branch in Virginia. Harry's call was met with indifference, and it was only through persistent entreaties that he gained permission to step inside and view the sitting-room and picture-gallery, where a portrait of his grandfather in the finery of Charles II's era hung alongside a likeness of his grandmother, resplendent in the courtly attire of the same era, her golden hair styled in delicate ringlets that seemed almost ethereal in their beauty. The housekeeper, however, grew impatient, for the family's return was imminent, and she ushered Harry out with a brusque reminder of the impending arrival of the Countess, her lord, and their retinue, including the Baroness Bernstein, aunt to the young ladies.

Harry, his mind still lingering on the portraits, scribbled his name and address on a scrap of paper from his pocketbook and left it on a table in the hall, hoping against hope that someone would recognize him and show some interest in his visit. But as he departed, he couldn't shake the feeling of disappointment that had settled over him like a shroud. He walked away, his footsteps echoing through the stillness, and made his way to the local alehouse, where he ordered a simple meal of bacon and eggs, feeling a pang of sadness that his ancestral home had failed to stir any warmth within him.

Later, as the sun dipped below the horizon, casting a warm orange glow over the landscape, Harry strolled across the bridge, lost in thought, his gaze fixed on the old house behind him. The rooks, returning to their nests in the elms, filled the air with their raucous calls, and the blacksmith's hammering echoed through the evening air, mingling with the distant chirping of crickets and the soft rustle of leaves. As he stood there, his mind wandered back to his childhood, and the stories his mother and grandparents had told him of their ancestors, of knights and cavaliers, of huntsmen and hawks. His thoughts turned, too, to his lost brother, and a pang of sorrow struck him, threatening to overwhelm him once more.
