"I count myself in nothing else so happy / As in a soul remembering my good friends." —Shakespeare.

Sir Hugo Mallinger was not as eager to depart for Genoa as Mr. Gascoigne had been, nor would Daniel Deronda leave until he had spoken with the baronet. Between Grandcourt's death and the recent upheaval in his own life, Deronda felt unable to write down the full truth about the mother who had appeared and vanished like a ghost. It was not until the fifth evening, upon receiving a telegram, that he met Sir Hugo at the station, waiting for his arrival between eight and nine. While gazing toward the platform, anticipating the sight of a kind, familiar face from his earliest memories, a faint smile touched Deronda's lips despite his recent tragedies. He imagined Sir Hugo's delight in finally becoming the undisputed master of his estates, free to leave them to his daughters—or, according to a new theory of inheritance that had recently seized Deronda's imagination, to adopt makeshift female heirs as intermediaries for a future grandson.

We would be ungrateful creatures if we could not share in another's joy, unless that joy conflicted with our strictest moral ideals. How sour our expressions become, how cold our glances, when someone else's happiness does not align with our vision of perfect righteousness! Yet, our own desires rarely conform to such high standards. We must learn to fellowship with imperfection; indeed, it is possible to feel genuine gratitude even when we recognize a mistake that caused harm, provided the mistake was carried out through a lifetime of kindly intentions.

Deronda's heart and judgment strongly opposed Sir Hugo's role in perpetuating a falsehood—there was no softer term for the deception under which he had been raised. Still, the baronet likely lacked clear knowledge of his mother's betrayal. With his breezy approach to life, Sir Hugo had probably deemed it reasonable to ensure his son was born an English gentleman, considering his daughter-in-law's eccentric refusal to separate from her child. Daniel's deep affection for Sir Hugo made him seek excuses rather than condemnation. It is as easy to hold firm principles while offering gentle blame as it is to be disturbed by disorder yet remain patient with the person who perceives it wrongly. Had Sir Hugo, in his bachelor days, viewed children merely as accessories to adult comfort, he had simply shared a common assumption of that era. Deronda, recalling his own painful childhood struggles, remembered clearly that such experiences had never entered Sir Hugo's worldview. Ignorant kindness can sometimes amount to cruelty, but to punish it as if it were deliberate malice would be a greater cruelty itself—the furthest thing from Deronda's generous spirit.

After the intense scrutiny of the last ten days, during which the veil had been lifted on lives unlike his own, Deronda found himself less inclined toward rash indignation, which often masks a desire for punishment. When he saw Sir Hugo's familiar figure step from the train, the lifelong bond of affection washed over him, drowning out any new grounds for blame.

"Well, Dan," Sir Hugo said, gripping Deronda's hand with earnest warmth. He offered no further greeting; the weight of their shared history required no words. Next came instructions for the courier, followed by a proposal to stroll slowly in the mild evening air, with no rush to reach the hotel.

"I traveled comfortably and am in fine shape," Sir Hugo remarked as they walked beneath the stars, still tinged with the day's fading light. "I took my time leaving so I could investigate matters a bit. In fact, I saw your letter to Lady Mallinger before I departed. But now, how is the widow?"

"Calmer," Deronda replied. "She appears to be avoiding the physical illness we might have feared following her plunge and the shock. Her uncle and mother arrived two days ago, and she is being cared for well."

"Do you think there's any chance of an heir?"

Based on what Mr. Gascoigne told me, I doubt it. He spoke as if the question was whether she would retain the estates for her lifetime.

"It won't be much of a wrench to her feelings, I imagine, losing her husband so suddenly?" Sir Hugo asked, glancing around.

"The suddenness was a great blow," Deronda said, carefully sidestepping the deeper implications.

"Wonder if Grandcourt mentioned anything in his will?"

"Do you know its terms, sir?" Deronda countered gently.

"Yes, I do," the baronet replied promptly. "Good heavens, if there's no legitimate heir, he left everything to a boy he had with a Mrs. Glasher—you know nothing of this, I assume? She was practically his wife for many years. There are three older girls, but the boy takes precedence. His name is already Henleigh, and he'll be known as Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. The Mallinger title means nothing to him, thankfully, but the young rascal will have plenty to manage during his fourteen-year minority. No need to plug the holes in my fifty thousand pounds spent on Diplow that he wasn't entitled to. Meanwhile, my dear beauty, the young widow, gets a mere two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere—a lovely form of exile if she chooses to lock herself away there, though I doubt she will. That woman has lived there for years. I'm utterly disgusted with Grandcourt. Though, regarding my own affairs, nothing in his life suited him better than dying."

"In my view, he erred greatly in marrying this woman—not necessarily in leaving the estate to the son," Deronda said dryly.

"I take no issue with giving the land to the lad," Sir Hugo agreed. "But since he married her, he should have provided a substantial allowance, enough for her to maintain the lifestyle her marriage afforded her. Four or five thousand a year and the London house for life—that's what I would have done. Since she started penniless, her friends surely couldn't demand a formal settlement beforehand. Otherwise, relying on a man's will after marriage is foolish. Even sensible men leak folly into their wills; my father did, I recall. If a man harbors spite or tyranny, he tends to hide it in legal documents. It's plain Grandcourt intended his death to extinguish his wife's prospects if she failed to bear him an heir."

"And in the other scenario, everything would have been reversed—illegitimacy leading to extinction?" Deronda asked, with a touch of scorn.

"Precisely. Gadsmere and the two thousand. It's peculiar. One annoyance is that Grandcourt named me executor. But as he was the son of my only brother, I cannot refuse. I'd rather mind it less if I can assist the widow. Lush suggests she wasn't unaware of the family situation behind closed doors and hinted at a strained relationship between the couple. But I believe you know best what Mrs. Grandcourt felt or didn't feel, don't you, Dan?"

Sir Hugo dropped his usual joking tone, speaking with a lowered, genuinely curious voice. Deronda realized evasion would be misunderstood.

"She was certainly unhappy. They were mismatched souls. As for the property division, based on what I've observed, I predict she will accept it contentedly."

"Then she isn't typical of her sex," Sir Hugo shrugged slightly. "Still, she must be extraordinary, given the entanglement between your fate and hers—eh? When that massive telegram arrived, Lady Mallinger's first remark was, 'How strange that it should be Daniel sending it!' But I've had similar moments in my own life. Once, at a foreign hotel, I encountered a woman abandoned by her husband with no money. Upon learning of it and stepping in to help, who should appear but an early flame of mine, foolish enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and short affection? It was purely a personal matter calling me there—no knight-errantry involved, any more than your trip to Genoa concerns the Grandcourts."

A brief silence fell between them. Sir Hugo had shifted to discussing the Grandcourts as a safer topic, yet both wished to bridge the gap toward complete honesty regarding the events affecting their relationship. Deronda sensed his earlier letter, sent after meeting his mother, had thickened the ice rather than melting it. He waited for Sir Hugo to break the tension.

Just as they neared the harbor, the baronet paused, turning for a final look at the port before speaking in a more serious tone. "And regarding the main reason for your coming to Genoa, Dan? Have you not been deeply distressed by what you've learned? Is there nothing that makes you reconsider your position? Remember, whatever happens to you remains important to me."

"I wish to honor your generosity with total confidence, sir," Deronda replied. "Yet I cannot answer those questions with a simple yes or no. Much of what I've heard about the past has pained me. Meeting and parting with my mother in her suffering state was also painful. But knowing my parentage clears up doubts, and for that, I am grateful. Regarding my position, my gratitude to you for your fatherly care and affection remains unchanged. However, discovering I was born a Jew may profoundly alter my life, and I am hardly ready to explain fully at this moment."

Deronda spoke the final sentence with determination overcoming his hesitation. He felt that the differences between Sir Hugo's nature and his own would eventually reveal themselves more sharply than necessary.
