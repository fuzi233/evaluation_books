The Temple Garden, once the picturesque backdrop for Shakespeare’s warring roses, had long since been abandoned by fashion. The soot-choked air had even discouraged the hardiest of roses from blooming, not that the lawyers and clerks scurrying past its gates much noticed. The garden’s charm was now the domain of sentimental antiquarians and bookish dreamers—those who could still conjure the ghosts of Sir Roger de Coverley, Oliver Goldsmith hunched in a summer-house fretting over his tailor’s bill, or the great Dr. Johnson lumbering along in his snuff-stained wig, trailed by his wine-flushed Scottish shadow.

Arthur Pendennis, being of a literary and romantic bent, was just such a dreamer. And so, on a tranquil Sunday evening—when the Temple lay deserted, its barristers feasting in Belgravia or Tyburnia, its clerks nursing modest pints at the Club—he found himself wandering the garden’s gravel paths, lost in thought.

The evening was absurdly picturesque. The Thames sparkled, the warehouses glowed rosy in the sunset, and the distant church bells rang as sweetly as those of Clavering, stirring memories of his mother’s arm linked in his on the terrace at home. The whole scene was so painfully idyllic that Pen, overcome by some unnameable emotion, abruptly stood, muttered, *“No, by Jove, I’ll go home,”* and marched straight into two small children.

“*Missa Pendennis!*” squealed one—Betsy-Jane, the bolder of yesterday’s tiny admirers. Her sister, Amelia-Ann, burst into tears when Pen hoisted her aloft in a fit of spontaneous affection. This commotion summoned their attendants: Mrs. Bolton in her finest cashmere shawl and Miss Fanny Bolton, whose eyes lit up like Paper Buildings at midnight.

What followed was a masterpiece of coincidence—the sort that only happens in novels (or, occasionally, to young men who’ve recently mentioned their fondness for evening strolls). Fanny, it transpired, had spent the night voraciously reading *Walter Lorraine*, Pen’s own novel, after a heroic quest through every lending library in the neighborhood.

“*Oh, I do like it so!*” she breathed, cheeks aflame.

Pen, torn between gratification and alarm, stammered promises to send her a copy before fleeing the scene—only to collide with the glowering figure of Mr. Bows.

What ensued was less a conversation than a duel. Bows, the embittered old fiddler, accused Pen of toying with Fanny’s heart. Pen, indignantly aristocratic in his duck trousers and multicolored cravat, retorted that he was hardly a villain from a melodrama. Yet when Bows—his voice cracking—pleaded not as a moralist but as a lonely old man who loved the girl like a daughter, Pen’s defiance crumbled.

By the end, apologies were exchanged, hands were shaken, and Pen vowed solemnly to steer clear of temptation. As Bows trudged away, Arthur shut his door with a sigh, wondering if the bells ringing across the city were laughing at him.
