A Rabbi once put it as an indisputable, unarguable truth: no man could ever carve his father and mother’s bones into spoons. Every hearer understood the quiet, stubborn sentiment behind the rule, even if no one could name a logical, practical reason to forbid the trade. The market for spoons would never expand far enough to make anyone ask, “Why not?” and claim human progress depended on turning beloved family remains into cutlery. The only check is sentiment—and those who do not believe sentiment is the richest, most precious wealth in the world will never be coerced by it at all.

All the while, Daniel Deronda had given up his fashionable daily rides in Rotten Row for a far quieter, less celebrated form of wandering. He spent hours drifting through the East End streets where London’s common Jewish families made their homes, slipping into synagogues during services, peering through shop windows, studying every face he passed, chasing the faint, soft promise of the clues Mirah had shared with him: a mother named Cohen, a brother named Ezra, both lost to her since she was a small, lonely child. It hardly seemed like a method that would turn up answers. He knew he could go straight to a prominent rabbi or a respected leader in the Jewish community for help, and he told himself he would do it—after Christmas.

But the truth was, for all his love of the poetry in ordinary, everyday lives, Deronda could not escape the heavy, unyielding weight of the hard, unpolished Actual when his heart was tangled up in a personal quest. Enthusiasm flows easy among grand, distant ideas: you can look back at medieval Jewish communities breathing garlic in candlelit synagogues, or imagine heroic martyrs defying crusaders at the height of the Hep! Hep! Hep! violence, and find nothing shabby about the scene. But when that ideal presses up against you as a living, breathing person you care for—when Mirah, with her soft voice and her tears for the family she lost, hangs over every one of your wandering steps—suddenly the dingy shop fronts and unremarkable faces of modern London feel far harder to face than any grand historical tragedy. Deronda could dream of sailing down the Rhine in the 11th century, hunting for Mirah’s kin among the persecuted Jews of Cordova, and never flinch at the grit. But seeing that same beloved idea of Mirah’s family made flesh in the harsh midday light of Whitechapel, with no dramatic martyrdom to gild it, only commonplace, half-repulsive ordinary life, that was the test. He knew his usual self would have scorned anyone too fastidious to see the beauty in broad, unvarnished human life—but with Mirah’s fragile hope wrapped up in every possible discovery, he could not shake the quiet fear that the family she had imagined as a sacred, gentle haven would turn out to be something she could never have dreamed of, something that would break her. Worse, a quiet, suppressed voice deep inside him whispered he might be running toward that same collision for his own hidden, unknown parentage too. He had no real reason to expect Mirah’s family to be wealthy, among the fancy Jewish circles that outdid the English lords at sport, so those distant people did not occupy his thoughts at all. He wandered those streets half hoping to prepare his own mind for whatever he might find, no more than that—like if Mirah had been related to Welsh miners, he might have walked their villages just to understand their lives, so he could explain their world to her gently if the time ever came.

He did not *want* to find anyone. Every time he glanced at the name painted over a shop door, he was relieved to see it did not spell *Ezra Cohen*. He particularly, selfishly hoped Ezra Cohen did not run a shop at all. Wishes are notoriously ominous, after all—the world seems to arrange itself so that if you find the idea of a squint unforgivably distasteful, your child will end up with one, and if you crave a squint, you will never get it. That despondent view of probability was the one Deronda leaned into, unconsciously, one grey morning when he turned down a small side street off the din and congestion of Holborn, tired of wandering, and paused to hail an approaching hansom cab.

A flash of chased silver in the shop window at his right hand caught his eye: a pair of exquisite old missal clasps, their details worn soft with centuries of use. He thought at once of Lady Mallinger, who had a great fondness for turning old Catholic religious relics into pretty Protestant trinkets, and imagined these would make a lovely bracelet. His gaze drifted over the rest of the window, and he saw it was a pawnbroker’s shop, one that dressed up its trade in bric-a-brac, displaying jewelry, lace, and fine trinkets on every shelf. A placard in the corner read *Watches and Jewelry Exchanged and Repaired*.

His survey had not gone unnoticed. A young man stepped out the shop door, around thirty years old, unmistakably Jewish, with a warm, encouraging smile. “Good day, sir,” he called. Deronda winced, sure the man was about to launch into a persuasive sales pitch, returned the greeting, crossed to the other side of the street, and waved the cab over. Then he looked back at the sign painted over the shop front, and his chest lurched. It read *Ezra Cohen*.

There might be a hundred Ezra Cohens running shops in London, but Deronda had never seen a single one. This young man, hovering to greet a possible customer, was almost exactly the age Mirah’s brother would be, old enough to be a grown man while she was still a tiny child. As he rode home in the cab, he tried his hardest to convince himself there was no reason at all to presume this man was Mirah’s brother. Even if by some impossible chance he *was*, and the investigation found Mirah’s mother was dead, Deronda told himself, it was not his duty to break the news to her. But the thought would not settle: he knew Mirah’s deepest, most devout religious wish was to know if her mother was gone, and if her brother still lived. How dare he shape another person’s life according to his own personal comfort? Was that not exactly the secret, cruel way his own life had been ordered, all his childhood hidden from him, no open daylight on his own family ties, no clear path to follow his most basic duties, like every other man?

The immediate relief was that he had made no real discovery at all. He would go back to the shop, he decided, as soon as he could conveniently, buy the silver clasps for Lady Mallinger, and learn enough facts to confirm there was no reason for him to make any choice at all. But Sir Hugo kept him busy for days on end: the older man was preparing an after-dinner speech on a fiery political topic, and wanted Deronda to dig up all the legal references for him, then argued with him for hours every day, their debates always ending in a friendly draw. “Confound it, Dan!” Sir Hugo would exclaim, half annoyed, half proud, when Deronda laid out his point clearly. “Why don’t you get up and say these things in public? You’re wrong, you know, the heavy mass of the country is against you—but that’s the perfect stage for a young man to make a name for himself. When I was your age, I’d have leapt at the chance. You’d be in Parliament before you know it, and that’s the very thing that would make me happy.”

“I’m sorry I can’t do what would please you, sir,” Deronda said. “But I can’t bring myself to treat politics as a profession.”

“Why not?” Sir Hugo pressed. “A man who isn’t born into public life has to work for it. The country’s business has to get done, as the old Duke said—you can’t wait around for a divine call from heaven or from your constituents.”

“I don’t want to make a living out of opinions,” Deronda told him. “Especially out of borrowed ones. I don’t judge other men for it—many better men than I don’t mind stepping up on a platform to praise their party’s work, but that life isn’t for me.”

“Any man who fights every little bit of humbug in the world is just a three-cornered, impossible fellow,” Sir Hugo laughed. “There’s good humbug too, the kind that oils the wheels and makes progress possible. If you want to lead people, you have to meet them where their ideas are. You don’t issue an Order in Council to wipe out public shallowness, you meet them halfway. A little acting is necessary for any action at all.”

“I’ll concede you sometimes have to make a temporary compromise,” Deronda said. “But there’s a world of difference between putting on a foolscap hat to grin for a specific occasion when you have no choice, and carrying a foolscap hat in your pocket, practicing grinning every day. I can’t treat my own success as proof that what I’m doing is right for the public.”

That conversation had left Deronda feeling jarred and unsettled when he finally set out for his long-planned second visit to Ezra Cohen’s shop. He entered the street from the end opposite the Holborn entrance, and a heavy reluctance slowed his steps, his thoughts circling back from the public compromises he and Sir Hugo had argued over to his own private dilemma: how far was it right to hide the truth about Mirah’s family, just to spare her pain? Most people would call that an over-refinement, but Deronda had come to hate secrets above all things, knowing how the hidden truths around his own birth had poisoned his whole youth. The idea of being forced to keep another unnecessary secret grated on his every nerve. He paused in front of every plausible shop window on the street, half deciding he would not learn any more about this modern Ezra Cohen, even though his own logic told him the odds were astronomically low that this man was Mirah’s lost brother.

One of the windows he paused at belonged to a second-hand bookshop, where a small table outside held a jumbled, charming mix of literature from Homer’s verse to cheap railway novels. Deronda spotted a copy of Solomon Maimon’s famous autobiography, the life of the wandering Polish Jew he had long wanted to read, slipped it into his pocket, and stepped inside to pay for it, expecting the usual morose, unenthusiastic secondhand bookseller who never tries to talk you into buying anything.

But the figure he saw behind the shelves was nothing like that. A thin man in threadbare clothing, his skin a pale, yellowed flatness like old carved ivory, sat on a stool leaning against the shelves, reading yesterday’s *Times*. When he lifted the paper and looked up at Deronda, Deronda thought he had never seen a face more like the portrait of an ancient Hebrew prophet from the Babylonian exile, or a medieval Jewish poet. It was a sharp, clearly cut face, worn thin with a lifetime of strenuous, eager searching that had never given him easy, immediate joy, the dark eyes far-off and intense, as if he was perpetually waiting for a messenger to arrive, bringing either salvation or terrible news. “That book has no price mark on it,” he said, not rising from his seat. “Mr. Ram is out at dinner, I’m minding the shop. What will you give for it?”

Deronda asked if he knew its market value, and the man leaned forward, voice deepening with sudden, electric eagerness. “Have you read it? Are you a scholar, interested in Jewish history?”

“I am interested in Jewish history,” Deronda said, curiosity overriding his surprise at the strange, intense inspection.

Then the stranger rose, reached out, and gripped Deronda’s arm tight with his thin, bony hand. “Are you of our people?” he whispered, hoarse with excitement, barely loud enough to hear.

Deronda flushed deeply, shook his head, and said “No.” The grip fell away instantly. All the light and excitement drained out of the man’s face, melting into flat, distant melancholy, as if the spirit that had leapt up in him at the sight of Deronda had crawled back to hide in the deepest recesses of his bones. “Half a crown will do, sir,” he said, holding out the book with cold, polite distance. The rejection left Deronda oddly humiliated, as if some high dignitary had looked him over and dismissed him unfit. He paid his half crown, bade him good morning, and left the shop carrying the book, still confused and a little annoyed that their strange, charged conversation had ended so abruptly.

He turned and stepped through the door of Ezra Cohen’s pawn shop seconds later. Ezra himself was leaning over the counter, his round, plump face glistening, haggling over two plated stoppers and three silver teaspoons with a customer on the other side of the glass. He looked up, grinned, and called “Mother! Mother!” over his shoulder, before nodding warmly at Deronda. “Coming, sir—be with you in a second.”

Deronda’s heart sank a little when a robust, vigorous woman in her fifties came out from the back of the shop to serve him. She was not nearly coarse or unkind enough to rule out the terrible possibility that she was Mirah’s mother. Deronda found himself trying to wipe away the fat that had softened the outlines of her youth from her face, trying to see what she had looked like at twenty. The line of her eyebrows was almost identical to Mirah’s. It was not impossible, he thought, that this woman could have once been the mother of a gentle, beautiful girl like Mirah. “How can I serve you, sir?” she said, in a soft, guttural tone.

Deronda asked for the old silver clasps in the window. Ezra leapt forward before his mother could stretch to reach them, laughing and handing them to Deronda. “My mother likes to do everything herself,” he explained cheerfully. “She’d hurt herself stretching, so when we get a gentleman customer, I always call her out to make sure she gets to greet them proper.” His mother laughed a soft, amiable gurgle, clearly proud of her adoring son.

“They’re only three guineas, sir,” she said, encouragingly.

“First-rate workmanship, worth twice that,” Ezra put in. “I got them cheap, all the way from Cologne.”

Two new customers stepped into the shop just then, and Ezra called “Addy!”—and a whole swarm of Cohen family members emerged from the back. A bright young woman with black ringlets and a huge coral necklace, Addy, carried a tiny black-eyed baby, set it down on the counter, followed by a sturdy six-year-old boy and a little girl barely four, all of them dark-eyed and vividly, unmistakably Jewish. Deronda patted the eldest boy’s head, amused, and asked his name.

“Jacob Alexander Cohen,” the little boy said, perfectly clear and unshakably confident, hands stuffed in the pockets of his tiny knickerbockers.

“Named after your grandfather?” Deronda asked.

“Grandpa sells knives and razors and scissors,” Jacob announced, pulling a tiny pocketknife out of his pocket, flipping open two blades and a corkscrew in one quick, practiced motion. “He gave it to me.”

Deronda turned to the grandmother. “Isn’t that a little dangerous for him to play with?”

“Bless you, he’ll never hurt himself!” she said, beaming at him.

Jacob stepped even closer, his small voice hoarse and sharp, like an old, experienced merchant. “Do you have a knife?” he asked.

Deronda smiled, pulled his small penknife out of his waistcoat pocket, and held it out for the little boy to see.
