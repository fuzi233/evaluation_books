Mr. Draper, still seething with the indignity of his morning's confrontation with Harry, fancied he heard the young prisoner muttering behind him. In truth, Harry had risen, a half-sob caught in his throat to summon the lawyer back. But pride was a heavy armor for the young man; he checked his words, and Draper, offended, refused to halt. To humble himself before the lawyer, to yield solely because of a lack of coin, wounded Harry's spirit deeply. "An hour hence will suffice," he muttered, sinking sulkily back onto the bed.

"No," he whispered to the empty room, "I do not care for Maria Esmond!" The shame of his entrapment burned hotter than any anger. That woman, cunning and seasoned, had preyed upon his boyish ardor, exploiting him just as her brother had exploited him at the gaming table. They were his own flesh and blood, yet they had betrayed the sanctity of hospitality, turning the trusting kinsman into a victim for their selfish ends. His heart felt bruised, his head fell back against the pillow, and bitter tears soaked the linen. "Had they come to Virginia," he thought, his voice breaking, "I would have offered them a different welcome!"

This despondency was shattered by the grinning face of Gumbo at the door. "A lady has come to see Master Harry," the servant announced. Behind him stood the lady in the capuchin, the very woman they had just discussed. Harry sat up, pale and haggard. The lady, overcome with emotion, rushed toward the prisoner before the servant could retreat. With a sob, she threw her arms around his neck, embracing him with a maternal tenderness that pierced the gloom of the cell. She sobbed over his feverish cheek, kissing it amidst a flood of tears.

"Oh, my Harry! Did I ever, ever think to see thee here?"

Harry recoiled, startled by her sudden presence, but she sank to her knees beside the bed, seizing his burning hand and clutching his knees. There was genuine regard in her eyes; the wretched state of her kinsman filled her heart with sincere pity.

"I—I thought none of you would come!" Harry groaned.

More tears, more kisses of his hot young hand, more desperate clasps were her reply for a moment.

"Oh, my dear! My dear! I cannot bear to think of thee in misery!" she sobbed.

Though hardened by circumstance, her heart was not entirely stone; her life was not a barren desert. Her love for him surpassed even that of his own mother, her tones more tender than any he had known.

"Some of these debts," she confessed, her voice trembling, "were owing to my own extravagance! You bought trinkets and jewels to please me. Oh, how I hate them now! I little thought I could ever feel such regret! I have brought them all with me, and more—here! And here! And every guinea I possess in the world!"

She poured a cascade of brooches, rings, a watch, and a score of gold coins into Harry's lap. The sight stirred him profoundly, touching him to his core.

"Dearest, kindest cousin!" he sobbed.

His lips failed him, yet his expression spoke volumes of gratitude and affection. Soon, a strange light returned to his eyes. He smiled as he sorted through the gifts, planning which trinkets to send to Maria, and recounted the peril he had fallen into by selling goods on credit, and how the lawyer had just insulted him over it. He would not accept Maria's money—he had enough for the present—but he valued her twenty guineas as if they were twenty thousand. He vowed never to forget her kindness; it had buoyed him when he stood on the brink of total despair. He must tell his mother of her goodness. Heaven bless her for it!

The dark day seemed brighter after Maria's visit; the imprisonment felt less crushing. The world was not entirely cold and selfish. Here was a creature who truly loved him. Even Castlewood was not as terrible as he had imagined. He expressed profound grief at his inability to assist his kinsman, noting that the Baron was hopelessly in debt, losing every shilling he won the next day. Yet, he promised to do anything in his power. He would soon see Mr. Warrington, who was currently in waiting, a prisoner just as Harry was. Thus, the cousins conversed cheerfully and affectionately until twilight deepened. Then, with a sigh, Maria took her leave.

Scarcely had the door closed upon her when it swung open again to admit Draper.

"Your humble servant, sir," the attorney said. His voice grated on Harry's nerves, his presence an affront.

"I had expected you some hours ago, sir," Harry replied curtly.

"A lawyer's time is not always his own, sir," Draper retorted, having just consulted with a bottle of port at the Grecian. "Never mind, I am at your orders now. Presume it is settled, Mr. Warrington? Packed your trunk? Why, you are still in your bedgown. Let me go down and finalize matters while you summon your black man to freshen up. I have a coach at the door, and we shall dine with the old lady."

"Are you going to dine with the Baroness de Bernstein, pray?"

"Not me—no such honor. I have already eaten. It is you who are dining with your aunt, I suppose?"

"Mr. Draper, you suppose far more than you know," Mr. Warrington said, his voice fierce and tall as he wrapped his brocade dressing-gown tighter.

"Good heavens, sir, what do you mean?" Draper asked, confused.

"I mean, sir, that I have given my word to a faithful and honorable lady, and it does not become me to withdraw it."

"Confound it, sir!" shrieked the lawyer. "I tell you she has lost the paper! There is nothing binding you—nothing! Why, she is old enough to be—"

"Enough, sir!" Mr. Warrington stamped his foot. "You seem to think you are speaking to some other pettifogger. I take it, Mr. Draper, you are not accustomed to dealing with men of honor."

"Pettifogger, indeed!" Draper cried, his face purple with rage. "Men of honor, indeed! Let me inform you, Mr. Warrington, that I am as good a man of honor as you. I may not know so many gamblers and horse-jockeys. I have not gambled away my patrimony, nor lived as a nobleman on two hundred a year. I have not bought watches on credit and pawned them—touch me if you dare, sir!" The lawyer lunged for the door.

"That is the way out, sir. You cannot go through the window, for it is barred," Mr. Warrington said calmly.

"And the answer I take to my client is No, then!" Draper screamed.

Harry stepped forward, fists clenched. "If you utter another word," he warned, "I'll—" The door slammed shut before he could finish, and Draper departed in a fury to Madame de Bernstein. Though he delivered the most damning version of his story, he received only fiercer language from the Baroness than he had from Mr. Warrington himself.

"What? Shall she trust me, and I desert her?" Harry paced his room, his brocade gown rustling loudly. "Dear, faithful, generous woman! If I lie in prison for years, I shall remain true to her."

Once the lawyer was dismissed after the stormy interview, the desolate old woman was forced to sit down to the meal she had hoped to share with her nephew. The chair remained empty before her, the glasses gleaming beside the silver. Dish after dish was placed before her by the silent major-domo, tasted, and pushed away. Finally, the man pressed his mistress. "It is eight o'clock," he said. "You have eaten nothing all day. It is good for you to eat." She could not eat. She demanded coffee. Let Case fetch it. The lacquies cleared the table, leaving their mistress sitting alone before the vacant chair.

Soon, the old servant re-entered without the coffee, his face etched with strange fear. "Mr. WARRINGTON!" he cried.

The old woman gasped, rising from her armchair only to sink back, trembling violently. "So you are come, sir, are you?" she asked, her voice shaking. "Bring back the—Ah!" She screamed, "Gracious God, who is it?" Her eyes widened in wild terror; her white face looked ghastly beneath her rouge. Clinging to the arms of her chair for support, she watched as the visitor approached.

A gentleman entered whose face and figure were strikingly identical to Harry Warrington, his voice carrying tones strangely familiar. He bowed to the Baroness.

"You expected my brother, madam?" he said. "I have only just arrived in London. I went to his house and met his servant at your door, bearing this letter for you. I thought I would deliver it to your ladyship before proceeding to him," he added, placing a letter before Madame Bernstein.

"Are you"—the Baroness gasped—"are you my nephew, that we supposed was—?"

"Was killed—and is alive! I am George Warrington, madam. I ask your kinsfolk, what have you done with my brother?"

"Look, George!" the bewildered old lady exclaimed. "I expected him here tonight—that chair was set for him! I have waited for him, sir, until now, until I am quite faint. I do not like—I do not like being alone. Do stay and sup with me!"

"Pardon me, madam. Please God, my supper will be with Harry tonight!"

"Bring him back! Bring him back here on any conditions! It is but five hundred pounds! Here is the money, sir, if you need it!"

"I have no want, madam. I carry money that can be employed nowhere better than in my brother's service."

"And you will bring him to me, sir! Say you will bring him to me!"

Mr. Warrington made a stately bow in reply and left the room, passing the amazed domestics and calling with an air of authority for Gumbo to follow him.

Had Mr. Harry received no letters from home? Master Harry had not opened all his correspondence in the last day or two. Had he received no letter announcing his brother's escape from the French settlements and return to Virginia? Oh, no! No such letter had come; otherwise, Master Harry certainly would have told Gumbo. Quick, horses! Quick to the Strand, to Temple Bar! Here comes the house of Captivity and the Deliverer to the rescue!
