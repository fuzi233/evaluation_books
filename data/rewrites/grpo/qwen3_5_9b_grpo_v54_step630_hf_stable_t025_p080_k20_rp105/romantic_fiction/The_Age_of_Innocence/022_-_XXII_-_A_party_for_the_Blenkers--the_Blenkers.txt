"A party for the Blenkers—the Blenkers?"

Mr. Welland paused his knife and fork, his gaze fixed with a mixture of anxiety and disbelief upon his wife. She adjusted her gold-rimmed spectacles, her voice rising in a tone of theatrical delight as she read from the card:

"Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R. S. V. P."

"Good gracious—" Mr. Welland gasped, as if a second reading were required to fully grasp the sheer absurdity of the situation.

"Poor Amy Sillerton," Mrs. Welland sighed, her expression one of practiced pity. "You never can tell what her husband will do next. I suppose he's finally discovered the Blenkers."

Professor Emerson Sillerton was a persistent irritant within Newport society, a thorn that could not be plucked because it grew from a venerable and venerated lineage. He was, as the town whispered, a man who had enjoyed every conceivable advantage. His father was Sillerton Jackson's uncle; his mother, a Pennilow of Boston. On both sides, wealth and status intertwined seamlessly. Yet, as Mrs. Welland often noted, nothing in the world compelled Emerson Sillerton to become an archaeologist, or a professor, or to reside in Newport during the winter, or to engage in any of the other radical pursuits he favored. Still, if he intended to shatter tradition and defy social norms, he need not have married poor Amy Dagonet, a woman entitled to expect "something different" and possessing enough fortune to maintain her own carriage.

No one in the Mingott circle could comprehend why Amy Sillerton had submitted so quietly to the eccentricities of a husband who populated his home with long-haired men and short-haired women, or who dragged her to explore tombs in Yucatán rather than visiting Paris or Italy. But there they were, entrenched in their habits, seemingly unaware that they differed from everyone else. Consequently, when they hosted one of their dreary annual garden parties, every family on the Cliffs, bound by the Sillerton-Pennilow-Dagonet connection, had to draw lots and send an unwilling representative.

"It's a wonder," Mrs. Welland remarked, "that they didn't choose Cup Race Day! Do you recall, two years ago, when they threw a party for a black man on the day of Julia Mingott's *thé dansant*? Fortunately, this time there is nothing else scheduled that I know of—for of course, some of us will have to attend."

Mr. Welland sighed nervously. "'Some of us,' my dear—more than one? Three o'clock is such an inconvenient hour. I must be here by half-past three to take my drops; it is truly futile to attempt Bencomb's new regimen without systematic adherence. If I join you later, I shall inevitably miss my drive." At the thought, he lowered his cutlery again, a flush of worry coloring his finely wrinkled cheek.

"There is no reason for you to go at all, my dear," his wife replied, her cheerfulness now entirely automatic. "I have cards to deliver at the other end of Bellevue Avenue, and I shall stop by around half-past three, staying long enough to assure poor Amy that she has not been slighted." She glanced hesitantly at her daughter. "And if Newland's afternoon is arranged, perhaps May can drive you out with the ponies to try their new russet harness."

It was a fundamental tenet of the Welland family that a person's days and hours should be meticulously "provided for." The melancholy prospect of having to "kill time"—particularly for those indifferent to whist or solitaire—haunted Mrs. Welland with the same terror that the specter of unemployment strikes a philanthropist. Another of her principles dictated that parents should never, at least visibly, interfere with the plans of their married children. The challenge of reconciling this respect for May's independence with the demands of Mr. Welland's schedule was met only through an ingenuity that ensured not a single second of Mrs. Welland's own time remained unaccounted for.

"Of course I'll drive with Papa—I'm certain Newland will find something to do," May said, her tone gently reminding her husband of his lack of initiative. It was a constant source of distress to Mrs. Welland that her son-in-law displayed such little foresight in planning his days. Often, during the fortnight he had spent under her roof, when asked how he intended to spend his afternoon, he had answered paradoxically, "Oh, I think for a change I'll just save it instead of spending it." Once, when she and May had been forced to endure a long-postponed round of afternoon calls, he had confessed to lying all afternoon beneath a rock on the beach below the house.

"Newland never seems to look ahead," Mrs. Welland had once ventured to complain to her daughter. May had answered serenely, "No; but you see it doesn't matter, because when there is nothing particular to do, he reads a book."

"Ah, yes—like his father!" Mrs. Welland had agreed, treating it as an inherited oddity, after which the subject of Newland's idleness was tacitly dropped.

Nevertheless, as the day for the Sillerton reception approached, May began to show a natural concern for his welfare, suggesting a tennis match at the Chiverses' or a sail on Julius Beaufort's cutter as a means of atoning for her temporary absence. "I shall be back by six, you know, dear; Papa never drives later than that." She was not reassured until Archer declared his intention of hiring a runabout and driving up the island to a stud-farm to inspect a second horse for her brougham. They had been searching for this horse for some time, and the suggestion was so agreeable that May glanced at her mother as if to say, "You see, he knows how to plan his time as well as any of us."

The notion of the stud-farm and the brougham horse had taken root in Archer's mind on the very day the Emerson Sillerton invitation was first mentioned, yet he had kept it secret, as if the plan itself were clandestine and discovery might thwart its execution. He had, however, taken the precaution of securing a runabout with a pair of old livery-stable trotters capable of covering eighteen miles on level roads. At two o'clock, hastily leaving the luncheon table, he sprang into the light carriage and drove off.

The day was perfect. A breeze from the north drove small puffs of white cloud across an ultramarine sky, beneath which the sea ran bright and clear. Bellevue Avenue was empty at that hour. After dropping the stable-lad at the corner of Mill Street, Archer turned down Old Beach Road and drove across Eastman's Beach.

He possessed a feeling of unexplained excitement, reminiscent of starting off into the unknown on half-holidays at school. Taking his pair at an easy gait, he calculated that he would reach the stud-farm, located not far beyond Paradise Rocks, before three o'clock. Thus, after examining the horse—and testing him if he seemed promising—he would still have four golden hours to dispose of.

As soon as he heard of the Sillerton party, he had told himself that the Marchioness Manson would certainly arrive in Newport with the Blenkers, offering Madame Olenska another opportunity to spend the day with her grandmother. At any rate, the Blenker habitation would likely be deserted, allowing him to satisfy a vague curiosity without indiscretion. He was not certain that he wished to see the Countess Olenska again; yet, ever since he had looked at her from the path above the bay, he had felt an irrational, indescribable desire to see the place where she lived, to follow the movements of her imagined figure as he had once watched the real one in the summer-house. This longing persisted day and night, an incessant, undefinable craving, akin to the sudden whim of a sick man for food or drink once tasted and long since forgotten. He could not envision beyond the craving, nor picture where it might lead, for he was not conscious of any wish to speak to Madame Olenska or hear her voice. He simply felt that if he could carry away the vision of the spot of earth she walked upon, and the way the sky and sea enclosed it, the rest of the world might seem less empty.

Upon reaching the stud-farm, a quick glance revealed that the horse was not what he desired. Nevertheless, he took a turn behind it to prove to himself that he was not in a hurry. But at three o'clock, he shook out the reins over the trotters and turned into the by-roads leading to Portsmouth. The wind had died down, and a faint haze on the horizon indicated that a fog was waiting to steal up the Saconnet on the turn of the tide; yet all around him, fields and woods were steeped in golden light.

He drove past grey-shingled farmhouses nestled in orchards, past hayfields and groves of oak, past villages with white steeples rising sharply into the fading sky. Finally, after stopping to ask the way from some men working in a field, he turned down a lane bordered by high banks of goldenrod and brambles. At the end of the lane lay the blue glimmer of the river. To the left, standing before a clump of oaks and maples, he saw a long, dilapidated house with white paint peeling from its clapboards.

On the roadside facing the gateway stood one of the open sheds where New Englanders shelter their farming implements and where visitors "hitch" their "teams." Archer jumped down, led his pair into the shed, and after tying them to a post, turned toward the house. The patch of lawn before it had reverted to a hayfield, but to the left, an overgrown box-garden filled with dahlias and rusty rose-bushes encircled a ghostly summer-house of trellis-work that had once been white, surmounted by a wooden Cupid who had lost his bow and arrow but continued to take ineffectual aim.

Archer leaned for a while against the gate. No one was in sight, and not a sound emanated from the open windows of the house; a grizzled Newfoundland dozing before the door seemed as ineffectual a guardian as the arrowless Cupid. It was strange to think that this place of silence and decay was the home of the turbulent Blenkers, yet Archer was sure he was not mistaken.

For a long time he stood there, content to absorb the scene, gradually falling under its drowsy spell. But eventually, he roused himself to the awareness of passing time. Should he look his fill and then drive away? He stood irresolute, wishing suddenly to see inside the house, to picture the room where Madame Olenska sat. There was nothing preventing him from walking up to the door and ringing the bell; if, as he supposed, she was away with the rest of the party, he could easily give his name and ask permission to enter the sitting-room to write a message.

Instead, he crossed the lawn and turned toward the box-garden. As he entered it, he caught sight of something brightly colored in the summer-house, and presently made it out to be a pink parasol. The parasol drew him like a magnet; he was certain it was hers. He went into the summer-house and, sitting on the rickety seat, picked up the silken object and examined its carved handle, crafted from some rare wood that emitted an aromatic scent. Archer lifted the handle to his lips.

He heard a rustle of skirts against the box and sat motionless, leaning on the parasol handle with clasped hands, letting the rustle approach without lifting his eyes. He had always known this must happen...

"Oh, Mr. Archer!" exclaimed a loud, young voice. Looking up, he saw before him the youngest and largest of the Blenker girls, blonde and blowsy, dressed in bedraggled muslin. A red blotch on one of her cheeks suggested it had recently been pressed against a pillow, and her half-awakened eyes stared at him with a hospitable but confused warmth.

"Gracious—where did you drop from? I must have been sound asleep in the hammock. Everybody else has gone to Newport. Did you ring?" she inquired incoherently.

Archer's confusion was greater than hers. "I—no—that is, I was just going to. I had to come up the island to see about a horse, and I drove over hoping to find Mrs. Blenker and your visitors. But the house seemed empty, so I sat down to wait."

Miss Blenker, shaking off the fumes of sleep, looked at him with increasing interest. "The house *is* empty. Mother isn't here, nor the Marchioness—or anybody but me." Her glance became faintly reproachful. "Didn't you know that Professor and Mrs. Sillerton are giving a garden-party for Mother and all of us this afternoon? It was too unlucky that I couldn't go; but I've had a sore throat, and Mother was afraid of the drive home this evening. Did you ever know anything so disappointing? Of course," she added gaily, "I shouldn't have minded half as much if I'd known you were coming."

Signs of a lumbering coquetry became visible in her, and Archer found the strength to interrupt: "But Madame Olenska—has she gone to Newport too?"

Miss Blenker looked at him with surprise. "Madame Olenska? Didn't you know she'd been called away?"

"Called away?"

"Oh, my best parasol! I lent it to that goose of a Katie because it matched her ribbons, and the careless thing must have dropped it here. We Blenkers are all like that... real Bohemians!"
