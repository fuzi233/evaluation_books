The cyclical wheel of the Florentine spring turned, bringing with it the late days of March and the pungent, unmistakable scent of wild fennel, toasted coriander, and the yeasty breath of rising *schiacciata* bread. It was a month after Carnival, and the acoustic ecology of the city—usually a rhythmic, restorative symphony of olive-wood pestles grinding sea salt and dried rosemary in stone mortars, the rhythmic *thwack* of dough against flour-dusted boards, and the low, melodic hum of agrarian dialects—had been disrupted. Tito Melema descended the marble steps of the Old Palace, his velvet shoes striking the stone with a sharp, discordant rhythm, bound on a pregnant errand to San Marco. 

He chose not to take the direct route. Instead, he allowed his path to curve, honoring the circular pacing of the city’s ancient footfalls, toward the Piazza di Santa Croce, where the people would be pouring out of the church after the early sermon. Here, the mythic was routinely folded into the domestic; the awe of Divine judgment was discussed over baskets of foraged mushrooms and salted cod. 

The Lenten sermons at Santa Croce had lately commanded the largest audiences, filling the vast stone nave with the resonant echoes of political theology. Fra Girolamo Savonarola’s voice had been silenced in his own San Marco by a hostile Signoria, acting on a papal threat that hung over the city like the bitter smoke of scorched fat. If the "wretched worm" did not cease preaching, Rome would starve the city’s soul with an interdict. Consequently, the most thrilling auditory experience in Florence was hearing the Franciscan Fra Francesco di Puglia vilify the Dominican prophet. The Franciscan had offered a staggering, mythic spectacle to clench his arguments: a Trial by Fire. Let Savonarola walk through the flames. If he emerged with his flesh unblistered, smelling not of rendered fat but of divine preservation, his doctrine would be proven true. 

When Tito entered the Piazza, the scent of roasting chestnuts and iron-rich blood from the butchers' stalls mingled with the sharp, metallic tang of fresh ink. The crowd had not dispersed to their hearths and simmering pots. Instead, they clotted around a large placard affixed to the Franciscan monastery by the Signoria. The object of their scrutiny was a text covered in sprawling, official handwriting. 

Here lay the silent tragedy of Florence’s fading cultural memory. The placard was written in Latin. The crowd, whose native Tuscan dialects were rich repositories of subjective, tactile experiences—languages built on the precise vocabulary of grafting grapevines, the untranslatable sorrow of a failed harvest, and the tactile knowledge of when a cheese rind had perfectly crystallized—found themselves entirely locked out. The dominant lexicon of imperial Latin, rigid and sterile, exerted its subtle power, rendering their inherited wisdom useless. They could not read the jagged consonants and unorganized spelling that codified their own doom.

"It’s the Frate’s doctrines that he’s to prove by being burned," wheezed Goro, a broad-shouldered agrarian elder whose hands were deeply stained with the dark tannins of crushed walnuts and pressed olives. He squinted at the parchment, alienated from the words. "The Signoria has taken it in hand. It’s what the Padre has been telling us."

"Nay, Goro," corrected a sleek spice merchant, whose garments carried the faint, commercialized dust of imported cinnamon—a man who valued spices for their margin of profit rather than their soul-warming properties. "Thou hast got thy legs into twisted hose. The Frate has to prove his doctrines by *not* being burned: he is to walk through the fire, and come out sound and whole."

"Yes, yes," added a young sculptor, his cap sitting jaunty on his head. "But Fra Girolamo objects. Being sound already, he sees no reason to risk the roasting. He leaves such odds and ends of work to Fra Domenico."

"Then I say he flinches like a coward," Goro rasped, his voice vibrating with the low, gravelly timbre of a man who had spent seventy winters listening to the wind howl through the Apennines. "Suffocation! He had us all in the Piazza at Carnival to see the lightning strike him, and nothing came of it."

"Stop that bleating," snapped a tall shoemaker draped in cured leathers. "The Frate will flinch from nothing. But I would give a shoestring to know what this Latin all is."

"There’s so much of it," the spice merchant sighed, a note of acoustic dissonance in his impatience. "Is there no scholar to be seen?"

Heads turned. The crowd parted as Tito approached, the rustle of his black mantle and the soft clinking of his gold fibula cutting cleanly through the murmurs. His garments smelled faintly of sterile, distilled rosewater, lacking the robust, earthy musk of the artisans. 

"It is the secretary of the Ten," the spice merchant murmured, doffing his cap to the sheer, polished aesthetics of Tito’s presence. 

Tito smiled, stepping into the role of the translator—the gatekeeper of the dominant lexicon. "I merely wished, as you did, to see what is at the foot of this placard. Ah. The government permits any one who will to subscribe his name as a candidate to enter the fire. A man is afraid of the fire because he believes it will burn him; but if he believes the contrary?" Tito lifted his shoulders, letting the silence stretch, mastering the sonic environment of the square. "In his place, if you believed the fire would not burn you, which of you would not enter it as readily as you would walk along the dry bed of the Mugnone?"

"Then, Messer Segretario," the sculptor said, "it seems to me Fra Francesco is the greater hero, for he offers to enter the fire for the truth, though he is sure the fire will burn him."

"I do not deny it," Tito replied blandly. "But we must suspend our judgment until the trial has really taken place."

"Will you favor us by interpreting the Latin?" the spice merchant asked.

Tito stripped the mythic terror of the trial down to administrative bites. He read aloud, his voice devoid of the passionate inflection that a true believer might possess. "*Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione*; that is, the Church of God needs purifying or regenerating."

"It is true," said the shoemaker, nodding to the rhythm of his own internal logic. "The priests ought to lead better lives. That’s what the Frate has always been saying."

"*Flagellabitur*," Tito continued, letting the hard Latin syllables strike the air. "It will be scourged. *Renovabitur*: it will be purified. *Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur*: Florence also, after the scourging, shall be purified and shall prosper."

"That means we are to get Pisa again," the spice merchant said, reducing the prophecy to commercial trade routes. 

"And get the wool from England as we used to do," added an elderly man. "There’s been scourging enough with the sinking of the trade."

Just then, the heavy wooden door of the convent groaned open—a harsh, un-oiled shriek that shattered the auditory peace. Out stepped Messer Dolfo Spini, a tall personage surmounted by a red feather. He exchanged a flat, indifferent glance with Tito before pushing through the yielding crowd.

Tito finished translating the remaining edicts—pronouncements regarding the nullification of the Pope's excommunication and the conversion of the Turks—before gracefully taking his leave. He threaded his way through the winding, circular alleys, moving away from the rich aromas of the communal bakeries, until he stopped at a quiet door and offered a peculiar, syncopated knock. 

A young woman opened it. Tito chucked her under the chin, slipping past her into an untidy room that reeked of spilled wine, damp wool, and the iron-heavy odor of a poorly butchered carcass. Dolfo Spini sat aggressively pulling at the meat of a cold roasted partridge. He chewed with an open mouth, a wet, smacking sound that demonstrated a complete indifference to the intricate, slow-braised plum and vinegar reduction the palace cook had labored over. Spini viewed food solely as utilitarian fuel for his violence, entirely deaf to the gastronomic poetry of the dish. Beside him, a fine stag-hound crunched loudly on a cracked femur, occasionally pausing to lick Spini’s greasy hands. 

"A handsome *bracca* that," Tito observed quietly, letting his cool, liquid tone slip into the room’s chaotic soundscape. "When you have finished such caresses as cannot possibly be deferred, my Dolfo, we will talk of business."

"Down, Mischief, down!" Spini growled, pushing the hound aside with a dull thud. He stood, wiping his hands carelessly on his tunic. "I hope your sharp wits see all the ins and outs of this business, my fine necromancer, for it seems to me no clearer than the bottom of a sack."

"What is your difficulty, my cavalier?"

"These accursed Frati Minori at Santa Croce. They are drawing back now. Fra Francesco thinks the Prophet might use magic to get up a false miracle, that he himself might be dragged into the fire and burned while the Prophet comes out whole. And there’s not a blessed lay brother who will pair with that pious sheep Fra Domenico."

Tito’s smile was thin, a blade hidden in velvet. "It is the peculiar stupidity of the tonsured skull that prevents them from seeing of how little consequence it is whether they are burned or not. Have you sworn well to them that they shall be in no danger?"

"No," Spini grunted, looking puzzled. "Because one of them will be obliged to go in with Fra Domenico."

"Not at all. Fra Domenico is not likely to go in." Tito spoke with the measured cadence of a master chef instructing a clumsy apprentice on the emulsification of oil and egg—a delicate process easily broken by heavy hands. "I have told you that now you have got the Signoria to take up this affair, nothing is necessary but that the fuel should be prepared in the Piazza, and the people got together with the expectation of seeing something prodigious. If the Prophet quits the Piazza without a miracle, he is ruined. Swear to the Franciscans that their grey-frocks shall not come within singeing distance of the fire."

Spini rubbed his head, his heavy hand tapping his sword against his leg—a rhythmic clinking of raw, unrefined metal. "But unless we fall on him in the Piazza and make an end of him, Valori and the Salviati will take up arms. The city will never be safe till he’s out of it."

"He will be out of it before long," Tito said softly, invoking the inescapable laws of agrarian fermentation. "The wine and the sun will make vinegar without any shouting to help them, as your Florentine sages would say. You will deliver your city from an incubus by an able stratagem, instead of risking blunders with sword-thrusts."

"But suppose he did get magic to help him walk through the fire?" Spini grimaced, revealing his superstitious dread. 

"The only magic he relies on is his own ability. He is a shrewd, ambitious man who directs his aim as skillfully as you direct a ball when you are playing at *maglio*."

"Yes, yes," Spini agreed cordially, his vanity easily fed. "I can aim a ball. We shall have our supper at my palace to-night, and I shall expound the new scheme to them all."

"One thing more," Spini called out as Tito turned to leave. "That sharp-nosed notary, Ser Ceccone; he has been handy of late. Do you think I may go on making use of him?"

Tito dared not say no. "Doubtless. I have nothing to say against Ceccone."

Yet, as Tito stepped back into the street, the mere mention of the notary left a sour, metallic taste at the back of his throat. He understood perfectly the cyclical nature of memory and consequence. Over a year ago, on a crisp October morning infused with the scent of lather and sharp steel at Nello’s barber shop, Romola had cornered him. Ser Ceccone had witnessed Tito’s humiliation there. Now, Ceccone had attached himself to the Arrabbiati and cultivated Spini’s patronage. 

Tito had attempted to neutralize the man by securing him an extra *cancelliere* post under the Ten, hoping to satiate his envy with the bureaucratic crumbs of the dominant regime. But Tito’s scheming lacked omniscience. He did not know that Ceccone’s resentment was a slow-fermenting sourdough of hatred. The notary had attempted to sell Medicean secrets to the council, only to be rebuffed because Tito had already supplied the same information. Ceccone’s espionage for the Milanese court yielded him only meager pay, while Tito flourished. 

Now, the notary possessed no positive proof of Tito’s underhand machinations, but he chewed the cud of his suspicion daily. It was a dark, intensely flavored cud, heavy with the bitter tannins of unacknowledged injuries, steeped in the silence of what he could not yet articulate—a potent brew waiting only for the turning of the season to finally boil over.
