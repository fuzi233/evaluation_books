Ethne had intended to slip away unseen, but Mrs. Adair was already waiting. Seated upon the terrace in the cool shadow of the house, near the open window of the drawing-room, she watched Ethne cross the stone floor and sprint down the steps into the garden. The suddenness of her departure struck Mrs. Adair as peculiar—a desperate flight rather than a casual stroll.

From her vantage point, Mrs. Adair had witnessed the entire sequence. She had seen Ethne extinguish the lamp, plunging the room into darkness. The transformation from light to shadow, suggesting a clandestine intimacy, had been a torment to Mrs. Adair. Yet, instead of fleeing the discomfort, she remained seated, listening. As the faint strains of music drifted out onto the garden—filled with a thrilling happiness and a note of yearning—followed by hushed whispers, a jealous fire ignited within Mrs. Adair. Then, abruptly, Ethne took to her heels.

Mrs. Adair quickly dismissed the idea of a quarrel; she knew Ethne was not the type to fight, nor to flee if caught in a dispute. Yet, something else occurred. Inside the darkened room, Durrance continued to speak. Though the words were indistinct, the tone reached Mrs. Adair's ears clearly. It was evident he believed Ethne was still present.

Rising silently on the tips of her toes, Mrs. Adair approached the open window. She heard Durrance laughing lightly, recounting his triumphs with the satisfaction of a man who has finally unraveled a complex knot.

"I began to discover," he was saying, "even on that first afternoon at Hill Street two months ago, that there was only friendship on your side. My blindness aided me. With your face and your eyes before me, I would have believed unquestioningly whatever you wished me to. But you no longer possessed those defenses. I, on my part, grew sharper. I began to see. For the first time in my life, I began to see."

Mrs. Adair held her breath. Durrance spoke as if expecting no reply, reliving the relief of his realization.

"I should have ended our engagement immediately," he continued. "For I still believed, as firmly as ever, that there must be more than friendship between us. But I had grown selfish. I warned you, Ethne, that selfishness is a blind man's particular failing. I waited, I deferred the wedding, I made excuses. I led you to believe there was hope for my sight when there was none. I hoped, as men do, that time might transform your friendship into love. As long as that chance existed, I could not let you go. I listened for a new softness in your voice, a buoyancy in your laughter, a deeper resonance in your playing... How much I longed for it! Well, tonight I have burned my boats. I admitted to you that I knew your thoughts were limited to friendship. I confessed that my sight cannot be restored. I even revealed what I have hidden for so long: my meeting with Harry Feversham and the danger he faced. Why? Because for the first time tonight, I heard exactly what I waited for. The new pride in your voice, the lightness in your laughter—they were audible all evening. The tension was gone. When you played, it was as though someone with your skill allowed their heart to speak freely through the music, something you have never done before. Ethne, Ethne!"

At that precise moment, Ethne was already in the secluded garden where she had met Captain Willoughby earlier that day. Surrounded by privacy, accompanied only by her collie, she sought the silence that had become essential to her. If Durrance had uttered just a few more words, her carefully constructed facade of affection—the wall she had built around him—would have crumbled. Durrance was already peering over that wall, staring at her with amazement, but Ethne did not know this. To prevent him from seeing the truth, she had fled.

Moonlight lay silver upon the creek; the tall trees stood dreamily under the stars; the gentle lap of the tide sounded softer than a river's song. Ethne sat on a bench, trying to absorb the tranquility of the summer night, seeking lessons in patience and perseverance from the surrounding nature. But the day's events had exhausted her. Earlier that morning, amidst the same garden, she had received good news and reclaimed Harry Feversham. Tonight, however, the bad news arrived, and she feared losing him forever.

Harry Feversham intended to pay for his past faults to the utmost limit, and Ethne cried out against his rigidity, a trait she realized she had taught him.

"Surely," she thought, "he could have been satisfied. By redeeming his honor in the eyes of one of the three concerned, he has redeemed it in the eyes of all."

Yet, he had traveled south to join Colonel Trench at Omdurman. Of that squalid, shadowless town, of its barbarities and horrors, Ethne knew nothing. But Captain Willoughby had hinted enough to fill her imagination with terror. He had offered to explain the realities of imprisonment in Omdurman, and she remembered wringing her hands in refusal. What cruelties awaited him? Perhaps even now, on this summer night, he was suffering—but she dared not let her thoughts drift that way.

The rhythmic lapping of the tide reminded her of a specific river that had sung in her ears five years ago, watching out another summer night until dawn. Never had she hungered so intensely for her homeland, for the companionship of its brown hills and streams. Not even this afternoon, when she had watched the lights flicker on the creek, had she felt such a pull toward Donegal. It held a sanctity for her, setting her apart from earthly taints. As her heart yearned outward, a fierce loathing arose within her for the deceptions, the shifts, and the maneuvers she had practiced—and still must practice. Great weariness settled upon her, yet her resolve remained unshaken. Two lives would not be ruined simply because she lived in the world. Tomorrow, she could gather her strength and begin anew. Durrance must never know that another occupied the foremost place in her thoughts.

Meanwhile, inside the drawing-room, Durrance concluded his confession.

"So you see," he said, "I could not mention Harry Feversham until tonight. I feared that what I had to tell you would hurt you deeply. I feared that, despite five years, you still remembered him. I knew, of course, that you were my friend, but I doubted whether in your heart you loved him more than me. Tonight, however, I could speak without fear."

Now, he expected an answer. Mrs. Adair, still standing by the window, heard him shift in the shadows.

"Ethne!" he called out, surprised by the lack of response. Rising, he walked toward the chair where Ethne had been sitting. Mrs. Adair could now see him clearly. His hands groped for the back of the chair; he bent over it, assuming she was leaning forward with her hands on her knees.

"Ethne," he said again, and this time his voice held more trouble and doubt than surprise. It seemed to Mrs. Adair that he dreaded finding her silently weeping. He began to wonder if his inference—that Ethne's recent recapture of her youth signaled a thaw in her feelings—was correct. Was the shadow of Feversham truly falling between them? He leaned closer, reaching out with his hand, when suddenly a string of Ethne's violin twanged loudly. She had left it resting on the chair, and his fingers had brushed it.

Durrance straightened up, frozen in shock, bewildered. He passed his hand across his forehead twice, then, without calling out again, moved toward the open window.

Mrs. Adair held her breath. There was only the width of the sill separating them. Moonlight struck fully upon Durrance's face, and she saw comprehension dawning as he realized someone stood beside him.

"Ethne," he said for the third time, appealing now. He stretched out a hand timidly and touched her dress.

"It is not Ethne," he exclaimed with a start.

"No, it is not Ethne," Mrs. Adair replied quickly.

Durrance stepped back from the window, silent for a moment. "Where has she gone?" he asked eventually.

"Into the garden. She ran across the terrace and down the steps very quickly and silently. I saw her from my chair. Then I heard you speaking alone."

"Can you see her now in the garden?"

"No; she crossed the lawn toward the trees and their great shadows. There is only moonlight in the garden now."

Durrance stepped across the window sill and stood beside Mrs. Adair. Ethne's sudden, unexplained flight had betrayed her to the man who had grown sharp-sighted. There could be only one reason for her behavior. He had told her that Feversham had wandered south from Wadi Halfa into savage territory, speaking his fears openly, believing she had forgotten him and perhaps blaming her for her apparent callousness. That callousness was merely a mask; she had fled because she no longer had the strength to maintain it. His initial suspicions were correct. Feversham still stood between Ethne and Durrance, holding them at arm's length.
