Even more peculiar than the mundane trickling of a standard faucet mimicking the very air around it was the secondary effect it produced: it ignited, or perhaps merely revealed, an unnatural hypersensitivity to the ambient noises of the old house within Oleron. It is often noted that silence achieves its most profound weight only when shattered by the faintest whisper; yet, truth be told, this place was rarely truly silent. Perhaps the gentle warmth of the spring air had loosened the torpor of its ancient timber bones, or maybe Oleron's own hearth fires encouraged the structure to stretch its weary frame. Certainly, a teeming universe of insect life drilled and burrowed through its beams and joists. Regardless of the cause, Oleron required only to sit quietly in his chair and wait a brief moment to perceive a shift in the auditory landscape akin to a man who believes the midsummer woods to be utterly still, only to suddenly find his ears attuned to the crackling of a million tiny lives. A smile touched his lips as he considered humanity's arbitrary division between the living and the dead. Here, distinct from recognizable sounds like the scurrying of mice, the crumbling of plaster behind paneling, or the popping of embers from the fire, lay an entire house speaking to him—if only he possessed the key to its tongue. Beams settled with a weary sigh into their mortises; creatures ticked rhythmically within the walls; joints cracked, boards groaned; and though the air remained still, window sashes shifted position with a soft knock against their frames. Whether the dwelling possessed life in this literal sense or not, it undeniably held a charming personality. Within an hour of contemplation, Oleron conceived the notion that just as his physical form stood in a harmonious relationship with his soul, so too might his residence, through some fantastical extension and attenuation, exist in a similar rapport with itself. He even amused himself with the far-fetched fantasy that he might so completely identify with the place that a future occupant, upon taking possession, would regard it as haunted. It would be a cruel irony if he, a perfectly harmless author burdened only by the necessity of restarting a novel, inadvertently laid the groundwork for a future specter.

Yet, in direct proportion to this deepening attachment to the fabric of his home, Elsie Bengough's initial indifference curdled into a pronounced aversion, which she was not above voicing openly. "This place does not belong to today at all, and for someone like you, it is particularly ill-omened," she declared with firm conviction. "You are far too prone to release your grip on tangible realities and drift into apathy; *you* require a setting with concrete floors, a modern gas meter, and an elevator serviced by tradesmen. It would do you immense good to secure employment that forces you to scramble and jostle among your fellow humans. Now, if I could arrange a job for you—perhaps two or three days a week—that leaves ample time for your true vocation, would you accept it?"

Somehow, the clinical diagnosis stung Oleron slightly. He thanked Miss Bengough, though without a smile. "Thank you, but I do not believe so. After all, each of us must lead his own life," he added, unable to suppress the sentiment. "His own life to live!..."

"How long has it been since you stepped outside, Paul?" she pressed.

"Approximately two hours."

"I do not mean buying stamps or posting letters. How long has it been since you had anything resembling a real outing?"

"Oh, some time, perhaps. I am unsure."

"Since I was last here?"

"I have not ventured out much."

"And has *Romilly* progressed significantly better while you have been confined here?"

"I believe she has. I am laying the foundations. I shall commence the actual writing shortly."

It appeared Miss Bengough had momentarily forgotten their earlier struggle regarding the first draft of *Romilly*. She frowned, turned partially away, and then swiftly pivoted back. "Ah!... So you still harbor that ridiculous notion in your head?"

"If you mean," Oleron replied slowly, "that I have discarded the old *Romilly* and am working on a new iteration, then yes, you are correct. That idea remains firmly in my head."

Something discordant in his tone struck her, yet she was a fighter. His own absurd sensitivity only hardened her resolve. She emitted a sharp "Pshaw!" of impatience. "Where is the original?" she demanded abruptly.

"Why?" Oleron asked.

"I wish to see it. I intend to show portions of it to you. I want, if you are not entirely lost in reverie, to bring you back to your senses."

This time, it was Oleron who turned his back. However, when he rounded upon her again, his voice had softened. "It is futile, Elsie. I am responsible for the direction I take, and you must permit me to proceed—even if it appears erroneous to you. Believe me, I am giving it serious consideration.... The manuscript? I was on the verge of burning it, but I did not. It lies in that window-seat, should you insist on viewing it."

Miss Bengough hastened to the window-seat and lifted the lid. Suddenly, she let out a small cry and pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. Speaking over her shoulder, she said, "You ought to hammer those nails in, Paul."

He strode to her side instantly. "What? What is it? What is the matter?" he asked, alarmed.

"I did hammer them in—or, rather, pull them out," she replied, displaying her hand. From her upper wrist down to the knuckle of her little finger, a swelling, crimson wound was visible.

"Good gracious!" Oleron exclaimed.

"Here, come to the bathroom and wash it immediately—"

He hurried her to the bathroom, turned on the tap, and bathed and cleansed the deep gash. Still holding her hand, he switched to cold water, uttering fragmented phrases of astonishment and concern. "Good heavens, how did that occur! To the best of my knowledge, I had... is this water too cold? Does that hurt? I cannot fathom how on earth... there; that will suffice—"

"No—one moment longer—I can endure it," she murmured, her eyes shut tight.

Presently, he led her back to the sitting-room and bound the hand with one of his handkerchiefs; yet, his face retained an expression of genuine perplexity. He had spent half a day opening and rendering serviceable the three window boxes, and he could not comprehend how he had failed to notice a rusted nail protruding an inch and a half from the wood. He himself had opened the lids of each box a dozen times without detecting any nail; yet there it was.

"It shall be removed now, at any rate," he muttered as he fetched a pair of pliers. There was no doubt about it this time.

Elsie Bengough had sunk into a chair, her face pale; but clutched in her uninjured hand was the manuscript of *Romilly*. She had not yet finished with *Romilly*. Soon, she resumed her charge.

"Oh, Paul, it will be the greatest mistake you have ever, *ever* made if you do not publish this!" she cried.

He hung his head, genuinely distressed. He could not shake the image of the nail from his mind, and *Romilly* took a secondary place in his thoughts for the moment. Yet she persisted; and when he finally spoke, it was almost as if he were asking her forgiveness for something. "What can I say, Elsie? I can only hope that when you view the new version, you will see how right I am. And if, despite everything, you still do not like her, well..." He made a helpless gesture. "Do you not realize that I *must* be guided solely by my own judgment?"

She fell silent.

"Come, Elsie," he said gently. "We have gotten along well thus far; let us not fracture over this."

The words had barely cleared his lips before he regretted them. She had been cradling her injured hand, her eyes closed once more; but her lips and eyelids trembled in unison. Her voice shook as she spoke. "I cannot help saying it, Paul, but you have changed so profoundly."

"Hush, Elsie," he murmured soothingly. "You have suffered a shock; rest for a while. How could I possibly change?"

"I do not know, but you have. You have not been yourself since arriving here. I wish you had never set eyes on this place. It has halted your work, it is transforming you into a stranger I hardly recognize, and it has rendered me terribly anxious for you.... Oh, how my hand begins to throb!"

"Poor child!" he whispered. "Shall I take you to a doctor to have it properly dressed?"

"No—I shall be fine soon—I will keep it elevated—"

She propped her elbow on the back of the chair, and the bandaged hand rested lightly upon his shoulder. At that contact, a wholly new anxiety surged within him. Hundreds of times previously, during their outings and excursions, she had slipped her hand into his arm as casually as a sister might, and he had accepted the affectionate gesture as a brother would. But now, for the first time, a hundred startling questions flooded his mind. Her eyes remained closed, her head fallen pathetically backward, and a lost, ineffable smile graced her parted lips.
