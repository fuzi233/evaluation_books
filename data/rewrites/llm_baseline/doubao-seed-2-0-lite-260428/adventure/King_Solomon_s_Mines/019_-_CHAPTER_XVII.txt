We were still catching our breath, picking our way through the grisly statues and shrouded dead of the Place of Death, when we noticed Gagool was missing from our huddle. The ancient witch, who could move with the speed of a hunting mongoose when she deigned to, had scrambled up onto the great stone table of the dead before any of us saw her, hobbling straight to where Twala’s corpse sat propped under the cold dripping water. Good snickered under his breath that she was going to check if the dead king was “pickling” properly, but we soon saw it was no trivial errand. She bent down to press her cracked lips to Twala’s icy mouth in a mockery of a loving greeting, then limped slowly back along the table, pausing every few steps to mutter familiar greetings to one shrouded king after another as if she was stopping by old friends’ parlors for tea.

When that strange, horrifying ritual was done, she squatted directly beneath the towering White Death statue, and began to chant low, guttural prayers that made the hair stand up on the back of my neck. We didn’t dare speak above a whisper in that charnel hush, and the sight of that wicked old thing pouring out dark supplications to whatever evil god she worshipped spurred us to speed our search.

“Gagool,” I called softly, “lead us to the treasure chamber now.”

She scrambled down the table as fast as a scuttling beetle, leering up at my face. “My white lords are not afraid, are they?”
“Lead on,” Sir Henry said, grim.

Gagool hobbled around the base of the great White Death statue, set her gourd full of oil down on the cave floor, and leaned against the rock wall. “Here is the chamber. Light your lamp, and enter.”

I pulled the last few remaining matches from our tin, lit the wick of our rush lamp, and stared at the smooth, unbroken solid rock in front of us. No door, no crack, nothing. Gagool cackled, a high, sharp sound that echoed off the stone walls. “I jest not, my lords. Look.”

As I held the lamp higher, we watched a slab of solid rock, nearly ten feet tall and five feet thick, rise slowly up from the floor, vanishing into a hidden cavity carved into the rock above. It must have weighed thirty tons at the least, moving on some ancient counterweight system as smooth as a modern sash window. None of us saw Gagool trigger it, of course — she’d made sure to shield the secret lever from our view. In seconds the whole slab had vanished, leaving a dark, gaping doorway in its wake.

My hands started shaking so hard the lamp guttered. Centuries of legend, the old Portuguese explorer Da Silvestra’s half-rotted map we’d carried across the desert and over the mountains, it all led to this. Were we really about to stand in Solomon’s treasure vault?

“Enter, white men from the stars,” Gagool croaked, stepping into the passage. “But first hear me. The bright stones you seek were dug from the great pit where the Silent Ones stand guard, stored here longer ago than even my memory reaches. Only one other outsider has ever breached this chamber since the builders fled in haste. A white man, over a thousand years ago, came over the mountains — that is the one who sits as the fifth king at the table of the dead.” She pointed back toward the Place of Death. “He came with a local woman, who stumbled by pure chance on the secret of this door. A thousand years of searching would never lead you to that secret, otherwise. The man went inside, filled a goat-skin bag the woman carried for food full of diamonds, and as he fled he grabbed one more large stone to hold in his hand. Then something terrified him so badly he dropped the entire goat-skin bag, and ran for his life with only that one big stone, which the king of that age seized. It is the very same stone you, Macumazahn, took from Twala’s forehead on the battlefield.”

We all froze, breathless. How could she possibly know that?
“Has no one else entered here since?” I asked, peering down the dark passage.
“None. The kings have kept the door’s secret, but none dared step inside. There is a curse: all who enter the chamber will die within a moon, just as that old white man died up in the mountain cave where you found his skeleton, Macumazahn. Enter, and you shall see the goat-skin bag still lying right where he dropped it — and you shall learn for yourselves if the curse holds true.”

She snatched the lamp from my hand and hobbled down the passage ahead of us. Good cursed, charged after her, Foulata following hard on his heels, shivering so hard we could see her fear even in the dim light. The passage was hewn straight out of living rock, and a few yards in we spotted half-height stacks of large stone blocks, left abandoned halfway through walling the treasure chamber off forever. A heap of ancient mortar and two trowels, still sharp-edged, sat untouched beside the blocks. Foulata went deathly pale then, said she could not go another step, her head was spinning. We set her down gently on the half-built wall, left our basket of provisions beside her to keep her company, and pushed on.

Fifteen paces further, we came to an elaborately carved painted wooden door, standing wide open. Slumped across its threshold was a tattered goat-skin bag, heavy with stones that jingled when Gagool nudged it with her foot. “Behold!” she cackled. “Just as I said. The white man fled so fast he left his loot behind.”

Good stooped to lift it, his eyes wide. “By Jove, that’s full of diamonds.”

Sir Henry grabbed the lamp out of Gagool’s hand, shouldered through the open doorway, and held the light high over his head. We crowded in after him, and for a second our breath caught. The whole chamber was hewn straight out of solid rock, stacked floor to domed ceiling with hundreds of top grade elephant tusks — more ivory than would make all three of us wealthy for life, enough to build a whole royal throne of ivory, just as the old Bible tales said Solomon had owned. On the far wall stood twenty red-painted wooden chests, the lids warped and rotted by time. I pushed my hand through the broken lid of the nearest one, and pulled out a handful of gold coins stamped with ancient Hebrew characters no one of us recognized. Thousands of them, stacked to the brim.

“Gagool is right,” the old witch cackled from the doorway, her voice sounding farther away than it had a second before. “The biggest bright stones are in the darkest nook over there. Three stone chests, two sealed, one left open.”

We hurried to the shadowed niche she pointed out, and Sir Henry held the lamp over the open stone chest. A wave of silvery light dazzled us for a moment before our eyes adjusted. It was three-quarters full of uncut diamonds, huge ones, that unmistakable soft soapy texture rubbing off on my palms when I reached in to grab a handful. Millions of pounds worth, at the very least. We were the richest men alive. We started laughing, wild, hysterical, giddy, as Gagool hopped behind us cackling that we could eat the diamonds, drink them, stuff our pockets full to bursting.

That was the last we saw of her for a full ten seconds.

A shriek cut through our laughter, sharp and desperate, coming from back down the passage. Foulata’s voice: “Bougwan! Help! The stone is falling! She stabbed me!”

We ran full tilt down the passage, and the light from the lamp showed us the great stone door was already closing, barely three feet above the ground. Foulata was bleeding from a stab wound to the gut, but she’d wrapped both her arms around Gagool’s waist, holding the squirming old witch back as she tried to crawl out under the falling slab. Gagool bit and scratched, broke free, and threw herself flat on the ground to slip under the narrowing gap. But it was too late. The tonnage of the rock slammed down on her midsection, pinning her to the floor. Her shriek was the most unholy sound any of us had ever heard, before a sick, wet crunch silenced her forever — and the door clicked shut, perfectly sealed, no gap left at all.

We fell on our knees beside Foulata. Her wound was fatal, she was dying fast. “Tell Bougwan I love him,” she gasped to me, her voice faint, as Good held her tight. “I know the sun cannot mate with the dark, and white cannot belong to black, but I have carried a bird in my chest ever since I first saw him, that will fly and search all the stars for him when we are gone. Even if I am still black, and he is still white.” She went limp in Good’s arms, and he wept, great heaving sobs, tears running down his face.

Then Sir Henry went dead still.
“Guys,” he said, quiet and grim. “We are buried alive.”

The truth hit us like a fist to the gut. The only person in the whole world who knew the secret to opening that door was Gagool — crushed to pulp under thirty tons of solid rock. There was no lever on the inside, no catch, no way to move five feet of living rock, not even with a hundred men pushing. We searched every inch of the passage walls, scraped at every crack, found nothing. The curse Gagool had joked about was no joke. She’d planned this trap for us from the very first day we stepped foot in Kukuanaland, determined to watch the three white men she hated slowly starve to death surrounded by all the treasure in the world.

We grabbed our basket of provisions, carried Foulata’s still, gentle body back into the treasure chamber, and laid her down beside the chests of gold. Our rations added up to four tiny portions of dried biltong each, two quarts of water total — barely enough to stretch two days, at most. The lamp began to gutter, the last of the oil running out. It flared bright for one last second, casting light over all the stacked ivory, the heaps of diamonds glinting in the dark, Foulata’s peaceful face, and the three of us gaunt, trapped, staring at the fortune that had become our tomb.

Then the flame died, and we were left in total, absolute blackness.
