The warm Florentine sun gilded the edge of Tito’s crimson tunic as he turned away from the Via de’ Bardi, light and giddy with a relief so complete it felt like he had shed lead shackles he had not even known he carried. For weeks he had carried the dread that his old life, and the enemy he had thought lost forever, would rise up to drag him down from the gilded future he had built beside Romola. Now that peril had vanished, swept away by lucky chance, and his mind, no longer occupied by the immediate presence of Romola and her blind father, turned back on the foolish, panicked choices he had made in those hours of terror.

Chief among them was the ring he had hocked for fifty-five florins to fend off starvation and capture. He would never have parted with it deliberately, he told himself now: the gem was so familiar to Romola and their circle that its absence would spark quiet questions unless he invented a graceful story to explain it, and at the moment he felt sick to death of spinning lies. He was safe now, he reminded himself. No harm had come of that first desperate deception, and Baldassarre, the ragged old man he had once called father, was surely lost somewhere far off, too broken and destitute to ever track him down. What was the harm in that? No one of consequence was hurt. He swore he would not entangle himself in any more hidden shackles, no matter how small, that might chafe at the perfect happiness he had won with Romola. He did not realize that very delight in escaping consequence was already dulling the soft sensibilities that might have stopped him from stumbling into new traps.

The sale of the ring was a small thing, all said and done. But what of little Tessa? The soft, round, innocent country girl was still wrapped up in the silly carnival farce that had led her to believe she was his wife, filling her small head with impossible, doomed hopes. Should he track her down alone and undeceive her at once? Or leave the disillusionment to time? Happy dreams were sweet, he reasoned, and they faded easy enough when the bright light of day and the noise of ordinary life came crashing in. The sweet, pouting little thing! He could not stop thinking of her. For a moment he considered bringing her a pretty gift, checking if her cruel stepfather was treating her any better now her mother was dead. He even toyed with the idea of telling Romola the whole silly story, so they could find the poor child a safe, happy home somewhere far from the abuse of her stepfamily.

No. That absurd farce of the cerretano carnival wedding, the way he had let Tessa drift away believing they were married, could never be allowed to reach Romola’s ears. Romola was too proud, too unyielding in her sense of honour, she would never forgive such a betrayal. And even soft little Tessa might grow bitter, once she realized the man she thought loved her was never coming back. It was better he never saw her again. The whole silly adventure would fade soon enough: some ruddy, broad-shouldered young contadino from the valley would court her, and she would forget the pretty Greek signor who had danced with her at the fair, and realize she was free to marry him. *Dunque*—goodbye, Tessa, he murmured to himself, half smiling. All the best to you. That trivial affair would have no further consequences for him. People were always very sure that resolutions made entirely in their own self-interest would hold fast.

As for the fifty-five florins from the ring, Tito already knew exactly how to spend a portion of them. He had hit on a clever, graceful little scheme that would let him explain the absence of the ring to Romola without a hint of sordidness, and even push back against the grim, monkish visions Savonarola had been filling her head with lately, the fancies that made her frown at their shelves of classical poetry. He would commission a custom painted artifact, something that honoured her father Bardo’s love of ancient fables, and tell Romola he had traded the ring to a master craftsman for the piece as a special gift. With that thought fixed in his mind, he set off for the Via Gualfonda, to find Piero di Cosimo: the wild, eccentric painter famous across Florence for the fantastical mythological scenes that were exactly what Tito needed.

When he stepped into the courtyard outside Piero’s home, he found the heavy iron door knocker thickly wrapped in wool, bound tight with ingenious knots of twine. Everyone in Florence knew Piero stuffed his ears with cotton wadding to block out the clatter of the street and the shouts of passersby, so Tito was not surprised at the door’s defense against the thunder of impatient visitors. He tapped gently at first, then shook the door harder, all to no avail. He was already turning to leave, annoyed at himself for wasting time when he could simply wait to see Piero at Nello’s bookshop later, when a tiny girl trotted into the courtyard with a basket of boiled eggs slung over her arm. She stood on tiptoe, slid open a small iron hatch set into the door, and pressed her mouth to the gap, piping out “Messer Piero!” at the top of her small voice.

In moments the bolts clattered, the door swung open, and Piero stood there in a frayed red nightcap, a shapeless loose brown serge tunic with the sleeves rolled all the way up to his shoulders. He darted a surprised look at Tito, but ignored him entirely, stretching out his hand to take the egg basket from the child, disappearing inside for a moment to return with the empty basket. “How much?” he asked.

“Two grossoni, Messer Piero. My mother says they’re all boiled fresh this morning.”

Piero fished the coins out of the leather purse at his belt, and the little girl trotted away, glancing back over her shoulder a few times in awed admiration at the handsome exotic young signor lingering by the door. Piero’s own glance was far less complimentary. “What do you want hanging around my door, Messer Greco? I saw you at Nello’s this morning. You could have asked then. I don’t let any man step foot in this house unless I know his business and we’ve agreed on it beforehand.”

“Pardon me, Messer Piero,” Tito replied, unshakably good-humoured as ever. “I acted without thinking. I was so caught up in admiring your famous skill for inventing charming, whimsical fancies that I came straight over the moment the idea struck me.”

Piero pulled his habitual grimace, the one he made whenever anyone flattered him: he grinned, stretched the corners of his mouth wide, and pressed his brows down so no one could possibly guess what he thought of the compliment. After a long pause he said, “What exactly do you need?” Secretly, he was already tempted by the chance to exercise his creative fancy on a new commission.

“I want a very delicate miniature design taken from the old poets’ fables, that only you would know how to combine to perfection. It will be painted on a small wooden case, I’ll show you the exact dimensions, in the form of a triptych. The inside can be simple gilding: all the art is on the outside. It’s a subject you Florentines adore—the triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne, but treated in a new way. Ovid’s story will give you the hints you need. Young Bacchus will be seated on the deck of a ship, his head bound with clusters of grapes, a spear twined with vine leaves in his hand. Dark-berried ivy will wind up the masts and over the sails, the oars will be shaped like thyrsus staffs, flowers will wreathe all around the stern. Leopards and tigers will crouch at his feet, dolphins leaping through the waves all around the hull. And I want fair-haired Ariadne right there beside him, made immortal with her starry golden crown—that’s not in Ovid, but no matter, you’ll conceive it perfectly. Overhead there will be your little winged Loves, the kind you paint so beautifully, shooting arrows tipped with rose petals.”

“Say no more!” Piero cut him off. “I’ve got the vernacular Ovid inside. I don’t care to be choked to death by other people’s ideas. Come in.”

Piero led him through the first room, where the basket of eggs sat on the open hearth next to a heap of empty eggshells and a mound of cold ash. Oddly out of place with that sordid litter stood a low carved ebony bed, draped carelessly with a scrap of rich oriental carpet that looked as if it had once covered the steps of a church Madonna’s throne, and a large carved painted cassone chest. There was barely any other furniture, just plaster casts, wooden steps, easels, and rough crates, all draped in fine cobwebs.

The second room was even larger, far more crowded. It looked as if Piero was keeping a festa to himself, for the double doors beneath the skylight that lit the studio were thrown open to a wild tangled garden, half thicket, where fig trees and vines grew trailing wild among nettles and hemlocks, a tall dark cypress rising out of a mass of stifling yellow mulberry leaves. It seemed as if that dank overgrown greenery was already creeping through the walls: tufts of long grass and feathery dark fennel pushed up through cracks in the dirt floor amid a heap of broken marble fragments and rusted armour, a large stone vase tilted on its side poured trailing ivy out across the flagstones. The walls were hung with pen and oil sketches of fantastic sea monsters, dances of satyrs and maenads, Saint Margaret climbing out of the devouring dragon, Madonnas glowing with holy light, studies of wild plants and grotesque, distorted faces. On rough uneven shelves a few books lay scattered among drooping bunches of dried corn, bullock horns, pieces of dried honeycomb, stones patched with rare coloured lichen, skulls and bones, peacock feathers and huge spread bird wings. Propped up out of the messy litter on the floor were lay figures: one dressed in the frock of a Vallombrosan monk, topped absurdly with a barred helmet, another smothered in hastily tossed brocade and animal skins. Pigeons perched and strutted unafraid, too tame to fly at the arrival of visitors, three fat toads crawled slowly near the doorstep, and a fluffy white rabbit, clearly the model for the small creature startling Cupid in the unfinished Mars and Venus on the central easel, twitched its nose contentedly on a crate full of bran.

“All right, Messer Greco,” Piero said, nodding Tito to a low stool by the door, then standing over him with his arms folded. “Don’t try to gawk at everything all at once like some overcurious saint. Tell me how big you want this triptych.” Tito indicated the dimensions, and Piero marked them down on a scrap of paper. “Now for the book,” Piero said, reaching for a handwritten manuscript volume.

“There’s no mention of Ariadne crowned there,” Tito said, pointing out the passage he wanted. “But remember: her likeness, her golden hair, right beside Bacchus.”

“Ha!” Piero said abruptly, pursing his lips. “You want them to be portraits, don’t you?” He stared straight down into Tito’s face.

Tito laughed, a faint flush rising to his cheeks. “I know you are unsurpassed at portraiture, Messer Piero. But I can’t ask Ariadne to sit for you, the painting is a secret gift.”

“Perfect! That works for me. Giovanni Vespucci wants me to paint Oedipus and Antigone at Colonus, just as he’s laid the story out for me. I’ve a great fancy for the subject, and I’ve been dying to get old blind Bardo and his daughter Romola to sit for the two figures. You ask them. If you get them to agree to sit, I’ll put Romola’s likeness straight into Ariadne’s face.”

“Agreed, if I can prevail on them. And what is your price for the Bacchus and Ariadne scene?”

“Bah! If you get them to let me paint them, that’s payment enough. I don’t want your florins. You can just pay for the wooden case itself.”

“When do you want me to sit for Bacchus?” Tito said, smiling. “We can hardly have Ariadne’s perfect likeness without the other half of the pair.”

“I don’t need you to sit,” Piero said. “I already have your likeness done. Only I made you look terrified. I’ve got to rub that fright out for the merry Bacchus.” As he spoke he laid down the book and rummaged through a stack of paintings propped face-first against the wall. He returned holding a small oil sketch. “I call this one of the best portrait bits I’ve ever painted,” he said, holding it out. “Yours is a bright, merry face, so it expresses fear better than any dour man’s. I noticed it the very first day I saw you. The rest of the painting is barely sketched, but I’ve painted you perfectly.”

Tito stared. There he was, his right hand raised holding a wine cup, caught mid gesture of triumphant revelry, but his face was turned away from the cup, his eyes wide and dilated, his lips pale with an expression of raw, unmasked, overwhelming terror. A cold trickle of dread ran down his spine, as if he was suddenly caught in the same paralyzing fear his painted self wore.

“You’re starting to look like the sketch already,” Piero said, with a short dry laugh, pulling the painting away. “That fine young Greek has just seen a ghost. I’ll finish it one day, when I work out which kind of ghost is most terrible—solid, like a dead man come back to drag you off, or half transparent, like a mist.”

Tito laughed, shaking off the strange, unwelcome prick of unease that warred with his usual easy self-possession. “That’s a subject made for you, Messer Piero: a merry revel interrupted by a ghost. You love blending the terrible with the gay. That’s why your shelves are full of death’s heads while you’re painting those roguish little Loves running off with Mars’ armour. I think you’re a secret cynic philosopher, hiding behind the mask of a clever painter.”

“Not at all, Messer Greco. A philosopher is the last sort of fool I’d ever want to be like. It’s enough for me to just live, without spinning lies to make up reasons for all the messy parts of life. Fowls cackle, asses bray, women chatter, philosophers spin false explanations—that’s what the sight of the world makes them do. Me? I’m just an animal that paints, instead of cackling or braying or lying.” He gave Tito a pointed look, nudging him gently toward the door. “Our business is done, then? You’ll keep your end of the bargain about the Oedipus and Antigone sittings?”

“I’ll do my best,” Tito said, getting the clear hint to leave immediately.

“Send word to me via Nello’s. No need to trudge all the way back out here again.”

“Understood,” Tito said, laughing, lifting his hand in a friendly wave as he stepped out into the courtyard, the memory of that painted face of terror lingering, unshakable, at the back of his mind.
