Durrance departed for his lodgings alone, the silence of the street amplifying the echo of a memory from two years prior. That day, born of a whimsical decree by the aged Dermod Eustace, had dragged him unwillingly to the Lennon River in Donegal, only to deposit him in the orbit of Dermod's daughter, Ethne. She was a paradox to those who first encountered her; she defied expectation. During the solitary night he spent in her father's house, she had performed on her violin, her back turned to the room, a posture dictated by a discipline that refused to allow a glance or a gesture to fracture the integrity of her concentration. Yet, the melodies she drew from the strings haunted Durrance now, resonating with a frequency that transcended the physical instrument. The girl possessed a rare alchemy; the violin responded to the questions posed by her bow, translating intent into sound.

Among these sounds lingered a specific overture—the *Melusine*—which carried within it the very sob of the crashing waves. That night, Durrance had sat transfixed, wondering how such an instrument could articulate truths unknown to its player. It had spoken of perilous voyages across silver seas, of beckoning voices rising from the desert's edge, of joys so lofty they seemed unattainable, and of griefs so profound they acquired a noble permanence. It voiced longings that eluded language, yet never once descended into mere complaint. It was a testament to a spirit untouched by petty grievance. So it had sounded that night, with Ethne's face averted; so it sounded now, as Durrance realized with a chilling clarity that her face would remain turned away from him for the entirety of his remaining days. From that performance, he extracted a singular axiom, one he guarded with fierce determination: True music does not complain. Consequently, as he mounted his horse the following morning to ride into the Row, his blue eyes surveyed the world from his bronzed visage with the same unblemished friendliness that had characterized him always.

He stationed himself at half-past nine beside the cluster of lilacs and laburnums at the Row's terminus, adhering to the sacred tryst established since their graduation from Oxford. Yet, Harry Feversham did not appear. Nor did he arrive for the subsequent three weeks. The rumors of war, previously a murmur, had hardened into a certainty, casting a shadow over the city. When Feversham finally kept the appointment, Durrance was prepared with news. "Luck has smiled upon me," Feversham announced, his voice vibrating with an exhilaration that ignited an unmistakable spark of envy in Durrance's eyes. "I depart for Egypt on General Graham's staff. Whispers suggest a descent down the Red Sea to Suakin follows."

"It is harsh on you," Durrance remarked, masking the pang of his own ambition with sympathy, "that your regiment remains behind."

Feversham rode in silence beside him, the weight of the moment pressing between them. Upon reaching the shaded alcove beneath the trees, Feversham broke the quiet. "That was anticipated. On the night of our dinner, I submitted my papers."

"That night?" Durrance queried, twisting in his saddle. "After we had departed?"

"Yes," Feversham conceded, accepting the correction. A question flitted through his mind regarding the intentionality of the timing, but Durrance rode onward, silent. Once more, Feversham sensed a reproach in his friend's silence, and once more, he misinterpreted it. Suddenly, Durrance laughed, a hearty sound that shattered the tension. "I recall. You provided your reasons that night. Yet, I cannot suppress the wish that we had ridden out together. When do you sail for Ireland?"

"To-night."

"So soon?"

They turned their horses westward, descending the avenue of trees. The morning air retained its freshness; the lime and chestnut trees held their verdant canopy, and since the May had arrived late, delicate white blossoms still hung like snow against the dark rhododendrons, glowing crimson in contrast. The park shimmered in a haze of sunlight, while the distant roar of the city mimicked the tumbling of river waters.

"It has been ages since we bathed in Sandford Lasher," Durrance observed.

"And froze in the Easter snow-gully on Great End," Feversham replied.

Both men felt the closing of a volume in their shared biography. Having enjoyed a pleasant interlude, they stood uncertain about the chapters to follow, gazing backward through the leaves before committing the book to storage.

"You must stay with us, Jack, when you return," Feversham urged.

Durrance had trained himself not to flinch, and he did not, even at the anticipatory "us." Should his left hand tighten upon the reins, the signal would escape detection. "If I return," Durrance stated simply. "You know my creed. I could never pity a man who dies on active service. I would very much like to meet that end myself."

It was a creed of stark simplicity, devoid of melancholy or foreboding. It posited that a death with dignity outweighed many years of life. Nevertheless, Durrance feared his friend might interpret these words differently, and he glanced sharply at Feversham. He saw only that enigmatic glint of envy in the other's eyes.

"There are worse fates," Durrance continued, dismissing the fear with a shake of his broad shoulders. "Disablement, for instance. Some clever souls might endure it. But what should I do if forced to sit in a chair for the remainder of my days? It sends a chill through me."

"Well, this is the final ride. Let us gallop."

Durrance released his horse, and Feversham followed suit. Side by side, they raced down the sand, the wind whipping around them. At the base of the Row, they halted, exchanged a handshake, and parted with the utmost courtesy. Feversham rode out of the park; Durrance turned back, walking his horse toward the seats beneath the boughs.

Even as a boy in Southpool, Devonshire, near the wooded creeks of the Salcombe estuary, Durrance had harbored a restless soul, dreaming of sailing past the familiar woods into outlandish realms. This restlessness had intensified, transforming "Guessens"—now an inheritance of farms and land—from a destination into a burden. He deliberately amplified this sentiment now, setting it against the truth Feversham had spoken: Ethne Eustace would find little happiness beyond her native Donegal. Thus, he reasoned, even if fate had woven a different tapestry, a collision of worlds might have occurred. Perhaps it was providential that Harry Feversham was destined to wed Ethne, and no other.

With these arguments cycling through his mind, the riders vanished from his sight, leaving behind the ladies in their colorful frocks beneath the tree line. The trees receded into ragged mimosas; the brown sand expanded into a golden circle; black stones piled up like coal, flashing like mirrors in the sun. Deep in his anticipation of the Sudan, Durrance heard a soft female voice calling his name. Looking up, he found himself beside the railway rails.

"How do you do, Mrs. Adair?" he asked, halting his horse.

Mrs. Adair extended her hand across the tracks. She was his neighbor in Southpool, a few years his senior—a tall woman distinguished by the myriad shades of her thick brown hair and a complexion of peculiar pallor. Yet, today, her face had brightened, flushed with color.

"I bear news for you," Durrance declared. "Two distinct items. First, Harry Feversham is to be married."

"To whom?" the lady asked, eager.

"You ought to know. It was in your Hill Street residence that Harry first encountered her; I facilitated the introduction. He has been cultivating the acquaintance in Dublin."

Mrs. Adair understood immediately; the news was received with visible relief. "Ethne Eustace!" she cried. "Will the marriage proceed swiftly?"

"There is no impediment."

"I am glad," she sighed, the tension draining from her frame. "And what is your second item?"

"As favorable as the first. I depart on General Graham's staff."

Silence descended. Anxiety clouded her eyes, and the color drained from her cheeks. "You are very glad, I presume," she said slowly.

Durrance's tone left no room for doubt. "I believe I am. I leave soon, and the sooner the better. I shall dine here one evening before I go, if permitted."

"My husband will be delighted to see you," Mrs. Adair replied, her voice cooling perceptibly.

Durrance overlooked the chill. He possessed his own motivations for seizing this fleeting opportunity; he poured forth his enthusiasm, speaking words that served more to fortify his own resolve than to inform the listener. Indeed, his knowledge of Mrs. Adair remained vague. She was handsome in a peculiar, coastal manner, possessing fine hair and impeccable dress, always friendly. Her primary merit, in his estimation, lay in her friendship with Ethne Eustace. Yet, he was destined to know her far better.

Riding away from the park, Durrance carried the old regret that his fortune and his friend's had been severed that morning. Unbeknownst to him, he had that very morning begun weaving a new strand of rope, one that would bind them together again in a strange and terrible relationship.
