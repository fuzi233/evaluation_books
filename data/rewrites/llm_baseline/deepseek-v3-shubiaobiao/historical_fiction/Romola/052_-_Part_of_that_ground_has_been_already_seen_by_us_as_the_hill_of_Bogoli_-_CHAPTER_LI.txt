Romola had not misled her cousin when she spoke of another awaiting her—yet she little guessed the depth of Brigida’s need. As they had turned toward the Piazza earlier, the pair had spotted the band of white-robed youths halting before Tessa. Seeing the girl’s distress, Romola murmured, *“Wait for me, cousin,”* only for Monna Brigida to blurt, *“Ah, no—I’ll retreat to Boni’s shop! Fetch me there.”*

The truth prickled beneath Brigida’s silks. Her rouge, her false coils, her pearl-trimmed *berretta*—vanities the Piagnone preachers called soul-traps—now weighed on her like stolen gold. The air itself seemed thick with judgment. Even Romola, radiant in youth, walked a path of stark simplicity that cast shadows over Brigida’s own indulgences.

At fifty-five, a widow whose joys had long been measured in admiring glances and the rustle of finery found her mirror a cruel foe. Each morning brought a battle: if she stripped away the paint and braids, would virtue alone suffice? But the glass showed sallow cheeks, sagging lines, hair like tarnished silver—and the thought of Monna Berta’s laughter (that harpy, barely fifty-two!) made her fingers clutch the rouge pot anew. Without artifice, men would address her as a grandmother; with it, she could still command a second glance. So the ritual endured—the crimsons, the curls, the necklaces artfully arranged to outshine Berta’s scrawny throat.

Yet today, as she prepared for the Piagnone Carnival, doubt had gnawed sharper. The struggle left her so shaken that she’d fled toward the druggist’s sanctuary rather than face the Anathema’s collectors without Romola’s shield.

Too slow. The white phalanx had marked her before she turned. Tessa’s swift surrender owed something to the greater prize: a woman jeweled and painted, ripe for salvation.

The vanguard was a chorus of youthful zeal. A cherub of ten, olive wreath crowning his innocent brow but eyes alight with mischief, barred her path. Behind him, a taller boy gripped a basket’s handle and intoned, *“Venerable mother! The blessed Christ commands you to surrender these Anathema—”*

*“—these lies weighting your soul,”* chimed another, parroting Fra Girolamo’s very cadence. *“Will you strut in the poor’s hunger, crowned with God’s curse?”*

The cherub tilted his head. *“You’re old, *buona madre.* Ugly, too, with that red and black hair. Only Satan admires it. Your angel weeps. Here—”* He thrust forth a silk scarf. *“Wipe the sin away.”*

Brigida’s defiance crumbled. No audience of peers stood witness; only these white-clad judges, their candor terrible as scripture. Her confessor, Fra Cristoforo, was nowhere to bolster her doubts. Trembling, she took the cloth and scrubbed her cheeks raw.

*“Good,”* approved the second boy. *“Now the hair. Heaven’s dew awaits.”* Mischief glinted as he plucked a pearl-headed pin. The braid unraveled, dragging her headgear forward. With a whimper, she surrendered the beloved *berretta*, its seed-pearls glinting as it fell into the basket. The false hair followed, a coiled serpent of deceit.

The boys brandished their trophies. *“See, *madonna*! Freed from ugliness! Now you resemble Saint Anna, pure and—”*

A rough hand thrust between them, waving a wooden cross. *“Four *quattrini*, blessing included! Profit? Hardly a whiff! All for the poor.”* The peddler’s grizzled face split in a grin. *“Life’s but scummed soup, *madonna*. Buy grace cheap!”*

Brigida, eyes swimming, fumbled for her purse—then salvation came.

*“Romola!”* she wailed, clutching the girl’s hands as the troop scattered.

Romola stifled a smile at the sight of her cousin, stripped of pretense. *“Hush, dear.”* She draped her own shawl over Brigida’s bare head. *“No one will stare now. Come.”*

As they hurried toward the Via del Palagio, Brigida clung to Romola’s arm. *“I’m a scarecrow! But I’ll be good—I swear it!”*

The peddler dogged them. *“The cross, *madonna*! Four *quattrini—”*

With a groan, Brigida pressed a *grosso* into his palm. *“Take it and go!”*

*“Wise choice,”* he cackled, vanishing. *“Better a singeing than a burning!”*

Brigida turned watery eyes to Romola. *“If Piagnone I must be, let my face proclaim it!”*

Romola kissed her cheek. *“Cousin, you’ve never looked kinder. Paint hid you. Now—ah, now a painter would capture the truth.”*

Brigida sniffed. *“At sixty, perhaps. But fifty-five? Monna Berta—”*

*“—is a crow,”* Romola finished. *“Age writes its lines in dignity, unless spite etches them. You’ve a face that would’ve made my mother smile.”*

Brigida sighed. *“Well. One rots or ripens. But if ripening means piety—”* She squared her shoulders. *“Lead on, *figlia mia*. Today, I choose the better path.”*

And though her step faltered, it moved forward—a small victory, but hers.
