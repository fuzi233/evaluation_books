The morning light breached the grime-streaked panes of my attic window, casting a pale, indifferent glow across the squalor of my waking hour. I sat upright, listening to the cacophony of the city—a roaring tide of hooves, carts, and shouting voices—that declared the day had commenced long before I had stirred. Reaching instinctively for my pocket watch, I found only emptiness; the timepiece was missing from its customary perch on the rickety dressing table. Rising fully, I surveyed the wreckage of the night: my garments lay scattered like debris on the floorboards. One boot sat solitary and abandoned in the chair by the fireplace, while its partner vanished into the shadows of the room.

During the hours of darkness, my head had swollen with a peculiar, phantom weight. I pondered, with a detached curiosity, whether I had mistakenly donned Minikin's footwear; if so, I would gladly swap them for my own. The current pair was a top-heavy monstrosity, throbbing with a pain that defied description. Suddenly, the fragmented memories of the previous night surged forward, shaking me from my stupor. From a neighboring spire, church bells tolled with mechanical precision. I counted carefully: eleven o'clock. I sprang from the bed and immediately collapsed onto the floor.

I recalled how, clutching the bedpost, I had felt the entire room executing a wild, drunken waltz. Even now, it had not quite stabilized. It continued to rotate, no longer whirling violently but staggering feebly, exhausted from its all-night revelry. Crawling to the washstand, I managed, after several clumsy attempts, to plunge my head into the basin. Pulling on my trousers with trembling fingers and collapsing into the easy-chair, I began to review the situation, tracing the timeline backward from the present misery.

I was acutely ill. The diagnosis was undeniable. Something had revolted within me.

"That strong cigar," I whispered to the empty room, my voice thin and reedy. "I should never have risked it. And then that stifling little room packed with strangers. On top of that, I have been working myself to the bone. I simply must resume exercising."

It offered a sliver of comfort to realize that, despite my shuffling gait and cowardly disposition, I was not easily duped.

"Nonsense," I told myself harshly. "Do not attempt to fool yourself. You were intoxicated."

"Not drunk," I pleaded internally. "Do not use that crude term. Some individuals cannot tolerate sweet champagne, I have heard. It affected my liver. Please, classify this as a hepatic issue."

"Drunk," I insisted relentlessly. "Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk—drunk as any 'Arry following a Bank Holiday."

"It was the first time," I murmured.

"It was your first opportunity," the internal voice countered.

"Never again," I vowed.

"The standard cliché," the voice replied.

"How old are you?"

"Nineteen."

"So you lack even the shield of youth. How do you know this sensation will not consume you? Having started this downward spiral, how can you guarantee you will not sink deeper until you become a confirmed sot?"

My heavy head bowed into my palms, and a groan escaped my throat. Countless temperance sermons read on Sunday afternoons flooded my mind. In the foggy theater of my imagination, I watched myself rushing toward a drunkard's grave, sometimes fighting heroically against the urge, other times yielding weakly as the craving tightened its grip. Through the haze, I saw my father's pallid face and my mother's sorrowful eyes. I thought of Barbara, and the scorn that could dance around her bewitching lips; I thought of Hal, radiating a terrible disdain for all forms of weakness. The shame of the present and the terror of the future tore at my psyche.

"It shall never happen again!" I cried aloud. "By God, it shall!" (At nineteen, passion is a volatile currency.) "I will leave this house immediately," I continued, addressing the silence. "I will escape its poisonous atmosphere. I will erase it from my mind, everything connected to it. I will make a fresh start. I will—"

A vague consciousness from the recesses of my brain coalesced into focus: the flabby silhouette of Miss Rosina Sellars. What was she doing here? What authority did she possess to obstruct my rebirth?

"The rights of your betrothed," my inner conscience explained, offering a grim smile.

"Did I truly proceed that far?"

"We shall not dissect the details," I replied, refusing to linger on the specifics. "That was the outcome."

"I was—I was not entirely myself at the time. I did not know what I was doing."

"As a rule, we are unaware when we commit folly; yet we must endure the consequences regardless. Unfortunately, this occurred in the presence of witnesses, and she is not the type of lady to be casually dismissed. You will marry her and establish a household with her in two cramped rooms. Her family will become your family. You will learn them intimately within days. Among them, she is revered as 'the lady,' a title that speaks volumes about their character. A delightful beginning to your career, is it not, my aspiring young friend? A splendid mess you have concocted!"

"What am I to do?" I asked.

"Honestly," I answered, "I have no clue."

I endured a miserable day. Too ashamed to confront Mrs. Peedles or even the scullery maid, I confined myself to my room, locking the door against the outside world. As dusk settled, feeling marginally improved—or perhaps merely less incapacitated—I crept out and partook of a sparse meal at a nearby coffee-house: tea without sugar and a kippered herring. Another gentleman seated himself opposite, ordering hot buttered toast, prompting me to flee hastily.

At eight o'clock in the evening, Minikin visited from the office to inquire about my condition. Seeking refuge in confession, I revealed the truth to him.

"Figured as much," he responded. "Looks like an A1 performance judging by your appearance."

"I am relieved it has concluded, now that it is behind me," I told him. "It will be a lesson etched forever in my memory."

"I agree," Minikin said. "Nothing compares to a fair and square intoxication for restoring one's spirits; superior to any sermon."

In my distress, I required counsel. Although Minikin was younger, I knew he possessed far greater experience in the rough currents of worldly affairs.

"That is not the worst," I confided. "What do you think I have accomplished?"

"Murdered a policeman?" Minikin suggested.

"Became engaged."

"No one like you quiet types for launching into chaos once you begin," Minikin observed. "Nice girl?"

"I do not know," I admitted. "All I know is that I do not desire her. How can I extricate myself?"

Minikin removed his left eye and began polishing it vigorously against his handkerchief, a nervous tic he employed whenever uncertainty clouded his judgment. Gazing into the reflection, he seemed to draw strength.

"A 'take-her-at-her-word' sort of girl?"

I indicated that he had correctly identified Miss Rosina Sellars.

"Do you know her earnings?"

"She knows I reside in this attic and prepare my own meals," I answered.

Minikin glanced around the room. "Must be fond of you."

"She believes I am intelligent," I explained, "and that I will succeed in life."

"And she is willing to wait?"

I nodded.

"Well, I would allow her to wait," Minikin replied, replacing his eye. "Plenty of time remains."

"But she is a barmaid, and she expects me to escort her, to take her out on Sundays, to visit her acquaintances. I cannot fulfill these demands. Furthermore, she is correct: I intend to advance. Consequently, she will cling to me. It is dreadful!"

"How did it occur?" Minikin asked.

"I do not know," I replied. "I was unaware I had committed the act until it was finished."

"Witnesses present?"

"About half a dozen," I groaned.

The door swung open, and Jarman entered; he never deemed knocking necessary. Instead of his customary boisterous greeting, he moved silently across the room and grasped my hand with solemn gravity.

"Friend of yours?" he asked, gesturing to Minikin.

I introduced them.

"Pleased to make your acquaintance," Jarman said.

"Glad to hear it," Minikin replied. "Though you do not appear to have much else to be proud of."

"Don't mind him," I explained to Jarman. "He was born that way."

"Wonderful gift," Jarman responded. "Tell me, if I possessed such a trait, what would I do? Break up evening parties? Have you ever considered this seriously?"

Minikin stated he would give the proposition due thought.

"Make your fortune patrolling the suburbs," Jarman assured him. "Pity you were not here last night," he continued. "Might have spared our young friend considerable trouble. Has he informed you of the news?"

I explained that I had already briefed Minikin on all particulars.

"Now that you possess a good, steady eye," Jarman said, fixing his gaze upon Minikin's polished orb, "how does he seem?"

"As well as can be expected under the circumstances, wouldn't you agree?" Minikin answered.
