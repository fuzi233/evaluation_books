I can barely recall what transpired at Hotherstone’s Farm. There was a warm welcome, a massive supper that could have fed an entire village in India, and a spotless bedroom—save for one abhorrent relic of our ancestors’ folly: a featherbed. The night passed restlessly, punctuated by the constant flickering of matches and the repeated lighting of a single candle. When dawn broke, I felt an immense relief—the promise of waking up, of moving forward.

Earlier, Betteredge and I had agreed I would call for him on our way to Cobb’s Hole as early as I pleased. For me, “early” meant the moment I could possibly rise. Without waiting for breakfast, I grabbed a crust of bread and set out, uncertain whether I’d find Betteredge still asleep. To my great relief, he was already awake—and just as eager as I was. He stood ready, stick in hand.

“How are you this morning, Betteredge?” I asked.

“Very poorly, sir,” he replied.

“Sorry to hear it. What’s troubling you?”

“I’ve come down with a new disease—of my own invention. I don’t want to alarm you, but you’ll catch it before the day is through.”

“The devil I will!”

“You feel a strange heat in your stomach? A pounding in your head? Ah! Not yet? It’ll take hold of you at Cobb’s Hole, Mr. Franklin. I call it detective-fever. I first caught it from Sergeant Cuff.”

“Aye, aye!” I said. “And the cure, in this case, is opening Rosanna Spearman’s letter, I suppose? Let’s go.”

Though it was still early, the fisherman’s wife was already busy in her kitchen. After Betteredge introduced me, Mrs. Yolland performed a ritual reserved—so I later learned—for distinguished guests. She placed a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes on the table, then opened the conversation with, “What news from London, sir?”

Before I could answer, a figure emerged from the shadows—a pale, wild girl with strikingly beautiful hair and sharp, intense eyes. She hobbled toward me on a crutch, stopped at the table, and stared at me as though I were both fascinating and repulsive.

“Mr. Betteredge,” she said, not taking her eyes off me, “say his name again, please.”

“This gentleman’s name,” Betteredge replied, emphasizing *gentleman*, “is Mr. Franklin Blake.”

The girl turned away abruptly and left the room.

Mrs. Yolland likely offered some explanation for her daughter’s behavior; Betteredge probably translated it into polite terms. But I didn’t notice—I was listening to the sound of the crutch: *thump-thump* up the stairs, *thump-thump* across the floor above, *thump-thump* back down. Then, suddenly, there she was at the door, holding a letter and beckoning me outside.

I left behind whatever apologies were being made and followed her—limping faster and faster—down the beach slope. She led me past boats, out of sight of the villagers, and then stopped, facing me for the first time.

“Stand there,” she said. “I want to look at you.”

There was no mistaking her expression—contempt, loathing, horror. I won no credit from her gaze. I won’t claim no woman had ever looked at me like that before—but I’ll say this: never before had I seen such a look so plainly.

A man can endure only so much scrutiny. I tried to redirect her attention.

“I think you have a letter for me,” I began. “Is it the one in your hand?”

“Say that again,” she replied.

I repeated it, like a child reciting a lesson.

“No,” she muttered to herself, eyes fixed. “I can’t see what she saw in his face. Can’t guess what she heard in his voice.” She turned away, resting her head wearily on her crutch. “Oh, my poor dear,” she whispered, softening for the first time. “My lost darling—what could you see in this man?”

She lifted her head sharply and faced me again.

“Can you eat and drink?” she asked.

“Yes,” I said.

“Can you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“When you see a poor girl in service, do you feel any remorse?”

“Certainly not. Why should I?”

Without another word, she thrust the letter into my face.

“Take it!” she exclaimed fiercely. “I’ve never seen you before. God forbid I ever see you again.”

Then she limped away at top speed.

I could only conclude one thing: she was mad.

But now, the letter—Rosanna Spearman’s letter—was mine. I read the address:

“For Franklin Blake, Esq. To be given into his own hands (and not to be trusted to anyone else), by Lucy Yolland.”

I broke the seal. Inside the envelope was a letter. Inside that, a slip of paper.

I read the letter first:

> Sir,—
> If you are curious to know the meaning of my behaviour while you stayed at Lady Verinder’s house, do what you are told in the enclosed memorandum—and do it alone.
> Your humble servant,

Then the slip:

> Memorandum:
> Go to the Shivering Sand at the turn of the tide.
> Walk out on the South Spit until the Beacon and the flagstaff at the Coast-guard station above Cobb’s Hole are in a straight line.
> Lay down a stick or any straight object exactly in that line, one end at the edge of the rocks overlooking the quicksand.
> Feel along the stick among the seaweed (starting from the end near the beacon) for the chain.
> Once found, follow it until it reaches the edge of the rocks, descending into the quicksand.
> _Then pull the chain._

As I read the last words—underlined in the original—I heard Betteredge behind me.

“I can’t stand it anymore, Mr. Franklin. What does the letter say? For mercy’s sake, tell us!”

I handed him the papers. He read the letter without emotion. But when he reached the memorandum, his face lit up.

“The Sergeant said it!” he cried. “From beginning to end, the Sergeant believed she had a note about the hiding place. And here it is! Lord save us, Mr. Franklin—here’s the secret that baffled everyone, from Cuff himself, right here, waiting for you! The tide’s ebbing now. How long till it turns?”

He called out to a boy mending nets nearby.

“Tammie Bright! When’s the tide turning?”

“In an hour.”

We checked our watches.

“We can go around the coast,” Betteredge said. “Reach the quicksand in time. What do you say, sir?”

“Come along.”

On the way, I asked Betteredge to refresh my memory of Rosanna Spearman’s actions during Sergeant Cuff’s investigation. With his help, I reconstructed the events: her trip to Frizinghall under pretense of illness, her locked door and burning candle all night, her purchase of the japanned tin case and two dog chains from Mrs. Yolland, the Sergeant’s certainty that she’d hidden something at the Shivering Sand—yet no idea what it was.

All these fragments returned to me as we reached the quicksand and stepped onto the low rock ledge known as the South Spit. Together, we positioned ourselves so the Beacon and the flagstaff aligned. We laid my stick carefully along the line. Then we checked our watches—twenty minutes remained.

I suggested waiting on the dry sand instead of the wet, slippery rocks. As I sat down, Betteredge prepared to leave.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

“Look at the letter again,” he said. “You’re to make the discovery alone.”

Ah. I remembered—Rosanna had insisted on solitude.

“It’s hard to leave you,” Betteredge said. “But she died a terrible death. And I feel a duty—to honor her wish. Besides,” he added quietly, “there’s nothing stopping you from telling afterward. I’ll wait in the fir-plantation. Don’t linger, sir. Detective-fever isn’t easy to bear under these circumstances.”

With that, he vanished.

Twenty minutes stretched into eternity. Suspense weighed heavier than time. In moments like this, smoking becomes a lifeline. I lit a cigar and sat on the beach slope. Sunlight bathed everything in golden clarity. The air was crisp and fresh—living itself felt like a luxury. Even the lonely cove seemed cheerful. The quicksand, glittering gold, hid its deadly nature beneath a fleeting smile.

It was the finest day I’d seen since returning to England.

The tide turned before my cigar burned out. I saw the sand begin to heave—then the shiver spread across its surface, as if some unseen terror stirred deep below.

I tossed aside the cigar and returned to the rocks.

Following the memorandum, I began feeling along the stick, starting from the end near the beacon. Halfway, nothing. Then—just beyond—an inch or two further—a narrow crack. My finger touched metal.

The chain.

I tried to follow it toward the quicksand, but thick seaweed blocked my way—entangled in the fissure since Rosanna’s time. I couldn’t pull it loose or push through.

I marked the spot where the stick ended nearest the quicksand, then devised my own plan: to “sound” beneath the rocks, hoping to trace the chain where it entered the sand.

I knelt at the edge of the South Spit. My face was inches from the trembling surface. The sight of it—shuddering intermittently—sent a chill through me. A grotesque fantasy seized me: that Rosanna’s ghost might rise from the sand, pointing to the treasure. I closed my eyes as the tip of the stick entered the quicksand.

Then—immediately—it struck something solid.

The chain.

I gripped the seaweed with my left hand, leaned over the edge, and reached under the rock. My fingers found the chain. I pulled it up easily.

At the end hung the japanned tin case.

The chain was rusted shut. I jammed the case between my knees and strained—finally forcing the lid open.

Inside: a white substance. I reached in. Linen. Crumpled with it: a letter. I saw my name on it—set it aside—and removed the linen entirely.

It came out rolled tight, shaped by the case, preserved from sea and time.

I carried it to dry sand, unrolled it—smoothed it out.

It was a nightgown.

One side showed only folds. The other—

There it was: the smear of paint—identical to the stain on Rachel’s boudoir door.

My eyes locked onto it. My mind flashed back to Sergeant Cuff’s words:

> “Find any dress with paint on it. Find who owns it. Find how they got into the room between midnight and three. If they can’t explain—look no further. You’ve found the thief.”

The words echoed, relentless, mechanical.

A voice broke the silence.

I looked up. Betteredge was approaching, visible between the dunes.

His presence snapped me back. The inquiry wasn’t over. I’d found the stain. But whose nightgown was it?

My first instinct: check the letter. But then I realized—there was a quicker way.

The nightgown itself might bear a mark.

I picked it up. Found the label.

And read—MY OWN NAME.

There it was. Plain. Unmistakable. My name.

I looked up. The sun. The bay. Betteredge drawing closer.

I looked back at the label.

My own name.

Plainly written—my own name.

“I will lay my hand on the thief who took the Moonstone,” I had vowed when leaving London.

I had uncovered the secret the quicksand guarded from all others.

And now, by the unanswerable evidence of the paint-stain, I had discovered myself as the thief.
