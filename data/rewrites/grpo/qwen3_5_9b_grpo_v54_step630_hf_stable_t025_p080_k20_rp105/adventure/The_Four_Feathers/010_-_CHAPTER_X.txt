The month of May brought a restless energy to Durrance, whose gaze lifted from the dusty horizon of Wadi Halfa, eager to turn toward home. Yet, unaware of the gravity unfolding five hundred miles to the south, across the vast Nubian expanse and the treacherous Belly of Stones, a fate far more critical to his own destiny was being woven in silence.

On the abandoned track between Berber and Suakin, the Wells of Obak lay buried deep within shifting dunes. To the east, a fringe of trees marked the boundary between the sands and a stony plain crowned with granite hills; to the west, the desert stretched fifty-eight miles of unbroken waterlessness toward Mahobey and the Nile. It was a landscape of deceptive flatness, where a mere tuft of grass could be mistaken for a tree promising shade, and a small pile of stones could loom like a mountain. In this particular May, no place could be more desolate. The sun hammered the earth from dawn until dusk with unbearable intensity, while all night long a biting wind swept across the sands, sculpting pyramids of sand only to level them, carving valleys and filling slopes, ensuring the face of the country was in constant flux. Vultures and sand-grouse held dominion over this wasteland, their presence undisturbed. Scattered among the dunes lay the bleached skeletons of camels, silent testimony to caravans long vanished, alongside the rotting remains of a branch-and-hoop house where Arabs once herded goats. Here, the sun rose and set over a hollow circle of honey-colored earth. Silence hung heavy, like night over water, turning the absolute stillness into a place of mystery and waiting.

Yet, in this month of May, one man sojourned secretly at the wells. Each morning at sunrise, he drove two camels—swift riding-mares of the pure Bisharin breed—from the shelter of the trees. He watered them, then sat by the well-mouth for three hours, watching the dunes and the distant town of Berber. Afterward, he drove them back into the trees, feeding them delicately on *dhoura* spread upon a cloth, and vanished for the rest of the day. For five mornings, he repeated this ritual, unseen and unapproached.

On the sixth morning, just as he prepared to return to his hiding place, figures appeared against the sky on a sand crest: a man driving a donkey. The Arab sitting by the well glanced at the animal, noting its grey coat, and half-risen, he paused. His gaze shifted to the driver, whose *jellab* was pulled tight over his face against the glare, revealing bare legs of ebony blackness against the pale sand. The driver was a negro. The Arab sat back down, feigning the most complete indifference, waiting for the stranger to approach. He did not flinch when he heard the soft tread of feet behind him.

"Salam aleikum," the negro said, stopping. He carried a long spear, a short one, and a hide shield, which he laid on the ground before sitting beside the Arab.

The Arab bowed his head. "Aleikum es salam," he replied, waiting.

"It is Abou Fatma?" the negro asked.

The Arab nodded.

"Two days ago," the negro continued, "a man of the Bisharin, Moussa Fedil, stopped me in the market-place of Berber. Seeing I was hungry, he gave me food. When I had eaten, he charged me to drive this donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak."

Abou Fatma glanced at the donkey with careless detachment, as if noticing it for the first time. "Tayeeb," he said equally casually. "The donkey is mine." He sat motionless, signaling that the business was concluded and the stranger might depart.

But the negro held his ground. "I am to meet Moussa Fedil again on the third morning from now, in the market-place of Berber. Give me a token to carry back, so he knows I have fulfilled the charge and can reward me."

Abou Fatma drew his knife from his small of the back, picked up a stick from the ground, and notched it thrice at each end. "This shall be a sign to Moussa Fedil," he said, handing the stick to his companion. The negro secured it in the corner of his wrap, loosened the water-skin from the donkey's back, filled it at the well, and slung it over his shoulders. Picking up his spears and shield, he began the laborious climb up the loose sand slope. Abou Fatma watched him disappear over the crest, then rose hastily in his turn. When Harry Feversham had departed from Obak six days prior to cross the barren desert to the Nile, this grey donkey had carried his water-skins and provisions.

Abou Fatma drove the donkey down among the trees and fastened it to a stem. Examining the animal's shoulders, he found a tiny incision in the left shoulder, neatly stitched with fine thread. He cut the stitches, pressed the wound open, and extracted a tiny package no larger than a postage stamp. It was a goat's bladder containing a note written in Arabic and folded small. Abou Fatma had not served as Gordon's body-servant in vain; he had learned to read during his tenure. Unfolding the note, he read:

*"The houses which were once Berber are destroyed, and a new town of wide streets is building. There is no longer any sign by which I may know the ruins of Yusef's house from the ruins of a hundred houses; nor does Yusef any longer sell rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week."*

The author of this letter was Harry Feversham. Clad in the patched *jubbeh* of the Dervishes over his stained skin, his hair frizzed on his crown and falling in matted, seaweed-like locks down his neck, he wandered the wide, pit-ridden streets of New Berber in search of Yusef. To the south, separated by a mile of desert, lay the old town where Abou Fatma had once slept and hidden letters—a warren of ruined houses facing narrow alleys. Front walls had been pulled down, roofs carried away, leaving only bare inner walls standing. As Feversham wandered among them at night, the place seemed a labyrinth of long, crumbling courts, indistinguishable from one another save by degrees of decay. Foxes already dug their burrows beneath the foundations.

He had calculated that one night should suffice for his stay in Berber. His plan was to creep through the gate at dusk and face the journey to Obak before the stars faded. Now, however, he must move steadily among the crowds like a man with urgent business, dreading conversation lest his tongue betray him, listening intently for the name Yusef to strike his ears. Despair visited him at times, fear always. Yet from these sharp pangs arose a kind of madness, a frenzy of obstinacy, a fanatical belief that he could not fail and that the universe held no injustice great enough to burden the one man least fitted to bear it, only to destroy him callously for his effort.

Fear gripped him on that morning, three days after leaving Abou Fatma, as he crested a slope and saw the sand stretch like a lagoon to the dark brown walls of the town, with the overshadowing foliage of great date palms rising on the Nile bank beyond. Within those walls crowded the Dervishes. It seemed sheer madness to imagine escaping detection there, even for an hour. Was it right, he wondered, even to try? The longer he stood, the more insistent the question became. The low mud walls grew sinister; the welcoming green of the palms, after so many arid days of sun and stone, became an ironic invitation to death. He began to wonder if he had already done enough for honor by venturing so near.

The sun beat down upon him; his strength ebbed as though his veins were opened. If he were caught—as surely he would be—he saw the fanatical faces crowding fiercely about him. Were mutilations practiced here? He looked about, shivering in the strong heat, the great loneliness of the place striking him so hard his knees shook. He turned and began to run, leaping in panic across the naked desert under the sun, while feeble, inarticulate cries broke from his throat.

He ran only a few yards before the very violence of his flight stopped him. Were four years of anticipation nothing? Had he schooled himself in the tongue, lived in the bazaars, for no end? Was he still the craven who had surrendered his papers? The quiet confidence with which he had revealed his plan to Lieutenant Sutch in the Criterion grill-room was merely the vainglory of a man deceiving himself? And Ethne?

He dropped to the ground, drawing his coat over his head until he was a brown spot indistinguishable from the sand, an irregularity in the great waste. He shut out the prospect, and over thousands of miles of continent and sea, he summoned Ethne's face to his mind. Soon, he was back in Donegal. The summer night whispered through the open doorway of the hall; in a nearby room, people danced to music. He saw the three feathers flutter to the floor; he read the growing trouble in Ethne's face. If he could accomplish this thing, and the even harder task lying beyond, perhaps someday he might see that face cleared of its sorrow. Significant words echoed in his ears: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

As the sun began to set, he rose from the ground and walked down toward Berber, passing between the gates.
