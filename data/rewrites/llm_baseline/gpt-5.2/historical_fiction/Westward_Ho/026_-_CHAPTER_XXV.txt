A fortnight and more had worn itself away in hard labor—hard, yet not harder than the stubborn work these men had so often taken for their daily bread. They had bidden farewell, once and for ever, to the green, heaving emptiness of the eastern plains; had climbed the Cordillera till the air thinned and rang clearer about the ear; and from a height had looked longingly toward Santa Fe, lying like a white patch among rich gardens on its lofty mountain table-land. It was, as they had foreseen, too great a place for any handful of desperate English to meddle with. They passed it by; but they had not passed idle.

Their Indian lad had found, by those subtle threads of rumor which run through every forest and valley quicker than any horse, that a gold-train would go down from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena. For that they waited—crouched beside a miserable rut that called itself a road, camped in a forest of oaks which for an instant might have cheated the heart into thinking of Devon or of home, had not the undergrowth been tree-ferns, damp and glossy as if forever newly washed; had not the earth fallen away at their very feet into gorges so deep that, while their brows were swept by the cool breath of the temperate zone, they could look down into a continual bath of steaming vapor, and through it, dimly, see the gigantic shapes and wet colors of the tropic wood below.

They had chosen their lair above a place where the path crept along a steep side, with a sheer cliff beneath, dropping many hundred feet. There had been a road there once—perhaps in the days when these hills belonged to a kingdom that knew order, and measured its labor, and set stones where stones were needed. All that Spanish rule had left was a few broken steps slipping from their beds at the bottom of a narrow ditch of mud. The rest had gone the way of aqueducts and bridges, post-houses and garden-terraces, llama-flocks and husbandry; for where men run mad for gold, they let everything rot that does not glitter. Only the great stones of churches endured—splendid cathedrals lifted above the ruins of older temples; and even that “milder” worship, as it named itself, had shown these English before now how it could demand blood.

Now the vegetation had taken back its own. A vine would seize the fallen column as readily as it seized a living throat; ferns sprang where men had trodden; the place had the damp, sweet smell of leaf-mould and bruised green, with here and there the sharpness of resin and the sour breath of hidden rot. Within a few miles of a Spanish settlement they might as well have been in the middle of the Orinoco.

Their search for sulphur and nitre had failed them. No yellow crust on stone, no white bloom in cave or crevice—nothing to please Yeo’s fierce longing for powder and ball. Much to his disgust they must trust, as they had trusted before, to steel and yew.

“Be it so,” Amyas had said. “Drake took Nombre de Dios and the train with no better.”

So they felled a great tree across the road above them, wedging its trunk into the bank; and they sat among flowers which seemed to have been painted into life and then drenched, chewing coca in default of honest meat and drink—green, bitter mouthfuls that numbed hunger and made the tongue thick, while the body, cheated of its rightful complaint, went on. It was no meal for men who remembered bread’s warm smell, or the crackle of fat on a pan; but it served, and served too well, deadening more than the belly’s craving.

At night, as they lay, another thing had troubled them: a mysterious roar, heard nightly in their wake ever since they left the Meta. Not jaguar, nor monkey, nor any sound they knew. Always behind, always following, as if some huge throat in the dark were calling them by name. In such a land of wonders a man might learn to mistrust even his own ears; yet to these sea-dogs, used to reading weather by the smallest shift of sound upon water, the noise bit into the mind. Still, the gold-train mattered more than any phantom.

At last, from below, came a sharp crack and a loud cry—neither snapping branch nor woodpecker, neither parrot scream nor monkey howl.

“That was a whip’s crack,” said Yeo. “And a woman’s wail. They are close, lads!”

“A woman’s?” whispered Amyas. “Do they drive women in their gangs?”

“Why not, the brutes? There—did you see the basnets glitter?”

Amyas’s voice dropped till it was little more than breath. “Men! not a bowstring drawn till I do. Then one arrow—then out swords, and in among them. Pass it.”

Up they came, slowly, grinding their way step by broken step, and the English hearts beat harder for every jingle of metal and every shuffle of bare foot on stone.

First came about twenty soldiers—only half of them walking. The rest were borne, each in a chair on the back of a single Indian; while those who marched had shoved their heavier pieces—arquebuses and the like—into the hands of attendant slaves, pricked on at will by the pike of the soldier behind.

“The men are mad to let their ordnance out of their hands,” whispered Amyas.

“Oh, sir,” murmured Yeo, with a bitter curl, “an Indian will pray to an arquebus not to shoot him. They be sure their artillery’s safe enough.”

“Look at the proud villains,” breathed another. “To make dumb beasts of human creatures so!”

“Ten shot,” counted Amyas, coldly, as if he were counting barrels aboard ship. “And ten pikes. Will can tackle them above.”

Last of that troop came an inferior officer, also in his chair. As he went he turned his face toward the laboring file behind, and every other second drew the cigar from his lips to fling back a volley of oaths—thick, sticky blasphemies that seemed to cling to the air like smoke to hair and clothes.

“The blasphemous dog!” hissed Yeo, fingers worrying his bowstring as if the cord would snap with his restraint. Amyas laid a hand on his arm; and in that instant another procession came into view, and anger hardened into something worse.

A line of Indians, Negroes, and Zambos—naked, emaciated, scarred with whips and the bite of irons—were chained by the left wrist and driven upward. Each carried a basket held by a strap across the forehead, the leather biting into skin already raw. Sweat ran down their ribs; breath came in short, rasped pulls; some moved like men in sleep, their eyes open and yet not seeing. Among them were old men and youths, and women too—slender girls, and mothers with children running at their knees, the little ones stumbling in the mud. Three or four drivers, armed with whips of manati-hide, lingered along the file and paid for every lagging step, every slip of toe on stone, with a crack like a pistol-shot across bare flesh. The blows made a sound that stayed in the ear like a snapped string, and after each crack came the smaller sounds: a stifled grunt, a sob swallowed, a child’s startled breath.

A low murmur rose from the ambushed Englishmen—no mere anger for robbed gold, but the indignation of free men at the sight of bondage.

Yet the first forty bore a burden that dragged every eye forward. Each basket held a square package, carefully corded in hide. Amyas knew that look as a sailor knows the shape of a cloud that brings storm.

“What’s in they, captain?” whispered one.

“Gold,” said Amyas.

At that word there ran through the hidden men a rustle like wind through dry reeds—greed waking, quick and shame-faced. Amyas’s whisper cut it like a knife.

“Be men—be men—or you’ll spoil all!”

The last twenty Indians carried larger baskets, more lightly loaded—manioc, maize-bread, and food for the party. Then came twenty soldiers more, and after them the officer in charge, smiling in his chair and twirling two huge moustachios as if the world were made only to stroke his vanity. The ambush was complete; only the moment was wanting.

Amyas felt that old shrinking from drawing in cool blood on men who had no thought of danger—even though he saw cruelty at work with every step. His mind sought for any cleaner beginning, some act that would make the fight not murder but judgment.

Judgment came of itself.

The last but one in the chained line was an old gray-headed man; behind him, a slender, graceful girl of eighteen or so. Amyas’s heart tightened at the sight of their weakness under the load. Just then the foremost of the file rounded the corner above; there was a bustle and a shout.

“Halt, señores! There is a tree across the path!”

“A tree across the path?” bellowed the officer, and poured out fresh oaths. The Indians, told to halt above and driven on by blows below, surged helplessly on the broken steps. The old man went down, grovelling on his face.

The officer leaped from his chair and hurried up. Of course he came upon the fallen wretch.

“Sin pecado concebida! Grandfather of Beelzebub! Is this a place to lie worshipping your fiends?” And he pricked the prostrate body with his sword-point.

The old man strove to rise; the load and the strap across his forehead held him down. He fell again and lay still.

The driver laid the manati-hide across his loins once—twice—each blow sounding sharp in the narrow pass, echoing off rock as if the cliff itself repeated the cruelty. Still the body did not stir.

“Gastado, Señor Capitan,” said the driver, shrugging. “Used up. He has been failing these three months.”

“What does the intendant mean by sending me worn-out cattle?” roared the officer. “Forward there! Clear away the tree, señores, and I’ll soon clear the chain. Hold it up, Pedrillo!”

The driver lifted the chain fastened to the old man’s wrist. The officer stepped back, flourishing a Toledo blade whose beauty—its cold, bright line—made Amyas, in spite of himself, covet it as a craftsman covets a perfect tool.

The Spaniard was tall and handsome, broad-shouldered, with the look of birth and breeding that makes a villain more hateful. Amyas thought he meant to show his strength by severing the chain at a stroke.

He did sever something with one stroke. The blade flashed once—twice—and fell, not on iron but on living flesh.

There was a shriek, a crimson spurt; the hand dropped away, and the chain and its prisoner were parted indeed.

For one instant Amyas’s arrow yearned toward the murderer’s throat; the next, vengeance came from a swifter source.

The girl sprang like a tiger-cat. With the whole strength of passion in her thin arms she clasped the officer, and together they went—one dark body and one glittering—over the narrow ledge and out into the abyss.

A rush, a shout; all faces bent over. The girl hung by her chained wrist, caught for a moment on the edge. The officer was gone. There was a dreadful pause, and then, far below, the sound of his body crashing through tree-tops.

“Haul her up! Hew her in pieces! Burn the witch!” cried the driver, seizing the chain and pulling with all his might, while soldiers, springing from their chairs, stooped over the brink.

Now was Amyas’s time. Heaven had delivered them into his hands, and his mercy was already spent.

At ten yards his arrow went clean through the driver. Amyas leapt with a roar into the midst of the astonished Spaniards.

His first thought was for the girl. He jerked her up by sheer strength and flung her safe upon the road, while the Spaniards recoiled, as if some mountain-giant or ghost had burst among them. His Devon hurrah undeceived them; a cry rose—“English! Lutheran dogs!”—but too late.

A storm of arrows answered. Five Spaniards dropped dead at once; a dozen more went down or staggered bleeding. Down leapt Salvation Yeo, white hair streaming, with twenty good swords behind him, and then the narrow path was a shambles.

The Spaniards fought like lions; but they had no time to set arquebuses on their crutches, no room on that cramped ledge to use their pikes. The English had the wall of them; and to have the wall there was to hold a man’s life between stone and steel. Five desperate minutes, and not a living Spaniard stood upon those steps; and certainly no living one lay in the green abyss below. Two only, who had chanced to be behind the rest and in full armor, escaped mortal wound and fled down the hill.

“After them!” cried Amyas. “Michael Evans and Simon Heard—catch them, though they run a league.”

The two long, lean Clovelly men ran like deer, trained by cliff and forest, and in ten minutes they returned, their faces set, having done what they were sent to do.

Amyas and the rest hurried past the chained file to help Cary and the party above, where shouts and musket-shots told of another sharp affray. Their coming ended it. Spaniards fell; three or four scrambled down the crannies of the cliff.

“Let not one escape! Slay them as Israel slew Amalek!” cried Yeo, bending over; and before the wretches could find shelter, arrows found them. Their bodies rolled lifeless down the rocks.

“Now then,” said Amyas, breath coming fast, “loose the Indians!”

They found armorers’ tools on one of the dead and went to work. Iron rang; links gave way; shackles fell with dull thuds into mud. Amyas spoke to them through signs and what words their lad could lend him.

“We are your friends. All we ask is that you help us carry this gold down to the Magdalena, and then you are free.”

A few of the younger fell at his knees and kissed his feet, staring up as at something fallen from the sky, calling him the child of the Sun. But most sat where they stood when the irons came off, staring into vacancy as if the very act of freedom were beyond understanding. Their bodies had been trained to obey; their minds had been trained not to imagine. Even the mountain’s cleaner air and the birds’ bright calls could not enter them. The iron had bitten deeper than flesh.

Only the girl moved as if she still had blood in her. Once she was loosed, she sprang to the old man’s body. Without a word she lifted him—how that thin frame did it none could tell—laid him across her knees, kissed the fallen lips, stroked the furrowed cheeks, and murmured low, inarticulate sounds like the cooing of a woodland dove. None knew what she said but she—and he who could not hear, for his soul had fled.

Then truth struck her. She drew one long, heaving breath, rose erect with the body in her arms, and before any could grasp her intent, leaped with him into the abyss.

They watched her dark limbs, twined about the corpse, turning over and over in the green air until a crash among leaves and a scream of startled birds told that she had struck the tree-tops. The living roof hid her.

“Brave lass!” shouted a sailor, awed.

“The Lord forgive her,” said Yeo. “But, your worship, we must have these rascals’ ordnance.”

“And their clothes too,” said Amyas, forcing his mind back to necessity. “If we mean to go down the Magdalena unchallenged, we must pass through the heart of Spanish settlements and by Saint Martha itself. Listen, my masters! We have won, by God’s good grace, gold enough to serve us the rest of our lives, and without losing a man. May we win more, if we be wise and He thinks good. But remember Mr. Oxenham and his crew. Do not make God’s gift our ruin by faithlessness, greediness, or mutinous haste.”

“You shall find none in us!” cried several. “We know your worship. We can trust our general.”

“Thank God,” said Amyas. “Now—no shame nor sin to make these men carry it, saving the women—God forbid we should burden them. How many lie here?”

“Thirteen here, and about ten up above,” said Cary.

“Then near twenty are missing. Who will go down over cliff and bring up their spoil?”

“I—and I—and I—” A dozen stepped out at once, as always when Amyas asked for volunteers; for they knew he meant to set his own hand to the work.

“Follow me,” said he. “Sir John—take the Indian lad for interpreter. Try to comfort these poor souls. Tell them they shall all be free.”

Then Sir John, looking down the road, cried out, “Why, who is that comes up?”

All eyes turned. Up came—wonder of wonders—Ayacanora herself, blow-gun in hand, bow upon her back, and all her feather bravery about her, though travel had dulled and torn it. She saw Amyas, gave a cry that was half joy and half pain, quickened into a run, and at last fell panting at his feet.

“I have found you,” she said. “You ran away from me, but you could not escape me.” She fawned round him like a dog that has found its master, then flung herself upon the bank and burst into wild sobs.

“God help us,” muttered Amyas, clutching his hair as he looked on the beautiful, weeping creature. “What am I to do with her, over and above all these poor heathens?”

There was no time. Over the cliff he scrambled with the volunteers, searching among branches and rock-shelves for where the fallen had lodged. Ayacanora, seeing the main body of English stayed, perched on a point of rock to watch him, still as a hawk, the forest’s soundscape shifting around her—the drip of water, the far call of some unseen bird, the sudden flap and scold of frightened wings.

After half an hour of hard work they hauled up weapons, clothes, and armor of the fallen Spaniards, and bundled them among the men. The remaining corpses were flung over the precipice, to make an end; and they started again toward the Magdalena, while Yeo snorted like a war-horse at the joy of once more handling powder and ball.

“We can face the world now, sir! Why not go back and try Santa Fe, after all?”

But Amyas thought enough was as good as a feast, and held on downward. The freed slaves followed—no thanks, no laughter of deliverance; only meek obedience to new masters, and now and then a sign or grunt of surprise that no blow fell, that no pike prodded them to hurry. Some caught sight of the little calabashes of coca in the Englishmen’s hands. That woke them. They began coaxing abjectly for a taste of the miraculous herb—bitter green pellets that could silence the belly and lend breath to lungs in the keen air, and, worse kindness, for a while at least drive away the fallen Indian’s most unpitying foe: the malady of thought.

As the cavalcade turned the mountain corner, they paused for one last look at the place of that fierce triumph. Lines of vultures were already sliding out of the vastness, as if the sky had bred them for this purpose alone. In a few hours there would be no trace but a few white bones among untrodden beds of flowers, and the forest would close its mouth over the story.

And now Amyas had leisure to question Ayacanora. He wished her anywhere but where she was; yet now she was here, what heart could quite harden against her? He spoke with a tenderness born partly of pity, partly of fear of wounding her; and that gentleness, new to his voice, made her cling closer.

She told him—eager, breathless—how she had followed their track day and night, and every evening made sounds, as loud as she dared, hoping they would hear and wait, or come back to see what caused the noise.

Amyas remembered at once the nightly roaring.

“Noises? What did you make them with?”

Ayacanora lifted a finger with a proud little air of mystery. Then from under her feather cloak she drew an object at which Amyas had hard work to keep his countenance.

“Look!” she whispered, as if half afraid the thing itself could listen. “I have it—the holy trumpet!”

There it was—the bone of contention: a handsome earthen tube near two feet long, neatly glazed, painted with quaint grecques and figures of beasts—a relic of some dead civilization, its colors dulled by travel, yet still bright enough to catch the eye. Brimblecombe rubbed his small fat hands.

“Brave maid! You have cheated Satan this time,” quoth he.

Yeo’s face darkened. He advised that the “idolatrous relic” be “hove over cliff” without more ado.

“Let be,” said Amyas. “What means this, Ayacanora? And why have you followed us?”

She poured out a long tale; and from it Amyas gathered, as best he could, that the trumpet had for years been the torment of her life—the one thing in the tribe held higher than she, the one thing she was forbidden even to see because she was a woman. Therefore she had sworn to prove a woman as good as a man; and hence her hatred of marriage, her Amazonian pride. Yet still the Piache would not show her the trumpet nor tell where it lay hid; and to seek it openly she had feared the tribe’s superstitious fury.

But the day after the English left, the Piache, it seemed, chose to celebrate their departure; and, as might be expected, master and pupil fell to quarreling. The quarrel ended—she confessed it without shame—in her burning the old rogue’s hut over his head. He escaped, losing his conjuring gear, and fled raging into the woods, vowing he would carry the trumpet to a neighboring tribe. At that, by sudden impulse, she took plenty of coca, her weapons, and her feathers, and ran him down just as he was unveiling the precious mystery. The sight terrified her; she had been half inclined to flee. But she remembered how the white men laughed at the whole matter; and so she rushed upon the old conjuror and bore off her prize in triumph.

“I hope you have not killed him?” said Amyas.

“I did beat him a little,” she said simply; “but I thought you would not let me kill him.”

Amyas was half amused—half troubled—at her frank confession of his authority over her. She went on quickly:

“And then I dare not go back to the Indians; so I was forced to come after you.”

“And is that your only reason for coming after us?” asked Amyas—stupidly, busy still with roads and risks, and deaf to the finer meanings that cannot be translated into plain words.

He had struck some secret string. The girl drew herself up, her cheeks flushing scarlet beneath the paint and travel-stain.

“You never tell lies,” she said. “Do you think I would tell lies?”

And with that she fell back to the rear and followed them steadfastly, speaking to none, yet fixed as fate on following them to the world’s end—while in the high air above the gorge the vultures drifted, and below, far under their feet, the tropic forest steamed and murmured on, swallowing screams, prayers, and the last unspoken words of those whose tongues had never been allowed a name for freedom.
