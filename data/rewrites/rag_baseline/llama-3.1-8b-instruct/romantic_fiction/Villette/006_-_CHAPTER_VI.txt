As I emerged from the confines of my chambers, the first of March dawned on me like a revelation. Gazing out the window, I beheld the risen sun struggling to penetrate the fog-shrouded atmosphere of London. The dome of St. Paul's Cathedral loomed above, a solemn, dark blue presence that stirred my inner self and set my spirit aflame with anticipation. In that moment, I felt as though I was finally awakening to life, my soul expanding with an unbridled sense of possibility, akin to Jonah's gourd sprouting in the night.

"I did well to come," I mused, hastening to dress with a newfound sense of purpose. "This city's vibrant energy is precisely what I've been seeking – a place where one can cultivate their faculties and shun the stagnation of obscurity." As I prepared for the day ahead, I pondered the dichotomy between provincial towns and metropolitan centers, where individuals are free to exercise their agency and pursue their passions without constraint.

Upon descending to the lobby, I encountered a seasoned waiter, a grey-haired man with a weathered air about him. Our conversation flowed effortlessly, as he revealed a twenty-year tenure at the establishment and a familiarity with my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, who had frequented the inn during their visits to the city some fifteen years prior. Upon establishing my connection, our rapport shifted, and he treated me with a warmth and courtesy that put me at ease. His words of comparison to my uncle Charles carried weight, as they echoed the sentiments expressed by Mrs. Barrett, my esteemed benefactress.

With a renewed sense of confidence, I ventured forth into the streets, taking in the tranquil atmosphere of the narrow thoroughfare outside my window. The few passersby seemed unremarkable, akin to those found in provincial towns, and I felt assured that I could navigate the city unaccompanied. My breakfast having satiated my hunger, I set out to explore, my heart filled with elation and a sense of adventure.
