M. Paul Emanuel was notoriously, acutely sensitive to interruption during his lessons, no matter the cause. To cross his classroom mid-lecture was a prospect every teacher and pupil at the pensionnat considered a risk to life and limb.

Even Madame Beck herself, when forced to brave it, would scurry across with skirts tucked tight, edging around his raised teaching platform like a ship skirting jagged breakers. Poor Rosine the portress, whose half-hourly job it was to fish pupils out of his class to take music lessons scattered across the building, regularly grew nearly tongue-tied with terror after two or three trips in a row, struck dumb by the searing, darting glares he sent her way through his spectacles.

One morning, I sat in the adjacent workroom, stitching embroidery a pupil had left half-finished, my ears drinking in the rising, stormy crescendo of his voice from the next room, every note more unquiet, more ominously sharp than the last. A thick stone wall separated me from his gathering temper, and the glass door leading to the courtyard stood open for easy escape if the storm drifted my way, so I fear I found more amusement than alarm in his brewing irritation. Rosine, though, had no such shelter: five times already that blessed morning she had braved his class, and now a sixth summons came, to fetch yet another pupil out from under his very nose.

“Mon Dieu, what am I to do?” she whimpered. “He will kill me, I swear it, he is in such a rage!”
Nerved by pure desperation, she wrenched the door open.
“Mademoiselle La Malle for her piano lesson!” she called.
Before she could slip back out or even pull the door shut, his voice boomed out: “From this moment, this class is forbidden territory. The first person who opens this door, or even steps across this threshold, will be hanged—if it is Madame Beck herself.”

Barely ten minutes had passed since that edict was issued when I heard Rosine’s soft slippers shuffling down the corridor again.
“Mademoiselle,” she said, “I would not take five francs to step into that class right now. Monsieur’s spectacles are truly terrifying, and there’s a messenger from the Athénée waiting with an urgent note. I told Madame Beck I dared not deliver it, and she says you have to go.”
“Me? That is hardly my job. Come, Rosine, bear your own burden. Be brave, go just once more.”
“I? Impossible! I have crossed him five times already today. Madame ought to hire a gendarme for this work. I am quite done in!”
“Coward,” I teased, though I was half smiling. “What is the message, then?”
“Precisely the kind of summons he hates most. He is to report to the Athénée at once, an official inspector has arrived and he *must* meet him. You know how he rebels against anything that tries to command him.”

I knew well enough. The stubborn, fiery little man detested being prodded or ruled, and would bristle at any order, no matter how reasonable. Still, I accepted the task—not without a flicker of fear, but tangled up in equal parts curiosity, and that strange, quiet thrill I always felt when I dared cross paths with his temper. I turned the handle slowly, slipped inside, and shut the door behind me as softly and quickly as my slightly unsteady hand would allow: to rattle a latch, or leave the door gaping, was a crime far more likely to enrage him than the intrusion itself.

There he sat, hunched over his desk, his mood clearly nearly at its worst. He was teaching arithmetic, a subject he despised more than any of his pupils feared it, and every last girl in the room was trembling at the sound of his sharp, staccato voice listing off sums. He made no move to look up, too proud to acknowledge that someone had dared defy his explicit order, and that gave me time to walk the length of the long classroom. I had never minded facing his anger close up, far less than I feared its distant, menacing threats.

I paused at the foot of his raised platform, just out of his immediate line of sight. I was not tall enough to see over the edge of his desk, and after two quick, unobserved peeks around the corner to catch a glimpse of his face—dark, sharp, almost tiger-like with suppressed irritation—my third glance was caught, pinned straight through the pupil of my eye, by the sharp, unblinking lenses of his spectacles. Rosine was right: those glasses held a blank, unyielding terror that outstripped even the fury of his bare eyes.

Luckily for me, those same short-sighted spectacles were useless for inspecting a trespasser standing right under his nose. He took them off at once, and suddenly we were on far more equal footing.

I was not really afraid of him, not close up, and when he grumbled about finding rope and a gallows to carry out his sentence, I had the presence of mind to pass him a threaded needle from my embroidery work, the thread already looped into a noose, tucked discreetly over the back of his chair where no one but he could see.

“Que me voulez-vous?” he growled, the sound rumbling deep in his chest, his teeth still clenched so tight it seemed impossible a smile could ever break free.
“Monsieur,” I said, unflinching, “I have come to ask for the impossible, the unheard of.” I wasted no time beating around the bush, and in a low, quick voice I delivered the summons from the Athénée, exaggerating its urgency just a little to push him over the edge into complying.

Of course, he immediately protested that he would not go, that he would not leave his class for any inspector, any king, any government body in Villette. But I knew duty and his own professional interests would force him to comply, so I stood silent, waiting, as if he had not spoken a word at all. When he snapped at me to say what else I wanted, I only answered that I needed his answer to give the waiting messenger.

I reached out, before he could stop me, for his battered Greek cap resting on the windowsill. He watched the daring movement with a look of half-amused, half-incredulous pity, that I would ever presume to touch his things.
“Ah,” he muttered, “if Miss Lucy is going that far, she might as well put the cap on herself, turn newsboy, and go to the Athénée in my place.”
I set the cap gently on his desk, where its tassel seemed to nod at me like a tiny, solemn judge.
“I will write a note of apology,” he said, still trying to evade the task. “That will be enough.”

I knew very well it would not. I gave the cap a soft nudge, pushing it towards his hand. It slid down the smooth, unlined slope of his polished desk, knocked his discarded spectacles right over the edge, and they clattered onto the wooden platform. A dozen times before I had seen them fall unharmed—this time, by some cruel twist of my bad luck, both lenses shattered into a thousand glittering, shapeless pieces.

Dismay flooded me, sharp and hot. I knew how precious those glasses were: M. Paul’s sight was strange, hard to fit, and this pair had been perfectly adjusted to his eyes. He often called them his most treasured possession. My hands shook as I picked up the broken fragments, and for several seconds I dared not look him in the face. I was far more sorry than I was afraid.

“Là,” he said, and to my shock there was no anger in his voice at all. “Now I am a widower, robbed of my spectacles. I think Mademoiselle Lucy will admit she has more than earned the rope and the gallows. You tremble, waiting for your doom. Ah, traitress! Traitress! You are determined to leave me blind and helpless in your hands.”

I lifted my eyes, and his face was not furrowed with rage at all. It was soft, lit up by that bright, warm bloom of a smile I had seen on him that night at the Hotel Crécy. He was not angry, not even grieved. The accident I had thought would ruin any chance of convincing him to go had turned out to be my greatest help. Stubborn and unmanageable as long as I had done nothing to cross him, he turned gentle, pliant, almost indulgent the second I stood before him a guilty, contrite offender.

He kept teasing me gently, calling me a terrible Englishwoman, a reckless little breaker of things, saying no one could dare refuse a woman bold enough to smash his spectacles to get her way, just like the great Emperor smashing a vase to cow his enemies. At last he settled his Greek cap on his head, took the broken glasses from my hand with a little squeeze of pardon and encouragement, bowed, and left for the Athénée in the highest of good spirits.

And yet, for all that unexpected amiability, I found myself quarrelling with him again before the sun had fully set on that day—and I could not help it.

It was his frequent, charming custom to arrive unannounced in the evenings, bursting into the quiet hour of study to take over our occupations, order all the pious, droning readings led by half-asleep pupils put away, and pull out a thick volume of drama or a stack of new Parisian feuilletons to read aloud. He would pour his own fiery, bright verve into every line, cutting out every unsuitable phrase for the young girls in the room, and whenever a passage would be left gaping and meaningless after his cuts, he would improvise whole new paragraphs far more vivid and proper than the parts he had struck out.

That evening, we were all sitting silent as nuns in retreat, pupils bent over their books, teachers over their work. I was stitching a small, intricate piece I meant as a gift, and the deadline to give it was close, so my fingers moved fast, entirely focused on the work.

We heard his familiar sharp ring at the bell, then his quick, familiar step down the corridor. The moment every girl whispered *“Voilà Monsieur!”* the double doors flew open, and he stood in the middle of the room.

Zélie St. Pierre, the senior teacher, jumped up at once to offer him her seat by the lamp, smiling that wide, sharp fake grin that stretched all across her face but never reached her eyes. But M. Paul, as capricious as they said women could be, never even seemed to see her. He walked straight past her, stopped right beside me, and whispered *“Ne bougez pas”* before I could stand up to make space, settling himself right between me and Ginevra Fanshawe, who had been crowding my side all winter, prodding me with her elbow every few minutes for warmth. I had even taken to tucking a small pin in my sash to deter her.

It was all very well for him to tell me not to move, but I had to make space for him. I pulled my embroidery basket back, shifted myself a full yard down the bench to leave him plenty of room, the sort of polite, respectful gap any reasonable man would have welcomed. But M. Paul Emanuel was never reasonable. Flint and tinder, he caught fire the instant he saw the empty space I had left between us.

“You do not want me as your neighbour,” he growled, scowling. “You put on airs of rank, you treat me like a pariah. Very well! I will fix this.”
He ordered all the girls to stand, file over to the other long table, then carefully carried my work basket, my silk, my scissors, every last one of my tools, over to the far end of my bench, and planted himself at the completely opposite end, a full ten feet away from me.

No one dared laugh at the absurd, dramatic arrangement. I sat completely calm, stitching away, quiet and not at all unhappy, even cut off from everyone else in the room.
“Is this distance enough for you?” he demanded.
“You are the judge of what is enough, Monsieur,” I said.
“You know very well it is not. You created this enormous empty gap, I had no part in it,” he insisted, and opened his book to begin reading.

He had chosen a terrible French translation of what he called “a drama by Williams Shakespeare, that false god of those silly pagan Englishmen”—a title he never would have used, I knew, if his temper had not already been ruffled. The translation was clunky, full of absurd mistranslations, and I did not bother hiding the little flickers of amused contempt that crossed my face. His spectacles were sharp, and he caught every single one of my tiny eye rolls. Soon he was fuming so hard, exiled at his end of the bench, that he was hotter than he would ever have been sitting right under the full glow of the overhead lamp.

When the reading ended, no one was sure if he would leave without saying a word, or let his anger spill over. I had not spoken a single sound, so he had no real grounds to scold me. The plates of bread and tepid diluted milk for supper were brought in, and everyone ate, chatting softly across the other table, while I stayed right on my end of the bench, working as I munched my bread, perfectly calm. The strange, restless, thorny energy of him across the room seemed to draw all the jittery, unsettled feelings out of me like a magnet, leaving nothing but quiet, steady peace.

He stood up, turned for the door. I thought he would leave without a word. But he turned back, fumbled for his forgotten pencil case, snapped the point of his pencil against the table, re-sharpened it, and then walked straight over to me.

He stood behind my chair. He asked what I was making.
“A watchguard,” I said.
“For whom?” he demanded.
“For a gentleman friend of mine,” I answered.

He leaned down, and hissed in my ear, voice sharp with jealous, tender irritation, that I was the most exasperating woman he had ever met, that no one could be on friendly terms with me for five minutes without me turning all harmony to discord. He said he wished me no harm, he had always meant well by me, but I treated him as if he was a nuisance, full of stubborn mutiny against every gentle thing he tried to do for me.

“Vivacities? Mutiny?” I said, surprised enough to interrupt him. “I never—”
“Chut! There you go again, as fiery as gunpowder,” he fumed, but there was no real bite in it. He said he grieved for my stubbornness, that I was wasting my good qualities on being too fond of vain, pretty, tall people who cared more about their looks than anything of substance, that I ought to be more sober, less flighty, less silly. He chided me for my recent clothes, too: the flowers in my bonnet, the embroidered collars, the time he had seen me in a bright scarlet gown.

“It was not scarlet!” I protested, half indignant half laughing. “It was pale pink, and covered over with black lace.”
“Pink, scarlet, all the same, all those giddy, flaunting colours,” he sighed, mourning my supposed degeneracy. When I asked him what possible fashion he could find in my plain winter merino dress and simple white collar, he said there was a ribbon bow tied at my neck, far too frivolous. I held up the half-finished silk and gold watchguard in my hand, teasing him if he disapproved of a ribbon bow on a woman, he would surely disapprove of a watchguard for a man too. He only groaned, as if my levity pained him deeply.

He sat in silence for a few minutes, watching my fingers fly over the stitching of the chain, before he spoke again, soft, almost quiet.
“Tell me,” he said. “After all the scolding I have just done—will you utterly detest me now?”
