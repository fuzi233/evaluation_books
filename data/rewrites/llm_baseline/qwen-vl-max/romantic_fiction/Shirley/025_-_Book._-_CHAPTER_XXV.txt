Not all who wrestle with fate emerge victorious. Night after night, the sweat of anguish may glisten on a brow; the soul may cry out in silence—its voiceless plea rising to the unseen, pleading: "Spare my beloved. Heal what is most dear to me. Do not take from me what has become part of my very being. O God, bend, hear, have mercy!" And yet, when dawn breaks, it may bring no triumph—only the quiet horror of defeat. The morning that once greeted him with the sigh of zephyrs and the song of larks now breathes through lips grown pale and cold: “Oh, I’ve had such a terrible night. I’m worse this morning. I tried to rise… I cannot. Dreams I never knew before have haunted me.” Then the watcher draws near, sees the familiar face reshaped by suffering, feels the terrible truth—the moment of farewell is near. It is God’s will, and he bows his head, his heart crushed beneath the weight of inevitable sorrow.

But happy Mrs. Pryor! She was still lost in prayer, unaware that the summer sun had already climbed above the hills, when her daughter stirred gently in her arms. No piteous moan—no sound that drains strength even from the strongest resolve—preceded her waking. No long silence followed. Instead, the first words were clear, bright, full of life: “Mamma, I’ve slept so well. I only dreamed twice and woke up.”

Mrs. Pryor started upright, hiding her tears before her daughter could see them—the word “mamma,” spoken so naturally, the warmth of her return, filling her with joy she dared not fully claim. For days, she had allowed herself only fleeting hope, trembling with each flicker of improvement. A spark would flare—then fade. Excitement gave way swiftly to exhaustion. Caroline struggled to appear better, though her body often refused to obey her will. Her efforts to eat, to speak, to smile, faltered again and again. There were hours when Mrs. Pryor feared the thread of life might snap at any moment—even if its breaking could be delayed.

In those quiet days, mother and daughter seemed almost alone in Briarfield. It was late August. The weather had been harsh—dry, dusty, baked under an unrelenting east wind that had blown for weeks. The sky was cloudless, yet a faint, sickly haze hung over everything, dulling the blue of heaven, bleaching the green of earth, dimming the light of day. Nearly every household had fled to escape the heat and discomfort. Miss Keeldar and her friends were at the seaside, as was Mrs. Yorke’s family. Mr. Hall and Louis Moore, drawn together by shared views and temperaments, had journeyed northward on foot to the Lakes. Even Hortense, who longed to stay and help care for Caroline, had been persuaded by Miss Mann to return to Wormwood Wells, where her own health was failing under the oppressive air. Unable to refuse a request that touched both her kindness and her pride, she left reluctantly.

As for Robert, he had gone from Birmingham to London, where he remained.

While the desert wind parched Caroline’s skin and fevered her blood, her recovery lagged behind her returning peace of mind. But then came a change. The wind ceased its mournful groaning at the rectory’s eastern gable and the church’s oriel window. A small cloud, shaped like a hand, rose in the west. Gusts carried it forward, spreading it wide. Rain fell, fierce and brief, followed by thunder. When it passed, the sun emerged warmly, the sky regained its deep blue, and the land turned green again. The pallid, unhealthy tinge vanished from nature. The hills stood clear against the horizon, freed from the lingering haze.

Now, Caroline’s youth could aid her. So could her mother’s care—and God’s grace. Together, they brought the soft, cleansing west wind into her room, drifting through the open lattice like a whisper of healing. At last, Mrs. Pryor saw it—true convalescence had begun.

It wasn’t just that Caroline smiled more brightly or spoke with renewed cheer. Something deeper had shifted. A look—terrible, indescribable—had left her face. Those who have watched beside a dying bed will know the expression: hollow, distant, beyond pain, yet terrifying in its stillness. That look was gone. Before her cheeks filled or color returned, her whole presence softened. Where once there had been marble and glass, now there was life—fragile, yes, but unmistakably alive. She was no longer a statue, but a girl recovering.

She stopped asking constantly for water. The words “I am so thirsty” faded from her lips. Sometimes, after swallowing a morsel, she’d say, “That revived me.” Food no longer tasted uniformly bitter. She began to show preferences—tiny, precious signs of returning vitality. How carefully her mother prepared each dish! How closely she watched as Caroline ate. Nourishment brought strength. Soon, Caroline could sit up. Then she longed to breathe fresh air, to see her flowers, to watch how the fruit had ripened.

Her uncle, generous as ever, had bought a garden chair for her use. He carried her down himself, placed her gently in it, and William Farren stood ready to wheel her about the paths. He showed her what he had done among her plants, took her directions for new work. They talked easily—of bees burrowing under the cherry tree, of hedge-sparrows nesting in the hedges, of pearly eggs and young fledglings. If *Chambers's Journal* had existed then, it would have been their favorite reading. Both believed in the wisdom of animals, loved minute observations, and found wonder in the natural world. It explained why Caroline insisted on William guiding her chair—why his company made her outings meaningful.

Mrs. Pryor walked nearby, wondering how her daughter could feel so at ease with a man of the people. To her, William Farren was stiff, coarse, ill-matched. She couldn’t speak to him without formality, feeling a vast divide between their worlds. To cross it, she thought, would be to lower herself. One evening, she asked softly, “Are you not afraid, my dear, to talk so freely with him? He might grow bold, or too forward.”

“William presume?” Caroline replied, surprised. “You don’t know him. He never does. He’s too proud, too sensitive. William has very fine feelings.”

Mrs. Pryor smiled, skeptical—how could a rough-handed, fustian-clad man possess such delicacy?

Farren, for his part, met her with a sullen glance. He sensed her judgment, and when misunderstood, he became stubborn and hard to manage.

But evenings belonged entirely to mother and daughter. Then, alone together, no shadow of distance remained. Mrs. Pryor cherished these moments—when love flowed freely, unmediated by society or propriety.

During the day, however, she reverted to her usual reserve. With Mr. Helstone, their interaction was strictly formal—respectful, but distant. Familiarity would have bred contempt in either of them. Yet, through careful courtesy, they managed harmony. Toward the servants, her manner was cool, shy, even chilly. Perhaps it was not pride, but diffidence, that made her seem aloof—but Fanny and Eliza didn’t distinguish. They resented her, and she felt it. It weighed on her, making her dissatisfied with herself, melancholy, silent.

Only Caroline could melt this ice. Not with words of affection—those would have seemed insincere to Mrs. Pryor—but with quiet dependence, fearless trust. She leaned on her mother, confided in her, claimed her help with easy intimacy. “Mamma, do this.” “Please, mamma, fetch me that.” “Read to me, mamma.” “Sing a little, mamma.”

No one else had ever looked to her so naturally, so confidently. Others kept their distance, aware of her flaws. But Caroline saw none of that—not now, not ever. Yet she could still find fault. Blind to inherited weaknesses, she noticed habits that could change. On certain matters, she lectured her mother—not harshly, but with gentle firmness—and instead of being offended, Mrs. Pryor felt pleased. To be corrected by her daughter—by someone who felt so completely at home—was a comfort.

“Mamma,” Caroline said, “I won’t let you wear that old gown anymore. It’s not becoming. Too tight in the skirt. You must wear your black silk every afternoon. You look nice in it. And for Sundays—a real black satin dress. Not satinet, not a sham. And when you get it, you must wear it.”

“My dear,” Mrs. Pryor replied, “I thought the black silk would serve me for years. I meant to buy you things with the money.”

“Nonsense, mamma. Uncle gives me cash for what I want. He’s generous. And I want to see you in satin. Get it soon. Let me choose the pattern. You always try to hide yourself—like a grandmother. You make people think you’re old and plain. But you’re not! When you’re well dressed and cheerful, you’re very comely. Your smile is lovely, your teeth are white, your hair is still a beautiful light color. You speak like a young lady—with such a clear, sweet voice. And you sing better than anyone I’ve ever heard. Why do you wear such dresses and bonnets—ones no one else wears?”

“Does it bother you, Caroline?”

“Very much. It vexes me. People say you’re miserly. But you’re not—look at your gifts to the poor, to religious societies. Only you give quietly, so few know. But I’ll be your maid. When I’m stronger, I’ll help you. And you must do as I say.”

Caroline sat close, smoothing her mother’s handkerchief, brushing her hair back. “My own mamma,” she murmured, delighting in the thought. “Who belongs to me, and to whom I belong. I’m rich now. I have something I can love deeply—and not fear loving.”

“Who gave you this brooch, mamma?” she asked, gently unpinning it.

“My sister—my only sister, Cary. I wish Aunt Caroline had lived to see her niece.”

“Do you have anything of papa’s? A trinket? A gift?”

“I have one thing.”

“That you prize?”

“That I prize.”

“Valuable and pretty?”

“Invaluable and sweet to me.”

“Show it, mamma. Is it here or at Fieldhead?”

“It’s talking to me now. Leaning on me. Its arms are around me.”

“Oh, mamma—you mean your teasing daughter, who never lets you rest, who follows you like a dog.”

“Whose face still gives me a strange thrill sometimes. I half fear your beauty, child.”

“You don’t. You can’t. Mamma, I’m sorry papa wasn’t good. I wish he had been. Wickedness ruins everything. It kills love. If we thought each other wicked, we couldn’t love, could we?”

“And if we couldn’t trust each other, Cary?”

“We’d be miserable. Mother, before I knew you, I feared you—thought you weren’t good. That dread made me hesitant to meet you. But now—I’m joyful. I find you perfect—almost. Kind, clever, kind-hearted. Your only fault is being old-fashioned. And I’ll fix that.”

“Put down your work, mamma. Read to me. I like your southern accent—it’s so pure, so soft. No rough burr, no nasal twang like everyone else’s here. Uncle and Mr. Hall say you’re a wonderful reader. Mr. Hall said no lady read with such expression or purity of tone.”

“I wish I could say the same of you, Cary. But the first time I heard your dear friend read and preach, I couldn’t understand his broad northern speech.”

“Could you understand me, mamma? Did I sound rough?”

“No. I almost wished you had. I wished you looked unpolished. Your father, Caroline, spoke beautifully—correctly, gently, smoothly. You’ve inherited his gift.”

“Poor papa. When he was so agreeable, why wasn’t he good?”

“Why he was as he was—something you, child, cannot comprehend—I cannot say. It is a mystery. The key lies in His hands. There I leave it.”

“Mamma, you keep stitching. Put it down. I hate sewing. It takes up your lap—where I want my head. It holds your eyes—where I want them on a book. Here’s your favorite—Cowper.”

These little demands were her mother’s joy. If she hesitated, it was only to enjoy hearing them repeated—to relish her daughter’s playful urgency. And when she gave in, Caroline would say, mischievously, “You’ll spoil me, mamma. I always thought I’d like to be spoiled—and now I find it very sweet.”

And so did Mrs. Pryor.
