Though the Baroness Bernstein had been bested in their previous encounter, she greeted her niece, Lady Maria, with perfect composure the next time they met. There was no trace of resentment in her manner—only the cool detachment of a woman who had long since mastered the art of concealment.

“Naturally, my dear Lady Maria,” she began, “you cannot expect me to rejoice at the prospect of my nephew Harry marrying a woman nearly his mother’s age and without a penny to her name. But if he insists on such folly, the affair is none of my concern. And I daresay my offer of five thousand pounds during our little quarrel was made more in heat than sincerity. So, this match was settled at Castlewood, was it? Had I known matters had progressed so far, I might have spared myself the trouble of opposition. After all, what use is scolding over a shattered vase?”

“Madam!” Lady Maria interjected sharply.

“Peace—I cast no aspersions on your honor. Harry vouches for you, and you for yourself. What more could one ask?”

“You have spoken to Mr. Warrington?”

“And he confessed to making you a promise at Castlewood—in writing, no less.”

“I have that writing, madam,” Lady Maria replied stiffly.

“Ah.” The Baroness did not flinch. “And I admit I misjudged the nature of your letters to him at first. They do, however, implicate other members of the family—”

“Who have slandered me relentlessly and sought to poison Mr. Warrington’s mind against me. Yes, I wrote to defend myself.”

“And now you fear those letters might fall into unscrupulous hands and become public scandal. Hence your distress.”

“Precisely,” said Lady Maria.

“Upon my word, Maria, I begin to admire you. It seems I have underestimated you these twenty years.”

“I am gratified, madam, that you finally grant me justice,” the younger woman replied coldly.

The Baroness’s eyes gleamed with amusement. “When I saw you dancing with Harry last night, do you know what I recalled?”

“I cannot fathom what might occupy your thoughts, madam.”

“Your performance of the same minuet with the dancing-master at Kensington, my dear.”

“That is a vile falsehood!”

“For which the poor man was thrashed, though entirely innocent!”

Lady Maria’s face flushed with fury. “To dredge up such calumny is cruel indeed. I shall not remain under the roof of anyone who repeats it.”

“You wish to leave? I daresay Tunbridge will grow rather warm for you if those letters surface.”

“They contain nothing against you, madam—rest assured of that.”

“Harry said as much, and gave you full credit. Well, my dear, we weary of each other. A separation will do us good.”

“I could not agree more,” Lady Maria said, dropping a curtsey.

The Baroness smiled. “Mr. Sampson can escort you to Castlewood. A postchaise will suffice for you and your maid.”

“A postchaise will suffice, and Mr. Sampson may escort me,” echoed Lady Maria. “You see, madam, I comply as a dutiful niece should.”

“Tell me, my dear—do you suppose Sampson has those letters?” the Baroness asked bluntly.

“The same thought has crossed my mind.”

“And you mean to coax them from him in the chaise? Delilah! Well, they are no use to me. I hope you succeed. When will you depart?”

“The sooner the better.”

“We are women of the world, Maria. We trade insults in passion, but part with civility. Shall we attend Lady Yarmouth’s card party? A change of scene will steady your nerves, and cards are better medicine than any doctor’s draught.”

Lady Maria agreed. She was ready first, waiting in the drawing-room when the Baroness descended. The elder lady noted that Maria’s door stood ajar.

*She carries them on her person,* Madame Bernstein thought.

Together, they presented a picture of perfect cordiality at the Countess’s gathering—proof that women could feign amity even after the bitterest quarrels.

That night, as Lady Maria prepared for bed, Madame Bernstein’s maid, Mrs. Brett, appeared in place of the absent Betty, who was—shamefully—incapable of service after a night of revelry with Gumbo and other servants. Lady Maria dismissed Mrs. Brett, insisting she needed no assistance.

*The letters are hidden in her stays,* the Baroness concluded.

The next morning, Betty endured a scolding for her overindulgence in Gumbo’s rum punch. Meanwhile, Case, the Baroness’s usually sober manservant, was conspicuously absent—ill, it was said, from the same excess.

At supper, the Baroness was all solicitude. She prescribed fortifying tonics for Maria’s recurring fainting spells and urged her to consult a London physician. Then, casually, she mentioned that Harry would soon travel to the city on her behalf.

Maria’s face fell. “You are sending him away?”

“Only for two days. I wish my solicitor, Mr. Draper, to include him in my will. And since I plan to travel soon, I shall entrust him with my jewel case—highwaymen are so bold these days.”

Maria protested, hoping Harry might escort her to Castlewood instead.

“Nonsense,” said his aunt. “The boy has been tethered too long. Let him have his freedom.”

Harry, eager for adventure, promised to guard the diamonds with his life. He would lodge at an inn—some lively place in Covent Garden, perhaps.

“Ah, I once dreamed of making this journey,” he said, his voice softening.

“With whom?” asked Lady Maria.

“One who promised to share it with me.” His grief was still fresh, though time had dulled its edge.

Madame Bernstein watched him with sympathy. “He had more heart than most,” she murmured.

The next day, Harry set off, his spirits lifting with each mile. London unfolded before him—Greenwich’s grand domes, the bustling Thames, the looming Tower. Gumbo gaped at the sights, while Harry marveled at the reality of scenes he had only read of in books.

At Middle Temple Lane, he delivered the jewel case to Mr. Draper, who received it with marked interest. The lawyer, eager to play host, suggested a tour of the city. Harry, though feigning nonchalance, was dazzled by the Strand’s thronging crowds, the rebel heads on Temple Bar, and the storied dueling ground of Leicester Fields.

That evening, over a lavish supper, Draper regaled him with tales of the Esmonds. Yet Harry’s mind wandered—until he spotted a familiar shabby figure in the gardens.

“That is Mr. Johnson, the author,” he said, bowing.

Draper sneered. “Avoid such men, sir. They bring nothing but trouble.”

Harry bristled but held his tongue. The lawyer’s condescension grated, yet he endured it for the sake of his aunt’s business.

The following days passed in a whirl—Harry bought fine horses, wandered through Westminster Abbey and the Tower, and lost himself in the city’s wonders. Yet beneath his excitement lurked a restless dissatisfaction.

News from Virginia in the morning paper struck a deeper chord. The colony’s gentlemen had taken up arms against the French.

“Why are we not there, Gumbo?” he cried.

The servant grinned. “Why indeed, Master Harry?”

But the call of duty was drowned by the distractions of London. That night, as St. Paul’s bells rang for morning prayers, Harry thought of Sampson’s impending sermon—and smiled. He had learned to see through hollow words.

The storm brewing in his heart, however, was another matter entirely.
