They set Denys in the very midst of them, elbowed and hemmed as if his grief itself were a kind of weapon. Yet no cord could bind what tore inside him. He rode like a man who has swallowed fire: now spitting it at heaven and earth in raving curses, now dropping into a silence so black and sharp that even rough soldiers, who had heard dying men rattle prayers through split throats, looked sideways and decided it was wiser to take his arms away.

All his thought went in one ring, round and round, wearing the same groove deeper: for his sake Gerard had forsaken the German road to Rome—and for that mercy what came? Gerard left alone in the very belly of Burgundy, where vineyards climbed the slopes like patient hands and every lane might hide a knife. Denys felt the bargain of friendship turn to ashes in his mouth.

They overtook their leader just beyond the town gates, and the whole cavalcade drew bridle and baited at the Tête d’Or. The inn steamed like a pot set on coals. From the kitchen came the fat, consoling breath of broth, onions sweated down to sweetness, a hare somewhere turning on a spit and weeping its own perfume into the smoke; the clack of trenchers and the hiss of ale drawn bright and quick were a kind of rough music—one that should have steadied a man’s heart. But Denys heard none of it rightly. The clang of harness, the barked orders, the scrape of boots on rushes all rasped him raw, as though sound itself had turned against him.

The young landlady—nimble as a cook who can turn from sauce to guest without spilling either—was much taken up with the count, and more still with the bastard; yet her eyes, sharp as a carver’s knife, found Denys in his misery. She came near enough to smell the road-dust on him and asked, with an uneasiness she tried to cover, what had become of his young companion.

Denys’s grief broke out like a pot boiling over. He told her all—how Gerard was parted from him—and begged her to send after him.

“Now he is sundered from me, he will maybe listen to my rede,” he cried; “poor wretch, he loves not solitude.”

The landlady tossed her head, colour rising in her cheeks as if the very request had touched a sore place. “I trow I have been somewhat over-kind already,” said she.

“You will not?”

“Not I.”

Then Denys—who had nothing left but the sting of his tongue—poured out a volley of curses that made even the nearest trooper shift. She turned her back as if he had flung dirty dishwater at her, and went off whimpering that she was not used to be cursed at. Yet, even while she spoke so, she snapped orders to her hind to saddle two mules.

Denys rode north with his troop, mute now, drooping over the saddle like a man whose spine had been cut. He did not know—could not know—that the same “veracious” young woman, who had sworn him no help, made herself ready in forty minutes as briskly as one trussing a capon, threw on cloak and riding-gear, and spurred away with her servant in the opposite direction. Her haste had the clean, clipped rhythm of purpose; her horse’s hoofbeats dwindled on the road like a receding drum.

At dark, after a long march, the bastard and his men reached the White Hart. Their arrival turned the inn inside out. Doors banged; voices rose; dogs yelped; the stable rang with iron on stone. Pots were set to boil and ladles struck rims in a hurry, and the air filled with the sharp-sweet promise of bread warming and meat being sliced too fast, the knife scraping bone. In that uproar Denys moved like a shadow.

It took Manon time to find him among so many. When at last she did, her only sign was a sudden heightening of colour, as if she had leaned too close to a hearth. She did not claim the acquaintance. She stood in her worshipful place now, among those who mattered, and the poor soul within her had already begun that slow new scorn of the ladder she had climbed—those base degrees by which she did ascend. Denys saw it all and could not so much as twist his mouth into a smile. This inn, with its smells of stew and spilled wine, its familiar smoke and grease and trampled rushes, reminded him too much of Gerard—of shared crusts, of meat divided by a blade and laughter caught between mouthfuls. His loss sat in his throat like dry bread.

Before the night closed, the wind changed, and with it the whole sound of the place: the draught found cracks and whistled; the sign creaked; a far-off owl called as if counting the living. Manon looked into the room and beckoned Denys with her finger.

He rose sulkily—and his guards rose with him, for they watched him like men watch a dog that has been beaten but may still bite.

“Nay,” said Manon, quick and low, “I would speak a word to thee in private.”

She drew him to a corner where the noise thinned to a dull wash, and there, under her breath, she asked would he do her a kindness.

Denys answered aloud, brutal as a man who has been rubbed raw and then asked for velvet. “No, I will not. I am not in the vein to do kindnesses to man or woman. If I did a kindness it should be to a dog; and not that if I could help it.”

“Alas, good archer,” said Manon, humbly as a beggar at a buttery hatch, “I did you one eftsoons—you and your pretty comrade.”

“You did, dame, you did,” he said, the edge dulling a little at Gerard’s name. “Well then, for his sake—what is’t to do?”

“Thou knowest my story,” she murmured. “I had been unfortunate. Now I am worshipful. But a woman did cast him in my teeth this day. And so ’twill be ever while he hangs there. I would have him ta’en down; well-a-day!”

“With all my heart,” said Denys, fierce with a sudden, misplaced generosity.

“And none dare I ask but thee,” she whispered. “Wilt do’t?”

“Not I,” he said, and the word struck like a mallet, “even were I not a prisoner.”

On this stern refusal Manon sighed and clasped her palms together, a gesture as old as hearth-prayer, despairing. Denys, who had no patience left for her delicacy, told her not to fret. There were soldiers of a lower stamp—men who would not make two bites of such a cherry. It was a mere matter of money. If she could find two angels, he would find two soldiers to do the dirty work.

The suggestion sat ill on her tongue. She had wanted the deed washed clean by friendship and secrecy, not bought like salt pork. Yet she reflected—slowly, as one who turns a thought over like a dumpling to see if it is cooked through—that soldiers were birds of passage: drinking here to-night, knocked on the head there to-morrow. At last she said, soft as if ashamed of her own voice, “Send them out to me. But prithee, tell them that ’tis for one that is my friend; let them not think ’tis for me; I should sink into the earth. Times are changed.”

Denys found two warriors glad to win an angel apiece so easily. He sent them out, and instantly, with contempt for the whole matter, dropped his mind back into its old trench, brooding on his lost friend as a man worries a crust he cannot swallow.

Manon and the mail-clad fellows soon came to understanding. Yet even agreed sin has its housekeeping: what were they to do with the body when it was taken down? Manon’s voice trembled, but her words were practical—she had thought upon it more than once, as a cook thinks upon storage and spoilage.

“The river is nigh the—the place,” she murmured.

“Fling him in, eh?” one said, blunt as a cleaver.

“Nay, nay; be not so cruel!” she breathed, horror and need wrestling. “Could ye not put him—gently—and—with somewhat weighty?”

It was all speedily settled, save the time of payment. The mail-clad itched for their coin and asked for it in advance. Manon demurred. Did she doubt their word? Then let her come along with them, or watch from a distance.

“Me?” Manon cried, as if they had asked her to taste blood. “I would liever die than see it done.”

“Which yet you would have done,” they answered, with the rough justice of men who hear only the clink of money.

“Ay,” she said, and the confession came out like a cough, “for sore is my need. Times are changed.”

She had already forgotten her own caution to Denys. The night swallowed their footsteps. An hour later that disagreeable relic of caterpillar existence—hung up between past and present—ceased to canker the worshipful matron’s public life, and the grim eyes of what had been stopped looking down into a white hind’s clover field. The village air, so long tuned to the creak of rope and the small, constant complaints of the dead, seemed suddenly altered: the night had one less harsh note in it, as if a string had been cut.

Manon went on to make the landlord an average wife, and a prime house-dog, and to outlive everybody; for such is the stubbornness of lives that root themselves in bread and habit. But that was after.

When the two soldiers came back from their errand—having followed, with a kind of medieval naïveté, the precept “Off wi’ the auld love”—they received a shock. They found the market-place black with groups; it had been empty an hour ago. Voices swarmed like bees. Lanterns bobbed. The very air was thick with whispering, and every sound—shuffling feet, quick prayers, a child’s sudden cry—seemed to strike the ear too loud.

Conscience smote them, for even hard men feel the world listen when they have meddled with the dead. The bolder of the two, heartened by darkness, stole forward alone and slyly mingled with a group. He listened a moment, then ran back to his fellow and said, in a tone of reproach not strictly reasonable:

“Ye born fool, it is only a miracle.”
