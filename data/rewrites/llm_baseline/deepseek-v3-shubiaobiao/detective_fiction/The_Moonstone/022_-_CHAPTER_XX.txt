The news had raced ahead of us. By the time we reached the house, the servants were in turmoil. As we passed Lady Verinder’s door, it flew open with a violent force. My mistress stormed out, her composure shattered, her face a mask of horror. Behind her, Mr. Franklin followed, his attempts to calm her proving futile.

"You are responsible for this!" she cried, her voice trembling with fury as she leveled an accusing finger at Sergeant Cuff. "Gabriel! Pay this man his wages and rid me of his presence!"

The Sergeant alone remained unshaken. He met her outburst with quiet authority. "My lady, I am no more to blame for this tragedy than you are," he said, his tone respectful but firm. "If, in half an hour, you still wish me gone, I will accept my dismissal—but not your money."

His words struck home. My mistress hesitated, then allowed Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside. As the door closed, the Sergeant’s sharp eyes swept over the gathered servants, lingering on Penelope, the only one among them in tears.

"When your father has changed into dry clothes," he said to her, "join us in his room."

Before the half-hour had passed, I had dressed and lent the Sergeant what spare clothing I could offer. Penelope entered, her eyes still damp, and sat beside me. I held her close, struck by the depth of her loyalty. She buried her face against my shoulder, and for a moment, we sat in silence—both of us, I think, mourning the poor lost soul who had met such a wretched end.

The Sergeant stood by the window, his back to us, lost in thought. I thanked him for his consideration, knowing full well that folk of our station seldom had the luxury of indulging in grief. Life demanded we swallow our sorrow and carry on.

When the questioning began, Penelope confirmed what I had suspected: Rosanna’s despair had been born of love—love for Mr. Franklin. Yet she had kept silent, for Rosanna’s sake and his.

"I’ll not have him burdened with guilt," I added. "If Rosanna died for love of him, let him leave this house without that knowledge weighing on his conscience."

The Sergeant murmured agreement, then fell silent, as if weighing Penelope’s words against some private theory.

Soon after, my lady’s bell summoned me. In the hall, I crossed paths with Mr. Franklin, who informed me that Lady Verinder was ready to receive the Sergeant—with me in attendance, as before. But first, he wished to speak to Cuff himself.

As we walked, Mr. Franklin paused, his gaze fixed on the railway timetable. "You mean to leave us, then, sir?" I asked. "Surely Miss Rachel will come around, given time?"

"She will," he replied, his voice hollow. "When she learns I am gone—and that she need never see me again."

It was not anger that drove him, but resignation. He had seen, at last, how even the mention of his name inflamed Miss Rachel’s temper. The realization had wounded him deeply. Now, resolved to spare her further distress, he would depart.

What he had to say to the Sergeant was brief. He conveyed my lady’s regret for her earlier outburst and asked whether Cuff would now accept his fee and leave the matter of the Diamond unresolved.

The Sergeant’s refusal was firm. "My duty is not yet done," he said. "When I have laid out the facts plainly for Lady Verinder, and advised her on how to proceed, then—and only then—will I consider my obligation fulfilled. The choice to continue will be hers."

There was no arguing with such integrity. As I prepared to escort him to my lady, he asked if Mr. Franklin wished to join us.

"Not unless Lady Verinder insists," Mr. Franklin replied. Then, in a low voice to me, he added, "I know what he means to say about Rachel. I love her too much to hear it and remain calm. Leave me be."

I left him there, leaning heavily against the window, his face hidden in his hands. Penelope hovered nearby, her heart aching for him. Had I been in his place, I might have called her in—for there is no solace like a woman’s sympathy when another has wounded you.

But I had my duty. With the Sergeant at my side, I went to face my lady.

The last time we had stood before her, she had scarcely looked up from her book. Now, her gaze was steady, her bearing unshaken. The Verinder spirit burned in her eyes. Whatever the Sergeant had to say, she would meet it head-on—a worthy match for any man, even one as formidable as Sergeant Cuff.
