Mrs. Henry van der Luyden heard her cousin’s account as she heard everything: without haste, without interruption, as though words were a broth that must be left to settle so the fat could rise and be skimmed. Mrs. Archer, who had rehearsed her story on the drive up, told it again in that high, white Madison Avenue drawing-room where even sound seemed domesticated—muffled by gauze-draped ormolu and shrouded frames, softened as if the house itself feared the least clatter. The air held a faint, stale sweetness, like linen long laid away with sachets; and beneath the monumental tick of the clock on the marble mantel, Mrs. Archer’s indignation seemed to fall, drop by drop, into a silence that did not answer back.

It was all very well to remind oneself, in advance, that Mrs. van der Luyden was always silent, and that her silence—trained into her, like a manner of serving—could be a kind of kindness. Yet the chill came all the same. The room was too lofty, too pale, too prepared: brocaded armchairs uncovered for the occasion as carefully as a family silver tureen uncovered for guests; the great painted faces—the Gainsborough ancestress and Huntington’s “perfect likeness”—watching with that unblinking authority which had outlasted generations of dinners, engagements, scandals, and reconciliations.

Newland Archer, sitting beside his mother, watched Mrs. van der Luyden beneath her own portrait and thought how little time did to her. She still wore black velvet and Venetian point when she admitted society into her rooms—since she never dined out, she received as one performs a rite. Her fair hair had faded without turning grey; her pale blue eyes looked, not coldly, but as if preserved—life held in suspension, like fruit in ice. He esteemed her, as he had been taught to; but her sweetness—bent, compliant, and always deferring—was less approachable than the blunt refusals of the fierce old spinsters in his family, who said “No” on principle and only afterward asked what had been proposed.

Mrs. van der Luyden’s face, on the other hand, said neither yes nor no. It leaned toward mercy until her thin lips wavered into the shadow of a smile and the customary words appeared, laid on the conversation like a lid: “I shall first have to talk this over with my husband.”

Archer had often wondered—watching the two of them, so exactly matched—how, after forty years of living in one another’s thoughts, two such merged identities ever separated enough for anything as controversial as a “talking-over.” Yet it was the consecrated formula; and Mrs. Archer, having set forth the case, waited resignedly for it.

But Mrs. van der Luyden did something unaccustomed. Her long hand went out toward the bell-rope.

“I think,” she said, “I should like Henry to hear what you have told me.”

A footman appeared, soundless as a shadow. Mrs. van der Luyden addressed him gravely: “If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper, please ask him to be kind enough to come.”

She said “reading the newspaper” as another woman might have said “presiding at a Cabinet meeting”—not from vanity, but because her whole life, and the posture of her world toward her husband, had taught her that his smallest acts carried weight. Then, as if to keep her promptness from being mistaken for commitment, she added, with the gentlest look: “Henry always enjoys seeing you, dear Adeline; and he will wish to congratulate Newland.”

The double doors reopened with the same solemnity as a church’s, and Mr. Henry van der Luyden appeared between them: tall, spare, frock-coated, his faded fair hair brushed back, his straight nose dividing pale grey eyes that held the same frozen gentleness as his wife’s. He offered Mrs. Archer cousinly affability, gave Newland low-voiced congratulations in nearly the same words Mrs. van der Luyden had used, and seated himself in a brocaded armchair with the unstudied simplicity of a man born to be deferred to.

“I had just finished reading the Times,” he said, laying his long fingertips together. “In town my mornings are so much occupied that I find it more convenient to read the newspapers after luncheon.”

Mrs. Archer, ever ready with the right response, answered as one hands across the table the dish expected of her. “Ah, there’s a great deal to be said for that plan—indeed I think my uncle Egmont used to say he found it less agitating not to read the morning papers till after dinner.”

“Yes: my good father abhorred hurry. But now we live in a constant rush,” said Mr. van der Luyden, looking about him with a measured benevolence, as though the shrouded room were an emblem of the world he regretted.

“But I hope you had finished your reading, Henry?” his wife interposed quickly.

“Quite—quite,” he assured her.

“Then I should like Adeline to tell you—”

“Oh, it’s really Newland’s story,” Mrs. Archer smiled; and as if beginning again at the beginning were as necessary as the careful repeating of a recipe, she rehearsed once more the monstrous tale of the affront to Mrs. Lovell Mingott: the dinner planned, the invitations sent, the Leffertses’ refusal, and the whispered implication that the Countess Olenska was too questionable to be met.

“Of course,” she ended, “Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott both felt that, especially in view of Newland’s engagement, you and Henry ought to know.”

“Ah—” said Mr. van der Luyden, and drew a deep breath.

The silence that followed made the clock’s tick swell until it seemed to pound like a signal gun. Newland looked at the two slender, faded figures sitting side by side in viceregal rigidity, mouthpieces of that remote ancestral authority which the city still obeyed even while it pretended to outgrow it. In his mind—because the van der Luydens always roused in him the image of sheltered routine—he saw them far away at Skuytercliff, in the hush of clipped lawns where the loudest thing might be a distant mower or a sparrow’s quarrel, bending over their invisible weeds and playing Patience in the evenings. Their lives were arranged like a carefully planned household menu: simple, repetitive, and so exact that any new flavor—any foreign spice—was treated as a danger to digestion.

Mr. van der Luyden spoke first. “You really think this is due to some—some intentional interference of Lawrence Lefferts’s?” he asked, turning to Newland.

“I’m certain of it, sir,” Archer said. He felt again, as he spoke, the ugly relish of Lefferts’s maneuver—the way a man could salt his own sins by loudly condemning another’s supposed impropriety. “Larry has been going it rather harder than usual lately—if cousin Louisa won’t mind my mentioning it—having rather a stiff affair with the postmaster’s wife in their village, or some one of that sort; and whenever poor Gertrude Lefferts begins to suspect anything, and he’s afraid of trouble, he gets up a fuss of this kind, to show how awfully moral he is, and talks at the top of his voice about the impertinence of inviting his wife to meet people he doesn’t wish her to know. He’s simply using Madame Olenska as a lightning-rod; I’ve seen him try the same thing often before.”

“The Leffertses!” said Mrs. van der Luyden, and in her faint emphasis there was horror, as if someone had suggested serving fish with a sweet sauce.

“The Leffertses!” echoed Mrs. Archer. “What would uncle Egmont have said of Lawrence Lefferts’s pronouncing on anybody’s social position? It shows what Society has come to.”

“We’ll hope it has not quite come to that,” Mr. van der Luyden returned firmly.

“Ah, if only you and Louisa went out more!” Mrs. Archer sighed—and instantly felt the mistake in her own ears, as one hears a dish crack in the kitchen and knows at once it cannot be repaired.

The van der Luydens were morbidly sensitive to criticism of their seclusion. They bore, like a hereditary burden, the city’s expectation that they should sit as judges; yet they were shy, retiring people, with no taste for public power. They lived as much as possible away from New York’s roar, pleading Mrs. van der Luyden’s health when invitations came, and protecting their quiet as farmers protect seed-corn. Newland, hearing his mother’s stumble, stepped in at once.

“Everybody in New York knows what you and cousin Louisa represent,” he said. “That’s why Mrs. Mingott felt she ought not to allow this slight on Countess Olenska to pass without consulting you.”

Mrs. van der Luyden glanced at her husband; he glanced back. Their pale eyes held together in that long, inward conference which always preceded a decision, as if they were translating the city’s noisy gossip into the older, more cautious dialect of their own conscience.

“It is the principle that I dislike,” Mr. van der Luyden said at last. “As long as a member of a well-known family is backed up by that family it should be considered—final.”

“It seems so to me,” Mrs. van der Luyden added, with the air of offering a new thought rather than repeating an inherited rule.

“I had no idea,” Mr. van der Luyden continued, “that things had come to such a pass.”

He paused, then looked again at his wife. “It occurs to me, my dear, that the Countess Olenska is already a sort of relation—through Medora Manson’s first husband. At any rate, she will be when Newland marries.” He turned to Archer. “Have you read this morning’s Times, Newland?”

“Why, yes, sir,” Archer answered. He had read it over his coffee, as he read half a dozen papers—New York feeding itself on print as steadily as it fed itself on oysters and roast.

Husband and wife exchanged another look. In that gaze, a plan formed with the quiet certainty of a household decision: what was to be served, to whom, and when—because in their world an invitation could be as decisive as a verdict. A faint smile fluttered over Mrs. van der Luyden’s face; she had evidently guessed and approved.

Mr. van der Luyden turned to Mrs. Archer. “If Louisa’s health allowed her to dine out—I wish you would say to Mrs. Lovell Mingott—she and I would have been happy to—er—fill the places of the Lawrence Leffertses at her dinner.”

He left a pause, letting the irony sink in as naturally as a spoon left standing in a thick custard.

“As you know, this is impossible,” he added.

Mrs. Archer gave the sympathetic assent required.

“But Newland tells me he has read this morning’s Times; therefore he has probably seen that Louisa’s relative, the Duke of St. Austrey, arrives next week on the Russia. He is coming to enter his new sloop, the Guinevere, in next summer’s International Cup Race; and also to have a little canvasback shooting at Trevenna.”

Mr. van der Luyden’s measured voice went on, growing almost kindly with the prospect of action. “Before taking him down to Maryland we are inviting a few friends to meet him here—only a little dinner—with a reception afterward. I am sure Louisa will be as glad as I am if Countess Olenska will let us include her among our guests.”

He rose, bending his long body with stiff friendliness toward his cousin and her son. “I think I have Louisa’s authority for saying that she will herself leave the invitation to dine when she drives out presently: with our cards—of course with our cards.”

Mrs. Archer, who understood at once that the famous seventeen-hand chestnuts would not be kept waiting, rose with hurried murmurs of gratitude. Mrs. van der Luyden beamed on her with a look like a gentle intercession; but Mr. van der Luyden lifted a protesting hand.

“There is nothing to thank me for, dear Adeline; nothing whatever. This kind of thing must not happen in New York; it shall not, as long as I can help it,” he pronounced with sovereign gentleness, steering them toward the door as though conducting them from a private audience.

Outside, the city’s noise resumed—wheels, calls, the restless scrape of modern life against old stone—but the van der Luydens’ decision moved through it like a calm current.

Two hours later, everyone knew that the great C-spring barouche in which Mrs. van der Luyden took the air at all seasons had been seen at old Mrs. Mingott’s door, where a large square envelope had been handed in. It was discussed the way menus are discussed in kitchens and drawing-rooms: not only for what it contained, but for what it signified—what it would permit, what it would forbid, what it would make impossible to say aloud thereafter.

That evening at the Opera, Mr. Sillerton Jackson was able to state, with the pleased precision of a man citing a document, that the envelope had contained a card inviting the Countess Olenska to the dinner the van der Luydens were giving the following week for their cousin, the Duke of St. Austrey.

Some of the younger men in the club box exchanged a smile at the announcement and looked sideways at Lawrence Lefferts. He sat carelessly in the front, pulling his long fair moustache, hearing—if he heard at all—the layered soundscape of the house: the soprano’s suspended note, the soft stir of silk, the whisper that traveled faster than music. Then, as the singer paused, he remarked with authority, as if nothing else mattered and his taste were the only law worth naming:

“No one but Patti ought to attempt the Sonnambula.”
