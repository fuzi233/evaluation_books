One of the penances novice Gerard laid upon himself was this: when Lodovico came near, he must meet him with gentleness—though, in the hidden place of his mind, the man still felt like something cold and slick that had once slid through blood. He took that task as a fasting of the heart, and for a little while it bit him hard. Yet no abstinence is so wisely chosen as the kind that opens a door; and when a door is once ajar, a whole household of better motions can steal in on their own feet.

At first Lodovico’s bearing had in it something almost laughable—an assassin wearing, as if it were a new velvet doublet, the complacency of having spared a life. He would look at Gerard as a cook looks at a dish that did not burn, and marvel in secret at his own restraint. But it is a great thing, even for a ruined man, to taste the sweetness of saving instead of spoiling. That taste lingers on the palate. It begins as a sharp, surprising note—like vinegar on the tongue—then warms, then calls for bread.

So the miscreant, finding the saving of life pleasant, grew by degrees averse to taking it; and from that aversion stumbled into remorse; and from remorse, as from a dark corridor, into something so like penitence that it began to shape his days. Teresa, who had long managed him as a housewife manages a hearth that will smoke unless watched, helped the change with the one threat she knew would reach him: not for the first time she told him she would leave him—leave him utterly—unless he consented to live honestly.

The good fathers of the convent, who understood that souls, like bodies, sometimes mend better when removed from old air and old sounds, lent their counsel. And so Lodovico and Teresa were sent by sea to Leghorn, where Teresa had friends. There, where the quay creaked under barrels and the tide made its steady lap against stone, the assassin settled down and became a porter.

At first he found it miserably dull work, and said so—said it with the sulk of a man whose ears have been fed on sudden cries and now must learn the long, patient music of labour. A porter’s day has its rhythm like kneading: lift, set down, tighten the rope, bear the weight, breathe. It is not the rattle of a stiletto in a sleeve, nor the quickened hush before a blow. It is the plain chant of bodies at work: the thud of bales, the grating of sledges, the calls of boatmen crossing one another like counterpoint over the water. Dull, yes—but better, one may think, than the brief excitement of being hewn in pieces by Princess Claelia’s myrmidons. His exile saved the unconscious penitent from that end.

The princess, balked of her revenge, turned inward as a lamp turned down too low: she brooded until her very rooms seemed to swallow their echoes, and she fell into a profound melancholy. In her restlessness she dismissed her confessor and took a new one—one with a great reputation for piety—to whom she poured out what she called her griefs.

The new confessor was no other than Fra Jerome.

She could not have fallen into harder hands, nor better. He heard her grimly out—standing like a mast in a storm, letting her words beat against him. Then, as a cook will shake a maid out of her dainty airs when the broth is scorching and there is no time for affectation, he took her and shook the delusions out of her as roughly as if she had been a kitchen-girl dawdling over spice. For, to do this hard monk justice, on the path of duty he feared the anger of princes as little as he did the sea.

He showed her, in a few words all thunder and lightning, that she was the criminal of criminals.

“Thou art the devil,” he cried, “that with thy money hath tempted one man to slay his fellow; and then, blinded with self-love, instead of blaming and punishing thyself, art thirsting for more blood—of guilty men, but not so guilty as thou.”

At first she resisted, for she was used to priests who smoothed and sweetened, like merchants adulterating wine for noble lips; and she told him, with a wounded pride, that she was not accustomed to be taken to task by her confessors. But Fra Jerome did not barter truth. He overpowered her; he threatened her with the Church’s curse here and hereafter; he tore the scales from her eyes as one scrapes burnt crust from a pot; he thundered and crushed her until the lofty lady—so practised in masks and measured speech—sank down and grovelled with remorse and terror at the feet of the gigantic Boanerges.

“Oh, holy father,” she sobbed at last, “have pity on a poor weak woman, and help me save my guilty soul. I was benighted for want of ghostly counsel like thine, good father. I waken as from a dream.”

“Doff thy jewels,” said Fra Jerome sternly.

“I will. I will.”

“Doff thy silk and velvet; and in humbler garb than wears thy meanest servant, wend thou instant to Loretto.”

“I will,” said the princess faintly, as though the sentence had struck the breath from her.

“No shoes; but a bare sandal.”

“No, father.”

“Wash the feet of pilgrims both going and coming; and to such of them as be holy friars tell thy sin, and abide their admonition.”

The princess shivered as if the very word wash had turned the water cold. Yet she whispered, “Oh, holy father—let me wear my mask.”

“Humph!”

“Oh, mercy! Bethink thee! My features are known through Italy.”

“Ay,” he said with a hard contempt, “beauty is a curse to most of ye. Well—thou mayst mask thine eyes; no more.”

On this concession she seized his hand, like a drowning woman snatching at wood, and was about to kiss it; but he snatched it rudely away.

“What would ye do?” he said. “That hand handled the eucharist but an hour agone: is it fit for such as thou to touch it?”

“Ah, no.” Her voice broke into the small sounds of one unmade. “But oh, go not without giving your penitent daughter your blessing.”

“Time enow to ask it when you come back from Loretto.”

Thus that marvellous occurrence by Tiber’s banks left its mark on all the actors, as prodigies are said to do: not only in the great gestures men remember, but in the daily motions—what hands lifted, what feet bore, what mouths learned to confess.

The assassin, softened by saving the life he had been paid to take, turned from the stiletto to the porter's knot. The princess went barefoot to Loretto, weeping her crime and washing the feet of base-born men—learning, with every basin filled and emptied, that the same act cannot be named “grief” by a lady and “sin” by a monk without changing its weight, and that some truths refuse the fine vocabulary of power.

And Gerard—carried from the Tiber into that convent a suicide—now passed for a young saint within its walls. Loving but experienced eyes were on him, watching the quiet signs by which men read souls as bakers read dough: the patience under pressure, the rising after collapse, the steadying of the breath when old temptations hissed like fat in a pan.

Upon a shorter probation than usual, he was admitted to priest’s orders. Soon after, he took the monastic vows and became a friar of St. Dominic. Dying to the world, the monk parted with the very name by which he had lived in it, and so broke the last link of association with earthly feelings.

Here Gerard ended, and Brother Clement began.
