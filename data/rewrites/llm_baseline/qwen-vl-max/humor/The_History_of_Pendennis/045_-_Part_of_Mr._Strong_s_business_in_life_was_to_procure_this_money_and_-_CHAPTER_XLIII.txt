CHAPTER XLIII. Alsatia Bred up, like a bailiff or a shabby attorney, about the purlieus of the Inns of Court, Shepherd’s Inn is always to be found in the close neighbourhood of Lincoln’s-Inn Fields and the Temple. Tucked away behind the soot-stained gables and chimney-stacks of Wych Street, Holywell Street, and Chancery Lane, it lies hidden from the outer world—accessible only by winding alleys where sunlight never quite dares to linger. Here, slop-sellers, brandy-ball hawkers, hard-bake purveyors, theatrical print vendors, and dealers in second-hand furniture (which looks as though it might have been used for something other than sleeping) line the narrow walls with their wares. The doors are many-belled; dirty children swarm around steps and shellfish trays, their voices rising above the clatter of pattens on damp pavement and the ever-present mud that seems to ooze from the earth itself.

Ballad-singers arrive with guttural cries, belting out satirical ditties against the Whig administration, bishops, dignified clergy, and even the German relatives of the royal family. Punch sets up his tiny theatre, confident of an audience—and sometimes a halfpenny or two. Women shriek at their children for loitering in the gutter, or worse still, at husbands staggering home from the gin-shop. It's a ceaseless din, a whirlwind of life, until you step into the quiet, old-fashioned quadrangle of Shepherd’s Inn.

At the centre, a mangy patch of grass hosts the statue of Shepherd, guarded by iron railings from the occasional assault of over-enthusiastic boys. The hall, bearing the founder’s arms, lines one side of the square; tall, ancient chambers stretch along the others, and a central archway leads into Oldcastle Street and beyond into the great London thoroughfare. Once perhaps inhabited by lawyers, the Inn now welcomes all comers—the legal profession having long since moved on. No major firms occupy these chambers anymore.

Instead, we find the offices of the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines, which have yet to surface from under water, and the Registry of Patent Inventions and Union of Genius and Capital Company—a name that sounds like a joke but is taken seriously by its clerks. The only gentleman whose name appears both here and in the “Law List” is Mr. Campion, who sports a fine pair of mustachios and arrives twice or thrice a week in his cab. At his West End offices in Curzon Street, Mayfair, he is known as Mr. Somerset Campion, where Mrs. Campion entertains nobility and gentry whom her husband lends money to. But here, he’s simply Campion and Co., and the same tuft of hair adorns both his chin and the firm’s official card.

It’s a sight to behold when his cab rolls up: the horse, decked in harness blazing with heraldic emblems, snorts froth from his nostrils as he chafes beneath the shining bit. The groom’s reins and breeches gleam white as snow, casting a kind of artificial sunshine over the otherwise gloomy courtyard. Captain Costigan has watched this spectacle many times, trailing through the court in carpet slippers and dressing-gown, his old hat cocked jauntily over one eye. He suns himself after breakfast when the weather permits, visits the porter’s lodge, pats the heads of the children, and chats with Mrs. Bolton about theatres and her daughter Leedy Mirabel.

Mrs. Bolton once danced at the Wells Theatre—she was the thirteenth of Mr. Serle’s forty pupils—and still remembers those days with pride. Costigan lives on the third floor at No. 4, in rooms once occupied by Mr. Podmore, whose name remains on the door—though most doors in Shepherd’s Inn bear names not their own. When Charley Podmore (a pleasing tenor, T.R.D.L., and star of the Back Kitchen Concert Rooms) married and moved to Lambeth, he left the chambers to Mr. Bows and Captain Costigan, who now share them. On fine days, you may hear the soft strains of Mr. Bows’s piano drifting through open windows—as he practices for fun or teaches his theatrical pupils, including Fanny Bolton, the porteress’s daughter.

Fanny has a good voice, a pretty face, and dreams of following in her mother’s footsteps. She keeps house for Messrs. Costigan and Bows, making beds, preparing meals, and listening to stories—while receiving music lessons in return. Though she thinks the Captain’s drinking habit is unfortunate (and suspects it’s common among fashionable men), she considers him the finest gentleman in the world, believes every word of his tales, and adores Mr. Bows too. And Bows, for his part, has developed a fatherly fondness for her—he can’t help loving someone, really. His heart is full of kindness, and he feels uneasy unless he’s doing so.

Costigan has seen carriages of distinction stop before his humble door. Listen to him talk in the morning, and you’d swear Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel were regular visitors, bringing the select nobility to see “the old man, the honest old half-pay Captain, poor old Jack Costigan,” as he calls himself. The truth? Lady Mirabel once left her husband’s card stuck in the little mirror over the mantelpiece at No. 4, where it’s remained for months. She did visit him personally—once upon a time—but not lately.

After her marriage, she settled a small pension on her father, who occasionally dined with her and Sir Charles. At first, Cos behaved with admirable solemnity—“in the hoight of poloit societee,” as he put it. He dressed in his best clothes, chose the longest words in his vocabulary, and addressed everyone with exaggerated courtesy. “Was your Leedyship in the Pork to dee?” he’d ask. “I looked for your equipage in veen:—the poor old man was not gratified by the soight of his daughther’s choriot.” He’d mention seeing Sir Chorlus at the Levee, recall past duties at Dublin Castle, and thank the footman for a “little dthrop more champeane.”

He was magnificent in his manners—addressing not just guests but servants, who struggled to keep straight faces while serving him. For the first few visits, he stayed sober, saving his revelry for the Back Kitchen, where he’d boast about Sir Charles’s wine and food until his sixth tumbler of whisky-punch made speech impossible.

But familiarity bred contempt—or rather, drunkenness. Soon, poor Cos began to disgrace himself at Sir Charles’s table, falling into premature inebriation. A carriage would be summoned, the hospitable door shut firmly behind him. Often, he lamented to friends at the Kitchen how much he resembled King Lear—how he had a thankless child, bedad—how he was a pore worn-out lonely old man, driven to drink by ingratitude, seeking solace in punch.

It’s painful to record such weaknesses, but we must also note that when his credit ran dry and his funds vanished, Costigan didn’t hesitate to beg from his daughter—sometimes telling less-than-truthful stories. Once, a bailiff was about to take him to prison, and he wrote: “Unless the—to you insignificant—sum of three pound five can be forthcoming to liberate a poor man’s grey hairs from gaol.” Lady Mirabel sent the money—with a stern warning to be more economical.

On another occasion, he broke a plate-glass window in the Strand and was held liable. Again, Lady Mirabel paid. Her servant delivered the funds to the Captain’s slipshod aide-de-camp, who then took a cab to the Roscius Head in Harlequin Yard, Drury Lane. There, the Captain was indeed in pawn—and enjoying several glasses of rum-and-water with his staff.

A third time, he claimed illness and needed money for a physician. Alarmed, Lady Mirabel drove to Shepherd’s Inn herself. At the gate, the porteress curtsied and pointed: “No. 4, third floor, name of Podmore over the door.” She entered, climbed the dingy stairs, and found her father opening the door in shirt-sleeves, ready to grill mutton-chops. Not exactly the image of a sick man.

And poor Sir Charles! He suffered constant letters addressed to him at Brookes’s, announcing that Captain Costigan waited in the hall. Or when playing whist at the Travellers’, he’d have to dash out of his brougham, lest the Captain seize him. Worse, he’d walk across Pall Mall, wearing that dreadful cocked hat, eyes fixed on the club windows.

Sir Charles was weak, elderly, prone to fits. He cried to his wife—whom he adored with senile devotion—about the horror of seeing his father-in-law again. He insisted he must go abroad, live in the country, or he’d die. Only after a second visit, where Lady Mirabel threatened to cut off his allowance entirely if he continued pestering her husband, did peace return.

She also rebuked Mr. Bows sharply: “Papa’s conduct is bringing me to the grave,” she said (though she looked perfectly healthy). “And you, as an old man, Mr. Bows, and one who pretended to care for us, ought to be ashamed of abetting him.”

That was the thanks honest Bows received for years of friendship and devotion. Yet, perhaps he wasn’t worse off than many others. Life has its burdens.

On the second floor of the next house, No. 3, lived two more acquaintances: Colonel Altamont, agent to the Nawaub of Lucknow, and Captain Chevalier Edward Strong. Their door bore no name. Strong preferred privacy—his cards listed a Jermyn Street hotel. As for Altamont, he wasn’t officially accredited to any court, merely on a confidential mission independent of the East India Company or Board of Control.

“In fact,” Strong explained, “Colonel Altamont’s goal is financial—selling some of the crown jewels of Lucknow. He doesn’t wish to report to India House or Cannon Row, but rather negotiate privately with capitalists, with whom he’s done important business both here and abroad.”

Since Sir Francis Clavering arrived in London, Strong’s chambers had become quite comfortable. He could proudly tell visitors that few retired captains lived as snugly as he in his crib in Shepherd’s Inn.

Three rooms below: the office where Strong conducted his mysterious affairs (still furnished with desks and railings from previous tenants), his bedroom, and sitting-room. A private stair led to two upper apartments—one occupied by Colonel Altamont, the other serving as kitchen and bedroom for Mr. Grady, the attendant.

These rooms were level with those of Bows and Costigan next door. From Grady’s kitchen window came fragrant aromas—Grady excelled at Irish stew, Altamont at pilau and curry, and Strong? Well, Strong could cook anything. French dishes, Spanish stews, fricassees, omelettes—all to perfection. And when his purse was full, he was the most hospitable man in England. He could give a friend a good dinner, a good glass of wine, and a good song afterward.

Poor Cos often heard the roar of Strong’s choruses and the clinking of glasses from his own room—so near, yet so far. It wasn’t wise to invite Costigan regularly. His drinking was terrible, and when sober, he bored guests with endless stories; when drunk, he dissolved into maudlin tears.

Each man had a story. They’d all seen luck and misfortune. Most carried schemes in their pockets—plans for rapid wealth. Jack Holt had fought for Don Carlos while Strong fought against him; now he was organizing a tobacco-smuggling operation that promised thirty thousand pounds a year—for just fifteen hundred to bribe the last Excise officer who knew about it.

Tom Diver, former Mexican naval officer, knew of a specie-ship sunk during the war, carrying three hundred eighty thousand dollars and one hundred eighty thousand pounds in bars and doubloons. “Give me eighteen hundred pounds,” he said, “and I’m off tomorrow. Four men, a diving-bell, and I’ll return in ten months to sit in Parliament and buy back my estate!”

Keightley, manager of the underwater Tredyddlum and Polwheedle Copper Mines, sang a fine second and ran a Smyrna Sponge Company and a quicksilver venture—soon to make him rich.

Filby had been everything: corporal of dragoons, field-preacher, missionary-agent, actor at a Greenwich fair-booth—where his father’s attorney found him after the old man died, leaving him a famous property from which he now drew no rent, and whose location nobody quite remembered.

Then there was Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., who enjoyed their society despite lacking strong convivial talents. But he was admired for his wealth and status. He told little stories, sang little songs with affability, and had his own past—prisons, stamped papers, and secrets.

When Altamont returned from Paris, stripped of riches despite his diamond-laden mission, Clavering sent Strong to fetch him, pay his bill, and invite him to stay. Strong soon grew tired of the arrangement.

“This fellow’s no gentleman,” he complained to Clavering. “I don’t like walking with him. He dresses like a nigger on a holiday. Took him to the play the other night—he abused the villain actor, swore at him so loudly the boxes wanted to throw him out. Then came ‘The Brigand’—Wallack dies wounded. Altamont burst into tears, called it a d——d shame, sobbed and cursed. Another row. Everyone laughing. Then he wanted to take off his coat and fight the man who laughed. Who is he? Where does he come from? You must tell me the whole story, Frank. You and he have robbed a church together, that’s my belief. You’d better get it off your mind.”

“Hang him!” Clavering replied, his face darkening. “I wish he was dead.”

Strong thought better of pressing further—at least for now. But he resolved, if need arose, to uncover the secret bond between Altamont and Clavering himself.
