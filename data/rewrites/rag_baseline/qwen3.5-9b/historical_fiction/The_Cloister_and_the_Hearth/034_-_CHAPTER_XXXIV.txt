The question rang out, sharp and demanding: "Where be the true men?" The answer returned not as a whisper, but as a roar of solidarity. "Here be we. God bless you all! God bless you!" A surge of movement erupted toward the stairs, where half a dozen hands, hardened by violence yet warm with brotherhood, reached out to grasp the rescuers. "Y'have saved our lives, lads," cried Denys, his voice thick with relief, "you've saved our lives this very night."

Before the echoes of gratitude could fade, the reality of the scene assaulted the senses. The room was a cavern of flickering torchlight, illuminated by the gleaming breastplates of the archers and the bronzed, battle-worn faces of their kin. In stark contrast lay the white, terrified cheeks of the bound thieves and the grotesque tableau of the bleeding giant, whose lifeless form the hard men had discarded amidst its own gore. Gerard moved through the circle of warriors, gripping each hand with eyes glistening with unshed tears. In response, the archers pressed their lips to his palm; this time, he returned the gesture, sealing a bond forged in blood. Turning to a handsome archer of his own age, Gerard's voice trembled. "Prithee, good soldier, watch over me. A strange drowsiness overtakes me. Let no one cut my throat while I sleep—for pity's sake." The archer laughed, dismissing the plea as jest, unaware that death was already knocking. Within moments, Gerard succumbed to a profound slumber. Denys observed the collapse with surprise but chose silence; the unconscious boy served his immediate purpose perfectly.

Elsewhere, two archers prodded the Abbot's body, flipping it with their boots to examine the wound. "Which of the two flung this enormous rogue from the upper storey?" they muttered, seeking the mechanics of the fall. Denys initially scoffed, feigning ignorance, fearing that admitting his role would invite the young vagabond to claim divine intervention and render him a fool. But seeing Gerard lost to dreams, Denys shifted tactics. "Well, comrades," he declared, his tone shifting to authoritative bravado, "I drove my sword through this one to the hilt. Others swarmed about me; I needed steel or I needed death. So I planted my foot against his stomach, gave a tug with my hand, and launched myself upward. I sent him flying to kingdom come! He died in the air, and his carrion rolled down among you without ceremony. Made you jump, I warrant me." He raised his cup. "But pikes and pillage! What avail prattle of trifles once they are gone? Buvons, camarades, buvons!"

The archers noted the irony of drinking where no liquor existed. "Nay," Denys countered, his nostrils flaring, "I'll soon find you wine. My nose has a natural alacrity for scenting vintage. Follow me." Leading the way with a torch, they descended a short flight of stone steps into a large, low cellar. The air was thick and dank, the walls encrusted with what looked like cobwebs but proved to be crystallized saltpeter oozing from the damp stones. "Oh! the fine mouldy smell," Denys murmured. "In such places, good wine still lurks. Advance thy torch."

Suddenly, a shape crouched in the corner, pale as ash and shivering violently. "Why, it is the landlord," Denys announced. "Get up, thou craven heart!" shouted an archer. "The thieves are bound, and we are thirsty. Rise and show us your wine; no bottles are visible here." "What, be the rascals bound?" the landlord stammered, his voice cracking. "Good news... wine? That will I, honest sirs." He rose with uncertain joints, offering to guide them. Denys intercepted him sharply. "You are all in the dark, comrades. He is in league with the thieves." "Alack, good soldier, me in league with the accursed robbers! Is that reasonable?" "The girl said so anyway." "The girl! What girl? Ah! Curse her, traitress!"

"The girl is gone to the bailiff," another archer interjected. "Let the burghers decide if this craven is guilty; we caught him not in the act. Let him draw us our wine." "One moment," Denys said, his eyes narrowing. "Why cursed he the girl? If he be a true man, he should bless her as we do." "Alas, sir!" the landlord pleaded. "I have but my good name to live by. I cursed her because you accused her of bellying me." "Humph! I trow thou art a thief, and where is the thief that cannot lie with a smooth face? Hold him, comrades. A prisoner can draw wine if his hands are not bound." The landlord offered no resistance, hoping to sell his remaining stock despite his overdue rent. The archers smiled grimly at his naivety. One laid a firm hand on his shoulder; the other led the way with the torch.

At the threshold, Denys halted. "Wait. Here be bottles in this corner; advance thy light." The torch-bearer approached, removing his scabbard to probe the heap where the landlord had crouched. "Nay, nay," cried the landlord. "The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing here." "Nothing is mighty hard, then," Denys replied, reaching into the pile and pulling out an object. It was a bone. He tossed it onto the floor; it rattled. "There is nought there but the bones of the house," the landlord insisted. "Just now 'twas nothing. Now that we have found something, 'tis nothing but bones. Look at this one, comrade. Come, look, and bring your smooth-faced accomplice."

Philippe, the archer with the torch, examined the bone closely. "If this were a battlefield," he mused, "I'd say it was a man's shankbone. But 'tisn't a battlefield, nor a churchyard; 'tis an inn." "True, mate; but yon knave's ashy face is as good a light as any field. Read the bone by it. When the chine is missing and the house dog hides his tail, who was the thief?" Denys continued, his voice dropping to a menacing rumble. "My mind misgives me. The deeper I thrust, the more there be. Mayhap if these bones could speak, they'd make true men's flesh creep." "Alas! young man, what hideous fancies!" the landlord protested. "These are bones of beeves, sheep, and kids. Holy saints preserve us!" "Hold thy peace! Thy words are air. Thou hast not got burghers by the ear, but soldiers who sought their comrades and found their bones picked clean by crows—and perhaps by thee."

Denys thrust a small object into the landlord's face. "Men and women, saidst thou? Prithee, when spake I of women's bones? Wouldst make a child suspect thee? Field of battle, comrade! Was not this house a field of battle half an hour ago?" He grabbed the landlord and forced him closer. "Read his face. Now, what is this, thou knave?" He shoved a tiny fragment into the man's eyes. "Alas! I know not." "Too like the thumb bone of a man's hand. Mates, my flesh creeps. Churchyard! How know I this is not one?" Drawing his sword, Denys began raking the heap of earth and broken crockery. The landlord assured him it was futile, claiming they were merely sinners giving short measure, not assassins. "How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven's lightning strike me dead if there be any bones there but such as have been used for meat. 'Tis the kitchen wench who flings them here. I swear by God's holy mother, by holy Paul, by holy Dominic, and by Denys my patron saint..."

Denys held up a bone under the torch in dead silence. It was unmistakably human. The sight silenced the lying lips and paralyzed the heartless heart. The landlord's hair rose on his head like spikes, and his knees buckled as if his limbs had been severed. The archers dragged him upright, forcing him to stare at the dead skull, white as the living cheek opposite it, glaring back at its murderer. The landlord's pale lips parted, unable to utter a sound. "Ah!" Denys roared, trembling with rage. "Look on the sockets out of which thou hast plucked the eyes, and let them blast thine own, that crows may pick them out ere this week ends." Holding the skull, he threatened the quaking wretch with his naked sword until the man, groaning, took hold of the grisly relic. Denys wanted every murderer to feel this terror, to see the cold skull and fear their own heads becoming like it.

Soon, the heap yielded many more skulls, each discovery drawing a moan from the culprit. Suddenly, Denys, usually bold and hard, emitted a cry of distress. He held up a mass of long, glossy, golden hair—a woman's beautiful hair. The archers shook the craven wretch instinctively; he whined.
