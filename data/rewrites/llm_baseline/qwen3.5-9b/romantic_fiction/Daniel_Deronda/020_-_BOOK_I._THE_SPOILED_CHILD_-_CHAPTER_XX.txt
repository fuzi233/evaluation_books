It is rarely disputed that even within this fragile and fallen world, we occasionally encounter individuals whose very bearing and demeanor, coupled with the entirety of their lives, bear such an unmistakable seal of virtue that our judgment of them becomes an immediate intuition rather than the product of prolonged scrutiny. —Alexander Knox, quoted in Southey's *Life of Wesley*.

Mirah claimed she had slept peacefully that night. When she descended in Mab's black dress, her dark hair curling in fresh tendrils as it dried from its abundant bath, she appeared as one beginning to find solace after the long sorrow and vigilance that had pale her cheeks and cast blue shadows beneath her eyes. It was Mab who brought her breakfast and escorted her downstairs, displaying a certain pride in the effect of a pair of tiny felt slippers she had hurried out to purchase, for there were no shoes in the house small enough for Mirah. Her borrowed dress ended at her ankles, revealing the modest garments that molded themselves to her feet, appearing as exquisite as the sheaths of budding flowers. The farthing buckles were jewels indeed.

"Oh, if you please, Mamma?" cried Mab, clasping her hands and bowing toward Mirah's feet as they entered the parlor. "Look at the slippers; how beautifully they fit! I declare she resembles Queen Budoor—'two delicate feet, the work of the protecting and all-recompensing Creator, support her; and I wonder how they can sustain what is above them.'"

Mirah gazed down at her own feet with a childlike wonder and then smiled at Mrs. Meyrick, who was thinking silently, *One could scarcely imagine this creature capable of an evil thought. Yet, wisdom dictates caution.* She returned Mirah's smile and replied, "I fear those feet have had to carry their burden a little too often recently. But today she shall rest and be my companion."

"And she will tell you so many things, and I shall not hear them," grumbled Mab, feeling herself thrust into the first volume of a delightful romance, forced to skip chapters due to her duties with pupils.

Kate had already departed to sketch along the river, and Amy was away on business errands. This arrangement suited the mother perfectly; she desired solitude with this stranger, whose narrative must inevitably be fraught with sorrow, yet one that needed to be spoken.

The small front parlor resembled a temple that morning. Sunlight bathed the river, and a soft breeze drifted through the open window. The walls bore witness to a glorious, silent cloud of art: the Virgin soaring amidst her cherubic entourage; Grand Melancholia with her solemn universe; the Prophets and Sibyls; the School of Athens; the Last Supper; mystical groups where distant ages converged into a single moment; the grave heads of Holbein and Rembrandt; the Tragic Muse; children of the previous century lost in thought or play; Italian poets—all present through the medium of black and white. The neat mother, having weathered her trials and emerged with a face still cheerful, sorted colored wools for her embroidery. Hafiz purred on the windowsill, the mantel-clock ticked without haste, and the occasional rumble of wheels seemed to lie outside the heavier, central silence. Mrs. Meyrick felt this quiet might be the finest invitation for her companion to speak, choosing not to disturb it with comment. Mirah sat opposite in her usual posture, hands clasped on her lap, ankles crossed, her eyes initially drifting slowly over the surroundings before settling with a placid reverence upon Mrs. Meyrick. Finally, she began to speak softly.

"I remember my mother's face better than anything else; yet I was not seven when I was taken away, and I am nineteen now."

"I can understand that," said Mrs. Meyrick. "Some earliest impressions endure the longest."

"Oh, yes, it was the very beginning. I think my life commenced with waking up and loving my mother's face; it was so near to me, her arms encircling mine, and she sang to me. One hymn she sang so frequently, so endlessly: and then she taught me to sing it with her; it was the first song I ever knew. They were always Hebrew hymns she sang; and because I never understood the meaning of the words, they seemed filled with nothing but our love and happiness. When I lay in my little bed, with whiteness above me, she would bend over me, between me and the light, and sing in a sweet, low voice. I can dream myself back into that time when I am awake, and it often returns to me in my sleep—my hand was very small; I would raise it to her face, and she would kiss it. Sometimes in my dreams I begin to tremble, thinking that we are both dead; but then I wake, and my hand lies here, and for a moment I hardly recognize myself. But if I could see my mother again, I would know her instantly."

"You must expect some alteration after twelve years," said Mrs. Meyrick, gently. "Observe my grey hair; ten years ago it was bright brown. Days and months pass over us like restless little birds, leaving the marks of their feet backward and forward; especially when they are birds with heavy hearts—then they tread heavily."

"Ah, I am certain her heart has been heavy for lack of me. But to feel her joy if we could meet again, and for me to make her know my love and give her deep comfort after all her mourning! If that were possible, I would mind nothing; I would be glad simply to have survived my tribulation. I did despair. The world seemed miserable and wicked; no one helped me so that I could bear their looks and words; I felt my mother was dead, and death was the only path to her. But then, in the final moment—yesterday, when I longed for the water to close over me—and I thought death was the best image of mercy—goodness came to me, living. I felt trust in the living. And—it is strange—but I began to hope that she was living too. And now, with you—here—this morning, peace and hope have flooded into me. I desire nothing; I can wait; because I hope and believe and am grateful—oh, so grateful! You have not thought evil of me; you have not despised me."

Mirah spoke with low-toned fervor, remaining as still as a painting throughout.

"Many others would have felt as we do, my dear," said Mrs. Meyrick, feeling a mist rise in her eyes as she looked at her embroidery.

"But I did not meet them—they did not come to me."

"How was it that you were taken from your mother?"

"Ah, I am taking a long time to reach that point. It is dreadful to speak of, yet I must tell you—I must tell you everything. My father—he was the one who took me away. I thought we were merely embarking on a short journey; I was delighted. There was a box containing all my little treasures. But we boarded a ship and drifted farther and farther from the land. Then I became ill; I thought it would never end—it was the first misery, and it seemed endless. But eventually, we landed. I knew nothing then, and believed what my father said. He comforted me, assuring me I would return to my mother. But it was America we had reached, and it was many long years before we returned to Europe. At first, I often asked my father when we were going back; I tried to learn writing quickly because I wanted to write to my mother. But one day, when he found me attempting to compose a letter, he lifted me onto his knee and told me that my mother and brother were dead—that was why we did not return. I remember my brother faintly; he carried me once, but he was not always at home. I believed my father when he said they were dead. I visualized them beneath the earth when he mentioned it, with their eyes forever closed. I never suspected it might not be true; and I used to cry every night in my bed for a long while. Then, when she visited me so often in my sleep, I thought she must be living nearby, though I could not always see her, and that comforted me. I was never afraid in the dark because of that; and very often during the day I would shut my eyes, bury my face, and try to see her and hear her singing. Eventually, I came to do this even without closing my eyes."

Mirah paused, her face radiant with a sweet contentment, as if immersed in a happy vision, gazing out toward the river.

"Still, I hope your father was not unkind to you," said Mrs. Meyrick after a moment, anxious to bring her back to the present.

"No; he doted on me and took pains to instruct me. He was an actor; and I discovered later that the 'Coburg' I used to hear him mention at home was a theater. But he had more involvement with the theater than acting. He had not always been an actor; he had been a teacher and knew many languages. His acting was not particularly good, I think, but he managed the stage, wrote, and translated plays. An Italian lady, a singer, lived with us for a long time. Both of them taught me, and I also had a master who made me memorize and recite texts. I worked quite hard, though I was so young; and I was not nine when I first stepped onto the stage.
