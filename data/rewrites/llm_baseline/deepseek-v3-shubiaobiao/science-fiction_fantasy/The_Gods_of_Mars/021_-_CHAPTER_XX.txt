Two hours past dusk, our cruiser sliced through the thin air of Barsoom, leaving the spires of Helium behind. Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I reached Hastor as the twin moons cast their pallid glow over the waiting fleet. Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Hor Vastus had already departed for Thark—our forces divided, yet united in purpose.

The transports stirred first, creeping southward like shadows. The battleships would follow, overtaking them by dawn on the second day.

At Hastor, Kantos Kan’s precision was evident. Within minutes, the first vessel lifted from its dock, and soon the night was alive with the silent ascent of warships, their lines stretching for miles—a serpent of steel ready to strike.

Only in the quiet of Kantos Kan’s cabin did I think to ask the date. His answer struck like a dagger. Three hundred sixty-five days had passed.

I had miscalculated.

Dejah Thoris was beyond rescue.

The weight of it threatened to crush me, but I buried the truth beneath cold resolve. This was no longer a mission of salvation—it was vengeance. Let the therns and the First Born learn the cost of their deception. Let Barsoom see the lies that had damned generations to the horrors of Issus.

If we could tear open the veil of superstition shrouding the Valley Dor, if we could reclaim its fertile lands from the jaws of fanatics and beasts, then her sacrifice would not be in vain.

---

Dawn of the second day revealed the transports, their hulls dark against the ice cap. Signals flickered between ships—coded, cautious. Radio silence was a necessity in war; the therns had ears everywhere.

Our scouts lanced ahead, flanking our formation, a web of watchers. Then—a warning.

Kantos Kan’s jaw tightened. “Zat Arras’ spies did their work well.”

I gave my orders swiftly: ten ships to seal the entrance to Omean. The rest formed a spearhead, a V aimed at the heart of the thern armada. We would carve a path for the transports, unleash the green warriors upon the temples, and let the therns drown in their own blood.

The fleet surged forward.

The enemy’s ragged lines filled the horizon, unprepared for the fury of our charge. Our battleships tore through them, the V widening like jaws. Transports shot through the gap, disgorging warriors into the gilded gardens of the therns.

Above, the sky became a maelstrom.

Kantos Kan’s ships wheeled in a deadly dance, circling the thern fleet, broadsides roaring. The enemy ships fell like stricken birds, their hulls splitting in midair. Yet even as victory seemed certain, a new shadow darkened the north.

Zat Arras.

His message crackled across the void: *“Surrender.”*

The therns, sensing our impending doom, redoubled their assault. Now we fought on two fronts—Zat Arras’ treachery at our backs, the therns before us.

I ordered the transports back to the gardens. “Make them remember this day,” I told the green horde. Then I turned to face Zat Arras’ flagship.

Our vessels clashed, grapplers biting into hulls. I led the boarding party, cutting through Zodangan soldiers until I stood before Zat Arras himself.

“Yield,” I demanded.

For a heartbeat, he hesitated. Then, with a snarl, he flung himself over the rail—into the abyss.

The battle raged on, a chaos of fire and steel. But the tides shifted. The therns and the First Born turned on each other, their ancient hatred boiling over. Seizing the discord, I regrouped our fleet, withdrawing southwest as the two factions tore themselves apart.

Night fell.

We had one chance left: a strike at Issus herself. Xodar would lead the transports to the temple grounds. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and I would take the remaining ships through Omean’s depths, emerging beneath her sanctum.

But as we approached the shaft, a new horror awaited.

The First Born’s fleet had returned.

Our ten sentinel ships fled before them, harried and outnumbered. There was no time for caution. We plunged into the black maw of Omean, praying the enemy would mistake us for their own.

Four hundred ships slipped inside before the alarm was raised. The battle was swift—their harbor defenses were lax, their ships outdated. We captured their vessels, jammed them into the shaft to bar the way, and pressed onward.

On the island of the submarine, I found Yersted among the prisoners. His smirk faltered when I asked of Dejah Thoris.

“She lives,” he admitted. “Issus keeps her close—in the Temple of the Sun.”

A spark of hope. The Martian year was longer than Earth’s. I had not failed her yet.

But Yersted’s warning lingered: *“Issus will not give her up. If you set foot in that temple, Dejah Thoris will vanish into its heart—forever.”*

The Temple of the Sun. A revolving tomb. One chance each year to reach its chambers.

And I would tear it apart to find her.
