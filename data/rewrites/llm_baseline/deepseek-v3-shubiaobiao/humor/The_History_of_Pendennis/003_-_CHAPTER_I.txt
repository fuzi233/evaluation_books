Major Arthur Pendennis was a man of routine, and his routine was *exquisite*. Every morning at precisely a quarter past ten, he graced his favorite Pall Mall club with his presence, looking every inch the retired military gentleman—if military gentlemen retired with immaculate gloves, suspiciously lush whiskers, and a cane that practically announced his arrival with a fanfare. His boots were polished to a mirror finish, his cravat crisper than a fresh banknote, and his waistcoat buttons bore the royal crown—because, naturally, even his *buttons* had connections.

The club staff treated him like visiting royalty, which suited the Major just fine. His usual table—strategically positioned by the fire but near the window—was his throne, and woe betide any upstart who dared claim it. (Two had tried, once. They did not try again.) His mail was already arranged for his inspection: elegant little notes from marchionesses, solemn dinner invitations from bishops, and one particularly thick envelope from the Marquis of Steyne, requesting his presence at Richmond for an evening of French conversation (which the Major spoke *flawlessly*, thank you very much).

As he sifted through this mountain of social obligation, one letter sat apart—plain, provincial, and decidedly *not* from a duke. Its postmark read *Clavering St. Mary’s*, and its seal was distressingly homemade. The Major, with the air of a man forced to acknowledge a distant and slightly embarrassing cousin, finally broke it open.

Inside, disaster.

His sister Helen’s handwriting trembled with maternal distress: *Arthur—my dearest boy—has lost his mind. He insists on marrying an actress. She is twelve years his senior. Help.*

The Major’s breakfast nearly became his last.

A second letter—this one from Arthur himself—confirmed the horror. The boy had fallen headlong into the clutches of one *Miss Fotheringay* (née Costigan), a thespian of allegedly ancient Irish royalty, if her father’s drunken boasts were to be believed. Arthur’s prose was a masterpiece of youthful melodrama: *"A love like mine is contracted once and for ever!"* he declared, as if he were the first idiot to say such a thing. *"I shall die without ever knowing another passion!"* (The Major made a mental note to send the boy a dictionary, lest he confuse *passion* with *lunacy*.)

Worse still, Arthur had the gall to invoke *Virgil*—*"qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes"*—as if quoting Latin somehow made eloping with a middle-aged tragedienne more respectable. The Major’s eye twitched. His evening at Richmond—*gone*. His plans to dine with bishops—*postponed*. Instead, he would spend the night rattling in a mail coach toward the countryside, where he would have to disentangle his nephew from the clutches of a woman old enough to know better.

As he penned furious cancellations to his illustrious hosts, one thought burned brighter than his indignation: *This is what comes of letting boys read poetry.*
