As the antiquary sat in his dimly lit study, surrounded by dusty tomes and flickering candles, he gazed intently at the yellowed pages of the _Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum_. The words danced before his eyes, a testament to the rich history of the Premonstratensian abbey of Steinfeld. His fingers moved deftly over the parchment as he transcribed the Latin text, the sounds of the language coming alive in his mind like a gentle brook babbling through the forest.

"Ah, yes," he murmured to himself, "I should translate this for the sake of posterity." And so, with a flourish, he rendered the text into English:

Up to this very day, whispers of a hidden treasure belonging to Abbot Thomas still circulate among the Canons of Steinfeld. Though they have searched far and wide, their efforts remain fruitless. According to legend, Thomas, in the prime of life, secreted a substantial hoard of gold within the monastery walls. When questioned about its whereabouts, he would chuckle and say, "Job, John, and Zechariah will reveal its location to you or your descendants." On occasion, he would add that he harbored no ill will towards those who might uncover the treasure.

Among Abbot Thomas's many accomplishments, one stands out – the exquisite stained-glass window in the eastern portion of the south aisle of the church. The images, masterfully crafted on glass, are a testament to his artistic vision. His effigy and coat of arms, proudly displayed within the window, serve as a lasting tribute to his legacy. Furthermore, he oversaw the restoration of the Abbot's lodging, transforming it into a sanctuary of beauty. A well, dug in the courtyard, features intricate carvings in marble, a reflection of his refined taste. Abbot Thomas passed away in the seventy-second year of his life, in the year 1529.

The antiquary's current pursuit was to identify the origins of the painted glass windows that once adorned the Abbey Church of Steinfeld. Following the dissolution of the monasteries, a vast quantity of these precious artworks found their way to England, enriching the country's parish churches, cathedrals, and private chapels. Steinfeld Abbey, a significant contributor to this influx, left behind a distinct trail that could be followed through the inscriptions and subject matter of the windows.
