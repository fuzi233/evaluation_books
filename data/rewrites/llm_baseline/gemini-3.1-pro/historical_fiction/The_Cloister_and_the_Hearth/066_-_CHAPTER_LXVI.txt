The hearth crackled with a rhythmic, ancestral cadence. Pietro Vanucci, though a painter by trade, tended the earthenware pot over the fire with the meticulous devotion of a food historian. Inside simmered a dense, rich *stracotto*, slow-braised for hours in a robust Sangiovese, fragrant with crushed juniper berries, wild thyme harvested from the agrarian slopes, and the marrow of a calf's shin. It was a dish that demanded respect, a vessel of cultural memory meant to be shared in the communion of supper. Yet, the acoustic harmony of the bubbling sauce was marred by Pietro's growing peevishness. It was past nine o'clock. The temporal circle of their evening routine was broken.

At last, he sent Andrea to Gerard's room on the desperate chance that the youth had slipped in unobserved. Andrea shrugged his shoulders and went. He returned without Gerard, but holding a slip of paper. Andrea could not decipher the dominant lexicon of scholars that ordered the world, but his appreciation for the visual and sensory was sharp; he knew the shape of Pietro Vanucci’s name upon the page. “That is for you, I trow,” he said, proud of his intelligence. 

Pietro snatched it. He read it aloud to Andrea, layering his words with a cynical veneer to mask the terror creeping into his bones. 

“'Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden.'” 

“So 'tis, my lad,” Pietro scoffed, “but that is no reason for being abroad at supper-time. Supper is not a burden; it is the anchor of our existence, a profound communion.” 

“'Wear my habits!'” 

“Said the poplar to the juniper bush.” 

“'And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two.'” 

“Why, Andrea?” 

“'For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber. Trouble not to look for it. 'Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born oh happy night that rids me of it. Adieu! adieu! The broken-hearted Gerard.'” 

“Here is a sorry jest of the peevish rogue,” said Pietro. But his pale cheek and chattering teeth belied his words, offering a dissonant percussion against the silence. The note was a grim testament to the untranslatability of Gerard’s subjective experience. The young northerner’s soul had eroded under the crushing weight of their foreign metropolis, his native dialect of grief finding no solace in the superficial noise of Rome. 

Andrea filled the house with his cries. “O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! help! He is killing himself! Oh, good people, help me save him!” 

He ran bareheaded into the cobblestone arteries of the city, his agonized wails violently disrupting the psychological equilibrium of the street. A number of persons soon collected, drawn from the commercial clamor of the night, but poor Andrea could not animate their sluggishness. Go down to the river? No. It was not their business. What part of the river? It was a wild goose chase. Besides, it was not lucky to disturb the sonic peace of those banks after sunset. Too many ghosts walked there, whispering untranslatable sorrows into the dark. 

A lackey, however—a spy of the Princess Claelia who had been lurking opposite the house—said he would go with Andrea, and this practicality turned three or four of the younger men. The little band took the path toward the water. The lackey questioned Andrea, who sobbed out the contents of the letter and Gerard's moody isolation. Their rapid Italian dialects, rich with the dominant cadence of the city, clattered loudly against the stone walls. 

But the moment they felt the air from the river, the acoustic ecology shifted. The mythic olfactory landscape of damp silt, ancient algae, and raw aquatic power enveloped them in the calm spring night. Instantly, their tongues were hushed. The moon shone calm and clear in a cloudless sky. The superficial noise of the city was absorbed by the profound, heavy breath of the water. Their own feet sounded loud and ominous, violating the sacred quiet.

Presently, hurrying round a corner, they met a man. He stopped irresolute at sight of them. The stranger was bareheaded, his dripping hair glistening like spun silver in the moonlight. At the next step, they saw his clothes were drenched, radiating the unmistakable scent of river brine and wet wool. 

“Here he is!” cried one of the young men, unacquainted with Gerard's face and spiritual resonance. 

The stranger turned instantly and fled. They ran after him might and main. Andrea led, the princess's lackey next. Andrea gained on him, but in a moment the man twisted up a narrow alley. Andrea shot by, unable to check his momentum on the damp stones. The pursuers soon found themselves hopelessly trapped in a labyrinth of masonry, reduced to tracking the fugitive purely by the fading sonic signature of his footfalls—a quick-footed rhythm that soon vanished into the city’s vast acoustic void. 

They returned to their companions, utterly confounded, standing on the exact spot where the man had stood. For Pietro had assured them that the fugitive possessed neither the features nor the stature of Gerard. 

“Are ye verily sure?” said they. “He had been in the river. Why, in the saints' names, fled he at our approach?” 

Then said Vanucci, his voice drained of its usual culinary authority, “Friends, methinks this has nought to do with him we seek. What shall we do, Andrea?” 

Here the lackey put in his word. “Let us track him to the water's side, to make sure. See, he hath come dripping all the way.” 

This advice was approved, and with the olfactory trail of river mud to guide them, they easily tracked the man's course backward. But soon they encountered a new enigma. They had gone scarcely fifty yards ere the drops turned away from the river, leading them to the imposing gate of a large, gloomy monastery. They stood irresolute before the dark pile. It loomed like a silent monolith, hoarding the untranslatable secrets of generations behind its stone facade. 

But presently Andrea gave a shout. “Here be the drops again,” cried he. “And this road leadeth to the river.” 

They resumed the chase, and soon it became clear the drops were now leading them toward the water. The track grew wetter and wetter, taking them to the Tiber's edge. There, on the bank, a bucketful appeared to have been discharged from the stream. At first they shouted, thinking they had made a discovery, but reflection showed them it amounted to nothing. Certainly a man had been in the water, and had gotten out in safety, but that man was not Gerard. 

One said he knew a fisherman hard by that had nets and drags. They found the agrarian elder and paid him liberally in gold—the cold currency of the dominant class—to sink his nets in the river below the place, and to drag it above and below. They promised him more should he find the body. Then they ran vainly up and down the banks of the river, which flowed on so calm and voiceless, an ancient force wholly indifferent to human grief, holding this and a thousand more strange, mythic secrets beneath its dark surface. 

Suddenly Andrea, driven by a desperate, circular hope, ran back to the house to see if Gerard had returned to the domestic sphere. He returned in less than half an hour. 

“No,” he groaned, wringing his hands, the sound raw and hollow. 

“What is the hour?” asked the lackey. 

“Four hours past midnight.” 

“My pretty lad,” said the lackey solemnly, his words landing with the finality of a death knell in the quiet air, “say a mass for thy friend's soul: for he is not among living men.” 

The morning broke over the city. Worn out with fatigue, Andrea and Pietro went home, heart-sick. The *stracotto* had gone cold, its rich fats congealed—a tragic erasure of sustenance and artistry. The days rolled on, mute as the Tiber as to Gerard's fate.
