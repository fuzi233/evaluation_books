The morning light in London was already a commodity, bought and sold alongside the newsprint of the *Advertiser*, which arrived with breakfast. Mr. Warrington read the invitation to the trial of skill between Sutton and Figg, a declaration of war disguised as sport. He decided to attend. He drove his newly purchased horses to Marybone Fields, the Wooden House. The driver, a young charioteer unfamiliar with the geography of ambition, veered wildly through green lanes, tangling the route past Whitfield's tabernacle and the hospital grounds until the destination was finally reached.

Outside, the air was thick with the smell of unwashed bodies and anticipation. A crowd of London blackguards pressed against the doors of the temple of British valour. Among them, the horses and equipages of the fashionable few, including Mr. Warrington, sought patronage. Beggars and cripples hustled for coins; shoe-black boys scrambled for the privilege of polishing his boots; women selling flowers and fruit offered their wares to Mr. Gumbo. Piemen, tramps, and strollers filled the perimeter. A flag snapped in the wind. From the stage, accompanied by a drummer and a horn-blower, a manager announced the imminent beginning of the noble English sports.

Warrington paid his fee and secured a seat in a gallery offering a clear view of the platform. Below, in the amphitheatre, Mr. Gumbo settled in, or retreated to the outer world to drink beer or gamble with coachmen when fatigue set in. Around the venue, the old society moved with a heavy encumbrance of servitude. Gentlemen and women could scarcely walk without a retinue of liveried vassals. Every theatre possessed a footman's gallery; armies of servants crowded chapel doors, swarmed anterooms, sprawled on hallways, and guzzled in corners. They bullied visitors for tips, played cards, and debauched the peace of the streets. That noble, melancholy race—the footman—is nearly extinct. Only a few thousand remain, visible perhaps on levee days with their nosegays and buckles, their plush coats and powdered hair. Like the Red Indians of America, whose camps and villages once dotted the continent, they are passing out of the world where they once walked in glory. The fatal decree has gone forth. Uncas with his tomahawk and Jeames with his cocked hat are fading into the mist.

Before the principals appeared, minor warriors tested the air. A boxing match occurred, but neither fighter was particularly game, nor severely punished, yielding little pleasure to the spectators. Cudgel-play followed, resulting in broken heads of negligible note and wounds so trifling they provoked hissing and grumbling. "The masters!" the crowd shouted. Finally, the champions emerged.

First walked the intrepid Sutton, sword in hand, saluting the company with a warlike grace, making an especial bow to a private box occupied by a stout gentleman of apparent importance. He was swiftly followed by the famous Figg, to whom the stout gentleman waved approval. Both men were shirtless, their heads shaved clean, bearing the cracks and scars of numerous glorious battles. On their burly sword-arms, they wore ribbons of their respective colours. The gladiators shook hands. As a contemporary poet noted, "The word it was bilboe."

Figg struck first. The blow was tremendous, intended to shear Sutton's honest head off as cleanly as a knife chops a carrot. But Sutton intercepted the blade with his own sword. The force was so mighty that Figg's weapon broke in his hands, less constant than the heart of the man who wielded it. New swords were distributed. Blood spouted from Figg's panting side amidst a yell of delight from Sutton's supporters. Yet, the veteran appealed to his audience, specifically the stout individual in the private gallery, revealing that the wound stemmed from the broken sword of the previous encounter.

During the pause, a gentleman in a riding-frock and plain wig entered the stout personage's box. It was Lord March and Ruglen, Warrington's friend from Tunbridge Wells. Lord March, usually sparing with politeness, displayed singular deference to the stout gentleman, receiving bank-bills with a profound bow. Then, spotting the Virginian, the lord crossed to Warrington's gallery and greeted him warmly.

They watched the combat, which shifted from swords to quarter-staffs. Victory ultimately remained with the ancient champion, Figg. Throughout the battle, a thunderstorm broke over the building. Warrington gladly accepted a seat in Lord March's chariot, leaving his own phaeton to be driven home by his groom. Harry was in great delectation, pronouncing this the finest sport he had seen since arriving in England. He sighed, wishing his elder brother were there to share the amusement, then stopped himself.

"What do you wish and what don't you wish?" Lord March asked.

"I was thinking," Harry replied, "of my elder brother. We promised to have our sport together at home. Many times we talked of it. But he wouldn't have liked this rough sort of sport, nor fighting, though he was the bravest lad alive."

"Oh! he was the bravest lad alive, was he?" the lord queried, lolling on his cushion.

"You should have seen him in a quarrel with a very gallant officer," Harry continued. "It was hard to keep George off him. I never saw a fellow so cool, nor more savage and determined. Ah! For the honour of the country, I wish he could have come here instead of me, and shown you a real Virginian gentleman."

"Nay, sir, you'll do very well," the amused nobleman said. "What is this I hear of Lady Yarmouth taking you into favour?"

"I will do as well as another," Harry admitted. "I can ride, and I think I can shoot better than George. But then my brother had the head, sir, the head!" He tapped his own skull. "He had read almost every book ever written; could play the fiddle and harpsichord; could compose poetry and sermons most elegant. What can I do? I am only good to ride, play cards, and drink Burgundy." The penitent hung his head. "But them I can do as well as most fellows."

"By Gad, Mr. Warrington," Lord March said, relishing the young man's naivete like a jaded voluptuary enjoying wholesome mutton-chops, "you ought to be taken to Exeter Change and put in a show. A gentleman from Virginia who has lost his elder brother and absolutely regrets him. The breed isn't known in this country. Upon my honour, I believe you would like to have him back again."

"Believe!" cried the Virginian, turning red.

"That is, you believe you believe you would like him back. But depend on it you wouldn't. 'Tis not in human nature, sir."

As they approached fine houses—Sir Richard Littleton's, Lord Bingley's—they discussed the town's chaotic expansion. Montagu House had become a museum of stuffed birds and rhinoceroses; a new road had been cut behind Bedford House Gardens, spoiling the Duke's comfort. Where would the town stop? Would they dine at Pall Mall or with Warrington? Spend the evening with the Queen of Spades?

"With the Queen of Spades, if your lordship pleases," Warrington blushed.

The equipage drove to his hotel in Covent Garden. The landlord, recognizing Lord March, bowed his wig onto the nobleman's shoes in humble welcome. A rich young English peer in the reign of George the Second; a wealthy patrician in the reign of Augustus. Which would you prefer?

After the best English dinner possible, wine flowed in plenty, of quality sufficient for an epicurean earl. Talk turned to fireworks at Vauxhall or cards. Harry, having seen only a dozen squibs at Williamsburg on the fifth of November—a display he considered sublime—yielded to his guest's preference for piquet. They were soon absorbed in the game.

Harry began by winning, as usual. But within half an hour, luck turned to favour Lord March. Just then, Mr. Draper, Warrington's man of business, bowed into the room to accept an invitation to sit and drink. Warrington always invited everyone to partake of his best. If he had a crust, he divided it; a haunch, he shared it; a jug of water, he drank with a kindly spirit; a bottle of Burgundy, he consumed with a thirsty friend. Such virtues are rare in our selfish world. We may bring them from the country when young, but they mostly wither after transplantation, drooping and perishing in the stifling London air.

Draper cared little for wine but delighted to be in the company of a great man. He protested that nothing pleased him more than watching piquet played by two consummate players of fashion. Undismayed by the sidelong scowls of his lordship, he surveyed the game. Harry was no match for the experienced club player, especially with Lord March holding superior cards.

Their stakes were no concern of Draper's. The gentlemen agreed to play for shillings, counting gains and losses in an undertone with scarce conversation. A bow, a grave manner, and the game continued.
