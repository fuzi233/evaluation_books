A man may lower a bucket into the deep, echoing well of his own self-deception, but what he draws up is rarely the clear, pure water of truth. Rather, he tastes only what his palate has been conditioned to expect. Halfway along the road to Sevenbergen, Gerard listened to the rhythmic crunch of his boots on the packed dirt and tried to decipher the acoustic landscape of his own heart. He told himself he was merely walking out of scholarly curiosity to understand why Margaret had jilted him, a neatly reasoned excuse masking a visceral hunger. He assured himself that he was perfectly satisfied with the sterile, reverberant halls of his prospective church benefice, content to live within the dominant, imperial lexicon of ecclesiastical Latin, entirely insulated from the fickle, untranslatable dialect of a woman’s affections.

Yet, as the chaotic, jarring clamor of Rotterdam’s commercialized artistry faded—the screeching iron wheels and shouting tradesmen replaced by the restorative acoustic ecology of the countryside—his senses betrayed his logic. He found Peter Brandt’s cottage nestled within a symphony of agrarian life. There sat Margaret in the doorway, the steady, domestic *thwip-thwip* of her sewing needle harmonizing with the deep, resonant thrum of a longbow. A stalwart figure leaned upon the weapon, conversing with her. Gerard felt an unaccountable, dissonant pang of territorial panic, a sudden disruption in his internal soundscape. However, the man proved to be past fifty years of age—Martin, an old agrarian soldier who read the wind and the soil with a seasoned archer's precision.

Another minute and the youth stood before them. Margaret looked up, her face flushing the bright, bruised color of crushed raspberries before chilling into a mask of pale, unyielding flour. She dismissed these physical signs of her agitation with a protective silence, turning far more chill and indifferent than she would have had she not been caught off guard.

"What! is it you, Master Gerard? What on earth brings you here, I wonder?" Her voice was deliberately flat, devoid of its usual melodic cadence. 

"I was passing by and saw you; so I thought I would give you good day, and ask after your father."

"My father is well. He will be here anon."

"Then I may as well stay till he comes."

"As you will. Good Martin, step into the village and tell my father here is a friend of his."

"And not of yours?" Gerard asked.

"My father's friends are mine."

"That is doubtful," Gerard pressed, the tension thickening the air between them. "It was not like a friend to promise to wait for me, and then make off the moment my back was turned. Cruel Margaret, you little know how I searched the town for you; how for want of you nothing was pleasant to me."

"These are idle words; if you had desired my father's company, or mine, you would have come back. There I had a bed laid for you, sir, at my cousin's, and he would have made much of you, and, who knows, I might have made much of you too. I was in the humour that day. You will not catch me in the same mind again, neither you nor any young man, I warrant me."

"Margaret, I came back the moment the Countess let me go; but you were not there."

"Nay, you did not, or you had seen Hans Cloterman at our table; we left him to bring you on."

"I saw no one there, but only a drunken man, that had just tumbled down."

"At our table? How was he clad?"

"Nay, I took little heed: in sad-coloured garb."

At this, Margaret's face gradually warmed. Assuming a facade of incredulity and severity, she put many shrewd questions to him, all of which Gerard answered most loyally. Finally, the subjective experiences that had divided them—untranslatable in their isolation—found a shared vocabulary. The misunderstanding of the drunken Hans Cloterman was laid bare. The acoustic tension in the courtyard released into a soft sigh of revulsion against their own stubbornness, followed by a tender rush of mutual confessions. Neither had been happy since; neither ever would have been happy but for this intersection of their timelines.

Then Gerard’s eye caught a manuscript Vulgate lying open upon the rough-hewn oak table. He pounced upon it with the fervor of a meticulous historian. The heavy, vellum leaves bore the official weight of the Church's Latin, but before he could immerse himself in that dominant, rigid lexicon, two white hands clapped flat upon the page, and a red face leaned over them.

"Nay, take away your hands, Margaret, that I may see where you are reading, and I will read there too at home; so shall my soul meet yours in the sacred page. You will not? Nay, then I must kiss them away."

He kissed them so often that for very shame they were fain to withdraw, and, lo! the sacred book lay open at, "An apple of gold in a network of silver."

"There, now," she said, "I had been hunting for it ever so long, and found it but even now—and to be caught!" With a touch of inconsistency, she pointed it out to Gerard with her white finger.

"Ay," said he, "but to-day it is all hidden in that great cap."

"It is a comely cap, I'm told by some."

"Maybe; but what it hides is beautiful."

"It is not: it is hideous."

"Well, it was beautiful at Rotterdam."

"Ay, everything was beautiful that day," she murmured with a little sigh.

And now Peter came in. The traditional agrarian elder brought with him the rich, grounding aroma of turned loam and damp pine needles, his booming, resonant voice a balm against the memory of the town's industrial noise. He welcomed Gerard cordially and insisted he stay for supper. 

Margaret vanished to the hearth. When she reappeared, the mythic occurrences of love had seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine. She had shed the heavy, utilitarian cap; her hair was caught in the silver net, shimmering in the firelight. She moved with the grace of a passionate chef, spreading a supper that spoke the native, gustatory dialect of their ancestors—a profound cultural memory preserved in smoke and salt. There was a rustic hearth-baked loaf, its thick, blistered crust crackling as it was torn, giving way to the steaming, earthy crumb of heritage wheat; a rich, slow-simmered pottage of marrow-bone and foraged root vegetables that filled the room with an olfactory landscape of wild thyme and roasted garlic. Gerard, savoring the profound meaning in this sustenance, found his soul nourished far beyond the capacity of his dry manuscripts. Margaret shot him glances half arch, half coy, beaming bright with gaiety as they shared the meal.

In the cool evening, guided by the ambient, nocturnal chirping of crickets and the rustle of alder leaves, Gerard coaxed her out. She objected, yet came; he coaxed her onto the road to Tergou, and she declined, yet came. They strolled up and down, hand in hand, weaving the threads of their past sorrows into a pledge never to quarrel or misunderstand one another again. A long, loving kiss sealed their vow, and Gerard walked home on wings.

From that day, the cycle of Gerard's evenings belonged to Margaret. Their attachment deepened through shared meals and shared silences, a multi-generational saga of human connection repeating its eternal loop, until the hours they spent together were the only hours they truly lived; the rest they merely counted and underwent. At the outset, this deep attachment went smoothly. Obstacles there were, but they seemed distant and small to the eyes of hope, youth, and love. The dominant powers and societal prejudices that their union would thwart gave no warning smoke to show their volcanic nature and power. The course of true love ran smoothly, placidly, until it had drawn these two young hearts into its current for ever. And then—
