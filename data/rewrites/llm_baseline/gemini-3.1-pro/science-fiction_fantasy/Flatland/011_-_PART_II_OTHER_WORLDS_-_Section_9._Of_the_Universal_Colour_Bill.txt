I stand before the hearth, stirring a clear, ancestral consommé—a recipe passed down in cyclical rhythm through generations of Circular chefs—and listen to the delicate, rhythmic simmer that once served as the metronome for our people’s psychological well-being. My grandfather, an agrarian elder of impeccable circumference, taught me to read the statics and kinetics of existence in the subtle acoustics of a boiling pot and the complex, earthy aromas of harvested root-polygons. But as I fold the memory of his kitchen into the daily domestic routine of my own, the mythic grace of those old ways feels impossibly distant. The intellectual Arts, once the very sustenance of our culture, were fast decaying. 

The profound Art of Sight Recognition—a practice intimately tied to the acoustic resonance of angles and the nuanced, untranslatable dialects of form—was no longer practiced. The meticulous studies of Geometry, Statics, and Kinetics, the very ingredients of a well-balanced intellect, came to be considered superfluous, falling into disrespect even at our University. The tactile, inferior Art of Feeling speedily experienced the same fate at our Elementary Schools. Our native language of sensory appreciation was dying. 

In this era of sensory erosion, the Isosceles classes—who once toiled in the agrarian terraces to cultivate the bitter spices that gave our society flavor—asserted that they were no longer needed as educational Specimens. Refusing to pay their customary tribute to the service of Education, they waxed daily more numerous and insolent. Their immunity from this old burden stripped away a wholesome tradition that had formerly tamed their rough, dissonant natures and kept their excessive numbers in check. 

Year by year, the Soldiers and Artisans began to vehemently assert that there was no great difference between them and the very highest class of Polygons. They argued that the profound, generational difficulties of life could be solved not by the delicate acoustics of Statics or the slow-braised wisdom of Kinetics, but by the crude, instant gratification of Colour Recognition. Colour was a synthetic, superficial commercialization of existence. It was an industrial pigment that smeared the palate, flattening the nuanced flavor of our society into a loud, dissonant visual noise. 

Not content with the natural decay of Sight Recognition, the lower classes boldly demanded the legal prohibition of all "monopolizing and aristocratic Arts." They sought the abolition of all endowments for the studies of Mathematics and Feeling. They insisted that because this cheap, garish Colour had destroyed the need for aristocratic distinctions, the Law should enforce an absolute, utilitarian equality. Finding the higher Orders wavering, the Revolutionaries advanced further, demanding that all classes—even the Priests and the Women—must do homage to Colour by submitting to be painted. 

When traditionalists objected that Priests (the perfect, round vessels of our society) and Women (the slender, needle-straight lines resembling a master’s carving knife) had no sides to paint, the Revolutionaries retorted with a coarse expediency. They demanded that the front half of every human being—the half containing the eye and mouth, the sacred organs of tasting and speaking—should be distinguishable from the hinder half. Thus, they brought before a general Assembly a Universal Colour Bill: in every Woman, the front half should be coloured red, like a harsh, artificially synthesized paprika, and the hinder half green, like a chemically extracted mint. The Priests were to be painted identically—the front semicircle red, the hinder semicircle green.

There was a bitter, cunning power dynamic embedded in this dominant new lexicon. This proposal did not emanate from an Isosceles; no being so lacking in geometric refinement could have devised such a recipe for state-craft. It was the masterwork of an Irregular Circle—a creature who, spared from childhood culling by foolish indulgence, grew up devoid of any true acoustic harmony or gustatory appreciation, viewing society only as a utilitarian mechanism to be disrupted. 

His first objective was to win the Women over to the Chromatic Innovation. By assigning Women the same two artificial colours as the Priestly Circles, the Revolutionaries ensured that the subtle visual dialect of our world would be irrevocably corrupted. To understand this untranslatable subjective experience, one must visualize the geometry of our kitchens. Imagine a Woman decorated according to the new Code: her front half (containing the mouth) red, her hinder half green. Look at her straight, linear form from the side. Obviously, you will see a straight line, half red, half green. 

Now imagine a Priest, a circular elder whose mouth forms the center point of his front red semicircle, while his hinder semicircle is green. If you contemplate this Great Man so that your eye rests on the exact same straight line as his dividing diameter, what you will see is a straight line, of which one half will be red, and the other green. The whole line will perhaps be shorter than a full-sized Woman, shading off more rapidly toward its extremities, but the loud, artificial identity of the colours would instantly override the subtle, sensory details of the old ways. 

Given the decay of Sight Recognition, and the certainty that Women would quickly learn to physically mimic the subtle shading of the Circles, it was obvious that the Colour Bill would confound a Priest with a young Woman. The acoustic ecology of the marketplace would be shattered by endless mistakes. Women anticipated this confusion with delight. By standing quietly in the domestic sphere, appearing visually as Priestly Circles, they could eavesdrop on ecclesiastical recipes and political secrets intended only for their husbands. Out of doors, this striking, dissonant combination of red and green would grant Women the societal deference previously reserved for the Circles. As for the subversion of the Constitution or the scandal befalling the Circular Class, the Female Sex gave no thought to the erosion of our cultural memory. Even in the pristine households of the Nobles, the Women hungered for the Universal Colour Bill.

The Bill’s second, more insidious objective was the gradual demoralization of the Circles themselves. Amidst the general intellectual decay, the Nobles had preserved their pristine clearness of understanding. From earliest childhood, familiarized in their colourless, silent kitchens, they practiced the Sacred Art of Sight Recognition. They learned to discern truth through the pure acoustics of form and the unadulterated flavors of unfiltered reality. 

The artful Irregular intended to destroy this domestic sanctuary. By forcing the Hierarchy to submit to the pollution of Colour, he would strip their homes of the silent, colorless environment required to train the intellect. Once subjected to this chromatic taint, the parental and childish Circles would demoralize one another. An infant, trying to discern between Father and Mother, would no longer rely on the subtle, native dialects of geometry, but would be confounded by maternal impostures wrapped in red and green visual noise. The child's faith in logical, sensory conclusions would be shattered. Thus, by degrees, the rich, intellectual luster of the Priestly Order would wane, leaving the road open for the total destruction of our Aristocracy and the permanent silencing of our culinary and acoustic heritage.
