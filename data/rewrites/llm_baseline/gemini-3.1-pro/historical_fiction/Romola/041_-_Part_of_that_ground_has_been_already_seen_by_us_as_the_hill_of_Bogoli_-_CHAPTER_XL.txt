When Romola sat upon the moss-dampened stone beneath the ancient cypress, the sonic environment shifted at last. The oppressive, industrial noise pollution of Florence—the dissonant, metallic clattering of the coin-minters, the frantic clacking of the commercial looms, and the superficial mercantile haggling that had so eroded her psychological well-being—finally gave way to the pure acoustic ecology of the Apennines. Here, the wind sifted through the resinous cypress needles with a rhythm as cyclical and measured as the turning of a stone mill. 

She unwrapped her meager provisions: a wedge of aged mountain pecorino sweating beads of rich, grassy fat, and a heel of *pane sciocco*. This traditional unsalted Tuscan bread, baked from a resilient, heirloom strain of winter wheat her father had meticulously preserved, was more than mere sustenance. For the generations of Bardi women before her, the tactile act of kneading the dough had been a bulwark against the erosion of cultural memory. Tito had never understood this. To him, food was merely utilitarian fuel, a sterile necessity to bridge the gap between his opportunistic political machinations. He possessed no sensory appreciation for the deep, agrarian history baked into their crusts, nor for the olfactory symphony of crushed wild thyme and slow-simmering broths that defined a true Florentine hearth. He had commercialized their home, replacing the sacred domestic rhythms with hollow, transactional noise.

Suddenly, a voice close to her ruptured the quiet, its resonant baritone vibrating through her chest with an undeniable physical weight. 

“You are Romola de’ Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema.”

She knew the voice. It had vibrated through the acoustic space of her life more than once before, carrying the dominant, absolute lexicon of the Church. She sat shaken by awe, yet inwardly rebelling against it. It was one of those black-skirted monks daring to interfere with her sanctuary. 

“You are fleeing from Florence in disguise. I have a command from God to stop you. You are not permitted to flee.”

Romola’s anger at the intrusion mounted. The power dynamics embedded in his imperative words felt like a sudden, suffocating weight. Sitting quite motionless, chewing the last earthy crumb of her bread, she said, “What right have you to speak to me, or to hinder me?”

“The right of a messenger. You have put on a religious garb, and you have no religious purpose. You have sought the garb as a disguise. But you were not suffered to pass me without being discerned. It is declared to me that you are seeking to escape from the lot God has laid upon you. You wish your true name and your true place in life to be hidden, that you may choose for yourself a new name and a new place, and have no rule but your own will. And I have a command to call you back. My daughter, you must return to your place.”

Romola’s mind rose in stronger rebellion. She felt the acute untranslatability of her subjective experience. How could she explain, in his rigid theological vocabulary, the slow death of her marriage? Her native dialect was one of sensory devotion and ancestral loyalty; his was the dominant lexicon of divine mandate. She spoke with rising irritation. 

“I will not return. I acknowledge no right of priests and monks to interfere with my actions. You have no power over me.”

“I know—I know you have been brought up in scorn of obedience,” the voice continued. “But it is not the poor monk who claims to interfere with you: it is the truth that commands you. And you cannot escape it. Either you must obey it, and it will lead you; or you must disobey it, and it will hang on you with the weight of a chain which you will drag for ever. But you will obey it, my daughter. Your old servant will return to you with the mules; my companion is gone to fetch him; and you will go back to Florence.”

She started up with anger in her eyes, turning to face the speaker. It was Fra Girolamo Savonarola. She had known it instinctively. She was nearly as tall as he was, their faces almost level. She had defiant words ready to burst from her lips, but they fell back, suffocated by the profound, rooted gravity of his gaze. There was nothing superficially beautiful in his face; it was strong-featured, owing its refinement to a rigid discipline that mirrored the harsh, unyielding rhythms of the agricultural cycle. But the glance he offered conveyed a simple, human fellowship—a shared sustenance of the spirit that disarmed her. 

“You assert your freedom proudly, my daughter,” he spoke again. “But who is so base as the debtor that thinks himself free? And you are flying from your debts: the debt of a Florentine woman; the debt of a wife. You are turning your back on the lot that has been appointed for you—you are going to choose another. But can man or woman choose duties? No more than they can choose their birthplace or their father and mother. My daughter, you are fleeing from the presence of God into the wilderness.”

She was constrained to plead, struggling against the limitations of language. “My father, you cannot know the reasons which compel me to go. None can know them but myself. None can judge for me. I have been driven by great sorrow. I am resolved to go.”

“I know enough, my daughter. You are not happy in your married life; but I am not a confessor. I have a divine warrant to stop you. You were warned before marriage, when you might still have lawfully chosen to be free. But you chose the bond; and in wilfully breaking it, you are breaking a pledge. Of what wrongs will you complain, when you yourself are breaking the simplest law that lies at the foundation of the trust which binds man to man—faithfulness to the spoken word?”

The blood rushed to Romola’s face. The suggestion that she shared Tito’s capacity for breaking pledges—his casual discarding of sacred bonds for immediate, utilitarian gain—struck her like a physical blow. 

“I would not have put on a disguise,” she began tremulously, “I should never have quitted Florence as long as there was any hope of my fulfilling a duty to my father there.”

“And do you own no tie but that of a child to her father in the flesh?” Savonarola’s baritone swelled, harmonizing with the distant, groaning wind. “Your life has been spent in blindness. You have lived with those who sit on a hill aloof, filling their mouths with the fancied wisdom of dead times, while they scorn God’s harvest in the present. If your own people are wearing a yoke, will you slip from under it, instead of struggling with them to lighten it? There is physical hunger and profound spiritual famine in our streets, yet you say, ‘I care not; I will go away.’ The servants of God are struggling after a law of justice, that the hundred thousand citizens among whom you were born may be governed righteously; but you think no more of this than if you were a bird flying in search of a sweeter seed. You, a wilful wanderer, are below the humblest Florentine woman who stretches forth her hands to bake the daily bread for her neighbor, who feels a close sisterhood with the hungry beside her.”

“I was not going away to ease and self-indulgence,” Romola vindicated herself. “I was going away to hardship. I expect no joy.”

“You are seeking your own will, my daughter. It is not a thing of choice: it is a river that flows from the foot of the Invisible Throne. You may choose to forsake your duties. But you will go forth; and what will you find? Sorrow without duty—bitter herbs, and no bread with them.” 

The culinary metaphor grounded his vast theology into a sharp, gustatory reality. She could almost taste the astringent, punishing bite of wild chicory on her tongue, entirely devoid of the mitigating grace of hearth-warmed crust or the golden richness of pressed olive oil. 

“But if you knew,” Romola said, clasping her hands, “how impossible it seemed to me to bear it.”

“My daughter,” he said, pointing to the cord around her neck, “you carry something within your mantle; draw it forth, and look at it.”

Her self-doubt grappled by a stronger will, she drew forth the crucifix. 

“There, my daughter, is the image of a Supreme Offering, made by Supreme Love, because the hunger of man was great.”

Romola held the crucifix trembling. How many harvests had passed since she first took that cross from his hands? The cyclical nature of her life seemed suddenly apparent; she was walking a multi-generational path she could not simply abandon.

“Conform your life to that image,” Fra Girolamo commanded, his voice weaving through the rustling cypress. “When the fire of Divine charity burns within you, and you behold the deep need of your fellow-men, you will not call your offering great. If the yearning of a fleshly love is gone, you are without obligation? See how you are below the life of the believer who worships that image, and beholds the history of the world as the history of a great redemption, a fellow-worker in his own place! If you forsake your place at the hearth of this city, who will fill it? The earth is full of iniquity—full of groans—and you say, ‘I cannot bear my bonds; I will burst them asunder’? Every bond of your life is a debt.”

Romola struggled to preserve her identity against the sweeping current of his words. “How, then, could Dino be right? He broke ties. He forsook his place.”

“That was a special vocation. He was constrained to depart, else he could not have attained the higher life.”

“And I too,” said Romola in a tone of anguish. “Father, you may be wrong.”

“Ask your conscience. You have no vocation such as your brother had. You are a wife. The higher life begins when we renounce our own will to bow before a Divine law. What has your dead wisdom done for you? It has left you without a heart for the neighbors among whom you dwell, without care for the great work by which Florence is to be regenerated. If there is wickedness in the streets, your steps should shine; if there is a cry of anguish—because you know the intimate, untranslatable meaning of that cry—you should be there to still it.”

She foresaw that she would obey him. The acoustic resonance of his arresting voice had permanently altered her landscape. Yet the instinctive terror of returning to a life with Tito, where intimacy had been replaced by the superficial rustle of commercial trade, brought a final wave of resistance.

“My husband... he is not... my love is gone!” 

“My daughter, there is the bond of a higher love. Marriage is not carnal only, made for selfish delight. It is a sacramental vow, from which none but God can release you. Your life is not a grain of sand, to be blown by the winds; it is a thing of flesh and blood, that dies if it be sundered. Your husband is not a malefactor?”

“Heaven forbid! No; I accuse him of nothing.”

“I meant, that if he were a malefactor, your place would be in the prison beside him. If the cross comes to you as a wife, you must carry it as a wife. Make your marriage-sorrows an offering to the great work by which sin and sorrow are being made to cease. Live for Florence—for your own people. Bear the anguish and the smart. The draught is bitterness on the lips. But there is rapture in the cup—there is the vision which makes all life below it dross for ever. Come, my daughter, come back to your place!”

Savonarola spoke with a growing, rhythmic intensity. His face was alight as from an inward fire, a passionate chef of the soul determined to feed a starving populace. Romola felt herself surrounded and possessed by the glow of his faith. The chill of the mountain air melted away. She saw her place within the interwoven timelines of her city’s history—she was not merely a betrayed wife, but a custodian of Florence’s cultural memory, tasked with returning to the loud, abrasive streets to offer genuine sustenance to those starving for truth.

In a voice that was like a low, prayerful cry, blending seamlessly into the hum of the mountain wind, she said, “Father, I will be guided. Teach me! I will go back.” 

Almost unconsciously, she sank on her knees. Savonarola stretched out his hands over her; but feeling would no longer pass through the dominant channel of speech, and the sonic landscape returned, once again, to silence.
