Marianne would have deemed herself utterly inexcusable had she possessed the strength to sleep the first night following her departure from Willoughby. Yet, shame held no sway over her; she rose from her bed in a state of profound exhaustion that rendered the concept of disgrace irrelevant. Sleep had eluded her entirely, replaced by a vigil of weeping that drained her spirit until she emerged with a splitting headache, mute, and refusing sustenance. She inflicted upon her mother and sisters a constant, gnawing agony, rejecting all attempts at solace with a stubborn intensity. Her sensibility was a weapon of mass destruction, potent and unyielding.

By breakfast's end, she retreated into the solitude of Allenham, wandering the village grounds to indulge in the bitter juxtaposition of past joy and present ruin. The evening mirrored the morning's despair. She subjected herself to a ritual of grief, playing every melody she had once shared with Willoughby, staring fixedly at the musical lines he had penned for her until her heart grew so heavy that further sorrow seemed impossible to extract. This nourishment of mourning was her daily diet. Hours were consumed at the pianoforte, alternating between singing and sobbing, her voice frequently silenced by the sheer volume of her tears. Literature followed suit; she devoured only those volumes they had once read together, courting the misery inherent in their contrasts. Such violent affliction could not sustain itself indefinitely; within days, it settled into a calmer, deeper melancholy. Nevertheless, her solitary walks and silent meditations continued to trigger eruptions of sorrow as vivid as ever.

No letter arrived from Willoughby, nor did Marianne expect one. Her mother, Mrs. Dashwood, found the silence alarming, while Elinor's unease mounted. However, Mrs. Dashwood possessed a peculiar ability to rationalize ambiguity. "Recall, Elinor," she reasoned, "how frequently Sir John retrieves our mail personally. We have already conceded that secrecy may be prudent; thus, we cannot fault him if their correspondence passes through his hands." Elinor acknowledged the logic yet remained unsettled. A solution existed, direct and simple: she proposed asking Marianne outright whether she was engaged to Willoughby. "From you, her mother, so kind and indulgent, such a query could not offend," Elinor argued. "She was wont to be unreserved, especially with you."

"I would never dare ask such a question," Mrs. Dashwood retorted, her romantic delicacy overriding common sense. "Suppose they are not engaged; what devastation would such an inquiry cause? It would be ungenerous. I shall never deserve her trust again if I force a confession of what she intends to conceal. I know Marianne's heart; she loves me dearly, and she will reveal the truth when circumstances permit. I would never coerce the confidence of anyone, certainly not a child, for a sense of duty prevents denying her wishes." Elinor viewed this excessive generosity as a failure of judgment regarding her sister's youth, urging the matter further, but in vain. Common prudence drowned in Mrs. Dashwood's sentimentalism.

Days passed before Willoughby's name was spoken before Marianne by her family. Sir John and Mrs. Jennings lacked such nicety; their witticisms only compounded her suffering. Then, one evening, Mrs. Dashwood, picking up a volume of Shakespeare, remarked, "We have never finished *Hamlet*, Marianne; our dear Willoughby departed before we could conclude it. We shall resume when he returns..." "Months!" Marianne cried, startled. "No—nor many weeks." Mrs. Dashwood regretted her slip, but it pleased Elinor, for it elicited a response from Marianne that betrayed her certainty of Willoughby's intentions and knowledge of his plans.

About a week after his departure, Marianne was persuaded to join her sisters for their customary walk, abandoning her habit of evading companionship. Previously, if they headed toward the downs, she fled to the lanes; if they discussed the valley, she climbed the hills, ensuring her absence. But Elinor's disapproval of such isolation proved effective. They traversed the road through the valley, mostly in silence, for Marianne's mind remained untethered, and Elinor, content with securing her presence, refrained from further interrogation.

Beyond the valley's entrance, where the landscape shifted from wild richness to open expanse, a familiar stretch of road appeared—the path taken upon their arrival at Barton. Stopping to survey the vista, a new element entered the scene: a man on horseback approaching them. Within minutes, he was identified as a gentleman. Instantly, Marianne rapturously declared, "It is he; it is indeed—I know it is!" Hastening to meet him, she was intercepted by Elinor's cry, "Indeed, Marianne, I think you are mistaken. It is not Willoughby. The stature is insufficient, and the bearing lacks his distinctive air."

"He possesses it! He possesses it!" Marianne insisted, convinced. "His air, his coat, his horse. I knew precisely when he would arrive." Walking eagerly ahead, she ignored Elinor's growing certainty that her hope was misplaced. As they closed the distance to within thirty yards, Marianne's gaze faltered. Her heart sank. Abruptly turning, she hurried backward, only to be detained by her sisters' voices. A third voice, nearly as recognizable as Willoughby's, joined theirs, pleading for her to stay. With surprise, she turned to behold Edward Ferrars.

He was the sole individual in the world capable of forgiveness for failing to be Willoughby, the only one capable of extracting a smile from her. Yet, she dispelled her tears to offer him one, momentarily forgetting her own disappointment in her sister's happiness. Dismounting and handing his horse to his servant, he walked back with them to Barton, ostensibly visiting them. He was greeted with cordiality by all, but Marianne displayed a warmth of regard surpassing even Elinor's. To Marianne, however, this reunion felt like a continuation of the inexplicable coldness she had observed at Norland. On Edward's part, there was a distinct deficiency of the demeanor a lover should exhibit. He appeared confused, scarcely registering the pleasure of the encounter, devoid of rapture or gaiety. He spoke little, offering only forced responses to questions, and bestowed no marks of affection upon Elinor.

Marianne watched and listened with mounting astonishment, eventually developing a nascent dislike for Edward. Every feeling, inevitably, circled back to Willoughby, whose manners formed a stark, unfavorable contrast to those of his supposed rival. After a brief pause following the initial greetings, Marianne asked if Edward had come directly from London. "No," he replied, "I have been in Devonshire for two weeks."

"Two weeks?" she repeated, shocked that he had remained in the same county as Elinor for so long without prior contact. He looked somewhat distressed adding that he had stayed with friends near Plymouth. "Have you recently visited Sussex?" Elinor inquired. "Yes, I was at Norland about a month ago."

"And how does dear, dear Norland appear?" Marianne cried. "Dear, dear Norland," Elinor responded, "it probably resembles its usual self at this time of year. The woods and paths are heavily blanketed with dead leaves."

"Oh!" Marianne exclaimed, her voice filled with longing. "With what transporting sensation have I formerly witnessed them falling! How I delighted, as I walked, to see them driven in showers about me by the wind! What feelings the season, the air, altogether inspire! Now, there is no one to regard them. They are seen merely as a nuisance, swept hastily aside and banished from sight."

"It is not everyone," Elinor noted, "who shares your passion for dead leaves."

"No," Marianne admitted, "my feelings are rarely shared, rarely understood. But sometimes they are." As she spoke, she sank into a reverie for a moment before rousing herself. "Now, Edward," she said, drawing his attention to the landscape, "here lies Barton Valley. Look upward and strive for tranquility. Observe those hills! Have you ever seen their equals? To the left stands Barton Park, amidst those woods and plantations. You may discern the end of the house. And there, beneath that distant hill rising with such grandeur, lies our cottage."

"It is a beautiful country," he replied, "though these valleys must be filthy in winter."

"How can you conceive of filth with such sights before you?"

"Because," he smiled, "among the other objects before me, I perceive a very dirty lane."

"How strange!" Marianne murmured to herself as they proceeded. "Do you find an agreeable neighborhood here? Are the Middletons pleasant people?"

"Not all of them," Marianne answered, her voice low. "We could not be more unfortunately situated."

"Marianne!" her sister cried. "How can you say so? How can you be so unjust? They are a very respectable family, Mr. Ferrars, and have behaved in the friendliest manner toward us. Have you forgotten, Marianne, how many pleasant days we owe them?"

"No," Marianne whispered, "nor how many painful moments."

Elinor paid no heed to this remark, redirecting her focus to their guest and attempting to sustain conversation by discussing their current residence and its amenities, extorting occasional questions and comments from him.
