Two months prior to this moment, the inhabitants of a small coastal town nestled between Naples and Rome gathered on the beach, their gaze fixed seaward upon a vessel struggling against a ferocious gale that blew directly onto the shore. The ship appeared to weather the storm, prompting cheers from the crowd, only for the wind and surf to drive her visibly closer, eliciting a secret satisfaction among the spectators they would never admit to.

The vessel, though unsuited for open-sea navigation by scientific standards, was perfectly engineered for grounding, boasting an extravagant poop that caught the wind and lines resembling a cocked hat turned inside out. To the onlookers, the battering wooden frame seemed alive, panting against death; yet those on deck knew better, sensing a multitude of hearts and natures exposed to the terrifying hour.

Mariners stumbled wildly, handling ropes with chaotic intent, alternating curses and prayers. Passengers huddled around the mast—some seated, some kneeling, others prostrate—clutching the bulwarks as the ship rolled in mighty waves. Among them stood Gerard, a handsome young man whose ashen cheeks and compressed lips revealed a fierce battle between terror and self-respect. He held a shroud tightly, wincing with every swell. Amid the trembling throng, prayers and vows rose like incense, creating a cacophony of deities. While sailors relied on a single goddess, shifting her titles from "Queen of Heaven" to "Haven of Safety," the landsmen embraced polytheism with fervor. An English merchant vowed gold to Our Lady of Walsingham; a Genoese pledged a silver collar to Our Lady of Loretto; a Tuscan noble promised wax lights to Our Lady of Ravenna. Each pledged allegiance to a specific local manifestation of the divine rather than the universal truth.

Suddenly, a violent gust caught the sail at a disadvantage. Rotten shrouds snapped, and the sail tore away with a loud crack, fluttering downwind like a sheet of paper until it vanished into the sea. Before the helmsman could adjust, a wave crashed over the quarter, drenching the crew to the bone and offering a foretaste of chill death. Vows erupted anew: one man vowed to become a Carthusian monk if Saint Thomas saved him; another promised a pilgrimage to Compostela in armor and bare feet if Saint James intervened. Others invoked Thomas, Dominic, Denis, and especially Catherine of Siena.

Two Neapolitan traders shivered nearby. One shouted, "I vow a waxen image of St. Christopher's own weight if I reach land safely!" His companion nudged him, warning, "Brother, take heed; selling all you possess won't buy that much wax." "Silence, fool," the first retorted, whispering, "If I survive, I'll not give him a straw."

Others lay flat, praying to the sea itself: "Oh, merciful sea! Oh, generous sea! Be gentle, preserve us!" Wails of animal terror rose as the ship became a mere plaything in the arms of the waves.

Seated amidst the wailing was a humble Roman woman with her child at her breast. Her cheek was pale, her eyes calm, her lips moving in silent prayer. She neither wept nor bargained with the gods. When the ship seemed to vanish beneath them and bearded men squealed, she simply kissed her child. She sat patiently, suckling him in the jaws of death, refusing to deny him any joy she could offer. In her veins flowed undiluted Roman blood; the instinct of her ancient race taught her to meet death with dignified composure.

A gigantic friar stood on the poop, feet planted wide like the Colossus of Rhodes, ignoring the peril rather than defying it. Reciting verses from the Canticles with an unwavering voice, he invited passengers to confess. Some knelt, receiving absolution as if in a secure sacristy. Gerard, drawn by an instinct to seek the impregnable, moved closer. The friar's composure dwarfed the courage of heroes facing fleshly odds. Here, two figures maintained the dignity of their race: a woman, tender yet heroic, and a monk steeled by faith against mortal fear.

With the sail gone, sailors cut down the mast, and its fall seemed to relieve the ship slightly. Yet the hull, no longer propelled by canvas, could not outrun the sea. Waves struck the poop repeatedly, blows as heavy as those from a rocky mountain. The captain, pale as death, left the helm to cry, "Lighten her! Fling everything overboard, or we shall founder before we strike!" As sailors executed the order, the captain spoke with a calmness alien to an English skipper in peril. "Friends," he said, "last night a globe of fire approached us. If both Castor and Pollux appear, we are lucky; if one comes alone, we are doomed. Prepare to die."

A loud wail answered him. When asked how long they had to prepare, he replied, "Perhaps half an hour; after that, it is impossible. She leaks like a sieve." Passengers seized everything on deck and threw it overboard. They grabbed a heavy sack from under an elderly, seasick man. As they dragged it to the side, the owner leaped up with an unearthly shriek. "Holy Moses! What are you doing? It is my all! My silver candlesticks, plates, brooches!" "Let go, hoary villain," cried others. "Shall we all die for your ill-gotten gear?" "Fling him in with it," shouted one. "It is this Hebrew we Christians are drowned for." They wrestled the man away, and the sack splashed into the waves. The owner cried out in anguish, his white hair streaming, and lunged to jump after it. But it sank, and he staggered, tearing his hair and cursing the ship, the sea, and all powers of heaven and hell.

"Now," cried the captain, "see a church in sight. Steer for it, and pray to the saint, whoever he may be." They steered toward the church, praying to an unknown god. A tremendous sea broke over them, snapping the rudder and jamming it immovable while flooding the deck.

Superstitious terror gripped some, who turned to Gerard. "Here is the cause," they cried. "He has never invoked a saint. He is a heathen, a pagan aboard." "Alas, good friends, say not so," Gerard stammered, teeth chattering. "Call these heathens who pray to the sea. I honor the saints, but I dare not pray to them now; there is no time. What avail Dominic, Thomas, and Catherine? Nearer God's throne sits St. Peter, and if I pray to him, I shall be drowned before he can plead my case. Oh, sweet Jesus, pitiful Jesus, who walked on Genezareth when Peter sank and wept for Lazarus, save poor Gerard—for dear Margaret's sake!"

At that moment, sailors prepared to desert the sinking ship in the lifeboat. A rush of egotists ensued, crowding thirty souls into the vessel. Three remained bewildered, running to and fro, unable to act. Two sat paralyzed by terror, like heaps of wet rags. One knelt, praying over a wooden statue of the Virgin Mary, which the sailors had detached from the mast. The statue washed about the deck as water poured in, and the poor soul followed it on his knees, clasping his hands to it. Beside him sat the Jew, palsied not by fear but by a different affliction. No longer capable of petty passions, he sat cross-legged, bemoaning his lost bag, shaking his fist at the spray and cursing the Nazarenes, their gods, their devils, and their waters to eternity.

The gigantic Dominican, having shriven the entire ship, stood calmly communing with his spirit. The Roman woman sat pale and patient, drawing her child closer as death approached.

Gerard saw this and his manhood awakened. "See! See!" he cried. "They have taken the boat, leaving the poor woman and child to perish." His heart set his wit working. "Wife, I will save thee, please God." He ran to find a cask or plank, finding none. His eye fell on the wooden image of the Virgin. Catching it in his arms, heedless of the wail from its worshipper, he ran aft. "Come, wife," he cried. "I will lash thee and the child to this. It is worm-eaten, but it will serve."

She turned her great dark eyes upon him, saying a single word: "Thyself?!" But with wonderful magnanimity and tenderness. "I am a man and have no child to care for." "Ah!" she replied, her face animating with a desire to live. He lashed the image to her side. With the hope of life, she lost some of her heroic calm; her body trembled, but her eye remained steady.

The ship was so low in the water that using an oar as a lever, he could slide her into the waves. "Come," he said, "while yet there is time." She turned her wet Roman eyes upon him. "Poor youth! God forgive me! My child!" He launched her onto the surge, keeping her from battering against the ship with his oar.

A heavy hand fell on him, and a deep, sonorous voice sounded in his ear: "'Tis well. Now come with me." It was the gigantic friar. Gerard turned, and the friar took two strides, seizing the broken mast. Gerard did the same, obeying instinctively. Together, after a prodigious effort, they hoisted the remainder of the mast and carried it off. "Fling it in," said the friar, "and follow it." They threw it in, but one of the bewildered passengers had run after them, jumping first onto one end. Gerard seized the other, the friar the middle.

It was a terrible situation. The mast rose and plunged with each wave like a kicking horse, spray flogging their faces and blinding them. A long grating noise echoed ahead; the ship had struck. Soon, stationary, they were hurled against her with tremendous force. Their companion's head struck the broken rudder with a horrible crack, smashing like a coconut by a sledgehammer. He sank directly, leaving no trace but a red stain on the water and a white clot on the jagged wood, his death cry ringing in their ears as they drifted under the lee of the black hull. The friar uttered a short Latin prayer for his soul and took his place composedly.

They rolled along; one moment they seemed in a basin of watery hills, the next catching glimpses of the shore speckled with people throwing up their arms in wild Italian gestures to encourage them. The black boat drove bottom-upwards, and between it and them, the woman rose and fell. She had found a paddle and held her child tight with her left arm, paddling gallantly with her right.

After a long time, the friar said quietly, "I touched the ground." "Impossible, father," Gerard replied. "We are more than a hundred yards from shore. Leave not our faithful mast." "My son," said the friar, "you speak prudently. But know I have business for Holy Church and may not waste time floating when I can walk in her service. There I felt it with my toes; see the benefit of sandals, not shoes. Again; and sandy. Thy stature is less than mine: keep to the mast! I walk." He left the mast and, extending his powerful arms, rushed through the water. Gerard soon followed. At each overpowering wave, the monk stood like a tower, closing his mouth and throwing his head back to encounter it, disappearing entirely before emerging to plough lustily on.

Finally, they came close to shore, but the outward suction baffled all attempts to land. Natives sent stout fishermen into the sea, holding long spears in a triple chain, and so dragged them ashore. The friar shook himself, bestowed a short paternal benediction on the natives, and walked on to Rome, eyes bent on earth according to his rule, without pausing or casting a glance back at the sea that had nearly engulfed him but lacked power to harm him without his Master's leave.

While the friar stalked alone to Rome, I, not in the service of Holy Church, stop to note that the reader and I were within six inches of this giant once before, escaping him then. Now I fear we are in for him. Gerard grasped every hand on the beach. They brought him to an enormous fire and, with a delicacy rare in the north, left him to dry alone. Only then did he consent to don a fisherman's dress, leaving his own by the fire, and descend to the beach.

What he saw was brief. The captain stuck by the ship, convinced it was idle to resist Castor or Pollux, whichever had come for him in a ball of fire. Nevertheless, the sea broke up the ship, sweeping the poop, captain and all, clear of the rest, taking him safely ashore. Gerard had a principal hand in pulling him from the water. The disconsolate Hebrew landed on another fragment, offering a reward for his bag upon touching earth, which excited little sympathy but some amusement. Two more were saved on pieces of the wreck. The thirty egotists came ashore one by one, dead; one breathed still. The natives, with excellent intentions, took him to a hot fire. So he too retired from this shifting scene.

As Gerard stood by the sea, watching his late companions washed ashore with mixed horror and curiosity, a hand was laid lightly on his shoulder. He turned. It was the Roman matron, burning with womanly gratitude. She took his hand gently, raising it slowly to her lips to kiss it, so nobly that she seemed to confer an honor upon a deserving hand. Then, with a face beaming and moist eyes, she held her child up to kiss his preserver. Gerard kissed the child more than once; he was fond of children. But he said nothing.
