Before we follow Miss Lydia Van den Bosch’s adventures any further, we owe readers an explanation for that puzzling line in Mr. Warrington’s letter to his brother, referencing Lady Maria Esmond—a secret no properly respectable person could ever be expected to guess at first glance. How could innocent, well-bred young folks possibly imagine that a noblewoman of ancient line, long acquaintance with the world, the same lady we saw so deeply smitten with her young cousin Harry Warrington a mere dozen months prior, would throw all decorum to the wind? My very fingertips flush as I write it: she did not only fall in love with a man of far humbler birth, years her junior, but she married him, half in secret, behind the gossips’ backs, no less, with Parson Sampson tying the indissoluble knot at his little Mayfair chapel.

Do not condemn her out of hand, I beg you. Because Lady Maria once indulged, and then outgrew, a foolish partiality for her young cousin, must she never be allowed to love anyone else ever again? Are men the only souls granted the privilege of changing their hearts, and women to be shrieked at for daring to snatch a small share of comfort when it comes their way? Few invectives are ruder, less logical, than Hamlet’s rages against his mother over her second marriage. The truth of it is, tender, clinging creatures crave something to twine themselves around: when Hamlet Senior was gone, she wrapped herself round Claudius. We have known women so determined to attach their affections somewhere that they can twine round two gentlemen at once, if need be. Why should there be no new marriage feasts after the funeral baked meats are eaten? If you said grace over your dinner yesterday, is that any reason to go hungry today? The healthy appetite that relishes tonight’s supper is proof you will no doubt be ready for your dinner again at eight tomorrow evening. For my own part, whenever I have been the object of a fine lady’s favour (you may picture them as ravishing beauties of the highest rank, if you please), I have always reminded myself that a day will come when they will be fond of someone else. We are served à la Russe in this world, eaten one course at a time, just like the captives in Polyphemus’s cave: today me, tomorrow you. Some of these love devourers will consume dozens of us, young and old, tender and tough, plump and lean, fair and plain, with no discrimination, and one by one we slip down their omnivorous maws. Look at Lady Ogresham! We all remember how she gobbled up poor Tom Kydd last year, picked his bones clean, and tossed him aside. Now she has Ned Suckling trapped in her den, quivering and transfixed under her great predatory eyes. She creeps closer, he draws nearer, and soon there will be a few feeble pleas for mercy, and hobblegobble—he will vanish. Ah, it is a pity, too. I knew Maria Esmond had lost her heart many times before Harry Warrington ever stumbled on it, and I had fondly imagined he would be the last to hold it, that she would mourn her ill-fated love and cherish it in decent, quiet celibacy for the rest of her days. If I, in some foolish moment of dotage, were to fall in love tomorrow, I should still delude myself I owned the full fee simple of my beloved’s heart, not that I was merely a short-term tenant of a shabby old furnished apartment, where the dingy wine glasses have been clouded by a hundred pairs of lips, and the lumpy sofas are still mud-stained from the last lodger’s boots.

But this Maria, living a century ago, was a woman of no steady mind. You, dear reader who knows the world (and do not pretend you have no experience, for you have heard your mother tell the tales), know that an old flirt, when she tires of one passion, will inevitably run headlong into another. Flirting is just like drinking: when the brandy bottle is empty, the confirmed drinker will fall to gin. So if Maria Esmond found a successor for Harry Warrington, and set a new sultan on the throne of her heart, what else could anyone possibly have expected? That realm was like the Low Countries, long used to conquest, forever open to new invasions.

And the man who had won her was none other than Mr. Hagan, the young Irish actor who had played the lead in George Warrington’s tragedy. His voice was thrilling, his eye bright, his bearing noble; he looked so impossibly handsome in his gilt leather armour and great buckled periwig, speaking the poet’s glowing lines, that the lady surrendered her whole heart to him as Ariadne did to Bacchus, the moment her affair with Theseus was well and truly over. The young actor was both flattered and elated by the highborn lady’s attachment. He might have preferred a Lady Maria Hagan a few years younger, but no woman alive could have been more tenderly devoted to him. She clung to him so closely that when their secret marriage finally leaked out, and her furious noble family disowned her entirely, she retired with him to his humble little lodgings in Westminster without a second thought.

General Lambert brought the news home to his family from his office in Whitehall one day, and made merry over the tale with his wife and girls. Those were homely times, and a joke was none the worse for being a little broad: a fine lady would laugh over a raucous page of Fielding and weep over a letter in *Clarissa* that would make any modern lady’s eyes start from her head in horror. The merry General cracked every witticism he could think of: he joked about George accidentally having a hand in the match, about how furious Barry Lyndon would be when he heard the news, swore it was a shame cousin Hagan had not asked George to be his groomsman, that their first child ought to be named Carpezan or Sybilla after the tragedy, that they could never afford a full coach but might beg a chariot with pasteboard dragons from the manager of Rich’s Theatre, that the baby could be christened in Macbeth’s cauldron, with Harry Warrington and Harlequin as godfathers.

“Why shouldn’t she marry him if she likes him?” little Hetty piped up. “Why shouldn’t he love her just because she is a little older? Mamma is a little older than you, and you love her no less. You told me yourself, sir, that you were very poor when you married my mamma, and you were perfectly happy, and no one laughed at you!” The innocent child spoke out of pure unworldliness, with no knowledge of Lady Maria’s long history of follies.

“So her whole family has deserted her?” Miss Hett continued, after George confirmed how wild with rage all the Esomonds were: Lady Castlewood had gone into full formal mourning, Mr. Will swore he would cut the rascal actor’s ears off, and Madame de Bernstein was the most furious of all, half mad with mortification. “It is an insult to the family, of course, to have a relative of their rank marry a man of Mr. Hagan’s station, and I can imagine how indignant those fine ladies must be. But to desert her entirely? That is quite another matter.”

“Indeed, my dear child,” Mrs. Lambert intervened, “you do not understand what you are talking about. After Lady Maria’s conduct, no respectable person can possibly pay her a visit.”

“What conduct, mamma?”

“Never you mind,” her mother said, brushing the question away. “Little girls cannot be expected to know, and ought not to be curious to ask about, the sort of behaviour that has brought her ladyship to this pass. Suffice it to say I am shocked she was ever received in this house, and no honest person ought to associate with her now.”

“Then Aunt Lambert, I must be whipped and sent to bed!” George declared with mock gravity. “I will own, though I did not confess it before, while the affair was still private, that I have already been to visit my cousin the player and his lady wife. I found them in very shabby lodgings in Westminster, where that good fellow has the modesty to shelter not just his noble wife, but his old widowed mother, and a little brother he is putting through school. I came away quite liking the man, almost respecting him, even if I own his marriage was wildly improvident. But then, people are endlessly improvident when it comes to marriage, aren’t they, Theo?”

“Improvident, yes, when they marry spendthrifts like you,” the General laughed. “Master George went off to visit his long-lost relations, and I will wager he left his purse behind with them.”

“Not the purse itself, sir,” George said, smiling soft and tender at Theo. “Theo made that purse, after all. But I will own it came home perfectly empty. Mr. Rich, the theatre manager, is in a fine fury. He says he hardly dares put Hagan on the stage now, for fear of a riot, just like the trouble Garrick had over the foreign dancers. This would be a riot of fine gentlemen: the macaronis are all furious, and swear they will pelt Hagan off the boards and cudgel him afterwards. My cousin Will took a public oath at Arthur’s Club that he will tear the actor’s ears off. Hagan has not dared perform, so they have cut his salary off entirely, and now that luckless pair of lovers have no way at all to buy their daily bread and cheese.”

“And you took supplies to them? That is just like you, George!” Theo breathed, gazing at him with adoration in her eyes.

“Your purse took the supplies, dear Theo,” George corrected her.

“Mamma, you must go and visit them tomorrow!” Theo begged her mother.

“If she doesn’t, I shall divorce you, my dear!” the General laughed. “Come here and kiss me, you little pet—with your new husband’s permission, of course.”

“Monsieur mon beau fiddlestick, papa!” Miss Lambert retorted, and I have no doubt she complied with her father’s affectionate demand at once. It was the first time George Esmond Warrington, Esquire, had ever been called a fiddlestick.

Any man who has made an imprudent marriage, even in our modern day, knows well the gauntlet of family abuse, scorn, wrath, and misplaced pity he must run afterwards. If your respectable relatives shriek at you for marrying a poor curate’s charming daughter, or agreeing to wed a young barrister whose only income comes from writing court reports, who will have to pay off his Oxford debts out of your small fortune—if they rage at those comparatively sensible matches, you can imagine the wild uproar that followed the news of Lady Maria Hagan’s marriage, among both her noble family and the actor’s own kin.

Take old Mrs. Hagan, for a start. Her son had dutifully kept her in quiet comfort ever since he left Trinity College after his father’s death, and made his debut as Romeo at Dublin’s Crow Street Theatre. His salary had long been enough to send his little brother to school, help his sister who worked as a lady’s companion, and keep the old clergyman’s widow in warm fire, soft tea, and plenty of plain dinners. Now all at once, a fine noble lady with every extravagant habit in the world had moved into their little home, taken a seat at their small table, and claimed the cream off the milk, the wings off every chicken, the best half of every meal, where there had only ever been just enough for three before. If Hagan had not been such a fierce, high-spirited fellow, and the old mother not so very afraid of crossing him, I doubt Lady Maria’s life in those Westminster lodgings would have been very comfortable at all. But love, when it takes a passionate, romantic hold of a person, never stops to count consequences or measure available space. Who has not known that reckless mood? What thrifty wife has not seen her husband come home, cheeks flushed, with a devil-may-care grin, and announce he has invited twenty people to dinner next Saturday, even though he knows the dining table only seats sixteen? Never mind, he will say, two of the pretty girls can sit on the young gentlemen’s knees, some of the guests will not show up, there will surely be enough food. Drunk on love, people throw themselves into that kind of reckless housekeeping, never calculating the monthly butcher’s and fishmonger’s bills that will land on the poor housekeeper’s doorstep all too soon.

Old Mrs. Hagan’s quiet grumbling was nothing next to the deafening clamour in Lady Maria’s noble circle at Kensington. Think of all the tittering whispers between the ladies in waiting at the Princess’s court, where Lady Fanny held a post; the crude jokes from Will’s brother officers at the usher’s table; the waggeries printed in the daily newspapers and magazines; the huffs of outraged prudes, the laughter in the gentlemen’s clubs, the sneers of the ungodly! The second she got the news, Madame de Bernstein had hysterical fits and fled to the solitude of her beloved Tunbridge Wells rocks, where she claimed she never saw more than forty people a night at cards. Lord Castlewood refused to see his sister ever again, and his countess, dressed in full mourning, called on her royal patroness, who very graciously condoled with her on the terrible disgrace that had fallen on their house. For three whole days the entire town was amused and absorbed by the scandal—until fresh news came: a great victory in Germany, uncertain dispatches from the American colonies, a general officer recalled home to face a court martial, a ravishing new Italian soprano, and the public entirely forgot Lady Maria huddled in her garret, eating the hard-earned meals of the actor’s family.
