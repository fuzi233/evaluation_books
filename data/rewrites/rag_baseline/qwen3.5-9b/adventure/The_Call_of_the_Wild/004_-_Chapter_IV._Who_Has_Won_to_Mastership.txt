"Two devils," François muttered the following morning, his voice rough with exhaustion as he inspected the carnage. "I spoke the truth when I said Buck killed two devils." He dragged the massive wolf toward the dying embers of the campfire, using the flickering light to highlight the gashes tearing through his pelt.

Perrault surveyed the damage, his expression grim. "Spitz fought like hell," he observed, noting the severity of the wounds.

"And Buck fought like two hells," François corrected, a savage grin splitting his face. "Now we move. No more Spitz, no more trouble. We are cleared."

While Perrault methodically packed the camp gear and loaded the sled, François began the ritual of harnessing. Buck trotted instinctively to the vacant spot where Spitz had led, but François, oblivious to the shift in dynamics, secured Sol-leks—the remaining senior dog—in the lead position. In François's pragmatic assessment, Sol-leks was the finest candidate available. Instantly, Buck lunged, a surge of pure kinetic fury driving Sol-leks backward. With a single, authoritative movement, Buck planted himself in the leader's slot.

"Eh? Eh?" François roared, slapping his thighs in delighted disbelief. "Look at this beast! He kills Spitz, and now he thinks to take the command!"

"Get away, Chook!" François barked, reaching out to grab Buck by the scruff. Though the dog growled with a threat that vibrated in his chest, François hauled him aside and shoved Sol-leks back into place. The older dog hesitated, his tail tucked, clearly intimidated by the newcomer's dominance. François was stubborn, refusing to yield, but the moment he turned his back, Buck surged forward again, displacing Sol-leks without hesitation. The subordinate dog did not resist; he accepted the demotion.

Anger flared in François's eyes. "By Gar, I feel you!" he screamed, returning with a heavy club raised high.

Buck froze. The memory of the man in the red sweater flashed through his mind, triggering a primal caution. He retreated slowly, circling just beyond the effective range of the weapon. He snarled with bitter resentment, his hackles raised, yet his movements were calculated. He tracked the club's trajectory, dodging with the precision of a creature who had learned the lethal geometry of violence. François went about his work, calling for Buck to resume his post beside Dave. Buck stepped back. François advanced. Buck retreated further. This dance continued until François, convinced the animal feared a beating, tossed the club aside.

Mistake.

Buck was not fleeing punishment; he was demanding authority. It was his by right, earned through blood and battle. He would accept nothing less.

Perrault intervened. Together, the men herded Buck in a tight circle for nearly an hour, hurling clubs at him. He danced around them, evading every swing. They unleashed a torrent of curses—invectives directed at him, his lineage, and every descendant to come, cursing every hair on his body and drop of blood in his veins. Buck answered with a low, continuous snarl, staying just out of reach. He did not flee the camp; instead, he patrolled the perimeter, signaling clearly that once his demand was acknowledged, he would submit to their control.

François sat, scratching his head in confusion. Perrault checked his watch and swore. Time was slipping away; they should have been on the trail an hour prior. François shook his head, offering a sheepish grin to the courier, who shrugged in acknowledgment of their defeat. Finally, François approached Sol-leks and called to Buck. The wolf laughed—a sharp, barking sound unique to canines—and maintained his distance. François released Sol-leks's traces, moving him back to the rear. The team stood harnessed in a rigid line, ready for departure. There was no vacancy except at the front. François called again. Buck laughed, triumphant, and stayed away.

"Toss the club," Perrault ordered.

François complied. Immediately, Buck trotted forward, his laughter echoing in the crisp air, and swung into the lead position. Traces were fastened, the sled broken loose, and with both men sprinting alongside, they launched onto the river trail.

François had underestimated Buck's potential, believing his ferocity was limited to killing rivals. By midday, he realized he had vastly undervalued the wolf. At a single bound, Buck assumed the duties of leadership. Where judgment, rapid cognition, and decisive action were required, he proved superior even to Spitz, a feat François had never witnessed in his career.

Yet, Buck's true genius lay in enforcing the law and compelling his pack to adhere to it. Dave and Sol-leks accepted the transition without complaint; their sole purpose was toil, and they cared little for hierarchy provided their burden remained unchanged. Even Billee, the gentle giant, would lead if necessary, so long as order was preserved. However, the remainder of the team, unsettled by Spitz's recent decline, found themselves suddenly disciplined.

Pike, who habitually saved his strength, was shaken repeatedly for loafing. Before the first day ended, he was hauling weights far beyond his previous capacity. That first night, Joe, the sour-mouthed dog, received a thorough punishment—a correction Spitz had never successfully administered. Buck simply used his superior mass to smother Joe, biting and cutting until the larger dog ceased snapping and whined for mercy.

The team's cohesion snapped back instantly. They moved as a single organism, leaping in unison within the traces. At the Rink Rapids, two native huskies, Teek and Koona, joined the line. The speed with which Buck integrated them stole François's breath.

"Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!" François exclaimed, awe-struck. "Never! He is worth one thousand dollars, by Gar! What do you say, Perrault?"

Perrault nodded. They were already breaking records, gaining ground daily. The trail was pristine, hard-packed, and free of fresh snow. Temperatures hovered at fifty degrees below zero, a consistent chill that demanded endurance. Men rode and ran in rotation, keeping the dogs on the jump with minimal stops.

On the Thirty Mile River, partially frozen, they covered in a single day what had previously taken ten. In one explosive run, they raced sixty miles from the base of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids. Crossing Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of interconnected lakes—they flew so fast that the runner had to tow behind the sled at the end of a rope. By the final night of the second week, they crested White Pass and descended the steep sea slope, the lights of Skaguay and the shipping fleet visible below.

It was a record-breaking journey. For fourteen consecutive days, they averaged forty miles per day. Upon arrival in Skaguay, Perrault and François paraded through the main street, chests heaving, deluged with offers of drink while the team became the object of worship for crowds of dog-busters and mushers. Soon after, three or four notorious Western desperados attempted to raid the town, only to be riddled like pepper boxes. Public attention shifted elsewhere, followed quickly by official dispatches.

François summoned Buck, throwing his arms around the wolf's neck and weeping openly. That marked the end of François and Perrault's tenure. Like other transient figures, they faded from Buck's life forever.

A Scotch half-breed took command, leading Buck and his mates back over the weary trail to Dawson. This was no record run; it was heavy toil, laden with the mail train carrying news from the civilized world to the gold seekers huddled under the Arctic pole's shadow.

Buck disliked the monotony but adapted with the stoicism of Dave and Sol-leks. He ensured his mates performed their fair share, regardless of their personal feelings. Days blurred into identical cycles: cooks rising, fires lit, breakfast consumed, camps broken, dogs harnessed, and travel commencing before dawn. Nights were spent pitching tents, gathering wood, fetching water, and feeding the pack. For the dogs, the meal was the day's pinnacle, followed by an hour of loafing amidst the fifty-seven companions. Among them were fierce fighters, but three brief encounters with the strongest established Buck's absolute mastery. When he bristled and bared his teeth, they parted.

Most cherished, perhaps, was lying near the fire, hind legs tucked, forelegs extended, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Occasionally, memories of Judge Miller's sun-drenched estate in Santa Clara Valley surfaced—the cement pool, Ysabel the hairless Mexican, Toots the Japanese pug—but more often, he recalled the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the epic struggle with Spitz, and the flavors of food he craved. He was not homesick. The Sunland was a dim, distant abstraction; those memories held no sway. Instead, the instincts of his ancestry, dormant for years, quickened. These were not mere memories but ancestral habits waking to life.

Sometimes, crouching by the fire, the flames seemed to belong to another realm. In this vision, he saw a different man: shorter-legged, long-armed, with stringy, knotted muscles rather than rounded bulk. His hair was long and matted, his head slanted back, hiding his eyes. He made strange sounds, terrified of the encroaching darkness, clutching a stick weighted with a stone.
