Jeeves—my valet and occasional savior—is a marvel of efficiency. The man knows everything. If you asked him the capital of Patagonia or the best way to remove red wine from a white waistcoat, he’d have the answer before you finished the question. He’s like one of those railway information chaps who can rattle off train schedules while half-asleep.

Take the time I spotted my pal Monty Byng strolling down Bond Street in a sharp grey check suit. Envy hit me like a ton of bricks. I cornered him for the tailor’s details and had my own version underway within the hour.

That evening, I announced my sartorial triumph to Jeeves.

“I’m getting a check suit like Monty’s,” I declared.

“Ill-advised, sir,” he replied. “It will not suit you.”

“Nonsense! It’s the pinnacle of fashion.”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

The suit arrived. I put it on. One glance in the mirror nearly knocked me flat. Jeeves was right—I looked like a circus clown who’d lost a bet. Yet Monty had looked impeccable in the same fabric. Life’s unfair like that.

But Jeeves’s genius extends beyond fashion. Once, I stumbled upon a surefire tip for the Lincolnshire race.

“Jeeves,” I said, eager to share the wealth, “if you want to make a quick buck, back Wonderchild.”

He shook his head. “I would refrain, sir.”

“Why? It’s a guaranteed winner!”

“The horse is intended to place second, sir.”

I dismissed his warning as drivel. Naturally, Wonderchild led until the final stretch, only to be pipped by Banana Fritter. I slunk home, humbled.

“From now on,” I vowed, “I consult you before making any decisions.”

“Very good, sir.”

And so, when my friend Corky Corcoran turned up in my flat, dragging a doe-eyed girl named Muriel Singer behind him, my first instinct was to summon Jeeves.

“We’re engaged,” Corky blurted, “but my uncle will murder me if he finds out.”

Muriel gazed at me with pleading eyes. “We were hoping you might help us break the news gently.”

Corky’s uncle, Alexander Worple, was a tyrant with two passions: jute (whatever that is) and birds. Corky survived on a meager allowance by pretending to share his uncle’s ornithological obsession. The mere idea of Corky marrying an actress—Muriel was in the chorus of *Choose Your Exit*—would send Worple into orbit.

I pondered. Then it hit me.

“Leave it to Jeeves,” I said, ringing the bell.

Jeeves materialized, as always, at the perfect moment.

“Sir?”

“We need a scheme.” I outlined Corky’s predicament.

Jeeves steepled his fingers. “I propose Miss Singer authors a volume titled *The Children’s Book of American Birds*, dedicating it to Mr. Worple. A limited edition, privately published, with generous references to his own work. Upon receipt, he will be so charmed, he’ll insist on meeting her.”

Corky paled. “But Muriel can’t write!”

“A ghostwriter can be engaged for a modest fee,” Jeeves assured.

The plan was set in motion. A struggling scribe named Sam Patterson was hired, and soon, a glossy book arrived. It was a masterpiece of flattery, praising Worple’s expertise on everything from finches to cuckoos.

A few days later, Corky burst in, waving a letter. Worple was *delighted* to make Miss Singer’s acquaintance!

Months passed. I returned to New York and spotted Muriel dining alone.

“Where’s Corky?” I asked.

She blinked. “Oh, we’re not together anymore.”

“Had a row?”

“No, I’m married.”

Before I could react, a booming voice cut in. “Muriel, darling!”

I turned. There stood Alexander Worple, beaming.

“Meet my husband,” Muriel said.

I nearly fainted.

Back at my flat, I poured myself a stiff drink.

“Jeeves,” I groaned, “Muriel married the *uncle*.”

He didn’t bat an eye. “A foreseeable outcome, sir.”

“Foreseeable? You might have warned us!”

“I hesitated to overstep, sir.”

I avoided Corky for months, guilt-ridden. When I finally worked up the nerve to visit, I found him painting a severe-looking woman holding a baby.

“Ah—” I stammered, backing toward the door.

Corky waved me in. “Just finishing up.” He dismissed the nanny, then sighed.

“Life’s full of surprises, Bertie.”

I nodded. Some surprises, it seemed, were best left to Jeeves.

There’s a saying—probably by Shakespeare or some other sharp lad—that just when a chap’s feeling particularly spiffing, Fate creeps up behind him and bonks him on the noggin with a lead pipe. Truer words were never spoken, and my own case was no exception. Take the curious affair of Lady Malvern and her offspring, Wilmot. One moment, life was a bowl of cherries. The next, it was a bowl of something far less pleasant.

It all began on a sparkling morning, fresh from a cold shower, when I was feeling roughly two years old and full of beans. My mood was especially buoyant because, for once, I had stood my ground against Jeeves. Normally, the man oppresses me like a tyrannical governess. I didn’t mind so much when he made me ditch one of my new suits—his taste in tailoring is impeccable—but when he tried to veto my beloved cloth-topped boots, it was nearly the last straw. Then came the Great Hat Debate. He was all for the Longacre (as sported by John Drew), while I had set my heart on the Country Gentleman (favored by some other theatrical bloke). After a spirited tussle, I emerged victorious. Thus, as I stood there toweling my spine and humming a jaunty tune, I was practically radiating manly independence.

Then came the knock at the door.

“What ho, out there?”

“Lady Malvern wishes to see you, sir,” intoned Jeeves.

“Who?”

“Lady Malvern, sir. She is waiting in the sitting room.”

“Jeeves, old thing,” I said sternly, for I draw the line at practical jokes before breakfast, “you know perfectly well no one calls at this hour. The sun’s barely up!”

“Her ladyship mentioned arriving from an ocean liner at an ungodly hour, sir.”

Ah. That tracked. I recalled my own ghastly pre-dawn arrival in America the year prior, ejected onto foreign soil before the civilized world had stirred.

“Who *is* Lady Malvern, Jeeves?”

“She did not confide in me, sir.”

“Alone?”

“Accompanied by a Lord Pershore, sir. Presumably her son.”

“Right. Lay out something respectable. I suppose I must dress.”

“The heather-mixture lounge is prepared, sir.”

“Lead me to it.”

As I wrestled with my shirt studs, it struck me. Lady Malvern—a crony of my Aunt Agatha’s! I’d met her at some dismal London luncheon. A fearsome specimen. Wrote books. One about India after the Durbar, full of depressing facts.

Then came the snag.

“Pardon me, sir—not *that* tie.”

I stiffened. Hadn’t I just quelled this insubordination the night before? This was a test.

“What’s wrong with this tie? It’s a perfectly cheerful pink.”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

“Jeeves, this is *my* tie.”

“Very good, sir.”

A frosty silence. The man was wounded, but I stood firm. Tied the offending article, donned the rest, and marched into the sitting room.

“Ah! Mr. Wooster!” Lady Malvern boomed. She was one of those robust, overwhelming women who make a chap feel like a schoolboy caught filching jam. Tallish, but built like a steamer trunk, with bulging eyes, a haystack of yellow hair, and enough teeth to equip a small orchestra. Beside her sat Motty—pale, thin, and blinking like an owl in daylight. His hair, the same shade as his mother’s, was plastered down with mathematical precision. He had the air of a fellow who had been raised in a cupboard.

After the usual how-d’you-dos, Lady Malvern honeyed her way to the point.

“I should be *so* grateful if you’d take dear Motty in while I tour America. A month at most. He’s a quiet boy—vegetarian, teetotaler, devoted to reading.”

I shot Motty a glance. He was gumming his walking stick, utterly vacant. The notion of hosting this human barnacle sent a chill down my spine. But before I could protest, she steamrolled on, detailing her plans to inspect Sing-Sing prison and other cheerful landmarks. Then, with a final effusion of thanks, she swept out, leaving Motty’s luggage—and my doom—behind.

I summoned Jeeves.

“Jeeves! We’ve been saddled with a pill!”

“Pill, sir?”

“The excrescence! Lord Pershore!”

“Ah. Breakfast is ready, sir.”

The man was *frosty*. Still miffed about the tie, no doubt. Well, let him sulk.

Motty returned that evening—or rather, was *deposited* on the doormat by unseen hands. Jeeves and I hauled him inside. One whiff told the tale.

“Great Scott! He’s pickled!”

“Indeed, sir.”

The vegetarian teetotaler was stewed to the gills.

Next morning, however, he was chipper as a sparrow, praising Jeeves’s miraculous hangover cure and declaring his intent to “yield to temptation” nightly henceforth. And yield he did—with gusto. Night after night, he reeled home, having sampled every vice New York offered. I, meanwhile, withered under the strain, awaiting the inevitable reckoning with Aunt Agatha.

The final straw came when Motty acquired Rollo—a bull-terrier of uncertain temper, won in a raffle and tied to my furniture. The beast viewed me as a chew toy, reserving his Jeevesian admiration solely for the man himself.

It was all too much. Something had to give. And, as always, it was up to Jeeves to set it right—provided I could coax him out of his sartorial sulk.

One of life’s great mysteries, as I sip my morning tea and observe Jeeves in his natural habitat—tidying studs, arranging shirts, and generally making the world a better-dressed place—is how I’d manage if the fellow ever decided to hand in his notice. In London, the threat was constant: a swarm of predatory aristocrats lurked in the shadows, waving blank checks and whispering sweet nothings about superior trouser-pressing. Reggie Foljambe once tried to poach him with double wages, and Alistair Bingham-Reeves’ valet gazed upon Jeeves with the sort of hunger usually reserved for rare steak. Pirates, the lot of them.

But I digress. The point is, Jeeves is unnervingly competent. Even the way he inserts a shirt stud suggests a man who could, if pressed, solve world hunger between breakfast and lunch. So when dear old Bicky Bickersteth came hurtling into my life, knees-deep in familial bouillon, I knew Jeeves would sort it.

The crisis unfolded thus: Bicky’s uncle, the Duke of Chiswick—a man so thrifty he’d haggle with a lamppost—had booted him across the Atlantic with instructions to "learn ranching" in Colorado. Bicky, whose idea of animal husbandry was avoiding horses (vicious biters, the lot), had instead taken up residence in New York, fibbing to the duke about a lucrative "business wheeze." All was well until the duke announced his impending visit, forcing Bicky to either produce a ranch or a convincing lie.

Enter Jeeves, stage left.

“Might I suggest, sir,” he murmured, “that you lend Mr. Bickersteth this flat? His grace could be led to believe it is his nephew’s own establishment, with myself as Mr. Bickersteth’s valet. You would, of course, be merely a guest.”

A stroke of genius. The duke arrived, took one look at the lavish surroundings, and promptly canceled Bicky’s allowance on the grounds that "the boy was clearly rolling in it." Disaster.

Bicky, now facing financial ruin, briefly considered chicken farming (“Eggs, Bertie! Twenty-five cents per seven!”) before Jeeves unveiled Plan B: monetizing the duke himself.

“The American public, sir,” Jeeves explained, “has a peculiar enthusiasm for shaking hands with prominent personages. For a modest fee, say two or three dollars, one might arrange introductions.”

Bicky was skeptical. “You mean to tell me people would pay just to *meet* my uncle?”

“Undoubtedly, sir. My own aunt once paid five shillings to host a moving-picture actor for tea.”

Thus, the scheme was born. Unfortunately, the market proved sluggish. Our sole investor was a delicatessen owner who offered payment in ham. Then, salvation: Jeeves secured *eighty-seven* eager handshake enthusiasts from Birdsburg, Missouri.

How? I never asked. With Jeeves, it’s best not to question the machinery—just admire the results.

The duke, blissfully unaware he’d become a tourist attraction, shook hands with the masses. Bicky’s finances were restored. And I? I learned two valuable lessons:

1. Never underestimate the American appetite for nobility.
2. Always let Jeeves handle the details.

The moustache, by the way, was quietly retired. Some battles aren’t worth fighting.

I want to tell you about my old friend Bobbie Cardew. It’s a cautionary tale—one of those stories that makes you nod sagely and say, *"There but for the grace of God go I."* If you’re a man, take heed. If you’re a woman, well, you’ll probably enjoy watching a fellow human being hoist by his own petard.

Now, Bobbie wasn’t always the forgetful sort—or rather, he was, but in those days it was charming. A lovable scatterbrain, the kind of chap who’d lose his head if it weren’t fastened on. If you wanted him for dinner, you’d need to send a telegram, a letter, and a messenger in a taxi just to be sure. And even then, half the time he’d already left town.

Then he got married.

Marriage, as they say, changes a man. In Bobbie’s case, it turned him from an affable dolt into a man perpetually one misstep away from disaster. His wife, Mary, was a hospital nurse—practical, sharp, and possessed of a will that could bend steel. She adored him, mind you, but she also expected him to *remember things*. A fatal flaw in their otherwise picturesque union.

The trouble began with their first anniversary. Bobbie, in his usual fashion, forgot it entirely. Not just forgot to buy flowers—forgot the *date itself*. He’d arranged tickets for the opera, a nice dinner, the whole romantic works. But when the evening arrived, he wandered home with no recollection of the occasion until Mary’s icy stare jogged his memory.

You’d think that would’ve been lesson enough. But no. Bobbie’s memory was like a sieve with particularly large holes. Birthdays, errands, promises—all slipped through unnoticed. Mary, being both patient and terrifying, endured it. Until she didn’t.

One morning, Bobbie burst into my club like a man pursued by wolves.

*"She’s gone!"* he gasped.

*"Who?"* I asked, though I knew.

The note was brief and brutal:

> *"Dear Bobbie—I’ve gone away. When you remember my birthday, I’ll come back. Until then, you’ll find me at Box 341, London Morning News."*

Bobbie stared at me, wild-eyed. *"I don’t know her birthday."*

There followed a frantic detective saga—checking theater programs, rifling through old receipts, even consulting astrological guides (*"February women are determined and expect full return from their companions"*—which, frankly, described Mary perfectly). At last, by sheer dumb luck (and a forgotten cheque to the Ritz), we pinned it down: May 8th.

I rang Mary at her hotel.

*"He’s remembered,"* I said.

A pause. Then, softly: *"Was he very worried?"*

I grinned. *"Worried? My dear woman, he’s been in such a state that if anxiety were currency, he’d own the Bank of England."*

Silence. Then the faintest laugh. *"Tell him… tell him I’ll be home tomorrow."*

And that, dear reader, is how Bobbie Cardew learned the hard way that even the most absent-minded of men must, on occasion, *remember*.

I don’t mean to bore you with all the usual rot, but I simply must tell you about poor old Freddie Meadowes. Now, I’m no literary genius—I leave that to the chaps who get paid for it—but I’ll do my best, and you can always imagine some polished scribbler tidying it up later.

Freddie’s been a dear old pal of mine since the days when we used to pinch each other’s sweets at school. So you can imagine my shock when I walked into the club one morning and found him slumped in a corner, staring into space like the last surviving guest at a particularly dismal party. Normally, Freddie’s the life and soul of any gathering—a real spark, always good for a laugh. But there he was, looking as if he’d just been informed that the last bottle of champagne in the world had gone flat.

Jimmy Pinkerton—a frightfully brainy chap who writes plays—was with me at the time, and between us, we pried the truth out of Freddie. It was, of course, a girl. Angela West, the poor blighter’s fiancée, had handed him his hat after some mysterious row. Wouldn’t see him, wouldn’t speak to him, wouldn’t even open his letters. A dashed unpleasant situation.

Now, I knew exactly how Freddie felt. I’d once been hopelessly gone on a girl named Elizabeth Shoolbred, who wouldn’t have looked at me if I’d been the last man on earth—a tragedy I’ll cover in my memoirs if I ever get round to writing them. So I prescribed the only cure I knew.

“What you need, old bean,” I said, “is a change of scenery. Come down to Marvis Bay with me. I’ve rented a cottage. Jimmy’s joining us on the twenty-fourth. It’ll be a jolly little party.”

Jimmy nodded sagely. “Change of scene’s the ticket. Knew a fellow once—girl chucked him, so he went abroad. Two months later, she wired, *‘Come back. Muriel.’* Chap sat down to reply, then realized he’d forgotten her surname. Never answered.”

Freddie, however, remained inconsolable. He just sat there looking like a man who’d swallowed a bad oyster. Still, I managed to drag a promise out of him to come to Marvis Bay. He said it didn’t matter where he was, so he might as well be miserable by the seaside.

Marvis Bay, in Dorsetshire, isn’t exactly a hotspot of excitement. You bathe, you lounge on the sand, and in the evening, you take a stroll along the shore with the local gnats, who treat you like a mobile banquet. By nine o’clock, you’re slapping ointment on your wounds and collapsing into bed.

Oddly enough, the place seemed to suit Freddie. Once the moon was up, you couldn’t drag him off that beach with a team of wild horses. The gnats adored him—they’d queue up for a chance to feast on him, ignoring perfectly good passersby just to get a taste of Freddie.

It was all very peaceful, but by the end of the first week, I was wishing Jimmy Pinkerton had arranged to come down earlier. Freddie, bless him, wasn’t exactly lively company. When he wasn’t chewing his pipe and scowling at the carpet, he was hunched over the piano, massacring *“The Rosary”* with one finger. He never got past the third bar before something went wrong, and he’d start all over again.

One morning, as I returned from a swim, he was at it again.

“Reggie,” he said hollowly, looking up, “I’ve seen her.”

“Seen her? Miss West?”

“At the post office. We met in the doorway. She cut me dead.”

He attacked *“The Rosary”* again, stumbling in the second bar.

“Reggie, you never should have brought me here. I must leave.”

“Leave? Don’t be absurd. This is the best thing that could’ve happened. Now’s your chance to show some backbone.”

“She *cut* me.”

“Never mind that. Be a sportsman. Try again.”

“She looked straight through me!”

“Of course she did. But leave it to me. I’ll handle this. What you need,” I said, “is to put her under some obligation to you. Make her grateful. What you want—”

“But *what* is she going to be grateful for?”

I pondered.

“Save her from drowning,” I suggested.

“I can’t swim,” said Freddie.

Typical. A splendid chap in many ways, but utterly useless in a crisis.

He went back to torturing the piano, and I made a strategic retreat to the beach.

As I wandered along the shore, turning the problem over in my mind, I spotted a blue dress in the distance. By Jove, it was *her*—Angela West. I’d never met her, but Freddie’s bedroom was practically wallpapered with her photographs, so I recognized her at once. She was sitting on the sand, helping a small, round child build a castle. An elderly lady—presumably an aunt—was reading nearby.

The child soon grew bored and started whimpering. Angela took him off to a sweets stall, and I continued my walk.

Now, I’ll admit, I’m not what you’d call a mental giant. The Peppers have never been famed for their intellect. But every now and then, I get a dashed clever idea—and one struck me then. I doubt even the brainiest chap you could name would’ve thought of it.

On my way back, I saw the child again, this time alone, poking a jellyfish with a spade. No Angela in sight. And then—*inspiration*. If I *borrowed* the child for a bit, and then Freddie “found” him wandering lost and returned him heroically, Angela’s gratitude might just melt her resolve.

So I scooped up the child and made for home, already picturing the touching reunion.

Freddie, however, was slow to grasp the brilliance of the scheme. When I marched in with the kid, he didn’t exactly cheer. The child was howling by then, and Freddie looked as if he’d prefer to be swallowed by the nearest whale.

“Stop it!” he snapped. “Do you think you’re the only one with troubles? What the devil is this, Reggie?”

I fetched honey to quiet the child, then explained my plan. Gradually, Freddie saw the light.

“You’re not *always* a complete idiot, Reggie,” he conceded. “This might work.”

He took the child to “find” Angela on the beach.

I was feeling frightfully pleased with myself—until Freddie returned, looking like a man who’d just been told his entire family had emigrated to Australia. The child was still with him.

“Couldn’t you find her?” I asked.

“Oh, I found her,” he said bitterly.

“Well then—?”

“This isn’t her cousin, you *imbecile*. She’d never seen the child before in her life.”

“*What?* Then whose is he?”

“How should I know? But congratulations—you’re now a kidnapper. I hope you enjoy prison.”

It took him a while to tell the full story, what with all the insults he kept throwing in. Angela had listened to Freddie’s heroic tale of rescuing the lost child, then given him a look that could have frozen the Atlantic. He’d slunk away, thoroughly crushed.

Now it was *my* problem. I spent hours combing Marvis Bay for the child’s parents, with no success. Eventually, the sweet-stall man informed me that the boy was named Tootles Medwin and lived at *Ocean Rest* in Beach Road.

I raced there, knocked, and got no answer. Then a whiskered face appeared at an upper window.

“Hi!” it shouted.

“What do you mean, *‘Hi’*?” I said.

“You can’t come in. Mumps quarantine. Hello, Tootles!”

After some confused shouting, the man—Mr. Medwin—explained that his daughter had mumps, and Tootles couldn’t risk infection. He insisted I take the child until his brother arrived from London to fetch him—which *might* be within a week.

Before I could protest, a package of breakfast cereal hit me on the head, and the window slammed shut. Defeated, I led Tootles away.

On the way back, we met Angela.

“Wah!” said Tootles, pointing.

She smiled. “So father found you again?”

*Father?!* Before I could correct her, she was gone.

Freddie, when informed, had a small nervous breakdown.

“We can’t spend our lives as nursemaids!” he wailed.

But we had no choice. The next few days were a blur of sweets, tantrums, and desperate improvisation.

Then Jimmy Pinkerton arrived, took one look at the chaos, and declared it perfect material for a farce.

“Parted lovers reunited by a lisping child!” he said. “It’s gold! All we need is for the kid to say something like *‘Kiss Freddie!’* and the girl will melt.”

The only problem? Getting Angela within earshot.

Fate, however, took care of that. The very next day, as I was leading Tootles outside, Angela appeared. Before I could stop her, she was on the veranda, cooing over the child—while Freddie obliviously massacred *“The Rosary”* inside.

“Your piano’s being tuned?” she asked. “Could your man come to us next?”

“Er—not yet!” I said. “Artistic temperament, you know.”

Just then, the music stopped.

“He’ll be out in a moment,” she said, settling in to wait.

I was weighing the merits of bolting versus feigning sudden illness when Tootles, bless him, seized his moment.

“Kiss Freddie!” he piped up.

Jimmy’s rehearsals had paid off.

Angela blinked. Freddie, emerging from the house, froze.

And then—well, you can guess the rest.

Somehow, against all odds, it *worked*.

One of the most absurd predicaments I’ve ever stumbled into—and given my habit of meddling in other people’s affairs, that’s saying something—was the whole George Lattaker debacle in Monte Carlo. I wouldn’t dream of boring you, but really, it’s too good not to share.

We’d arrived in Monte Carlo aboard the *Circe*, the yacht of an old sport named Marshall. The party included myself, my valet Voules, Mrs. Vanderley, her daughter Stella, Mrs. Vanderley’s maid Pilbeam, and George.

George was an old chum of mine, and in fact, I was the one who’d wrangled him an invite. You see, George was due to meet his Uncle Augustus, who, now that George had turned twenty-five, was supposed to hand over a tidy legacy left by one of George’s aunts. The aunt had passed away when George was just a tot, so this payday had been circled on his calendar for years. An income is all well and good, but a lump sum? That’s *real* money. Uncle Augustus was in Monte Carlo and had initially planned to come to London to settle up, but I’d convinced George it’d be far more amusing to meet the old boy there instead—combine business with pleasure, so to speak. So George had tagged along, and when the trouble started, we were anchored in Monaco Harbour, with Uncle Augustus due the next day.

Looking back, I’d say the whole mess began at the ungodly hour of seven in the morning, when I was ripped from blissful slumber by a proper row outside my stateroom. The main players were a female voice wailing, *“Oh, Harold!”* and a male voice bellowing in a manner most un-Voules-like. Normally, Voules speaks as if he’s been carved from marble, but in private, apparently, he lets loose. And at that hour, I was in no mood for theatrics.

*“Voules!”* I roared.

The noise stopped dead. A moment of silence, then fading sobs, and finally, a polite knock. Voules entered, looking as composed as if he’d just announced, *“My lord, the carriage awaits.”* You’d never guess he’d just been in the middle of a melodrama.

*“Voules,”* I said, *“are you under the impression I’m about to be crowned May Queen? Seven o’clock is *not* the hour for domestic upheavals.”*

I gaped. Voules, engaged? It was like discovering a statue had taken up tap dancing. But sensing I’d stumbled into private grief, I changed tack.

*“I think I’ll rise,”* I declared.

So I breakfasted in solitude, then wandered up on deck to smoke. The morning was postcard-perfect: blue sea, glittering Casino, cloudless sky—the whole picturesque lot. Gradually, the others emerged. Stella Vanderley was among the first. She looked a bit peaky, admitting she hadn’t slept well. No surprise there—without eight solid hours, one’s a wreck.

*“Seen George?”* I asked.

Something in her expression frosted over. Odd, given that she and George had been thick as thieves the entire voyage. I’d half-expected George to burst in any moment, crowing, *“It’s happened, old bean! She loves me!”*

*“I haven’t,”* she said coolly.

I let it drop. George’s stock was clearly plummeting.

The next act opened when the morning papers arrived. Mrs. Vanderley let out a shriek.

*“Shocking business,”* muttered old Marshall.

*“I knew him in Vienna,”* Mrs. Vanderley sighed. *“He waltzed divinely.”*

I scanned my own paper and pieced it together: His Serene Highness the Prince of Saxburg-Leignitz (why *serene*, I’ll never know) had been brutally assaulted the night before in a shadowy street near the Casino. The Prince, apparently fond of solo strolls, had been ambushed, thrashed senseless, and left in the gutter until a passerby found him and hauled him back to his yacht, where he remained unconscious.

*“This won’t end well for someone,”* I mused. *“What’s the penalty for roughing up royalty? Worse than clocking a bobby, I’d wager.”*

Old Marshall read aloud: *“‘The pedestrian who discovered His Serene Highness has been identified as Mr. Denman Sturgis, the renowned private investigator. Mr. Sturgis has offered his services to the police and is understood to possess a critical clue.’ That’s the chap who cracked the Chicago kidnapping case. If anyone can nab the culprit, it’s him.”*

Five minutes later, a boat hailed us. A tall, gaunt man climbed aboard, scanned the group, and zeroed in on Marshall.

I trotted below, knocked on George’s door. No answer. I peeked inside—empty. The bed hadn’t been slept in.

*“He’s vanished,”* I reported.

*“Gone ashore, perhaps,”* Marshall said. *“He’ll be back for breakfast. Join us?”*

The stranger agreed. As the gong sounded, they filed below, leaving me alone.

I was halfway through a cigar when a hoarse whisper nearly made me jump overboard.

I turned. There, disheveled, hatless, and coated in dust, stood George in evening dress.

He collapsed into my chair, eyes shut. The man looked like he’d been through a wringer.

*“I say—”* I began, tapping his shoulder.

He shot up with a yelp. *“Don’t *do* that! My nerves are in shreds!”*

*“*What?*”*

*“Approximately two minutes. Proposed at ten, accepted, was about to kiss her when that confounded maid Pilbeam interrupted. Have you ever been accepted by the woman you love, Reggie?”*

*“Then you can’t fathom the euphoria. I was *bursting*. Had to kiss the nearest thing at hand. Might’ve been the ship’s cat. Wasn’t. It was Pilbeam.”*

*“Precisely. She broke it off. I rowed ashore in a frenzy, drowned my sorrows, got into a brawl in some alley—next thing I knew, I woke up in the Casino gardens. Hat’s gone.”*

I thrust the paper at him.

He paled. *“This is dire.”*

*“You didn’t *actually* attack the Prince?”*

*“That’s not the point! They’ve got a *clue*.”*

*“My *hat*, Reggie. Must’ve lost it in the scuffle. Sturgis probably found it—it’s got my *name* inside!”*

*“George, you need to *bolt*.”*

Just then, Voules materialized with a letter.

Once Voules left, George emerged. The letter was from Uncle Augustus:

*“My dear George—Prepare for a curious twist. Your twin Alfred, presumed dead, has surfaced in Buenos Aires. He’s en route to meet me. This may delay your inheritance until we verify his claim.”*

A lightbulb blazed in my brain.

*“George, this is your *out*.”*

*“You’ll *be* Alfred. Twins are identical—who’ll know? Your uncle can vouch for you. Just play the part long enough for the detective to clear off.”*

George’s eyes lit up. *“Genius!”*

Then Voules coughed behind us.

Negotiations ensued. After some haggling—Voules drove a hard bargain—we secured his silence for £100 upfront and another £100 upon our safe departure.

George transformed into Alfred, complete with a fictional mole (“*You’d have noticed if George had one, right?*”), and charmed Stella into forgiving “George.”

But then Uncle Augustus arrived—except he wasn’t.

*“There is no uncle,”* George groaned. *“No Alfred. No money. He’s spent it all, fled to South America, and left me to impersonate a twin while a detective hunts me.”*

I sighed. *“Old chap, things are in a bit of a pickle.”*

*“You don’t say,”* muttered George.

Now that the dust has settled on the whole Rockmetteller Todd affair, I must confess there was a moment when I feared even Jeeves’s formidable intellect might falter. The man appeared—dare I say it?—stumped.

For those unacquainted with the dynamic duo that is Bertram Wooster and his gentleman’s personal gentleman, a brief introduction: Jeeves, in the strictest technical sense, is employed to press my trousers, mix my cocktails, and perform other such essential duties. In practice, however, he functions more as a hybrid of oracle, life-preserver, and occasional moral compass. I lean on him as heavily as a chap with a sprained ankle leans on an ebony walking stick.

So when Rocky Todd burst into my boudoir one spring dawn, gibbering about his aunt, I naturally enlisted Jeeves from the jump.

The Rocky Todd debacle erupted at an hour so uncivilized that even the larks were still hitting the snooze button. There I was, submerged in blissful slumber, when the bedroom door flew open, a bony finger jabbed my ribs, and the mattress began shuddering like a nervous thoroughbred. After some vigorous eyelid flapping, I identified the assailant as Rocky—which, given the circumstances, struck me as wildly improbable.

You see, Rocky resided in some bucolic Long Island backwater, miles from the nearest hint of civilization. Moreover, he had repeatedly assured me—with the pride of a man boasting a unique talent—that he never rolled out of bed before noon, and 1 p.m. was more his style. By disposition, Rocky was the laziest organism in America, a title he defended with the tenacity of a champion. His chosen vocation? Poet. Or, more accurately, he wrote poetry on the rare occasions he did anything at all. The bulk of his existence was spent in a sort of vegetative trance. He once told me he could spend hours watching a worm and pondering its life choices.

His routine was exquisitely calibrated. Once a month, he’d rouse himself for three days to scribble verse; the remaining 329 were devoted to horizontal meditation. I’d never imagined poetry could sustain a chap, even one living on Rocky’s austere plane, but it turns out American editors will pay handsomely for inspirational drivel—provided you avoid rhymes like the plague. Rocky once showed me one of his creations. It began:

*Be! Be! The past is dead. To-morrow is not born. Be to-day! To-day! Be with every nerve, With every muscle, With every drop of your red blood! Be!*

It was printed in a magazine opposite a picture of a muscle-bound fellow leering at the sunrise, encircled by a decorative scroll. Rocky claimed it earned him a hundred dollars, which financed a month of lying in bed till teatime.

Financially, his future was secure, thanks to a wealthy aunt in Illinois. Being her only nephew—and christened Rockmetteller in her honor—his inheritance was as certain as taxes and hangovers. He once confided that upon receiving his fortune, he intended to abandon labor entirely, save for the occasional poem urging the youth of America to light a pipe and prop their feet on the mantel.

And *this* was the human sloth currently jabbing me awake at an hour better suited to vampires!

“Read this, Bertie!” he yelped, brandishing a letter under my nose. “Wake up and digest this horror!”

Reading before tea and cigarettes is beyond my powers. I groped for the bell.

Jeeves materialized, looking as fresh as a daisy (how he manages it remains one of life’s mysteries).

“Tea, Jeeves.”

“Very good, sir.”

He glided out—Jeeves doesn’t walk so much as *ooze*—while Rocky resumed waving the letter like a flag at a revolution.

“What *is* it?” I groaned.

“Read it!”

“Can’t. Tea first.”

“Then *listen*.”

“Who’s it from?”

“My aunt.”

At this point, I drifted back to sleep. I was roused by Rocky’s despairing wail:

“So what the devil am I supposed to *do*?”

Jeeves re-entered with the tray, serene as a swan, and clarity dawned.

“Read it again, Rocky,” I instructed. “Jeeves must hear it. Mr. Todd’s aunt has penned a rather peculiar epistle, Jeeves, and we require your counsel.”

“Very good, sir.”

He stood at attention, radiating quiet competence, and Rocky began:

*“MY DEAR ROCKMETTELLER.—After much reflection, I’ve concluded it was remiss of me to delay so long in doing what I now resolve to do.”*

“Thoughts, Jeeves?”

“The opening is enigmatic, sir, but no doubt all will be revealed.”

“It’s revealed as pure insanity!” Rocky snapped.

“Continue, old thing,” I said, attacking the toast.

*“You know how I’ve yearned to experience New York’s glittering life. Alas, my health makes this impossible. I am old, frail, and devoid of vitality.”*

“Tragic, Jeeves, no?”

“Most poignant, sir.”

“Poignant my foot!” Rocky said. “She’s as healthy as a horse. Her doctor told me so last Christmas. But she’s convinced a trip to New York would kill her, so she stays put, even though it’s been her lifelong dream.”

“Like the fellow whose heart was ‘in the Highlands a-chasing of the deer,’ Jeeves?”

“Apt, sir.”

“Onward, Rocky.”

*“So I’ve decided that if I cannot enjoy the city myself, you shall do it for me. Inspired by a touching poem about a man who achieved his dream too late, I’ve arranged for a New York law firm to pay you a generous monthly allowance—on one condition: You must live in New York and live *my* dream. Be my proxy. Immerse yourself in its dazzling nightlife. Attend supper parties. And—this is non-negotiable—write me weekly letters detailing everything you do, so I may live vicariously through you. No detail is too small.—Your affectionate Aunt, ISABEL ROCKMETTELLER.”*

Rocky stared at me, wild-eyed. “Well?”

“Well?” I echoed.

“What am I to *do*?”

Only then did I grasp the full absurdity of his distress. Here was a man suddenly handed a fortune on a silver platter, and he was behaving like a condemned prisoner.

“Aren’t you *pleased*?” I said.

“Pleased! This is a death sentence!”

“If I were you, I’d be doing cartwheels.”

He let out a sound like a stepped-on terrier.

“Cartwheels! To be *forced* into New York? To abandon my peaceful cottage for some stifling, overpriced hovel? To endure nightly mobs of hysterical revelers who mistake noise for joy? I *loathe* this city, Bertie. I’d never set foot here if editors didn’t lurk in its shadows. It’s a moral cesspool! The thought of staying here *indefinitely* makes me ill. And you call this *good news*?”

I felt like one of Job’s less patient friends. I’d had no idea Rocky could muster such eloquence.

“New York would *kill* me,” he ranted. “To breathe the same air as six million people! To wear *stiff collars*! To—” He gasped. “Good Lord! I’d have to *dress for dinner* every night! It’s monstrous!”

I was scandalized.

“My dear fellow!” I said sternly.

“Do *you* dress for dinner nightly, Bertie?”

“Jeeves,” I said icily. “How many evening suits do I own?”

“Three full evening dress suits, sir; two dinner jackets—”

“*Three*.”

“For practical purposes, two, sir. The third remains unwearable. We also possess seven white waistcoats.”

“Shirts?”

“Four dozen, sir.”

“White ties?”

“The first two shallow shelves in the wardrobe are devoted exclusively to them, sir.”

I turned to Rocky. “You see?”

He convulsed like a stepped-on accordion.

“I *won’t* do it! I *can’t*! I’ll be *damned* if I’ll live like that! Most days, I don’t change out of pyjamas till five, and then it’s just a sweater!”

Jeeves winced—a rare crack in his professional armor.

“Then what’s the plan?” I asked.

“That’s what *you’re* for!”

“You could write and explain.”

“And have her sprint to her lawyer to cut me out? No, thank you.”

Jeeves cleared his throat delicately.

“The crux, sir, is that Mr. Todd must supply his aunt with detailed letters about his New York experiences. If he remains in the country, this necessitates a proxy to gather said experiences and relay them for him to, ah, *embellish*.”

Rocky blinked. “Translate, Bertie.”

“Simple! Find someone to gallivant around New York for you, take notes, and you’ll spin them into letters. Correct, Jeeves?”

“Precisely, sir.”

Hope ignited in Rocky’s eyes. He gaped at Jeeves like a man witnessing a miracle.

“But *who*?”

“Jeeves!” I said. “Let Jeeves do it.”

“Would he?”

“You’re game, aren’t you, Jeeves?”

For the first time in our association, I swear I saw the ghost of a smile flicker across his face.

“It would be a pleasure, sir. I’ve already sampled some of New York’s attractions on my evenings off. The task would be most agreeable.”

“Splendid! Your aunt wants cabarets, Rocky. Jeeves, start with Reigelheimer’s on Forty-second.”

Jeeves shook his head.

“Regrettably, sir, Reigelheimer’s is passé. The current vogue is Frolics on the Roof.”

I grinned. “Trust Jeeves. He *knows*.”

Happiness reigned. Jeeves was in his element, flexing his brain and basking in Broadway’s glow. I spotted him one night at the Midnight Revels, sipping champagne and scribbling notes like a society columnist. Rocky was content, back in his pastoral idyll. And Aunt Isabel? Thrilled. Her letters brimmed with excitement.

Rocky’s fabricated dispatches were masterpieces. Where I might scrawl:

Rocky produced epics:

*“Dearest Aunt,—Fifth Avenue dazzles! The gowns! Last night, we dined at the new Forty-third Street hotspot, then to the Revels. Georgie Cohan had us in stitches! Fred Stone popped in, Doug Fairbanks performed acrobatics, and Diamond Jim held court. Enclosed: a programme. Tomorrow, Frolics on the Roof!”*

I congratulated Jeeves.

“A triumph! How do you *notice* so much?”

“Merely habit, sir.”

And then—disaster.

One afternoon, as I lounged post-prandially, Jeeves materialized, uttering the two words every Wooster dreads:

“Miss Rockmetteller.”

In marched a battleship of a woman.

I gaped. The notion of Rocky’s aunt *leaving* Illinois was as plausible as a penguin in Piccadilly. She looked about as “frail” as a steamroller.

“Afternoon,” I croaked.

“Mr. Cohan?”

“Afraid not. Wooster. Bertie Wooster.”

Her face fell. “Where’s Rockmetteller?”

Panic! I couldn’t very well say, “Watching earthworms in his pajamas.”

Jeeves coughed discreetly.

“Mr. Todd motored out with friends this afternoon, sir. He anticipates a late return.”

“Right! Very late. Possibly dawn.”

Jeeves evaporated. The aunt sat, eyeing me like a suspicious tuna.

“You’re *very* at home here. Are you and Rockmetteller *close*?”

“Like brothers!”

She frowned. “You’d need to be, to treat his flat like your own.”

I was stunned. Me—an *intruder*?

Tea was my Hail Mary.

“Tea?” she repeated, as if I’d offered hemlock.

“Capital after traveling! Revives the spirits!”

I fled to Jeeves.

“She thinks this is *Rocky’s flat*!”

“A natural assumption, given the letters’ return address, sir.”

I groaned. “And she *loathes* me! We must summon Rocky *immediately*.”

“Already done, sir. I took the liberty of wiring him.”

“Jeeves, you’re *superhuman*.”

“Thank you, sir. Toast with the tea?”

I returned to find her still bolt upright, gripping her umbrella like a weapon.

“Surprise visit, what?” I ventured.

“Why surprising?”

“Oh, not at all! Just—unexpected!”

Jeeves brought tea. She sipped, shuddered, and fixed me with a glare.

“You *expect* me to drink this?”

“Bucks you up!”

“*What* does that mean?”

“Invigorates!”

“You’re English, aren’t you?”

The horror had only just begun.
