The days that followed were marked by a heavy, suspended silence. Willoughby neither visited nor wrote, leaving Elinor with no cause to regret her decision to seek her mother's counsel. By the week's end, the family was summoned to attend Lady Middleton at a gathering, an event from which Mrs. Jennings was excused due to the illness of her youngest child. Marianne, utterly despondent, cared little for her appearance or the propriety of attendance; she seemed equally indifferent to going or staying. After tea, she sat by the drawing-room fire, rigid and unchanging, lost in a private torment that rendered her blind to Elinor's presence. Only when they were summoned to the door did she start as if waking from a trance, having momentarily forgotten the world outside.

Upon arriving at the destination, they navigated the crowded carriages and ascended the stairs into a hall resplendent with light and stifling heat. The room was packed, a suffocating sea of society. After offering the requisite courtesies to the hostess, they were swept into the throng, adding to the oppressive atmosphere. While Lady Middleton engaged in a game of Cassino, Marianne, lacking the spirit to move, and Elinor, fortunate in securing nearby seats, settled themselves close to the table.

It was not long before Elinor spotted Willoughby, standing a few yards away in animated conversation with a fashionable young woman. He caught her glance and bowed, yet made no attempt to approach Marianne, despite clearly seeing her, and continued his discourse. Elinor turned instinctively to Marianne, hoping the sight would pass unnoticed. It was then that Marianne first laid eyes upon him. Her face flushed with sudden, overwhelming joy, she would have rushed toward him had not her sister's hand firmly restrained her.

"Good heavens!" Marianne cried out, her composure shattering. "He is there—he is there! Why does he not look at me? Why cannot I speak to him?"

"Be composed, pray," Elinor urged, her voice tight with anxiety. "Do not reveal your feelings to everyone here. Perhaps he has not yet seen you."

But this was a lie Marianne could not believe, nor could she summon the strength to accept. She sat in an agony of impatience that twisted every feature of her face. Finally, Willoughby turned and regarded them both. Marianne sprang up, calling his name with affectionate fervor, and extended her hand. He approached, addressing Elinor rather than Marianne, seemingly determined to avoid her gaze, and inquired hurriedly after Mrs. Dashwood and their time in London. Elinor, robbed of her wits by the shock, stood speechless. Her sister's emotions, however, poured forth immediately. Her face burned crimson as she exclaimed, "Good God, Willoughby! What is the meaning of this? Have you received my letters? Will you not shake hands with me?"

He could not evade the contact, yet her touch seemed painful to him; he held her hand for only a fleeting moment. Throughout the encounter, he appeared to be struggling desperately to maintain his composure. Elinor watched his face, noting a gradual return to tranquility. After a brief pause, he spoke calmly.

"I did myself the honor of calling in Berkeley Street last Tuesday," he said, "but regretted greatly that I was not fortunate enough to find you and Mrs. Jennings at home. I trust my card was not lost."

"But have you not received my notes?" Marianne shrieked, her anxiety wild and frantic. "There must be some mistake—some dreadful mistake! What can be the meaning of it? Tell me, Willoughby; for heaven's sake, tell me what is wrong?"

He offered no reply. His complexion drained, and his embarrassment returned in full force. Yet, as if sensing the gaze of the young lady with whom he had been speaking, he felt compelled to exert himself. Recovering his poise, he replied, "Yes, I had the pleasure of receiving the information of your arrival in town, which you were so good as to send me," before turning hastily away with a slight bow to join his friend.

Marianne, now looking dreadfully pale and unable to stand, sank back into her chair. Elinor, fearing she might faint, shielded her from the prying eyes of the crowd while administering lavender water to revive her.

"Go to him, Elinor!" Marianne cried the moment she could speak. "Force him to come to me! Tell him I must see him again—must speak to him instantly. I cannot rest; I shall have no peace until this is explained. Some dreadful misunderstanding must exist. Oh, go to him this very moment."

"How can that be done? No, my dearest Marianne, you must wait. This is not the place for explanations. Wait only till tomorrow."

With great difficulty, Elinor prevented her from following him herself. To persuade Marianne to check her agitation, to wait with the appearance of composure until she could speak to him privately and effectively, was impossible. Marianne continued to yield to the misery of her feelings, murmuring exclamations of wretchedness. Soon, Elinor saw Willoughby leave the room via the staircase. Telling Marianne he was gone, she urged the impossibility of another meeting that evening as a final plea for her to remain calm. Instantly, Marianne begged Elinor to entreat Lady Middleton to take them home, claiming she was too miserable to stay another minute.

Though Lady Middleton was mid-game, she was too polite to object to Marianne's distress. Making over her cards to a friend, the sisters departed as soon as a carriage could be secured. Not a word was spoken on the journey back to Berkeley Street. Marianne was in a silent agony, too overwhelmed even for tears. Fortunately, Mrs. Jennings had not yet returned, allowing them to go straight to their room, where hartshorn helped restore Marianne to herself. She was quickly undressed and tucked into bed, desiring solitude. Elinor left her to wait for Mrs. Jennings, finding time in the quiet to reflect on the day's events.

She could not doubt that an engagement had once existed between Willoughby and Marianne, nor that Willoughby was now weary of it. However Marianne might still nourish her own hopes, Elinor could not attribute such behavior to mere mistake or misapprehension. Nothing short of a thorough change of sentiment could explain it. Her indignation would have been far greater had she not witnessed the evident embarrassment that spoke of a consciousness of his own misconduct; this prevented her from believing him so unprincipled as to have toyed with her sister's affections from the start without a design worthy of investigation. Absence might have weakened his regard, and convenience might have driven him to overcome it, but that such a regard had once existed was a truth she could not bring herself to deny.

As for Marianne, reflecting on the pain such an unhappy meeting must have caused, and the even severer suffering that awaited her in its probable consequences, filled Elinor with the deepest concern. Her own situation seemed preferable in comparison; while she might be separated from Edward in the future, she could still esteem him, and her mind would always find support. But every circumstance seemed to unite to heighten Marianne's misery, sealing her fate in a final separation from Willoughby—an immediate and irreconcilable rupture.
