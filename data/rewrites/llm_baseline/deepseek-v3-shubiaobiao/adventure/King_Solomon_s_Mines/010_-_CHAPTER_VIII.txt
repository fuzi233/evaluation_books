The sun hung low in the sky as we marched along the ancient roadway, its stonework worn smooth by countless generations. The path curved ever northwest, flanked by towering cliffs carved with markings whose origins even the wisest among the Kukuanas could not decipher. Infadoos walked beside me, his voice low with reverence as he spoke of his people’s past.

“This road was old before our fathers’ fathers drew breath,” he said. “No man alive knows its makers, not even Gagool, who has whispered secrets since the days of the first kings.”

I glanced at the hieroglyphs etched into the rock. “And these writings?”

“The same hands that laid the stones left their mark. We do not read them. They are as forgotten as the men who carved them.”

The land itself was a marvel—rolling hills thick with grass, crisp mountain air carrying the scent of wild herbs. Far ahead, the snow-capped peaks known as Sheba’s Breasts pierced the heavens, their summits wreathed in mist. Infadoos followed my gaze.

“It was there our ancestors came, ten thousand generations past, driven like storm winds from the northern lands. The mountains trapped them, but the soil was rich, and so they stayed. Now we are as countless as the stars, and when Twala calls his armies, the earth trembles beneath their tread.”

“Yet if your borders are closed, who do your warriors fight?” I asked.

His expression darkened. “The mountains do not wall us in completely. Sometimes invaders come—men from beyond, hungry for blood. The last great battle was a generation ago. We broke them, and since then, the spears have rested.”

But not all conflicts, it seemed, were with outsiders. Infadoos spoke carefully of a fratricidal war—of twin brothers, one destined to rule, the other doomed to die. Yet through a mother’s love, the weaker child survived, hidden until the day he seized the throne by treachery.

“And the true heir?” I pressed.

“Dead,” Infadoos said flatly. “The queen fled with her son into the wilds. None saw them again.”

A shadow crossed his face, but before I could question further, the road dipped, revealing a vast kraal in the valley below—a fortress of thatched huts encircled by a deep trench.

“That is where the queen vanished,” Infadoos murmured. “And where we rest tonight.”

As we descended, movement stirred at the kraal’s gates. Regiments poured forth, their plumed ranks assembling with disciplined precision. Sir Henry gripped my arm. “Either we are honored guests,” he muttered, “or walking into a trap.”

Infadoos caught the tension. “Fear not. These are my men.”

The warriors were magnificent—tall, broad-shouldered, their white shields gleaming, spears tipped with blades wide as a man’s palm. As we passed, each company raised their weapons in salute, a thunderous cry of *“Koom!”* echoing across the plain.

The kraal itself was a marvel of order, its pathways clean, huts arranged in perfect squares. Women watched from the sidelines, their dark eyes curious but restrained. Unlike the Zulu, these people carried themselves with a quiet dignity, their gazes lingering only briefly on Good’s unfortunate flannel shirt—which flapped mercilessly in the wind—before turning away.

At the center of the settlement, Infadoos led us to a spacious hut. “Rest, Sons of the Stars,” he intoned. “Eat, drink, and let your weariness fade.”

Food came in abundance—roasted meat, milk, honey—and as we feasted, Scragga, the king’s son, watched us with a mix of awe and suspicion. The illusion of our divinity faded with every bite we took.

That night, pipes in hand, we learned our journey would resume at dawn. Messengers had already been sent to King Twala, who prepared for the great festival at Loo.

“Two days’ march,” Infadoos said, rising to leave. “If the rivers allow it.”

As the fire burned low, we took turns keeping watch. The air hummed with the unknown—of kingdoms built on secrets, of a lost prince, and of the shadow of a king who might yet see us as threats rather than guests.

Sleep came uneasily. Beyond the kraal’s walls, the mountains loomed, silent witnesses to the past—and the trials yet to come.
