"I count myself in nothing else so happy / As in a soul remembering my good friends."

Sir Hugo Mallinger did not hasten his departure for Genoa with the same impatience that Mr. Gascoigne had displayed, nor did Daniel Deronda wish to leave until he had finally seen the baronet. Between Grandcourt's drowning and the tumultuous upheaval of Daniel's own life, reasons abounded for why his oldest friend would crave the unbridled communication of speech. In letters, Daniel had felt unable to divulge the particulars regarding the mother who had appeared and vanished like a spectral vision. It was not until the fifth evening, guided by a telegram, that Daniel waited for Sir Hugo at the station, expecting an arrival between eight and nine. As he gazed toward the platform, anticipating the sight of a kind, familiar face rooted in his earliest memories, a faint smile, despite his recent tragedies, curved his lips. This smile was fueled by the thought of Sir Hugo's joy in regaining mastery of his estates, the ability to leave them to his daughters, or—per a newly formed theory of inheritance pressing upon Daniel's mind—to adopt makeshift feminine offspring as intermediaries to secure a satisfactory male heir.

We might call ourselves churlish creatures if we were incapable of sharing in our fellows' joys, save when those joys align with our theories of righteous distribution and our highest ideals of human good. How sour our mouths would turn, how frozen our glances become! Yet, our own possessions and desires rarely adjust themselves to such lofty ideals. We must maintain some comradeship with imperfection; indeed, it is happily possible to feel gratitude even where we discern a mistake that may prove injurious, especially when the vehicle of that mistake is an affectionate intention pursued through a lifetime of kindly offices. Daniel's feelings and judgments stood strongly against Sir Hugo's action in becoming the agent of a falsehood—yes, a falsity—he could offer no gentler term for the concealment under which he had been reared. Still, the baronet likely possessed no clear knowledge of his mother's breach of trust. With his light, easy disposition toward life, Sir Hugo had deemed it a reasonable preference that his nephew be made an English gentleman, considering the mother's eccentric refusal to part from her child or remain merely a distant figure to him.

Daniel's affectionate gratitude toward Sir Hugo prompted him to seek grounds for excuse rather than blame. For it is as possible to be rigid in principle yet tender in censure as it is to suffer from the sight of things hung awry while remaining patient with the hand that hangs them. Had Sir Hugo, in his bachelorhood, been beguiled into viewing children primarily as products intended to make life agreeable for the fully grown, whose sole convenience mattered in their disposal, well, he had shared an assumption that, if not formally avowed, was massively acted upon in that era of world history. Daniel, with his keen memory of the painful inward struggles of his boyhood, could also recall the many signs that his harrowing experience had been entirely shut out from Sir Hugo's conception. Ignorant kindness might have the effect of cruelty; but to condemn it as if it were direct malice would be an ignorant *unkindness*, utterly remote from Daniel's large imaginative lenience toward others. Perhaps, after the searching scenes of the last ten days, wherein the curtain had lifted from the secrets of lives unlike his own, he was more than ever disposed to curb that rashness of indignation or resentment which bears an unpleasant likeness to the love of punishment. When he saw Sir Hugo's familiar figure descend from the railway carriage, the lifelong affection which had always been well accustomed to make excuses flowed in and submerged all newer knowledge that might have seemed fresh ground for blame.

"Well, Dan," said Sir Hugo, with a serious fervor, grasping Daniel's hand. He uttered no other words of greeting; there was too strong a rush of mutual consciousness. The next step was to give orders to the courier, followed by a proposal to walk slowly in the mild evening air, as there was no hurry to reach the hotel.

"I have taken my journey easily, and am in excellent condition," Sir Hugo remarked as they emerged under the starlight, which still held the faint lingering sheen of day. "I did not hurry in setting off, because I wished to inquire into things a little, and thus I caught sight of your letter to Lady Mallinger before I departed. But now, how fares the widow?"

"Growing calmer," Daniel replied. "She appears to be escaping the physical illness one might have feared following her plunge and terrible excitement. Her uncle and mother arrived two days ago, and she is being well attended to."

"Is there any prospect of an heir being born?"

"From what Mr. Gascoigne told me, I conclude there is not," Daniel said. "He spoke as if the question were whether the widow would retain the estates for her life."

"It will not be much of a wrench to her affections, I fancy, this loss of the husband?" Sir Hugo asked, glancing around at Daniel.

"The suddenness of the death has been a great blow to her," Daniel answered quietly, gently evading the deeper implications.

"I wonder whether Grandcourt gave her any notion what the provisions of his will contained?" Sir Hugo pressed.

"Do you know what they are, sir?" Daniel parried.

"Yes, I do," the baronet said quickly. "Good heavens! If there is no prospect of a legitimate heir, he has left everything to a boy he had by a Mrs. Glasher. You know nothing of the affair, I suppose, but she was a sort of wife to him for many years, and there are three older children—all girls. The boy is to take his father's name; he is already known as Henleigh, and henceforth he shall be Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. The Mallinger fortune will be of no use to him, I am happy to say; but the young dog will have more than enough with his fourteen years' minority. No need to have had holes filled up with my fifty thousand pounds for Diplow that he had no right to touch. Meanwhile, my beauty, the young widow, is to endure a paltry two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere—a nice sort of banishment for her if she chooses to shut herself up there, which I do not think she will. The boy's mother has lived there for recent years. I am perfectly disgusted with Grandcourt. I do not know that I am obliged to think the better of him because he is drowned, though, so far as my affairs are concerned, nothing in his life became him like leaving it."

"In my opinion, he did wrong when he married this wife—not merely in leaving his estates to the son," Daniel observed, somewhat dryly.

"I say nothing against his leaving the land to the lad," Sir Hugo countered. "But since he had married this girl, he ought to have given her a handsome provision, sufficient for her to live in a style fitting the rank he had raised her to. She ought to have had four or five thousand a year and the London house for her life; that is what I should have done for her. I suppose, as she was penniless, her friends could not stand out for a formal settlement; otherwise, it is ill-trusting to the will a man may make after marriage. Even a wise man generally lets some folly ooze out of him in his will—I know my father did, and if a fellow harbors any spite or tyranny within him, he is likely to bottle off a good deal for keeping in such a document. It is quite clear Grandcourt meant that his death should place an extinguisher on his wife, should she bear him no heir."

"And, in the other case, I suppose everything would have been reversed—an illegitimate child receiving the extinguisher?" Daniel asked, with some scorn.

"Precisely—Gadsmere and the two thousand. It's queer. One nuisance is that Grandcourt has made me an executor; but seeing he was the son of my only brother, I cannot refuse to act. And I shall mind it less if I can be of any use to the widow." Sir Hugo paused, his expression shifting. "Lush thinks she was not in ignorance about the family matters under the rose, nor the purport of the will. He hints that there was no very good understanding between the couple. But I fancy you are the man who knew most about what Mrs. Grandcourt felt or did not feel—eh, Dan?"

Sir Hugo did not pose this question with his usual jocularity, but rather with a lowered tone of interested inquiry. Daniel felt that any evasion would be misinterpreted. He answered gravely.

"She was certainly not happy. They were unsuited to each other. But as to the disposal of the property—from all I have seen of her, I should predict that she will be quite contented with it."

"Then she is not much like the rest of her sex; that's all I can say," Sir Hugo remarked, with a slight shrug. "However, she ought to be something extraordinary, for there must be an entanglement between your horoscope and hers—eh? When that tremendous telegram arrived, the first thing Lady Mallinger said was, 'How very strange that it should be Daniel who sends it!' But I have had something of the same sort in my own life. Once, at a foreign hotel, I found a lady abandoned by her husband without money. When I stepped forward to assist her, who should she be but an early flame of mine, who had been foolish enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and short affection?
