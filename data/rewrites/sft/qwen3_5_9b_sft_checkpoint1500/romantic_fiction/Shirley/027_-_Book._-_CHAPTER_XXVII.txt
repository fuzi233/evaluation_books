Miss Keeldar and her uncle were ill-suited companions, a discord that had persisted from the beginning. He was a man of irritability and domination; she was spirited and craved liberty. He was thoroughly worldly, whereas she harbored a romantic nature.

Mr. Sympson had descended to Yorkshire with a singular purpose: to arrange his niece's marriage, secure a suitable match, and thereby liberate himself from the responsibility of guardianship forever. However, a fundamental disagreement plagued their union regarding the definitions of "suitable" and "proper." While Mr. Sympson adhered strictly to his own utilitarian standards, Shirley had never accepted his definitions, and there was little indication she would do so now.

The trial of her resolve came swiftly. Mr. Sympson formally proposed his son, Samuel Fawthrop Wynne, to the family. With enthusiastic approval, the uncle declared the match "decidedly suitable" and "most proper," citing Wynne's unencumbered estate, substantial wealth, and respectable connections. He summoned Shirley to the oak parlour, locked the door, and presented the proposal with absolute authority, demanding her immediate consent.

Her refusal was instant and unequivocal.
"No," she stated firmly. "I shall not marry Samuel Fawthrop Wynne."

Undeterred, Mr. Sympson demanded reasons. "In all respects, he is more than worthy of you," he argued, listing his financial advantage, social standing, and intellect.
"And I ask," Shirley countered, standing pale against the white marble fireplace, her eyes wide and unsmiling, "in what sense is that young man worthy of *me*?"

"He possesses twice your fortune, twice your common sense, and equal connections," her uncle insisted.
"Even if he possessed fifty times my fortune," Shirley replied, "I would take no vow to love him."

Pressed to specify her objections, she revealed her disdain for his "despicable, commonplace profligacy." To her, his conduct sank him into a gulf of inferiority. Furthermore, she criticized his narrow intellect, blunt feelings, coarse tastes, and vulgar manners.
"The man is a respectable, wealthy gentleman!" Mr. Sympson exclaimed, shocked. "To refuse him is presumption."
"I refuse point-blank!" Shirley cried, forbidding any further discussion of the subject. "Is it your intention to remain a spinster?"
"I deny your right to claim an answer to that question."
"When do you expect a peer of the realm to demand your hand?"
"I doubt if any peer breathes on whom I would confer it."
Mr. Sympson, losing his composure, accused her of eccentricity bordering on madness, daring her to sully their name with a *mésalliance*.
"Our name!" Shirley retorted. "Am I called Sympson?"
"Be on your guard!" he warned, trembling with rage. "I will not be trifled with!"
"What power have you over me?" she challenged. "Why should I fear you?"
"Take care, madam!"
"Scrupulous care I will take, Mr. Sympson," she vowed. "Before I marry, I am resolved to esteem—to admire—to *love*."
"Preposterous stuff! Indecorous, unwomanly!"
"To love with my whole heart. I know I speak in an unknown tongue, but I feel indifferent whether I am comprehended or not."
"And if this love falls on a beggar?"
"On a beggar it will never fall. Mendicancy is not estimable."
"On a low clerk, a play-actor, a playwright..."
"Take courage, Mr. Sympson! For literature and the arts, I have a taste. And there lies the problem with your Fawthrop Wynne: he cannot write a note without error, reads only sporting papers, and was the booby of Stilbro' Grammar School!"
"Unladylike language! To what will she come?" he cried, lifting his hands in despair.
"Never to the altar of Hymen with Sam Wynne."
"To what will she come? Why are not the laws more stringent?"
"Console yourself, uncle," Shirley declared, dismissing him. "Were Britain a serfdom and you the Czar, you could not compel me. I will write to Mr. Wynne. Give yourself no further trouble."

Fortune, often called changeful, sometimes repeats her strokes with remarkable consistency. Miss Keeldar's refusal of Samuel Fawthrop Wynne created a sensation throughout the district. Three additional offers followed, all from eligible gentlemen of ample means and unexceptionable character. Each was pressed upon her by her uncle, and each was rejected. The local gossips, initially perplexed by her insolent fastidiousness, eventually concluded they had discovered the key to her conduct.

Consequently, Mr. Sympson's demeanor underwent a radical transformation. The tension at Fieldhead, previously exacerbated by his aunt's inability to reconcile with her quarrelsome nephew, dissipated. The atmosphere shifted from one of chilling dread to tranquility.

The narrative focus then turned to the village of Nunnely, specifically its ancient priory and its owner, Sir Philip Nunnely, a baronet. Unlike the other estates in the region, Nunnely boasted a man of actual title. During Shirley's stay at Cliffbridge, she had encountered Sir Philip frequently. He was solitary, unpretending, and seemingly content with their company, though he never condescended to them.

Shirley found him kind and modest, delighting in the power to amuse him. A slight drawback, however, marred the friendship: Sir Philip possessed a fervent literary turn. He composed poetry—sonnets, stanzas, ballads—and was prone to reciting them. Shirley, finding his verses lacking in musicality and fire, often winced when he revisited the subject, diligently steering the conversation elsewhere. Nevertheless, he persistently invited her on moonlit walks along the bridge solely to pour forth his ballads, leading her to secluded spots where he would read his sonnets in a voice tremulous with emotion. Unaware that his rhymes were merely mechanical exercises devoid of true poetic spirit, he failed to perceive Shirley's mortification.

Despite her efforts to wean him from this monomania, Sir Philip remained devoted to his muse. Eventually, following Shirley's advice to return to his ancestral halls, he traveled to the Priory in late September. He soon extended visits to Fieldhead, praising the hospitality of the oak-beamed manor. His presence was assiduous, covering Shirley with distinction and earning the admiration of her uncle, who now viewed her not as a "mad girl," but as a "superior person" destined for a splendid future.

As Sir Philip's courtship progressed, strange feelings permeated Fieldhead. Restless hopes and anxious anticipations haunted the household. It became clear that Sir Philip was a man of genuine sensibility and intelligence, free from the vulgarity Shirley had condemned in Samuel Wynne. Although physically slight and boyish in appearance, Shirley perceived a nobility in his heart that outweighed physical perfection.

Meanwhile, a subplot involving Louis Moore, Shirley's tutor, unfolded. Mr. Yorke, observing Robert's absence, advised him to return home immediately, noting that Robert had squandered his chance with Miss Keeldar to a rival aristocrat. Yet, despite the universal cessation of rumors linking Shirley to Robert, her interest in him lingered. This was evidenced by her sudden, intense concern when Louis Moore fell ill with a fever contracted from visiting the poor.

While convalescing, Louis summoned Shirley to his bedside. Dressed elegantly, she entered the somber room, offering him grapes and tender inquiries. Though he politely dismissed her offers, sensing her discomfort, she pressed him, revealing her keen hearing. She recounted overhearing Sir Philip Nunnely outside, describing a scene where they stood on the steps, engaging in conversation until he kissed her hand—a gesture she witnessed clearly from her chamber above.
"And can I do nothing?" she pleaded.
"Nothing," Louis replied gently. "You cannot guarantee me a night's peaceful rest, and it is all I at present want."
Later, as she departed, she bid him "Good-night," a formal courtesy that left him reflecting on the complex dynamic between his former pupil and the wealthy baronet.

After recovering, Louis and Shirley resumed their lessons, the distance between them bridged by shared memories. One afternoon, alone in the garden, Louis summoned her to the schoolroom. Inside, the atmosphere was intimate and nostalgic. He requested she read a passage from St. Pierre's *Fragments de l'Amazone*, instructing her to adjust her hair so he could read the page. As she complied, the setting sun illuminated her profile, casting her in a soft, statuesque light.

She read the text, struggling slightly with her accent, but soon fell into rhythm. Louis recited the famous opening lines of the poem—the myth of Eve and the serpent—and then launched into the narrative of the lone savage girl, Eva, living in a forest valley. The tale depicted her isolation, her growth amidst nature, and her eventual encounter with a divine presence. As the story unfolded, the room seemed to dissolve into the primordial landscape described. The girl, Eva, sat alone as day gave way to night, calling out for guidance.
