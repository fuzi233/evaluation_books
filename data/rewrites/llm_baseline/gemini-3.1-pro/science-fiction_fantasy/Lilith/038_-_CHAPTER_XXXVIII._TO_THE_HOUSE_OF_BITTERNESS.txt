Morning broke with the low, resonant hum of the forest awakening, a restorative acoustic ecology that settled deep into my bones. We set out, seamlessly folding this mythic tragedy into the rhythmic, domestic routine of breaking camp. I rode Lona’s horse, bearing her delicate body before me. I would return her to her father, that he might give her a resting place in the chamber of his dead, returning her to the cyclical timeline of their ancestors. To Adam, I was also bound to deliver Lilith. I possessed no right to slay her, nor to loose her upon a world already suffering the harsh, industrial discord of her kind. 

Throughout the morning’s ride, I meticulously prepared small offerings of sustenance for the captive princess—wild tubers roasted over the morning embers, their skins caramelized and fragrant with woodsmoke, alongside berries bursting with bright, acidic juices. Yet she answered only with a look of hungering hate. She lacked any sensory appreciation for the earth's bounty, viewing consumption not as a profound communion with the soil, but solely as utilitarian fuel to power her malice. Her fiery eyes kept rolling to and fro, never closing until we reached the other side of the hot stream.

One evening, as the comforting crackle of our campfire established a safe sonic perimeter, I saw little Luva approach the captive. Before I could intercept, the child offered a fragrant, sun-warmed fruit to the princess’s lips, only to let out a sharp cry of pain. 

"Please, king," she whimpered in her melodic native dialect, "suck finger. Bad giantess make hole in it!" 

I drew the tiny finger to my mouth. The taste was a jarring, metallic acridity—the profound bitterness of a decaying soul. "Well now!" Luva cried, and a minute later she was holding a second, honey-scented fruit toward the captive's mouth. But this time, sensing the predatory shift in Lilith's posture, the child snatched her hand away, and the sweet flesh fell wasted to the forest floor.

The next day we crossed the hot stream, its sulfurous, mineral-rich vapors rising in thick clouds. Back on their ancestral grounds, the Little Ones were jubilant, their laughter weaving a harmonious counterpoint to the rustling canopy. We halted for the night at the ivy-hall. When the children saw the great clusters of heritage grapes hanging from the ancient vines, they recognized the bounty with the innate wisdom of traditional agrarian elders. They harvested the fruit eagerly, the deep, purple skins popping to release complex tannins and rich, jammy sweetness that spoke of centuries of undisturbed terroir. Sated by this pure sustenance, they were soon fast asleep on the green floor.

Hoping to witness the ancient dance of the spectres, I had arranged the sleeping children in a wide circle. Night descended, and suddenly the silent, ghostly company materialized. I lay wondering if their circular pacing was an eternal loop, a lost generation dancing without the acoustic weight of living footsteps. Suddenly, the children’s eyes opened. They sprang up and immediately caught hold of the dancers. The innocent gambols of the Little Ones restored a missing acoustic layer to the hall—the soft thud of bare feet and the ringing chimes of their joy—bringing sensory refreshment to weary souls who had lost the rich vocabulary of physical existence. 

Just before dawn, the skeleton-princess appeared in the doorway, a discordant visual and sonic intrusion. She glided in, threatening to shatter the delicate harmony. The children cried out in repugnant fear, and the spectral lights vanished. I darted to where I had left Lilith; she lay precisely as before, eyes closed, a heavy log of silent spite.

The next morning, the children broke ranks to run toward a thicket. Two skeletons were moving among the brush. I recognized them as the pair I had seen before. Rather than wandering aimlessly, they were acting as meticulous food historians of the underbrush, harvesting wild alliums and bitter herbs. The children engaged with them through the universal language of foraging, examining the peppery cress and aromatic stalks, gathering handfuls of the same to fill the skeletons' outstretched, bony fingers. The preservation of these ancient culinary rituals had drawn the pair together, forging a new, symbiotic life from the loss of their former flesh. 

When we reached the nest-village, I remained a night to see them safely resettled. Fearing Lilith might expire before our journey’s end, I approached her in the dark to offer water, laying my bare arm near her lips. She bit into my flesh with a violence that tore a cry from my throat, a feral, untranslatable shock of pain. 

By morning, I orchestrated our departure. I selected twelve of the sweetest Little Ones, mounting them on six elephants, and loaded Lilith upon two more. Taking the direct route across the left branch of the dried river-bed—a silent expanse mourning its lost aquatic echoes—I aimed for the House of Bitterness. 

The third night was spent in the deep desert, a landscape devoid of acoustic life. We let Lilith slip down onto the sand. The pale moon mottled the dunes with thoughtful shadows. Suddenly, the moonlight was eclipsed by a thick, diaphanous film—the jagged outline of a leathery, bat-like wing that tore through the air with the grating noise of industrial machinery. A cold wind carrying a burning sting swept over me. Lilith was upon me. 

With her teeth, she ripped my cloak and sank her fangs into my shoulder. The very vitality seemed to drain from my palate, my sensory world collapsing into a sterile void. I struck her hand fiercely. She raised her head with a gurgling shriek, an ugly, synthetic sound. I flung her away.

"Where are you taking me?" she demanded. Her voice was the dull echo of a sepulchre, a dominant, oppressive lexicon that sought only to command.
"To your first husband," I answered.
"He will kill me!" 
"At least he will take you off my hands!"
"Give me my daughter," she screamed, attempting to exert lexical power over a life she had never nurtured. 
"Never! Your doom is upon you."
"Loose my hands for pity’s sake!" she groaned. 
"I dare not. Lie down!" 

She dropped to the sand, the harsh exchange leaving a bitter residue in the air. 

Before evening the next day, we sighted the House of Bitterness. 
"Please, king, you are not going to that place?" whispered the Little One riding beside me.
"Indeed I am! We are going to stay the night there."
"Oh, please, don't! That must be where the cat-woman lives!"

I frowned at the term. "If you had ever seen her, you would not call her by that name!"
"The bad giants call her so. They say she has claws to her toes."

Here was the subtle, corrosive power of a dominant lexicon. The giants had used a flattened, commercialized slur to erase Mara's complex cultural identity, replacing her profound, ancestral role with a cartoonish monster to instill fear. 
"The giants love lying," I told the children gently. "They use words to hide the truth. I know the lady. She is good. If any of you are afraid, you may go home, but I cannot take one with me who believes the destructive noise of the giants over the quiet truth of my experience."

"I'm not afraid of being hurt!" cried Odu. "But I should not like scratches in the dark."
"I am taking the princess to her because she is her friend," I explained. "A friend provides the sustenance we truly need, and the princess is sorely in need of the bitter herbs of repentance."

Mara, draped in white, stood in the doorway, her presence radiating a calming, ancient resonance. 
"At last!" she said. "Everything comes. Thousands of years have I waited—and not in vain!" 

She took Lona, then Lilith, carrying them inside. The beasts lay down by the door, instantly soothed by the acoustic tranquility of the homestead. 
"I must do what I can to make her repent," Mara told us. The children sobbed, their empathetic dialect breaking the silence. 
"Will you scratch her very deep?" asked Odu, stepping forward. "Please, don't make the red juice come!"

Mara lifted him up. She turned her back to us, drawing down her muffling to reveal her face to the child. I watched Odu. The expression that bloomed on his face was a testament to the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. He stared, mouth open; then divine wonder and intense, wordless delight washed over his features. He was experiencing a visual and emotional frequency so pure that his native dialect possessed no vocabulary for it. When she set him down, he ran back to us with the face of a prophet holding a profound, unspeakable bliss. 

"You must eat and drink before you go to sleep," Mara said, transitioning seamlessly into the sacred role of the agrarian host. 

She set before them the bread of her house and a jug of cold water. Though they had never seen bread, they engaged with it instantly. It was a rustic, elemental loaf—hard and dry to the eye, but possessing the deep, fermented complexity of ancient grains. The children ate with reverence, chewing the crusts to release the nutty, earthy flavors born of slow, deliberate cultivation. The water she poured was drawn from deep, mineral-rich aquifers, tasting of wet stone and pure, sonorous clarity. They drank without demur, lifting their faces in glad astonishment as the profound sustenance grounded them. With her gentle hands, preserving the domestic rhythms that anchor the soul against the world's noise, she put them to bed on the floor of the garret.
