The evening hung heavy and still, a warm, suffocating blanket promising a sultry future. Around the sinking sun, clouds bloomed in hues of bruised purple, casting a summer glow that seemed more Indian than English across the horizon. Rosy reflections danced upon the hillside, the front of the house, the trunks of trees, the winding roads, and the rolling pastures. Slowly, the two girls descended from the fields. By the time they reached the churchyard, the bells had fallen silent, swallowed by the vast crowd within the sanctuary. The scene outside was utterly solitary.

"How peaceful and calm it is," said Caroline.

"And how stifling it will be inside," Shirley replied, shaking her head. "What a tedious, drawn-out sermon Dr. Boultby will deliver! And how the curates will hammer away at their rehearsed speeches! For my part, I would rather stand here than enter."

"But Uncle will be furious if he catches us missing," Caroline warned.

"I shall take the full force of his anger; he will not eat me alive. I am afraid to miss his sharp wit. I know his speech will be all logic for the Church and all biting satire for the schismatics. He will surely not forget the Battle of Royd Lane. I shall also regret denying myself Mr. Hall's earnest, friendly homily, rich with those rough Yorkshire dialects; but here I must remain. This grey church and its grayer tombs look divine bathed in this crimson light. Nature is now offering her evening prayers; she kneels before those red hills. I see her prostrate on the great steps of her altar, praying for a fair wind for sailors on the seas, for travelers in the deserts, for lambs on the moors, and for unfledged birds in the woods."

She paused, her gaze distant and dreamlike. "Caroline, I see her clearly now, and I will tell you what she resembles. She is like what Eve was when she and Adam stood alone upon the earth."

"And that is not Milton's Eve, Shirley."

"Milton's Eve! Milton's Eve!" Shirley insisted passionately. "No, by the pure Mother of God, it is not! Cary, we are alone; let us speak freely. Milton was a giant, yes; but was he *good*? His intellect was sound, but what of his heart? He beheld heaven; he gazed down into hell. He saw Satan, and Sin his daughter, and Death their terrible offspring. Serried angels flashed their adamantine shields before him, reflecting back the unutterable splendor of the divine realm. Devils gathered their legions in his sight; their dim, discrowned, tarnished armies marched rank upon rank before his vision. Milton sought to see the First Woman, but Cary, he saw her not."

"You are bold to speak thus, Shirley."

"Not bolder than truthful. It was his cook he saw; or it was Mrs. Gill, as I have seen her, making custards in the heat of summer within the cool dairy, surrounded by rose-trees and nasturtiums near the lattice window, preparing a cold repast for the rectors—preserves and 'dulcet creams.' Pondering what choice to make for the finest delicacy, arranging flavors so they did not clash, creating an elegant sequence of tastes, sustained by the kindest changes."

"All very well indeed, Shirley."

"I would remind him that the first men of the earth were Titans, and that Eve was their mother. From her sprang Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus; she bore Prometheus..."

"Pagan that you are! What does that signify?"

"I say, there were giants walking the earth in those days—giants striving to scale heaven. The first woman's breast, heaving with life on this world, birthed the daring spirit capable of contending with Omnipotence, the strength to endure a thousand years of bondage, the vitality to sustain a vulture-death through countless ages, an unexhausted life and uncorrupted excellence, sister to immortality itself. These qualities, after millennia of crime, struggle, and woe, enabled her to conceive and bring forth a Messiah. The First Woman was heaven-born. Vast was the heart from which gushed the wellspring of the blood of nations, and grand the undegenerate head where rested the consort-crown of creation."

"She coveted an apple and was cheated by a serpent; yet you have mixed Scripture and mythology until it is impossible to follow you. You have not yet told me what you saw kneeling on those hills."

"I saw—I now see—a Woman-Titan. Her robe of blue air stretches to the outskirts of the heath, where yonder flock grazes; a veil white as an avalanche sweeps from her head to her feet, bordered with arabesques of lightning flame. Beneath her breast I see her zone, purple like the horizon, through whose blush shines the star of evening. I cannot picture her steady eyes, but they are clear, deep as lakes, lifted and full of worship, trembling with the softness of love and the lustre of prayer. Her forehead possesses the expanse of a cloud, paler than the early moon, risen long before darkness gathers. She reclines her bosom upon the ridge of Stilbro' Moor; her mighty hands are joined beneath it. Knealing thus, face to face, she speaks with God. That Eve is Jehovah's daughter, as Adam was His son."

"She is very vague and visionary. Come, Shirley, we ought to go into the church."

"Caroline, I will not; I will stay out here with my mother Eve, in these days called Nature. I love her—an undying, mighty being! Heaven may have faded from her brow when she fell in Paradise, but all that is glorious on earth shines there still. She is drawing me to her bosom, revealing her heart. Hush, Caroline! You will see her and feel as I do, if we remain silent together."

"I will humour your whim; but you will begin talking again before ten minutes have passed."

Miss Keeldar, upon whom the soft excitement of the warm summer evening seemed to work with unwonted power, leaned against an upright headstone. She fixed her eyes on the deep-burning west and sank into a pleasurable trance. Caroline, moving slightly apart, paced to and fro beneath the rectory garden wall, dreaming in her own quiet way. Shirley had spoken the word "mother." That single syllable ignited a different fire in Caroline's imagination—not the mighty, mystical parent of Shirley's visions, but a gentle, human form—the shape she ascribed to her own mother, unknown, unloved, yet not unlonged for.

"Oh, that the day would come when she would remember her child! Oh, that I might know her, and in knowing, love her!"

Such was her aspiration.

The longing of her childhood flooded her soul once more. The desire that had haunted many a sleepless night in her crib, nearly extinguished recently by the fear of illusion, flared suddenly anew, glowing warm within her heart. It burned with the hope that her mother might arrive on some happy day, send for her to her presence, look upon her with fond, loving eyes, and speak tenderly in a sweet voice, "Caroline, my child, I have a home for you; you shall live with me. All the love you have needed, and never tasted, from infancy, I have saved for you carefully. Come; it shall cherish you now."

A noise on the road shattered Caroline's filial hopes and drew Shirley from her Titan visions. They listened intently, hearing the tramp of hooves. They looked, and saw a glitter through the trees. Through the foliage, they caught glimpses of martial scarlet; helms shone, plumes waved. Six soldiers rode silently and orderly, passing softly by.

"The same ones we saw this afternoon," whispered Shirley. "They have been halting somewhere until now. They wish to be as little noticed as possible, seeking their rendezvous at this quiet hour while the people are at church. Did I not say we should see unusual things ere long?"

Scarcely had the sight and sound of the soldiers vanished when another, somewhat different, disturbance broke the night-hush—a child's impatient scream. They turned to look. A man emerged from the church, carrying in his arms an infant—a robust, ruddy little boy of about two years old—who roared with all the power of his lungs. He had likely just awakened from a church-slumber. Two little girls, aged nine and ten, followed closely. The influence of the fresh air and the attraction of some wildflowers gathered from a grave quickly quieted the child. The man sat down with him, dandling him on his knee as tenderly as any woman might; the two little girls took their places, one on either side.

"Good-evening, William," said Shirley, after scrutinizing the man. He had seen her before and appeared to be waiting for recognition. He now removed his hat and grinned a smile of genuine pleasure. He was a rough-headed, hard-featured figure, not old, but very weather-beaten. His attire was decent and clean; that of his children was singularly neat.

It was our old friend Farren. The young ladies approached him.
