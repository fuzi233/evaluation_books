It was nearing four in the afternoon. Eli sat in his shop, while his eldest and youngest sons were away from home. Catherine and her frail daughter had been anxious all day about Gerard, and now they’d walked a short way down the road, hoping against hope to catch sight of him returning. In the quiet sitting-room, only Giles remained—alone with the furniture, and with himself.

Let me describe this room, as it stood then: plain, but clean, and full of character. The Dutch people had long been known for their ingenuity and industry. They claimed to have invented printing (with wooden type), oil painting, banking, gardening, and even liberty. But above all, years before this tale began, they had perfected cleanliness. While English nobles trod through floors covered in stale rushes, littered with bones, spoiled food, spittle, dogs, eggs, and every sort of filth, this humble hosier’s house in Tergou had floors of polished Dutch tiles—so bright and often washed that one could eat off them without fear.

There was one large window, its central stone crosswork thick and bold, rising like a true cross in the eyes of those who looked upon it. It stood out in relief, and during prayers, the family would turn their gaze toward it as if it were a sacred symbol. The glass panes were small, shaped like diamonds, and joined together with strips of lead—a style still seen today in country cottages.

The chairs were rough and simple, save for one armchair, whose back rose straight up at a right angle to the seat, so tall that the head of anyone seated in it barely reached the top. It was carved from oak, with a design at its peak. Beside it stood a copper pail, shaped like a woman's waist, filled with holy water, and next to it a tiny hand broom used to sprinkle blessings across the room. A long, narrow oak table dominated the space—and perched on its edge, clinging by his teeth, was Giles, the dwarf.

Nature, it seemed, had not meant to make Giles small out of spite. She had formed his head and torso with care—but then, distracted, she left the rest to chance. The result was a human wedge, an inverted cone. His upper body was massive, his lower limbs thin and stunted. He might well have quoted Horace: *“Amphora coepit institui; currente rota cur urceus exit?”* —“A jar was begun to be made; why did a jug come out?” His center of gravity was far from his physical center. If cut in half, the upper part would outweigh three times the lower. Yet this very imbalance gave him astonishing strength. His arms, powerful and unburdened by weight, allowed him to climb vertical poles like a squirrel or hang from a tree branch by one hand, suspended like a cherry on its stem. Had he possessed the suction power of a lizard’s feet, he might have walked across ceilings.

And yet, this miniature athlete had a peculiar passion: gripping the dinner table with both hands and swinging from it. And when he reached the height of joy? He’d clamp his jaws around the edge, release his hands, and hang there, trembling with delight, held fast by his great white teeth.

But such pleasures rarely last. Little Kate caught him mid-swing, and froze in horror. She was her mother’s daughter—her heart lay with order and propriety, not with the antics of a twelve-month-old gymnast.

“Oh, Giles! How can you? Mother is coming!” she cried.

“Go tell her, little tattletale,” growled Giles, refusing to let go. “You’re the one who causes all the trouble.”

“Am I?” she asked calmly. “That’s news to me.”

“The biggest mischief-maker in Tergou,” he snarled, resuming his grip.

“Oh, indeed?” she replied dryly.

The remark landed like a dart. Giles didn’t flinch. Instead, Kate sat down quietly and began to cry.

At that moment, her mother entered. Giles dropped instantly beneath the table, glaring up from the shadows.

“What’s going on now?” the woman demanded sharply.

Her eyes swept from Kate to the hidden figure under the table. Seeing his position and the guilty look on his face, she raised a hand as if to strike.

“Nay, mother,” said Kate softly. “It was only a foolish word. I wouldn’t have minded at another time—but I was tired, and worried about Gerard, you know.”

“Let no one worry over me,” whispered a weak voice at the door.

Gerard stumbled in—pale, dusty, exhausted. Hands shot up in surprise and delight, mingling with concern and joy. He collapsed into the nearest chair, spent from his journey.

He had raced through Rotterdam like a hunted animal, fleeing after Margaret, then endured a long, grueling ride home. The effort had worn him down completely. But youth is resilient. Soon, he revived—and became the focus of everyone’s attention.

They clamored to hear about the prizes first. Then Gerard told them how he’d been allowed to view all the competitors’ works laid out in a vast hall before the judges delivered their verdicts.

“Oh, mother! Oh, Kate!” he exclaimed. “When I saw the goldsmiths’ pieces—I nearly fell to my knees. I thought all the goldsmiths in the world couldn’t have produced so much gold, silver, jewels, and artistry. Truly, all crafts are divine.”

To please the women, he described the reliquaries, feretories, chalices, croziers, crosses, pyxes, monstrances—the sacred treasures—and then the goblets, hanaps, watches, clocks, chains, brooches—each piece dazzling in its detail. Their mouths watered at the thought.

“But when I came to the illuminated manuscripts from Ghent and Bruges,” he continued, “my heart sank. Mine looked like dirt beside theirs. For a moment, I almost wept. But I prayed for a better spirit, and soon I could admire them—thank God for such beauty, and for the patient artisans who created them. I forgot all about black-and-white work.”

Then, the next day, when all other prizes had been awarded, they came to the writing competition.

“And whose name do you think was called first?” he asked.

“Yours,” Kate guessed.

The others laughed at her.

“You may laugh,” Gerard said, “but it was true. Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou—that was the name the herald shouted. I stood stunned. They pushed me forward. Everything swam before my eyes. I found myself kneeling on a cushion at the Duke’s feet. He spoke to me, but I was too flustered to reply. Then he placed his hand at his side—not to draw a sword and behead me, but to give me a gold medal. Here it is.”

A cry erupted—almost a scuffle.

“And then,” he added, “he gave me fifteen golden angels. I’d seen one before, but never held one. Here they are.”

“Oh, Gerard! Oh, Gerard!”

“One for you, our eldest son,” he said, handing out coins. “One for you, Sybrandt. One for you, Little Mischief. Two for you, Little Lily—because God has afflicted you. One for myself—to buy colors and vellum. And nine for her who nursed us all and risked two crowns on poor Gerard’s hand.”

The gold brought out their true natures. Cornelis and Sybrandt grabbed their coins, eyes gleaming with greed—and envy, especially toward Kate, who received two. Giles snatched his and rolled it across the floor, chasing it like a child. Kate set aside her crutches, sat down, and stretched her arms toward Gerard with a gesture of pure love.

The mother, overwhelmed at first by the shower of gold landing in her apron, finally cried out: “Stop kissing him, Kate—he’s my son, not yours. Ah, Gerard! My boy! I’ve not loved you as you deserved.”

Gerard knelt beside her. She embraced him tightly, weeping with joy and pride upon his neck.

“Good lad! Good lad!” the hosier said, emotion in his voice. “I must go tell the neighbors. Lend me the medal, Gerard—I’ll show it to my friend Peter Buyskens. He always boasts about how his son Jorian won a tin mug at the archery butts.”

“Ay, go,” said Gerard. “And show him one of the angels. Tell him there are fourteen more just like it. But mind—you bring it back!”

“Stay a minute, father,” Gerard said, flushing with joy. “There’s better news.”

“Better than this?” someone gasped.

Gerard smiled. Then he told them of his meeting with the Countess—and the house rang with jubilation.

“Now, God bless the good lady—and bless the dame Van Eyck! A benefice? Our son? My cares are ended. Eli, my dear friend and master—we can die happy whenever our time comes. This dear boy will carry on for us. None of these children shall want a home or a friend.”

From that moment, Gerard was seen as the pillar of the family. He was not merely a son—he was special. Always right, always worthy. Nothing was too good for him.

Cornelis and Sybrandt grew more jealous with each passing day. They longed for the day he would leave for his benefice—when the favorite would be gone, and the priest’s purse would be open to them. Secretly, Gerard encouraged their hopes.

The wound love had dealt him throbbed less and less. His success, the affection of his parents, and the admiration of the village lifted his spirit. He began to resent Margaret’s coldness more fiercely. Yet she still held power over him—enough to make him wary of all women. For that reason, he now desired ordination more than ever.

He already knew the Vulgate better than most priests, studied church rubrics and doctrine with monks, and when the bishop visited, he applied to be admitted as an exorcist—the third step in holy orders.

The bishop questioned him—and ordained him immediately.

Gerard knelt. After a brief prayer, the bishop handed him a small manuscript filled with exorcisms.

“Take this, Gerard,” he said, “and have power to lay hands on the possessed—whether baptized or catechumens.”

Gerard accepted it reverently, and returned home bearing the Church’s authority to cast out demons.

As he stepped from the church, he met little Kate on her crutches.

“Oh, Gerard!” she said. “Who do you think sent for you? The burgomaster himself!”

“Ghysbrecht Van Swieten!” Gerard frowned. “What does he want?”

“I don’t know,” she replied. “But he seems urgent. You’re to go to his house at once.”

“Well,” Gerard said, “he is the burgomaster—I’ll go. But I don’t like it. Kate, I’ve seen him look at me… not like a friend. No matter. Such looks warn the wise. To be sure—he knows.”

“Knows what, Gerard?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“Kate, I’ll go.”
