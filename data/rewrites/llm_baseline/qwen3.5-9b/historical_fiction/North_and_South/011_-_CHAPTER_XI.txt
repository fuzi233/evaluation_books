"There's iron, they say, in all our blood, And a grain or two perhaps is good; But his, he makes me harshly feel, Has got a little too much of steel." — *Anon.*

"Margaret!" Mr. Hale called out as he returned from escorting his guest downstairs. "I couldn't help watching your face with genuine anxiety when Mr. Thornton confessed to having once been a shop-boy. I already knew the truth from Mr. Bell, so I was fully prepared for what was coming; yet, I half expected you to rise abruptly and flee the room."

"Oh, Papa! Do you truly think I am so foolish?" Margaret protested, her tone lightening instantly. "In fact, I preferred that admission above anything else he said. Every other detail repelled me due to its sheer hardness. Yet, when he spoke of himself, it was with such simplicity—and so little of the artificiality that often defines the vulgarity of shopkeepers—that I felt less urge to leave than when he boasted of Milton as if no other place existed, or when he casually dismissed people for their careless improvidence without ever considering it his duty to offer them guidance or the training his mother had given him. No! His revelation of his humble origins was, by far, the most endearing part of his story."

"I am surprised at you, Margaret," her mother remarked gently. "You who have always accused others of being 'shoppy' at Helstone! I don't think, Mr. Hale, you did quite right in introducing someone with such a background without warning us. I was genuinely terrified of displaying my shock at certain parts of his speech. What about his father 'dying in miserable circumstances'? It sounds as though he might have ended up in the workhouse."

"I cannot be certain it wasn't worse than the workhouse," her husband replied gravely. "Before we arrived, Mr. Bell shared considerable details about his earlier life. Since he has recounted a portion, allow me to complete the picture. His father engaged in wild speculation, failed catastrophically, and subsequently committed suicide, unable to bear the disgrace. All his former acquaintances recoiled from the revelations regarding his dishonest gambling—desperate, futile struggles using other people's money to recover his modest fortune. No one stepped forward to aid the mother and this boy. There was another child, a girl, too young to earn a living, who naturally had to be supported. Initially, no friends came forward, and Mrs. Thornton, I suspect, is not the type to wait for belated kindness to discover her plight. Consequently, they abandoned Milton. I knew he had entered a shop, and that his wages, combined with a small inheritance secured for his mother, sustained them for a long period. Mr. Bell mentioned they literally subsisted on water-porridge for years—the method remained unknown to him. Long after creditors had despaired of recovering any funds from Old Mr. Thornton's debts—if they ever truly harbored hope following the suicide—young Mr. Thornton returned to Milton. He quietly visited each creditor, paying the first installment owed. There was no clamor, no assembly of debtors; it was executed with profound silence and quietude, yet eventually, everything was settled. This was aided significantly by the circumstance of one particularly crusty creditor, whom Mr. Bell described, accepting Mr. Thornton as a sort of partner."

"That is truly admirable," Margaret observed. "It is a pity such a noble nature should be tarnished by his status as a Milton manufacturer."

"Tarnished?" her father queried.

"Oh, Papa, by judging everything solely through the lens of wealth. When he discussed mechanical powers, he clearly viewed them merely as novel methods to expand trade and generate profit. Furthermore, the impoverished men surrounding him—they were poor because they were vicious, excluded from his sympathies because they lacked his 'iron nature' and the capabilities it bestowed for accumulating riches."

"Not vicious; those were not his words," her father corrected. "He used the terms 'improvident' and 'self-indulgent.'"

Meanwhile, Margaret began gathering her mother's sewing materials, preparing to retire. Just as she was about to depart, she paused. She felt compelled to make an acknowledgment that would please her father, yet one that, to be entirely truthful, must also admit a degree of personal discomfort. Nevertheless, she voiced it.

"Papa, I consider Mr. Thornton a remarkably extraordinary man; however, on a personal level, I do not like him at all."

"And I do!" her father laughed warmly. "Personally, as you phrase it, and all. I do not elevate him to the pedestal of a hero or anything similar. Good night, child. Your mother appears exceedingly fatigued tonight, Margaret."

Margaret had noted her mother's worn-out appearance with growing concern for some time. Her father's comment sent her upstairs with a vague dread settling heavily upon her heart. Life in Milton differed so drastically from what Mrs. Hale had known in Helstone, both within the home and outdoors. In Helstone, they were perpetually immersed in fresh, invigorating air; here, the atmosphere seemed devoid of any revitalizing quality. Domestic anxieties pressed upon every member of the family with such intensity and in such a sordid, unfamiliar guise that there was legitimate cause to fear her mother's health was deteriorating seriously.

Several other indicators suggested something was profoundly wrong. Mrs. Hale and Dixon frequently engaged in secretive consultations in the bedroom, emerging with Dixon crying and irritable—a reaction she displayed whenever her mistress's distress demanded her sympathy. On one occasion, shortly after Dixon departed, Margaret entered the chamber and found her mother kneeling. As she slipped away unnoticed, she overheard fragments of what appeared to be a prayer for strength and patience to endure severe physical suffering.

Margaret yearned to restore the bond of intimate confidence severed by her prolonged absence at Aunt Shaw's. She endeavored to win her mother's trust through gentle touches and soothing words, striving to occupy the warmest corner of her mother's heart. Though she received affection and tender words in abundance—sufficient to have delighted her previously—she sensed a secret being withheld, one she believed pertained critically to her mother's health.

She lay awake for a significant portion of the night, devising plans to mitigate the detrimental influence of their Milton existence on her mother. She considered hiring a servant to assist Dixon permanently, thereby freeing her to dedicate her entire time to seeking relief for her mother, ensuring she received the constant personal attention she had grown accustomed to throughout her life.

For several days, visiting registration offices and encountering a myriad of improbable individuals, with very few appearing remotely suitable, consumed Margaret's time and thoughts. One afternoon, she encountered Bessy Higgins in the street and halted to speak with her.

"Well, Bessy, how are you? I hope you're feeling better now that the wind has shifted."

"Better and not better, if you understand what that implies," Bessy replied.

"Not exactly," Margaret smiled. "I'm better in that I am no longer torn apart by coughing fits at night, but I am weary and exhausted by Milton, longing to escape to the Land of Beulah. Yet, as I realize I drift further and further away, my heart sinks, and I am no better; indeed, I am worse."

Margaret turned to walk beside the girl as she progressed feebly toward home. For a moment or two, neither spoke. Finally, Margaret whispered softly, "Bessy, do you wish to die?"

For she, too, shrank from death, holding onto life with the natural fervor of youth and health. Bessy remained silent for a similar duration. Then she answered, "If you had lived the life I have endured, become as weary of it as I am, and occasionally thought, 'perhaps it will last fifty or sixty years—it does for some,' and felt dizzy and disoriented as each of those sixty years spun around me, mocking me with the length of its hours and minutes, and its endless fragments of time... oh, wench! I tell you, you would be grateful enough when the doctor declared he feared you would never see another winter."

"Why, Bessy, what sort of life has yours been?"

"Nought worse than many others', I reckon. Only I fretted against it, whereas they did not."

"But what was it? You know, I am a stranger here, so perhaps I am not as quick to grasp your meaning as someone who has lived their entire life in Milton."

"If you had come to our house when you promised, I might have explained. But Father says you are just like the rest of them; it is 'out of sight, out of mind' with you."

"I don't know who 'the rest' refers to, and I have been very busy. To tell the truth, I had forgotten my promise—"

"You offered it; we asked for nothing," Bessy interjected.

"I forgot what I said for the time being," Margaret continued calmly. "I suppose I would have remembered sooner if I were less occupied. May I accompany you now?"

Bessy cast a swift glance at Margaret's face to discern whether the expressed desire was genuine. The sharpness in her eyes softened into a wistful longing as she met Margaret's soft, friendly gaze. "I have no one so many to care for me; if you care, you may come."
