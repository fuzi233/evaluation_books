Mrs. Manderson stood by the window of her sitting-room at White Gables, staring out at a landscape blurred by fine rain and mist. The weather had finally broken—a rare occurrence in that region in June. Wreaths of white vapor drifted upward from the fields toward the sullen sea, while the sky remained an unbroken, dead grey, shedding pinpoint drops of moisture that occasionally struck the glass with a crackling sound of despair. The widow surveyed the dim, chilling scene with a face etched in sorrow. It was a fitting day for a woman bereaved, alone, and stripped of purpose.

A knock interrupted her reverie. "Come in," she called, straightening her posture with an unconscious gesture that signaled her awareness of the encroaching weariness of the world.

"Mr. Trent has arrived," the maid announced. "He apologized for calling so early but insisted on speaking to you immediately regarding a matter of urgent importance."

"I shall see him," Mrs. Manderson replied.

She moved to a mirror, studying the olive-toned face reflected within. Shaking her head at herself with a fleeting grimace, she turned to greet her visitor as Trent was ushered in. She noted immediately that his appearance had altered. Gone was the usual half-smile of fixed good humor; in its place sat a reserved, jaded expression, the face of a sleepless man, carrying a weight that her quick sensibilities interpreted as ominous.

"May I come to the point at once?" he asked, taking her hand.

"There is a train I must catch at Bishopsbridge at twelve," Trent continued, his voice steady despite the fatigue evident in his eyes. "But I cannot depart until I settle this matter concerning you alone, Mrs. Manderson. I have worked half the night and spent the rest in contemplation. I now know exactly what I must do."

"You look terribly tired," she said gently. "Won't you sit? This is a very comfortable chair. Naturally, it concerns this terrible business and your duties as a correspondent. Please ask me anything you deem proper. I trust you will not make my situation worse than necessary while performing your duty. If you insist on seeing me, I assume it is because you feel compelled to act."

"Mrs. Manderson," Trent said, choosing his words with deliberate slowness, "I promise not to make it worse for you than I can possibly help. However, I am bound to make it difficult for you—at least, privately, I hope so. Regarding what you may tell me, you will judge that yourself. But let me state this on my honor: I intend to ask only enough to determine whether I should publish or suppress certain grave discoveries I have made regarding your husband's death. These are facts unknown to anyone else, and unlikely to be suspected soon.

"What I have uncovered—what I believe I have practically proven—will shock you regardless. Yet, it could prove worse than a simple shock. If you provide me with reason to believe that publishing it would cause you further harm, I will suppress this manuscript entirely. Nothing within it will ever see print."

He placed a long envelope on the small table beside him. "It contains a short private note to my editor, followed by a lengthy dispatch intended for the *Record*. You need not answer now. If you refuse, my professional obligation compels me to take this to London today and leave it to my editor's discretion. I do not claim the right to suppress it based merely on a vague possibility arising in my imagination. However, if I discern from you—and from no other source—that there is substance to that imagined peril, then, acting as a gentleman and someone who wishes you well, I shall withhold the dispatch. In some matters, I choose not to aid the police. Do you follow my reasoning?"

Her face, save for its pallor, showed no sign of distress. Her hands were clasped before her, her shoulders pulled back in a pose of rigid, practiced calm. She looked exactly as she had appeared at the inquest.

"I understand perfectly," Mrs. Manderson said in a low voice. She drew a deep breath. "I do not know what dreadful discovery you have made, nor what specific fear has entered your mind, but it was honorable of you to bring it to me directly. Will you please explain?"

"I cannot do that," Trent replied firmly. "If the information belongs to my newspaper, it remains theirs. Should I discover it belongs to you, you shall have the manuscript to read and destroy. Believe me, I detest such games of secrecy from the depths of my soul; however, I did not create this mystery. This is the most painful hour of my life, and you exacerbate it by refusing to treat me with candor.

"The first thing I require you to confirm is this: Is it true, as you testified at the inquest, that you had absolutely no idea why your late husband changed his attitude toward you, becoming mistrustful and withdrawn during the final months of his life?"

Mrs. Manderson's dark brows shot upward, and her eyes flared. She rose abruptly from her chair. At the same instant, Trent stood up, retrieving his envelope. His demeanor indicated he believed the interview concluded. But she raised a hand, color returning to her cheeks and urgency to her voice.

"Do you realize what you are asking, Mr. Trent? You ask me if I committed perjury."

"I do," he answered, unmoved. After a pause, he added, "You already knew I had not come to preserve polite fictions, Mrs. Manderson. The notion that a respectable person, sworn to an oath, cannot withhold a fragment of the truth under any circumstance is merely a polite fiction."

He waited silently for dismissal, but she remained still. Walking to the window, she left him standing miserably, watching the slight tremor of her shoulders until it ceased. Turning her face away, gazing out at the dismal weather, she finally spoke clearly.

"Mr. Trent," she said, "people generally trust you, and I feel that secrets I wish to keep hidden are safe with you. I know you have a very serious reason for this line of questioning, though I do not grasp what it is. Perhaps revealing the truth would serve justice in some way. To understand that truth, you must know the history of my marriage. Many could recount it better than I, but it was certainly not... a particularly successful union.

"I was only twenty. I admired his force, his courage, and his certainty; he was the strongest man I had ever encountered. Yet, it did not take long for me to realize he valued his business over me. I suspect I discovered even sooner that I had deceived myself, blinding myself to reality by promising myself impossible things and wilfully misinterpreting my own feelings, dazzled by the prospect of spending more money than any English girl could dream of. I have despised myself for that deception for five years.

"As for my husband's feelings toward me... well, I cannot speak of that. What I must say is that alongside whatever existed, he harbored a firm belief that I was destined for greatness in society. He expected me to throw myself into high society with enthusiasm, to become a notable figure and reflect great credit upon him. That was his enduring illusion, persisting even as others faded. I was merely a component of his ambition. His deepest bitterness stemmed from my failure to succeed socially. I believe he was too shrewd not to know, in his heart, that a man of his age, burdened with immense responsibilities and caring for nothing else, faced a significant risk of unhappiness by marrying a girl like me—one raised on music, books, and impractical ideals, accustomed to enjoying life in my own solitary way. Nevertheless, he calculated on me as a wife who would manage the honors of his worldly position. I found I could not fulfill that role."

Mrs. Manderson had spoken herself into a more emotional state than she had previously displayed to Trent. Her words flowed freely, her voice ringing with a natural expressiveness that must have been dulled recently by shock and self-restraint. Suddenly, she turned swiftly from the window to face him. Her beautiful face was flushed and animated, her eyes gleaming, her hands making slight, emphatic gestures as she surrendered to the impulse to articulate things long suppressed.

"The people!" she exclaimed. "Oh, those people! Can you imagine the experience of stepping from a world where creative work held dignity, where men and women pursued professions and arts, where ideals mattered and debates were spirited—some wealthy, some poor—into a realm where one must be shamefully rich merely to exist? A world where money is the sole currency and the primary obsession, where the millionaires are so exhausted by labor that sport becomes their only recreation, and the idle are often duller and more vicious than the workers? And the women? They live solely for display, trivial amusements, and petty immorality. Do you know how awful that existence is?

"Certainly, there are clever individuals and people of taste in that circle, but they are swamped and spoiled; ultimately, it amounts to the same thing: empty, utterly empty! Oh! I suppose I am exaggerating, and I did make friends and enjoy some happy moments; yet, that is how I feel in retrospect. The seasons in New York and London—how I hated them! Our house parties and yacht cruises—all the same faces, all the same void."
