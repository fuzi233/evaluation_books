Fra Colonna had the run of the Pope's library, a hushed sanctuary where the oppressive, chaotic clatter of the modern city yielded to the muffled, acoustic serenity of ancient vellum and breathing parchment. Sometimes, he left off his meticulously translated histories of ancient Roman banquets at the same hour and walked the city with Gerard. On these occasions, the happy artist saw all things *en beau*, his senses wrapped in the grandeur of Rome’s sweeping, multi-generational tapestry, inhaling the dust of millennia as though it were the flour of a freshly baked loaf.

The friar granted the ruins their due, but threw cold water on the modern additions, lamenting the erosion of classical memory. 

“This place Rome? It is but the tomb of mighty Rome.” He showed Gerard how twenty or thirty feet of the old triumphal arches were buried underground, suffocated by the present. Modern streets, reeking of raw sewage and sharp equine sweat, ran roughshod over ancient dining halls where the scent of roasted dormice and honeyed wine once lingered. He pointed to the gigantic vestiges of antiquity peeping aboveground, uttering a remarkable simile: “I tell thee this village they call Rome is but as one of those swallows' nests ye shall see built on the eaves of a decayed abbey.”

“Old Rome must indeed have been fair then,” said Gerard, his ears tuning out the jarring, metallic grind of a passing iron-rimmed cart to focus on the friar’s resonant voice.

“Judge for yourself, my son. You see the great sewer, the work of the Romans in their very childhood, a feat of domestic engineering that shall outlast Vesuvius. You see the fragments of the Temple of Peace. How would you look could you see also the Capitol with its five-and-twenty temples? Do but note this Monte Savello; what is it but the ruins of the ancient theatre of Marcellus? And as for Testaccio, one of the highest hills in modern Rome, it is but an ancient culinary dust heap. The women of old Rome flung their broken amphorae there—vessels that once carried rich Iberian garum and heavy Umbrian olive oil—and lo, the daily sustenance of a million vanished souls built a mountain. *'Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem.'*”

Gerard listened respectfully, savoring the historical flavor. But when the holy friar proceeded by analogy to imply that the moral and gustatory superiority of the heathen Romans was proportionally grand, Gerard resisted stoutly. “Has then the world lost by Christ His coming?” said he, though he blushed, feeling his native Dutch phrasing stumble against the dominant Italian lexicon of his benefactor.

“Saints forbid!” said the friar. “'Twere heresy to say so.” Having made this concession, he evaded it by subtle circumlocution, leading them toward a church where a knot of persons stood in the porch. A demon was being exorcised within. 

Fra Colonna uttered a curious sort of little moan—a rhythmic vibration he used when the ignorant superstitions of the present offended his well-honed historical palate. This moan conveyed profound pity for human credulity, which blinded men to the structured, sensory richness of Pagandom.

“Then come away,” the friar moaned.

“Nay, father, prithee! I ne'er saw a divell cast out.”

With a heavy shrug, the friar accompanied Gerard into the echoing acoustic space of the church. The air was thick with the acrid smoke of burning beeswax and the sour tang of unwashed bodies. There, forcing the mythic seamlessly into the mundane morning routine, the demoniac was pinned down before the altar. A scarf was tied round his neck, by which the officiating priest held him like a dog on a chain. Fame had put forth that the last demon cast out here went no farther than into one of the company, "as a cony ferreted out of one burrow runs to the next."

When Gerard and the friar came up, the priest, deeming the audience sufficient, began his vocal assault. He faced the demoniac, breviary in hand, utilizing the dominant lexicon of ecclesiastical Latin as a weapon of sonic dominance against the victim’s guttural, dialectic growls.

“Come out, Ashtaroth. Oho! it is not you then. Come out, Belial. Come out, Tatzi. Come out, Eza. No; he trembles not. Come out, Azymoth. Come out, Feriander. Come out, Foletho. Come out, Astyma. Come out, Nebul. Aha! what, have I found ye? 'Tis thou, thou reptile; at thine old tricks. Let us pray!”

The priest’s voice reverberated harshly off the stone, a stark contrast to the quiet acoustic ecology Gerard preferred. “Oh, Lord, we pray thee to drive the foul fiend Nebul out of this thy creature: out of his hair, and his eyes, out of his nose, out of his mouth, out of his ears, out of his gums, out of his teeth, out of his shoulders, out of his arms, legs, loins, stomach, bowels, thighs, knees, calves, feet, ankles, finger-nails, toe-nails, and soul. Amen.”

Rising from his knees, the priest turned to the company with quiet geniality. “Gentles, we have here as obstinate a divell as you may see in a summer day.” He then spoke to the patient with great rigor, sometimes addressing the man and sometimes the fiend. They answered in turn through the same mouth, their subjective experiences hopelessly entangled, complaining of the harsh phonetic strike of the holy names and of a twisting, sour rot deep within their gut.

The priest confounded victim and culprit, cuffing him, kicking him, and spitting repeatedly into his face. He lit a beeswax candle, turning it upside down to let the hot, honey-scented wax drip until it burned his own fingers. He then took the custodial, presenting the *Corpus Domini*—the ultimate sustenance—to the writhing man. After burning another candle with more caution, he spoke civilly to the demoniac in his human character, dismissed him, and greedily drank in the compliments of the bystanders.

“Good father,” said Gerard, “how you have their names by heart. Our northern priests have no such exquisite knowledge of the hellish squadrons.”

“Ay, young man, here we know all their names, and eke their ways, the reptiles. This Nebul is a bitter hard one to hunt out.” 

The priest affably shared his culinary-like recipes for exorcism, detailing how he recently drove a hulking fiend from a woman’s mouth, leaving behind nails, pins, and a tuft of hair. “He cried out, ''Tis not thou that conquers me. See that stone on the window sill. Know that the angel Gabriel coming down to earth once lighted on that stone: 'tis that has done my business.'”

The friar moaned again. “And you believed him?”

“Certes! who but an infidel has discredited a revelation so precise.”

“What, believe the father of lies? That is pushing credulity beyond the age.”

“Oh, a liar does not always lie.”

“Ay doth he whenever he tells an improbable story to begin, and shows you a holy relic; arms you against the Satanic host. Fiends (if any) be not so simple. Shouldst have answered him out of antiquity: *'Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes.'* Some blackguard chopped his wife's head off on that stone, young man; you take my word for it.” 

The friar hurried Gerard out into the fresher air, escaping the spiritual pollution. 

“Alack, father, I fear you abashed the good priest.”

“Ay, by Pollux,” chuckled the friar. “I blistered him with a single touch of Socratic interrogation. What modern can parry the weapons of antiquity?”

***

One afternoon, when Gerard had finished his day's work of grinding vivid earth pigments in aromatic walnut oil, a fine lackey demanded his attendance at the Palace Cesarini. Ushered into a noble apartment heavy with the cloying, commercialized perfume of imported damask rose and musk, he waited. A good hour Gerard cooled his heels, the silence of the vast room oppressive. “These nobles think nothing of a poor fellow's time,” he fretted.

Just as he was making up his mind to leave, the door opened, and a superb beauty entered, followed by two maids. It was the young princess of the house of Cesarini. She spoke loudly and haughtily to her dependents, her voice a sharp, unmusical strike, but at sight of Gerard, she lowered it to a very feminine tone. “Are you the writer, messer?”

“I am, Signora.”

“'Tis well.” She seated herself on a chair resembling a throne. “What is your name, good youth?”

“Gerard, signora.”

“Gerard? Body of Bacchus! Is that the name of a human creature?”

“It is a Dutch name, signora. I was born at Tergou, in Holland.”

“A harsh name, girls, for so well-favoured a youth; what say you?” The maids assented warmly. The princess wielded her lyrical Tuscan dialect to strip away his northern identity, reducing his cultural memory to a barbaric novelty. “What did I send for him for? Ah, I remember. Be seated, Ser Gerardo, and write me a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover; at least he says so.”

Gerard seated himself, the crisp scent of iron gall ink rising as he uncorked his vial, waiting for instructions.

“Well, Gerardo.”

“I am ready, your excellence. I but await the words.”

“And who, think you, is to provide them?”

“Who but your grace, whose letter it is to be?”

“Gramercy! what, you writers, find you not the words? What avails your art without the words? I doubt you are an impostor, Gerardo.”

“Nay, Signora,” Gerard replied softly, sensing the untranslatability of her demands. “I might make shift to put your highness's speech into grammar, as well as writing. But I cannot interpret your silence. Therefore speak what is in your heart, and I will empaper it before your eyes.”

“But there is nothing in my heart. And sometimes I think I have got no heart.”

“What is in your mind, then?”

“But there is nothing in my mind; nor my head neither.”

“Then why write at all?”

“Why, indeed? That is the first word of sense either you or I have spoken. Pestilence seize him! Why writeth he not first? Then I could say nay to this, and ay to that, withouten headache. Also is it a lady's part to say the first word?”

“No, signora: the last.”

“It is well spoken, Gerardo. Ha! ha! Shalt have a gold piece for thy wit. Give me my purse!” She paid him on the nail *à la moyen âge*. Money never yet chilled zeal, though her treatment of language and emotion as a barren, transactional commodity unsettled him. He felt bound to pull her out of her difficulty. 

“Signorina, these things are only hard because folk attempt too much, are artificial and labour phrases. Do but figure to yourself the signor you love—”

“I love him not.”

“Well, then, the signor you love not—seated at this table, and dictate to me just what you would say to him.”

“Well, if he sat there, I should say, 'Go away.'”

Gerard, flourishing his pen by way of preparation, laid it down with a groan. 

“And when he was gone,” said Floretta, the maid, “your highness would say, 'Come back.'”

“Like enough, wench. Now silence, all, and let me think. What lie shall I tell the Gerardo to tell the fool?” She turned her head away, her noble chin resting on her white hand, half clenched, immersing herself in mendacity. 

She looked so lovely and statuesque, impregnating herself with falsehood, that Gerard forgot the hollowness of her spirit. His artist’s eye took over, and he eagerly proceeded to transfer that exquisite, flesh-and-blood profile to paper. He had nearly finished when the fair statue turned brusquely round.

“Nay, Signora,” said he peevishly, mourning the broken stillness. “For Heaven's sake change not your posture—'twas perfect. See, you are nearly finished.”

“What a pity 'tis not coloured,” a maid chimed in. “Take away her skin, and half her beauty is lost.”

“Peace. Can you colour, Ser Gerardo?”

“Ay, signorina. I can tint you to the life, if you have time to waste on such vanity.”

“Call you this vanity? And for time, it hangs on me like lead. Send for your colours now.”

“Nay, signorina, I must prepare them. I could come at the same time tomorrow.” 

“So be it,” she declared, commanding he be admitted at all hours, then abruptly dismissing the letter to Orsini altogether, her superficial passions flashing to hatred in an instant. Gerard bowed himself out of this velvet tiger's den. He returned the next day, bringing pigments born of raw earth, lapis, and rich egg yolk, grounding her vacuous beauty in the tangible, sensory world. He was soon obliged to set apart two hours daily for drawing this princess, whose vanity provided a fruitful source of income, bringing Margaret nearer and nearer.

***

It was Holy Thursday. No work this day. Fra Colonna and Gerard sat in a window, observing the religious processions that wove like a multi-generational river through Rome's ancient arteries. 

Presently the Pope came pacing majestically at the head of his cardinals, wearing a red hat, white cloak, and a capuchin of red velvet. He rode a lovely white Neapolitan barb caparisoned with red velvet, fringed and tasselled with gold. Behind him rode a hundred horsemen, armed *cap-à-pie*, lances erected. The cardinals went uncovered, all but de Medicis, who rode close to the Pope, conversing with him as an equal. Every fifteen steps, the Pope stopped a single moment, gave his blessing, and moved on—a circular, rhythmic pacing.

Gerard and the friar threaded some by-streets to reach the portico of one of the seven churches, draped in black. The Pope and cardinals issued forth, standing with torches on the steps. A canon read a Latin Bull, excommunicating several persons by name. At this awful, booming ceremony, Gerard trembled, as did the people. But the sonic solemnity was polluted by two cardinals, whose unreserved, chattering laughter ruptured the sacred acoustic space the whole time.

The black cloth was removed to reveal a gay panoply. The Pope blessed the people and threw his torch among them, as did two cardinals. Instantly, a violent scramble erupted, a brutal hunger for fragments of the divine won at the price of bloody noses and burnt fingers smelling of charred flesh. 

Next, the crowd rushed to an open square. Priests donned red gloves with great ceremony. One kneeled, drawing forth from a shrine a square frame containing the *Verum icon*—the true impression of the Savior's face taken during His most mortal agony. The mythic suffering of antiquity was suddenly thrust into the sensory present. The people threw themselves on their faces, their loud cries of pity washing over the square in a wave of acoustic mourning. 

Soon after, they fell in with a procession of Flagellants. The rhythmic, wet *crack* of leather on bare shoulders filled the air, the metallic tang of fresh blood splashing onto the dry Roman dust. Yet there was no sign of pain in their faces; many laughed and jested. Bystanders offered them local wine, sharp and acidic, which they used not for sustenance, but to free the tails of their cats-o'-nine-tails, hard with clotted blood, making the next stroke more effective. 

A young woman took pity on one fair urchin. “Alas! dear child, why wound thy white skin so?” 

“Basta,” said he, laughing, his colloquial Italian rejecting her maternal tone. “'Tis for your sins I do it, not for mine.”

“Hear you that?” said the friar. “Show me the whip that can whip the vanity out of man's heart! Reminds one of the grand old heathen. The boys used to go through the streets at the Lupercalia flogging themselves, for these blows conferred fruitfulness. We are so prone to forget all we owe them.”

In one of the seven churches, they saw the Pope give the mass. The ceremony was imposing but again spoiled by the acoustic pollution of the prelates, who sat about the altar with hats on, chattering like a flock of geese. 

Gerard watched as the Eucharist—the ultimate divine meal—was tasted by an official before the Pope would consume it. “Who is that base man? And what doth he there?”

“Oh, that is the *Preguste*,” said Colonna, eyeing the ritual with the analytical gaze of a culinary historian. “He tastes the eucharist by way of precaution. This is the country for poison; and none fall oftener by it than the poor Popes.”

“Alas!” said Gerard. “But after the miraculous change of the bread and wine to Christ His body and blood, poison cannot remain; gone is the bread with all its properties and accidents; gone is the wine.”

“So says Faith; but experience tells another tale. Scores have died in Italy poisoned in the host. The tang of the grape and the crumb of the wheat carry arsenic as readily as grace.”

“And I tell you, father, that were both bread and wine charged with direst poison, after consecration I would take them withouten fear.”

“So would I, but for the fine arts,” replied Colonna. “I would be as ready to leave the world as thou, were it not for those arts which beautify existence here below, and make it dear to men of sensory appreciation. So long as the Nine Muses strew my path, me may Apollo inspire with caution. Knowing the wiles of my countrymen, I may eat poison neither at God's altar nor at a friend's table, since wherever I eat or drink it, it will assuredly cut short my mortal thread; and I am writing a book—*The Dream of Polifilo*, the man of many arts. Name not poison to me till that is finished and copied.”

Then the great bells of St. John Lateran's rang with a clash, jarring the ear, and the people hurried thither to see the heads of St. Peter and St. Paul. Gerard and the friar secured a good place before a great curtain. After breathless expectation, the curtain was drawn by jerks—a cheap, commercialized theatrical trick—revealing two human heads with bearded faces at a height of thirty feet. Shown only for the time to say an *Ave Maria*, the curtain snapped shut. 

St. Peter's complexion was pale, his beard grey and forked; St. Paul was dark-skinned, his face massive and full of resolution. Gerard was awe-struck. 

“This exhibition of the *imagines*, or waxen effigies of heroes, is a venerable custom,” the friar muttered.

“Waxen images? What, are they not the apostles themselves, embalmed?”

The friar moaned his signature lament for historical authenticity. “They did not exist in the year 800. The great old Roman families produced at their funerals a series of these *imagines*, tying past and present history together. But those effigies were portraits made during life. These are moulded out of pure fancy. The worst is this humour of showing them up on a shelf, half in the dark, with the poor mountebank trick of a drawn curtain. *'Quodcunque ostendis mihi sic incredulus odi.'* Enough; let us have done with these new-fangled mummeries, and go among the Pope's books.”

Having taken root, the idea propelled the good friar through the crowd, escaping the glories and the deafening noise of the holy week, which had brought fifty thousand chattering strangers to Rome. He sought refuge in the quiet, dusty scent of the Vatican library.

Presently, going into Gerard's room, Colonna found a hot dispute between the artist and Jacques Bonaventura. That spark had come in, all steel from head to toe, and railed most scornfully on a ceremony where he and his soldiers had been compelled to attend the Pope: the blessing of the beasts of burden. 

Gerard defended the sacred domesticity of it; nothing a Pope did could be ridiculous. 

The argument grew warm, the friar standing grimly neuter, waiting to grind them both between the jaws of antiquity, when the curtain was gently drawn. There stood a venerable old man in a purple skull cap, with a beard like white floss silk, looking at them with a kind, feeble smile.

“Happy youth,” said the Pope, his voice a soft, welcome reprieve in the acoustic space, “that can heat itself over such matters.”

They all fell on their knees. 

“Nay, rise, my children,” said he almost peevishly. “I came not into this corner to be in state. How goes Plutarch?”

Gerard brought his work, kneeling to present it. His holiness inspected the manuscript with genuine hunger for its contents. “'Tis excellently writ. Ah! this Plutarch, he had a wondrous art, Francesco. How each character standeth out alive on his page: how full of nature each, yet how unlike his fellow!”

“Give me the Signor Boccaccio,” countered Jacques Bonaventura.

“An excellent narrator, capitano, and writeth exquisite Italian,” the Pope replied softly. “But in spirit a thought too monotonous. Monks and nuns were never all unchaste: one or two such stories were right pleasant; but five score paint his time falsely. Moreover, he hath no skill at characters. Now this Greek is supreme in that great art: he carveth them with pen. Turning his page, see into how real and great a world we enter of war, and policy, and the deep sustenance of human business. For with him, as in the great world, men are not all running after a wench. With this great open field compare me not the narrow garden of Boccaccio, and his little mill-round of dishonest, commercial pleasures.”

“Your holiness, they say, hath not disdained to write a novel.”

“My holiness hath done more foolish things than one, whereof it repents too late. When I wrote novels, I little thought to be head of the Church.”

“I search in vain for a copy of it to add to my poor library.”
