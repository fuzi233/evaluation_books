My hand slipped from the edge of the curtain I’d been peering through. Do not mistake me, though – the overwhelming, mortifying awkwardness of being caught eavesdropping was not the first thought that crossed my mind. My sisterly devotion to Mr. Godfrey was still so fierce, I did not even pause to wonder why he had skipped the public concert we had all been expected to attend. No, I could only fixate on the startling, resolute line I had just heard him utter to the empty room: *“I will do it today.”*

What on earth was he planning to do? Something more deplorably unworthy of his spotless reputation than any misstep he had made yet? Would he renounce his faith? Abandon the Mothers’ Small Clothes Sewing Circle? Would his angelic smile never again grace our committee meetings? Would his matchless oratory never again rouse crowds to donate to good causes at Exeter Hall? The very idea of such catastrophes felt so grave that I was seconds away from bursting out of my hiding spot, ready to beg him in the name of every Ladies’ Benevolent Committee in London to explain himself – when a second voice cut through the quiet.

It boomed right through the curtain: loud, bold, lacking every single soft, feminine grace a young lady ought to possess. It was Rachel Verinder.

“Why have you come up here, Godfrey?” she said. “Why didn’t you go into the library?”
He laughed softly. “Miss Clack is in the library.”
“Clack in the library!” She dropped immediately onto the ottoman in the back drawing-room. “You’re quite right. We are far better off staying here.”

One moment before, I had been burning with agitation, unsure what to do next. Now I turned icy cold, and there was no doubt at all: I could not step out and show myself after what I had overheard, and the only other way out would have required me to climb into the fireplace. I was trapped in a full martyrdom, and so I did the only sensible thing: I noiselessly rearranged the curtain slits so I could see and hear perfectly, and faced my trial with the steadfast spirit of an early Christian saint.

“Don’t sit on the ottoman,” Rachel went on. “Pull a chair opposite me. I like people to sit facing me when I speak to them.”
He dragged the nearest seat over – a low, squashed armchair – and his tall, broad frame looked absurdly crammed into it. I had never seen his long legs look so awkward.
“Well?” she said. “What did you tell the people who came to take us to the concert?”
“Exactly what you asked me to, dear Rachel. That your mother was unwell today, and you did not feel right leaving her to go out.”
“That mamma was not at all well today? And that I didn’t quite like leaving her to go to the concert?”
“Those were my exact words. They were grieved to miss you at the performance, but they understood completely. They all sent their love, and expressed great hope that Lady Verinder’s indisposition would pass quickly.”
“You don’t think it’s serious, do you, Godfrey?”
“Not at all. I am fully confident all will be well in a few days.”
“I think so too. I was frightened at first, but I believe that now. It was very kind of you to go and make my excuses to people who are almost strangers to you. But why didn’t you go with them to the concert? It seems so unfair that you should miss the music too.”
“Do not say that, Rachel! If you only knew how much happier I am right here, with you!”
He clasped his hands and turned his face toward me, and my stomach lurched. It was that exact same soft, pleading, tear-pricked expression that had charmed thousands on the Exeter Hall platform when he begged for donations for destitute, starving communities across the empire. The man was using his charity speech face on *Rachel Verinder*.

“You ought to break your bad habits, Godfrey,” she said. “Do try to stop paying compliments, to please me.”
“I have never paid you a compliment in my life, Rachel. Successful love may flatter, I admit – but hopeless love, dearest, only ever speaks the truth.”
He pulled his chair closer, and reached for her hand as he said those last two words. There was a short silence. It was no wonder his oratory could thrill entire crowds: it had clearly stunned her too. I thought I understood now what he had meant when he said he would “do it today.” He was declaring his love, right that moment.

“Have you forgotten what we agreed on, Godfrey, when we talked out in the country?” Rachel said. “We said we would be cousins, and nothing more.”
“I break that agreement every time I see you.”
“Then stop seeing me.”
“Useless. I break it every time I think of you. Oh, Rachel! Only the other day you told me my place in your estimation was higher than it had ever been. Am I mad to build hope on those dear words? Am I wrong to dream that one day your heart might soften to me? Do not tell me I am wrong. Leave me that delusion to cherish, if I have nothing else!”
His voice trembled, and he lifted his white handkerchief to his eyes. Exeter Hall again. Nothing was missing from the performance but the cheering crowd, and the glass of water the committee always handed him mid-speech. Even Rachel’s stubborn, hard nature softened. I saw her lean a little closer, her tone shifting to one of unaccustomed interest.
“Are you really sure you care for me that much?”
“Sure! You know what I was, Rachel. Let me tell you what I am now. I have lost every interest in life but you. A transformation has come over me I cannot even explain. Would you believe my charitable work has become an unbearable nuisance? That the sight of any Ladies’ Committee now makes me wish I could flee to the very ends of the earth?”

I do not think the entire recorded history of religious backsliding could produce a more shocking apostasy than that. I thought of the Mothers’ Small Clothes, the Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision society, all the dozens of other good works that leaned on Mr. Godfrey like a tower of strength. I thought of all those hardworking women on our boards who relied on him to keep their operations running – this very same man, who had just called their sacred work a nuisance, who had said he would run to the ends of the earth to avoid meeting with them. It took every scrap of my pious self-discipline to clamp a gloved hand over my mouth and swallow my righteous fury without making a sound. I did not miss a single syllable of what followed.

“You have made your confession,” Rachel said. “I wonder if it would cure you of this unhappy attachment to me, if I made mine?”
We both started. We assumed she was finally about to reveal the great unsolved mystery of the stolen Moonstone.
“Would you look at me and guess,” she went on, “that I am the most miserable girl alive? It is true. What greater wretchedness is there than to live degraded in your own eyes? That is my life now.”
“My dear Rachel, you could never have any reason to speak of yourself that way!”
“How do you know that?”
“Because I know you. Your silence has never made you less worthy in the eyes of your true friends. The disappearance of your precious birthday diamond may seem strange, your unexplained link to that event may seem stranger still—”
“Are you talking about the Moonstone, Godfrey?”
“I assumed you were referring to that—”
“I am not talking about the diamond at all. I can hear the Moonstone discussed by anyone, and not feel degraded in the slightest. If the full story of that Diamond ever comes out, it will be clear I took on a dreadful responsibility, and kept a terrible secret – but no one will ever be able to say I did anything mean. You have misunderstood me. It is my fault for not speaking plainer. Suppose you were not in love with me, but in love with some other woman?”
“Yes?”
“Suppose you found out that woman was utterly unworthy of you? That it would be a disgrace to waste another thought on her? That the very idea of marrying her made your face burn with shame?”
“Yes?”
“And suppose, in spite of all of that – you could not tear her out of your heart? Suppose the feeling she stirred in you back when you trusted her was something you could not shake? That the love that creature inspired is both the breath of my life, and the poison that is killing me – all at once? Go away! I must be out of my mind to speak this way. No, do not leave. I will not let you carry away the wrong impression. Listen to this: he does not know, and he never will know, what I have just told you. I will never see him again, no matter what happens. Do not ask me his name. Change the subject. Are you a good enough doctor, Godfrey, to tell me why I feel like I am suffocating? Is there a kind of hysteria that bursts out in words instead of tears? It does not matter. You will get over all this trouble I have caused you easily enough now, will you not? I have fallen in your estimation, haven’t I? Do not pity me. For God’s sake, go away!”
She twisted away, beating her hands wildly against the back of the ottoman, then dropped her head onto the cushions and burst into sobs. Before I could even process that display, I was horrified by Mr. Godfrey’s next move. I swear solemnly, he dropped to *both* knees at her feet. Then he put his arms around her, and the two words he roared out with the full, booming force of his public speaking voice were enough to freeze me solid.

That was all. But it was such a thunderous, passionate performance that Rachel did not even try to push his arms away. She sat there, stunned or fascinated, I could not tell which. My sense of propriety was completely scrambled. I was so torn between closing my eyes and stopping my ears that I did neither, and I clung to the curtain edge for dear life – doctors will confirm that suppressed hysterics always make people hold tight to something.
“Yes!” he said, his rich evangelical tone oozing with charm. “You are a noble creature! A woman who speaks the truth for truth’s own sake, who will sacrifice her own pride to spare the honest man who loves her pain – that is the most priceless treasure on earth. If a man marries such a woman, and wins only her esteem and regard, that is more than enough to ennoble his whole life. You spoke of your place in my estimation. Judge for yourself how high it is, when I beg you on my knees, to let me be the one to heal your poor wounded heart. Rachel! Will you honour me, will you bless me, by being my wife?”

I was just about to clamp my hands over my ears, when Rachel spoke the most sensible words I had ever heard pass her lips.
“Godfrey! You must be mad!”
“I have never been more reasonable. For your sake, as well as mine. Are you going to sacrifice your happiness to a man who does not even know how you feel, and who you are determined to never see again? Is it not your duty to yourself to forget this ill-fated attachment? And will you ever forget it moping around this house, the way you are now? Surround yourself with better, nobler interests than the trivial, empty pleasures of the world. A home with peaceful duties, a husband who loves and honours you – try that consolation, Rachel. I do not ask for your love. I will be content only with your affection and regard. The rest we can leave to your husband’s devotion, and to Time, which heals even the deepest wounds.”

She was already softening. Good heavens, what a terrible upbringing that girl must have had! How differently I would have behaved in her position.
“Do not tempt me, Godfrey,” she said. “I am already wretched enough and reckless enough as it is. Do not make me even more reckless.”
“One question, Rachel. Do you have any personal objection to me?”
“I! I always liked you. After what you have just said, I would be completely insensible if I did not respect and admire you too.”
“Do you know how many wives there are who only respect and admire their husbands, and still live perfectly happy lives together? How many brides walk down the aisle without perfectly pure hearts, and still have marriages that work out? The truth is, more women turn to marriage as a refuge than they will ever admit – and they are almost always happy with that choice. At your age, with your attractions, you will never be able to sentence yourself to a single, lonely life. It is only a matter of time. You could marry some stranger years from now, or you could marry the man at your feet, who prizes your respect and admiration more than any other woman’s love on earth.”
“Gently, Godfrey. You are putting a new thought in my head, when all my other prospects are closed off. I am so miserable and desperate right now, if you say one more word, I will marry you on your own terms. Take the warning and go!”
“I will not stand up from my knees until you say yes!”
“If I say yes, you will regret it, and I will regret it, when it is far too late.”
“We will both bless the day I pressed you, and you agreed.”
“Do you really feel as confident as you sound?”
“Judge for yourself. I speak from my own family. My own mother was once exactly where you are. She loved a man who was unworthy of her, and she married my father only out of respect and admiration. You saw how happy they were, living at Frizinghall. Is that not all the encouragement we could ever need?”
“…You will not rush me?”
“All the time in the world is yours.”
“You will never ask me for more love than I am able to give?”
“My angel! I only ask for you.”
“Take me.”

She accepted him. He burst into an unholy rapture, pulling her closer and closer until their faces touched – and I, fumbling to shut my eyes, was a single second too late. I had foolishly expected her to resist. She did not. For any right-thinking woman of my station, that told me everything I needed to know. I waited for them to walk off arm in arm to announce their engagement, but there was one last formality Godfrey insisted on. He sat down on the ottoman right next to her, unchallenged.
“Shall I speak to your mother?” he asked. “Or will you?”
She refused both options. “Do not tell anyone until mamma is better. I want this kept a secret for now, Godfrey. Go home, and come back this evening. We have been alone together for far too long.”

She stood up, and for the first time looked straight toward the small curtained nook I was hiding in.
“Who drew those curtains closed?” she exclaimed. “The room is stuffy enough without blocking all the air out.”
She stepped straight toward me, hand outstretched to yank the curtain open – and right before she could, the voice of the ruddy young footman echoed up the stairs, loud and panicked.
“Miss Rachel! Where are you, Miss Rachel?”
She sprang back from the curtain and ran for the door. The footman stumbled inside, all his colour drained away.
“Please come downstairs at once, Miss! Lady Verinder has fainted, and we cannot wake her.”

In the chaos that followed, I was completely forgotten. I slipped out of my hiding spot, down the hall, and Godfrey rushed past me in the hall, heading out to fetch the doctor. “Go help them inside!” he said, pointing toward the parlour. I found Rachel on her knees by the sofa, her mother’s head resting on her bosom. One look at my aunt’s face, knowing what I knew, was enough to tell me the dreadful truth. The doctor arrived only minutes later, sent Rachel out of the room, and told the rest of us that Lady Verinder was dead.

For pious folk looking for proof of how little the worldly value salvation, it will interest you to know that the doctor did not even glance at my collection of evangelical tracts laid out on the nearby table. Later that night, I peeked into the breakfast room and the library. My aunt had died without opening a single one of the pious letters I had sent her over the past weeks. I was so shocked by that loss that it never even occurred to me until several days later, that she had also died without ever giving me the small, promised legacy she had left me in her will.
