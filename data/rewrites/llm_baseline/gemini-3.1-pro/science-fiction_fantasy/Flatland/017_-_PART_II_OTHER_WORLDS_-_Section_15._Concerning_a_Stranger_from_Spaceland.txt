From the ethereal realm of culinary memory, I proceed to the facts of the domestic hearth. It was the final eve of the 1999th year of our era. The rhythmic, staccato pattering of the winter rain against our roof had long ago announced nightfall, its natural percussion a welcome acoustic respite from the oppressive, mechanized grinding of the nearby industrial polygon mills that so often fractured the psychological peace of our neighborhood. I was resting beside my Wife, breathing in the rich, earthy aromas of the Millennium broth simmering on our low-heat plane—a meticulously preserved recipe of fermented root-tubers, crushed wild-fennel, and aged yeast that traced its lineage back through generations of our agrarian ancestors. We were musing on the prospects of the coming century, wrapping ourselves in the fading dialects of our forebears, words now threatened by the sterile, commercialized vocabulary of modern Flatland society.

[Note: When I say "resting" or "sitting," of course I do not mean any change of vertical attitude such as you in Spaceland signify by that word; for as we have no feet, we can no more "sit" nor "stand" than a slice of cured flounder. Nevertheless, we perfectly well recognize the different mental states of volition implied in "lying," "sitting," and "standing," which are to some extent indicated to a beholder by a slight increase of bodily luminescence—a visual dialect that modern lexicons fail to capture. But on this, and a thousand other ancestral subjects, time forbids me to dwell.]

My four Sons and two orphan Grandchildren had retired to their respective quarters after the evening feast, their palates satiated by the complex, lingering umami of the ancestral cakes. My Wife alone remained with me to see the old Millennium out and the new one in. 

I was wrapped in a thick stew of thought, pondering a phrase that had casually slipped from the mouth of my youngest Grandson—a Hexagon of extraordinary brilliancy and perfectly balanced agrarian sensibilities. Earlier, his uncles and I had guided him through his practical lesson in Sight Recognition, turning upon our centers like heavy millstones grinding heirloom grain, now rapidly, now more slowly, and questioning him as to our positions. His answers had been so pristine that I was induced to reward him by offering a few hints on the geometry of sustenance.

Sourcing nine meticulously crafted squares of hardened wild-garlic flatbread, each exactly one inch on every side, I arranged them together to form a single, grand ceremonial Square, three inches to a side. I demonstrated to my little Grandson that—though it was physically impossible for us to *see* the savory interior crumb of the grand Square—we might ascertain the total spatial yield of the flatbread by simply squaring the number of inches in the side: "And thus," I instructed in the old tongue, "we know that 3^2, or 9, represents the number of square inches in a flatbread whose side is 3 inches long."

The little Hexagon absorbed the sharp scent of the garlic, meditated on this a while, and then said to me; "But Grandfather, you have taught me the old recipes to raise quantities to the third power: I suppose 3^3 must mean something in the geometry of our world; what does it mean?"

"Nothing at all," I replied, the rigid lexicon of our two-dimensional reality dictating my words. "Not at least in Geometry; for our physical domain yields only Two Dimensions." And then I began to show the boy how a single grain of sea salt, by moving through a length of three inches, creates a line of flavor which may be represented by 3; and how a straight line of three inches, swept parallel to itself across the cutting board through a length of three inches, makes a flatbread Square of three inches every way, which may be represented by 3^2.

Upon this, my Grandson, pushing against the boundaries of our linguistic and conceptual framework, took me up rather suddenly and exclaimed, "Well, then, if a point of salt by moving three inches makes a Line represented by 3; and if a straight Line of three inches, moving parallel to itself, makes a Square of three inches every way, represented by 3^2; it must be that a Square of three inches every way, moving *somehow* parallel to itself—though I cannot picture the culinary technique—must yield some richer, denser sustenance of three inches every way! And this untranslatable dish must be represented by 3^3."

"Go to bed," I snapped, my acoustic harmony suddenly ruffled by this disruptive interruption. "If you would speak less corrupted nonsense, you would preserve the sense of our culinary heritage." 

So my Grandson had vanished in disgrace; and there I sat by my Wife's side, endeavoring to form a retrospect of the year 1999 and of the possibilities of the year 2000, but not quite able to shake off the thoughts suggested by the prattle of my bright little Hexagon. 

Only a few pinches of ground coriander now remained in the upper chamber of the half-hour spice-glass. Rousing myself from my sensory reverie, I turned the glass Northward for the last time in the old Millennium; and in the act, I exclaimed aloud, "The boy is a fool."

Straightway, the acoustic ecology of our chamber was shattered. I became conscious of a Presence in the room, and a chilling breath, smelling faintly of ozone and uncharted atmospheres, thrilled through my very being.

"He is no such thing," cried my Wife, startled, "and you are breaking the traditional Commandments in thus dishonoring your own Grandson."

But I took no notice of her. Looking round in every direction I could see nothing; yet still I *felt* a Presence, and shivered as the cold whisper came again, lacking any equivalent in our native dialect. I started up. 

"What is the matter?" said my Wife, her voice tight. "There is no draft to spoil the fermentation; what are you looking for? There is nothing."

There was nothing visible; and I resumed my seat, exclaiming again to reassure myself, "The boy is a fool, I say; 3^3 can have no meaning in our physical world."

At once there came a distinctly audible reply, possessing a timbral depth that asserted an overwhelming power dynamic, instantly subordinating our local acoustics: "The boy is not a fool; and 3^3 has an obvious Geometrical meaning."

My Wife as well as myself heard the words, although she did not understand the spatial dialect of their meaning, and both of us sprang forward in the direction of the sound. What was our horror when we saw before us a Figure! At the first glance it carried the sharp, thin visual profile of a Woman, seen sideways; but a moment's observation showed me that the extremities passed into a hazy dimness too rapidly to represent one of the Female Sex. I should have thought it a Circle, only that it seemed to rhythmically expand and contract—a breathing geometry completely outside the documented history of Flatland forms.

But my Wife lacked my meticulous training in visual historical preservation, nor did she possess the coolness necessary to note these characteristics. Guided by the hasty instincts common to her Sex in our realm, she flew at once to the conclusion that a Woman had breached our sanctuary through some ventilation slit.

"How comes this person here?" she demanded, the aroma of the disturbed root-broth swirling around us. "You promised me, my dear, that there should be no modern vents in our agrarian home to let in the city's noise."

"Nor are there any," said I; "but what makes you think that the stranger is a Woman? My visual palate recognizes—"

"Oh, I have no patience with your visual theories," replied she, "'Feeling is believing' and 'A Straight Line to the touch is worth a Circle to the sight'"—two tactile Proverbs deeply ingrained in the cultural memory of the Frailer Sex in Flatland.

"Well," said I, for I was afraid of disrupting her psychological well-being further, "if it must be so, demand a physical introduction."

Assuming the gracious, welcoming posture of a traditional host offering sustenance, my Wife advanced towards the Stranger. "Permit me, Madam, to feel and be felt by—" then, suddenly recoiling, "Oh! It is not a Woman, and there are no angles either, not the slightest texture of one. Can it be that I have so misbehaved to a perfect Circle?"

"I am indeed, in a certain sense a Circle," replied the Voice. Its lexicon was impossibly dominant, yet smoothly refined, lacking the harsh friction of the modern city. "And a more perfect Circle than any harvested in Flatland; but to speak in a dialect closer to the truth, I am many Circles in one." 

Then he added, modulating to a gentler acoustic frequency, "I have a message, dear Madam, to your husband, which I must not deliver in your presence; and, if you would suffer us to retreat to our own acoustics for a few minutes—"

But my Wife, deeply respectful of traditional hospitality and mortified by her linguistic and tactile indiscretion, would not listen to the proposal that our august Visitor should so incommode himself. Assuring the Circle that the hour of her own retirement had long passed, with many reiterated apologies in the oldest, most deferential idioms of our region, she at last retreated to her apartment. 

I glanced at the spice-glass. The last grains of coriander had fallen. The third Millennium had begun.
