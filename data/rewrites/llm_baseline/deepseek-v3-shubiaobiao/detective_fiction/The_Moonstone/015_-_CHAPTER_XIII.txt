I entered my lady’s sitting room to find her visibly unsettled. The mere mention of Sergeant Cuff’s request to speak with her drew a sharp reaction—her lips tightened, her fingers twined restlessly in her lap.

*"Must* I see him?" she asked. "Can you not handle this in my stead, Gabriel?"

Her reluctance puzzled me, and I must have shown it plainly, for she sighed and continued with uncharacteristic hesitation.

"My nerves are not what they should be," she confessed. "There is something about that London policeman—something ominous. I cannot shake the feeling that his presence here will bring nothing but trouble. Foolish, I know, but there it is."

I scarcely knew how to respond. The more I spoke with Sergeant Cuff, the more I found him a shrewd and reasonable man. Yet my lady, ordinarily so composed, seemed almost superstitious in her aversion.

After a moment, she steadied herself, that iron resolve I had long admired reasserting itself. "Very well. If it must be done, it must. But I will not face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and remain with us."

It was the first time I had seen her so affected since her youth. I returned to the boudoir, where Mr. Franklin excused himself to join Mr. Godfrey in the garden—the latter’s departure now imminent. Meanwhile, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to my lady’s chamber.

I could not miss the way her face paled slightly at his entrance. Yet she held herself with dignity, inquiring if he objected to my presence.

"Not at all," the Sergeant replied smoothly. "Mr. Betteredge’s insights have already proven valuable. I welcome his attendance."

With that, we took our seats, and the Sergeant began without preamble.

"I have formed certain conclusions regarding the Moonstone’s disappearance," he said, "though I will reserve those for the present. My immediate concern is the smear upon Miss Verinder’s sitting-room door—paint, as I’ve noted, transferred from a garment. That garment must be found before we proceed further."

My lady’s gaze flicked to me. "Does this mean you believe the thief will be uncovered?"

"I do not yet say *thief*, my lady," Cuff corrected. "Only that the Diamond is missing. The stained fabric may lead us to it."

"And how do you propose to find it?" she asked. "My servants have already endured one indignity at the hands of your predecessor. I will not subject them to another."

(Here, I confess, my admiration for her swelled. A mistress who defends her staff so fiercely is rare indeed.)

Sergeant Cuff inclined his head. "Precisely the difficulty I wished to address. Superintendent Seegrave’s heavy-handedness has made the servants wary. If they suspect they are singled out, they may resist cooperation. Yet their wardrobes *must* be examined—not for the Diamond, but for the marked clothing."

A standoff, then. My lady said as much, though in finer words.

"I have a solution," Cuff offered. "Let me explain the situation openly—to all the household. If they understand the search is universal, extending even to your own wardrobe, my lady, they will see it as fair. No one singled out; no cause for resentment."

I frowned. "The women will still take it as accusation."

"Not if they are told *everyone* who slept here that night is subject to inspection," Cuff countered. "A formality, yes—but one that assures them of impartiality."

My lady considered this, then nodded. "You are certain it is necessary?"

"It is the swiftest path to the truth."

She moved to ring for her maid. "You shall address them with the keys to my own wardrobe in hand."

But Cuff hesitated. "One precaution first. We must ensure *all* in the house consent—not only the servants."

My lady looked puzzled. "Miss Verinder is the only other lady present. As for gentlemen, my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite, will hardly refuse."

At that moment, as if summoned, Mr. Godfrey knocked to bid farewell—Mr. Franklin accompanying him. When the situation was explained, Mr. Godfrey promptly ordered his luggage brought back inside and handed his key to the Sergeant without protest.

"A mere formality," Cuff acknowledged with a courteous nod. "But the example set by gentlemen eases the way with the staff."

With Mr. Godfrey’s departure deferred, only Miss Rachel’s compliance remained. My lady excused herself to secure her daughter’s keys.

We waited. And waited.

At last, a note arrived—not with keys, but with a few penciled lines from my lady. Miss Rachel had refused outright. Pressed for reasons, she had wept, then declared, *"I won’t, because I won’t. Force me if you must, but I yield to nothing else."*

Dismayed, I relayed this to the Sergeant. His reaction was infuriatingly calm.

"Ah," he said. Just that—*Ah.* As if he had expected it.

"Does this end the search?" I demanded.

"It does. We cannot proceed partially. Mr. Ablewhite’s luggage may go, and return the washing-book to the girl who brought it." He set the book aside, idly trimming his nails with sudden focus.

"You seem untroubled," I observed.

"I am."

"Why would Miss Rachel hinder you? It is in her interest to aid this inquiry!"

"Patience, Mr. Betteredge. All in good time."

Had I been less devoted to Rachel, or sharper of wit, I might have grasped his meaning then. But I did not.

"What now?" I asked.

Sergeant Cuff closed his penknife. "Let us walk in the garden," he said. "I should like to see the roses."
