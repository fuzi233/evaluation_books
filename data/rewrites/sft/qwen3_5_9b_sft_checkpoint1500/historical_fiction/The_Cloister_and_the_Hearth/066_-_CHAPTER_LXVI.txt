Two nights following the previous evening, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea waited together for Gerard's return, expecting supper. By nine o'clock, patience had worn thin, and Pietro, growing irritable, dispatched Andrea to Gerard's chamber on a slim hope that his friend might have slipped inside unnoticed. Andrea, shrugging off the uncertainty, ventured forth.

He returned empty-handed regarding Gerard, yet carrying a crumpled scrap of paper. Though illiterate in the scholarly sense, Andrea possessed a sharp eye and knew precisely how Pietro Vanucci inscribed his thoughts upon parchment.

"That is for you, I believe," Andrea declared, pride swelling in his chest at his own deduction.

Pietro seized the note and read it aloud, his voice laced with biting satire as he recited the lines: *"Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden."* To which Pietro retorted, "So 'tis, my lad, yet that is no cause for wandering at supper-hour. Food is no burden."

*"Wear my habits!"* continued the voice of the letter. *"And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two."* Pietro paused, asking, "Why, Andrea?"

*"For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber,"* the note concluded. *"Trouble not to look for it. 'Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born—oh happy night that rids me of it. Adieu! adieu! The broken-hearted Gerard."*

"Here is a wretched jest from a peevish rogue," Pietro muttered, though his pallid cheeks and trembling teeth betrayed a far deeper terror than his words allowed. Andrea, however, could contain himself no longer. Filling the house with his screams, he cried out, "O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! Help! He is destroying himself! Oh, good people, aid me to save him!"

His frantic alarm drew a crowd. Yet, despite Andrea's desperate pleas, the gathered folk remained sluggish. "Go down to the river?" someone suggested doubtfully. "Nay, 'twas not their affair." "What section of the river?" another queried. "A wild-goose chase," whispered others. Indeed, venturing near the Tiber after sunset was ill-advised; too many spirits haunted those banks through the long, quiet hours of night.

However, a lackey who had lingered opposite the house stepped forward, offering to accompany Andrea. This offer swayed three or four of the younger bystanders. A small band thus formed, making its way toward the river.

As they approached, the lackey questioned Andrea. Sobbing, the boy recounted the letter and Gerard's recent brooding. Unbeknownst to most, this lackey served as a spy for Princess Claelia. As they drew nearer, their rapid exchange of Italian fell silent when they caught the cool breath of the stream and the faint, earthy scent of the water under the calm spring sky. They froze.

Above them, the moon hung bright and unclouded in a vast expanse. Their footsteps echoed loudly and ominously against the silence. Their tongues were stilled. Turning a corner, they encountered a solitary figure who halted, hesitating at their sudden presence.

The man wore no hat, his hair matted and dripping with moisture that gleamed in the silver light. At the next stride, they saw his garments were soaked through.

"He is here!" cried one of the youths, unfamiliar with Gerard's visage.

The stranger spun instantly and bolted. They pursued him with furious speed, Andrea leading, the Princess's lackey close behind. Andrea closed the gap briefly, but the fugitive twisted abruptly into a narrow alleyway. Andrea, unable to brake his momentum, shot past, leaving the pursuers in a labyrinthine maze where chasing a swift-footed ghost-knight who knew every stone was futile. They could follow him only by sound.

Returning to their companions, they found them standing exactly where the man had stood, utterly bewildered. "Are ye surely certain?" they asked. "He must have entered the river. In God's name, why did he flee at our approach?"

Then Vanucci spoke softly, "Friends, I think this has naught to do with whom we seek. What shall we do, Andrea?"

The lackey interjected, "Let us track him to the waterside to confirm. Observe, he came dripping all the way." This counsel was accepted, and with ease, they traced the path of the wet footprints.

Yet, soon a fresh puzzle arose. After traveling barely fifty paces, the trail of droplets veered sharply away from the riverbank, guiding them instead toward the heavy iron gates of a large, somber building—a monastery. They stood frozen before the dark edifice, feeling it harbor some terrible secret.

Suddenly, Andrea let out a cry. "Behold, the drops return!" he exclaimed. "And this path leads to the river."

Resuming the chase, it quickly became evident that the moisture was once again pointing home. The trail grew wetter and heavier until they reached the edge of the Tiber. There, on the muddy bank, lay what appeared to be a bucketful of water spilled directly from the stream.

At first, shouts rang out, convinced they had solved the riddle. But reflection set in, revealing the truth: it meant nothing. A man had indeed entered the water and emerged safe, but he was not Gerard. One suggested knowing a fisherman nearby who owned nets and drags. They located the man, paying him generously to cast nets below the spot and drag them upstream and downstream, promising gold if he found the body.

Thus, they ran uselessly up and down the silent, voiceless river, guarding its strange secrets. Suddenly, Andrea returned to the house with a cry of hope. He arrived within half an hour, only to groan and wring his hands.

"What hour is it?" asked the lackey.

"It is four hours past midnight," Andrea replied.

"My pretty lad," the lackey said solemnly, "pray a mass for thy friend's soul, for he is no longer among the living."

Morning broke, weary and cold. Exhausted by fatigue, heartbroken by failure, Andrea and Pietro returned home, their spirits crushed. The days that followed rolled on, as mute and still as the Tiber itself, speaking no word of Gerard's fate.
