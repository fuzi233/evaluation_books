For the remainder of the afternoon, we pressed onward along a magnificent roadway trending steadily toward the northwest. Infadoos and Scragga walked beside us, while their followers marched a hundred paces ahead, forming a disciplined vanguard.

"Infadoos," I asked eventually, "who constructed this road?"

"It was made, my lord, in ancient times, by hands forgotten by history, not even by the Wise Woman Gagool herself, who has lived for countless generations," he replied. "Our ancestors cannot remember its creation. No living craftsman can fashion a path like this today, yet King Twala ensures not a single blade of grass grows upon it."

"And whose hand penned the mysterious inscriptions on the walls of the caves we traversed?" I continued, gesturing to the Egyptian-style sculptures etched into the stone.

"The same hands that built the road wrote these wonders," Infadoos answered solemnly. "Their names are lost to us."

"When did the Kukuana people first arrive in this land?"

"My lord," he explained, pointing northward, "this race descended here like a storm's breath ten thousand moons ago, fleeing from the great northern realms. They were halted by the towering mountains that ring this land—a barrier spoken of in the voices of our fathers and confirmed by Gagool, the Smeller of Witches. Yet the soil was fertile, and they settled, growing strong until their numbers swelled like the sea-sands. When King Twala calls his regiments, their plumes darken the plains to the horizon."

"If the land is walled by mountains," I mused, "against whom do these mighty armies fight?"

"Nay, my lord," Infadoos corrected. "Though enclosed, the north opens to the sky. From unknown lands, warrior clouds descend periodically, sweeping down to devour us. For three parts of a man's life, there has been war. Thousands fell, yet we destroyed those who sought to eat us alive. Since that final battle, peace has reigned."

"Must your warriors not grow weary of standing guard with their spears?"

"There was one war," Infadoos admitted, his tone shifting. "A civil strife. 'Dog ate dog.' My master, the late King Kafa, died when we reached adulthood. His successor, Imotu, reigned briefly and fathered a son with his favorite wife. However, when the infant was merely three years old, a famine swept the land, leaving the people starving and desperate, like lions seeking prey.

"Gagool, the terrible and immortal prophetess, then proclaimed to the mob: 'The King Imotu is no king!' At that moment, Imotu lay ill within his kraal, unable to move. Gagool emerged from a hut, leading out Twala, my own half-brother. Though born of the same womb as Imotu at the same hour, Twala had been hidden away in the caves by his mother, who loved the feebler child. Stripping Twala of his waist-cloth, Gagool revealed the mark of the sacred snake coiled around his middle—the sign of the rightful heir—and cried aloud, 'Behold your king, whom I have saved for you!'"

"But I knew it was false!" Infadoos confessed, his voice trembling slightly. "Imotu, the elder twin, was the lawful sovereign. Just as the crowd roared, 'The King! The King!', Imotu, dragging his wife by the hand and followed by his son Ignosi—the Lightning—emerged from his hut. 'What is this noise?' he asked. 'Why cry for a king?'

"Then Twala, rushing forward, seized his brother by the hair and stabbed him through the heart. The fickle people, worshipping the rising sun, clapped their hands, crying, 'Twala is King! Now we know it!'

"And what befell Imotu's wife and her son, Ignosi?" I asked.

"Nay, my lord," Infadoos said. "Upon seeing her husband fall, the Queen seized the child and fled. Two days later, starving and shunned by the populace who hated the fallen, she reached a distant kraal. There, a little girl shared her corn, blessing the act before vanishing into the mountains with the boy. Neither mother nor child have been seen since."

"If Ignosi had lived," I pondered, "he would be the true King of the Kukuanas?"

"That is so, my lord," Infadoos affirmed. "The sacred snake marks him. If he lives, he rules. Alas, he is long dead."

He then pointed to a vast enclosure below us, surrounded by a deep ditch and a sturdy fence. "That is the kraal where the Queen and the Prince vanished. It is here we will sleep tonight, unless..." he hesitated, glancing dubiously at the others. "...unless my lords require rest upon this earth."

"When we dwell among the Kukuanas," I declared grandly, turning to address Good, who trudged sullenly behind, frustrated by his flannel shirt billowing in the breeze, "we shall do as the Kukuanas do."

To my surprise, I collided with Umbopa, who had been walking directly behind me. He had clearly listened intently to my conversation, his face a mask of confusion as if trying to recall a forgotten dream.

As we pressed toward the undulating plain, the mountains we had crossed loomed higher, their peaks "Sheba's Breasts" veiled in diaphanous mist. The landscape transformed into an earthly paradise; the vegetation was lush yet temperate, the air warm and fragrant, blowing a gentle breeze over the slopes. In beauty, natural wealth, and climate, Kukuanaland surpassed everything I had seen in the Transvaal.

Almost immediately upon starting, Infadoos dispatched a runner to warn the kraal of our approach. This messenger moved with extraordinary speed, a feat Infadoos noted he maintained constantly, as running was a highly practiced art among his people.

By the time we were within two miles, the result of his warning became evident. Company after company poured from the gates, marching toward us. Sir Henry laid a hand on my arm, noting the appearance of a warm reception.

"Do not fear, my lords," Infadoos hastened to assure us, sensing my unease. "There dwells no guile in my breast. This regiment falls under my command, and they march out by my order to greet you."

I nodded, though anxiety lingered. Half a mile from the gates, the terrain rose gently, providing a staging ground. Here, the companies formed a spectacular display. Each unit, numbering roughly three hundred, charged swiftly up the incline, spears flashing, plumes waving, to take their appointed stations. Before we reached the slope, twelve such companies—totaling 3,600 men—had filed past and arrayed themselves along the road.

The first company stopped as still as bronze statues. Upon a signal from their commander, distinguished by a leopard-skin cloak, every spear lifted in unison. From three hundred throats erupted the royal salute, a sudden roar of "Koom!" As we passed, the company fell back behind us, joining a column that trailed the entire regiment, known as the "Greys" for their white shields, shaking the ground with their heavy tread.

Branching off Solomon's Great Road, we approached the wide fossé surrounding the kraal, a mile-long moat fenced by a palisade of tree trunks. A primitive drawbridge spanned the gateway, lowered by the guards to admit us. Inside, the settlement was meticulously organized. Wide pathways intersected at right angles, dividing the dome-shaped huts into square blocks, each quarter housing a specific company. Unlike Zulu huts, these featured doorways and were surrounded by verandas paved with hard-packed lime dust.

Hundreds of women lined the central pathway, drawn by curiosity. For a native race, they were strikingly beautiful: tall, graceful, with aquiline features and fine, curly hair. Yet, what struck us most was their profound dignity. They observed us with quiet decorum, devoid of the ruder astonishment common elsewhere. Even when Infadoos discreetly pointed out the "snowy legs" of Good—a stark contrast to their darker complexions—they offered no comment, allowing only silent admiration.

Reaching the center, Infadoos halted before a large hut encircled by smaller dwellings. "Enter, Sons of the Stars," he boomed in a magniloquent voice. "Rest in our humble habitations. Food shall be brought to you—honey, milk, and an ox or two—to relieve your hunger."

"It is good," I agreed. "We are weary of traveling through realms of air; now let us rest."

Inside, couches of tanned skins awaited us, alongside water for washing. Soon, a line of damsels entered bearing milk, roasted maize, and honey pots, followed by youths driving a fat young ox. With dexterity, a youth slit the beast's throat. Within ten minutes, the carcass was skinned and jointed; the finest cuts were reserved for us, while the rest were distributed to the surrounding warriors as a tribute.

Umbopa began cooking our portion in a large earthenware pot over an outdoor fire, assisted by a prepossessing young woman. When the meal neared readiness, we sent word for Infadoos and Scragga, the King's son, to join us. They arrived shortly, sitting on small stools rather than squatting, as is customary elsewhere.
