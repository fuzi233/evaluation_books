After leaving Vienna—long before the Danube ever thinks of Budapest—the river loosens its collar and goes feral. The stern banks that taught it manners fall away, and the water spreads as though it has forgotten it is meant to be a single thing. On the maps, that whole expanse is brushed in a thin, uncertain blue that pales as it wanders from the main line, and across it, in straggling letters that look half-erased, stands the word Sümpfe: marshes.

In high flood the place is almost drowned outright: sand and shingle and the low islands vanish under one continuous, heaving sheet. But in an ordinary season—if anything here can be called ordinary—the willows rule, a vast sea of humble bushes that never become trees. They have no stubborn trunks, no honest backbone. They remain rounded, soft-topped, pliant as the grasses, and they answer the least pressure of wind with a collective shiver. Their leaves turn and flash; the green surface lifts in long swells like waves, and then, as the branches twist, the underside shows—silver-white, bright as fish bellies in sun—so that the whole plain looks alive, moving, breathing, pretending.

The Danube is happy here, too happy. Released from banks, it wanders among an intricate web of channels that cut the islands into pieces and remake them again. Water pours down broad avenues with a shouting sound, making whirlpools that suddenly open like mouths, eddies that spin in place, rapids that foam and snap at the shallows. It claws at sandy edges, pulls whole clumps of willow into itself, and builds new islands by the dozen—little temporary lives of shingle and root that change shape from day to day and may be obliterated entirely when flood-time comes and wipes the slate clean.

This part of the river’s life begins properly soon after Pressburg, and we—two men in a Canadian canoe with a gipsy tent and frying-pan stowed like domestic necessities—reached it on the crest of a rising flood in mid-July. That same morning, when the sky was only beginning to redden before sunrise, we slipped through still-sleeping Vienna. A couple of hours later it was nothing but a smudge of smoke against the blue hills of the Wienerwald. We took breakfast below Fischeramend beneath birches that roared in the wind, and I remember the simple comfort of it—bread broken with the hand, a sharp-smelling cheese, cold slices of something smoked and salty from Pressburg, the butter softening in the heat. Even the river air had a taste then: clean and wet, with the faint metallic tang that comes off stones when water is running fast.

After that, the current did most of our traveling for us. We swept past Orth, Hainburg, Petronell—old Roman Carnuntum, where Marcus Aurelius once wrote his sober thoughts while the world made war around him—and then under the frowning heights of Thelsen where the March steals in from the left and the frontier is crossed between Austria and Hungary. The canoe raced at twelve kilometers an hour; the muddy water—sure sign of flood—sent us grinding onto shingle-beds more than once, and in sudden belching whirlpools it twisted us like a cork until Pressburg’s towers showed against the sky. Then the canoe, leaping like a spirited horse, shot under grey walls, negotiated the sunken chain of the Fliegende Brücke ferry, turned sharp to the left, and plunged on yellow foam into the wilderness beyond—the land of willows.

The change was abrupt, like a set of pictures snapping down in a bioscope: streets and roofs and chimneys, and then—without warning—water and wind and a low horizon crowded with whispering growth. In less than half an hour there was no boat, no fishing-hut, no red roof, no signal of people anywhere. The isolation dropped over us like a garment. We laughed and said we ought to have carried some special passport to enter this separate kingdom of water and willow—this place reserved for other tenants with older rights. The laughter was genuine, yet it had the edge of men aware, even while joking, that a line has been crossed and no one asked permission.

The wind had been hammering us all day, and though it was still early afternoon the ceaseless buffetings made us tired in the bones. We began at once to hunt a place to camp. Landing was difficult; the swirling flood carried us in and flung us out again. The willows clawed at our hands when we seized the branches to stop the canoe. We pulled whole yards of sandbank into the water in our struggle. At last a great sideways blow from the wind shoved us into a backwater, and we beached the bows in a burst of spray. We lay panting and laughing on the hot yellow sand, sheltered from the gale, the sun beating down from a cloudless blue, while all around us the willows danced and shouted. Their leaves clapped and flashed as though applauding our success. The air was full of their smell: green sap bruised by wind, wet bark, and that faint sourness of marsh where old water lies beneath new.

“What a river,” I said, thinking of the way we had followed it from the Black Forest, and how my companion—my friend the Swede—had often been obliged to wade and push in those first shallow miles back in June.

“It won’t stand much nonsense now, will it?” he answered, hauling the canoe farther up the sand. Then, with the practical peace of a man who can sleep anywhere, he made a pillow of his rolled coat and closed his eyes.

I lay beside him, feeling for a moment utterly content in the bath of elements—water and wind and sand and the great fire of the sun. I thought of the distance behind us and of the long stretch ahead to the Black Sea. I thought, too, how lucky I was in my companion, for we had made many journeys together and I knew his steadiness. He had a way of turning even hardship into routine, as if the world, however wild, could be managed with the right knots and a well-tended pan.

Of all the rivers I knew, the Danube impressed us from the beginning with its aliveness. From its small bubbling birth among the pinewood gardens of Donaueschingen to this moment when it began its great game of losing itself among deserted swamps, it seemed like watching the growth of a creature. Sleepy at first, later violent with desire as though becoming conscious of its own deep soul, it rolled through the countries on its mighty shoulders, rough with us sometimes but always friendly—until we could not help but regard it as a Great Personage.

And it told us its secret life, chiefly by sound. At night, lying in our tent, we heard it singing to the moon—an odd sibilant note peculiar to itself, said to come from pebbles tearing along its bed so fast they rasp and whisper. We knew the voices of its gurgling whirlpools, bubbling suddenly where the surface had been calm; the roar of shallows and swift rapids; the deep steady thunder under all surface noise; the ceaseless gnaw of icy water at the banks. When rain fell flat on its face it stood up and shouted. When the wind blew upstream and tried to halt it, its laughter rolled out in great, derisive gusts. It splashed unnecessarily against bridges as if making a point. It chattered self-consciously when there were hills to look at. It assumed an affected dignity passing through towns, too important to laugh. And there were stretches where the sun found it in a slow curve and poured down until steam rose—faint, sweet whisperings then, as though it spoke softly only when no one was near.

In its early life it had tricks. Up in the Swabian forests, before the world had told it what it must become, there were places it chose to vanish through holes in the ground, to appear again beyond porous limestone and begin a new river under another name—leaving so little in its own bed that we had to climb out and push the canoe through miles of shallows. It would lie low like Brer Fox just before little turbulent tributaries came in from the Alps, and refuse to acknowledge them, running for miles side by side with a sharp dividing line and even different levels—pretending the newcomer was beneath notice. Below Passau it gave up that particular vanity, because there the Inn arrives with a power impossible to ignore. It shoves the parent river this way and that against the cliffs, crowding it in the long twisting gorge that follows, forcing it to hurry with great waves and much dashing, and our canoe—slipping from shoulder to breast—had the time of its life among the struggling water. The Inn taught the old river a lesson, and after Passau it no longer pretended not to see new arrivals.

All that was days behind us, and we had since watched other moods. Across the Bavarian wheat plain near Straubing it wandered so slowly in blazing June that one could imagine only the surface inches were water while below, hidden as by a silken mantle, a whole army of Undines moved silently toward the sea, leisurely so as not to be discovered. Much, too, we forgave the Danube because of its friendliness to birds and animals along its shores. Cormorants lined lonely banks like short black palings. Grey crows crowded shingle-beds. Storks fished in shallow vistas between islands. Hawks, swans, marsh birds filled the air with glinting wings and petulant cries. Once, at sunrise, we saw a deer leap with a splash and swim past the bows, and after that it was hard to resent the river’s tricks. Fawns peered at us from underbrush; stags stared with calm brown eyes as we charged round a corner into a new reach. Foxes haunted the banks, tripping daintily among driftwood, vanishing so suddenly one could never quite understand how.

But after Pressburg everything altered. The Danube became serious. It ceased trifling, as though aware it was half-way to the Black Sea and already within smelling distance of stranger countries where games are not permitted. It split into three arms that did not meet again for a hundred kilometers, and for a canoe there was no sign at all which was meant to be followed.

“If you take a side channel,” the Hungarian officer had told us in the Pressburg shop while we bought provisions, “and the flood goes down, you may find yourselves forty miles from anywhere, high and dry, and you may easily starve. There are no people, no farms, no fishermen. I warn you not to continue. The river is rising, and this wind will increase.”

The rising water did not alarm us. But the idea of being stranded when the river withdrew, leaving us marooned on bare shingle amid miles of swamp, was a practical danger. We laid in extra provisions—more bread than usual, smoked bacon, onions and garlic, a small hard cheese that would keep, and a precious little sack of flour that would at least allow a kind of rough pan-cake if all else failed. The officer’s prophecy proved accurate in one respect, at any rate: the wind, blowing down a perfectly clear sky, increased steadily until it had the dignity of a westerly gale.

We camped earlier than usual, though the sun still stood a good hour or two above the horizon. My companion slept again on the hot sand, and I wandered about our little “hotel” as one examines a kitchen before deciding where to set the fire and how to keep supplies dry. The island was less than an acre, a mere sandy bank only two or three feet above the river. It was triangular, with its apex upstream. At the far end, pointing toward the sunset, flying spray lifted from broken wave-crests and blew away in sheets, and the air there tasted of raw water—cold on the tongue despite the heat.

I stood at that point for several minutes watching the impetuous crimson flood bear down with a shouting roar, dashing against the bank as though it meant to sweep the entire island away, then swirling by in two foaming streams on either side. The ground seemed to tremble with the shock and rush. The willows, lashed by wind, increased a curious illusion that the island itself moved—as if it were not fixed land at all but something afloat, only pretending to be solid.

Above, for a mile or two, I could see the river descending upon us like a sliding hill white with foam, leaping everywhere to show itself to the sun. From the lower end, where shadow gathered, the water looked dark and angry. Only the backs of waves were visible, streaked with foam, pushed forcibly by great puffs of wind that fell upon them from behind. The river poured in and out among islands for a short mile, then vanished with a huge sweep into the willows, which closed around it like a herd of monstrous antediluvian creatures crowding down to drink. They made me think of sponge-like growths that sucked the river up into themselves—an image both absurd and unpleasant, for it suggested hunger.

The rest of the island was too thickly grown with willow to make walking pleasant, but I made the tour. Everywhere the bushes pressed close, innumerable, shoulder to shoulder. Altogether it was impressive—the loneliness, the bizarre suggestion—and as I gazed, long and curiously, something stirred unbidden in me. Midway through delight at the wild beauty, a feeling of disquietude crept in, almost alarm.

A rising river always carries something ominous: many of the little islands in sight would likely be gone by morning, swept away without drama. The resistless thundering flood touched the sense of awe. Yet I knew the uneasiness lay deeper than awe. It was not simply the power of wind—this shouting gale that might almost lift acres of willow and scatter them like chaff. The wind seemed to be enjoying itself, and I was aware of sharing its great game with a kind of exhilaration. No, the distress was vaguer, harder to seize. It had something to do with our insignificance, perhaps, and with the thought that we had come—lightly, with tent and pan and a man’s confidence in his own habits—into the reach of forces that could take us without noticing.

But more than the river, more than the wind, the willows troubled me. Not any single bush, of course, but their multitude—acres upon acres of them, crowding, swarming, pressing upon the water as if to suffocate it, standing mile after mile beneath the sky with an attitude I could only describe, irrationally, as watchful. The mind, besieged by their number, began to invent intention. They seemed a power—mighty, patient, and not altogether friendly.

Mountains overawe, oceans terrify, forests cast their spell; but these things, at some point, connect with human experience. They exalt even when they frighten. This willow world felt different. Something emanated from it that pressed upon the heart. Awe, yes—but awe with a vague terror running through it like a colder current. Their serried ranks, darker as shadow thickened, moving furiously yet softly, woke the unwelcome suggestion that we had trespassed on the borders of an alien country, where we were intruders—unwanted, uninvited, and perhaps in danger.

The feeling did not yet become outright menace. Still, it did not leave me, even when I returned to practical matters: putting up the tent in the teeth of the wind, setting our few belongings in order, making our temporary home against the coming night. I said nothing to my companion. He was, in my opinion, a man without imagination; I could not have explained what I meant, and he would have laughed, missing the point by inches and calling it a mile.

There was a slight depression in the center of the island, and there we pitched the tent, using the surrounding willows as a partial break against the gale.

“A poor camp,” the imperturbable Swede observed when the canvas at last stood upright. “No stones and precious little firewood. I’m for moving on early tomorrow—eh? This sand won’t hold anything.”

We had both endured tents collapsing at midnight and had learned a few devices. We anchored the gipsy house as securely as we could, then set about collecting a store of wood to last until bed-time. Willow bushes do not willingly drop branches; driftwood was our only fuel. We hunted the shores, the two of us moving like housekeepers in a storm, scanning for anything dry enough to burn. Everywhere the banks were crumbling as the rising flood tore at them, carrying away whole slices of the island with a splash and a gurgle.

“The island’s smaller than when we landed,” said the accurate Swede. “It won’t last long at this rate. We’d better drag the canoe close to the tent and be ready to start at a moment’s notice. I shall sleep in my clothes.”

He was a little distance off along the bank. I heard his rather jolly laugh as he spoke, and then, a moment later—

“By Jove!”

His voice changed. The laugh fell out of it.

“What in the world’s this?” he cried again, and now there was seriousness—something even he could not file away as mere inconvenience.

I ran to him. He stood pointing over the river at something in the water.

“Good heavens, it’s a man’s body!” he cried. “Look!”

A black thing turned over and over in the foaming waves as it swept rapidly past about twenty feet from shore. It vanished and returned to the surface, helpless-looking. Opposite where we stood it lurched, and for an instant it seemed to look straight at us. We saw eyes reflect the low sun—odd yellow gleams in a dark wet face—then the thing gave a swift gulping plunge and dived out of sight.

“An otter,” we exclaimed together, and laughed too loudly, the relief coming out of us as noise.

It was an otter, alive and hunting; yet for several seconds it had looked exactly like a drowned man turning in the current. Far below it surfaced again, black skin wet and shining in the sunlight, and was gone.

We turned back with our arms full of driftwood, already thinking of flame and the pan—of making something hot, of bringing the day under control with salt and simmering water and the familiar comfort of eating. The river had other ideas. Another interruption pulled us to the bank.

This time it really was a man—and a man in a boat.

Even on the Danube, a small boat is not common in such a reach. Here, in this flooded deserted place, it was startling enough to feel like an event. We stood and stared.

Whether it was the slanting sunlight, or refraction from the brilliantly lit water, I cannot say, but I found it difficult to focus on the apparition. It seemed a man standing upright in a flat-bottomed craft, steering with a long oar, being carried down the opposite shore at a tremendous pace. He appeared to be looking across toward us; then he began gesticulating. His voice came over the water in furious shouting, but the wind ate the words whole. Not a syllable arrived intact. Something about him—the posture, the frantic movements, the violence of his silent message—made an impression on me far beyond its visible cause.

“He’s crossing himself!” I cried. “Look—he’s making the sign of the Cross!”

“I believe you’re right,” the Swede said, shading his eyes and watching until the man was swallowed by the willows downstream, melting into that crimson wall where the sun caught the bend and set the bushes aflame with color. A thin mist had begun to rise; the air turned hazy, softening distances as if the marsh were exhaling.

“But what in the world is he doing at nightfall on this flooded river?” I said, more to myself than to him. “Where is he going—and what did he mean by those signs? Do you think he meant to warn us?”

“He saw our smoke and thought we were spirits,” my companion laughed, though his laughter did not sit as comfortably as usual. “These Hungarians believe in all sorts of rubbish. You remember the shopwoman at Pressburg warning us that no one ever landed here because it belonged to beings outside man’s world. Fairies and elementals, perhaps—demons too, no doubt. That peasant saw people on the islands for the first time in his life,” he added after a pause, “and it scared him, that’s all.”

His voice tried for easy contempt, but his manner had altered; it lacked its customary hardness of certainty. I noticed it at once.

“If they had enough imagination,” I laughed loudly—I remember making as much noise as I could, as though sound itself might keep the world familiar—“they might well people a place like this with the old gods of antiquity. The Romans must have haunted all this region with shrines and sacred groves and elemental deities.”
