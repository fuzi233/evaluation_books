I lay down beneath the shade of a great tree, watching as the children dispersed one by one, or in small clusters, scaling up to their nests high above. They were creatures of perfect rhythm; exhausted by night, refreshed by dawn, they seemed equally content to surrender to sleep and rise anew. Though weariness weighed upon me, I remained awake. Lona had not bid me goodnight, and I was certain she would return.

The moment I saw her again, I was struck by her uncanny resemblance to the Princess. There could be no doubt she was the daughter Adam had spoken of. Yet, in Lona, the dazzling brilliance of Lilith was softened by an innocent childlikeness, deepened by a profound sense of motherhood. "She is probably busy," I murmured to myself, "caring for the child of the woman I encountered fleeing!"—a woman Lona had described as being woefully inadequate in her maternal duties.

Soon, she arrived. She sat beside me, and after a few moments of silent joy, expressed mostly through the gentle stroking of my face and hands, she recounted everything that had transpired since my departure. As we spoke, the moon rose, casting intermittent, trembling glows through the leaves onto her beautiful face—a countenance filled with thought and a burden of care that somehow redeemed and glorified it.

"How can such a child be born of such a mother?" I wondered aloud. "Such a woman, such a princess—it defied understanding." Fortunately, she possessed two parents—or perhaps three. She drew my heart toward her with qualities that mirrored my own inner self. I loved her as one who, regardless of the perfection she might eventually achieve, would forever remain a child. I knew then that I loved her deeply, and that the hope of reunion had been my greatest solace. Every word she spoke seemed to bypass reason and strike directly at my heart, purifying it like truth itself.

She explained that after I left the Orchard Valley, the Giants began to believe more firmly in the actual existence of their neighbors. Consequently, their hostility grew. At times, the Little Ones would witness the Giants trampling furiously, reacting to mere shadows or imagined signs of their presence, while the Little Ones stood nearby, laughing at their foolish rage. Soon, however, this animosity took a practical turn: the Giants began destroying the very trees that bore the fruit the Little Ones depended on.

This crisis drove the Mother of them all to devise a counter-strategy. She assigned her sharpest scouts to eavesdrop at night and discovered that the Giants believed I was hiding nearby, planning to ambush them in the dark once I regained my strength. Concluding that the only way to halt the destruction was to convince the Giants they had abandoned the area, she formulated a plan. The Little Ones must relocate to the forest—beyond the Giants' reach, yet close enough to tend their own trees during the night.

The primary obstacle was the forest floor, which lacked the dense undergrowth needed for concealment. But she reflected that where there were birds, there could be homes for the Little Ones. Possessing eager sympathies for all forms of life, they could learn from the wildest creatures. Why not seek refuge from the cold and their enemies in the tree-tops? Why not abandon hollows in the brushwood for lofty foliage? Why not construct nests where birds built theirs? All that birds could do, the Little Ones could emulate—with one exception: flight.

She addressed the children on the matter, and they listened with approval. They could already climb with ease and had often watched birds constructing their dwellings. The forest trees, though massive, did not seem daunting; they reached higher toward the sky than the Giant-trees, spreading their branches wide, some even stretching downward as if inviting the Little Ones to join them. Perhaps, in the crown of the tallest tree, they might find the bird that laid baby-eggs, sitting upon them until hatched, then tumbling them down to free the young!

Thus reasoned the Lovers, eagerly adopting Lona's suggestion. Soon, they were as at home in the canopy as the birds themselves. The Giants, finding no trace of them on the ground, concluded they had been driven out of the country. Satisfied with this belief, they neglected their trees and nearly ceased to acknowledge the existence of their tiny neighbors.

Lona asked if I had noticed that many of the children had grown. I admitted I hadn't, though I readily believed it possible. She assured me they had matured physically, but added that the true proof lay in their minds. Their intellectual growth since migrating upward had significantly mitigated the alarm the discovery had caused her.

As twilight faded and the moon shone brightly, the Little Ones descended to the valley to gather enough fruit for the following day. The Giants never ventured out in the twilight; to them, it was absolute darkness, and they loathed the moon—they would have extinguished it if they could. However, the Little Ones soon realized that fruit harvested under the pale light spoiled by noon. A new dilemma arose: was it better to pretend abandonment, or to force the bad Giants to leave?

Having explored the forest, they had befriended its inhabitants. Knowing the strength, wisdom, and docility of various animals, they approached them with loving, playful gestures. They quickly secured alliances, addressing horses and elephants as "Brother" or "Sister," eventually assigning individual names to each. It took slightly longer to win over the Bears, but that followed swiftly. One day, she heard a little boy cry, "Ah, Sister Serpent!" to a snake that had bitten him during rough play. Most avoided caterpillars unless observing their transformation, but when the chrysalis emerged with wings, everyone hailed it as "Sister Butterfly," congratulating it on its metamorphosis—a concept they called *repentance*, regarding it as sacred.

One moonlit evening, while gathering fruit, they encountered a woman sitting on the ground with a baby in her lap—the same woman I had met on my journey to Bulika. Mistaking her for a Giantess who had stolen one of their infants (for they considered all babies their property), they attacked her in a multitude, beating her in a chaotic yet bewildering manner. She attempted to flee, but a boy threw himself down, holding her by the feet. Regaining her composure, she recognized her attackers as the very children seeking her hospitality and immediately handed over the baby.

Lona appeared and cradled the infant. While the woman noted that the children struck carefully to avoid harming the babe, the Little Ones observed that, as she surrendered, she hugged and kissed her child exactly as they desired. Concluding she must be a Giantess akin to the "good Giant," they brought her fruit and showered her with childish attentions.

The woman, previously perplexed about where to go—dreading a return to the City where the Princess would surely discover who had lame her leopardess—was delighted by the kindness of the little people. She decided to stay temporarily; she had no trouble with her infant and hoped to find a way back to her wealthy, gem-rich husband, who was rarely unkind.

Here, I must supplement Lona's account with conjecture based on what I knew. Like the other inhabitants of Bulika, she understood the tradition that the Princess lived in terror of an infant destined to bring about her destruction. Unaware of the horrific methods the Princess employed to preserve her youth and beauty—and noting her deteriorating physical state required increased usage of these means—the people interpreted her growing hostility toward children as a sign that her doom was nearing. Although no one dared speak of attempting anything, this fear nurtured hopes of change.

Now, the woman conceived an idea: to fulfill the shadowy prophecy or, at the very least, use the myth to restore herself to her husband. What seemed more probable than that the fated child lay among these very children? They were marvelously brave, whereas the Bulikans were cowards in abject terror of animals! If she could ignite in the Little Ones the ambition to seize the city, then amidst the chaos of the assault, she could slip away unrecognized, hide in her home, and await the outcome. Should the children succeed in expelling the Giants, she would immediately propose this loftier goal while they were still flushed with victory.

By nature, the children were unfit for warfare; they rarely quarreled, never fought, loved every living thing, and hated both inflicting and suffering pain. Yet, they were easily influenced and capable of learning any exercise within their strength. Instantly, she instructed some of the younger ones to throw stones at a target. Soon, they were engrossed in the new game, growing increasingly skilled.

The first practical application of this skill was my own rescue. While gathering fruit, they found me asleep. Returning home, they held a council and returned the next day accompanied by their elephants and horses. Overwhelming the few Giants guarding me, they carried me off. Jubilant over their victory, the smaller boys were childishly boastful; the older boys were less ostentatious, while the girls, though their eyes flashed with intensity, were unusually quiet.

The woman of Bulika undoubtedly felt emboldened. That night, we conversed extensively, primarily about the children's growth and what it signified. Familiar with Lona's ability to perceive truth, I had long admired it; now, I was astonished by her practical wisdom. Had I been more of a child myself, I might have wondered less.

It was still far from morning when I sensed a slight fluttering and scrambling. Rising on my elbow, I saw many Little Ones descending from their nests. They vanished, and silence returned within minutes.
