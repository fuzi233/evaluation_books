Archer had been stunned by old Catherine’s news. It was only natural that Ellen should have come rushing back from Washington at her grandmother’s summons; but that she should mean to stay under that roof—now, too, when Mrs. Mingott had almost regained her formidable health—was harder to understand, and harder still to endure.

Not that Archer believed Ellen’s decision had been coaxed by money. He knew too exactly the paltry sum Count Olenski had allowed her after their separation, knew how little it amounted to in the Mingott arithmetic of comfort. Without her grandmother’s purse it was scarcely a “living” by any standard the family would have named with approval; and now that poor Medora Manson—who drifted in Ellen’s wake like an ineffectual gull—had been ruined, the same few dollars would barely keep the two women fed, clothed, and warm. Yet Ellen had never had the prudence of the needy. She was capable of lavishness from habit and of renunciation from disdain; she could do without the things her relatives called necessities, and Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Welland had long lamented that any one who had known the cosmopolitan plenty of the Olenski establishments should care so little for “how things were done.”

Besides, months had passed since Ellen’s allowance had been cut off; and in all that interval she had made no effort to placate her grandmother. If she had shifted her course now, it was not to win back money. Archer did not have far to seek the reason.

On the way from the ferry she had told him they must remain apart—yet she had said it with her head on his breast. There had been no calculated coquetry in it; she was fighting her fate as he fought his, clinging with desperate fingers to the resolve that they should not betray the people who trusted them. But during the ten days since her return she had perhaps read something in his silence, and in his making no attempt to see her: that he was brooding over a decisive step, a step from which there would be no return. At that thought, fear of her own weakness might have seized her; and she might have felt that, after all, it was safer to accept the compromise society kept ready for such cases—to take the line of least resistance, and let custom do the dirty work.

An hour earlier, when he had rung Mrs. Mingott’s bell, Archer had fancied his path lay straight before him. He meant to have a word alone with Ellen; failing that, to learn from her grandmother on what day and by which train she returned to Washington. That train he intended to take with her, to Washington or farther—Japan, perhaps, the word had flashed through him like a bright strange flavour on the tongue. Wherever she went, he would go: she would understand that at once. And for May he meant to leave a note that would cut off every other alternative.

He had fancied himself not only nerved for the plunge but eager. Yet his first sensation, on hearing the course of events had changed, was relief. And now, walking home from Mrs. Mingott’s, he became aware of a growing distaste for what lay before him. There was nothing unknown in the path he was presumably to tread; he had trodden it before as a free man, accountable to no one, able to lend himself with amused detachment to the game of precautions and prevarications, concealments and compliances, that the part required.

It was called “protecting a woman’s honour.” The novels he had read and the after-dinner talk of his elders had long since initiated him into every detail of the code. But now he saw it differently, and his part in it seemed singularly diminished. It was, in fact, the very role he had watched Mrs. Thorley Rushworth play toward her fond and unperceiving husband: a smiling, bantering, humouring, watchful, incessant lie. A lie by day, a lie by night; a lie in every touch and every look; in every caress and every quarrel; in every word and every silence.

It was easier, and less dastardly, for a wife to play such a part. A woman’s standard of truthfulness was tacitly held to be lower: she was the subject creature, versed in the arts of the enslaved. She could plead nerves and moods, and the right not to be held too strictly to account; and even in the most strait-laced societies the laugh was always against the husband.

But in Archer’s world no one laughed at a wife deceived, and a measure of contempt attached to men who continued their philandering after marriage. In the rotation of crops there was a season for wild oats; they were not to be sown twice. Archer had always shared the view. In his heart he found Lefferts despicable.

Yet to love Ellen Olenska was not to become like Lefferts. For the first time Archer found himself face to face with the dread argument of the individual case. Ellen was like no other woman; he was like no other man; their situation resembled no one else’s; they were answerable to no tribunal but their own judgment.

Yes—but in ten minutes he would be mounting his own doorstep; and there were May, and habit, and honour, and all the old decencies he and his people had believed in. At his corner he hesitated, then walked on down Fifth Avenue.

Ahead of him, in the winter night, loomed a big unlit house. As he drew near he thought how often he had seen it blazing with light, its steps awninged and carpeted, carriages waiting in double line to draw up at the curb. It was in the conservatory, along the side street where the house stretched its dead-black bulk, that he had taken his first kiss from May; it was under the myriad candles of the ballroom that he had seen her appear, tall and silver-shining as a young Diana.

Now the house was as dark as the grave, except for a faint flare of gas in the basement and a light in one upstairs room where the blind had not been lowered. As Archer reached the corner he saw that the carriage at the door was Mrs. Manson Mingott’s. What an opportunity for Sillerton Jackson, if he should happen by.

Old Catherine’s account of Ellen’s attitude toward Mrs. Beaufort had moved Archer; it made New York’s righteous reprobation look like a passing-by on the other side. But he knew well enough what construction the clubs and drawing-rooms would put on Ellen Olenska’s visits to her cousin. He paused and looked up at the lighted window. No doubt the two women were sitting in that room together; Beaufort had likely sought consolation elsewhere. There were rumours he had left New York with Fanny Ring, though Mrs. Beaufort’s posture made the tale seem improbable.

The night gave Archer a Fifth Avenue almost to himself. At that hour most people were indoors, dressing for dinner; and he was secretly glad that Ellen’s exit was likely to be unobserved. As the thought crossed him the door opened, and she came out. Behind her was a faint light, as though some one had carried a lamp down the stairs to show her the way. She turned to say a word to someone; then the door shut and she came down the steps.

“Ellen,” he said in a low voice as she reached the pavement.

She stopped with a slight start—and at that moment he saw two young men in fashionable cut approaching. There was a familiar air in their overcoats, in the way their smart silk mufflers were folded over white ties; and he wondered how youths of their quality happened to be dining out so early. Then he remembered that the Reggie Chiverses, a few doors above, were taking a large party that evening to see Adelaide Neilson in Romeo and Juliet, and guessed the two were among them. They passed under a lamp, and he recognised Lawrence Lefferts and a young Chivers.

A mean desire not to have Ellen seen at the Beauforts’ door vanished as he felt the penetrating warmth of her hand in his.

“I shall see you now—we shall be together,” he broke out, hardly knowing what he said.

“Ah,” she answered, “Granny has told you?”

While he watched her he saw that Lefferts and Chivers, on reaching the far side of the corner, had discreetly struck away across Fifth Avenue. It was the kind of masculine solidarity he himself had often practised; now he sickened at their connivance. Did Ellen truly imagine they could live like this? And if not—what did she imagine?

“Tomorrow I must see you—somewhere we can be alone,” he said, in a voice that sounded almost angry to himself.

She wavered and moved toward the carriage. “But I shall be at Granny’s—for the present, that is,” she added, as if conscious her change of plans required some explanation.

“Somewhere we can be alone,” he insisted.

She gave a faint laugh that grated on him. “In New York? But there are no churches… no monuments.”

“There’s the Art Museum—in the Park,” he said, as she looked puzzled. “At half-past two. I’ll be at the door…”

She turned away without answering and got quickly into the carriage. As it drove off she leaned forward, and he thought she waved her hand in the obscurity.

He stared after her in a turmoil of contradictory feelings. It seemed he had been speaking not to the woman he loved but to another—a woman he was indebted to for pleasures already wearied of. It was hateful to find himself trapped in this hackneyed vocabulary.

“She’ll come,” he said to himself, almost contemptuously.

Avoiding the popular “Wolfe collection,” whose anecdotic canvases filled one of the main galleries of the queer wilderness of cast iron and encaustic tiles known as the Metropolitan Museum, they wandered down a passage to the room where the “Cesnola antiquities” mouldered in unvisited loneliness. They had this melancholy retreat to themselves, and seated on the divan that enclosed the central steam radiator, they stared silently at the glass cabinets in ebonised wood containing the recovered fragments of Ilium.

“It’s odd,” Ellen said at last. “I never came here before.”

“Ah—well. Some day, I suppose, it will be a great Museum.”

“Yes,” she assented absently, as though the word “great” had no nourishment in it.

She rose and wandered across the room. Archer, remaining seated, watched the light movements of her figure—so girlish even under heavy furs—the cleverly planted heron wing in her fur cap, the way a dark curl lay like a flattened vine spiral on each cheek above the ear. His mind, as always when they met, was absorbed in the delicious particulars that made her herself and no other, as though her very presence had a scent and texture the world had lacked before.

Presently he rose and approached the case before which she stood. Its glass shelves were crowded with small broken objects—hardly recognisable domestic utensils, ornaments, and personal trifles—made of glass, clay, discoloured bronze, and other time-blurred substances.

“It seems cruel,” she said, “that after a while nothing matters… any more than these little things, that used to be necessary and important to forgotten people, and now have to be guessed at under a magnifying glass and labelled: ‘Use unknown.’”

“Yes; but meanwhile—”

“Ah, meanwhile—”

As she stood there in her long sealskin coat, hands thrust into a small round muff, veil drawn down like a transparent mask to the tip of her nose, the bunch of violets he had brought her stirring with her quickly taken breath, it seemed incredible that such harmony of line and colour should ever be altered by the stupid law of change.

“Meanwhile everything matters—that concerns you,” he said.

She looked at him thoughtfully, then turned back to the divan. He sat down beside her and waited; but suddenly he heard a step echoing far off down the empty rooms and felt the pressure of the minutes.

“What is it you wanted to tell me?” she asked, as if she too had heard the warning.

“What I wanted to tell you?” he rejoined. “Why—that I believe you came to New York because you were afraid.”

“Afraid?”

“Of my coming to Washington.”

She looked down at her muff, and he saw her hands stir in it uneasily.

“Well—?”

“Well—yes,” she said.

“You were afraid? You knew—?”

“Yes. I knew…”

“Well, then?” he insisted.

“Well, then: this is better, isn’t it?” she returned, with a long questioning sigh.

“Better—?”

“We shall hurt others less. Isn’t it, after all, what you always wanted?”

“To have you here, you mean—in reach and yet out of reach? To meet you in this way, on the sly? It’s the very reverse of what I want. I told you the other day what I wanted.”

She hesitated. “And you still think this—worse?”

“A thousand times!” He paused. “It would be easy to lie to you; but the truth is I think it detestable.”

“Oh, so do I!” she cried, drawing a deep breath of relief.

He sprang up impatiently. “Well, then—it’s my turn to ask: what is it, in God’s name, that you think better?”

She hung her head and continued to clasp and unclasp her hands in her muff. The step drew nearer, and a guardian in a braided cap walked listlessly through the room like a ghost in a necropolis. They fixed their eyes simultaneously on the case opposite; and when the official figure had vanished down a vista of mummies and sarcophagi Archer spoke again.

“What do you think better?”

Instead of answering she murmured: “I promised Granny to stay with her because it seemed to me that here I should be safer.”

“From me?”

She bent her head slightly without looking at him.

“Safer from loving me?”

Her profile did not stir, but he saw a tear overflow on her lashes and hang in the mesh of her veil.

“Safer from doing irreparable harm. Don’t let us be like all the others!” she protested.

“What others? I don’t profess to be different from my kind. I’m consumed by the same wants and the same longings.”

She glanced at him with a kind of terror, and a faint colour crept into her cheeks.

“Shall I—once come to you; and then go home?” she suddenly hazarded in a low clear voice.

The blood rushed to Archer’s forehead.

“Dearest,” he said, without moving. It was as if he held his heart in his hands like a full cup, and the least motion might spill it. Then her last phrase struck his ear and his face clouded.

“Go home? What do you mean by going home?”

“Home to my husband.”

“And you expect me to say yes to that?”

She raised troubled eyes to his. “What else is there? I can’t stay here and lie to the people who’ve been good to me.”

“But that’s the very reason why I ask you to come away!”

“And destroy their lives, when they’ve helped me to remake mine?”

He stood looking down on her in inarticulate despair. It would have been easy to say: Yes—come; come once. He knew what power she would put into his hands if she consented; he could then persuade her not to go back. But something silenced the words. There was in her a passionate honesty that made it inconceivable he should lure her into the familiar trap.

“If I were to let her come,” he said to himself, “I should have to let her go again.” And that could not be imagined.

Yet he saw the shadow of her lashes on her wet cheek and wavered.

“After all,” he began again, “we have lives of our own… There’s no use attempting the impossible. You’re so unprejudiced about some things, so used, as you say, to looking at the Gorgon, that I don’t know why you’re afraid to face our case and see it as it really is—unless you think the sacrifice is not worth making.”

She stood up too, her lips tightening under a rapid frown. “Call it that, then—I must go,” she said, drawing her little watch from her bosom.

She turned away. He followed and caught her by the wrist.

“Well, then: come to me once,” he said, his head turning suddenly at the thought of losing her. For a second or two they looked at each other almost like enemies. “When?” he insisted. “Tomorrow?”

She hesitated. “The day after.”

“Dearest—!” he said again.

She had disengaged her wrist; but they continued for a moment to hold each other’s eyes, and he saw that her face, which had grown very pale, was flooded with a deep inner radiance. His heart beat with awe: he felt he had never before beheld love made visible.

“Oh, I shall be late—good-bye. No, don’t come any farther than this,” she cried, walking hurriedly away down the long room, as if the reflected radiance in his eyes had frightened her. When she reached the door she turned a moment to wave a quick farewell.

Archer walked home alone.

Darkness was falling when he let himself into his house. He looked about at the familiar objects in the hall as though he viewed them from the other side of the grave. The parlour-maid, hearing his step, ran upstairs to light the gas on the upper landing.

“Is Mrs. Archer in?”

“No, sir; Mrs. Archer went out in the carriage after luncheon, and hasn’t come back.”

With a sense of relief he entered the library and flung himself into his armchair. The parlour-maid followed with the student lamp and shook fresh coals onto the dying fire. When she left he sat motionless, elbows on knees, chin on clasped hands, eyes fixed on the red grate.

He sat without conscious thought, without sense of time, in a deep grave amazement that seemed to suspend life rather than quicken it.

“This was what had to be, then… this was what had to be,” he kept repeating, as if he hung in the clutch of doom. What he had dreamed of had been so different that there was a mortal chill in his rapture.

The door opened and May came in.

“I’m dreadfully late—you weren’t worried, were you?” she asked, laying her hand on his shoulder with one of her rare caresses.

He looked up astonished. “Is it late?”

“After seven. I believe you’ve been asleep!” She laughed, and drawing out her hatpins tossed her velvet hat on the sofa. She looked paler than usual, but sparkling with unwonted animation. “I went to see Granny, and just as I was going away Ellen came in from a walk; so I stayed and had a long talk with her. It was ages since we’d had a real talk…”

She had dropped into her usual armchair facing his, running her fingers through her rumpled hair. He fancied she expected him to speak.

“A really good talk,” she went on, smiling with what seemed to Archer an unnatural vividness. “She was so dear—just like the old Ellen. I’m afraid I haven’t been fair to her lately. I’ve sometimes thought—”

Archer stood and leaned against the mantelpiece, out of the lamp’s radius.

“Yes—you’ve thought—?” he echoed as she paused.

“Well, perhaps I haven’t judged her fairly. She’s so different—at least on the surface. She takes up such odd people—she seems to like to make herself conspicuous. I suppose it’s the life she’s led in that fast European society; no doubt we seem dreadfully dull to her. But I don’t want to judge her unfairly.”

She paused again, a little breathless with the unwonted length of her speech, lips slightly parted, a deep blush on her cheeks. As Archer looked at her he was reminded of the glow that had suffused her face in the Mission Garden at St. Augustine. He became aware of the same obscure effort in her, the same reaching out toward something beyond the usual range of her vision.

“She hates Ellen,” he thought, “and she’s trying to overcome the feeling, and to get me to help her overcome it.”

The thought moved him. For a moment he was on the point of breaking the silence between them, of throwing himself on her mercy. But she went on:

“You understand, don’t you, why the family have sometimes been annoyed? We all did what we could for her at first; but she never seemed to understand. And now this idea of going to see Mrs. Beaufort, of going there in Granny’s carriage! I’m afraid she’s quite alienated the van der Luydens…”

“Ah,” said Archer, with an impatient laugh. The open door had closed between them again.

“It’s time to dress; we’re dining out, aren’t we?” he asked, moving from the fire.

She rose also, but lingered near the hearth. As he walked past, she moved forward impulsively, as though to detain him. Their eyes met, and he saw hers were of the same swimming blue as when he had left her to drive to Jersey City. She flung her arms about his neck and pressed her cheek to his.

“You haven’t kissed me today,” she whispered; and he felt her tremble in his arms.
