Two elderly gentlemen sat on the terrace of Broad Place, the newspapers spread open before them like maps of a conquered world. It was early summer, the air already heavy with the promise of heat, yet the country around them remained a patchwork of green and gold, untouched by the chaos of foreign sands.

"The news from the East grows darker," observed the older man, turning a fresh page. "Osman Digna has returned to Suakin. There will be blood spilled soon, I fear."

"And what harm can he do?" asked the younger man, Lieutenant Sutch, setting his paper down with a soft thud. His gaze had already drifted away from the tranquil English lawn. To his mind, the rolling hills of Kent had dissolved. Now, standing before him was the blinding white glare of Suakin, shimmering under a relentless sun. He saw the endless, dusty plains tangled with halfa grass, stretching out until the jagged silhouette of the hills near Khor Gwob pierced the horizon.

"A stifling place, surely?" General Feversham remarked, his voice brisk.

"Appalling," Sutch agreed. "I heard tell of an officer who collapsed on parade at six in the morning, struck down by the sun right through the steel of his helmet. It is a hell of a damp heat." Yet, despite his words, a profound sense of gratitude warmed his chest. "However, I am glad I was there," he admitted, his tone shifting with genuine emotion. "Very glad."

"Indeed," Feversham nodded, leaning forward slightly. "And did you find your ibex?"

"No," Sutch replied firmly. "Every single ibex within a mile's radius had been shot down by the English garrison."

"So? What else kept you busy?"

"There was something to do," Sutch said, his voice steady. "Just that."

For a moment, the silence returned, broken only by the rustle of paper and the distant chirping of insects. Then, a footman approached, placing two small stacks of letters onto the terrace tables. General Feversham swept his fingers over the envelopes, his eyes moving with practiced speed, before selecting one and letting out a low grunt of contentment. From his case, he produced a pair of spectacles, adjusting them carefully onto his nose.

"A letter from Ramelton?" Sutch asked, lowering his own paper.

"From Ramelton," Feversham confirmed. "First, however, a cigar."

He laid the letter down on the wooden table between them, reached into his pocket, and with deliberate slowness—despite Sutch's visible impatience—he cut and lit the tobacco. An old man like Feversham treated a letter from home not merely as information, but as a feast to be savored with every accessory of comfort. He settled deeper into his chair, extended his legs, and smoked long enough to ensure the draw was perfect before finally unrolling the paper.

"To him?" Sutch ventured.

"No," Feversham murmured. "To her."

Sutch let out a sharp intake of breath. "Ah."

Feversham read the letter through once, his expression unreadable, then paused, turning back to the beginning to read it again.

"Is there anything new?" Sutch asked, feigning a casual indifference.

"They are delighted with the house now that it stands rebuilt."

"And nothing else?"

"Yes. Harry has completed the sixth chapter of his history of the war."

"Excellent!" Sutch exclaimed. "You'll see how well he does it. He possesses true imagination. He knows the terrain intimately, having walked it while the fighting raged. Furthermore, he wandered the bazaars; he has seen the underside of things."

"Yes," Feversham conceded. "But I doubt you and I will read it, Sutch."

"No," Feversham corrected himself immediately, a smile touching his lips. "That is wrong. You may read it, for you still have many years ahead of me."

He folded the letter and set it aside. Again, Sutch pressed gently. "Anything further?"

"Yes. They are arriving here in two weeks' time."

"That is splendid," Sutch said, his face lighting up. "I shall remain."

He paced along the terrace, returning to his seat where he found Feversham staring at the letter with a look of deep perplexity etched upon his brow.

"You know, Sutch," the General said, breaking the silence, "there are certain things I never fully grasped. Did you?"

"Yes, I believe so," Sutch offered quietly.

He did not attempt to elaborate. It seemed wise, he felt, that Feversham should never understand the weight of those moments, for understanding required a burden of self-reproach that the General simply could not bear.

"Do you ever see Captain Durrance?" Feversham asked suddenly.

"Yes, I see him often. He is currently abroad."

Feversham turned to face his friend. "He visited Broad Place when you departed for Suakin. We spoke for half an hour. He was Harry's best man. Well, that, too, remains a mystery to me. Did you comprehend it?"

"Yes, I understood that perfectly," Sutch lied smoothly.

"Oh!" Feversham sighed, the question passing through him like smoke. As always, he chose to set such unanswerable mysteries aside, burying them beneath the surface of his thoughts. He did not glance at his remaining mail. Instead, he sat, cigar in hand, gazing out over the verdant summer countryside, listening intently to the life rising from the fields.

Sutch had gone through every envelope in the stack before the General spoke again.

"I have been reflecting," Feversham said, his eyes brightening. "Have you noticed the date, Sutch?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Yes," he replied. "Next day marks the anniversary of our assault on the Redan—and Harry's birthday."

"Precisely," Feversham smiled. "Why not revive the 'Crimean Nights' again?"

Sutch sprang from his chair. "Splendid idea!" he cried. "Do you suppose we can gather a table full of guests?"

"Let us see," Feversham said, ringing the handbell on the table. The servant appeared instantly, summoned to fetch the Army List. The two veterans bent over the book, planning their reunion.

Yet, the story of this chapter would not end with them. That same night, after the invitations had been dispatched from Broad Place and the last light extinguished from the windows, a solitary figure leaned against the railing of a steamer anchored at Port Said. The man listened to the rhythmic chant of the Arab coolies, whose footsteps echoed as they carried coal baskets between the barges and the ship's side. The roar of the town drifted across the water to him—a chaotic symphony of street cries and market noise. In his mind, he visualized the flare of braziers lining the quays, the glowing portholes, and the black funnels of the anchored ships forming a silent procession.

Attended by a servant, he had returned to the East. By early morning, the steamer pushed through the canal, and as sunset approached, it emerged into the chilling waters of the Gulf of Suez. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, Tamanieb—the memory of McNeil's zareeba flashed before his eyes. Captain Durrance relived those golden years of his activity, the era of plenty. Within the vast lands to the west, preparations were underway, steady and inexorable, destined one day to roll up the Dervish Empire and grind it to dust. On the scarred ramparts of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had vowed to join that grand endeavor, but the desert he loved had rejected him, casting him out. Nevertheless, the boat steamed southward into the Red Sea. Just three nights hence, though he would not witness it, the Southern Cross would rise obliquely into the twilight sky.
