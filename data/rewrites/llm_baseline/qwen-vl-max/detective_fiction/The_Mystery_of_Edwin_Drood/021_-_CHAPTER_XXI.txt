The night passed quietly, undisturbed by any alarm or unease—like a bird resting in peace, the weary soul awoke refreshed. At ten o’clock sharp, Mr. Grewgious arrived, accompanied by Mr. Crisparkle, who had made the journey from Cloisterham without delay, arriving straight from the river’s edge.

“Miss Twinkleton was so anxious,” Mr. Crisparkle explained to Rosa, “that she came rushing over to my mother and me with your note, all agitated and bewildered. To calm her, I offered to come at once—the very first train of the morning. I confess I wished you had turned to me first, but now I see that your choice was wise: coming to your guardian was the right course.”

“I did consider you,” Rosa replied, “but Minor Canon Corner was so close to him—”

“I understand perfectly,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “It was entirely natural.”

“I’ve already told Mr. Crisparkle everything you shared with me last night,” Mr. Grewgious added gently. “Of course, I would have written to him immediately—but his arrival could not have been more timely. And it was most thoughtful of him to come, especially since he’d only just left.”

Rosa turned to both men, her voice filled with concern. “Have you decided what should be done for Helena and her brother?”

“Honestly,” Mr. Crisparkle admitted, “I’m at a loss. If even Mr. Grewgious, whose mind is far more seasoned than mine, cannot settle on a plan after a full night of thought, then what hope do I have?”

Just then, the Unlimited appeared at the door—having knocked and received permission to enter—announcing a gentleman who wished to speak briefly with someone named Crisparkle, if such a person were present. Should no such man be there, he apologized for the mistake.

“There is such a gentleman,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “but he’s occupied just now.”

“Is it a dark gentleman?” Rosa asked, stepping back toward her guardian.

“No, miss,” the servant replied. “More of a brown gentleman.”

“You’re certain he doesn’t have black hair?” Rosa pressed, gaining courage.

“Quite sure, miss. Brown hair and blue eyes.”

Mr. Grewgious paused, his usual caution surfacing. “Perhaps, reverend sir, it might be prudent to meet him—if you don’t object. When one is uncertain, one never knows where help might appear. It’s a principle I follow: keep every avenue open. I could tell a story to illustrate, but that would be premature.”

“If Miss Rosa will allow it,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “let the gentleman come in.”

The visitor entered, politely apologizing for interrupting. With a modest yet sincere grace, he turned to Mr. Crisparkle and smiled. Then, with an unexpected twist, he asked: “Who am I?”

“You are the gentleman I saw smoking beneath the trees in Staple Inn just moments ago,” Mr. Crisparkle replied.

“True,” the man acknowledged. “And there, I saw you. But who else am I?”

Mr. Crisparkle studied the handsome, sun-bronzed face before him. Slowly, a memory stirred—a ghost of a younger boy rising faintly in the room. The man noticed the flicker of recognition and smiled again.

“What shall we have for breakfast this morning?” he asked lightly. “You’re out of jam.”

“Wait!” Mr. Crisparkle exclaimed, raising his hand. “One moment! Tartar!”

They embraced warmly, their hands clasping tightly, then each placing a hand on the other’s shoulder, gazing into one another’s eyes with unmistakable joy.

“My old fag!” Mr. Crisparkle declared.
“My old master!” the other replied.
“You saved me from drowning!”
“And afterward, you took to swimming, didn’t you?”
“God bless my soul!”
“Amen!”

They shook hands again, heartily, as if sealing a bond long overdue.

“Imagine,” Mr. Crisparkle said, eyes shining, “Miss Rosa Bud and Mr. Grewgious—imagine Mr. Tartar, when he was barely a junior, diving into the water to save me, a heavy senior, grabbing me by the hair, and swimming like a water-giant toward shore!”

“And imagine me refusing to let him sink,” Mr. Tartar added, “even though I was his fag. But the truth is—he was my greatest protector, my truest friend. He did me more good than all the masters combined. So when I saw him struggling, an instinct took hold: either I save him—or go down with him.”

“Hem!” Mr. Grewgious interjected, stepping forward with extended hand. “Permit me the honor—for it is truly an honor. I am proud to make your acquaintance. I hope you didn’t catch cold. I hope the water didn’t trouble you. How have you been since?”

His words seemed unformed, as if spoken without full awareness, though clearly meant to convey warmth and respect. Rosa watched, thinking: *If Heaven had sent such courage and strength to my poor mother!* And to think—this man had once been so slight, so young.

“I don’t wish to be praised,” Mr. Tartar said humbly, “but I believe I have an idea.”

He began pacing across the room in a sudden, almost erratic manner—so unusual that everyone stared, wondering if he were choking or seized by cramp.

“I _think_,” he announced, “I believe I’ve had the pleasure of seeing Mr. Tartar’s name listed as tenant of the top set in the house next to the corner one?”

“Yes, sir,” Mr. Tartar replied. “You’re correct so far.”

“Tick that off,” Mr. Grewgious said, tapping his thumb against his palm. “Now, might you know the name of your neighbor in the top set on the other side of the party wall?” He leaned close, his short sight demanding proximity.

“Landless.”

“Tick that off,” Mr. Grewgious said, taking another quick step, then returning. “No personal knowledge, I suppose?”

“Only slight—but some.”

“Tick that off,” he repeated, pacing again. “Nature of your knowledge, Mr. Tartar?”

“I thought he looked like a young man in difficult circumstances. Only recently—within a day or two—I asked his permission to extend my flower garden to his windows—that is, to share my blooms with him.”

“Would you kindly take seats?” Mr. Grewgious requested. “I _have_ an idea.”

They sat. Mr. Tartar complied readily, despite seeming slightly disoriented. Mr. Grewgious, seated in the center, hands resting on his knees, spoke with his customary precision—though it was clear he’d rehearsed the words.

“I cannot yet decide whether it’s safe to maintain contact, under current conditions, between the fair member of this company and Mr. Neville or Miss Helena. I know a local associate of ours—on whom I beg to bestow a passing, but heartfelt, curse, with the kind indulgence of my reverend friend—moves stealthily, skulking about. He may have an informant: a watchman, porter, or some other lowly figure attached to Staple. If not himself, he might have planted someone to monitor Mr. Neville’s chambers. That person would report to our friend, who would then deduce identities from prior knowledge. But no one can watch every chamber, nor track every visitor—unless, perhaps, mine.”

“I begin to grasp your meaning,” Mr. Crisparkle said, nodding. “And I fully approve of your caution.”

“I know nothing yet of the reason or motive,” Mr. Tartar said, “but I understand your intent. Let me say plainly: my rooms are yours to use.”

“There!” Mr. Grewgious declared, smoothing his head with triumph. “Now we all have the idea. Do you understand, my dear?”

“I think so,” Rosa replied, blushing slightly as Mr. Tartar glanced toward her.

“You’ll go to Staple with Mr. Crisparkle and Mr. Tartar,” Mr. Grewgious explained. “I’ll move in and out alone, as usual. You’ll go up to Mr. Tartar’s rooms, inspect his flower garden, wait for Miss Helena’s appearance, or signal her that you’re nearby—and communicate freely. No spy will suspect a thing.”

“I’m afraid I’ll be—” Rosa began.

“Be what, my dear?” Mr. Grewgious asked gently.

“Not frightened,” she answered shyly. “But… in Mr. Tartar’s way. We seem to be borrowing his home so casually.”

“I assure you,” Mr. Tartar said warmly, “if your voice echoes in those rooms—even once—I shall always count it a blessing.”

Rosa, unsure how to respond, lowered her eyes and turned to Mr. Grewgious. “Shall I put on my hat?”

“Nothing better you could do,” he replied. She withdrew to prepare.

While she was gone, Mr. Crisparkle gave Mr. Tartar a brief account of Neville’s troubles and Helena’s plight—the time sufficient, as the hat required careful adjustment.

When they returned, Mr. Tartar offered Rosa his arm. Mr. Crisparkle walked ahead, separate, leading the way.

“Poor, poor Eddy,” Rosa thought as they moved along.

Mr. Tartar waved his right hand, leaning down to speak animatedly. Rosa glanced at it—once so small, so pale, yet now strong and weathered. *It must have been steady and fearless even then,* she mused.

He told her he had been a sailor, roaming the seas for years.

“When are you going back?” she asked.

“Never,” he said.

Rosa wondered what the girls would think if they could see her crossing the wide street on the arm of a sailor. She imagined passers-by seeing her—small, delicate—beside this powerful figure, capable of lifting her from danger, carrying her miles without rest. His blue eyes, she noted, seemed used to watching threats approach from afar, meeting them without flinching.

Then, lifting her own gaze, she realized he was looking at her—thinking something about *her*.

A flush rose in her cheeks. Perhaps it explains why, later, she couldn’t quite remember how she climbed (with his help) to his rooftop garden, or how it felt like stepping into a magical land—suddenly blooming, like the country atop the enchanted beanstalk.

May it flourish forever.
