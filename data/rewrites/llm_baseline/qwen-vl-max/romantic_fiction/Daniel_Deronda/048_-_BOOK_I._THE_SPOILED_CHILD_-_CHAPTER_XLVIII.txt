It is a bitter and unrewarding task to plan every contingency with the sole aim of securing one’s own safety—so Machiavelli observed when speaking of Cæsar Borgia, who had foreseen every possible danger after his father’s death, except one: he never imagined that his own life would end so swiftly upon the very moment of his father’s passing.

Grandcourt’s influence in society was not forged by action or ambition, but by inheritance. His importance lay in land, not in ideas. The shifting currents of politics, social reform, or even financial crises passed him by like distant thunder, touching only the surface of his life through the rise and fall of his rents. He skimmed the leading columns of newspapers, yet his opinions on such matters were as narrow as they were dismissive—he lumped all Germans, all businessmen, and all voters who used the wrong kind of soap into one scornful category: “brutes.” He did not act on these views; instead, he met any mention of them with a cold glance beneath lowered lids and an icy silence that unsettled those who dared to speak. But within the sphere of his own desires, Grandcourt displayed traits akin to the most ruthless continental diplomats—the kind who command without needing to explain.

He watched Gwendolen’s every movement around Deronda with sharp attention. He would have denied being jealous, for jealousy implied uncertainty, and Grandcourt believed himself entirely in control. It did not trouble him that his wife might prefer another man’s company; what mattered was that she knew, as clearly as if her hand were cuffed shut, that no inclination could ever override his will. There had been hesitation in his decision to marry—but none in how he interpreted the marriage bond. He did not regret it. In fact, it had given his life direction, new objects to dominate. And he did not regret choosing Gwendolen. Her beauty, her bearing, the shape of her nails, the delicacy of her ears—all met his exacting taste. He wanted a woman elevated by him, admired by others, intelligent enough to spar with wit, yet prone to flare up when provoked—because that made her submission more satisfying. He took pride in knowing she married him not out of love, but from pressure, expectation, and perhaps a measure of fear. For men who value dominion over affection, why should the mind change its nature at the altar?

Grandcourt did not believe he had chosen poorly. Once he had assumed the role of husband, he refused to be mocked or seen as weak. This was not jealousy—it was something colder, sharper. Yet his behavior often mirrored jealousy as closely as yellow resembles yellow: different causes, same effect.

He had come to London earlier than usual, not merely for legal business—arranging his will, transferring mortgages, finalizing the deal with his uncle over Diplow, secured by the quiet lure of ready cash—but also to display his bride. Though he claimed not to care about admiration, such indifference required an audience to admire or envy. Like someone who enjoys staring stonily at smiling faces, he needed the smiles to exist. Grandcourt paraded Gwendolen through London’s grandest circles—attending lavish receptions, riding in elegant carriages, appearing beside her at every notable event. He wanted her sought after, desired, spoken of. He enjoyed seeing other men eager to talk to her, escort her—within his sight. Even a touch of haughty coquetry on her part pleased him. But he despised her interactions with Deronda.

After the musical gathering at Lady Mallinger’s, where Grandcourt had observed the exchange between Gwendolen and Deronda with the same keenness as Hans, he invited Deronda to the next gathering—alongside the Mallingers—without a hint of concern. He avoided suggesting that Deronda’s presence mattered to him. Nor did he directly confront Gwendolen about her behavior that evening. To do so might have revealed too much disgust, and pride forbade it.

A few days later, however, he remarked casually, without regard for context:

“Nothing makes a woman look more awkward than watching people and showing temper in public. A woman ought to have good manners. Otherwise, it’s unbearable to appear with her.”

Gwendolen understood the implication—and felt a pang of alarm. She, too, disliked things deeply, and preferred that her distaste included admirers. But now, the shadow of disapproval only heightened her longing for any chance encounter with Deronda. Her city life, though exhausting, was exhilarating—a whirlwind like foreign travel. Inside, she might feel despondent, but there was always a schedule, a performance, a distraction. Yet whenever she met Deronda—even briefly—the moment expanded in her mind. Each word between them grew in significance, each glance seemed charged with meaning. How could Deronda avoid this? He did not shun her. On the contrary, he gently reassured her that her trust in him had not been misplaced—that it had not diminished his respect. And he liked being near her. How could he not? She was not just a puzzle; she was a radiant woman, whose mind and fate stirred his care. Though he saw their paths diverging, he could not ignore the weight of her need—the way she turned to him, beseeching, persistent, as if he had once saved her from harm, and now she expected him to save her again.

One morning, during breakfast, Gwendolen, seized by a sudden resolve, toyed with her prawns and said, “I think I’ll take singing lessons while we’re in town. I want to become accomplished.”

“Why?” Grandcourt asked, languidly.

“Why?” she echoed, playing at defiance. “Because I can’t eat pâté de foie gras to make me sleepy, I can’t smoke, and I can’t go to the club to enjoy leaving. I need variety in my boredom. What time would suit you best—when you’re busy with your lawyers—to have lessons from that little Jewess whose singing is all the rage?”

“Whenever you like,” he replied, pushing away his plate and leaning back, his eyes fixed on her with their usual reptilian stillness as he idly played with the tiny spaniel on his lap. Gwendolen detested the dogs—they fawned on him too eagerly.

Then, with the same bored tone, he added, “I don’t see why a lady should sing. Amateurs make fools of themselves. A woman can’t risk herself like that in society. And no one wants to hear squalling in private.”

“I like frankness,” Gwendolen said, lifting her chin slightly, turning her gaze away. “That’s a husband’s charm. But”—she paused, swallowing her humiliation—“I suppose you don’t object to Miss Lapidoth singing at our party on the fourth? I thought of hiring her. Lady Brackenshaw had her. The Raymonds, who are very particular about music, did too. And Mr. Deronda, who’s a musician and a first-rate judge, says no one sings with such taste for a drawing-room. I think his opinion counts.”

She meant to throw a small stone.

“It’s indecent of Deronda to praise that girl so openly,” Grandcourt said, voice flat.

“Indecent?” Gwendolen exclaimed, flushing, startled. “To praise?”

“Yes. Especially since she’s under Lady Mallinger’s patronage. He should keep quiet. Men can see what his connection with her must be.”

“Men who judge others by themselves,” she shot back, her face whitening, instantly struck by the danger of her words.

“Of course,” he said, relishing the pinch. “And a woman should follow their judgment—or she’ll run headlong into the wrong place. I suppose you consider Deronda a saint.”

“Oh dear no,” she said, summoning her strength, her voice high and brittle. “Only a little less of a monster.”

She rose, pushed her chair back slowly, and walked out as if afraid of revealing she’d drunk too much wine. She locked her dressing room door and sat down, pale and still. Even after reading the poisonous letter, she had never felt such agony. Emotion had reached a point where it blurred into physical pain. Deronda—no longer the noble figure she believed him to be—had become a grotesque apparition. She couldn’t process whether it was truth or fiction. Worse, she realized how fragile her faith in him had been—how little she truly knew of his life. His severity, his rebukes, even his poetry—all now seemed monstrous. His grave face, once inspiring, now appeared like a mask worn by ordinary men.

This storm passed through her like a fever dream. Then, suddenly, sunlight pierced the gray morning, flooding the room. She moved gently, looked around—there was a world outside this nightmare. The dream meant nothing. She stood, arms raised, hands clasped in her familiar gesture of relief, walking through the beams.

“It’s not true! What does it matter if *he* believes it?” she whispered—not faith restored, but a desperate cry against suffocation.

How could she endure the day? With sudden impulse, her mind raced toward actions that would prove her belief: she would question Lady Mallinger, write to Deronda, pour out her fury. No—she would go to Mirah. That was feasible. She had the excuse of asking her to sing at the party. What else would she say? She didn’t know—she couldn’t wait to find out. If that idea were alive, she would strangle it without thinking.

She rang for her maid, asked if Grandcourt had left—confirmed he had—and ordered the carriage. She began dressing, then wandered the drawing room like a trapped creature, not recognizing herself in the mirrors, oblivious to the gilded prison around her. Her husband would discover her absence—and punish her. But she cared for nothing now but certainty.

She had Mirah’s address. Soon, she was on her way, surrounded by fine equipage, her heart pounding, waiting for answers she didn’t know how to ask. She was unaware of the journey until she reached Mirah’s house and heard Deronda’s voice behind a folding door. She knew it instantly—by instinct, by dread. She trembled, unbuttoning and rebuttoning her gloves, biting her lips, pretending difficulty, while the door opened and Mirah stepped forward with calm grace and a gentle smile.

Relief washed over Gwendolen. She smiled back, extended her hand silently. As she sat, hearing Deronda’s voice still, she felt a surge of energy—some vague sense that the truth could not be what she feared.

Mirah drew her chair close, as if to muffle their conversation, and looked at her with quiet anticipation.

“Perhaps you wonder to see me,” Gwendolen began softly, almost shyly. “I should have written—but I wanted to make a request.”

“I’m glad you came instead,” Mirah said, noticing the changed expression of the “Vandyke duchess” as Hans had called her. Her own face was warm and composed, a stark contrast to Gwendolen’s pale, agitated beauty beneath the plumed hat.

“I hoped,” Gwendolen continued, “you wouldn’t mind singing at our house on the fourth—like Lady Brackenshaw’s party. I’d be so grateful.”

“I’d be honored,” Mirah said. “At ten?”

“At ten,” Gwendolen replied, then paused, realizing she had nothing else to say. She couldn’t leave. She had to speak. The words burst out:

“Mr. Deronda is in the next room.”

“Yes,” Mirah answered calmly. “He’s reading Hebrew with my brother.”

“You have a brother?” Gwendolen asked—she’d heard it before, but hadn’t paid attention.

“Yes,” Mirah said, her voice softening. “A dear brother, ill—consumptive. Mr. Deronda has been the best friend to both of us. He found me when I was about to drown myself—so poor, so forsaken. You would have thought me a beggar. But he treated me as if I were a queen’s daughter. He brought me to the best woman. He found my brother. And he honors him—though he too was poor, nearly destitute. And my brother honors him. That’s no small thing,” Mirah said, her voice deepening, shaking her head. “My brother is wise and great-minded. And Mr. Deronda says there are few men like him.”

A flash of Jewish pride flared in her gratitude—and anger, directed at Gwendolen for doubting him.

But Gwendolen was like a thirsty soul drinking pure water. She didn’t notice Mirah’s anger. All she felt was the overwhelming certainty that Deronda’s life was nothing like Grandcourt’s slander. Even Mirah’s words dissolved into the flood of relief. She pressed Mirah’s hand, whispering, “Thank you, thank you,” then rose, murmuring, “I must go—I’ll see you on the fourth—I’m so grateful,” and left, moving mechanically, while Mirah watched, puzzled by the sudden retreat into cold dignity.

Gwendolen had no warmth left for Mirah. The desperate need to refute Grandcourt’s lies had been satisfied—now she only wanted to escape. She felt out of place, afraid of Deronda seeing her. Back in the carriage, she faced the return home.

When she arrived at Grosvenor Square, Grandcourt was arriving with a cigar in his fingers. He tossed it aside, helped her out, and followed her upstairs. She slipped into the drawing room, avoiding further contact. She sat, weary, removing her gloves, rubbing her forehead, making her presence seem as insignificant as possible. But he sat too—close, directly across from her.

“May I ask where you’ve been at this hour?” he said.

“Oh, yes,” she replied, placing her gloves on the table. “I went to Miss Lapidoth’s to ask her to sing for us.”

“And to inquire about her relationship with Deronda?” he asked, voice low, sneer colder than ice.

For the first time since their marriage, Gwendolen lashed out—without hesitation.

“Yes,” she said sharply. “And what you said is false—a vile, wicked lie.”

“She told you so?” he returned, the sneer distilled to poison.

Gwendolen was silent. Her anger curdled into mute fury. What proof could she offer? Her inner certainty crumbled under his breath. There was no evidence—only her feeling, which he would call folly. She turned away, glaring at the far wall. She tried to rise—but he blocked her path.

Grandcourt saw his victory. “It doesn’t matter about the singing,” he drawled. “You may have her if you wish.” Then, after a pause, in his lowest, most commanding tone: “But you will not go near that house again. As my wife, you must accept my judgment on what is proper. When you became Mrs. Grandcourt, you agreed not to make a fool of yourself. You did today. And if you continue, you’ll soon be talked about at the clubs in ways you won’t like. What do *you* know of the world? You married *me*. You must follow my judgment.”

Each sentence cut deeper than the last. If a doctor had delivered a fatal diagnosis, she could not have been more helpless. But she was allowed to move now. And Grandcourt never mentioned it again. He knew the power of his words. Had this man with the rigid profile been sent to govern a rebellious colony, he might have earned fame. He understood that it was safer to destroy than to placate. And he would not hesitate to do it.
