To comprehend the architecture of my current condemnation, one must first understand the absolute paralysis of my spirit during the two hours of suspended animation in which I was held captive. The influence of this probation upon *my* temperament was singular and absolute: I felt physically incapable of remaining still in any single location, and morally incapable of addressing a single human soul until I had first extracted every syllable of truth from Ezra Jennings' lips.

In this fractured state of mind, I did not merely abandon my contemplated visit to Mrs. Ablewhite; I actively recoiled from the very possibility of encountering Gabriel Betteredge himself. Returning to Frizinghall, I deposited a note for Betteredge, informing him that an unexpected summons had detained me for a few hours, but assuring him that he might certainly expect my return towards three o'clock in the afternoon. I instructed him, in the interim, to order his dinner at the usual hour and to amuse himself as he saw fit. As I well knew, Betteredge possessed hosts of friends in Frizinghall; he would be at no loss regarding how to fill the void of time until I reappeared at the hotel.

Having dispatched this errand, I made the swiftest possible journey out of the town again, roaming the lonely, windswept moorlands that surround Frizinghall until my watch signaled the final hour to return to Mr. Candy's house.

I found Ezra Jennings ready and waiting for me.

He sat alone in a bare, spartan chamber that communicated via a glazed door with a surgery. The barren buff-colored walls were adorned with hideous, colored diagrams depicting the ravages of hideous diseases. A bookcase, filled with dingy medical tomes and crowned at the top with a skull in lieu of a customary bust, stood sentinel nearby. A large deal table lay copiously splashed with ink; wooden chairs of the rustic variety, typically seen in kitchens and cottages, flanked it; a threadbare drugget rug stretched across the middle of the floor; and a sink of water, with a basin and waste-pipe roughly embedded in the wall, hovered horribly suggestive of its grim connection to surgical operations. These elements comprised the entirety of the room's furniture. Outside the window, bees hummed among a few potted flowers; birds sang in the garden; and the faint, intermittent jingle of a tuneless piano from a neighboring house forced itself now and again upon the ear. In any other setting, these mundane sounds might have spoken pleasantly of the everyday world outside. Here, they intruded violently upon a silence which nothing but human suffering possessed the privilege to disturb. I gazed at the mahogany instrument case and the huge roll of lint, occupying their own distinct spaces on the shelves, and shuddered inwardly as I conjured the sounds, familiar and appropriate to the daily use of Ezra Jennings' room.

"I make no apology, Mr. Blake, for the place in which I am receiving you," he said softly. "It is the only room in the house, at this hour of the day, in which we can feel quite sure of being left undisturbed. Here are my papers ready for you; and here are two books to which we may have occasion to refer before we conclude. Bring your chair to the table, and we shall be able to consult them together."

I drew up to the table; and Ezra Jennings handed me his manuscript notes. They consisted of two large folio leaves of paper. One leaf contained writing which only covered the surface at intervals, sparse and fragmented. The other presented writing, in both red and black ink, which completely filled the page from top to bottom, a dense forest of script. In the irritated state of my curiosity at that precise moment, I laid aside the second sheet of paper in despair.

"Have some mercy on me!" I cried. "Tell me what I am to expect before I attempt to read this."

"Willingly, Mr. Blake! Do you mind my asking you one or two more questions?"

"Ask me anything you like!"

He looked at me with a sad smile curving his lips and a kindly interest burning in his soft brown eyes.

"You have already told me," he said, "that you have never—to your knowledge—tasted opium in your life."

"To my knowledge," I repeated.

"You will understand directly why I speak with that reservation. Let us proceed. You are not aware of ever having taken opium. At this time, last year, you were suffering from nervous irritation, and you slept wretchedly at night. On the night of the birthday, however, there was an exception to the rule—you slept soundly. Am I right, so far?"

"Quite right!"

"Can you assign any cause for the nervous suffering, and your want of sleep?"

"I can assign no cause. Old Betteredge made a guess at the cause, I remember. But that is hardly worth mentioning."

"Pardon me. Anything is worth mentioning in such a case as this. Betteredge attributed your sleeplessness to something. To what?"

"To my leaving off smoking."

"Had you been an habitual smoker?"

"Yes."

"Did you leave off the habit suddenly?"

"Yes."

"Betteredge was perfectly right, Mr. Blake. When smoking is a habit, a man must possess no common constitution who can leave it off suddenly without inflicting some temporary damage to his nervous system. Your sleepless nights are accounted for, to my mind. My next question refers to Mr. Candy. Do you remember having entered into anything like a dispute with him—at the birthday dinner, or afterwards—on the subject of his profession?"

The question instantly awakened one of my dormant memories concerning the birthday festival. The foolish wrangle which took place on that occasion between Mr. Candy and myself will be found described at much greater length than it deserves in the tenth chapter of Betteredge's Narrative. The details there presented of the dispute—so little had I thought of it afterwards—entirely failed to recur to my memory. All that I could now recall, and all that I could tell Ezra Jennings, was that I had attacked the art of medicine at the dinner-table with sufficient rashness and sufficient pertinacity to put even Mr. Candy out of temper for the moment. I also remembered that Lady Verinder had intervened to stop the dispute, and that the little doctor and I had "made it up again," as the children say, becoming as good friends as ever before we shook hands that night.

"There is one thing more," said Ezra Jennings, "which it is very important I should know. Had you any reason for feeling any special anxiety about the Diamond, at this time last year?"

"I had the strongest reasons for feeling anxiety about the Diamond. I knew it to be the object of a conspiracy; and I was warned to take measures for Miss Verinder's protection, as the possessor of the stone."

"Was the safety of the Diamond the subject of conversation between you and any other person, immediately before you retired to rest on the birthday night?"

"It was the subject of a conversation between Lady Verinder and her daughter—"

"Which took place in your hearing?"

"Yes."

Ezra Jennings took up his notes from the table and placed them in my hands.

"Mr. Blake," he said, "if you read those notes now, by the light which my questions and your answers have thrown upon them, you will make two astounding discoveries concerning yourself. You will find: First, that you entered Miss Verinder's sitting-room and took the Diamond in a state of trance, produced by opium. Second, that the opium was given to you by Mr. Candy—without your own knowledge—as a practical refutation of the opinions which you had expressed to him at the birthday dinner."

I sat with the papers in my hand, completely stupefied.

"Try and forgive poor Mr. Candy," said the assistant gently. "He has done dreadful mischief, I own; but he has done it innocently. If you will look at the notes, you will see that—but for his illness—he would have returned to Lady Verinder's the morning after the party, and would have acknowledged the trick that he had played you. Miss Verinder would have heard of it, and Miss Verinder would have questioned him—and the truth which has lain hidden for a year would have been discovered in a day."

I began to regain my self-possession. "Mr. Candy is beyond the reach of my resentment," I said angrily. "But the trick that he played me is not the less an act of treachery, for all that. I may forgive, but I shall not forget it."

"Every medical man commits that act of treachery, Mr. Blake, in the course of his practice. The ignorant distrust of opium (in England) is by no means confined to the lower and less cultivated classes. Every doctor in large practice finds himself, every now and then, obliged to deceive his patients, as Mr. Candy deceived you. I don't defend the folly of playing you a trick under the circumstances. I only plead with you for a more accurate and more merciful construction of motives."

"How was it done?" I asked. "Who gave me the laudanum, without my knowing it myself?"

"I am not able to tell you. Nothing relating to that part of the matter dropped from Mr. Candy's lips, all through his illness. Perhaps your own memory may point to the person to be suspected."

"No."
