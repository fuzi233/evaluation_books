The chapter opens on that soft, thrumming verse, humming in Daniel Deronda’s chest as he lifts his hand to rap on the door of his mother’s suite at the Hotel Italia, the boyhood jitters he thought he’d long outgrown surging back unbidden. The two servants loitering in the antechamber stare, perplexed; they’d assumed the distinguished lady their mistress had summoned a doctor to meet would be receiving a physician, not this striking young gentleman who even makes the severe lines of a tailored evening coat glow like something stitched to adorn a prince. But Deronda notices nothing of their curious gazes, not until the second door swings open, and he steps into a lamplit velvet-draped great room, to find a figure waiting for him at its far end.

She is swathed from the crown of her silver-threaded raven hair to the hem of her sweeping train in loose, flowing black lace, only her face and bare elbows peeking out above a set of heavy, glinting gold bracelets, her head held with the sharp, fine poise of a performer who has held thousands of crowds breathless. Deronda crosses the room in two long strides before he has time to think, lifting the outstretched hand she offers to his lips. She keeps her fingers curled in his, studying him so intently he can feel her gaze prickle under his skin, his face flushing hot and girlish even as he marvels at the strangeness of it: for years he has spun endless, vivid fantasies of this meeting, of the lonely, grieving mother he imagined waiting for him, and none of them ever felt half as real, or half as unknowable, as this. He cannot even guess what language she will use to speak to him, when suddenly she drops his hand, presses both of hers to his shoulders, and her face lights up so bright every line of age and pain melts away, softening into something like the glow of a young woman in her prime.

“You are a beautiful creature,” she says, her voice low and melodious, every syllable rounded with a soft, charming foreign lilt. “I knew you would be.” She presses a cool, formal kiss to each of his cheeks, and he returns the gesture, the greeting feeling less like a mother welcoming her long-lost son than two monarchs meeting for a state audience.

The deep lines of grief and fatigue creep back across her face a moment later, her tone turning cold, flat, unapologetic: “I am your mother. But you can have no love for me.”

“I have thought of you more than any other living soul on earth,” Deronda says, his voice trembling so hard he can barely get the words out.

“I am nothing like what you imagined,” she says, stepping back to fold her arms again, looking him over as if inviting him to see every sharp, unlovely part of her clearly. He had always pictured his mother sharing his face, soft and tender; there is a likeness there, yes, but shadowed by so many sharp, vivid differences she feels almost otherworldly, a Melusina tied to some realm far removed from the quiet, gentle life he has always known.

“I used to lie awake wondering if you were suffering,” Deronda says, desperate not to wound her. “I used to dream I could be the one to comfort you.”

“I *am* suffering. But it is not the kind you can fix,” the Princess says, her tone hardening as she crosses to a velvet sofa piled high with silk cushions arranged for her ease. “Sit.” She gestures to the empty seat at her side, then relents a fraction, her voice softening. “Not right now, at least. I am at ease enough to speak.”

Deronda sits, and for a long quiet moment he waits, feeling less like he is in the presence of the mother he has pined for his whole life, than face to face with some ancient, unyielding fate he has chased unconsciously for years.

“I did not send for you to comfort me,” she says, after a long pause. “I never expected you to love me, not when we have never met, never shared a single day of your life. But I thought I chose a better path for you than the one I was forced down. I did not think I was robbing you of anything worth having.”

“Surely you cannot believe your love would have been something worthless to me?” Deronda says, when she trails off, clearly waiting for him to speak.

“I am not here to pretend I was some perfect saint of a mother,” she snaps, a bright, proud fire flaring in her tone. “I never had much affection to give you. I was suffocated by obligation, by other people’s demands on my life. I wanted to live out the fire in me, not be tied down to other lives that were not my own. You wonder what I was? I was no princess back then. I was the greatest singer in Europe, and I could act better than any performer on the stage. Men followed me across borders just to hear me sing one note. I lived a thousand different lives in a single lifetime. I did not want a child weighing me down.”

Her tone is sharp, defensive, unapologetic, and Deronda can almost feel the blood thrumming in his ears, as if he is watching her perform some strange sacred rite he does not understand, clinging to his coat collar like he is treading water. She goes on, the pale fire still burning in her face.

“I never wanted to marry. I was forced to wed your father, your cousin Ephraim, by my father’s commands — but it was my escape hatch. I could rule my husband, but I could never rule my father. I had a right to freedom, to escape the bondage I hated.”

She sinks back into the sofa cushions, her eyes and pressed lips still thrumming with unspoken words, and Deronda stays standing, breathless, until she looks up at him, her defiance softening into a quiet plea.

“And the bondage I hated for myself, I kept you far away from. What more loving mother could have done? I freed you from the curse of being born a Jew.”

“Then I *am* a Jew?” Deronda bursts out, his voice ringing so deep and loud it makes her flinch back against her cushions. “My father was Jewish, and you are a Jewess too?”

“Yes. Your father was my cousin,” she says, watching him, her gaze sharpening with a flicker of fear, as if she has only just now realized he might not react the way she expected.

“I am glad,” Deronda says, the words tearing out of him unbidden, hot with a wild, unnameable passion he has never dared name before. He is furious at her — furious that she bore him unwillingly, that she chose to be a stranger to him, that she only agreed to meet him now under duress — and that anger makes him bristle, even as he fights to get ahold of himself.

The Princess is shaking now, visibly, her whole frame trembling with a rage of her own, her eyes huge and bright with pale, angry color. “Why would you be glad? I made you an English gentleman! I fought to give you that life!”

“You had no right to steal my birthright from me, no right to choose it for me,” Deronda says, dropping sideways into his chair, leaning his arm over the back, staring away from her, forcing himself to breathe, horrified at how sharp he has been with this dying woman, at this moment that will define the rest of both their lives.

A long, heavy silence hangs between them before she speaks again, her tone steady, hard, unyielding. “I chose for you what I would have chosen for myself. How was I to know you would carry my father’s stubborn, pious fire in you, that you would love everything I spent my whole life running from?”

Deronda breathes slow, deep, fighting to master his anger, to meet her with the gentle understanding he has spent his whole life cultivating. He turns back to her, his face calm, soft, and her gaze locks on his, fascinated, transfixed, no soft motherly warmth in it, only a sort of awed, wary curiosity.

“Forgive me for speaking so hastily,” he says, quiet and grave. “Why did you decide now, after all these years, to tell me the truth, when you worked so hard to keep me ignorant? Why, if you are angry that I am glad to know it?”

“Ah — the reasons people do things!” she scoffs, a sharp, sarcastic lilt in her voice. “No one ever wants to admit they are not following some neat, socially acceptable list of motives. Everyone thinks a mother has to feel exactly the same soft, tender love as every other mother, or she is a monster. I am not a monster. I just never felt what other women claim to feel. I was glad to be free of you. But I made sure you were provided for, gave you all your father’s fortune. Now I am dying. I have a fatal illness that has been eating at me for a year, and the shadows are closing in all around me. The dead man — my father — he won. He haunts me. I had to do the last thing he commanded me to do.”

She goes on to tell him the whole story: of her childhood in Genoa, her iron-willed father who tried to cram her into a tiny box of pious domesticity, who forbade her from singing, who demanded she obey every last Jewish law, bind tefillin, keep kosher, marry the cousin he picked out for her, shrink her enormous, talented soul down to the size of a “good Jewish wife.” She tells him how she pretended to obey, waited patiently for him to die, then ran off to the stage, how Deronda’s father gave up his whole banking career, his whole life, just to follow her, to wait on her hand and foot, because he loved her more than he loved his own soul.

Deronda reaches out, takes her small, ringed hand — the very same hand as his, long, delicate, made for art — and all his anger melts away. “Mother,” he says, his voice soft, thick with reverent tenderness. “Take us all into your heart — the living, the dead. Forgive all the hurts of the past. My affection is yours, I have carried it for you my whole life.”

She looks at him, not with maternal love, but with the awe of one great artist beholding another, leans in to press a soft kiss to his forehead, then sinks back into her cushions. “I reject nothing,” she says, her tone thick and tired, beautiful as a fading aria. “But I have nothing left to give.”

Deronda goes pale, stinging with the pain of that gently rejected tenderness, and she sees the hurt flash across his face. For the first time, her perfect, performer’s poise cracks, softens, just a little.
