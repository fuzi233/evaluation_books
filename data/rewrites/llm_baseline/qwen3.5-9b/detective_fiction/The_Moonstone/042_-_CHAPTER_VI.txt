I walked toward the railway station, accompanied, as was customary, by Gabriel Betteredge. Clutched in my pocket was the incriminating letter, and tucked securely inside a small bag was my nightgown. Both items were destined for immediate scrutiny by Mr. Bruff before I retired that night. We departed the house in heavy silence. For the first time in my acquaintance with him, I found old Betteredge keeping my company without offering a single word. Since I had much to say, I initiated the conversation the moment we cleared the lodge gates.

"Before I depart for London," I began, "I have two questions to pose to you. They concern myself, and I fear they may come as a shock."

"If these inquiries manage to banish that poor soul's letter from my mind, Mr. Franklin, they may do whatever they please with me. Pray, begin to astonish me, sir, at your earliest convenience."

"My first question, Betteredge, is this: Was I drunk on the night of Rachel's birthday?"

"_You_ drunk!" the old man exclaimed. "It is the great flaw in your character, Mr. Franklin, that you only drink with your dinner and never touch a drop afterward!"

"But the birthday was a special occasion. Might I have abandoned my usual habits on that particular night?"

Betteredge paused for consideration. "You did indeed stray from your habits, sir," he admitted. "And let me explain why. You looked dreadingly ill, and we persuaded you to have a sip of brandy and water to lift your spirits."

"I am not accustomed to brandy and water. It is entirely possible—"

"Hold on, Mr. Franklin. I knew you weren't used to it either. I poured you out half a wineglass of our fifty-year-old Cognac; and (though I blush to admit it!) I drowned that noble spirit in nearly a full tumbler of ice-cold water. No child could have gotten drunk on that mixture, let alone a grown man!"

Knowing Betteredge's impeccable memory regarding such matters, I accepted his testimony. It was physically impossible that I had been intoxicated. Moving swiftly to my second query:

"Before I was sent abroad, Betteredge, you spent considerable time with me during my childhood. Tell me plainly: Do you recall anything unusual about me after I went to bed at night? Did you ever catch me walking in my sleep?"

Betteredge halted, fixed his gaze upon me for a moment, nodded slowly, and continued walking.

"I see your drift now, Mr. Franklin!" he declared. "You are attempting to explain how you acquired paint on your nightgown without conscious intent. It won't work, sir. You are worlds away from the truth. Sleepwalking? You never did such a thing in your entire life!"

Once again, I felt compelled to agree. My life, both at home and abroad, had never been solitary. Had I been a somnambulist, hundreds of people would have noticed, warned me of the danger, and taken steps to restrain me. Yet, despite this logic, I clung with stubbornness to one of the only two explanations available to me to resolve my untenable situation. Seeing that I remained unconvinced, Betteredge shrewdly referenced later events concerning the Moonstone, scattering my theories to the winds forever.

"Let us approach this differently, sir," he suggested. "Hold your own opinion and see where it leads you toward the truth. If we are to believe the nightgown—which I certainly do not—you not only smudged paint onto the door unknowingly, but you also stole the Diamond without awareness. Is that correct so far?"

"Quite right. Proceed."

"Excellent, sir. We assume you were drunk or sleepwalking when you took the jewel. That explains the night and morning following the birthday. But how does this account for everything that has transpired since? The Diamond was taken to London subsequently. It was pledged to Mr. Luker shortly thereafter. Did you perform those acts unknowingly as well? Were you drunk when I saw you off in the pony-chaise on that Saturday evening? And did you sleepwalk to Mr. Luker's once the train delivered you to your destination? Pardon me for stating the obvious, Mr. Franklin, but this ordeal has so unsettled you that you are unfit to judge your own state of mind. The sooner you align your thoughts with Mr. Bruff's, the sooner you will escape this deadlock."

We arrived at the station with barely a minute to spare. I hastily provided Betteredge with my London address, requesting correspondence if needed, and promised to share any news I received. Just as I prepared to bid him farewell, my glance drifted toward the bookstall. There stood Mr. Candy's peculiar assistant once more, conversing with the stall-keeper! Our eyes locked simultaneously. Ezra Jennings removed his hat to me; I returned the gesture and boarded a carriage just as the train pulled away.

It was perhaps a relief to my frayed nerves to focus on a subject seemingly unrelated to my personal crisis. Regardless, I commenced the momentous journey back to Mr. Bruff, wondering—absurdly, I grant you—that I had encountered the man with the piebald hair twice in a single day!

Arriving in London late enough to rule out visiting Mr. Bruff at his office, I drove directly to his private residence in Hampstead. I found the elderly lawyer dozing alone in his dining room, his favorite pug resting on his lap and a bottle of wine beside him. Once I had recounted my tale, Mr. Bruff ordered lights and strong tea for his study and dispatched a message to his family, strictly forbidding their intrusion.

After these preparations, he first inspected the nightgown and then read Rosanna Spearman's letter. Upon finishing, he addressed me for the first time since we had secluded ourselves in his room.

"Franklin Blake," the old gentleman said solemnly, "this is a grave matter in multiple respects. In my view, it concerns Rachel almost as deeply as it concerns you. Her bizarre behavior is no longer a mystery. She believes you have stolen the Diamond."

I had shuddered at reaching that repulsive conclusion myself, though it had inevitably forced its way into my mind. My determination to seek a personal audience with Rachel stemmed precisely from this realization.

"The initial step in this investigation," the lawyer continued, "is to appeal to Rachel. She has maintained silence thus far for reasons I, who know her character, can easily comprehend. However, given recent events, such silence cannot endure. She must be persuaded—or compelled—to reveal the basis of her conviction that you took the Moonstone. Should we succeed in breaking through Rachel's habitual reserve, the likelihood is that this entire case, however serious it appears, will collapse."

"That is a remarkably comforting perspective for _me_," I replied. "I confess I would like to know—"

"You would like to know how I can justify it," Mr. Bruff interrupted sharply. "I shall explain in two minutes. First, understand that I view this solely through a legal lens. It is a question of evidence. Well, the evidence fails immediately on one crucial point."

"On what point?"

"You shall hear. I concede that the monogram proves the nightgown belongs to you. I concede that the paint marks prove the gown created the smear on Rachel's door. But what evidence exists to prove that *you* were the one wearing it on the night the Diamond vanished?"

The objection struck me with renewed force, echoing a doubt I had harbored internally.

"As for this," the lawyer proceeded, picking up Rosanna Spearman's confession, "I acknowledge that the letter is painful for _you_ to analyze. I understand your hesitation to scrutinize it impartially. But _I_ am not in your position. I can apply my professional expertise to this document just as I would to any other. Without dwelling on the woman's criminal past, I note that her letter admits she is skilled at deception. From this, I infer she may not have told the whole truth. I will not theorize now about what she might have done. Simply put: if Rachel suspects you based _only_ on the nightgown, there is a ninety-nine percent probability that Rosanna Spearman showed it to her. In that scenario, consider her letter: she confesses jealousy of Rachel, admits to changing the roses, and reveals she saw hope in a quarrel between Rachel and you. I need not ask who stole the Moonstone; a thief in love with you, desperate for a pretext, would have seized fifty diamonds if offered the chance. She hadn't decided on suicide at that moment, yet possessing the opportunity, it fits her character and position perfectly to use the theft to drive you and Rachel apart for life. What do you say to that?"

"Some such suspicion," I admitted, "crossed my mind the moment I opened the letter."

"Precisely! And after reading it, you pitied the poor creature and lacked the heart to suspect her. Do you credit me, my dear sir—do you credit me!"

"But suppose it turns out I did wear the nightgown? Then what?"

"I see no way to prove it," Mr. Bruff stated. "However, assuming proof were possible, clearing your name would be no simple task. Let us set that aside for now. Let us await whether Rachel suspects you based solely on the nightgown."

"Good heavens, how calmly you discuss Rachel suspecting me!" I erupted. "What right does she have to accuse me of theft on any evidence whatsoever?"
