The tale Harry Warrington shared with his newfound kinswoman painted a picture of life at Castlewood—one that Madam Bernstein, with her sharp wit and worldly wisdom, surely dissected with private amusement. To a woman of her refined tastes, the rustic existence of her American sister seemed dreary indeed. Yet Rachel Warrington, content in her dominion, knew no greater ambition than the management of her estate. She was a woman of ceaseless industry, her days filled with the minutiae of plantation life: overseeing the kitchens, directing the spinning maids, riding out to inspect the fields, and doctoring ailing slaves with her formidable collection of remedies. Her neighbors, a mix of threadbare gentry and hospitable squires, lived in much the same manner—though few matched her thrift.

At her side stood Mrs. Mountain, the widow of an old regimental companion, who had crossed the Atlantic to serve as companion and confidante. Their friendship was a tempestuous one, marked by quarrels and swift reconciliations, yet bound by mutual loyalty. Mrs. Mountain brought laughter to the household, played cards with her mistress, and fended off the advances of eager suitors with a cheerful refusal. She had seen enough of marriage, she declared, and preferred the security of Castlewood to any uncertain future.

Madam Warrington, for her part, enjoyed the admiration of her neighbors, accepting their flattery with regal complacency. She was a woman who relished deference, and more than one hopeful gentleman found himself redirected toward Mrs. Mountain when his attentions grew too pointed. The widow’s pride in her lineage was unshakable; she would entertain no notion of remarriage, though she took pleasure in the game of courtship—much like the great Elizabeth herself.

Her sons, however, chafed under her whims. George, sharp-tongued and perceptive, saw through the sycophants who clustered around his mother, while Harry, ever the sportsman, joined their hunts and revels without a second thought. Their education, first under an indifferent tutor and then under the zealous Mr. Ward—a protégé of the fiery preacher Whitfield—became a battleground. George, disdainful of Ward’s ignorance and pomp, provoked him at every turn, while Harry, though more amiable, bristled at the man’s heavy-handed piety.

Matters came to a head when George’s defiance pushed his mother to reclaim her authority by force. Persuaded by Ward, she resolved to restore discipline with the rod—a common enough practice, but one that George, proud and resentful, could not abide. In a moment of cold fury, he shattered a cherished heirloom, his grandfather’s cup, declaring, *"You will never mend that again."* The act was a challenge, a defiance that could not be undone.

When Ward attempted to carry out the punishment, Harry intervened with a violence none had anticipated. Seizing a blade from the wall, he struck the tutor, bloodying his head and vowing, *"If one lifts a hand to me or my brother, I’ll have his life."*

The household stood divided—the mother aghast, the tutor wounded, and the sons united in rebellion. The echoes of that day’s strife would linger, for bonds had been strained, and the old order of Castlewood had been shaken to its core.
