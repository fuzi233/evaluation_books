It was entirely reasonable for Paulina to refuse further communication with Graham until her father gave his blessing. Yet, Dr. Bretton could not exist within a league of the Hôtel Crécy without finding a way to visit it frequently. Though both lovers initially intended to maintain a distance, keeping their intentions intact regarding overt displays of affection, their hearts drew nearer with every passing moment.

Every noble trait in Graham sought expression in Paulina's presence; whatever was best in him awakened and flourished around her. While his former admiration for Miss Fanshawe may have stemmed from intellectual appreciation, his entire intellect and highest tastes were now fully engaged. Like all his faculties, they were active, hungry for nourishment, and eager for satisfaction when it arrived.

I cannot claim that Paulina deliberately steered conversations toward books, nor that she consciously set out to refine his mind or improve his understanding. She considered him already perfect. It was Graham himself who, by sheer chance, mentioned a book he had recently read. Upon hearing her response, which resonated with a harmonious sympathy, he spoke on with increasing pleasure, discussing matters more deeply and eloquently than ever before. She listened with delight and replied with animation. With each exchange, Graham perceived a music becoming finer and more profound; in each reply, he discovered a persuasive, magical accent that unlocked a hidden treasury within his own mind, revealing unsuspected powers and, even better, latent goodness in his heart.

They delighted in the way the other spoke—the voice, the diction, the expression. They savored the flavor of each other's wit, meeting meanings with startling speed, their thoughts matching like carefully chosen pearls. Graham possessed a natural abundance of mirth, whereas Paulina, lacking such an innate flow of animal spirits, tended naturally toward thoughtfulness and pensiveness. Yet, in her lover's genial company, she seemed as merry as a lark; in his presence, she glowed like a soft, joyful light. How beautifully she transformed in her happiness! I could scarcely describe it, yet I marveled at the change. What of that gentle ice she had relied upon, that reserve she had cultivated? Where was it now? Ah! Graham would not tolerate it long. He brought a generous influence that quickly thawed her timid, self-imposed restrictions.

Soon, the old days of the Brettons were recounted. At first, perhaps brokenly, with a smiling diffidence, they moved toward open candor and growing confidence. Graham had created a superior opportunity compared to what I had hoped to provide; he had earned his independence from the reluctant assistance Lucy had denied. All his memories of "little Polly" found their proper expression in his own pleasant tones, spoken by his kind and handsome lips—how much better than if suggested by me.

On several occasions, when we were alone, Paulina confessed to me how wonderful and curious it was to discover the richness and accuracy of his memory regarding these events. As he looked at her, recollections seemed to quicken instantly in his mind. He reminded her that she once gathered his head in her arms, caressed his lion-like grace, and cried out, "Graham, I *do* like you!" He recalled how she would place a footstool beside him to climb onto his knee. Even today, he claimed to remember the sensation of her small hands smoothing his cheek or burying themselves in his thick mane. He remembered the touch of her tiny forefinger, placed half-tremblingly, half-curiously, in the cleft of his chin, the lisp, and the look with which she named it "a pretty dimple," then sought his eyes to ask why they pierced so, telling him he had a "nice, strange face; far nicer, far stranger, than either his mamma or Lucy Snowe."

"Child as I was," Paulina remarked, "I wonder how I dared be so venturesome. To me, he seems now all sacred; his locks are inaccessible. And, Lucy, I feel a sort of fear when I look at his firm, marble chin, at his straight Greek features. Women are called beautiful, Lucy; he is not like a woman, therefore I suppose he is not beautiful. But what is he, then? Do other people see him with my eyes? Do *you* admire him?"

"I'll tell you what I do, Paulina," I answered once amidst her many questions. "*I never see him.* I looked at him twice or thrice about a year ago, before he recognized me, and then I shut my eyes. If he were to cross their balls twelve times between each day's sunset and sunrise, except from memory, I should hardly know what shape had passed by."

"Lucy, what do you mean?" she whispered.

"I mean that I value vision, and dread being struck stone-blind."

It was best to answer her strongly at once, silencing forever the tender, passionate confidences that left her lips tasting of sweet honey and sometimes dropping molten lead into my ear. From that moment, she ceased commenting on her lover's beauty.

Yet, she would speak of him nonetheless. Sometimes shyly, in quiet, brief phrases; sometimes with a tenderness of cadence and a music of voice exquisite in itself, though which chafed me miserably at times. Then, I knew, I gave her stern looks and words. But cloudless happiness had dazzled her native clear sight, and she only thought Lucy fitful.

"Spartan girl! Proud Lucy!" she would say, smiling at me. "Graham says you are the most peculiar, capricious little woman he knows; but yet you are excellent; we both think so."

"You both think you know not what," I replied. "Have the goodness to make me as little the subject of your mutual talk and thoughts as possible. I have my sort of life apart from yours."

"But ours, Lucy, is a beautiful life, or it will be; and you shall share it."

"I shall share no man's or woman's life in this world, as you understand sharing. I think I have one friend of my own, but am not sure; and till I *am* sure, I live solitary."

"But solitude is sadness."

"Yes; it is sadness. Life, however, has worse than that. Deeper than melancholy lies heart-break."

"Lucy, I wonder if anybody will ever comprehend you altogether."

Among lovers, there exists a certain infatuated egotism; they demand a witness to their happiness, regardless of the cost. Paulina had forbidden letters, yet Dr. Bretton wrote; she had resolved against correspondence, yet she answered, merely to chide. She showed me these letters. With something of the spoiled child's wilfulness and the heiress's imperiousness, she *made* me read them. As I read Graham's, I scarce wondered at her demand and understood her pride: they were fine letters—manly and fond, modest and gallant. Hers must have appeared equally beautiful to him. They were not written to display her talents, nor primarily to express her love. On the contrary, she seemed to have undertaken the task of hiding that feeling and bridling her lover's ardor. But how could such letters serve such a purpose? Graham had become dear as her life; he drew her like a powerful magnet. For her, there was an unspeakable influence in all he uttered, wrote, thought, or looked. With this unconfessed confession, her letters glowed; it kindled them from greeting to adieu.

"I wish papa knew; I *do* wish papa knew!" began to be her anxious murmur. "I wish, and yet I fear. I can hardly keep Graham back from telling him. There is nothing I long for more than to have this affair settled—to speak out candidly; and yet I dread the crisis. I know, I am certain, papa will be angry at first; I fear he will dislike me almost; it will seem to him an untoward business; it will be a surprise, a shock: I can hardly foresee its whole effect on him."

The fact was—her father, long calm, was beginning to be slightly stirred. Long blind on one point, an importunate light was beginning to trespass on his eye.

To *her*, he said nothing; but when she was not looking at, or perhaps thinking of him, I saw him gaze and meditate on her.

One evening—Paulina was in her dressing-room, writing, I believe, to Graham; she had left me in the library, reading—M. de Bassompierre entered. He sat down; I was about to withdraw, but he requested me to remain, gently, yet in a manner showing he desired compliance. He took his seat near the window, at a distance from me. He opened a desk and took from it what looked like a memorandum-book. He studied a certain entry for several minutes.

"Miss Snowe," he said, laying it down, "do you know my little girl's age?"

"About eighteen, is it not, sir?"

"It seems so. This old pocket-book tells me she was born on the 5th of May, in the year 18--, eighteen years ago. It is strange; I had lost the exact reckoning of her age. I thought of her as twelve—fourteen—an indefinite date; but she seemed a child."

"She is about eighteen," I repeated. "She is grown up; she will be no taller."

"My little jewel!" said M. de Bassompierre, in a tone which penetrated like some of his daughter's accents.

He sat very thoughtful.
