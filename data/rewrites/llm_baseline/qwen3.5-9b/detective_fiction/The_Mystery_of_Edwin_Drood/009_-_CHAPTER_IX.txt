Rosa had lived her entire life within the walls of the Nuns' House, save for the brief, tragic memory of her childhood home. Since she was seven, Miss Twinkleton had been her sole mother. Her only recollection of her biological mother was a vision of a tiny, lovely girl, scarcely older than Rosa herself, who had been carried home by her father only to drown during a festive outing. The image burned itself into Rosa's mind with terrifying clarity: the vibrant colors of her summer dress, the long, wet hair tangled with crushed flower petals, and the heartbreaking stillness of the body laid out on the bed. This tragedy shattered her father's spirit; he died of a broken heart exactly one year later.

From this sorrow sprang the betrothal. His grief was soothed by his oldest friend, Edwin Drood, a fellow widower who had lost his wife young. Fate, in its mysterious way, united the two young people, leaving them where they stood today.

Since arriving in Cloisterham, Rosa had never truly escaped the atmosphere of pity that surrounded her. As she aged, becoming happier and more beautiful, the hue of this pity shifted—from gold to rose to blue—but it always lingered, casting a soft, peculiar light upon her. The community's overwhelming desire to console and coddle her meant she was treated as a perpetual child, regardless of her actual age. Rivalries flared over who would bring her gifts, who would visit her during holidays, and who would write to her most faithfully. Even within the convent, these gentle competitions sometimes masked deeper, hidden conflicts beneath the veil of piety.

Consequently, Rosa grew into an amiable, spirited, and somewhat willful creature. She was spoiled in the sense that she expected kindness from everyone, yet she reciprocated with genuine warmth. Her nature was a bottomless well of affection that had brightened the Nuns' House for years, though no one dared to guess what would happen when that well finally ran dry.

How the news of a violent altercation between the two young men reached the convent before breakfast remains unknown. Did it arrive on the wings of birds, blown in on the wind through the open windows, baked into the morning bread, or delivered in the milk? Or perhaps the maids swept it up along with the dust? Certain only is that the rumor permeated every corner of the old building before Miss Twinkleton even stepped out of her bedroom. In the logic of the kitchen staff, a knife suggested a fork, and eventually, the cook insisted he had hurled *all three*, plus a decanter, at Edwin Drood. Just as the nursery rhyme demands proof of the "peck of pickled pepper" Peter Piper picked, the psychological need arose to understand *why* Miss Landless's brother attacked Edwin Drood with such weaponry.

According to the cook, Miss Landless's brother claimed to admire Miss Bud. Edwin Drood had sternly told him he had no business doing so. Enraged, the brother reportedly "up'd"—a term the cook used with precision—launching bottles, knives, forks, and a decanter at Edwin.

Poor Rosa, hearing these rumors, instinctively plugged her ears and retreated to a corner, begging to hear no more. Meanwhile, Miss Landless, seeking permission to confront her brother, pressed her case so firmly that she was granted leave to travel to Mr. Crisparkle's office for the truth. Upon returning, after conferring privately with Miss Twinkleton to filter out any offensive details, she shared only the bare essentials with Rosa. She emphasized the provocation her brother suffered, glossing over the fact that the conflict stemmed from his lover's easy-going nature, and handed Rosa a heartfelt letter of forgiveness from her brother before abruptly ending the topic.

It fell to Miss Twinkleton to quell the gossip. Entering the schoolroom with the dignity of a queen, she commanded silence. Standing beside her was Mrs. Tisher, poised like a historical figure at a fortress gate. With a theatrical flourish, Miss Twinkleton invoked William Shakespeare, the Bard of Avon, noting that he famously depicted Rumour as a creature with many tongues. She declared that the recent scuffle between two young gentlemen, occurring less than a hundred miles away, had been grotesquely exaggerated by the town's gossips. After citing various students to illustrate the absurdity of the rumors, she dismissed the subject, urging everyone to focus on their studies.

Yet, the rumor proved resilient. By dinner, Miss Ferdinand had donned a paper mustache and attempted to throw a water bottle at Miss Giggles, who defended herself with a spoon.

Rosa dwelt constantly on this unfortunate quarrel, feeling an uneasy sense of involvement, as if her own engagement placed her in a precarious position. She was rarely comfortable when separated from her fiancé, and now, with Helena avoiding the topic delicately, Rosa was forced to brood alone. Then, her guardian arrived.

Mr. Grewgious was a man of impeccable integrity but distinct physical oddities. He resembled dried sand, capable of crumbling into powder if ground. His hair was a sparse, yellowish crop that looked more like a worn fur collar than human hair, leading one to suspect it was a wig, despite the sheer unlikelihood of anyone choosing such a style. His face was carved from stone, marked by deep lines and notches on his forehead that suggested Nature had abandoned the sculpting process prematurely. Tall-necked, lanky-legged, and possessing a clumsy, hesitant gait, he walked with a nearsighted squint that failed to notice the excessive amount of white sock visible above his black trousers. Despite these flaws, he possessed a strange ability to make others feel at ease.

Mr. Grewgious entered the study, finding Rosa and Miss Twinkleton. He appeared deeply uncomfortable, fearing scrutiny. He greeted them politely, offering Rosa a chair while gently suggesting Miss Twinkleton step aside—a request she gracefully declined, moving her desk instead to avoid obstructing the view.

"My visits," Mr. Grewgious remarked, settling by the fire, "are like those of angels—few and far between."

"I refer," he clarified, placing a hand on Rosa's arm, "to the other young ladies."

Miss Twinkleton quietly resumed her writing. Mr. Grewgious then produced a pocket notebook and a pencil, consulting his memoranda.

"Well and happy," he read. "Are you well and happy, my dear?"
"Yes, indeed, sir," Rosa replied.
"For which," he noted, "we owe thanks to the maternal kindness of the lady in the corner."

Next came the financial ledger: "Pounds, shillings, and pence." He acknowledged the dryness of the subject but stressed its importance. "Life is pounds, shillings, and pence. Death is... not." He paused, remembering the deaths of Rosa's parents, and softened his tone. He confirmed that Rosa's allowance was sufficient and that she owed no debts. Rosa laughed at the notion of debt, finding it a comical fantasy.

Then, the heavy topic arrived: "Marriage."

Mr. Grewgious approached closer, his voice dropping to a confidential whisper. "I touch now upon the direct cause of my visit. Being an angular man, I am ill-fitted to intrude, yet I must. I feel like a bear with cramps in a youthful cotillion." Rosa laughed heartily at the simile.

"Mr. Edwin has visited as arranged," Grewgious continued calmly. "You like him, and he likes you. You correspond."
"We write to one another," Rosa corrected, pouting slightly.
"That is the correspondence I mean," he smiled. "All proceeds well. However, by next Christmas, it will be necessary to provide formal notice to the lady in the corner regarding your departure in six months. While your relationship is personal, a residue of business remains. Business is business. As an angular man unused to giving things away, I require a competent proxy to handle the arrangements."

Rosa indicated that a substitute could be found if needed.
"The dancing master," Grewgious suggested. "He knows how to advance and retire with proper grace, satisfying the clergyman, the bridegroom, and all parties."

Next, he consulted his notes on "Will." Though Rosa already knew the terms of her father's will, he insisted on handing her a certified copy. He also intended to send a copy to Mr. Jasper, acting as Edwin's trustee.
"Not in his own!" Rosa exclaimed sharply. "Can't the copy go to Eddy himself?"
"Why, yes, if you wish," Grewgious conceded. "But I spoke of Mr. Jasper as his trustee."
"I *do* wish it," Rosa insisted earnestly. "I dislike Mr. Jasper coming between us."
"It is natural," Grewgious observed, "that your young husband should be all in all. Yet, I am a particularly unnatural man, lacking knowledge of such things."
