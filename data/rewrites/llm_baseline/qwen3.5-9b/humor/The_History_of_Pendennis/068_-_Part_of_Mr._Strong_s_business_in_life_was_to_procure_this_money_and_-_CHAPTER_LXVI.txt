Sir Francis Clavering's primary objective in life was to secure capital, and currently, he was residing in a state of genteel destitution at Clavering Park. Once a bustling estate, the great house now resembled a skeleton stripped of its flesh; the majority of the mansion stood shuttered and silent. The Baronet confined himself to a handful of ground-floor rooms, where a beleaguered housekeeper and her lodge-gate assistant served him with the somber efficiency of refugees. They prepared portions of the venison he hunted during his dreary mornings, while his former manservant, Lightfoot, had defected to Lady Clavering's household.

As Pen learned via a letter from Mr. Smirke—the officiant who had quietly married Mrs. Bonner to Lightfoot—the situation was evolving. Mrs. Bonner, despite her advanced years, possessed a magnetic charm for the youthful Lightfoot and had generously transferred her savings to him. Their shared dream was to transform Lady Clavering's domain into the "Clavering Arms" inn. Until Quarter Day arrived to effectuate this transfer, however, they remained in service, waiting for the moment when Pen graciously pledged to hold his election dinner at their future establishment. This promise was contingent upon the Baronet vacating his parliamentary seat—a favor Uncle Arthur had seemingly coerced from the reluctant Sir Francis.

Consequently, Arthur descended upon Clavering Park in September. The owner, eager for companionship and perhaps a loan, welcomed the young man. True to form, Pen supplied his host with a modest sum of cash within two days. Instantly, Sir Francis Clavering vanished, citing urgent business in Chatteris and various neighboring watering-places. As one might expect, these affairs were conducted amidst the dust of county racecourses and the smoke-filled billiard rooms.

Arthur, conversely, proved surprisingly self-sufficient. A man of considerable mental resources, he entertained himself with walks alongside the gamekeeper and an extensive library collection that kept him occupied until late. Yet, within mere days, he found the company of Sir Francis utterly insufferable. With a mischievous relish, he then offered the Baronet further financial assistance, effectively funding his escape from the house.

Beyond his domestic maneuvers, Pen embarked on a campaign of political ingratiation. Remembering his past unpopularity among the simple folk of Clavering, he resolved to erase any lingering odium. His natural reserve dissolved into a performance of boisterous joviality; he laughed with everyone, shook hands with a dexterous cordiality, and attended every market day and farmers' ordinary. He played the role of the consummate hypocrite with gusto, mimicking the behavior of high-born gentlemen seeking votes. How easily we are duped by a glib tongue and a ready smile! We recognize the falseness of such coin, yet we accept it, preferring the cheap flattery of universal distribution to the silence of indifference.

Pen moved through Clavering as a laboriously simple, adroitly pleasing figure, a stark contrast to the scornful dandy the villagers recalled from a decade prior. The Rectory stood empty; Dr. Portman had fled to Harrogate with his gout and family—an exile Pen lamented deeply in a letter, expressing regret at missing his old mentor's counsel. Instead, Pen cultivated alliances with Mr. Simcoe, the opposition preacher, the partners of the local cloth factory, and the Independent minister, all convened at the newly established Clavering Athenaeum. Founded by the Liberal party to reflect the age's progressive spirit—and likely to exclude the aristocratic old reading room where *The Edinburgh Review* had once struggled for entry—this venue became Pen's stage.

He wooed the younger cloth-factory partner with a friendly dinner invitation at the Park, gifted Honorable Mrs. Simcoe game from the estate along with a complimentary copy of her husband's latest sermon, and exploited a minor illness to solicit medical attention from Mr. Huxter. Old Pendennis would have been proud of his pupil's antics. Pen found the sport delightful, his success breeding a wicked sort of good humor.

Yet, as he departed Clavering at nightfall, having "presided" over an Athenaeum meeting or spent an evening with Mrs. Simcoe—who viewed him with awe due to his London reputation—different tides rose within him. Walking across the familiar bridge over the rushing Brawl, hearing the water's eternal roar, and glimpsing his childhood cottage of Fairoaks silhouetted against the stars, memories struck with painful clarity. He recalled the warm light in the window where his saintly mother had spent hours in prayer and care. That faint light seemed to follow him now, a wan, reproachful gaze, as if her spirit watched and warned.

How clear the night air was! How sharp the stars! The Brawl flowed ceaselessly, and the ancient trees whispered overhead, waving their dark branches over the cottage roof. There lay the terrace where, as a boy, he had wandered in summer evenings, ardent and trustful, untouched by doubt or passion, sheltered in his mother's loving bosom. Suddenly, the town clock tolled midnight, shattering his reverie and driving him onward toward his lodging in Clavering Avenue, beneath the rustling lime arches.

When he returned the following afternoon, the scene had transformed. The cottage smiled in the sunset; the bedroom windows were open, filled with light. His tenant, Captain Stokes of the Bombay Artillery (accompanied by his mother, Mrs. Stokes), greeted him with great warmth. Stokes showed him the grounds and a new pond dug in the garden from the stables, eagerly inviting Pen to inspect the roof and chimneys.

Pen, having stayed for a fortnight, confessed with a straight face that he lacked the heart to call sooner. "I understand you, sir," Captain Stokes replied. Mrs. Stokes, having retreated at the bell's ring (a gesture that seemed oddly formal to Pen), emerged in her finest gown, surrounded by her children. The youngsters clamored around Stokes; the boy leaped into an armchair—Pen's father's very own armchair. Arthur remembered days when sitting in that chair seemed as impossible as ascending the King's throne. He asked if Miss Stokes, who resembled her mother, could play the piano. She obliged. The notes drifted from the aged instrument, weak but recognizable. Yet, Pen did not truly listen to the music; he heard Laura singing as in their youth, and saw his mother bending beside the girl, beating time.

Later, at a dinner hosted by Captain Stokes in Pen's honor, attended by Squire Hobnel, the vicar of Tinckleton, and others, the atmosphere was initially stiff and melancholy. The mood shifted dramatically when Hodson, a waiter formerly known to Pen as a street urchin and now a barber, accidentally dropped a plate over Pen's shoulder. Squire Hobnel chuckled, remarking, "I suppose, Hodson, your hands are slippery with bear's-grease. He's always dropping the crockery about, that Hodson is—haw, haw!" Hodson blushed crimson, looking thoroughly disconcerted, causing Pen to burst into laughter. Good humor and hilarity took command of the evening. For the second course, a hare and partridges were served top and bottom. After the servants withdrew, Pen jokingly told the Vicar, "Mr. Stooks, you really should have asked Hodson to carve the hare." The clergyman seized the jest immediately, followed swiftly by Captains Stokes and Glanders, and finally Mr. Hobnell, who arrived late but with an immense guffaw.

While Pen orchestrated these rural schemes, events unfolded in London involving the object of his affection, Blanche. Having traveled from Tunbridge Wells for shopping and business accompanied by Mrs. Bonner, the old nurse, the pair sought to part ways with gifts. Mrs. Bonner, intending to become Lightfoot's wife and landlady of the Clavering Arms, wished to leave a token of respect. Blanche, guided by her mother's taste, selected items for herself and her mother, including a massive Bible for the elder woman and a large Johnson's Dictionary for the younger. Mrs. Bonner also invested in linens for the future inn and purchased a red-and-yellow neckerchief clearly destined for Lightfoot.

Younger than herself by twenty-five years, Mrs. Bonner viewed the youth with a blend of maternal and conjugal affection, lavishing jewelry upon him at her own expense. In the Strand, aided by Blanche, she completed her purchases. As the old lady departed, Blanche, radiant in a shot-silk dress and a magnificent mantle, politely asked a shop assistant for directions to Shepherd's Inn.

"Shepherd's Inn?" the shopman queried. "Do you want to see Mr. Strong?"

"I'd like to see the Captain very well," Blanche admitted, though her true intent was elsewhere. "But I'm not looking for him. I want to see a dear little girl who was so kind to Mr. Arthur when he was ill last year; she almost saved his life. I want to thank her." She glanced at Mrs. Bonner with an expression of virtuous pride.

Mrs. Bonner muttered, "Pretty girl... I know I want no pretty young women about Lightfoot," mentally populating the Clavering Arms with a harem of hideous chambermaids. Meanwhile, Blanche, adorned with pink ribbons, blue feathers, and a charming parasol, presented a vision of elegance that bewildered Mrs. Bolton, a scrubwoman cleaning the floors of Shepherd's Inn.
