Sleep took me like a hand over the eyes. When it let go, the sun was climbing, slow and clear, as if it had all the time in the world to expose how empty mine was.

I went again to the top of the height and looked back. The hollow I had crossed by moonlight lay as smooth and still as a cooled pan, not a tremor of leaf or fur, not a smudge of smoke to say a hearth had ever tried to live there. Yet I could not rid myself of the thought that the calmness might be only a lid—below it, ravenous things in crowds, moving with the blind appetite of mouths. I turned the other way.

Before me stretched a wide waste, the colour of old iron, and far off a stain of deeper tone—green, or something pretending to be green—that might be a wood. No shadow of plough, no scab of roof, no line of beasts, no dust-thread of travel. The sky was a clean bowl with nothing floating in it; even the air looked rinsed.

So I went toward that distant blot of life, because on this side of it there could hardly be anything at all.

When I reached the plain I found I had been walking on the bed of a river that had forgotten how to be wet. Rock everywhere: here flattened and scored like a butcher’s board after long use, there reared in humps and little pinnacles, as if the water, leaving, had pulled shapes up after it and then dropped them. Innumerable channels ran and re-ran, braided and broken, but not one held the least dampness—not even the cool, honest smell of buried moisture that clings to stone after rain. In some grooves lay a dry moss, grey-green and brittle as old herb tied for winter and then forgotten. On certain faces clung lichens, pale and stubborn, almost as hard as the rock itself—poor seasoning, ground too fine and still refusing to melt.

The air that should have been filled with the pleasant clatter and hush of waters was silent—worse than silence, a kind of stopped ear. Yet through the noon, while the sun glared as if polishing everything to a glare, I was haunted by sound: an aural mirage, as palpable as thirst. I heard—distinct as if my head were bent over a pot at full simmer—the voice of many streams. There were little lively runs that chuckled as they went, and broad heavy thrusts of current that made the deep, steady note a river makes when it is at work. My eyes insisted on dryness; my hearing argued back with such confidence that I almost believed the stones were lying.

All day it took me to reach the patch. It was a forest indeed, and when, near sunset, I climbed out of the last rock-worn trench and entered between trees, it was like stepping from an oven into a larder built in shadow. The sun, already below the crowns, sent his rays in at slants between pillar-like boles, laying long bright bars on the ground as if he were showing me where to put my feet.

I had expected pines—some uniform, disciplined stand—but here were many sorts together. Some bore a strong family likeness to trees I knew, though with a turn of branch or thickness of leaf that made them feel like words pronounced in a neighbouring dialect: recognizable, yet carrying a different mouth’s history. Others were marvels, as if an older botany had kept its own names and refused mine.

I threw myself down under what seemed a eucalyptus in blossom. Its scent hit me at once—sharp and medicinal, like crushed leaves rubbed between fingers, then sweetened by something airy and faintly honeyed. But the flowers were wrong in the way a familiar dish can be wrong when the cook has changed one essential spice. Each bloom sat in a hard calyx like a little skull, the top of it lifting like a lid to let the froth-like flower-brain overflow its cup. The sight made my stomach tighten as if I had tasted bitterness where I expected sugar.

From under those falchion leaves my gaze wandered into deep after deep of the forest. At first it seemed spacious, aisles and corridors and glades with room enough to lay out a long table and feed a village. But as the light failed, doors and windows closed. The forest drew in upon itself. The warmth ran away. Cold came, instant and sharp, as if someone had opened a cellar door inside my bones.

Night there was not merely absence of light; it was a thing with texture, like damp wool laid over the world. The tree I leaned against rose straight for a long way before it branched; yet its boughs bent so low that, in the dimming, they seemed eager to fold over me and shut me in like a pot-lid. I sat half drowsing, half staring, letting my eyes drift among the clumps of foliage that, in that uncertain light, began to suggest other shapes than their own.

A light wind began to move—first a cautious stirring, like a hand testing the surface of a fermenting vat. It set a neighbouring tree rocking. Every branch took up the motion; every twig, every leaf contributed its little sound and sway, all of them blending into the larger rhythm. In that moving mass my fancy found a pack of wolves, straining as if against a wizard’s leash. The leaves snapped and hissed like teeth. The whole clump surged and recoiled, savage with effort. I watched, and the more the wind gathered, the more the shapes acquired a dreadful life.

Another heavier mass presented a group of horses’ heads and forequarters thrust out from stalls. Their necks went up and down with impatience, a rhythm that should have been almost comical—like farm horses at feeding time, smelling the bucket—had the heads not been so gaunt, so strange. Several were bare skulls. One had skin drawn tight over bone, like meat dried too close to the fire. One had lost its lower jaw and hung low, unutterably weary; then suddenly it would heave high, as if trying to ease a bit in a mouth that was no longer there. Above them, perched at the end of a branch, floated upright the form of a woman, arms waving in imperious gesture, as if conducting with no music but rage.

The definiteness of these leaf-bodies first surprised me and then began to discompose me. It was as though the forest had a vocabulary of shapes and, in the twilight, was choosing the most violent nouns it knew. What if it should overpower my brain with seeming reality? What if, by looking, I consented?

Darkness thickened. The wind dropped. The shapes folded back into leaf and branch and shadow. The forest shut its mouth. I fell asleep.

It was still dark when I began to be aware of noise far off—a confused rushing, mingled with faint cries, as if a distant market had broken into a riot. It grew. The sound swelled through the trees until the whole wood seemed filled with gathering multitudes. From all sides at once the tumult drew nearer. The place where I lay became, impossibly, the centre of a commotion that extended everywhere.

I scarcely moved hand or foot. I lay as one lies when a wild creature is near—still, listening, trying to make one’s breath small.

At length the moon approached the forest and came slowly into it. With her first gleam the noises increased to a deafening uproar, and dim shapes rose out of the dark around me. As she climbed and brightened, the shapes sharpened, and the sound became more than sound: it pressed on the skin, it rattled the teeth. It was like being inside a vast iron kitchen where a thousand pans were being struck, dragged, dropped—only the clanging had voices in it.

A battle raged around me.

Wild cries and roars of rage, the shock of onset, the long grind of struggle—everything mingled with words, articulated and yet made monstrous by the mouths that uttered them. Curses and credos, snarls and sneers, laughter and mockery, sacred names and howls of hate came huddling together, each trying to drown the other, a chaos of tongues insisting on their own righteousness. I heard phrases that might have been prayers if they had not been flung like stones.

Skeletons and phantoms fought in maddest confusion.

Swords swept through the phantoms; they only shivered, like mist cut by a knife, and surged back. Maces crashed on the skeletons, shattering them hideously; yet not one fell or ceased so long as a single joint held two bones together. Bones of men and horses lay scattered and heaped; and grinding and crunching them under foot, the skeletons fought on. Everywhere charged white steeds, gaunt as famine, their hooves striking sparks in moonlight. Everywhere, on foot or astride battle-horses made of wind-blown vapour, raged and ravened the indestructible spectres. Weapons and hoofs clashed and crushed; jaws that had no flesh and throats that were only suggestion swelled the tumult with the war-cry of every opinion—bad or good—that had ever bred strife and injustice and cruelty in any world.

The holiest words went with the most hating blow. Truths, bent out of shape by lies, flew hurtling like javelins. Every moment some one would turn against his comrades and fight more wildly than before, “THE TRUTH! THE TRUTH!” still his cry—like a cook screaming “purity” while poisoning the pot.

One I noted who wheeled ever in a circle and smote on all sides, unable to stop, unable to choose a single enemy because everything offended him. Now and then, worn out, a pair would sit for a minute side by side—as if taking breath between courses—then rise and renew the fierce combat. None stooped to comfort a fallen one. None stepped wide to spare him. The ground was a chopping-block, and they made mince of one another with endless appetite.

The moon shone till the sun was near, and all the night long I had glimpses of a woman moving above the strife-tormented multitude, as if she floated on a current only she could feel. Now she was on this front, now on that, one outstretched arm urging the fight onward, the other pressed against her side. And always her voice cut through the din with the cruel clarity of a command shouted in a crowded hall:

“Ye are men: slay one another!”

When I saw her dead eyes and the dark spot upon her, I remembered the leaf-woman of the evening before and felt the same sickness rise. She was as much part of the wood’s language as the wolves and horses had been: an old word for domination, spoken without mercy.

Such was the battle of the dead which I saw and heard as I lay under the tree.

Just before sunrise a breeze went through the forest, not like the earlier wind that had played at making shapes, but like a clean draught through a room long shut. A voice cried—not screamed, not raged, but uttered as if it were a proverb older than any quarrel:

“Let the dead bury their dead!”

At the word, the contending thousands dropped noiseless. The uproar ceased as if a hand had turned it off. When the sun looked in, he saw never a bone, never a phantom—only here and there a withered branch that might have been broken by wind or age.

I rose and resumed my journey through as quiet a wood as ever grew out of quiet earth. The morning wind had ceased when the sun appeared, and the trees were silent. Not a bird sang. Not a squirrel, mouse, or weasel showed itself. Not even a belated moth crossed my path. The forest held its breath, and the hush was not peace but a kind of enforced muteness, like a song whose language has been forbidden until the singers forget the words.

As I went, I kept watch over myself and did not dare let my eyes rest on any forest-shape. For all the time I seemed to hear, faint under the silence, sounds of mattock and spade and hurtling bones—as if somewhere, behind the bark of the world, labourers were digging and piling and throwing, busy with the debris of unended wars. Any moment my eyes might open on things I would not see. Daylight prudence muttered that perhaps, to appear, ten thousand phantoms awaited only my consenting fancy—only my mind lending them the names that would make them real.

In the middle of the afternoon I came out of the wood and found before me another net of dry water-courses. At first I thought I had wandered from my line and turned back, but I soon saw I had not; it was another branch of the same vanished river-bed, or a second channel of the same old life, split and lost.

I began at once to cross it. I was in the bottom of a wide channel when the sun set. I sat down to await the moon, and sleepiness came on me like hunger. I stretched myself on the moss.

The moment my head lay down I heard the sound of rushing streams—suddenly, intimately, as if I had put my ear to a cask of fermenting cider and heard it working, or to a shell that carries a remembered sea. All sorts of sweet watery noises came at once: little trickles stitching over stones, broader glides, occasional chuckles where water would have caught at roots. The melody was veiled, molten, as if poured from a ladle in the dark. It sang me into a dreamless sleep.

When I woke, the sun was already up, and the wrinkled country lay widely visible. Covered with shadows, it was striped and mottled like the skin of some wild animal. As the sun rose, the shadows diminished, and it seemed as if the rocks were re-absorbing the darkness that had oozed out of them during the night, drinking it back as dry earth drinks spilled broth.

Hitherto I had loved my Arab mare and my books more, I fear, than live man or woman. Now at length my soul was athirst for a human presence. I longed even for those inhabitants of this alien world whom the raven had so vaguely described as nearest my sort—anyone with a hearth-sound in their speech, anyone whose day was measured by hunger and giving and the small domestic ceremonies that keep memory alive.

With heavy yet hoping heart, and mind haunted by a doubt whether I was going in any direction at all, I kept wearily travelling north-west and by south.
