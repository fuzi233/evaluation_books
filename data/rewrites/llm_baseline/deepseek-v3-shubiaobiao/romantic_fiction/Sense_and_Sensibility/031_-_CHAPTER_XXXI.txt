Marianne awoke the next morning to the same heavy weight of sorrow that had burdened her when she closed her eyes. Elinor, ever patient, encouraged her to speak freely, and before breakfast was served, they had revisited the subject again and again—Elinor steady in her counsel, Marianne fluctuating between desperate hope and bitter despair.

At times, she convinced herself Willoughby was as unfortunate as she, as blameless. Then, just as swiftly, the illusion shattered, leaving her raw and inconsolable. She vacillated between indifference to the world’s scrutiny and a longing to retreat from it entirely. Yet one resolve held firm: she avoided Mrs. Jennings whenever possible, enduring her presence in resentful silence.

*She cannot understand,* Marianne thought bitterly. *Her kindness is not compassion—it is curiosity.*

Elinor, though pained by her sister’s harsh judgment, recognized it as the distortion of grief. Marianne’s tender heart, so attuned to feeling, often mistook others’ intentions.

A disturbance came mid-morning when Mrs. Jennings swept into their room, beaming, a letter clutched in her hand.

“Here, my dear,” she said brightly, “something to lift your spirits!”

For one delirious moment, Marianne’s pulse raced—*Willoughby. It must be Willoughby.* She envisioned a page brimming with remorse, an explanation, a plea for forgiveness. Then reality crashed upon her: her mother’s handwriting, not his. The disappointment was a physical blow. Tears, hot and furious, spilled down her cheeks.

Mrs. Jennings, oblivious to the pain she had unwittingly inflicted, patted Marianne’s shoulder and withdrew, urging her to take comfort in the letter. But when Marianne read it, her mother’s affectionate words—so full of faith in Willoughby’s constancy—only deepened the wound.

Elinor, watching helplessly, resolved to write to their mother at once, though the task filled her with dread. She had barely begun when a sharp rap at the door startled them both.

“Who could that be?” Elinor murmured.

Marianne glanced out the window. “Colonel Brandon,” she said with a frown. “Must he intrude?”

Elinor assured her he would not enter without Mrs. Jennings present, but moments later, his voice echoed in the hall. Marianne retreated to her room, unwilling to endure another well-meaning visitor.

Colonel Brandon’s face was grave as he entered. “Forgive the intrusion,” he said to Elinor. “I had hoped to speak with you alone.”

His manner was so earnest that Elinor’s apprehension softened. “You have news?”

He hesitated. “It concerns Willoughby.”

Her breath caught. “Tell me.”

What followed was a tale of betrayal and sorrow—a story of a young woman named Eliza, deceived and abandoned, left to ruin while Willoughby moved unscathed through the world. The parallels to Marianne’s own heartbreak were undeniable.

“I thought,” Brandon finished quietly, “that knowing the truth might spare your sister greater pain.”

Elinor’s hands trembled. “You were right to tell me.”

His gaze was weary. “I only pray it helps her.”

As he took his leave, Elinor sat motionless, the weight of his words settling upon her. She would share them with Marianne—not to wound her further, but to arm her against false hope.

For sometimes, the cruelest kindness was the truth.
