The courtyard of the Nuns' House lay bathed in a moonlight that offered no warmth, only a stark illumination of the brazen door-plate. It stared back at the two young men with the cold indifference of a judgment seat. Behind them, the figures of the damsels had vanished into the shadows of the cloisters. The silence stretched, taut and thin, until the two men exchanged a glance that spoke volumes of shared defeat and walked away together down the perspective of the street.

"Do you stay here long, Mr. Drood?" Neville asked, his voice cutting through the damp air.

"Not this time," Edwin replied, the tone casual, almost dismissive. "I leave for London tomorrow. But I shall drift back and forth until next Midsummer. After that, I depart Cloisterham, and England itself, for many a long day."

"Are you going abroad?"

"Going to wake up Egypt a little," Edwin said, the condescension dripping from his words like water off a duck's back.

"Are you reading?"

"Reading?" Edwin repeated, a flicker of contempt crossing his features. "No. Doing. Working. Engineering. My father, a former partner, left a portion of the Firm's capital to me. Until I come of age, I am a liability to the Firm; then I inherit my modest share. Jack—you met him at dinner—is my guardian and trustee until then."

"I heard from Mr. Crisparkle regarding your other good fortune."

"What do you mean by 'other good fortune'?"

Neville had advanced cautiously, his movements a mixture of predatory intent and fearful shyness, embodying that peculiar tension of being both hunter and hunted. Edwin's retort was abrupt, lacking all polish. They halted, locking eyes in a heated, silent contest.

"I hope," Neville ventured, "there is no offense, Mr. Drood, in my innocently referring to your betrothal?"

"By George!" Edwin cried, accelerating his pace. "Everyone in this chattering old Cloisterham refers to it. I wonder no public-house hasn't been erected with my portrait for the sign of *The Betrothed's Head*. Or *Pussy's* portrait. One or the other."

"I am not accountable for Mr. Crisparkle mentioning the matter to me so openly," Neville began.

"No; that is true; you are not," Edwin conceded.

"But," Neville resumed, "I am accountable for mentioning it to you. And I did so, on the supposition that you could not fail to be highly proud of it."

Two currents of human nature drove this exchange. Neville Landless, already captivated by Little Rosebud, felt a surge of indignation that Edwin Drood—a man beneath her station—should treat his prize so lightly. Conversely, Edwin Drood, sufficiently smitten by Helena, resented that Helena's brother, a man far beneath her, should dispose of him so coolly, effectively removing him from the equation.

"The last remark deserves an answer," Edwin stated. "I don't know, Mr. Neville—adapting that mode of address from Mr. Crisparkle—that what people are proudest of, they usually talk most about. Nor do I know that what they cherish most, they desire others to discuss. But I lead a busy life. I speak under correction by you, the readers, who ought to know everything, and I daresay do."

By now, both men had descended into savagery. Neville stood exposed in the open; Edwin hid beneath the transparent cover of a popular tune, pausing occasionally to feign admiration of the picturesque moonlight ahead.

"It does not seem very civil in you," Neville remarked finally, "to reflect upon a stranger who comes here, not having had your advantages, trying to make up for lost time. But, to be sure, I was not raised in a 'busy life,' and my ideas of civility were formed among Heathens."

"Perhaps, the best civility, regardless of our upbringing," Edwin retorted, "is to mind our own business. If you will set me that example, I promise to follow it."

"Do you know that you take a great deal too much upon yourself?" Neville shot back angrily. "And in the part of the world I come from, you would be called to account for it?"

"By whom, for instance?" Edwin asked, halting to survey the other with a look of disdain.

Suddenly, a firm right hand landed on Edwin's shoulder. Jasper stepped between them. It seemed he, too, had circled the Nuns' House, emerging from the shadowy side of the road.

"Ned, Ned, Ned!" Jasper exclaimed. "We must have no more of this. I dislike this. I have overheard high words between you. Remember, my dear boy, you are almost host tonight. You belong to the place, representing it to a stranger. Mr. Neville is a stranger; you owe him the obligations of hospitality. And, Mr. Neville," placing his left hand on the younger man's shoulder, walking between them, hand-to-hand, "you will pardon me, but I appeal to you to govern your temper. What is amiss? Why ask! Let there be nothing amiss, and the question is superfluous. Are we all three on a good understanding?"

After a silent struggle to determine who would speak last, Edwin broke the silence. "As far as I am concerned, Jack, there is no anger in me."

"Nor in me," Neville said, though less freely, or perhaps less carelessly. "But if Mr. Drood knew all that lies behind me, far away from here, he might understand why sharp-edged words cut so deep."

"Perhaps," Jasper soothed, "we should not qualify our good understanding. We should avoid anything resembling reproof or condition; it might not seem generous. Frankly and freely, you see, there is no anger in Ned. Frankly and freely, there is no anger in you, Mr. Neville?"

"None at all, Mr. Jasper." Yet, not quite so frankly, or so freely, or perhaps so carelessly.

"All over then! My bachelor gatehouse is a few yards hence, the heater is on the fire, and wine and glasses await on the table. It is not a stone's throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you depart tomorrow. We will bring Mr. Neville in for a stirrup-cup."

"With all my heart, Jack."

"And with all mine, Mr. Jasper." Neville found himself unable to refuse, though he wished to linger. He sensed he had lost control of his temper; Edwin's coolness, far from being contagious, burned him red-hot.

Jasper, walking centrally, hands on shoulders, beautifully refrained a drinking song, and they ascended to his rooms. Upon entering, the first object visible, illuminated by the lamp Jasper lit beside the fire, was the portrait above the mantelpiece. It was an object unlikely to improve the atmosphere between the two young men, awkwardly resurrecting the source of their discord. Both glanced at it consciously but said nothing. Jasper, however, seemingly unaware of the depth of their rift, drew immediate attention to it.

"You recognize that picture, Mr. Neville?" Jasper shaded the lamp to cast its glow upon it.

"I recognize it, but it is far from flattering the original."

"Oh, you are hard upon it! It was done by Ned, who gave it to me as a present."

"I am sorry for that, Mr. Drood." Neville apologized sincerely. "If I had known I was in the artist's presence—"

"Oh, a joke, sir, a mere joke," Edwin interjected with a provoking yawn. "A little humorizing of Pussy's points! I'm going to paint her gravely, one of these days, if she's good."

The air of leisurely patronage and indifference with which this was delivered, as the speaker threw himself back in a chair, clasping his hands behind his head as a rest, was exasperating to the excitable Neville. Jasper watched them, smiling faintly, then turned his back to mix a jug of mulled wine at the fire. The process required considerable mixing and compounding.

"I suppose, Mr. Neville," Edwin said, quick to resent the indignant protest against himself visible in Neville's face, "that if you painted the picture of your lady love—"

"I can't paint," Neville interrupted hastily.

"That's your misfortune, not your fault. You would if you could. But if you could, I suppose you would make her (regardless of her reality) Juno, Minerva, Diana, and Venus, all in one. Eh?"

"I have no lady love, and I can't say."

"If I were to try my hand," Edwin said, a boyish boastfulness rising in him, "on a portrait of Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you; in earnest—you should see what I could do!"

"My sister's consent to sit for it being first obtained, I suppose? Since that will never happen, I fear I shall never see what you can do. I must bear the loss."

Jasper turned from the fire, filled a large goblet for Neville, filled one for Edwin, handed them out, then filled his own.

"Come, Mr. Neville, we drink to my nephew, Ned. As it is his foot in the stirrup—metaphorically—our stirrup-cup is dedicated to him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!"
