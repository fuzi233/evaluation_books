The Flying Stars

“*The most beautiful crime I ever committed,*” Flambeau would say in his later years, when morality had finally caught up with him, “*was also, by a strange twist of fate, my last. It happened at Christmas.*” As an artist of villainy, he had always believed that a crime should be tailored to its setting—like a painter choosing the right landscape for his masterpiece. A nobleman’s fraud deserved a long oak-paneled hall; a Jewish merchant’s downfall, the glittering confusion of Café Riche. In England, if he were to rob a dean, he’d do it amid cathedral lawns and grey towers. In France, stealing from a wealthy peasant meant placing the scene against a row of clipped poplars and the vast, silent fields where Millet’s spirits wandered.

“So,” he’d continue, “my final act was a Christmas crime—a cozy, middle-class English affair, straight out of Charles Dickens. I did it in a fine old house near Putney: crescent drive, stable beside it, gates bearing the family name, and a monkey tree in the garden. You know the type. I think I captured the Dickensian spirit quite well—almost too well. It’s a shame I repented the same night.”

And then, with quiet pride, he’d recount the tale—not as a confession, but as a performance. Even from within, the story was odd. From without, it made no sense at all. And so, let us begin not from the inside, but from the outside—the only place where truth can be seen clearly.

It began on Boxing Day, as the front doors of the Putney house opened onto the garden, where the monkey tree stood like a sentinel. A young girl stepped out, wrapped in thick brown furs, carrying bread for the birds. Her face was lovely—bright eyes, warm cheeks—but her body was almost hidden beneath layers of fur, making her look less like a girl and more like a small, amiable bear.

The afternoon light was deepening into dusk, painting the bare flowerbeds in crimson shadows. On one side of the house stood the stable; on the other, a laurel-lined path led to the back garden. The girl scattered crumbs—again, perhaps for the fourth or fifth time that day, since the dog kept eating them—and then slipped through the laurels into the evergreen grove behind.

There, she stopped short.

Above the high garden wall, silhouetted against the fading sky, sat a figure—perched like a rider on an invisible horse.

“Oh, don’t jump, Mr. Crook!” she called, startled. “It’s much too high!”

The man leapt down lightly, landing beside her as if it were nothing. He was tall and angular, with dark hair standing up like bristles, sharp features, and a sallow complexion that seemed foreign. But it was the red tie he wore—the only part of his clothes that seemed cared for—that drew attention. Perhaps it was a statement.

“I think I was born to be a burglar,” he said calmly. “I’d have been one, too, if I hadn’t been born next door. I can’t see any harm in it.”

“How can you say such things?” she protested.

“Well,” he replied, “if you’re born on the wrong side of the wall, why shouldn’t you climb over?”

“I never know what you’ll do next,” she said, smiling.

“I don’t often know myself,” he answered. “But now I’m on the right side.”

“And which side is the right one?” she asked.

“The side you’re on,” he said.

They walked together toward the front garden when suddenly, three sharp blasts echoed from a motor horn. A sleek, pale-green car sped up the drive, stopping with a hum before the front doors.

“Hullo, hullo!” said the man in the red tie. “Here’s someone born on the right side after all. Didn’t know, Miss Adams, that your Santa Claus was this modern.”

“That’s my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer,” she said. “He always comes on Boxing Day.”

After a brief pause—too long to be natural—she added, “He’s very kind.”

John Crook, journalist and occasional revolutionary, had heard of Sir Leopold Fischer, the powerful City financier. But Fischer had never heard of him—except perhaps in *The Clarion* or *The New Age*, where Crook had once criticized him sharply. Yet Crook said nothing. Instead, he watched as the chauffeur and manservant helped their master from the car, carefully unwrapping him like a priceless parcel.

Rugs, furs, scarves—layer after layer fell away, revealing a large, friendly-looking man with a grey goat-beard and a broad smile. He rubbed his fur gloves together, still adjusting to the cold.

Before the process ended, Colonel Adams—father of the girl—emerged from the house, wearing a red smoking cap that gave him the air of a colonial governor. With him was James Blount, his brother-in-law, recently returned from Canada—a big, boisterous farmer with a yellow beard. Also present was Father Brown, the quiet priest from the nearby Catholic church. The colonel’s late wife had been Catholic, and the children had been raised in the faith. Though unassuming—his name was Brown, and he looked it—he had become a trusted guest.

Inside, the entrance hall was spacious enough to hold the entire party. The porch and vestibule were unusually large, forming a sort of grand room between the front door and the staircase. Over the fireplace hung the colonel’s sword. There, Sir Leopold finished removing his wraps and presented his gift to Ruby.

From a deep pocket, he pulled a black oval case. With a flourish, he opened it—and the room seemed to catch fire.

Three white diamonds, larger than eggs, lay nestled in orange velvet. They sparkled like stars, casting light across every face. The “Flying Stars”—the three great African diamonds, stolen so many times they were legendary. Fischer smiled proudly.

“I’ll put them back now,” he said, tucking the case into his coat. “Had to be careful coming down. They’re worth a fortune—and dangerous. Any thief might have taken them on the way. Could’ve lost them.”

“Quite natural,” muttered Crook. “If people ask for bread and you give them nothing, I wouldn’t blame them for taking the stone.”

“You mustn’t talk like that,” Ruby said, flushing.

“You’ve only started since you became a Socialist,” she added, half-joking.

“A saint,” said Father Brown quietly.

Sir Leopold frowned. “Ruby means a Socialist.”

“A radical isn’t a man who eats radishes,” Crook retorted. “A Conservative isn’t one who makes jam. And a Socialist isn’t someone who wants tea with a chimney-sweep. A Socialist wants all chimneys swept—and the sweeps paid.”

“But won’t let you own your own soot,” murmured the priest.

Crook looked at him with interest. “Does one want to own soot?”

“One might,” said Brown. “Gardeners use it. And once, when the conjuror didn’t come, I made six children happy at Christmas—using soot. Applied externally.”

“Oh, splendid!” Ruby laughed. “Do it for us now!”

Blount roared with laughter. Fischer looked mildly offended. Then came a knock at the front door.

Father Brown opened it. Outside, the garden glowed under a violet sunset. A dusty messenger stood there, holding a letter.

“Any of you gentlemen Mr. Blount?” he asked.

Blount took the letter, tore it open, and read. His face darkened slightly—then cleared.

“I’m sorry to be a nuisance, colonel,” he said cheerfully. “But an old friend of mine is coming tonight—Florian, the French acrobat and comic actor. I knew him in the West. He says he has business with me.”

“Of course,” said the colonel. “Any friend of yours.”

“He’ll black his face,” Blount joked. “Or everyone else’s eyes. I don’t care—I like pantomime. Man sits on his top hat.”

“Not on mine,” Fischer said firmly.

“Well,” said Crook, “don’t let’s argue. There are lower jokes than sitting on a hat.”

Fischer, annoyed by Crook’s closeness to Ruby, sneered, “No doubt you’ve found something lower. What is it?”

“Letting a top hat sit on you,” said Crook.

Blount laughed. “Now, now, don’t spoil the evening. Let’s do something fun. A proper old English pantomime—clown, columbine, harlequin. I saw one at twelve. It burned in my mind like a bonfire. I came back last year and found it gone—only fairy tales now. I want a hot poker and a policeman turned into sausages. They give me princesses moonlighting. Blue Beard’s more my style.”

“I’m all for turning a policeman into sausages,” said Crook. “That’s better than some recent definitions of Socialism. But costumes? I haven’t killed a policeman lately.”

Blount slapped his thigh. “We can! I’ve got Florian’s number. He knows every costumier in London. I’ll phone him to bring a police outfit.”

He dashed to the telephone.

“Oh, glorious!” Ruby cried. “I’ll be columbine, and you’ll be pantaloon, godfather.”

Fischer stiffened. “I think, my dear, you should find another pantaloon.”

“I’ll be pantaloon,” said Colonel Adams, taking his cigar out of his mouth—for the first and last time.

“We need a statue,” Blount said, returning. “Mr. Crook will be clown—he knows all the old jokes. I’ll be harlequin. Florian’s bringing the costume. We can perform here. Audience on the stairs. Doors for the backdrop. Open, it’s a moonlit garden. Closed, it’s a drawing room. Magic.” He drew a line across the floor with billiard chalk—footlights.

How they managed it in minutes remains a mystery. But youth, reckless and energetic, took over. The columbine wore a lampshade-like skirt. The clown and pantaloon painted themselves white with flour, red with rouge. Harlequin, already covered in silver paper from cigar boxes, nearly shattered the chandelier until Ruby produced old paste jewels from a fancy dress party.

Blount, wild with excitement, placed a paper donkey’s head on Father Brown, who bore it patiently. He even tried to attach a tail to Fischer’s coat—until Ruby stopped him.

“Uncle’s too absurd,” she said to Crook, dangling a string of sausages around his neck.

“He’s harlequin to your columbine,” Crook said. “I’m just the clown who tells the old jokes.”

“I wish you were the harlequin,” she said, leaving the sausages swinging.

Father Brown, though he had helped make a pillow into a pantomime baby, sat among the audience, solemn as a child at a first matinee. Sir Leopold sat in front, his massive fur-collared figure blocking the view—but whether that mattered, no one knows.

The pantomime was chaotic, yet strangely alive. Crook, as clown, became everything: author, prompter, musician. At key moments, he bounded to the piano and pounded out ridiculous tunes—“The Pirates of Penzance,” “Where Did You Get That Hat?”—each note matching the madness.

Then, the moment arrived. The front doors flew open, revealing the moonlit garden—and the professional guest: Florian, dressed as a policeman.

Crook played the constabulary chorus. The crowd erupted. Florian mimed the perfect officer—then was hit by the harlequin. He turned, astonished. Hit again. Then, the harlequin tackled him, and Florian collapsed—perfectly limp.

He was swung like a sack, twisted like a club, lifted off the ground—while Crook played “I Arise from Dreams of Thee.” When the harlequin shuffled him across his back, the music changed to “With My Bundle on My Shoulder.” Finally, the policeman was dropped with a convincing thud—and the piano burst into jingles: “I sent a letter to my love, and on the way I dropped it.”

At that point, Father Brown’s view was blocked. Sir Leopold rose abruptly, rummaging in his pockets. He sat, fumbled, stood again. For a moment, it seemed he would cross the stage. Then he turned a glare at Crook—and left the room silently.

Brown watched the harlequin dance slowly backward into the garden, the silver costume glowing under the moon. The applause was thunderous—until someone touched his arm.

“Come to the study,” a voice whispered.

He followed, unsure. Inside, Colonel Adams sat in pantaloon costume, eyes sad. Fischer leaned against the mantelpiece, breathing hard.

“This is painful, Father Brown,” said Adams. “The diamonds—those we saw earlier—are gone. And since you—”

“As I,” Brown interrupted, grinning, “was sitting directly behind him—”

“Nothing like that shall be suggested,” Adams said firmly. “I only ask for your help—as a gentleman.”

“Which is turning out his pockets,” said Brown, and did so. Seven and sixpence, a ticket, a crucifix, a breviary, a chocolate bar.

Adams stared. “I’d rather see the inside of your head than your pockets. My daughter is one of your people. She has lately—”

“She has lately,” Fischer snapped, “opened her father’s house to a cut-throat Socialist who admits he’d steal from the rich. Now look—the rich man is poorer.”

“If you want my head,” said Brown, weary, “you can have it. But first, I notice: men who plan to steal diamonds don’t talk about Socialism. They denounce it.”

Both men shifted. Brown continued: “We know these people. That Socialist wouldn’t steal a diamond—or a pyramid. We must look at the one man we don’t know—the policeman. Where is he now?”

Adams strode out. A silence followed. Fischer stared at Brown. Brown read his breviary.

Adams returned. “The policeman is still on stage. The curtain has risen and fallen six times. He hasn’t moved.”

Brown dropped the book. His eyes went wide. Then, slowly, understanding dawned.

“Colonel,” he said, “when did your wife die?”

“Two months ago,” Adams replied. “Her brother James arrived a week too late.”

Brown jumped. “Come on! We must go to the stage!”

They rushed past the actors, who were whispering quietly. Brown knelt beside the prostrate policeman.

“Chloroform,” he said, rising. “I just realized it.”

And in that moment, the true crime revealed itself—not in theft, but in betrayal.
