An unspeakable, unnameable horror seized me first—no sharp edge to it, no defined boundary of fear, which made it all the worse. There was utter darkness, but not the soft, familiar dark of Flatland’s nights; then a dizzy, roiling sickness rose in me, the sensation of seeing without any of the sight I had known my whole life. I stared at a Line that had no thickness, that stretched beyond any measurement of length I could name; Space that folded out past every direction I had ever mapped, so vast it could not be contained even by my wildest mathematical conjectures. I was both myself, A Square, sitting in my study, and something unmoored, unshackled from the two dimensions I had always called home. When I fumbled my way to speech, I shrieked aloud in raw agony, “Either this is madness, or I have fallen straight to Hell.”
“It is neither,” the calm, deep voice of the Sphere rumbled at my side, unshaken by my terror. “It is Knowledge. It is Three Dimensions. Open your eyes once more, and look steadily.”
I did as he bade, and a new world burst open before me. There, standing fully, tangibly real before my eyes, was every scrap of perfect circular beauty I had ever inferred, conjectured, or dreamed of across my decades of mathematical study. What I would once have called the very center of the Stranger’s form lay open to my view—yet I could see no heart, no lungs, no arteries, no soft internal flesh. There was only a beautiful, harmonious, unbroken Something for which I had no name in the Flatland tongue: what you, my readers in Spaceland, would call the smooth, perfect surface of a Sphere.
I prostrated my whole mind before my guide, awestruck. “How is it, O divine ideal of consummate loveliness and wisdom, that I can see deep into your very core, and yet cannot discern your heart, your lungs, your arteries, your liver?”
“What you think you are seeing, you do not truly see,” he replied, gentle but firm. “No being, not even the highest of my kind, can lay eyes on their own internal parts. I am of an entirely different order of being from any that exists in Flatland. Were I merely a Circle, cut from your own two-dimensional plane, you would be able to peer straight through my outline to my intestines. But I am a being made of many Circles stacked one atop the other, the Many contained in the One—what we call here a Sphere. Just as the outer face of a Cube looks like a Square to you, the outer face of a Sphere presents itself as a Circle to any Flatlander’s eyes.”
Bewildered as I was by my teacher’s strange, riddling words, I no longer chafed against his lessons. I worshipped him in silent, humbled adoration. He spoke again, his voice softening.
“Do not distress yourself if you cannot grasp the deepest mysteries of Spaceland at first. They will dawn on you, little by little. Let us begin by turning our gaze back to the land you came from. Come with me for a short while back to the flat plains of Flatland, and I will show you something you have reasoned out for years, but never once seen with your own eyes: an angle, visible, laid bare before you.”
“Impossible!” I cried out—but the Sphere already led the way, and I followed as if in a dream, until his voice halted me.
“Look down yonder. Behold your own pentagonal house, and every one of its inmates.”
I looked below, and saw with my own physical eye every tiny, intimate detail of my household that I had until that moment only been able to infer with cold logic. How thin, how pale, how shadowy my old guesswork was next to the blazing, vivid reality laid out under my gaze! My four sons slept calm and still in the northwestern chambers, my two orphaned grandsons curled up asleep in the south wing. The servants, the butler, my daughter—each of them rested in their own separate apartments, fully visible to me. Only my dear, affectionate Wife, alarmed by my long, unexplained absence, had left her bed and was pacing back and forth in the main hall, anxious for my return. Even the page boy, woken by my earlier screams, had snuck out of his room on the pretense of checking if I had fainted somewhere, and was prying open the locked cabinet in my study to rummage through my papers. I did not have to infer any of this. I saw it, plain as day. As we drifted closer, I could even make out every last thing inside my cabinet: the two chests of gold, the mathematical tablets the Sphere had mentioned to me only minutes before.
My chest ached at the sight of my Wife’s quiet distress, and I would have sprung downward at once to reassure her—only to find I could not move a single inch of my own volition.
“Do not trouble yourself for your Wife,” my Guide said. “She will not be left in anxiety much longer. For now, let us take a wider survey of all Flatland.”
Once more I felt myself rising through empty Space. It was exactly as the Sphere had promised. The further we drifted from the ground below, the wider my field of vision stretched. My whole native city lay spread before me in miniature: the inside of every house, every living creature walking the streets, no walls, no blinds, no secrets hidden from my sight. We climbed higher still, and the hidden depths of the earth were laid bare: the dark mines sunk far below the surface, the innermost secret caverns carved into the hillsides.
Awestruck at the sight of all these unveiled mysteries I had never dared hope to see, I said to my companion, “Behold! I have become as a God. The wise men of Flatland have long taught that to see all things—what we call Omnividence—is an attribute only the Divine can claim.”
There was a faint edge of scorn in the Sphere’s voice as he answered. “Is that so? Then every last pickpocket and cutthroat in my country ought to be worshipped as a God by your wise men, for every one of them can see every inch of your Flatland, just as much as you do right now. Take my word for it: your sages are very wrong.”
“But then—” I pressed, “is Omnividence a trait other beings, not just Gods, possess?”
“I do not know for certain,” the Sphere said. “But even if a thief or a murderer in Spaceland can see every single thing that exists in your two-dimensional world, that is no reason to call him a deity. This Omnividence, as you name it—we do not use that word much in Spaceland—does it make you more just? More merciful? Less selfish? More loving? Not a single bit. So how can it make you more divine?”
“More merciful, more loving!” I protested. “Those are the traits of mere Women, of Straight Lines! We all know a Circle is a far higher being than a Straight Line, for knowledge and wisdom are worth far more than soft, trivial affection.”
“It is not my place to rank the worth of all human faculties,” the Sphere replied. “Yet many of the best, wisest souls in Spaceland value the affections far more than cold intellect, and hold your so-called despised Straight Lines in higher esteem than your most lauded, perfect Circles. But we will speak no more of that. Look yonder. Do you recognize that building?”
I looked out across the plains, and saw an immense polygonal structure rising ahead of us. I knew it at once: the General Assembly Hall of the States of Flatland, ringed by dense grids of pentagonal buildings set at sharp right angles, the streets of the great Metropolis. We were approaching the capital.
“Here we descend,” my Guide said.
It was the first hour of the first day of the two thousandth year of our era, the start of a new millennium. True to their long, unbroken custom, the highest Circles of the realm had gathered for a solemn conclave, just as they had met at the same hour on the first day of the year 1000, and the first day of the year 0. The meeting minutes from those two prior gatherings were read aloud by a figure I recognized at once: my own brother, a perfectly symmetrical Square, the chief clerk of the High Council. The records from both sessions were identical, word for word:
“Whereas the States have long been troubled by ill-intentioned persons claiming to have received revelations from a world beyond our own, and producing false demonstrations that drive both themselves and others to frenzy, the Grand Council resolves unanimously that on the first day of each millenary, special injunctions shall be sent to every district Prefect in Flatland, to conduct strict searches for all such misguided persons. No formal mathematical examination shall be granted them: all Isosceles Triangles of any rank found guilty shall be put to death, regular Triangles shall be publicly scourged then imprisoned, Squares and Pentagons shall be committed to the district asylum, and any person of higher rank shall be arrested at once and sent to the capital to be judged by the Council itself.”
“You hear your fate,” the Sphere said to me, as the Council voted to pass the formal resolution for the third time. “Death or life imprisonment awaits you, the apostle of the Gospel of Three Dimensions.”
“Not so,” I cried, frantic to make them understand. “The truth is so clear to me now, the nature of real Space so palpable, that I swear I could make even a small child understand it. Let me descend this very instant, and I will enlighten all of them.”
“Not yet,” my Guide said. “The time for that will come. For now, I must fulfill my own mission. Stay here, where you are.”
With that, he moved with incredible dexterity, dropping right into the “sea” of Flatland below me, dead in the center of the circle of seated Councillors.
“I come!” his voice boomed out across the chamber, as his circular cross-section widened before all their eyes. “I come to proclaim that there exists a land of Three Dimensions!”
Many of the younger Councillors scrambled back in open, unmasked horror as the shape of the Sphere bulged and shifted right in front of their faces. But the presiding High Circle did not flinch, did not show the slightest trace of surprise or alarm. At a single subtle signal from him, six low-rank Isosceles guards charged in from all sides of the chamber, throwing themselves at the strange intruder.
“We have him!” they shouted. “No—wait, yes, we still have him! He’s moving! He’s gone!”
“My Lords,” the President said to the Junior Circles of the Council, his voice perfectly calm. “There is no call for surprise whatsoever. The secret archives, to which only I hold access, record that an identical occurrence took place at the start of the last two millennia. You will of course say nothing of these trivial events outside this council chamber.”
He raised his voice to call the guards. “Arrest the policemen. Gag them. You know your duty.”
The wretched, ill-fated officers—unwitting witnesses to a State secret they would never be allowed to share—were dragged away to their fate. The President turned back to the assembled Councillors. “My Lords, all official business of this Council is now concluded. I have only to wish you all a very happy New Year.”
Before he dismissed the assembly, he drew aside the Clerk—my excellent, deeply unfortunate brother—and expressed his sincere regret that, in accordance with ancient precedent and for the sake of State secrecy, he must condemn him to life in perpetual imprisonment. He added, for my brother’s comfort, that so long as he never spoke a word of what he had witnessed that day, his life would be spared.
