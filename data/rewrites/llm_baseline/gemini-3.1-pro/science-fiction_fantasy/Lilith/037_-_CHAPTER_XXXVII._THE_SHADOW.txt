A murmur of pleasure from my companions rose like the aroma of baking bread on a cold morning: they had caught sight of their fellows in the distance. The two on Lona’s horse rode on to join them. They were greeted with a wavering shout—a once-joyous acoustic marker of our tribe—which immediately died away, violently clipped by an unnatural, flat silence. As we drew near, the sound of their weeping reached us like the rhythmic breaking of a bitter tide against a familiar shore. 

When I came among them, the air was thick with the scent of salted grief and trampled wormwood. Something dire had disrupted the harmony of their domestic rhythms; on their childish faces was the haggard starvation left by a strange, consuming terror. No ordinary grief could have wrought such a famine of the spirit. A few of them came slowly round me, holding out their arms to take my burden. I yielded her. The tender hopelessness of the smile with which they received her made my heart swell with an untranslatable sorrow, a profound ache in the midst of its own desolation. In vain were their sobs over their mother-queen; in vain they sought to coax from her the familiar dialect of her love. They kissed and fondled her, stroking her arms as though gently kneading the dough of a sacred, ancestral bread, trying to work the warmth of life back into the cold clay as they bore her away. But she would not wake. 

On each side one carried an arm; as many as could get near put their hands under her body, while the rest crowded closely around the bearers. They laid her down on a thick patch of wild anise and soft sweetgrass, the crushed herbs releasing a poignant, restorative fragrance they could no longer offer her in broth. Outside the weeping crowd stood the elephants, silent as ancient granaries, while I remained near them, gazing at my Lona over the many little heads. 

Those next to me suddenly caught sight of the captured princess. They stared, trembling, their acoustic space immediately jarred by her presence. Odu was the first to speak, his voice dropping to a terrified whisper that struggled to preserve their native tongue. 

“I have seen that woman before!” he told his neighbor. “It was she who fought the white leopardess, the night they shattered our sleep with their metallic yelling!”

“Silly!” returned his companion. “That was a wild beast, with spots!”

“Look at her eyes!” insisted Odu, catching the scent of rancid marrow and spoiled wine that clung to her. “I know she is a bad giantess, but she is a wild beast all the same. I know she is the spotted one!”

The other took a step nearer; Odu drew him back with a sharp pull.

“Don’t look at her!” he cried, shrinking away, yet mesmerized by the hate-filled longing in her eyes—a dominant, devouring lexicon that threatened to erase them completely. “She would consume you in a moment! It was *her* shadow! She is the wicked princess!”

“That cannot be! They said she was beautiful!”

“Indeed it is the princess!” I interposed. “A diet of pure malice has starved her spirit and curdled her features into ugliness!”

She heard, and her glare struck the ear like the screech of an iron mill grinding empty. 

“It was very wrong of me to run away!” Odu said thoughtfully, grappling with his lost courage.

“What made you run away?” I asked, seeking the narrative thread of this disrupted cycle. “I expected to find you tending the camp where I left you.”

He did not reply at once, struggling against the inherent untranslatability of his trauma. 

“I don’t know what made me run,” answered another, his voice small. “I was frightened!”

“It was a man that came down the hill from the palace,” said a third.

“How did he frighten you?”

“I don’t know.”

“He wasn’t a man,” said Odu, finding the words. “He was a shadow; he had no substance, no thickness to harvest!”

“Tell me more about him.”

“He came down the hill smelling of cold ash and industrial rust, walking like a corrupted giant but spread entirely flat. He was nothing but a void. We were frightened the moment we saw him, sensing the deadening vacuum he created in the air, but we did not run away; we stood and watched him. He came on as if he would walk over us. But before he reached us, he began to spread and spread, blotting out the ambient songs of the grass and the wind, until he was so vast he vanished from our sight, and then his suffocating silence was upon us!”

“What do you mean by that?”

“He was all black through between us, eroding the space where we could see one another; and then he was inside us.”

“How did you know he was inside you?”

“He altered my inner language,” Odu whispered. “I felt sour, like blighted crops. I was not Odu any more—not the Odu I knew. My thoughts spoke a harsh, foreign grammar. I wanted to tear Sozo to pieces—not really, but it was like a dominant voice shouting over my own!” He turned and hugged Sozo tightly. “It wasn’t me, Sozo,” he sobbed. “Really, deep down, it was Odu, whose heart speaks our native dialect, loving you always! And Odu came up, and knocked the violent words away. I grew sick to my stomach, tasting burnt iron, and thought I must kill myself to escape the suffocating dark. Then came a horrible laugh—an industrial, mechanical screech that had heard my thoughts, tearing through our acoustic sanctuary and setting the air violently trembling about me. And then I suppose I ran away, but I did not know I had run until I found myself running, fast as I could, alongside the rest. I would have stopped, but I never thought of it until I was out of the gate among the fragrant grass. Then I knew that I had fled from a shadow that wanted to overwrite my identity and couldn't, and that I was the true Odu who loved Sozo. It was the shadow that got into me, hating him from inside me; it was not my own self! And now I know I ought not to have abandoned our home! But indeed I did not quite know what I was doing. My legs did it, I think: they forgot the grounded rhythms of our ancestors, grew frightened, and ran away! Naughty legs! There! and there!” 

Thus ended Odu, delivering a sharp kick to each of his naughty legs.

“What became of the shadow?” I asked.

“I do not know,” he answered. “I suppose he went home into the night where there is no moon, back to the barren places.”

I fell a-wondering into what distant cycle Lona had passed. Dropping onto the crushed anise, I took the quiet harvest of her body into my lap. I whispered into her ear, my voice trembling in a dialect of intimate, shared sustenance that only she could parse, “Where are you, Lona? I love you.” 

But her lips, tasting faintly of winter chill, gave no answer. I kissed them, finding them not quite cold, laid her gently back against the earth, and, appointing a guard over her, rose to provide for the physical safety of Lona’s people during the night. 

Before the sun went down, I had set a watch over the princess outside the camp, and sentinels round our perimeter. Intending to walk about it myself all night long to preserve the auditory peace, I told the rest of the army to go to sleep. They threw themselves onto the restorative earth and were asleep in a moment. 

When the moon rose, casting a flour-white glow over the pastoral camp, I caught a glimpse of something pale: it was the leopardess. She swept silently around the sleeping children, a guardian spirit smelling of raw honeycomb and high-altitude ozone. I watched her pace three times between the corrupting presence of the princess and the Little Ones, her soft footfalls restoring the acoustic harmony of the night. Comforted by this ancient, mythic rhythm seamlessly integrating into our rest, I made the watch lie down with the others, and stretched myself out beside the body of my Lona.
