While Baldassarre stood transfixed by Savonarola’s words, lost in the storm of emotion they stirred within him, he did not notice that another man had slipped through the doorway behind him. This newcomer paused a few paces away, watching silently. It was Piero di Cosimo, who had come not to hear the sermon, but to see the prisoner who had escaped from custody. He paid no attention to the preacher’s voice, his gaze fixed solely on the man before him.

As the crowd fell into a hush—caught in a wave of unspoken feeling—Piero stepped forward and gently touched Baldassarre’s arm. The prisoner turned, tears still tracing slow paths down his cheeks, but with a deep breath, as if releasing something long held within. His expression was weary yet resolute.

“Shall I cut your cords?” Piero whispered. “I’ve heard how you were taken.”

Baldassarre hesitated, his eyes narrowing with suspicion. Then, after a moment, he nodded once. “If you will.”

“Better go outside,” Piero said, already turning toward the door.

Again, Baldassarre studied him warily. Piero, sensing the doubt, smiled faintly, pulled out a knife, and severed the bonds. As he did so, he thought: *He might be mad—there’s something wild in his face. But if he causes trouble, they’ll bind him again. Let him have this chance, at least.*

“You’re afraid of me,” Piero murmured. “You won’t tell me anything about yourself.”

Baldassarre rubbed his arms, savoring the return of sensation after long confinement. His tone softened slightly, though caution remained. “No,” he said quietly. “I have nothing to say.”

“As you wish,” Piero replied. “But perhaps you need shelter. You don’t know how Florence treats strangers—especially those with torn clothes and empty stomachs. There’s an hospital for travelers just beyond the gate. And if you like, I can show you where it is. Your French soldier has been sent away.”

Baldassarre gave a single nod and followed without a word. Together, they left the church.

“Would you mind sitting for a portrait?” Piero asked as they walked along the Via dell’Oriuolo, heading toward Santa Croce. “I’m a painter. I’d pay you well.”

A flicker of distrust returned to Baldassarre’s eyes. “No,” he said firmly.

“Aha,” Piero muttered, short and sharp. “Then go straight ahead—you’ll find the Porta Santa Croce. Outside, there’s the hospital. So you won’t accept any help from me?”

“I thank you for what you’ve done,” Baldassarre answered. “I need nothing more.”

“Very well,” Piero said, shrugging. They parted ways.

*What a curious creature,* Piero thought. *Like an old tiger—ugly, deeply lined, as if life had plowed its fields across his heart. A striking contrast to my smooth Messer Greco—my Bacco trionfante, who married Antigone against all reason and history. Ah, yes—the scholar’s blood must have curdled when that man gripped him.*

When Piero returned to the Piazza del Duomo, the crowd was streaming out from every entrance. The urgency with which people dispersed showed how deeply the sermon had gripped them—how essential it had felt to pause their daily lives for these words.

Leaning against the corner of the Baptistery, Piero watched the throng pass by, delighting in the diversity of faces and garments—faces much like those Masaccio had painted half a century earlier, or those Ghirlandajo still captured today. This morning was exceptional. Savonarola’s audience had drawn from every stratum of Florentine society.

There were noble men—some newly prominent in opposing the Medici and championing popular rule—men now fervent followers of the friar, calling San Marco the new seat of divine leadership. Among them were figures like Francesco Valori, bold and proud, thrilled by the idea that God Himself sanctioned their rebellion; others, like Soderini, more cautious, blending piety with political wisdom. Then there were men of lineage like Piero Capponi—no theologians, but patriots who valued liberty above doctrine, ready to fight rather than debate.

Beside them stood scholars and lawyers whose studies had not dulled their passion—men like Luca Corsini, whose scholarly hands would one day hurl stones in defense of faith and freedom. And among the dignitaries in black robes or fur-lined cloaks, there were quieter souls: scholars bearing noble names like Strozzi and Acciajoli, already considering the monk’s habit; artists inspired by Savonarola’s message, such as the young painter who had recently adorned the friar’s cell with a fresco of the Christ Child—unaware that one day he too would take vows and become Fra Bartolommeo.

There was Girolamo Benevieni, the mystic poet, rushing perhaps to bring news of Savonarola’s coming to his friend Pico della Mirandola—who would never live to see dawn again. Women of high birth moved among them, dressed with austere elegance, their grace marked not by finery but by restraint.

Yet most numerous were the *popolani*—the middle class, members of both Major and Minor Arts, burdened by war taxes and fearful for their livelihoods. And then came the poorer artisans and tradesmen—men whose faith in Savonarola ranged from simple trust in a champion of the poor to intense devotion to biblical mysteries, finding meaning in scripture during long hours of labor. For them, the friar’s words lit a flame in the dimness of their workdays—a light they believed divine.

But not all present were true disciples. Some were Mediceans pretending reverence out of fear or calculation. Others were republicans who saw Savonarola not as a saint, but as a powerful tool—ambitious, half-crazed, yet effective. Still others were enemies: aristocrats determined to reclaim power, or reckless youths who hated him for spoiling their pleasures.

The sermons had become events—political spectacles as much as spiritual ones. Men like Niccolò Macchiavelli came to observe, to report. Men like Dolfo Spini came to hate, to gather evidence against a figure they deemed a nuisance.

Savonarola’s influence was immense—but his audience was a mosaic of motives. His preaching reached far beyond theology. It spoke to the noblest ideals, yes—but also to greed, curiosity, and superstition. His visions, allegories, and claims of divine knowledge captivated minds of every kind. Even as he preached with fire and conviction, his need for dominance shaped his message. Yet within his soul, that ambition was tempered by genuine piety, a deep sense of justice, and a fierce love for humanity.

For the masses, however, it was not the depth of his faith that mattered—it was the certainty of his revelations, the drama of his warnings, the way his words echoed like political pronouncements. Once he held them, he could not let go. To keep them, he had to lower his standards—to meet their fears, their desires, their ignorance.

And so, inevitably, the man changed. No leader retains power over such a mixed multitude without being altered by it. His vision narrowed. His truth bent to fit their needs.

Yet in Savonarola, we see not a villain, nor a saint—but a complex human being. His life is a tragedy of conflicting forces: zeal and pride, holiness and hunger for control. Up to this point, his desire for power may have burned hidden beneath the greater flame of his mission.

It was once common, when preparing an ox for sacrifice to Jupiter, to paint over its dark spots, making it seem pure white. Let us discard that false whitewash. Yes, the victim is flawed. But does that make his sacrifice meaningless? No. For even with blemishes, his mighty heart rests upon the altar of mankind’s highest hopes—and that, alone, gives his life weight.
