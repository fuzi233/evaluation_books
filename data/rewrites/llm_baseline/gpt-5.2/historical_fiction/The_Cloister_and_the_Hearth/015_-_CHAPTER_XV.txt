Where is the woman that cannot put on another face? Where is she who will not, for one fierce instant, borrow craft from fear, and use it—neatly, swiftly—to save the man she loves? On such nights Nature herself turns actress, and even the simplest girl learns how to blind Solomon.

The men had no sooner clumped from the chamber than Margaret slid out of the bed as silently as a cat in a dairy. She was still dressed beneath the linen—skirt and petticoat, stockings, and over all the nightdress she had flung on in haste—yet she moved with the practised thrift of a housewife who knows every board that creaks and every latch that sings.

The long chest, that had served her for a bed, stood by the wall. With both hands she lifted the lid, and, with it, the bedclothes, and set the whole—lid and coverlet together—upright against the plaster, as one props a kneading-board. Then she glided to the door and pressed her ear to the wood.

Footsteps, heavy with beer and authority, dwindled through her father’s room and down the stair; their iron-shod soles beat time on the boards, then sank into the duller hush below. A last mutter. A door. The house settled, as houses do, into its own breathing.

Now that chest had a secret, the sort of household ingenuity that grows in poor rooms the way leaven grows in a warm bowl. A strip of boarding had once been broken; Gerard had been asked to mend it, and, being short of timber, he had sawed away a space just wide enough to receive Margaret’s so-called bed. With the planks so stolen back from necessity, he had patched the floor till no stranger’s eye would note the theft. The chest, in truth, rested on the rafters a foot beneath the boards; it looked scarce a foot deep, but it held near two. In such small carpentry lay the difference between a hearth kept and a life taken.

All was quiet.

Margaret sank to her knees on the bare boards and gave thanks—quickly, breathlessly—because gratitude, too, can be fear’s habit. Then she rose and bent over the chest, her hair loosening about her cheeks, and whispered as one calls a child from a pantry:

“Gerard.”

No answer.

She whispered again, a shade louder, careful still—as careful as when one lifts a lid from simmering pottage not to let the steam betray its savour to a passer-by.

“Gerard, all is safe, thank Heaven! You may rise; but oh—be cautious!”

No answer.

A coldness went along her spine. She reached down and laid her hand upon his shoulder.

“Gerard!”

Nothing—no shifting of cloth, no rasp of breath, no impatient little sound a man makes when cramped.

“Oh, what is this?” she breathed, and her fingers flew, not tender now but blind, over his face and breast as if searching in meal for a lost coin. She took him by the shoulders; she shook him; she lifted; but he slipped from her trembling grasp and fell back—not like a man who resists sleep, but like a weight that has forgotten will.

Dread—thick as smoke—filled her mouth. The lid had been down. She had lain upon it. The men had stood there, questioning, listening, lingering. The air inside that hidden depth—how little there is, how soon it turns sour, as when a crock is sealed too tight and the milk within goes wrong.

With the strength of frenzy she tore him out of the chest. Splinters bit her palms; she did not feel them. She bore him to the window as if he were a sack of grain, precious and ruinously heavy. She flung the casement wide.

Night rushed in—cool, clean, smelling of wet earth and distant peat-smoke; it carried, too, the village’s sleeping music: a far dog’s single bark, the faint creak of a sign in the wind, the soft settling of thatched roofs. The air poured over Gerard’s face, but he did not drink it.

She laid him in the moonlight, on the sill and against the wall where the draught could find him. His skin was the colour of ashes; his mouth slack; his whole body limp, as dough before the first working.

She put her hand to his breast.

Horror: the heart beneath her fingers was as still as stone.

Horror of horrors: she had smothered him with her own body—the very warmth that had seemed a shelter had turned to a weight, and the hiding place, meant to keep him from hands and ropes, had become a coffin of boards.

The mind will not swallow such a thing at once; it sticks in the throat like dry bread. Gerard had slid alive into that chest scarce five minutes before. How could death have travelled so quickly through so little space?

She called him by all the names love invents in the instant it dares to speak—names she had never given him aloud, though her tongue had shaped them in secret as she kneaded and stirred and swept. She kissed him—forehead, cheek, lips—pressing her mouth to his as if she might blow life back into him the way one fans a coal to wake it. She coaxed and implored him, urgent and low, as if he were only sulking.

No answer.

Then, trembling from head to foot, she began to say over that grey face little foolish things, broken and dreadful, as a child babbles over a spilled pot.

“Oh, Gerard! I am very sorry you are dead. I am very sorry I have killed you. Forgive me for not letting the men take you; it would have been better than this. Oh, Gerard! I am very, very sorry for what I have done.”

And suddenly the dam in her mind burst; words rushed out wild, not chosen, not measured—like water let loose from a millpond.

“No! no! such things can’t be, or there is no God. It is monstrous. How can my Gerard be dead? How can I have killed my Gerard? I love him. Oh, God! you know how I love him. He does not—I never told him. If he knew my heart, he would speak to me; he would not be so deaf to his poor Margaret. It is all a trick to make me cry out and betray him; but no! I love him too well for that. I’ll choke first!”

She seized her own throat, crushing down the scream that fought to tear free—because she heard, even through madness, the house’s listening: the timbers that carried sound, the stairwell that drank it and returned it, the night itself that would carry her voice like a hunted thing across roofs and yards.

“If he would but say one word. Oh, Gerard! don’t die without a word. Have mercy on me and scold me, but speak to me: if you are angry with me, scold me! curse me! I deserve it—the idiot that killed the man she loved better than herself. Ah, I am a murderess—the worst in all the world. Help! help! I have murdered him. Ah! ah! ah! ah! ah!”

She tore at her hair, and the screams that she had tried to swallow tore out at last—shriek after shriek, so wild, so piercing, they rang like a death-bell in the ears of Dierich Brower and his men below. All started to their feet and looked at one another.
