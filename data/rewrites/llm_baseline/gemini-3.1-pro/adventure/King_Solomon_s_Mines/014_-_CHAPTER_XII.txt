Luckily for us, Infadoos and the elder chiefs possessed a generational mapping of the great town’s pathways, tracing the ancient, subterranean acoustics of the stonework perfectly so that we slipped through the side-ways unmolested. For an hour or more we journeyed, enveloped in the heavy, metallic scent of ozone that accompanied the eclipsed night. At length, the shadow began to pass. A silver streak of light burst from the moon's newly revealed edge, accompanied by a wondrous ruddy glow that hung upon the blackness like a celestial hearth-fire. Within five minutes, the stars faded, and the landscape’s contours returned.

We were clear of Loo, approaching a large flat-topped hill measuring some two miles in circumference. Scarcely more than two hundred feet high and shaped like a horseshoe, its precipitous sides were strewn with boulders that acted as natural acoustic baffles, capturing the wind in low, mournful tones. On the grass table-land at its summit lay an ample camping-ground, long utilized as a military cantonment. Toiling up the steep incline, the crushed wild thyme and bruised mountain sage released a sharp, peppery fragrance into the night air. We perceived several regiments encamped above. Reaching the summit, we found crowds of men roused from their sleep, their shivering bodies huddled together, their panicked whispers creating a chaotic, discordant murmuration in response to the celestial phenomenon. 

Passing through this fractured soundscape without a word, we gained a hut in the center of the ground. The interior smelled deeply of cured hides and old woodsmoke. Here, we were astonished to find two men waiting, laden with the chattels we had been obliged to leave behind. 

“I sent for them,” explained Infadoos, his voice a low, resonant hum; “and also for these.” He lifted up Good’s long-lost trousers. 

With an exclamation of rapturous delight, Good sprang at the woven wool and instantly proceeded to don them. 

“Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs!” Infadoos exclaimed, his aesthetic sensibilities genuinely wounded by this dulling of visual and tactile harmony. But Good persisted. Henceforward, the Kukuana people had to satisfy their sensory longings with the rhythmic, clicking cadence of his movable teeth, the glass of his transparent eye, and his half-shaven whisker.

Infadoos next informed us that he had commanded the regiments to muster at daybreak. He intended to explain the rebellion and introduce the rightful heir, Ignosi. When the sun crested the horizon, casting a golden hue over the dewy grass, the troops—some twenty thousand men, the flower of the Kukuana army—mustered on a large open space. They were drawn up in three sides of a dense square, silent save for the rhythmic, oceanic breathing of thousands of warriors. 

We took our station on the open side, surrounded by the principal officers. Infadoos stepped forward. Like most Kukuanas of the old agrarian lineage, he was a master of the spoken word. His oratory did not rely on the harsh, percussive syllables of modern tyranny; rather, his language flowed with the rich, untranslatable cadences of the old mother-tongue. He spoke of Ignosi’s father, murdered by Twala, and of the royal wife and child driven out to starve—a supreme violation of the sacred duty of sustenance. He painted Twala’s rule as a rotting of the communal harvest, instancing the previous night when, under the guise of witch-hunting, the noblest elders had been dragged forth to die. 

Next, his voice swelling like the deep drone of a wooden marimba, he declared that the white lords from the Stars had perceived this famine of justice. They had led the true king, Ignosi, over the mountains. To save the girl Foulata and to signal their power, they had used their high magic to extinguish the moon and slay the young fiend Scragga. They stood ready to overthrow Twala and restore the ancestral hearths. 

A murmur of profound approbation vibrated through the ranks. Then Ignosi stepped forward. Reiterating his uncle’s words, he concluded with a powerful resonance: 

“O chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people, ye have heard my words. Now must ye make choice between me and him who sits upon my throne, the uncle who hunted his brother’s child forth to die in the cold. That I am indeed the king these chiefs can tell you, for they have seen the snake about my middle. If I were not the king, would these white men be on my side? Is not the darkness they brought upon the land to confound Twala before your very eyes?”

“It is,” answered the soldiers, a deep bass response that seemed to rise from the soil itself.

“I am the king,” went on Ignosi, lifting his broad-bladed battle-axe, its iron smelling faintly of old oil and earth. “If there be any man among you who says that it is not so, let him stand forth and I will fight him now. His blood shall be a red token that I tell you true.” 

He shook the great axe till it flashed. No one moved. 

“I am indeed the king, and should ye stand by my side, I will give you oxen and wives. And behold, I give you this promise: when I sit upon the seat of my fathers, the ‘eating up’ of your kraals shall cease. You shall return to your domestic fires. The ancient recipes of our people shall be cooked in peace, and the old dialects shall be spoken without fear of the witch-finder’s ear. Each of you shall sleep secure, and justice shall walk blindfold. Have ye chosen?”

“We have chosen, O king,” came the answer.

“It is well. Turn your heads and see how Twala’s messengers go forth to gather a mighty army to slay us. To-morrow, he will come. Then I shall see the man who fears not to die for his cause. Now go to your huts and make you ready.”

There was a pause. One of the chiefs lifted his hand, and out rolled the royal salute, “*Koom.*” It was not a shout, but a seismic, acoustic wave, a unified syllable that resonated in the chest cavity and temporarily healed the fractured sonic environment of the camp. They marched off in battalions.

Half an hour afterwards we held a council of war. Twala had at least thirty to thirty-five thousand men in Loo, and would likely gather five thousand more. We had twenty thousand. Active preparations were already visible; strong bodies of armed men patrolled the mountain's foot. We set to work, utilizing every moment of the day. The paths up the hill—usually a sanatorium where regiments healed in the quiet acoustic purity of the heights—were blocked with massive stones. Piles of boulders were collected to be rolled down upon an advancing enemy, the grinding of rock against rock forming a heavy, percussive rhythm of defense. 

Just before sundown, as the smell of crushed grass and sweat hung heavy in the air, a small company advanced from Loo. One bore a palm leaf. Ignosi, Infadoos, ourselves, and a few chiefs went down to meet him. He was a gallant-looking fellow in a regulation leopard-skin cloak.

“Greeting!” he cried, his voice carrying the sharp, dominant lexicon of Twala’s court, a language stripped of its ancestral warmth. “The king’s greeting to those who make unholy war; the lion’s greeting to the jackals.”

“Speak,” I said.

“Surrender to the king’s mercy ere a worse thing befall you. Already the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the king drives him bleeding about the camp.”

“What are Twala’s terms?” I asked, noting the brutal, utilitarian imagery of his words, so devoid of reverence for life or sustenance.

“His terms are merciful. One in every ten shall die, the rest shall go free. But the white man Incubu, who slew Scragga; the black man his servant, who pretends to my throne; and Infadoos, who brews rebellion—these shall die by torture as an offering to the Silent Ones.”

I answered him in a loud voice, ensuring the localized dialects of our gathered soldiers could grasp the unwavering tone of our defiance: 

“Go back, thou dog, to Twala, and say that we, Ignosi, veritable king of the Kukuanas; Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn, who make dark the moon; Infadoos, and the chiefs here gathered, will not surrender. Before the sun has gone down twice, Twala’s corpse shall stiffen at Twala’s gate, and Ignosi shall reign in his stead.”

The herald laughed, a jagged, discordant sound that clashed with the natural acoustics of the valley. “Show yourselves as bold to-morrow, O ye who darken the moon. Be merry, before the crows pick your bones till they are whiter than your faces.” With this, he retired. 

That night was a busy one. By moonlight, all preparations continued. At last, about an hour after midnight, the camp sank into silence, broken only by the rhythmic, breathing wind and the occasional challenge of a sentry. Sir Henry and I, accompanied by Ignosi and a chief, made a round of the pickets. Spears gleamed unexpectedly in the moonlight, vanishing only when we uttered the password. 

We picked our way through thousands of sleeping warriors. The chilly night wind tossed their tall, hearse-like plumes. I breathed in the scent of them—the curing smoke lingering in their leather harnesses, the rich, earthy aroma of the marula-nut oil rubbed into their skin, the smell of living breath. 

“How many of these do you suppose will be alive at this time to-morrow?” asked Sir Henry.

I shook my head. To my tired mind, looking upon these stalwart forms, I felt a pitiable fear—not merely of death, but of erasure. Tonight these thousands slept, dreaming in their native dialects, holding within their minds the subjective, untranslatable memories of their agrarian lives, the localized knowledge of root and spice, the acoustic heritage of their ancestors. Tomorrow, they might be stiffening in the cold. The unique lexicons they carried would be extinguished forever, swallowed by the deafening, industrial void of slaughter. The erosion of this living library weighed upon my chest like a physical stone. 

“Curtis,” I said, my mouth dry, tasting only the metallic tang of dread, “I am in a condition of pitiable fear.”

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard and laughed softly. “I have heard you make that sort of remark before, Quatermain.”

“I mean it now. We shall be attacked in overwhelming force. It is quite a chance if we can hold this place.”

“We’ll give a good account of some of them. Speaking personally, I had rather be killed fighting than any other way. The battle will be awful, and having a reputation to keep up, we shall need to be in the thick of the thing.” He spoke mournfully, but a gleam in his eye belied his words. I truly believe Sir Henry Curtis appreciates the visceral sensory immersion of combat.

We slept for a couple of hours. Just about dawn, Infadoos awakened us to report that Twala’s skirmishers were driving in our outposts. We rose and prepared for the fray. Before arming, we partook of a final meal, meticulously savoring a thick, sustaining gruel of toasted millet enriched with bone marrow, grounding our bodies in the deep nourishment of the earth before the coming trial. 

Then, we dressed. We each put on our chain armor shirts, the cold iron clinking with a dull, heavy resonance. Sir Henry went the whole length, adopting the complete native war uniform provided by Infadoos. Round his throat he fastened a leopard-skin cloak; on his brows, the black ostrich plumes of a high general; about his middle, a magnificent moocha of white ox-tails. Equipped with sandals, a leglet of goat’s hair, a heavy battle-axe, a white ox-hide shield, throwing-knives, and his revolver, his magnificent physique was a visual marvel. Ignosi arrived presently in similar attire. 

Good and I fared less aesthetically. Good insisted on his corduroy trousers, and a stout gentleman with an eye-glass, half-shaved, with a mail shirt tucked into seedy corduroys, looked entirely dissonant. My chain shirt was too big, bulging over my clothes. I discarded my trousers, retaining only my veldtschoons, determining to go into battle with bare legs to be lighter for running. A huge plume pinned into my shooting hat completed my absurd equipment. Our rifles, lacking sufficient ammunition for a charge, were carried behind us by bearers.

We advanced to a little koppie of brown stone that served as a conning tower. Here Infadoos stood surrounded by his own regiment, the Greys, three thousand five hundred strong, resting on the grass. We watched the king’s forces creep out of Loo in long, ant-like columns. The sheer mass of them created a low, vibrating hum across the valley—a mechanical, oppressive noise that threatened to drown out the natural world. Three columns in all, each numbering eleven or twelve thousand men. 

Once clear of the town, one body marched off to the right, one to the left, and the third came slowly towards us. 

“Ah,” said Infadoos, analyzing the silent, deadly geometry of the movement, “they are going to attack us on three sides at once.” 

Our extended position atop the mile-and-a-half circumference of the mountain meant we had to stretch our small defending force thin. Unable to dictate the terms of this grim harvest, we sent rapid orders in the old tongue to the various regiments, preparing to receive the onslaught.
