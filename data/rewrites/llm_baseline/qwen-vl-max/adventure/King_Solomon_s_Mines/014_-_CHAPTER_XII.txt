By fortune, Infadoos and the chiefs knew every path through the great town of Loo like the back of their hands. We slipped past its outer walls unseen, moving cautiously through shadowed alleys and hidden lanes. Though the night was thick with darkness, we pressed forward steadily, guided only by memory and instinct. For over an hour we traveled in silence, until at last the moon began to reappear—its edge, once swallowed by the eclipse, now slowly emerging from the void. Then, suddenly, a streak of silver light burst forth from it, followed by a deep, ruddy glow that hung like a glowing lantern against the black sky. It was a sight both strange and wondrous, stirring the soul with awe.

Five minutes later, the stars began to fade, and enough light returned for us to see our surroundings clearly. We realized then that we had escaped the city and were approaching a broad, flat-topped hill—some two miles around, rising no more than two hundred feet above the plain. Its shape was like a horseshoe, with steep, rocky slopes scattered with boulders. Atop this plateau lay a vast expanse of grassland, long used as a military encampment. Normally garrisoned by three thousand men, it now held several regiments, their tents and fires dotting the land like a constellation of war.

As we climbed the slope under the returning moonlight, we saw hundreds of soldiers stirred from sleep, huddled together in fear, trembling at the unnatural event they had witnessed—the darkening of the moon. We passed them without word or glance, making our way to a hut near the center of the camp. There, to our astonishment, stood two men waiting—our belongings piled beside them, including Good’s long-lost trousers.

“I sent for them,” Infadoos said simply. “And also for these.” He lifted up the trousers with a smile.

Good let out a cry of joy and rushed forward, pulling them on immediately. “Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs!” Infadoos lamented, half-joking.

But Good refused to be swayed. Once again, the Kukuana people caught a glimpse of those famed limbs—but only once. From then on, they would have to content themselves with the sight of his single whisker, his clear eye, and his famously mobile teeth. Good, modest to the core, never allowed such exposure again.

Still admiring the trousers, Infadoos told us he had ordered the regiments to assemble at dawn. He wished to explain the rebellion, reveal the true heir to the throne—Ignosi—and rally the troops to his cause. As the sun rose, some twenty thousand men—nearly the entire strength of the Kukuana army—gathered in a wide open field. They formed a dense square, three sides filled with armed warriors, the fourth left open for the leaders.

We took our place there, soon surrounded by chiefs and officers. Silence fell, and Infadoos stepped forward. With eloquence and fire, he spoke of Ignosi’s father—how Twala, the tyrant king, had murdered him, driven his wife and child into exile to die. He described the suffering under Twala’s rule, recalling the horrors of the previous night when innocent nobles were dragged from their homes and slain under false accusations.

Then he told how the white lords from the Stars—us—had seen their plight. We had come not by chance, but to restore justice. We had rescued Ignosi, led him across the mountains, and even brought down the moon itself to prove our power. We had destroyed Scragga, Twala’s monstrous son, and saved Foulata. Now, we stood ready to help overthrow tyranny and place the rightful king upon the throne.

His words sparked murmurs of approval. Then Ignosi stepped forward.

He repeated Infadoos’s tale, then added his own call to arms:
“O chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people—you have heard my words. Now choose between me and the man who sits on my throne: the uncle who killed his brother, who chased his nephew into the cold and night. I am the true king. These chiefs know it—they have seen the snake about my middle. If I were not the king, would these white men stand with me, wielding their magic? Look! The darkness they summoned still lingers—even in full moonlight! Is it not proof?”

“It is!” came the reply from the ranks.

“I am the king,” Ignosi declared, raising his battle-axe high. “If any man here denies it, let him step forward. I will fight him now, and his blood shall mark my truth.”

No one moved.

“Then hear this,” he continued. “If you stand with me in battle, and I win, you shall share victory and honor. I will give you oxen, wives, and rank beyond your dreams. If you fall, I will fall with you. And I swear this: when I sit upon my father’s seat, bloodshed shall end. No more shall justice bring death without cause. No more shall witch-finders hunt you down. No man shall die unless he breaks the law. Your kraals shall be safe. You shall sleep in peace. Justice shall walk blindfolded throughout the land. Have you chosen?”

“We have chosen, O king!” thundered the response.

“Then turn and see,” Ignosi said. “Twala’s messengers are already spreading out—east, west, north, south—to gather his armies. Tomorrow—or the next day—he will come with all who follow him. Then I shall see who is truly mine—those who do not fear death for their cause. Such men shall not be forgotten when the spoils are divided. I have spoken. Go now, prepare for war.”

A pause. Then one chief raised his hand. The crowd roared the royal salute: _“Koom!”_

It was the sign of acceptance. The soldiers marched away in disciplined columns.

Half an hour later, we convened a council of war. All regimental commanders were present. It was clear: we would be attacked soon. From our vantage point, we could see troops gathering in Loo, runners speeding outward to summon reinforcements.

We had twenty thousand men—seven of the finest regiments. Twala, we estimated, had thirty to thirty-five thousand in Loo, and could add five thousand more by midday. Desertions might occur, but we couldn’t rely on them. Already, patrols circled the base of the hill. Signs of attack were everywhere.

Infadoos believed no assault would come that day—only preparation. But tomorrow, they said, the storm would break.

So we set to work. Every man was mobilized. Paths up the hill were blocked with stones. Boulder traps were placed along likely routes. Regiments were assigned positions. We fortified what we could, using every ounce of ingenuity.

Just before sunset, a small group approached from Loo—one bearing a palm leaf, a symbol of truce. Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and we descended to meet him.

The herald wore the leopard-skin cloak of a nobleman. “Greeting!” he called. “The king’s greeting to those who wage unholy war against the king. The lion’s greeting to the jackals who snarl at his heels!”

“Speak,” I said.

“The king commands: surrender to his mercy before worse befalls. Already, the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the king drives him bleeding through the camp.”

“What terms does Twala offer?” I asked.

“Merciful ones—worthy of a great king. This is Twala, the one-eyed, mighty, husband of a thousand wives, keeper of Solomon’s Road, beloved of the Silent Ones, Calf of the Black Cow, Elephant whose tread shakes the earth, Terror of the evil-doer, Ostrich whose feet devour the desert—this is Twala’s word: ‘I will spare most. One in ten shall die. The rest go free. But the white man Incubu, who slew my son Scragga; the black man who claims my throne; and Infadoos, my brother, who stirs rebellion—these shall die by torture, offered to the Silent Ones.’ Such is Twala’s mercy.”

After brief counsel, I replied aloud, so all could hear:

“Go back, dog, to Twala. Tell him this: We, Ignosi, true king of the Kukuanas; Incubu, Bougwan, Macumazahn, the wise ones from the Stars who darken the moon; Infadoos of the royal line; and all gathered here—answer: We will not surrender. Before the sun sets twice, Twala’s corpse shall lie stiff at his own gate, and Ignosi, whose father he slew, shall reign in his place. Now go—before we send you back in pieces. Beware how you raise a hand against us.”

The herald laughed. “You frighten no men with such bold words,” he shouted. “Show your courage tomorrow, O you who darken the moon. Be brave, fight, and be merry—before the crows pick your bones whiter than your faces. Farewell. Perhaps we shall meet in battle. Do not flee to the Stars. Wait for me, I pray, white men.”

With that, he turned and vanished. The sun sank below the horizon.

That night, though weary, we worked by moonlight—finishing preparations, sending messengers, holding councils. By midnight, all was done. The camp fell silent, save for the occasional challenge of a sentry.

Sir Henry and I, with Ignosi and a chief, walked the picket lines. As we moved, spears flashed in the moonlight from hidden posts—then disappeared when we gave the password. No soldier slept at his post.

Returning, we passed thousands of sleeping warriors. In the pale light, their forms looked ghostly—arms outstretched, limbs twisted. Their plumes fluttered in the chill wind, their spears gleaming like bones. It was a haunting scene.

“How many of these,” Sir Henry asked, “do you think will live to see this time tomorrow?”

I shook my head. To my tired eyes, Death seemed already among them. I saw which men would fall, felt the weight of fate pressing down. A great sorrow washed over me—the futility of life, the suddenness of death.

Tonight, thousands slept soundly. Tomorrow, many would be dead. Wives would become widows, children fatherless. Their names would vanish. Only the moon would rise, the wind blow, the earth rest—unchanged, eternal.

Yet man does not truly die. His breath stirs the pines. His words echo. His thoughts live on in ours. His passions drive us. His joys and sorrows are ours. Even the end he feared—we too must face.

The universe is full of ghosts—not spirits in sheets, but the living essence of every soul, once born, forever changed, never gone.

Such thoughts filled my mind—as I grow older, I regret to say, I find myself thinking more often.

“Curtis,” I said, “I’m in a state of pitiable fear.”

Sir Henry stroked his beard and laughed. “I’ve heard you say that before, Quatermain.”

“But I mean it now. I doubt if any of us will survive tomorrow night. We’ll be outnumbered. Holding this place is a gamble.”

“We’ll make a good account of ourselves,” he said. “Look, Quatermain—this is ugly business. We shouldn’t be involved. But we are. So we must do our best. Personally, I’d rather die fighting than any other way. And now that we’re unlikely to find my brother, it makes it easier. Fortune favors the brave. We may win. Either way, the battle will be terrible. And since I have a reputation to keep, I’ll be right in the thick of it.”

He said it mournfully, yet his eyes sparkled with something fiercer. I suspect Sir Henry Curtis actually enjoys battle.

We slept a few hours. At dawn, Infadoos woke us—great activity in Loo. Skirmishers were driving in our outposts.

We dressed for war. Each donned chain armor—grateful for it. Sir Henry went further. “When in Kukuanaland, do as the Kukuanas do,” he said, slipping on the steel shirt like a glove.

Then came the full war gear: leopard-skin cloak, black ostrich-feather plume, white ox-tail moocha, sandals, goat-hair leglet, rhino-horn axe, iron shield, throwing-knives—all native accoutrements. Yet he added his revolver.

The look was fierce, savage—but I’ve rarely seen a finer sight. His physique, enhanced by the costume, was magnificent. When Ignosi arrived, similarly clad, I thought: never have I seen two more splendid men.

Good and I fared less well. Good insisted on keeping his trousers—so a stout gentleman with an eyeglass, half-shaved face, and mail shirt tucked into corduroys looked more absurd than fearsome.

I, too, struggled. My chain shirt was too large, so I wore it over everything—making me look bulky. I discarded trousers, wearing only veldtschoons. I decided bare legs would make me lighter, should we need to retreat fast.

My gear: mail coat, spear (which I didn’t know how to use), shield, two tollas, revolver, and a huge plume pinned to my hat for a “bloodthirsty finish.” Plus rifles—carried by bearers, since ammunition was scarce and useless in close combat.

At last equipped, we ate hastily and headed to the battlefield.

On the plateau stood a small brown koppie—used as headquarters and lookout. There, Infadoos waited, surrounded by his regiment—the Greys, the finest in the army. Three thousand five hundred strong, they were held in reserve, lying in companies, watching the enemy emerge from Loo.

Three long columns crawled out—each, we judged, eleven or twelve thousand strong. Once clear, they formed ranks. One marched right, one left, the third advanced slowly toward us.

“Ah,” Infadoos said, “they attack on three sides at once.”

That was serious. Our position, a mile and a half wide, was stretched thin. We couldn’t concentrate forces fully—but we had no choice. Orders were sent: prepare for separate assaults.

The battle loomed.
