At dawn, we set forth, pressing through the forest with urgent haste. I rode Lona’s horse, her lifeless form cradled before me in her own cloak. I would carry her to her father—where she would rest among the honored dead, or if he refused her, I would leave her body in the desert until time reduced it to dust. Yet I believed he would welcome her. After all, she had long since passed beyond breath and light.

And Lilith—I bore her too, though not by choice. I could not force her repentance. I lacked even the right to end her life, much less release her into a world that might tremble at her presence. To be her keeper forever felt like a curse. Yet I had no other path.

On the road, I offered her food again and again. She only answered with eyes aflame—hungering, hateful, never still. Her gaze darted endlessly across the void, unblinking until we crossed the scorching river. Then, at last, they closed. Not again until we reached the House of Bitterness.

One night, as we camped beneath the stars, I saw a small girl approach her. I rushed forward, fearing harm—but too late. The child had placed something to Lilith’s lips and screamed in agony. “Please, king,” she whimpered, clutching her tiny finger, “suck it! The giantess made a hole!”

I drew the wound close to my mouth and soothed it. “There!” she cheered, already reaching for another fruit. But this time, Lilith snatched her hand back. The fruit fell. The girl was named Luva.

The next day, we crossed the hot stream. On their native ground, the Little Ones danced with joy. But their nests lay far ahead, so we stopped at the ivy-hall, where clusters of grapes hung heavy and sweet. As soon as they saw them, the children surged forward, devouring the fruit greedily. Within minutes, they slumbered—some on the mossy floor, others curled beneath the trees.

I had hoped to witness the dance once more. Expecting them to sleep through it, I left a wide circle in the center. I laid down beside Lona, but sleep did not come. Night fell, and suddenly—the company arrived.

I wondered: would they dance eternally? Would I one day be drawn into their endless motion, bound by my own stubbornness? Then the children stirred. Their eyes snapped open. They leapt up, and each seized a dancer. Together, they bounded into the rhythm, skipping and laughing. The spectres seemed to recognize them, to welcome them. Perhaps they remembered childhood themselves—once lost, now returning. Or perhaps the innocence of the Little Ones brought comfort to souls stripped of present and future, clinging only to memory.

They played harmless tricks, never cruel. When they disrupted the dance, the ghosts smiled—not with laughter, but with relief. They had nothing else to smile with.

Just before dawn, I sat up—startled. In the doorway stood the skeleton-princess, eyes wide and glowing, the black mark upon her side unmistakable. She glided forward as if to join the dance. I rose in alarm. A cry of horror erupted from the children—and the lights vanished. Only the low moon remained, casting pale light over the scene. I saw them huddled together, trembling. The ghosts were gone. The princess had disappeared.

I dashed to where she had lain. She rested there, eyes shut, as if untouched. I returned to the hall. The children were already settling into sleep.

Next morning, as we moved on, two skeletons appeared in a thicket nearby. The Little Ones broke ranks and ran toward them. I followed, now walking without pain or aid. I recognized them—the pair I had seen before. The children clung to their arms, stroked their bony fingers. The creatures welcomed their touch. They walked together, united by shared loss. Deprived of everything, they had found something new: companionship.

They gathered herbs, likely to rub upon their bones—how else could nourishment reach such hollow frames? The children examined the plants closely, then collected the same kinds, filling the skeletons’ outstretched hands. They bid farewell, promising to return. “We didn’t know nice people lived here,” one whispered.

When we reached the nest-village, I stayed a night to see them settled. Lona still looked untouched by time. There was no need to rush.

Lilith had eaten nothing. Her eyes remained closed. Fearing she might die before our journey ended, I went to her in the dark. I placed my bare arm against her lips. She bit down—fiercely. I screamed. How I escaped, I do not know. I woke miles away, lying beyond her reach. Dawn was breaking. I began our departure at once.

I chose twelve of the sweetest, merriest Little Ones, mounted six on elephants, and took two wise Clumsies—those the children called "wise"—to bear the princess. I still rode Lona’s horse, carrying her body wrapped in her cloak. I took the direct route across the left branch of the dead river, aiming for the House of Bitterness. I hoped to learn how best to cross the wider, rougher stretch and avoid the basin of monsters—a place I feared for the elephants, and worse, for the children.

That third night, in the desert between the river branches, was terrible. We halted the elephants in a sheltered spot and let the princess fall between them, to lie on the sand until morning. She seemed dead—but I knew better. I lay some distance from her, Lona’s body beside me, guarding both the dead and the dangerous.

The moon hung low in the west, pale and thoughtful, casting shadows across the dunes. Suddenly, it was eclipsed—not hidden, but veiled. A thin, translucent film covered its face, distorting its beauty. Then I saw the edge—jagged, wing-like, torn and hooked.

A cold wind blew, stinging with heat. Lilith was upon me.

Her hands were bound, but with her teeth, she tore the cloak Lona had made for me from my shoulder and sank her fangs into my flesh. I lay paralyzed. Life flowed from me into her. Then I remembered—and struck her hand.

She shrieked, gurgling, and shivered. I flung her off and rose. She knelt, rocking back and forth. Another blast of icy fire swept over us. The moon reappeared, clear and bright. I saw her face—gaunt, bloodied, ghastly.

“Down, devil!” I shouted.

“Where are you taking me?” she whispered, voice like an echo from a tomb.

“To your first husband,” I said.

“He will kill me!” she moaned.

“At least he’ll take you off my hands.”

“Give me my daughter!” she screamed, grinding her teeth.

“Never. Your doom is at hand.”

“Loose my hands—for pity’s sake! I’m in torment. The cords are deep in my flesh.”

“I dare not. Lie down.”

She collapsed like a log. The rest of the night passed quietly. By morning, she seemed dead again.

Before evening, we sighted the House of Bitterness. Instantly, one of the elephants came alongside my horse.

“Please, king,” whispered the rider, “you’re not going *there*?”

“I am,” I replied. “We’ll stay the night.”

“Oh, please don’t! That must be where the cat-woman lives!”

“If you’d seen her, you wouldn’t call her that.”

“Nobody sees her. She’s lost her face. Her head is all around—back and side.”

“She hides it from dull, discontented souls. Who taught you to call her ‘cat-woman’?”

“The bad giants.”

“What did they say?”

“That she has claws to her toes.”

“False. I know her. I spent a night in her house.”

“But she *might* have claws! You could see her feet, and her claws folded inside her shoes!”

“Then perhaps you think I have claws?”

“No! You’re good!”

“The giants told you that about me?”

“We wouldn’t believe them about you!”

“Are the giants good?”

“No. They love lies.”

“So why believe them about her? I know she’s good. She cannot have claws.”

“How do you know?”

“How do you know I’m good?”

I rode on. He waited, then told his friends what I had said. They hurried after me.

“I wouldn’t bring you here if I didn’t trust her,” I said.

“We know that,” they answered.

“If I did something that frightened you—what would you say?”

“The beasts scared us at first,” one said. “But they never hurt us.”

“That was before we knew them,” added another.

“Exactly. When you see the woman in that cottage, you’ll know she’s good. You may wonder at what she does—but she will always be good. I know her better than you know me. She won’t hurt you. Or—if she does…”

“Oh, you’re not sure, king dear! You think she *might* hurt us!”

“I’m certain she’ll never be unkind—even if she harms you.”

They were silent.

“I’m not afraid of being hurt—just a little! A lot!” Odu declared.

“But I don’t want scratches in the dark! The giants say the cat-woman has claw-feet all over her house!”

“I’m taking the princess to her,” I said.

“Why?”

“Because she is her friend.”

“How can she be good then?”

“Little Tumbledown is her friend. So is Luva. I’ve seen them try to feed her grapes.”

“Little Tumbled down is good! Luva is very good!”

“And that’s why they’re friends.”

“Will the cat-woman—no, the woman who isn’t the cat-woman—give her grapes?”

“She’s more likely to give her scratches.”

“Why? You say she’s her friend!”

“Precisely. A friend gives what is needed. The princess needs a terrible scratching.”

Silence.

“If any of you are afraid,” I said, “you may go home. I won’t stop you. But I cannot take one who trusts giants over me—or calls a good woman the cat-woman.”

“Please, king,” one said, “I’m so afraid of being afraid.”

“My boy,” I replied, “there’s no harm in fear. Only in obeying it. Fear is not your master. Laugh in its face—and it flees.”

“There she is—in the door, waiting!” one cried, covering his eyes.

“How ugly she is!” another gasped, doing the same.

“You don’t see her,” I said. “Her face is covered.”

“She has no face!”

“She has a beautiful face. I’ve seen it. As beautiful as Lona’s.”

“Then why does she hide it?”

“I think I know. She has reason.”

“I don’t like the cat-woman! She’s frightful!”

“You can’t like or dislike what you haven’t seen. And you must not call her that.”

“What should we call her?”

“Lady Mara.”

“A pretty name!” said a girl. “I’ll call her Lady Mara. Maybe she’ll show me her beautiful face!”

Mara stood in the doorway, wrapped in white. She stepped forward, took Lona’s body from my arms, carried it into the house, then returned for Lilith. The princess shuddered, but offered no resistance. The beasts lay down by the door. We followed, the Little Ones solemn.

Mara placed Lilith on a rough settle, unbound her, then turned to us.

“Mr. Vane,” she said, “and you, Little Ones—I thank you. This woman would not yield to kindness. Now harder measures must follow. I shall do what I can to make her repent.”

The children began to sob.

“Will you hurt her very much, Lady Mara?” asked the girl who had spoken earlier, placing her warm hand in mine.

“Yes. I fear I must. I fear she will make me. It would be cruel to hurt her too little. We’d have to do it again—worse.”

“May I stay with her?”

“No, child. She loves no one. Therefore, she cannot be *with* anyone. There is One who will be with her—but she will not be with Him.”

“Will the shadow that came down the hill be with her?”

“The great Shadow will be *in* her, I fear. But He cannot be *with* her—or with anyone. She will know I am near—but that will not comfort her.”

“Will you scratch her very deep?” Odu asked, stepping close, hand in hers.

“Please, don’t make the red juice come!”

She lifted him, turned her back to us, and lowered the muffling from her face. She held him at arm’s length so he might see.

For a moment, he stared—mouth agape. Then, a divine wonder bloomed in his eyes, swiftly turning to rapture. He gazed, entranced. Then she set him down. He stood, transfixed, looking up at her—then ran to us, face radiant with a bliss he could not name.

Mara pulled her veil back into place and turned to the others.

“You must eat and drink before sleep,” she said. “You’ve had a long journey.”

She brought bread—hard, dry—and water in a jug. They had never seen either. Yet they ate the bread without complaint, drank the water with faces lit by astonishment.

Then she led the youngest to the garret. With gentle hands, she tucked them onto the floor. And the Little Ones, for the first time, slept under a roof that held no fear—only peace.
