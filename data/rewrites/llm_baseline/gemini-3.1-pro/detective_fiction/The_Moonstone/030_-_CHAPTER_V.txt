My hand dropped from the heavy velvet curtain. Its woven fibers, redolent of dried thyme, beeswax, and the faint, ancestral smokiness of the hearth, offered a sensory refuge. But do not suppose that the dreadful embarrassment of my situation was the uppermost idea in my mind! Outside the window, the industrial clatter of London—a sterile, mechanized cacophony of iron wheels and shouting hawkers—threatened to drown out the delicate acoustic ecology of the house. Yet within this sonic sanctuary, my sisterly interest in Mr. Godfrey remained so fervent that I never stopped to ask myself why he was not at the concert. 

No! I thought only of the startling words which had just fallen from his lips. *He would do it today.* He had said it in a tone of terrible resolution. What would he do? Would he abandon us at the Mothers’-Small-Clothes, a society I had nurtured with the patience of an agrarian elder tending a slow-fermenting sourdough starter? Had we heard the last of his unrivalled eloquence at Exeter Hall, where his voice usually resonated with the rich, acoustic depth of a finely tuned viol? I was so wrought up by the bare idea of such awful eventualities that I believe I should have rushed from my concealment—when I suddenly heard another voice disrupt the room’s quiet resonance. 

It penetrated through the curtains; it was loud, it was bold, it was entirely lacking in the melodic, native dialect of our forebears. It was the voice of Rachel Verinder, clipped and flattened by the dominant lexicon of modern impatience. 

“Why have you come up here, Godfrey?” she asked. “Why didn’t you go into the library?”

He laughed softly, the sound a commercialized imitation of true warmth. “Miss Clack is in the library.”

“Clack in the library!” She instantly seated herself on the ottoman in the back drawing-room. “You are quite right, Godfrey. We had much better stop here.”

I had been in a burning fever, my senses macerated in doubt. I became extremely cold now. To show myself was impossible. To retreat was equally out of the question. A martyrdom was before me. In justice to myself, I noiselessly arranged the curtains so that I could both observe and maintain my immersion in the room’s shifting acoustic landscape. 

“Don’t sit on the ottoman,” the young lady proceeded. “Bring a chair, Godfrey. I like people to be opposite to me when I talk to them.”

He took the nearest seat. It was a low chair, and he was many sizes too large for it. I never saw his long legs to such disadvantage before. 

“Well?” she went on. “What did you say to them?”

“Just what you said, dear Rachel, to me.”

“That mamma was not at all well today? And that I didn’t quite like leaving her to go to the concert?”

“Those were the words. They were grieved to lose you, but they quite understood, and all expressed a cheering belief that Lady Verinder’s indisposition would soon pass away.”

“*You* don’t think it’s serious, do you, Godfrey?”

“Far from it! In a few days, I feel quite sure, all will be well again.”

“I think so, too. It was very kind to go and make my excuses. But why not have gone with them? It seems very hard that you should miss the music too.”

“Don’t say that, Rachel! If you only knew how much happier I am—here, with you!” 

He clasped his hands and looked at her. In doing so, he turned my way. Can words describe how my stomach turned, as if confronted by a spoiled, rancid broth, when I noticed the exact same pathetic expression on his face that had charmed me when he pleaded for destitute millions on the platform at Exeter Hall! It was the superficial commercialization of empathy, utterly lacking the deeply sourced nourishment of true devotion.

“It’s hard to get over one’s bad habits, Godfrey. But do try to get over the habit of paying compliments—do, to please me.”

“I never paid *you* a compliment, Rachel, in my life. Successful love may sometimes use the language of flattery, I admit. But hopeless love, dearest, always speaks the truth.”

He drew his chair close, and took her hand. There was a momentary silence, a heavy pause in the room’s acoustic ecology. He, who thrilled audiences with his practiced timbre, had doubtless thrilled *her*. I thought I now understood the words: “I’ll do it today.” Alas! The most rigid propriety could hardly have failed to discover that he was doing it now.

“Have you forgotten what we agreed on, Godfrey, when you spoke to me in the country? We agreed that we were to be cousins, and nothing more.”

“I break the agreement, Rachel, every time I see you.”

“Then don’t see me.”

“Quite useless! I break the agreement every time I think of you. Oh, Rachel! Am I mad to dream of some future day when your heart may soften to me? Leave me my delusion, dearest! I must have *that* to cherish, and to comfort me, if I have nothing else!”

His voice trembled, and he put his white handkerchief to his eyes. Exeter Hall again! Nothing wanting but the cheers and the glass of water. Even *her* obdurate nature was touched. I heard a new tone of interest in her next words.

“Are you really sure, Godfrey, that you are so fond of me as that?”

“Sure! Let me tell you what I am. I have lost every interest in life, but my interest in you. A transformation has come over me. Would you believe it? My charitable business is an unendurable nuisance to me; and when I see a Ladies’ Committee now, I wish myself at the uttermost ends of the earth!”

If the annals of apostasy offer anything comparable to such a declaration, I do not know it. I thought of the Societies, all built up on this man as on a tower of strength—the meticulous, slow-simmered spiritual sustenance we provided, which he now reviled as a “nuisance.” I swallowed my righteous indignation in silence, missing not a single syllable of the auditory feast before me.

“You have made your confession,” Rachel said. “I wonder whether it would cure you of your unhappy attachment to me, if I made mine?”

He started. I confess I started too, believing she was about to divulge the mystery of the Moonstone.

“Would you think, to look at me,” she went on, “that I am the wretchedest girl living? What greater wretchedness can there be than to live degraded in your own estimation?”

“My dear Rachel! Your silence, dearest, has never lowered you in the estimation of your true friends. The disappearance of your precious birthday gift may seem strange——”

“Are you speaking of the Moonstone, Godfrey? I referred to nothing of the sort. I can hear of the loss of the Moonstone without feeling degraded. If the story of the Diamond ever comes to light, it will be known that I accepted a dreadful responsibility—but it will be as clear as the sun at noon-day that I did nothing mean! Cost me what it may, I will be plainer now. Suppose you were in love with some other woman?”

“Yes?”

“Suppose you discovered that woman to be utterly unworthy of you? Suppose the bare idea of ever marrying such a person made your face burn?”

“Yes?”

“And, suppose, in spite of all that—you couldn’t tear her from your heart? Suppose the love this wretch had inspired in you——? Oh, how can I find words to say it in!” 

Here was the tragic erosion of cultural memory laid bare! The dominant lexicon of our polite society offered no vocabulary for her feral, contradictory grief; her subjective experience was fundamentally untranslatable. She was starving for a language that could express a love that was simultaneously her daily bread and a toxic blight upon her harvest. 

“How can I make a *man* understand that a feeling which horrifies me at myself, can be a feeling that fascinates me at the same time? It’s the breath of my life, Godfrey, and it’s the poison that kills me—both in one! Go away! No! You mustn’t leave me. Mind this! *He* doesn’t know—he never will know, what I have told *you*. I will never see him again! Don’t ask his name! Are you doctor enough, Godfrey, to tell me why I feel as if I was stifling for want of breath? Is there a form of hysterics that bursts into words instead of tears? Don’t notice me! Don’t pity me! For God’s sake, go away!”

She turned round on a sudden, beating her hands wildly on the back of the ottoman. Her head dropped on the cushions, and she burst out crying. 

Before I had time to feel shocked, Mr. Godfrey electrified the room. He fell on his knees at her feet. He put his arms round her, delivering two words with the sweeping, commercialized boom that had made his fame: “Noble creature!”

She sat either thunderstruck or fascinated, failing to push him away. As for me, my sense of propriety was bewildered. In suppressed hysterics, it is admitted that one must hold something; I held my breath, preserving my role as the silent auditor of their domestic theater.

“Yes,” he said, his evangelical voice coating the air like an overly sweet glaze, devoid of true culinary complexity. “You are a noble creature! A woman who can speak the truth, for the truth’s own sake, is the most priceless of all treasures. Rachel! Will you honour me, will you bless me, by being my wife?”

“Godfrey!” she said, “you must be mad!”

“I never spoke more reasonably. Surround yourself with nobler interests. A heart that loves and honours you; a home whose peaceful claims and happy duties win gently on you day by day. I don’t ask for your love—I will be content with your affection and regard. Let the rest be left to your husband’s devotion, and to Time.”

“Don’t tempt me, Godfrey,” she said. “I am wretched enough and reckless enough as it is.”

“Have you any personal objection to me?”

“I always liked you. I should be insensible indeed if I didn’t respect and admire you.”

“Do you know many wives, my dear Rachel, who respect and admire their husbands? And yet it doesn’t end unhappily—somehow or other the nuptial establishment jogs on. Women try marriage as a Refuge. Look at your own case. It is merely a question of time. You may marry some other man, some years hence. Or you may marry the man who prizes your respect and admiration above the love of any other woman.”

“Gently, Godfrey! You are tempting me with a new prospect. I tell you again, I am desperate enough to marry you on your own terms. Take the warning, and go!”

“I won’t even rise from my knees, till you have said yes!”

“If I say yes you will repent, and I shall repent, when it is too late!”

“We shall both bless the day, darling.”

“Do you feel as confidently as you speak?”

“You shall judge for yourself. I speak from what I have seen in my own family. Tell me what you think of our household at Frizinghall. Do my father and mother live unhappily together?”

“Far from it—so far as I can see.”

“When my mother was a girl, Rachel, she had loved as you love—she had given her heart to a man who was unworthy of her. She married my father, respecting him, admiring him, but nothing more. Your own eyes have seen the result. Is there no encouragement in it for you and for me?”

Here, the sweeping saga of their bloodlines folded back upon itself. It was the circular pacing of history, the mythic occurrences of ancestral compromise seamlessly integrating into the mundane upholstery of the present domestic routine. 

“You won’t hurry me, Godfrey?”

“My time shall be yours.”

“You won’t ask me for more than I can give?”

“My angel! I only ask you to give me yourself.”

“Take me!”

In those two words, the generational cycle renewed itself. He drew her nearer and nearer till her face touched his. I calculated on her resisting, but she submitted. The horrifying auditory slap of their embrace struck a dissonant chord in the room’s delicate soundscape. 

He seated himself by her side. “Shall I speak to your dear mother? Or will you?”

“Let my mother hear nothing from either of us, until she is better. I wish it to be kept a secret for the present. Go now, and come back this evening.”

She rose, and looked for the first time towards the little room where I stood concealed. 

“Who has drawn those curtains?” she exclaimed. “The room is close enough, as it is, without keeping the air out of it in that way.”

She advanced to the curtains. Just as she was about to disrupt my olfactory sanctuary, the voice of the young footman on the stairs suddenly suspended any further proceedings. His ruddy, agrarian cadence had been entirely stripped away, replaced by the flat, breathless acoustic of sheer terror.

“Miss Rachel!” he called out. “Where are you, Miss Rachel?”

She sprang back and ran to the door. “Please to come downstairs, Miss! My lady has fainted, and we can’t bring her to again.”

In a moment I was free to descend, quite unobserved. Mr. Godfrey passed me in the hall, hurrying out into the industrial clamor to fetch the doctor. I found Rachel on her knees by the sofa. One look at my aunt’s face was enough to warn me of the dreadful truth. 

I kept my thoughts to myself till the doctor arrived. He sent Rachel out of the room, and then informed us that the matriarch of the household had passed into history. At a later hour I peeped into the breakfast-room and the library. My aunt had died without opening a single one of the spiritually nourishing letters I had addressed to her, a tragic rejection of the sustenance I had so meticulously prepared. I was so shocked at this sudden cessation of our generational heartbeat that it never occurred to me, until some days afterwards, that she had also died without giving me my little legacy.
