Tucked between the frayed, mildew-stained leather covers of the handwritten narrative that follows, its edges spotted with faint, unidentifiable dark splotches that are certainly not ink, is a loose sheet of paper covered in Doctor Hesselius’s sharp, slanted, unforgiving scrawl. The note is elaborate, deliberately phrased, and cross-referenced directly to his little-circulated Essay on the uncanny, uncategorizable phenomenon this manuscript illuminates.

Hesselius dissects that terrible, mysterious subject in his Essay with his signature erudition, his razor-sharp eye for overlooked detail, and a blunt, unflinching economy of language no other scholar of his generation dared apply to the weird, disavowed fringe of human experience. The work will stand as one standalone volume in the late, extraordinary man’s collected, rarely shared papers.

I have chosen to present the full account in this volume not to debate the metaphysics of the case with learned men of science, but to let the lay reader feel the full, unfiltered weight of the terror the narrator endured. For that reason, after long careful consideration, I have made the deliberate choice not to preface her story with any summary of Hesselius’s line of reasoning, nor to print a single excerpt from his own formal statement on the matter. He himself wrote that the subject “involves, not improbably, some of the profoundest arcana of our dual existence, and its intermediates” — secrets no man can distill into a dry précis without stripping them of the uncanny, visceral power that made them so fatal to the people caught up in them.

When I first unearthed this manuscript, I wrote at once, desperate to re-open the correspondence Hesselius had begun decades earlier with the clever, careful woman who had penned this account. I had a hundred questions, gaps I hoped she could fill, small details of the uncanny events she might have omitted out of reluctance to be dismissed as hysterical. To my unshakable, creeping regret, I found that she had been long dead in the interval, her passing barely noted in local parish records, no trace left of her but these yellowed pages.

She probably could have added little to the narrative she lays out in the pages that follow. It is stitched through with such conscientious, unflinching particularity that no reader could doubt every line was written by a woman who lived every terrible moment of the unspeakable horror it describes.

In the lonely backcountry of Styria, we are not wealthy people by any stretch, but we live in a schloss—our feudal little castle, perched on a rise swallowed by pine forest. A tiny income stretches farther here than you could ever imagine: eight or nine hundred English pounds a year works small miracles, a sum that would barely have kept us afloat among London’s rich. My father is English, and I carry an English name, though I have never once set foot in the country. Here, where everything is quiet and unthinkably cheap, I cannot fathom how far more money could ever add to our comforts, let alone our luxuries. He served the Austrian army for decades, retired on his pension and the last of his family patrimony, and bought this whole crumbling estate for a song, as if the old stone walls had been waiting years for someone foolish enough to move into them.

There is no place more solitary. A narrow, overgrown medieval road runs past a drawbridge that has never been raised in my lifetime, spanning a moat thick with perch, gliding swans, and fleets of white water lilies that drift across its still surface like unmarked shrouds. Beyond it, the schloss rises with its rows of glinting windows, turrets, and the tiny Gothic chapel half overgrown with ivy. Before our gates, the forest falls back in a ragged, sun-dappled glade, and to the right a steep, arching stone bridge carries the road over a stream that winds away into deep, unbroken shadow.
You will understand how remote we are when I tell you: from our front hall door, the woods stretch fifteen miles to the right and twelve to the left. The nearest inhabited village lies seven English miles away to the west. The nearest other occupied schloss, that of old General Spielsdorf, is nearly twenty miles east. And three miles past that village, closer to Spielsdorf’s estate, there stands a dead settlement: roofless stone cottages, a crumbled little church whose aisles hold the moldering, cracked tombs of the Karnstein family, long extinct. Their own half-ruined chateau juts from the pine treeline above the empty village, silent and windowless. There is a wild local legend about what made the whole place empty overnight, but I will not speak of it now.

Our household is shockingly small. I do not count the servants who live in the outbuildings attached to the schloss. My father, the kindest man alive but growing frail with age, and I—nineteen at the time I write this, eight years after the events I am about to relate—make up the core of the family. My mother was a Styrian lady, dead before I could form a memory of her. I had Madame Perrodon, a soft, round governess from Berne, whose gentle face I cannot remember a time without, to fill the gap her loss left. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, our finishing governess who spoke fluent French and German, rounded out our little dinner table. My father and I spoke English every day, half to keep the language from fading among us, half out of stubborn patriotism, and between the four of us we made a babel of tongues that always made traveling strangers laugh. We had occasional visits from two or three girls my age, but those weeks were rare. Our “nearest” neighbors were five or six leagues away at minimum. Mine was a life of almost total quiet, of listening to the wind rattle the schloss panes for hours on end.

The very first memory I have that has never faded, that left a mark on me so deep I still feel it some nights when I wake alone, is so trivial most people would laugh to hear it told. But you will soon see why it matters.

My nursery, all to myself, was a vast room under the steep, raftered oak roof of the upper story. I could not have been more than six years old when I woke one midnight, the guttering candle on my nightstand sputtering low. My nurse was gone. The nursery maid was gone. I thought I was entirely alone. I was not afraid at first—no one had ever told me ghost stories, no tales of fairies or wandering bogles, nothing that would make me yank the covers over my head at the sound of a creaking door, or flinch from the dancing shadow of a bedpost on the wall. I was only cross, insulted to think they had forgotten me, and I opened my mouth to wail at the top of my lungs.

That was when I saw her.
A very pretty, solemn young lady knelt right beside my bed, her face level with mine, her hands slipped under the edge of my coverlet. I stared at her, bewitched, and stopped my whimpering. She stroked my hair gently, then climbed into the bed beside me, pulled me soft against her chest, and smiled. I felt instantly soothed, lulled by the strange, sweet smell of jasmine and damp earth that clung to her clothes, and drifted back toward sleep.

Then two sharp, burning needles drove straight into my breast, at the exact same time, deep enough to make me scream until my throat rawed.
The lady reared back, her eyes wide and black, no white visible around the irises, and slipped off the edge of the bed to vanish out of sight, as if she had melted straight down into the floorboards.

That was when real terror seized me. I screamed so loud the rafters seemed to rattle, and the nurse, the nursery maid, the housekeeper all came bursting through the door at a run. They hushed me and petted me, laughing off my story as a bad dream at first, but I could see their faces were pale as linen, tight with a fear they would not name. They ripped every cupboard open, dragged the curtains from the windows, knelt to peer under every table and every inch of the bed frame. The housekeeper pressed her palm to the warm spot on the sheets where the strange lady had lain, and whispered so quiet I almost missed it: “Someone did lie here. It is still warm.” They pulled my nightgown open to examine my chest, where I swore I had been pricked, but there was no mark to be seen.

After that night, two of the maids sat up with me in the nursery every single hour until I turned fourteen. A sallow, elderly doctor came every other day for weeks, his face pocked with smallpox, his chestnut wig smelling of snuff, forcing me to swallow bitter iron medicine I loathed. I could not bear to be left alone for even a minute, even in full daylight. My father came to my bedside, patting my shoulder, laughing and telling me it had only been a bad dream that could not hurt me—but I saw his hands shook a little when he said it. The maids tried to tell me it had been one of them who had snuck in to lie beside me, that I had just misremembered their faces, but I knew they were lying. I had seen that stranger’s eyes.

By the end of that day, a white-haired old priest in a black cassock came into the room with the housekeeper and nurse. His face was gentle, but his hands were ice cold, and he folded my small hands between his own, told me to whisper after him while they prayed: “Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus’ sake.” I have never forgotten those words. He knelt on the bare oak floor, the three women knelt behind him, and he prayed in a wobbly, earnest voice for so long that the candle on the mantel burned almost all the way down. The whole air of that old shadowy room, lit only by thin light through the small lattice window, smelled of incense and something sour and dead I still cannot name.

Every other memory of my early childhood dissolves into fog and darkness around that day. But that one scene— the lady at my bedside, the burning pricks in my chest, the priest praying in the dim light—burns bright, sharp and vivid as a lantern held up against a wall of total black.

I am about to tell you a thing so tangled in shadow, so impossible to square with the soft, ordinary rules of the world you know, that you will strain every last scrap of your faith in my honesty to believe it. It is true, all of it. I was there. I saw it.

It was the kind of hush-heavy summer evening that clings to your skin like damp silk, the air thick with the cloying scent of linden bloom, no birdsong left, no hum of late bees. My father asked me, as he often did, to wander with him down that long, tree-shrouded vista cutting through the forest in front of our schloss. “General Spielsdorf will not be coming to us as soon as we hoped,” he said as we walked, his boots crunching softly over pine needles fallen thick across the path.

He was meant to arrive the next day, for a stay of weeks, bringing his niece and ward Bertha Rheinfeldt, the girl I had dreamed of meeting for months. I had woven endless daydreams of long rambles through the woods with her, of sharing my books and secrets, of breaking the quiet monotony of our isolated life up here in the hills. No city girl could ever understand how hollow that news landed, how much my whole world had shrunk in the space of a sentence. “When will he come then?” I asked, tight in the throat.

“Not for two months. Not till autumn,” he said. “And I am glad now, my dear, that you never met Bertha.”

I stared at him, stung and confused. “Why?”

“Because she is dead,” he said, and I realized then how tight his mouth was, how pale his knuckles were where he held the crumpled letter in his hand. He had got it that evening, when I was out gathering wild strawberries, had not had a chance to tell me yet. The General’s last note, weeks prior, had only mentioned her being a little unwell, no hint of danger, not even a flicker of what was to come.

We sat down on a rough hewn bench under the largest of the lindens, the sun bleeding down behind the treeline in streaks of bruised crimson that dyed the winding little river at our feet the color of fresh blood. He passed me the letter. It was scrawled in ink that ran in smudges where tears had fallen, some lines crossed out so violently the pen had torn the paper. I read it twice, the second time aloud, my voice cracking on the words, and still I could not make sense of it unless grief had unspooled the General’s mind entirely.

*I have lost my darling daughter. I loved her as my own. I did not know the danger she was in until it was far too late. Now I know all. The fiend that wormed its way into my home, that I welcomed like a long-lost friend, did this. God in heaven, what a fool I was. Thank Him she died never knowing what was eating her alive, never guessing the thing that preyed on her night after night. I will spend the rest of my days hunting that monster, tearing it out of whatever dark hole it hides in. I curse my own pride, my own stupid skepticism, my blindness. It is too late for Bertha, but not for anyone else. I cannot write the rest. I dare not. I am not myself right now. When I can think straight I will travel, as far as Vienna if I must, to find the truth. I will come to you in two months, if I live, and tell you everything I cannot put on paper. Pray for me.*

Tears were running down my cheeks before I finished. I had never even met Bertha, but her death felt sharp, personal, like a warning I could not quite hear. The sun had dipped below the horizon entirely by then, and the dark was creeping fast across the glade. We loitered on the path back, whispering about the strange, furious letter, wondering what horror the General had been too afraid to commit to ink. When we stepped out onto the drawbridge, Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine were there, no bonnets, their faces lit silver by the full moon that hung low and too bright in the sky, so bright it bleached all the color from the grass and the rocks.

A thin mist was stealing across the low meadows, coiling like smoke from a smothered fire, wisping around our ankles. The river glinted below the bridge, silver as a blade. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, half German, half mystic, was chattering away about the moon, saying a moon that bright hummed with a kind of hungry magic, that it pulled at dreams, pulled at unwell minds, pulled at things that did not belong to the waking world. She told us of her cousin, the ship’s mate, who napped face up on deck on a night just like this, woke screaming because an old woman’s clawed hand was dragging at his cheek, and his face had been twisted lopsided ever after, never right again. “The moon tonight is full of hunger,” she said, gesturing back at the schloss, its windows glowing like they had been lit from inside by no human hand.

My father went quiet then, quoting that Shakespeare line about inexplicable sadness, about a weight you cannot name hanging over you. “I feel it,” he said. “Like some great misfortune is breathing down our necks. It must be the General’s letter, that is all.”

That was when we heard it: carriage wheels screaming over stone, the thunder of hooves, coming fast from the hill above the bridge, an unheard of sound out here in the quiet hills. Two outriders came into view first, then the grand traveling carriage drawn by four black horses, then two more men riding behind. Before we could even gape at the sight, one of the lead horses reared, eyes rolling milky white, and the whole team bolted, wild as things possessed, thundering down the road towards us so fast the carriage swayed on its wheels. A long, keening, almost inhuman scream tore out of the carriage window, rising over the thunder of the hooves.

We all ran forward, half horrified, half curious. The horses swerved at the old stone cross by the linden tree, one wheel slamming hard into the thick, gnarled roots of the ancient tree. The carriage tipped hard onto its side, two wheels spinning uselessly in the air. Two horses went down snorting, the rest rearing. A tall woman dressed head to toe in heavy black velvet climbed out, her face dead pale, her dark eyes sweeping over the scene with no trace of fear, only sharp, urgent impatience.

They lifted a young woman out of the wreck next, limp, eyes closed, so still I thought for a second she was dead. My father knelt by her wrist, fumbling for her pulse, and told the dark-cloaked woman who claimed to be her mother that her heart was still beating, faint but steady.

The woman’s hands flew up to the sky, a theatrical gesture that did not match the cold, sharp glint in her eyes. “I am on a journey of life and death,” she cried, “lose an hour and all is lost. I cannot stay. My daughter will not be fit to travel for weeks. The nearest village is miles away, no inn fit to leave her in. What am I to do?”

I tugged at my father’s sleeve, breathless. “Papa, please, let her stay with us. We will care for her. It will be such a blessing, after Bertha, after the General’s visit fell through.”

My father offered, of course. The woman protested at first, as if she had been waiting exactly for those words, then drew him aside a few paces, out of earshot. I watched their faces, and the shadow of the linden tree swallowed hers for a second, and for a flash I saw her teeth, sharp and white, glinting in the moonlight as she spoke fast, low, urgent, to him. He nodded, once, twice, like he was caught in a light trance.

She walked back to the girl, knelt, pressed a cold, dry kiss to her forehead that left no warmth behind. Then she climbed back into the righted carriage, the postilions cracked their whips, and the whole party vanished down the road at a gallop, the dust they kicked up hanging in the silver moonlight like fine shroud silk.

The girl stirred, then, lying propped against the bank, her red lips curving into a slow, sleepy smile, her eyes still closed, like she had known exactly where she was, all along.

That was how Carmilla came to be our guest.

We stood at the schloss gate, our eyes locked to the dwindling cortege until the horses, the carriage, even the faint silhouettes of the running servants dissolved entirely into the mist that coiled thick around the pine forest. There was no echo left of hoofbeats, no crunch of gravel under wheels—only the hush of night, so deep and empty it threatened to swallow our own breathing, and for a wild, stupid second I thought the whole evening’s chaos had been no more than a shared hallucination.

The proof it was real stirred at our feet. The young stranger’s lashes fluttered, and she woke, her face turned half away from us, her head lifting slow as if she had been asleep a hundred years. A voice dripped like melted honey, plaintive and soft, “Where is mamma?”

Madame Perrodon bent to soothe her, murmuring soft reassurances, and the girl’s next words came slow, confused: “Where am I? The carriage is gone. Where is Matska?”

Madame answered every question she could parse, and by degrees the girl recovered the thread of the accident, gasping with relief to hear no one from her party had been hurt. When we told her her mother had left her here, to return in three months’ time, great silent tears spilled down her cheeks. I stepped forward to add my own comfort, but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine’s hand closed hard on my arm, her fingers ice-cold. “Don’t approach. She cannot bear more than one person at a time just now. Too much excitement will shatter her.”

I resolved to slip to her room the second she was settled in bed. My father had already dispatched a mounted servant for the physician, his cottage two leagues deep in the woods, while the maids lit fires and dragged fresh linens to the finest spare chamber on the west wing. The stranger rose, leaning light as a feather on Madame’s arm, and crossed the drawbridge into the castle, the heavy oak gate thudding shut behind her as if it had swallowed her whole.

Our drawing room, where we gathered later for tea, is long, walled with ancient carved oak, its tapestries frayed at the edges, stitched full of huntsmen and hawks that seem to shift shape when the candlelight flickers. We talked over the night’s strange events while the new guest slept deep, watched over by a single maid.

“What do you make of her?” I asked Madame the moment she stepped back into the room. “Tell me everything.”

“She is the prettiest creature I have ever seen,” Madame said, soft with wonder. “Just about your age, so gentle, so quiet.”

“Beautiful is the word,” Mademoiselle De Lafontaine put in, her brow furrowed. “I peeked in at her just now. And—did any of you see the woman who never stepped out of the broken carriage, after the men righted it?”

We shook our heads.

“Hideous,” she said, her voice dropping to a whisper. “Swathed all in black, with a bright colored turban wound round her head, leaning half out the window, grinning and nodding at us like she knew some terrible secret. Her eyes glowed, black as pitch, the whites of them so bright they shone in the dark, her teeth bared like she was about to snarl.”

“And the men-servants with the carriage?” Madame added. “All of them lean, dark, sullen, their faces so ill-favored I could not bear to look at them long.”

My father stepped into the room just then, nodding grim agreement. “The worst hang-dog rogues I ever laid eyes on. I half expected them to cut our throats before they drove off. Still, they fixed that carriage in less than a minute. I only hope they don’t rob their own mistress on the road back through the woods.”

“Travel has worn them thin, I expect,” Madame said. But none of us sounded convinced.

My father smiled then, a strange, secret little smile, like he held a piece of the puzzle none of the rest of us could see. It pricked our curiosity sharp, and we hounded him the second we were alone to tell us what the lady in the black velvet travelling dress had whispered to him before she climbed back into her carriage.

He hardly needed persuading. “She said right off that she was loath to burden us with her daughter’s care, that the girl was delicate, nervous—but no fits, no visions, perfectly sane. It was so odd, to volunteer that before we even asked. Then she leaned in, and she said she was on a journey of vital importance—she spat that word out like it was a curse—rapid, secret. She would return for her daughter in three months, and in that time, the girl must not speak of who they are, where they came from, where they are going. Not a word. She held my gaze then, stared so hard I felt the hair on the back of my neck stand up, and she repeated the word ‘secret.’ Then she climbed back into the carriage, and they were gone before I could even think to object. I hope I have not been a fool, to agree to this.”

I did not care. I was giddy, hungry to meet our new guest. In this schloss, where the nearest neighbor is ten miles away, where whole weeks pass with no visitor, no new face, a stranger is a miracle. I could no more have slept than I could have run down the carriage that vanished into the mist.

The doctor did not arrive until one in the morning. He came down to the drawing room beaming, to say his new patient was perfectly well, her pulse steady, no injury at all, the shock of the carriage crash already gone. He said there was no harm at all if I went up to visit her for a few minutes, so I sent a maid to ask if she would receive me.

The maid returned at once, saying the lady desired nothing more. I flew up the stairs.

Her room was dim, stately, the tapestry opposite her bed showing Cleopatra pressing the asp to her breast, the threads faded so the little viper looked as if it was twitching, alive. Candles guttered on the nightstand. She was sitting up, wrapped in the soft flower-embroidered silk dressing gown her mother had laid over her on the grass.

The second my eyes landed on her face, my greeting died in my throat. I staggered back a step, the blood turning to ice in my veins.

It was her. The very same face that had crept into my bedroom when I was a small child, twelve years prior, the face I had woken screaming from nightmares about, that I had never once been able to forget. It was beautiful, yes, but holding the same melancholy, sharp look I remembered—then it melted into a slow, fixed smile of recognition.

A full minute of dead silence stretched between us. Then she spoke, her voice soft with wonder. “How wonderful. Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me every single day since.”

“Wonderful indeed,” I forced out, fighting down the cold terror that clamped around my chest. “Twelve years ago, I saw you too. In vision, or in truth. I have never forgotten your face.”

Her smile softened then, the odd sharp edge of it fading, her cheeks dimpling, and I felt the tension in my shoulders unspool. I welcomed her, told her how overjoyed we all were to have her here, how glad I was for her company, and reached for her hand. Her skin was cold as tomb stone, but she laced her fingers through mine, leaning in, her dark eyes glowing bright as she smiled and blushed.

She told me her story first: how when she was six years old, she woke in a dark strange room, not her nursery, with wainscoted walls, a dented two-pronged iron candlestick on a table, and found me there, a little girl with golden hair and blue eyes, knelt on the floor. She had climbed into the bed with me, wrapped her arms around me, then woke back in her own nursery, and had carried my face in her head every day since.

I told her my own matching nightmare, of the dark lady who crept into my room when I was small, who climbed into my bed, and I woke screaming. We laughed, a little shakily, at how absurd it was, two children across the world sharing the exact same terrible dream.

“I do not know which of us ought to be more afraid of the other,” she said, smiling, her dark eyes fixed on mine, no flicker of a blink. “But you are so pretty, I cannot be scared of you. It feels like we have known each other our whole lives. Have you never felt drawn to someone like that, as if you were always meant to be friends?”

I did feel that pull, warm and bright, but under it there was a thin, thrumming thread of repulsion, something that made my skin prickle when she leaned too close. The attraction won out, though. She was beautiful, bewitching, and I could not find it in me to pull away. When I saw exhaustion stealing over her, I bade her good night, offered her a maid to sit up with her, but she shook her head.

“I cannot sleep with a stranger in the room,” she said, and her arms wound tight around my waist, pulling me close. Her breath was cold against my ear. “I lock my door every night, ever since robbers broke into our home and killed two of our servants. You will not mind, will you? Good night, darling. I cannot bear to part with you. We will see each other tomorrow, late.”

She sank back against the pillows, her eyes following me all the way to the door, murmuring “good night, dear friend” so soft I barely heard it.

By the next morning, that initial shock of recognition had faded. I thought I could not have found anything more lovely than our new guest, the most beautiful creature I had ever seen. We laughed together over our matching childhood nightmares, brushed off that momentary, absurd little horror as nothing more than the weird coincidence of two lonely girls finding each other.

I did not notice, as we laughed, that she still had not blinked once.

I told you I was charmed by her, almost entirely. But there were small, sharp edges to that charm, things that niggled at the back of my throat like a half-swallowed splinter, that I could not bear to name even to myself back then.

Let me start with what I can still see so clearly, ten years later, that I could trace every line of it with a shaking finger: she was taller than most women, slender, so graceful she moved like water poured through silk. The only thing that marked her as anything other than perfectly healthy, at first glance, was that languor — that terrible, heavy, unhurried slowness to every gesture, as if the world moved too fast for her to bother keeping pace. Her skin was rich, glowing, like warm honey under rose; her features small, carved so finely they looked like they might shatter if you touched them too hard; her eyes huge, dark, so lustrous they seemed to glow in dim light, even when the candles had burned low. But her hair — heavens, her hair — I have never seen anything like it, before or since. Thick, so thick I could cradle great handfuls of it in both my palms and laugh at the unexpected weight of it when it spilled loose down her back, fine and soft as spider silk, a deep dark brown shot through with threads of burnished gold that caught the firelight like living embers. I used to sit behind her in her armchair, playing with it, braiding it, spreading it across my lap to admire it, when she would lean back and talk to me in that low, honeyed voice of hers. If I had only known then what that soft, heavy weight held.

I said there were things that did not please me. She had won all my trust that very first night, but I soon found she guarded every scrap of her own life like a miser guards gold: her mother, her childhood, her home, any small trivial detail of who she was or where she had come from, all locked tight behind that soft, smiling melancholy. I know I was unreasonable. I know I ought to have respected the solemn promise my father made to that tall woman in black velvet the night Carmilla arrived. But curiosity is a feral, greedy thing, and no girl can bear to have every question she asks met with nothing but a gentle, evasive smile. What harm could it possibly do? I would have kept any secret she gave me to my grave. Why would she not trust that? That cold, unbreakable reserve, far older than her seeming twenty years, stung more than any outright quarrel could. She would not quarrel, of course. She would only pull me close, tuck my head against her shoulder, and murmur that I would know everything in good time, and I could never stay angry at her for long.

All she ever gave me, after weeks of gentle prying, amounted to three vague scraps: her name was Carmilla. Her family was ancient, noble. Their home lay west of us. No name for her family, no coat of arms, no name for their estate, not even the name of the country they came from.

And sometimes, in those moments where the air went thick and warm, she would wrap her pretty arms tight around my neck, press her cheek to mine, and breathe words against my ear that made my skin prickle even as my eyes grew heavy: “Dearest, don’t think me cruel for the law I must obey. My wild heart bleeds when yours aches. I live now in your warm life, and you shall die — sweetly, gently die — into mine. I cannot help it. As I draw near you, you will draw near to others, and learn that strange rapture of cruelty that is only love. For a little while, ask no more of me. Trust me.” She would kiss my cheek then, her lips hot and soft, and a trance would steal over me. I would forget how to pull away, forget how to protest, and I would only rouse myself, bewildered and breathless, when she finally let go. In those moments I did not like her. I felt a wild, churning excitement, half pleasure, half a nameless, crawling fear and disgust I could not name. I loved her then, and I hated her, all at once. It is a paradox, I know, but there is no other way to describe it.

I write all this now, ten years later, with a trembling hand, the full horror of that unknowing ordeal only just beginning to sort itself out from the tangled, dim memory of those uncanny passionate hours. Most of the time, though, she was just a girl — bright, funny, endlessly interesting, even if she never woke before one in the afternoon, and ate nothing but a single cup of chocolate when she came down. We would go for those slow, meandering saunters through the woods that ringed our schloss, and within five minutes she would be leaning on my arm, winded, so we would sit on the weathered oak benches tucked between the trees, while she talked of strange old customs from far off countries, of places far stranger than the sleepy rural corner of Styria we called home.

One soft golden afternoon, as we sat there, a funeral procession came winding down the path. It was the young forest ranger’s daughter, the pretty thing I had passed a hundred times gathering berries in the woods, his only child, the poor man trailing behind the coffin with his face streaked with tears, the peasants following two by two, singing their slow, sweet funeral hymn. I stood up to bow my respects, and joined in the singing — before Carmilla’s fingers bit hard into my wrist, shaking me roughly.

“Stop,” she said, her voice sharp, no trace of that soft lilt. “That sound is hideous.”

I was vexed, and afraid the procession would notice, so I tried to keep singing. She clapped her tiny fingers over her ears, almost snarling. “It pierces my ears like needles. How do you know your religion is mine? These forms wound me. And what a fuss over nothing. Everyone dies. Everyone is happier for it. Come home at once.”

“The girl was the one who swore she saw a ghost at her bedside a fortnight ago,” I said. “She’s been wasting away ever since. The swineherd’s young wife died a week before that, too — she said something seized her by the throat in the night and almost strangled her. Papa says it’s just a frightful fever going about.”

At that, Carmilla’s face changed. It went dark, livid, a horrible ash gray, her teeth clenched so tight her jaw bulged, her hands curled into fists, her whole body shaking in hard, uncontrollable spasms like she was fighting a fit from deep inside her bones. A low, guttural cry of pain tore out of her, and she stared down at her feet like there was something crawling up her dress from the dirt. It took minutes for the shuddering to stop. When it did, she laughed a thin, shaky laugh. “There — that’s what your dreadful hymns do to me. Hold me. It’s passing now.” She was unusually bright, unusually chatty, all the way home, like she was trying to scrub the odd, terrifying scene right out of my memory.

That was the first time I saw her show anything resembling sickness, or temper. The second time came a few days later, when we were leaning out of the long drawing room window watching the hunchback mountebank cross the drawbridge. He visited our schloss twice a year, loaded down with trinkets and tricks: a magic lantern, two stuffed “monsters” stitched together out of hedgehog and monkey and parrot feathers that he called a salamander and a mandrake, a fiddle, foils, conjuring tricks, all strung from the belts crisscrossing his chest. His ragged dog stopped dead at the edge of the courtyard, and began to howl, long and low, as if it could smell something rotten inside the walls.

The mountebank bowed, scraped his fiddle to a jaunty tune, and called up to us in his rough, guttural mix of bad French and worse German, hawking amulets: slips of vellum covered in cabalistic marks, to pin to your pillow, to ward off the oupire that was creeping through the woods, killing villagers one by one. Carmilla and I both bought one, amused — until he squinted up at Carmilla, his sharp black eyes narrowing, and pulled out a little leather case of dental tools. “You, miss,” he grinned, showing his yellow fangs, “you’ve got the sharpest tooth I’ve ever seen, long and thin as a needle, sticking out just past your lip. Let me file it down for you, no pain at all — no more biting your tongue by accident, eh?”

Carmilla reeled back from the window so fast she nearly knocked over the vase on the sill. Her face was purple with rage. “How dare that insolent wretch insult us like that? Tell your father — my father would have had him tied to the pump, flogged with a cartwhip, and branded with a hot iron for that.” The rage faded as fast as it had flared, though. Within five minutes she was back to her usual soft, languid self, as if the outburst had never happened.

That evening, my father came in looking grave. He said another peasant girl on our estate, only a mile away, had fallen ill. She swore something had seized her by the throat in the night, and she was sinking fast, no doctor could do a thing for her. “It’s nothing but mass hysteria,” he told us. “The villagers feed each other their silly superstitions, and scare themselves half to death.”

Carmilla shivered, and snuggled up against my side. “I’m so afraid I’ll start seeing those things too,” she said. “It would be just as bad as the real thing.”

My father patted her hand. “We are all in God’s hands. Nothing happens that He does not permit.”

“God? Nature,” Carmilla corrected him, soft and sharp. “All things live and die as Nature ordains, don’t they? Even this sickness.”

The doctor came later that day, and shut himself in my father’s study for an hour. When they came out, my father was laughing, shaking his head. “I can’t believe a sensible man like you is talking of hippogriffs and dragons, old friend.”

The doctor only smiled, and shrugged. “Life and death are both great mysteries. We know scarcely anything of the resources of either.”

I did not understand what he meant, then. But I think I know, now. Ten years later, when I wake screaming in the dark, I know exactly what he was trying to tell us.

The fire in the schloss hall spat thin, blue-edged smoke that stung our eyes when the messenger rode up at dusk, his horse lathered from ten leagues of hard travel out of Gratz. He was the grave, dark-bearded son of the local picture restorer, his cart stacked heavy with two great iron-hooped oaken crates crammed full of the old family portraits we’d sent away months prior to scrape a century’s worth of soot and woodsmoke from their canvases. In our isolated mountain hold, any visitor from the tiny nearby capital was a novelty; we all clustered in the entrance hall the moment his boots clomped over the cold stone threshold, hungry for scraps of news from the world beyond our pine woods.

The crates sat heavy and silent against the wall while the servants fed the man his supper, and by the time he trundled back in with chisels, hammers, and screwdrivers, we’d all gathered around the hearth to watch the unpacking. Carmilla slouched low in her velvet armchair, half-lidded eyes gliding over the flames, listless as a cat that had napped the whole afternoon away. One by one, the restorer pried the old portraits free of their linen wrappings: stiff dark canvases almost all of them, heirlooms from my mother’s ancient Hungarian line, my father checking each off against a crumpled list in his hand. Most I’d never properly seen before, so caked with decades of grime they’d been nothing but blurry dark blobs hanging on the walls of unused corridors.

“Ah, there’s one I’ve been half-curious to see,” my father said, tapping his list. “Top left corner, faint name Marcia Karnstein, dated 1698. It was so blackened I could never make out the subject.”

I remembered that small, nearly square unframed canvas, the size of a dinner table leaf, hidden away in a lumber room for as long as I could recall. The restorer fished it out of tissue paper a moment later, holding it aloft with obvious pride.

My breath caught in my throat. It wasn’t just restored. It breathed. The paint looked almost wet, the girl in the portrait smiling soft, like she was just about to step right out of the frame. It was Carmilla. Down to the very curl of her lip, the long dark lashes brushing her cheek, the tiny brown mole half-hidden in the curve of her throat.

“Carmilla, it’s a miracle!” I blurted before I even thought. “It’s you, exactly. Look, Papa—the little mole on her throat, you can’t tell them apart!”

My father laughed, but it didn’t reach his eyes. He glanced away fast, like forcing himself to be casual, and turned back to chat with the restorer about the finer points of the paint work, never lingering on the portrait the way I couldn’t stop myself from. The longer I stared, the more my skin prickled. The girl in the portrait was dressed in 17th century velvet, her hair coiled the exact lazy, unstudied way Carmilla always wore hers.

“Can I hang this in my bedroom, Papa?” I asked.

“Of course, my dear,” he smiled. “If you think it’s that good a likeness, it must be far prettier than I remembered.”

Carmilla didn’t so much as glance at the painting. She was leaning back in her chair, her eyes fixed fully on me, a strange, dreamy rapture softening her face, like she was seeing something no one else could.

I leaned in to squint at the writing in the corner, now perfectly clear, picked out in faded gold leaf, a tiny coronet etched above it. “That’s not Marcia. It says Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, Anno Domini 1698. Mama was descended from the Karnsteins, you know.”

Carmilla’s voice was slow, honey-thick, lazy. “So am I, I think. A very old, very long line. Are there any Karnsteins left living, do you know?”

“None that bear the name, far as I know,” I told her. “The family was ruined in some old civil war centuries ago. Their castle ruins are only three miles out in the woods.”

“Fascinating,” she murmured, not sounding half as interested as she should. She nodded at the hall door, propped half open to the night. “Look at that moonlight. Let’s take a little turn around the courtyard. We can see the river and the road winding down the valley.”

“It’s exactly the same kind of night you arrived here,” I said.

She sighed, a soft, shaking little sound, and stood. We looped our arms around each other’s waists, stepping out onto the cold flagstones. We walked slow, no sound but the faint crunch of our slippers on the gravel at the courtyard edge, out to the old drawbridge where the whole moon-drenched valley spread out below us.

“You were thinking about the night I came?” she whispered, her breath warm against my ear. “Are you glad I’m here?”

“More than glad, dear Carmilla,” I said.

“You asked for that portrait that looks like me, to hang by your bed,” she sighed, her arm tightening so tight around my waist it almost hurt, and she rested her heavy, perfumed head on my shoulder. “You’re so romantic, Carmilla,” I laughed, a little nervous. “I bet all your stories are full of some great old grand romance.”

She kissed me, soft and silent, her lips colder than they should have been.

“I’m certain you’ve been in love before,” I said, my voice wobbly. “That you’ve got some great secret affair going on right now.”

“I’ve never loved anyone,” she breathed right against my neck, her hand sliding into mine, “and I never will. Unless it’s you.”

She was so beautiful in the silver moonlight, her skin pale as bone, no shadow stretching behind her on the flagstones at all. She hid her face in my hair then, heaving great, tumultuous sighs that verged on sobbing, her hand trembling hard in mine, her cheek hot as a fever against mine.

“Darling, darling,” she murmured, “I live in you. You would die for me. I love you so much.”

I jolted back from her, my heart hammering so hard I could hear it in my ears.

Her face was blank. All the light, all the fire, all the warmth was gone. Her eyes were glassy, empty, her skin as colorless as a sheet of parchment.

“Is the air… chill?” she mumbled, drowsy, like she was just waking up from a very deep, very strange dream. “I’m shivering. Did I fall asleep? Let’s go back inside. Come. Come in now.”

“You look unwell, Carmilla,” I said, already nervous, reaching to steady her. “Faint. You must have a glass of warm wine.”

“Yes. That would be nice,” she said, slow, as we trundled back toward the hall door. “I’m fine now. I’ll be myself in a minute. Wait—let’s look out a second more. It might be the last time we see moonlight like this together.”

A cold prickle of fear crawled up my spine. The villagers had been whispering about a strange wasting sickness creeping through the nearby homesteads, making young girls fade and die for no doctor could name. “Papa would be heartbroken if you were sick and we didn’t call for help right away,” I told her. “We have a very skilled physician staying nearby, he saw Papa earlier today—”

“I know how kind you all are,” she cut me off, that lazy little smile back on her face, like nothing had ever happened. “I’m not sick. Just weak, that’s all. People say I’m too languid to exert myself, I can barely walk as far as a three year old. Sometimes all my little strength gives out, and I drift off like that. I bounce right back, see?”

It was true. By the time we got back to the fireside, she was chattering animatedly, laughing at my father’s little jokes, no trace left of that strange fevered moment out by the drawbridge. The rest of the evening slipped by quiet and uneventful, no more of her odd, passionate little fits to make me squirm or frighten me.

But that very night, something happened that turned all my quiet, unspoken worries sharp and new—something that jolted even Carmilla’s endless, sleepy languor into sudden, sharp, alive energy.

When we stepped back into the drawing room after Carmilla’s earlier spell, the fire guttered low behind the brass fender, and the air smelled of burnt coffee and bitter chocolate. Carmilla drank not a drop of either, but she appeared whole again, her colour restored, that soft, sleepy smile tugging at her lips as Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine pulled a card table up to the hearth for a casual evening game. Halfway through their hand, Papa wandered in for what he called his “dish of tea,” a folded local newspaper tucked under his arm, its margins scribbled with reports of the strange wasting sickness creeping through the valley villages.

He settled on the sofa beside Carmilla, his voice tight with quiet anxiety, and asked if she had received word from her mother since arriving at the chateau.
“No,” she said, her tone so soft it nearly got swallowed by the crackle of the fire.
“Then do you know where a letter would find you, now?” he pressed.
“I cannot say for certain,” she answered, vague and distant, her gaze drifting to the dark window panes. “But I have been thinking I ought to leave. You have been far too hospitable, far too kind. I have been nothing but trouble. I mean to hire a carriage tomorrow, to post in pursuit of her. I know where I will find her, eventually, though I dare not tell you where just yet.”
The relief flooded through me when Papa protested at once. “You must not dream of such a thing. We cannot bear to lose you. I will not consent to you leaving us except under the explicit care of your mother, who herself bade you stay here in our company until she returned. I would rest far easier knowing you had word from her, but these new reports of the sickness are growing more alarming by the hour, and the weight of guarding you, without your mother’s guidance, sits heavy on me. But you will not leave. Not without her clear instruction to do so. We would grieve too much to part with you easily.”
“Thank you, sir, a thousand times over,” she murmured, smiling shyly, her eyelashes fluttering low. “I have seldom been so happy in all my long life as I am here, in this beautiful chateau, under your care, and in the company of your dear daughter.” Papa, ever the old-fashioned gentleman, lifted her hand to his lips to kiss it, and I noticed he did not remark that her skin was still as cold and unyielding as polished marble, no pulse beating against his mouth.

I walked her back to her chamber as usual, the hall candles casting shadows that stretched far longer than the walls ought to allow, and sat perched on the edge of her bed to chat while she fumbled her way through preparing for rest.
“Do you think,” I said at last, “that you will ever confide fully in me?”
She turned to face me, still smiling, but said nothing, only held that unblinking, soft smile fixed on my face.
“You won’t answer?” I said, flustered. “I shouldn’t have asked. It was rude of me.”
“You have every right to ask me anything,” she purred. “You do not know how dear you are to me, or you would not think any confidence too great to demand. But I am bound by vows, severer than any nun ever swore. I dare not tell my story yet, not even to you. But the time is very near. You will think me cruel, selfish—but love is always selfish. The more ardent it burns, the more selfish it grows. You cannot fathom how jealous I am of every breath you take that is not for me. You must come with me, loving me, to death; or hate me, and still come with me. Hate me through death, and after. There is no such word as indifference in my apathetic nature.”
“Carmilla, you are talking that wild nonsense again,” I said hastily, a prickle of cold creeping up my spine.
“Not nonsense. For your sake I will speak plainly. Have you ever been to a ball?”
I laughed, confused. “No, never. What is it like? It must be so lovely.”
“I almost forget. It was years and years ago.”
“You cannot be that old,” I teased. “Your first ball cannot be faded from memory yet.”
“I remember every part of it, if I strain hard enough. I see it the way divers see the light through dark rippling water: dense, murky, but just transparent enough to make out shapes. Something happened that night that blurred all the colours, confused the picture. I was nearly assassinated in my bed, wounded right here,” she said, pressing one long, cold finger to the centre of her breast, “and I was never the same after.”
“Were you close to dying?”
“So very close. A cruel, strange love, that wanted to tear my life right out of me. Love always demands sacrifices. No true sacrifice ever comes without blood. Let us sleep now. I feel so very, very lazy. I cannot be bothered to get up and lock my door.”
She curled up on the bed, her tiny hands tucked under her cheek, buried in the mass of her wavy dark hair, and her glittering eyes tracked every step I took across the room, a half-hidden, unreadable smile playing on her lips. I bade her good night, and slipped out the door with a heavy, uncomfortable weight in my chest.

I often found myself wondering if Carmilla ever said her prayers. I had certainly never seen her kneel. She never came down to breakfast until long after our family morning devotions were over, and she never once left the drawing room to join our short evening prayers in the hall. If a passing offhand comment in one of our careless chats had not revealed she had been baptized, I would have doubted she was any kind of Christian at all. She never spoke a single word on the subject of faith. If I had been more worldly, that strange antipathy would not have struck me as so unnatural.

The small, nervous quirks of the people we grow close to rub off on us, after a time. I had adopted Carmilla’s habit of locking my bedroom door tight from the inside, spooked by her endless tales of midnight prowlers and hidden assassins. I had even started doing her little ritual, patrolling under my bed and checking behind the armoire before I settled in, just to make sure no lurking intruder was “ensconced” in my room, just as she always did. With all those precautions taken, I climbed under my sheets, my nightlight burning steady on the bedside table—an old, lifelong habit I would never, ever give up, no matter how silly people called me. I thought that flame would keep me safe.

But dreams do not care about locked doors. They seep through stone walls, snuff out candles or light them of their own accord, drift past every lock and bolt to do as they please, laughing at locksmiths the whole while. The dream I had that night was the very start of a very strange, unending agony. I cannot call it a nightmare, for I was fully conscious that I was asleep. But I was equally certain I was still lying in my own bed, in my own room, exactly where I was when I closed my eyes. I could see my familiar furniture, just as I had left it, only the room had gone far darker than it ought to be, even with the candle flickering on my nightstand. Something was slinking back and forth at the foot of my bed, and at first I could not make out what it was.
Then I saw it: a sooty, pitch-black animal, shaped like a monstrous cat, nearly four or five feet long, stretching the full length of my hearthrug when it padded across it. It moved with the lithe, sinister restlessness of a wild beast trapped in a cage, back arching, no sound coming from its paws. I could not scream. My throat was frozen shut, and I was paralyzed with terror. It paced faster and faster, and the room grew darker and darker, until I could no longer make out the shape of the cat at all—only its two huge, glowing amber eyes, bobbing back and forth in the dark.
I felt it spring light as a feather onto the foot of my bed. The two broad eyes drifted closer, and suddenly I felt a searing stinging pain, as if two giant, sharp needles had been driven deep into my breast, an inch or two apart, right over my heart.
I woke screaming. The nightlight was still burning, bright and steady. Standing right at the foot of my bed, just a little to the right, was a female figure. She wore a loose, dark gown, and her long hair fell thick over her shoulders. She was as still as a carved block of tomb stone. Not a single breath stirred, not the hem of her dress rippled. I stared, frozen, as she seemed to glide across the room, no footsteps sounding on the floorboards, until she was pressed right up against my door. The latch turned slow, silent, on its own, and she slipped out, the door clicking softly shut behind her.
I was suddenly able to move, my lungs dragging in ragged gasps of air. My first thought was that Carmilla had played some cruel trick on me, that I must have forgotten to lock my door. I stumbled over to it, fumbling with the deadbolt, and found it still turned fast, locked on the inside, exactly as I had left it. I was too horrified to twist the bolt and yank it open. I dove back into my bed, dragged all the heavy bedclothes over my head, and lay there, shaking, more dead than alive, until the first pale grey light of dawn seeped through my curtains.

VII. Descending
I cannot possibly put to words the ice-cold, festering horror that coiled in my chest when I thought of that night, even years later. This was no fleeting, dream-born dread that dissolves with the first slant of sunlight through the curtains. It seeped deeper by the hour, settling into the grain of the oak floorboards, the frayed damask of my armchair, the chipped rim of the water jug on my nightstand — as if the apparition had left a greasy, invisible print on every surface it had brushed that night.

The next day, I could not bear to be left alone for even ten seconds. I ached to tell my papa, but two warring fears tied my tongue: one part of me was certain he would laugh it off as a foolish girl’s nightmare, and I could not stomach the thought of that awful, sharp terror being dismissed as a silly jest. The other part feared he would mark me as another victim of the strange, wasting sickness that had been snatching villagers on the edge of our estate for months. I knew I was not ill with that ordinary malady — and papa had been weak with a lingering chest complaint for weeks, I could not bear to rattle him so badly it would send him to bed again.

I clung to the soft, easy company of Madame Perrodon and bright, laughing Mademoiselle Lafontaine all that day. They noticed my dragged steps, my jumping at every small creak of the house’s old bones, and at last I spilled the whole heavy, ghastly story that had been weighing on my chest. Mademoiselle snickered, but Madame Perrodon’s lips thinned, her eyes darkening with unspoken worry.
“Speaking of frights,” Mademoiselle said, still grinning, “that long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla’s bedroom window? Everyone says it’s haunted.”
“Nonsense,” Madame cut in sharply, clearly wanting to turn the conversation away from such ill-timed tales. “Who has been filling your head with such foolishness, child?”
“Martin the groundskeeper,” she replied. “He showed up twice before sunrise last week to fix the old yard gate, and both times he saw the same female figure gliding slow as smoke down that lime avenue.”
“Then he likely saw one of the milkmaids heading out to the river pastures,” Madame huffed.
“Maybe,” Mademoiselle laughed. “But Martin was so pale when he told us, you’d think he’d seen a ghost’s bared teeth. I never saw a grown man shake so bad over nothing.”
“Not a word of this to Carmilla,” I cut in quickly. “She can look right down that walk from her window, and she’s even more of a coward than I am, I think. She’d lock herself in her room for three days.”

Carmilla came down to the drawing room later than usual that day, her dark hair half-loose, her face pale as cream. “I was so frightened last night,” she breathed, the second we were left alone on the settee. “I know I would have met something unspeakable if I hadn’t had that charm I bought from that little hunchback peddler, the one I snapped at so sharply when she came to the door. I dreamed something black and slithering was creeping around the foot of my bed, and I jolted awake gasping, and for whole seconds I swore I saw a tall dark shape looming by my chimney piece. But I fumbled under my pillow for the charm, and the second my fingers brushed the fabric, the shape vanished. I know for certain if I hadn’t had it, it would have lunged for me, throttled me like it did those three poor farm girls we heard the priest bury last month.”

I told her my own encounter then, and her eyes went wide, her hands flying to her mouth. “And did you have the charm near you?” she asked, urgent as anything.
“No,” I admitted. “I left it dropped in that blue china vase on the drawing room mantel. But I swear I’m tucking it under my pillow tonight, if it works half as well as you say.”

Even now, I cannot fathom how I found the courage to lie alone in my room that night. I pinned the little charm ribbon straight to my pillowcase, right next to where my cheek rested, and I fell asleep almost instantly, deeper than I had slept in months. The next night was just as quiet — no shadows, no sounds at the chimney piece. My sleep was heavy, dreamless, soft as sinking into warm, dark silk. But when I woke, there was a heavy, sweet lassitude clinging to my bones, a gentle melancholy that did not hurt at all, almost luxurious in its soft drag.
“I told you it would work,” Carmilla said, when I told her how restful my nights had been. “I pinned mine to the breast of my nightdress, you see — I had it too far away under my pillow the first night. It’s not magic, of course, our doctor told me so. Those wandering fevers that drift through the countryside, they knock at the door of your nerves to get in, and if they can’t force their way through, they leave you with nothing but a bad fright. The charm’s been soaked in some kind of medicinal herb fumigation, that’s all — it’s an antidote to the bad air, the malaria. Evil spirits aren’t scared of bits of ribbon, are they? It’s nothing supernatural, just science, it repels the wandering sickness before it can sink its teeth in.”

I wanted so badly to believe her. I tried to, and the sharp edge of my terror dulled, softened, melted into that strange, sweet languor. For nights on end I slept deep and dreamless, but every morning I woke slower, heavier, like my bones were filled with warm lead. A strange, soft melancholy crept over me, the kind I never wanted to push away. Dim, half-formed thoughts of death fluttered at the edges of my mind, the feeling that I was slowly, gently sinking down, down, and it did not feel like something to fight. It was sad, yes, but the quiet mood it wrapped me in was so achingly sweet my soul leaned into it, unresisting.

I refused to admit I was ill. I would not tell papa, would not let anyone send for the doctor. Carmilla grew more tender, more devoted to me than ever, her long languid stares full of a hungry adoration that made me flinch sometimes, like catching a flash of something mad and feral behind her soft dark eyes. The weaker I grew, the brighter that strange ardor burned in her gaze.

I had no idea I was already deep in the throes of the strangest, most unholy malady any mortal had ever suffered. The early symptoms were so laced with honeyed fascination I did not even mind that I could barely drag myself up the stairs before noon. That fascination grew, swelled, until the horrible began to seep in, mixing with that soft sweetness, darkening, curdling, until it would twist every corner of my life into something unrecognizable.

The first shift was almost pleasant, the very edge of the slope that led straight down to Avernus. Vague, strange sensations began to visit me in my sleep. The first was a cold, lovely thrill, the kind you feel wading into a cool river, moving slow against the pull of the current. It was followed by interminable dreams, so vague I could never recall a single face or place or line of the story when I woke — but they left me breathless, drained, like I had spent hours running for my life through dark woods. Every time I woke, I could still remember being in a place almost completely black, talking to people whose faces I could never make out — especially one low, clear female voice, speaking slow, from far away, that wrapped around me with a solemn, unspeakable fear. Sometimes I felt a soft hand dragging slow down my cheek, my neck. Sometimes warm lips kissed me, soft, lingering, trailing lower, until they pressed right against my throat, and the kiss stayed there. My heart thundered, my breath came fast and shallow, a sobbing rose in my throat that turned to strangulation, until my vision swam and I blacked out completely.

Three weeks had passed since the first night I saw that apparition by my chimney. In the last seven days, the wasting had started to show on my face: my skin was pale as parchment, dark bruised circles bloomed under my wide, dilated eyes, the languor that had lived in my bones for so long was written all over my slack, tired features. Papa asked me over and over if I was unwell, but some stupid, stubborn reserve made me insist I was perfectly fine. In a way, it was true — I felt no sharp pain, no ordinary bodily ache. My sickness felt like it was in my imagination, or my nerves, and no matter how horrible the nights got, I clung to that morbid secrecy, sharing almost none of it. It could not be that terrible sickness the peasants called oupire — no one afflicted with that lasted more than three days, before they wasted away and died. Carmilla complained of bad dreams and low fevers too, but never half as bad as mine. If I had been clear-headed enough to see what was happening to me, I would have begged for help on my knees. But some heavy, unseen narcotic was thrumming through my veins, numbing all my good sense, blurring my perceptions.

Then came the dream that led straight to the oddest discovery of all. One night, instead of that low distant voice I was used to hearing in the dark, I heard another: sweet, tender, and brimming with terror, whispering, “Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin.” At the same instant, a bright light flared to life in my dark bedroom, and I saw Carmilla, standing at the foot of my bed in her white nightgown, bathed from her chin to her bare feet in one great, glistening stain of blood.

I woke screaming, the only thought in my head that Carmilla was being murdered. I sprang out of bed, and my next memory is standing on the lamplit landing, screaming for help. Madame and Mademoiselle came running out of their rooms half-dressed, alarmed out of their wits. They heard the story spilling out of me in gasps, and we all stumbled straight to Carmilla’s door, knocking as hard as we could. No answer. We pounded, we screamed her name, but there was nothing. The door was locked, and all of us were shaking so bad we could barely stand, too frightened to be in the quiet dark for another second. We ran back to my room and rang the bell so long and furiously the metal rope was almost hot under my fingers. Papa’s rooms were on the other side of the castle, too far away to hear all our racket, and none of us had the courage to run down that long dark corridor to fetch him.

The servants came thundering up the stairs at last. I had thrown on my dressing gown and slippers by then, and the other two ladies were similarly clad. When we heard the servants’ voices on the landing, we marched back out, pounded on Carmilla’s door once more to no avail, and I ordered the men to force the lock. They drove their shoulder into it once, twice, and the lock snapped. We all crowded in the doorway, holding our lamps high, and stared into the room. We called her name. No reply. Everything was exactly as we had left it the night before, her gown draped over the chair, her book open on the nightstand, the sheets turned back on her bed.

Carmilla was gone.

The second we burst through the splintered lock of Carmilla’s chamber, the sharp, iron-thick chill of a long-shut crypt curled up our throats, and the riot of shouting, scrambling servants died dead. The room was untouched, save for the splintered wood hanging off its hinges: her silk counterpane still bore the faint indent of a body, but it was ice-cold to the touch, no residual warmth clinging to the sheets.

We slowed, pulses hammering, and dismissed the muttering, fidgeting servants at Mademoiselle’s urging. She reasoned the uproar at the door must have woken Carmilla, sent her panicking to hide in a wardrobe or behind the heavy damask curtains, too frightened to step out while the majordomo and his men tramped through the space. We hunted every shadow then, calling her name until our throats were raw. It was useless.

Perplexity curdled into thin, hot panic. The windows were bolted tight from the inside, dust caked thick on the latches where no hand had disturbed them for weeks. I called out to her, voice cracking, begged her to drop this cruel trick, to step out and end the twisting anxiety gnawing at my ribs. Nothing. I knew she was not in this room, nor in the dressing room adjacent—its door was still locked from our side, the key where we had left it hours before. She could not have slipped past it.

I was utterly, horribly stumped. The old housekeeper’s half-forgotten tale pricked at my mind: secret passages woven through the schloss walls, their exact locations lost to centuries of ghost stories, tunnels that ran down beneath the foundations to the overgrown stream at the edge of the grounds. It was past four o’clock, and the dark outside the glass hung thick as pitch, no hint of dawn bleeding over the pine treetops. I fled down the gallery to spend the rest of the dark hours in Madame’s quarters, never once shaking the prickling feeling that something stepped just out of my line of sight every time I glanced over my shoulder.

Daylight brought no answers. The whole household seethed with agitation come morning, my father at its head. They searched every nook of the chateau: unused attics stacked with rotting lumber, cobwebbed cellars, every closet and cranny on every floor. They tramped the grounds, beat the bramble thickets, ran their hands over every stretch of wainscoting looking for hidden latches. No trace of her. They were even hauling iron drag hooks down to the stream, ready to comb every inch of its dark, slow-moving water. My father was half distracted with despair, sick at the thought of having to break the news of the girl’s disappearance to her highborn, mysterious mother when she returned. My own grief was a different, hollower thing: the faint, throbbing bite mark on my neck ached like it had been scraped raw, and I felt half-empty, as if a piece of me had wandered off with Carmilla in the night.

By one o’clock, there was still no tidings. I ran back up to her chamber, and my breath caught in my throat. She was there, standing at her dressing table, her white lace nightgown floating around her bare ankles as if caught in a draft—though every window in the room was latched shut. Her skin glowed like polished pearl, her dark eyes so wide and dark they seemed to swallow the daylight, and she beckoned me over with a single slim finger, never making a sound.

I flew to her, wild with joy, kissing her cold, unbreathing lips over and over, wrapping my arms around her until my chest ached. I sprinted for the bell pull and rang it so hard the cord snapped clean in two, desperate to call everyone up and ease my father’s distress.
“Carmilla, my dear—what happened to you? We have all been in agony over you,” I gasped. “Where were you? How did you get back here without any of us seeing?”
“Last night was a night of wonders,” she said, her voice soft as silk, no trace of sleep or fatigue.
“For mercy’s sake, explain what you can,” I begged.
“I was fast asleep in my bed just after two,” she said, “both my doors locked tight— the dressing room door, and the one out to the gallery. I slept straight through, no dreams that I recall, and woke only just now, stretched out on the sofa in the dressing room. The door between our two rooms was standing open, and the gallery door was forced. How could this happen without waking me? There must have been such noise, and I wake at the faintest creak of a floorboard. How could someone carry me out of my bed without startling me?”

By then Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and a horde of servants had crowded into the room. Carmilla was swamped with questions, relieved smiles, warm welcomes. She told the exact same story every time, looking as bewildered as the rest of us, no hint of a lie on her face.

My father paced the room, thinking, and I caught a flash of Carmilla’s dark eyes tracking his movements, a sly, sharp glance that vanished the second he turned to look at her. He sent the servants away, Mademoiselle hurried off to fetch valerian and salvolatile for my frayed nerves, and soon only the three of us—my father, Madame, and I—remained with her. He came to her gently, took her hand, and led her to the sofa to sit beside him.
“My dear, will you forgive me if I risk a guess and ask one question?” he said.
“You have every right,” she smiled, soft. “Ask anything. But I know nothing—my whole night is nothing but darkness and confusion. And you know the limits my mother has placed on what I am allowed to speak of, of course.”
“Perfectly, my child. I will not touch the subjects she has asked us to avoid.” He nodded, leaning back. “The whole strange incident comes down to this: you were removed from your locked, bolted chamber, no disturbance waking you, no sign of forced entry from outside. I have a theory to explain it, and one simple question first. Have you ever been known to sleepwalk?”
“Not since I was very small, no.”
“But you did walk in your sleep, as a small child?”
“Yes. My old nurse told me so, many times.”
My father smiled, nodding. “There is our answer. You got up in your sleep, unlocked the gallery door, took the key out of the lock and locked it behind you, wandered off to one of the dozens of unused rooms on this floor, or upstairs or down. This old schloss is crammed full of closets, heavy unused furniture, piles of old lumber—we would need a full week to search every inch of it thoroughly. Do you see?”
“I follow, but not all of it,” she said. “How do you explain me waking on the dressing room sofa? We searched that room twice, top to bottom, last night. There was no one there.”
“You wandered back to it while you were still deep in sleep, after our search was done, and woke all on your own, just as confused as the rest of us.” He laughed, light and relieved. “I wish all mysteries were as harmless as this one. No drugging, no lock tampering, no burglars, no poisoners, no witches—nothing that need alarm any of us for our safety.”

Carmilla was radiant, her cheeks flushed the soft pink of wild roses, that lazy, graceful languor of hers clinging to her every movement. My father sighed, glancing at my own pale face. “I wish my poor Laura looked half as well as you do,” he said.

All our alarms were ended, it seemed. Carmilla was home, safe, back with us. But no one thought to pry at the loose wainscot panel behind the dressing room sofa, the one the old housekeeper had once said hid a passage that ran straight down to the stream bank. No one noticed that her shadow, stretched long on the wall behind her, was coiled and writhing like a sleeping serpent, even as she sat perfectly still. No one thought to check the drag hooks they had left stacked by the water’s edge, until later.

Carmilla had dug her heels in, refusing point-blank to suffer even a lady’s maid to sleep a single night inside her chamber. To head off any repeat of the unaccountable midnight wanderings that had left the household on edge, my father arranged for a strapping serving girl to drag a straw pallet right up against her door, her shoulder pressed to the oak panels so closely no one could slip out without rousing her. The night passed without a sound. No soft scrabbling of a turning lock, no bare feet pattering down the dark corridor.
By dawn, Father had already sent for the old physician without breathing a word of it to me. I had not the faintest suspicion anything was amiss until Madame escorted me to the library that morning, and there I found the grave little white-haired doctor I’d met once before, his spectacles slipping down his thin nose as he waited, his black medical bag sitting heavy on the table beside him.
I told him everything: the creeping cold that slithered up my calves in the dead of night, the weight on my chest that stole my breath, the sharp, stabbing pricks at my throat that felt exactly like two fine needles driving through my skin. The more I spoke, the stonier his face grew. We stood face to face in the deep stone window recess, the fog of the early morning pressing against the glass, and when I fell silent, he leaned his shoulders back against the cold wall, his eyes fixed on mine so intently I flinched—there was sharp concern there, tangled thick with a flicker of unmistakeable, ancient horror.
He stood silent for a full minute, thinking hard, before he asked Madame if he might speak to my father. Father came in smiling, teasing that he expected the doctor to call him an old fool for dragging him all the way out to the schloss, but that smile died fast, fading to a pale shadow when the doctor waved him over to the window recess, his face still grim.
The two of them talked in low, urgent tones, too quiet to carry across the huge, high-ceilinged library, its shelves crammed with leather-bound tomes that had sat untouched for a hundred years. Madame and I hung back at the far end, burning with curiosity, but the deep hollow of the window niche swallowed their voices whole. We could make out nothing but the faint glint of the doctor’s spectacles, the flash of Father’s arm as he gestured sharply, the toe of his boot tapping the stone floor, no single word distinguishable.
When Father finally stepped out of the recess, his face was as white as chalk, his jaw tight, and I could see his hands trembling at his sides.
“Laura, dear, come here. Madame, we won’t need you for a little while,” he called.
A prickle of cold fear skittered down my spine as I walked towards them. I felt weak, yes, drained of all the bright, easy energy I’d had only a few weeks prior, but I did not feel ill. I’d always assumed strength was something you could reclaim with a good night’s sleep, whenever you chose.
Father held out a hand to steady me, his eyes still fixed on the doctor’s. “It is very strange. I scarcely understand it myself. Laura, pay close attention to Doctor Spielsberg, and try to recall everything he asks.”
“That prick like two needles piercing your skin, the night of your first terrible nightmare,” the doctor said, his voice soft but sharp as a scalpel. “Just below your throat, you said. Is there any soreness there, even now?”
“None at all,” I answered.
“Can you point exactly to the spot?”
I touched the place just an inch or two below the edge of my lace collar, the fabric of my morning dress falling thick over it.
“Your father will only lower the neck of your dress a very little way, I promise,” the doctor said. “It is absolutely necessary, to confirm the symptom of the complaint that has been wearing you down.”
I nodded, acquiescing. I felt Father’s fingers brush my neck as he pulled the fabric down, and he gasped so loudly I jumped back.
“God in heaven—there it is,” he breathed, his face going paler still.
“You see it for yourself now,” the doctor said, a grim, terrible satisfaction in his tone. “A small blue bruise, no bigger than the tip of your little finger, faint at the edges, two tiny pinpricks visible dead at its centre.”
A jolt of pure terror shot through me. “What is it? What’s wrong with me?”
“Nothing that cannot be set right, my dear girl,” the doctor said quickly. “I do not see any reason you should not start recovering immediately. That spot, that is the very point where the choking sensation begins, is it not?”
I nodded.
“And that cold, thrumming feeling you described, like a current of ice-cold water pressing against your skin—was it centred here, too?”
“I think it was,” I whispered.
The doctor turned back to my father, his eyebrows raised. “Now, Madame, if you would step over. My young patient is far more unwell than she knows. It is not fatal, not if we act quickly, but there are steps we must take at once. The first, and most non-negotiable: you must not leave Miss Laura alone for a single moment. Not to fetch a book from the next room, not to light a candle, not even to step outside for a breath of air. She is never to be unwatched.”
Madame assured him she would obey, without a single question. Father added his own thanks, then turned to me. “You will follow the doctor’s instructions, won’t you, my love?”
“Doctor, there is another guest in our house,” Father went on, turning back to the physician. “A young lady, our Carmilla. Her symptoms are far milder, but they are the very same, I would swear to it. You said you would be passing this way again this evening—stay for supper at seven, and you can examine her then. She does not rise and come downstairs until the afternoon.”
The doctor agreed, repeating his strict orders that I was never to be left unaccompanied, before he and Father walked out together, pacing the grassy stretch between the schloss moat and the forest road, heads bowed deep in conversation. I watched him mount his horse a few minutes later, tip his hat, and ride away east into the trees.
Almost at once, our post boy rode up from Dranfield, swinging his leather post bag down from his saddle to hand it to Father.
Madame and I spent the rest of the hour spinning wild theories, none of them touching the truth. She was convinced the doctor feared I would have a sudden, fatal seizure, that no one would be there to save me if I was left alone. I, for my part, thought the orders were only to keep me from overexerting myself, or eating unripe berries from the orchard, the kind of foolish mischief girls my age were always liable to get up to.
Half an hour later, Father came back into the parlour, a crumpled open letter in his hand. “This was delayed on the road,” he said. “It’s from General Spielsdorf. He might have been here yesterday, he might not arrive until tomorrow… he could turn up at any moment now.”
He pushed the letter into my hand, but he was not smiling the way he always did when our dear old friend the General came to visit. He looked, in fact, as if he wished the man was a thousand miles away, sunk to the bottom of the Red Sea. There was something dark and heavy weighing on his mind, something he would not speak of.
“Papa,” I said, laying my hand on his arm, looking up at him as pleadingly as I could. “Is the doctor right? Am I very ill?”
He smoothed my hair back from my face, his touch gentle but distracted. “No, my love. If we do all the right things, you will be well, fully on the road to recovery in a day or two. I only wish the General had chosen any other time to visit. I wish you were well enough to welcome him without strain.”
“Then tell me what is wrong with me,” I begged.
“Stop pestering me with questions,” he snapped, sharper than I had ever heard him speak to me. When he saw my face fall, hurt, he kissed my forehead, softening. “You will know everything, everything that I know, in a day or two. Don’t trouble your pretty head about it for now.”
He turned to leave, then popped his head back in the door to say he was driving out to Karnstein, the ruined village and the old castle, to call on the priest who lived near those abandoned grounds. He told me and Madame to come with him, and said Carmilla could join us later with the governess, who would bring out a picnic hamper to set up in the ruins of the old Karnstein keep.
By twelve o’clock I was ready, and the three of us set off in the carriage, crossing the drawbridge, turning right over the steep old Gothic bridge, heading west towards the overgrown woods that swallowed the path to Karnstein. There could be no lovelier drive. The road wound over soft, rolling hills and hollows thick with unpruned old wood, no formal straight lines, no sign that human hands had ever tended the trees, curving around the sides of mossy hollows and steep slopes, turning so many unexpected bends I never knew what I would see around the next turn.
We rounded one sharp bend, and there he was: General Spielsdorf, riding towards us, his mounted servant trailing behind him, his portmanteaus loaded in a hired cart trundling a little way behind. He dismounted the second we pulled the carriage to a stop, and after we exchanged greetings, he agreed easily to take the empty seat beside us, sending his horse and his servant ahead to the schloss.

X. Bereaved
Ten months had stretched thin since we last laid eyes on the General, and that span had carved years of raw ruin into his face. He had wasted away to nothing, his frame sharp as a splintered grave stake, all the warm, cordial serenity that once defined his soldierly ease eaten away by a smothering cloak of gloom and a ragged, unblinking anxiety. His dark blue eyes, always sharp, now gleamed with a hard, feral light beneath shaggy, matted grey brows, as if he had spent months staring at something no human was ever meant to witness. The change was not the soft, hollow hollowing that grief alone brings – there was a fire in his bones, a fury that had gnawed through every last scrap of his old disbelief, eating him alive from the inside out.

We had barely gotten the carriage rolling again when the General spoke, blunt and unflinching as he always was, naming the loss that had gutted him: the death of his beloved niece, his ward, his whole world. Then his voice snapped, cracking with a bitterness so sharp it made the air in the cold carriage taste like iron, and he raged against the “hellish arts” that had devoured her. He snarled, more furious than pious, how he could not fathom why Heaven would suffer such monstrous cruelty, such unbridled lust and malignity from the pits of hell to run free unpunished.

My father, quick to see that something far beyond ordinary grief had befallen him, asked – soft, careful, not to prod a raw wound – if he could bear to lay out the circumstances that justified such ferocious language.

“I would tell you every last bit of it gladly,” the General bit out, “but you will not believe me.”

“Why ever would I not?” my father asked.

“Because,” he snapped, testy as a man who has spent weeks fighting something that will not die, “you only believe what fits neatly inside your little mundane rules, your pretty little illusions. I was once like you. I know. But I have learned better.”

“Try me,” my father said. “I am no such stubborn dogmatist as you assume. And I know you better than that – you never credit anything without hard proof. That makes me far more inclined to take what you say with the utmost seriousness.”

“You are right that I did not stumble into belief in the unholy marvels I have seen lightly,” the General said, his voice dropping to a growl, eyes fixed on the dark, overgrown glades of the wood unspooling ahead of the carriage. “What I have lived through is unholy. I was forced by evidence I could not dismiss, no matter how hard I tried, to accept something that blew every last one of my long-held theories to ash. I was made the fool, the prey, of a preternatural conspiracy.”

I saw my father glance at him then, a flicker of quiet worry, suspicion that the old soldier had snapped under the weight of his grief – until the General spoke again, not looking at either of us, his shoulders hunched like he was listening for footsteps in the trees behind us.

“You are headed for the Karnstein Ruins, are you not?” he said.

“Yes – what a strange coincidence,” my father said. “I was about to ask you to turn the carriage that way, so we might explore them. I have a very specific reason to go. There is a ruined chapel there, is there not? Crammed full of the tombs of that long-dead family?”

“Very much so – remarkably atmospheric, fascinating ruins,” my father said, smiling a little. “I do hope you are not thinking of staking a claim to the old Karnstein title and estates?”

He spoke lightly, joking, but the General did not so much as twitch a smile, did not honor the jest with even a huff of amusement. He only looked grimmer, fiercer, his jaw working as he chewed over a thought that made his knuckles white where he gripped the edge of his seat.

“Something far different than that,” he rumbled, gruff as stones grinding together. “I mean to dig some of those fine people up. With God’s grace, I will commit a pious sacrilege here – rid this earth of the monsters that skulk beneath those tombs, so that honest folk can sleep in their beds without being set upon by blood-drinking murderers. I have stories to tell you, old friend, that I would have laughed out of the room as impossible a mere few months ago.”

My father’s smile died on his lips. The look he turned on the General now was not suspicion of madness – it was sharp, bright with a cold, rising alarm, the kind that settles in your bones when you remember the old, half-forgotten vampire tales your dead wife whispered to you on moonlit nights, when the wind howls down from the abandoned Karnstein valley.

“The House of Karnstein has been extinct for a hundred years at the very least,” he said, quiet. “My own wife was maternally descended from that line. The name, the title, everything is long gone. The castle is a heap of rubble, the very village deserted – not a single wisp of chimney smoke has been seen there for fifty years. Not a roof left standing, anywhere.”

“Quite true,” the General said. “I have heard every tale there is to hear of it since last we met. Tales that will turn your blood to ice. But I will lay everything out for you, in the exact order it happened.” His voice softened, just a little, the rage ebbing for a second to make way for raw, bleeding grief. “You knew my dear ward – my child, I called her. No creature alive was more beautiful. Three months ago, she was blooming, bright as a rose in summer.”

“Yes, poor girl,” my father said, his voice gentle now. “The last time I saw her, she was so lovely I could scarce take my eyes off her. I was grieved more than I can say when I heard. I knew what a blow it must be for you.”

He reached over and took the General’s calloused soldier’s hand, and they exchanged a soft, heavy pressure. Tears welled in the old man’s eyes, and he did not even try to wipe them away.

“We have been friends for decades,” he said, thick with tears. “I knew you would grieve for me. I have no children of my own. She was my whole world. Her laughter was the only thing that made my empty house feel like a home. All of that is gone. I have not many years left to me on this earth. But before I die, with God’s mercy, I will do a service to every living soul in this valley. I will deliver Heaven’s vengeance on the fiends who murdered my sweet child, cut down in the very spring of her youth, her beauty, all the hope she had left.”

“You said you would tell me the story exactly as it happened,” my father pressed, leaning forward in his seat. “Please. It is not mere curiosity that makes me ask.”

By then we had reached the fork in the road, the rutted Drunstall path the General had ridden down branching off the lane we had been following towards the Karnstein valley. The General leaned forward, staring hard down the dark empty road that stretched ahead, the ruins half a league away, hidden somewhere behind that wall of twisted pine.

“How much farther to the ruins?” he asked, his voice tight, anxious, as if he could hear the monsters buried there calling his name.

“Half a league, no more,” my father answered. “Tell us the story you promised us. We are listening.”

“With all my heart,” the General said, the words wrenched out of him like he was prying shards of broken glass from his own chest. He paused long enough that his knuckles blanched where he gripped the oak arm of his chair, his breath hitching, sorting through the tangled, half-buried memory he had spent decades trying to smother. It was the strangest, most uncanny tale I ever heard told.
“My dear child had been giddy for weeks, counting down the days to the visit you had arranged for her to your charming daughter,” he said, bowing to my father with a gallantry that was frayed at the edges, heavy with old unwept grief. “In the interim, we received an invitation from my old friend Count Carlsfeld, whose schloss sits six leagues on the far side of Karnstein. The fete he was throwing, you will recall, was in honor of his illustrious visitor, Grand Duke Charles.”
“Indeed, and all accounts say it was splendid beyond measure,” my father said.
“Princely! His hospitality was regal, as if he had rubbed Aladdin’s very lamp to conjure the whole thing. The night that my sorrow began was a grand masquerade. The entire grounds were thrown open, every bough strung with colored lamps that stained the leaves red as fresh blood. There was a fireworks display so grand even Paris would have gaped at it, leaving acrid smoke hanging in the air that reeked of burnt hair. And the music—music is my great weakness, you know—ravishing, perfect, so clear and sweet it could not possibly have come from human throats or hands. The finest opera singers in Europe, the most celebrated instrumental band alive, and their voices would steal out of the dark silence of an oak grove or rise unbidden from the boats drifting on the lake, so that I half felt I had stepped straight into a romance from my youth, bewitched.
“When the fireworks faded and the ball commenced, we filed back into the gilded suite of rooms opened for the dancers. A masked ball is always a beautiful sight, but I never saw one so dazzling, so packed with lords and ladies of rank, that I was almost the only common ‘nobody’ in attendance.
“My poor girl looked radiant, no mask on her face, her excitement lending her usual soft loveliness an edge of bright magic. That was when I noticed her: a young lady, dressed in rich deep silk, masked in black velvet, staring at my ward with a fixed, hungry intensity no polite guest should ever display. I had spotted her earlier that evening in the great hall, then again on the terrace beneath the castle windows, hovering a few feet from us, her gaze still pinned to my child. A stately older lady, masked, draped in dark unadorned silk with not a single jewel to catch the light, followed her as chaperon, carrying herself like a royal. Had the younger girl not worn a mask, I would have marked her interest sooner. I have no doubt now she planned every second of it.
“We drifted to a smaller salon after a few dances. My dear girl was breathless, and she sank into a chair near the door to rest, and I stood at her side. The two masked women made their way over. The younger one slid straight into the empty seat next to my ward, so close their knees brushed, while her chaperon stepped up beside me, and spoke to her charge in a low, murmurous tone for a minute.
“Then she turned to me, leaning on the privilege of her mask, and called me by my name like we were old, old friends. She referenced scenes I had not thought of in forty years: court receptions I’d nearly forgotten, a silly drunken duel I’d never breathed a word of to anyone, a summer romance in the Tyrol I’d buried. Every memory she named jumped bright and sharp to the front of my mind, as if she had been there, watching.
“Every second I grew more frantic to know who she was. She parried every question, adroit and amused, toying with me like a cat with a stunned mouse, laughing softly as I stumbled from one wild guess to the next.
“All the while, the younger girl—her mother called her Millarca, once or twice, a name I could not place, odd and ancient—had struck up an easy, charming conversation with my ward. She introduced herself by saying her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine, joked that a mask gave you leave to be bold, admired my girl’s dress, complimented her beauty so prettily my child blushed bright pink. She mocked the over-dressed fools crowding the ballroom with such sharp wit, my girl was cackling in minutes, leaning in to her like she was a long-lost sister. After a little while, she slipped her mask down, and her face was so stunningly lovely it made my skin prickle cold. I had never seen her before, nor had my ward, but her features felt familiar—like the carved face on a broken, weathered tombstone I had once stumbled on in the overgrown Karnstein graveyard. My poor girl was utterly smitten in that first minute, and it was clear the stranger was just as taken with her.
“I laughed, leaning into the masquerade’s light, and pressed the older lady again. ‘You have puzzled me utterly,’ I said. ‘Is that not enough? Won’t you do me the kindness to remove your mask, let us meet as equals?’
“‘What an unreasonable request,’ she purred. ‘Ask a lady to give up an advantage! Besides, how do you know you would recognize me? Years change all things.’
“‘As you see,’ I said, bowing, with a bitter little laugh.
“‘As the philosophers say,’ she replied. ‘And how do you know seeing my face would help you at all?’
“‘I will take that chance,’ I told her. ‘It is useless pretending you are old. Your figure betrays you.’
“‘Many years have passed since you last saw me,’ she said, her tone dropping soft. ‘Millarca here is my daughter. I cannot be young, not to anyone who knows how time eats at faces. I do not wish to be compared to the girl you remember. You have no mask to remove. You offer me nothing in trade.’
“‘I beg you out of pity, show me your face.’
“‘And I beg you out of yours, let it stay where it is,’ she said.
“‘Fine, then at least tell me if you are French or German. You speak both tongues so perfectly I cannot tell.’
“‘I think I will not tell you that, General. You are planning some trick, working out what question to ask next.’
“‘At all events, you will not deny me the right to know how to address you. Shall I call you Madame la Comtesse?’
“She laughed, and I am sure she would have evaded me again—if any part of that night was not pre-planned, every detail calculated with a cunning so deep I could not fathom it then. She parted her lips to answer, and never got a word out. A man in plain black evening dress, no masquerade costume at all, stepped up to us. He was elegant, distinguished, but his face was the color of cold corpse wax, his lips ashen, no spark of life in his flat dark eyes. He bowed low, courtly, no trace of a smile.
“‘Will Madame la Comtesse permit me a very few words that may be of great interest to her?’ he said.
“The masked woman turned to him so fast her silk skirts swirled, pressed one finger to her lips to signal silence, then turned back to me. ‘Keep my place for me, General. I shall return the moment I have finished speaking with him.’
“She walked off with him, the pair moving slowly through the glittering crowd, speaking too quietly for me to overhear, until they vanished from sight.
“I stood there, wracking my brains to place her, half ready to turn and join my ward and Millarca, certain I could dig up her name and her home before she came back. Just then, the pale man in black returned, alone.
“‘I shall come back and inform Madame la Comtesse the moment her carriage is at the door,’ he said, and bowed again, and withdrew.
“I never saw either of those two women again that night. Not until it was far, far too late.”

The gaslight of the grand ducal ball gilded the edges of every shadow, smothering the room in a thick, perfumed heat as I bowed low to the masked Countess. “Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse only for a few hours, I hope?”

Her laugh was low, thrumming like something ancient moving deep under still water. “It may be only hours. It may be weeks. That meddling fool who accosted me by the pillar ten minutes ago has ruined all our plans, unlucky as sin. Do you know who I am, now?”

I assured her I did not.

“Ah, but you shall,” she said, leaning in, her voice dropping so no one else could overhear, “but not yet. We are far older, better friends than you suspect. Old enough that I carry a thousand soft, sweet recollections of you that I have never let slip. Three weeks from now, I will ride past your beautiful schloss, whose location I have been making quiet enquiries after, and I shall knock for an hour or two, to renew that friendship. But a piece of news struck me like a thunderbolt this very minute. I must leave at once, take a winding dark road nearly a hundred miles, ride day and night as fast as my horses can carry me. My troubles are piling up, and the only thing stopping me from laying a very strange request at your feet is the hard rule I keep about never speaking my name.”
She nodded toward the quiet, dark-eyed young woman lingering by the pillar, her daughter Millarca. “My poor child never fully recovered her strength after her horse fell at a hunt she rode out to watch. Her nerves still jangle with the shock, and our doctor has forbidden all exertion for months. We traveled here at a crawl, barely six leagues a day. Now I must rush off on a mission of life and death — one that will explain every last secret, I promise you, when we meet again in three weeks.”

She laid out her petition next, in a tone that did not beg for a favor so much as grant me one, entirely unconsciously, as if she was royal and I a vassal. Her words themselves were soft, deprecatory, almost humble: all she asked was that I take Millarca into my home for the weeks she was gone.

It was an audacious, almost unthinkable ask. But she disarmed me entirely, naming every possible objection myself, laying all the strangeness of it bare, throwing herself unreservedly on my chivalry. And as if fate had preordained the whole thing, my own dear ward slipped to my side at that very second, whispering in my ear that she had already struck up a friendship with Millarca, and begged me to invite her home. The girl would be overjoyed.

There was no time to think. The two of them pressed me together, Millarca’s face so uncannily beautiful, her bearing so steeped in the unplaceable grace of old high birth, that I caved entirely, far too easily, and agreed to take charge of the girl the Countess called Millarca — a girl whose last name I had never even heard.

The Countess beckoned her daughter over, and Millarca listened with grave, unblinking attention as her mother laid out the sudden summons, the arrangement she had made, adding that I was one of her oldest, most trusted friends. I murmured all the expected polite platitudes, and even as I spoke, a cold prickle crept up the back of my neck: I had landed myself in a position I already hated, blind to every detail of the strangers I had just welcomed into my orbit.

That pale, silent man in black who had hung in the Countess’s shadow all evening returned, and escorted her from the room with such overwrought, deep ceremony that I knew she was no minor noble, no casual visiting aristocrat. Her last charge to me, spoken soft as she turned to go, was that I make no attempt to dig into her identity, not until she returned. “Neither I nor my daughter could have stayed here a single extra day. I slipped my mask for half a careless hour an hour ago, and feared you had seen me then — I would have begged you on your honor to keep my secret for weeks, if you had. As it is, I think you saw nothing. But if you ever guess, in the days ahead, who I am, I trust entirely to your honor to hold your tongue. Remind Millarca now and then, too, lest she thoughtlessly blurt it out.”

She whispered a few sharp, fast words in her daughter’s ear, kissed her twice quick, like a nip to the cheek, and vanished into the crowd, the pale man gliding at her heels.

“There is a window in the next room that looks out on the hall door,” Millarca said, her voice soft, almost plaintive. “I want to see the last of her, blow her one last kiss goodbye.”

We went with her. Down below, a great carved black carriage sat waiting, no crests painted on its panels, a troop of dark-cloaked couriers mounted around it. The pale man held up a thick velvet cloak, wrapped it around the Countess’s shoulders, and pulled the hood down so far it swallowed her entire face. She touched his hand for half a second, nodded, and he bowed so low his forehead nearly brushed the cobblestones before the carriage door slammed, and the horses rumbled off, no lanterns lit along its sides.

“She’s gone,” Millarca sighed.

I repeated the words to myself, and that was the first moment, in the rush of all that had happened, that I realized what a complete fool I had been to say yes.

“She never even looked up at the window,” the young woman said, her tone hurt.

“Your mother must have removed her mask,” I said, to soothe her. “She did not want anyone to catch a glimpse of her face, and never could have known you were watching.”

She turned those great dark eyes on me, and her beauty was so arresting that my misgivings melted at once. I felt guilty for the unspoken, ungenerous doubt I had nursed, and resolved to make her stay as warm and welcome as I possibly could, to make amends.

She slipped her half-mask back on, and persuaded my ward and I to return to the terrace, where the open-air concert was set to resume. We walked back and forth under the castle windows, and Millarca grew quickly intimate with us both, regaling us with sharp, light, unmalicious stories of every great noble strolling past. I had been cooped up in my isolated schloss for so long, far from the world of high society, that she was delight itself. I thought of how she would chase all the quiet loneliness out of our long, dull evenings at home.

The ball dragged on until the sun was nearly peeking over the horizon. The Grand Duke had insisted on dancing until dawn, and no loyal guest dared leave, no one dared even think of bed.

We had just pushed through a sweltering, thronged saloon when my ward turned to me, wide-eyed, and asked where Millarca had gone. I had thought the girl was at her side. She had thought Millarca was at mine.

She was gone.

I tore through every room, every empty antechamber, every shadowed corner of the vast grounds, searching for her, and found nothing. It was then the full weight of my idiocy crashed down on me: I was responsible for a young woman whose name I did not even know, bound by a stupid, undefined promise not to describe her missing mother or frame my enquiries in any way that might turn up answers.

Daylight broke pale and grey, and I gave up the search. It was not until nearly two o’clock the next afternoon that a servant knocked on my niece’s door, to say a young lady in great evident distress was asking for General Baron Spielsdorf, and the daughter who had been left to care for her.

It was Millarca, of course. God in heaven, I wish we had never found her.

She wove us a soft, tearful story: she had gotten separated in the crowd, wandered hopelessly lost for hours, stumbled at last on the housekeeper’s private bedroom, and fallen into a deep, dead sleep so long it had barely restored her after the exhaustion of the ball. That same day, we brought her home to our schloss. I was absurdly, stupidly glad to have secured such a charming, gentle companion for my dear girl. I had no idea what kind of hungry, old thing I had just invited across my threshold.

It wasn’t long before the cracks in Millarca’s facade began to show, sharp and unignorable as thorns pushing through graveyard loam.
First came that dragging, endless languor—the leftover “weakness” from her supposed illness that kept her locked in her room until the sun was dipping low over the treetops. Then came the impossible discovery: though she always turned the key in her lock from the inside, and never moved it an inch until the maid called to help her dress, she was repeatedly spotted slipping through the schloss grounds in the pale, ash-grey first light of dawn, barefoot, moving east through the oaks like one half-dead, caught in a trance. I thought at first it was sleepwalking—but that answer did not fit the riddle no one could untangle: how did she slip out of her locked chamber without unbarring a single door or window to the rest of the house?
I had no time to puzzle it out before a far sharper, screaming anxiety coiled around my throat. My poor Laura, the old General told us, his voice thick with mingled grief and rage, was fading. The bloom drained from her cheeks, the light from her eyes, wasting away in a way no doctor could name, no tonic touch. It started with nightmares so appalling she woke screaming, unable to draw breath. Then she swore a specter haunted her bedroom, some nights looking just like Millarca, other nights a shapeless hulk of fur, creeping slow in circles around the foot of her bed. Then came the strange sensations: first a trickle of something icy, like glacial melt, dragging slow across the skin of her breast, not quite painful, but so wrong it made her teeth ache. Then a pair of burning, needle-sharp pricks, just below her throat, stabbing deep enough to leave her gasping. After that came the slow, choking, convulsive sense of being strangled, until the dark swallowed her whole.

Every word the General spoke landed in my chest like a shard of cold glass. We were driving over the short, springy grass that lined the road to that roofless, long-abandoned village—one that had not breathed a single wisp of chimney smoke for more than fifty years—and I recognized every single symptom he described as my own. The sickness eating me alive, the shadow that haunted my bed, the stranger who sat beneath my roof smiling soft and sweet while she feasted on my life: it was Carmilla, every strange, vicious quirk of her, laid bare.
The forest parted suddenly, and we were staring up at the jagged chimneys and half-collapsed gables of the dead village, the broken battlements of the old Karnstein castle rising from the low eminence above it, girdled by ancient trees so tall they blotted out half the sky. I stumbled off the carriage like I was still trapped in a nightmare, none of us speaking a word as we climbed the path up to the ruins, our boots crunching over fallen stone and years of dead leaves, through vast empty chambers, winding staircases draped in cobwebs thick as shrouds, corridors so dark the noon sun barely pierced them.
“This was once the palace of the Karnsteins,” the General said at last, leaning against a gaping window frame, staring out over the rolling miles of forest below. “It was a cursed line. Their annals are written in blood, right here on these very walls. It is a cruelty beyond measure that they still plague the living, a century after their deaths, driven by the same ravenous, atrocious lusts.” He pointed down the steep slope, through the tangles of ivy, to the grey, crumbling walls of a half-buried Gothic chapel. “That is the Karnstein family chapel, down there. And I hear a woodman’s axe, ringing through the trees around it. He may know what I need: the location of the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. The villagers still hold the old local tales, long after the rich noble families forgot their own buried skeletons once their lines die out.”

“We have a portrait of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein at home,” my father put in. “Would you care to see it?”
“Plenty of time for that, my friend,” the General said. “I swear I have seen the real woman, walking my halls in my own home. That is why I came to you earlier than I planned: to find this chapel, and what lies beneath it.”
“See Mircalla?” My father blinked, bewildered. “But she has been dead for more than a hundred years!”
“Not half as dead as you think,” the General growled.
“General, you are making no sense,” my father said, and I saw a flicker of that old, unspoken suspicion he had held of the old man a few days prior. But there was no madness in his face, only white-hot, unshakable fury.
“I have only one purpose left in the few years I have left on this earth,” he said, as we stepped under the heavy stone arch of the old chapel, its dimensions grand enough to count as a full church. “I will have my vengeance, and thank God, a mortal hand can still deliver it.”
“What vengeance do you speak of?” my father asked, horrified.
“To cut the monster’s head from her shoulders,” the General roared, face flushing dark red, his boot slamming down on the stone floor so hard the echo boomed and bounced off the hollow walls. He curled his fist, shaking it in the air like he already held the sharp edge of an axe, swinging it at an invisible enemy. “With a hatchet, a spade, any blade sharp enough to cleave through her murderous throat. I will tell you the rest.” He hurried forward, gesturing to a thick squared block of oak half-buried in the grass growing through the chapel’s cracked pavement. “Sit, child, you are worn thin. I will finish this dreadful tale in a few words.”

I sank down on that rough wood bench, my bones aching, as the General called out to the woodman. The old man stepped out from behind a gnarled oak, axe slung over his shoulder, his face crisscrossed with years of sun and scar tissue, his boots caked in dark loam that looked almost black. He told us he knew nothing of the chapel’s old graves, but there was a forest ranger, nearly as old as the trees, staying at the priest’s cottage two miles out, who could point to every last Karnstein monument. For a few coins, he said, he would borrow one of our horses and be back with the ranger in half an hour, no more.
“Have you worked these woods long?” my father asked him.
“All my life,” the woodman said, his voice thick with the local patois. “My father before me, his father before him, back as many generations as I can name. I can show you the very cottage in that dead village down there where all my people lived, once.”
“Why was the village abandoned?” the General asked.
“Revenants, sir.” The woodman’s face went tight, pale under his tanned skin. “We tracked a dozen of them to their graves, tested them the old way, drove stakes through them, cut off their heads, burned the remains—but not before half the village was dead. Even after that, the torment did not stop. Then a Moravian nobleman, traveling through, heard what was happening. His people know all these old tricks. He climbed the chapel steeple one night, right after the sun went down, when the moon was full, and he watched the churchyard below. He saw the revenant climb out of its grave, lay the linen winding sheet it was buried in on the grass beside the stone, and glide off to the village to kill. The stranger snuck down, grabbed that winding sheet, and climbed back to the top of the steeple. When the revenant came back and found its clothes gone, it roared so loud the windows rattled, and the Moravian called down, waving the linen, telling the monster to come up and get it back. It climbed the steeple, leaving black, oily handprints all over the stone, and the second its head cleared the battlements, the Moravian swung his sword and split its skull clean in two. He threw the body down into the churchyard, climbed back down, cut its head off, and the next day the villagers impaled what was left and burned every last ash. That same Moravian had the authority of the then-head of the Karnstein family to dig up Mircalla’s tomb, too, and he hid it so well no one has found it since.”
“Can you point to where it stood?” the General leaned forward, voice sharp with urgency.
The woodman shook his head, smiling a grim, thin smile. “Not a soul alive in this valley can tell you that now. They say her body was moved, but no one living knows for sure.”
He slung his axe over his shoulder, turned, and vanished into the trees, leaving the three of us alone in the cold, still chapel ruin, the only sound the faint sigh of wind through broken glass, and the unshakable feeling that eyes were watching us, from somewhere out in the dark, overgrown graveyard.

The General’s knuckles blanched white where he clamped down on the carved head of his walking stick, cold graveyard moss seeping through his breeches as he leaned in, his voice frayed thin with decades-old grief. “My beloved ward was wasting away by the hour. The local physician bled her, dosed her, and every symptom grew worse: dark bruise-like circles bloomed under her eyes no matter how much she slept, she woke screaming at dawn gasping that something cold had fastened to her throat, two tiny pricks oozing slow, thick blood he could not explain. He saw I was half mad with fear, and suggested a consultation with a more learned man from Gratz.

It was three days before that old physician arrived, pious and sharp, his coat dusted with road grime. The two men examined my girl together, then withdrew to my library to confer, leaving me pacing the next room, waiting. Their voices rose, not in calm debate, but in something sharp, ugly: the local doctor cackling, snorting, sneering at the older man’s theory. I banged on the door and stepped in, and the laughter died on his lips.
“Sir,” the local physician said, nodding mockingly at his peer, “my learned friend seems to think you need a conjuror, not a doctor.”
“Pardon me,” the Gratz doctor said, his face tight with quiet fury, “I will state my case properly elsewhere. I grieve, General, that my science can do nothing for your child. But before I leave, I must write you something that cannot wait.”

He sat at the table, scribbling furiously. When I turned to leave, the other doctor jerked his thumb over his shoulder at the writing man, tapped his own forehead with a smirk, and shrugged. The consultation had left me no further ahead than I started. I wandered out into the overgrown park, half distracted, ready to scream, when the old Gratz physician caught up with me ten minutes later. He apologized for following, said he could not in good conscience leave before he warned me.
“There is no natural disease that matches her symptoms,” he said, his voice low, urgent. “She has a day, maybe two, left to live. One more visit from what haunts her, and the last spark of her life will be snuffed out forever.”
“What visit?” I begged him.
“I have written every detail in this note,” he said, pressing the sealed paper into my hand. “You are not to open it until the nearest clergyman stands at your side. If you read it alone, you will think me a madman, and this is a matter of life and death. If the priest is away, then you may break the seal.”

Before he left, he urged me to send for a man he knew, a scholar who had spent his life studying exactly the strange affliction laid out in that letter, and pressed me to waste no time. But the priest was off visiting a dying parishioner, miles away. I broke the seal alone. Any other day, any other circumstance, I would have laughed the letter off as the raving of a lunatic. But when every cure has failed, and the life of the person you love most is hanging by a thread, you grasp at even the most impossible straws.

The letter said she was being preyed on by a vampire. Those two tiny pricks at her throat? The marks of the creature’s long, sharp fangs. The livid, bruised ring around the wounds? The trace of the demon’s lips. Every one of her waking nightmares, every lurch of her fading pulse, matched every recorded case of vampire visitation to the letter. I still did not believe such a thing could exist. I thought the good old doctor had gone senile, that some odd hallucination had warped his years of learning. But I could not do nothing. I followed his instructions to the letter.

I hid in the dark dressing room that opened off her bedchamber, a single tallow candle guttering on her nightstand. My sword lay on the small table beside my post, just as the note ordered. I peered through the crack in the door, watched her fall asleep, until just after one in the morning, I saw it: a great, shapeless black mass, crawling slow over the foot of her bed, oozing upward until it spread over her throat, swelling into a great, throbbing, palpitating lump, as if it was feeding on her very life. I stood petrified for a long, cold heartbeat. Then I burst through the door, sword raised high. The black creature recoiled, fast as a striking snake, glided off the bed, and stood on the stone floor a yard from her bed foot. It was Millarca. Her eyes burned with that skulking, feral, inhuman glare, fixed right on me. I swung my sword with all my strength. The blade passed right through empty air. She was standing by the bedroom door, unharmed, a smile tugging at her lips. I screamed, lunged again. She was gone. My sword shattered against the oak door in a spray of steel splinters.

I cannot tell you the full horror of that night. The whole house woke, servants stumbling through the halls with lanterns, screaming. The specter Millarca was nowhere to be found. But my ward was sinking fast. Before the first pale streak of dawn painted the sky, she was dead.”

The General fell silent, his shoulders heaving. None of us spoke. My father wandered a few paces off, squinting at the moss-choked inscriptions carved into the weathered tombstones, before stepping through the arch of a side chapel to read the carvings on the old sarcophagi inside. The General leaned against the cracked base of a broken monument, wiping tears from his face with a shaking hand, and sighed so heavy it sounded like a death rattle.

I thought I heard Carmilla and Madame’s voices approaching from the path, but the sound died away before they came into view. The graveyard was wrapped in thick, oppressive shadow, the oak and ivy canopy so dense not a sliver of sun touched the flagstones under our feet. The air smelled of turned earth and rot, and a cold dread crept over my skin, settling in my bones. The story the General had just told was too close, too familiar, matching every strange, waking nightmare I had lived these past months. I waited for Carmilla and Madame to step through the arch, but no one came.

Then I saw her. Under the narrow arched doorway of the chapel, crowned by a leering, grotesque gargoyle carved in that old Gothic style, its face twisted into a cynical, hungry grin, Carmilla stepped into the dim light. Her face was soft and beautiful, her smile warm, that peculiarly engaging curl to her lips that always made my pulse flutter. I lifted my hand to wave, about to stand and greet her.

The General moved faster than I could follow. He snatched up the woodman’s axe that had been propped against the nearby mausoleum wall, and lunged forward with a snarl. The soft smile dropped from Carmilla’s face in a single, horrifying instant. A brutal, inhuman fury warped her features. She crouched back, hissing. Before I could even draw breath to scream, the General swung the axe with all his force at her head. She ducked under the blow, her small, ice-cold hand fastening around his wrist. He struggled to yank free, but his fingers went numb, dead, the axe clattering to the stone with a sharp, echoing crack. A blink later, she was gone.

The General staggered back against the wall, his grey hair standing on end, cold sweat dripping down his pale face like he was moments from death. The whole scene had lasted no more than three seconds. When I blinked, Madame was standing in front of me, her face tight with impatience, repeating the same question over and over.
“Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla? Where did she go?”
“I don’t know,” I stammered, pointing at the doorway she had just stepped through. “She was there, just a minute ago.”
“But I have been standing in that passage ever since she walked into the chapel,” Madame said, her voice rising in panic. “She never came back out.” She ran through every door, every corridor, calling Carmilla’s name at the top of her lungs, but no answer came. No sound but the wind hissing through the ivy.

“She called herself Carmilla?” the General said, his voice still shaking, his hand pressed to his chest to steady his breathing.
“Carmilla, yes,” I said.
“Aye,” he said, his eyes dark with ancient terror. “That is Millarca. That is the same woman who, a hundred and fifty years ago, was Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Run, my poor child. Leave this accursed ground at once. Drive straight to the clergyman’s house, and do not stop until you are behind his doors. Stay there until we come for you. Begone. You will never find Carmilla here. May you never see her face again.”

The damp, mildew-thick hush of the ruined chapel clung to my bones, the faint tang of old candle wax and crumbling stone sharp in my throat, when he stepped through the very archway Carmilla had glided in and out of days before. I have never seen a creature half so uncanny. He was tall, narrow-chested, stooped under a shock of long grizzled hair that spilled over his shoulders, his frame draped head to toe in faded black, an oddly peaked broad-brimmed hat perched over his brows. His face was brown and shriveled as dried leather, furrowed so deep they looked carved by centuries of wind, his gold spectacles glinting over eyes I could never quite catch sight of. He shambled slow, his thin arms swinging wide, his oversize old black gloves flapping loose around lank, knuckly fingers, his face tipping up to the cracked vaulted ceiling then dropping to the mossy flags in a rhythm that felt less human than some strange clockwork, a perpetual thin smile fixed on his lips like it had been set there a hundred years prior.

“The very man!” The General strode forward, his voice rough with unmistakeable, feral relief. “My dear Baron— I’d given up all hope of reaching you in time. How can I possibly thank you for coming?”

He gestured for my father, who had just stepped back into the chapel, and led the strange old gentleman over to make the formal introductions. The pair fell at once into low, urgent, earnest talk, the Baron pulling a tight roll of yellowed parchment from his coat pocket and spreading it flat across the pitted, worn surface of a nearby tomb monument. He traced faint silver lines with a stubby pencil, darting his gaze back and forth between the parchment and the far walls of the chapel again and again, and I realized the map was the very blueprint of the building. Every so often he flipped open a tiny, soiled leather book, its pages crammed with close, looping faded ink, and mumbled a line or two to the General.

They wandered the side aisle opposite where I stood, counting their paces off to measure distances, until at last they stopped short before a stretch of ivy-choked wall. They tore the clinging ivy away by the handful—its dark sap oozing sticky and viscous as old blood down their wrists—then rapped their sticks against the plaster, scraping away layers of grime and dust until a broad marble tablet emerged, carved letters standing sharp in relief. The woodman, who’d returned with a crowbar, cleared the last of the grime away, and there it was: the long-lost monument to Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, its escutcheon carved with the faint, glinting figure of a bat that seemed to twitch when I glanced away.

The gruff old General, who I’d never once seen pause to pray, lifted his hands and eyes to the rafters, holding that mute, trembling thanksgiving for long seconds.

“Tomorrow,” I heard him say, low and sharp, “the Imperial Commissioner arrives. The inquest will be held, per ancient law.” He turned to the Baron then, shaking both his hands hard enough to creak the old man’s bones. “You’ve delivered this whole region from a plague that’s scourged every village within twenty miles for more than a century. Thank God. The horrible enemy is finally tracked.”

My father drew the pair aside, out of earshot, and I knew he was recounting every last detail of my nightly fevers, my inexplicable wasting, the two tiny puncture wounds on my neck that never quite healed. They glanced over at me, fast, again and again, faces tight with grim pity, before my father crossed the chapel to me. He kissed my forehead over and over, his hand trembling on my shoulder, and led me out into the cool twilight.

“It’s time to go home, little one,” he said. “But first, we must call on the good priest who lives but a mile from here, and ask him to ride back with us to the schloss.”

We found the old priest at his cottage easily, and by the time we trundled up the drive to our home I was so bone-deep fatigued I could barely keep my eyes open. My relief curdled to cold, sinking dismay the second we stepped inside: there was no trace of Carmilla. No one had seen her return, no one could explain where she’d gone. No one offered me a single explanation for the strange scene we’d witnessed in the chapel, and it was clear my father had locked that secret tight in his chest for the time being. Her sinister, unexplained absence turned every memory of the past three months sharp and terrifying, every soft laugh she’d breathed against my neck, every glide of her cold fingers down my arm replaying in my head like a warning I’d been too blind to see.

The arrangements that night were stranger still. Madame and two wide-eyed, silent servants sat up fully clothed in chairs at the foot of my bed, while my father and the priest kept watch in the dressing room adjacent. The priest had walked the perimeter of my room earlier that evening, sprinkling holy water at every corner, swinging his thurible until the air was thick with the scent of myrrh and iron. I understood none of the rites, none of the extraordinary precautions they took for my safety as I slept—until a few days later, every last piece slotted into place. The moment Carmilla vanished, my nightly suffocating visions, my slow wasting, the sharp fangs that nipped at my throat in the dark, all stopped cold.

You have no doubt heard of the appalling, ubiquitous superstition that haunts Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, even far stretches of Russia: the ancient, well-documented belief in the Vampire. If hundreds upon hundreds of sworn, judicially recorded testimonies, collected by commissions of learned, upright men whose reports fill more volumes than exist for almost any other class of recorded supernatural event, hold any weight at all, it is almost impossible to deny, let alone doubt the reality of the phenomenon. For my part, I have never encountered a single theory that could explain all I saw, all I suffered, that did not align with that old, country-held belief.

The next dawn, the formal proceedings were held in the Karnstein chapel. The heavy lead lid of Mircalla’s tomb wrenched open, and the General and my father both recognized their beautiful, perfidious guest at once. One hundred and fifty years had passed since her funeral, and yet her cheeks were flushed the soft warm pink of a sleeping young woman, her lips full and cherry red, her dark lashes fanned over her open eyes. There was no trace of the cadaverous rot you would expect. The two attending physicians—one appointed by the state commission, the other called by the General—swore under oath that her chest rose and fell with a faint, but unmistakeable, slow respiration, a corresponding thud of a heartbeat thrumming steady under her elastic, warm flesh. Her limbs were perfectly flexible, no stiff rigor mortis clinging to them, and seven full inches of dark, glistening warm blood pooled in the bottom of the lead coffin, submerging her body up to her ribs.

Every single accepted, documented sign of vampirism was present. They lifted her body out of the blood, per ancient local custom, and drove a sharp, solid oak stake straight through her heart. At the impact, a piercing, unearthly shriek tore from her throat, loud enough to rattle the stained glass panes in the chapel windows, a cry indistinguishable from the final death agony of a living human woman. They struck off her head next, and a torrent of thick, dark blood gushed from the severed neck, spilling across the marble flags. Body and severed head were piled high on a pyre of dry oak, burned down to fine gray ash, which they scattered on the waters of the fast-flowing river that cut through the Karnstein estate. The ash was swept away downstream, and no vampire has ever plagued that region since that day.

My father keeps a full, official copy of the Imperial Commission’s report, signed by every last witness present that day, to verify every last shocking detail. It is from that sealed document that I have drawn every line of this account of the final, terrible ordeal that freed me at last from Carmilla.

You might imagine I set all this down with steady, composed hands, my memory sanded smooth of its sharper old terrors, but nothing could be further from the truth. My pen skates and stutters across the page as my fingers shake, and even now I can scarce think back on those days without my throat tightening with that old, unnameable dread. Nothing but your earnest, endlessly repeated pleas could have ever driven me to sit down to this task: one that has frayed my nerves so thoroughly I will carry their raw, unstrung edge for months to come, that has dragged back over me the shadow of that unspeakable horror that for years after my deliverance blighted every hour of my days and nights, turned empty rooms into haunts, and made even quiet solitude a terror I could not bear.

Let me tell you a little more of that odd, uncanny Baron Vordenburg, whose strange, ancient lore led us straight to the hidden grave of the Countess Mircalla. He lived in a cramped, mold-spotted garret in Gratz, surviving on a pittance of what was once his family’s vast princely estates across Upper Styria, devoting every waking hour to the minute, gruelling study of the well-documented, blood-chilling tradition of vampirism. He could recite every text on the subject by heart: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de Cura Pro Mortuis*, John Christofer Herenberg’s *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*, and hundreds more besides – a handful of which he lent to my father, their leather spines warped with damp, margins scribbled over in his spidery, ink-stained hand. He had compiled a massive, handwritten digest of every recorded judicial case of vampirism, from which he had mapped the unwritten, ghostly laws that govern the revenant’s unnatural half-life.

I must tell you, the deadly, corpselike pallor storybooks assign to these creatures is cheap melodramatic fiction. The vampire looks healthier, rosier, more vividly alive than any living mortal, both when they walk among us in human guise and when they lie at rest in their graves. When we pried open Mircalla’s coffin and spilled light over her, she bore every single mark the old texts enumerate to prove the long-dead countess walked again unquiet: no rot, warm flushed skin, dark crusted blood at her lips. No scholar has ever explained how they slip out of their graves each dusk and return before dawn, without displacing a single clod of clay, without leaving the slightest smudge or tear on their linen burial shrouds, no trace of disturbance to the sealed coffin at all. That amphibious half-life, sustained by their daily slumber in the cold dark grave, feeds the horrible ravenous lust for living blood that powers every waking moment.

The vampire does not merely hunt at random. It can fix upon a particular person with an overwhelming, all-consuming fixation that mimics the fiercest romantic love. It will deploy infinite patience, endless stratagems to reach that chosen victim, clearing every obstacle in its path, and never desist until it has drained the very last spark of life from the one it covets. For these chosen ones, it draws out its murderous enjoyment with the cruel refinement of an epicure, slow-feeding over months, lulling its prey into a false sense of safety with an artful, tender courtship, seeming to crave even the illusion of sympathy and consent before it strikes the final blow. For random victims, it wastes no time: it descends on them in their sleep, overpowers them with brute unhuman strength, and drains them dry in a single, gory feast.

The vampire is bound to strange, unbreakable rules, too. In Mircalla’s case, she could not take a name that was not a perfect anagram of her true one: not a single letter added, not a single letter omitted, no deviation allowed. Carmilla fit. Millarca fit. No other name would serve her.

My father kept Baron Vordenburg as our guest for two or three weeks after we destroyed Carmilla, and told him the old story of the Moravian nobleman who stumbled on the vampire haunting the Karnstein churchyard generations before. Then he asked the Baron how he had pinpointed the exact spot of Mircalla’s long-hidden, unmarked grave.

The Baron’s gaunt, grotesque face tugged into a strange, secret smile. He stared down at his worn spectacle case, twisting it between his ink-stained fingers, and when he lifted his gaze to speak his voice was low, as if he feared the walls might hear.
“I hold dozens of journals and private papers written by that very man you speak of – the so-called Moravian nobleman. The public tradition muddled his identity, of course: he had moved to Moravia later in life, so folk called him a Moravian lord, but he was born and raised right here in Upper Styria. Long before Mircalla’s early, violent death, he was her lover, the favored, adored suitor the whole court whispered about. Her death plunged him into inconsolable grief.

Vampirism spreads, you see, by a terrible, well-documented ghostly law. Take a land untouched by the pest: how does it begin? A person who has fallen to great wickedness ends their own life. Under the right dark circumstances, that suicide rises as a vampire. The specter slips into the beds of sleeping living folk, they waste away and die, and almost without fail, they climb out of their own graves as vampires too. That is exactly what happened to the beautiful Mircalla: a dark demon sank its fangs into her, and she rose unquiet. My own ancestor, the Vordenburg whose title I still bear, uncovered her secret. He studied the dark lore for decades, and learned far more still.

He soon guessed that sooner or later, the villagers would suspect the dead Countess Karnstein of vampirism, and dig her up to desecrate her remains. Even knowing what she had become, he could not bear the thought of his old beloved’s remains being subject to such posthumous violence. He wrote a whole treatise arguing that the moment you destroy a vampire, you do not end its suffering – you fling its soul into a far, far worse, more unendurable eternal torment. He resolved to save the Mircalla he had once loved from that fate.

He concocted his ruse: he traveled to Karnstein, faked a public ceremony to move her remains to the distant abbey, and in truth completely erased her tombstone, hid her grave beneath a thick layer of tangled brambles and wild grass where no one would ever think to look. But as he grew old, as he neared his own death, his perspective shifted. A cold, gnawing horror settled over him. He realized he had not saved her – he had given her centuries of unimpeded freedom to hunt, to drain young girls dry, to spread her curse across the countryside. He drew up the careful tracings, the coded notes that led me straight to her hidden grave, and wrote a full confession of the great deception he had wrought. He died before he could take any further steps to undo his mistake – and so it fell to me, his distant descendant, far too late for all the girls Mircalla devoured across those hundred and fifty years, to finally track the beast to her lair.”

We talked for some time after that, and he told me one last secret.
“There is one sign of the vampire’s unhuman strength in the grasp of its hand. That slender, soft hand of Mircalla’s closed around the General’s raised wrist like a vice of forged steel, when he lifted the hatchet to strike the first blow to her head. That residual power does not fade even when the creature is destroyed. The numbness she left in that limb clings. It may never fully go away.”

The next spring, my father dragged me away on a long tour of Italy. We stayed gone for more than a full year, wandering sunlit piazzas, sitting in the pews of grand marble cathedrals, trying to outrun the shadow that clung to my bones. It took far longer than that for the terror to fade. Even now, the image of Carmilla drifts back to me unbidden, shifting between two faces: half the time, she is the soft, playful, languid beautiful girl who would curl up beside me in my bed, smiling and murmuring sweet nonsense; the other half, she is the writhing, red-eyed fiend we dragged out of that ruined grave. More times than I can count, I have jolted straight out of a drowsy daydream, my heart hammering, swearing I have heard the light, soft bare footstep of Carmilla, pausing just outside the drawing room door.
