On the fourteenth of April, Romola returned to the walls of Florence. Her journey had been fraught with anxiety; unable to find peace in Pistoia due to conflicting rumors regarding the Trial by Fire, she had pressed onward to Prato. There, amidst a growing dread, she began to feel the inevitable pull toward the city she feared most. Yet, fate intervened sooner than she anticipated. As she traveled, she encountered a monk from San Spirito, the same priest who had served as her late husband's confessor.

From this Augustinian friar, Romola learned the grim totality of the recent upheaval: the arrest of Savonarola and the tragic end of her husband, Tito. The monk recounted how he had witnessed the procession carrying the condemned man into the Piazza, burdened by the weight of his crimes. He spoke of the general sentiment in Florence, noting that Tito had managed to escape a frenzied mob only by leaping into the Arno River, yet he had perished shortly after, struck down on the riverbank by an elderly man harboring a deep-seated grudge against him. Regarding Savonarola, the monk delivered his verdict with the characteristic bias of the Black Brothers (*Frati Neri*) toward a soul that had shown signs of purity beneath its dark habit: he had confessed himself a deceiver of the people.

Romola heard these words, but they registered differently in her heart than in anyone else's. To her, the tragedy was not merely political or religious; it was personal and absolute. That evening, she sat in Florence, enveloped in a heavy, agitated silence. Around her, the city erupted in a cacophony of joyous exclamations and mournful wails, interwoven with the gossipy narratives of neighbors. Among them was Monna Brigida, whose hair, previously dyed artificially during Romola's absence, had now been stripped back to its natural gray—a sacrifice she claimed she would gladly make if only her "dear child" would remain with her.

However, Romola remained unmoved by Brigida's dramatic declarations or the trivial gossip surrounding her. The crushing reality of Tito's death and Fra Girolamo's coerced confession of duplicity left her with little capacity to process minor circumstantial details. Under the weight of her awe-stricken grief, her entire mental energy focused on two singular, overriding imperatives: to locate Savonarola, and to discover the fate of Tessa and the children.

When Monna Brigida's rambling speech finally veered from current troubles into plans for Romola to reside permanently with her, Romola interrupted her abruptly.

"Cousin," she asked sharply, "since Tito's passing, has anything been seen or said concerning a young woman accompanied by two small children?"

Monna Brigida recoiled, her eyes widening in shock, her hands rising in a gesture of disbelief. "Cristo! No! What manner of man was he to treat you so poorly, my poor child? Ah, then, that explains your departure! You left me believing you had chosen to go of your own free will. If I had known the truth—that there was a young woman and children involved—I would not have judged you so strangely or called you flighty. In my heart, I often wondered, 'Why would she abandon her husband to his misfortunes just because they severed poor Bernardo's head? She possesses her father's fiery temper,' I thought. 'That is the root of it.' Never scold me, child; Bardo *was* fierce, you cannot deny it. But had you spoken the truth about the mother and the babes, I would have understood immediately. Has anything been seen or heard of her? Nothing, and the less the better. Enough evil is spoken of him already without adding to it. Since that was your reason for leaving—"

"No, dear cousin," Romola interjected earnestly, cutting her off. "Please, cease speaking thus. Above all else, I wish to find that young woman and her children, and to protect them. They are utterly helpless. Do not argue against it; that is my first and foremost duty."

"Well," Monna Brigida muttered, shrugging her shoulders while lowering her voice to a tone of confused discomfort, "if seeking them makes one a *Piagnone*, then I have been counting peas instead of reciting prayers. Fra Girolamo himself declared that widows should not remarry. Stepping through the front door is a sin and a shame, apparently, yet crawling down the chimney invites welcome. *Two* children—Santiddio!"

"Cousin," Romola replied gently but firmly, "the poor girl has committed no conscious wrong; she is ignorant of everything. I will explain later—not now."

Early the following morning, Romola proceeded to the house beyond San Ambrogio where she had once discovered Tessa. As she had feared, Tessa was gone. Romola reasoned that Tito must have dispatched her ahead to a location where he intended to meet her, for he would never willingly separate her from the children. This possibility brought fresh pain; if Tessa had fled Florence, locating her seemed nearly impossible. Romola imagined the frightened child waiting in vain at some lonely roadside, consumed by helpless misery.

Those who lived nearby offered no more than the fact that the old deaf woman, Lisa, had departed a week prior with her belongings, though no one knew her destination. With no concrete leads to follow, and hindered by both her natural reserve and a profound sensitivity that made her reluctant to publicly reveal Tessa's connection to Tito alongside her own quest, Romola found herself paralyzed by inaction.

Days stretched into weeks of anxious stillness. Even when urged by other concerns, her heart would race if she spotted a pair of round brown legs or a woman dressed in the attire of a country laborer (*contadina*). She never convinced herself that this search was an act of heroic charity or elevated virtue. Rather, she possessed a specific bond she felt compelled to honor; she yearned to embrace the children and earn their affection. Such a reunion would bring solace not only to herself but also serve as a redemptive fruit from her past sorrows.

She learned that Tito had left behind considerable property to which she held a claim. Yet, distrustful of the cleanliness of such tainted wealth, she resolved to surrender it all to the State, retaining only enough to purchase her father's library. This sum would suffice for the modest sustenance of Tessa and the children.

However, Monna Brigida consistently overshadowed these prudent plans. She clamored insistently that Romola must live with her, never abandoning her until she was safely ushered into Paradise, questioning why she had ever encouraged the conversion to the *Piagnoni*. Furthermore, Brigida argued that if Romola wished to raise other people's children, she should do so alongside her own household. Only, she insisted, must the children be found first. Romola sensed the full weight of this implication. Yet, strong feelings unfulfilled inevitably breed superstition, whether of hope or despair. Romola's was the superstition of hope: somehow, she would find the mother and the children.

Finally, a new avenue for inquiry presented itself. She learned that Tito had arranged for horses and mules to await him at San Gallo; consequently, he planned to depart Florence via the Gate of San Gallo. Determined to verify this, she decided to inquire with the gatekeepers whether anyone matching Tessa's description, accompanied by children, had passed through before the morning of the ninth of April.

Walking along the Via San Gallo, her face concealed beneath her long widow's veil, she scanned the surroundings vigilantly. Suddenly, she spotted Bratti haggling with a customer. That wandering merchant, she surmised, might prove useful. She need not fear discussing Tessa with him. Just as she removed her veil and stepped across the street toward him, her gaze fell upon something dangling from the corner of his basket. Her heart leaped with a surge of hope far greater than before.

"Bratti, my friend," she said suddenly, "where did you acquire that necklace?"

"Your servant, madonna," Bratti replied, glancing around with deliberate caution, his mind seemingly impervious to surprise. "It is a necklace of value, yet I shall gain little from selling it, for my heart is too tender for trade; I have pledged it."

"Pray, tell me where you obtained it—from a little woman named Tessa, is it not true?"

"Oh! If you know her," Bratti said, "and would redeem it from me at a small profit to return it to her, you would be performing a great charity. She wept bitterly when parting with it—you would have thought she was plunging into a brook. A small fee is all I ask. One florin suffices, for I dislike being hard-hearted."

"Where is she?" Romola demanded, handing over the coin and unclasping the necklace from the basket with trembling, joyful hands.

"Just outside the gate there, at the far end of the Borgo, at the house of old Sibilla Manetti. Anyone will show you the way."

With wings of anticipation beating at her heels, Romola thanked heaven for the Carnival incident that had allowed her to memorize the necklace's unique appearance. Soon, she stood before the house she sought. Inside the inner room, the young woman and the children had been languishing for over two weeks, possessing neither money nor means to pay the poor widow who housed them, save for their few garments. But since the lady recognized them...

Romola waited for no further explanation. She pushed the door open.
