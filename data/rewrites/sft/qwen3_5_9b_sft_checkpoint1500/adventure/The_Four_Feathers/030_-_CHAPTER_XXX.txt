The northern wind cut sharp and biting, chilling the air around them. Yet the camels, invigorated by the bite, pushed forward at a trot that grew steadily quicker.

"Push harder," Trench gritted out, his jaw clenched tight. "Idris could already be losing us."

"Even if he is ahead," Feversham replied, his voice steady despite the turmoil inside, "gathering a party for pursuit takes time. They need mounts, and it is already past dark."

Though his words offered hope, Feversham's gaze betrayed him. He swiveled his head repeatedly, staring blindly toward the distant, fading glow of Omdurman. The drumbeats had ceased, a silence falling over the land that felt heavy with threat. Here, men moved swiftly yet made no sound; there would be no neighing hooves to announce a chase. Even now, Ansar riders could be thirty paces behind, invisible shadows in the blackening void. Feversham squinted back into the abyss, expecting to glimpse the flash of a white turban.

Trench, however, refused to look back. His spine was rigid, his eyes locked on the empty horizon, though terror burned hotter in him than in Feversham. He did not dare glance at Omdurman; his mind, however, wandered there constantly. He saw the long, narrow streets behind them, the scattered fires flickering at street corners, and the frantic dash of men running house-to-house, searching for the two prisoners who had fled the House of Stone.

Only when Feversham spoke did Trench answer, still facing forward.

"What is it?"

"I no longer see the fires of Omdurman."

"The golden blot, eh? Very low down?" Trench murmured, his voice abstracted. Feversham needed no explanation, nor could Trench have given one. The words surfaced with sudden force, carrying a strange appropriateness: the vision that had been the final thing to meet Feversham's eyes as he left Omdurman on his mission was now returning, marking the completion of that same ordeal. Silence stretched between them until two figures materialized from the gloom at their feet.

"Instanna!" cried Abou Fatma in a whisper.

The camels halted and knelt.

"The new mounts?" Abou Fatma asked as two men vanished briefly, returning with four fresh camels. Simultaneously, the saddles were unbuckled and stripped from the beasts Trench and Feversham had ridden out of the city.

"Are these good camels?" Feversham asked, helping to secure the fresh gear.

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma confirmed urgently, peering eastward and listening intently. "Hurry! Hurry!"

"The arms?" Trench demanded. "Do you have them? Where?" His body bent low, scouring the ground.

"In a moment," Abou Fatma promised, yet Trench seemed unable to wait. He displayed a frantic energy in handling the weapons that rivaled his earlier fear of being caught.

"Ammunition?" he asked, his voice feverish.

"Yes, yes," Abou Fatma assured him. "Ample ammunition, rifles, and revolvers."

He led them twenty yards away, where long desert grass rustled against their ankles. Stooping, Abou Fatma dug into the soft sand with his bare hands.

"Here."

Trench threw himself onto the ground beside him, digging with both hands while emitting an inhuman, whining sound, like a foxhound at a scent. There was something primal and terrible about his posture—scraping at the earth on his knees, his hands moving with the rapidity of a dog's paws, his voice reduced to a whine—as if he were sinking back into an animal state. Within moments, his fingers closed around the lock and trigger of a rifle, and the man returned. The other arms were unearthed, the ammunition distributed.

"Now," Trench declared, a great thrill of joy breaking into his laugh. "Now I do not care. Let them follow from Omdurman! One thing is certain: I shall never return there—not even if they catch us." He cradled the rifle, speaking to it as if it were alive.

Two Arabs mounted the old camels and rode slowly back toward Omdurman. Abou Fatma and his companion stayed with the fugitives. Mounting the fresh beasts, they trotted northeastward. Less than fifteen minutes had passed since their halt.

Throughout the night, they rode through waist-high grass and mimosa trees, moving slowly until dawn revealed a landscape of flat, bare ground broken by small hillocks.

"Are the Effendis tired?" Abou Fatma asked gently. "Shall they rest and eat? Food awaits on each saddle."

"No," Feversham replied. "We eat as we travel."

Dates, bread, and water from a *zamsheyeh* constituted their meal, consumed while seated atop the camels. Freed from the dense scrub, the animals now trotted at their utmost speed. By sunset, they paused for an hour, then resumed their journey under the cover of darkness, pushing onward the following day. They strained every fiber of endurance—their own and that of the beasts—as they climbed rocky heights, traversed valleys, and galloped across plains of honey-colored sand. To each man, the pace felt agonizingly slow, akin to a funeral procession. A mountain would rise above the horizon at sunrise, dominating their view for the entire day, neither growing taller nor drawing nearer. Occasionally, a farmer tending a sparse patch of sorghum would cause their hearts to leap, forcing a wide detour; or a caravan spotted in the distance by Abou Fatma's keen eyes would compel them to kneel and crouch behind a rock, rifles drawn.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, a relay of fresh camels awaited them. Upon mounting, they traveled a day's march west of the Nile, passing through the desert lands of the Ababdeh until a broad gray tract barred their path.

"The road from Berber to Merowi," Abou Fatma noted. "North of it, we turn east toward the river. We cross tonight; if God wills, tomorrow evening we shall have crossed the Nile."

"If God wills," Trench echoed, his gaze darting nervously as safety drew closer. "If only He wills."

They were in territory traversed by caravans; daylight travel was no longer safe. Dismounting, they hid behind a belt of shrubs on high ground, watching the road and the travelers like specks moving along it. They descended and crossed the road in the deep dark, traveling hard toward the river for the remainder of the night.

As day broke, Abou Fatma ordered another halt. They stood in a desolate openness where the slightest shadow was magnified by the surrounding flatness. Feversham and Trench gazed eagerly to their right. Somewhere in that direction flowed the Nile, though it remained invisible.

"We must build a stone circle," Abou Fatma instructed. "Lie close to the ground within it. I will advance to the river, ensure the boat is ready, and confirm our friends await us. I return after dark."

Gathering stones quickly, they raised a low wall about a foot high. Inside, Feversham and Trench lay prone on the earth, a water-skinned beside them, rifles resting at their sides.

"You have dates, too," Abou Fatma reminded them.

"Yes."

"Do not stir from this hiding place until I return. I will take your camels and bring you fresh ones in the evening." With his fellow Arab, he rode off toward the river.

Trench and Feversham began scraping out the sand within the circle, lying down and watching the horizon through the gaps. For both, this perhaps proved the longest day of their lives. So near to safety, yet not truly safe, the time stretched endlessly. To Trench, it surpassed a single night in the House of Stone; to Feversham, it exceeded even those six years prior when he had waited above St. James's Park for the curtain to fall before venturing into the streets. They were nearing Berber, meaning the pursuit must be hot on their trail. Feversham lay pondering how he had ever summoned the courage to face Berber alone. The sun burned pitilessly upon their backs, offering no shade, and within the narrow ring of stones, there was no room to shift. They spoke scarcely at all.

At last, the sunset arrived, bringing the friendly darkness and a cool breeze that rustled through the desert.

"Listen!" Trench whispered.

Straining their ears, both men heard the soft padding of camels approaching. A low whistle called them from their shelter.

"We are here," Feversham said quietly.

"God be thanked!" Abou Fatma exclaimed. "Good news and bad news. The boat is ready, our friends are waiting, and camels await you on the caravan track by the riverbank near Abu Hamed. However, your escape is known. Roads and ferries are heavily guarded. Before sunrise, we must strike inland from the eastern bank."

Crossing the river cautiously around one o'clock in the morning, they sank the boat on the far shore. Camels waited, and they traveled inland, albeit more slowly than the fugitives desired due to the anxious state of flight.
