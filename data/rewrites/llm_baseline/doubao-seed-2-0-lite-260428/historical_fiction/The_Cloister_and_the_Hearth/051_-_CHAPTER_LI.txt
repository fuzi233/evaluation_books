Anyone who knew the pair could guess how much they had to pour out to one another in the days that followed. For Margaret, it was a time stitched half of honey and half of wormwood: she had gained the truest rough-and-tumble friend she’d known in years, but the news Denys brought curdled her joy at once. She learned, for the very first time, that her Gerard was wandering all alone in that far, strange Italian land, no kinsman, no familiar comrade at his back. She could not bring herself to share Denys’s breezy certainty that he would soon turn for home—if that had ever been his plan, he would have crossed the Channel and stepped back onto Dutch soil long before now.

Denys was a constant soft balm to her frayed nerves. He took to calling her his she-comrade, and chivvied her out of every dark mood with his raucous old marching songs and absurd tales of the road. In turn, Margaret fussed over him, fed him, and hectored him as gently as she hectored old Martin the weariest of the day, forbidding him to eat a single meal at an inn, and clapping him down whenever a coarse oath slipped out. “It spoils you, Denys,” she would chide, mock-frowning. “Good lack, to hear such ugly words tumble out of so comely a head! Forbear, or I shall cross my arms and turn you out of doors, so be civil.” At that Denys would pull himself up straight and do his very best to behave, and the ludicrous little battle between his natural gentleman’s politeness and the rough, swaggering ruffian manners he’d picked up on the road was the best comedy in Margaret’s little cottage that season.

And as the old saying goes, misfortunes never come singly, nor friends for that matter: other folks soon began seeking out Margaret’s company, too. Some weeks prior, she had penned a humble, careful letter to Margaret Van Eyck, telling her she knew full well she was no longer the favored ward of old, and would keep her distance so as not to presume—but that she could never forget all the kindness her old benefactress had shown her in better days. She wrote briefly of all the backbreaking ways she had scraped a living to keep her and her child fed, and ended with one earnest plea: that Van Eyck never breathe a word of where she was living, least of all to Gerard’s family. “I hate them,” she had scrawled, her pen digging deep into the paper. “They drove him away from me. And even after he was gone, their hearts never turned soft to me, as they would have if they’d ever truly repented the cruelty they dealt him.”

Margaret Van Eyck was perplexed by the letter, and at last she confided the whole secret to Reicht. The secret passed through Reicht quick as an arrow through a hollow tube, straight to Gerard’s mother Catherine. “Ay, and has she turned that bitter against us?” the good woman huffed. “She stole our son from under our noses, and now she hates us for not running to greet her with open arms? Natheless, it is a blessing she is alive, and no farther away than Rotterdam.”

At that very time, the English princess, now Countess of Charolois, was making a stately royal progress through the northern states of the Duchy of Burgundy, accompanied by her young stepdaughter, Marie de Bourgogne, the great heiress of all Burgundy. Old Duke Philip, the most magnificent prince in all of Europe, turned out his full splendor to do them honor: crowds of dazzling knights in gilded armor, bevies of noble ladies draped in silk and gemstones thronged the retinue. Minstrels, jongleurs, bards, actors, tumblers, and every kind of traveling entertainer followed in their wake, and every town the royal party passed through buzzed with fencing matches, dances, feasting, and unbridled joy. Richart, Gerard’s brother, invited all the Tergou clan to meet him in Rotterdam to watch the grand pageant, and so they all traveled south together.

They had been settled in Rotterdam for several days when Denys ran straight into Catherine on the street. They greeted each other warm as old friends, and Denys bade her rejoice at once, crowing loud that he had found his she-comrade at last. But Catherine doused his glee at once, pointing out he would have found her far sooner if he’d stayed quietly at home in Tergou instead of vagabondizing all over Holland chasing her shadow. “And now we’ve found her, what good is it? Her heart is set dead against us.”

“Pah, let that flea stick to the wall!” Denys laughed. “Come on, I’ll take you straight to her house.”

Catherine shook her head. She would not go bursting in where she was sure of a cold, ill welcome. “Folks that come unbidden sit on thorns,” she said. No, Denys would have to be their mediator, smooth things over, bring the two sides to a good understanding.

Denys seized the office at once, full of pomp and brimming confidence. He trotted straight back to Margaret’s cottage. “She-comrade!” he announced. “I met a friend of thine today.”

“Thou must have leaned over the Rotterdam canal and seen thine own reflection, then,” Margaret teased back.

“Nay, ‘twas a woman, one that longs for thy goodwill,” Denys said. “It was Catherine—Gerard’s own mother.”

Margaret’s smile died on her face. “Oh, was it?” she said, quiet and sharp. “Then you may tell her she comes far too late. There was a time I ached and longed for her, when I needed her most. But she held herself aloof from me then, and so now we will go on as we always have, strangers.”

Denys tried to shake her resolution. He coaxed, he wheedled, but Margaret was bitter, sullen, and would not budge an inch. At last he lost patience and scolded her roundly for her stubbornness—at which she burst into hysterical sobs. Terrified that his well-meaning scolding had brought on such a fit, Denys let his guard slip, and she trapped him into a solemn, sacred promise never to bring the subject of her and Gerard’s family up ever again.

He trundled back to Catherine crestfallen, and told her the whole sorry tale. Catherine flared up at the news, and went straight home to tell all the rest of the family how their overtures had been spurned. They all flared up in turn, and a bitter little feud ignited, burning hotter with every passing day. Only little Kate, the youngest sister, tried to make soft excuses for Margaret’s anger.

The very next day, a visitor knocked at Margaret’s cottage door. He walked in to find the whole household’s military order mightily degraded: Martin was up to his elbows in soapsuds scrubbing pots, Denys was fumbling awkwardly with a hot iron pressing linen, and Margaret was plaiting fine ruffs, her sharp eye keeping watch over her two clumsy raw recruits. The visitor was an old man, seeming venerable at first glance, but on closer look sharp-faced, wizened, and hard.

Margaret cried out “Ah!” the moment she saw him, then spun around and hid her face, her whole body stiff with invincible repugnance. “Oh, that man! That man!”

“Nay, fear me not,” said Ghysbrecht, the old former scheming burgomaster of Tergou. “I come on a friend’s errand. I bring thee a letter from foreign parts.”

“Mock me not, old man,” Margaret whispered, slowly turning around.

“Nay, look, see for thyself,” Ghysbrecht said, and held out a great, thick, folded letter. Margaret darted forward and seized it, her hands trembling so hard she nearly dropped it, her eyes glistening bright. It was Gerard’s handwriting, plain as day.

“Oh, thank you, sir, bless you for this!” she gasped. “I forgive you every ill thing you ever did to me.” She pressed the letter tight to her bosom with one hand, and slipped swiftly out of the room, alone with her treasure. She did not come back for hours, and at last Ghysbrecht left, casting a sour scowl at old Martha as he shut the door behind him. Margaret spent all those hours shut away, reading and re-reading the letter that had come all the way from her Gerard.
