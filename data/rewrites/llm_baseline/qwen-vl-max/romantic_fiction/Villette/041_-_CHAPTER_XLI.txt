Must I now account for the Freedom and Renovation I claimed on that night of celebration? Must I confess how I tested the two steadfast companions I brought home from the glowing park, when they vowed to liberate me from love’s chains?

I tried them the very next day. They had spoken boldly of strength when they rescued me from my bondage, but when I asked not for words, but deeds—for proof of comfort, of relief—Freedom excused himself, saying he was poor and unable to help. And Renovation? He never spoke again. He had died in the night, suddenly, silently.

Left alone, I could only hope that my imagination had run too fast, too far. I clung to the idea that jealousy might have distorted my vision, and I sought solace in the memory of its cruel tricks. But after a brief, futile struggle, I found myself once more bound by the old torment of uncertainty—pulled back into the same cage of suspense, strained anew by the weight of waiting.

Would I see him before he leaves? Will he remember me? Does he plan to come? Will today—or this very hour—bring him? Or must I endure once more the slow, gnawing ache of prolonged absence, the sudden, brutal agony of final parting—the silent, fatal wrench that severs hope and doubt alike, shaking life itself? And the hand that delivers such violence cannot be soothed, for distance stands between us like an unyielding wall.

It was the Feast of the Assumption. No classes were held. The boarders and teachers had gone on a long walk into the countryside, to take their afternoon meal at a farmhouse after morning mass. I did not go with them. Only two days remained before the *Paul et Virginie* sailed, and I clung to what little time was left—like a survivor clinging to the last raft after shipwreck.

There was some carpentry work to do in the first classroom—repairs to benches or desks. Holidays were often used for such tasks, which couldn’t be done when pupils filled the rooms. Sitting alone, I meant to go out to the garden and leave the room clear, but I was too weary to move. Then I heard footsteps approaching.

Foreign workers always come in pairs: I suspect it would take two Labassecourien carpenters to hammer a single nail. As I tied on my bonnet—until then hanging loosely from my fingers—I noticed something odd: only one worker approached. I also observed that he wore shoes, not wooden clogs. I assumed it must be the master carpenter, coming to inspect before sending his men.

I wrapped my scarf around me. He entered. I turned away from the door. A strange tremor passed through me—a fleeting sensation, too quick to name. I turned. There stood the man I thought was the artisan. But as I looked toward the doorway, I saw a figure fill it—and my eyes fixed upon M. Paul.

How many prayers we offer in silence, begging for miracles, only to receive no answer. Yet once in a lifetime, one perfect gift falls into our hands—unasked, unexpected, radiant and complete, minted fresh from the heart of fulfillment.

M. Emanuel wore the clothes he likely intended for travel—a velvet-trimmed surtout. I thought he was ready to depart immediately, though I believed two days still remained until the ship sailed. He looked well, cheerful, kind—gentle even. He stepped forward eagerly, reaching me in seconds. His presence was warm, open, full of affection. Perhaps it was the joy of being a groom that lit his face. Whatever stirred him, I could not meet his light with shadow. If this were my last moment with him, I would not waste it in coldness or pride. I loved him deeply—too deeply to let jealousy stand between us, even in farewell. One kind word, one soft look from him, would sustain me for all the years ahead; it would be comfort in solitude, a healing draught. I would accept it. I would taste it—no matter how much pride might protest.

The meeting would be brief, of course. He would say the same thing to me as to each student—take my hand for a few minutes, touch my cheek once, gently, and then—nothing more. Then the final parting, the vast separation, the chasm I could never cross to reach him—across which, perhaps, he would not even glance to recall me.

He took my hand in one of his, and with the other lifted my bonnet. He looked into my face. His bright smile faded. His lips seemed to form words without sound—like a mother seeing her child changed by illness or sorrow. A pause came.

“Paul! Paul!” called a woman’s voice behind him, urgent and sharp. “Come into the salon. I have so much to say to you—conversation for the whole day. Victor is here. Josef is here. Come, Paul, come to your friends.”

Madame Beck, drawn by watchfulness or some hidden instinct, pressed close—so close she nearly stood between us.

“Come, Paul!” she repeated, her gaze slicing through me like a steel stylus. She pushed against her kinsman. I thought he would yield. I thought he would leave. The pain pierced deeper than I could bear—something I could no longer hide. I cried out—

“My heart will break!”

What I felt was not metaphor. It was real, crushing grief. But then—another flood opened. One breath from M. Paul, the whisper, “Trust me!”—lifted a weight, opened a passage. I sobbed deeply, shivered, trembled—but also found relief. I wept.

“Leave her to me,” said Madame Beck calmly. “It is a crisis. I will give her a cordial, and it will pass.”

To be left with her and her cordial felt like being handed over to a poisoner with her cup. But M. Paul answered sharply, low, and firm:

“Laissez-moi!”

In that harsh tone, I heard a strange music—rough, fierce, yet alive.

“Laissez-moi!” he repeated, nostrils flaring, muscles quivering.

“But this will never do,” Madame insisted, sternly.

Even fiercer, he replied:
“Sortez d’ici!”

“I’ll send for Père Silas,” she threatened, “right now.”

“Femme!” he shouted—not in his usual deep voice, but in a high, trembling key. “Femme! Sortez à l’instant!”

He was roused—and I loved him in that rage more than ever before.

“What you do is wrong,” she persisted. “It is typical of men like you—unreliable, emotional. Impulsive, unwise, inconsistent. An act unworthy of someone steady and resolute.”

“You do not know what I am capable of,” he said. “But you will learn. Time will teach you. Modeste,” he added, softer now, “be gentle. Be compassionate. Look at this face—see how it suffers. You know I am your friend, and the friend of your friends. Despite your mockery, you know I can be trusted. I have no hesitation in sacrificing myself—but my heart aches for what I see. It _must_ have comfort. _Leave me!_”

This time, in the “_leave me_,” there was a command so bitter, so absolute, I wondered how even Madame Beck could resist. But she stood firm. Her eyes met his—hard, unflinching. She opened her mouth to speak. I saw fire rise in M. Paul’s face. I don’t know how he moved—it wasn’t violent, but precise, courteous. He offered his hand. It barely touched hers. She spun away, fled from the room, vanished in a heartbeat. The door closed behind her.

The storm passed quickly. He smiled, telling me to wipe my eyes. He waited quietly, offering soothing words now and then. Soon I sat beside him again—calmer, not desperate, not desolate. Not friendless, not hopeless, not longing for death.

“It made you very sad to lose your friend?” he asked.

“It kills me to be forgotten, Monsieur,” I whispered. “These long days—no word from you. I feared you’d leave without saying goodbye.”

“Must I tell you what I told Modeste—that you do not know me? Must I show you my character? You _will_ have proof that I can be a loyal friend? Without proof, this hand will not rest in mine, nor trust my shoulder as shelter? Very well. The proof is here. I’ve come to justify myself.”

“Say anything, teach anything, prove anything, Monsieur,” I said. “I can listen now.”

“Then, first—you must go with me into the town. I came specifically to bring you.”

Without question, without hesitation, I retied my bonnet. I was ready.

We walked along the boulevards. He made me sit several times on benches beneath the lime trees. He didn’t ask if I was tired—he simply watched, and knew.

“All these weary days,” he echoed my words, mimicking my voice, my accent, with a playful warmth I had come to love. “All these weary days”—he paused—“I have not forgotten you for one hour. Faithful women make a mistake: they think themselves the only faithful ones in the world. I, too, once dared not count on faith from anyone. But look at me.”

I raised my eyes—happy now, truly happy, or they wouldn’t have mirrored my soul.

“Well,” he said after a moment’s study, “there is no denying that signature: Constancy wrote it. Her pen is of iron. Was it painful to see?”

“Severely painful,” I admitted. “Take your hand, Monsieur. I cannot bear its pressure any longer.”

“Elle est toute pâle,” he murmured, speaking to himself. “This face—she hurts me.”

“Oh,” I said, almost involuntarily, “I am not pleasant to look at—?”

I couldn’t help it. The words slipped out. I had always feared how I appeared—this fear gripped me now with fresh force.

A softness fell over his face. His violet eyes glistened beneath thick lashes. He rose abruptly. “Let us walk on.”

“Do I displease your eyes _much_?” I ventured, gaining courage. It mattered—deeply.

He stopped. He gave me a short, strong answer—one that silenced me, humbled me, yet satisfied me utterly. From that moment, I knew what I was to _him_. What I was to the world beyond, I ceased to care about.

Was it weak to place so much value on his opinion of my appearance? Perhaps. I fear it was. But if so, I confess I carried my share of weakness. I admit a deep dread of displeasing him—and a quiet desire to please him, just a little.

Where we walked, I scarcely knew. The journey was long, yet felt short. The path was lovely, the day radiant. M. Emanuel spoke of his voyage—he expected to stay three years. Upon returning from Guadaloupe, he hoped to be free of obligations, clear of debt. And what, he asked, did I plan to do during his absence?

Once, he reminded me, I had spoken of becoming independent—of running my own small school. Had I given up that dream?

“No,” I said. “I’m doing everything I can to save enough to make it real.”

He didn’t like leaving me in the Rue Fossette. He feared I’d miss him too much—feel lonely, grow sad.

That was certain. But I promised to try to endure.

“Still,” he said, voice low, “there is another reason to leave your current home. I want to write to you sometimes. It wouldn’t be wise to risk losing letters. In the Rue Fossette—well, our Catholic discipline, though reasonable, might under certain circumstances be misused—perhaps abused.”

“But if you write,” I insisted, “I _must_ have your letters. Ten directors, twenty directresses—none shall keep them from me. I am a Protestant. I will not submit to such control. Monsieur, I _will not_.”

“Doucement—doucement,” he replied. “We’ll find a way. We have resources. Soyez tranquille.”

And so he paused.

We were returning now. We reached the middle of a clean, quiet faubourg. The houses were small but neat. Before a white doorstep of a tidy house, M. Paul stopped.

“I call here,” he said.

He didn’t knock. Instead, he took a key from his pocket, unlocked the door, and stepped inside. He ushered me in and closed the door behind us. No servant appeared. The vestibule was small, freshly painted, tastefully decorated. At the end, a French window framed by vines—tendrils and green leaves brushing the glass. Silence filled the house.

He opened an inner door, revealing a tiny parlor—small, but charming. The walls glowed with a soft blush. The floor was polished. A square carpet covered the center. A round table gleamed like the mirror above the hearth. A small couch, a delicate chiffonnière with crimson silk curtains half-open, showing porcelain shelves. A French clock, a lamp, biscuit figurines. The window recess held a green stand with three pots of blooming plants. In a corner stood a guéridon with a marble top, bearing a workbox and a glass of violets in water. The lattice was open—fresh air flowed in, mingling with the sweet scent of flowers.

“Pretty, pretty place!” I exclaimed. M. Paul smiled, pleased by my delight.

“Must we wait here?” I whispered, half awed by the stillness.

“We’ll explore a few other corners of this little shell,” he said.

“May I wander freely?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said quietly. “I dare.”

He led me. I saw a small kitchen with a stove, oven, bright brasses, two chairs, a table. A cupboard held a compact set of earthenware.

“There’s a china coffee service in the salon,” he said, pointing to six green-and-white dinner plates, four dishes, cups and jugs to match.

Up the narrow, clean staircase, I glimpsed two neat bedrooms. Then, back downstairs, we halted before a larger door.

Producing a second key, M. Emanuel fitted it into the lock. He opened it, gesturing for me to enter first.

“Voici!” he declared.

I found myself in a modest but clean room—bare compared to the others, but orderly. The floorboards were scrubbed, though bare of carpet. Two rows of green benches and desks lined the walls, with a central aisle leading to a teacher’s platform, chair, and table. Behind it hung a tableau. Maps adorned the walls. A few hardy plants bloomed in the windows. This was a miniature classroom—complete, neat, inviting.

“It’s a school?” I asked. “Who teaches here? I’ve never heard of a school in this faubourg.”

“Will you kindly distribute a few prospectuses for a friend of mine?” he asked, pulling papers from his coat and placing them in my hands.

I read them—neatly printed:

And what did I say to M. Paul Emanuel?

Some moments are impossible to recall. Certain emotions—joy, grief, wonder—when revisited, feel like whirlwinds, blurred by speed, dim as spinning wheels.

I cannot remember the thoughts or words of the ten minutes after that revelation—no more than I can trace my earliest memories. But the first clear image is of myself speaking rapidly, repeating over and over:

“Did you do this, M. Paul? Is this your house? Did you furnish it? Did you print these papers? Do you mean me? Am I the directress? Is there another Lucy Snowe? Tell me—say something!”

But he remained silent. His quiet smile, his amused gaze downward, his posture—all remain vivid.

“How is it? I must know everything—_everything_,” I pleaded.

The packet of papers dropped to the floor. He had extended his hand—and I had seized it, oblivious to all else.

“Ah,” he said, “you said I had forgotten you all these weary days. Poor old Emanuel! These are the thanks he gets for spending three weeks trudging from painter to upholsterer, cabinet-maker to charwoman—thinking only of Lucy and Lucy’s cot!”

I hardly knew what to do. First, I stroked the soft velvet on his cuff. Then, I gently rubbed the hand beneath it. It was his foresight, his kindness—his quiet, powerful goodness—that overwhelmed me. The certainty of his constant care struck me like a divine light. And his look—his tender, loving gaze—shook me beyond words. Yet I forced myself to focus on practical matters.

“The trouble!” I said. “And the cost! Did you have money, M. Paul?”

“Plenty!” he said warmly. “Selling my teaching position gave me a good sum. I decided to spend part of it on the richest treat I’ve ever known or ever will know. I’ve dreamed of this hour day and night. I didn’t come near you before because I didn’t want to spoil it. Reserve isn’t my virtue or vice. If I had placed myself in your power, and you’d begun asking—Where have you been, M. Paul? What have you been doing? What is your secret?—my deepest secret would have spilled out. Now,” he continued, “you will live here. You will have a school. You will work while I am away. You will think of me. You will care for yourself—for my sake. And when I return—”

He paused.

I promised to do all he asked. I vowed to work hard, willingly. “I will be your faithful steward,” I said. “I trust that when you return, the account will be ready. Monsieur, monsieur—you are _too_ good!”

Words failed me. My feelings surged, but language broke and froze in the attempt. He watched me, still. Gently, he raised his hand to stroke my hair. It brushed my lips. I pressed it close—paid tribute. He was my king. That hand had bestowed royal favor. To honor it was both joy and duty.

Evening settled over the quiet faubourg. M. Paul claimed my hospitality. He had been walking since morning and needed refreshment. He said I should offer him chocolate in my gold-and-white china set. He went out to order from the restaurant. Then he arranged the small guéridon and two chairs on the balcony outside the French window, beneath the vine-covered trellis.

With shy joy, I served him. I arranged the tray, poured the chocolate, presented the rolls and fruit—cherries and strawberries nestled in green leaves. Our meal was simple, but it was more than a feast to us both. I took immense pleasure in tending him.

Behind us lay gardens. Beyond, fields stretched under the sky. The air was still, mild, fresh. Above the poplars, laurels, cypresses, and roses, the moon rose—so beautiful, so serene, that the heart trembled beneath her glow. A star shimmered beside her, pure and unassuming, like a quiet love.

Nearby, in a large garden, a fountain jetted upward. A pale statue leaned over the play of water.

M. Paul spoke to me. His voice blended with the soft sigh of wind, the murmur of water, the rustle of leaves—a lullaby of twilight.

Happy hour—stay a moment! Lower your plumes, rest your wings. Bow to me, brow of Heaven! White Angel, let your light linger. Leave its reflection on future clouds. Bequeath your peace to the time that needs a ray in memory!

Our meal was simple, but it was what we both desired more than any banquet. I delighted in caring for M. Paul. I asked if his friends—Père Silas, Madame Beck—knew of his plans, if they had seen my house.

“Mon amie,” he said, “only you and I know what I’ve done. This joy is ours alone—unsullied, untouched. To be honest, there’s a refinement of pleasure in this secrecy I wouldn’t wish to share. Besides” (with a smile), “I wanted to prove to Miss Lucy that I _could_ keep a secret. How often has she teased me for lacking dignity and caution? How many times has she saucily hinted that all my affairs are the secret of Polichinelle?”

It was true. I hadn’t spared him on that point—or any other. Magnificent-minded, noble-hearted, dear, flawed little man—you deserved honesty, and from me, you always received it.

I pressed further: Who owned the house? Who was my landlord? How much was the rent?

He instantly gave me written details—everything foreseen, prepared.

The house was not his—I suspected that. He was not the sort to buy property. I suspected a lack of thrift in him—he could earn, but not save. He needed a treasurer. The building belonged to a man in the Basse-Ville—a man of means, M. Paul said. He startled me by adding: “A friend of yours, Miss Lucy. Someone who holds you in great respect.”

To my delight, I learned the landlord was none other than M. Miret—the irritable yet kind-hearted bookseller who had kindly found me a seat that fateful night in the park. It seemed M. Miret was wealthy and respected, owning several houses in this faubourg. The rent was modest—half what it would cost for a similar house nearer the city center.

“And if fortune does not favor you,” M. Paul added, “though I believe it will, I have the comfort of knowing you’re in good hands. M. Miret won’t be greedy. Your first year’s rent is already in your savings. After that, Miss Lucy, you must trust God—and yourself. But now—what will you do for pupils?”

“I must distribute my prospectuses.”
