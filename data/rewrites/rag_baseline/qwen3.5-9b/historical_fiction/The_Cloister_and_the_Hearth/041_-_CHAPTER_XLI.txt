Denys, positioned squarely between his companions like a sacrificial offering to prevent any further attempts at flight, was consumed by an agony of grief and remorse that bordered on madness. He bore the crushing weight of betrayal; for his sake, Gerard had forsaken the direct German road to Rome, only to be abandoned utterly in the heart of Burgundy. This realization—the sheer isolation of his friend amidst a hostile landscape—was the poison that drove Denys to rage against the heavens and the earth alike, oscillating between frenzied shouting and a silence so dark and menacing that his captors felt compelled to bind him tightly.

They seized their leader just beyond the town limits, halting the entire cavalcade at the *Tête d'Or*. The young landlady, though distracted by the Count and increasingly wary of the Bastard, spotted Denys and approached with anxious inquiry regarding his missing companion. Overcome by a fresh torrent of sorrow, Denys confessed everything, imploring her to dispatch someone immediately to retrieve Gerard. "Now that he is severed from me, he may yet heed my counsel," he pleaded, his voice breaking. "Poor wretch, he cannot endure solitude." The landlady tossed her head dismissively, her cheeks flushing with irritation. "I believe I have shown you enough kindness already," she retorted sharply. "Will you not?" "Certainly not," Denys snapped. "Then"—he unleashed a barrage of curses and vitriol upon her. She spun on her heel, fleeing the scene with whimpers, claiming she was unaccustomed to such abuse, yet she swiftly commanded her stablehand to saddle two mules.

Denys rode north with his troop, head bowed low over his saddle, oblivious to the fact that the truthful young woman behind him was already preparing an equestrian disguise in a mere forty minutes. Driven by urgency, she spurred away with her servant in the exact opposite direction. That evening, after a grueling march, the Bastard and his men arrived at *The White Hart*, sparking a commotion of unprecedented scale. It took considerable time before Manon discerned her old friend amidst the throng; when she finally recognized him, her reaction was subtle—a deepening flush across her cheeks—but she made no overt claim to their shared history. The poor soul, however, began to recoil, his pride hardening into scorn for the humble origins from which she had risen. Denys observed this transformation but could not muster a smile. The inn itself seemed a grotesque echo of Gerard's fate.

Before night fully claimed the sky, the wind shifted. Manon entered the room, beckoning him with a single, urgent finger. He rose sullenly, dragging his guards in tow. "Nay, I wish to speak a word to thee in private," she insisted. Drawing him to a shadowed corner, she lowered her voice. "Wouldst thou do me a kindness?" Denys answered loudly, refusing to whisper, "No. I am not in the mood to bestow kindnesses upon man or woman. Were I to grant a favor, it would be to a dog—and even then, only if necessity demanded it."

"Alas, good archer," Manon said humbly, her voice trembling, "thou hast done me one favor once ere now, thou and thy pretty comrade." "Thou hast, dame, thou hast," Denys replied coolly. "Well then, for his sake—what is required?" "Thou knowest my tale. I was unfortunate, but now I am worshipful. Yet a woman cast him in my teeth today, and so it shall remain while he hangs there. I desire him taken down; alas!" "With all my heart," Denys declared. "But none dare I ask save thee. Wilt thou do it?" "Not I, even were I not a prisoner." Upon hearing this stern refusal, the tender Manon sighed, clasping her palms together in despair. Denys reassured her, telling her not to fret. There were soldiers of a lower caliber who would not hesitate to accept such a task for coin. It was merely a matter of money; if she could provide two angels, he would secure two men to perform the dirty work of *The White Hart*.

Though the prospect was distasteful, Manon reflected that soldiers were transient creatures, drunk in one tavern tonight and silenced in another tomorrow. Softly, she conceded, "Send them out to me. But pray, tell them it is for a friend; let them not think it is for me. I should sink into the earth; times have changed." Denys readily found warriors eager to win an angel each so effortlessly. He dispatched them, instantly dismissing the subject with contempt, and sat brooding on his lost friend. Soon, Manon and the hired hands reached a general accord. But what of the body once removed? She murmured, "The river lies near—the place." "Cast him in, then?" "Nay, nay; be not so cruel! Could ye not lay him gently—with something weighty beneath him?" She must have pondered the logistics in detail, for she was not one whose ideas arrived swiftly. Everything was settled rapidly, save for the timing of payment. The mail-clad warriors itched for their due, seeking it in advance. Manon hesitated. Did she doubt their word? Then let her accompany them, or watch from a distance. "Me?" Manon cried out in horror. "I would sooner die than witness it." "Yet that is precisely what thou wouldst have done." "Ay, for sore is my need. Times have changed." She had already forgotten her earlier admonition to Denys.

An hour later, the disagreeable relic of their caterpillar-like existence ceased to mar the worshipful matron's public life, and the grim eyes of the past stopped casting malignant glances down into the white hind's clover field. She had secured herself an average husband and a prime house-dog, destined to outlive everyone else. Her troops, returning from executing the medieval maxim "Off wi' the auld love" with naive brutality, received a profound shock. They found the marketplace choked with black-clad groups; it had stood empty an hour prior. Conscience struck them hard. Such was the price of meddling with the dead. Nevertheless, the bolder of the two, emboldened by the encroaching darkness, stole forward alone. He slily merged into a gathering crowd, then returned to his companion with a tone of reproach that lacked strict reasonableness. "Ye born fool," he muttered, "it is nothing but a miracle."
