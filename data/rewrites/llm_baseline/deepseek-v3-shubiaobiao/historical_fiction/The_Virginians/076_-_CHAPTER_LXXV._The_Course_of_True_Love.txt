Had your father, dear children, possessed even a grain of common sense, this chapter—nay, your very existence—might never have come to pass. But no, he must fling prudence to the wind, and thus you were born to plague him in a thousand ways: clattering through the halls when he craved quiet, rousing him from his after-dinner doze, misplacing his spectacles, pilfering his newspaper, and draining his purse with tailors’ bills, tutors’ fees, and mantua-makers’ extravagances. And when one of you fell ill—ah, then came the true torment! For though he might have slept soundly, your mother would not permit it, fretting and pacing as if the world depended on her vigilance. And so, between the lot of you, his peace was forever shattered.

Now that you have outgrown childhood’s afflictions—petticoats, chickenpox, scarlet fever, and the like—what fresh torment arises? Your mother, ever the schemer, now speaks of preparing the south rooms as a nursery for future grandchildren. She insists that Miles must marry early, as we did, to save him from the perils of Brookes’s and Goosetree’s, as though a young man’s wild oats were not his natural due. Marry! My dear, we had no business marrying at all! By every law of prudence, I should have withdrawn from my engagement to Miss Theo and sought a wealthy bride instead. Your Uncle John was a parson, too meek to call me to account; Charley but a schoolboy; and your grandfather too aged to challenge me with pistols. Ours was a match of pure folly, and our kin were right to disapprove. What are relations for, if not to disapprove?

Mark this, George: when Hester weds, you must quarrel with her choice if it displeases you. And Hester, when George takes holy orders, you must turn up your nose at his bride. As for you, my little Theo—no, you shall never leave me. You must stay to play Haydn and crack my walnuts.

*(Here, in a girlish scrawl upon the margin, is written: “I never intend to go.—THEODOSIA.” And beneath it: “Nor I.—HESTER.” Yet they did, both of them—Theodosia to Joseph Clinton, son of the Reverend Joseph Blake, and Hester to Captain F. Handyman, R.N., as the family Bible records.)*

In those days, your parents dwelt in a fool’s paradise, blind to the world’s meddling. Rinaldo was no more enchanted by Armida’s garden than I was by Theo’s presence. What magic did she wield, that neither glory nor gaming nor the company of wits could lure me from her side? Our hours together were endless, our talk more delightful than the finest wit in London. I scarce noticed when the King of Prussia lost his capital—what were Leipzig or Berlin to us? That house in Dean Street was an Eden, and I have known idleness since, but never such joy.

Yet once, and briefly, that joy was eclipsed. A shadow fell, so dark that I contemplated highway robbery, enlisting under Prussia’s banner, or sailing to India on some desperate venture—anything to escape the agony of separation. For we *were* parted, and the pain of it was such that your mother still whispers of it in dread. Had we remained so, what would have become of us? The thought turns the world to ash.

The blow came from an unexpected quarter. My English kin, deeming themselves wise, had written to Madam Esmond in Virginia, decrying my engagement as folly. Aunt Bernstein led the charge, lamenting Theo’s lack of fortune and beauty. Lady Warrington, ever pious, draped her disapproval in scripture. Even the new Countess of Castlewood, lofty in her rank, deemed it her duty to intervene. Their letters crossed the sea, and one bitter April morning, I found not Theo’s welcome smile, but the Lambert’s grief-stricken faces.

Madam Esmond’s letter lay before me, its words courteous but cold. She implied that the Lamberts had ensnared me, that they had schemed first for Henry, then for me. She urged them, for their daughter’s sake, to end the engagement. The General, pale as death, said, “She will insist on it herself. She is too dutiful to defy her parents.”

And she did. I saw her once more—only once. She came to me, pale but steady, and said, “Good-bye, dear George.” Then she was gone.

The days that followed were torment. I kept my word and stayed away, but by night I haunted their street, watching her window. I bribed her brothers with cakes and coins, dined with them, clung to any thread of connection. The doctor who attended her became my sole confidant, for he had known sorrow himself and pitied me. He told me she was ill—not gravely, but listless, fading. “She bears it with angelic patience,” he said.

Then came a morning when, waiting in his parlor, I saw a veiled figure. A voice said, “Don’t you know me, George?” And there was Hetty—dear, brave Hetty—who had stolen away to bring me comfort.

But that is another tale.
