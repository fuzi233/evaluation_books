It would have been far stranger still if the local men lingering on the riverbank that evening, armed with nothing but vague guesses and the faint, half-erased smudges of footprints in the damp grass, had ever guessed what had unfolded there. The event that had just played out on that very spot was, taken all together, something almost without parallel in the whole recorded history of mankind—perhaps to remain unmatched for all ages to come. It shall be told plainly, a few words from me first, and then the rest straight from the mouth of one who stood in the thick of it.

When Gerard had finished scrawling his brief, bitter final adieu to his friends Pietro and Andrea, he slipped away down to the river as dusk bled purple over the water. He had gone about his errand with that cold, dogged resolution common to people who have set their hearts on self-destruction and will not be turned aside. He stuffed every last silver groat and copper farthing he owned into the pockets of his tunic, heavy weights to drag him straight down to the riverbed, no chance of floating back to the world he hated. Thus laden, he hurried to the stretch of stream he had marked out days before: a lonely, untrafficked bend, far from the washerwomen’s stepping stones and the fishermen’s mooring posts.

There are people, especially women, who hover on riverbanks half-hoping someone will spot them and step in to save them. But Gerard scanned every shadow only to make sure no one was there to stop him. To his sharp irritation, he spotted a single figure leaning against the mouth of a dark alley, watching him. He affected to stroll idly away, waited a few minutes, then circled back to his intended spot. Lo, the same man had drifted out of a side street, loitering casually right between him and the water.

“Is he watching me? Can he possibly know what I came here for?” Gerard thought. “Impossible. I have no enemy alive who would care what I do.” He strode briskly off, walked two blocks over, made a wide detour and came back a second time. The man had vanished. But when Gerard turned in a full circle to confirm he was truly alone, there he was, a few yards behind him, pretending to kneel and fasten his bootlace. The moonlight glinted cold off a steel gauntlet peeking out from the edge of the stranger’s cloak. This is an assassin, Gerard realized.

Strangely, it never once crossed his mind that he was the man’s intended target. He had no idea he had made a foe in the world. He walked straight up to the waiting bravo, face tight with desperate eagerness.
“Good friend,” he cried, clutching the man’s wrist, “sell me the use of thy arm! One single stroke, that is all I ask. Look—here is every coin I own, take all of it!” He forced the crumpled, heavy handful of silver and copper into the assassin’s free palm. “I beg of thee, do one act of mercy tonight, and rid me of this hateful, worthless life!” Even as he spoke, he fumbled open the laces of his doublet and bared his gaunt, pale breast to the blade he expected to meet.

The bravo stared back at him, frozen, as if stunned into immobility.
“Why do ye hesitate?” Gerard shrieked, his despair curdling into rage. “Hast no pity at all? Is it such a great labor to lift thine arm and strike? Is it because I have no gold to give thee, only this paltry change? Coward! Thou art only fit to stab men in the back, never face one eye to eye and strike! Go on then—turn thy head, hold thy tongue, and I will do the work myself!” With a snarl of pure contempt, he turned and ran full tilt for the dark current, throwing himself headfirst into the cold, churning water before the other man could move. The last sound on his lips as he went under was a single soft cry: “Margaret!”

The heavy, resounding splash jolted the bravo out of his stupor. He let out a sharp, strangled cry of his own, ran to the edge of the bank, and plunged straight into the icy river after the man he had been sent to kill.
What follows will be told in the assassin’s own words.
