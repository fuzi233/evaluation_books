Felix had not inherited his father's inability to generate wealth. Over the span of twenty years, since Thomas departed for Europe, he had amassed a fortune. While he refused to share this accumulation with his aging father, he was entirely content to see it expended on their shared vendetta. This financial backing explained the stern instruction Felix delivered to his brother before departing for Montgomery:

"Spend the money; spend whatever you choose. When your mission concludes, there will still be funds remaining for your leisure."

Thomas nodded solemnly. *The laborer is worthy of his hire,* he thought internally. Turning his gaze around the sparse, empty walls—which now seemed to lack any justification for existing since his father's passing—he asked, "And you? Will you stay here?"

Felix's reply was curt yet absolute. "No. I depart for New York tomorrow. I have secured a residence there, which you may eventually wish to inhabit. Under the alias 'Adams, Felix Adams,' I have leased it. Address me thus in Montgomery. The name 'Cadwalader' must never leave your lips in this town, nor must you forget that my identity is widely known there; otherwise, our reliance on your efforts for the success of our revenge would be compromised." A smile played on his lips, revealing his full awareness that he possessed the superior handsomeness of the two brothers. "Now, regarding the introductions from Paris that we instructed you to secure..."

***

The narrative of the ensuing weeks is best illuminated by examining the correspondence Thomas sent to Felix alongside a private journal maintained by Thomas for his own introspection. The letters, presented in the left column, offer a factual summary of events. The entries, in the right column, chronicle Thomas's internal emotional landscape. Both perspectives are essential to understanding the shifting dynamics of the situation.

*I am here; I have seen her. As you predicted, she is a pale blonde. Tomorrow, I shall present my credentials to John Poindexter. Based on my initial interactions, I anticipate a favorable reception.*

*I could not write the true account of today's encounter to Felix. Why? And why must I record it here? Perhaps to distract my mind from dwelling on it? Maybe. I struggle to comprehend my own feelings, torn between a growing dread of my task and an ardent drive to complete it.*

*I have seen her. I reported this to Felix, omitting the location of our meeting and the circumstances. How could I explain it? Would he understand how a descendant of the Poindexters could perform a gracious act? Or how I, consumed by a purpose that has blackened my heart like hellfire since I embraced it, could find that very heart swelling and my resolve sinking at the first glimpse of the face I have sworn to dedicate to agony and tears? Surely, Felix would have been stronger. Yet...*

*I traveled from the train station directly to the cemetery. Before attending to my lodging or settling in, I sought Evelyn's grave. It was the place where, nineteen years prior, my father had raised me—a four-year-old child—holding me aloft as a threat toward John Poindexter's home. I managed to locate the old, neglected stone marking her resting place. Bending in the golden sunset light to inspect it, I was startled by the rustle of a woman's skirt. A young girl approached, bathed in a nimbus of rosy light that seemed to elevate her from the earth, lending an ethereal quality to her delicate frame and strangely illuminated hair, a radiance her pure features and tender demeanor confirmed. Clutched in her arms was a massive bouquet of snow-white lilies. Noticing her eyes were fixed not on me, but on the grave beside which I stood, I stepped into the shadow of nearby bushes to observe her as she scattered these flowers—symbols of innocence—over the mound I had just visited.*

*What did this signify? Who was this young girl honoring the grave of my long-buried sister with such graceful tribute? As she rose from her task, I could no longer suppress my emotion or the intense curiosity her actions had sparked. Stepping forward, I greeted her with the utmost respect appropriate to her appearance. Observing that her countenance was even more radiant when lifted in conversation than when bowed in solemnity over her blooms, I asked her, in the detached tone of a stranger, who lay buried here and why a girl of her youth would drop flowers upon a grave whose moss-covered stone indicated it had been opened decades before her birth.*

*Her response struck me with the force of a thunderclap, amidst a life recently filled with startling revelations. "I strew flowers here," she replied, "because the girl interred beneath this stone shares my birthday. I never saw her, it is true, but she perished in my father's house when she was no older than I am today. Since becoming a woman and realizing the tragedy of dying young, I have made it a custom to share my birthday blossoms with her. They say she was a lily in both appearance and character, so I bring her lilies."*

***

*I have shaken the hand of John Poindexter. To gain favor with the daughter, I must please the father, or at least capture his attention. I believe I have achieved this.*

*I no longer feel like a true man. John Poindexter appears cold, his manner is rigid, and his opinions are inflexible. Yet, he does not inspire the abhorrence I anticipated, nor does he awaken the singular thought owed to the memory of my sister. Is it because he is Eva's father? Has the daughter's loveliness cast a protective halo over the parent? If so, Felix has every right to execrate me and my father for...*

***

*The introductions you provided have granted me access everywhere. There is considerable wealth here and many magnificent estates. Consequently, I find myself immersed in a congenial society, of which she is the undisputed star. Did I mention that he, as of old, remains the chief man of the town?*

*She is beautiful. She possesses the delicacy of a lily and the vibrant flush of a rose. Yet, it is not her beauty that moves me; it is the strange sweetness of her nature. Remarkably, this sweetness contains no weakness; on the contrary, it exhibits a peculiar strength that becomes instantly apparent when duty calls. Could Felix have imagined such a Poindexter? I cannot reconcile such loveliness with the execrable sin that invites vengeance upon this household. I cannot even dwell upon my past life. Everything dark, threatening, secretive, and vengeful slips from my grasp under her gaze. Instead, I dream of what is pure, true, satisfying, and ennobling. And this transformation occurs solely through the influence of her smile, rather than her words. Have I been given an angel to degrade? Or am I so blinded that I perceive a saint where others—Felix, perhaps—would see merely a pretty woman with unexpected attractions?*

***

*Rides, dances, games, and general frivolity. My interest in this young lady is beginning to be publicly acknowledged. She alone seems unaware of it. Sometimes I wonder if our scheme will fail due to her impassivity and her more-than-conventional innocence. I fear she may never learn to love me. Nevertheless, I have exerted myself to please her. Indeed, I could not have tried harder. Today, I rode twenty-five miles on horseback to procure a trifling item she fancied.*

*Everything will not unfold as easily as Felix imagines. Eva Poindexter may be a country girl, but she possesses her own standards. Mere grace and accomplishment are insufficient to win her heart. Do I possess the other qualities she demands? That remains to be seen. I hold one quality she never dreams of. Will its shadow overwhelm the rest, causing her naturally pure spirit to recoil from me precisely when I believe I have claimed her? I cannot tell, and this uncertainty creates a personal hell within me. Something deeper, stronger, and more imperious than my revenge compels me to view winning this girl's heart as a necessity. I have forgotten my original purpose in this desire. I have forgotten everything save that she is the one woman of my life, and that I can never rest until her heart is wholly mine. Good heavens! Have I become a slave where I hoped to be a master? Have I, Thomas Cadwalader, surrendered my soul into the keeping of this innocent girl? I do not even pause to inquire. To win her—that is the sole purpose for which I now live.*

***

If she ever comes to love me, it will be with the force of a very strong nature. The pale blonde has a heart.*
