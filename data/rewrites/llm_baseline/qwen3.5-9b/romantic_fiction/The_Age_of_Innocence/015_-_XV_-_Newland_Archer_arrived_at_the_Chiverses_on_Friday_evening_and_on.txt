Friday evening brought Newland Archer to the Chiverses', and Saturday unfolded with the meticulous ritual of a weekend guest at Highbank. By morning, he was already airborne in an ice-boat alongside his hostess and a select group of spirited companions. The afternoon belonged to Reggie, with whom he toured the estate, listening in the grandly appointed stables to lectures on equine excellence that stretched on with impressive gravity. Following tea, he retreated to a shadowed corner of the fire-lit hall to confide with a young lady who had once declared herself shattered by his engagement news but now eagerly detailed her own romantic prospects. As the clock neared midnight, the festivities escalated: he helped deposit a goldfish into a guest's bed, costumed a burglar within a nervous aunt's bathroom, and participated in a pillow fight that swept from the nurseries down to the basement.

Sunday brought a shift in pace. After luncheon, Archer borrowed a cutter and drove toward Skuytercliff. Legend held that the house there was an Italian villa—a belief held fast by those who had never visited Italy, and surprisingly, by some who had. Built by Mr. van der Luyden in his youth upon his return from the Grand Tour, anticipating his marriage to Miss Louisa Dagonet, the structure was a large, square wooden edifice. Its walls, painted pale green and white, featured tongued-and-grooved siding, a Corinthian portico, and fluted pilasters framing the windows. Perched on high ground, a series of terraces bordered by balustrades and urns cascaded in the style of a steel engraving down to a small, irregular lake edged in asphalt and draped with rare weeping conifers. To the sides, famous weedless lawns dotted with "specimen" trees rolled outward to distant ranges of grass crowned with intricate cast-iron ornaments. Below, nestled in a hollow, lay the four-roomed stone house constructed by the first Patroon on land granted in 1612.

Against the stark sheet of snow and the grey winter sky, the Italian villa loomed somewhat grimly. Even in summer, it maintained a formidable distance; the boldest beds of coleus dared not venture closer than thirty feet from its imposing front. As Archer rang the bell, the prolonged tinkle seemed to reverberate through a mausoleum. When the butler eventually appeared, his surprise was palpable, as if he had been roused from a final slumber. Fortunately, Archer was of the family. Despite the irregularity of his visit, he was entitled to learn that the Countess Olenska was absent, having departed for afternoon services with Mrs. van der Luyden precisely forty-five minutes prior.

"The Master," the butler continued, "is present, sir. However, I suspect he is either concluding his nap or reviewing yesterday's *Evening Post*. I recall hearing him mention, upon returning from church this morning, his intention to peruse the paper after luncheon. Should you wish, sir, I could approach the library door and eavesdrop—"

Archer thanked him politely, stating he would proceed to meet the ladies. Relieved, the butler closed the door with majestic finality. A groom took the cutter to the stable, and Archer strode through the park onto the main road. The village of Skuytercliff lay only a mile and a half away, yet he knew Mrs. van der Luyden never walked; he must stay on the road to intercept the carriage.

Soon, descending a footpath crossing the highway, he spotted a slender figure in a red cloak, accompanied by a large dog bounding ahead. He quickened his pace. Madame Olenska halted abruptly, offering a welcoming smile.

"Oh, you've come!" she exclaimed, withdrawing her hand from her muff. The red cloak rendered her vibrant and lively, reminiscent of the Ellen Mingott of yore. Laughing, he took her hand and replied, "I came to discover what you were fleeing from."

Her expression darkened. "Well—you shall see, presently."

The response baffled him. "Do you mean you've been overtaken?"

She shrugged, executing a slight movement akin to Nastasia's, before adopting a lighter tone. "Shall we walk? I am quite chilled after the sermon. Besides, what matters now that you are here to protect me?"

Blood rushed to his temples as he seized a fold of her cloak. "Ellen—what is it? You must tell me."

"Oh, later! Let us run a race first; my feet are frozen to the ground," she cried. Gathering the cloak tight, she dashed across the snow, the dog leaping around her with playful barks.

For a moment, Archer watched, captivated by the flash of the red streak against the white expanse. Then he launched himself after her. They met, breathless and laughing, at a wicket leading into the park. She looked up, smiling. "I knew you'd come!"

"That proves you wanted me to," he returned, his joy swelling disproportionately to their jest. The white glitter of the trees filled the air with a mysterious luminescence, and as they traversed the snow, the ground seemed to sing beneath their feet.

"Where did you come from?" Madame Olenska asked.

He recounted his journey, adding, "It was because I received your note."

After a pause, her voice carrying a faint chill, she said, "May requested you to watch over me."

"I required no request."

"You imply—I am so manifestly helpless and defenseless? What a pitiful creature you must all consider me! Yet women here seemingly... never feel the need: no more than the blessed in heaven."

He lowered his voice. "What manner of need?"

"Ah, do not ask! I do not speak your tongue," she retorted petulantly.

The answer struck him like a physical blow. He stood motionless in the path, gazing down at her. "And what did I come for, if I do not speak yours?"

"My friend—" She placed a light hand on his arm. He pleaded earnestly, "Ellen—why refuse to tell me what has occurred?"

Another shrug. "Does anything ever transpire in heaven?"

Silence fell between them as they walked a few yards without exchange. Finally, she spoke. "I will tell you—but where, where, where? One cannot find solitude for a single minute in that vast seminary of a house, with every door ajar and servants constantly delivering tea, firewood, or newspapers! Is there no corner in an American home where one may exist alone? You are so shy, yet so public. I always feel as though I am in a convent—or on a stage before a dreadfully polite audience that offers no applause."

"You dislike us!" Archer exclaimed.

They passed the residence of the old Patroon, its squat walls and small, square windows clustered tightly around a central chimney. Shutters stood wide, and through a freshly washed window, Archer glimpsed the glow of a fire.

"Why—the house is open!" he noted.

"No; only for today, at least. I wished to inspect it, and Mr. van der Luyden had kindled the fire and opened the windows so we might rest there on our way back from church this morning."

She ascended the steps and tested the door. "Still unlocked—what fortune! Enter, and we may speak quietly. Mrs. van der Luyden has driven to visit her aunts in Rhinebeck; we shall not be missed for another hour."

He followed her into the narrow passage. Spirits, previously dampened by her earlier words, surged upward with an irrational leap. The modest cottage stood there, its wood paneling and brass fixtures gleaming in the firelight, as if magically conjured to receive them. A deep bed of embers glowed in the kitchen chimney beneath an iron pot suspended from an ancient crane. Rush-bottomed armchairs faced one another across the tiled hearth, while rows of Delft plates lined the wall shelves. Archer stooped to toss a log onto the embers. Madame Olenska, removing her cloak, seated herself in one of the chairs. Archer leaned against the chimney, studying her.

"You laugh now, yet when you wrote, you were wretched," he observed.

"Yes." She paused. "But I cannot feel wretched when you are here."

"I shall not remain long," he rejoined, his lips tightening with the effort to say no more.

"No; I know. But I am improvident: I live in the moment of happiness."

The words slipped through him like a seduction. To shut his mind to it, he stepped away from the hearth, gazing out at the black tree trunks against the snow. Yet, it seemed she had shifted position as well, and he still perceived her, situated between himself and the trees, drooping over the fire with her lazy smile. Archer's heart beat in rebellion. What if she had fled because of him, waiting to reveal the truth only when they were secluded in this private sanctuary?

"Ellen, if I am truly useful to you—if you genuinely desired my presence—confess what is wrong. Tell me what you are escaping," he insisted. He spoke without moving, without turning to face her; if the revelation were to occur, it must happen thus, with the entire breadth of the room separating them, his eyes fixed on the outer snow.
