There is no moment a priest wears his sacred calling more fully than when he stands in the chamber of the newly dead. Gerard performed every last rite the Church ordains for the departed, every prayer, every anointing, exactly as he would have done for the humblest cottager in his Gouda parish. His movements were automatic, his mind reeling under the weight of the sudden, cataclysmic blow that had fallen on him before he could even brace for it; the raw, gnawing pangs of true bereavement had not yet had time to sink in through the stunned numbness. Then, caught up in a sharp, fiery rush of religious devotion, he dropped to his knees and thanked Heaven she had been granted so gentle, pious a Christian end.

No sooner had that prayer left his lips than every thought in his head narrowed to a single purpose: he would not leave her buried among strangers, far from the land she knew. He would bring her home, to lay her in the hallowed ground of his own parish churchyard.

That very same dusk, a single-horse cart draped in heavy black cloth set out on the road to Gouda. Inside the cart rested a plain lead coffin, and beside it Gerard lay huddled, his arms slung tight over its cold surface, his chin propped on the lid. The short, fierce burst of energy that had carried him through all the hasty, necessary preparations for the journey had burned out entirely, leaving him crushed, clinging to the only piece of her he had left.

The carter who had hired out the cart walked at the horse’s head the whole miles, never speaking a word to the huddled mourner behind him. Even when he stopped to bait the beast, he spoke only in a soft whisper, his voice unconsciously hushed out of reverence for that quiet, searing agony unfolding behind him. When they halted for the night at a roadside inn, he and the landlord tried gently, out of simple kindness, to draw Gerard away from the coffin to eat a crust of bread and stretch out for a few hours of rest. But Gerard beat them back, and when they pressed their plea, he almost snarled at them like a loyal hound guarding its dead master, clinging to the cold lead lid through the whole long night. The two men said no more after that, laid a thick wool cloak over his huddled shoulders, and left him to his vigil.

By noon the next day, the sorrow-laden cart rumbled up the drive of Gerard’s manse. A full score of his parishioners had gathered there, each with some small, trivial trouble to bring to their parson—they had already felt the gap left by his absence, and had come seeking his counsel. When they saw the black draped cart, and their gentle, steadfast healer bowed almost to breaking point beneath his grief, they lifted a loud, unfeigned wail of sorrow. The sound cut through the thick, numb lethargy of Gerard’s mourning, and he blinked, saw the familiar faces of his flock all clustered around him, and tried to speak. “Oh, my children! My children!” he cried, but the words choked in his throat, thick with anguish, and he could say nothing more.

The next day, despite every protest and plea from his parishioners that he rest and let another priest lead the service, Gerard insisted on burying her himself. He read the full burial rite over the open grave, his voice only trembling now and then, steady in its devotion. A hundred hot tears fell on the dark earth piled beside her resting place. When the last words of the service were spoken, he did not move. He stood there like a marble statue, rooted to the spot, until the crowd of mourners slipped quietly out of the churchyard, out of respect for his private grief.

He stood lost in a waking dream for what felt like hours, until the sexton—an ordinarily decent man, but as unthinking as most folk who work with death day in and day out—picked up his spade and began shoveling earth down into the grave, no warning given. At the first heavy thud of soil landing on the lid of her coffin, Gerard let out a high, piercing scream that split the quiet of the churchyard.

The sexton froze mid-motion, his spade hanging in the air. Gerard staggered, one hand flying up to press hard against his chest. The sexton caught him before he could fall, shouting for help. Jorian Ketel, who had lingered at the edge of the churchyard mourning the benefactress who had been so kind to him, ran over at once, and the two men half-carried, half-supported the reeling Gerard back to the manse.

“Ah, Jorian! Good, faithful Jorian!” gasped Gerard, his voice thin and shaking. “Something snapped inside me. I felt it. I heard it, right here, Jorian—right here.” He pressed his palm hard against his breast, his face ashen white.
