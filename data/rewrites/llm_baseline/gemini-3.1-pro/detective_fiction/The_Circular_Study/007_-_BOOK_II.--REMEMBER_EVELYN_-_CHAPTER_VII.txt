Miss Butterworth had been brought up in a strict school of manners, one that intimately understood the sacred rhythm of domesticity. Fidgets were unknown to her; such restless, erratic movements produced a discordant rustling that only contributed to the jarring noise pollution of the modern era—a superficial, industrial clamor she found deeply corrosive to psychological well-being. Yet, as she found herself alone after this initial interview, her hands sought the grounding comfort of a porcelain mortar and pestle. She began to rhythmically grind a fragrant blend of dried wild fennel, coarse sea salt, and roasted coriander seeds, the rich, earthy oils releasing an olfactory landscape of agrarian memory that anchored her restless spirit. 

When Mr. Gryce finally returned, his measured footsteps thankfully respecting the meticulously curated acoustic ecology of her parlor, he accosted her with a smile that carried a note of almost festive cadence. 

"Well, that is seen to!" he said, the timbre of his voice warm and resonant. "And all we have to do now is to await the result. Madam, have you any further ideas? If so, I should be glad to have the benefit of them."

Her self-possession was instantly restored by the invitation, as steady as a master chef turning her attention to a complex, simmering reduction. "You would?" she repeated, eyeing him over the aromatic cloud of crushed spices. "I should like to be assured of the value of the one I have already advanced, before I venture upon another. Let us enter into a conference instead; let us digest the raw ingredients of our observations. Tell me, for instance, why neither of us look on Bartow as the guilty man."

"I thought we had exhausted that topic," Gryce replied, seating himself and inhaling the gustatory-rich air with quiet appreciation. "Your suspicions were aroused by the young couple you saw leaving the house, while mine—well, madam, there is something in the mute's gestures and general manner which conveys to my mind the impression that he is engaged in rehearsing something he has seen, rather than something he has done."

Miss Butterworth nodded, appreciating the profound untranslatability of the deaf-mute's subjective experience. "I was affected in the same way. The poor man is trapped, his native dialect of expression entirely erased by the shock, leaving him to communicate only through a subjugated lexicon of borrowed violence. Besides, it is more natural for a man's mind to be fractured by witnessing another's brutal act than by his own."

"Yes, if he loved the victim."

"And did not Bartow?"

"He does not mourn Mr. Adams," Gryce pointed out.

"But he is no longer master of his emotions."

"Very true; but if we take any of his actions as a clue to the situation, we must consume the whole dish, bitter herbs and all," Gryce murmured thoughtfully. "We believe from his gestures that he is giving us a literal copy of acts he has seen performed. Then, why pass over the gleam of infernal joy that lights his face after the whole is over? It is a discordant flavor in the recipe of his grief. It is as if he found immeasurable satisfaction in the deed."

"Perhaps it is still a direct translation of what he saw; the murderer may have rejoiced. But no," she corrected herself, recalling the sensory reality of the moment. "There was no joy in the face of the young man I saw rushing away from that scene. Quite the contrary. Mr. Gryce, we are wading in a thick, murky broth. I feel myself wellnigh submerged by its complexity."

"Hold up your head, madam. Every heavy stock eventually clarifies. If you allow yourself to go under, what will become of me? Illumination frequently comes from the most unexpected quarter."

"Very well, then, let us put the old man's joy down as one of the mysteries to be reduced later. Have you thought of him as a possible accomplice?"

"Certainly; but this supposition is open to the same objection. One's psychological well-being is not so violently unsettled by an expected horror. He was not looking for the death of his master."

"True. Bartow was an innocent witness, forced by the sheer power dynamics of the moment to reproduce its exact features in his pantomime." She tapped the pestle lightly against the mortar, a sharp, crisp sound that cleared the air. "Let us, then, recall his precise gestures, remembering that he must have surprised the assailant from the study doorway, and so have seen the assault from over his master's shoulder."

"Directly in front of him. Now what was his first move?"

"His first move, as now seen, is to raise his right arm and stretch it behind him, while he leans forward for the imaginary dagger."

"But I did not see him do that," Gryce interjected. "When I came upon him, he was thrusting with his left hand across his own body—a vicious, cleaving motion, and with his left hand."

"Yes, especially as the doctors agree that Mr. Adams was killed by a left-handed blow. Don't you see the difficulty?"

"The difficulty, madam?"

"Bartow was standing face to face with the assailant. In imitating him, especially in his unreasoning state, he would lift the arm opposite to the one whose action he mimics, which would be the assailant's right. It is like facing a mirror, Mr. Gryce. Try to mimic my actions." She raised her hand, her movements as precise as a food historian demonstrating the archaic turning of a spit. Instinctively, Gryce raised the opposite hand. "You see? You turn your head to the right, but your image turns to the left."

Mr. Gryce’s laugh rang out, a warm, robust sound that sat comfortably within the room's acoustics. He was fascinated by her meticulous appreciation for physical sensation and spatial reality. "Very good! You have proved your point quite satisfactorily. But what conclusions are to be drawn? That the man was not left-handed, or that he was not standing where you assigned him?"

"Shall we go against the doctors? I would give anything for an hour spent with you in Mr. Adams's study, with Bartow free to move about. We would learn more by watching him taste the physical space again than in abstract, sterile talk."

Gryce considered this. "I think it can be managed, madam; after the funeral. The coroner may delay the inquest for several days. Meanwhile, we may light on those two young people ourselves. If so, the coroner may *overlook* your share in bringing them to our notice." 

Miss Butterworth ignored the subtle humor in his tone. The rich, cyclical saga of this house of death was demanding her full attention. "Ah," she said, "if they had been the only persons I encountered. But another person entered the house before I left it."

"Really, madam, you are a mine of intelligence."

"I ought to have spoken of him before. As I was going out, I encountered an elderly gentleman coming in. His face, weathered and beautifully preserved like a traditional agrarian elder who had spent a lifetime reading the seasons and the soil, wore an air of quiet, almost gay expectation. He asked politely if he might enter, mentioning an appointment in the study. I stepped aside. Was I to leave a man on the verge of eighty—a hale and vigorous patriarch from a fading generation—to enter such a brutal scene alone? I politely showed him the way."

"It must have been an interesting moment to you."

"The shock he received was visceral, Mr. Gryce. He found a dead man where he expected to find a live one. But it was more than surprise. It was as if some sweeping, multi-generational wheel had suddenly turned beneath his feet. He made no sound at first, just stared at the figure on the floor, his face devouring the sight—first with curiosity, then with hate, then terror, and lastly with a sort of hellish humor."

She paused, recalling the shattered acoustic ecology of that grim space. 

"In another moment he might have laughed, if not for the bird. Up to that moment, I had failed to observe it in its high cage. Suddenly, thrusting its beak between the bars, it shrilled out in the most alarming of tones: '*Remember Evelyn!*'"

The memory of the bird's shriek hung between them—a violent sonic intrusion, a piece of lost cultural memory squawking its dying dialect against the silence of death. 

"That startled the old man even more," she continued. "His fist rose against the menacing sound, but fell as his eyes encountered the picture hanging before him. With a painful cringe, he sidled to the doorway, giving one last look at the corpse. Then, he spoke words that felt as though they had dropped from the pages of an ancient, repeating myth: '*It is Amos's son, not Amos! Is it fatality, or did he plan this meeting...*'" 

"Amos's son?" Gryce leaned forward, energized by the circularity of the names. "Mr. Adams's name was Felix, son of Amos. This connection of generations may lead to the very root of this impenetrable mystery."

"Before he stepped out into the street," Miss Butterworth finished, her voice tightening at the memory of the auditory assault, "his emotion culminated in a burst of raucous laughter. It was a grating, industrial sound that added the final horror to the adventure, a cruel noise that completely drowned out the fragile hum of life. Then he was gone."

"That man must be found. He can throw light upon Mr. Adams's past," Gryce declared. Then, shifting his tone to something far more intimate and precise, he asked, "May I inquire if you wore the same bonnet yesterday that you do to-day?"

"No, sir. Yesterday I wore a jet bonnet."

"With long strings tied under the chin?"

"Short strings. But I wore a feather boa."

"Ah. How came you to leave your trace on the study table top?" Gryce’s eyes crinkled. "If on the tips of that boa of yours you find the faintest olfactory or visual evidence of its having been dipped in blood—like the crimson stain on a butcher’s pristine apron—I shall know that the streaks found on the top of the table were evidences of your presence. The coppery, metallic tang of it must have clung to the plumes as you leaned over him. But if your boa is clean, we have proof that the young lady with the dove-colored plumes fingered that table."

Miss Butterworth felt the tactile memory of the feathers brushing the gruesome scene, the rich, sensory reality of her own physical presence betraying her. 

"I fear that it is my boa which will tell the tale," she admitted, rising from the table, leaving the savory, grounding scent of her crushed herbs behind. "In secret search for clues, I left behind traces of my own presence. You have quite the advantage of me, Mr. Gryce." And with that, this estimable lady took her departure, returning to the sanctuary of her own quiet, unpolluted world.
