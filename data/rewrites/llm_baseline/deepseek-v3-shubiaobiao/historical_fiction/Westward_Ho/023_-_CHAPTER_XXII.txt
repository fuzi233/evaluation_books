The tale I must now tell is a dark one—a fleeting glimpse into an abyss of human cruelty. Yet it must be told, for in its horror lies the truth of what English souls once faced in those merciless days.

Three weeks have passed. The scene is no longer the open sea or the wild coasts of the Indies but a dim, suffocating corridor in the heart of Cartagena. The air is thick with the scent of damp stone and despair. A door stands ajar, and within its confines, two cloaked figures linger—one familiar, one unknown.

Eustace Leigh is the first. The second, a silent attendant of the Holy Office, holds a lamp aloft. Its flickering light spills over a bed of straw and the figure of a sleeping man. The face is pale, the brow high and noble—Frank Leigh, spared from the savagery of the jungle only to face the calculated malice of men who call themselves holy.

He had endured the question that very afternoon. Now Eustace, the architect of his betrayal, has come to reason with him—or ensnare him? Even Eustace does not know. He tells himself he would give his life to save his cousin, yet what is his life now but a tool of higher wills? Duty has led him here, and duty demands he wake the sleeper, coax or threaten him into recantation while his spirit is still raw from the rack.

But Frank sleeps peacefully. Is it a trick of the lamplight, or does a faint smile linger on his lips? Eustace leans closer, the flame trembling in his grasp. Then he hears it—a whisper, soft as a breath: his mother’s name, and then another, holier still.

Eustace steps back. “Let him rest,” he murmurs. “I fear my words will do little good.”

The familiar’s voice is dry. “None, I think. He scoffed at their holinesses. A heretic to the bone.”

Eustace’s jaw tightens. “The devil’s power is great indeed.”

They move on, but Eustace hesitates as they pass another door. A woman’s voice rises within—sharp with pain, trembling with defiance.

“Witchcraft? Against Don Guzman? What need had I of witchcraft, in God’s name?”

A murmur follows, indistinct, then a sound that stops Eustace’s heart—a wail, raw and rending, tearing through the stone walls. He staggers, clutching at the corridor’s edge, but his companion pulls him away.

The cries follow him, even as he stumbles into the open air, into a garden bathed in moonlight, lush and perfumed as paradise. But there is no escape. The echoes of her agony cling to him, relentless.

*What have I done?* The question claws at him. *Was there no other way?*

His companion’s voice is smooth, almost amused. “The Holy Office is merciful. Pain purifies the soul.”

Eustace nods, his throat tight. “Yes—yes. A father’s chastisement.”

“And the evidence was clear. Her companion, that English crone—you yourself named her a witch.”

“I did. There was no choice.” He speaks as much to convince himself as the other. “The flesh is weak, but my spirit is willing.”

Yet that night, Eustace seeks the head of the Jesuits. He speaks in broken confessions, begging for release—for exile, for any escape from the torment of his own making. The Jesuit listens, then arranges his passage far from Cartagena. By dawn, Eustace is gone.

Where? It does not matter.

Eustace Leigh is no longer a man. He is a instrument, a shadow without will. He has surrendered his soul to the Society, believing it salvation. But in truth, he is dead—twice dead.

Let the dead bury their dead. Our story has no more room for Eustace Leigh.
