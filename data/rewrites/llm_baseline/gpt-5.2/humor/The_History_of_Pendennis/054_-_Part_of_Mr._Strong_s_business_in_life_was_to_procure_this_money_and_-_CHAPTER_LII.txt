Doctor Portman’s letter went off to London with all the solemnity of a parish procession: sealed, directed, stamped, and entrusted to that iron-hearted creature the post, which will carry a love-letter and a dun alike, without any flutter of compassion. The worthy clergyman, having thus discharged ink and conscience together, set himself to the more delicate business of calming Mrs. Pendennis—who, being a gentlewoman of strong feelings, did not find it in the least calming to be told, every quarter of an hour, that she ought to be calm.

For a day or two, too, there was no question of her rushing up to town like an injured prophetess and confronting her son amid his sins and his lodging-house furniture. The apothecary, that domestic tyrant with a pill-box for sceptre, forbade her to stir so much as to Fairoaks. And so, on the following morning, there she was again installed upon her sofa at home, with Laura by her side—faithful as a shadow, and almost as silent.

Unluckily—for him, for her, for everybody who had any stake in Arthur Pendennis’s skin and soul—Pen never read the Doctor’s homily until weeks after it had been written. The letter, poor thing, travelled punctually enough: it was posted; it was delivered into the Lamb Court box; it was carried upstairs with the rest of “his lordship’s correspondence;” it was laid on his writing-table by Mrs. Flanagan the laundress (the same domestic minister whose zeal for spirits we have had occasion to observe). But letters, like dinners, require a consumer; and Pen, who could be induced to swallow a tumbler of punch at an hour’s notice, had a singular inability to take in advice.

Day after day the widow waited for a reply—some word, any word—while her illness fed upon each unanswered post. Laura had to carry, not only her own anxiety, but Helen’s suffering; and then, as if that were not enough, to endure that cold estrangement which pain will sometimes set up between hearts that most need to cling. She did it as she did everything: without parade, without clatter—like good cooking, which is never the dish itself, but makes the dish possible. Her strength came, not with trumpet and testimonial, but in the quiet of her own chamber; and therefore we must speak of it lightly, as one speaks of a fragrance that would be spoiled by being pointed at. Only this: a good woman is a rare herb; she seasons the whole household, and yet sits modestly at the back of the cupboard.

Meanwhile Pen’s chief enemy—whom many of us know and harbour and pamper—was not a rival editor, nor a malicious aunt, nor an underbred porter with a grievance. It was Mr. Pen himself. He had indulgenced that gentleman all his life; and, like every spoiled servant, the fellow had grown insolent. The moment anyone proposed a little wholesome self-denial, Mr. Self stamped and swore, upset the tray, and declared he would not be controlled.

Pen had resolved—resolved magnificently—that he would not see Fanny; and he didn’t. He tried to banish her by all the recognised recipes for banishing a thought: by work, by exercise, by society, by dissipation, by walking and riding until his very bones had opinions; by eating, drinking, and smoking as if he meant to cure his heart by pickling it. The cigars smoked; the punch went down with a friendly gurgle; the streets of London rang with wheels and footsteps; the very chimneys had their soot-stifled cough. Yet, in all that city clatter—the constant rasp of urban life that never lets the mind hear its own birds—little Fanny’s image stayed, as a tune stays, and will not be drowned. At the end of a week of this heroic discipline, our young gentleman’s triumph was complete: he achieved a fever, and took to bed in Lamb Court.

Let any philanthropist who values matrimony as a public institution employ Cruikshank or Leech, and have illustrated for the nation, in a series of plates, “The Horrors of a Bachelor’s Chambers.” Begin with the lonely breakfast: the black kettle sulking on a fire that is either indecently hot in midsummer or offensively dead at Christmas; the room smelling of yesterday’s smoke and to-day’s damp; the master shivering while he plays housemaid—hunting coals, coaxing flame, and discovering that Mrs. Flanagan is absent without leave. Proceed to the bachelor’s shirt—wanted at dinner-time, found with buttons missing like teeth in a drunkard. Add the bachelor’s return after a merry country Christmas: he lights his dismal candle at the sputtering stair-lamp, enters the blank familiar room, and finds that the only things waiting to greet him, as if they had affection, are the Christmas bills spread out on his table like cards in a hand. Crown the whole with “The Bachelor Ill”—and the Temple will be depopulated, and the rents will fall, and young men will fly into respectable domesticity as into a refuge.

To be well in chambers is lonely enough; but to be ill there is to discover how thin a civilisation is when it comes to a glass of water at two in the morning. Long nights, hot pillow, ticking watch, medicine administered by one’s own feverish hand; no kindly foot on the stair, no familiar voice, no soft household rustle—nothing but the city’s distant grinding and rattling, like a great machine that does not care whether you live or die so long as it turns. We will not dwell on the tragedy. We will only pity those who endure it, and in particular Mr. Arthur Pendennis when his hour came.

He went to bed one night ill, and woke the next morning worse. Besides Mrs. Flanagan and her moral support (which chiefly consisted of moistened sympathy), his only visitor was the Printer’s Devil from the Pall Mall Gazette, to be fed—like a young raven—with copy. Pen struggled to satisfy him; and each effort at political wisdom and leading-article fire only blew upon his fever. Shandon was away; Warrington was not in town; and the Gazette’s columns looked as blank as a larder when the cook has fainted.

Mr. Finucane, warm-hearted Irishman, rushed up, found Pen in a state unfit for either party politics or human company, and—like a man who will put his shoulder to the cart even when it is laden with other people’s nonsense—set about doing what he could. The paper next day blossomed (if that be the word) with pieces in which Ireland rose like yeast through every paragraph. The genius and virtue of that injured country bubbled magnificently from his pen; and Shandon, enjoying himself at Boulogne-sur-Mer, recognised the hand at once, and laughed as he tossed the sheet to his wife. “Look here, Mary, my dear—here is Jack at work again.”

Indeed Jack had a faithful habit of remembering, whatever the subject, that Rafferty was the greatest painter in Europe; that the Academy’s refusal to make him an R.A. was petty jealousy; that Mr. Rooney, M.P., was “generally reported in the West End” to be appointed Governor of Barataria; and that any political crisis could be improved by a compliment to the Round Towers or the Giant’s Causeway. Besides doing Pen’s work, he offered—good fellow—to spend his Saturday and Sunday nursing Arthur; but Pen, who could still be proud when he could no longer be well, insisted the other should take his holiday, and declared (with that brave selfishness sick people often call independence) that he could bear it best alone.

Finucane, taking his supper at the Back Kitchen on Friday night after the paper had been got through, mentioned Pen’s illness to Captain Costigan. The Captain remembered it two days later, and on Sunday afternoon marched to Lamb Court to pay his visit.

He found Mrs. Flanagan in tears in the sitting-room, and extracted from her, between sob and sniff, a report as gloomy as her gin-bottle was cheerful. Pen had alarmed her; and she, to enable herself to bear the shock of his sufferings, had been obliged to have recourse to brandy. Then, hanging about the bed, trying to “minister,” she had become intolerable to the invalid, who begged her peevishly to keep away. Hence her tears, and the redoubled grief, and the renewed application to the bottle which she used as an anodyne. Captain Costigan rated her soundly for her intemperance, and foretold the fatal consequences which must ensue if she persisted—addressing her as if he were a temperance lecturer and she the whole gin-trade.

Pen, fevered as he was, was genuinely pleased at the Captain’s coming. He heard the well-known voice in the outer room, and called eagerly, and thanked him as if he had been brought a banquet. Costigan felt the pulse with great gravity—his own tremulous, clammy hand growing steady for the instant with the importance of the touch. The pulse was racing; the face haggard and hot; the eyes bloodshot and gloomy; and his “bird” (the Captain afterwards pronounced the word with much relish) had not been shaved for nearly a week. Pen made him sit down, and, tossing in that comfortless bed, tried to talk gaily about the Back Kitchen, about Vauxhall and when they should go again—and then, as inevitably as a hungry man comes round to food, he came round to Fanny.

How was little Fanny?

How, indeed. We know how she went home, very sad, the previous Sunday evening—after seeing Arthur light his lamp in his chambers, while he had that interview with Bows. Bows returned presently to his own rooms, and as he passed the lodge looked in upon Mrs. Bolton’s, as was his habit; but with a face so melancholy you might have thought he had been practising adagios on his own heart.

Fanny had a weary night. Restless, she woke the little bedfellows more than once. She dared not read further in Walter Lorraine: her father was at home and would allow no light; so she kept the book under her pillow, feeling for it in the dark as if it were a talisman. She had only just drifted into sleep when the children began to stir with the morning almost as early as the birds—those real birds outside, whose small clean songs were the only music in that quarter not paid for, not advertised, and not banged out to sell somebody’s pleasure.

Though she was angry with Bows, she went to his room at her accustomed hour; and there the good-hearted musician began to talk.

“I saw Mr. Pendennis last night, Fanny,” he said.

“Did you? I thought you did,” she answered, looking fiercely at the melancholy old gentleman.

“I’ve been fond of you ever since we came to live in this place,” he continued. “You were a child when I came; and you used to like me, Fanny, until three or four days ago—until you saw this gentleman.”

“And now, I suppose, you are going to say ill of him,” said Fanny. “Do, Mr. Bows—that will make me like you better.”

“Indeed I shall do no such thing,” Bows answered. “I think he is a very good and honest young man.”

“Indeed! you know that if you said a word against him, I would never speak a word to you again—never!” cried Miss Fanny, clenching her little hand and pacing. Bows watched her—admiring and sorrowful. Her cheeks flushed; her frame trembled; her eyes shone love, anger, defiance all in one beam. “You would like to speak ill of him,” she said; “but you daren’t—you know you daren’t!”

“I knew him many years since,” Bows went on, “when he was almost as young as you are, and he had a romantic attachment for our friend the Captain’s daughter—Lady Mirabel that is now.”

Fanny laughed. “I suppose there was other people, too, that had romantic attachments for Miss Costigan,” she said. “I don’t want to hear about ’em.”

“He wanted to marry her; but their ages were quite disproportionate—and their rank in life. She would not have him because he had no money. She acted very wisely in refusing him; for the two would have been very unhappy, and she wasn’t a fit person to go and live with his family, or to make his home comfortable. Mr. Pendennis has his way to make in the world, and must marry a lady of his own rank. A woman who loves a man will not ruin his prospects, cause him to quarrel with his family, and lead him into poverty and misery for her gratification. An honest girl won’t do that—for her own sake, or for the man’s.”

At that, Fanny’s defiance collapsed into dismay, and dismay into pleading. “What do I know about marrying, Bows?” she cried. “When was there any talk of it? What has there been between this young gentleman and me that’s to make people speak so cruel? It was not my doing; nor Arthur’s—Mr. Pendennis’s—that I met him at Vauxhall. It was the Captain took me and Ma there. We never thought of nothing wrong, I’m sure. He came and rescued us, and he was so very kind. Then he came to call and ask after us; and very, very good it was of such a grand gentleman to be so polite to humble folks like us! And yesterday Ma and me just went to walk in the Temple Gardens, and—and—”

Here she broke out into that unanswerable feminine argument which has silenced more philosophers than logic ever did: she cried. “Oh! I wish I was dead! I wish I was laid in my grave; and had never, never seen him!”

“He said as much himself, Fanny,” Bows said; and she asked through sobs why, why should he wish that? Had she harmed him? She would perish rather than harm him. Whereupon the musician told her of the conversation of the day before, and showed her—gently, painfully—that Pen could not and must not think of her as a wife, and that she, if she valued her honest name, must try to forget him. She promised to avoid the danger; and yet, being convinced in mind and unconvinced in heart (as most of us are, when convinced at all), she went back to the lodge and told her mother everything.

She spoke of her love with that artless extravagance which is half poetry, half hunger. She bewailed the inequality that raised barriers between them; and straightway, as girls do, she borrowed language from the stage to dress feelings which had no words of their own. “There’s the Lady of Lyons,” she said. “Oh, Ma! how I did love Mr. Macready when I saw him do it; and Pauline, for being faithful to poor Claude, and always thinking of him; and he coming back to her, an officer, through all his dangers! And if everybody admires Pauline—and I’m sure everybody does—for being so true to a poor man—why should a gentleman be ashamed of loving a poor girl? Not that Mr. Arthur loves me—oh no, no! I ain’t worthy of him; only a princess is worthy of such a gentleman as him. Such a poet! writing so beautifully, and looking so grand! I am sure he’s a nobleman, and of ancient family, and kep’ out of his estate. Perhaps his uncle has it. Ah, if I might—oh, how I’d serve him, and work for him, and slave for him! I wouldn’t ask for more than that, Ma—just to be allowed to see him of a morning; and sometimes he’d say ‘How d’you, Fanny?’ or ‘God bless you, Fanny!’ as he said on Sunday. And I’d work, and work; and I’d sit up all night, and read, and learn, and make myself worthy of him. The Captain says his mother lives in the country, and is a grand lady there. Oh, how I wish I might go and be her servant, Ma! I can do plenty of things, and work very neat; and—and sometimes he’d come home, and I should see him!”

Her head dropped on her mother’s shoulder, and she cried as if tears were the only language left her. The matron joined in, because mothers will weep with a daughter even while they are laying plans for her. “You mustn’t think no more of him, Fanny,” she said. “If he don’t come to you, he’s a horrid, wicked man.”

“Don’t call him so, Mother,” Fanny pleaded. “He’s the best of men, the best and the kindest. Bows says he thinks he is unhappy at leaving poor little Fanny. It wasn’t his fault, was it, that we met?—and it ain’t his that I mustn’t see him again. He says I mustn’t—and I mustn’t, Mother. He’ll forget me, but I shall never forget him. No! I’ll pray for him, and love him always—until I die—and I shall die, I know I shall—and then my spirit will always go and be with him.”

“You forget your poor mother, Fanny,” Mrs. Bolton said, “and you’ll break my heart by goin on so. Perhaps you will see him. I’m sure you’ll see him. I’m sure he’ll come to-day. If ever I saw a man in love, that man is him. When Emily Budd’s young man first came about her, he was sent away by old Budd—a most respectable man, and violoncello in the orchestra at the Wells—and his own family wouldn’t hear of it neither. But he came back. We all knew he would. Emily always said so; and he married her; and this one will come back too; and you mark a mother’s words and see if he don’t, dear.”

At that point Mr. Bolton entered for his evening meal; and instantly all romance was snuffed like a candle. Mrs. Bolton turned to coaxing and clatter. “Lor, Mr. B., who’d have thought to see you away from the Club of a Saturday night. Fanny, dear, get your pa some supper. What will you have, B.? The poor gurl’s got a gathering in her eye, or somethink in it—I was lookin at it just now as you came in.” She squeezed her daughter’s hand as a signal for prudence and secrecy. Fanny’s tears dried as if she had been paid to dry them. By that wondrous hypocrisy—call it self-defence, call it training—which women practise and nature arms them with, every trace of emotion vanished; she took her work and sat demure in the corner, so that the careless male parent never suspected a thing beyond the mysterious “gathering.”

Thus, as if fate were determined to keep the poor child’s passion at a full boil, everything about her helped to stir it. Her mother applauded it; and the very words Bows used to quench the flame only fanned it. Pen was not wicked: he was high-minded in staying away. Pen loved her: the magnificent youth with the gold chains and the scented auburn hair! And perhaps he did—perhaps he would have five years earlier, before the world hardened the reckless boy; before he learned that society forgives much except being found out; before he grew ashamed of foolish tenderness and throttled it, not as a crime, but as a scandal.

Any respectable person will say he was right not to marry an ill-educated girl of low degree, with relations a gentleman could not acknowledge and manners that would sit oddly in his new station. Any philosopher will add that passions are like summer flies, and should be brushed away; that nobody dies about a woman (or a man) except in novels; that, being disappointed in one object, we must choose another and digest our mortification. And yet—there is sometimes something in the other side too. Bows, perhaps, was right to admire the blind heat which would stake everything; perhaps if self-sacrifice be a virtue, worldly prudence is not the noblest imitation of it. Let each moralist settle it in his own dialect, and quarrel with his neighbours about the words.

This is certain: with the experience Mr. Pen now had, he would have laughed at and scouted the idea of marrying a penniless girl out of a kitchen. That point fixed, he was doing the honest thing, in his own view, by crushing any fondness he felt.

So Fanny waited—waited as only the young can wait, believing time is a servant and must come when called. She waited a whole week. And it was at the end of that time that she heard, from Captain Costigan, of the illness under which Arthur was suffering.

It chanced—on that very evening after Costigan had visited Pen—that Arthur’s uncle, the excellent Major, arrived in town from Buxton, where his health had been mended. He sent his valet, Morgan, to inquire for Arthur and to request that gentleman would breakfast with the Major next morning. The Major was merely passing through London on his way to the Marquis of Steyne’s place, Stillbrook, where he was engaged to shoot partridges.

Morgan came back with a very long face. He had seen Mr. Arthur. Mr. Arthur was very bad indeed. Mr. Arthur was in bed with a fever. A doctor ought to be sent; and Morgan thought the case most alarming.

Gracious goodness! Here was a pretty breakfast invitation. The Major had hoped Arthur might come down to Stillbrook; he had arranged it; he had obtained an invitation from Lord Steyne. He must go himself—he couldn’t throw Lord Steyne over. The fever might be catching. It might be measles. He had never himself had measles; and measles were dangerous at his age. Was anybody with Mr. Arthur?

Morgan said there was somebody a-nussing of Mr. Arthur.

Had Mr. Pendennis taken any advice?

Morgan said he had asked that question, and had been told that Mr. Pendennis had had no doctor.
