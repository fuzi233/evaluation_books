Arthur Pendennis trundled over to his best mate George Warrington’s chambers the next morning swaddled in his most faded, coffee-stained dressing gown, as was his custom—all fired up to spill every last detail of his chaotic late-night chat with his uncle, and beg George to talk him off whatever dumb impulsive ledge he’d been teetering on that week.

The only soul he found in the familiar, book-strewn rooms was Mrs. Flanagan, their laundress, who was clucking over a pile of crumpled manuscript pages like a mother hen guarding stolen potatoes. “Arrived at six this morning I did, sir, and there was the dear gentleman slouched over his desk, one candle burned clean down to a puddle of tallow on his dog-eared college Plato, and his bed hadn’t been slept in at all! Said he had work to finish, grabbed his carpet bag ten minutes after, and was off to his brother’s place in Suffolk before the baker even delivered the crusty rolls.”

Which was true enough: George had bailed on his club the second the post-midnight poker brawl got too loud to bear, trundled home, and pulled a full all-nighter cranking out book reviews and sharp satire so he didn’t have to sit and think about the heartache sitting heavy in his chest. The next week’s quarterly review ran his piece, full of snark and clever little references, and all the posh London bookworms cooed over how clever the author was—no one had a clue half the lines were written through puffy red eyes, his desk chair pulled so close to the window the cold November dawn was practically breathing on his neck. Freelancers the world over know that vibe, right? You’re sniffling over a broken heart, or a sick kid, or the landlord banging on the door for rent, and you just keep scribbling, and no one reading your work ever guesses you were one second away from flinging your inkwell across the room.

The table was a mess, as usual: pens stuck in congealed ink, half a eaten scone, crumpled drafts torn to bits, a stick of sealing wax chewed almost in two from George’s nervous habits. Pen flopped into his empty friend’s chair, and noticed the gap on the bookshelf right next to that beat-up Plato: Helen’s Bible was gone. Oh. He got it immediately.

“Dear, dumb, wonderful old George,” Pen mumbled, scrubbing at his eyes so hard he saw stars. Who else was half as good? Half as brave? No wonder—well, of course she loved him. The man was a walking gentleman, no performative nonsense, no silly posturing. Pen resolved right there that he and Laura would be George’s honorary siblings for the rest of their lives, no more, no less: they’d look out for him, send him Christmas cake, never let him be lonely. Then he knocked over the half-full inkwell on the desk, and Mrs. Flanagan shooed him out of the room calling him a great big clumsy lummox.

Back at his own place, Pen spotted a crumpled note stuffed in his door letterbox, in George’s messy scrawl:
> Dear Pen,
> You’ll be halfway through your bacon and eggs by the time you read this, I’m already trundling north. My two cents on the business we chatted about on J Street? I’d be the world’s most awkward third wheel if I stuck around, so I’m off to camp out at my brother’s for Christmas, maybe even go further up to Norfolk to shoot partridges. Don’t be daft, don’t get yourself in trouble.
> Vale,
> P.S. Give my very best to your cousin Laura.

Well, that was that. George was gone, and Mrs. Flanagan had claimed his empty rooms as her personal territory to hang her drying laundry.

Pen trotted over to call on his uncle the next day, only to be told the old man was too under the weather to see visitors, so naturally he popped over to Lady Rockminster’s apartments, where the 70-something dowager’s eyes lit up like she’d just been handed the juiciest piece of town gossip. “Where’s Bluebeard? That George fellow! Bring him to dinner tonight at once!”

“Bluebeard’s done a runner to Suffolk,” Pen said, and passed her George’s little note. Laura took it, glanced at it, didn’t meet Pen’s eye, and handed it right back before wandering over to the window. Pen immediately launched into a dramatic 10 minute eulogy about how George was the finest man alive, so earnest Lady Rockminster blinked at him. “Well I never,” she said, blunt as a brick. “I didn’t think you had it in you to care that much about anyone who wasn’t you or your own silly drama.”

A few days later, Pen was wandering through Waterloo Place, heading to the hotel where Laura was staying, when he spotted a familiar face stepping out of Gimcrack’s fancy jeweller’s: Harry Foker, his old school chum, decked out head to toe in full deep mourning, his brougham, his coachman, even his horse all wearing little black armbands. The late Mr. Foker had finally kicked the bucket, and Harry was now the owner of the massive Foker & Co brewery, 15 thousand a year, and the entire Logwood estate. A shopman trailed behind him carrying armfuls of parcels.

“Foker! You old rascal!” Pen yelled, waving. Harry jumped like he’d been caught stealing a bottle of his own beer, held out a gloved little hand that had three inches of snowy cuff peeking out of the black sleeve, and looked like he was about to bolt. His other hand was clamped around a tiny morocco jeweller’s case like it held top-secret government documents.

“Le Roi est mort! Vive le Roi!” Pen teased. Harry blushed bright red all the way to his ears. “Ah! Yes! Thank you! Very busy! Goodbye!” he yelped, scrambled into his black brougham, and sat hunched behind the coachman like a little black cloud of misery the whole time he drove off.

Pen snickered to himself, strolling off. Classic Harry. The old brewery bosses are all bowing and scraping to him now, the draymen are cheering their new boss like he’s a royal, the bankers are all being extra polite because he’s now the one signing the cheques. Lucky there was never any money nonsense between him and Laura, right? He said as much to her later.

Laura’s eyes immediately filled with tears. “Arthur! Why would you even say that? You’d never have been tempted by anything so mean! You make me blush for you when you pretend you’re that selfish.”

They bickered back and forth gently, Pen being his usual over-skeptical self, rambling on about that annoying little familiar named But that follows every cynic around—tapping you on the shoulder in church, hanging over your wedding pillow, hiding behind your deathbed curtain, going “but what if it’s all garbage?”—until Lady Rockminster swept into the room after finishing her three-hour elaborate toilette that involved two maids, three bottles of lavender water, and a ridiculous amount of lace.

“What on earth are you two yammering about?” she huffed. “Pen, you’re over here so often the doorman thinks you live in the lobby.”

Pen explained he’d just run into the newly wealthy Harry Foker, which made Lady Rockminster’s eyes go wide with glee—she had top-tier tea to spill. “Oh! That boy’s my cousin! You haven’t heard the news? Lady Ann Rosherville, the girl he’s been engaged to for years? The one everyone thought was going to marry him for the 15 thousand a year? She snuck out last week after breakfast, ran off to the little church on their estate, and eloped with her father’s chaplain. Red-haired widower, two little snot-nosed kids. Lord Rosherville is absolutely apoplectic. He’s tried to make Harry marry one of his other daughters, but one’s covered in smallpox scars and the other’s 38 years old and collects taxidermied hedgehogs. The whole ton will be cackling about this for months.”

Pen’s jaw dropped. Oh! That’s what Harry was buying the fancy jewellery for, wasn’t it? He wasn’t sneaking trinkets for some opera dancer, he was about to go propose to someone else, the poor lovestruck idiot.

Laura, meanwhile, had suddenly gotten very weirdly urgent about getting Pen’s wedding to Blanche sorted as fast as humanly possible. She wrote him three notes a day, begging him to speed up the plans, offered to go stay with Blanche down in Tunbridge Wells the second Lady Rockminster let her leave, even defied the old dowager for the first time in her whole life to insist she go—so desperate to get Pen married off you’d think there was a fire under her skirts.

A whole flurry of fake-sweet notes passed between Blanche and Laura:
*Oh dearest Laura, how horribly unfortunate! The spare bedrooms are full! That dreadful Mrs Planter decided to come down and catch a cold that won’t let her leave for another week, so there’s nowhere for you to stay!*
*Dearest Blanche! That’s such bad luck! Don’t worry, I’ll wait, no rush at all! Oh by the way, Pen is such a wonderful, kind man, I can promise you that with my life!*
*Dearest Laura! What on earth is in this completely wild, weird, reserved letter I just got from A.P.?! What’s going on?! There’s a mystery here and I need answers!*

And the answers? They’re all in the wild, unexpected letter Arthur Pendennis had just sent her, of course.
