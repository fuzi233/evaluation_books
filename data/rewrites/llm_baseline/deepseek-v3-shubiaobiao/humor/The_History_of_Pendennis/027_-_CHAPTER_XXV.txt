Lady Clavering was, to put it kindly, not the sharpest quill in the inkpot. Sir Francis had once remarked—with the brutal honesty of a man who’d married for money—that his wife’s intellect was roughly on par with that of a particularly dim garden shrub. And while this was uncharitable, it wasn’t entirely *inaccurate*.

Her education had been a slapdash affair—two years in "Ackney" (a place she stubbornly refused to correctly pronounce) before being shipped off to Calcutta at fifteen. It was on this voyage, aboard the *Ramchunder East Indiaman*, that she met her first husband, Mr. Amory—third mate, professional scoundrel, and all-around disaster. The details of their courtship were best left unexplored, though it involved mutiny, irons, and Captain Bragg (a man whose personality matched his name).

Amory, ever the enterprising rogue, eventually weaseled his way into marrying the wealthy attorney’s daughter, despite her father’s understandable objections. What followed was a spectacular career of failure—indigo planter, newspaper editor, and finally, fugitive from Calcutta’s creditors. By the time he fled to New South Wales, even the local judges had tired of his antics.

Left in the wreckage, Lady Clavering (then Mrs. Amory) returned to Europe with her daughter Blanche, a long-suffering nurse named Bonner, and a deep-seated terror of Captain Bragg’s judgmental glares. She bounced between boarding houses across the continent, leaving behind a trail of half-finished curries, paid-up rent, and bewildered fellow guests who couldn’t decide if her mangled English was charming or alarming.

By the time she stumbled upon Sir Francis Clavering in Rome (himself dodging creditors), she was more than ready for a fresh start. Grief over Amory’s death was conspicuously absent.

Blanche had spent her formative years trailing after her mother like a melancholy shadow, absorbing all the melodrama of European boarding schools and the occasional French pension. She fancied herself a poetess, a misunderstood soul, and—most importantly—a *victim*.

Little Frank’s arrival had been a particular blow. Once the presumed heiress, Blanche now found herself eclipsed by a shrieking, sticky toddler who had the audacity to be male and, therefore, infinitely more valuable. Resentment festered. She wrote maudlin verses about her “angel brother” while simultaneously pinching him under the table.

Arthur Pendennis, ever the brooding philosopher, had developed a hopeless infatuation with Blanche. Unfortunately, his primary emotions were self-doubt and a crippling fear of rejection.

*"How could he, a penniless dreamer, aspire to a girl like Blanche? She lived in a manor! She had servants! Meanwhile, his mother counted coins at Fairoaks!"*

Helen Pendennis, ever perceptive, saw through Blanche’s act. The girl was all flutter and no substance—a selfish creature wrapped in pretty verses. Laura, initially enchanted, soon reached the same conclusion after witnessing one too many tantrums directed at governesses and hapless maids.

The final straw came when Blanche, in a fit of pique, reduced her maid Pincott to tears. Laura, usually the soul of patience, erupted.

*"You call yourself sensitive, yet you torment anyone beneath you!"* she snapped. *"You write poems about love and then bully your own brother!"*

Blanche, unaccustomed to defiance, gaped like a startled goldfish. Laura stormed out, leaving the Muse to sulk in her boudoir, no doubt composing a sonnet about betrayal.

Earlier that day, Blanche had played her trump card—love poems Pen had written her.

*"Oh, Laura, you mustn’t tell a soul!"* she’d whispered, fluttering the pages like a conspirator.

Laura read them, her stomach twisting. They were achingly earnest, full of nymphs and undying devotion. She handed them back without a word.

Blanche, mistaking silence for awe, trilled a Rossini aria, smug as a cat with cream.

But Laura was done. By the time they linked arms for luncheon, she had already decided: Blanche Amory was a fraud wrapped in lace and self-pity.

And as for Pen? Well. That was a problem for another chapter.
