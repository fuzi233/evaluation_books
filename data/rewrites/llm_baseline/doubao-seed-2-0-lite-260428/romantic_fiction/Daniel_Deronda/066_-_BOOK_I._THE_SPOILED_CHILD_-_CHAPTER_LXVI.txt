“One day still fierce ’mid many a day struck calm.” —BROWNING: *The King and the Book*

Gwendolen Harleth had never once spared a thought for Ezra and Mirah Lapidoth when she daydreamed of Daniel Deronda, but in that quiet, lull between storms, the threads tying all three of their fates together were weaving tighter, glowing brighter, drawn closer by the sharp, unshakable bond of shared care.

The man who was their father, Lapidoth, had vanished from Mirah’s doorstep the second her purse was in his hand, ruled entirely by that ravenous, unthinking urge to stake every coin he laid claim to, no matter how small, on a game of chance. As long as he could afford to sate his hunger for the turn of a card or the roll of the dice, he never spared a single thought for Mirah, or for the uncomfortable, stern encounter with his son that he had instinctively shrunk back from the moment they parted. The itch of gambling is fiercer than bodily hunger, after all: a meal can be forgotten if a thought or a feeling burns bright enough, but the fevered, breathless hush of waiting for luck to swing your way eats every other kind of pleasure or discomfort alive. At its most unforgiving stage, it is the hollow, joyless pastime of demons, chasing cheap thrills across the hot burning marl of their own impending ruin.

Every vice, no matter how abstract, still needs a crust of bread to keep its host breathing, though. Lapidoth cared little enough for fancy food or drink, but he had long since slipped into a shabby, hardscrabble life where a bite to eat required ready coin. He lost all thirty of Mirah’s shillings in short order at a parlour that advertised “Pyramids” behind its frosted blind, doubling and trebling his stake before the wheel turned against him one final time, and he stepped back onto the street with her empty purse in his pocket already weighing two options: pawn the purse for a few pennies to fund another bet, or march back to Mirah, play the wronged, repentant man, and tell her he’d spent every last penny paying off an overdue debt he could no longer put off. He still clung to that old, unshakable belief that his children owed him everything they owned, that his son’s anger could never override his natural right to leech off whatever good fortune they had stumbled into. The longer he thought about facing Ezra, the less he flinched at the idea: he was far more fixated on the easy, risk-free money he could charm out of them than any possible sting of public humiliation. His luck had been bad for weeks, it had to turn soon—wouldn’t that turn start with Mirah, and the wealthy, generous friends she’d mentioned making? Lapidoth still believed his clever, quick-witted charm was an unbreakable spell, a trick that had gotten him out of trouble since he was a bright, curly-haired young man, and he set off for their quiet Brompton square determined to wait around the corner until he spotted Mirah leaving or returning, so he could slip into the house without awkwardness.

Dusk was falling fast, the evening after he’d first run into his daughter on the street, and when weariness overtook him after an hour of waiting, he decided he would simply ring the bell, and if Mirah was out, he could ask when she would be home. But before he even lifted his hand to the knocker, he knew she was inside: he could hear her voice, rising soft and bright through the closed front door, pouring out the notes of *Herz, mein Herz* at the piano, while Ezra sat nearby with his eyes closed, drinking in every note.

Mrs. Adam opened the door a few minutes later, her face flustered with embarrassment. “A gentleman downstairs says he’s your father, miss.”

Mirah jumped to her feet at once, her gaze flying straight to her brother.
“No, Mirah, don’t go down to him,” Ezra said, steady and unyielding, before turning to Mrs. Adam. “Show him up here.”

Mirah stood twisting her fingers tight in the fabric of her sleeves, her stomach twisting with sick anxiety, never taking her eyes off Ezra. He had risen too, his frame visibly shaking, but there was a look on his face she had never seen before: his brows were knitted hard, his lips set into a sharp, unforgiving line, the same unyielding fire glinting in his gaze.

Mrs. Adam stepped back to let Lapidoth in, and could not help glancing between the two men before she closed the door behind her, thinking to herself, well, the family likeness is plain as day. Their bone structure, the outline of their jaws, was identical at first glance—but a lifetime of wildly different choices had carved their expressions into total, unrecognizable opposites, like the stern, unyielding face of a revolutionary Cromwell sprung from the exact outward mould of a mild, ordinary parish clerk.

Lapidoth had schooled his face to a soft, melancholy expression before he knocked, but he still flinched a little as he stepped forward. “Ezra, my boy—after all these years, I doubt you even know me.”

“I know you,” Ezra said, slow and solemn, every word sharp enough to cut. “Too well, father.” That last word fell not as a title, but as a rebuke.

“Ah, you’re displeased with me. I don’t blame you at all, appearances have been stacked against me,” Lapidoth rattled off, his old glib charm snapping back into place like it had never faded. “When a man gets trapped in straits he can’t climb out of, he can’t do right by himself or anyone else. I’ve suffered more than you could ever imagine, I swear it.” He turned to Mirah, holding out her empty purse. “Here’s your little purse, my dear. I knew you’d be worried about that scrap of writing tucked inside. I emptied it, I know, but every penny went to paying off a long-overdue bill for food and lodging. I knew you’d want me to clear that debt, and here I stand, not a single farthing to my name, at the mercy of my own children. You can turn me out without a fuss, no police needed. Just say the word, Mirah: say ‘Father, I’ve had enough of you—you spoiled me, spent every penny you had on me when I was small and couldn’t survive without you, but I can do better on my own now,’ and I’ll vanish like a spark in the wind. I’ll never spoil your happiness again.” His voice cracked, thick with simulated tears, just like it always used to when Mirah was a little girl.

“I could never say that, father,” Mirah answered, even as every part of her ached, even as she saw right through every one of his lies, and knew all he wanted was to stay under their roof.

“Mirah, my sister—leave us,” Ezra said, in that quiet tone of authority that brooked no argument. She looked at him, faltering, pleading, awed by the unshakable resolve on his face, yet unwilling to abandon the man who had once been all the family she had known. She stepped close to him, lacing her fingers through his, her voice low but not quiet enough for Lapidoth to miss: “Remember Ezra—you said our mother would never have shut him out.”

“Trust me. Go,” Ezra said.
Mirah slipped out of the room, but sank down onto the top step a few feet away, her heart hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears. What if Ezra drove him away? The thought made her breath catch.

Lapidoth thought he had the situation handled. This gaunt, grave son of his, speaking like he had stepped straight out of a sepulchre, would no doubt launch into a tedious moral lecture, and Lapidoth would pull out his well-worn act of sorrow and repentance, and it would all blow over. He had built up a thick, callous skin for just such moments over decades of selfish living, the kind of man who would duck into an open cathedral to escape a hailstorm and let the hymns wash over him without ever bothering to listen to the words. This was not a trait he had been born with—it was a habit he had earned, over years of choosing ease over decency.

“This home we share here,” Ezra began, his voice tight with the weight of decades of unspoken grief, “is sustained half by the generosity of our dearest friend Daniel Deronda, who supports me, and half by your own daughter’s hard work, that earns her the bread she eats. As long as we have a roof over our heads, we will not shut you out. We will not cast you out to be torn apart by your own vices. You are our father. You broke every bond you ever swore to keep, but we will not break ours. But I will never trust you. You ran off with stolen money, left your debts unpaid. You abandoned my mother. You stole her little child away from her, and broke her heart piece by piece. You became a gambler, and where shame and conscience once lived, there is nothing now but that ravenous, insatiable hunger. You were ready to sell my sister—you *had* sold her, only the buyer was denied you. No man who has done these things will ever earn our trust again. We will share our food with you. You will have a warm bed, clean clothing. We will do this duty to you, because you are our father. You are an evil man, who destroyed our mother’s happiness. That you are our father is a brand burned into our flesh, that will ache until we die. But the Eternal has laid this cross upon us. Even if human justice dragged you through the streets and flogged you for your crimes, even if your body fell helpless before the scorn of every stranger who passed by, we would still step forward and say ‘This is our father. Make way. Let us carry him out of your sight.’”

Lapidoth had braced himself for a lecture, but he had never expected this: raw, unflinching words searing straight out of his son’s very soul, a force so new to him it felt like lightning had struck straight through his chest. He collapsed into the nearest chair, sobbing like a woman, burying his face in his arms against the table. Half that hysterical weeping was genuine shock at the ferocity of Ezra’s grief, half that old physical trick he had mastered as a young man, the one he had always used to melt away anyone’s anger or disapproval before it could turn against him.

Ezra sank back into his own chair, silent, completely drained. For years he had carried all that rage, all that sorrow, locked up alone in silence, and the effort of pouring it all out left his thin hands trembling on the chair arms. He felt so worn out he could barely have found the breath to answer a simple question, like he had taken one step straight toward calling Death to his side.

Mirah’s sharp, waiting ear caught the sound of her father’s ragged sobs, and she could not stay outside the door another second. She burst into the room, her first thought for Ezra, rushing straight to his side to take his trembling hand in hers. He squeezed back, drawing strength from her touch, but did not speak, did not even lift his head to look at her. Lapidoth, his face still streaked with tears, felt her presence at once, and lifted his head to press his handkerchief to his eyes, reaching a shaky hand out to her. “Goodbye, Mirah. Your old father will never trouble you again. I deserve to die like a stray dog by the side of the road, and that’s what I will do. If your mother had lived, she would have forgiven me. Thirty-four years ago, I put the ring on her finger under the Chuppa, and we were made one flesh. She would have forgiven me, we would have grown old together. But I don’t deserve that. Goodbye.” He stood up, as if he was about to walk straight out the door forever.

Mirah caught his hand before he could move a single step further, her face white with fear. “No, father, no!” She turned to Ezra, her voice breaking. “Ezra, you have not forbidden him to stay? Stay, father, please. Leave all the wrong things behind. Ezra, I cannot bear this. How could I ever say to my own father, ‘Go and die alone’?”

“I did not say it,” Ezra forced out, with every last scrap of his remaining strength. “I said he may stay. He will be sheltered here.”

“Then you will stay, father, and be taken care of,” Mirah said, tugging him gently toward the door. “Come with me.” That was exactly what Lapidoth had wanted all along. For a moment, wrapped in his daughter’s soft, dutiful care, he even felt a flicker of genuine comfort, like changing his old, ruinous habits might actually be possible. She led him down to her cosy parlour on the ground floor, and showed him the small bedroom tucked off the back. “This is all yours. Think that you have come back to my mother, and she has forgiven you—she speaks to you through me, right now.” Her voice was soft and imploring, but she could not bring herself to give him the kind of warm, careless hug she had showered on him when she was a small, trusting child.

Lapidoth recovered his old easy composure within minutes, chattering cheerfully to her about how much her singing voice had improved, and other light, harmless topics. When Mrs. Adam came to lay out his supper, he charmed her too, determined to prove he was far from some common vagabond, even if his clothes were shabby and worn. But late that night, lying awake in his new bed, Ezra’s stern, blazing speech faded fast from his mind. He was already wondering how much money Mirah had stashed away somewhere, replaying every old roulette game he had ever played on the Continent, turning over his old betting strategies to find the trick that would finally beat the wheel. Ezra’s shadow drifted across the edges of his gambling-fogged mind only once, a thin, insubstantial ghost, while the spinning numbers and the roll of the dice ate away at every other thought, weaving themselves into the very fabric of his dreams.
