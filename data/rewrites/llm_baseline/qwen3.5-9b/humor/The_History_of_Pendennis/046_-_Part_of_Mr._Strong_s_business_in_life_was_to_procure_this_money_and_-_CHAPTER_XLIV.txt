Early one morning, shortly after the dinner at Grosvenor Place where Colonel Altamont had made such a scene, the Colonel emerged from his room in the upper story of Shepherd's Inn. He walked straight into Captain Strong's sitting-room, where the Chevalier sat comfortably in his chair, reading the newspaper and puffing on a cigar.

Captain Strong was a man who managed to make his quarters feel like home wherever he stopped. Long before Altamont arrived, he had already demolished a hearty breakfast of fried eggs and broiled bacon, prepared by Mr. Grady with military precision. Cheerful and loquacious, Strong preferred any company to solitude. Though he held no affection for his fellow lodger and wouldn't have minded if the accident Sir Francis Clavering prayed for had befallen him, he maintained polite relations with the Colonel. He had tucked Altamont into bed with genuine kindness the night before, even removing his candle to prevent fires. Finding his spirit bottle empty—which he had relied on for midnight comfort—he had happily washed it down with a glass of water over his pipe before drifting off to sleep. Such contentment was his constant companion; possessing an easy temper, a flawless digestion, and a perpetually rosy complexion, the worthy captain slept soundly whether facing battle or the Fleet, waking each day ready to tackle whatever struggles, difficulties, or pleasures awaited him.

The Colonel's first move was to roar for Grady to bring a pint of pale ale. Grady poured it into a pewter flagon, which the Colonel then lifted directly to his lips. He drained the tankard, drew a deep breath, and wiped his mouth with his dressing gown. (Captain Strong had long noticed the discrepancy between the color of the Colonel's beard and his dyed whiskers, and suspected his hair was fair beneath the black wig, but wisely chose not to comment). Refreshed by the draught, the Colonel declared, "Nothing beats that beer when the coppers are hot. I've drunk a dozen pints of Bass in Calcutta, and—"

"And at Lucknow, I suppose?" Strong laughed. "I fetched the beer specifically for you; I knew you'd need it after last night."

Seizing the opportunity, the Colonel launched into a tale of his previous evening's exploits.

"I simply can't help myself," the Colonel exclaimed, thumping his head with his large hand. "Once I have liquor aboard, I'm a madman unfit to handle a spirit bottle. Once I start, I can't stop until the vessel is empty, and then—who knows what I say or don't say? I dined quietly at home. Grady gave me exactly two tumblers, and I planned to spend the evening at the Black and Red as sober as a parson. Why did you leave that cursed sample bottle of Hollands in the cupboard, Strong? Grady had to step out, leaving the kettle boiling for tea. It was futile; I couldn't resist it. I washed it all down, by Jove, and I'm convinced I had more later at that infernal little thieves' den."

"Thieves' den?" Strong asked. "Were you there too, before you reached Grosvenor Place? That seems early for a clear-out."

"Early hours for drinking and disappearing before nine, yes. But that's how it was. Like a great fool, I went there anyway. Found Blackland and young Moss dining, along with two or three other rogues. If we'd tried Roulette, I surely would have won. But we didn't gamble on black and red. Hang 'em, they knew I'd crush them at the tables—I must have crushed them, I tell you! But they were too cunning. That rascal Blackland rigged the bones, and we played Hazard on the dining table. I dropped every penny you lent me that morning. Damn my luck! That drove me wild, and I assume my head was feverish, for I set off to borrow more money from Clavering. Then... then I don't remember much until this morning, when I heard old Bows at Number 4 banging on his piano."

Strong lit his cigar with a piece of coal and pondered for a moment. "I'd like to know how you manage to extract money from Clavering, Colonel," he said.

The Colonel erupted in laughter. "Ha, ha! Because he owes it to me!"

"That isn't a valid reason for Frank to pay," Strong countered. "He owes plenty to others besides you."

"Well, he pays me because he adores me," the Colonel grinned with a sneer. "He loves me like a brother, you know he does, Captain. —No? He doesn't? Well, perhaps he doesn't. And if you promise not to ask questions, perhaps I'll promise not to lie, Captain Strong—put that in your pipe and smoke it, my boy."

"But I shall quit that confounded brandy bottle," the Colonel continued after a pause. "I must, or it will ruin me."

"It makes you say strange things," the Captain observed, staring Altamont squarely in the eye. "Recall what you said last night at Clavering's table."

"Said? What did I say?" the other demanded hastily. "Did I spill anything? Darn me, Strong, did I spill anything?"

"Ask me no questions, and I'll tell you no lies," the Chevalier replied. Strong recalled the words Altamont had uttered and his sudden exit from the Baronet's dining room upon recognizing Major Pendennis, or Captain Beak as he called him. However, Strong decided to seek an explanation elsewhere and refused to dredge up the memory for the Colonel. "No," he said calmly, "you didn't 'spill,' as you call it. It was merely a trap of mine to see if I could make you speak. But you said nothing anyone could understand; you were far too intoxicated for that."

Relieved, Altamont sighed heavily. Noticing the emotion, Strong said nothing, and since the Colonel was in a storytelling mood, he pressed on.

"Yes, I admit my faults," the Colonel continued. "There are three things I cannot resist, no matter what I try: a bottle of brandy, a box of dice, and a beautiful woman. No man of courage and spirit, no man worth his salt, has ever escaped them. Hardly a country exists where these three haven't landed me in trouble."

"Indeed?" Strong asked.

"Yes, since I was fifteen, when I ran away from home to become a cabin boy on an Indiaman, until now, at nearly fifty, women have always been my undoing. Why, it was one of them—with jet-black eyes and jewels around her neck, draped in ermine like a duchess—that swept away most of the thousand pounds I carried when I fled. Wasn't I telling you about this before? Well, I don't mind repeating it. Initially, I was cautious, keeping my money tight and living like a gentleman—Colonel Altamont at Meurice's Hotel, and so forth. I rarely played, sticking to public tables where I usually won more than I lost.

"One day, however, I spotted a chap at the hotel and the Palace Royal, a proper swell with white kid gloves and a tuft on his chin. His name was Bloundell-Bloundell, and somehow we became acquainted. He invited me to dinner and took me to the soirées of Madame the Countess de Foljambe. Such a woman, Strong! Such an eye! Such a talent at the pianoforte! Bless you, she would sit down and sing to you, gazing intently until she warbled your soul right out of your body. She invited me to her Tuesday evenings without fail. Did I buy opera boxes and treat her to dinners at the restaurateur? Of course I did. But despite my luck at the tables, it wasn't the dinners or the boxes that drained poor Clavering's funds. No, by Jove, it vanished in another way.

"One night at the Countess's, we were supping—Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, the Honourable Deuceace, the Marquis de la Tour de Force—all top-tier nobility, sir, the pinnacle of fashion. We had supper, followed by ample champagne, and then some of that cursed brandy. I demanded more—I insisted on continuing. The Countess mixed punch for me, and after supper, we played cards alongside grog. I played and drank until I don't know what I did. I was just like last night. Taken away and put to bed somehow, I didn't wake until the next day, suffering a throbbing headache, to find my servant saying the Honourable Deuceace wished to see me and was waiting in the sitting-room.

"'How are you, Colonel?' he asked, entering my bedroom. 'How long did you stay last night after I left? The game was getting too expensive for me, and I'd lost enough to you for one night.'

"'To me?' I asked. 'How's that, my dear fellow? For though he was an Earl's son, we were as familiar as you and I.' He explained he had borrowed thirty louis from me at Vingt-et-Un, giving me an I.O.U. the night prior, which I had placed in my pocket-book before he departed. I produced my card-case—the Countess had crafted it for me—and there was the I.O.U., sure enough. He paid me thirty louis in gold right on my bedside table.
