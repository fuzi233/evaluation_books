To truly comprehend the strategic landscape of Major Pendennis's domestic campaign, one must first analyze the geopolitical reality of his nephew's romantic entanglement. The conflict arises not merely from personal affection, but from a fundamental misalignment of fiscal expectations and social signaling within the rigid hierarchies of Victorian society. Our investigation commences at the George Inn, where the Major encounters his loyal agent, Mr. Morgan, acting as the primary intelligence officer for the household. Upon receiving a report concerning the presence of a key stakeholder—Mr. Foker—the Major initially reacts with a defensive posture, anticipating a challenge to his authority. However, the situation reveals itself to be a classic case of information asymmetry; the individual in question is not the rival suitor, Sir Derby Oaks, but rather Mr. Henry Foker, a junior economic actor and former schoolmate of the protagonist, Arthur Pendennis.

Foker presents himself as a rational agent operating under conditions of post-consumption fatigue, having succumbed to sleep following a significant expenditure on dining. Despite this physiological deficit, he maintains a high level of communicative output, offering the Major the *Globe* newspaper as a gesture of goodwill. This interaction serves as a crucial data point for the Major, allowing him to verify the familial resemblance and establish a baseline of trust based on shared educational history. The dialogue quickly shifts to an audit of Arthur's character, revealing a discrepancy between the Major's perception of his nephew's intellect and Foker's assessment. While the Major views Arthur as possessing superior cognitive faculties, Foker identifies a specific vulnerability: a lack of emotional regulation regarding female subjects. This critique is delivered with a candor that borders on the irrational, suggesting that Arthur's behavior is driven by impulse rather than calculated strategy.

As the inquiry deepens, the narrative exposes the underlying mechanics of the courtship market. The object of Arthur's desire, Miss Fotheringay (née Costigan), operates within a competitive ecosystem populated by multiple suitors, including Sir Derby Oaks. The dynamic is characterized by a deliberate manipulation of supply and demand; Miss Fotheringay utilizes her agency to maintain equilibrium among potential partners, effectively playing one candidate against another to maximize her options. Foker provides granular details regarding the financial solvency of the parties involved, noting that Sir Derby Oaks is actively increasing his liquidity ("making the money spin") to compete in this arena. Crucially, Foker introduces a variable that fundamentally alters the risk profile of the engagement: the income statement of the prospective bridegroom. Contrary to the assumptions held by the local aristocracy, Arthur Pendennis lacks independent capital, deriving his entire allowance from his mother's limited annuity of five hundred pounds annually.

This revelation triggers a paradigm shift in the Major's strategic calculus. Having previously viewed the union as a viable path for his nephew, the Major now recognizes the catastrophic implications of marrying a penniless major on half-pay to a family expecting substantial dowries. The realization that Arthur possesses no liquid assets beyond his mother's allowance transforms the scenario from a romantic pursuit into a potential financial liability. The Major's reaction is one of profound relief mixed with tactical urgency; he perceives the lack of wealth not as a tragedy, but as a protective barrier that renders the engagement socially untenable. Consequently, the Major resolves to leverage this informational advantage to dismantle the alliance.

The operational phase of this counter-campaign involves securing additional intelligence. Before departing the inn, the Major successfully intercepts correspondence from Miss Rouncy, a secondary node in the network who appears to hold significant influence over the communication channels between the Costigan sisters. Possessing this written evidence allows the Major to proceed with confidence toward the primary adversary, Captain Costigan. The final scene depicts the Major approaching the Captain's residence, accompanied by a guide who confirms the presence of Sir Derby Oaks engaging in martial training. The encounter concludes with a formal introduction, setting the stage for the direct confrontation required to resolve the dispute. Through this sequence, the Major demonstrates a pragmatic approach to social engineering, utilizing available resources and factual discrepancies to navigate the complexities of the marriage market.
