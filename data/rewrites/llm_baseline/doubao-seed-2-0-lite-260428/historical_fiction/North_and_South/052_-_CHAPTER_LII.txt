“For joy or grief, for hope or fear,
For all hereafter, as for here,
In peace or strife, in storm or shine.”

Edith moved about the house on tiptoe the next morning, hushing Sholto mid-laugh every time he raised his voice too loud, as if a single sudden clatter of a child’s toy or a servant’s dropped tray might shatter the delicate, closed-door conference unfolding in her drawing-room. The clock crept past noon, past one, and finally struck two, and still the pair within sat murmuring behind the shut door. At last a man’s footsteps thudded fast down the stairs, and Edith poked her head out of the adjacent parlour, all wide-eyed interrogation.
“Well, Henry?” she asked, eager for news.
“Well!” he returned, rather shortly. “Come in and eat lunch.”
“No, thank you, I cannot spare the time. I have already lost far too much of my working day here.”
“Then it is not all settled?” Edith’s tone dropped, despondent.
“No, not at all. And it never will be settled, if the ‘it’ you are fretting over is what I think. That will never come to pass, Edith, so put the idea out of your head entirely.”
“But it would be so pleasant for all of us,” Edith pleaded. “I would never lie awake worrying that Margaret would run off to Cadiz for good, if she was settled here close by me with her own home.”
“I shall try, when I marry,” Henry Lennox said, half-teasing, half-sharp, “to find a young lady who has a gift for managing small children. That is all I can promise you. Miss Hale will not have me, and I shall never ask her again.”
“Then what on earth have you been talking about for hours on end?”
“A thousand tedious things you would not care to understand: investments, lease covenants, fluctuating land values for mill property around Milton.”
“Oh, go away then,” Edith groaned, shooing him off. “You and Margaret will be insufferably dull together if you waste whole days nattering on about such dry, weary business.”
“Very well. I am coming back tomorrow, and bringing Mr. Thornton with me, to continue these talks with Miss Hale.”
“Mr. Thornton! What has he to do with any of this?”
“He is Miss Hale’s tenant at Marlborough Mills,” Mr. Lennox said, turning for the door. “He wishes to surrender his lease.”
“Oh, very well, I cannot abide fussy details, so do not bother to explain them to me.”
“The only detail I require you to remember,” he said, pausing on the threshold, “is to make sure the back drawing-room remains entirely undisturbed tomorrow, just as it was today. Normally the children and servants drift in and out every five minutes, so one never has the peace to hash out complicated arrangements properly, and tomorrow’s negotiations are of great importance.”

No one ever learned why Mr. Lennox never arrived to keep his appointment the next day. Mr. Thornton came exactly at the agreed hour, however, and after being kept waiting nearly an hour, Margaret Hale stepped into the room, very pale and visibly anxious.
“I am so sorry Mr. Lennox is not here,” she began hurriedly, “he could have explained all this far better than I can. He is my legal advisor in the matter—”
“I am sorry I came if it distresses you,” Thornton said, rising immediately. “Shall I go round to his chambers in town to fetch him?”
“No, thank you.” Margaret shook her head, twisting the edge of her apron. “I only wanted to tell you how grieved I was to hear you intend to give up the mill. But Mr. Lennox says things are sure to brighten soon—”
“Mr. Lennox knows little of the matter,” Thornton said quietly. “Happy and fortunate in every thing a man cares for, he cannot understand what it is to find oneself no longer young, thrown back to the very starting point where only the reckless, hopeful energy of youth can carry you forward. To feel half your life gone, and nothing done—nothing left of all your wasted opportunities but the bitter memory of what you might have been. Miss Hale, I would rather not hear Mr. Lennox’s opinion of my affairs. Those who have never known ruin are all too apt to make light of the misfortunes of others.”
“You are unjust,” Margaret said gently. “Mr. Lennox does not make light of it. He only says he is certain you will redeem—more than redeem—all you have lost. Do not speak until I have finished, pray do not.” She steadied herself, fumbling rapidly through stacks of legal papers and bound account statements, her hands trembling. “Oh! Here it is. He drew up a proposal for me, and I wish he was here to lay it out clearly: if you would take a sum of mine, eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds, that sits idle in the bank just now earning only two and a half per cent. interest, you could pay me a far fairer return, and keep Marlborough Mills running.”
Her voice cleared, growing steadier as she spoke, determined to frame the arrangement as a purely practical, mutually beneficial business deal where she stood to gain the greater advantage. While she hunted for the separate paper listing all the required terms of security, to leave no shadow of impropriety hanging over the agreement, her breath caught entirely at the sound of his voice. It was hoarse, thick with a tender passion she had never dared to dream he might feel for her, when he said, soft as a sigh:
“Margaret!”
For one startled instant she looked up, then dropped her forehead to her hands to veil the bright, luminous glow in her eyes. He stepped nearer, and spoke her name again, tremulous and eager:
“Margaret!”
She bowed her head lower, hiding her face more completely, until her forehead nearly rested on the tabletop. He crossed the few feet between them, knelt beside her so his face was level with her ear, and whispered, half the sound catching in his throat:
“Take care. If you do not speak now, I shall claim you as my own in some strange, presumptuous way. Send me away at once, if I must go—Margaret!”
At that third call, she turned her face toward him, still covered by her small white hands, and rested her cheek against his shoulder, hiding it there. The soft, warm press of her skin against his was so sweet that he did not wish to move her hands to see her blushing face or her loving eyes, and he clasped her close. For long minutes neither of them spoke.
At last she murmured, her voice breaking: “Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not good enough!”
“Not good enough?” He laughed, soft and shaky. “Do not mock my own deep, heavy feeling of unworthiness, that I have carried for years.”
After a minute or two, he gently pried her hands away from her face, and laid her arms across his chest, just as they had once been placed to shield him from the rioters outside the mill gates.
“Do you remember, love?” he murmured. “And how I requited you that kindness with my cruel insolence the very next day?”
“I only remember how wrongly I spoke to you then,” she said.
“Look here. Lift up your head. I have something to show you.”
She faced him slowly, her cheeks glowing with a soft, beautiful shame.
“Do you know these roses?” he said, drawing his worn pocket-book from his coat, where a handful of dried, pressed flowers had been treasured for months.
“No,” she replied, innocent and curious. “Did I give them to you?”
“No, vain thing—you did not. Though you very likely wore sister roses to these.”
She leaned in to look, spotted the distinct deep indentations in the edges of the leaves, and smiled. “They are from Helstone, are they not? I would know those leaf marks anywhere. Oh—have you been there? When did you go?”
“I went there right after I returned from Havre,” he said. “Even in the worst of times, when I had no hope at all of ever calling you mine. I had to see the place where Margaret grew into the woman I love.”
“You must give them to me,” she said, laughing, trying to gently tug the little bunch of flowers out of his hand.
“Very well. Only you must pay me for them.”
After a long, soft, delicious silence, she whispered: “How shall I ever tell Aunt Shaw?”
“Let me speak to her.”
“Oh no! I owe her so much—but what will she say?”
“I can guess. Her first exclamation will be, ‘That man!’”
“Hush!” Margaret giggled, pressing a hand over his mouth. “Or I shall show you exactly what your mother’s indignant tones will sound like when she cries, ‘That woman!’”
