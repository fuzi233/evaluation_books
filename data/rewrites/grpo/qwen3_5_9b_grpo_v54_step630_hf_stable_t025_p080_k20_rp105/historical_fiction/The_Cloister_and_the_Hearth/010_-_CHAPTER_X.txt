The banns of marriage were proclaimed three times, as the custom demanded, yet with a peculiar haste born of desperation. The young couple, Margaret and Gerard, managed to persuade the newly arrived curé to conduct all three readings within a single twenty-four-hour period. They were announced on Monday at matins and vespers, and to their immense relief, no one from Tergou appeared in the church to object. The following morning, they stood before the altar, hearts pounding with anxiety, only to be met with horror when a stranger rose to forbid the union. He claimed the parties were underage and lacked parental consent.

Outside the church doors, Margaret and Gerard trembled in a consultation of despair. Before they could formulate a plan, the man who had obstructed them approached, feigning regret. He explained that while his inclination was to bless their happiness, his livelihood depended on forbidding such banns. "The young people give me a crown, and I undo my work handsomely," he offered, suggesting Gerard tell the curé he had been misinformed.

"A crown!" Gerard exclaimed eagerly, offering a golden angel instead. The man accepted the bribe with equal eagerness, returning to the curé to claim he had made a ridiculous mistake. The priest, satisfied, agreed to marry the couple the next day at ten. The professional impediment to bliss departed with Gerard's angel, only to succumb to his own folly. He proceeded to a local hostelry, where a green was dedicated to archery and common sports. Drunk on his success, he boasted of his exploit to a frequent patron named Sybrandt. Sybrandt, a ne'er-do-well, ran home to tell his father, who was away in Rotterdam purchasing cloth. Sybrandt signaled his elder brother, and the two conspirators—Gerard's black brothers—convened.

Idleness was a vitiating force for these two brothers, whose minds were consumed by the inheritance they believed was theirs by right. Their parents' parsimony, originally a virtue born of industry and love, had been twisted in their selfish hearts into avarice, a fertile source of crime. They decided not to inform their mother, whose sentiments were uncertain, but to seek the burgomaster first. They were cunning enough to sense his aversion to the match, though they could not fathom the reason.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten saw through them immediately but maintained a mask of magisterial dignity. Hearing their story, he declared, "Since the father of the family is not here, his duty falleth on me, who am the father of the town. I know your father's mind; leave all to me; and, above all, tell not a woman a word of this, least of all the women that are in your own house: for chattering tongues mar wisest counsels." He dismissed them with a touch of supercilious shame.

Upon returning home, they found Gerard seated at their mother's knee. She caressed his hair, promising to take his part with his father and thwart his love no more. This sudden change of heart stemmed from a moment of female irritation; she had previously cut Margaret's picture to pieces. Watching Gerard turn pale as death, holding the fragments with tear-blinded eyes, she had been terrified by her own violence. Her heart smote her bitterly, and though she dared not admit it, she resolved to make amends. Her bowels yearned over her son, and her feeble anger died a natural death. She was transferring her fatal alliance to Gerard when the two black sheep entered. Gerard, inexperienced in the intricacies of female emotion, mistook her kindness for forgiveness, kissing her repeatedly and sleeping happily, unaware that his fate was sealed.

The next morning at ten, Gerard and Margaret stood in the church at Sevenbergen. Gerard was radiant; Margaret blushed. Only Peter and Martin Wittenhaagen were present as witnesses; secrecy was paramount. Margaret had declined Italy, unable to leave her helpless, learned father. Instead, they planned to retire to Flanders for a few weeks until the storm at Tergou passed. The curé did not keep them waiting long. He stood at the altar, called them forward, and opened his book.

But ere he uttered a single word of the sacred rite, a harsh voice cried, "Forbear!" Constables of Tergou surged up the aisle and seized Gerard in the name of the law. Martin's long knife flashed out instantly.

"Forbear, man!" cried the priest. "What! draw your weapon in a church, and ye who interrupt this holy sacrament, what means this impiety?"

"There is no impiety, father," said the burgomaster's servant respectfully. "This young man would marry against his father's will, and his father has prayed our burgomaster to deal with him according to the law. Let him deny it if he can."

"Is this so, young man?"

Gerard hung his head.

"We take him to Rotterdam to abide the sentence of the Duke."

Margaret uttered a cry of despair. The lovers, so happy a moment before, fell to sobbing in each other's arms so piteously that the instruments of oppression drew back, ashamed. Yet, one of them, pretending to separate them, whispered to Margaret, "Rotterdam? it is a lie. We but take him to our Stadthouse."

They mounted Gerard on horseback, sending him on a dozen halts and sly detours back to Tergou. Just outside the town, a rude vehicle covered with canvas awaited. Gerard was placed inside and, by five in the evening, was secretly conveyed to the prison of the Stadthouse. He was taken up several flights of stairs and thrust into a small room lit only by a narrow window with a vertical iron bar. The sole furniture was a huge oak chest.

Imprisonment in that age was a highroad to death. Even in its mildest form, it implied cold, unbroken solitude, torture, starvation, and often poison. Gerard felt he was in the hands of an enemy. "Oh, the look that man gave me on the road to Rotterdam," he thought, kneeling to commend his soul to God. "There is more here than my father's wrath. I doubt I shall see no more the light of day."

Rising, he sprang at the iron bar of the window, clutching it to press his knees against the wall and look out. For a minute, he saw a sight only a captive could appreciate: Martin Wittenhaagen's back. Martin sat quietly fishing in the brook near the Stadthouse. Gerard whistled, and Martin instantly ceased fishing, turning to see the prisoner. He made a signal, took up his line and bow, and quickly departed. Gerard realized his friends were not idle, yet he preferred Martin had stayed; the sight of him was a comfort. Holding on, he watched the soldier's retiring form until he could no longer see, then fell back and wrenched the rusty iron bar, held only by rusty nails, away from the stone-work.

Just then, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten opened the door stealthily. The burgomaster's eye fell instantly on the displaced iron, then glanced at the window, but he said nothing. The window was a hundred feet from the ground. If Gerard fancied jumping, why should he balk it? The burgomaster brought a brown loaf and a pitcher of water, setting them on the chest in solemn silence. Gerard's first impulse was to brain him with the iron bar and flee down the stairs, but the burgomaster, sensing something wicked in his eye, coughed. Three stout fellows, armed, appeared at the door.

"My orders are to keep you thus until you shall bind yourself by an oath to leave Margaret Brandt, and return to the Church, to which you have belonged from your cradle."

"Death sooner."

"With all my heart." And the burgomaster retired.

Martin hastened to Sevenbergen, finding Margaret pale and agitated but full of resolution. She was finishing a letter to the Countess Charolois, appealing against the violence and treachery of Ghysbrecht.

"Courage!" cried Martin on entering. "I have found him. He is in the haunted tower, right at the top of it. Ay, I know the place: many a poor fellow has gone up there straight, and come down feet foremost."

He recounted how he had looked up and seen Gerard's face at a window like a slit in the wall.

"Oh, Martin! how did he look?"

"What mean you? He looked like Gerard Eliassoen."

"But was he pale?"

"A little."

"Looked he anxious? Looked he like one doomed?"

"Nay, nay; as bright as a pewter pot."

"You mock me. Stay! then that must have been at sight of you. He counts on us. Oh, what shall we do? Martin, good friend, take this at once to Rotterdam."

Martin held out his hand for the letter. Peter, who had sat silent, pondering keenly, spoke up. "Put not your trust in princes."

"Alas! what else have we to trust in?"

"Knowledge."

"Well-a-day, father! your learning will not serve us here."

"How know you that? Wit has been too strong for iron bars ere to-day."

"Ay, father; but nature is stronger than wit, and she is against us. Think of the height! No ladder in Holland might reach him."

"I need no ladder; what I need is a gold crown."

"Nay, I have money, for that matter. I have nine angels. Gerard gave them me to keep; but what do they avail? The burgomaster will not be bribed to let Gerard free."

"What do they avail? Give me but one crown, and the young man shall sup with us this night."

Peter spoke so eagerly and confidently that Margaret felt a fleeting hope, but she caught Martin's eye, which dwelt on Peter with benevolent contempt.

"It passes the powers of man's invention," she sighed deeply.

"Invention!" cried the old man. "A fig for invention. What need we invention at this time of day? Everything has been said that is to be said, and done that ever will be done. I shall tell you how a Florentine knight was shut up in a tower higher than Gerard's; yet did his faithful squire stand at the tower foot and get him out, with no other engine than that in your hand, Martin, and certain kickshaws I shall buy for a crown."

Martin looked at his bow, turning it in his hand as if interrogating it, but remained as incredulous as before. Peter then told his story of the faithful squire rescuing a knight from a high tower at Brescia. The maneuver, like most truly scientific things, was so simple that their wonder was they had taken for impossible what was not even difficult.

The letter never went to Rotterdam. They trusted to Peter's learning and their own dexterity.

It was nine o'clock on a clear moonlight night. Gerard, senior, was still away; the rest of his family had been abed. A figure stood by the dwarf's bed, white in the moonlight. With an unearthly noise, between a yell and a snarl, the gymnast rolled off his bed and under it by a single unbroken movement. A soft voice followed him.

"Why, Giles, are you afeard of me?"

Giles's head peeped cautiously up, revealing it was only his sister Kate. She put her finger to her lips. "Hush! lest the wicked Cornelis or the wicked Sybrandt hear us." Giles's claws seized the side of the bed, and he returned to his place by one undivided gymnastic.

Kate revealed that she had heard Cornelis and Sybrandt mention Gerard's name. Being in great anxiety at his absence, she had listened at their door and made a fearful discovery. Gerard was in prison, in the haunted tower of the Stadthouse, by their father's authority. But there must be treachery; how could their father order such a cruel act when he was in Rotterdam? She entreated Giles to accompany her to the foot of the haunted tower to comfort Gerard and let him know their father was absent and would surely release him upon his return.

"Dear Giles, I would go alone, but I am afeard of the spirits that men say do haunt the tower; but with you I shall not be afeard."

"Nor I with you," said Giles. "I don't believe there are any spirits in Tergou. I never saw one. This last was the likest one ever I saw; and it was but you, Kate, after all."

In less than half an hour, Giles and Kate opened the house door cautiously and issued forth. She made him carry a lantern, though the night was bright. "The lantern gives me more courage against the evil spirits," she said.

The first day of imprisonment is very trying, especially when the horror of captivity is compounded by the horror of utter solitude. In their own day, many persons committed suicide during the first twenty-four hours of solitary confinement. This was doubtless why their Jairi abstained so carefully from the impertinence of watching their little experiment upon the human soul at that particular stage.

As the sun declined, Gerard's heart sank; with the waning light, the embers of hope went out. He was faint with hunger, afraid to eat the food Ghysbrecht had brought him, for hunger alone cowards men. He sat upon the chest, arms and head drooping, a picture of despondency. Suddenly, something struck the wall beyond him sharply, then rattled on the floor at his feet. It was an arrow; he saw the white feather. A chill ran through him—they meant to assassinate him from the outside. He crouched. No more missiles came. Crawling on all fours, he took up the arrow; there was no head to it. He uttered a cry of hope: had a friendly hand shot it? He examined it and found a soft substance attached. His eccentricity proved useful; his tinder-box enabled him to strike a light. It revealed two things that made his heart bound with delight. Attached to the arrow was a skein of silk, and on the arrow itself were words written.
