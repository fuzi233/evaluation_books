As the rogue Sybrandt once slurred, a lie is not like a blow from a curtal axe. The consequences of any strike with a physical weapon can be guessed, more or less, but a lie knows no bounds at all: by its very nature it ramifies far beyond any human ability to calculate. In ordinary life, a single lie has cost a man his life, or laid waste the homes of two or three. In this tale, that one heartless falsehood wove such tremendous, unforeseen harm the traitors who spun it reaped barely a scrap of the gain they’d chased.
The two brothers who invented the lie only to cut Gerard out as a claimant to their father’s property found their scheme backfiring the moment little Gerard, the orphaned son of their dead sibling, stole their mother’s entire affection. Worse still, Eli openly proclaimed one day that with Gerard lost and presumed dead, he had drafted a new will that provided handsomely for little Gerard and Margaret, his poor son’s widow. The look that passed between the two black sheep of the family was a warning to every traitor who thinks their plots will turn out as planned. Cornelis had the words “Gerard is most likely still alive” on the tip of his tongue, but caught his mother’s sharp, suspicious eye and bit his tongue in time.
Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the other co-conspirator in that lie, was now a frail, failing old man. He could see his end fast approaching, when all his hoarded wealth would slip from his cold fingers, and all his misdeeds would cling to his soul for eternity. Too shrewd to fool himself entirely, he had never been free of sudden, sharp gusts of remorse. When he intercepted Gerard’s letter to Margaret, he struck a petty, cowardly compromise: his ravenous Avarice insisted “I will not give up the land and the money I stole from her,” while his tiny, cowering Conscience added “But I will cause her no unnecessary pain.” He pried open the seal first, found no mention of the stolen deed anywhere in the text, then hand-delivered the letter to Margaret, patting himself on the back for the small decency – a self-assigned offset to his treachery, measured on the lopsided scale of a man who acts as judge of his own crimes.
The birth of Margaret’s child shocked him to his core, and cast his old treachery in a horrifying new light. If his lie held, he would be the one to bring public dishonour to the daughter of one old friend, the granddaughter of another, all while he continued to hoard her stolen land. These thoughts gnawed at him at a time of life when the body’s strength ebbs, and the memory of long-dead loved ones rises sharp and vivid, filling him with gloomy, waking horrors. Still he dared not confess: the local cure was an honest man, who would have forced him to disgorge every stolen penny. For him, Avarice was a habit rooted deep in his bones, while Penitence was no more than a passing, weak sentiment.
Things stood thus, when one day he returned from the town hall to his home, and found a veiled woman waiting in his vestibule, holding a small child in her arms. He assumed she was there to beg indulgence for some shameful affair, and addressed her magisterially – until she dropped her veil and looked him full in the face. It was Margaret Brandt.
Her sudden appearance and blazing earnestness startled him so thoroughly he could not hide his confusion.
“Where is my Gerard?” she cried, her bosom heaving. “Is he alive?”
“For aught I know,” he stammered. “I hope so, for your sake. Prithee, step into this room – the servants will hear us.”
“Not a step,” Margaret said, and she grabbed his shoulder, holding him fast with all the raw, fiery energy of a heartbroken, desperate woman. “You hold the secret that is breaking my heart. Why has not my Gerard come home, nor sent a single line these many months? Answer me, or I will shout it loud enough for every soul in this town to hear, let alone your servants. My misery is too great to be played with as a trivial jest.”
He persisted in claiming he knew nothing of Gerard, but Margaret told him the people who had sent her to him had told her a very different tale. “You do know why he neither comes nor sends,” she said, steady as a rock.
At this Ghysbrecht turned paler and paler, but he summoned all his remaining dignity and snapped, “Would you believe those two knaves against a man of worship?”
“What two knaves?” Margaret shot back, sharp as a needle.
He stammered and floundered. “Said ye not –? There I am a poor old broken man, whose memory is all shaken. You confuse me so, I know not what I say.”
“Ay sir, your memory is shaken right enough, or you would never be my enemy. My father dragged you out of the plague ward when no other soul would come within an arm’s length of you, and was your loyal friend all his days. My grandfather Floris pulled you out of early poverty, and loved you from boyhood to manhood. Three generations of us you have known. This is the fourth.” She held the child up to him. “This is your old friend Peter’s grandchild, and your old friend Floris’s great-grandchild. Look down on his innocent face, and think of theirs!”
“Woman, you torture me,” Ghysbrecht sighed, sinking heavily onto a stone bench.
Margaret saw her advantage at once. She knelt before him, and set the little boy right on his knees. “This fatherless babe is poor Margaret Brandt’s, who never did you ill, and comes of a race that loved you. Nay, look at his face. It will melt you more than any word of mine. Saints of heaven, what can a poor desolate girl and her babe have done to wipe out all memory of your own young days, when you were as guiltless as he is, now looking up in your face and imploring you to give him back his father?” She held the child up higher and higher, the little one gurgling soft under the old man’s gaze.
Ghysbrecht cast one wild, anguished look at the child, then another at the kneeling mother, and leaped up shrieking, “Avaunt, ye pair of adders!” The sting of his guilty soul lent his frail old limbs a burst of unnatural vigour, and he paced rapidly back and forth, wringing his hands, tearing at his own white hair. “Forget those days? I forget all else. Oh, woman, woman, sleeping or waking I see nothing but the faces of the dead, hear nothing but the voices of the dead, and soon I shall be among the dead myself. There, there, what is done is done. I am in hell. I am in hell.”
That unnatural force drained out of him as quickly as it came. He staggered, and would have fallen flat if Margaret had not caught him with her one free arm, crying out for help. A pair of servants came running, and carried him away half-swooning. The last Margaret saw of him was his old furrowed face, white and helpless as the white hair that hung loose over the servant’s elbow.
“Heaven forgive me,” she whispered. “I doubt I have killed the poor old man.”
Her attempt to pierce the mystery had left it as dark as ever, if not darker. When she pondered every word he had spoken, her suspicion only hardened that Ghysbrecht did know Gerard’s secret. “And those two knaves he thought had let slip the truth to me?” she murmured. “Oh, my Gerard, my poor deserted babe, you and I are wading in waters far deeper than we ever dreamed.”
The journey to Tergou had cost her more money than she could spare, and a customer who owed her a large sum ran off without paying his debt. Once more, she was forced to fetch Catherine’s gold angel out of its hiding place. But strange to say, when she came down the stairs clutching it in her hand, she found loose silver scattered across the table, with a scrap of writing laid atop: For Gerard his wife.
She fell on the note with a cry of shock that swelled, moments later, into a cry of wild joy. “He is alive! He sends me this by some friendly hand!” She kissed the scrap of paper over and over, and tucked it deep in her bodice, next to her heart.
Time rolled on, and still no news of Gerard. But roughly every two months, a small sum of silver found its way into her home. Sometimes it lay on the kitchen table. Once it was flung through her bedroom window, tucked in a small leather purse. Once it was tucked at the bottom of Luke the peddler’s basket, after he had stopped off at the public house to chat with a friend. The giver, or his agent, was never caught. Catherine disclaimed all knowledge of it. Margaret Van Eyck swore she had no hand in the gifts. So did Eli. Every time the money arrived, Margaret would turn to little Gerard and murmur, “Oh, my poor deserted child, you and I are still wading in deep waters.” She poured at least half of that modest but useful supply into dressing the little boy far neater than most children of their station. “If this money does come from Gerard, he shall find his boy spotless the moment he steps through the door.” Soon all the other mothers on the street began to sneer at her, especially the ones whose own brats ran around in rags, elbows poking through their tattered sleeves.
The months rolled on, and the sharp, burning agony of anxious waiting faded into a cold, dead numbness in her heart. She returned to her first darkest fear: Gerard must be dead. She would never see her boy’s father again, never lay eyes on their marriage lines. This last grief, which had been softened for a time by Eli and Catherine’s open recognition of their betrothal, now revived in full force: strangers would never look so favourably on her story. Often she moaned over the fear of her boy being marked as illegitimate. “Is it not enough that we are bereaved of all we love? Must we be dishonoured too? Oh, that we had ne’er been born.”
A change came over Peter Brandt then. His mind, clouded for nearly two years after his paralytic stroke, seemed to clear at last. He had long intervals of full lucidity, where he and Margaret would sit and talk of Gerard, until the fog drifted back over his wits. But one day, when Margaret returned home after a few hours’ absence, she found him conversing easily with Catherine, a thing he had not managed since the stroke first fell.
“Eh, lass, why did you have to wander out?” Catherine said. “I told him every last thing, and we sat and cried together over thy troubles.”
Margaret stood silent, joy glowing in her face, looking back and forth between the two. Peter smiled softly at her, and held out his hand. “Come, let me bless thee.”
She knelt at his feet, and he blessed her most eloquently, telling her she was the most loving, truest, most obedient daughter Heaven had ever sent to a poor old widowed man. “May thy son be to thee what thou hast been to me!”
He dozed off shortly after that, and the two women whispered together. Catherine said, “All our talk just now was of Gerard. It lies heavy on his mind. His poor foggy head must have been listening to us all the months he seemed completely senseless. Margaret, he is a very sharp man, and thought of all kinds of things: ‘He may be in prison,’ he said, ‘or forced to go fighting for some foreign king, or sent to Constantinople to copy old manuscripts there, or gone into the Church after all.’ He always had a strong leaning that way.”
“Ah, mother,” Margaret whispered sadly in reply, “he only deceives himself, the same as we do.”
Ere she could finish the sentence, a strange, loud interruption burst out. A great voice cried, “I SEE HIM, I SEE HIM.” The old man’s eyes were wide and dilated, as if he were staring straight through the cottage walls. “IN A BOAT, ON A GREAT RIVER, COMING THIS WAY. His face is sore disfigured – but I knew him. Gone! Gone! All dark now.”
He sank back weakly, fumbling. “Where is Margaret?”
“Dear father, I am by thy side,” Margaret sobbed. “Oh, mother! Mother, what is this?”
“I cannot see thee now,” he whispered feebly, “but a moment agone I saw every corner of the whole world. Aye, aye. I am ready. Is this thy hand? Bless thee, my child, bless thee. Weep not! The tree is ripe.”
The old physician had read the signs aright. Those calm, soft words were his last. The next moment, he drooped his head, and gently, placidly, drifted away from earth, as soft as an infant sinking off to sleep. The torch of his life had flared bright one last time, before it went out for good.
