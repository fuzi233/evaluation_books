I rose before dawn, my body still heavy with sleep but my mind already awake and focused. Kneeling on the cold dormitory floor beside the central stand, I finished the final stitches of the guard I had been weaving throughout the night. The dim glow of the last watch of the night-lamp barely lit the room, but it was enough for me to see the delicate strands of silk and beads take shape under my fingers. Every thread had been used—every bead carefully selected. I had doubled the length, knowing from experience that richness and precision mattered when crafting something meant for a man whose taste demanded both subtlety and strength. To finish it, I needed only a clasp. Fortunately, I possessed one: the fastening of my sole necklace. I detached it with care, reattached it to the end of the chain, then wound the completed piece tightly into a small box I’d purchased especially for this purpose—a shimmering shell case of “nacarat” hue, adorned with a tiny crown of blue stones that sparkled like frozen stars. Inside the lid, using the tip of my scissors, I etched three initials: P. C. D. E. A quiet promise, hidden in plain sight.

The reader may recall Madame Beck’s annual fête—the grand celebration marked by gifts from the school. Each year, a collective offering was made in her honor, a tradition reserved solely for her and, in a quieter form, for her cousin and mentor, M. Emanuel. His recognition was not arranged or forced; it arose naturally from the respect his students held for him. Though he disdained jewels and fine things—clearly stating he would accept neither silver nor gold—he valued sincerity over cost. A single flower, a drawing, offered without pretense, pleased him more than any glittering ring or ornate box. He was not wise by worldly standards, yet there was something tender in his nature, a deep empathy for truth—even when it was painful. He could forgive misfortune if it was confessed honestly, but if he found deceit, if he sensed a lie beneath the surface, he became merciless. With a gaze that pierced through illusion, he could expose the hidden flaws of the soul—the twisted thoughts, the secret wounds, the false masks men and women wore. And when he did, he did so with brutal clarity, as if justice demanded nothing less. I often wept for those he unmasked. I resented him too. Yet I knew he believed himself righteous.

M. Paul’s fête fell on the first of March, a Thursday. The day dawned bright and clear, the sun warm upon the windows. It was also the morning of mass, and the afternoon brought a half-holiday—time to walk, shop, visit. These combined joys inspired a rare freshness among the girls. Clean collars appeared, the usual dull woolen uniforms replaced with lighter garments. Even Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, usually so cautious with money, wore a silk dress—unheard of in Labassecour, where such extravagance was frowned upon. She even summoned a hairdresser to style her hair, and whispers spread that she had dabbed her hands and handkerchief with a new perfume. Poor Zélie—she often lamented her life of confinement, her debts, her lack of freedom. She dreamed of marriage, of a husband who would pay her bills, clothe her, and allow her, at last, to “goûter un peu les plaisirs.” Rumors said she was enamored of M. Emanuel. And indeed, he watched her often—intently, silently. I’ve seen him fix his gaze on her for minutes while the class wrote in silence, his eyes sharp and unreadable. She flinched under his attention—half-pleased, half-afraid—and he seemed to know every tremor of her spirit. He saw beyond words, beyond smiles, into the depths where shame and longing hid. He could be cruel, yes—but never unjust. Not when the truth was laid bare.

After breakfast and mass, the bell rang. The classroom filled with girls dressed neatly, each holding a bouquet of spring flowers—tender blossoms, fragrant and fresh. Only I sat with empty hands. I liked flowers growing wild, rooted in soil. Once cut, they seemed hollow, fleeting. Their likeness to life saddened me. I never gave them to those I loved, nor did I wish to receive them. Zélie noticed my absence. Her eyes swept over me, searching for some hidden bloom, some discreet token. But there was none. She smiled, satisfied.

“How wise of you, Miss Lucie,” she said, “to save your money. I foolishly spent two francs on these hot-house flowers.”

She displayed her bouquet proudly.

Then—footsteps. _His_ footsteps. They came as always, brisk and precise, but today they carried a different weight. We felt it—an unspoken warmth, a hint of something softer beneath the usual rigidity. When he entered, the room brightened. He looked… changed. Gone was the dark paletôt, its folds hiding his figure. Instead, he wore a clean coat and a silk waistcoat, neat and refined. The defiant bonnet-grec was absent; he walked bare-headed, a Christian hat tucked in his gloved hand. He looked better than ever—his blue eyes clear, his dark face glowing with quiet kindness. One might forget his nose was broad, his cheek thin, his brow severe. He stood before us not as a tyrant, but as a man capable of grace.

He placed his hat and gloves on the desk. “Bon jour, mes amies,” he said—not with cheer, nor with the oily tone of a priest, but with a voice that belonged only to him, one that spoke when his heart moved his lips. That heart, though irritable, was not stone. Beneath its surface lay a tenderness that drew him toward children, toward girls, toward women—though he fought it, he could not deny the bond.

“We all wish you a good day, Monsieur,” Zélie began, stepping forward. “We offer our congratulations on your fête.”

She laid her bouquet before him. Then one after another, the girls passed by, leaving their nosegays. Soon, the desk was crowned with a towering pyramid of flowers—so lush, so vibrant, it nearly swallowed him whole. Silence followed. We waited.

Five minutes passed. Ten. Still no word.

Then, from behind the floral mountain, a voice emerged—low, deep, almost growling.

“Est-ce là tout?”

Zélie glanced around.

“Have all given their bouquets?” she asked.

Yes. All had offered theirs. The senior mistress confirmed it.

“Est-ce là tout?” he repeated, lower now, heavier.

Zélie stepped forward again, smiling sweetly. “Monsieur, I have the honor to inform you that, with one exception, every girl has presented her bouquet. Miss Lucie, being a foreigner, may not have known our custom—or perhaps she found it too frivolous to observe.”

“Famous!” I muttered under my breath. “You’re quite the speaker, Zélie.”

From the estrade, a hand rose—gesturing, silent, dismissing words.

Then, slowly, he emerged. Standing before the map of the world on the wall, he stared straight ahead, and once more, in tones that seemed almost tragic:

“Est-ce là tout?”

I could have corrected it. I had the little shell box in my hand. I had planned to give it. But first, the absurdity of his performance amused me. Then Zélie’s intervention sparked defiance. I had no desire to defend myself against her insinuations. And besides—M. Paul had taken my refusal so seriously. He deserved to be annoyed. So I kept my box, my composure, and sat like stone.

“It is well,” he said at last. A shadow crossed his face—anger, sorrow, resolve. He swallowed hard, then launched into his customary speech.

I don’t remember what he said. I didn’t listen. The effort to suppress his emotion, the abrupt shift from pain to control, left me oddly unsettled. The humor faded.

Near the end of his talk, a distraction broke the tension. I dropped my thimble. In bending to pick it up, I struck my head on the corner of the desk. The noise startled him. He abandoned his forced calm, cast aside dignity, and erupted—this time, not in French, but in English.

Somehow, during his speech, he had crossed the Channel and landed on British soil. And there he stood, scathing.

A quick glance swept the room—sharp, cynical—settling on me. Then he turned his fury on “les Anglaises.”

I had never heard English women treated so harshly. He spared nothing—their minds, morals, manners, appearance. He mocked their height, their necks, their arms, their clothes, their education, their skepticism, their pride, their hypocrisy. He ground his teeth, as if he longed to say worse. He was bitter, venomous, ugly.

“Little wicked creature,” I thought. “Am I to fear offending you? No. You shall be indifferent to me—as the shabbiest bouquet in your pile.”

But I couldn’t hold it. At first, I endured it. Fifteen minutes passed, and I remained still. But then he attacked our greatest names, our heroes, our history—spreading lies, defiling symbols, dragging the Union Jack through mud. Zélie grinned, the whole class laughed. They hated England secretly. And then—I struck my desk sharply, opened my mouth, and shouted:

“Vive l’Angleterre, l’Histoire et les Héros! A bas la France, la Fiction et les Faquins!”

Silence. Shock. I suppose they thought I’d gone mad. The Professor pulled out his handkerchief, pressed it to his lips, and smiled—darkly, triumphantly. He had won. He had made me angry. Instantly, he softened. He resumed his poetic tone, praised the flowers, compared the girls to blossoms, complimented Zélie’s bouquet, and announced a spring outing—breakfast in the country, for “those he could call friends.”

“Donc je n’y serai pas,” I said, without thinking.

“Soit,” he replied. He gathered the flowers, strode out of the room, and I, gathering my things, climbed the stairs. I don’t know if he was angry—but I was.

Yet within an hour, sitting on the edge of my bed, replaying his look, his words, I began to smile. A pang of regret stirred—had I not offered the box? I had meant to please him. Fate had other plans.

Later, remembering that desks were not sacred, and fearing someone might open mine—especially since it bore the initials P. C. D. E.—I descended to the classroom.

It was empty. The day pupils had gone home, the boarders were walking, the teachers shopping. The rooms were silent. The grande salle, with its globe, chandeliers, and closed piano, rested in peace. I paused at the first classe door—it was slightly ajar. Unusual. Usually locked. I hesitated, then heard a sound—a step, a chair shifting, the creak of a drawer opening.

“Madame Beck is inspecting,” I thought. But when I peered inside, it wasn’t her. It was a man—M. Emanuel—sitting in my chair, his hand rummaging through my desk, his face buried in my papers. He wore his ordinary paletôt, the bonnet-grec discarded on the floor.

I knew he visited my desk. Often. He left signs—exercises corrected overnight, books mysteriously appearing, pamphlets tucked into my basket. All smelled of cigars. I’d tried to air them once. He caught me, took the book from my hands, and would have thrown it into the stove. I snatched it back. After that, I stopped protesting.

But I’d never seen him. Until now.

And here he was—smoking, his pale blue breath curling from his lips as he leaned over my desk. I stepped quietly forward, standing behind him, leaning over his shoulder.

My heart ached. After this morning’s hostility, after my silence, after his hurt—he had brought me two handsome volumes, chosen with care. Now, he stirred my papers gently, rearranging them, not destroying. I watched him, unconscious, doing me good. My anger melted. I did not dislike him.

He turned. He heard me breathe. His nerves were sharp, but he didn’t flinch.

“I thought you were in town,” he said, regaining his composure. “It’s fortunate you’re not. Do you think I care about being caught? Not I. I come here often.”

“I know,” I said.

“You find books. But you don’t read them—they’ve passed under this,” he gestured to his cigar.

“They have. But I do read them.”

“Without pleasure?”

“Monsieur must not be contradicted.”

“Do you like them? Are they acceptable?”

“You’ve seen me reading them a hundred times. I have few diversions. I value what you provide.”

“I mean well. If you see that, and enjoy my efforts—why can we not be friends?”

“A fatalist would say—because we cannot.”

“This morning,” he said, “I woke cheerful. I came happy. You spoiled my day.”

“No, Monsieur. Only a few hours. Unintentionally.”

“Unintentionally? No. It was my fête. Everyone wished me joy. The little girls gave violets, whispered blessings. You—nothing. Not a leaf, not a glance. Was that unintentional?”

“I meant no harm.”

“Then you truly did not know our custom? You would have spent a few centimes on a flower, had you known it was expected?”

“I knew. I was prepared. Yet I spent nothing.”

He studied me. Then, slowly, he nodded. The moment hung between us—quiet, charged. And I knew, in that silence, that something had shifted.
