The mountain Dante described, heavy at the base yet growing lighter with every ascent, became Gwendolen's metaphor for her current exile. It was scarcely a week since her mother's return that Gwendolen yielded to the prospect of staying in Genoa. Paradoxically, the desire to flee that jewel of the sea rallied her fractured spirit; for what location, however verdant the valley of Enna, does not transform under the inward eye into a ring of torment where blossoms burn like tongues of fire against the soles of one's feet?

"I shall never look upon the Mediterranean again," Gwendolen confessed to her mother, who perceived the depth of her daughter's aversion even without the explicit mention of her late husband.

Mrs. Davilow, though formally bound to regard this season as one of catastrophic loss, found herself experiencing life with a vigor she had not known since Gwendolen's marriage. It appeared her beloved daughter had returned not merely with the old affection, but with a conscious, possessive cherishing of her mother's proximity, akin to clutching a treasure nearly lost forever.

"Are you there, Mamma?" Gwendolen cried out in the dead of night, a bed having been prepared for her mother beside her own. She spoke with the vulnerability of a frightened child in her early girlhood, needing the anchor of presence.

"Yes, dear; can I help you?"

"No, thank you; I simply like to know you are there. Do you mind my waking you?" This question, however, bore none of the innocence of her youth.

"I was not asleep, darling."

"It didn't seem real that you were with me. I wanted to make it real. I can endure anything if you are here. But you must not lie awake, anxious for me. You must be happy now. You must let me make you happy at last—or else, what shall I do?"

"God bless you, dear; I possess the greatest happiness possible when you hold me in such high regard."

Yet the following night, hearing her mother sighing and restless, Mrs. Davilow offered, "Let me give you your sleeping draught, Gwendolen."

"No, Mamma, thank you; I do not wish to sleep."

"It would be so beneficial for you to rest more, my darling."

"Do not speak of what *should* be good for me, Mamma," Gwendolen retorted, her voice impetuous. "You do not know what is good for me. Neither you nor Uncle Hugo must contradict me or dictate what benefits me when I feel it brings harm."

Mrs. Davilow fell silent, unsurprised that the poor child was becoming irritable. Soon, Gwendolen whispered, "I was always naughty to you, Mamma."

"No, dear, no."

"Yes, I was," Gwendolen insisted, her voice rising. "It is precisely because I was always wicked that I am miserable now."

She erupted into sobs and cries. The desperate determination to remain silent regarding the grim facts of her married life and its sudden end had reacted violently, manifesting in these bursts of enigmatic distress.

However, faint lights of interpretation began to break upon the mother's mind through information relayed from Sir Hugo to Mr. Gascoigne, and subsequently, with selective omissions, to herself. The kindly baronet, while overseeing the proper dignities surrounding his nephew's death and the possibility of recovering the body, deemed it the kindest act to utilize his cordial relationship with the rector to communicate the substance of Grandcourt's will. His aim was to spare the clergyman the additional shock of returning home with illusions intact. Perhaps Sir Hugo would have shared the news regardless of this motive, but he genuinely felt driven by it. He broke the unwelcome tidings gradually: first implying his fear that the widow was not as generously provided for as Mr. Gascoigne, or indeed as the baronet himself had anticipated; only finally, after vague references to significant claims on Grandcourt's fortune, did he reveal the prior relations that, in the unfortunate absence of a legitimate heir, had redirected all the wealth elsewhere.

The rector was deeply wounded, remembering with renewed vividness how offensively proud and repellent the deceased's manners had been toward him. He recalled also receiving hints during that transitional period before the new occupant arrived at Diplow regarding the late gentleman's former entanglements and an excessive addiction to pleasure, though he had not foresaw that the pleasures, presumably swept into private refuse heaps, would ever resurface as a swarm of live caterpillars devouring the green flesh of respectable society. Yet, he did not voice these retrospective thoughts to Sir Hugo, nor did he lower himself by expressing indignation on purely personal grounds. Instead, he conducted himself as a worldly man who had become a conscientious clergyman. His opening remark was, "When a young man makes his will in health, he usually counts on living a long while. Probably Mr. Grandcourt did not believe this document would ever have its present effect." After a pause, he added, "The effect is painful in more ways than one. Female morality is likely to suffer from this marked advantage and prominence being given to illegitimate offspring."

"Well, in point of fact," said Sir Hugo, in his comforting manner, "since the boy is there, this was truly the best alternative for disposing of the estates. Grandcourt had no one nearer than his cousin. And it is a chilling thought that you leave this life solely for the benefit of a cousin. A man derives a small pleasure in making a will if it benefits his own 'curly heads'; but it becomes a nuisance when you are bequeathing everything to a used-up fellow like yourself, someone you do not care two straws for. It is the next worst thing to retaining only a life interest in your estates. No; I forgive Grandcourt for that portion of his will. But, between ourselves, what I cannot forgive him for is the shabby manner in which he has provided for your niece—our niece, I must say—not a position superior to that of a doctor's widow. Nothing grates on me more than that posthumous stinginess toward a wife. A man ought to possess some pride and fondness for his widow. *I* certainly do. I consider it a test of a man's character that he feels at ease about his own death when he can envision his wife and daughters comfortable thereafter. I recall those fellows in the Crimean War who were willing to sink to the bottom of the sea if their widows were provided for."

"It has certainly surprised me," admitted Mr. Gascoigne, "all the more so because, standing in the place of a father to my niece, I had demonstrated my reliance on Mr. Grandcourt's apparent liberality by making no prior claims for her. That seemed to me the only honorable course under the circumstances. Probably you deem me blameworthy."

"Not blamable exactly. I respect a man for trusting another. But take my advice. If you marry another niece, though it be to the Archbishop of Canterbury, bind him down legally. Your niece cannot be married for the first time twice over. And if he is a good fellow, he will wish to be bound. As for Mrs. Grandcourt, I can only say that I feel my connection to her drawn closer because I believe she has not been well-treated. And I hope you will urge her to rely on me as a friend."

Thus spoke the chivalrous Sir Hugo, disgusted that the young and beautiful widow of a Mallinger Grandcourt was left with merely two thousand pounds a year and a residence in a coal-mining district. To the rector, that income appeared less shabby and devoid of mortifying privations; yet in this conversation, he consumed a much sharper sense of the humiliation cast upon his niece—and her closest confidants—by the public revelation of her husband's liaison with Mrs. Glasher. Like all men who are devoted husbands and fathers, he felt this humiliation vicariously through the minds of the women primarily affected by it. Consequently, the initial annoyance of hearing the facts was far outweighed by the pain of communicating them to Mrs. Davilow and anticipating Gwendolen's reaction whenever her mother deemed it appropriate to disclose them. For the good rector held an innocent conviction that his niece was unaware of Mrs. Glasher's existence, reasoning with masculine logic from what maidens and wives typically know, do, and suffer, despite having possessed a most imperfect observation of the specific maiden and wife in question.

Not so Gwendolen's mother. Now, she believed she saw an explanation for much that had remained enigmatic in her child's conduct and words before and after the engagement. She concluded that in some inconceivable manner, Gwendolen had been informed of this clandestine marriage and the existence of the children. She trusted that opportunities would arise during moments of affectionate confidence before and during their journey to England, allowing her to gradually discern how fully the truth was clear to Gwendolen and to prepare her for whatever disappointments awaited. But she was spared the burden of devising strategies on the subject.

"I hope you do not expect that I am going to be rich and grand, Mamma," Gwendolen said, not long after the rector's communication. She had been dressed and sat for a long time in quiet meditation.

Mrs. Davilow was startled but replied, after a moment's reflection, "Oh yes, dear, you will have something. Sir Hugo knows all about the will."

"That will not decide," Gwendolen stated abruptly.

"Surely, dear: Sir Hugo says you are to receive two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere."
