Of old Deleglise’s Sunday suppers—those solemn, jubilant rites performed in a great kitchen as bright as a conscience that has forgotten everything except cleanliness—much has already been written by persons who knew, or at least who knew the correct people to say they knew. Deleglise, buttoned into linen from throat to wrist as though he were about to be served instead of serving, moved about his stove with a flushed, almost devotional earnestness. The stove shone; the copper shone; the saucepans shone; and in the intervals of that shining there would be the soft, brisk whisper of Madeline’s white cap as she leaned, nodded, darted—an ancient bonne with hands that could fold a napkin into a swan while her nose judged a béchamel.

Behind him, round the massive oaken table, sat guests waiting in the reverent hush that falls when food is not yet on the tongue. The glass cut the lamplight into little crystals. The silver made small, domestic chimes when a spoon was shifted or a fork was set down. People talked in underbreaths until Deleglise turned, radiant and reborn, bearing the savoury triumph steaming between his hands. Then, as though somebody had struck a chord, talk would swell again—first a murmur, then a roar—like music returning after a pause that had been holding everybody’s nerves by the collar.

They were famous men—names that were already household words and in some cases have remained so. Artists, musicians, actors, writers, scholars, and those droll persons who do not belong to a profession so much as to an atmosphere. Their epigrams and anecdotes can be had in memoirs; and if you, gentle reader, find them less dazzling on the page than we did in the room, blame time and print and the fact that wit, like soufflé, does not travel.

So I shall not attempt to warm up old dishes you can order elsewhere. My own little entry into that charmed circle was brief; what matters more to my tale are the afternoon At Homes that Dan and I held in Queen Square, on the second floor of a quiet old Georgian house where even the staircase seemed to creak in an accent older than modern London. On those days cook and housemaid had a morning of it. And because we had no sensible authority to keep us from quarrelling, Dan and I made a compact: each should supervise the other’s department. I judged Dan’s cooking; Dan judged my housekeeping. It was as harmonious as asking two cocks to arrange the sunrise.

“Too much soda,” I would say, tasting the cake with the wary air of a man stepping on ice.

“You silly Juggins! It’s meant to taste of soda—it’s a soda cake.”

“I know it is. It isn’t meant to taste of nothing but soda. There wants to be some cake about it also. This thing is a solidified Seidlitz powder. You can’t give people Seidlitz powders for tea, even if you set them in a plate and call them ‘home-made.’”

Dan would fume, but I would remain granite. The soda cake would be laid aside like a moral error, and something else attempted. Dan’s cookery was the one thing he was obstinate about. He would never admit wrong in it; and his most ghastly failures he would later devour himself with an expression of martyr-like enjoyment. I have known him finish a sponge cake whose centre needed a teaspoon, and declare it delicious—maintaining, with a theorist’s passion, that a dry sponge cake was like eating dust, that sponge cake ought to be “a trifle syrupy towards the centre.” Afterwards he would be strangely silent and drink brandy out of a wine-glass, as though to rinse his principles.

Then Dan would turn on me.

“Call these knives clean?”

“Yes, I do.”

Dan would draw his finger across one and produce a mark like a landscape in chiaroscuro.

“Not if you go fingering them. Why don’t you leave them alone and go on with your own work?”

“You’ve just wiped them, that’s all.”

“Well, there isn’t any knife-powder.”

“Yes, there is.”

“Besides, it ruins knives, over-cleaning them—takes all the edge off. We shall want them sharp to cut those lemon buns of yours.”

“Over-cleaning them! You don’t take any pride in the place.”

“Good Lord! Don’t I work from morning to night?”

“You lazy young devil!”

“Makes one lazy, your cooking. How can a man work when he is suffering all day long from indigestion?”

But Dan would not rest until I had fetched the board and polished every blade to his entire satisfaction, as if each knife were being prepared to carve a reputation. Perhaps it was as well; once a week at least the household was driven, by mutual insult, into order. After lunch cook and housemaid vanished. Two carefully dressed gentlemen—who had the air of men who would rather have stayed in aprons—were left alone to receive their guests.

Our company came partly from Dan’s journalistic acquaintance, partly from my theatre friends. Occasionally Minikin and Jarman appeared; even Mrs. Peedles once or twice came breathless up our stairs. Left to myself I should not, perhaps, have invited them to mingle with the rest; they belonged to a different London—one that smelt less of cut flowers and more of honest damp and lodging-house stair carpets. But Dan, once introduced, insisted.

“My dear Lord Chamberlain,” Dan would reply when I hinted timidly at “fitness,” “an ounce of originality is worth a ton of convention. Little tin ladies and gentlemen stamped out to pattern—one can find them everywhere. Your friends would be an acquisition to any society.”

“But are they quite—good form?” I ventured.

“I’ll tell you what we will do,” said Dan. “We will forget that Mrs. Peedles keeps a lodging-house in Blackfriars. We will speak of her as our friend, ‘that dear, quaint old creature, Lady P.’ A title that is an oddity, whose costume always suggests the wardrobe of a provincial actress! My dear Paul, your society novelist would make a fortune out of her. Her anecdotes, instead of being about third-rate theatrical folk, shall concern Earl Blank and the Baroness de Dash. Society journals shall pay me a shilling a line. Jarman—yes, Jarman shall be the son of a South American millionaire. Vulgar? Nonsense! you mean racy. Minikin—who looks much more like forty than twenty—shall be an eminent scientist. His head will then appear natural size; his glass eye can be the result of a chemical experiment; his uncompromising rudeness will become a lovable peculiarity. We’ll put a yard of red ribbon across his shirt-front and call him Herr Professor. It will explain his English and his accent. They shall be our lions. We will invite commonplace people to meet them.”

And to my terror Dan did it.

Jarman entered into the spirit of the joke as though he had been born into it. I am convinced that, so far as he was concerned, our guests were deceived from beginning to end. The more he swaggered, the more he boasted, the more he talked about himself—the greater became his success. It amused me to see Dan’s journalistic friends trying to excite his cupidity with visions of “new papers” that needed only a couple of thousand pounds to command, by the sheer brilliance of their “ideas,” a circulation of hundreds of thousands. But when I watched my actress friends exert themselves to fascinate him—luring him into corners, gazing at him with languishing eyes, performing all their little tricks for his benefit, quarrelling about him like children over a toy—I began to feel my conscience prick. Jokes, like soufflés, can collapse into a mess if you open the oven door at the wrong moment.

Fortunately Jarman was a gentleman of uncommon sense. He enjoyed his good time while it lasted. Later he married the eldest daughter of a small blacking factory. She was plain, but pleasant; and she brought him, in due course, possession of the factory itself. When I meet him now—stout and rubicund—he gives the impression of a man who has attained his ideals and sits down inside them, comfortable as in an armchair.

Minikin gave us more trouble. Some guests came equipped with scientific smatterings; we had to explain that the Professor never talked shop. Others turned out to know German and insisted on using it; we had to explain that Herr Professor was in London to learn English and had taken a vow neither to speak nor listen to his native tongue during his stay. It was remarked that his acquaintance with English slang was unusually complete for a foreigner. Occasionally he was too rude even for a scientist: informing ladies, clamouring to know how he liked English women, that he didn’t like them silly; telling an important friend of Dan’s, who asked what he got his yard of ribbon for, that he got it for fourpence. We explained him as a man soured by a love disappointment. Ladies forgave him; gentlemen said it was a damned lucky thing for the girl. In short, Minikin took explaining as a rich sauce takes stirring.

Lady Peedles, our guests decided among themselves, must be the widow of somebody in the City who had been knighted in a crowd. They made fun of her behind her back but were effusive to her face. “My dear Lady Peedles” became the refrain whenever she was present. At the theatre “my friend Lady Peedles” was much spoken of—generally in loud tones. My own social position improved because her ladyship liked me. It went abroad that I was her presumptive heir. I was courted as a gentleman of expectations, which is a title that costs nothing but earns you many smiles.

The fishy-eyed young man became a regular visitor. Dan won his heart by never laughing at him.

“I like talking to you,” he said one afternoon to Dan. “You don’t go into fits of laughter when I remark it’s been a fine day; most people do. On the stage I don’t mind. I know I’m a funny little devil. I get my living by being a funny little devil. There’s a photograph of me in the lobby. I saw a workman stop and look at it the other day; I was just behind him. He burst into a roar. ‘Little—! He makes me laugh to look at him!’ he cluttered to himself. Well, that’s all right. I want the man in the gallery to think me funny, but it annoys me when people laugh at me off the stage. If I’m out to dinner and ask somebody to pass the mustard, I never get it; instead they burst out laughing. I don’t want people to laugh at me when I’m having my dinner. I want my dinner. It makes me very angry sometimes.”

“I know,” said Dan sympathetically. “The world never grasps that a man is a collection, not a single exhibit. I remember being at a house once where the chief guest happened to be a great Hebrew scholar. One tea time a Miss Henman, passing the butter in a hurry, let it slip. ‘Why is Miss Henman like a caterpillar?’ asked our learned guest in a sepulchral voice. Nobody knew. ‘Because she makes the butter fly.’ It never occurred to any one of us he could joke. Dead silence for a minute. Then our hostess, grave as a judge, remarked, ‘Oh, do you really think so?’”

“If I were to enter a room full of people,” said the little man, “and tell them my mother had been run over by an omnibus, they’d think it the funniest story they’d heard in years.”

He was playing a principal part now in the opera, and he undoubtedly drew the house. Yet he was not happy.

“I’m not a comic actor, really,” he explained. “I could play Romeo, so far as feeling is concerned, and play it damned well. There’s a fine vein of poetry in me. But of course it’s no good with this face of mine.”

“But are you not sinning your mercies?” Dan retorted. “There’s young Kelver here. At school his trouble was he could give us a good time and make us laugh, which nobody else could do. His ambition was to kick a ball as well as a hundred other fellows could kick it. He could tell us a good story now if he’d only write what the Almighty intended him to write, instead of gloomy rigmaroles about suffering Princesses in Welsh caves. I don’t say it’s bad, but a hundred others could write the same sort of thing better.”

“Can’t you understand,” said the little man, “the poorest tragedian that ever lived never wished himself the best of low comedians. The court fool had an excellent salary, no doubt, and likely enough had got two-thirds of the brain in the palace. But not a wooden-headed man-at-arms but looked down on him. Every gallery boy who pays a shilling to laugh at me regards himself as my intellectual superior; while to a fourth-rate spouter of blank verse he looks up in admiration.”

“Does it matter so very much,” suggested Dan, “how the wooden-headed man-at-arms or the shilling gallery boy happens to regard you?”

“Yes, it does,” snapped Goggles, “because we happen to agree with them. If I could earn five pounds a week as juvenile lead, I’d never play a comic part again.”

“There I can’t follow you,” said Dan. “I can understand the artist who would rather be the man of action, the poet who would rather be the statesman or the warrior—though my sympathies are the other way, with Wolfe who thought it more glorious to write a great poem than to burn so many cities and kill so many men. We all serve the community. Some feed it, some clothe it. The churchman and the policeman look after its morals and keep it in order. The doctor mends it; the lawyer helps it to quarrel; the soldier teaches it to fight. We Bohemians amuse it, instruct it. We can argue we’re the most important: the others cater for the body, we for the mind. But their work is showier and attracts more attention—and attracting attention is the aim and object of most of us. For Bohemians to quarrel among themselves which is greatest is without reason. The story-teller, the musician, the artist, the clown—we’re members of a sharing troupe; one makes the people’s flesh creep; another makes them hold their sides with laughter. The tragedian, soliloquising on his crimes, shows us how wicked we are; you, looking at lovers from under a scratch wig, show us how ridiculous we are. Both lessons are necessary. Who shall say which is the superior teacher?”

“Ah, I’m not a philosopher,” sighed the little man.

“Ah,” sighed Dan, “and I’m not a comic actor on my way to a salary of a hundred a week. We all want the other boy’s cake.”

The O’Kelly became another of our frequent visitors. The attic in Belsize Square had been closed. In vain had the O’Kelly wafted incense, burned pastilles, sprinkled eau-de-Cologne. In vain had he talked of rats and hinted at drains.

“A wonderful woman,” groaned the O’Kelly, in tones of sorrowful admiration. “There’s no deceiving her.”

“But why submit?” we argued. “Why not say you are going to smoke, and do it?”

“It’s her theory, me boy,” he explained, “that the home should be kept pure—a sort of a temple, ye know. She’s convinced it’s bound in time to exercise an influence upon me. It’s a beautiful idea, when ye come to think of it.”

Meanwhile, in the rooms of half-a-dozen sinful men the O’Kelly kept his own particular pipe and his own particular smoking mixture; and one pipe and one tobacco jar stood always on our mantelpiece like contraband displayed with shameless honesty.

In the spring the forces of temptation raged round that feeble but excellently intentioned citadel—the O’Kelly’s conscience. The Signora had returned to England and was performing then at Ashley’s Theatre. The O’Kelly would sit for long spells of silence, puffing vigorously, as if to smother thought in smoke. Or he would fortify himself with paeans in praise of Mrs. O’Kelly.

“If anything could ever make a model man of me”—he spoke in the tones of one whose doubts were stronger than his hopes—“it would be the example of that woman.”

It was a Saturday afternoon; I had just returned from the matinee.

“I don’t believe,” he continued, “I don’t really believe she has ever done one single thing she oughtn’t to, or left undone one single thing she ought, in the whole course of her life.”

“Maybe she has, and you don’t know of it,” I suggested, thinking the notion might ease him.

“I wish I could think so,” he answered. “I don’t mean anything really wrong,” he corrected himself quickly, “but something just a little wrong. I feel—I really feel I should like her better if she had.”

“Not that I mean I don’t like her as it is, ye understand,” he corrected himself again. “I respect that woman—I cannot tell ye, me boy, how much I respect her. Ye don’t know her. There was one morning, about a month ago. That woman—she’s down at six every morning, summer and winter; we have prayers at half-past. I was a trifle late meself: early rising was never me strong point, as ye know. Seven o’clock struck; she didn’t appear, and I thought she had overslept. I won’t say I didn’t feel pleased for the moment; it was an unworthy sentiment, but I almost wished she had. I ran up to her room. The door was open, the bedclothes folded down as she always leaves them. She came in five minutes later. She had got up at four that morning to welcome a troupe of native missionaries from East Africa on their arrival at Waterloo Station. She’s a saint, that woman; I am not worthy of her.”

“I shouldn’t dwell too much on that phase of the subject,” I said.

“I can’t help it, me boy. I feel I am not.”

“I don’t for a moment say you are,” I returned; “but I wouldn’t harp on it. It’s not good for you.”

“I never will be,” he persisted, gloomily. “Never!”

He was on a dangerous train of reflection. To shunt him, I led the talk to champagne.

“Most people like it dry,” he admitted. “Meself, I’ve always preferred it with just a suggestion of fruitiness.”

“There was a champagne,” I said, “you used to be rather fond of—years ago.”

“I think I know the one ye mean,” he said. “It wasn’t at all bad, considering the price.”

“You don’t happen to remember where you got it?”

“It was in Bridge Street,” he recalled, “not so very far from the Circus.”

“It’s a pleasant evening,” I remarked. “Let us take a walk.”

We found the place—half wine-shop, half office—with the air of somewhere that did business by day and conscience by night.

“Just the same,” commented the O’Kelly as we pushed in. “Not altered a bit.”

As barely twelve months had likely elapsed since his last visit, the miracle of stability was not extraordinary; but clearly the O’Kelly was counting time by sensation. I ordered a bottle and we sat. Being prejudiced against the brand, I called for claret. The O’Kelly finished the bottle. The virtue of that wine had not departed. With every glass he became morally more elevated, as though bubbles rose not only in the drink but in his resolutions.

He left the place determined to be worthy of Mrs. O’Kelly. Walking down the Embankment, he asserted he would buy an alarm-clock that very evening. At Westminster Bridge he fell suddenly silent. Looking to discover why, I saw his eyes fixed on a poster representing a charming lady standing on one leg upon a wire, with mountain peaks beneath her; the artist had even caught the likeness. I cursed our luck; then, lacking experience, felt reassured.

“Me dear Paul,” said the O’Kelly, laying a fatherly hand on my shoulder, “there are fair-faced, laughing women—sweet creatures, that ye want to put yer arm around and dance with.” He shook his head disapprovingly. “There are the sainted women, who lead us up, Paul—up, always up.”

A look, such as the young man with the banner might have carried to fields of snow and ice, suffused his handsome face. Without another word he crossed the road and entered an American store, where for six-and-elevenpence he purchased an alarm-clock the man assured us would awake an Egyptian mummy. With this in his hand he waved me good-bye, jumped on a Hampstead ’bus, and I strolled alone towards the theatre, reflecting that modern civilisation can sell you machinery for waking, but cannot provide the corresponding mechanism for going to bed.

Hal returned a little after Christmas and set himself up in chambers in the City—the nearest, so he said, that he dared venture to civilisation.

“I’d be no good in the West End,” he explained. “For a season I might attract as an eccentricity, but your swells would never stand me longer—no more would any respectable folk, anywhere. We don’t get on together. I commenced at Richmond. It was fashionable then, and I thought I was going to do wonders. I had everything in my favour, except myself. I do know my work; nobody can deny that. My father spent every penny he had, poor gentleman, in buying me an old-established practice: fine house, carriage and pair, white-haired butler—everything correct, except myself. It was no use. I can hold myself in a month or two; then I break out, the old original savage that I am under my frock coat. I feel I must run amuck, stabbing, hacking at the prim, smiling Lies mincing round about me.

“I can fool a silly woman for half-a-dozen visits—bow and rub my hands, purr round her sympathetically. All the while I’m longing to tell her the truth:

“‘Go home. Wash your face; don’t block up the pores of your skin with paint. Let out your corsets. You’re thirty-three round the abdomen if you’re an inch: how do you expect digestion to do its work when you’ve squeezed it into twenty-one? Give up gadding about half the day and most of the night. You’re old enough to have done with that. Let the children come, and suckle them yourself. You’ll be the better for them. Don’t loll in bed all the morning. Get up like a decent animal and do something for your living. Use your brain—what there is of it—and your body. At that price you can have health to-morrow, and at no other. I can do nothing for you.’

“And sooner or later I blurt it out.” He laughed his great roar. “Lord! you should see the real face coming out of the simpering mask.

“Pompous old fools strutting into me like turkey-cocks! By Jove, it was worth it! They’d dribble out looking half their proper size after I’d done telling them what was the matter with them.

“‘Do you want to know what you’re really suffering from?’ I’d shout when I could contain myself no longer. ‘Gluttony, my dear sir; gluttony and drunkenness and over-indulgence in other vices that shall be nameless. Live like a man. Get a little self-respect from somewhere. Give up being an ape. Treat your body properly and it will treat you properly. That’s the only prescription that’ll do you any good.’”

He laughed again. “‘Tell the truth, you shame the Devil.’ But the Devil replies by starving you. It’s a fairly effective retort. I’m not the stuff successful family physicians are made of. In the City I may rub along. One doesn’t see so much of one’s patients; they come and go. Clerks and warehousemen chiefly. The poor man doesn’t mind being told the truth about himself; it’s a blessing he’s accustomed to.”

We spoke but once of Barbara. Her photograph, in her bride’s dress, stood upon my desk. Occasionally—first fitting the room for the ceremony, sweeping away all impurity even from under the mats, and dressing myself with care—I would set it amid flowers, and kneeling, kiss her hand where it rested on the back of that absurd, top-heavy chair without which no photographic studio seems complete.

One day Hal took it up and looked at it long and hard.
