"Filled with awe," I wrote, the ink feeling heavy on the nib. Above, the sun burned with a golden intensity; below, the sea stretched out in a vast, indifferent blue. The same world that sent green shoots toward the heat and birds into the air I had breathed since infancy. Yet I knew this splendor was merely a backdrop, a painted drop-scene ready to be lifted at any moment to reveal the machinery of something far more terrible.

Shortly after my father's death, I sat in the library. I had been staring listlessly at the portrait hanging among the volumes—a distant ancestor I knew only as a face on paper—wishing I could pierce the canvas to find the man behind it. Instead, I picked up a book and began to read.

Glancing up, I saw him approaching. He did not walk between me and the door, but cut across the space between me and the portrait. A thin, pale man in rust-colored black. He moved with a sharp, predatory eagerness, possessing a nose so prominent it reminded me instantly of a specific jug my sisters used to mockingly call "Mr. Crow."

"Finding myself in your vicinity, Mr. Vane, I have given myself the pleasure of calling," he said. His voice was peculiar, yet devoid of malice. "Your honored grandfather treated me—I may say it without presumption—as a friend, having known me from childhood as his father's librarian."

At the time, I failed to register the sheer antiquity of the creature before me. "May I ask where you live now, Mr. Crow?" I asked, trying to maintain a veneer of composure.

He smiled, a expression that didn't quite reach his eyes. "You nearly hit my name," he rejoined. "Which shows the family insight. You have seen me before, but only once, and could not then have heard it!"

"Where was that?"

"In this very room. You were quite a child, however!"

Memory is a fallible construct. For a fleeting second, I thought I recognized him, but the image dissolved. "Could you clarify your identity?" I demanded.

"There is such a thing as remembering without recognizing the memory within it," he remarked calmly. "For my name—which you have near enough—it used to be Raven."

The name struck a chord. Marvellous tales had brought it to me before. "It is very kind of you to visit," I said, forcing politeness. "Will you not sit down?"

He seated himself immediately. "You knew my father, then, I presume?"

"I knew him," he answered with a curious tilt of the head. "But he did not care about my acquaintance, and we never met. That gentleman, however," he added, gesturing sharply toward the portrait, "Old Sir Up'ard, his people called him, was in his day a friend of mine yet more intimate than ever your grandfather became."

Then the interview began to feel strangely off-key. It was hardly stranger that my visitor should remember Sir Upward than that he should have served as my great-grandfather's librarian!

"I owe him much," he continued, leaning forward. "Although I had read many more books than he, yet, through the special direction of his studies, he was able to inform me of a certain relation of modes which I should never have discovered of myself, and could hardly have learned from any one else."

"Would you mind telling me all about that?"

"By no means—as much at least as I am able: there are not such things as wilful secrets," he answered, and launched into his tale.

"That closet held his library—a hundred manuscripts or so, for printing was not then invented. One morning I sat there, working at a catalogue of them, when he looked in at the door and said, 'Come.' I laid down my pen and followed him—across the great hall, down a steep rough descent, and along an underground passage to a tower he had lately built, consisting of a stair and a room at the top of it. The door of this room had a tremendous lock, which he undid with the smallest key I ever saw. I had scarcely crossed the threshold after him, when, to my eyes, he began to dwindle, and grew less and less. All at once my vision seemed to come right, and I saw that he was moving swiftly away from me. In a minute more he was the merest speck in the distance, with the tops of blue mountains beyond him, clear against a sky of paler blue. I recognised the country, for I had gone there and come again many a time, although I had never known this way to it."

"Many years after, when the tower had long disappeared, I taught one of his descendants what Sir Upward had taught me; and now and then to this day I use your house when I want to go the nearest way home. I must indeed—without your leave, for which I ask your pardon—have by this time well established a right of way through it—not from front to back, but from bottom to top!"

"You would have me then understand, Mr. Raven," I said, my voice tightening, "that you go through my house into another world, heedless of disparting space?"

"That I go through it is an incontrovertible acknowledgement of space," returned the old librarian.

"Please do not quibble, Mr. Raven," I rejoined, stepping closer. "Please to take my question as you know I mean it."

"There is in your house a door, one step through which carries me into a world very much another than this."

"A better?"

"Not throughout; but so much another that most of its physical, and many of its mental laws are different from those of this world. As for moral laws, they must everywhere be fundamentally the same."

"You try my power of belief!" I snapped. "You take me for a madman, probably?"

"You do not look like one."

"A liar then?"

"You give me no ground to think you such."

"Only you do not believe me?"

"I will go out of that door with you if you like: I believe in you enough to risk the attempt."

"The blunder all my children make!" he murmured, shaking his head. "The only door out is the door in!"

I began to think he must be insane. He sat silent for a moment, his head resting on his hand, his elbow on the table, his eyes fixed on the books before him.

"A book," he said louder, snapping me back to attention, "is a door in, and therefore a door out.—I see old Sir Up'ard," he went on, closing his eyes tight, "and my heart swells with love to him:—what world is he in?"

"The world of your heart!" I replied. "—that is, the idea of him is there."

"There is one world then at least on which your hall-door does not open?"

"I grant you so much; but the things in that world are not things to have and to hold."

"Think a little farther," he rejoined. "Did anything ever become yours, except by getting into that world?—The thought is beyond you, however, at present!—I tell you there are more worlds, and more doors to them, than you will think of in many years!"

He rose abruptly, left the library, crossed the hall, and went straight up to the garret, moving with the familiarity of a man who owns the building. I followed, studying his back. His hair hung down long and dark, straight and glossy. His coat was wide and reached to his heels. His shoes seemed absurdly large for his feet.

In the garret, light filtered through the edges of the great roofing slabs, illuminating patches where there was no flooring. We stepped carefully from joist to joist. In the middle of one of these gaps rose a partition with a door. Through it I followed Mr. Raven into a small, obscure chamber whose top contracted as it rose, going slanting directly through the roof.

"That is the door I spoke of," he said, pointing to an oblong mirror that stood on the floor and leaned against the wall.

I stepped in front of it and saw our figures dimly reflected in its dusty surface. Something about it made my stomach tighten. It looked old-fashioned and neglected, yet the eagle perched with outstretched wings on the top frame appeared threatening, its talons seemingly poised to strike.

"As a mirror," said the librarian, "it has grown dingy with age; but that is no matter: its clearness depends on the light."

"Light!" I rejoined. "There is no light here!"

He did not answer me. Instead, he began to pull at a little chain on the opposite wall. I heard a distinct creaking: the top of the chamber began to turn slowly. He ceased pulling, checked his watch, and began to pull again.

"We arrive almost to the moment!" he said. "It is on the very stroke of noon!"

The top went creaking and revolving for a minute or so. Then he pulled two other chains, alternating between them, before returning to the first. A moment later, the chamber grew significantly brighter. A patch of sunlight had fallen upon a mirror on the wall opposite the one leaning against it, casting a beam across the dust. I traced the path of the reflected rays to the mirror on the ground. But from the latter, no reflection was returned; the beams seemed to pass cleanly through, vanishing into nothingness. There was nowhere in the chamber a second patch of light!

"Where are the sunrays gone?"
