Leaving Bretton, a journey I undertook only weeks after Paulina's departure, I carried with me a naive conviction that I would return to those quiet, ancient streets again. Little did I know then that the path was closed forever. I returned home after an absence of six months. It is natural to assume I rejoiced in the embrace of my family; let this pleasant supposition stand unchallenged. Indeed, I shall allow you to envision me for the next eight years as a vessel drifting through a sea of perfect calm, anchored in a harbor so still it mirrors the sky. The helmsman lies sprawled on the deck, face turned upward, eyes shut in eternal prayer. Many women and girls spend their lives in such a suspended animation; why should I be different?

Imagine me then: idle, basking, round, and content, stretched upon a cushioned deck, bathed in unceasing sunlight, rocked by winds of lazy gentleness. Yet, history dictates that in such a scenario, one must eventually fall overboard, or the ship must finally meet its ruin. I recall vividly a season of profound cold, of peril, of bitter contention. To this very day, when nightmares seize me, I taste the salt rush in my throat and feel the icy grip of the waves crushing my lungs. I knew there was a storm, and it was not a fleeting squall. For days and nights, sun and stars vanished; we threw our own rigging overboard; a tempest of immense weight crushed us down; every shred of hope for survival was extinguished. In the end, the ship was lost, and the crew perished.

As far as I remember, I voiced no complaints to anyone. To whom could I complain? Mrs. Bretton had faded from my sight. Barriers erected by others years prior had severed our bond. Time, too, had altered her: the substantial fortune she managed for her son, once invested in joint-stock ventures, had reportedly dwindled to a mere fraction. Graham, I learned through scattered rumors, had chosen a profession; both he and his mother had departed Bretton for London. With no possibility of relying on others, I was left to myself. Though I was not naturally self-reliant or active, circumstance forced these qualities upon me, as it does for countless others. When Miss Marchmont, a wealthy spinster of our neighborhood, summoned me, I obeyed, hoping she might offer me some task.

Miss Marchmont resided in a magnificent house, yet she was a rheumatic cripple, unable to move her feet or hands, a condition she had endured for twenty years. She lived upstairs; her drawing-room opened directly onto her bedroom. I had heard of her reputation for eccentricity, but until now, I had never seen her. She presented as a woman of furrowed brow and grey hair, grave in her solitude, hardened by long affliction, and prone to irritability. A maid, or rather a companion, who had served her for years, was preparing to marry. Hearing of my bereavement, she had sent for me, intending for me to fill the void. She made the proposal after tea, sitting alone by her fireside.

"It will not be an easy life," she admitted candidly. "I require considerable attention, and you will be much confined. Yet, perhaps, compared to the existence you have recently led, it may seem tolerable."

I reflected. Logically, it ought to seem tolerable; yet, by some strange fatality, it did not. To live here, in this stifling room, watching suffering, perhaps becoming the target of her temper, through the remainder of my youth, while all that was gone had passed without even a moment of true bliss! My heart sank, then revived. Though I forced myself to acknowledge the evils, I suspect I was too prosaic to idealize them, and thus to exaggerate them.

"My doubt is whether I possess the strength for this undertaking," I observed.

"That is my own hesitation," she replied. "For you look like a worn-out creature."

And I did. In the glass, I saw myself in mourning, a faded, hollow-eyed specter. Yet, I cared little for this wan appearance. I believed the blight was merely external; I still felt life pulsing at its source.

"What else have you in view?" she asked.

"Nothing clear as yet: but I may find something."

"So you imagine: perhaps you are right. Try your own method, then; and if it fails, test mine. The chance I have offered shall remain open to you for three months."

This was kind. I expressed my gratitude. As I spoke, a sudden paroxysm of pain seized her. I attended to her, applying remedies as she directed. By the time she was relieved, a certain intimacy had already blossomed between us. From her manner of enduring the attack, I learned she was a woman of firm patience, steadfast under physical pain, though perhaps excitable under the weight of long mental anguish. In turn, her goodwill in accepting my care revealed that she could influence my sympathies. She summoned me the next day, and for five or six successive days, she claimed my company. Closer acquaintance, while revealing her faults and eccentricities, simultaneously unveiled a character worthy of respect. Stern and even morose as she could be, I found I could wait on her and sit beside her with a calm that blessed us both, knowing our presence soothed her. Even when she scolded me—which she did, now and then, very tartly—it was delivered without humiliation, leaving no sting. It resembled an irascible mother chiding her daughter rather than a harsh mistress lecturing a dependent. A vein of reason ran through her passion; she was logical even when fierce. Soon, a growing sense of attachment made the thought of staying with her as a companion seem entirely new. Within a week, I had agreed to remain.

Two hot, close rooms became my entire world; a crippled old woman was my mistress, my friend, my everything. Her service was my duty—her pain, my suffering—her relief, my hope—her anger, my punishment—her regard, my reward. I forgot the fields, woods, rivers, seas, and the ever-changing sky outside the steam-dimmed lattice of this sick chamber; I was almost content to forget it. All within me narrowed to my lot. Tame and still by habit, disciplined by destiny, I demanded no walks in the fresh air; my appetite required no more than the tiny messes served for the invalid. Beyond this, she offered me the originality of her character to study: the steadiness of her virtues, the power of her passions, the truth of her feelings. I clung to her for these things.

For these things, I would have crawled with her for twenty years, if her life of endurance had been prolonged that long. But another decree was written. I must be stimulated into action. I must be goaded, driven, stung, forced to energy. My small morsel of human affection, which I prized as a solid pearl, must melt in my fingers and slip away like a dissolving hailstone. My small adopted duty must be snatched from my easily contented conscience. I had wished to compromise with Fate, to escape occasional great agonies by submitting to a lifetime of privation and small pains. Fate would not be so pacified; nor would Providence sanction this shrinking sloth and cowardly indolence.

One February night—I remember it well—a voice was heard near Miss Marchmont's house, audible to every inmate, though perhaps understood by only one. After a calm winter, storms were ushering in the spring. I had put Miss Marchmont to bed; I sat by the fireside sewing. The wind wailed at the windows; it had wailed all day, but as night deepened, it took a new tone—an accent keen, piercing, almost articulate to the ear; a plaint, piteous and disconsolate to the nerves, trilled in every gust.

"Oh, hush! hush!" I whispered in my disturbed mind, dropping my work and making a vain effort to stop my ears against that subtle, searching cry. I had heard that very voice before, and compulsory observation had forced upon me a theory as to what it boded. Three times in the course of my life, events had taught me that these strange accents in the storm—this restless, hopeless cry—denote a coming state of the atmosphere unpropitious to life. Epidemic diseases, I believed, were often heralded by a gasping, sobbing, tormented, long-lamenting east wind. Hence, I inferred, arose the legend of the Banshee. I fancied, too, that I had noticed—though I was not philosopher enough to confirm the connection—that we often hear of disturbed volcanic action in distant lands, of rivers suddenly rushing above their banks, and of strange high tides flowing furiously upon low sea-coasts. "Our globe," I had said to myself, "seems at such periods torn and disordered; the feeble amongst us wither in her distempered breath, rushing hot from steaming volcanoes."

I listened and trembled; Miss Marchmont slept.

About midnight, the storm in one half-hour fell to a dead calm. The fire, which had burned low, glowed up vividly. I felt the air change, becoming keen. Raising the blind and curtain, I looked out and saw in the stars the keen sparkle of a sharp frost.

Turning away, the object that met my eyes was Miss Marchmont awake, lifting her head from the pillow and regarding me with unusual earnestness.

"Is it a fine night?" she asked.

I replied in the affirmative.

"I thought so," she said. "For I feel so strong, so well. Raise me. I feel young tonight," she continued. "Young, light-hearted, and happy. What if my complaint be about to take a turn, and I am yet destined to enjoy health? It would be a miracle!"

"And these are not the days of miracles," I thought to myself, wondering to hear her talk so. She went on directing her conversation to the past, recalling its incidents, scenes, and personages with singular vividness.

"I love Memory tonight," she said. "I prize her as my best friend. She is just now giving me a deep delight: she is bringing back to my heart, in warm and beautiful life, realities—not mere empty ideas, but what were once realities, and that I long have thought decayed, dissolved, mixed in with grave-mould. I possess just now the hours, the thoughts, the hopes of my youth. I renew the love of my life—its only love—almost its only affection; for I am not a particularly good woman: I am not amiable. Yet I have had my feelings, strong and concentrated; and these feelings had their object; which, in its single self, was dear to me, as to the majority of men and women, are all the unnumbered points on which they dissipate their regard. While I loved, and while I was loved, what an existence I enjoyed! What a glorious year I can recall—how bright it comes back to me! What a living spring—what a warm, glad summer—what soft moonlight, silvering the autumn evenings—what strength of hope under the ice-bound waters and frost-hoar fields of that year's winter! Through that year my heart lived with Frank's heart. O my noble Frank—my faithful Frank—my *good* Frank! so much better than myself—his standard in all things so much higher! This I can now see and say: if few women have suffered as I did in his loss, few have enjoyed what I did in his love. It was a far better kind of love than common; I had no doubts about it or him: it was such a love as honoured, protected, and elevated, no less than it gladdened her to whom it was given. Let me now ask, just at this moment, when my mind is so strangely clear,—let me reflect why it was taken from me? For what crime was I condemned, after twelve months of bliss, to undergo thirty years of sorrow?

"I do not know," she continued after a pause. "I cannot—*cannot* see the reason; yet at this hour I can say with sincerity, what I never tried to say before, Inscrutable God, Thy will be done! And at this moment I can believe that death will restore me to Frank. I never believed it till now."

"He is dead, then?" I inquired in a low voice.

"My dear girl," she said, "one happy Christmas Eve I dressed and decorated myself, expecting my lover, very soon to be my husband, would come that night to visit me. I sat down to wait. Once more I see that moment—I see the snow twilight stealing through the window over which the curtain was not dropped, for I designed to watch him ride up the white walk; I see and feel the soft firelight warming me, playing on my silk dress, and fitfully showing me my own young figure in a glass. I see the moon of a calm winter night, float full, clear, and cold, over the inky mass of shrubbery, and the silvered turf of my grounds. I wait, with some impatience in my pulse, but no doubt in my breast. The flames had died in the fire, but it was a bright mass yet; the moon was mounting high, but she was still visible from the lattice; the clock neared ten; he rarely tarried later than this, but once or twice he had been delayed so long.

"Would he for once fail me? No—not even for once; and now he was coming—and coming fast—to atone for lost time. 'Frank! you furious rider,' I said inwardly, listening gladly, yet anxiously, to his approaching gallop, 'you shall be rebuked for this: I will tell you it is *my* neck you are putting in peril; for whatever is yours is, in a dearer and tenderer sense, mine.' There he was: I saw him; but I think tears were in my eyes, my sight was so confused. I saw the horse; I heard it stamp—I saw at least a mass; I heard a clamour. *Was* it a horse? or what heavy, dragging thing was it, crossing, strangely dark, the lawn.
