Beneath a high ceiling lined with well-loved leather-bound books, a deep-bellied antique oak desk sat pulled close to the tall sash windows overlooking the green sweep of St James’s Park. The spacious, tastefully decorated room bore the unmistakeable mark of a long-time bachelor: a half-smoked cigar smouldered in a crystal ashtray, a pair of well-worn riding boots stood propped by the door, and no stray decorative frill cluttered the clean lines of the furniture. John Marlowe slid a brass key in the desk’s lock, reached deep into its farthest compartment, and drew out a long, thick manila envelope.

“I gather you’ve already gone over this,” he said, nodding at Mr. Cupples, who was perched on a deep velvet sofa, his round, benevolent face scanning the room like a curious sparrow.
“Read it for the first time 48 hours ago,” Cupples replied. “We’ve picked over every line between us.”

Marlowe crossed to the table and set the envelope down in front of Trent. “That’s your full manuscript. I’ve read it three times cover to cover. I don’t think another living soul could have dug up half as much of the truth as you’ve set down here.”

Trent ignored the compliment. He sat rigid by the hearth, staring hard into the leaping flames, his long legs twisted awkwardly under his chair. “You mean there’s still the rest of the truth to lay out,” he said, tugging the envelope toward him. “We’re all ears whenever you’re ready. The longer the story, the better as far as I’m concerned— I want to understand every last thread. First, though, we’d both like a full picture of who Sigsbee Manderson really was, and what your relationship to him was. It struck me from day one that the dead man’s strange character had to be the hidden engine driving this whole mess.”

“You were dead right,” Marlowe said grimly. He crossed the room and settled on the edge of the tall, tufted fender, his lean frame loose and perfectly controlled, a far cry from the haggard, jittery wreck Trent had met at the Marlstone estate 18 months prior. His brow was open, his bright blue eyes steady—though they still held that faint, unplaceable edge of wariness that had pricked at Trent the first time they shook hands. Only the tight, set lines of his mouth betrayed that he knew he was standing on dangerous ground, and intended to face it straight on.

“Sigsbee Manderson was never a normal man,” Marlowe began in his soft, even voice. “Most of the wildly rich men I met in America made their fortunes off abnormal greed, abnormal grind, abnormal brute force of will, or just abnormal luck. None of them had the raw brainpower to back it up. Manderson hoarded wealth like a dragon, worked 18 hour days chasing it, had a will you could bend steel around, and had more luck than any three of his rivals put together— but what made him one of a kind was his intellect. Back home, the papers called him the Napoleon of Wall Street, and everyone who knew him thought his ruthlessness was his defining trait. But hundreds of other men would have burned every last bridge to get what they wanted, if they’d been clever enough to dream up Manderson’s schemes.
“Americans aren’t stupid, far from it— as a nation they’re ten times sharper than us. But I never met another one who had that level of icy sagacity, that foresight, that perfect memory and sheer relentless mental grit behind every move he made. He never forgot a single useful fact. He ran his business the same way Napoleon ran his campaigns, he’d devour prepped digests of coal market data, wheat futures, railway holdings in every spare minute he could snatch. He’d dream up bolder, cleverer plays than any man on the Street. Everyone learned to expect Manderson to never take the obvious path, but no one could ever guess what he’d do instead. The whole market would rattle like a loose tin when word got out the old man was out hunting, and half his opponents would surrender before he even made his move. A scheme that would take most people months to plot out in full? Manderson could map it down to the last tiny detail while he shaved.
“I’ve always suspected that faint strain of Iroquois blood in him explained that unholy mix of cunning and cruelty. Hardly anyone even knew that line existed— only he and me. He asked me once, because I dabbled in genealogy, to dig into his obscure family history, and that’s how I found he was a direct descendant of Montour, the legendary Iroquois chief, and his French wife, that wild, fearsome woman who ruled tribal politics across the Wilderness 200 years back. The Mandersons ran fur trading posts on the Pennsylvania border back then, and more than a few married Indigenous women. That secret landed a year before he died, and he was horrified. He was so terrified of anyone finding out he had mixed blood, he made me swear to take it to my grave. I kept that promise while he lived, but I’m convinced that’s when he started turning against me.”

“Did Manderson have any definable religious beliefs?” Mr. Cupples asked so sharply the other two men jumped.
Marlowe paused for a beat. “None I ever saw. He never prayed, he never set foot in a church, never even mentioned God. But he’d had a strict religious upbringing as a child, and for all that, he was almost ascetic— no drinking, no women, nothing but cigars for a vice. I lived with him four years, and in all that time, I never once heard him utter an outright verbal lie. He’d lie through every business trick, every misleading market rumour, every deception he could pull off— but he would never, ever say a direct falsehood over the smallest trivial thing. Can you wrap your head around that? It’s like a soldier who’s scrupulously honest face to face, but will burn villages and fake dispatches to fool the enemy. For him, the whole business world was a war zone. The rules of the game let him lie through action, but never through his words.”

“It’s a sad old world,” Cupples observed.
“Couldn’t agree more,” Marlowe nodded. “And that unbreakable habit of telling no direct lie? The first time I ever heard him say a straight-up falsehood was the night he died. And hearing that lie is what saved me from hanging for his murder.”

Trent shifted restlessly in his chair. “Before we get to that part,” he cut in, “tell us exactly what your position was with Manderson for all those years.”
“We got on perfectly, right up until the end. It was never friendship— he was the sort who never let anyone get close enough to be a friend. But I was his most trusted secretary. I came to him straight after I finished my Oxford degree. I was supposed to go into my father’s business back home, but my dad suggested I take a couple of years off and see the world, and this secretary post popped up, and I stayed four years. The whole thing happened because of chess, of all things.”

At the word, Trent smacked his palms together, letting out a sharp exclamation. The other two stared at him.
“Chess!” Trent repeated, standing up and stepping toward Marlowe. “You know what the first thing I noticed about you the day we met? Your eyes. I couldn’t place it then, but now I know exactly where I’d seen that exact look before. It was on Nikolay Korchagin, the grandmaster, the man I sat across from in a railway carriage for two whole days once. That cold, faraway, calculating chess-player’s eye. I couldn’t put a name to it when I saw it on you, that’s all.”

“I’ve played since I was a kid, ran in the Oxford chess circles,” Marlowe said simply. “Dr. Munro at Queen’s, the one I never once beat, sent for me my last term. Told me some rich American businessman was looking for an English secretary, and the only hard requirements were chess, a hunting licence, and an Oxford education. That’s how I got the job.
“I loved it, at first. You don’t get a lot of dull days trailing a vibrant, at-the-top-of-his-powers American plutocrat. And then my father’s business hit a rough patch not long after I started, so I was grateful to not need an allowance from him. A year in, Manderson doubled my salary. ‘Big money,’ he told me, ‘but I don’t lose a penny of it.’ By then I was doing far more than ride horses with him in the morning and play chess after dinner. I ran his houses, his Ohio farm, his Maine hunting lodge, his stable, his cars, his yacht. I could recite every British railway timetable from memory, I knew every cigar shop in London by name. The work was fun, varied, I had plenty of time to go out and enjoy myself, plenty of money to spend. I made a fool of myself over a girl once, back then— that was the hard patch, and it was Mrs. Manderson who helped me through it. You can ask her about it, if she wants to tell you. But Manderson? He never treated me anything but fair, nothing but generous. I never had the faintest inkling he was anything but happy with the work I did for him. Right up until the night he died. That night, he showed me the depths of the raving, crazy hatred he’d been hiding for me all that time.”

Trent and Cupples exchanged a long, quiet look.
“You never suspected he hated you before that?” Trent asked.
“To what end do you think that hatred was aimed?” Cupples added at the same time.

“I never had a clue,” Marlowe said. “How long he’d felt that way, I have no idea. I still can’t work out why. I thought, in those terrible days right after his death, that he must have been in the grip of some madman’s delusion, convinced I was plotting against him. You can’t plumb the depths of a lunatic’s mind. Can you even imagine a state of mind where a man will plot his own death, just to make sure the person he hates gets hanged for his murder?”

Cupples jolted upright in his chair. “You’re saying Manderson killed himself?”
Trent shot him an impatient glance, his gaze never leaving Marlowe’s face.
“I am,” Marlowe said flatly, meeting his eye. Cupples nodded.

“Wait, hold on,” Trent cut in gently, laying a hand on Cupples’s arm before the old man could launch into a philosophical tangent. “Tell us the facts of that Sunday night, step by step.”

Marlowe flushed at the faint emphasis Trent put on the word “facts”. He sat up straighter.
“Bunner and I dined with Mr. and Mrs. Manderson that Sunday evening. It was exactly like every other quiet dinner the four of us had. Manderson was quiet, sullen, the way he’d been the last few months. The rest of us made conversation, we finished dinner around nine. Mrs. Manderson went to the drawing room, Bunner headed up to the little hotel nearby to meet a friend. Manderson asked me to walk out to the orchard behind the house with him, said he needed to talk to me. We paced up and down the path, far enough from the house that no one could overhear us, and he talked to me in that cool, calm, deliberate tone he used for big business. He never seemed saner, or more friendly. He said he had a secret big deal going, no one knew about it, not even Bunner, and he needed me to do exactly what he told me, no questions asked.
“That was normal for him, that’s how he always operated. I told him I was ready. He told me there was a man named George Harris, using that alias, who was supposed to take the noon Southampton to Havre ferry to Paris the next day. He’d had me book that very berth in Harris’s name a week earlier. But Harris couldn’t leave now, he said. He needed that man to stay in England. Someone else had to take the wallet of papers to Paris instead, otherwise the whole multi-million dollar deal would collapse. He wanted me to go.
“I didn’t love the idea, but I was never going to let him down. I said sure, I’d do it. He told me my face was known to all his rivals, so I’d have to disguise myself, travel the whole way as George Harris, no contact with him at all, no letters, no phone calls, until I’d handed over the wallet in Paris. I was to drive his car all through the night straight to Southampton, leave it at a garage under a fake name, meet Harris at the Bedford Hotel if he was there, if not just board the ferry, check into the Hotel St Petersbourg in Paris, wait for a note addressed to George Harris telling me where to drop the locked wallet off.
“I ran back to my room, stuffed a few things in a kit bag, my head spinning. Manderson loved this over-the-top, secretive melodrama stuff, so I thought nothing of it. I grabbed the car from the garage behind the house, and just as I was pulling it around to the front of the house, I realised I had barely a few shillings in my pocket.
“I was flat broke. A few months earlier, I’d tried dabbling in stock speculation, lost every penny, ended up deep in debt. I’d gone to Manderson, told him the whole mess, and he’d advanced me a huge chunk of my future salary to clear the debts. He knew I had no cash, he knew I’d borrowed pocket change off Bunner to get by until my next paycheque came in.
“I walked back into the library and told him I needed cash for the journey. That’s when I saw the first thing that made me stop and feel like something was very, very wrong. He went to reach for his left hip pocket, the one he always kept his hundred quid in notes in— a habit so automatic he did it every time he paid for anything. Then he froze mid-movement, and swore under his breath. I’d never heard him curse before. I thought he’d lost his little note case. But a week earlier, when I’d been up in London running errands for him, I’d drawn a thousand pounds in small notes from his bank for him, and I’d seen him fingering that thick stack of cash on his desk earlier that same day.”
