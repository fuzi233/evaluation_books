*"The kindliest symptom, yet the most alarming crisis in the ticklish state of youth; the nourisher and destroyer of hopeful wits; the servitude above freedom; the gentle mind’s religion; the liberal superstition."*

—Charles Lamb

The first whisper of an unexpected storm was as light as the faintest white cloud against the blue. Anna knew Rex’s secret—though for the first time in their lives, he had not confided in her. And for the first time, Anna could not speak what weighed on her heart. She might have resented his affection shifting to another, if not for the fear that gnawed at her—the certainty that Gwendolen would never return his love.

Anna admired her cousin. "Gwendolen is always kind to me," she would say with simple sincerity, accepting her lesser place without question. Yet she watched her with wary fascination, as one might study a beautiful, untamed creature—something dazzling and unpredictable, capable of careless destruction. And now, Anna’s heart ached with the unspoken truth: Gwendolen would never care for Rex. What he cherished, she dismissed; where he offered devotion, she would offer indifference—or worse, amusement. Poor Rex! And poor Anna, who had imagined years as his companion before any rival could appear. How could any heart be so cold as to reject him? The thought turned her admiration to something close to dislike.

To Rex, the weeks had been alive with meaning, though the world around them remained oblivious. Had anyone asked, he would have declared his hopes openly—he meant to speak to his father soon. Yet he found himself guarding his feelings, even his actions, with uncharacteristic secrecy. Anna noticed, and it unsettled her. Every private word from her parents made her tense, dreading some mention of Rex and Gwendolen. But the elders saw nothing. Their gazes lingered on newspapers, not the silent drama unfolding before them.

"Where are you going, Rex?" Anna asked one dull morning, watching him fasten his riding gloves. Their father had already left for the sessions, their mother with him.

"To see the hounds off at the Three Barns."

"Are you taking Gwendolen?" The question slipped out, timid.

"Did she tell you?"

"No. But I thought—does Papa know?"

"Why would he mind?" Rex shrugged.

"You’re riding his horse again."

"He knows I do."

"Rex," Anna’s voice trembled. "Don’t let her follow the hounds."

He laughed. "Why not?"

"Papa and Mama don’t wish it. They say it isn’t proper."

"And you think she’d listen to me?"

"Gwendolen does as she pleases," Anna said, frustration sharpening her words.

"Then she certainly wouldn’t heed me," Rex teased, but Anna’s eyes welled with tears.

"Oh, Rex, you’ll break your heart. She doesn’t care for you—I know she never will!"

The words struck deeper than he’d admit. He left without another word, but they clung to him as he rode—an unwelcome prophecy, laughed at yet unsettling.

By the time he reached the avenue, Gwendolen was already mounted, her chestnut mare pacing restlessly. The groom waited, but she dismissed him with a wave as Rex approached. Together, they set off, the morning bright around them. Gwendolen was radiant, her laughter light, her riding dress framing her like some vision of effortless grace. To Rex, she was perfection—beauty, wit, and spirit entwined. Few would have blamed her if she felt the same of him, with his open, handsome face and steadfast heart. But Gwendolen’s charm held something sharper beneath its glow, a hint of recklessness that gave admirers pause.

The day was crisp, the lanes alive with winter’s muted hues—berry-red hedgerows, the rich brown of turned earth, the soft gray sky. Their voices mingled with the chime of hooves, laughter threading through the air. It was youth at its sweetest, untouched by doubt.

"Anna was convinced you’d chase the hounds today," Rex said, testing the waters.

"Was she?" Gwendolen’s smile was bright. "What a little seer she is!"

"Will you?"

"I don’t know. Clairvoyants are often wrong. I prefer the unlikely."

"Then I’ll never predict you," he said, grinning.

"Good. Surprise is the only interesting thing."

Their banter danced on, but beneath it, Rex’s heart pressed forward. "What would you like to do, if you could do anything?" he asked, earnest.

"Oh—race steeplechases, or sail to the North Pole, or be a queen in the East," she said airily. The words meant nothing, but they delighted him.

"You wouldn’t refuse marriage for such adventures?"

"Not refuse. Only, I wouldn’t marry as others do."

"If you married a man who loved you above all else—"

"Don’t speak of Mr. Middleton," she cut in, flushing. "That’s Anna’s refrain. Listen—the hounds!"

She urged her horse forward, leaving Rex no choice but to follow, his confession unspoken.

The hunt was a blur of color and motion. Gwendolen, at the center of it all, shone like a jewel. Rex lost sight of her quickly, his father’s steady mare no match for her spirited chestnut. Then disaster struck—a misplaced step, a fall, and Rex was on the ground, his shoulder wrenched, his pride bruised. A rough-handed boy helped him home, but his thoughts were all for Gwendolen. Had she come to harm?

His father’s study was a cold reception.

"You fool," Mr. Gascoigne said, eyeing his son’s pallor. "Chasing after a girl who’ll lead you nowhere."

Rex’s voice broke. "Father, I love her. I must know if she feels the same."

Mr. Gascoigne sighed. "You’re young. This is a passing fancy. First cousins shouldn’t marry, and you’ve your studies to think of."

"I can’t give her up."

"You must. Life is full of disappointments."

Rex’s heart rebelled. "Then I’ll see her again. I won’t obey."

"Wait till morning," his father said, firm. "We’ll speak then."

Rex agreed—but his resolve was unshaken. Love, once awakened, would not be so easily put aside.
