"Far, far from hence
The Adriatic breaks in a warm bay
Among the green Illyrian hills, and there
The sunshine in the happy glens is fair..."

Thus sang Matthew Arnold of distant shores, yet even such delights pale before the rugged splendor of the combs that slice through the high tablelands bordering Devon and Cornwall. These gorges open toward the boundless Atlantic, each possessing a landscape unlike any other in England. They feature sheer inland walls of rich oak, giving way near the coast to dark green furze, smooth turf, and finally, jagged black cliffs that stretch far into the deep sea—castles, spires, and wings of ironstone. Within lies a narrow strip of fertile meadow crossed by crystal trout streams, feeding gray stone mills where water sparkles around dripping wheels. Above the tide mark lie dark rock pools where salmon gather after autumn floods, while ridges of blown sand glitter with golden trefoil and crimson lady's finger. Below, gray banks of polished pebbles carry the stream's rush to the sea.

At the mouth of each comb lies a "jaw" of shark's-tooth rock paving the cove, streaked with pink shell sand and laced with white foam from the eternal surge. These strata, set upright or tilted at strange angles by ancient earthquakes, form a barrier so formidable that a single rasp of its teeth could grind the timbers of the stoutest ship. Landward, the scene offers richness, softness, and peace; seaward, a howling wilderness of rock and roller, barren to fishermen and hopeless to the shipwrecked.

Of all these mouths, only one provides a landing for boats—a long sea-wall protecting it from the Atlantic's rollers. This is Marsland, the domain of the White Witch, Lucy Passmore. Here, as Sir Richard Grenville correctly surmised, the Jesuits had fled. Yet before their arrival, two figures stood on that lonely beach beneath a bright October moon: Rose Salterne and the White Witch herself. Driven by feverish curiosity and superstition, allured by the wildness and potential peril of the spell, Rose had kept her appointment. Just before midnight, she stood on the gray shingle with her counselor.

"You be safe enough here tonight, miss," said the old woman. "My old man is snoring soundly, and no soul else sets foot here save the mermaids now and then. Goodness, Father, where's our boat? It ought to be up on the pebbles!"

Rose pointed to a sandy strip forty yards nearer the sea where the boat lay. "Oh, the lazy old villain! He's been fishing for pollock among the rocks and never bothered hauling the boat ashore. I'll trounce him when I return. Only pray he's made her fast properly! He's more plague to me than all my money combined. O deary me!"

The goodwife bustled toward the boat, with Rose trailing behind. "Yes, 'tis fast, sure enough, and the oars aboard too! Well, I never! Oh, the lazy thief, leaving them exposed to theft! I'll just sit in the boat, dear, and watch him, while you descend to the sea. You must be entirely alone, or you'll see nothing. Here's the looking-glass; now go, dip your head three times, and mind you don't glance at land or sea until after speaking the words and beholding the glass. Hurry, it's nearly midnight."

Coiling herself in the boat, the old woman watched as Rose faltered down the remaining twenty yards of sand. Slipping off her garments, the girl stood shivering and trembling for a moment before entering the water. Flanked by towering rock walls—the left rising twenty feet high in deepest shadow, the right lower but catching the full blaze of the midnight moon—she felt the weight of the scene. Festoons of live and purple seaweed hung from the cliff, shading dark crevices fit for sea goblins. To her left, rock peaks frowned ghastly black; to her right, the downs slept bright and cold. The breeze had ceased; not even a wave broke the perfect stillness of the cove. Gulls rested asleep upon the ledges. Over all hung a profound autumn silence—one that could be heard.

Awed, Rose listened, hoping for any sound confirming she was not utterly alone. A faint bleat, like a newborn lamb, echoed from above. She started upward, then a wail rose from the cliffs—as of a child in pain—answered by another from the opposite rocks. They were merely snipe and an otter calling to its brood, yet to her they sounded like supernatural goblins summoned by her presence. Still, they only heightened her anticipation; the witch had assured her not to fear them. If the rite were performed correctly, no harm could befall her.

Yet she could feel her own heart pounding as she stepped forward, mirror in hand, into the cold water. Wading hastily as far as she dared, she halted, aghast. A ring of flame encircled her waist; every limb bathed in lambent light, the myriad life of the autumn sea erupted into sudden glory. Around her swarmed the lamps of sea nymphs—myriad fiery globes—heaving and panting. Rainbows of crimson, azure, and emerald shattered into star-showers, illuminating the wine-dark depths of the crystal gardens of Nereus. Every shell crawling on the white sand at her feet became visible, as did every rock-fish playing in the crannies, staring back with broad, bright eyes. Great palmate oarweeds waving along the chasm seemed to beckon her downward with long brown hands toward a grave amidst their chilly bower.

Turning to flee, she realized she had gone too far to retreat. Hastily dipping her head three times, she hurried to the sea's edge and, gazing through her dripping locks at the magic mirror, spoke the incantation:

*"A maiden pure, here I stand,
Neither on sea, nor yet on land;
Angels watch me on either hand.
If you be landsman, come down the strand;
If you be sailor, come up the sand;
If you be angel, come from the sky,
Look in my glass, and pass me by;
Look in my glass, and go from the shore;
Leave me, but love me for evermore."*

Hardly finishing, her eyes strained into the mirror, expecting perhaps a vision, when instead she saw only the sparkle of droplets from her own hair. Suddenly, the sound of rushing pebbles announced the hasty footsteps of men and horses. Darting into a cavern of the high rock, she hastily dressed herself. Peering out, half-dead with terror, she witnessed four men, two having just leapt from their horses and abandoned them, while the others ran the boat ashore.

From the stern sheets arose, like an angry specter, the portly figure of Lucy Passmore, shrieking in a shrill treble: "Eh! Ye villains, ye rogues, what do ye want stealing poor folk's boats by night like this?"

The entire party recoiled in terror. One turned to run up the beach, shouting at the top of his voice, "'Tis a marmaiden—a marmaiden asleep in Willy Passmore's boat!"

"I wish it were any such good luck," Rose heard Will mutter; "'tis my wife, oh dear!" Cowering, he awaited the hearty cuff due him as the White Witch, leaping from the boat, dared any man to touch it. Thundering orders to her husband to retire to bed, the wily dame maintained this delay primarily to buy time for her pupil. Yet she possessed more solid motives: recognizing in the ungainly figure of one of the party the same suspicious Welsh gentleman she had long suspected, she held in extreme horror her husband's meddling with such "Popish skulkers"—unless compensated handsomely.

In vain Parsons thundered, Campian entreated, Mr. Leigh's groom swore, and her husband danced in an agony of mixed fear and greed.

"No!" she cried. "As I am an honest woman and loyal! Is this why you left the boat down to the shore, you old traitor? To facilitate such noxious trade escaping Her Majesty's jurisdiction? Eh? Stand back, cowards! Will you strike a woman?"

This declaration, as usual, signaled her intent to attack. Grabbing an oar, she swung it fiercely, striking Father Parsons across the shins with such force that he retreated with a howl.

"Lucy, Lucy!" shrieked her husband in a shrill Devon falsetto. "Are you mazed? Are you mazed, lass? They promised me two gold nobles before I'd lend them the boat!"

"Tu?" shrieked the matron with a tone of infinite scorn. "And do you call yourself a man?"

"Tu nobles! Tu nobles!" he shrieked again, hopping about at arm's length. "Tu? And would you sell your soul for less than ten?"

"Oh, if that is all," cried poor Campian, "give her ten, give her ten, brother Pars—Morgans, I mean—and take care of your shins, Offa Cerbero, you know—Oh, virago! Furens quid faemina possit! Surely she is some Lamia, some Gorgon, some—"
