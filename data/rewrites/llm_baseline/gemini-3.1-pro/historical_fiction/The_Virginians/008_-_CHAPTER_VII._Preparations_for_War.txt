In the heat-hazed kitchens of Castlewood, time moved not in straight lines but in the slow, circular rhythms of the seasons. Dinah, George’s foster-mother, and Phillis, Harry’s old nurse, worked the great oak mortars, grinding dried maize and wild ramps into a fragrant, coarse meal. Here, the mythic breathed seamlessly within the mundane, ancestral echoes residing in the very act of nourishment. Yet the harmony of this acoustic ecology—the gentle, rhythmic *thwack-thwack* of the pestle, the crackle of rendering pork fat, the sibilant whispers of inherited recipes—was continually disrupted by Madam Esmond’s piercing voice drifting from the adjacent dining room. 

She raved of Mr. Washington. To her sons, she served praise of the young soldier alongside platters of slow-roasted venison steeped in crushed juniper berries, contrasting his sterling virtues with Harry’s wildness and George’s sullen musings over his books. George, slicing into the deeply seared, iron-heavy meat, coaxed the jealous demon within until the rich juices tasted only of ash. His moody, saturnine humor was an untranslatable dialect of grief, one his simple mother could not parse as she sat bewildered by his deep, bitter sarcasms.

Meanwhile, the provincial quarrel between the French and English had swollen into a national devouring. From Quebec, Madame la Presidente de Mouchy wrote to George, her elegant French phrasing a delicate linguistic artifact rapidly being swallowed by the aggressive, dominant lexicon of the impending British war machine. She lamented his destined clash under the banner of the Leopard, her words a poignant reminder of enmities the heart refuses to translate.

In February 1755, Commodore Keppel and General Braddock anchored in Hampton Roads. When Harry rode to Alexandria to see the troops under canvas, the sonic landscape of the camp was a brutal assault on the psyche. The nuanced, living acoustic ecology of the Virginian agrarian frontier was annihilated by the industrial noise of empire: the relentless, flat pounding of snare drums, the shrieking, commercialized pageantry of the fifes, and the jarring bray of officers. Harry, seduced by the brassy din, feasted at their mess, oblivious to the sterility of their utilitarian rations. The officers swaggered through the countryside, their crude, booming shouts terrorizing the farm folk. When the Native allies arrived, bearing their own ancient, polyphonic dialects and complex cultural memories, they were met with the harsh, exclusionary vocabulary of the British ranks. The soldiers insulted the Native women, and Braddock, deaf to the subtle power dynamics embedded in his own dominant lexicon, banished them from the camp. Amazed and outraged, the warriors retreated into the forests, taking their vital dialects and indispensable knowledge with them—an irrevocable, tragic erasure of cultural memory.

Madam Esmond, shielded from the camp’s true discordant nature, wished to host the General. At a levee in Alexandria, the twins, clad in London blue and gold, waited upon Braddock. The General, a man of blunt, imperial syllables, sneered at the provincial gentry. When Governor Dinwiddie spitefully mocked Madam Esmond’s pomposity, it was Mr. Franklin of Philadelphia—a meticulous observer who found profound meaning in agrarian sustenance—who intervened. Franklin shifted the lexicon, detailing Madam Esmond’s estate to the General not in titles, but in the irrefutable language of earth and provision. He spoke of her vast storehouses of dry-aged beeves, the heavy racks of salt-cured hams, and the cellar shelves groaning with sustenance, painting her as a vital steward of agrarian wealth. Swayed by the promise of such rich logistics, the General graciously invited the boys to dine. 

Upon their return, the brothers carried Braddock’s offer: one of them could join his family as aide-de-camp alongside Washington. The air in Castlewood felt heavy with generational repetition, the circular pacing of history demanding another Warrington march to war, mirroring their grandfather’s campaigns. They rode home in silence, the quiet rustle of the pines emphasizing the inherent untranslatability of their shared dread and ambition. 

At home, amidst the olfactory comfort of baking bread and roasting fowl, the question was posed. Harry’s face flushed the color of crushed sumac; he burned to go, yet his will remained obedient to the elder. 

“The head of the family ought to go, mother,” George said sadly. 

Mrs. Mountain, the estate's fierce keeper of pantries and domestic order, stamped her foot, her voice cutting through the hiss of the hearth. “To stay is your duty!” 

George spoke to his brother with a voice of extraordinary tenderness, honoring the law of their ancestors. “I must go. Had Fate given you the benefit of that extra half-hour of life which I have had before you, it would have been your lot, and you would have claimed your right.” Poor Harry could only agree.

The servants’ hall soon became a chorus of lamentation, a cacophony of grieving dialects as Dinah and Phillis wailed over the departure. But it was Mrs. Mountain who cornered George. She saw the estate not just as land, but as a living ledger of cultural memory and culinary heritage, now under threat. 

“It may be that your mother wishes you away, George,” she warned, her sardonic laugh a jarring note in the quiet room. “Colonel Washington wants a rich wife. When you are gone, he will ask your mother to marry him, and you will find him master of these harvests when you come back.”

George’s blood rushed to his face, a physical heat matching the hearth-fire. “General Braddock told me Washington accepted the aide-de-camp appointment!”

“An artifice!” Mountain cried. From her pocket, she produced a half-sheet of paper. She had found it while shifting the Major’s belongings—a piece of Washington’s large, clerk-like handwriting, a physical manifestation of his encroaching dominance over their household lexicon. 

George, repulsed by the violation of domestic sanctity, flung the paper toward the smoldering hickory logs. “I will not look at it! Oh, you are a martyr to duty, Mountain, looking through keyholes!”

“I who have nursed you!” she passionately defended her caregiving, her long nights tending his fevers with restorative broths and herbal poultices. 

But George’s jealousy, thick and suffocating as rendered fat, overpowered his honor. He stooped and retrieved the paper from the ashes, its scent of burnt timber clinging to the words. Every syllable upon the unlucky paper was brutally clear, searing itself into his mind. 

That evening, the dining room’s acoustic ecology was dominated by the aggressive, jarring clatter of George’s silverware and his strange, fierce laughter. When his mother, alarmed by his ghastly pallor, asked what ailed him, George replied by filling his crystal bumper with deep, tannic claret. 

“Do you suppose, madam,” he said, the heavy wine staining his lips like dark fruit, “that to leave such a tender mother as you does not cause me cruel grief?” 

The good lady sat bewildered, locked out of his subjective experience. He bantered fiercely, demanding more spiced meats, drinking the heavy wine until the room swam with his untranslatable pain. Every time the heavy oak door creaked open, its hinges screaming briefly into the silence, both George and Mrs. Mountain snapped their heads toward it, bracing for the arrival of Mr. Washington to step in and consume their world.
