I’d had the pony-chaise harnessed and waiting all day, just in case Mr. Franklin was stubborn enough to catch the overnight train out. When his luggage came thumping down the stairs, followed by the man himself, I knew for once in his flighty, indecisive life, he’d stuck to a resolution.
“So you’re really set on this, sir?” I said, meeting him in the front hall. “Why not bide a day or two longer? Give Miss Rachel another chance to come round.”
All that fancy foreign polish he’d picked up on his travels had melted right off him now that goodbye was staring him in the face. Instead of answering out loud, he pressed Lady Verinder’s latest letter into my palm. Most of it repeated what I’d already gotten in her earlier note, but there was a postscript about Miss Rachel at the end that explained exactly why Mr. Franklin was so dead set on leaving, no questions asked.
“You will likely wonder,” her ladyship wrote, “how I could let my own daughter keep me completely in the dark. A diamond worth twenty thousand pounds is missing, and I am forced to infer that the riddle of its disappearance is no riddle at all to Rachel—that some unfathomable vow of silence has been forced on her by people I have never met, for a purpose I cannot even begin to guess. You might think no mother would put up with being trifled with in such a way. But in Rachel’s current state, I have no choice. She is a wreck of jangled nerves, pitiable to see. I dare not broach the subject of the Moonstone again until time has softened her agitation. To that end, I already sent the Sergeant Cuff packing. The mystery that stumps us stumps him too—no stranger can help us here. His presence only adds to my distress, and the very sound of his name sends Rachel into fits.
“My own plans are settled as far as they can be. I intend to take Rachel to London shortly, partly for the distraction of a complete change of scene, partly to consult the best nerve specialists in the city. May I ask you to meet us there? My dear Franklin, you must learn patience as I have, and wait for a kinder hour. The invaluable help you gave us in searching for the stolen jewel is still an unforgivable sin in Rachel’s current state of mind. You moved through this affair blindfold, and your efforts to uncover the truth inadvertently threatened to lay her secret bare, piling more weight on her already overburdened shoulders. I cannot defend the perversity that makes you responsible for consequences neither of us could have imagined. She will not listen to reason—she can only be pitied. It grieves me to say it, but for now, you and Rachel are far better apart. The only advice I can offer is to give her time.”
I handed the letter back to him, genuinely sorry. I knew how deep his love for our young lady ran, and I could see her mother’s account of her suffering had cut him to the quick. “You know the old proverb, sir,” I said. “When things are at their worst, they’re bound to mend. They can’t get much sorrier than they are right now, Mr. Franklin.”
He folded the letter up tight, and my little bit of comfort didn’t seem to land at all.
“When I came down from London with that accursed diamond,” he said, “I would have sworn there wasn’t a happier household in all of England. Look at us now! Scattered, divided, the very air of the place reeking of mystery and suspicion. Do you remember that morning out at the Shivering Sand, when we talked of my uncle Herncastle, and that cursed birthday gift? The Moonstone has done his work of vengeance for him, Betteredge, in ways the old colonel never could have dreamed up himself.”
With that he shook my hand hard, and stepped out to the pony-chaise.
I followed him down the steps. It was a wretched sight, watching him leave the old house where he’d spent every happy summer of his boyhood like this. Penelope, red-eyed and upset by all the trouble we’d had, came hurrying round to say goodbye. Mr. Franklin kissed her forehead, and I waved a hand like to say he was more than welcome. A handful of the other maids peeked round the corner, crying quiet, too—he was the kind of gentleman every last woman on the place adored. At the last minute I held the chaise back, begging him as a favor to write and let us know where he’d gone. He hardly seemed to hear me, staring around at every old tree and gable, as if he was memorizing the whole property for a last goodbye. “Tell us your destination, sir!” I called, hanging on to the side of the chaise to drag the answer out of him. Mr. Franklin pulled his hat down low over his eyes. “Destination?” he echoed, hollow. “I’m off to the devil, for all I care.” The pony reared at the words, like even the beast was scandalized. “God bless you, sir, wherever you end up!” I called out, before he whipped the pony around and vanished down the drive. A sweet, kind, foolish man he was, through all his faults—he left a gaping empty spot in the whole house when he left that day.
That long summer Saturday night closed in dull as ditchwater.
I kept my spirits from sinking by clinging fast to my pipe and my well-thumbed copy of *Robinson Crusoe*. The rest of the women (bar Penelope) gossiped nonstop about Rosanna’s suicide, all stubbornly convinced the poor girl had stolen the Moonstone and drowned herself for fear of being caught. My daughter, of course, stuck firm to her own theory that the girl had killed herself over unrequited love for Mr. Franklin. Oddly enough, that story left exactly the same loose end as Rachel’s claim of innocence: no explanation for Rosanna’s secret trip to Frizinghall, no explanation for all her strange meddling with the stained nightgown. Pointing that out to Penelope did as much good as throwing a raindrop at a waterproof coat. Truth be told, my daughter inherited my tendency to ignore inconvenient logic, and she’s long since outstripped me at it.
Sunday rolled around, and the closed carriage we’d left at Mr. Ablewhite’s returned empty. The coachman brought a verbal message for me, and written instructions for my lady’s personal maid and Penelope.
The message said Lady Verinder was set to leave for her London house with Rachel on Monday. The written notes laid out what clothes the two maids needed to pack, and told them to meet their mistresses in town at a set hour. Most of the rest of the household would follow shortly. Rachel had been so unwilling to step foot back in the house after all the trouble here, her ladyship had decided to go straight to London from Frizinghall, no detours home. I was ordered to stay behind at the country house, to see to the property indoors and out until further notice. The few remaining servants would be put on board wages.
That reminder of Mr. Franklin’s words about our scattered, broken household made me uneasy about him. The more I turned his fate over in my head, the more worried I got. By the Sunday post, I sent a letter to his father’s valet, Mr. Jeffco, an old acquaintance of mine, begging him to let me know what Mr. Franklin planned to do once he got to London.
Sunday evening was even duller than Saturday. We finished our supposed day of rest the way half the country does every week—all of us nodding off in our armchairs long before bedtime.
I don’t know how the rest of the household fared on Monday, but that day gave me a right good shake. The first of Sergeant Cuff’s three predictions came true right on schedule: I heard from the Yollands.
I’d just waved Penelope and my lady’s maid off on the London train with all the luggage, and was ambling around the grounds checking the hedges, when I heard someone call my name. I turned, and came face to face with Limping Lucy, the fisherman’s daughter. Barring her lame foot and a leanness that I’ve always thought a terrible flaw in a woman, she was a good-looking enough girl: sharp dark eyes, a clear voice, and a fine thick head of chestnut hair. Her crutch was a constant burden, and her temper was a whole other flaw you couldn’t miss.
“Well, my dear,” I said. “What can I do for you?”
“Where’s that man you call Franklin Blake?” she said, fixing me with a glare hot enough to burn, leaning all her weight on her crutch.
“That’s no way to speak of a gentleman,” I told her. “If you mean my lady’s nephew, you’ll refer to him as Mr. Franklin Blake, if you please.”
She limped one step closer, looking like she could have swallowed me whole. “*Mr.* Franklin Blake?” she snarled. “Murderer Franklin Blake would suit him far better.”
My years of putting up with my late wife’s temper came in handy then. When a woman tries to rattle you, turn the tables and rattle her first. They’re ready for every snappy defence you can make, but never for that. One word was all it took. I looked at her pleasantly, and said, “Pooh!”
Her temper blew sky high. She balanced on her good foot, lifted her crutch, and pounded it three times hard into the grass. “He’s a murderer! He’s a murderer! He’s a murderer! He killed Rosanna Spearman!” She screamed the words at the top of her lungs. A couple of the gardeners working nearby glanced up, saw who it was, knew exactly what Lucy’s tantrums were like, and went right back to their work.
“He killed Rosanna Spearman?” I repeated. “What on earth makes you say that, Lucy?”
“What do any man care? If she’d only thought the way I do, she’d still be alive today!”
“She was always kind enough to me, poor soul,” I said. “And I always tried to be kind to her, for what it’s worth.”
I spoke as soft as I could. I hadn’t the heart to tease her any more. I’d noticed her temper first, but now I noticed the raw, ragged misery in her face. Misery in working folk, you’ll find, is very often rude. My words melted her. She bowed her head down, resting it on the top of her crutch.
“I loved her,” she said, quiet as a whisper. “She’d had a terrible life, Mr. Betteredge—vile people had hurt her, led her astray, and none of it had spoiled her sweet temper. She was an angel. We could have been happy together. I had it all planned: we’d move to London, live like sisters, make our living with our needles. Then that man showed up, and spoiled every last bit of it. He bewitched her. Don’t tell me he didn’t mean it, don’t tell me he didn’t know. He ought to have seen it. He ought to have pitied her. ‘I can’t live without him,’ she’d say to me, ‘and he never even looks at me.’ Cruel, cruel, cruel. I told her no man was worth grieving that much over. And she said, ‘There are men worth dying for, Lucy, and he is one of them.’ I had all our savings put aside, I’d talked it all over with my ma and pa. I was going to take her far away from all the shame she felt here. We’d have had a tiny little lodging, lived quiet, happy. She had a fine education, you know, beautiful handwriting, quick with a needle. I’m educated too, I can write well enough, I’m just not as fast with the needle as she was. We’d have got by. And then this morning? Her letter came. Told me she was done with living. Said goodbye forever. Where is he?” she cried, lifting her head from the crutch, flaming back through her tears. “Where’s this fine gentleman I’ve got to speak of with respect? The day’s not far off when the poor rise up against the rich, Mr. Betteredge. And I pray to Heaven they start with him. I pray Heaven they start with him.”
There was a good Christian girl for you, pushed so far past her breaking point her piety all fell to pieces. Even the parson himself couldn’t have lectured her out of that temper. All I did was keep her on track, hoping for some scrap of new information.
“What do you want with Mr. Franklin Blake?” I asked.
“I have to see him.”
“Is there something specific you need?”
“I’ve got a letter for him.”
“From Rosanna Spearman?”
“Yes.”
“She sent it to you, to pass on?”
“Yes.”
I thought for a second the dark mystery of the Moonstone was finally going to lift, that every secret I’d been dying to uncover was going to drop right into my lap. Then I felt that old detective fever Sergeant Cuff had left behind in the air prickle in my bones.
“You can’t see Mr. Franklin,” I told her.
“I have to. And I will.”
“He left for London last night.”
Lucy stared hard at my face, saw I was telling the truth, and turned on her crutch to head back to Cobb’s Hole without another word.
“Wait!” I called after her. “I’m expecting news of him tomorrow. Give me the letter, and I’ll post it straight to him.”
Lucy steadied herself, looked back over her shoulder.
“I have to put it in his hands myself,” she said. “No other way will do.”
“Shall I write and tell him what you said?”
“Tell him I hate him. And you’ll be telling him the truth.”
“Yes, yes—but what about the letter—?”
“If he wants it, he can come back here, and get it from me.”
With that she limped off down the path. The detective fever burned away all my dignity. I chased after her, pestering her to tell me more, but it was no use. It was my misfortune to be a man, and Limping Lucy got great joy out of disappointing me. Later that day I tried her mother, who could only cry and reach for the Dutch gin bottle. I found her father mending nets on the beach, who just grunted that it was “a bad job” and went back to his work. Neither of them knew a thing more than I did. The only remaining hope was the post the next morning, to write to Mr. Franklin Blake directly.
You can imagine how I hovered by the gate waiting for the postman on Tuesday. He brought two letters. The first was from Penelope, barely skimmed, telling me my lady and Miss Rachel had arrived safe in London. The second was from Mr. Jeffco, and the news it held shocked me: Mr. Franklin had already left England entirely.
The second he got to London, he’d gone straight to his father’s house. He’d arrived at the worst possible time: his father was up to his ears in parliamentary business, fiddling with some private bill the House was voting on. Jeffco led Franklin straight into his study. “My dear Franklin! What’s this surprise? Is something wrong?” “Yes, something’s wrong with Rachel. I’m devastated.” “Sorry to hear it, but I can’t stop to listen now.” “When can you hear me out?” “My dear boy, I won’t lie to you. Not until the end of the session. Good night.” “Thank you, sir. Good night.” That was the whole conversation inside the study, Jeffco wrote. Outside the door, it was even shorter. “Jeffco, what time’s the tidal train out tomorrow morning?” “Six forty, Mr. Franklin.” “Wake me at five.” “Off abroad, sir?” “Off wherever the railway decides to take me.” “Shall I tell your father, sir?” “Tell him at the end of the session.”
Next morning Mr. Franklin was gone, bound for the continent. No one, not even him, had the faintest idea where he was going. He might turn up next anywhere in Europe, Asia, Africa, or America. Jeffco said the odds were perfectly even across all four corners of the globe.
That news closed off the last chance I had to bring Lucy and Mr. Franklin together, and killed all my progress solving the mystery. Penelope’s theory that Rosanna had killed herself over unrequited love for Mr. Franklin was confirmed as far as the rest of the household was concerned, and that was all. No one could say if the letter Rosanna left for Franklin was the confession Sergeant Cuff had suspected she wanted to tell him, or just a private farewell note about her hopeless, secret love. It stayed sealed in Limping Lucy’s hands, hidden from everyone, even her own parents. We all begged her to tell us what it said, but none of us could get a word out of her. The servants, still clinging to the belief that Rosanna had stolen the diamond and hidden it, poked and prodded all along the rocks by the Shivering Sand, and found nothing. The tide ebbed and flowed, summer turned to autumn, and the quicksand that had swallowed Rosanna’s body swallowed her secret right along with it.
You already know the Tuesday post brought me Franklin’s travel news and the update that Rachel and her ladyship were settled in London. Wednesday came and went, nothing new. Thursday brought another long letter from Penelope.
She wrote that the great London doctor they’d consulted for Rachel had earned his guinea by saying the best cure was amusement. Flower shows, operas, balls—an endless round of parties had been planned, and to her mother’s shock, Rachel threw herself into every single one of them. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite had called, still as sweet-tongued as ever with his cousin, even after the cold shoulder he’d gotten on her birthday. Much to Penelope’s disgust, Rachel had welcomed him warmly, and even signed her name up to one of his ladies’ charity committees on the spot. Lady Verinder was reported to be low-spirited, and had held two long closed-door meetings with her lawyer. Penelope rambled on about Miss Clack, that poor relation I mentioned sitting next to Godfrey at the birthday dinner, who had a very fine taste in champagne. She was astonished Miss Clack hadn’t come crawling over to the house yet, as she always did, to mooch off her ladyship. That bit’s not worth mentioning, I know, except for one thing: I’ve been told the next part of this story is going to be narrated by that very same Miss Clack.
