Two months before ever Eli’s hearth began to glow in our sight, there was a little sea-town between Naples and Rome where the people left their nets to dry, their olives to cure, their pots to simmer, and streamed down to the strand as if summoned by a bell. The whole beach was alive with bodies and voices—old men with salt in their beards, women with wet hands still smelling of dough and brine, boys whose pockets held nothing but figs and pebbles—and all eyes were flung seaward, fixed upon a ship that laboured as a beast does under a cruel load.

The gale blew dead on shore. The sea did not merely move; it shoved and shouldered, it hammered with a rhythm that made the very air throb. There was a soundscape to it—wind keening through rigging like a thin flute, surf booming like a drum struck with a wet cloth, cries snapped short by spray—and on that harsh music the town’s murmuring rode: pity spoken aloud, and something meaner kept under the tongue, for men are seldom sorry to watch another man’s peril so long as their own feet are dry.

The vessel was ill-fated in her very bones: not built with the clean, clever lines that slip through water, but with an extravagant poop that caught the wind like a sail set wrong; she was, if truth be told, better fashioned for going ashore than for avoiding it. From the beach the battered frame of wood looked almost living—struggling, panting, driven back, lunging forward—yet if one could have been carried onto her deck, one would have found not one heart but many, and many sorts of courage and baseness breaking through as the hour grew black.

The sailors staggered and lurched, hands on ropes as each man’s fear taught him; they cursed, then prayed, then cursed again, as if oaths were ballast. The passengers were huddled amidships about the mast—some sitting, some kneeling, some flat as if they would press themselves into the planks—and every roll tore at their stomachs and their dignity alike. Among them stood a comely young man with an ashen cheek and lips clamped tight, as though he could bite terror into obedience. He held fast by a shroud, wincing with each heave of the sea.

It was Gerard.

Around the mast, vows rose as thick as spray. To listen, one might think there were nearly as many gods aboard as souls. The mariners, at least, clung to one Queen, though they dressed her in a dozen titles—Star of the Sea, Haven of Safety, Mistress of the World—each name flung upward like a rope. But the landsmen’s prayers were a market of divinities. An English merchant vowed gold to Our Lady of Walsingham; a Genoese promised a silver collar to Our Lady of Loretto; a Tuscan noble pledged ten pounds of wax lights to Our Lady of Ravenna; even the Cross itself must be specified by city, as if God’s wood needed a street-name to be found. In such moments men bargain with Heaven the way cooks bargain in a fish-market: loudly, greedily, and with a curious confidence that the price named must bind the seller.

Then came a gust more vicious than the rest. It struck the sail at the worst angle; rotten shrouds snapped. There was a crack like a cleaver through bone, and the torn sail went away downwind, dwindling, blackening, fluttering, and at last fell into the sea half a mile off like a paper dropped by a careless hand. Before the helm could bring the ship’s head about, a wave caught her on the quarter and drenched the whole poor company to the marrow, giving them a chill foretaste of death—salt water in the mouth, salt in the eyes, and the taste of fear that is like iron.

Vows turned frantic. One cried he would turn Carthusian if St. Thomas saved him. Another swore he would go barefoot to Compostella, bareheaded and with nothing but mail on naked skin, if St. James would draw him out. Names flew—Thomas, Dominic, Denys, and above all Catherine of Siena—like crumbs scattered to birds.

Two petty Neapolitan traders stood shivering, their teeth chattering a drumbeat on that furious sea-song. One bellowed, “I vow to St. Christopher at Paris a waxen image of his own weight if I win safe to land!”

The other nudged him, half drowned and half sensible. “Brother, brother, take heed. Sell all you have by auction and ’twill not buy his weight in wax.”

“Hold thy tongue, fool!” the other roared—then, bending close so the wind would steal the words, hissed, “Think ye I am in earnest? Let me but touch land and I’ll not give him a rush dip.”

Some, in their terror, prayed not to saints at all but to the sea itself, as if it were a capricious lord who might be softened by flattery. “Merciful sea! bountiful sea! beautiful sea! be gentle—be kind—preserve us!” And others only moaned, the animal sound that comes when speech breaks and the body remembers it is only flesh.

Yet amid this wailing throng there sat a Roman woman of the humbler sort, a child at her half-bared breast. She was pale as ash, but her eye was calm. Her lips moved at times in silent prayer; she neither wept nor bargained. When the ship seemed to sink under their feet and men with beards squeaked like frightened boys, she only kissed her child and held him closer, letting him draw what comfort he could while there was still milk and warmth in the world. There was something ancient in her—an unruined gravity, as if sixteen hundred years had not diluted the old blood; she died, if she must die, with decent dignity, and fed her child at the edge of the grave as naturally as another woman might do so beside a hearth.

On the poop stood a gigantic friar, feet apart as if planted in rock. He did not so much defy the danger as ignore it. His voice rang out steady and sonorous above wind and wave as he recited verses from the Canticles, and he invited the passengers to confess. Some crawled to him on their knees, clutching planks, and he heard them and laid his hands on them and absolved them as if he stood in a snug sacristy and not on a deck that pitched like a tossed trencher.

Gerard, drawn by an instinct older than thought, edged nearer and nearer to that immovable man—toward composure as a moth goes to flame. Heroic courage against fleshly odds might have looked small beside the friar’s vast, quiet self-command.

The sail was gone. The sailors cut down the useless mast a foot above the board and sent it, with its remaining hamper, over the side. The ship seemed to lift a little, relieved of that top-hampering weight, but now the hull could not keep ahead of the sea. Again and again the waves struck her on the poop; each blow felt like a mountain of rock, not water.

The captain left the helm and came amidships, pale as paste. “Lighten her!” he cried. “Fling all overboard, or we shall founder ere we strike, and lose the one little chance of life.”

As sailors began to obey, the captain—more like a superstitious pilgrim than a blunt skipper—spoke to the faces crowding him. “Friends,” said he, “last night when all was fair—too fair, alas—there came a globe of fire close to the ship. When a pair of them come, it is good luck, and nought can drown her that voyage. We mariners call them Castor and Pollux. But if Castor come without Pollux, or Pollux without Castor, she is doomed. Therefore, like good Christians, prepare to die.”

A wail went up, and the wind snatched it away in tatters. “How long?” a voice trembled.

“She may, or may not, last half an hour,” said the captain. “Over that, impossible. She leaks like a sieve. Bustle—lighten her!”

So the deck became a frantic larder being emptied in a fire: chests, bundles, spare gear, all heaved overboard with hands that shook. Presently they seized a heavy sack. An old man lay on it, sea-sick and helpless. They dragged it from under him; it rattled. Two began to heave it to the side.

Up sprang the owner with a cry raw enough to curdle milk. “Holy Moses! What would you do? ’Tis my all—the whole fruits of my journey—silver candlesticks, silver plates, brooches, hanaps—”

“Let go, thou hoary villain!” others shouted. “Shall all our lives be lost for thy ill-gotten gear?”

“Fling him in with it!” cried one. “’Tis this Ebrew we Christian men are drowned for.”

They wrenched the sack away and cast it into the sea. It splashed, then sank as if the water had swallowed it with appetite. The old man gave one cry of anguish and stood with white hair streaming, glaring like a mad prophet. He would have leapt after it had it floated; but it went down and was gone. He staggered, tore his hair, and cursed them, the ship, the sea, and all powers of heaven and hell together, as if he would season the water with blasphemy.

Then the captain cried, pointing: “See! there is a church in sight. Steer for that church, mate; and you, friends, pray to the saint, whoever he be.”

They tried to steer, and prayers shifted to this new, unknown patron like a sauce altered at the last moment because the cook has run out of herbs. But a tremendous sea broke over the stern, smashed the rudder, jammed it immovable, and flooded the deck. Panic turned to suspicion, and suspicion sought a scapegoat as naturally as hunger seeks bread.

Some, wild with superstition, came on Gerard. “Here is the cause of all! He has never invoked a single saint. He is a heathen—a pagan aboard!”

“Alas, good friends, say not so,” Gerard stammered, his teeth chattering with cold and terror. “Rather call these heathen that lie praying to the sea. Friends, I do honour the saints—but I dare not pray to them now—there is no time. What avail me Dominic, and Thomas, and Catherine? Nearer God’s throne than these St. Peter sitteth; and if I pray to him, I shall be drowned ere he has time to plead my cause with God. I must go straight to Him that made the sea, and the saints, and me.”

And he cried out into the storm, not bargaining now but begging like a man whose mouth is full of salt water: “Our Father which art in heaven, save these poor souls and me that cry for the bare life! Oh, sweet Jesus—pitiful Jesus—that didst walk Genezaret when Peter sank, and wept for Lazarus dead when the apostles’ eyes were dry—oh, save poor Gerard—for dear Margaret’s sake!”

At that moment the sailors were seen preparing the little boat every ship carried. A rush of selfishness answered them: thirty souls crowded in, pushing with elbows and curses. Left behind were three bewildered who ran to and fro wringing hands without the wit to leap; two more sat paralyzed like heaps of wet cloth. One man knelt over a wooden statue of the Virgin Mary, as large as life, which the sailors had reverently detached from the mast. As water slushed in and out through scuppers, the image washed about the deck, and the poor devotee followed it on his knees, hands clasped toward it, as if the statue were a floating loaf he dared not let drift away.

The Jew sat cross-legged, not palsied by fear but emptied of it—capable now only of grief for his lost silver. When spray lashed him, he shook his fist at the sea and cursed Nazarenes, their gods, their devils, their ships, and their waters, to all eternity.

The gigantic Dominican, having shriven the whole ship, stood calmly communing with his own spirit.

And the Roman woman still sat, pale and patient, only drawing her child closer as death came nearer.

Gerard saw her, and something in him hardened into manhood. “See—see!” he cried. “They have taken the boat and left the poor woman and her child to perish.”

His heart set his wits to work. “Wife,” he said to her—calling her so with the sudden fellowship of peril—“I’ll save thee yet, please God.” He ran seeking a cask, a plank, anything that would bear her. There was none.

Then his eye fell on the wooden image of the Virgin. He caught it in his arms. A wail burst from the kneeling worshipper like a child robbed of its toy, but Gerard, heedless, ran aft with it.

“Come,” he cried. “I’ll lash thee and the child to this. ’Tis sore worm-eaten, but ’twill serve.”

The woman lifted her great dark eyes to him and spoke one word, rich with question and tenderness: “Thyself?!”

“I am a man,” said he, “and have no child to take care of.”

“Ay,” she breathed; and the hope of life touched her face like sudden warmth. He lashed the image to her side—rope biting into old wood, knots drawn tight with numbed fingers, as if he were trussing a cask for travel. Her body trembled a little now, but her gaze held steady. The ship rode so low that Gerard, using an oar as a lever, could slide her into the waves.

“Come,” he urged, “while yet there is time.”

She turned to him, eyes wet. “Poor youth—God forgive me—my child!”

He launched her onto the surge, and with the oar kept her from being battered against the ship’s side, pushing her clear as one might guide a fragile bowl through a crowded kitchen.

Then a heavy hand fell on Gerard, and a deep voice sounded in his ear. “’Tis well. Now come with me.”

The gigantic friar.

Gerard turned, and the friar took two strides and seized the broken mast. Gerard did likewise, obeying by instinct. Together, with a prodigious effort, they hoisted the remainder and carried it.

“Fling it in,” said the friar, “and follow it.”

They cast it into the sea. But one of the bewildered passengers had chased them and leapt first, scrambling onto one end. Gerard seized the other; the friar took the middle. The mast rose and plunged with each wave like a kicking horse. Spray flogged their faces, filled their mouths with brine, and blinded them as if the sea were trying to knock them off by sheer violence.

A long grating noise sounded ahead. The ship had struck. Being now stationary, she became a wall for the waves to use. The three on the mast were hurled against the black hull with tremendous force. Their companion’s head struck the jagged upper part of the broken rudder with a crack that ended speech for ever; it was smashed like a nut beneath a hammer. He sank at once, leaving only a red stain on the water, a white clot on the splintered rudder, and a death-cry ringing in their ears as Gerard and the friar drifted clear under the lee of the hull.

The friar murmured a short Latin prayer for the dead man’s soul, then set himself again with composed strength.

They rolled along, alternately swallowed and lifted. One moment they saw nothing but the dim basin of watery hills; the next, a glimpse of shore speckled bright with people, arms flung up in wild Italian gestures. They saw, too, the black boat bottom-up, and between it and them the woman—rising and falling. She had come by a paddle and held her child with her left arm, paddling with her right—gallant, steady, her movements timed to the sea’s brutal rhythm. Even in that chaos there was a domestic obstinacy to her: the ancient duty of keeping a small mouth fed and a small body warm, even while the world roared.

After long tumbling, the friar said quietly, as if remarking upon a road: “I touched the ground.”

“Impossible, father,” cried Gerard. “We are more than a hundred yards from shore. Prithee, prithee, leave not our faithful mast.”

“My son,” said the friar, “you speak prudently. But know I have business of Holy Church on hand, and may not waste time floating when I can walk in her service. There—I felt it with my toes again. See the benefit of wearing sandals, and not shoon. Again; and sandy. Thy stature is less than mine: keep to the mast! I walk.”

He left the mast and, extending his powerful arms, rushed through the water. Gerard soon followed. At each overpowering wave the monk stood like a tower: he closed his mouth, flung his head back, vanished under the green weight, then emerged and ploughed on. Near shore, the outward suction fought them, dragging at their legs like unseen hands. The natives sent stout fishermen into the sea holding long spears, linked in a triple chain, and so hauled them ashore.

The friar shook himself like a great dog, bestowed a short paternal benediction, and went on toward Rome with eyes bent on the earth according to his rule, not pausing, not looking back upon the sea that had so nearly taken him. It could harm him no more than his Master permitted.

Gerard, who was not bound by a rule that forbade looking, stayed. Hands seized him, voices pressed him with questions, and they brought him to an enormous fire whose crackle and roar sounded, after that savage ocean, like sanity itself. The heat drew salt out of his clothes; steam rose from him; the smell of wet wool and smoke mingled with the sharp, clean stink of seaweed drying on stones. With a delicacy he might not have met farther north, they left him alone to dry.

Then he did a thing that showed what he valued most. From his bosom he drew a parchment and a paper, and he dried them with infinite care—turning them, shielding them from sparks, warming them gradually so ink would not run—tending them as a cook tends precious yeast, knowing that a careless heat can kill what time has made. Not until this was done to his mind did he consent to put on a fisherman’s dress, leaving his own garments by the fire, and go down to the beach to see what the sea would give back.

The rest may be told briefly. The captain clung to the ship, less from gallantry than from a conviction that it was idle to resist Castor or Pollux, whichever fiery messenger had come for him. Yet the sea broke up the vessel and swept the poop—captain and all—clear away, and then, in mockery, took him safe ashore. Gerard had a principal hand in dragging him out of the water.

The disconsolate Hebrew landed on another fragment, and the moment his feet touched earth he offered a reward for his bag. The offer stirred little sympathy and some amusement.

Two more were saved on pieces of wreck. The thirty who had crowded into the boat came ashore too—but one at a time, and dead; only one breathed still. The natives, with excellent intention and no knowledge, took him close to a hot fire. So he, too, went out of this shifting world, as if warmth were always kindness.

As Gerard stood by the sea, watching with horror and a grim curiosity the bodies of his late companions washed ashore to the sound of surf and lamentation, a hand touched his shoulder lightly.

He turned. It was the Roman woman, her face burning with gratitude as if life itself were a flame in her. She took his hand gently and raised it to her lips and kissed it—so nobly it seemed she conferred honour upon a deserving hand rather than begged favour from it. Then, with moist eyes and cheeks alight, she lifted her child and made him kiss his preserver.

Gerard kissed the child more than once; he was fond of children. But he did not speak. The woman spoke scarcely at all—only with her gaze and that antique, stately gesture—large, simple, and untranslatable into the little coinage of common words. Perhaps she was right. Gratitude is not a thing of talk.

Next day toward afternoon Gerard—twice as old as last year, thrice as learned in the ways of men, no boy now but a man who had shed blood in self-defence and grazed the grave by land and sea—reached the Eternal City; post tot naufragia tutus.
