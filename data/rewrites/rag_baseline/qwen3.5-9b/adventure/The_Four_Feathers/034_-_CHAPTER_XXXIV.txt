In the verdant haze of early summer, the following year, two elderly figures sat upon the terrace of Broad Place, the morning air thick with the scent of blooming hedges and cooling tea. They held their newspapers like shields against the passing world, the printed columns offering a fragile tether to reality. The elder, General Feversham, turned a page with a deliberate rustle, his eyes scanning the headlines until they landed on a dispatch regarding the Sudan.

"I see Osman Digna has returned to Suakin," he murmured, his voice carrying the weight of decades. "There is talk of conflict brewing in the sands."

"Oh," replied the younger man, Lieutenant Sutch, lowering his paper with a dismissive sigh. "He will not inflict much damage."

As Sutch spoke, the pastoral tranquility of the English countryside—the rolling green hills, the distant church spires, the familiar rhythm of village life—dissolved before his mental eye. In its place rose the blinding glare of the Red Sea coast. He saw the white-washed city of Suakin shimmering under a relentless sun, the arid brown plains stretching outward, tangled with the dry stalks of halfa grass, and far in the distance, the rugged silhouette of the hills leading toward Khor Gwob.

"A stifling place, Suakin, is it not, Sutch?" Feversham asked, turning to his companion.

"Appallingly so," Sutch confirmed, shivering slightly despite the warmth. "I recall an officer who collapsed on parade at dawn, struck down by the sun even beneath the protection of a regulation helmet. It is a town of suffocating heat. Yet, I confess, I was glad to be there—very glad," he added, a flicker of genuine emotion breaking through his stoicism.

"Yes," Feversham agreed briskly, tapping ash from his pipe. "And the ibex? Are they still roaming the rocks?"

"No," Sutch replied flatly. "Every single ibex within miles has been hunted down by the English garrison."

"No? Nothing else to occupy your time, then? That is all?"

"Yes, that is it, Feversham. Something to do."

Both men returned to their papers, the conversation dissolving into the background hum of the morning. However, shortly thereafter, a footman appeared, placing a small stack of letters before each of them. Feversham scanned the envelopes with a practiced, rapid glance, selecting one specific packet and emitting a low grunt of contentment. From his breast pocket, he withdrew a case of spectacles, sliding them onto his nose.

"From Ramelton?" Sutch asked, setting his newspaper aside.

"From Ramelton," Feversham confirmed. "First, however, I shall light a cigar."

He laid the letter upon the garden table, positioned precisely between the two veterans. With the patience of a man accustomed to command, he produced a cigar-case, cutting and lighting the tobacco with meticulous deliberation, ignoring Sutch's growing impatience. Time had transformed the old general into an epicure; a letter from Ramelton was not merely news, but a luxury to be savored amidst every accessory of comfort. He settled deeper into his chair, extending his legs, and smoked long enough to ensure the draw was perfect before finally unsealing the envelope.

"Is it from him?" Sutch queried, feigning nonchalance.

"No," Feversham corrected gently. "It is from her."

Sutch's eyes widened imperceptibly. "Ah!"

Feversham read the letter slowly, his brow furrowed in concentration, while Sutch struggled to keep his gaze fixed on the empty space above the table rather than peeking across the wood grain. Once the general had finished, he turned the page and began reading from the beginning once more, savoring the words.

"Any news?" Sutch asked, adopting a casual tone.

"They are very pleased with the house now that it has been rebuilt."

"And anything further?"

"Yes. Harry has completed the sixth chapter of his history of the war."

"Excellent!" Sutch exclaimed. "You will find he writes well. He possesses imagination, he knows the terrain intimately, and he was present throughout the conflict. Furthermore, he wandered the bazaars; he witnessed the underside of things."

"Yes," Feversham nodded. "But you and I shall not read it, Sutch."

"No," Sutch admitted immediately. "I was mistaken. You may, for you possess many more years to spare."

Turning back to his own correspondence, Sutch pressed on. "Anything more?"

"Yes. They are arriving here in a fortnight."

"Capital," Sutch declared. "I shall remain."

He paced briefly along the terrace, returning to find Feversham seated with the letter resting on his knees, a profound expression of perplexity etched upon his face.

"You know, Sutch, I never fully comprehended..." Feversham began.

"Did you?"

"Yes, I believe I did." Sutch offered no explanation. He felt it prudent that Feversham should never attempt such an understanding, for it required a depth of self-reproach that the general was incapable of summoning.

"Do you ever see Durrance?" the general asked suddenly.

"Yes, I encounter him frequently. He is currently abroad."

Feversham turned toward his friend. "He visited Broad Place when you departed for Suakin and conversed with me for half an hour. He served as Harry's best man. Well, that too remains beyond my comprehension. Did you understand it?"

"Yes, I grasped that perfectly."

"Oh!" General Feversham sighed. He requested no further elucidations. As he always had, he took the questions that baffled him and set them aside, banishing them from his thoughts entirely. Yet, he did not proceed to open his remaining letters. Instead, he continued smoking his cigar, gazing out over the summer landscape, listening intently to the distinct sounds rising from the fields below.

Sutch had already perused his entire correspondence before Feversham spoke again.

"I have been reflecting," the general said. "Have you noticed the date of the month, Sutch?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Yes," he replied. "Next week, on this same day, marks the anniversary of our assault upon the Redan, and Harry's birthday."

"Precisely," Feversham affirmed. "Why should we not revive the Crimean Nights?"

Sutch sprang from his chair. "Splendid!" he cried. "Do you suppose we can muster a tableful?"

"Let us ascertain," Feversham said, ringing a handbell on the table to summon the servant for the Army List.

Bending over the list, the two veterans might have been left to their planning, but the narrative demands a final word concerning another figure in this tale.

That night, after the invitations had been dispatched from Broad Place and the last light extinguished within the house windows, a solitary figure leaned over the railing of a steamer anchored at Port Said. He listened to the rhythmic chanting of Arab coolies as they shuffled up and down the gangplanks, balancing coal baskets between the barges and the ship's hull. The chaotic clamor of the town drifted across the water to his ears. In his mind's eye, he visualized the flare of braziers upon the quays, the glowing port-holes, and the dark funnels looming ahead and behind in the procession of anchored vessels.

Attended by a servant, he had returned to the East.

Early the next morning, the steamer pushed through the canal, and as sunset approached, emerged into the chilling waters of the Gulf of Suez. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, Tamanieb, the siege of McNeil's zareeba—Durrance relived these moments, re-immersing himself in the golden years of his vigor, the era of abundance. Within that western land, the long preparations were advancing steadily, destined one day to roll up the Dervish Empire and crush it into dust. Upon the glacis of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had pledged his hand to this grand endeavor, yet the desert he loved had struck him down and cast him forth. Nevertheless, the boat steamed southward into the Red Sea.

Three nights more passed, and though he would not witness it, the Southern Cross would rise obliquely into the twilight sky.
