Mrs. Shaw’s dislike of Milton rose in her like a rash the instant she had set foot again on its pavements. The place offended her on every sense at once. The air was a soot-laden broth that clung to the palate and to the curtains; the streets rang with iron-shod wheels and the ceaseless clatter of work—noise that did not merely fill the ear but pressed on the nerves like a hand. Even the richer women she passed seemed to her dressed too loud, too showy, as if finery could drown the roar of the mills; and the poor, moving in and out of the factory-gates, looked to her not so much unfortunate as unbecomingly stained by the very town.

Margaret, weakened to the point where the simplest choice felt like a labour, endured her aunt’s earnest tirade as one endures a draught one cannot swallow and cannot refuse. Mrs. Shaw feared her own “nerves;” she feared, too, that Margaret’s colour would never come back in an air that tasted of smoke. Margaret must return to London with her—quickly. At last, with her spirit bruised and her body worn thin, Margaret yielded. When Wednesday was over—when her father had been laid in the ground far away from the green fields of his youth and far away from the grave where his wife lay among strangers—then she would go. Dixon should be left to settle accounts, dismiss furniture, close the house.

It was the distance that wrung Margaret most: not merely the miles, but the way death had scattered the Hales like seed blown from a torn pod—one in Milton’s little rented rooms, one in Oxford’s quiet earth, one in Helstone’s memory. She tortured herself with the thought that, had she not fallen into that heavy stupor of the first days, she might have contrived some tenderness of arrangement—might have brought husband and wife nearer even in burial. Now there was only the irrevocable.

Before that mournful Wednesday came, a letter arrived from Mr. Bell.

It was all Mr. Bell—blunt, affectionate, bustling, with jests thrust in like cloves into an apple to keep the sweetness from cloying. He could not return when he had intended; there was some rare duty at Oxford calling the Fellows together. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton were there. The captain, smart and well-meaning, had offered to go to Milton and help in searching for the will—though Mr. Bell was certain there was none, or Margaret would have found it already if she had followed his directions. The captain had declared, too, that he must take Margaret and his mother-in-law home; in Edith’s condition he could not remain away beyond Friday. Mr. Bell, with his practical kindliness, proposed to put matters into the hands of his Milton attorney if no will appeared; he distrusted the captain’s business capacity even while admiring his moustachios.

There would have to be a sale. Margaret must choose what she wished reserved. And then came the part that made her cheeks burn and her throat tighten—not the talk of sale and lists and attorneys, but the paternal assumption wrapped around her like a cloak she had not asked for and could not put off.

Mr. Bell reminded her that she was to have his money and goods when he died—not that he meant to die yet—only that she might understand what followed. Since these Lennoxes were fond of her now and might or might not continue so, it would be best to begin with a formal agreement: she was to pay them two hundred and fifty pounds a year as long as they found it pleasant to live together. That would secure her from being thrown adrift if the captain ever wanted his household to himself; she could take herself and her two hundred and fifty pounds elsewhere—unless Mr. Bell claimed her first to keep house for him. As for dress, Dixon, and “confectionery” (which he insisted all young ladies ate until wisdom came by age), he would consult some lady acquaintance and settle an allowance after ascertaining what Margaret would have from her father.

Then, under all the brisk command, the old love showed itself plainly: he had loved her father five and thirty years; he had stood by him on his wedding-day; he had closed his eyes in death. He was her godfather; if he could do her little spiritual good, he would at least endow her materially. He had no known relation on earth—“who is there to mourn for Adam Bell?”—and his whole heart was bent upon this one thing. Margaret Hale was not the girl to say him nay. She must write by return—two lines, if only two lines—and “no thanks.”

Margaret took up a pen. Her hand trembled; the ink seemed too black, too heavy for her weakened fingers. She wrote, slowly, as if each stroke were a weight lifted and set down:—

“Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay.”

She vexed herself even as she wrote; the words were his, not hers. Yet she had no strength to do better, and when the paper was folded her pulse beat with fatigue as if she had run a mile. She lay down again and tried not to think.

Mrs. Shaw, fluttering about her, peered at her with anxious softness. “My dearest child! Has that letter vexed or troubled you?”

“No,” said Margaret, though her voice was scarcely more than breath. “I shall be better when to-morrow is over.”

“I feel sure you won’t be better till I get you out of this horrid air. How you can have borne it these two years I can’t imagine.”

“Where could I go to? I could not leave papa and mamma.”

Mrs. Shaw softened at once into the complacent consolation of those who believe their comforts are proof of Providence. “Well, don’t distress yourself. I dare say it was all for the best; only I had no conception of how you were living. Our butler’s wife lives in a better house than this.”

“It is sometimes very pretty—in summer; you can’t judge by what it is now. I have been very happy here,” said Margaret; and she closed her eyes to stop the talk, as one shuts a door upon wind.

Yet the house, under Mrs. Shaw’s directions, teemed with comfort compared with the bare economies of the past months. Fires were lit in every bedroom—little islands of warmth against Milton’s damp chill. Mrs. Shaw bought delicacies and soft luxuries as if feeding and pampering could mend what grief had torn. There were broths that smelled of slow-simmered bones and herbs, jellies trembling on china, oranges gleaming like captured sun. But all this seemed to Margaret no more than a kindly ritual performed around her; it reached her only as gratitude to her aunt, not as temptation to appetite. Her own senses had dulled to one persistent taste—iron, like tears.

Restlessness drove her, though she was weak. All day long she kept herself from thinking of Oxford and the grave being opened there by wandering from room to room and touching, lifting, setting aside the things she meant to keep. The task had the quiet insistence of domestic duty—fold this, tie that—yet every object she handled gave back a faint echo, as if the house were an instrument and her grief kept striking the same note. Dixon followed her at Mrs. Shaw’s desire, ostensibly to take instructions, but in truth with orders to coax her into rest.

“These books, Dixon, I will keep. All the rest will you send to Mr. Bell? He will value them for themselves, as well as for papa’s sake.” Margaret paused, her fingers resting on a volume as if feeling for the warmth it once held. “This—this I should like you to take to Mr. Thornton after I am gone. Stay; I will write a note with it.”

She sat down hastily, as though afraid that if she stood still she would remember too much. The pen moved again over paper.

“DEAR SIR,—The accompanying book I am sure will be valued by you for the sake of my father, to whom it belonged.

“Yours sincerely,
“MARGARET HALE.”

Then she rose and went on, turning over shabby, old-fashioned things known to her since childhood with a kind of reluctant caress. The very textures spoke: worn cloth smoothed by years of hands; the faint smell of cedar in a drawer; a crack in porcelain that had been there since she was a girl. Dixon talked on steadily—little homely speeches meant to divert the mind—but afterwards she told Mrs. Shaw she doubted whether Miss Hale heard a word.

The consequence of being on her feet so long was that in the evening her body gave up, utterly spent. The exhaustion was merciful; it carried her into the first decent night’s rest she had had since her father died.

At breakfast next morning she said she wished to go and bid one or two friends good-bye.

Mrs. Shaw objected, scandalised and nervous. “I am sure, my dear, you can have no friends here with whom you are sufficiently intimate to justify you in calling upon them so soon; before you have been at church.”

“But to-day is my only day; if Captain Lennox comes this afternoon, and if we must—if I must really go to-morrow—”

“Oh, yes, we shall go to-morrow. I am more and more convinced this air is bad for you; it makes you look so pale and ill. Besides, Edith expects us; and she may be waiting for me; and you cannot be left alone, my dear, at your age. No; if you must make these calls, I will go with you. Dixon can get us a coach, I suppose?”

So Mrs. Shaw went, wrapped in shawls as though Milton’s very sound could chill her bones, and her maid followed behind to manage cushions and coverings. Margaret’s face was too sad for smiling at such parade. She had often walked alone to these places at any hour, with only the steadying rhythm of her own step and the familiar voices of the streets. Now she sat while the coach rattled, the iron tyres striking sparks of sound from the stones, and she dreaded naming one of her destinations: Nicholas Higgins’ court, where washing-lines strung from house to house slapped and snapped in the wind, where wet clothes breathed out a sour steam, and where the noise of children and neighbours was never quite still. She hoped her aunt’s delicacy would refuse to alight.

There was a small battle in Mrs. Shaw between ease and propriety; ease triumphed. With many warnings to Margaret not to catch fever—always lurking, Mrs. Shaw said, in such places—she permitted her to go in alone.

Nicholas was out. Only Mary was there, and one or two of the Boucher children, their faces pinched and inquisitive. Margaret was vexed with herself for miscalculating the time. She had meant to say a hundred small things—tender, practical, encouraging—things to tie the past to the future like knots in a cord. But Mary, blunt in mind though warm in heart, understood at once why Margaret had come, and began to cry openly, with such violent sobbing that words became useless.

Margaret could only try to comfort her with the vague, thin hope that they might meet again—sometime, somewhere—and she begged her to tell her father that if he could manage it, she wished him to come in the evening when work was done.

As she was leaving, she stopped and looked round the room, fixing it in her mind—the poor hearth, the worn table, the corner where Bessy had lain. Then she hesitated before she spoke, and when she did her voice shook.

“I should like to have some little thing to remind me of Bessy.”

At once Mary’s generosity flared. What could they give? There was so little; poverty makes even kindness consider its stock. Margaret’s eyes fell on a common drinking-cup, plain and cheap, but haunted for her by remembrance: Bessy’s feverish lips, and that cup always standing ready with water.

Mary cried, “Oh, take summat better; that only cost fourpence!”

“That will do, thank you,” said Margaret quickly; and she went away before she could see Mary’s face change again. For a moment the pleasure of giving—of having still something to offer—lit Mary’s features like a candle.

Now to Marlborough Mills’ master’s house—now to Mrs. Thornton. It must be done.

Margaret sat rigid in the coach, her hands clasped in her lap as if keeping down a trembling. The streets changed as they went, yet the town’s voice did not: a persistent undertone of machinery, a hammered rhythm that made conversation feel thin. It was not merely loud; it was incessant, and in that incessancy there was a kind of tyranny, as if the place insisted on its own vocabulary—iron, steam, profit—and drowned out gentler dialects.

She had hard work finding the right words to explain to her aunt who Mrs. Thornton was, and why she must go. Mrs. Shaw would not content herself with remaining in the coach at this respectable door; she alighted.

They were shown into a drawing-room where a fire had only just been kindled. The room still held the chill of unused air; the flame, newly fed, smelt of singeing dust and resin before it settled into steadier warmth. Mrs. Shaw huddled in her shawl and shivered. “What an icy room!”

They waited. At length Mrs. Thornton entered.

There was some softening in her now that Margaret was going out of sight. She remembered Margaret’s spirit more than her own resentments; and grief had carved Margaret’s face into something that commanded even Mrs. Thornton’s austere attention. She greeted her with a blander countenance, and there was—rarety indeed—a shade of tenderness when she observed the tear-swollen whiteness and heard the quiver Margaret struggled to steady.

“Allow me to introduce my aunt, Mrs. Shaw,” said Margaret. “I am going away from Milton to-morrow; I do not know if you are aware of it, but I wanted to see you once again, Mrs. Thornton, to—to apologize for my manner the last time I saw you; and to say that I am sure you meant kindly—however much we may have misunderstood each other.”

Mrs. Shaw looked extremely perplexed. Apologies, thanks, misunderstandings—she did not know what sort of intimacy could make these necessary between ladies. But Mrs. Thornton answered with her accustomed sense of duty.

“Miss Hale, I am glad you do me justice. I did no more than I believed to be my duty in remonstrating with you as I did. I have always desired to act the part of a friend to you. I am glad you do me justice.”

“And,” Margaret went on, blushing painfully, “will you do me justice, and believe that though I cannot—I do not choose—to give explanations of my conduct, I have not acted in the unbecoming way you apprehended?”

Her voice was low; her eyes pleaded. The charm of manner—hitherto a thing to which Mrs. Thornton had seemed armoured—touched her at last. “Yes, I do believe you. Let us say no more about it. Where are you going to reside, Miss Hale? I understood from Mr. Bell that you were going to leave Milton. You never liked Milton, you know,” she added, with a grim half-smile; “but for all that you must not expect me to congratulate you on quitting it. Where shall you live?”

“With my aunt,” said Margaret, turning towards Mrs. Shaw.

“My niece will reside with me in Harley Street,” said Mrs. Shaw, smoothing the statement into a social fact. “She is almost like a daughter to me. And I am glad to acknowledge my own obligation for any kindness that has been shown to her. If you and your husband ever come to town, my son and daughter, Captain and Mrs. Lennox, will, I am sure, join with me in wishing to do anything in our power to show you attention.”

Mrs. Thornton thought—sharply—that Margaret had not troubled to enlighten her aunt as to the relationship between the Thorntons and these fine offers. She replied shortly, cutting through the soft patronage.

“My husband is dead. Mr. Thornton is my son. I never go to London; so I am not likely to be able to avail myself of your polite offers.”

At that instant Mr. Thornton entered.

He had only just returned from Oxford; the black of his mourning declared why he had been there. His face bore that set look of one who has done what had to be done and will not speak of it. His mother said, “John, this lady is Mrs. Shaw, Miss Hale’s aunt. I am sorry to say that Miss Hale’s call is to wish us good-bye.”

“You are going then!” he said, and his voice came out low, as if he had spoken into his own chest.

“Yes,” said Margaret. “We leave to-morrow.”

“My son-in-law comes this evening to escort us,” added Mrs. Shaw.

Mr. Thornton turned away. He did not sit. He stood by the table as if some object there demanded his whole attention, as if he had discovered an unopened letter and must consider it immediately. It was hard to tell whether he heard anything else. He did not even seem aware when they rose to go.

Yet when they moved towards the door he started forward and handed Mrs. Shaw down to the carriage with the mechanical civility of a man who has learned to keep his hands busy when his heart is not. Margaret and he stood close together on the step for a moment, and it was impossible for either not to remember another day—how the crowd had surged, how the air had been full of shouting and stones, how her body had been between him and violence. The sound of Milton was in their ears now, too: not the roar of a riot, but the constant grinding chorus of industry, as if the town itself refused quiet even for farewell.

Into his mind came, linked and burning, the speeches of the day after—the remembrance of her words, passionate and cruel, that there was not a man in all that desperate crowd for whom she did not care as much as for him. At the recollection his brow grew stern, though his heart beat thick with longing.

No. He had put it to the touch once, and lost. Let her go—with her stony heart and her beauty. How terrible her look was now, for all the loveliness of her features! She feared he would speak, and that speaking would demand some stern repression. Let her go. Beauty and heiress as she might be, she would find it hard to meet with a truer heart than his. Let her go.

He said good-bye with no tone of regret, no visible emotion. His offered hand was taken; he dropped it as carelessly as if it were a dead, withered flower.

No one in his household saw him again that day. He was busily engaged—or so he said.

Margaret’s strength, already scant, was utterly exhausted by these visits. She could not resist her aunt’s watchfulness, the petted arrangements, the sighing “I told you so’s.” Dixon said she was as bad as on the first day she heard of Mr. Hale’s death, and she and Mrs. Shaw consulted anxiously about delaying the journey. When Mrs. Shaw reluctantly proposed a few days’ delay, Margaret writhed as if in acute pain.

“Oh! let us go,” she said. “I cannot be patient here. I shall not get well here. I want to forget.”

So the preparations went on. Captain Lennox came, bringing news of Edith and the little boy, and the easy, indifferent talk of a man who was kind without being too deeply entangled in sorrow did Margaret good, as plain food sometimes steadies a sick stomach better than rich dainties. She roused herself a little. When she knew she might expect Higgins, she left the room quietly and waited in her own chamber for the summons.

He came in with the awkward tenderness of the strong for the wounded, and stood turning his cap in his hands.

“Eh!” said he. “To think o’ th’ oud gentleman dropping off as he did! Yo’ might ha’ knocked me down wi’ a straw when they telled me. ‘Mr. Hale?’ says I; ‘him as was th’ parson?’ ‘Ay,’ says they. ‘Then,’ says I, ‘there’s as good a man gone as ever lived on this earth, let who will be t’other!’”

He spoke on, breathy with indignation at the obstruction he had met: the women in the kitchen who would not tell Margaret he was there, saying she was ill. He stared at her, troubled.

“Butter me, but yo’ dunnot look like the same wench. And yo’re going to be a grand lady up i’ Lunnon, aren’t yo’?”

“Not a grand lady,” said Margaret, and for a moment the ghost of a smile touched her mouth.

“Well! Thornton said—says he, a day or two ago, ‘Higgins, have yo’ seen Miss Hale?’ ‘No,’ says I; ‘there’s a pack o’ women who won’t let me at her. But I can bide my time, if she’s ill. She and I knows each other pretty well; and hoo’ll not go doubting that I’m main sorry for th’ oud gentleman’s death, just because I can’t get at her and tell her so.’ And says he, ‘Yo’ll not have much time for to try and see her, my fine chap. She’s not for staying with us a day longer nor she can help. She’s got grand relations, and they’re carrying her off; and we shan’t see her no more.’”

Higgins lifted his head, his rough pride rising. “‘Measter,’ says I, ‘if I dunnot see her afore hoo goes, I’ll strive to get up to Lunnon next Whitsuntide, that I will. I’ll not be baulked o’ saying her good-bye by any relations whatsomdever.’ But bless yo’, I knowed yo’d come. It were only for to humour th’ measter, I let on as if I thought yo’d mebbe leave Milton without seeing me.”

“You’re quite right,” said Margaret. “You only do me justice. And you’ll not forget me, I’m sure. If no one else in Milton remembers me, I’m certain you will—and papa too. You know how good and tender he was.”

She went to a drawer and drew out the Bible, holding it as if it were warm, though it was only leather and paper. It seemed to carry her father’s very touch, his voice silently moving between its leaves.

“Look, Higgins. Here is his Bible. I have kept it for you. I can ill spare it; but I know he would have liked you to have it. I’m sure you’ll care for it, and study what is in it, for his sake.”

“Yo’ may say that,” Higgins answered hoarsely. “If it were th’ deuce’s own scribble, and yo’ axed me to read in it for yo’r sake and th’ oud gentleman’s, I’d do it.”

Margaret’s hand moved again, quick, almost furtive, as she pressed money towards him.

“Whatten’s this, wench!” he cried, starting back. “I’m not going for to take yo’r brass, so dunnot think it. We’ve been great friends, ’bout the sound o’ money passing between us.”

“For the children—for Boucher’s children,” Margaret said hurriedly, as if afraid of her own tears. “They may need it. You’ve no right to refuse it for them. I would not give you one penny,” she added, and the faintest humour steadied her voice; “don’t think there’s any of it for you.”

He stood a moment, conquered not by the coin but by the purpose—by the way she named the children as if they were a duty laid on all human hands alike. Then he took it, as one takes a trust.

“Well, wench! I can nobbut say—Bless yo’! and bless yo’!—and amen.”
