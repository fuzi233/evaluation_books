While the burgomaster of Tergou railed against Gerard to the town council, far off in the quiet village of Sevenbergen, Margaret nursed a quiet, heavy distress of her own. It was a housewife’s grief, stoked by love, deeper than modern sensibilities can easily grasp.
She found old Martin Wittenhaagen, the weathered ex-soldier of the Duke’s wars, with bright tears glistening in her eyes. “Martin, there’s not a crumb left in the larder, and Gerard will step through that door any hour now. The lad is so thoughtless when he’s deep in his copying work he forgets to break his fast half the day. The second he lays down his pen he runs straight here, no stopping for a bite, and half the time he stumbles over the threshold faint as a lamb. I’ve nothing warm to set before the dear soul that loves me so.”
Martin scratched his grizzled head, calloused from decades of pulling bowstrings. “What’s an old fellow like me to do about that, mistress?”
“Today’s Thursday,” she said, brightening a little. “It’s your licensed day to shoot, after all. Truth be told, I was counting on you to bring home game today.”
“Nay now,” Martin shook his head, digging a crumpled parchment out of his leather pouch, its great wax seal cracked and stained with years of use. “I can’t loose an arrow in the Duke’s woods if his grace or his lords are out on the chase—see for yourself, though I can’t read a line of it.”
That parchment was the lifetime reward Philip, Duke of Burgundy, had granted the old archer for taking a crossbow bolt meant for the Duke right in the thigh on a French battlefield. It promised him four merks a year in stipend, and the rare right to shoot three arrows, one single day a week—Thursday, and no other—anywhere in the Duke’s Holland forests, so long as he did not harm a seven-year-old buck or a doe heavy with fawn. The fine for breaking that rule? Forfeit his salary, his good name, and very likely his head, plus a penny administrative fee. It explicitly banned hunting on any day the Duke or his noble party was out chasing game.
Margaret’s shoulders slumped, and she fell quiet, sighing soft.
“Cheer up now, lass,” Martin said, patting her arm gently. “For your sake I’ll risk the Duke’s displeasure. I’ve thrown my life on the line for folks not worth the tip of your finger before, and this is no great danger at all. I’ll just slip to the very edge of the forest, not ten minutes’ walk from here. Likely enough the beaters will drive a hare or a young fawn right within arrow range before anyone notices me.”
“Very well,” Margaret said, but her voice was tight with worry. “But you must promise me you won’t go far, and you won’t let a soul spot you. Far better Gerard eats a dry crust tonight than any harm comes to you, faithful old Martin.”
He gave his word, slung his six-foot English yew bow over his shoulder, took his three permitted arrows, and slipped silent as a shadow into the treeline, barely a furlong from the village cottages. The faint, winding blare of hunting horns drifted on the wind from deep in the woods, and every beast of the field was already up and running for cover. “Easy now,” Martin thought, grinning to himself. “I’ll have the pot boiling before anyone’s the wiser.”
He tucked himself behind the thick gnarled trunk of an ancient oak, which gave him a clear line of sight across an open sun-dappled glade, and strung his bow. It was a legendary weapon, six feet two inches of solid yew, carved thick enough to stop a light blow from a broadsword. Martin, broad-chested, his arms hard as forged iron wrapped in sinew, had been drawing bows since he was a small boy, and he could pull a three-foot war arrow all the way back to his ear. When that arrow flew it moved so fast the human eye could barely track it, and the twang of the bowstring sang out as clear as a plucked harp string. That very bow had sent dozens of armed men tumbling from their saddles in the brutal civil wars between the Hoecks and the Cabbeljaws, back when battlefields were thick with men rather than gunsmoke: every archer saw exactly where his arrow landed, and far fewer shots went to waste than the lead bullets of later days.
A hare came cantering out into the glade, then stopped stock still, her long ears pricked up to make a perfect sharp V. Martin levelled his great bow, loosed his arrow in a hurry. It missed her by a bare inch, hissed right across the short grass under her belly, and vanished into the underbrush. The hare jumped three feet straight in the air before bolting off at top speed. “Clumsy oaf,” Martin muttered to himself—sure proof he was no habitual bad shot, else he’d have blamed the hare for moving.
He had barely nocked his second arrow when a fat wood pigeon fluttered down to perch right on the lowest bough of the oak he was hiding behind. “Aha,” he thought, smiling. “Small, but sweet eating, roasted over a fire.” This time he took his time, drew the arrow slow and steady, loosed it smooth. It looked to fly straight through the bird, sending feathers floating up like dust into the air. But the pigeon was only torn in the breast, not killed outright. It fluttered feebly up over the treetops, flew fifty yards, and dropped out of sight behind the thick foliage, where he could not reach it.
“Luck’s turned against me today,” he grumbled, nocking his third and last permitted arrow. He peered sharp across the glade, and a few minutes later he heard a crash of brush behind him. He spun around just in time to see a magnificent full-grown buck leap across the open clearing, gone before he could even draw his bow. He threw his bow down on the turf with a vicious curse.
Just then a long, spotted tawny shape slithered past him after the deer, its belly almost brushing the ground as it ran. Martin snatched his bow back up instantly. That was the Duke’s own trained hunting leopard. The hunters could not be more than a few hundred yards behind her. “Best get out of here,” he thought. “Gerard will go supperless tonight.”
He plunged deeper into the trees, heading for home, following the path the buck and leopard had taken. He had not gone far when he heard a terrible commotion up ahead: leaves rustling wildly, the ground trampled under heavy hooves. He ran toward the noise, and found the leopard perched on the buck’s back, tearing at his neck with claws and fangs. The buck was bounding in wild, convulsive circles, blood pouring down his flanks.
Martin made a desperate, foolhardy decision. He would take that buck home to Margaret. He drew his third arrow all the way back to his head, and buried it deep in the deer’s heart. The buck, still carrying the leopard on his back, leapt high into the air, then dropped stone dead. The leopard did not even pause, just kept tearing at the buck’s throat as if nothing had happened.
Martin waited, hoping the big cat would gorge itself so full of blood it would let him drag the carcass away unmolested. After a few minutes he stepped forward bold as brass, and laid his hand on the buck’s front leg. The leopard let out a roar that shook the leaves off the branches, and fixed him with a savage glare. Old tales said no wild beast could bear to meet a man’s unblinking gaze. So Martin stood tall, stared the beast straight in the eyes. The leopard stared right back, never breaking eye contact for a second. Brutally ignorant of that old bit of folk wisdom, the creature let out a shriek and launched itself straight at his face, jaws stretched wide.
He barely had time to clamp one calloused hand around its throat before its fangs could crush his skull. One of its front claws tore straight through his jerkin, raking deep gashes into his shoulder. The other claw, aimed straight for his cheek, got caught fast in the thick leather scapulary he wore over his head instead of a hat, the same tough material as his tunic. Martin squeezed its throat harder, his iron arms never slackening, as the cat’s claws ripped deeper and deeper into his shoulder, the pain like blunt reaping hooks grinding into his flesh. The agony did not cow the old soldier—it lit his blood on fire. His teeth were bared in a snarl just as ferocious as the leopard’s, and the two pairs of eyes blazed at each other in the dim wood.
The leopard realized it was being throttled, and thrashed wildly to break free. It dragged Martin’s leather cowl right over his eyes, blinding him, and tore its claw out of his shoulder leaving a ragged trail of flesh behind. But Martin never loosened his iron grip, squeezing harder and harder. First the leopard’s long tail, held high in the air, went limp. “That’s it!” Martin gasped, grinning through the pain, and squeezed like death. Soon the big cat’s body lost all its tension, then stopped moving entirely. He dashed it against the ground, then tore the cowl off his face, panting for breath. The Duke’s prized hunting leopard lay dead at his feet, tongue lolling out, one paw caked in his blood. For the first time, a cold wave of terror washed over Martin. I’m a dead man, he thought. I’ve killed the Duke’s own leopard.
He grabbed a handful of leaves and tossed them over the cat’s body to hide it, then slung the heavy buck’s carcass over his shoulders and staggered home, leaving a winding trail of blood behind him—half the deer’s, half his own. He burst through the door of Peter’s cottage a sight to stop a man’s breath, covered in gore from head to toe, and dropped the buck on the floor with a heavy thud. “There,” he panted. “No questions now, just broil me a thick steak of that venison, for I’m about to faint dead away.”
Margaret did not notice his wounds at first, assuming all the blood belonged to the deer. She hurried to stoke the hearth fire, and Martin slipped off to the corner to tear strips from his undershirt to stanch and bind the ragged cuts on his shoulder. By the time Gerard walked through the door, the three of them were sitting down to a feast of juicy broiled venison. Gerard, to everyone’s surprise, had brought a full flask of strong Schiedam gin, and the drink soon warmed old Martin up enough to tell them the whole wild story of how he’d gotten the meat. They laughed loud over his mad exploit, all three of them light and happy around the fire.
Their merriment died sudden and cold. Margaret’s eyes went wide and fixed, her face blanched pure white with terror. She gasped for breath, too stunned to speak, and pointed a trembling finger toward the window. The two men followed her gaze, and there in the twilight, crouched just outside the glass, was a dark spotted shape, its two eyes glowing bright green like smouldering embers.
It was the leopard.
They all stood frozen in place, transfixed by those eyes of fire, when a single deep, baying cry echoed out from the woods. Martin shook like a leaf. “They missed her trail, and have set their muzzled bloodhounds on her scent. They’ll track her right here, find her and the buck. Goodbye, my friends. Martin Wittenhaagen’s life ends tonight.”
Gerard snatched Martin’s bow from the wall and pushed it into his hands. “Be a man!” he shouted. “Shoot the beast dead, drag her back into the woods before the hunters get here. No one will ever know.”
More hound voices joined the first, louder and closer by the second. “Curse that brute!” Martin roared, nocking an arrow. “I spared her once, now either she dies or I do, and likely both of us otherwise.” He drew the arrow all the way back, ready to loose.
“Nay! Stop!” Margaret screamed, lunging forward to grab the arrow. It snapped clean in two, the two halves clattering to the floor on either side of the bow. The baying of the hounds filled the whole house now, they were so close, hot on the scent.
“What have you done, girl?” Martin groaned. “You’ve put the hangman’s noose straight around my neck.”
“No!” Margaret cried, snatching his long hunting knife from his belt, yanking it almost out of its sheath. “I’ve saved you. Both of you, stand back from the window right now!” She darted out of the room, even as the hunters’ shouts and the baying of the hounds surrounded the little cottage, and the glowing green eyes of the wounded leopard never moved from the dark glass.
