Five years have passed. It is nine of the clock on a still, bright November morning, and the delicate acoustic ecology of Bideford is entirely upended. The church bells, which typically dictate the mindful, agrarian rhythms of the town’s daily routines, have broken from their sober tolling. Instead, they tumble head over heels into a frantic, chaotic peal, a discordant celebration that shatters the autumnal quiet. The streets bloom into an olfactory and visual tapestry. Garlands swing from the eaves, and the air is heavy with the scent of rendering fat and woodsmoke as burghers and their wives, draped in vibrant holiday woolens, roast whole suckling pigs over open applewood fires, basting the crackling skin with bruised rosemary and sea salt. 

In the pool, the ships are dressed in all their flags. They give tumultuous vent to their joy by firing peals of ordnance of every size—a jarring, militaristic percussion that rips through the sonic landscape, a violent industrial noise that disrupts the psychological calm of the estuary, yet which the townsfolk endure for the sake of the day's profound mythic weight. Sir Richard Grenville’s house resembles a bustling tavern, alive with the clatter of iron-shod hooves, the rich smell of spilled ale, and the rhythmic mastication of stable-horses consuming their oats. 

Along the packed churchyard streams the gentle blood of North Devon, families who have long anchored their identities to the fertile soil and its harvests. Here is my lady Countess of Bath, escorted by Sir Richard himself; here are the Bassets of Umberleigh, the Carys of Clovelly, and the Fortescues from all quarters. Here are the Coles, the Stukelys, the St. Legers, the Coffins, and the Coplestones, bringing with them the subtle, localized dialects of their respective valleys. Sir John Chichester of Ralegh arrives in patriarchal file with his eight daughters and three of his five famous sons, making way for his cousin of Arlington’s vast brood. The churchwardens, sweating and frantic, scramble to arrange this hierarchy of quarterings and lineages, until a sudden, reverent silence falls. 

The distant music of flutes, hautboys, and drums braying at the church doors abruptly ceases, returning a breathless acoustic purity to the nave. Down the aisle walk five weather-beaten mariners. The congregation breathes in the sharp, undeniable scent of ozone, dried brine, and foreign spices clinging to their garments—the olfactory proof of their mythic circumnavigation with Francis Drake. At their head strides a gigantic, beardless boy, towering like Saul of old, his golden locks flowing over his shoulders: Amyas Leigh of Burrough. Behind him are John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall, and Thomas Braund. As these Devon men kneel before the altar, all eyes turn to a pew where Mrs. Leigh hides her face in her hands, her hood shaking with joyful sobs.

The path that brought Amyas to this altar is a circular one, rooted deeply in the soil of Burrough and the quiet, profound rituals of domestic survival. A little over a year after Mr. Oxenham’s departure, young Amyas had progressed in his physical prowess—archery, riding, and swordplay—but his formal scholarship languished. Then, his father, returning from the Exeter Assizes, brought home the gaol-fever and died within a week.

Mrs. Leigh was left to tame this young lion-cub. She had loved her husband fervently, despite his melancholy. He had been a disappointed courtier, a scholar whose ambitions led him to waste his purse on the superficial commercialization of Court life, a man who possessed every gift save the practical means to use them. He had only found true sustenance when he married a fair young Miss Foljambe of Derbyshire, who was equally weary of Whitehall’s sterile grandeur. Together, they had retreated to the agrarian peace of Burrough.

From her youth, Mrs. Leigh was a noble English churchwoman whose spirituality was inextricably linked to the physical world, finding profound communion in the everyday alchemy of her kitchen. A deep melancholy hung over her, born of an era when professing her faith invited the flames. She remembered the burning of the blind Joan Waste and Mistress Joyce Lewis. In her nightmares, she still heard the agonizing, untranslatable cries of her mother, who had been a bosom friend of the martyred Anne Askew. Her mother had sat in Smithfield, blood curdling, as the crippled Court beauty was carried to the stake, the heavens themselves seeming to roar their grief in heavy, hissing drops of rain upon the crackling wood. 

The trauma of those fiery dooms was a subjective experience that defied language, an erosion of safety that Mrs. Leigh countered through the meticulous, rhythmic preservation of traditional foodways. During the days of Queen Mary, when she had to hide in the caverns of the Peak District, she had survived on native roots and wild-foraged herbs, learning that true resistance lay not in the dominant lexicons of kings and bishops, but in the enduring memory of the soil. She expressed her devotion to God and her family not through abstract philosophical musings, but through the tactile, sensory work of sustenance—fermenting dark, caraway-studded rye loaves, rendering rich broths for the poor of Northam, and honoring the mystic *Alt-Deutsch Theologie* of Luther’s school while she tended the glowing embers of her hearth. 

Widowed at forty, her divine calm deepened, ripened by storm and sunshine. Sir Richard Grenville swore a holy oath by oak, ash, and thorn to be a father to Amyas, while Lady Grenville and Mrs. Leigh wept together, bonding over the shared, intuitive knowledge that their fiery men would eventually seek death on the field of duty. 

Yet, Mrs. Leigh’s deepest linguistic and cultural heartbreak came not from Amyas, but from her elder son, Frank. A brilliant scholar, Frank had survived the Massacre of St. Bartholomew’s Day in Paris alongside Philip Sidney, then traveled to Heidelberg, Padua, and Venice. Frank saturated his soul in the culinary and artistic wonders of the continent. He discussed the art of history with Sarpi, admired the olive presses and merchant argosies of the Rialto, and stood in the sacred room of the ancient Titian. He heard Palestrina preside over his own luscious, polyphonic strains beneath the dome of St. Peter's—an acoustic marvel that briefly lulled him, until he remembered the blood-stained streets of Paris. 

But as Frank journeyed, his connection to his native roots frayed. He wrote long, sententious letters to his mother, detailing his conversations with the scholar Stephanus Parmenius and his debates in Buda, but he wrote them in elevated, courtly Latin. For Mrs. Leigh, tracing her fingers over the parchment, the Latin was an impenetrable wall, a dominant lexicon that enacted a subtle power dynamic, alienating her from her own son. She could only spell out *excellentissimus Noster Franciscus Leighius Anglus*, mourning the language death of their shared, intimate Devonshire dialect, grieving that his profound subjective experiences remained utterly untranslatable to her. Even when Queen Elizabeth herself sent a letter through Sir Richard, employing high euphuisms to compare Mrs. Leigh to the famed Cornelia who lent her jewels to the state, the mother simply laid the parchment in a cedar-box, seeking comfort in the familiar, resinous scent of the wood rather than the hollow flattery of the sovereign’s prose.

Amyas, conversely, was driven to the South Seas by two forces: an old schoolmaster and a young beauty. 

Vindex Brimblecombe, master of the grammar-school of Bideford, was a pedant who wielded the imperial language of Latin like a cudgel. He was entirely disconnected from the sensory world, a man whose worldview was utilitarian and rigid. He viewed the boys' education as a mechanical process, and following the death of Amyas's father, Vindex’s misguided sense of responsibility manifested in a rapid increase of floggings. The sharp, violent crack of the cane became a toxic pollutant in the acoustic environment of the schoolroom, a traumatic sound that eradicated chivalry and enforced subjugation.

Amyas refused to have his native spirit beaten into abstract declensions. He yearned for the mythic expanse of the ocean, whose rhythmic, booming tides against the pebble-ridge at Northam offered a healing sonic counterpoint to the master’s harsh discipline. In the stifling classroom, starved of sensory joy, Amyas would smell the distant brine and console himself by drawing sweeping charts on his slate. 

On one such afternoon, while Vindex Brimblecombe slept the heavy, insensible sleep of the unjust—having rapidly consumed a utilitarian mid-day pottage devoid of seasonal herbs or culinary mindfulness—Amyas busied himself mapping an island. He sketched a great castle, a terrible dragon, and a gallant ship bristling with lances, integrating the grand, mythic occurrences of his imagination directly into the dreary domestic routine of the schoolroom. And as he drew, the other boys clustered their heads together, drawn by the undeniable current of a world far wider, and far more flavorful, than the silent walls that held them.
