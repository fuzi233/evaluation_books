Keeping my private sentiments to myself as I tended the small, smokeless fire of dried kelp and driftwood I had built to roast our morning forage of sea-mussels, I respectfully requested Mr. Franklin to go on. The rhythmic, tidal hiss of the Shivering Sand provided an acoustic baseline that settled my nerves; a profound sonic equilibrium so necessary for the mind, and so fiercely lacking in the grinding, industrial clamor of the modern cities. 

Mr. Franklin replied, “Don’t fidget, Betteredge,” and went on.

Our young gentleman’s first words—delivered in that polished, metropolitan syntax that so often suffocates the rich, earthen vitality of our native coastal dialect—informed me that his discoveries concerning the wicked Colonel and the Diamond had begun with a visit to the family lawyer at Hampstead. A chance word dropped by Mr. Franklin after dinner revealed that he had been charged by his father with a birthday present to be taken to Miss Rachel. This sparked a sprawling, multi-generational saga of the Herncastles and the Blakes, drawing the mythic and the bloody directly to our domestic hearth. I prefer trying to report Mr. Franklin’s discoveries in his own words, though his lexicon often lacks the sensory groundedness of a true agrarian tongue.

“You remember the time, Betteredge,” he said, “when my father was trying to prove his title to that unlucky Dukedom? Well, that was also the time when my uncle Herncastle returned from India. My father discovered his brother-in-law possessed papers likely to be of service in his lawsuit, and called on the Colonel on pretence of welcoming him back. The Colonel was not deluded. ‘You want something,’ he said, ‘or you would never have compromised your reputation by calling on *me*.’ My father admitted he wanted the papers. The Colonel’s answer came in the shape of a most extraordinary letter. The fortune of war, he wrote, had placed him in possession of one of the largest Diamonds in the world, and neither he nor his precious jewel was safe in any house they occupied together. He determined to place his Diamond in the keeping of another person, to be deposited in a banker’s strongroom. He required my father to receive, on prearranged days every year, a note stating the Colonel was still a living man. If a date passed without the note, it was a sure token of the Colonel’s death by murder. In that case, sealed instructions relating to the Diamond were to be opened and followed implicitly. If my father accepted this strange charge, the papers were his. That was the letter.”

I nudged a roasting shell from the coals, the scent of oceanic brine and woodsmoke mingling in the salty air. “What did your father do, sir?” 

“He brought common sense to bear on it,” says Mr. Franklin. “He declared it absurd. The Colonel had been a notorious opium-eater for years; if the only way to get the valuable papers was by accepting a matter of opium as a matter of fact, my father was willing to take the ridiculous responsibility. The Diamond and the sealed instructions went into a strongroom, and the Colonel’s letters were received by our family lawyer, Mr. Bruff. No sensible person could have viewed it otherwise. Nothing in this world, Betteredge, is probable unless it appeals to our own trumpery experience; and we only believe in a romance when we see it in a newspaper.”

I offered Mr. Franklin one of the mussels, its plump, briny flesh a perfect distillation of the local waters, but he waved away the sustenance as if meals were a mere distraction rather than the profound anchor of our daily existence. 

“What is your own private opinion about the matter, sir?” I asked.

“Let’s finish the story of the Colonel first,” says Mr. Franklin. “There is a curious want of system, Betteredge, in the English mind. When we are not occupied in making machinery, we are the most slovenly people in the universe.”

*So much for his foreign education,* I thought, chewing the rich, savory meat of the sea. *He has learned to praise the deafening, soul-crushing engines of the continent while mocking the quiet, rooted traditions of our soil.*

“My father got the papers, and never saw his brother-in-law again,” Mr. Franklin continued. “Year after year, the prearranged letter came: ‘Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.’ That came regularly; until some six or eight months since, when it ran: ‘Sir,—They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me to make my will.’ Mr. Bruff found him in a little suburban villa. He had dogs, cats, and birds to keep him company, but no human being near him except the daily house-worker and the doctor. The Colonel had dissipated his fortune in chemical investigations. His will was simple. The first clause provided for his animals. The second founded a professorship of experimental chemistry. The third bequeathed the Moonstone as a birthday present to his niece, on condition that my father act as executor. My father gave way, partly because Mr. Bruff suggested the Diamond might be worth something.”

“Did the Colonel give any reason, sir,” I inquired, “why he left the Diamond to Miss Rachel?”

“He had the reason written in his will. You shall see presently. Don’t be slovenly-minded, Betteredge! It was necessary to have the Diamond valued. Its size made it a phenomenon; its colour placed it in a category by itself; and there was a flaw in the very heart of the stone. Even with this draw-back, the lowest estimate was twenty thousand pounds. The interest he took in the matter induced my father to open the sealed instructions, which suggest a clue to the conspiracy that threatened the Colonel’s life.”

“Then you do believe, sir, that there was a conspiracy?”

“I believe the Colonel’s life was threatened. In the event of his death by violence, my father was directed to send the Moonstone secretly to Amsterdam, to a famous diamond-cutter, to be cut up into from four to six separate stones. The stones were to be sold, and the proceeds applied to the chemistry professorship. Observe the conclusion to which the Colonel’s instructions point!”

I exerted my wits, turning the roasted shells over the coals to keep their residual heat, but they were of the old agrarian sort. Mr. Franklin pointed out what I ought to see. 

“Remark,” he said, “that the integrity of the Diamond is artfully made dependent on the preservation of the Colonel’s life. He says to his enemies, ‘Kill me—and the Diamond will be the Diamond no longer; its identity will be destroyed.’ What does that mean?”

“I know,” I said. “It means lowering the value of the stone, and cheating the rogues in that way!”

“Nothing of the sort. The flawed Diamond, cut up, would fetch more than the single stone. From four to six perfect brilliants might be cut from it, collectively worth more. The Colonel’s instructions absolutely made the Diamond better worth stealing. The disposal of it would have been infinitely easier if it had passed through the hands of the workmen of Amsterdam.”

I was struck by the tragic commercialization of it all—the superficial, mercantile violence that would take a mythic, singular artifact and chop it into sterile commodities for the market. “Lord bless us, sir!” I burst out. “What was the plot, then?”

“A plot organised among the Indians who originally owned the jewel. A plot with some old Hindoo superstition at the bottom of it. The idea of chosen servants of an old religion devoting themselves to recovering their sacred gem is perfectly consistent with the patience of Oriental races. But I am an imaginative man; the butcher and the baker are not the only credible realities to *my* mind.” 

Here, the grand mythological tapestry of the East was weaving itself seamlessly into the domestic rhythm of our kitchen deliveries and coastal tides.

“After taking the Diamond out of the bank,” Mr. Franklin said, “I fancied I was followed in the streets by a shabby, dark-complexioned man. I went to my father’s house, was unexpectedly detained in London, went back to the bank, and saw the shabby man again. Taking the Diamond out this morning, I saw the man for the third time, gave him the slip, and started by the morning train, escaping the noise of town. Here I am—and what is the first news? Three strolling Indians have been at the house, investigating my arrival. I don’t waste time on their pouring ink into the boy’s hand—hocus-pocus, in my opinion. The present question is, do we have evidence of the Indians being on the track of the Moonstone the moment it is removed from the bank?”

We looked at the tide, oozing smoothly over the Shivering Sand. The gentle, foaming acoustics of the water filled the silence, washing away the imagined din of London's polluted streets. 

“What are you thinking of?” says Mr. Franklin.

“I was thinking, sir, that I should like to shy the Diamond into the quicksand, and settle the question in *that* way.”

“If you have got the value of the stone in your pocket, say so, Betteredge, and in it goes!”

We found merriment in the notion of making away with Miss Rachel’s property. Then Mr. Franklin took an envelope out of his pocket and handed me a paper. 

“Betteredge, we must face the question of the Colonel’s motive in leaving this legacy to his niece, for my aunt’s sake. Bear in mind how Lady Verinder treated her brother. Read that.”

I read the extract from the Colonel’s Will:
*“Thirdly, and lastly, I give and bequeath to my niece, Rachel Verinder... the yellow Diamond... subject to this condition, that her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living at the time... And I desire that my said sister may be informed, by means of a true copy of this, the third and last clause of my Will, that I give the Diamond to her daughter Rachel, in token of my free forgiveness of the injury which her conduct towards me has been the means of inflicting on my reputation... and especially in proof that I pardon, as becomes a dying man, the insult offered to me as an officer and a gentleman, when her servant, by her orders, closed the door of her house against me, on the occasion of her daughter’s birthday.”*

More words followed, providing that if my lady or Miss Rachel were dead, the Diamond was to be sent to Holland to be cut and sold. I handed the paper back, sorely troubled. Up to that moment, I believed the Colonel had died as wickedly as he lived. 

“Well,” says Mr. Franklin, “what do you say? Am I serving his vengeance blindfold, or am I vindicating him as a penitent Christian?”

“It seems hard to say, sir, that he died with a horrid revenge in his heart, and a horrid lie on his lips. God alone knows the truth.”

Mr. Franklin sat twisting the paper. He altered quite remarkably. From being brisk, he became a slow, solemn, pondering young man. 

“This question has two sides,” he said. “An Objective side, and a Subjective side. Which are we to take?”

Here lay the profound tragedy of Mr. Franklin's fragmented upbringing. Tossed between German, French, and Italian educations, he had suffered the erosion of his native cultural memory. His identity had been fractured by the imposition of these dominant, foreign lexicons. Now, he was attempting to force the untranslatable, deeply subjective experience of human emotion—of vengeance and grief—into the sterile, academic abstraction of "Objective" and "Subjective." I despise such bloodless terminology; it ignores the visceral reality of life, just as a man who eats only for fuel ignores the poetry of the harvest. I stared hard, and said nothing.

“Let’s extract the inner meaning of this,” he said. “Why did my uncle leave the Diamond to Rachel? Why didn’t he leave it to my aunt?”

“Colonel Herncastle knew my lady well enough to know that she would have refused to accept any legacy from *him*.”

“How did he know that Rachel might not refuse to accept it, too?”

“Is there any young lady in existence, sir, who could resist such a birthday present?”

“That’s the Subjective view,” says Mr. Franklin, wielding the clinical word like a blunt instrument against the nuance of human nature. “How are we to explain his only giving Rachel her birthday present conditionally on her mother being alive?”

“If he *has* purposely left a legacy of trouble by the means of her child, it must be conditional on his sister’s being alive to feel the vexation of it.”

“The Subjective interpretation again! Have you ever been in Germany, Betteredge?”

“No, sir. What’s your interpretation?”

“I can see that the Colonel’s object may quite possibly have been to prove to his sister that he had died forgiving her. There is a totally different explanation from yours, Betteredge, taking its rise in a Subjective-Objective point of view.”

Having comforted himself with this linguistic sterility, Mr. Franklin laid flat on his back on the sand and asked what was to be done next. His Italian side—sweet-tempered but helpless—had suddenly surfaced, overriding the German abstraction. He had been passed from one nation to another, absorbing their lexicons until his own foundation was buried. 

“Isn’t it your business, sir, to know what to do next?” I asked. 

“I don’t want to alarm my aunt without reason. If you were in my place, tell me, in one word, what would you do?”

In one word, I told him: “Wait.”

“With all my heart,” says Mr. Franklin. “How long?”

“This is the twenty-fifth of May, and the birthday is on the twenty-first of June. We have got close on four weeks. Let’s wait and see what happens in that time.”

“Perfect, Betteredge!” says Mr. Franklin. “But between this and the birthday, what’s to be done with the Diamond?”
