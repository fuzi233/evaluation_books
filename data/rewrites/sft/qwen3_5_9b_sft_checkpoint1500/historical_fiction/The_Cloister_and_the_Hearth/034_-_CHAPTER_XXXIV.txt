"Where are the righteous men?"

"Here stand we. God bless ye all!"

A sudden rush surged toward the staircase, where six sturdy, yet kindly, hands extended to grasp the rescuers warmly.

"You've saved our lives, lads," cried Denys, his voice thick with emotion. "You've saved us tonight."

Before the eyes of the rescued pair, the scene unfolded as a vision from hell. The chamber blazed with torchlight, illuminating the gleaming breastplates of the archers and their bronze-complexioned faces. Beside them lay the pale, bound forms of the thieves and the massive corpse of the giant, spilled in its own gore.

Gerard moved slowly around the line of soldiers, shaking hands with each one, his eyes glistening with unshed tears. In return, they all bowed their heads to kiss him. Turning to a handsome archer of similar age, Gerard spoke softly, "Pray, good soldier, watch over me. A heavy drowsiness overtakes me. See that no one cuts my throat while I sleep—for pity's sake."

The archer laughed, assuming Gerard jest, and promised faithfully. Almost immediately, Gerard sank into a deep slumber.

Denys observed this silence with surprise but refrained from interference; it served his immediate purpose. Nearby, two archers were examining the Abbot's lifeless form, kicking it gently aside to inquire, "Which of the two cast this enormous rogue from the upper story?" They sought to understand the mechanics of the fall.

At first, Denys scoffed, unwilling to boast. He feared that the young vagabond would later claim it was the finger of Heaven, making him look a fool. But seeing Gerard unconscious, Denys decided to speak.

"Well, comrades, you see," Denys began, his tone shifting to a rough, storytelling cadence, "my sword pierced this one right through to the hilt. Others swarmed about me, so I needed my blade or I perished. Thus, I planted my foot against his belly, gave a sharp tug with my hand, leaped, and sent him flying to kingdom come! He dropped in mid-air, and his carcass rolled onto you without ceremony—making quite a jump, I warrant. But pikewives and pillage! What use is chatter once the spoils are gone? Let us drink, friends! *Buvons*, camarades!"

The archers noted that saying "drink" was easy where no liquor existed, though doing so was harder.

"Nay, I shall find you drink quickly," Denys declared. "My nose has a natural alacrity for scenting wine. Follow me, and I shall lead the way with this torch!"

They departed the room, descending a short flight of stone steps into a vast, low cellar. It smelled close and dank. The walls were encrusted here and there with what appeared to be cobwebs, but proved to be saltpeter oozing from the damp stones and crystallizing.

"Oh! the fine moldy smell," murmured Denys. "In such places, good wine still lurks. Advance your torch."

"Diable!" exclaimed Denys suddenly. "What is that in the corner? A pile of rags?"

"No," replied the torch-bearer, stepping closer. "'Tis a man."

They gathered around the flickering flame. Lo! a figure crouched on a heap in the corner, pale as ash and shivering violently.

"Why, it is the landlord," Denys stated.

"Rise, thou craven heart!" shouted an archer.

"Why, men, the thieves are bound, and we who bound them are thirsty. Rise! Show us your wine; I see no bottles here."

"What, are the rascals bound?" the pale landlord stammered. "Good news... Wine? Yes, I will, honest sirs."

He rose with unsteady joints, offering to guide them to the wine cellar. But Denys interposed sharply. "You are all in the dark, comrades. This man is in league with the thieves."

"Alack, good soldier, I in league with the accursed robbers! Is that reasonable?"

"The girl said so anyway."

"The girl! What girl? Ah! Curse her, traitress!"

"Well," interrupted another archer, "the girl is not here; she fled to the bailiff. So let the burghers decide if this coward is guilty or not. We caught him not in the act, so let him draw us our wine."

"One moment," said Denys shrewdly. "Why did he curse the girl? If he were a true man, he would bless her as we do."

"Alas, sir!" sobbed the landlord. "I live only by my good name. I cursed her because you said she had lied about me."

"Humph! I trow thou art a thief, and where is the thief who cannot lie with a smooth face?" Denys commanded. "Seize him, comrades. A prisoner may draw wine even if his hands are bound."

The landlord offered no objection, instead hoping they would pay for whatever they drank, noting his rent was overdue by two months.

The archers smiled grimly at his simplicity. One laid a quiet, firm hand on his shoulder, while the other, bearing the torch, stepped forward.

As they reached the threshold, Denys cried, "Halt!"

"What is it?"

"Here be bottles in this corner. Advance your light."

The torch-bearer approached. Having just removed his scabbard, he probed the heap where the landlord had crouched.

"Nay, nay!" cried the landlord. "The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing here."

"Nothing is mighty hard, then," said Denys. Drawing his hand from the heap, he produced something.

It was merely a bone.

Denys tossed it onto the floor; it rattled harshly.

"There is naught there but the bones of the house," insisted the landlord.

"Just now 'twas nothing," Denys countered. "Now that we have found something, 'tis nothing but bones. Here is another. Humph? Look at this, comrade, and you, bring the smooth knave along."

Philippe, the torch-bearer, held the bone to the light, turning it over and over.

"Well?" asked Denys.

"Well, if this were a battlefield, I'd say 'twas a man's shankbone. No more, no less. But 'tis not a battlefield, nor a churchyard; 'tis an inn."

"True, mate; but that knave's ashen face shines brighter than any field of battle. I read the truth by it. Bring that face nearer, I say. When the chine is missing and the house dog cannot look at you without his tail creeping between his legs—who was the thief? Good brothers, my mind misgives me. The deeper I dig, the more I find. Mayhap if these bones could speak, true men's flesh would creep."

"Alas! Young man, what hideous fancies are these!" protested the landlord. "These are bones of cattle, sheep, and kids, not men and women. Holy saints preserve us!"

"Hold thy peace!" Denys roared. "Thy words are air. Thou hast not got burghers by the ear, those who know not a veal knuckle from their grandsire's ribs. These are soldiers-men seeking their fallen comrades, only to find their bones picked clean by crows—and perhaps by thee and thy mates. Men and women, saidst thou? And prithee, when did I speak of women's bones? Thou wouldst make a child suspect thee. Field of battle, comrade! Was not this house a field of battle half an hour ago? Drag him close, let me read his face."

Denys thrust a small object suddenly into the landlord's face.

"Alas! I know not."

"I would not swear either—but it looks too like the thumb bone of a man's hand. Mates, my flesh creeps. Churchyard! How do I know this is not one?"

Denys drew his sword and began raking the heap of earth, broken crockery, and bones across the floor.

The landlord assured him he wasted time. "We poor innkeepers are sinners," he pleaded. "We give short measure and baptize the wine; we are fain to do these things because the laws are so unjust. But we are not assassins. How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven's lightning strike me dead if there be any bones save those used for meat. 'Tis the kitchen wench who flings them here. I swear by God's holy mother, by Saint Paul, by Saint Dominic, and by Saint Denys, my patron saint—"

"Ach!"

Denys held a bone steadily before the landlord's eye in dead silence. It was a bone no man, however ignorant or lying, could confuse with those of sheep or oxen. The sight silenced the lying lips and paralyzed the heartless heart.

The landlord's hair rose on his scalp like spikes, and his knees buckled as if his limbs had been severed. The archers dragged him upright under the glare of the torch, staring fascinated at the dead skull, white as the living cheek opposite it, glaring back at its murderer. The assassin's pale lips opened and closed, but no sound escaped.
