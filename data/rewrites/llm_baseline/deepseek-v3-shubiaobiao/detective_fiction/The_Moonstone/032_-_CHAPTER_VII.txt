The events following Lady Verinder’s death left little room for sentiment. A month had passed before I saw Rachel again—this time under the same roof, where an unexpected turn in her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite demanded recording. With this final disclosure, my account of these painful affairs will be complete.

Lady Verinder was laid to rest in the quiet churchyard on her estate. Though invited, I could not bring myself to attend—not only due to the shock of her passing but also because the rector of Frizinghall, a man I had once seen playing cards at her whist table, was to officiate. Some lines, even in grief, cannot be crossed.

Rachel, now under the guardianship of Mr. Ablewhite the elder, proved difficult to settle. The London house was tainted by her mother’s death, Yorkshire by the scandal of the Moonstone, and Frizinghall was deemed too lively for mourning. Brighton, then, was chosen—a furnished house, where Rachel, her invalid cousin, and Aunt Ablewhite would reside, with Mr. Godfrey shuttling between them and London.

I was summoned to assist in hiring servants—a task I undertook with solemn diligence. My aunt, a woman of remarkable passivity, greeted me with her usual helplessness. “Drusilla, dear, I want some servants. You are so clever—please get them for me.” Resistance was futile.

In the adjoining room, Rachel stood by the window, frail in her mourning. To my astonishment, she approached me with an apology. “I have spoken foolishly to you before,” she said. “I hope you can forgive me.” Her reasoning—that she now sought comfort among her mother’s friends—was misguided, but it revealed an opening. If sentiment had stirred her conscience, conviction might yet take root.

Testing the waters, I probed delicately about her engagement. She evaded the subject, but her manner had softened from the defiance of our last meeting. Here was fertile ground for guidance.

Brighton was secured. I prepared meticulously—selecting servants of sound character, securing church sittings, and placing edifying literature in Rachel’s path. Doctrine would surround her at every turn.

Yet when the party arrived, Mr. Godfrey was absent, replaced by the lawyer Mr. Bruff. His presence unsettled me. That evening, after Rachel retired early with a headache, I sensed his influence. The next day, she walked out with him—returning pensive, almost transformed. Bruff departed with a meaningful glance, leaving Rachel locked in her room, refusing dinner.

The following morning, she confessed over tea: “I shall never marry Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.” The words struck like a thunderclap. Before I could press further, she dismissed me.

At breakfast, she was silent, restless. She played scandalous music—worldly distractions I allowed for the moment. When I returned from visiting friends, I found Godfrey himself in the dining room, unnervingly composed.

“Rachel has ended our engagement,” he said, as calmly as if discussing the weather. “She believes it best for us both.”

I stared. “What does it mean?”

He smiled—that same effortless charm. “Let me explain.”

And with that, the trap was set—though whether for Rachel, for Godfrey, or for myself, I could not yet say.
