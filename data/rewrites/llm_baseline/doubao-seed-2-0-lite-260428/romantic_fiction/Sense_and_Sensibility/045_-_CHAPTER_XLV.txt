Long after Willoughby had hurried out the door, long after the crunch of his carriage wheels faded completely down the gravel drive, Elinor stood frozen, her chest crammed full of warring feelings, all tangled together under a thick blanket of sadness that made her forget even to check on Marianne for several minutes. Less than an hour before, she had loathed that man as the most worthless, unforgivable scoundrel walking the earth – but now, for all his cruel choices, all the harm he had inflicted on her family, the raw, ragged confession of his suffering stoked a sharp, unshakable pity in her. She watched him vanish from their lives forever, and the soft, aching regret she felt for him was far more tied to the bright, foolish, ardent passion he still carried for Marianne than to any merit he could claim. She knew it was irrational: his unfair hold on her mood was stoked by nothing more than his rare, easy charm, his open, laughing warmth that cost him nothing to show, and that wild, hungry love for her sister that should never have been allowed to burn so long. But no matter how hard she tried to reason it away, the thought of him lingered, heavy in her bones.

When she finally slipped back upstairs to Marianne’s bedchamber, she found her sister just waking, refreshed by a long, deep, fever-breaking sleep that was exactly what the doctor had ordered. Elinor’s heart was so full it ached: the messy shock of Willoughby’s visit, the relief of Marianne’s safety, the imminence of their mother’s arrival all swirled together into a buzz of frantic energy that chased every trace of her own exhaustion away. She only feared she would burst into tears and give away all her turmoil to her still-fragile sister – but that fear was gone in less than half an hour, when the rumble of a second carriage rumbled up the drive.

Elinor raced for the hall before the footman could even reach the front door, desperate to spare her mother a single extra second of the horrifying suspense she must have endured on the journey. She threw the door open just in time to catch Mrs. Dashwood as she stumbled over the threshold, her face white with terror, so convinced Marianne was dead that she could not even find her voice to ask after her, could not even greet Elinor. Elinor poured the full joyful news out in one breathless rush, and Mrs. Dashwood, overcome, sobbed so hard she could not speak for minutes, hugging Elinor again and again, before turning to press Colonel Brandon’s hand tight, her face bright with unspoken gratitude that he had ridden all that way to fetch her, that he shared every ounce of her relief. He said nothing at all, his eyes glistening, his quiet joy even deeper than her own.

As soon as she could breathe steadily again, Mrs. Dashwood begged to see Marianne, and two minutes later she was at her daughter’s bedside, clinging to her as if she might slip away again, the girl made more precious than ever by her recent brush with death. Elinor watched their reunion with a soft, bright delight, only held back by the fear that the excitement would tire Marianne back into illness – but Mrs. Dashwood was calm, prudent, every bit the mother whose first priority was her child’s life, and Marianne, weak as she was, was happy to agree to the quiet rest the nurses insisted on. Mrs. Dashwood swore she would sit up with her the whole night, and only after repeated entreaties did Elinor agree to go to bed herself.

But even after a full night without sleep and days of agonizing anxiety, Elinor could not rest. Her mind spun endlessly back to Willoughby, to the point where she let herself whisper “poor Willoughby” under her breath, glad she had heard his side of the story at last, torn now between blaming herself for judging him too harshly and dreading the moment she would have to repeat his confession to Marianne. She feared what it would do to her sister’s fragile heart, wondered if after hearing how deeply he still loved her, Marianne could ever be happy with another man – for one wild, unguarded second, she even found herself wishing Willoughby’s wife would vanish. The thought made her burn with shame a heartbeat later, when she remembered Colonel Brandon’s years of silent suffering, his unwavering constancy. She knew his quiet devotion deserved Marianne far more than Willoughby’s selfish, reckless passion ever could, and she quickly amended her foolish wish, wanting anything but harm to come to Mrs. Willoughby.

The errand that had brought Colonel Brandon rushing to Barton had not hit Mrs. Dashwood nearly as hard as he had expected. She had already been frantic with worry over Marianne long before he arrived, so certain something was very wrong that she had already decided to leave for Cleveland that very day, no further updates needed. She had even arranged to send Margaret away to the Careys’ house to keep her safe from infection, with their carriage due to arrive any minute, before Brandon had even knocked at her door.

Marianne improved steadily every single day after that, and Mrs. Dashwood glowed so bright with joy she repeatedly declared herself the happiest woman alive. Elinor could not help but wonder, as she watched her mother’s giddy spirits, if she had spared even a single thought for the disappointment she had borne on Elinor’s behalf, about Edward Ferrars. But Mrs. Dashwood, lulled by Elinor’s gentle, understated account of her heartbreak, was too wrapped up in Marianne’s recovery to dwell on anything that might dim her joy. She had even begun to quietly blame herself: it was her own foolish, enthusiastic encouragement of Marianne’s attachment to Willoughby that had almost gotten her daughter killed. And soon enough, she had a new secret joy to share, the moment she and Elinor were alone together.

“At last we have a moment to ourselves, my Elinor,” she said, squeezing her daughter’s hands, grinning so wide her cheeks ached. “You don’t know how happy I truly am. Colonel Brandon is in love with Marianne. He told me so himself, on the ride over.”

Elinor felt a strange jumble of emotion, half pleased, half pained, half surprised and half as if she had known this truth for months, and she said nothing, waiting to hear more. “You are always so much calmer than I am,” Mrs. Dashwood laughed. “If I had sat down to wish for the best possible husband any of my girls could ever have, it would be Colonel Brandon. I truly believe Marianne will be happier with him than she ever would have been with Willoughby.”

Elinor half wanted to ask her what impartial logic led her to that conclusion, but she knew her mother would get carried away by her own fancy as soon as the topic was interesting, so she only smiled and said nothing.

“He poured his whole heart out to me when we were travelling, completely unplanned,” Mrs. Dashwood went on. “I was talking of nothing but Marianne, of course, and he could not hide how deeply he suffered for her. He must have known the world would think such fierce sympathy was more than mere friendship, and he could not hold it back any longer. He has loved her, my Elinor, from the very first moment he laid eyes on her.”

Elinor recognized that her mother was embroidering the truth a little, adding her own warm fancies to Brandon’s quiet, understated confession, but she did not correct her.
“His love for her is so far deeper, truer, steadier than anything Willoughby ever felt or pretended to feel,” Mrs. Dashwood said, her voice soft with reverence. “He has loved her through every single day of her infatuation with that worthless boy, never selfish, never pushing for hope, ready to see her happy with anyone else. There is no man on earth more noble, more sincere than he is.”

“Colonel Brandon’s character as a thoroughly excellent man is well established by everyone who knows him,” Elinor agreed.
“Of course it is!” her mother said, serious now. “If I had any doubt of that after the danger Marianne just faced, I would never dream of encouraging his affection. But the way he leapt to ride all the way to Barton for me, no hesitation, no complaint, that alone proves he is one of the worthiest men alive.”

“His good name does not rest on that one kind act,” Elinor said. “Mrs. Jennings and the Middletons have loved and respected him for years, and even I, who have only known him a short time, hold him in the highest esteem. If Marianne can be happy with him, I will be just as overjoyed as you are. What did you tell him? Did you give him reason to hope?”

“Oh, my dear, I could not speak of hope then, when Marianne was at death’s door!” Mrs. Dashwood said. “He did not come begging for permission to court her, he just poured out a secret he could not hold in any longer to a friend he trusted. But once we knew Marianne was out of danger, I told him that if she lived, my greatest wish in the world would be to see them married, and I have given him every encouragement I possibly can. A little time, that is all he needs! Marianne’s heart was never meant to waste away forever on a man as unworthy as Willoughby. Brandon’s good, gentle merits will win her over soon enough.”

“From what I have seen of his spirits, he is not nearly so sanguine about that as you are,” Elinor said gently.

“No, he thinks her attachment to Willoughby is far too deeply rooted to fade for many years,” Mrs. Dashwood agreed. “He even fears that even if her heart was free, their difference in age and temperament would make it impossible for her to ever return his feelings. But he is entirely wrong! His age is nothing but an advantage, it has made his character steady, his principles firm, and his disposition is exactly what Marianne needs to be truly happy. He is not as striking to look at as Willoughby, I grant that, but there is far more warmth and kindness in his face – and you must remember, there was always a sharp, unkind glint in Willoughby’s eyes that I never liked.”

Elinor could not recall any such glint, but her mother did not pause for her to agree. “Brandon’s manners, his soft attentiveness to everyone around him, his quiet, unassuming sincerity – that is far more suited to Marianne’s true nature than Willoughby’s flashy, often rude, ill-timed liveliness. I am fully convinced that even if Willoughby had turned out to be as good a man as he claimed, Marianne still never would have been half as happy with him as she will be as Colonel Brandon’s wife.”

She paused, grinning wider. “And at Delaford, he lives close enough that even if we stay at Barton, we will be within easy visiting distance. The village is large enough, there must be some small cottage nearby that would suit us perfectly, just as well as our current home.”

Poor Elinor – her mother had already begun planning to move the whole family to be close to the new couple, before Marianne had even spoken a single kind word to Brandon since her recovery. But she did not protest.
“And his fortune, of course,” Mrs. Dashwood added, almost sheepish. “At my age, you understand, one does not dismiss that kind of practicality. I do not know exactly how large it is, and I do not care to ask, but I know it must be more than enough to keep her comfortable for the rest of her life.”

A knock on the door interrupted them before Elinor could reply. She stepped away to her own room to think it all over, wishing with all her heart that the quiet, loyal Colonel would get the happy ending he so richly deserved – even as a soft, unshakable twinge of sad pity for the reckless, ruined Willoughby lingered, tucked away in the back of her mind.
