Marianne, who possessed little patience for anything resembling impudence, vulgarity, mediocrity, or merely differing tastes, was currently in such a fragile state of spirit that she was entirely incapable of finding pleasure in the presence of the Miss Steeles or encouraging their overtures. Consequently, her behavior toward them remained consistently cool, effectively halting any attempt at intimacy on their part. It was largely due to this unwavering reserve that Elinor observed a distinct shift in the sisters' demeanor; they began to favor her specifically, a preference that grew increasingly obvious, particularly in Lucy's actions. Lucy seized every opportunity to engage Elinor in conversation, striving to deepen their acquaintance through an air of ease and frankness.

Lucy was naturally quick-witted; her observations were often astute and amusing, and as a companion for thirty minutes, she could prove genuinely agreeable. However, her intellect had received no cultivation from formal education. She was ignorant and unlettered, and her lack of mental refinement, coupled with a deficit of general knowledge, could not be hidden from Miss Dashwood despite Elinor's best efforts to maintain a favorable impression. Elinor viewed Lucy's wasted potential with pity, mourning the talents that education might have dignified. Yet, she regarded Lucy's utter lack of delicacy, moral uprightness, and integrity with far less compassion. These deficiencies were glaringly exposed by Lucy's relentless attentions, her persistence, and her flattery at the Park. Elinor found no lasting satisfaction in the company of someone who combined insincerity with ignorance; whose lack of instruction prevented them from conversing as equals, and whose treatment of others rendered any display of deference toward herself utterly hollow.

"You will probably find my question peculiar," Lucy remarked one day as they strolled from the park toward the cottage. "But pray, are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law's mother, Mrs. Ferrars?"

Elinor *did* consider the query highly unusual, and her expression reflected this as she replied that she had never met Mrs. Ferrars.

"Indeed!" exclaimed Lucy. "I am surprised by that, for I assumed you must have encountered her occasionally at Norland. Then, perhaps, you cannot describe her character?"

"No," Elinor responded, carefully withholding her true opinion of Edward's mother and disinclined to satisfy what appeared to be idle curiosity. "I know nothing of her."

"I am certain you think me strangely inquisitive for asking in such a manner," Lucy said, watching Elinor intently. "Perhaps there are reasons... I hope I may venture; but regardless, I trust you will grant me the justice of believing I have no intention of being rude."

Elinor offered a polite response, and they walked in silence for several minutes until Lucy broke it, hesitating slightly. "I cannot bear for you to view me as curiously intrusive. I would sooner endure anything in the world than lose your good opinion, which is so valuable to me. I would gladly trust you; indeed, I would welcome your counsel regarding such an uncomfortable position as mine. However, there is no need to trouble you. I am simply sorry you do not happen to know Mrs. Ferrars."

"I am sorry I do *not*," Elinor said, taken aback. "If knowing my opinion of her could benefit *you*, I regret it. But truly, I never realized you had any connection with that family, which makes this serious inquiry into her character somewhat surprising."

"I suppose you are right, and I do not wonder at your reaction. But if I were to reveal everything, you would not be so astonished. Mrs. Ferrars is certainly irrelevant to me at present—but the future *may* bring changes. How quickly that arrives depends entirely on her; eventually, we may become intimately connected." She lowered her gaze, feigning bashfulness, casting only a fleeting glance at her companion to gauge her reaction.

"Good heavens!" cried Elinor. "What do you mean? Are you acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars? Could it be?" The prospect of such a sister-in-law held little appeal for her.

"No," Lucy replied. "Not Mr. *Robert* Ferrars—I have never seen him in my life; but..." She fixed her eyes upon Elinor. "...to his eldest brother."

What surged through Elinor at that moment was a profound astonishment, sharp and intense, tempered only by an immediate skepticism of the claim. She turned to Lucy in silent bewilderment, unable to fathom the motive behind such a declaration. Though her complexion flushed, she maintained her composure, resisting the urge to faint or succumb to hysteria.

"You have ample cause for surprise," Lucy continued. "Naturally, you knew nothing of this before. I daresay he never hinted at it to you or anyone in your family, for it was intended as a strict secret, which I have diligently guarded to this hour. No one among my relations knows except Anne, and I would never have disclosed it to you had I not felt absolute reliance on your discretion. I truly believed my persistent questioning about Mrs. Ferrars seemed so odd that it required explanation. I also do not think Mr. Ferrars would be displeased that I have entrusted you with this, since I know he holds the highest regard for your entire family and considers you and the other Miss Dashwoods akin to his own sisters."

She paused. Elinor remained silent for a few moments. Her initial shock was too overwhelming for speech, but gradually, she forced herself to speak, choosing her words with caution. With a calm demeanor that successfully masked her surprise and anxiety, she asked, "May I inquire if your engagement has been of long duration?"

"We have been engaged for four years."

"Four years!"

"Yes."

Though deeply shaken, Elinor still found it difficult to accept. "I was unaware," she said, "that you were even acquainted until recently."

"Our acquaintance, however, dates back many years. He was under my uncle's tutelage for a significant period."

"My uncle!"

"Yes; Mr. Pratt. Have you never heard him speak of Mr. Pratt?"

"I believe I have," Elinor replied, summoning strength as her emotions intensified. "He spent four years with my uncle, who resides at Longstaple, near Plymouth. That is where our acquaintance began, as my sister and I stayed with my uncle frequently. Our engagement was formed there, though not until a year after he ceased being a pupil; yet, he remained almost constantly with us thereafter. I was initially reluctant to proceed without my mother's knowledge and approval, but I was too young and loved him too dearly to exercise the prudence I should have shown. Although you do not know him as well as I do, Miss Dashwood, you must have observed enough to realize he is fully capable of inspiring sincere devotion in a woman."

"Certainly," Elinor answered, unsure of her own words. After a brief pause, she added, bolstered by her faith in Edward's honor and love, alongside her conviction of Lucy's deceit, "Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars! I confess I am so utterly stunned by your revelation that I must assume there is a mistake of identity or name. We cannot possibly mean the same Mr. Ferrars."

"We mean no other," Lucy cried, smiling. "Mr. Edward Ferrars, the eldest son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street and brother of your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood, is precisely whom I refer to. You must admit I am unlikely to be mistaken regarding the name of the man on whom my happiness rests."

"It is strange," Elinor murmured, trapped in agonizing confusion, "that I should never have heard him mention your name."

"No; given our circumstances, it was not strange. Our primary concern has been secrecy. You knew nothing of me or my family, so there was no occasion to mention my name. Furthermore, since he was perpetually fearful of his sister suspecting anything, that alone was sufficient reason for his silence."

She fell silent. Elinor's sense of security crumbled, though her self-control held firm. "For four years you have been engaged," she stated with steady resolve.

"Yes; and Heaven knows how much longer we may wait. Poor Edward! It leaves him completely disheartened." Taking a small miniature from her pocket, she added, "To avoid any misunderstanding, please examine this portrait. It fails to capture his true beauty, certainly, but I believe you cannot mistake the likeness. I have possessed it for over three years."

As she handed it to Elinor, the sight of the painting silenced all lingering doubts. Whatever fears Elinor harbored regarding hasty judgments or deception vanished instantly. She acknowledged the resemblance immediately and returned it.

"I have never been able," Lucy continued, "to reciprocate with my portrait, which troubles me greatly, as he has always been so eager to obtain it! I am resolved to send it the very next opportunity."

"You are entirely correct," Elinor replied calmly.

They walked a few steps in silence before Lucy spoke again. "I am absolutely certain," she said, "of your fidelity in keeping this secret, for you must understand its critical importance to us. Its exposure to his mother would be disastrous; she would never approve, I daresay. I possess no fortune, and I suspect she is an exceedingly proud woman."
