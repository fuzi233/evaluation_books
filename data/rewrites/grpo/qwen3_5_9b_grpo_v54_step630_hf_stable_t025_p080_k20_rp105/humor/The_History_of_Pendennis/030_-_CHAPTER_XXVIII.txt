The ballroom at Baymouth was less a place of celebration and more a pressure cooker of social friction. A civil war, waged entirely in whispers and shoves, raged around a corner window. Below, the street crowd hurled sarcastic projectiles like "Pitch into him!" and "Where's the police?" up through the open pane. Inside, the factions were clear: on one side, a ring of admirers including the formidable Madame Fribsby, besieging Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant; on the other, a cluster of gentlemen and ladies protecting Arthur Pendennis.

The Chevalier Strong, a man of considerable physical presence, shouldered his way through the melee, cutting a path that sent Madame Fribsby into a panic. "Save him, save him!" she cried, her voice fraying into something pathetic, though she was clearly charmed by the Chevalier's rugged appearance.

The spark of this domestic insurrection was a small, fiery chef from Sir Francis Clavering's kitchen. Earlier, while Mr. Pen had attempted to cool his boiling blood by gazing at the sparkling sea—trying desperately to compose himself after the humiliation of the waltz and Miss Amory's icy behavior—a hand had landed squarely on his shoulder. It belonged to Monsieur Mirobolant. To be touched by a French cook was an indignity that made the blood of the Pendennis line surge; Pen was astounded, bordering on enraged.

"You speak French?" Mirobolant asked, his pale face glowing with intensity.

"What is that to you?" Pen retorted in English.

"Then you understand?" Mirobolant bowed, rolling his R's with the force of a Gascon bull. "Vous me comprendrez alors, Monsieur Pendennis, quand je vous dis que vous êtes un lâche. Un lâche, entendez-vous?"

"What?" Pen spun around.

"Do you understand the weight of that word among men of honor?" the artist demanded, hand on hip.

"The consequence," Pen bellowed, stepping forward, "is that I will fling you out of this window, you impudent scoundrel!"

He was a match for the artist, and the window was right there. The threat hung in the air, ready to become reality, until Captain Broadfoot and a heavy officer threw themselves between them. The ladies screamed, the fiddles stopped dead, and the crowd surged forward. Just then, Laura appeared, her face a mask of alarm, asking what was wrong. Simultaneously, Strong emerged from the refreshment room, finding Alcides grinding his teeth and Pen looking wickedly calm.

"What has happened?" Strong asked the chef in Spanish.

"I am Chevalier de Juillet," the little man hissed, slapping his chest. "He insulted me."

"He called me... Cuisinier," the chef whispered.

Strong couldn't help laughing. "Come away with me, poor Chevalier. We must not quarrel before ladies. I'll carry your message to Mr. Pendennis. The poor fellow isn't right in his head," he whispered to the surrounding group.

Gathered around Pen, the ladies and guests demanded an explanation. Pen, bewildered, confessed that the man had offered his arm to a young lady, prompting Pen to call him a cook, which led to the chef calling him a coward and challenging him to a duel. "If you gentlemen hadn't stopped me, I would have thrown him out," Pen admitted.

"D—— him," the gentlemen grumbled. "Serve the impudent foreign scoundrel right."

"But I'm sorry if I hurt his feelings," Pen added softly.

Laura, radiant with forgiveness, stepped forward. "Foreigners have different manners, Pen. If you hurt a poor man's feelings, you'd be the first to apologize, wouldn't you, dear Pen?" Her gaze was angelic. Pen took her hands, promised he would, and felt a swell of affection. *How fond she is of me,* he thought. *Shall I speak to her now? No, not yet. I must resolve this absurdity with the Frenchman.*

Laura then asked if he would waltz with her. It was an affectionate but fatal suggestion. In his mind's eye, Pen saw himself tumbling over Miss Roundle and a dragoon, flinging Blanche against the wall, all while the room erupted in laughter.

"I shall never dance again," he declared, his face darkening. "Never. I'm surprised you should ask."

"Is it because you can't get Blanche?" Laura asked, her tone shifting to something captious.

"Because I don't wish to make a fool of myself," Pen snapped. "For you to laugh at me, Laura. I saw you and Pynsent. By Jove! No man shall laugh at me."

"Pen, don't be so wicked!" Laura cried, stung by his morbid vanity. "Who thinks the worse of you for stumbling?"

Just then, Mr. Pynsent arrived, bearing a command from Lady Rockminster to escort Laura to supper. "I was going with my cousin," Laura said, her eyes dropping guiltily.

"Oh, pray, no!" Pen interjected, seizing the moment. "You are in excellent hands. I'm going home."

"Good-night, Mr. Pendennis," Pynsent said dryly—a polite wrapper for a desire to box the insolent jackanapes' ears. Pen merely bowed and departed, leaving Laura imploringly behind.

Pynsent and Laura retreated to the supper room, where Laura, distracted and pained, wondered how her mother could expect her to give her heart to such a capricious, jealous, and violent man. Relief came only when a waiter delivered a pencil note from Pen: *I met Cooky below and asked his pardon. I'm glad I did. I wanted to speak to you tonight, but will wait until you return. God bless you. Dance away all night with Pynsent and be happy.—PEN.*

Pen descended to the hall, his heart reproaching him for his behavior toward Laura, longing to apologize but unable to do so before Pynsent. He found the Chevalier Mirobolant pacing below, ready for a fight.

"I've got him in the coffee-room with pistols and a candle," Strong announced, joining them. "Or swords on the beach? Mirobolant is a dead hand with foils."

"Confound it," Pen fumed. "I can't fight a cook!"

"He is a Chevalier of July," Strong countered.

"And do you ask me to duel a servant?" Pen roared.

Strong laughed. "I was joking. I came to settle quarrels, not fight them. I've told Mirobolant you didn't mean 'cook' offensively; it was a breach of household custom for a hired officer to offer his arm to the mistress's daughter." Strong then revealed the true secret: Mirobolant was laboring under a violent, romantic passion.

Upon hearing this, Pen burst into a hearty laugh. His rage evaporated. He realized he had been absurdly jealous himself all evening, longing to insult Pynsent. Remembering his own youthful jealousy of Oaks, he felt ready to pardon anything born of such passion. He entered the coffee-room, found Mirobolant, and delivered a fluent, heartfelt apology in French, declaring himself a *galant homme*. The Chevalier withdrew, regretting his outburst, and the matter was settled.

As they drove to the Brawl, Strong praised Pen's French, comparing him to Chateaubriand. Pen, accustomed to the language since youth, smiled as Strong finally stopped laughing.

By daybreak, the ball at Baymouth had ended. Madame Fribsby and Mirobolant departed in the Clavering fly; Laura was asleep in Lady Rockminster's bed with an easy heart; and the Claverings rested at the inn. Meanwhile, Blanche emerged from the refreshment room pale as lemon ice, whispering to her maid of a romantic adventure with a man who knew her murdered father, composing a sonnet to his memory before sleep.

Pen returned to Fairoaks without delivering his intended message to Laura, deciding to wait until her return the next day. He was not seriously jealous of Pynsent's advances, confident that as a man of twenty-three, he could simply ask and receive Laura, just as his mother could refuse nothing.

When Helen inquired about the Baymouth calamity, Pen laughed it off in a gay tone, dismissing the idea of declarations under such mishap. "There will be plenty of time for sentiment, dear mother, when Laura comes back," he said, admiring himself in the glass with a killing air. Helen, thinking no woman could resist him, was exceedingly happy.

While Helen fretted, Pen packed books, arranged papers, and cleaned his gun, preparing for a departure to London. At twenty-three, he decided it was time for serious business. He planned to take chambers at an Inn of Court, supported by two hundred pounds and his nearly finished plays. Once established at the bar, he would return to marry Laura, the sweet-tempered, good-looking girl who would steady him. "Yes, she'll steady me, won't she?" he asked his dog, Ponto, who licked his hand with devoted flattery.

Two days later, Laura returned home, driven by Pynsent. Her arrival brought a dark cloud to Pen's brow. Laura saw it and was pained, but Helen, eager to move the delicate question forward, retired early, leaving Pen in suspense. His dignity offended at being kept waiting, he slept on his disappointment until morning.

Helen woke him, declaring it the most beautiful morning in the world. "Laura has been out for an hour. Everybody ought to be in the garden."

Pen laughed, seeing the matchmaking schemer at work. "Oh, you profound dissembler! Can nobody escape your wicked tricks?" Helen laughed, flushed, and fluttered, her heart full of hope.

Pen dressed, his reflections dismal. *I am going to tie myself for life to please my mother,* he thought. *Laura is the best of women, and she has given me her money. I wish I hadn't received it. But as both women set their hearts on this match, I suppose I must satisfy them.* He felt grave, not elated, viewing the union as a great sacrifice.

In the garden, Laura wore her usual uniform: a straw hat with a ribbon streamer, a blouse fastened at the waist, and gauntlets protecting her hands from rose thorns, giving her a military air. She greeted Pen with the same smile she had worn the night before, offering him her glove to shake. Pen condescended, his face remaining tragic.

"Excuse my glove," Laura laughed, pressing his hand. "We are not angry again, are we, Pen?"

"Why do you laugh at me?" Pen asked. "You made a fool of me at Baymouth."

"My dear Arthur, I meant no wrong," she answered gently. "You and Miss Roundle looked so droll... it wasn't a serious fall."

"Confound Miss Roundle," Pen bellowed.

"She looked so pitiful," Laura said archly, mimicking Miss Roundle's distress before checking herself. "But I am sure we ought to laugh at you, Pen, if you're angry about such a trifle."

"You should not laugh at me, Laura," Pen said bitterly. "Not you, of all people."

"And why not? Are you such a great man?"

"Ah no, Laura, I'm such a poor one," Pen admitted. "Haven't you baited me enough?"

"My dear Pen, and how?" Laura cried, holding her hand out again. "Indeed, I didn't think to vex you. I thought such a clever man as you could bear a harmless joke from his sister."

"It is your kindness that humiliates me more than your laughter," Pen said. "You are always my superior."

"Superior to the great Arthur Pendennis?" Laura teased. "You can't mean that any woman is your equal?"

"Those who confer benefits should not sneer," Pen replied. "I don't like my benefactor to laugh at me. You scorn me because I have taken your money, and I am worthy to be scorned."

"Money! Obligation! For shame, Pen; this is ungenerous," Laura flushed. "May not our mother claim everything that belongs to us? Don't I owe her all my happiness? What matters a few paltry guineas if we can set her tender heart at rest? I would dig in the fields, I would be a servant, I would die for her. You know I would." She looked toward Helen's window, blessing the woman within. "My life is hers."

Unseen, Helen watched her children with deep emotion, longing for the fulfillment of her prayer.

"Is your life my mother's?" Pen asked, trembling. "You know what her great object is?"

"What, Arthur?" Laura dropped his hand, looking at him, then at the window, then down. She trembled too, sensing the crisis.

"Our mother has one wish above all others," Pen said, taking her hand again. "I own she has spoken to me of it. If you fulfill it, dear sister, I am ready. I am young, but I have had so many pains and disappointments that I am old and weary. I think I have hardly a heart to offer. Before I began the race, I am a tired man. My career has been a failure; I have been protected by those I should have protected. Your nobleness shames me, yet renders me grateful. When I heard from our mother what you did for me—that you armed me and bade me struggle once more—I longed to throw myself at your feet and say, 'Laura, will you come and share the contest with me?' Your sympathy will cheer me. Will you take me, dear Laura, and make our mother happy?"

"Do you think mamma would be happy if you were otherwise, Arthur?" Laura asked in a low, sad voice.
