Mr. Thornton left his own house without returning to the dining-room, and set off at once for Crampton, walking as men do who are accustomed to measure minutes by the striking of a mill-clock and the turning of a shaft. He had no mind to seem careless of punctuality to Mr. Hale, whom he had begun to honour as friend and teacher; so, when the church-clock sent out its half-past seven—clear, metallic, and a little lonely through the evening air—he was already at the Hales’ door, waiting while Dixon’s steps advanced with the particular slowness of one who resents the very act of opening.

He was shown into the little drawing-room. Mr. Hale met him with that gentle, cordial manner which made welcome seem less like ceremony than like habit. Mrs. Hale sat wrapped in a shawl, the pallor of her face and the droop of her figure apologising for the faint languor of her greeting more plainly than any words could have done.

Margaret, who had been lighting the lamp because the dusk pressed early upon Milton, turned as he entered. The flame gathered itself into steadiness, and the lamplight made an island in the room’s deepening shadow; beyond it the windows were not smothered up and blinded as they were in many town houses, but left to admit the night—its dimness, and its quiet, and what scanty starlight Milton’s air would allow. The room itself seemed to Mr. Thornton to be the opposite of the great, handsome, weighty place he had lately quitted, where everything—chair, table, very wall—appeared built for eating and drinking, and where the single sign of womanhood was the one fixed seat his mother took, because her will ruled the house like law.

Here there was nothing glittering to throw back the light; no mirror, no scrap of bright glass to make an answering gleam. Yet the room held warmth—sober colour, breadth, and a kind of tender persistence of home, as if comfort were not an effect to be produced, but a memory kept alive. The old Helstone chintz—dear, familiar, almost rustic in its faded roses—hung in curtains and covered chairs, like a dialect spoken softly among strangers. An open davenport stood in the window opposite the door; in the other, a tall white china vase lifted wreaths of ivy and pale birch, with beech-leaves that had that coppery tint of late autumn, as if some southern hedge-row had sent its own token north. Work-baskets waited where hands might fall to them without thinking; books lay on a table as if lately set down, not displayed for binding. And behind the door another table stood ready for tea, covered with a white cloth on which cocoa-nut cakes were set, and oranges and ruddy American apples heaped on leaves—fruit that looked, even in their stillness, like stored sunlight.

To Mr. Thornton it had the air of being no special preparation, but the family’s ordinary decency and grace—their daily bread made visible. It seemed of a piece with Margaret.

She stood by the tea-table in a light muslin gown touched with pink, as if she had brought a little of Helstone’s bloom into Milton’s smoke. Her hands—round, ivory, quiet in their movement—went among the cups with a noiseless quickness that belonged to careful practice. A bracelet on her taper arm slid down over her wrist, troublesome with its own prettiness. He found his eyes straying to it again and again, following its fall and her impatient little push to send it back, until it tightened the soft flesh, and then loosening again—dropping as inevitably as a leaf that will not stay.

So little remained to be done when he came in, that he almost regretted how soon the necessity of eating and drinking must interrupt his watching of these small domestic motions. When Margaret handed him his cup she did it with the air of a proud person compelled into service; yet she noticed with exactness the instant he was ready for more. He saw her father take her finger and thumb—using them as if they were sugar-tongs—while he laughed softly at his own device; and he saw, too, the quick upward look she gave him, eyes full of light, half laughter and half love, in that mute household play.

Her head ached still; her paleness and her silence might have confessed it. Yet she held herself as if she would not let any pause, any awkwardness, any sense of neglect fall on her father’s guest through her failing. But there was no need; Mr. Hale and Mr. Thornton were already deep in their subject. When the tea-things were taken away, Margaret drew into a corner near her mother with her work, and let her thoughts go, as people do when they feel they will not be suddenly called upon to mend a gap.

She was brought back by a low remark from Mrs. Hale, and, looking up, was struck afresh by the visible difference between her father and Mr. Thornton—difference that spoke of opposite natures meeting, and, by their very opposition, attracting. Mr. Hale’s figure was slight; there were soft, wavering lines in his face, and a perpetual tremor of expression passing over it as every change of feeling showed itself. His eyelids were large and arched, giving his eyes a languid beauty almost feminine; his brows were finely curved and lifted high by that dreamy breadth of lid.

Mr. Thornton’s brows fell straighter and lower over deep-set eyes that were intent—keen enough to seem as if they would pierce to the core of what they looked upon, yet not sharp in a petty way. The lines of his face were few and firm, as though carved rather than grown; chiefly about the lips, which were slightly compressed. When he smiled—and it was rare—the effect was like sudden sunlight breaking through cloud: it altered the whole countenance from resolved severity to an honest, childlike relish of the moment. Margaret, against her will, liked that smile. It was the first thing she had admired in him; and now, as she saw him and her father leaning towards each other in talk, she thought she understood how two men so unlike could feel so strongly drawn.

She set her mother’s worsted-work in order and went back to her own, almost forgotten by Mr. Thornton, so wholly was he absorbed in describing the steam-hammer—its tremendous power, and the delicate obedience with which that might could be measured, controlled, and made to do small, exact work. The very sound of it seemed to enter the room through his words: the imagined thunder of iron on iron, the shudder through the earth, the quick pause, the repeated stroke, ordered by human thought. Mr. Hale listened with the delight of a man to whom power becomes wonderful precisely when it is governed; and it called up for him the old tales where genii fill the sky one instant and the next compress themselves into a vessel a child can carry.

“And this imagination of power,” said Mr. Thornton, “this practical realisation of a gigantic thought, came out of one man’s brain in our good town. That very man has it in him to mount, step by step, from wonder to wonder; and if he were gone, I’ll be bound there are those among us who could spring into the breach and carry on the war that compels—all material power shall be compelled—to yield to science.”

“Your boast reminds me,” said Mr. Hale, smiling, “of the old lines—‘I’ve a hundred captains in England,’ he said, ‘As good as ever was he.’”

At the sudden leap from cog-wheels to Chevy Chace, Margaret looked up with a puzzled wonder, as if she had heard two dialects collide in one sentence.

“It is no boast of mine,” Mr. Thornton replied. “It is plain matter-of-fact. I won’t deny I am proud of belonging to a town—or district, rather—whose necessities give birth to such grandeur of conception. I would rather be a man toiling, suffering—nay, failing and successless—here, than lead a dull prosperous life in the old worn grooves of what you call more aristocratic society in the South, with their slow days of careless ease. One may be clogged with honey and unable to rise and fly.”

It was the word “honey,” perhaps—the easy contempt in it, as if sweetness were only a weight—that pricked Margaret into speech. She had sat quiet, working, keeping down her headache; but now her cheeks flushed, and angry tears came, because he had touched the place where her love of Helstone lived like an old song that will not be translated.

“You are mistaken,” she said, with fond vehemence. “You do not know anything about the South. If there is less adventure, or less progress—I suppose I must not say less excitement—from the gambling spirit of trade that seems requisite to force out these inventions, there is less suffering also. I see men here in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care—who are not only sufferers but haters. In the South we have our poor, but there is not that terrible expression in their faces of a sullen sense of injustice which I see here. You do not know the South, Mr. Thornton.”

And with that she shut her lips, angry with herself for speaking so much, and yearning, in the silence she forced upon herself, after the green lanes and homely fields of Hampshire—after the very sounds of Helstone, where the loudest thing was wind in the leaves or the distant ring of a church-bell that seemed made to bless, not to command. Here, even in a quiet room, Milton’s far-off roar was always somewhere—like a continual threat under speech, as if the town’s iron lungs could never truly rest.

“And may I say you do not know the North?” he asked. There was an inexpressible gentleness in his tone, because he saw he had hurt her.

She would not answer; she could not trust her voice.

“At any rate,” said Mrs. Hale, who had been listening with the fretfulness of a sufferer, “you will allow, Mr. Thornton, that Milton is a much more smoky, dirty town than you will ever meet with in the South.”

“I’m afraid I must give up its cleanliness,” he said, and that quick gleam of a smile came and went. “But we are bidden by parliament to burn our own smoke; so I suppose, like good little children, we shall do as we are bid—some time.”

“But I think you told me you had altered your chimneys so as to consume the smoke,” said Mr. Hale.

“Mine were altered by my own will, before parliament meddled. It was an immediate outlay; it repays me in the saving of coal. I’m not sure I should have done it if I had waited for the act. At any rate, I should have waited to be informed against and fined, and given all the trouble in yielding that I legally could. But laws that depend upon informers and fines become inert by the odiousness of their own machinery. I doubt if there’s been a chimney in Milton informed against for five years past, though some are constantly sending out one-third of their coal in what is called here unparliamentary smoke.”

“I only know,” Mrs. Hale persisted, “that it is impossible to keep muslin blinds clean here above a week together; and at Helstone we have had them up for a month and more, and not had them look dirty then. And as for hands—Margaret, how many times did you say you had washed your hands this morning before twelve o’clock? Three times, was it not?”

“Yes, mamma.”

The small domestic fact—hands made grimy by air itself—fell among the larger talk like flour on a dark board: it showed at once what could not be argued away. Margaret, looking down at her work, felt again that Milton did not merely look different; it sounded different, and smelt different, and laid its claim on skin and cloth and breath, as if it meant to write itself on every living thing.

“You seem to have a strong objection to acts of parliament and all legislation affecting your mode of management down here,” said Mr. Hale.

“Yes, I have. Many others have as well, and with justice, I think. The whole machinery—I don’t mean the wood and iron now—of the cotton trade is so new, there’s no wonder it does not work well in every part all at once. Seventy years ago what was it? And now what is it not? Raw, crude materials came together. Men of the same level, as regarded education and station, took suddenly the different positions of masters and men, owing to the mother-wit that made some far-seeing as to what future lay concealed in that rude model of Sir Richard Arkwright’s. The rapid development of a new trade gave those early masters enormous power—wealth and command. Not merely over the workmen; over purchasers—over the whole world’s market. Why, I can give you an instance: an advertisement, not fifty years ago, in a Milton paper, that so-and-so—one of the half-dozen calico-printers—would close his warehouse at noon each day; therefore all purchasers must come before that hour. Fancy a man dictating the time when he would sell and when he would not sell. Now, I believe, if a good customer chose to come at midnight, I should get up, and stand hat in hand to receive his orders.”

Margaret’s lip curled; yet she listened in spite of herself. She could not drift away now; his words had the hard savour of fact, like strong tea without sugar—bitter perhaps, but warming.

“I name such things to show what almost unlimited power the manufacturers had at the beginning of this century. The men were rendered dizzy by it. Because a man was successful in his ventures, there was no reason his mind should be well-balanced in other things. On the contrary, his sense of justice—his simplicity—were often smothered under the glut of wealth. They tell strange tales of the wild extravagance of living indulged in on gala-days by those early cotton-lords. There can be no doubt, too, of the tyranny they exercised over their work-people. You know the proverb, Mr. Hale—‘Set a beggar on horseback, and he’ll ride to the devil.’ Well—some of these early manufacturers did ride to the devil in magnificent style—crushing human bone and flesh under their horses’ hoofs without remorse. But by-and-by came a reaction: more factories, more masters; more men were wanted. The power of masters and men became more evenly balanced; and now the battle is pretty fairly waged between us. We will hardly submit to the decision of an umpire, much less to the interference of a meddler with only a smattering of the facts of the case, even though that meddler be called the High Court of Parliament.”

“Is there necessity for calling it a battle between the two classes?” asked Mr. Hale. “I know, from your using the term, it is one which gives a true idea of the real state of things to your mind.”

“It is true,” Mr. Thornton answered. “And I believe it to be as much a necessity as that prudent wisdom and good conduct are always opposed to—and doing battle with—ignorance and improvidence. It is one of the great beauties of our system, that a working-man may raise himself into the power and position of a master by his own exertions and behaviour; that every one who rules himself to decency and sobriety of conduct, and attention to duty, comes over to our ranks. It may not always be as a master; but as an overlooker, a cashier, a book-keeper, a clerk—one on the side of authority and order.”

“You consider all who are unsuccessful in raising themselves in the world, from whatever cause, as your enemies, then—if I understand you rightly,” said Margaret. Her voice was clear and cold; the words were like the click of a shut knife.

“As their own enemies, certainly,” he returned quickly, piqued by the haughty disapproval in her tone and in the very shape of her sentence. But almost at once his honesty checked him. The answer had been too nimble, too much of a dodge; and, proud as she was, scornful as she might choose to be, he owed it to himself to speak the truth of what he meant.

He could best explain by telling something of his own life. The thought made him flush—only for a moment, a dark colour rising in his cheek as if he disliked the exposure. Yet he went straight on.

“I am not speaking without book. Sixteen years ago my father died under very miserable circumstances. I was taken from school, and had to become a man—as well as I could—in a few days. I had such a mother as few are blest with—a woman of strong power and firm resolve. We went into a small country town, where living was cheaper than in Milton, and where I got employment in a draper’s shop—a capital place, by the way, for obtaining a knowledge of goods. Week by week our income came to fifteen shillings, out of which three people had to be kept. My mother managed so that I put by three out of those fifteen shillings regularly. That made the beginning. That taught me self-denial. Now that I am able to afford my mother such comforts as her age, rather than her own wish, requires, I thank her silently on each occasion for the early training she gave me. And when I feel that in my own case it is no good luck, nor merit, nor talent—but simply the habits of life which taught me to despise indulgences not thoroughly earned—indeed, never to think twice about them—I believe that the suffering Miss Hale speaks of, stamped on the faces of the people of Milton, is but the natural punishment of dishonestly-enjoyed pleasure at some former period of their lives. I do not look on self-indulgent, sensual people as worthy of my hatred. I look upon them with contempt for their poorness of character.”

“But you have the rudiments of a good education,” said Mr. Hale. “The quick zest with which you are now reading Homer shows me you do not come to it as an unknown book. You have read it before, and are only recalling old knowledge.”

“That is true. I had blundered along it at school; I dare say I was even considered a pretty fair classic in those days, though my Latin and Greek have slipt away since. But I ask you—what preparation were they for such a life as I had to lead? None at all. Utterly none at all. On the point of education, any man who can read and write starts fair with me in the amount of really useful knowledge that I had at that time.”

“Well! I don’t agree with you,” said Mr. Hale. “But there I am perhaps somewhat of a pedant. Did not the recollection of the heroic simplicity of the Homeric life nerve you up?”

“Not one bit!” cried Mr. Thornton, and laughed outright. “I was too busy to think about any dead people, with the living pressing alongside me—neck to neck—in the struggle for bread. Now that I have my mother safe in the quiet peace that becomes her age, and duly rewards her former exertions, I can turn to all that old narration and thoroughly enjoy it.”

“I dare say my remark came from the professional feeling of there being nothing like leather,” Mr. Hale replied, with a smile.

When at last Mr. Thornton rose to go, he shook hands with Mr. and Mrs. Hale. Then, as a frank familiar custom of the place, he advanced to Margaret, putting out his hand to her also.

Margaret was not prepared. She bowed—only bowed—her farewell. In the instant she saw his hand half offered and then quickly drawn back, she was sorry—sorry with a sudden, involuntary shame, as if she had refused not a courtesy but a common human warmth because she did not understand the local language of manners. But he did not know her sorrow. He drew himself up to his full height, and went away with a stiffness that was anger made into posture.

Outside, the town’s distant din seemed to meet him—the far rumble and faint metallic cries that Milton never altogether hushed. He walked off, muttering, the words bitter in his mouth, as if he wanted them to be as hard as wrought iron.

“A more proud, disagreeable girl I never saw. Even her great beauty is blotted out of one’s memory by her scornful ways.”
