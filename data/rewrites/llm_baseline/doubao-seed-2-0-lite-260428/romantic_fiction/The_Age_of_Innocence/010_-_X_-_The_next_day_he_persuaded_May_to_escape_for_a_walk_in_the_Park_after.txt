The next day, Newland Archer coaxed May Welland out for a walk in Central Park right after luncheon, a small, gentle rebellion against the old Episcopalian New York ritual that saw every respectable family troop to Sunday afternoon church services. Mrs. Welland didn’t protest the small truancy — she’d just spent the morning convincing May that a long engagement was nothing but a blessing, giving her plenty of time to stitch the hand-embroidered trousseau that would hold exactly the proper number of dozens of every linen, lace and ribbon the old society dictated a bride must own.

It was a perfect winter day. The bare, vaulted branches arching over the Mall stretched against a sky so vivid it might have been cut from solid lapis lazuli, and the snow under their boots glinted like shards of crushed crystal. The crisp, bright air lit May from the inside out, her cheeks flushed pink as a young maple’s frost-kissed leaves, and Archer swelled with quiet pride at the sidelong glances passersby cast her way. For a little while, the simple warm joy of being her suitor chased all his tangled, unnameable misgivings clean away.
“Isn’t it lovely?” she breathed, squeezing his arm. “Waking up every morning to smell lilies-of-the-valley on your dressing table! You sent them late yesterday, I know — you didn’t have time to drop them off before the morning rush —”
“But it means more than a standing order that leaves them on your doorstep at 8 a.m. sharp, doesn’t it?” he laughed. “Unlike Gertrude Lefferts, who got hers like clockwork every day right when her music teacher showed up, for all the world to see.”
“Exactly!” May giggled, nudging his shoulder. He glanced sideways at her soft, peach-ripened cheek, feeling uncharacteristically bold, and added, “Yesterday afternoon, when I dropped off your lilies, I spotted the most gorgeous bunch of yellow roses. I sent them over to Madame Olenska. Was that the right thing to do?”
“How sweet of you!” she said, lighting up. “She’d adore that. It’s so odd she didn’t mention it, though — she lunched with us today, and all she talked about was Mr. Beaufort sending her rare orchids, and cousin Henry van der Luyden’s whole hamper of carnations from Skuytercliff. She acts like no one’s ever sent her flowers before. She thinks it’s such a charming custom, and she says people never do that sort of thing casually in Europe.”
Archer’s jaw tightened, a sharp prick of irritation spiking through him. “No wonder my roses got completely overshadowed by Beaufort’s flashy gifts,” he muttered, and immediately kicked himself. He’d forgotten he hadn’t slipped a card in with the roses. Now he couldn’t very well admit he’d called on Ellen Olenska the day before, when she’d clearly not mentioned his visit to May — and keeping the secret made the whole thing feel far more sordid and mysterious than he wanted it to. To shake off the awkward lull, he steered the conversation to their own plans, the long engagement Mrs. Welland was insisting on.
“Long?” May blinked, laughing a little. “Isabel Chivers was engaged for two whole years, Grace and Thorley for almost a year and a half! We’re hardly waiting any time at all, compared to them.”
It was the exact, scripted line every nice young woman of their set was supposed to say, and Archer felt a flicker of ashamed pity. She wasn’t being dishonest, he told himself — she just echoed the lines the world had written for her. She was nearly twenty-two. At what age, he wondered, were “nice” women ever allowed to speak a thought that was entirely their own?
Never, if men like him never let them, he mused, remembering the heated thing he’d blurted out to Sillerton Jackson not long before: that women ought to be as free as men were. Soon, he told himself, it would be his job to peel the soft bandage from May’s eyes, to show her the whole wide world beyond the narrow bounds of New York society. But how many generations of her female ancestors had been buried in their family vaults, still wearing that same blindfold? He shivered, remembering a passage from his science books, the famous case of the Kentucky cave fish that had grown no eyes at all, because there was never any light for them to see. What if, when he told May to open hers, all she could do was stare blankly at a blank, empty world?
“We could be so much happier than that,” he said softly. “We could be together right away. We could travel, see all the places people only read about.”
Her face lit up. “That would be lovely,” she said, “but mother would never understand why we’d want to do things so differently. It’s just not how it’s done.”
“Shouldn’t doing things differently be the whole point?” he pressed.
“Newland, you’re so original!” she exclaimed, beaming at him.
His heart sank. He was saying exactly the same silly romantic lines every other young suitor in their set said, and she was reciting the exact scripted response tradition had taught her — even down to calling him original.
“Original?” he laughed bitterly. “We’re all cut from the same folded paper doll pattern, stencilled onto the same wall over and over again. Can’t we just break away from it, you and I, May?”
He stopped walking and turned to face her, and she looked back at him, bright-eyed and unclouded, full of nothing but adoration.
“Mercy — are you suggesting we elope?” she laughed.
“If you want to,” he said.
“Oh Newland, you do love me!” she sighed, soft and happy.
“Then why not be even happier?” he pushed.
“We can’t act like people in cheap novels, though, can we?”
“Why not?”
She looked a little bored by his insistence. She knew perfectly well they couldn’t, and it was tiresome to have to explain why. “I’m not clever enough to argue with you,” she said, relieved when the perfect excuse popped into her head. “That sort of thing is rather vulgar, isn’t it?”
Archer flinched. “Are you that afraid of being a little vulgar?”
She stared, thrown by the question. “Of course I hate it — and so do you,” she said, a little sharp around the edges.
He fell quiet, tapping his walking stick nervously against the toe of his boot. She’d clearly won the argument, and she brightened immediately, changing the subject. “Oh! Did I tell you I showed Ellen my engagement ring? She says it’s the most beautiful setting she’s ever seen, that there’s nothing half so lovely in all the Rue de la Paix in Paris. I love you so much for being so artistic, Newland.”

The next afternoon, Archer sat sullen in his study, smoking before dinner, having skipped his usual stop at the club on his way home from his law office. The slow, lazy, unchanging routine of it all was gnawing at his bones — sameness, endless sameness, the same faces behind the plate glass windows, the same conversations that always went exactly the same way. He didn’t even need to walk in to know they’d be dissecting the visiting Duke, and gossiping about Fanny Ring, the golden-haired mistress who’d been spotted driving her canary-yellow brougham down Fifth Avenue at the fashionable hour, a sight that had sent half the matrons of New York scurrying for their shawls. He could already hear Lawrence Lefferts holding forth, loud and self-righteous, about the total collapse of old society.
His sister Janey wandered in, hovering by his desk like a little Cassandra. He pretended to be deep in Swinburne’s new *Chastelard*, like he hadn’t noticed her come in.
“Mother’s very angry,” she announced, after sighing over his shelf of old French story books.
“Angry? About what?” he groaned.
“Miss Sophy Jackson just dropped by. She says her brother Sillerton is coming over after dinner to tell us all the details — he’s with cousin Louisa van der Luyden right now.”
“Janey, for heaven’s sake, start over. No human being on earth could follow that.”
“It’s no time to be irreverent, Newland! Mother was upset enough that you skipped church yesterday.”
He groaned and dropped his head back into his book.
“Newland, listen! Your friend Madame Olenska went to Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s party last night. She went with the Duke and Mr. Beaufort.”
A hot, senseless surge of anger boiled up in his chest. He laughed, loud and sharp, to smother it. “So what? I knew she planned to go.”
Janey paled, her eyes going wide. “You knew? And you didn’t stop her? Warn her?”
“Warn her? I’m not engaged to marry the Countess Olenska,” he said, and the words sounded absurd, too loud, hanging in the air between them.
“But you’re marrying into her family! You pushed for the engagement to be announced early so we could all help hold her up.”
“Uphold what? The stuffy old rules no one even wants to follow?” he jeered.
“Newland — don’t you care about Family?”
“Not a brass farthing.”
“Nor what cousin Louisa van der Luyden will think?”
“Not half of one, if all she thinks is silly old maid’s rubbish.”
“Mother is not an old maid,” Janey snapped, her lips pinching tight.
He bit back the retort bubbling up in his throat — that all of them, the van der Luydens, his own mother, everyone in their whole set, were nothing but old maids at heart, too scared to ever let real life brush against them. But Janey’s gentle face was already crumpling into tears, and he softened, ashamed. “Don’t be a goose, Janey — I’m not her keeper.”
“No, but cousin Henry only invited Ellen to the Duke’s dinner to do you a favor, to make you happy. Now they’re so upset they’re leaving for Skuytercliff tomorrow. You need to come downstairs. You have no idea how badly mother feels.”
Archer found his mother in the drawing room, her brow creased over her needlework. “Janey told you?” she said, when he walked in.
“Yes. And I can’t take it very seriously. They’re upset over a silly little thing like Ellen going to a party at a house they think is common?”
“Common? Mrs. Struthers lets people sing those terrible Paris songs on top of tables,” she said, horrified. “There’s smoking, champagne, all kinds of ungodliness on a Sunday night.”
“New York’s not the only place in the world, mother. The rest of the world hasn’t collapsed over parties like that.”
“Ellen Olenska came back here to get away from exactly that kind of fast European life! She’s a New Yorker. She should respect our ways.”
Before he could argue further, the butler pulled back the portières and announced Mr. Henry van der Luyden.
Archer’s mother scrambled to straighten her cap, and Janey jumped to push an armchair forward.
“We were just talking about you, sir,” Archer said, stepping forward to greet him.
Mr. van der Luyden drew off his gloves to shake their hands, smoothing his tall top hat shyly. “Ah — and about the Countess Olenska, I expect. I just came from visiting her. What a charming woman. She has a gift for arranging flowers, you know. I sent her some carnations from Skuytercliff, and instead of shoving them all in one big stiff bouquet like our gardener does, she scattered them loose all around her drawing room, every little nook. The Duke told me I had to come see how cleverly she’d decorated the place, and he was right. I do wish Louisa could go visit her too, if the neighborhood wasn’t so dreadfully unfashionable.”
A dead silence fell over the room.
“The truth is,” he went on, stroking his long grey leg, the old Patroon signet ring on his finger glinting, “I went over to thank her for her sweet note about the flowers, and to give her the gentlest little warning about letting the Duke drag her off to parties with him. You’ve heard the silly story about her going to Mrs. Struthers’s last night? Sillerton Jackson came to tell Louisa all about it, and she was quite upset. But I went straight to Ellen, dropped her the softest hint in the world, and she understood immediately. She was so grateful for the guidance.”
He looked around the room, his face soft with that mild, benevolent expression the whole society knew so well. “I told Sillerton Jackson before I left that there’s to be no gossip, no snubbing. I don’t invite anyone to my house that I don’t like, and the Countess Olenska will be welcomed anywhere she pleases to go. We’re off to dine early now, to take the Duke to the opera. Louisa’s waiting for me.”
When the door had closed behind him, no one spoke for a long minute.
“Gracious — how romantic!” Janey blurted out at last.
His mother sighed, shaking her head. “I just hope it all turns out for the best. Newland, you stay in and see Sillerton Jackson if he comes tonight. I shan’t know what to say to him.”
Archer laughed, leaning down to kiss the frown off her forehead. “Don’t worry, mother. He won’t come.”
Somewhere, far in the back of his mind, he was thinking of the yellow roses he’d sent Ellen, still with no card, still unmentioned. For the first time in months, that endless, stifling sameness of his life felt like it had cracked open, just a little, letting in a sliver of something bright and new. He didn’t know yet if it was a good thing.
