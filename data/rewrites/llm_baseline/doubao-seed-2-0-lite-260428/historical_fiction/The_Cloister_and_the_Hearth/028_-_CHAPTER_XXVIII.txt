The good and ill of this world balance with near-universal, almost miraculous precision – and on that rain-swollen Rhine afternoon, that rule held perfectly true. The heavy steel bow fixed to the arbalest slung over Denys’s back, hoisted high above his head as he floundered under the waves, had dragged him half-drowning beneath the surface. That very same bow, held high as it was, landed straight in Gerard’s outstretched grasp: one hand clamped hard to the steel frame, the other slipped neatly between the curved wood and its taut cord, a hold no drenching could break. The next instant, Denys was hauled upwards with such a sharp, eager jerk that half his torso bobbed clean out of the frigid water.
“Not a choke hold! Don’t make that fatal mistake!” Gerard shouted, his voice tight with mortal terror that the panicking soldier would drag him under too.
“Not fool enough for that,” Denys gurgled back through sputters of river water.
Seeing his companion still had his wits about him, Gerard calmed at once, hope blooming sharp in his chest. “Lie flat on thy back,” he commanded sharply, seizing the arbalest to stroke hard against the current and help Denys shift into position. “One hand on my shoulder. Slap the water hard with the other – no, downward, like so. Do naught else but what I bid thee.”
Gerard curled his fist in Denys’s long tangled hair, twisted it tight, and clamped the end between his side teeth. The strong muscles of his young, straining neck kept Denys’s head well above the waves without wasting a hand on the task, and he struck out lustily across the racing current. For a heartbeat he hesitated which bank to make for, never guessing how much hung on that trivial split-second choice; then he spotted the western shore was a hair closer, and struck out for it instead of swimming meekly back to Dusseldorf jail like an obedient citizen, a choice that would rewrite the rest of their days.
The current swept them far sideways as they swam, and after nearly a hundred yards of labour, Denys murmured uneasily through chattering teeth, “How much farther?”
“Courage,” Gerard mumbled around the lock of hair still between his teeth. “What any duck can do, a Dutchman can do better. Thou art safer than thou wouldst be tucked in thy own bed.”
The next moment, to their utter shock, their feet scraped pebbled riverbed, and they found themselves wading through thigh-deep shallow water onto the bank. Once on solid land, they stared at each other from head to toe as if their eyes might devour the other whole, then on the same impulse slung an arm round one another’s necks, panting there with hearts so full no words could come. At that sacred, breathless moment, life tasted sweeter than heaven to both of them – sweeter still, perhaps, for the exiled young lover who had just dragged his dearest friend back from the cold dark of the Rhine. What poet has ever climbed the height of that joy, or even dared to try? To save a human life – and that life, the one you love best? Moments like that are worth living three score and ten years for, a hundred times over.
When their pulses slowed at last, they took each other’s hands, and walked along the riverbank hand in hand like two sweethearts, hardly noticing or caring where their steps led them.
Back on the water, the boat’s party were all huddled safe on what had been a concave hull, now flipped convex like a turned tortoise shell, with Herr Turnip-Face, the so-called “Inverter of All Things,” wedged right in the middle. The whole chaos of capsizing had barely disrupted his habits: he had been mid-greeting when he shot Gerard and Denys into the Rhine, and mid-greeting still when they waded out again far down the bank.
“Shall we wait for them to right the boat?” Denys asked.
“Nay,” Gerard replied. “Our fare is paid. We owe them not a farthing. Let us press on, and quickly.”
Denys agreed, pointing out they could walk all the way to Cologne on this very bank.
“I am not bound for Cologne,” Gerard said calmly.
“What? Whither then?”
“Burgundy.”
“Burgundy? Ah no, that is far too good to be true.”
“Tis true, and sensible too. What does it matter to me what road I take to Rome?”
“Nay, you only say that to please me. The change is far too sudden. Do not think me so hard-hearted as to take you at your word. And did I not see your eyes sparkle at all the wonders of Cologne, the great churches, the painted statues, the holy relics?”
“How dull art thou, Denys,” Gerard laughed, soft and warm. “That was when we were to see them together. Churches? I shall pass a hundred of them, go what road I will to Rome. The bones of saints and martyrs? The world is full of them, heaven knows – but a friend like thee? Where on all God’s green earth shall I ever find another? I will not drag thee farther from the road that leads to thy home, and the lass that pines for thee there. Nor will I rob myself of thee by going our separate ways. Since I fished thee out of the Rhine, I love thee better than I ever did before. Thou art my pearl: I pulled thee from the water, and I mean to keep thee. Do not gainsay me, or thou wilt bring my old fever back – but cry courage, and lead on. Hey for Burgundy!”
Denys gave a wild, joyful caper. “Courage! To Burgundy! Oh, rest easy – that rogue of a fever is dead and buried for good this time.” And they turned their backs on the glinting Rhine.
No sooner had that decision settled than a commotion broke out on the far eastern bank, where the four officers had been watching them talk. They had not gone twenty paces before a voice boomed across the water. “HALT!”
Gerard turned, and saw one of the men holding up a silver badge of office and a rolled parchment warrant. His heart sank: he was a law-abiding citizen, bred to obey the call of the state that bade him turn back for Dusseldorf jail.
Denys shared no such scruples. He was a French soldier, who held every other nation, their laws, customs, and inhabitants all in equal contempt. He saw the situation for what it was, through a campaigner’s eye: superior force stuck on the opposite bank, no boats to hand, their own rear completely clear. A retreat could not be easier. He glanced back, saw the capsized barge still drifting in mid-stream, and no ferry for miles short of Dusseldorf.
“I shall beat a quick retreat up that yonder hill,” he said. “Once we are out of their sight, we can make tracks at full speed.”
They sauntered casually away, no sign of haste.
“Halt! In the bailiff’s name!” the voice shouted from the far shore.
Denys turned, ostentatiously snapped his fingers in the direction of the bailiff, and kept walking.
“Halt! In the archbishop’s name!”
Denys snapped his fingers at his grace, and kept walking.
“Halt! In the emperor’s name!”
Denys snapped his fingers at his imperial majesty, and kept walking.
Gerard watched this silly, useless pantomime with regret, and the moment they were over the brow of the hill out of sight of the river, said “There is only one path left us now: we run to Burgundy, not walk.” He set off at a full sprint, and ran nearly a full league without stopping to draw breath.
Denys was gasping for air by the end of it, and asked dryly what had become of Gerard’s famous fever. “I begin to miss the rascal sorely,” he panted.
“I dropped it in the Rhine, I think,” Gerard grinned back.
Presently they came to a tiny village, where Denys bought a crusty loaf of rye bread and a huge stone bottle of heady Rhenish wine. “We must sleep in some hidden hole or corner tonight,” he explained. “If we lie at an inn, they will clap us in our beds before dawn.” That was no more than the old soldier’s common prudence. The official network for catching lawbreakers, especially poor common ones, was woven tight as a fisherman’s net in that 15th century: the public rarely troubled themselves to interfere, but innkeepers everywhere were kept under strict surveillance, held even more accountable for the actions of their guests than for any harm that might befall them.
The friends rejoiced when the sun dipped below the horizon, and tramped on for miles under the stars – the moon, if memory serves, did not rise till three that morning – until they stumbled on a great barn belonging to a manor house set some way back from the road. Barley had been threshed there that very week: the heap of dry straw on one side of the threshing floor rose almost as high as the stacks of unthreshed grain on the other.
“Two royal beds, right here,” Denys declared. “Which shall we choose? The mow of unthreshed grain, or the soft straw heap?”
“Straw for me,” said Gerard.
They sat on the heap, tore at their brown bread and passed the wine bottle back and forth, before Denys proceeded to pile straw high over Gerard, leaving only a tiny gap for him to breathe through. “Folks say cold water kills fevered men,” he said. “I’ll make sure thou art warm as toast under this lot, no chill can touch thee.”
Gerard laughed and bade him set his mind at rest. “Those few drops of Rhine water can never chill me. I have heat enough in my bones now to parch a whole kennel of hounds, or boil a cloud right out of the sky.” The words were barely out of his mouth before his eyes fluttered shut, and he dropped straight into a deep sleep.
Denys lay awake for a little while, when he heard something that made him nestle deeper into his own patch of straw. The clatter of horses’ hooves came ringing up the road from Dusseldorf, and the wooden barn shook as a troop of riders thundered past, howling a war cry that was all too familiar in the 1400s, and as unknown in modern Europe as a red Indian’s battle whoop. Denys shook where he lay, but Gerard slept as sound as a top. The troop swept by, and the cries died away into the dark distance. The stout soldier drew a long, shaky breath, whistled soft as a whisper, closed his own eyes, and slept just as soundly.
When he woke at dawn, he reached out to shake Gerard, and his hand landed on the young man’s forehead, damp with sweat. Being an old campaigner who knew fever, he forbore to wake him. “Never rouse a sick man from sleep, nor break his sweat,” he muttered. “That much I know, even if I never tended a caged ape nor a gallows bird.”
He waited a full hour, till he was so ravenous with hunger he could bear it no longer, and rolled over to fall back asleep – a trick he had learned a hundred times in his hard wandering life, to outwait empty bellies. He woke again at high noon, prodded by Gerard moving beside him, to find the young man sitting up, straw clinging to him like smoke rising from a dung hill. The heat of his body had steamed all the damp out of the straw piled over him.
Gerard called him a lazy loon, and Denys just grinned.
They set out at once, and the first thing Denys did was hand Gerard his arbalest and his gear, before scrambling up a tall roadside oak to scout the road ahead. “Coast is clear all the way to the next village,” he reported when he climbed down, and they set off again.
Just before they reached the village, Denys halted all of a sudden, and turned to Gerard. “How dost thou feel, lad?”
“See for thyself!” Gerard laughed. “I feel as if Rome is no farther than that little hamlet yonder.”
“But thy body? Thy skin? No chills, no burning heat?”
“Neither one nor the other. Yesterday I was hot one minute, shivering the next. The only thing that plagues me is this tiresome old leg wound.”
“Le grand malheur,” Denys groaned, mock solemn. “Many of my comrades never found any trouble at all getting over wounds far worse than that.”
“Ah, there it goes again, itching fit to drive me mad.”
“Unhappy youth,” Denys said, deadpan, “the sum of all thy troubles is this: thy fever is gone, and thy wound is at last healing. Since that is so,” he added, more gently, “I shall tell thee a piece of news I had meant to keep hidden from thee.”
“What is it?” Gerard asked, eyes sparkling with curiosity.
“The hue and cry is out after us. And they are mounted on the fleetest horses in the district.”
“Oh!”
