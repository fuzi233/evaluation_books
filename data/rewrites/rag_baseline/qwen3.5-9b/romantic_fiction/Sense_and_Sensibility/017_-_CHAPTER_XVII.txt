Mrs. Dashwood's initial surprise at Edward's arrival dissolved instantly upon recognizing the sheer inevitability of his presence; in her estimation, his journey to Barton was the most logical progression of events imaginable. However, her fleeting astonishment gave way rapidly to a profound, enduring joy, her countenance softening with a warmth that rendered any prior reserve or coldness utterly obsolete. Before he had even crossed the threshold, the barriers of his shyness began to crumble under the weight of her genuine affection, completely overwhelmed by the captivating magnetism of her hospitality. Indeed, a man possessed of such feelings for either of her daughters found it impossible to withhold his devotion from her as well. Under the influence of her welcoming spirit, Edward quickly shed his defensive armor, his affections rekindling toward the entire household until his concern for their welfare became undeniably evident. Yet, despite this outward restoration, a palpable heaviness lingered within him. He praised the house, marveled at the view, and displayed attentiveness and kindness, but the underlying gloom remained. The entire family sensed this discrepancy, and Mrs. Dashwood, interpreting his despondency as a result of his mother's stinginess, sat down to dinner with a simmering indignation directed at all selfish parents.

"What are Mrs. Ferrars's current designs for you, Edward?" she asked once the meal concluded and the group gathered around the fire. "Are you still destined to be a reluctant orator against your own inclinations?"

"No," Edward replied, shaking his head. "I trust my mother has finally conceded that I possess neither the talent nor the desire for a public career."

"But how, then, is your reputation to be secured? For you must achieve distinction to satisfy your family's expectations. With no inclination for expenditure, no affinity for strangers, no profession, and no assurance, establishing a name seems a formidable task."

"I shall make no attempt," Edward stated firmly. "I harbor no wish to be distinguished, and I have ample reason to believe I never shall. Thank heavens! I cannot be coerced into genius or eloquence."

"You lack ambition, I am aware," Mrs. Dashwood noted gently. "Your aspirations appear entirely moderate."

"As moderate as those of the rest of humanity, I suspect," Edward countered. "Like everyone else, I wish for perfect happiness, though, naturally, in my own fashion. Greatness will not procure it for me."

"Remarkable that it wouldn't!" Marianne exclaimed. "What relevance do wealth or grandeur hold to happiness?"

"Grandeur holds little significance," Elinor corrected calmly, "but wealth plays a substantial role."

"Elinor, for shame!" Marianne protested. "Money can only generate happiness where nothing else exists. Beyond a modest competency, it offers no genuine satisfaction regarding the self."

"Perhaps," Elinor smiled, "we arrive at the same conclusion. Your 'competency' and my 'wealth' are remarkably similar; without them, according to the world's current standards, we would both agree that every form of external comfort is absent. Your ideals are simply nobler than mine. Come, what constitutes your competency?"

"Approximately eighteen hundred, perhaps two thousand pounds a year; certainly not more than that."

Elinor laughed softly. "Two thousand a year! One is my fortune! I suspected exactly how this conversation would conclude."

"And yet, two thousand annually is a truly moderate income," Marianne declared. "A family cannot reasonably be sustained on less. I assure you, I am not extravagant in my demands. A proper staff, perhaps two carriages, and hunting horses cannot be maintained on a smaller sum."

Elinor smiled again, hearing her sister so vividly describe their projected expenses at Combe Magna.

"Hunting horses!" Edward repeated. "But why must you have them? Not everyone hunts."

Marianne flushed slightly as she replied, "But most people do."

"I wish," Margaret interjected, striking upon a novel idea, "that someone would grant us all a substantial fortune individually!"

"Oh, that they would!" Marianne cried, her eyes sparkling with animation, her cheeks glowing with the radiant delight of such imagined bliss.

"We are all unanimous in that wish, I suppose," Elinor remarked, acknowledging the universal desire despite the scarcity of actual wealth.

"Oh dear!" Margaret sighed dreamily. "How happy I should be! I wonder what I would do with it!"

Marianne appeared equally uncertain, though her enthusiasm suggested otherwise.

"I should be quite puzzled to spend such a vast fortune myself," Mrs. Dashwood observed, "if my children were all to be wealthy without my assistance."

"You must commence your improvements on this house," Elinor advised. "Your difficulties will soon vanish."

"What magnificent dispatches would travel from this family to London in such an event!" Edward mused. "What a glorious day for booksellers, music merchants, and print shops! You, Miss Dashwood, would issue a general commission for every new print of merit to be sent your way. As for Marianne, knowing her magnanimous soul, there would not be sufficient music in London to content her. And books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would purchase them repeatedly, buying up every copy to prevent them falling into unworthy hands. She would acquire every volume instructing her on how to admire an ancient, twisted tree. Would you not, Marianne? Forgive me if I am being impertinent, but I wished to demonstrate that I have not forgotten our old debates."

"I delight in being reminded of the past, Edward—whether melancholy or joyful, I cherish recalling it—and you will never offend me by discussing former times. You are entirely correct in guessing how I would allocate my funds; at least, a portion of my liquid assets would certainly be invested in expanding my collection of music and literature."

"And the remainder of your fortune would be distributed as annuities to the authors or their heirs."

"No, Edward, I have other plans."

"Perhaps, then, you would bestow it as a reward upon the individual who penned the most capable defense of your cherished maxim—that no one can fall in love more than once in their lifetime. Your stance on this matter remains unchanged, I presume?"

"Undoubtedly. By my age, opinions are sufficiently fixed. It is unlikely I shall encounter anything to alter them."

"Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see," Elinor noted. "She has undergone no significant alteration."

"She has merely matured somewhat beyond her previous vivacity," Edward added.

"Nay, Edward," Marianne retorted, "you need not reproach me. You are not particularly cheerful yourself."

"Why should you assume so!" he replied, letting out a sigh. "Yet, cheerfulness has never been an intrinsic component of my character."

"Nor do I consider it a defining trait of Marianne's," Elinor observed. "I would hardly classify her as a lively girl. She is intensely earnest, fervent in all her pursuits—she speaks frequently and always with animation—but she is rarely genuinely merry."

"I believe you are correct," Edward admitted, "yet I have consistently categorized her as a spirited young woman."

"I have frequently caught myself committing such errors," Elinor confessed, "suffering from a total misapprehension of character in various aspects. I imagine people to be far more gregarious or somber, more ingenious or obtuse than they truly are, and I struggle to pinpoint the origin of this deception. Sometimes one relies on their own declarations, and very often on the assessments of others, failing to allow time for deliberation and independent judgment."

"But I believed it was proper, Elinor," Marianne insisted, "to be guided exclusively by the opinions of others. I thought our faculties of judgment were bestowed merely to serve the verdicts of our neighbors. This has always been your doctrine, surely."

"No, Marianne, never. My philosophy has never sought the subjugation of the intellect. All I have ever endeavored to influence is conduct. You must not conflate my meaning. I admit guilt in wishing you to accord our acquaintanceship generally with greater attention; but when have I advised you to adopt their sentiments or conform to their judgment in serious matters?"

"You have failed to convert your sister to your plan of universal civility," Edward told Elinor. "Do you make no progress?"

"On the contrary," Elinor replied, casting an expressive glance at Marianne.

"My judgment aligns entirely with yours on this issue," Edward stated, "though I fear my practice adheres much closer to my sister's. I never intend to cause offense, yet I am so painfully shy that I often appear negligent when I am merely held back by my innate awkwardness. I have frequently speculated that nature must have intended me for the company of the humble, for I feel so profoundly ill-at-ease among strangers of refinement!"

"Marianne possesses no shyness to excuse any neglect on her part," Elinor pointed out.

"She knows her own worth too well for feigned shame," Edward responded. "Shyness arises solely from a sense of inferiority in some respect. If I could convince myself that my manners were perfectly effortless and graceful, I would cease to be shy."

"But you would remain reserved," Marianne countered, "and that is worse."

Edward startled. "Reserved! Am I reserved, Marianne?"

"Yes, exceedingly."

"I do not comprehend you," he replied, flushing. "Reserved! In what manner? What am I to tell you? What could possibly lead you to suppose this?"
