My father chose, with a kind of dry punctuality that would have amused him had he been in a condition to be amused, to die on the morning of his birthday. We had been expecting him to go on—quietly ebbing, as he had been for weeks—so that when the end came it arrived like a stranger who has been in the room all the while, only one has not happened to turn one’s head.

“I have left him sleeping,” my mother whispered, gliding back into the little parlour in her dressing-gown as softly as if the boards themselves were sick and must not be jarred. “Washburn gave him a draught last night. We won’t disturb him.”

So we breakfasted in low voices. The house was one of those small, thin-walled places that hold sound as badly as they hold heat; a spoon against a cup seems to travel through lath and plaster with the insistence of a drum. The kettle hissed, the tea smelt of tannin and comfort, toast browned unevenly over the fire—its scorched edge giving off that sharp, homely perfume that, to this day, can make me think of narrow rooms and brave economies. We spoke as if words had weight.

Afterwards my mother crept upstairs; I followed, feeling as though my boots ought to have been taken off at the foot of the stairs like muddy shoes at a church door. She opened the bedroom door by a fraction and stood listening—her ear turned, not toward the keyhole, but toward the silence itself, as if silence were a jar and she might detect by its ring whether it had cracked.

The blinds were down; the room lay in a dusk that smelt faintly of medicine and the cooled sweetness of last night’s gruel. Outside, life had begun its daily street-chant with the sturdy indifference of London. The first costermonger went past—by the sound of it a girl, shrill and bright as a tin whistle—crying, “Watercreases, fine fresh watercreases with your breakfast-a’penny a bundle watercreases,” and farther off a hoarse boy wailed “Mee-ilk-mee-ilk-oi,” stretching the syllables as though he were pulling them on a rope.

Inch by inch my mother opened the door wider and we went in. He lay on his back, the lips slightly parted, as if he had been about to answer someone and had forgotten the rest of the sentence. His eyes were closed; his face, freed at last from effort, looked oddly young—too young for the tired lines I had grown used to. I had never seen death before. I could not make the thing fit the sight. All I could take in, at first, was that he was still there, and yet not there; like a clock that keeps its place on the shelf while its ticking has vanished.

It came to me only by slow degrees, as cold comes into a room when the fire is out and one does not notice until one’s hands begin to ache: he had gone away from us. For days—weeks—I would fancy I heard his step behind me in the street, his voice calling, see his face in a crowd and turn with a quick, foolish happiness, only to stand bewildered because it had melted into other faces. But at that first moment, curiously, I felt no pain at all. Pain, when it arrived, took its own time, and did not trouble to be punctual.

To my mother it was not an ending, merely a brief separation—an absence like a man gone on a journey whose boots still stand by the door. Fear and doubt had never been allowed to lodge in her faith; she seemed to carry it as naturally as she carried her keys. He was waiting for her. In God’s good time they would meet again. What need, then, of wailing? Without him the days would be slower, the house a little dull when the good man was away; but that was the worst of it. She spoke of him always—of his dear ways, of his little follies and oddities that had made us laugh—not with tears, but with a smile that was almost domestic in its calm, as though she were keeping his memory warm like bread in a cloth.

We stayed on in the old house though always, in talk, on the verge of moving. Year by year the great brick monster had crept closer, chewing up whatever was green or kindly. Field and garden, tree and cottage; the time-mellowed house that suggested story; the hedgerow that hid ugliness; the last scraps that reminded one there had once been sunshine in the neighbourhood—one by one the monster scrunched them between his ugly teeth. This east end of London was a world apart, a ghetto of the poor forever spreading, dreariness laid upon dreariness like soot on soot; endless rows of reeking cells where London herded her slaves.

Often on a misty afternoon, when we knew that beyond this city of the dead there must be a countryside where life was stirring in sunlight, we would go “house-hunting” in pleasant suburbs—some of them already being swallowed in their turn. We talked ourselves into hope at Highgate, then still a tiny town reached by coach from leafy Holloway; at Hampstead, with its ancient red-brick houses and wind-blown heath where you could look past woods and farms and see, far off, London’s domes and spires like grey bubbles on the horizon; at Wood Green, among pastures where smock-coated labourers discussed their politics and ale under wide-spreading elms; at Hornsey, then a village made of an ivy-covered church and one grass-bordered way. Again and again we found “the very thing for us,” praised its light, imagined curtains and furniture, judged its stairs by the needs of future years—and still we delayed.

“We must think it over,” my mother would say. “There is no hurry; for some reasons I shall be sorry to leave Poplar.”

“For what reasons, mother?”

“Oh—no particular reason, Paul. Only we have lived there so long, you know. It will be a wrench leaving the old house.”

Even to the making of a man goes the instinct of the clinging vine. We throw out our tendrils and fasten on whatever wall is nearest—castle-keep or pig-sty—and then rain and sunshine tie us tighter. Dying Scott hurrying home from Italy, anxious to breathe once more the damp mists of barren hills; an old woman I knew carried to the sea for her lungs, pining instead for her “home”—a slum attic—until the alley’s reek smelt to her like belonging, its riot like the voices of her own. Who shall understand such things save He who fashioned us?

So the old house held us to its dismal bosom; and I did not leave it until, within its homely but unlovely arms, first my aunt and later my mother had died, and I had said good-bye to Amy, crying amid littered emptiness.

My aunt died as she had lived: grumbling—making a last practical protest against the injustice of existence, as if death were another bill improperly made out.

“You will be glad to get rid of me, all of you!” she said, dropping, for the first and last time I can recall, into the retort direct. “And I can’t say I shall be very sorry to go myself. It hasn’t been my idea of life.”

That was a couple of weeks before the end. I don’t suppose she knew how near it was—perhaps if she had, she might have contrived a little sentiment; though it is hard to imagine my aunt with sentiment without some grumble attached, like a pin.

“Don’t be foolish,” said my mother. “You’re not going to die!”

“What’s the use of talking like an idiot,” snapped my aunt. “I’ve got to do it some time. Why not now, when everything’s all ready for it. It isn’t as if I was enjoying myself.”

“I am sure we do all we can for you,” said my mother.

“I know you do,” my aunt answered. “I’m a burden to you. I always have been.”

“Not a burden,” corrected my mother, as gently as one corrects a child’s bad grammar.

“What does the woman call it then,” my aunt retorted. “Does she reckon I’ve been a sunbeam in the house? I’ve been a trial to everybody. That’s what I was born for; it’s my métier.”

My mother put her arms about the poor old soul and kissed her.

“We should miss you very much,” she said.

“I’m sure I hope they all will!” my aunt replied. “It’s the only thing I’ve got to leave ’em worth having.”

My mother laughed; the laugh did not mock, it warmed.

“Maybe it’s been a good thing for you, Maggie,” grumbled my aunt. “If it wasn’t for cantankerous, disagreeable people like me, gentle, patient people like you wouldn’t get any practice. Perhaps, after all, I’ve been a blessing to you in disguise.”

I cannot honestly say we ever wished her back; but we did miss her—missed the odd angles of her, the corner ways that gave one something to bump against and therefore something to laugh at. It takes all sorts to make a world; and I dare say if only the perfect folk were left, existence would become unbearably smooth.

Amy, I believe, genuinely regretted her.

“One never knows what’s good for one till one’s lost it,” sighed Amy.

“I’m glad to think you liked her,” said my mother.

“You see, mum,” Amy explained, “I was one of a large family; and a bit of a row now and again cheers one up, I always think. I’ll be losing the power of my tongue if something doesn’t come along soon.”

“Well, you are going to be married in a few weeks now,” my mother reminded her.

But Amy remained despondent. “They’re poor things, the men, at a few words—the best of them,” she replied. “As likely as not just when you’re getting interested you turn round to find that they’ve put on their hat and gone out.”

After my aunt’s death my mother and I were very much alone. Barbara had gone abroad “to finish her education”—to learn, as old Hasluck phrased it, “the tricks of the Nobs’ trade.” I had left school and entered Mr. Stillwood’s office without salary—the arrangement being that I should study for the law.

“You are in luck’s way, my boy, in luck’s way,” old Mr. Gadley assured me. “To have commenced your career in the office of Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal will be a passport anywhere. It will stamp you, my boy.”

Mr. Stillwood himself was an extremely old and feeble gentleman—so old and feeble it seemed strange he had not long since retired to a country chair and a warm rug.

“I am always meaning to,” he explained to me one day soon after I began. “When your poor father came to me he told me very frankly the sad fact—that he had only a few more years to live. ‘Mr. Kelver,’ I answered him, ‘do not let that trouble you, so far as I am concerned. There are one or two matters in the office I should like to see cleared up, and in these you can help me. When they are completed I shall retire!’ Yet, you see, I linger on. I am like the old hackney-coach horse Mr. Weller—or is it Mr. Jingle?—tells us of; if the shafts were drawn away I should probably collapse. So I jog on, I jog on.”

He had married late in life a common woman much younger than himself; and she had brought him a horde of needy, greedy relatives that clattered about his quiet old age like knives in a tin dish. No doubt, as refuge from her noisy neighbourhood, the daily peace of Lombard Street was welcome to him.

We saw her occasionally. She was one of those blustering “managing” women who think making a disturbance is the same thing as setting things right. Ridiculously ashamed of her origin, she tried to hide it under what her friends assured her was “the air of a duchess”—but which resembled, in truth, rather the Sunday manners of an elderly barmaid. Old Gadley alone did not fear her; he kept her, instead, in continual fear of him, speaking with a candour that must have made her feel as though her past were always being held up to the light.

He had known her in the days she wanted buried in oblivion, and he resented, as a personal insult, her entry into the old established aristocratic firm of Stillwood & Co.

Her history was peculiar. Mr. Stillwood, when he was a blasé man about town verging on forty, had first seen her—a fair-haired, ethereal-looking child in spite of her dirt—playing in the gutter. To his lasting self-reproach it was young Gadley, accompanying his employer home from Westminster, who had drawn his attention to the girl by boxing her ears for having slapped his face with a convenient sprat as he passed. Stillwood, in a moment’s impulse, had dragged the unwilling child by the hand to her father’s “place of business”—a small coal shed in the Horseferry Road—and there made an arrangement that amounted practically to purchasing her. She was sent abroad to school; the coal shed was closed. Ten years later she returned a big handsome young woman; he married her; and learned at leisure the truth of the old saying: what’s bred in the bone will come out in the flesh—scrub it, paint it, dress it in satin, it will still insist on being itself.

Her constant complaint against her husband was that he was “only a solicitor,” a profession she considered vulgar; and nothing “riled” old Gadley more than listening to her.

“It’s not fair to the gals,” I once heard her say in a voice that could have been heard in Whitechapel had the wind been favourable. I was in the next room, and the door was not quite closed—a precaution quite wasted in her case. “What real gentleman, I should like to know, is going to marry the daughter of a City attorney? As I told him years ago, he ought to have retired and gone into the House.”

“The very thing your poor father used to talk of doing whenever things were going a bit queer in the retail coal and potato business,” grunted old Gadley.

Mrs. Stillwood called him a “low beast” in her most aristocratic tones and swept out.

Old Stillwood, for his part, never professed much affection for the law.

“I am not at all sure, Kelver,” he said to me once, “that you have done wisely in choosing the law. It makes one regard humanity morally as the medical profession regards it physically—as universally unsound. You suspect everybody of being a rogue. When people are behaving themselves, we lawyers hear nothing of them. All we hear of is roguery, trickery and hypocrisy. It deteriorates the character, Kelver. We live in a perpetual atmosphere of transgression. I sometimes fancy it may be infectious.”

“It does not seem to have infected you, sir,” I replied; for the firm of Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal was held in legal circles as the synonym for a rectitude almost old-fashioned.

“I hope not, Kelver, I hope not,” he answered; and then, with that mild smile of his that could make doubt itself seem a harmless hobby, added, “And yet, do you know, I sometimes suspect myself—wonder if I may not perhaps be a scamp without realising it. A rogue, you know, Kelver, can always explain himself into an honest man to his own satisfaction. A scamp is never a scamp to himself.”

His words alarmed me for a moment; acting on Gadley’s advice I had persuaded my mother to put all her small capital into Mr. Stillwood’s hands for reinvestment—an arrangement that had substantially increased our little income. But looking into the old man’s kindly eyes, my fear slipped away like steam from a cooled kettle.

Laughing, he laid his hand on my shoulder. “One person always be suspicious of, Kelver—yourself. Nobody can do you so much harm as yourself.”

Of Washburn we saw more and more. We both called him “Hal” now. With his gentle, masterful hands he had somehow slipped my mother’s shyness off her shoulders without her noticing the garment go; he established himself as one of the family. My mother regarded him as she might some absurdly bearded boy entrusted to her care; I looked up to him as to some wonderful elder brother who knew the trick of living.

“You rest me, Mrs. Kelver,” he would say, lighting his pipe and sinking into the deep leather chair that always waited for him in our parlour. Smoke would rise and hang, softening the shabby little room, turning its poverty almost into atmosphere. “Your even voice, your soft eyes, your quiet hands—they soothe me.”

“It is good for a man,” he would say, looking from one to the other of us through the hanging smoke, “to test his wisdom by two things: the face of a good woman, and the ear of a child—I beg your pardon, Paul—of a young man. A good woman’s face is the white sunlight. Under the gas-lamps who shall tell diamond from paste? Bring it into the sunlight: does it stand that test? Then it is good. And the children! they are the waiting earth on which we fling our store. Is it chaff and dust or living seed? Wait and watch. I shower my thoughts over our Paul, Mrs. Kelver. They seem to me brilliant, deep, original. The young beggar swallows them, forgets them. They were rubbish. Then I say something that dwells with him, that grows. Ah, that was alive, that was a seed. The waiting earth—it can make use only of what is true.”

“You should marry, Hal,” my mother would say. It was her panacea for the male sex, offered as calmly as tea for a headache.

“I would, Mrs. Kelver,” he answered once, “I would to-morrow if I could marry half a dozen women. I should make an ideal husband for half a dozen wives. One I should neglect for five days and be a burden to on the sixth.”

From anyone else my mother would have received the jest as an insult. But Hal’s smile coated anything; one could swallow it without choking.

“I am not one man, Mrs. Kelver,” he went on. “I am half a dozen. If I were to marry one wife she would be married to six husbands. It is too many for any woman to manage.”

“Have you never fallen in love?” asked my mother.

“Three of me have, but on each occasion the other five of me out-voted him.”

“You’re sure six would be sufficient?” my mother queried, smiling.

“Just the right number, Mrs. Kelver. There is one of me must worship, adore a woman madly, abjectly; grovel before her like the Troubadour before his Queen of Song—eat her slipper, drink the water she has washed in, scourge himself before her window, die for a kiss of her glove flung down with a laugh. She must be scornful, contemptuous, cruel. There is another I would cherish—a tender, yielding creature—one whose face would light at my coming, cloud at my going; one to whom I should be a god. There is a third I, a child of Pan—an ugly little beast, Mrs. Kelver; horns on head and hoofs on feet, leering through the wood, seeking its fit mate. And a fourth would wed a wholesome, homely wench, deep of bosom, broad of hip; fit mother of a sturdy brood. A fifth could only be content with a true friend—a comrade wise and witty, a sharer and understander of all joys and thoughts and feelings. And a last, Mrs. Kelver, yearns for a woman pure and sweet, clothed in love and crowned with holiness. Shouldn’t we be a handful, Mrs. Kelver, for any one woman in an eight-roomed house?”

But my mother would not be discouraged.

“You will find the woman one day, Hal, who will be all of them to you—all of them that are worth having, that is. And your eight-roomed house will be a kingdom!”

“A man is many, and a woman but one,” answered Hal.

“That is what men say who are too blind to see more than one side of a woman,” retorted my mother, a little sharply; for the honour and credit of her sex was dear to her. I have noticed this: the flag of Womanhood is upheld by true women, flouted only by the false; for a judge in petticoats is ever but a witness in a wig.

Hal laid aside his pipe and leant forward, eyes bright with mischief and inquiry. “Now tell us, Mrs. Kelver, for our guidance—we two young bachelors—what must the lover of a young girl be?”

Always serious on love, my mother answered gravely, as if she were measuring ingredients for something one could ruin by carelessness.

“She asks for the whole of a man, Hal—not merely for a sixth, nor any other part of him. She is a child asking for a lover to whom she can look up, who will teach her, guide her, protect her. She is a queen demanding homage, and yet he is her king whom it is her joy to serve. She asks to be his partner, his fellow-worker, his playmate—and at the same time she loves to think of him as her child, her big baby she must take care of. Whatever he has to give she has also to respond with. You need not marry six wives, Hal; you will find your six in one.”

“‘As the water to the vessel, woman shapes herself to man;’ an old heathen said that three thousand years ago,” Hal replied, “and others have repeated him. That is what you mean.”

“I don’t like that way of putting it,” said my mother. “I mean that, as you say of man, so in every true woman is contained all women. But to know her completely you must love her with all love.”

Sometimes they talked of religion; my mother’s faith was no dead ornament to be kept from air lest it crumble.

One evening Hal burst out, fresh from some bruising contact with misery: “Who are we that we should live? The spider is less cruel; the very pig less greedy, gluttonous and foul; the tiger less tigerish; our cousin ape less monkeyish. What are we but savages, clothed and ashamed—nine-tenths of us?”

“But Sodom and Gomorrah,” my mother reminded him, “would have been spared for the sake of ten just men.”

“Much more sensible to have hurried the ten men out,” growled Hal, “leaving the remainder to be buried with all their abominations under their own ashes.”

“And we shall be purified,” my mother continued; “the evil in us washed away.”

“Why have made us ill merely to mend us? If the Almighty were so anxious for our company, why not have made us decent in the beginning?”

“It is His way,” my mother answered. “The precious stone lies hid in clay. He has His purpose.”

“Is the stone so very precious?”

“Would He have taken so much pains to fashion it if it were not? You see it all around you, Hal, in your daily practice—heroism, self-sacrifice, love stronger than death. Can you think He will waste it, He who uses again even the dead leaf?”

“Shall the new leaf remember the new flower?”

“Yes, if it ever knew it. Shall memory be the only thing to die?”

Often, of an evening, I accompanied Hal on his rounds. Among the savage tribe he served and ruled he was regarded as medicine-man and priest combined, tyrant and slave at once—working early and late, yet bullying them unmercifully, enforcing his commands with tongue and, when needful, with fist; helper, counsellor, ruler literally of thousands. He could have made barely enough to live upon; but few men could have enjoyed more sense of power. I think that power held him there.

“Nature laid me by and forgot me for a couple of thousand years,” was his own explanation of himself. “Born in my proper period, I should have climbed to chieftainship upon uplifted shields. I might have been an Attila, an Alaric. Among the civilised one can only climb by crawling, and I am too impatient to crawl. Here I am king at once by force of brain and muscle.” So in Poplar he remained—poor in fees, rich in honour.

Justice was a passion with him. The oppressors of the poor knew him; feared him. Injustice once proved before him, vengeance followed sure. If the law would not help, he did not hesitate to use lawlessness, of which he could always command a satisfactory supply. Bumble might have the Board of Guardians; Shylock the legal support for his pound of flesh; but sooner or later the dark night brought punishment—ducking in dock basin or canal, a “Brutal Assault Upon a Respected Resident” (according to the local papers), the “miscreants” always escaping; for Hal was an admirable organiser.

One night he decided a child must go at once into the Infirmary.

“It ain’t no use my taking her now,” the mother explained, shifting the small bundle in her arms with that weary competence poverty teaches. “I’ll only get bullyragged for disturbing ’em. My old man was carried there three months ago when he broke his leg, but they wouldn’t take him in till the morning.”

“Oho! oho! oho!” sang Hal, snatching the child up as if it were no heavier than a loaf. He put on his hat with a flourish. “You follow me; we’ll have some sport. Tally ho! tally ho!” And off we went, Hal heading the procession through the streets, shouting a rollicking song. The baby stared at him open-mouthed, the way infants stare at thunder.

At the Workhouse gate Hal halted us like an impatient general.

“Now ring,” he cried to the mother. “Ring modestly, as becomes the poor ringing at the gate of Charity.”

The bell tinkled faintly—small sound, large irony.

“Ring again!” Hal commanded, stepping back into the shadow, his eyes gleaming like a boy’s before a planned practical joke.

At last the wicket opened.

“Oh, if you please, sir—my baby—” began the mother.

“Blast your baby!” returned a husky voice. “What d’ye mean by coming here this time o’ night?”

“Please, sir, I’m afraid it’s dying, and the Doctor—”

The man was no sentimentalist, and to do him justice, made no hypocritical pretence of being one. He consigned baby, mother, doctor and their urgency to Hell, and would have shut the wicket—but for the point of Hal’s stick.

“Open the gate!” roared Hal, his voice filling the night as if it were another street-vendor’s cry—only this one sold terror. “Open it quick, you blackguard! You gross vat-load of potato spirit, you—”

The Romans, born rulers, held that an officer must speak the language of the ranks. Hal believed it too. His vocabulary, when he chose, was so perfectly comprehensible to his people that it had the authority of scripture. In the time it took the trembling porter to fumble at bolts, Hal had painted him, in words, such a portrait of himself—seen from all angles—that the man must have felt as if he had been stripped and held up by the heels.

“I didn’t know as it was you, Doctor,” the porter explained, suddenly humble as a dog that has discovered whose boot it sniffed.

“No, you thought you had only to deal with some helpless creature you could bully. Stir your fat carcass, you ugly cur! I’m in a hurry.”

Inside, the House Surgeon was away. An attendant or two lounged about, which was singularly unfortunate for them; for Hal, being there, took it upon himself to go round the ward setting crooked things straight. He had the air of a man who has been given a kitchen and noticed the cook is lazy: the knives are seized, the fires poked up, orders barked, and before you can say “by your leave” the place is being run properly—at least for that night and under threat of explosion.

A busy and alarming time they had of it. Hal’s presence altered the very sound of the building: doors shut more briskly, footsteps hurried, voices dropped from sulky drawl to obedient answer. He moved among them like a storm with a medical diploma.

Not until a couple of hours later did he fling himself forth again into the street, having enjoyed himself greatly, and leaving behind him an institution that had, for once, been made to remember—by voice and will and the sharp crack of truth—that it existed for the living.
