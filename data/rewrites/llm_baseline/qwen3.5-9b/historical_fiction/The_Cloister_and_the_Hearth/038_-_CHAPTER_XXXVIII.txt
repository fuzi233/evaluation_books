Outside the town walls, the road was blanketed in fresh snow, heavily trampled by the countless tracks of wolves. Every footprint bore witness to their passage.

"We acted wisely in following the old man's counsel, Denys," Gerard remarked.

"Aye, we did," Denys agreed. "In fact, reflecting on it now, I recall hearing them last night—scuttling beneath our window, howling and whining for human flesh in that marketplace. Yet, not a single fat burgher showed pity for those poor vagrants, dropping a coin from their windows."

Gerard offered a smile, though it carried an air of distant abstraction. Together, they trudged on in silence.

"What are you contemplating so deeply?" Denys asked eventually.

"Your goodness," Gerard replied.

Denys bristled at the answer. While he possessed a remarkable tolerance for genuine impudence due to his naturally good humor, he would occasionally flare up where no just cause for anger existed.

"A civil inquiry deserves a civil response," he said dryly.

"Alas, I meant no offense," Gerard protested.

"Then why feign that you were pondering my goodness, knowing full well there is no goodness beneath my skin?"

"If anyone else had spoken thus, I would have retorted, 'Thou liest.' But to thee, I say, 'Thou hast no eye for men's virtues, only for women's.' Once more, I defy your unreasonable temper and affirm I was thinking of your kindness shown last night. You intended to marry me to the 'Golden Head'—or rather, the 'Golden Veal Head'—thereby leaving yourself entirely solitary."

"Oh, are you there, lad?" Denys recovered his cheerfulness instantly. "Truth be told, I meant that not out of goodness, but out of friendship and true fellowship. Let me confess, my young master, that my conscience pricks me even now for allowing you to turn your back on fortune and peaceful days. A truer friend might have intervened and somewhat hindered you. Then perhaps you would have lain smarting at the 'Golden Head' for a month or two; that skittish lass would have tended to you tenderly, and all would have been well. I had the blade ready to strike, but remembering how you detest pain—even a mere scratch—my cowardly heart failed me at the critical moment."

Denys wore an expression of humble apology for his lack of virtuous resolve when the path of duty appeared so clear. Gerard raised his eyebrows in astonishment at this monstrous yet thoroughly characteristic revelation; however, this new and delicate aspect of their friendship—the question of whether one ought to sever the Achilles' tendon of a beloved friend in acts of love—was never discussed. An incident soon intervened.

"Behold, one approaches from behind riding upon his neighbor's mule!" Denys shouted.

Gerard turned around.

"And how do you know it is not his own, pray?"

"Oh, blind fool! Because he rides it with no discretion." Indeed, the man came galloping like a fury. What astonished the friends most was that upon reaching them, the rustic rider's eyes widened saucer-like, and he jerked the reins so violently and abruptly that the mule planted its forelegs and slid between the pedestrians like a four-legged table on castors.

"I believe you are from the 'Golden Head,'" the rider called out.

They assented.

"Which of you is the younger?"

"He who was born later," Denys winked at his companion.

"Mercy upon the news!" the rider exclaimed. "Come, divine then!"

"And shall we?"

"Certainly. Your beard is ripe; your companion's is green. He shall be the younger. Here, youngster." He extended a paper packet. "You left this at the 'Golden Head,' and our mistress sends it to you."

"Nay, good fellow, I think I left nothing," Gerard said, feeling his pouch.

"Would you make our burgess a liar?" the rustic reproached, demanding a tip even more sharply before charging off ventre à terre.

"Nay, you shall receive a tip," Gerard said, handing him a small coin.

"A la bonne heure!" cried the clown, his features beaming with disproportionate joy. "The Virgin go with you! Come up, Jenny!" He returned "stomach to earth," as his countrymen phrase it.

Gerard untied the packet. It was roughly six inches square, containing another packet, which held yet another, and so on. By the fourth layer, he hurled the entire bundle into the snow.

Denys retrieved it and rebuked his petulance, excusing himself on the grounds of his hatred for affectation. Denys testified, "'The great toe of the little daughter of Herodias'—there was no affectation here, only a woman's clever wit. Doubtless the wrappings concealed something that, out of delicacy or her sex's cunning, she would not wish her hind to see bestowed upon a young man: namely, your garter."

"I wear none," Gerard noted.

"Then hers; or perhaps a lock of her hair. What is this? A piece of raw silk, fresh from the cocoon. Well, of all the love tokens!"

"Now, who but you ever dreamed she was so base as to send me love tokens? I saw no harm in her—save for her hands."

"Wait, here is something hard lurking within this soft nest. Come forth, I say, little nestling! Saints and pikes! Look at this!"

It was a gold ring featuring a large amethyst, glowing and sparkling with a full color yet pure as crystal.

"How lovely!" Gerard said innocently.

"And here is something written; read it yourself! I do not read as fluently as others when I know not the subject beforehand."

Gerard took the paper. "'Tis a posy, and fairly written." He read the lines, blushing like a maiden. They were remarkably naive, easily translated into English:

"The little dove!" Denys cooed. "The great owl! To risk her good name thus. However, thank Heaven she has played this prank with an honest lad who will never expose her folly. But oh, the perversity! Could she not bestow her affection upon thee?"

Denys sighed and shrugged. "Upon thee, who art as ripe for folly as she herself?"

Denys admitted that his young friend had echoed his very thoughts. It was strangely paradoxical to him that a damsel with eyes in her head should pass by a man and bestow her affections upon a boy. Still, he could not deny the bounty of Nature. Boys were human beings after all, and but for this occasional caprice of women, their lot would be too terrible; they would remain forever in the shade, blighted and achieving nothing, since only the fair could transform such unpromising material as a boy into a man.

Gerard interrupted this flattering discourse to request Denys's acceptance of the lady's ring. Denys refused it flatly, insisting that Gerard return immediately to the "Golden Head," ring and all, acting like a man and not allowing a poor girl to hold out her arms in vain.

"Her hands, you mean?"

"Her hand, holding the 'Golden Head'."

Failing in this, Denys attempted to place the ring on his friend's finger. Gerard declined.

"I wear a ring already."

"What, that sorry trinket? Why, 'tis pewter or tin at best; whereas this is virgin gold, besides the jewel."

"Yes, but Margaret gave me this one, and I value it above rubies. I will neither part with it nor give it a rival," Gerard declared, kissing the base metal and bidding it fear nothing.

"I see the owl has sent her ring to a goose," Denys said sorrowfully. Nevertheless, he persuaded Gerard to fasten it inside his bonnet. To this, the lad readily consented. For sovereign qualities were universally attributed to certain jewels, and the amethyst ranked high among these precious talismans.

Once this was arranged, Gerard earnestly requested his friend to let the matter drop, noting that speaking of the opposite sex to him made him pine so desperately for Margaret that he felt almost unmanned by the thought that each step took him farther from her.

"I am no general lover, Denys. There is room in my heart for one sweetheart and one friend. I am far from my dear mistress; and my friend, a few leagues more, and I must lose him too. Oh, let me drink your friendship pure while I may, and not dilute it with any of these foolish females."

"And shalt, honey-pot, and shalt," Denys said kindly.

"But regarding my leaving you at Remiremont, do not count on that! For" (swearing three times consecutively) "if I do... Nay, I shall propose to you to stay forty-eight hours there, while I kiss my mother and sisters, and the females generally, and then we shall proceed together to the sea."

"Denys! Denys!"
