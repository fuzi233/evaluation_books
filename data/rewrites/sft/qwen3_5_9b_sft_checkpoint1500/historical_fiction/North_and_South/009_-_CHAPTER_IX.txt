"Let China's earth, enriched with coloured stains,
Pencil'd with gold, and streaked with azure veins,
The grateful flavour of the Indian leaf,
Or Mocha's sunburnt berry glad receive."

The following morning, at an hour unbecoming a gentleman of leisure, Mr. Hale ascended the stairs to the small drawing-room. His movements betrayed a restless examination of the furniture, a physical manifestation of the hesitation that plagued him. He wished to speak, yet the words remained trapped behind a veil of nervous deference until, finally, he broke the silence.

"My dear!" he exclaimed, "I have invited Mr. Thornton to tea tonight."

Mrs. Hale reclined in her easy chair, eyes closed, her face bearing a habitual mask of suffering that seemed etched into her features by recent troubles. Yet, at her husband's announcement, she sprang upright, her composure shattered by querulous indignation.

"Mr. Thornton! And tonight! What possible purpose could drive a man like that to our door?" She waved a hand dismissively. "And consider Dixon! She is occupied with my muslins and laces, and how can she manage without soft water amidst these dreadful eastern gales? I fear Milton is condemned to such storms forever."

"The wind is shifting, my love," Mr. Hale replied, peering out at the smoke curling from the east. Though he lacked knowledge of the compass points, he adjusted his understanding of the weather to suit his immediate needs.

"Don't deceive me!" Mrs. Hale shivered, tightening her shawl around her frail form. "Whether east or west, I suppose he must come."

"Oh, Mother!" Margaret interjected, stepping between them. "That proves you have never truly observed Mr. Thornton. He possesses a spirit that revels in adversity, whether enemies, tempests, or circumstance. The stormier the weather, the more likely he is to appear. Besides, I shall assist Dixon; I am becoming quite proficient at starching. And, Father, he requires no diversion beyond conversation with you." She turned to her father with evident delight. "I am eager to witness the encounter between Pythias and Damon. We met once, but our tongues tripped over each other, leaving us ill-at-ease."

"I doubt you would find him congenial, Margaret," Mr. Hale cautioned gently. "He is no dandy."

Margaret twisted her neck in a gesture of mock disdain. "I hold no special reverence for 'ladies' men,' Father. But Mr. Thornton approaches us as a friend, one who has recognized your worth—"

"The only soul in Milton capable of doing so," Mrs. Hale muttered.

"Then let us extend a warm welcome, perhaps offering coconut cakes. Dixon would be delighted to prepare them, and I shall happily iron your caps, Mother."

Throughout that morning, Margaret harbored a fervent desire to keep Mr. Thornton at bay. She had envisioned a different array of duties: composing a letter to Edith, delving into Dante, or visiting the Higgins household. Instead, she stood before the ironing board, listening to Dixon's grievances, praying that an overflow of sympathy would prevent the laundry maid from recounting her woes to Mrs. Hale. Periodically, she had to summon her resolve regarding her father's esteem for Mr. Thornton to stifle the weary irritation rising within her, a sensation that threatened to trigger the severe headaches plaguing her recently. When she finally seated herself to inform her mother of her transformation from "Peggy the laundry-maid" to "Miss Margaret Hale," she intended it as a jest. However, her mother received the remark with grave seriousness, launching into a tirade about her former days as Miss Beresford and the absurdity of having to scrub floors to accommodate a tradesman.

"Oh, Mother!" Margaret interrupted, rising hastily. "Do not chastise me for this slip of tongue. I derive no pleasure from ironing, nor do I mind the labor, but I am a lady through and through, regardless of whether I am scouring a floor or polishing silver. I am merely fatigued for a moment; soon I shall be ready to resume. As for Mr. Thornton's profession, it is a circumstance beyond his control, poor fellow. His education, alas, offers few alternatives." With a sigh, she retreated to her chamber, unable to endure the atmosphere any longer.

Simultaneously, within the confines of Mr. Thornton's residence, a starkly different tableau unfolded. In a grand, imposing dining room, a formidable matron, well past middle age, sat engaged in needlework. Her frame, broad and powerful, mirrored the strength of her features. Her countenance moved slowly from one resolute expression to another, devoid of frivolous variation. To the observer, she appeared as an immovable monument, a woman who navigated life with unyielding purpose, refusing to bow to street etiquette or pause from her destined course.

Clad in sturdy black silk, not a single thread marred by wear or discoloration, she meticulously mended a vast tablecloth of exquisite texture, holding it aloft to inspect imperfections. Upon the massive sideboard beside her lay six volumes of Matthew Henry's commentaries, flanked by a tea urn and a lamp. From a distant room, the clatter of a piano practice session drifted forth—a *morceau de salon* played with frantic speed, notes frequently missed, chords often discordant, yet performed with a satisfaction that belied the technical errors.

Mrs. Thornton heard a footstep, steady and decisive, approach the dining-room door.

"John! Is that you?"

Her son entered, revealing himself.

"What brings you home so prematurely? I believed you were attending tea with that acquaintance of Mr. Bell's, Mr. Hale?"

"So I am, Mother. I returned to dress."

"Dress!" she scoffed, her tone dripping with condescension. "In my youth, young men changed clothes but once a day. Why the need to attire yourself for a cup of tea with an old clergyman?"

"Mr. Hale is a gentleman, and his wife and daughter are ladies."

"Wife and daughter!" Her eyes narrowed. "Do they instruct? What are their occupations? You have omitted mention of them hitherto."

"No, Mother, because I have never encountered Mrs. Hale; I saw Miss Hale for but half an hour."

"Beware lest you ensnare yourself with a penniless girl, John."

"I am not susceptible to entrapment, Mother, as you surely know. Nor do I believe any young lady has yet bestowed such futile efforts upon me."

Mrs. Thornton refused to concede the point, her pride demanding victory. "Well! I merely advise caution. Perhaps the girls of Milton possess too much spirit and sentimentality to court husbands openly; but this Miss Hale emerges from the aristocratic shires, where, if rumors hold truth, wealthy spouses are deemed prizes."

Mr. Thornton's brow furrowed, and he advanced a step into the room. "Mother"—he laughed briefly, a sound tinged with scorn—"you compel me to admit the truth. The sole instance I witnessed of Miss Hale, she offered me a haughty civility steeped in profound contempt. She maintained a distance from me as if I were a lowly, unwashed vassal and she a queen. Rest assured, Mother."

"Not assured!" she retorted, her voice rising. "Nor am I content! What business had she, the daughter of a renegade clergyman, to sneer at you! I would not dress for any of that saucy brood if I were you." As he departed, he added, "Mr. Hale is virtuous, gentle, and learned. He is not saucy. Regarding Mrs. Hale, I shall describe her appearance tonight, should you desire to hear." He left the room, closing the door firmly.

Alone, Mrs. Thornton fumed. "To despise my son! To treat him as a vassal! Humph! I wonder where she could possibly locate another suitor! Whether boy or man, he possesses the noblest, stoutest heart I have ever known. I need not be blind, though I am his mother; I perceive reality as it stands. I know the nature of Fanny, and I know the character of John. To despise him! I loathe her!"
