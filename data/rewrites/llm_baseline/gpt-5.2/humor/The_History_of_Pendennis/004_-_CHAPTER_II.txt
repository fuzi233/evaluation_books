Early in the Regency—when George the Magnificent was still being magnificent in public and short of small change in private—there lived in a west-country town called Clavering a gentleman who insisted, with a persistence worthy of a better cause, that he was a gentleman. His name was Pendennis.

There were people in Clavering, and more in Bath, with memories as sharp as vinegar, who could still see (with the inward eye, and sometimes with the outward) a certain board once painted with the word PENDENNIS in honest letters, and crowned—not with a coat-of-arms, mind you, but with a gilt pestle and mortar. It hung above a humble shop-door in Bath, where Mr. John Pendennis had pounded roots and seeds, rolled pills, mixed draughts, and, when the world was particularly unkind, sold a farmer’s wife a pennyworth of salts across the counter as if he had been born to it. If a lady wanted a plaster, he would cut it, fold it, and wrap it in brown paper with a neatness that felt almost like a compliment; if a gentleman wanted sal-volatile, he would pour it with a bow; and if anybody wanted tooth-brushes, hair-powder, or a cake of Windsor soap with a smell of London about it, he would condescend (oh, the condescension!) to supply those too.

Now, it was one of Mr. Pendennis’s private tragedies that he had excellent reasons—on paper—for despising the very counter he stood behind. He possessed a Cornish pedigree that trotted the Pendennises back through Normans and Druids and heaven knew what misty forefathers who probably ate acorns with a good deal of dignity. The family had, at a late period of its existence, intermarried with Normans as if doing a favour to the Conquest. There were shadowy connections, too, with Wales and Brittany; and Mr. Pendennis, if you gave him time and a quiet room, could make you related to almost anybody of consequence.

He had even begun a university career. At Cambridge, in his second year—at that very age when a young man should be learning Latin particles and the art of running up harmless bills—his father died insolvent, leaving the pedigree intact and everything else knocked into a hat. Necessity, that vulgar tyrant who never reads coats-of-arms, drove John Pendennis into apprenticeship under his mother’s brother, a London apothecary of low family. It was a bitter dose. He detested the trade; and detested, with an elegance all his own, the coarse-minded relative who had offered him shelter at the cost of self-respect.

As soon as he was able, he escaped to Bath and set up for himself with a modest medical ensign: the gilded pestle and mortar, the pink bottles, the clean window, the little interior smelling of camphor, cinnamon, spirits of lavender, and that indefinable sharp sweetness which belongs to fresh-mixed draughts. He struggled for a time; he kept his shop and its gilt ornaments decent by sheer scrubbing and thrift; he kept his bed-ridden mother in comfort by denying himself almost everything but the consolations of gentility.

And then—because history is made as often by collisions as by coronations—Lady Ribstone happened to be passing to the Rooms in a chair managed by an intoxicated Irish chairman. The man bumped her ladyship into Mr. Pendennis’s very door-post with a gusto as if he had been paid by the bruised inch, and the chair-pole went clean through the handsomest pink bottle in the surgeon’s window. There was a crash of glass, a flash of coloured liquid like some sacrificial cordial, and a scream that could have been heard in the Pump Room had the Pump Room not been busy making its own noises.

Lady Ribstone alighted in hysterics. Mr. Pendennis, with the coolness of a man who has long practised composure as an ingredient, placed her in a chair inside his shop. He revived her with cinnamon and sal-volatile—warm spice and sharp spirit, domestic comfort and medicinal sting—while the world outside stared at the wrecked bottle as if it were a fallen idol.

His manners were so soothing, so uncommonly gentlemanlike, that Lady Ribstone immediately appointed her “preserver” apothecary to her person and family—an enormous family, like a small parish with better linen. Sir Pepin Ribstone, of Codlingbury, Bart., had the sense to let his wife be grateful. Master Ribstone, home from Eton for Christmas, over-ate himself with the heroic appetite of a boy who considers digestion a superstition, and fell into a fever. Mr. Pendennis treated him with skill and tenderness; and from that day fortune stopped being quite so rude.

Bath patronised him—especially the ladies. His little shop became a smart one; then, step by gentlemanly step, he dropped the sale of tooth-brushes and perfumery, as unworthy of ancient lineage; then he shut up the shop altogether and kept only a surgery, attended by a genteel young man; then he acquired a gig, and a man to drive him; and at last, before she left this world of draughts and drafts, his mother had the happiness of seeing her beloved John step into a close carriage of his own—only one horse, true, but with the arms of Pendennis handsomely emblazoned on the panels, as if paint could undo the past.

“What would Arthur say now?” she asked, with the sharp triumph of a woman who can congratulate and reproach in a single breath. She meant a younger son of hers, a soldier. “Who never so much as once came to see my dearest Johnny through all the time of his poverty and struggles!”

“Captain Pendennis is with his regiment in India, mother,” Mr. Pendennis said, patiently, “and, if you please, I wish you would not call me Johnny before the young man—before Mr. Parkins.”

Presently the day came when she ceased to call him Johnny or anything at all. The house, which had always been half filled with her querulous kindness, went suddenly hollow. Mr. Pendennis altered the night-bell and placed it in the room where she had grumbled for years; he slept in the great bed there, as if to keep watch over the ghost of duty. He was past forty when this happened—before the war was over; before George the Magnificent came to the throne; before this history, indeed. But what is a gentleman without his pedigree? He had it handsomely framed and glazed, hanging between pictures of Codlingbury House and St. Boniface’s College, Cambridge, where he had once lived like a young man with a future. He had taken the pedigree out of a trunk—like Sterne’s officer calling for his sword—now that he was a gentleman and could show it.

About the time of Mrs. Pendennis’s death, another of his patients died too: old Lady Pontypool, daughter of Reginald twelfth Earl of Bareacres, and therefore great-grand-aunt to the present Earl; widow of John second Lord Pontypool, and likewise of the Reverend Jonas Wales of the Armageddon Chapel, Clifton—an accumulation of titles and bereavements like layers of heavy pastry. For the last five years of her life, Lady Pontypool had been attended by Miss Helen Thistlewood, a very distant relative of Bareacres, and daughter of Lieutenant R. Thistlewood, R.N., killed at Copenhagen.

Miss Thistlewood had, under Lady Pontypool’s roof, boarding and lodging and tyranny: the comfortable crust and the daily scourge. It was a particular, domestic kind of despotism—one of those infernal arrangements by which a woman who cannot command an army commands a companion. Mr. Pendennis, who visited at least twice a day, could not help remarking the angelical sweetness with which Miss Helen bore insults that would have made a saint cough. He observed her pale face, her quiet hands, her voice kept low as if loudness were a sin. He watched her serve tea to the old lady—tea brewed to Lady Pontypool’s exacting taste, strong enough to float a spoon yet clear enough to shame muddy water—while the elder criticised the very angle at which the cup was offered.

Then Lady Pontypool died. The Pontypool family descended upon the property like a flight of crows on a field, and everything was nailed into boxes, packed into hampers, swaddled with haybands, buried in straw, locked under three keys in green baize plate-chests, and carted away under Miss Helen’s eyes. Mr. Pendennis sat with her in the fourth mourning coach going to Bath Abbey, where her ladyship’s venerated remains were to be deposited, and as the wheels creaked and the horses’ hooves beat their melancholy measure, he looked at Miss Helen’s sweet pale face and made up his mind to ask a question which made his own pulse gallop up to ninety—at least.

He was more than twenty years older than she, and at no time the most ardent of men. Perhaps he had strangled a love affair in youth; perhaps, indeed, early love affairs should be drowned like blind kittens—painful, but tidy, and done before anyone gives them names. At three-and-forty he was a collected quiet little gentleman in black stockings, with a bald head and careful manners. A few days after the funeral he called to see Miss Helen; and while he felt her pulse he kept hold of her hand a moment longer than the pulse required. (Doctors have their indulgences too; only theirs are counted in seconds.)

He asked her where she was going to live, now that the Pontypools had carried off the plate and the very air of the house. Her eyes filled with tears. She said she did not know. She had a little money—Lady Pontypool had left her a thousand pounds—and she might go into a boarding-house, or into a school, or into any of those respectable limbos where gentlewomen wait for life to happen. In short, she did not know.

Then Mr. Pendennis, still holding the cold little hand, asked if she would come and live with him.

He spoke of his age as though it were an injury done to her; spoke of his birth, his principles, his temper; spoke of his prospects improving daily, and his loneliness, and his need of a kind and constant companion whom it would be his life’s study to make happy. In truth, he recited a small speech which he had composed that morning in bed and rehearsed in his carriage on the way to her—polite, proper, and phrased like a prescription: take one husband, twice daily, against melancholy.

Perhaps Miss Helen too had once imagined another fate—something warmer than being asked to marry a man who rapped his teeth and smiled artificially, who was laboriously polite to the butler as he slid upstairs and profusely civil to the lady’s-maid waiting at the bed-room door; a man whom Lady Pontypool used to ring for as she rang for a servant, and who came with even more eagerness. Yet she knew how worthy he was; how prudent, how honourable; how good he had been to his mother, and constant in his care. And so the upshot was that she blushed very much, made him an extremely low curtsey, and asked leave—to consider his very kind proposal.

They were married in the dull Bath season, which is the height of the season in London (so that Providence, with its usual humour, contrived that the very moment Mrs. Pendennis might have been exhibited to the world, the world was elsewhere). Through a professional friend, M.R.C.S., Mr. Pendennis secured lodgings in Holles Street, Cavendish Square, and carried his wife to town in a chaise and pair. He showed her the theatres, the Parks, the Chapel Royal; he showed her people going to drawing-rooms as if they were an educational exhibit; and gave her all the pleasures of the town which can be enjoyed by two persons who are still a little shy of each other and too well bred to confess it.

He left cards upon Lord Pontypool, upon the Right Honourable the Earl of Bareacres, and upon Sir Pepin and Lady Ribstone. Bareacres took no notice. Pontypool called, admired Mrs. Pendennis, and said Lady Pontypool would come and see her—an achievement accomplished by proxy, in the person of John the footman, who brought a card and an invitation to a concert five weeks off. By that time Mr. Pendennis was back in his one-horse carriage, dispensing pills and draughts again. But the Ribstones asked him and Mrs. Pendennis to an entertainment, and Mr. Pendennis bragged of it to the last day of his life, as a man will brag of a dish he once cooked for royalty, though he has boiled mutton ever since.

For the secret ambition of Mr. Pendennis had always been to be a gentleman. It takes much time and careful saving for a provincial doctor—not rich, only respectable—to lay by enough to purchase house and land; but besides his own prudence, fortune helped. He bought, very advantageously, a house and small estate close upon the village of Clavering. Words cannot describe his pride when he found himself a landed proprietor, and could walk over acres that belonged to him and not to an apothecary’s ledger. A lucky purchase of shares in a copper-mine increased his wealth, and he realised with uncommon prudence while the mine was still in vogue. Finally, he sold his business at Bath to Mr. Parkins for a handsome ready sum and an annuity to be paid for a certain number of years after he retired for ever from the handling of mortar and pestle.

Arthur Pendennis, his son, was eight years old at the time; so it is no wonder the boy, leaving Bath so young, almost forgot that such a place existed, and that his father’s hands had once been dirtied by compounding odious pills or spreading filthy plasters. The old man never spoke of the shop; never alluded to it; called in the medical practitioner of Clavering to attend his family when occasion required; sank black breeches and stockings altogether; attended market and sessions, and wore bottle-green coat and brass buttons with drab gaiters, precisely as if he had been born in a park and nursed on rent-rolls.

He stood at his lodge-gate and watched the coaches come in, bowing gravely as guards and coachmen touched their hats and drove by. He founded the Clavering Book Club and set up the Samaritan Soup and Blanket Society. He brought the mail, which used to run through Cacklefield, away from that village and through Clavering—an act of public spirit which Clavering praised as if the mail had been a family heirloom. At church he was active as vestryman and worshipper. At market every Thursday he went from pen to stall, looked at samples of oats, munched corn, felt beasts, punched geese in the breast, and weighed them with the air of a man who understood both providence and poultry. He did business with farmers at the Clavering Arms as knowing as any old frequenter. It became his shame, as it had once been his pride, to be called Doctor; those who wished to please him called him Squire.

Heaven knows where they came from, but a whole range of Pendennis portraits soon hung round his oak dining-room: Lelys and Vandykes, he vowed—every one of them—and when asked the history of the originals he would say vaguely that they were “ancestors of his.” You could see by Mrs. Pendennis’s looks that she disbelieved these genealogical miracles; she generally tried to turn the conversation when he commenced them. But little Arthur believed with all his might. Roger Pendennis of Agincourt, Arthur Pendennis of Crécy, General Pendennis of Blenheim and Oudenarde became as real to him as Robinson Crusoe or the Seven Champions of Christendom.

Mr. Pendennis’s fortune—at best not above eight hundred a year—did not permit the great people of the county to clasp him to their bosoms; but it provided a decent comfortable society of the second-best sort. They were not roses, perhaps, but they lived near roses and had a good deal of the odour of genteel life. They brought out their plate and dined each other round in moonlight twice a year, driving a dozen miles for the ceremony and eating as if distance gave appetite a moral character.

Besides the county society, there was Clavering town itself—more of it than the Pendennises desired. Mrs. Pybus was always poking about Helen’s conservatories, interfering with soup-tickets and coal-clubs as if charity were a public road. Captain Glanders (H.P., 50th Dragoon Guards) swaggered about the Squire’s stables and gardens, trying to enlist him in quarrels with the Vicar, the Postmaster, the Reverend F. Wapshot of Clavering Grammar School (for overflogging his son Anglesea), and, in short, with the entire parish. Mr. and Mrs. Pendennis often blessed themselves that Fairoaks was nearly a mile out of Clavering; otherwise their threshold would never have been free from prying eyes and practising tongues.

The lawn of Fairoaks came down to the little river Brawl. Across it lay the plantations and woods—what was left of them—belonging to Clavering Park and Sir Francis Clavering, Bart. When the Pendennises first came, the park was let out in pasture and fed down by sheep and cattle; the great house stood with shutters up, a splendid freestone palace with stairs, statues, and porticos, the kind you may admire in a book called Beauties of England and Wales, where ruins look very comfortable and cost nothing. Sir Richard Clavering, Sir Francis’s grandfather, began the family’s ruin by building the palace; his successor achieved it by living in it. Sir Francis himself was abroad somewhere; no one could be found rich enough to rent that enormous mansion with its deserted rooms, mouldy clanking halls, and dismal galleries. Arthur Pendennis, as a boy, walked there trembling many a time, frightened less by ghosts than by the echo of his own small feet.

At sunset from the lawn there was a sight which made even Clavering’s gossip pause: Fairoaks and the opposite park put on a rich golden tinge; the upper windows of the great house flamed so that your eyes winked; the little river ran off noisily westward and was lost in a sombre wood; behind which the towers of the old abbey church of Clavering rose in purple splendour. Little Arthur and his mother cast long blue shadows over the grass; and he would repeat in a low voice—because beauty made him hushed—certain lines beginning, “These are thy glorious works, Parent of Good; Almighty! thine this universal frame,” to Mrs. Pendennis’s great delight. Such walks ended commonly in a profusion of filial and maternal embraces, for to love and to pray were the main occupations of this dear woman’s life; and Arthur, in later wildness, used to declare that he felt sure of going to heaven because his mother never could be happy there without him.

John Pendennis did not much care for the view, nor did he share in poetry and caresses. At six o’clock, to the minute, dinner occurred; after it he had a nap with the Globe newspaper on his knee and a yellow bandanna handkerchief on his face—yellow handkerchiefs sent from India by Major Pendennis, his brother, who had helped in the purchase of the majority and was now restored to favour. Thus, when sunset softened the world at half-past seven, Mr. Pendennis was generally engaged in the serious contemplation of printed columns and his own digestion.

The tenderness between mother and child seldom took place in his presence. However frisky they were before, Mrs. Pendennis and Arthur grew hushed when Mr. Pendennis walked into the drawing-room with his newspaper under his arm. There, while the boy, buried in a great chair, devoured whatever books he could lay hands on, the Squire perused his articles in the Gardener’s Gazette, or took a solemn hand at picquet with Mrs. Pendennis or an occasional friend from the village. Even the sounds of the house had to respect him: the clock ticked as if keeping step, the fire cracked softly in orderly intervals, and servants moved with a caution that suggested not awe but punctuality.

Mr. Pendennis usually contrived that at least one of his grand dinners should take place when his brother visited: “My brother, Major Pendennis,” was a constant theme of his conversation, rolled out with as much relish as any well-basted joint. The Major came biennially to Fairoaks on the return of his regiment from India and New South Wales, having sold out and gone upon half-pay. He was the link binding the family to London and fashion, and every link was made to rattle. He brought down the latest news of nobility, and was in the constant habit of dining with lords and great folks. He spoke of them with soldierlike respect and decorum: “My Lord Bareacres has been good enough to invite me to Bareacres for the pheasant-shooting,” or “My Lord Steyne is so kind as to wish for my presence at Stillbrook for the Easter holidays.” Mr. Pendennis made sure the whereabouts of “my brother the Major” were known at the Reading-room, at justice-meetings, and in the county-town, as if the Major were a particularly valuable shipment.

Carriages came from ten miles round to call upon Major Pendennis when he was at Fairoaks. His fame as a man about town was established throughout the county. There had been talk of his marrying Miss Hunkle of Lilybank—old Hunkle the attorney’s daughter, with at least fifteen hundred a year. But the Major refused the negotiation, advantageous as it seemed to those who think matrimony a financial instrument. “As a bachelor,” he said, “nobody cares how poor I am. I have the happiness to live with people so highly placed that a few hundreds more or less make no difference in the estimation they are pleased to hold me. Miss Hunkle, though most respectable, is not in possession of either the birth or the manners which would entitle her to be received into the sphere in which I have the honour to move. I shall live and die an old bachelor, John; and your worthy friend, Miss Hunkle, will find some more worthy object of her affection than a worn-out old soldier on half-pay.” Time proved his worldly wisdom: Miss Hunkle married a young French nobleman and now lived at Lilybank under the title of Baroness de Carambole, separated from her wild young scapegrace of a Baron shortly after their union—so that she gained a title, lost a husband, and kept the income, which is a very French ending for a Clavering beginning.

The Major was a favourite with almost all the little establishment at Fairoaks. He was good-natured as well as well-bred, and had sincere liking and regard for his sister-in-law, whom he pronounced—with perfect truth—to be as fine a lady as any in England and an honour to the family. Mrs. Pendennis’s tranquil beauty, natural sweetness, simplicity, and the dignity which perfect purity bestows, made the praise seem not flattery but fact. She moved through the world regardless of comment. She did not seem to know she was admired or hated for being so perfect; she carried on calmly, saying her prayers, loving her family, helping her neighbours, and doing her duty, with a quietness that made other women—noisier in speech and silk—feel somehow rebuked.

Yet rebuked they were. Miss Pybus said she was cold and haughty. Miss Pierce declared she was too proud for her station. Mrs. Wapshot, as a doctor of divinity’s lady, demanded the pas of one who was only the wife of a medical practitioner. Mr. Pendennis, when he drove his wife into the county-town to assize balls or concerts, stepped into the assembly with her on his arm and looked the great folks in the face as much as to say: Look at that, my lord—can any of you show me a woman like that?

Arthur Pendennis, for his part, believed in his mother as he believed in light: not as a thing to be proved, but as a thing by which all other things were seen. During his childhood the boy thought of her as little less than an angel—wisdom, love, and beauty in one. And in that household—where a man could change his name from Doctor to Squire as easily as changing his coat, where portraits appeared from nowhere to people the walls with invented ancestors, where the mail-coach’s rumble was treated like music because it chose the “right” road—Mrs. Pendennis preserved, in her own gentle way, something older than pedigrees and more delicate than fashion: the private language of affection, spoken softly, often misunderstood by the world, and, like all true dialects, impossible to translate into mere titles.
