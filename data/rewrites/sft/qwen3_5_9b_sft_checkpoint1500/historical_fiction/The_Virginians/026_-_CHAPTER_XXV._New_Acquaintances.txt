Cousin Maria appeared, flanked by two gardener's boys carrying baskets brimming with fresh blooms. Their mission was clear: to transform Madame de Bernstein's drawing-room into a fitting stage for the arrival of her ladyship's distinguished guests. Behind them followed three footmen in liveries gorgeously laced with worsted, setting out twice as many card tables as needed. A major-domo, dressed in black with fine-laced ruffles and a heavy bag, trailed behind the lacqueys. He carried fasces of wax candles, placing a pair upon each card table and lighting the silver sconces that lined the wainscoted walls. As the sun's slanting rays gilded the interior, they also illuminated the prospect beyond—a green common dotted with rocks, tree-clumps, and distant houses twinkling in the sunlight. Outside, groups of figures in hoops, powder, and brocade sauntered across the lawn, dappling the plain with shifting shadows. Across the street lay the Pantiles, where a perpetual fair buzzed with the clamor of company. A band played music for the benefit of the visitors to the Wells.

While Madame Bernstein's chief sitting-room might not suit a recluse or a scholar, it was perfect for those who relished bustle, gaiety, bright light, and a view of the lively scene unfolding around them. Once the windows were lit, pedestrians walking below knew instantly that her ladyship was hosting a card assembly, accessible to anyone willing to seek an introduction. Speaking of the past, the night-life of society a century prior was undeniably darker. There was scarcely one candle for every ten seen in modern drawing rooms, let alone gaslight or electric illumination. Horrible guttering tallow smoked and stank through the passages; the candle-snuffer was a notorious officer in the theater. One need only look at Hogarth's pictures to see the darkness, the feasts begrimed with dripping fat. In "Marriage à la Mode," within Lord Viscount Squanderfield's grand saloons, where he and his wife yawned before a horror-stricken steward after their party, there were but eight candles—one per card table and half a dozen in a brass chandelier. If Jack Briefless had convoked friends to oysters and beer in his chambers at Pump Court, he would surely have employed double the number. Yet, we may comfort ourselves by recalling that Louis XIV held his revels in shadow, and blessing the likes of Mr. Price and other luminous benefactors who banished the abominable gloom of our ancestors' youth.

Maria, herself the fairest flower among them, arranged roses, sweet-williams, and other blossoms into vases scattered throughout the apartment, adorning the space with the best of her art. She lingered fondly over this bowl and that dragon-jar, casting only sly, timid glances at young cousin Harry. Her own blush would have become any young woman, and one might have surmised she intended to outstay her aunt indefinitely. However, the Baroness, seated in her armchair with a crooked tortoiseshell stick in hand, pointed imperiously to the servants, rating them soundly. She scolded Tom for a darn in his stocking and John for having greased his locks too profusely from the candle-box, maintaining a stern domination over the household. Another remark concerns the plight of James a hundred years ago: he slept two to a bed, four to a room—likely a cellar—and washed in a trough rarely seen outside the barracks of Her Majesty's Foot Guards today.

If Maria hoped for a present interview, her fond heart was disappointed. "Where are you going to dine, Harry?" asked Madame de Bernstein. "My niece Maria and I shall have a chicken in the little parlour—I think you should go to the best ordinary. There is one at the White Horse at three; we shall hear his bell in a minute or two. And you must understand, sir, that you ought not to spare expense, but behave like Princess Pocahontas's son. Your trunks have been taken over to the lodging I have engaged for you. It is not good for a lad to be always hanging about the aprons of two old women, is it, Maria?"

"No," said her ladyship, dropping her meek eyes, whilst the other lady glared in triumph. I think Andromeda had been exposed to the Dragon significantly during the last five or six days; if Perseus had cut off the latter's cruel head, he would have committed no unjustifiable monsticide. But he bore no sword or shield; he merely looked mechanically at the lacqueys in tawny and blue as they creaked about the room.

"And there are good mercers and tailors from London always here to wait on the company at the Wells. You had better see them, my dear, for your suit is not of the very last fashion—a little lace..."

"I can't go out of mourning, ma'am," said the young man, looking down at his sables.

"Ho, sir!" cried the lady, rustling up from her chair and rising on her cane. "Wear black for your brother till you are as old as Methuselah, if you like. I am sure I don't want to prevent you. I only want you to dress, and to do like other people, and make a figure worthy of your name."

"Madam," said Mr. Warrington with great state, "I have not done anything to disgrace it that I know."

Why did the old woman stop and give a little start as if struck? Let bygones be bygones. She and the boy shared a score of little passages where swords were crossed and thrusts rapidly dealt or parried. She liked Harry none the worse for his courage in facing her. "Sure, a little finer linen than that shirt you wear will not be a disgrace to you, sir," she said with a rather forced laugh.

Harry bowed and blushed. It was one of the homely gifts of his Oakhurst friends. He felt pleased somehow to think he wore it, thought of the new friends—who were so good, so pure, so simple, so kindly—with immense tenderness. While invested in this garment, he felt as if evil could not touch him. He said he would go to his lodging, making a point of returning arrayed in the best linen he possessed.

"Come back here, sir," said Madame Bernstein, "and if our company has not arrived, Maria and I will find some ruffles for you!" And under a footman's guidance, the young fellow walked off to his new lodgings.

Harry found not only handsome and spacious apartments but a groom in attendance waiting to be engaged by his honor, and a second valet, should he wish to hire one to wait upon Mr. Gumbo. Ere many minutes had passed in his rooms, emissaries from a London tailor and bootmaker awaited him with cards and compliments from their employers, Messrs. Regnier and Tull. The best articles in his modest wardrobe were laid out by Gumbo alongside the finest linen his thrifty Virginian mother had provided. Visions of the snow-surronded home in his own country, of crackling logs and trim, quiet ladies working by the fire, rose before him. For the first time, a small thought crossed his mind that the homely clothes were not quite smart enough, that the home-worked linen lacked the finesse it might possess. That he should feel ashamed of anything belonging to him or to Castlewood! That was strange. The simple folk there were only too well satisfied with all things done, said, or produced at Castlewood; and Madam Esmond, when sending her son forth on his travels, thought no young nobleman needed better provision. The clothes might have fitted better and been of a later fashion, to be sure—but still, the young fellow presented a comely figure when he issued from his apartments, his toilet complete. Gumbo called a chair, marched beside it, until they reached the ordinary where the young gentleman was to dine.

Here, he expected to find the beau whose acquaintance he had made a few hours before at his aunt's lodging, who had indicated that the White Horse was the most modish place for dining at the Wells. He mentioned his friend's name to the host, but the landlord and waiters, leading him into the room with many smiles and bows, assured his honor that he needed no other introduction than his own. They helped him hang up his coat and sword on a peg, asked whether he would drink Burgundy, Pontac, or champagne to his dinner, and led him to a table.

Though the most fashionable ordinary in the village, the White Horse did not happen to be crowded on this day. Monsieur Barbeau, the landlord, informed Harry that a great entertainment at Summer Hill had taken away most of the company. Indeed, when Harry entered the room, there were but four other gentlemen present. Two were drinking wine and had finished their dinner; the other two were young men in the midst of their meal. As the landlord passed, he must have whispered the newcomer's name to them, for they looked at him with some appearance of interest and made a slight bow across the table as the smiling host bustled away for Harry's dinner.

Mr. Warrington returned the salute of the two gentlemen, who bade him welcome to Tunbridge and expressed hope that he would like the place upon better acquaintance. Then they smiled and exchanged waggish looks with each other, of which Harry did not understand the meaning, nor why they cast knowing glances at the two other guests over their wine.
