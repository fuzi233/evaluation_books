*"The unripe grape, the ripe, and the dried. All things are changes, not into nothing, but into that which is not at present."*

Time pulses with deeds—each act, righteous or wicked, sends vibrations into eternity, echoing long after the moment has passed.

That evening, she summoned him again. The light was soft, the blinds drawn back, the windows open to the sighing sea. She sat motionless, her cheek resting on her hand, her gaze fixed on the water. Though less shattered than before, a deep melancholy shadowed her face, shifting into anxious hesitation as Deronda approached.

She did not offer her hand. Instead, she murmured, "How long ago it all seems." Then, softer still: "Will you sit beside me for a little while?"

He took his place near her, as he had done earlier. She turned to him, lips parting as if to speak, then faltered, her eyes drifting back to the window. The words died unspoken, held back by fear.

Deronda, sensing her struggle, averted his gaze to ease her distress.

At last, she whispered, "You won’t tell anyone else?"

"Never," he said firmly. "There is nothing to be done—no wrong to right, no justice to demand. Some burdens are ours alone to bear."

She was silent for a long moment, as if holding her breath. Then, in a voice barely audible: "But if I hadn’t hesitated—if I had thrown the rope at once—could I have stopped what happened?"

"No," he answered gently. "Even at your quickest, it would have been too late. The will to harm may poison our own souls, but it does not always shape the world outside us. What matters is what we do with that knowledge—how we let it change us."

Her fingers twisted in her lap. "I thought—I thought I could justify it all. That I could take what I wanted and make it right. But everything became twisted. And then the hatred came, and the terrible thoughts. I was afraid of myself."

Her voice broke. "You can’t even look at me now. You think I’m beyond redemption."

His heart ached. He turned to her, meeting her desperate gaze. "I believe you can become better than you have ever been. No one is doomed unless they choose to cling to their own darkness. You *have* tried. You will keep trying."

"But you must help me," she pleaded, her hands clasped tightly. "You must stay near me. If you had been there—if I could have spoken to you—things might have been different. Promise you won’t leave me."

His reply was swift, though he felt the weight of it settle upon him. "I will not forsake you."

Yet even as he spoke, uncertainty coiled in his chest. Promises made in sorrow could become chains in daylight. He steadied himself. "Sir Hugo will arrive soon. And your mother may follow. Her presence will give you strength."

She nodded absently, her thoughts drifting. "You’ll live with Sir Hugo now, won’t you? At the Abbey, or Diplow?"

His expression shifted slightly. "I don’t yet know where I will be."

She caught the evasion and fell silent. After a pause, she murmured, "I don’t know how to go on. Perhaps it would be better if I had nothing—if I had to work like others do."

"New purpose will come," he reassured her. "For now, focus on healing. Be as much yourself as you can before—"

"Before my mother sees me," she finished quietly. "Yes. I must be different now."

She turned to him suddenly. "Would you have recognized me? If we had met like this, after everything—would you have known it was me?"

His voice was soft. "Yes. The sorrow is in your eyes, not your face."

Her tears spilled over. "Don’t regret knowing me. Don’t say you wish we had never met."

"I could never wish that," he said. "Regret is useless. We find our duty in what *is*, not what might have been."

"You saved me," she whispered. "Without you, I would have been worse."

He rose, weary from the weight of the moment. "Rest now. Prepare yourself for those who love you."

She gave him her hand, submissive, but the moment he left, she collapsed to her knees, weeping as though her heart would break. The distance between who she had been and who she might have been was too vast to cross.

When she was found later, crumpled on the floor, it seemed only natural—such grief was to be expected from a woman who had watched her husband drown before her eyes.
