The air in the square thickened, not with heat, but with the static charge of impending violence. A murmur coalesced into a shout, then a roar. "Kneel," someone hissed, the voice trembling with the desperation of a man trying to buy time. "Kneel, sir, or you will be seen." Another voice, louder and uglier, screamed, "Kneel, you without beard, and do obeisance to the only Goddess, or by the old Gods I will make myself her priest and butcher you!" The demand for submission was absolute, a collective scream demanding the erasure of individuality in favor of divine order.

Yet, amidst the rising tide of fanaticism, a single name cut through the noise like a knife through wet wool: Deucalion. As the word spread, the rhythm of the mob shifted. The enthusiasm, previously a chaotic storm, began to curdle into calculation. They knew little of the Deucalion who had abandoned Atlantis twenty years prior to rule the distant lands of Yucatan; that man was a ghost. But they remembered the Deucalion who had ridden beside the Empress only days ago, beneath the canopy of snakes in the golden castle. The memory of his proximity to power acted as a damper on their rage. The shouts did not vanish; they simply lost their edge, becoming limp, weighted by the pragmatic fear of vengeance.

Silence, tentative and brittle, finally reclaimed the circle. In that vacuum, Phorenice turned. She saw me standing alone, a solitary vertical line amidst a sea of prostrate bodies. I stepped forward, moving between two massive stones until I reached the altar where she waited. I did not kneel. At the prescribed distance, I offered the salutation she had commanded when I became her chief minister—a gesture of formal decorum rather than religious surrender—and hailed her as Empress.

"Deucalion, man of ice," she retorted, her voice cool despite the chaos surrounding us.

"I still adhere to the old Gods!" I replied, the statement hanging in the air like a challenge.

"I was not referring to that," she said, tilting her head with a sidelong smile that suggested amusement rather than anger.

Just then, Ylga approached. Her face was the color of ash, her hands shaking violently. She threw herself between us, pleading for my life. "If he will not leave the old Gods yet," she stammered, "surely you will pardon him? He is a strong man, and does not become a convert easily. You may change him later. But think, Phorenice, he is Deucalion; and if you slay him here for this one thing, there is no other man within all the marches of Atlantis who would so worthily serve—"

The Empress seized the words from her throat. "You slut," she shrieked, the insult sharp and final. "I have you near me to appoint my wardrobe, to carry my fan, and do you dare to put a meddling finger on my policies? Back with you, outside this circle, or I'll have you whipped. Ay, and I'll do more. I'll serve you as Zaemon served my captain, Tarca. Shall I point a finger at you, and smite your pretty skin with a sudden leprosy?"

Ylga bowed her shoulders, defeated, and retreated into the shadows. Phorenice turned her attention back to me. "My lord," she said, her tone shifting to something lighter, almost playful. "I am like a young bird in the nest that has suddenly found its wings. Wings have so many uses that I am curious to try them all."

"May each new flight they take be for the good of Atlantis."

"Oh," she said, her eyes flashing with a sudden intensity, "I know what you have most at heart. But we will go back to the pyramid, and talk this out at more leisure. I pray you now, my lord, conduct me back to my riding beast."

It became clear that my refusal to offer worship was to be condoned, a minor infraction overshadowed by the larger political theater. Similarly, Ylga's intervention was dismissed as untimely meddling. Though the logic eluded me, I accepted the outcome, recognizing that these concessions advanced the scheme Zaemon had entrusted to me.

As the Empress extended her fingers—warm, delicate instruments capable of grasping the hilt of a weapon—I took them gravely. Together, we exited the great circle, leaving the profanation behind. I had expected the Lord Sun to strike down the sacrilege in that very moment, but the heavens remained silent. I understood then that retribution was a matter of timing, reserved for a chosen instrument, and I would not raise a puny arm to guard His honor prematurely.

We mounted the huge red mammoth, which stood tamely in the street, smelling faintly of the sacrifices performed earlier. Phorenice climbed the stairs to the golden saddle, bidding me take the seat beside her. The spot usually occupied by the fan-girl was empty, leaving us alone in the high perch. As we rode back through the crowded streets, the noise of the city faded into a dull hum, allowing us private conversation.

She was eager to hear of my encounter at the gate. I recounted the tale, emphasizing the worthiness of Nais and suggesting that with patience, the girl might be won back to allegiance. I withheld the specific commands Zaemon had given me in the sacred tongue, for such matters were forbidden to repeat outside the High Council sitting before the Ark of the Mysteries.

"You seem to have an unusual kindliness for this rebel Nais," Phorenice observed.

"She showed herself to me as more clever and thoughtful than the common herd."

"Ay," she sighed, a sound that seemed genuine enough, "an Empress loses much that meaner woman gets as her common due."

"In what particular?"

"She misses the honest wooing of her equals."

"If you set up for a Goddess—" I began.

"Pah!" she interrupted. "I wish to be no Goddess to you, Deucalion. That was for the common people; it gives me more power with them; it helps my schemes. All you Seven higher priests know that trick of calling down the fire, and it pleased me to filch it. Can you not be generous, and admit that a woman may be as clever in finding out these natural laws as your musty elder priests?"

"Remains that you are Empress."

"Nor Empress either. Just think that there is a woman seated beside you on this cushion, Deucalion, and look upon her, and say what words come first to your lips. Have done with ceremonies, and have done with statecraft. Do you wish to wait on as you are till all your manhood withers? It is well not to hurry unduly in these matters: I am with you there. Yet, who but a fool watches a fruit grow ripe, and then leaves it till it is past its prime?"

I looked at her, at her glorious beauty, but the sight left me cold. I recalled the command binding me and forced a smile. "I may have been fastidious," I said, "but I do not regret waiting this long."

"Nor I. But I have played my life as a maid, time enough. I am a woman, ripe, and full-blooded, and the day has come when I should be more than what I have been."

I clenched my hand around hers. "Take me to husband then, and I will be a good man to you. But, as I am bidden speak to Phorenice the woman now, and not to the Empress, I offer fair warning that I will be no puppet."

She glanced at me sideways, a flicker of triumph in her eyes. "I have been master so long that I think it will come as enjoyment to be mastered sometimes. No, Deucalion, I promise that—you shall be no puppet. Indeed, it would take a lusty lung to do the piping if you were to dance against your will."

"Then, as man and wife we will live together in the royal pyramid, and we will rule this country with all the wit that it has pleased the High Gods to bestow on us. These miserable differences shall be swept aside; the rebels shall go back to their homes, and hunt, and fight the beasts in the provinces, and the Priests' Clan shall be pacified. Phorenice, you and I will throw ourselves brain and soul into the government, and we will make Atlantis rise as a nation that shall once more surpass all the world for peace and prosperity."

Petulantly, she withdrew her hand. "Oh, your conditions, and your Atlantis! You carry a crudeness in these colonial manners of yours, Deucalion, that palls on one after the first blunt flavor has worn away. Am I to do all the wooing? Is there no thrill of love under all your ice?"

"In truth, I do not know what love may be. I have had little enough speech with women all these busy years."

"We were a pair, then, when you landed, though I have heard sighs and protestations from every man that carries a beard in all Atlantis. Some of them tickled my fancy for the day, but none of them have moved me deeper. No, I also have not learned what this love may be from my own personal feelings. But, sir, I think that you will teach me soon, if you go on with your coldness."
