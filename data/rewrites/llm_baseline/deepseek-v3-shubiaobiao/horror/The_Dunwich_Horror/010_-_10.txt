The three men from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, his beard white as bone, Professor Rice, broad-shouldered and iron-haired, and Dr. Morgan, lean and tense—climbed the mountain alone. They had left the telescope with the trembling crowd below, instructing them on its use before their ascent. The path was treacherous, and more than once, Armitage needed a steadying hand. Above them, the unseen horror moved with slow, deliberate malice, bending the trees in its wake.

Curtis Whateley—one of the few untouched by decay—watched through the lens as the scholars veered sharply from the creature’s path. Their goal was clear: a lesser peak that overlooked the swath ahead. Wesley Corey, gripping the telescope next, saw Armitage adjusting the sprayer in Rice’s hands. A murmur rippled through the crowd—this was the moment the thing would be revealed.

Then it happened.

A gray cloud, vast as a barn, flickered into existence near the summit. Curtis screamed. The telescope fell into the mud as he staggered back, eyes wild, mouth working soundlessly before he gasped:

The others seized him before he collapsed. Henry Wheeler snatched up the instrument, wiping mud from the lens, but Curtis could barely speak. His words came in ragged bursts:

*“Bigger than a barn… ropes, writhing… legs like barrels… jelly, shifting… eyes bulging… mouths—stovepipes, opening, closing… and the face—that half-face on top!”*

He fainted.

The crowd erupted into panic. Wheeler lifted the telescope again. Three figures stood at the summit, distant but clear. One raised his arms in rhythmic motion, and faint chanting carried on the wind. The whippoorwills shrieked in the valley below, their cries forming a dissonant counterpoint to the ritual above.

Then the sky darkened—not with clouds, but with something deeper, purpler. Thunder rumbled, though no storm brewed. The chanting grew louder. Lightning flashed, and for an instant, a mist seemed to cling to the altar-stone.

Then the voice came.

Not human. Not earthly. A sound torn from the gulfs between stars, a croaking, shuddering blasphemy that vibrated in the bones before it reached the ears:

The words faltered, then surged again, rising to a shrieking crescendo:

And then—silence.

A deafening crack split the air. A bolt of lightning seared down from the purple-black sky, striking the altar-stone. An invisible force roared across the hillside, flattening grass, twisting trees, choking the crowd with a stench like opened graves. Dogs howled in the distance. The whippoorwills fell dead, their bodies littering the withered earth.

When the Arkham men descended, their faces were ashen.

“It’s gone,” Armitage said, hollow-voiced. “Shattered back into what it was. A thing that should never have been.”

Curtis stirred, clutching his head, the horror still fresh in his mind.

Old Zebulon Whateley, his voice a dry whisper, spoke at last:

“Fifteen years gone… Old Whateley said we’d hear Lavinny’s child call its father’s name on Sentinel Hill…”

Armitage’s gaze was grim.

“Burn the diary,” he said. “Destroy the stones. What we saw today was only a fraction of what waits outside. And it hungers.”
