“Your cousin the Countess called on mother while you were away, Janey,” Janey Archer announced as soon as her brother had taken his seat at the small evening table.

Newland had expected, after his hurried flight back from Florida, the soft safety of familiar rooms: the measured scrape of cutlery, the reliable hush of old carpets, the mild crackle of a coal fire. Even the house’s sounds seemed trained to good behavior. Yet at Janey’s words he looked up sharply, and the knife in his hand paused above the roast as if it had forgotten its office.

Across from him Mrs. Archer’s eyes remained lowered to her plate. She did not approve of astonishment in her dining-room; surprise was a kind of noise.

“She had on a black velvet polonaise with jet buttons,” Janey went on, quickened by the importance of being first with a novelty, “and a tiny green monkey muff; I never saw her so stylishly dressed. She came alone, early on Sunday afternoon—luckily the fire was lit in the drawing-room. She had one of those new card-cases. She said she wanted to know us because you’d been so good to her.”

Newland laughed, and the laugh sounded too loud against the thick damask quiet. “Madame Olenska always takes that tone about her friends. She’s very happy at being among her own people again.”

“Yes, so she told us,” said Mrs. Archer. There was a faint emphasis on “told,” as if gratitude, like gravy, could be poured too freely. “I must say she seems thankful to be here.”

“I hope you liked her, mother.”

Mrs. Archer drew her lips together. “She certainly lays herself out to please, even when she is calling on an old lady.”

“Mother doesn’t think her simple,” Janey interposed, her eyes screwed upon her brother’s face with the exacting curiosity of one who has never missed a syllable at table.

“It’s just my old-fashioned feeling; dear May is my ideal,” Mrs. Archer said, cutting her meat with the conscientious neatness that was her manner of opinion.

“Ah,” Newland said, with the helpless honesty of a man who could still taste Florida oranges on his tongue, “they’re not alike.”

He had come back from St. Augustine with messages for old Mrs. Mingott, and within a day or two he went up to her house. The city’s winter streets met him with their iron-clad clatter—hooves, wheels, shouted names, the thin grind of sleigh-runners where the snow had not yet turned to soot. Yet Catherine Mingott’s drawing-room absorbed noise the way a great kitchen absorbs steam: thick curtains, heavy upholstery, and the soft, continuous rustle of servants moving as silently as well-trained hands at a sideboard.

The old lady received him with unusual warmth. She was grateful—practically jubilant—that he had persuaded Ellen Olenska to give up the idea of divorce; and when he confessed, with a shame that still carried a private sweetness, that he had deserted the office without leave and rushed down to St. Augustine simply because he wanted to see May, she gave an adipose chuckle and patted his knee with her puff-ball hand.

“Ah, ah—so you kicked over the traces, did you? And I suppose Augusta and Welland pulled long faces, and behaved as if the end of the world had come? But little May—she knew better, I’ll be bound?”

“I hoped she did; but after all she wouldn’t agree to what I’d gone down to ask for.”

“Wouldn’t she indeed? And what was that?”

“I wanted to get her to promise that we should be married in April. What’s the use of our wasting another year?”

Mrs. Manson Mingott screwed up her little mouth into a grimace of mimic prudery and twinkled at him through malicious lids. “‘Ask Mamma,’ I suppose—the usual story. Ah, these Mingotts—all alike! Born in a rut, and you can’t root ’em out of it. When I built this house you’d have thought I was moving to California! Nobody ever had built above Fortieth Street—no, says I, nor above the Battery either, before Christopher Columbus discovered America. No, no; not one of them wants to be different; they’re as scared of it as the small-pox. Ah, my dear Mr. Archer, I thank my stars I’m nothing but a vulgar Spicer; but there’s not one of my own children that takes after me but my little Ellen.”

She broke off, still twinkling, and asked with the casual irrelevance of old age, as if tasting a thought and finding it oddly pleasant: “Now, why in the world didn’t you marry my little Ellen?”

Newland laughed. “For one thing, she wasn’t there to be married.”

“No—to be sure; more’s the pity. And now it’s too late; her life is finished.”

She spoke with the cold-blooded complacency of the aged throwing earth into the grave of young hopes. A chill passed through him, sudden as stepping from sun into shade, and he hurried on: “Can’t I persuade you to use your influence with the Wellands, Mrs. Mingott? I wasn’t made for long engagements.”

Old Catherine beamed on him approvingly. “No; I can see that. You’ve got a quick eye. When you were a little boy I’ve no doubt you liked to be helped first.”

She threw back her head with a laugh that made her chins ripple like little waves. “Ah, here’s my Ellen now!” she exclaimed, as the portières parted behind her.

Madame Olenska came forward with a smile. Her face looked vivid and happy, as if some inner lamp had been trimmed; she held out her hand gaily to Archer while she stooped to her grandmother’s kiss.

“I was just saying to him, my dear: ‘Now, why didn’t you marry my little Ellen?’”

Madame Olenska looked at Archer, still smiling. “And what did he answer?”

“Oh, my darling, I leave you to find that out! He’s been down to Florida to see his sweetheart.”

“Yes, I know.” She still looked at him, steadily, as if listening for the undertone beneath his words. “I went to see your mother, to ask where you’d gone. I sent a note that you never answered, and I was afraid you were ill.”

He muttered something about leaving unexpectedly, in a great hurry, and having intended to write to her from St. Augustine.

“And of course once you were there you never thought of me again!” She beamed on him with a gaiety so bright it seemed almost a shield—a deliberate brightness, like a dining-room lit too early to hide a foggy afternoon.

If she still needs me, she’s determined not to let me see it, he thought, stung by her manner. He wanted to thank her for going to his mother, for crossing that threshold and facing that gentle scrutiny on his behalf; but under the ancestress’s malicious eye his gratitude stuck in his throat like dry bread.

“Look at him—in such hot haste to get married that he took French leave and rushed down to implore the silly girl on his knees!” cried the old lady. “That’s something like a lover—that’s the way handsome Bob Spicer carried off my poor mother; and then got tired of her before I was weaned—though they only had to wait eight months for me! But there—you’re not a Spicer, young man; luckily for you and for May. It’s only my poor Ellen that has kept any of their wicked blood; the rest of them are all model Mingotts.”

Archer was aware that Madame Olenska, who had seated herself at her grandmother’s side, was still thoughtfully scrutinising him. The gaiety had faded from her eyes; her voice, when she spoke, had the gentleness of one laying a cloth smoothly over a troubled surface.

“Surely, Granny, we can persuade them between us to do as he wishes.”

Newland rose to go, and as his hand met hers he felt—distinctly—that she was waiting for him to speak of her letter, to give it its due place between them. The silence was another kind of power, and he hated that he obeyed it.

“When can I see you?” he asked, as she walked with him to the door of the room.

“Whenever you like; but it must be soon if you want to see the little house again. I am moving next week.”

A pang shot through him at the memory of those lamplit hours in the low-studded drawing-room, where even the air had seemed different—smelling faintly of tea and old books and whatever foreign, unnamed thing she carried with her. Few as they had been, they were thick with memories.

“Tomorrow evening?”

She nodded. “Tomorrow; yes; but early. I’m going out.”

The next day was Sunday, and if she were going out on a Sunday evening it could only be to Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s. The idea pricked him—less because she went where she pleased (he liked that stubborn liberty in her, in spite of the van der Luydens), than because it was the kind of house where Beaufort would be, and she must have known it—might even have counted on it.

“Very well; tomorrow evening,” he repeated, inwardly resolved he would not go early. He would reach her door late enough either to keep her at home or to miss her altogether; and either course, he told himself, would be simpler.

Yet when the hour came he could not bear to wait. A singular restlessness drove him through the streets—the city’s Sunday hush broken by the occasional rattle of a carriage and the distant, thin tolling of a church bell that seemed to measure not devotion but constraint. It was only half-past eight when he rang the bell under her wisteria—not as late as he had intended by half an hour.

He reflected, as he stood in the narrow hall, that Mrs. Struthers’s Sunday evenings were not like a ball; people went early, as if punctuality could absolve them. But he had not counted on finding hats and overcoats there.

Why had she bidden him to come early if she was having people to dine?

As Nastasia took his coat, his resentment gave way to curiosity. The garments laid on the chair were the very strangest he had ever seen under a polite roof; and a glance assured him neither belonged to Julius Beaufort. One was a shaggy yellow ulster of “reach-me-down” cut; the other a very old, rusty cloak with a cape—something like what the French called a Macfarlane. It seemed made for a person of prodigious size, and its greenish-black folds gave out a moist, sawdusty smell suggestive of long sessions against bar-room walls. On it lay a ragged grey scarf and an odd felt hat of semiclerical shape.

Archer lifted his brows at Nastasia. She lifted hers in return with a fatalistic “Gia!” and flung open the drawing-room door.

His hostess was not there.

Instead he saw another lady standing by the fire, and beside her, in a cloud of cigar-smoke, two men in morning clothes they had evidently not taken off since morning. The room itself had the familiar scent of Madame Olenska’s house—flowers, beeswax, a hint of old leather and tea—but it was overlaid now by tobacco, thick and masculine, clinging to curtains and upholstery like an intruder who means to stay.

The lady was long, lean, and loosely put together. Her raiment was intricately looped and fringed, with plaids and stripes and bands of plain colour disposed in a design whose clue seemed missing. Her hair, which had tried to turn white and only succeeded in fading, was surmounted by a Spanish comb and a black lace scarf; silk mittens, visibly darned, covered her rheumatic hands.

One of the men Archer recognised, to his surprise, as Ned Winsett. The other, older and unknown, had the gigantic frame that must have filled the Macfarlane; his head was feebly leonine, his crumpled grey hair unkempt, and he moved his arms with large pawing gestures, as though distributing lay blessings to a kneeling multitude.

All three stood on the hearth-rug, their eyes fixed on an extraordinarily large bouquet of crimson roses, with a knot of purple pansies at their base, lying on the sofa where Madame Olenska usually sat.

“What they must have cost at this season—though of course it’s the sentiment one cares about!” the lady was saying in a sighing staccato as Archer came in.

They turned at his entrance. The lady advanced at once, holding out her hand.

“Dear Mr. Archer—almost my cousin Newland!” she said. “I am the Marchioness Manson.”

Archer bowed, and she continued with a fluttering importance: “My Ellen has taken me in for a few days. I came from Cuba, where I have been spending the winter with Spanish friends—such delightful distinguished people: the highest nobility of old Castile—how I wish you could know them! But I was called away by our dear great friend here, Dr. Carver. You don’t know Dr. Agathon Carver, founder of the Valley of Love Community?”

Dr. Carver inclined his leonine head.

“Ah, New York—New York—how little the life of the spirit has reached it!” the Marchioness went on, as if the city’s noises were proof of its moral defect. “But I see you do know Mr. Winsett.”

“Oh, yes—I reached him some time ago; but not by that route,” Winsett said, with his dry smile.

The Marchioness shook her head reprovingly. “How do you know, Mr. Winsett? The spirit bloweth where it listeth.”

“List—oh, list!” interjected Dr. Carver in a stentorian murmur, as if even words should be commanded into obedience.

“But do sit down, Mr. Archer,” the Marchioness urged. “We four have been having a delightful little dinner together, and my child has gone up to dress. She expects you; she will be down in a moment. We were just admiring these marvellous flowers, which will surprise her when she reappears.”

Winsett remained on his feet. “I’m afraid I must be off. Please tell Madame Olenska that we shall all feel lost when she abandons our street. This house has been an oasis.”

“Ah, but she won’t abandon you. Poetry and art are the breath of life to her. It is poetry you write, Mr. Winsett?”

“Well, no; but I sometimes read it,” Winsett said; and with a general nod to the group he slipped out of the room, as quietly as if he were escaping an argument rather than a drawing-room.

“A caustic spirit—un peu sauvage,” the Marchioness murmured. “But so witty; Dr. Carver, you do think him witty?”

“I never think of wit,” said Dr. Carver severely.

“Ah—ah—you never think of wit! How merciless he is to us weak mortals, Mr. Archer!” She sighed as if the sigh were a form of prayer. “But he lives only in the life of the spirit; and tonight he is mentally preparing the lecture he is to deliver presently at Mrs. Blenker’s. Dr. Carver, would there be time, before you start for the Blenkers’ to explain to Mr. Archer your illuminating discovery of the Direct Contact? But no; I see it is nearly nine o’clock, and we have no right to detain you while so many are waiting for your message.”

Dr. Carver looked slightly disappointed, but, having compared his ponderous gold time-piece with Madame Olenska’s little travelling-clock, he reluctantly gathered up his mighty limbs.

“I shall see you later, dear friend?” he suggested to the Marchioness.

“As soon as Ellen’s carriage comes I will join you,” she replied with a smile. “I do hope the lecture won’t have begun.”

Dr. Carver looked thoughtfully at Archer. “Perhaps, if this young gentleman is interested in my experiences, Mrs. Blenker might allow you to bring him with you?”

“Oh, dear friend, if it were possible—I am sure she would be too happy. But I fear my Ellen counts on Mr. Archer herself.”

“That,” said Dr. Carver, “is unfortunate—but here is my card.”

He handed it to Archer. In Gothic characters it read:

Agathon Carver  
The Valley of Love  
Kittasquattamy, N. Y.

Dr. Carver bowed himself out. The Marchioness, with a sigh that might have meant either regret or relief, waved Archer to a seat again.

“Ellen will be down in a moment; and before she comes, I am so glad of this quiet moment with you.”

Archer murmured something civil. The air in the room seemed thick with competing scents: the cigar-smoke still hanging in the curtains, the roses’ extravagant sweetness rising in warm waves, and beneath it all the faint, domestic odor of supper recently eaten—soup, roast, bread warmed and broken, the kitchen’s invisible labor absorbed into the drawing-room as politely as possible. It struck him with sudden bitterness how easily other people’s hands could handle the food, the flowers, even the story of a life—portion it, season it, rename it.

“I know everything, dear Mr. Archer,” the Marchioness continued. “My child has told me all you have done for her. Your wise advice: your courageous firmness—thank heaven it was not too late!”

Embarrassment reddened his mind. Was there anyone to whom Ellen had not proclaimed his intervention? He heard, in the Marchioness’s voice, the relish of repetition—how a tale grows glossy in the telling, like a sauce stirred too long.

“Madame Olenska exaggerates,” he said. “I simply gave her a legal opinion, as she asked me to.”

“Ah, but in doing it—in doing it you were the unconscious instrument of—of—what word have we moderns for Providence, Mr. Archer?” She tilted her head and drooped her lids mysteriously, as if searching her memory for a fashionable term. “Little did you know that at that very moment I was being appealed to: being approached, in fact—from the other side of the Atlantic!”

She glanced over her shoulder, as though fearful of being overheard, then drew her chair closer and raised a tiny ivory fan to her lips. Behind it she breathed: “By the Count himself—my poor, mad, foolish Olenski; who asks only to take her back on her own terms.”

“Good God!” Archer exclaimed, springing up. The words tore out of him before he could hush them, and the room seemed suddenly too small for the sound.

“You are horrified? Yes, of course; I understand,” the Marchioness said, unruffled. “I don’t defend poor Stanislas, though he has always called me his best friend. He does not defend himself—he casts himself at her feet: in my person.” She tapped her emaciated bosom. “I have his letter here.”

“A letter? Has Madame Olenska seen it?” Archer stammered, his thoughts whirling so fast he could hardly hold them.

The Marchioness shook her head softly. “Time—time; I must have time. I know my Ellen—haughty, intractable; shall I say, just a shade unforgiving?”

“But, good heavens, to forgive is one thing; to go back into that hell—”

“Ah, yes,” the Marchioness acquiesced. “So she describes it—my sensitive child! But on the material side, Mr. Archer, if one may stoop to consider such things; do you know what she is giving up?”

Her hand drifted toward the roses on the sofa, as though their fragrance were an argument. “Those roses there—acres like them, under glass and in the open, in his matchless terraced gardens at Nice! Jewels—historic pearls: the Sobieski emeralds—sables—but she cares nothing for all these! Art and beauty, those she does care for, she lives for, as I always have; and those also surrounded her. Pictures, priceless furniture, music, brilliant conversation—ah, that, my dear young man, if you’ll excuse me, is what you’ve no conception of here! And she had it all; and the homage of the greatest. She tells me she is not thought handsome in New York—good heavens! Her portrait has been painted nine times; the greatest artists in Europe have begged for the privilege. Are these things nothing? And the remorse of an adoring husband?”

As the Marchioness rose to her climax her face took on an expression of ecstatic retrospection, as if she were tasting a vanished feast and finding it richer than anything set before her now. Archer might have laughed at the absurdity, if he had not been numb with amazement. It was as if a whole foreign vocabulary had been poured into this little room—titles, pearls, emeralds, Nice—words meant to outweigh Ellen’s own, to translate her suffering into commodities. He felt, with sudden clarity, the power of such a lexicon: how it could rename a prison “a palace,” and call endurance “a wife’s duty.”

“She knows nothing yet—of all this?” he asked abruptly.

Mrs. Manson laid a purple finger on her lips. “Nothing directly—but does she suspect? Who can tell? The truth is, Mr. Archer, I have been waiting to see you. From the moment I heard of the firm stand you had taken, and of your influence over her, I hoped it might be possible to count on your support—to convince you…”

“That she ought to go back? I would rather see her dead!” Archer cried, the violence in him breaking loose like a pot boiling over.

“Ah,” the Marchioness murmured, without visible resentment.

For a while she sat opening and shutting the absurd ivory fan between her mittened fingers. The fan made a small, repetitive whisper—paper against air—like a private language of impatience. Then she lifted her head and listened.

“Here she comes,” she said in a rapid whisper; and then, pointing to the bouquet on the sofa, she added softly, insistently, as if pressing the last spoonful of a bitter remedy: “Am I to understand that you prefer that, Mr. Archer? After all, marriage is marriage… and my niece is still a wife…”
