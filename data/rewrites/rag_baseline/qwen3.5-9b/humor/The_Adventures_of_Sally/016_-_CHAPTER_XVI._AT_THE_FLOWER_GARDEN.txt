The air in the Flower Garden tasted of stale perfume and heated desperation. Mr. Reginald Cracknell stood trembling, his voice a wet, self-pitying rasp that competed poorly with the orchestra's relentless, mechanical thrumming.

"After all I've done for her," Cracknell whispered, his eyes glistening with the specific moisture of over-indulgence, "she throws me down."

Sally did not answer. Her feet were already failing her, stumbling erratically against his jerky, drunken steps. They navigated the crowded floor with the precision of two lost ships drifting in a fog bank, passing the table where Miss Mabel Hobson sat. Hobson, draped in old gold and black, watched the chaos with a regal, distant hauteur. As Cracknell shambled beneath her gaze, a sorrowful gulp escaped his throat.

"If I told you," he moaned, leaning close to Sally's ear, "what I had spent on that woman..."

His foot descended heavily on hers. Sally gasped, a stifled sound swallowed by the din. Cracknell seized upon the reaction as a sign of sympathy.

"I don't mean you're like that," he declared, his tone shifting instantly to gallantry. "You're different. I saw it the moment I laid eyes on you. You possess a sympathetic nature. A sensible, broad-minded girl. You understand."

He listed his sacrifices: the job secured, the pearls gifted, the star billing endured. All for a hat. A rotten hat, he insisted, which she refused to wear. Now silence. A week of radio silence.

"What would you do?" he asked, tears pooling in his pale, watery eyes. "I come here every night. I dance past her table until my legs give out. She won't look at me."

"I don't know," Sally said, her voice flat.

"Nor do I," Cracknell replied, seizing the ambiguity. "That's why I'm asking you. Keep this secret. I'm bringing a present tonight. A small one. It should soften her."

"A bigger one would work better," Sally offered.

Cracknell kicked her shin—a clumsy, dismayed gesture—and recoiled. "Perhaps you're right. Too late now. But maybe? Tell me, what do you think?"

"Yes," Sally said.

"I thought as much."

The orchestra crashed to a halt with a final, percussive thud. Cracknell clapped weakly in the center of the floor, a solitary figure in a sea of motion. Sally retreated to her table. Her former partner, having momentarily misplaced his dignity, zig-zagged away to find his seat. The room erupted into conversation, a cacophony drowning out the music. Heat pressed against Sally's temples, triggering a sharp, throbbing headache.

Nearly a month had passed since her return to Mr. Abrahams' employ. Time had stretched into a dull, leaden monotony, a series of lifeless days where existence felt like a bad dream. She felt severed from her own kind, a ghost haunting her own life. No familiar faces crossed her path; Fillmore avoided her, perhaps guilt-ridden, while Ginger pursued his destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

She lowered her hands, forcing her eyes open to survey the room. It was packed, as always. The Flower Garden was one of the many establishments that had ridden the wave of New York's dancing craze to popularity. Its proprietor, Isadore Abrahams, claimed it was a "supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing," a "wonder-place" with an "incomparable dance-floor" and "service de luxe." The claim held water; unlike its rivals, it maintained a steady, unvarying prosperity.

Once, Sally had found the place diverting, stimulating. Those days were gone. Now, the nightly work was a burden, weighed down by a listless depression that made the music feel like a hammer against her skull.

"Miss Nicholas."

The orchestra surged forward again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies with his gold teeth flashing, presented a new partner. Sally rose mechanically.

"This is the first time I've been in this place," the man announced as they collided onto the floor. He was large, clumsy, and possessed a gravity that made the entire room feel heavier. "We got nothing like this up-state."

He used Sally as a battering ram to clear space, and despite the pain of her earlier encounter with Cracknell, a strange wistfulness touched her. This man was undoubtedly the worst dancer in America.

"Give me li'l old New York," he said, unpatriotic and loud. "Good enough for me. Seen the 'Follies' this year?"

"No."

"You go!" he urged earnestly. "'Myrtle takes a Turkish Bath'? You seen that?"

"Not really."

"It's a scream! I've been to a show every night since arriving. Except this last one. I picked a lemon tonight, honest. 'The Wild Rose.' Made me wild all right. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away."

Something stirred in Sally's memory. The title rang a bell. Then, a shock of recognition hit her. It was Gerald's new play. For weeks, she had feared running into him outside her apartment, until a newspaper paragraph confirmed he was touring. "The Wild Rose" was the name.

"Is that Gerald Foster's play?" she asked quickly.

"I don't know who wrote it," the stranger replied, lighting a cigar with theatrical flair. "But let me tell you, he's lucky to be alive. Fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road have done less to deserve a sentence. 'Wild Rose!' I'll tell the world it made me go good and wild. Before the second act ended, people were beating for the exits. Someone shouted 'Women and children first,' or there'd have been a panic."

Sally found herself back at her table, the transition seamless and unremembered.

"Miss Nicholas."

This time, the voice was not Schoenstein's. The speaker had seated himself beside her, speaking in precise, clipped accents that sounded oddly familiar. The haze in her eyes cleared. It was Bruce Carmyle.

"I called at your place," Carmyle said, lighting a cigarette with an air of controlled composure. "The hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow. Hope you don't mind? May I smoke?"

His fingers trembled as he raised the match, betraying the violent excitement he strove to conceal. Bruce Carmyle's ideal was the strong man who rises superior to his emotions. He was acutely aware of the embarrassment but determined not to show it. He glanced sideways at Sally, noting with satisfaction that she looked prettier than ever, surpassing even the memory of Monk's Crofton.

"When did you land?" Sally asked, needing to fill the silence. Her mind was a chaotic jumble; she couldn't decide if his presence was a relief or a threat. Yet, there was something in his dark, cool, stiff English aspect that offered a curative calm. He was so remote from the feverish madness of the club that simply looking at him was restful.

"To-night," Carmyle said, turning to face her squarely.

"To-night!"

"We docked at ten."

He turned away, satisfied with the impact of his statement.

Sally remained silent. The significance of his arrival was undeniable; it was a challenge she must answer. Yet, it barely stirred her. She had fought so long, and now she felt utterly inert, like a swimmer exhausted beyond recovery, preparing to surrender to the numbness. The heat of the room pressed down like a smothering blanket. Her nerves screamed under the blare of music and the clatter of voices.

"Shall we dance this?" he asked.

The orchestra launched into a sensuous, creamy melody, a Broadway hit played to death by a hundred repetitions.

"If you like."

Efficiency was Carmyle's gospel. He did not attempt anything he could not execute perfectly. Dancing, he had decided early in life, was essential to a gentleman's education. As they swept onto the floor, Sally braced herself for the usual collision, the clumsy struggle typical of the Flower Garden. Instead, she found herself in the arms of a masterful expert. He danced better than she did.

Suddenly, a feeling washed over her—not love, but something akin to gratitude. A miraculous slackening of her taut nerves. A delicious peace. Soothed and contented, she yielded to the rhythm, her eyes half-closed. The music, stripped of its stale cheapness by the magic of the movement, transformed her attitude toward him completely.

She had never examined her feelings for him closely, but one thing was clear since their first meeting: he was physically distasteful. Despite his good looks and a certain sinister charm, she had always shrunk from him. Now, spirited away by the dance, that repugnance vanished. It was as if a barrier had crumbled.

"Sally!"

He tightened his arm around her, muscles quivering. She caught his face. His dark eyes blazed into hers, and she stumbled, struck by a sudden, overwhelming helplessness. With a shock that jerked her out of the half-dream, she realized the dance had not postponed the inevitable; it had accelerated it. In a hot whisper, carried away by the suddenly raucous music, he repeated the words he had spoken under the trees at Monk's Crofton that spring morning.

Resentment flared briefly at the unfairness of the timing, but her mind was numbed.
