The plan was simple, gilded, and utterly cinematic: arrive in New York, commandeer a suite at the St. Regis, and let the weight of her fortune wash over her before retreating to her modest, permanent sanctuary. But as she handed the taxi driver his fare at the dock, the script faltered. The idea felt less like a triumph of class and more like a scene from a melodrama she despised—a female Fillmore, playing at grandeur until the final act. Prudence dictated a temporary retreat to Mrs. Meecher's boardinghouse, a place where the air smelled of boiled cabbage and nostalgia, preferable to the moral stain of appearing frivolous. Home, she decided, was where the heart lay, even if the pantry stock consisted mostly of prunes deemed unsuitable for a gourmet palate.

Perhaps it was the comfort of doing the right thing, or perhaps the electric hum of anticipation regarding Gerald Foster, that transformed the familiar streets into a canvas of brilliant color. The morning was a study in crisp perfection: azure skies, amber sunlight, and ash-cans that gleamed with a deceptive vitality. The streetcars groaned with happy commuters; policemen directed traffic with jaunty indifference; white-clad sweepers performed their poetic duties with quiet relish. None of them knew she had returned, yet the city itself seemed to perform a silent ovation, as if the mere fact of her arrival warranted a special day.

The overture shattered instantly with Mrs. Meecher's voice. With a mixture of maternal pride and theatrical flair, the landlady announced that Gerald Foster had departed for Detroit that very morning, accompanied by Miss Doland. The handyman, wielding Sally's trunk as a blunt instrument against the hallway wallpaper, was dismissed with a sharp reprimand. "Monday," Mrs. Meecher noted, her tone dripping with insider knowledge, "they begin the trials of his new piece. Rehearsals have been underway since you vanished."

Disappointment prickled at Sally, yet the morning's brilliance refused to dim. Detroit could wait. There was always tomorrow. "Is Elsa in the company?" she asked, seeking a lifeline.

"Indeed," Mrs. Meecher confirmed, eager to dispense gossip. "And remarkably talented. Mr. Faucitt stopped by a rehearsal yesterday; he declared Miss Doland exquisite. He is notoriously difficult to please."

"How fares Mr. Faucitt?"

The landlady seized the opportunity to dampen Sally's spirits. "Poor soul. He succumbed to a severe headache last night and retired early. I visited this morning; he looks wretched. There is a plague of Spanish influenza sweeping the city. People are dying, if you read the papers correctly."

"Good heavens," Sally gasped. "Do you suspect..."

"He hasn't turned black yet," Mrs. Meecher conceded with regretful pragmatism. "Though the papers claim they do eventually. Naturally, that may happen later. The doctor arrives shortly. He seems content. Toto is keeping him company."

Sally's anxiety spiked. Everyone who called the boardinghouse a home shared her fierce loyalty to Toto, a shaggy, woollen creature whose reputation for canine virtue was tenuous at best. His persistent, high-pitched yapping was a torture device for the healthy and an impossibility for the infirm. Yet Mrs. Meecher, convinced of the beast's therapeutic powers despite seven years of evidence to the contrary, believed Toto was performing adequate nursing duties.

"I must ascend," Sally cried. "Poor old dear."

"Certainly. You know his quarters. Listen," Mrs. Meecher instructed, "Toto is conversing with him. He demands a cracker. That is his sole requirement post-breakfast."

Inside the room, Mr. Faucitt's weary eyes flickered toward the door. Upon recognizing Sally, they ignited with a miraculous, incredulous radiance. Any visitor would have sufficed; indeed, his patience had long expired. But Sally? To him, her arrival signaled a resurrection of the golden age.

"Sally!"

"One moment. Here, Toto!"

The dog, momentarily stunned by the prospect of sustenance, leaped from the bed, tilting his head in suspicion. He followed Sally into the corridor, where she tossed the cracker and barred the door. After a couple of yaps—perhaps gratitude, perhaps baffled rage—he trotted downstairs. Mr. Faucitt exhaled sharply.

"Sally, you return as an angel of mercy. Mrs. Meecher possesses a heart of gold, unmatched in kindness, yet she errs in believing her hound is a combination of nurse, medicament, and seaside cure. She insisted on bringing him here. He yapped then, as he yapped when you, with remarkable feminine ingenuity, spirited him away. Each bark pierced me like a hammer strike on tin sheets. You stand alone among women. You shine like a good deed in a corrupt world. When did you return?"

"Just now, in my hired barouche from the pier."

"And you came directly? I am honored. My dear Sally."

"Naturally. Would you expect me to discuss the weather after hearing you were ill? What do you mean by frightening everyone? Darling, do you feel terrible?"

"A thousand mice gnaw my spine, and I crave cool refreshment constantly. But your presence is a tonic. Tell me, how did our Sally endure foreign travel?"

"Our Sally lived her life."

"England?"

"Merely a transit."

"What did it resemble?"

"Moist. Excessively moist."

"It would," Mr. Faucitt mused indulgently. "Though I confess, amidst my happiness here, I occasionally pine for those London days. The cozy brown mist, the pavements sweating mud and water, the distant yellow glow of the Bodega lamps cutting through the haze like harbor lights. Not that I exclude Rule's or Short's; I found pleasant hours there too. You missed something, Sally, by not lingering."

"I know I did—pneumonia."

Mr. Faucitt shook his head reproachfully. "Prejudiced. You would have loved London had you the courage to brave its superficial gloom. Paris?"

"Partially. The remainder was by the sea. Glorious. I never would have returned had necessity not compelled me. Still, I wished to see you all. And to witness Mr. Foster's premiere. Mrs. Meecher mentioned you attended a rehearsal."

"I witnessed a dogfight labeled a rehearsal," Mr. Faucitt corrected sternly. "Rehearsal is a concept extinct in this era."

"Oh dear! Was it truly so dire?"

"The play is excellent. It possesses substance. But the acting..."

"Mrs. Meecher reported you praised Elsa."

"Our hostess did not distort your words. Miss Doland holds great promise. She recalls Matilda Devine, under whose banner I once performed at the Old Royalty in London. She carries the seeds of greatness, yet they are sown in barren soil for an insignificant role. There is only one part in the play—the one murdered by Miss Mabel Hobson."

"Murdered!" Sally's heart plummeted. Fear had already whispered this possibility to her, and hearing it confirmed was no comfort. "Is she frightful?"

"She possesses the visage of an angel and the dramatic capacity of that peculiar suet pudding Mrs. Meecher serves on Fridays. In my career, I have encountered the 'Lady Friend' masquerading as a star, but Miss Hobson eclipses them all. Recall the year '94, when a plutocrat presented a female object of his affection in a role demanding supreme talent. I supported her. At the first rehearsal, I told my friend Arthur Moseby—an excellent youth, alas, cursed with the habit of lifting his elbow—that I gave the venture two weeks. 'Max,' he replied, 'you are an incurable optimist. One consecutive night, lad.' We wagered a half-crown. He won. We opened at Wigan, the lead actress deserted us, and the show closed the following day. Watching Miss Hobson, I was forcibly reminded of that disaster."

"Oh, poor Gerald—"

"I do not share your pity for that young man," Mr. Faucitt stated austere. "Though you may be unfamiliar with him, he and I have crossed paths recently. Success, if it ever arrives, will destroy him. I dislike him, Sally. Without exception, he is the most selfish, self-centered youth I know. He resembles old Billy Fothergill, with whom I toured in the late eighties. Shall I recount the tale of Billy and the amateur..."

Sally felt no inclination to listen. The old man's innocent critique of Gerald stung deeply. The urge to defend him died instantly upon seeing Mr. Faucitt's pale, exhausted countenance. He meant no malice; how could he comprehend Gerald's significance to her? She shifted the topic abruptly.

"Have you seen Fillmore during my absence?"

"Fillmore? Curiously, I encountered him on Broadway a few days ago. He seemed altered—less rigid, less aloof. Perhaps unfairly judged, but lately he appeared slightly elevated. All that stiffness vanished. He greeted me warmly."

Sally regained her composure. Her lecture at the party had clearly resonated. However, Mr. Faucitt offered a counter-theory.

"I suspect," he ventured, "that the softening influence stems from his fiancée."

"What? Fillmore is not engaged?"
