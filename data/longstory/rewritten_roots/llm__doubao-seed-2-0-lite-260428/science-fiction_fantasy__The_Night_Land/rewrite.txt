The old half-forgotten verses hummed unbidden under my breath as the sun bleeded slow gold into the far horizon:
> *And I cannot touch her face
> And I cannot touch her hair,
> And I kneel to empty shadows--
> Just memories of her grace;
> And her voice sings in the winds
> And in the sobs of dawn
> And among the flowers at night
> And from the brooks at sunrise
> And from the sea at sunset,
> And I answer with vain callings ...*

It was the rapture of that very sunset that first drew us to speak. I had wandered miles from my estate, walking slow and alone, pausing again and again to stare up at the stacked, glowing battlements of cloud that rose across the western sky, and to drink in that soft, strange hush that descends over all the world as dusk creeps in.

The last time I halted, I was lost entirely to the solemn, thrumming joy of the coming dark. I laughed quiet, half to myself, standing there solitary in the gathering grey on the roadside. And then—someone answered that quiet laugh from the hedgerow trees to my right, a voice bright with glad recognition, as if it had spoken soft: *“And thou, too.”* I laughed again, throat tight, half-convinced no real human soul shared that exact mood with me—half-sure it was some sweet phantom, some spirit tuned to the same dusk-wrought rapture as my own.

But she called my name. I stepped to the verge of the road, squinting through the gloom to make her out, and my breath caught. It was her: Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, whose renown had spread through every village and farm of that soft Kentish countryside, and whose guardian’s vast estates marched directly against my own. We were distant cousins, as it turned out, though we had never once met before that night. I had spent years abroad on travels, and when I was home I buried myself in my books and my training—I had always been a natural athlete, quick and strong enough that no man I ever met could outmatch me, save in idle tales or the boasts of fools.

I doffed my hat immediately, and fumbled to reply to her gentle teasing, all the while peering at her through the dimming light. Rumour had never done her justice. She laughed unconstrained, bade me drop the formalities, called me by my boyhood name, and gave me full leave to call her nothing but Mirdath. She pointed out a little hidden gap in the hedge that she said was her secret escape route, the one she and her maid used to slip away disguised as village girls for unplanned little country frolics.

I clambered through that gap to stand beside her. She was tall, but I stood a full head higher. She invited me to walk with her back to her hall, and teased me gently for the years I’d neglected to call on her guardian. Then, in an instant, she went still, held a finger to her lips to hush me, and nodded toward the thick wood that bordered our path to the right. I heard it too: rustle of underbrush, then the sharp, clear crack of a dead twig in the still dusk.

Three men burst out of the trees at me, no cry, no warning. I shoved Mirdath behind my back with my left hand, hefted the solid oak staff I carried on all my walks, and shouted for them to stand off. I caught the glint of blades in the fading light, and moved fast, sharp, happy to test my strength before her. Behind me, a shrill silver note cut the air: she had blown the whistle that called her great boar-hounds.

But no help was needed. I drove the end of my staff hard into the gut of the leftmost man, and he crumpled like a sack of meal. A sharp crack of the staff across the second man’s skull dropped him where he stood. The third lunged, and I met him with a straight fist to the jaw, and he went down before he could even raise his knife. The whole fight was over before it had fairly begun, and I was half-smiling, quiet proud, as I turned back to see Mirdath staring at me, wide-eyed through the dark.

The three great hounds came bounding out of the wood moments later, spurred by her whistle, and she had to fight to hold them back from mauling the unconscious robbers. Minutes after that, lantern light bobbed through the trees, and her hall’s footmen came running, cudgels in hand, half-ready to mistake me for one of the attackers until they recognized me and saw the three bruised footpads splayed on the grass. Mirdath was watching me the whole time, and I could see that the easy, casual kinship she’d felt for me before that night had deepened into something far warmer. The servants asked what to do with the men as they began to come round, and I slipped them a handful of silver to see they got their just deserts—their distant yells of well-earned pain followed us up the drive long after we’d walked away.

She pulled me straight in to meet her guardian, old Sir Alfred Jarles, a venerable old gentleman I had long known in passing from estate business. She praised me to his face, half-teasing and half-earnest, and the old man thanked me so graciously that I was made a welcome guest at the hall from that hour onward. I stayed for dinner, and afterward we wandered out together into the park grounds, and she was warmer to me than any woman had ever been. It felt as if we had known each other our whole lives, every quirk and quiet joy of the other familiar, no surprise at all that we shared so many loves: the hush of evening, the strange glowing glamour of midnight, the soft magic of dawn.

One evening I will never forget, we wandered far into the park at dusk, and she half-mumbled that it was an elves-night. She stopped short, flushing, as if she expected me to laugh at the silly fancy. But my own heart leapt, and I answered quiet that the Towers of Sleep would rise that night, that I could feel it in my bones—that it was a night to find the Giant’s Tomb, or the Tree with the Great Painted Head, or— I cut off short, because she had gripped my arm hard, her hands shaking. She begged me breathless to go on, and I fumbled to correct myself, said I’d only meant the old, half-fanciful Moon Garden I had dreamed of as a boy.

At that, she gasped my name, and pulled me to a halt to face me. She questioned me, urgent, and I answered just as eagerly, and soon we both realized the impossible thing: we had both dreamed of the same strange, far-off dreamland, all our lives, and each of us had thought no other soul on earth knew it existed. Some of her dream-scapes were new to me, some of mine were foreign to her, but time and again one would start a half-remembered detail, and the other would finish it, the two of us giddy and amazed, wandering the park for hours talking of nothing else. We only realized how late it was when we heard the shouts of searchers and the baying of hounds coming for us, sent out by Sir Alfred, who had grown uneasy after the footpad attack. The dogs knew me by now, and bounded around us barking in delight, and the servants ran ahead to tell their master all was well.

That was how our acquaintance bloomed, and how my great, aching love for Mirdath the Beautiful began.

After that, I walked that country road every evening, slipping through that secret hedge-gap every time. I begged her to never wander the woods alone, to bring her three boar-hounds with her always, and she agreed, though she loved to tease me, to be willful and perverse, just to test how far I would let her go before I lost my temper.

One night I came to the gap and found two girls in rough village dress coming out of the wood, bobbing curtsies a little too graceful for farm maids. I suspected the taller one was Mirdath, so I followed them all the way to the village green, where a torch-lit dance was in full swing, a wandering fiddler scraping out a tune, ale flowing everywhere. They danced only with each other at first, doing their best to stay out of the torchlight, but when I stepped over and asked the taller one for a dance, she simpered and said she was promised, and immediately took the hand of a great lout of a farmer’s son, who stamped on her pretty feet the whole circuit of the green.

I knew for certain it was Mirdath then, and when I whispered her name and told her we were going home, she stamped her foot, turned away from me, and dragged the lout off for another dance, then asked him to walk her part of the way home. The moment they were out of sight of the torches, he and his friend grabbed at the girls’ waists, and Mirdath, furious and terrified, cried out my name. I grabbed the lout, hit him once hard enough to leave him with a throbbing memory for months, and tossed him off the side of the road. His friend ran for his life, everyone for miles knew how strong I was.

I grabbed Mirdath by the shoulders and shook her, furious at her recklessness, then sent her maid on ahead, and we walked quiet back to the hall. She was quiet all the way, but she never strayed far from my side, as if she was glad to be near me. The next day she was full of her usual cheeky teasing, until I told her how much I ached for her company, how scared I was for her safety. She went soft immediately, brought out her harp, and played all the old lullabies we had both known as children, and my love for her grew deeper still. I walked her back to the hall after she saw me to the hedge-gap that night, silent in the treeline until I saw her step safe through the door, even though she thought I was already long gone down the road.

The next evening, I arrived at the gap early, and found a foppish, finely dressed man with the look of the royal court leaning against it, talking to Mirdath. He refused to step aside to let me through, staring at me insolent, so I lifted him bodily out of my path. Mirdath rounded on me immediately, sharp and bitter, calling me uncouth, brutal, shaming me before the stranger so fiercely my heart ached. I bowed low to her, then apologized stiffly to the foppish little man, turned, and walked away. I tramped twenty miles that night, back to my estate, my chest tight with the first sharp, burning pain of losing the woman I had already grown to love more than life.

A week I took walks in every other direction, until I could not bear it any longer, and went back to the old road just to catch a glimpse of her. What I saw seared right through me: Mirdath walking at the edge of the wood, the foppish court man’s arm slung over her shoulders. She flushed bright red when she saw me, pushing his arm away and bowing, and I bowed low back, my heart feeling dead in my chest, and walked on, never looking back.

A month I stayed away, hurt in my pride, but that hurt and that love slowly softened me, taught me tenderness I had never known before. I started walking past the gap again, but I never saw Mirdath. Once, one of her boar-hounds came out of the wood and nuzzled my hand, friendly, before trotting back. Two more lonely weeks passed, until I could bear it no longer, and I slipped through the hedge-gap, making my way to the lawns around her hall. Lanterns strung in the trees, torches blazing, a great crowd dancing on the grass: it was her twenty-first birthday, the day her wardship ended. I hid in the treeline, watching her wander away from the dancers, pale and lovely, half a dozen young lords and gentry crowding around her, all vying for her favour. But her gaze kept drifting past them, out toward the trees, as if she was looking for someone. The court man was not there.

And I watched, heavy with longing and sharp, bitter jealousy, as she waited, and the night grew late, and the party went on without the man she loved.

Ever since Mirdath, my beautiful one, drew her last breath and left me adrift in a hollow, empty world, I have carried an agony of longing no words could ever rightly frame. I who once held all the world in her soft love and steady companionship, who drank deep of every joy life had to offer, now know a loneliness so sharp and total it stuns me even to turn my thoughts to it.

Yet here I sit, pen in hand again. A wondrous hope has taken root in me of late: night after night, I wake in my sleep not to my own dark bed, but to the far-flung future of this world, where I have walked through strange, impossible marvels and known the burn of life’s gladness once more. I have learned the promise woven into the very womb of Time, visited the places where she and I will meet again, pull apart in bitter, wounding sorrow, and find each other once more after vast stretches of uncounted ages, in a reunion bright enough to feel like a miracle made flesh.

This is the uncanny story of what I have seen. I must set it down, no matter how great the task, to ease the weight of longing in my own heart—and perhaps, give a spark of hope to some other suffering soul, pining as I do for a beloved one lost to death.

Some who read this will call it fantasy. Others will argue in its defence. I will say nothing to either, only “Read.” Once you turn these pages, you will have stood beside me at the very portals of Eternity.

The visions I speak of do not feel like dreams. They feel like waking: I blink and find myself standing in a world where the sun has long since guttered out, swallowed by the Everlasting Night that wraps the whole planet. Looking back to my own present age from that future feels like looking back on a vivid dream my soul knows was real—yet seen through new eyes, it glows far off, hallowed with a soft, peaceful light of a long-lost dawn.

Every time I wake into that future, a blurring grey mist wraps around me at first, clearing slowly like a dissipating dark cloud until I can see the vast dark world spread out ahead, dotted here and there with uncanny, glowing sights. I do not wake to ignorance: I know the names and natures of every glow, every terror of the Night Land, as naturally as a man waking from sleep in our own time knows his name, his home, the world he has grown up in. Beneath that native knowledge of the dark future, a quiet subconscious memory of my own present life lingers, sharpened only by the raw, hungry longing for Mirdath.

In my earliest clear memory of that place, I am seventeen years old, standing in an embrasure carved high in the side of the Last Redoubt: that gargantuan grey metal pyramid that shelters the last remaining millions of humanity, holding them safe from the inhuman Powers that hunt them beyond its walls.

I know every inch of that place so well it feels impossible no one in my own time has ever heard of it. I may speak too casually of its wonders and terrors, forgetting to explain things that will be utterly alien to you, reading this now. When I first woke there, I was not the grieving man of my own era, but that seventeen-year-old boy, full of all the natural, lived-in knowledge of seventeen years spent inside the Redoubt. Until that first vision, the man I am now had no inkling that other, future existence was waiting for me—but I woke to it as easily as you wake in your bed to the morning sun, knowing the world you open your eyes to. Even as I stood there in that vast metal embrasure, the memory of my present life lay deep under my new awareness, haloed like a half-remembered dream, but thrumming with a quiet, aching longing for the one my half-memory called Mirdath.

I leaned against the cold metal lip of the embrasure, staring northwest through a strange, polished spyglass, young, bold, and thrumming with a faint, delicious fear. I carried every lesson I had learned growing up inside the Redoubt in my bones: I could name every landmark spread across that dark landscape, rattle off the exact distance of each from the polished mathematical Centre-Point, the dimensionless marker set in the heart of the Redoubt’s Mathematics chamber where I studied every day.

Through the glass, the bright, roiling glow of the Red Pit blazed up, striking the underside of the massive, jutting “chin” of the Northwest Watcher: the ancient Watching Thing that had stood sentinel in that spot since the dawn of recorded time, never moving, never blinking. The old lines of Aesworpth, the ancient poet whose work had survived uncounted thousands of years, drifted through my head: *That which hath Watched from the Beginning, until the opening of the Gateway of Eternity*. But suddenly, the words felt incomplete. I looked deep inside myself, and saw the sunlit splendour of my own present age rising out of my soul like a memory made flesh. I was stunned.

I must make this clear to you: the boy I was, standing in that embrasure in the far future, woke that moment to a sudden, sharp awareness of my own present life, a past so far back it felt like the very dawn of eternity. We were the same soul. He saw vaguely the life I am living now, while I, the man of the present, looked forward to the life I will one day live. How strange, how impossibly tangled the threads of our existence are!

Before that awakening, the boy had only a dim, misty vision of that ancient, sunlit past, a haze that had confounded the learned men of the Redoubt time and time again. But from that moment onward, that half-memory hardened into something sharp, real, ten times more vivid. The hunger of my love for Mirdath leapt across the gulf of ages, turning that vague dream-memory into a searing, living pain of loss. From that moment on, I did nothing but listen, my whole life spent straining to hear any hint that she was out there, somewhere in the dark of the future.

I wrenched my thoughts back from that haze of longing, turning my gaze back to the unthinkable mystery of the Night Land spread before me. No one who lived in the Redoubt ever tired of watching that hideous, vast landscape: young and old alike stood watch from childhood to death, staring out at the black monstrosities the Redoubt’s walls kept at bay.

To the right of the Red Pit stretched the long, winding glow of the Vale of Red Fire. Beyond it, mile after mile of dreary blackness stretched out, lit by the cold, eerie blue radiance that drifted up from the Plain of Blue Fire. At the very edge of the Unknown Lands, a line of low volcanoes cast their light far out into the outer dark, gilding the tops of the Black Hills, where the Seven Lights burned. They never twinkled, never moved, never faltered for all Eternity, and not even the greatest, most powerful spyglass in the Redoubt could make any sense of them. No adventurer who had ever set out from the Pyramid to find them had ever returned to tell their story. The Great Library of the Redoubt held full accounts of every one of those lost explorers, their discoveries and their fates, men who had risked not just their bodies, but their very souls venturing out into the Night Land’s monstrousness.

The Last Redoubt’s own histories did not span thousands of years. They spanned millions, stretching back to what the people of that far future considered the world’s earliest infancy, when the sun still glowed dully, hanging low in the sky. Anything before that point was only myth, a story wise men treated with deep caution, never trusting it fully. Even the lullabies my future self’s nurse sang to him as a small child spoke of the sun, a mythical thing the children of the Redoubt half-believed was just a fairy tale.

To my right, far away to the North, the House of Silence stood atop a low hill. It glowed with steady, unwavering lights, and no sound had ever drifted out of it for as long as memory stretched. Not even the most sensitive distance-microphones the Redoubt had ever built could pick up a single whisper. It was accounted the single most dangerous place in all the Night Lands. Winding around it was the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk, a path leading out of the Unknown Lands, past the mist-shrouded home of the Ab-humans. It was the only structure out in the dark that the people of the Redoubt knew had been built by human hands, long long ago. Thousands upon thousands of books had been written arguing over its origins, all contradicting each other, none reaching any conclusion. Entire libraries had been filled with treatises on every monstrous sight, every uncanny glow in the Night Land, millions of pages that had crumbled to dust in the eras long before the Redoubt’s recorded history even began.

I stepped onto the central moving roadway that ran across the 1000th plateau of the Redoubt. It sat six miles and thirty fathoms above the dark plain, more than a mile wide. A few minutes ride brought me to the southeastern wall, where I looked out through the Great Embrasure at the Three Silver-Fire Holes, glowing far off in the southeast, right in front of the Thing That Nods. Closer to me, the vast bulk of the Southeast Watcher loomed, a squat, lumbering living mountain. Torches burned half a mile on either side of it, casting light that showed the brute’s massive head, lurching slowly forward through the dark, never sleeping.

It was the quiet hours of Sleeping-Time on the 1000th plateau, when I heard a far, dreadful sound roll up out of the lightless East. Then it came again: a strange, deep laughter, rumbling like thunder trapped between mountains. It drifted out of the unknown, unvisited stretch of land beyond the Valley of the Hounds, and humanity had named that no-man’s-land The Country Whence Comes The Great Laughter. I had heard the sound hundreds of times before, but every time it made my heart race, made me feel small, acutely aware of the utter terror that had shadowed the last millions of human beings on the planet.

When the laughter faded back into the eastern dark, I turned my spyglass on the Giants’ Pit, just south of the Giants’ Kilns. The giants tended those kilns, their fire burning red and fitful, casting wavering, dancing shadows across the pit’s mouth. I could see the huge, shapeless forms of giants clambering up out of the depths, their forms never quite clear through the shifting light. Behind the pit, a massive black headland jutted up between the Valley of the Hounds, home to the monstrous Night Hounds, and the Giants’ territory. The light of the kilns gilded its top edge, and I saw things time and time again peer over the lip, creep a little into the light, then dart swiftly back into the shadows. That headland had been named The Headland From Which Strange Things Peer, marked clearly on every map of the grim Night Land for uncounted ages.

I turned my glass to the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk, scanning it as I had done a hundred times in my youth. The sight of those robed, silent figures always stirred some deep, nameless part of me. At last, I spotted one, alone on miles of empty, grey road, cloaked from head to toe, moving at a steady, unhurried pace, never looking right or left. Folklore in the Redoubt said they would never harm a human who kept a respectful distance, but no one in their right mind would ever dare step close to one.

My gaze followed the figure far down the road, past the stretch lit weirdly by the glow of the Silver-Fire Holes, then curving southeast past the dark bulk of the Dark Palace, running south until it curved west, past the mountainous form of the South Watcher—the biggest monster in all the visible Night Lands. He loomed over the pale, soft glow of the Glowing Dome, a brooding, living hill of watchfulness. The learned order of the Monstruwacans, whose sacred duty it was to track and record every movement of the monsters outside the Redoubt, had recorded its entire slow advance out of the Southern Unknown Lands, starting a million years prior. It had crept closer for twenty thousand years, moving so slowly no single human lifetime could ever measure the shift in its position. It had been marching steadily toward the Redoubt when the Glowing Dome had risen out of the ground directly in front of it, growing slowly for centuries. The dome had halted it in its tracks. For uncounted ages, it had squatted there, staring toward the Pyramid across the pale glow, unable to move any nearer.

The fact that the dome had stopped it was proof to many scholars that not all the powers in the Night Land were evil. Even as the forces of Darkness hunted the last of humanity to the edge of extinction, there were other, unseen forces fighting back, working in ways no human mind could ever hope to understand.

No one could say for certain where all these monstrous things and dark powers had come from. Their rise began long before the Redoubt’s written histories, even before the sun had guttered out completely. Old, half-forgotten records spoke of sciences far beyond anything my present age has ever dreamed of, that tore open the veil between our world and the unnameable Outer Dimensions. They broke the ancient barrier that kept ab-human monsters and uncanny spirits at bay, letting them pour through. Some materialized on the physical plane as grotesque, ravenous creatures. Others, that could not take solid form, gained the power to prey directly on the human soul. As the world descended into chaos and degeneracy, the last remaining millions of unbroken, sane human beings banded together to build the Last Redoubt.

The great metal pyramid rose eight miles high, with 1,320 floors built to withstand every conceivable assault. A watchtower sat at its very peak, where the Monstruwacans kept their endless vigil. When it was complete, the last humans retreated inside its walls, turning it into a vast, self-sustaining city. Thus began the Second Age of Man.

Over thousands of years, terrible half-human, half-beast races rose up in the outer dark, launching repeated, devastating assaults on the Redoubt, only to be beaten back with massive slaughter. Eventually, the inhabitants lit the massive electric barrier around the entire base of the Pyramid, drawing power from the Earth’s own eternal current. They sealed off the lowest half-mile of the structure completely, and a long, unbroken era of quiet watching began, as every generation waited for the day the Earth’s Current would finally run out.

Countless bands of brave, foolhardy explorers ventured out into the dark over the centuries, almost never to return. There were eyes everywhere in that dark, powers that saw everything. As the endless night dragged on, the terror only grew, drawing more and greater monsters out of the outer void, attracted to the single last spark of human light in the endless dark, like infernal sharks circling a lone ship.

That is the world I woke to, every time my soul slipped across the gulf of ages, to the Last Redoubt, standing sentinel against an endless, hungry night.

For years I had heard the same tale told, not just on the thousandth floor of our great city, but across all 1320 tiered cities of the vast Pyramid that made up the Great Redoubt: somewhere out in the endless, desolate dark of the Night Lands lay a second Place of Refuge, where the last millions of a scattered remnant of humanity had gathered to hold out against the horrors of the dying world until their final breath.

I heard this story everywhere during the great mandatory journey I began on my seventeenth birthday, a journey that stretched three years and 225 days, when custom dictated we spend exactly one day in every city of the Redoubt as part of our coming-of-age training. It was a wondrous, bittersweet pilgrimage: I met dozens of souls to know was to love, whom I would never lay eyes on again, for human life was too short, our duties to the safety of the Redoubt too unyielding, to make space for casual reunion. For all our constant movement across the tiers, there were so many millions of us, and so few years to spare. And everywhere I went, the legend of the second refuge followed. In the libraries I had time to visit, entire volumes were dedicated to proving its existence: texts from the oldest eras of our history spoke of it as undisputed fact, no room for doubt, while the modern scholars who pored over those same crumbling records now shook their heads and called it myth. So it always goes.

For my part, I never once doubted it. My certainty took root the day I first heard the story from Master Monstruwacan, who ruled the Observation Tower at the very apex of the Pyramid, with a staff of hundreds of assistants. The Master and I had always shared a rare, close affinity, even when I was still a boy and he a fully grown, revered leader, and when I turned twenty-one, he granted me a coveted post within the Observation Tower – a stroke of fortune almost unimaginable to most in the Redoubt, for to work there was the highest possible honor, feeding that innate human hunger to peer out past the walls of our shelter into the unknowable dark, just as astronomy once fueled curiosity in the long-lost days of the sun.

Lest you think he favored me out of friendship alone, there was a very practical reason for his choice: I bore a rare, strange gift we called Night-Hearing, a fanciful name for an ability almost no other human in the millions of the Redoubt possessed. I could pick up the invisible vibrations thrumming through the aether far clearer than any of our man-made recording instruments, no tools required to catch the faint signals drifting through the perpetual night. For four years I had stood at my post, listening, since that day I woke in a tower embrasure at seventeen with the memory of a lost, sunlit life humming in my bones. I listened for the voice of Mirdath the Beautiful, whose face and laugh had lived in my dreams across uncountable aeons, who felt like she slept tucked in the very core of my soul, whispering to me out of every forgotten age.

It was the fifteenth hour, the start of official Sleep-Time, and I was drifting in thought over that half-remembered old love, marveling that my memory could hold the sound of a voice from a life lived so many eras ago. The line between memory and fancy blurred so far I could almost believe she was standing right beside me, murmuring sweet nothings in my ear.

And then I thrilled so sharp it felt like a physical blow. Out of the endless dark, a soft, distinct whisper was rippling through my subtler senses.

I had waited four years for that. I did not cry her name aloud, trained as I was to be wary of the night's traps. I sent the sacred, shared Master-Word rippling out through the dark with the force of my own brain waves: a word proven across untold ages of trial that the malicious, predatory Forces of the Night Land could never mimic or repeat. If this was some lure set by the House of Silence, or one of the half-human monsters, they would have no power to answer.

I stood perfectly still, forcing my muscles to relax – I knew tension killed aetheric receptivity – and then the Master-Word came back, thrumming round and round my very spirit, beating steady through the dark like a living pulse. I called her name then, with every ounce of longing in my soul, sending *Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!* surging out into the void.

At that exact moment, Master Monstruwacan stepped into my section of the Observation Tower. He saw my face, and froze silent. He did not have the Night-Hearing gift himself, but he was wise, observant, and had just stepped away from the main receiving instrument, where he thought he'd caught the faintest possible blip of the Master-Word – too weak to register properly on the machinery, which was why he'd come looking for me, the only one alive who could pick up such faint signals.

I told him everything then: the half-memories, the dreams of the Days of Light when the sun still shone in the sky, the long years I'd spent listening. He listened with sympathy, no trace of mockery – in that age, humanity had refined the art of mental science to a degree we now can barely conceive, and men spoke of such experiences with calm sanity, not dismissed as lunacy. All the while I spoke, I kept my inner ear tuned to the aether, and caught nothing more than a faint, soft wrap of happy laughter, gone as quickly as it came.

My tales of the sunlit old days had already made me known across hundreds of the Redoubt's cities. The learned men of the libraries would argue with me, refusing to accept the Days of Light were real, while their hearts drank in my stories like poetry. The rest of the Observation Tower staff would creep over from their posts to listen for hours, until Master Monstruwacan roused himself from his own fascination to chide them back to their instruments, and they would scatter guiltily, all of them hungry to hear more. I could have spent every hour of the day talking, if not for my duties.

Even the lowest ploughboys working the Underground Fields knew my stories. Those Fields were the greatest work humanity had ever built, a tiered pyramid of fertile land carved 100 miles deep into the bedrock beneath the Redoubt. The lowest field stretched 100 miles across every direction, and the 306 fields above it tapered slowly in size, until the topmost field, sitting directly below the Redoubt's lowest floor, was only four miles wide. Every level was sheathed and pillared with the same strange grey alloy that formed the Redoubt's outer walls, so no burrowing monster from the outer dark could dig through. The entire vast underground land was lit and fed by the Earth-Current, the glowing life-stream that ran through the planet's core, feeding the soil, ripening the grain, powering soft, sweet winds that rustled the poppies and carried the scent of flowers across the fields, where millions of people strolled on their off-hours, unafraid.

There was a well-tested, unyielding law in the Pyramid: no male could venture out into the Night Land before the age of twenty-two, and no female could ever go. If a man of twenty-two chose to make the dangerous journey after receiving three full lectures on the horrid fates that befell past adventurers, he was granted honor for adding to the Redoubt's store of knowledge – but first, a tiny capsule was implanted under the skin of the inner side of his left forearm. If he was ever trapped by the dark Forces, he was to bite it open instantly, so his soul might find safety in a quick, painless death before the monsters could inflict their unthinkable tortures. That grim precaution alone tells you how terrible the danger of the outer dark truly was. Even back then, the thought of venturing out to follow that quiet calling had already begun to take root in my mind. I sent Mirdath's name out into the dark again and again, and sometimes I felt faint answering ripples in the aether, weak as if they were coming from a distant soul with a far weaker gift than mine.

Then, on the thirteenth hour of one ordinary workday, the entire aether around me thrummed so hard it felt like the whole void had stirred. I made the official sign for total silence, and every soul in the tower held their breath, leaning over their breathing bells to make no sound at all. The gentle thrumming returned, and broke out into a clear, low voice speaking directly inside my head: it called my true, old Earth name, the name no one in the Redoubt had ever known. I sent the Master-Word surging back out into the dark, and a heartbeat later, the steady throb of the answering Word wrapped all around me. I spoke her name out loud through the main instruments, and she answered back with that private, silly old love-name that only my Mirdath had ever used for me.

I was elated, giddy, until she told me the truth: her name was Naani, not Mirdath. She had found an ancient, crumbling book in the library of her own small refuge, the story of a girl named Mirdath and her lost lover, who bore my exact name and that exact pet name. When she'd first picked up my endless, desperate calls for Mirdath drifting through the dark, she'd answered as the story's heroine, half on a whim. For a moment I was gut-punched, my lifelong dream of finding my long-lost love dissolving into nothing. Then she said, offhand, that she'd half expected my voice to be deeper, and a jolt of pure hope shot through me: in that old sunlit life I remembered, my voice had been very deep.

The scale of what we had just done hit me then. Two humans, separated by a million years of total silence, speaking across hundreds of miles of pitch-black, monster-haunted Night Land – no wonder news of the signal spread from city to city across the Redoubt in hours, Hour-Slip newssheets blaring the story to every tier, millions crowding public comms feeds waiting for updates.

Naani told me the full story of her refuge then, a story that confirmed every old record the modern scholars had dismissed as myth. Theirs was a small, three-sided pyramid barely a mile tall, less than three quarters of a mile wide at its base. It had been built ages before by a band of wanderers who'd fled the Great Redoubt after a petty disciplinary sentence, who'd grown tired of running from the roaming half-human monsters that haunted the dark after the sun died. Their leader, an architect who had once lived in the Great Redoubt, had led four million other restless refugees to a valley along the shore of the old long-gone sea, where they'd found a small vein of the Earth-Current. They'd carved a deep shaft miles down, tapped the faint life-stream, and built their small refuge, setting up their own order of Monstruwacans, speaking back and forth with the Great Redoubt for untold ages. Then their weak Earth-Current began to fail. Their instruments lost power, the connection died, and a hundred thousand years of total silence fell over them. Their population dwindled to only ten thousand, no new children born for half a million years, their bodies aging too fast, their will to live fading. Then, mere months before Naani sent her call, their small vein of the Earth-Current had surged back to life. New children were born again, their people grew strong, and Naani, the Master Monstruwacan's daughter, revealed herself as the Sensitive they'd waited a hundred thousand years for. They dug up their long-forgotten old instruments, fixed them, and sent the quiet call out into the dark that found me at last.

("Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night-- Treading, as moon-flakes step across a dark-- Kissing the very dew to holier light ... Thy Voice a song past mountains, which to hark Frightens my soul with an utter lost delight.")

It drew near the end of the sixteenth hour, when the millions of the Mighty Pyramid had already settled to their long rest, and the great girders hummed soft with the steady thrum of the Earth-Current that held all the dark of the outer world at bay. I had stripped off my heavy observation leathers to turn to my cot, when the aether all about my skin thrilled sharp and electric, a sensation I had come to know well in those strange, heady days. But this thrill carried a weight no signal had ever borne before, and Naani’s voice bloomed plain in my bones, no faint transmission, no distant hum: it was as if she spoke pressed to my ear.

I did not answer at once. Wariness ran deep in all who dwelt in the Redoubts, for the Night Land teemed with things that mimicked voices to lure the unwary to their doors. I sent the steady pulse of the Master-Word rippling out into the dark, and it beat back to me at once, solemn, unmistakeable. I sent my query then: why did she speak to me through the Observation Tower’s Instrument at this hour, when the watch of her father’s Monstruwacans had long since settled, and her people’s sleep-cycle had run five hours deep? She should have been abed, nowhere near the aether-transmitters, far from any spot she could speak to me across the hundreds of miles of outer dark.

She sent no direct reply. Instead, she laid a feeling on my spirit that wracked me to the marrow, and the words drifted soft through my skull:
“Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--”

I trembled so hard my knees went weak. Those were my own words, the poem I had scrawled in charcoal on the stone slab above Mirdath the Beautiful’s grave, a hundred thousand eons prior, in the last days when the sun still hung in the sky, before the light died and the world turned to everlasting night. No living soul in this age could have known those lines. My heart reeled with the force of ancient, long-buried grief, and I called out across the dark to demand she explain how she had come by them.

Again she did not answer. She spoke the lines a second time, and the truth hit me like a blow to the chest: it was not Naani speaking, not at first. It was Mirdath, my lost love, reaching out across the gulf of all the dead ages. I cried her name out with every fibre of my brain, and the voice answered once more, and I knew then that it was both. Naani was Mirdath, reborn, the other half of my soul that had walked with mine through every turning of the world since the first light dimmed. I called out to her, screaming my need through the aether, for hours on end, until my vision blurred and my limbs gave out, and I fell into a deep, exhausted sleep.

I woke gasping, the memory of that transmission seared into the front of my mind. I called her name three times, sharp and clear, and the Master-Word throbbed back at once, steady in the hum of the aether. Her voice came to me then, faint, as it always was when she spoke without the aid of the Instrument, using only the power of her own mind. I pressed her at once for answers about what had passed while the rest of her people slept, and she replied, confused: she had no memory of speaking to me at all, she had slept the whole night through, save for one very strange, vivid dream.

She let the memory of it spill across the aether to me: in the dream, she had stood in a small, dim room where a tall, broad man in clothing no one had worn for a million years sat hunched over a slab, weeping so hard his shoulders shook. She could not read the script he wrote, it belonged to a dead language, but the name Mirdath was scrawled at the top of the page, and she could recall one single line of the verses he had set down. Then she spoke it, soft, across the miles of dark:
“Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--”

I filled in the rest of the poem, my soul singing, and I felt the words strike her mind like a blow. She went still, dumbstruck, for a long stretch, as the gates of her long-buried past memories creaked open. When she spoke again, her voice was breaking, thick with the tears of her spirit, and she asked, half awestruck, half afraid, if it could be true, if she was really that Mirdath I had loved across all the dead eons. I was so shaken I could not speak for a long minute, the blood thudding loud in my ears, until the words finally came, and we both wept across the empty dark, our spirits meeting at last after a hundred thousand years apart.

But the wonder of that moment only stoked the fire in my chest. I had to reach her, to stand beside her in the flesh, no longer speaking only through the thrum of the aether. There was one fatal problem: no record, no map, no scholar in all the Mighty Pyramid could pinpoint the location of the Lesser Redoubt. The only vague guess the Monstruwacans and students held was that it lay somewhere between northwest and northeast of our walls. No compass worked, not out here: the immense power of the Earth-Current gushing from the Crack beneath the Pyramid spun every needle wild, no matter how carefully you balanced it, so no direction held any certainty. The Master Monstruwacan of the Lesser Redoubt ran tests on their own ancient compass, and it swung perpetually between north and south, never settling, as if our own Earth-Current’s pull dragged at it faint across the miles.

It was useless, all of it. But I noticed a strange thing: no matter how carefully I was blindfolded, no matter how many lead screens the Monstruwacans wrapped around me to block out all aetheric signals, I would always turn, unconsciously, to face that vague northward direction, my feet pointing the way to Naani even when I had no logical reason to know which way north was. The Master Monstruwacan of our Great Redoubt wrote a whole study of the quirk, published it across the Hour-Slips, and soon every household in the Pyramid was pestering me for demonstrations, calling through their home Instruments to hear more of my power to speak across the dark. I grew tired of the endless attention, but I did not let it distract me: I still itched to set out, to walk the Night Land, no matter what horrors lurked there, to find her.

Then the terror came. It was the seventeenth hour, the entire Pyramid wrapped in sleep, and I was on duty in the Observation Tower beside our own Master Monstruwacan, when Naani’s voice cut through the aether, thin, fraying, so faint my Night-Hearing could barely make out the words. The Earth-Current powering the Lesser Redoubt had failed, all at once, without warning. Their wards were flickering out. Their lights were dying. The hordes of unnamed Things that pressed against their walls were creeping closer, and dozens of their people had already broken, mad with the raw, unshielded terror of the dark outside, ripped open the great gate of the Lesser Pyramid, and run out into the black to be torn apart, their souls scattered no one knew where.

For a month, we talked, two lovers on the edge of eternal parting. Her voice grew fainter, every day, as the last dregs of their dying Earth-Current sputtered out. The millions of our Great Redoubt grieved for them, wrote sad poems, drafted hundreds of grand, impossible rescue plans that no one ever attempted, the talk of safe, overfed people who had never stepped foot outside the Pyramid walls. But I knew, deep in my bones, that I would have to make the journey, no matter how many miles of monster-haunted dark stretched between us, even if I died halfway, it would be better than to sit here and hear the last of her voice go silent forever.

Then the eighteenth hour struck, and the Master Monstruwacan shook me roughly awake. The monitoring Instruments had picked up the pulse of the Master-Word, faint but steady, and none of his other Monstruwacans had the soul-sensitivity to tell where it was coming from. I sat up, and the words hit me, loud, near, so near I thought Naani was standing right outside the Great Door: “We are coming! We are coming!”

I sent the Master-Word rippling out at once. No reply came from that close, nearby spot. Instead, the faint, fraying pulse of Naani’s own answering signal drifted back to me, from hundreds of miles out in the dark. She told me she had heard that loud, near transmission too, woken from a half-sleep, and thought we had sent an expedition out to her. I calmed her, told her the signal had come from close to the Great Pyramid, not from anyone who had yet set out north, bade her rest, then turned to tell the Master Monstruwacan what I had sensed.

We climbed to the highest platform of the Observation Tower, and trained the great spy-glass out over the Electric Circle that girded the Pyramid’s base. There, marching steadily past the glowing ward-lines, were five hundred strong, armed young men from the upper cities, all heading north into the dark, towards the direction of the Lesser Redoubt. They had bound the sleeping watch at the Great Gate, gagged them, tampered with the alarm systems, and slipped out through the narrow Eye-Gate cut into the top of the great door, before anyone could raise a hue and cry. The Master Watchman soon confirmed it, when he arrived at the gate to free his bound men.

New rules were written that very day, new checks added to the watch protocols, nightly tests of every aetheric alarm in the Pyramid. But it was far too late. Those five hundred brave, reckless men were already gone, vanished into the endless, hideous dark of the Night Land, chasing the faint, fading voice of a people they would never meet, risking every monster and trap the outer world held to reach the Lesser Redoubt before its last light died. Naani’s voice was still there, faint, when I checked on her that night, but every hour it grew a little quieter, a little further away, and the hushing of her voice had only just begun.

After the massacre that swallowed the ten thousand souls who had ventured forth from the Great Redoubt, there could be no remaining illusion that rescue was possible. The youths who had followed the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk were already far beyond any mortal aid we could offer, their fates tangled in the unseen forces that ruled the endless dark outside the pyramid walls.

We dared not sound the Home-Call, that thunderous world-spanning voice broadcast from the great machine atop the sealed apex of the Mighty Pyramid. To trigger it would have alerted every watching, crawling, waiting horror in the Night Land that humans were abroad outside the redoubt’s sheltering walls. We could only cling to the frail hope that the dark evil Powers had not yet marked the small band of youths for their own; no soul within the pyramid could ever claim to know the full scope of the dark things’ awareness or their blind gaps of ignorance.

Even then, we had long detected a strange, soft, pervasive influence drifting across the dark land, faint enough that our instruments could do no more than trace its passage, no more. We had long suspected it emanated from the accursed House of Silence, perched on its low bleak hill far north of the Great Road. Many of the Monstruwacan observers feared it was reaching out to fasten upon the travelling youths, though no proof could be had to confirm the dread. I laid my uneasy premonitions before the Master Monstruwacan, who trusted my strange sight and intuitive awareness of the aether’s tides — for he too had long suspected a secret, unnameable power dwelt within that silent house.

At last the influence swelled, roiling across the land, and through the Great Spy-Glass I watched the youths veer sharply off the road, breaking into a wild, unthinking run straight for the distant House of Silence, as if their feet had no will of their own. Without hesitation the Master Monstruwacan gave the order, and the Home-Call boomed out across the dead world, its thunder reaching even the poor doomed young men who hastened unknowing to their destruction. Accompanying the great voice was a direct visual message, warning them they were being dragged to their deaths by a force originating within the House of Silence, beseeching them to marshal every shred of their spirit to fight the pull, and if they could not break its hold, to slay themselves swiftly before they crossed the house’s threshold to utter, irreversible annihilation.

A hush fell over the millions crowded in the pyramid’s five hundred thousand observation embrasures and before every public View-Table, for the roar of the Home-Call carried a weight of doom no soul could miss. Unbidden, the combined will and prayers of every living soul in the Mighty Pyramid wove into a single great counter-force that surged through the aether, so powerful it thrummed against my spirit, and for a heartbeat the youths stumbled to a halt, huddled confused in a crowd, as bewildered as sleepwalkers woken mid-step at the edge of a precipice.

A roar of triumph rose from the pyramid’s inhabitants, loud as a gale, but the hope was premature. The dark force emanating from the House of Silence snapped the collective psychic surge apart, and the youths were pulled once more into their mindless run. A shaken, sorrowful silence fell, broken by weeping — but in that same instant, billows of mist bloomed before the young men: pure, pale shining mist made of cold fire that cast no light, a barrier raised by one of the gentle, ever-watchful Powers of Good that warded human spirits from utter ruin. The Monstruwacans reported that the dark influence vanished entirely from their instruments, the House’s hold cut clean away.

The Master Monstruwacan sounded the Home-Call a second time, beseeching the youths for the sake of their mothers, for the sake of all they loved, to turn homewards while the blessed barrier held and sanity was returned to them. For a moment it looked as if they would comply — but their chosen leader, Aschoff, champion athlete of the Nine-Hundredth City, driven by bold, unthinking courage, strode forward and leapt straight through the shimmering wall of cold fire.

The mist dissolved instantly into nothingness, and Aschoff turned once more to run for the House of Silence, all two hundred and fifty of his pure-hearted, wayward comrades following faithfully at his heels. They scrambled up the low hill, through the ageless gaping doorway that had stood open since the world first fell to everlasting night, a doorway that exuded a thick, palpable silence that could be felt across the breadth of the Night Land. They vanished through the uncased, lightless windows and that open archway, and never were seen by human eyes again.

Their mothers and fathers, watching from the pyramid’s embrasures, saw every moment of that horror play out. A profound, unearthly quiet fell across the entire Night Land — a stillness so thick and dreadful that I would have welcomed the distant roar of the giants, the baying of the blind hounds, the uncanny great laughter that rolled across the dark from time to time, even the thin whine that drifted up from the Silver-fire Holes near the Thing that Nods. Any of those familiar, dreadful sounds would have been a mercy next to that utter silence, for no sound of the youths’ suffering ever reached even my Night-Hearing, not in that moment nor all the long years that followed. They had passed into a silence no mortal heart could bear to imagine.

It was plain now to every soul in the Great Redoubt that no force could ever pierce the dark to rescue the people of the Lesser Redoubt. The thought settled heavy on my own heart like lead: for months I had clung to a foolish, bright hope that I might slip out secretly into the Night Land, find that tiny distant redoubt, and step out of the dark before Naani, my love who had been mine through a hundred forgotten eternities, and speak the Master-Word that would tell her I had come at last.

The thought that even then she might be facing some nameless horror at the hands of the dark things was enough to drive me half-mad. I seized my Diskos weapon, ready to run out into the dark half-prepared, to either find her or cast my life away in the attempt. Night after night I sent the Master-Word beating through the aether, calling out to her, warning her never to answer any voice that spoke to her from the dark unless it carried that exact Word, that no true friend would ever lure her out of her redoubt’s shelter. Again and again false voices mimicked her tone, whispering my name from the black, but none could ever speak the Master-Word back to me, and I silenced each with the Word before turning my mind back to thoughts of her.

My health wasted away under the strain, the Master Monstruwacan — who loved me as his own son — chided me gently to rest, but my heart ached too fiercely to heal. Then one night I woke from a tormented sleep, certain I heard Naani calling my old, forgotten love-name, her voice thick with anguish and supplication. I sent the Master-Word surging out into the dark, and for a heartbeat it echoed back, faint and trembling on the edge of my perception. I called to her again and again, but no more came, only that faint, thrilled vibration of the aether that told me she still lived, and was in great peril.

That was the moment I swore my oath: I would no longer hide safe within the Mighty Pyramid while the soul that had been mine through all time faced horror alone. I dressed swiftly, ran up to the Tower of Observation, and laid my plan before the Master Monstruwacan. He begged me long and hard to abandon the foolhardy quest, warned me I would be torn apart by the dark things within days, but he saw that my resolve was unshakable. He did the only thing he could do to keep me safe: he bade me undergo the full Three Days of Preparation, the sacred ritual designed to purify the spirit so that the dark Powers would have less hold over the soul that ventured out into the night.

The Preparation revealed to me all the hidden, unspeakable horrors of the Night Land that were never spoken of to the young — mutilations, abasements of the soul too terrible to set down in any book, horrors that made me wonder any man ever chose to leave the redoubt at all. But the fire of my love burned all the weakness and dross out of me, and I came through the three days and three nights cleansed, no fear left in my heart.

On the fourth day, my grey alien armour was brought to me, lined beneath with a close-woven heat-retaining suit that would fend off the killing cold of the Night Land. My rations — concentrated food and drink, prepared to sustain life for weeks on end — were stitched all about with the Mark of Honour, sewn by the loving hands of the pyramid’s women, to speed me on my way. I stood fully clad, ready at last to step out into the everlasting dark.

I pushed onward for a long stretch, keeping my gaze fixed firmly ahead, never glancing back, steeling my heart and spirit for what lay before me. My errand demanded every scrap of my manhood and courage if I was ever to reach the Maid trapped far away in the world’s endless dark, or meet a worthy death should that be the fate the Night Land held for me.

I moved steadily onward, every fiber of my being thrumming with the sharp soft pain of parting from my vast, mighty home, the tenderness and ancient wisdom that lay under every strict rule and quiet act of guidance the Redoubt’s people had ever known. My heart warmed at the memory of the Master Monstruwacan’s great honor: he had donned full armor and descended from his high tower to stand with the Full Watch at the gate, to buoy my spirit for this journey at the very moment I stepped out into the dark. All the while I walked, the aether of the night roiled around me, thrumming with the thoughts, blind hopes, and unspoken prayers of the millions I had left behind me.

When the edge of my raw emotion dulled a little, I grew sharply aware of the night air’s biting cold, and its strange, rich taste on my lungs and tongue. It carried a sharp, vivid tang that lingered on my palate, filling my lips with every breath, denser than the air that filled the plateau of my home Thousandth City. Every tier of the Redoubt had its own unique air composition, with the difference growing far greater between levels high up near the peak and those near the ground; many peoples migrated up or down to find the air that best suited their health, all governed by careful regulation of air pumps and vents, though a stubborn few refused to move no matter how ill the low air made them. I would note here that the depths of the Fields beyond the Redoubt hold air of every strange possible quality, some sweet and heady enough to entrance one wanderer, some sharp and bracing that would make another miserable elsewhere, so that any soul with the sense to wander could find a air that fit their lungs perfectly.

I walked on, caught between old memories, new wonder, and a quiet, thrumming thread of unnameable doubt and fear. It felt uncanny, almost surreal, to stand in the Night Land at last, even a short distance from the Redoubt’s walls. My imagination had wandered these dark wastes a thousand times as a child and a young man, but never once in my whole life had my feet touched the outer earth before. I know that this must sound a strange, quaint thing to the readers of this far future day.

At last I drew near the Circle of Light that ringed the Redoubt, and I stared in surprise to see it was far smaller than I had always pictured. Reason had told me the glowing barrier could not be the massive wall my distant childhood fancy had imagined, but I had still not expected it to be nothing more than a thin, clear glowing tube barely two inches thick. It blazed so bright that from a distance it had looked far larger, tricking my eye for years. It is a small, trivial detail to set down here, but it speaks to the raw newness of every sight around me, and I know every other child of the Redoubt will recall the same sight we all stared at through the great embrasure viewports, watching misshapen monsters and nameless horrors peer over that very thin line of light, their faces looming out of the dark before they skittered back, repelled by the glow.

We had spent hours on holiday as children, clustered around the great telescopes, competing to be the first to spot a monster peeking in at the Pyramid across the Circle’s glow. We took pride in spotting the ugliest, most horrifying brute, the one that would win our watch game, never quite losing that tiny, unacknowledged shiver under our excitement, the quiet thrill of knowing we were safe behind miles of solid metal, that made all the horror feel like nothing more than a grand, exciting game. Those memories of my youth, of all the beasts I had watched peek across that light, rose up in my mind all at once, and I flinched back unconsciously, my heart leaping at the sudden vivid thought of what might come lurching out of the dark beyond the Circle.

I stood still for a long moment, until my courage steadied, and turned at last to look back at my great home, to bid it a proper farewell. The sight stole my breath away: the last refuge of all the remaining millions of mankind rose before me like a measureless mountain of dark metal, climbing miles up into the night, spreading so far to my left and right it felt like it held the whole world cupped in its bulk. I knew its four sides bore twelve hundred thousand great embrasures in total, as every schoolchild learned from their history books and the quaint old world atlases we still preserved. The lowest tier of those great windows sat half a mile above the ground, and tier after tier stretched upward beyond it, all spilling warm golden light out into the dark. I craned my neck to stare up miles into the night, still able to make out every individual embrasure glowing in neat rows, until the highest ones blurred into a constant glimmering fire, shaping a shining peak that stabbed up through the black sky, dwindling to a tiny point of light that crowned the whole wonder of the world: the Observation Tower, where I had spent so many years at the Master Monstruwacan’s side.

The tiny dark shapes clustered against the lower embrasures were the millions of the Redoubt, all silhouetted against the light, small as insects against the vast bulk of the Pyramid. I knew they were all staring out at me, watching through their spyglasses. I lifted my Diskos high, saluting the Master Monstruwacan up in that far tower, knowing he had the Great Spyglass trained directly on me even though I could not see him. My heart felt full to bursting, but my soul grew stronger for that weight, not weaker. I heard a dim, distant murmur roll through the night, and saw the tiny shapes in the embrasures shift and surge, and I knew the millions had taken my salute for themselves, cheering and waving their own farewells, calling for me to come back safely.

I was just one lone man, staring up at that vast mountain of metal and living humanity, and I knew I could not linger long, or the weight of my homesickness would break my resolve. I lowered the Diskos, reversing the weapon in a gesture of respect that no lone young man offers to all the millions of his people. I sent one last look up the eight miles of dark to the tiny glowing star of the Observation Tower, to let my friend know I thought of him, and I know the multitudes in the upper tiers took that last look as a final farewell to them too, for a far, faint murmur drifted down from the monstrous height, soft as a ghost wind blowing through the night.

I lowered my Diskos, turned, and braced against the light Circle’s faint air resistance, stepping across the thin glowing tube into the total, lonesome dark of the Night Land. I made a hard, fast rule to not look back for a very long while, for I knew the sight of my home would soften my heart far too much to press on.

All around me the aether still surged, alive with the constant thoughts and prayers of my people, and for a little while it made me feel like I had company out in the dark. Then a cold thought struck me: that constant, roiling disturbance in the aether would tip off every evil, watching Force in the Night Land that there was a lone human wandering out in the open. But there was nothing I could do to stop it. Even if I had run back and explained the danger, there was no way to make millions of people stop thinking of one single man, no more than you could hold back a flood.

At first I walked blindly onward, moving fast just to put more distance between myself and the Redoubt until the ache of parting softened. Then I slowed, and stopped to plan my path. I reasoned it was far wiser to take a new, untrodden route through the Land, than to follow the old well-worn path the youth expeditions had used for generations, which would no doubt be heavily watched by the things that lurked in the dark. Instead of heading straight north, I would strike out northwest first, circle far behind the Northwest Watcher, swing around to the north side of the Plain of Blue Fire, then turn straight for my true northern path. This way I would avoid drawing anywhere near the House of Silence, the place that filled me with more dread than any other horror in the Night Land. It was a far longer journey, yes, but it was better to move slow and live than to rush headlong straight into destruction.

Some may wonder how I dared move so surely toward the north, without a compass or map to guide me. Part of it was an innate, unshakable inner knowing, part of it came from the years I had spent studying old, forgotten texts in the Great Library, piecing together clues that the rest of the Redoubt’s people had written off as fairy tales. Not long before I set out, I had found a small, ancient metal book, hidden in a forgotten niche, that had sat unread for at least a million years, no one could say exactly how long. Most of its contents were dismissed as nursery tales, the same way people of this modern age dismiss old myths of a great universal flood. But I had always loved these old stories, sure that beneath all the fancy and exaggeration lay a kernel of forgotten ancient truth.

This book spoke, in the voice of a man who had lived through the events, of a time long before the sun began to die, when a massive earthquake ripped a thousand-mile gash across the face of the world. An entire ocean poured into the rift, hitting the hot crust and exploding back out as steam with a roar that shook every city on the face of the old world, tearing the rift wider, covering the whole planet in thick mist and rain for weeks. The book went on to tell of how that great gash, over millions of years, settled into a thousand-mile deep valley, a thousand miles across, whose far western end caught the last red glow of the dying sun, lighting the depths where seas, forests, and ancient warm pockets held life long after the rest of the world’s surface turned cold and dead, full of strange, resurrected primeval beasts that existed nowhere else. It told of the Road Makers, a hardy ancient race of humans who spent hundreds of generations carving a great road down the length of that vast valley, building cities as they went, fighting monsters, exploring every corner of that warm hidden world, until they meddled with dark, hostile forces they did not fully understand, unleashing horrors that overran their civilization. The last surviving good humans fled, and built the great Pyramid Redoubt as their final, impenetrable refuge.

The Master Monstruwacan and I had discussed that book at length, and we had come to the same conclusion: the great Road that the Silent Ones of the Night Land walked was that same ancient road the Road Makers had carved a million years before. We were both certain the lost Lesser Redoubt, home of the Maid I journeyed to save, had to lie somewhere within that vast hidden valley, somewhere to the north, not west where the loathsome Ab-human hordes made their lair. The vast majority of the Redoubt’s people paid no heed to these old tales, believing they lived in the bed of an ancient gently sloping sea, with no idea that the whole of the Night Land was ringed by hundred-mile high cliffs hidden forever in the dark. Only the children and the gentle dreamers among us took the old stories seriously, and somewhere between their simple, unclouded belief and the studied skepticism of the wise men, the truth lay.

I moved northwest at a deliberate, wary pace, for hours on end, steering clear of the domain of the Great Watcher of the North-West even as I mapped out the shape of the journey ahead of me. I knew no one alive could say how far I would have to travel across the lightless wastes, nor what horrors waited beyond every dark ridge, so I resolved to pace myself rather than burn out in the first stretch. I laid out a strict routine for my days outside the Great Redoubt: travel for eighteen hours straight, stop to eat and replenish my strength every six hours, and rest for a full six hours each cycle, so my body would be sharp and ready when true danger found me. For a world crammed with nameless, ravenous perils, that prudence would be the difference between life and a silent, unmarked death in the dark.

Of course, I broke that rule within the first day. For twenty-one straight hours I crept and hid, ducking behind jagged rock spires and hunching low through moss thickets to stay out of the Watcher’s line of sight, too wired to feel hunger, too focused on not being seen to pause for rest. By the time the twenty-first hour ticked over, my limbs were heavy with exhaustion and my vision swam with faintness. I scanned the dark for shelter, and soon spotted a small fire-hole glowing low in the rock, the sort of small geothermal vent I had passed a handful of times already that night. I made my way toward it, grateful for the promise of warmth against the Night Land’s endless, bone-deep chill, and a dry spot to curl up and sleep.

When I drew close, it was a tiny, comforting oasis amid endless gloom: a pit no more than a few paces across, filled with dull, bubbling molten earth that puffed thin fumes of sulphur into the cold air. I sat down a safe distance from the edge, the Diskos I carried propped on the rock within arm’s reach. For a long minute I did not move, too tired to summon the energy to eat or drink, only turning to stare back at the colossal bulk of the Mighty Pyramid. Though I had walked for miles, its sheer size made it feel nearly close enough to touch, leaving me torn between two feelings: sharp elation at the nearness of my home, and cold, heavy dread at the scale of the impossible quest I had set out on. I knew millions of souls inside its walls were watching me in that moment, and I thought of Master Monstruwacan, my dear friend, surely peering through the Great Spy-Glass at my small, huddled shape, far out in the dark.

Shaking off my foolish reluctance, I opened my small pack of supplies, pulled out three compact nutrient tablets, and chewed them down. They were dense, concentrated sustenance, tiny enough to take up almost no space, and I shook a measured pinch of special chemical dust out of a sealed vial into my small drinking cup. The air reacted with the dust in a fizzing, boiling rush, and the powder swelled to fill the cup with clean, cool water—an old, reliable trick the Redoubt’s engineers had refined over eons, that let me carry months of food and water in a pack small enough to sling over one shoulder. It was plain fare, no feast, but more than enough to sustain me through the dark wastes.

After I ate, I sorted through the rest of my supplies, and pulled out the special compass Monstruwacan had built for me by hand, salvaged from an ancient relic in the Great Museum. Unlike olden compasses that pointed north, this one was tuned to lock onto the Earth-Current thrumming through the Great Pyramid’s foundations, so I would never lose my way home even if the total blackness swallowed the Pyramid’s lights entirely. I set it on the rock, and for a long time its needle only spun and wavered uselessly— I was still high above the vast spread of the Underground Fields, after all, not far above the legendary Crack that split the deep crust of the world. Later, hundreds of miles further into my journey, the needle would lock true on the Pyramid’s signal, a steady comfort. Even later, far out in the uncharted dark, the needle would jerk unexpectedly west, tugging hard toward some unknown colossal ancient power, and I would wonder if it was pulling at the fabled North Force my old texts and memory-dreams had warned of, or the faint, distant signal of the long-lost Lesser Redoubt, the home of the maid I had sworn to rescue.

I stowed the compass back in my pouch, wrapped my heavy travel cloak around my shoulders, and tucked the humming, warm Diskos inside the fabric to rest beside me, as if it were a trusted companion rather than a weapon. Half-drowsing at the edge of sleep, I could feel the aether thrum around me, humming with the collective sympathy of the millions of people in the Pyramid who were thinking of me, sending quiet well-wishes that wrapped around me like a second blanket. My last waking thought was of the girl I was traveling to find, and I fell asleep dreaming of her, of light that did not burn, free of the Night Land’s endless sorrow.

I jolted awake to the blaring, resonant roar of the Home-Call, howling across every inch of the dark. I snatched the Diskos’ haft in an instant, eyes snapping toward the Pyramid’s observation spire high overhead. The Home-Call was only ever used to wake travelers out in the dark to an immediate, mortal threat. Bright, strobing green flashes burst from the spire’s peak, spelling out the Set-Speech warning in sharp, fast bursts: a Great Grey Man, one of the hideously mutated man-beasts that roamed the outer wastes, had scented me, and was even now crawling toward me through the moss thickets behind the fire-hole. The message ordered me to dive left, into the nearest cover, to ambush the creature before it could lunge.

I was already moving before the last flash faded, huddling low in the shadow of a dense moss clump, my heart hammering so hard I could hear it over the distant rumble of the fire-hole. The great grey beast crept out of the thickets, enormous, every inch of its skin the colour of ash. The glow of the molten rock in the fire-hole clearly pained its sensitive eyes, and it pressed its snout flat to the ground, hunching its shoulders and craning its neck in a brutish, serpentine motion, trying to track my scent past the glare. It circled the vent three times to the left, three times to the right, and every time it vanished back into the thick dark under the moss, I was certain it had spotted me and was about to creep up to tear me apart from behind.

Then a clear, sharp voice spoke directly in my mind, sent by Master Monstruwacan himself, amplified by the low, thrumming vibration of the Master-Word through the aether to confirm its authenticity: the beast had found my trail, and was making a wide circle around my hiding spot to attack me from the rear. I launched myself out of my first hiding place and dove for a new moss clump no more than a second before a great, gnarled grey hand pushed aside the foliage where I had been crouched. The beast’s massive, hideous head poked through the opening, snuffling the empty spot where my scent still hung.

I struck before it could turn around. The Diskos blazed with spinning, white-hot fire as it cleaved through the creature’s skull and neck in a single blow. The great beast toppled, its legs twitching and curling as it died, the Diskos in my hand spitting sparks as if it rejoiced at the kill. When it went still, I walked to the far side of the fire-hole, held the spinning Diskos high above my head, so the telltale flash of its fire would reach the Great Spy-Glass, to prove I was unharmed and the threat was done.

Monstruwacan did not speak to me again after that. To send telepathic messages through the aether risked drawing the attention of the nameless, ancient Powers that lurked in the deepest dark of the Night Land, and our exchange had already been far riskier than was prudent. But I could feel the aether hum all around me, thrumming with the collective relief of millions of people inside the Redoubt who had watched the fight unfold, all of them cheering silently for me. It made me feel far less alone, even out in the endless dark.

When I checked my timepiece, I realized I had slept for ten full hours, far longer than the six I had scheduled, and I chided myself for my foolishness. I picked up my cloak and my scattered supplies, turned to glance once more up the impossible, mile-high slope of the Pyramid rising into the black, and did not wave or make any grand farewell. I did not want to stir the emotions of millions so strongly that I disturbed the aether further, drawing unwanted eyes from the dark. I turned my back on my home and set out again, the Diskos warm and light in my hand, as if it shared some quiet bond with me, just as every Redoubt warrior knew their own Diskos gained a unique affinity for its owner over years of use, so much so that a stranger could not wield it without clumsiness or injury. It was ancient custom that a warrior’s Diskos be buried with them on the Last Road in the Country of Silence, to return its thrumming Earth-Current power back to the planet’s core, a small, loving gesture to honour the dead.

I trekked on for six more hours, moving steadily toward the northwest, veering slightly west to give the Great Watcher of the North-West a wide berth. Foolishly, though, curiosity got the better of me, and I angled a little further north than was safe, just to catch a clear glimpse of the colossal monster from afar. The human heart is a strange, reckless thing: one minute you are frozen with terror, the next you are throwing all caution to the wind for no reason but to satisfy a useless, hungry curiosity.

I stopped every six hours to eat and drink, glancing back at the distant glow of the Pyramid, pausing to steel my courage before moving on. I crawled low across stony ground that reeked of sulphur fumes seeping up through the cracks, scraping my palms raw on sharp rock, until I pulled on the thick, reinforced gloves stowed in my pack, built to protect me from the harsh, sharp wastes of the Night Land. I kept my eyes scanning every shadow, every dark crevice, never letting my guard drop, as I drew closer and closer to the enormous, hidden shape of the Great Watcher.

I moved downward slow and silent, stepping into a Darkness so thick it settled over my skin like a physical weight, pressing against my eyes, my throat, my very soul. It was a night no soul of this age could ever fathom: no faint star-glow, no distant moon, no glimmer of horizon, nothing but a void so total I half lost all sense of self, drifting into the foolish fancy that I had already stepped off the face of the world, wandering for all eternity through empty black. That delusion shattered every time my boot crunched against a jutting rock, or I stumbled headlong into an unseen boulder, jolting me sharp back to the hard, unforgiving reality of the stone slope under my hands and feet.

Down, ever down, that was my only guide. Utter dark slowed my pace to less than a mile every two hours, and the frustration of being unable to take even a single unhurried free stride gnawed at me, sharp and bitter. At last I thought to turn to the Diskos at my hip: I would spin it for mere seconds at a time, its strange cold glow painting a narrow ten-foot sliver of the slope ahead, enough to etch every rut, loose stone, and drop into my memory, before the light faded and I pressed onward again. The Diskos’ small glow felt impossibly bright, a tiny sun against the monstrous dark that wrapped around me on every side, and I only ever called it to light when my stumblings grew too frequent, when the blackness threatened to swallow all sense of direction entirely. I wasted none of its stored power on frivolity, for I knew its strength was finite, and I needed every spark for true extremity.

Six long, bitter hours of this passed, and it had been more than twenty hours since I last closed my eyes to sleep. I hunkered down on the uneven stone, fumbling blindly for my rations, eating two nutrient tablets and sipping carefully from my water skin, every motion guided only by touch and straining hearing. I spread my wool cloak over the sharp rock, tucked my scrip and supply pouch under my head, curled the warm hilt of the Diskos against my chest, and fell fast asleep, Naani’s face the last soft thought drifting through my mind before slumber claimed me.

I woke as abruptly as if something had brushed my cheek in the dark, my muscles coiled tight before I was even fully conscious. My fingers locked around the Diskos hilt, and I held perfectly still, listening for long minutes. No sound breathed out of that endless night. No scrape of claw on stone, no distant snarl, no soft footfall. Still, the prickle at the back of my neck would not fade. I ate and drank by touch, slung my gear back over my shoulders, and set off again, that strange unease clinging to me for all the rest of the day: a quiet certainty that something followed me, slow and soundless, never close, never far. My ears caught no hint of it, but I stopped every few paces to hold my breath and listen, just the same.

Hours later, my foot slipped into a narrow hidden crevice, and I pitched hard forward. A jagged rock would have split my ribs open had my armour not absorbed the brunt of the fall. Shaken, I decided to travel the rest of the way on my hands and knees: feeling every inch of the path under my palms would cut down stumbles to almost nothing, and let me rely even less on the Diskos’ precious light. It was a miserable, crawling way to travel, but I had done far worse across the wastes of the Night Land, and I pressed onward.

After eighteen hours of crawling, three full stops to eat and rest, I found a flat patch of clear stone, brushed all the small pebbles away to make a rough bed, and settled to sleep once more. I thought of Naani as I drifted off, but the lingering unease of being followed niggled at my thoughts, and twice I propped myself up on my elbow to listen into the dark. I heard nothing, and told myself it was only weariness playing tricks, but even in sleep my senses stayed half-alert, tuned for the smallest hint of danger. Just as before, I woke two hours early, jolting fully awake in an instant, and still there was no sound, no sign that anything had been near.

The next full day of crawling passed much the same, until I almost pitched headlong into a yawning, mile-wide pit I had not seen, my chest bumping empty air an instant before I would have toppled over the edge. I scrambled back on my elbows, shaking so hard my teeth chattered, and sat for a long minute thinking of a way to avoid such hidden death traps. I had no rope, no line, until a thought struck: I unbuckled the long thin leather strap from my supply pouch, tied a heavy fist-sized rock to its free end, and secured it tight. As I crawled, I cast the stone out a few feet ahead of me, holding the other end of the strap. If the stone thudded against rock, the path ahead was safe. If the strap went suddenly slack, I knew empty drop waited before me, and I could divert my path. It was a clumsy, slow way to travel, but for the first time since I had stepped onto the Mighty Slope, I moved with surety.

I kept to that pace for three more days, crawling downward for eighteen hours at a stretch, sleeping just six, waking always before I planned, that quiet unseen presence at my back never close enough to harm, never far enough to dismiss. By that point I had been six full days in absolute, unbroken darkness, traveling so far downward it felt I must be crawling toward the very core of the world. I noticed the air had changed: the biting cold of the high Night Land had faded entirely, and the air grew thick, warm, heavy in my lungs, far richer than the thin, rarefied air that lay around the base of the Last Redoubt. I paused to think on that, as I crawled: the air of that lost age had been so thin that no flying thing could survive on the surface of the Night Land, even the ancient monstrous brutes that once ruled the skies of that era long past. The Monstruwacans had once joked, when I told them I would venture after Naani, that I take one of the old intact flying ships from the Great Museum, but I had laughed it off: no living soul knew how to pilot those hundred-thousand-year old craft, the air was too thin to hold them aloft, and even if I managed it, the roaring machine would have lit me up like a beacon for every horror of the Night Land to hunt. I wondered, too, why the ancient Road Builders who carved the great road from the upper world down into the vast valley had not simply flown down, before the air grew thin enough to make flight impossible, and I supposed they must have looked down that hundred-mile drop of unbroken night and feared they would never rise again.

All those thoughts drifted through my head as I crawled, until suddenly, I blinked, and a faint pinprick of glimmer shone far ahead in the dark.

I thought it was a hallucination, a trick of my starved eyes after six days of unbroken black. I rubbed my knuckles over my eyes, and it was still there, faint but unmistakeable. A wild, sharp hope surged through me so strong it made me lightheaded. I staggered to my feet and tried to run, only to trip over a half-buried boulder and go sprawling, scraping my palm raw on the stone. I bit back a curse, got back on my hands and knees, and crawled toward that tiny light as fast as I could. For nine straight hours I moved toward it, the glow growing steadily clearer, and soon a new sound drifted to me: a high, piercing monstrous piping, echoing down the length of the slope, growing louder by the minute. I dropped flat to the rock, crawling low to stay hidden from whatever made that sound.

When I rounded a wall of jagged basalt, the endless Mighty Slope ended. I was at the mouth of a vast gorge, walls rising so high they vanished into the black overhead. Ahead, a jet of raw gas gushed from a crack in the valley floor, burning with a vivid searing blue flame that danced and swayed, leaping a full thousand feet high, lighting the seven-mile-wide gorge clear. The far wall, miles distant, glowed bright blue in the firelight, and the piping sound was the roar of the gas rushing through deep rock vents, wild and whistling across the stone. The mountain on the gorge’s right side rose measureless into the dark above, and I stared, breath caught in my throat, after six days of unbroken night, for the first time, I stood at the bottom of the Mighty Slope, looking out at the uncharted depths of the world below the Last Redoubt.

Two more hours of careful descending, and I stepped clear of the great Gorge’s shadow out into the wide, blazing country beyond. For all the sharp, bewildered unease thrumming under my skin, I cannot put to words the rush of joy that hit me at the sight of its wild, uncurbed splendour.
Before I had left the Gorge’s high mouth, I had paused long enough to survey the vast expanse spread out below me, and counted twenty-seven massive volcanoes rising from the land. That tally did not include two monstrous distant ranges of fire-hills burning far off to my right, nor the hundred thousand smaller vents and steaming seeps scattered across every mile of the landscape.
This was truly a land of fire and water. A small active fire-hill jutted from a stretch of open sea no more than a mile from where I stood, and a score more dotted its waters behind it. I had counted three smaller inland seas, plus one vast ocean that stretched unbroken all the way to the red haze of the far volcanoes, vanishing completely from sight with no visible horizon end. Islands rose from the waves, each crowned with its own smoking crater, while elsewhere fire-hills jutted straight up from the surf. Steam hung thick over the near waters, as if the sea itself boiled at random points across its surface.
A low, constant muttering thunder hummed in the red-tinted air, shifting its source from near to far, rolling through the ground. I knew that noise for the voices of the fire-hills, the raw, living energy of the world breathing out loud. This was the old world’s unfiltered roar, sharper and far more present than the faint, muffled world-noise of my own age.
Let me set down what I saw before me as clearly as memory holds it. To the left of the Gorge mouth loomed a huge, blackened mountain, rising fifteen to twenty miles straight up into the perpetual dark overhead. A mighty peaked volcano jutted from its flank at a height I guessed to be five miles, and a second sat further up, nine or ten miles above the ground. As if that were not wonder enough, two more giant fire-hills smouldered on its far left crest, perched so high they glowed like smouldering twin suns hanging in the night sky. Below them, vast mountains of ash and cinder had poured down the slopes for uncountable eons, building grey, sombre monuments to the unhurried, dreadful glory of deep time.
To my right, sea stretched on endlessly, glowing red with the light of the distant burning hills. To my left, vast ancient forests rolled out, broken here and there by more monstrous fire-hills rising above the treeline. That was the first imprint the land left on my raw, astonished senses.
Once I had descended fully out of the Gorge, I halted, suddenly uncertain which path would lead me to Naani’s hidden refuge. I wandered back and forth for long minutes before I cursed my own foolishness, and climbed back up into the Gorge mouth to map my route first.
There, the obvious, only viable path lay clear before me. The endless sea blocked all routes to my right, but along the shoreline where land met water to my left, a clear, passable stretch ran as far as my eyes could see. It felt sure enough that if I followed it, I would find a way forward. I named this red-shining, fire-wreathed expanse the Country of the Seas, and descended back down to its floor.
By the time I reached the shore, twenty-four full hours had passed since I last slept. I hunted out a sheltered nook, finding a dry, warm rock basin ringed by three thick, gnarled trees. I ate three of my ration tablets, sipped from my water skin (my belly still ached for real, hot food), spread a soft bed of pine needles in the basin, and lay down. My thoughts drifted to Naani for only a few moments before I slipped over the edge of sleep.
I woke abruptly, half-submerged in hot, steamy water. By some mercy I had not drowned in my rest. I stood up fast to find the basin brim full of pungent, bubbling hot water, pouring in through a smoothed crevice on its far side. I realized then this was a periodic geyser vent that filled the basin every hour, then drained slowly back through fissures in the rock’s floor.
Shaking the water off, I stripped off my armour, fished all my supplies out of the rising pool, and spread my clothes and gear across a flat, sun-heated rock to dry. While they dried, I waded back into the almost empty warm pool for a pleasant soak, keeping my Diskos propped within arm’s reach on the edge. I sensed none of the crawling, horrid malevolence that had hung thick over every mile of the Night Land. All the evil forces of that dark realm, I reasoned, had clustered around the Mighty Pyramid, drawn to the concentration of millions of living human souls there just as sharks trail a ship carrying slaughtered cattle. I did not pause to puzzle over how those dark powers had crossed the boundary to this land, for such speculation was useless vanity.
My clothes dried quickly. I climbed out of the pool, dressed, strapped my armour back on, and felt far lighter in spirit. I curled up beside the now-empty basin again, and slept another six full hours, woken only once by the gurgling roar of the geyser refilling the basin.
When I woke, I was sharp, rested, and ready to push on. I ate my rations, drank, and set off at a fast, steady pace. The vast nameless forests stayed to my left, the shoreline to my right. The trees grew right down into the surf in many places, so I often cut a path through the woods. I had never known a wild, uncurbed mystery like that of these old forests, nothing like the solemn, quiet groves of the Underground Fields. Their sharp, sweet scent lifted my whole spirit.
Life moved in those dark woods all around me. I caught glimpses of glinting eyes peering out from the underbrush now and then, retreating back into shadow before I could make out their owners. None came close enough to harm me. Three times that day I came to small red spitting fire-hills, their roars carrying through the trees long before I reached them. A dead, desolate zone of blackened bare ground ringed each crater, but tiny fast-growing plants crept close to their edges, sprouting and living their whole short lives between eruption bursts. I passed thirty-seven steaming springs that day, some roaring so loud in the distance I mistook them for the cries of strange wild beasts.
When the eighteenth hour of travel rolled around, I sat down against a tree trunk as I had at the sixth and twelfth hour marks, ate two ration tablets, and sipped from my fizzing, mineral-rich water skin. I chose a bed spot that had a solid boulder at my back, so no creature could sneak up on me from behind. I drifted toward sleep, my mind lingering on Naani, as it had all day, half-convinced her spirit hovered close, trying to speak to me. I swore to push faster, to cover more ground, to reach her small hidden refuge in the dead world faster, and fell asleep instantly.
I woke sharp all at once. The bright red volcanic glow of the country showed me my danger in a heartbeat, no confusing shadows or half-dark to leave me suspended in terror as the Night Land always did. I was propped on one elbow, staring right at six squat, hunch-shouldered men crouched at the treeline no more than twenty paces off. Their necks and shoulders were thick as human bulls, their eyes glowing like wild beasts’ in the red light. My hand closed around the hilt of my Diskos. I stood up ready to fight, and in the blink of an eye, they were gone. I had kept my gaze fixed on that exact patch of shadow the whole time, and never saw them move. I knew they had been there, no illusion, but they had vanished completely.
I checked my time marker: I had slept five hours. I ate two rations standing, eyes scanning the treeline, drank a mouthful of water, and set off at a dead run. I was lean, hard, and covered in muscle after weeks of hard travel, and I was sure I could outpace any of those humped men. For thirty straight hours I pushed onward at that hard run, eating two rations every sixth hour, never slowing, never taking my eyes off the treeline. Twice or thrice I sensed things moving parallel to me through the woods on my left, keeping pace with my speed, always hidden, never breaking cover. I did not dare stop to sleep until I found somewhere safe.
Near the end of the thirtieth hour, I saw open water stretching ahead of me, separate from the endless sea on my right. I thought the sea curved inland at that point, but when I reached it, I found a wide, fast river pouring out of the interior forests to meet the ocean. A small island sat right at the river’s mouth. I thought for a second it would make a perfect refuge from the men tracking me, but I needed to get across to the far shore to keep following the sea path that stretched on ahead.
I could not swim, and even if I could, there was no telling what monstrous things lurked in that warm, fast-flowing river. I turned and followed the riverbank upstream, hoping to find a narrower crossing, and barely a mile inland I hit a second tributary pouring into the first. Now I was trapped: the sea to my south, one river ahead of me, the second river to my north.
The threat of the humped men spurred my wits awake. I hunted for fallen logs, dragged two long light ones down to the water, lashed them together tight with my spare armour belts and straps, and hacked a rough pole from a young sapling to make a rickety raft. All the while I never stopped scanning the treeline, terrified the humped men would break cover before I could push off. It took me half an hour of clumsy paddling to get across to the small island. I ate my rations, collapsed on the mossy ground, and slept nine full straight hours, thirty-three hours after my last rest. I slept so deep I might as well have been dead, the island feeling perfectly safe.
When I woke, I finished the paddle across to the far shore, retrieved my belts and straps from the logs, and set off again. I was sure I had left the humped men trapped on the near bank, though the thought later crossed my mind that there could be others on this side too. I moved fast all day, passing two giant fire-hills whose eruptions shook the whole surrounding landscape four times, ducking for cover four times to avoid passing monstrous beasts that never noticed me. My thoughts kept drifting back to Naani, the whole of my journey one long, unspoken promise to her, even when constant perils kept me from lingering on that thought. I passed thousands of acres of lush green forest, rich sweet air thick with life, and daydreamed for a moment that one day, when the Night Land’s last refuge grew too cold and airless to support human life, survivors might flee here and build a new home, if they could ever find a way past the evil things clustered around the Mighty Pyramid.
A little before the eighteenth hour of travel that day, I emerged from the woods and climbed a long low cliff that rose above the shoreline. A strange, tall pointed rock a hundred feet high stood directly ahead, and I moved toward it carefully, curious. What I had first thought was a single spire turned out to be a narrow flat platform perched on top of the spire, covered in small trees and moss, while the rest of the spire’s bare rock looked as if it had been scoured clean by some ancient blast. It looked like the perfect, defensible place to rest. I began climbing, and halfway up, I found a recessed ledge set a little back from the cliff face. I stopped there, ate and drank, and fell asleep before I could even think of Naani, heavy with the accumulated weariness of days of nonstop travel.
I woke abruptly seven hours later, my inner warning instinct screaming of immediate danger. I grabbed my Diskos, crept to the edge of the ledge, and looked down. Two humped men were scaling the spire below me, climbing fast and silent, their thick bull necks glistening with sweat. They had smelled me, and they were coming to kill me. I stepped back from the ledge edge, Diskos raised, ready to cut the first one down the second he pulled himself within reach, so there was no chance they could flank me while I fought the first.

All at once, the utter black weight of despair I had carried for so many leagues shattered into searing, unlooked-for gladness and sharp, bright hope: it felt I must stand beside my dearest love within the hour. This was an over-hasty expectation, of course, not destined for swift fulfillment. All I could plainly make out was the looming shape of a great pyramid, rising endless upward into the unblinking night.

I knew it must stand atop a central hill in that cursed dark country—only such elevation could make it loom so vast against the distant faint volcanic glows. I set off running headlong downhill toward it, my wits scattered by the shock of what I thought I had found.

I ran barely minutes before I pitched headlong over a hidden jagged rock, pain lancing so sharp through my neck I was certain I had broken my spine. I could not move for a long, stretched minute, left half-moaning and helpless on the cold earth, easy prey for any slavering thing that might stumble across me. At last I managed to sit upright, clutching my stinging neck in both hands, and the ache faded slow enough that I could stagger back to my feet. Now I moved with great caution, a cold prickle of unease coiling in my chest: why was the pyramid so utterly dark, no glimmer of the Lesser Refuge’s sentinel lamps, no faint hum of the ancient Earth Current I knew? A fear bloomed that this was no haven, but a House of Evil, some dark power weaving a false facade to lure me to ruin. Even so, the shape was plain against the far fires of the land, and stubborn hope refused to let me turn away.

In the first wild instant I spotted the pyramid I had been too unhinged by months of grueling, lonely search to do anything but run blind toward it. I had even meant to call out to Naani with my augmented brain-parts, send the Master-Word first, then tell her I had come. But now caution held me: I would find out what that utter dark truly hid, before I gave away my position.

I pressed onward down the slope, careful at first, then seized again by fierce eagerness, the soreness of my fall fading fast under the pull of my longing. I had taken thirteen hours to climb the upper plain of the great volcano behind me; I descended its full length in ten, slowed only by the lingering unsteadiness of my fall, until I stepped out onto the vast plain of the Night Land. The distant dark hulk of the pyramid on its hill blocked all view of the refuge’s lights, but I could see a great dark bulk rising between me and the far glows of the volcanic vents, and knew that was the hill I sought.

I tramped four more hours across the plain, past small fire-holes that spilled dim red light across the rock and sparse gnarled brush. The land was never fully black, lit by that faint, hellish luminescence, until the base of the hill rose up before me, cutting off all distant glows and swallowing the world in pitch dark that way. It was another full hour before I reached its foot, and over those five hours since I had left the volcano, I heard the pad of unseen running things in the dark over and over, once a great giant’s roar echoing from leagues away, followed by a high, unholy screaming that curdled my blood.

I began to climb the hill. For the first stretch, wild excitement seized me, so that I nearly shouted Naani’s name aloud into the dark, fool enough to hope she might hear me. But the euphoria faded fast, cold caution wrapping around me again, as if my attuned spirit sensed a horror my conscious mind could not yet name. Three hours of climbing brought me almost to the summit, where the grim, desolate silhouette of the great dark pyramid loomed upward into the night. A sudden, overwhelming shaking terror took me, unshakable and primal: my soul knew no human lived in that vast, lightless bulk. Monstrous, horrid things nested there, waiting to tear my soul apart. I slipped back down the hill as quiet as a shadow, put as much distance between me and that accursed place as I could.

It took four long hours to put the hill behind me, and I wandered half-blind, no heed to my path, until I found myself standing on the shore of a long-dead ancient sea. I had no memory of veering that far off my path; I can only guess the dry seabed curved far around, or there were multiple such dead inland seas scattered across the Night Land.

I sank down weak and bewildered, my heart feeling as if it had turned to ash inside my chest. I knew from the certain knowledge of my spirit that the Lesser Refuge had fallen. Its people were all slaughtered or scattered, and the pyramid was now a roost for evil things. If that was true, I had come too late to save the maid I had crossed the world to find. In my hollow despair, I found myself almost hoping some monster would stumble across me, that I might fight it and die fast, for there was nothing left in all the dark world that made life worth living.

I sat there for a long stretch, until a thought struck me sharp: I would send the Master-Word out into the dark. How else would I ever know if Naani still lived? Even if no answer came from her, and an Evil Power found me instead, it would only end the heartache I carried.

I stood, scanning the dark around me, and sent the Master-Word rippling out through the aether with every fiber of my augmented mind, then called Naani’s name three times after it.

Instantly, it seemed, the Master-Word thrummed back around me, low and solemn, beating through every shadow of the night. A faint, lone voice sounded directly in my brain, so distant it felt like it had drifted from the very end of the dead world. It was Naani’s voice, and it was the voice of Mirdath, the lost love of my countless olden memories, calling me by the name no one but she should have known.

I nearly choked on a sob of overwhelming, searing joy, all my despair vanishing like smoke. My dearest Naani lived. I had not heard that voice in what felt like an eternity of grim labor and creeping horror. Even as I stood dazed with joy, a cold prickle ran up my spine: someone was only a little way off, hiding in the brush beside the nearest fire-hole, my spirit had sensed them before my eyes did. I did not answer Naani across the dark world. I slipped fast into a thick bush near the fire-hole, pressed aside the branches to peer through.

There knelt a slim, small figure, sobbing softly on the ground beside the red glow of the vent, harkening with such desperate focus she barely breathed. It was Naani. My soul knew her in that single bright, perfect moment. She was listening for my spiritual call, sure it had come all the way from the distant Mighty Pyramid she had left behind—for weeks of lonely wandering she had cried out to me, over and over, and had never gotten a reply. She was too weak now to cast her voice far through the aether; all her desperate calls had only brushed the edge of my senses, faint flickers I had taken for hallucinations on the road.

I drew in a sharp breath, set my teeth to steady my trembling lips, and spoke her olden name, soft out of the bush: “Mirdath.”

The maid stopped sobbing mid-gasp. She looked this way and that, tears glinting in the firelight, her face torn between fresh terror and a wild, impossible hope she dared not name. I pushed through the branches, stepping out into the open in my dented grey armor, and stood frozen for a long moment, my heart screaming to pull her into my arms, as if I had found Mirdath again after a lifetime of lost eons. She was Naani, and she was Mirdath, and she was a stranger to my eyes, small and dainty, shattered by grief and terror and loneliness.

For the first split second she screamed and scrambled backward, trying to flee into the brush, thinking I was some monster that had snuck up on her. Then she saw I was human. I spoke the Master-Word aloud, and told her my name, that I was the one she had called for across the dark months. She knew it before my words even finished sounding. She cried out in a broken, shaking voice, and ran to me, shoving her small hands into my grip, then collapsed into great heaving sobs, so weak I could barely steady her. I had no armored gloves on, so I held her hands soft, saying nothing, letting her cry.

She leaned against me, light as a child, trembling so hard I could feel it through my armor. I realized then she was starving. She had wandered alone for weeks, near to death when I stumbled upon her. I laid her gentle against a smooth warm rock at the edge of the fire-hole, stripped off my thick wool cloak, and wrapped it tight around her—her own clothes were tattered almost to rags, torn apart by thorns and sharp rock, half the shivers wracking her from cold, half from hunger and exhaustion.

I pulled my scrip from my back, broke a nutrient tablet into my drinking cup, mixed it with water, and set it to warm on a hot rock at the edge of the fire-hole to make a thin, warm broth. Her hands shook too badly to hold the cup, so I fed it to her slow, drop by drop. The warm food calmed her, the sobs fading to quiet hitching breaths. She still would not look up at me, until suddenly she pulled free, slipped to her knees, and kissed my hand, starting to cry again. I was so abashed I stood stupidly for a moment, before I knelt in front of her, freed my hand, and kissed both of hers soft, to show her I was no hero to be revered—only a man who had crossed the Night Land to find the other half of his soul. She sobbed harder, overwhelmed that I had traveled so far through every horror to reach her.

I lifted her gentle into my arms, no careless caress, just holding her safe, stroking her tangled hair, murmuring both her names, Naani and Mirdath, whispering half-remembered soft things to her that she would recall later when she was strong. She went quiet, resting against my chest, content to be held for the first time in weeks, her sobs slowing to nothing. She nestled closer, looking up at me with soft, wet eyes, and began to speak, pausing often for lack of strength, the story pouring out of her in broken, trembling bursts.

The Lesser Redoubt’s Earth Current had failed long before, she told me, weakening everyone who lived there until a handful of terrified, half-mad citizens had pried open the Great Outer Door and fled out into the dark. The horrors of the Night Land had poured in right behind them, slaughtering hundreds, and chasing the rest out into the wilderness. Her father, the Master Monstruwacan, had been torn apart by a brutish shaggy man-monster right before her eyes. She had escaped with three other young maids, but night creatures had stolen two of them while they slept in the brush, the third had run screaming in the dark and vanished, and she had been entirely alone ever since.

She had survived on moss growing on the rocks, bitter strange berries, and the lukewarm sulphurous water from the hot springs, which made her sick often, but saved her from far deadlier poisons in some of the plants. She had hidden from the monsters that hunted her, listening for their approach with her sharpened spiritual sense, fleeing in the dark until her clothes had been torn to rags. That very night, she had been so weak she could barely move, a circle of squat, silent spider-crabs huddled at the edge of the firelight, waiting for her to die. She had thought death was moments away, until the Master-Word thundered through her mind. She had believed it was me calling from the distant Mighty Pyramid, never daring to hope I had crossed all that leagues of horror to find her.

Glancing around while she spoke, I saw the spider-crabs were still there, huddled in a patient ring, watching us. Hot anger surged up in me. I strode to the edge of the firelight, kicking the stupid, skittish creatures left and right, stamping my foot until a dozen of them scuttled away, and the entire rest of the pack fled into the dark, too cowardly to attack even though they outnumbered me a hundred to one.

I went back to my maid, sat beside her, and wrapped my arms around her again, as the soft red glow of the fire-hole wrapped around us, and for the first time since I left the Mighty Pyramid, I felt peace.

The air felt almost bright after the suffocating, lonesome gloom of the ancient seabed, and I quickly realized I had emerged onto the dark landscape east of the point where I first stepped down onto the old sea floor during my outward trek to find Naani. Fire-holes dotted the ground all across my field of vision, their warm glows a welcome sight that made my heart light – though I moved with deliberate care toward them, for I knew well every uncanny, predatory thing that drew to their heat in the cold of the Night Land.

I glanced down at Naani, who looked back up at me and drifted closer, soft and sweet, the most wondrous thing I had ever seen. But her face was pale, and she moved with the heavy drag of utter exhaustion, and I berated myself for overpushing her: my own frame had been hardened by years of wandering the Pyramid’s outer dark, as if forged from iron, while she was a tender, sheltered maid of the Lesser Redoubt. She would not let me chide myself long, standing close to gaze up at me with luminous eyes, and I wrapped my arms around her to kiss her, before lifting my head to scan the horizon and plot our path home.

Far ahead, I spotted the broad blue radiance I had first glimpsed from the mouth of the Upward Gorge – the faint, constant glow I had steered by all the way across the pitch black seabed, the only fixed marker that had kept me from wandering in blind, endless circles through the dark. After a long moment of careful orientation, I worked out that the Gorge must lie to my left, a fair distance off: far enough that I could not see it, but close enough that I could make out the smoldering red glare of the giants’ great fire-pit glowing over the horizon in that direction. I knew we must steer well clear of both that pit and the unsettling blue Shine burning across the far western stretch of the land, which every instinct in me warned was a place of unnameable evil.

I pointed left over the dark, empty expanse and told Naani the Gorge lay somewhere out that way, hidden from sight, located only by the landmarks I had memorized on my journey outward. She had been studying the landscape quietly as I oriented myself, and asked after my planned route. I told her we would go as straight as we could, staying far from both the Shine and the giants’ fire pit. She gestured to the direct path I had named, pointing out distant fire-holes rimmed with a sickly, bilious green glow. That marked a vast band of fatally poisonous gas, she told me – the people of the Lesser Redoubt had mapped it centuries prior with their instruments, and no living thing could breathe it in and survive. The gas zone stretched all the way to the northwest, where the blue Shine burned, and she warned me the Fixed Giants lurked hidden in that light, only visible when the roiling glowing mists parted to reveal their forms. I recognized them immediately as kin to the Great Watchers that ringed the Mighty Pyramid, and thought of the monstrous, colossal face I had glimpsed in the Shine’s smoke the day I first stepped out of the Gorge: a force of ancient, unthinkable cruelty.

For a long minute I was stumped how to reach the Gorge without straying too close to the Shine, until I remembered my own outward path: I had wandered past the far edge of the gas band by pure chance, never even knowing it existed. Naani proposed the perfect plan: we would drop back down onto the ancient seabed, trek several hours southwest along the coast far below the poison gas’s reach, then climb back up onto the land once we had cleared the zone, to make straight for the Gorge’s mouth while staying safely distant from the giants’ fire pit. The idea was so sound it made me smile, and a soft, warm feeling swelled in my chest to watch her think and plan aloud, every part of her sharp, curious mind open to me with no pretense.

It had been nearly 17 hours since either of us last slept, and Naani was dead on her feet, so we made for a moderately distant fire-hole a little north of our position, to thaw our frozen bones before we rested. It took a full hour to reach it, far larger than it had looked from far off: a great rocky hollow holding a gulf of roaring red flame that cast a wide warm glow across the dark land. I signaled Naani to stay dead silent, drew my Diskos from my hip, and crawled on hands and knees to the lip of the hollow, gesturing for her to follow. We peered down at the fire, and saw no immediate large monsters lingering nearby – but I did not let my caution slip, for I knew too well what horrors lurked close to heat sources.

Naani was shivering so hard her teeth chattered, begging to climb down to the fire just long enough to chase the bitter chill out of her bones. My head told me it was unwise, but my heart could not resist: the constant distant dangers of the Night Land felt small and unreal next to the sharp, biting cold that was turning her lips blue. I searched the hollow thoroughly first, and found no large threats, only three small snakes and two scorpion-like creatures that hid motionless in rock crevices, neither advancing nor retreating. But I knew those venomous, heat-loving things would creep toward us while we slept, so I said nothing of them to Naani, letting her enjoy the warmth until she spotted a snake slither past the rocks herself, and begged me to find a secure spot far from any crawling things.

We found a tiny, dry cave set 30 feet up the face of a tall rock spire rising from the middle of the hollow, no crannies or hiding nooks for small creatures to lurk in. We climbed inside, the fire’s soft red glow spilling over us, a tiny haven cut off from the vast, menacing dark of the Night Land. My heart stayed tense, listening for every faint distant sound, but Naani trusted me utterly, no flicker of fear in her that I would not keep her safe – a trust so sweet it almost eased the constant, heavy dread I carried for our journey home. We curled together wrapped in our shared cloak, and slept seven full hours, 24 full hours from our last rest, jolting awake at the exact same instant.

Screams ripped through the night, raw, human, thick with terror. My heart clenched: it was a band of survivors from the Lesser Redoubt, running for their lives. I burned to leap to their aid, but I could not leave Naani unguarded. Thunderous hoarse roars echoed across the dark, voices like rolling boulders, and the ground shook under the heavy thud of massive, running feet. Naani trembled hard in my arms, for she had heard those exact sounds every night during her month lost and wandering in the Night Land. A young woman’s scream cut through the dark, abruptly snuffed out, followed by more cries, the chase drawing closer. A ragged, half-naked band of men and women ran past the mouth of the hollow, torn and bleeding from rocks and thorns, wild with exhaustion, all hope gone from their faces. Four giants lumbered past after them: three shaggy, brutish, dark-skinned, the fourth a livid, blotched white thing, a monster stitched together from unholy life. The chase faded off into the dark, and a final distant wail marked the end of the last of the fleeing survivors.

A hot, bitter anger burned in my chest – I thought they should have turned to fight, even if every last one of them died, for they were doomed anyway, and might have at least taken one giant down with them. But I knew the truth: the people of the Lesser Redoubt had been starved of the life-giving Earth Current for a hundred thousand generations, robbed of some of that sharp, feral courage that lived in those raised near the Mighty Pyramid. Then I spotted a young naked woman stumble over the lip of the hollow, cowering under a low rock ledge below our spire. A squat, bull-broad shaggy giant slipped silently down after her, moving as quiet and deadly as a stalking cat.

I did not hesitate. I leaped the full 20 feet from the cave mouth down to the hollow floor, my Diskos screaming as it spun in my hand. I could not reach her in time: the brute ripped her apart before my eyes, her final cry ringing out across the fire-glow. The enraged monster lunged at me, leaping silent, and I swung the Diskos hard, slicing half its monstrous arms clean off, before it hurled me across the hollow, my armour clanging loud against the rocks. I was unhurt, back on my feet in an instant, and swung the whirling blade again, shearing straight through the giant’s head and shoulder, dropping it dead.

I carried the dead girl’s torn body and laid it gently in the fire, so no scavenger would ever feed on her. I turned back to the cave, and Naani was running toward me, the belt knife I had given her for defense held tight in her hand, ready to fight at my side. She was pale, but unshaking, staring at the dead monster’s hulking bulk in silence. The look in her eyes was so full of soft respect and unspoken love that it humbled me, a memory I would carry for every remaining day of my life. Only once I pulled her close did the trembling set in, the full horror of what we had both just seen crashing down on her.

We climbed back up to the cave, ate two nutrient tablets each and drank our fill of water, and waited a full hour listening intently for any sign of returning giants, before climbing back down, gathering our gear, and slipping out of the hollow. We moved slow, our ears pricked for every faint sound, taking two full hours to reach the edge of the ancient seabed. No threat drew near, and the vast, silent dark of the Night Land stretched unbroken all around us, as we set foot once more on the path of our homeward journey.

I had carried Naani a full hour down the winding, shadow-choked gorge, as I have told, when exhaustion stole so deep into my bones that I staggered with every step, my feet fumbling over the sharp volcanic rock as if they belonged to a stranger. I grew so heavy with drowsiness that I near nodded off mid-stride, and knew I would topple headlong with my precious maid in my arms if I did not find a place to rest, and quickly.

Shaking the fog from my wits, I dragged my attention to the gorge walls, and realized I had walked that entire hour as a sleepwalker, half-dreaming, no clear memory of the path beneath me. Now that I forced my will to perceive my surroundings, the whole narrow, fire-glowing cut felt new to my eyes, until the sharp, well-trained map of my outward journey rose unbidden in my mind. A great ruddy fire-vent burned not far ahead, I recalled, and set into the left cliff near its glow were seven small caves I had marked when I passed that way climbing upward, my heart then swollen with the desperate wish that I might bring my Own Safe to shelter in one of them on our journey back. That quiet, old longing now felt like a gift come true, and my tired heart lifted so light I near forgot the ache in my legs.

A dozen paces further, and I found them exactly as I remembered: seven small hollows cut high in the cliff face, the topmost of them dry, sheltered, and almost impossible for any scaled thing or abomination from the dark upper lands to climb without making noise enough to wake me. I dared not assume we had outrun the evil things that hunted us in the Redoubt’s dark country, and I knew if we nested in that high cave, any monster that tried to reach us would have to climb straight up against the edge of my Diskos before they could lay a claw on us.

I kissed Naani where she lay limp in my arms to wake her, and she kissed back soft and drowsy without opening her eyes, so deeply asleep she did not rouse. My love for her swelled so big it near hurt my chest, and I kissed her again, shook her gentle by the shoulder until her lashes fluttered, and told her we had reached a place safe enough for us to sleep. She blinked at me, still fogged with rest, and immediately chided herself for sleeping while I bore her weight all that way, her cheeks pink with shame. I kissed her again where she stood swaying on her feet, her eyes still soft with the haze of sleep, and she leaned into me, half-gone still, and whispered that she loved me, utterly and forever, for all the days left to us.

I bade her walk slow back and forth along a flat stretch of rock to wake herself fully, then climbed the cliff face up to the top cave first to scout it out. It was sweeter than I dared hope: warm from the underground fires that burned deep beneath the rock, dry as dust, the red glow of the nearby fire-vent leaking in soft and dim, no damp crevices for creeping things to hide. I called down to Naani that it was fit for our rest, then climbed back down to help her make the short, steep climb up, until we both stumbled over the threshold and stood inside, breathless, and giddy with the quiet certainty that for a little while, we were safe.

Before we could lay down, I slung our scrip and pouch over one shoulder, climbed back down to the gorge floor, and hauled up a boulder as heavy as I could carry, strapping it to my back to bring it up. Naani waited for me at the cave mouth, her face tight with anxiety that I might slip and dash myself against the rocks below, and when I climbed back in, I set the boulder right on the lip of the cave entrance, balanced so light that the faintest nudge from something climbing upward from below would send it thundering down the hundred foot drop. It would not kill a large monster, but the roar of its fall would rouse me from even the deepest, most exhausted sleep.

Naani had already spread our thick wool cloak across the smooth cave floor for us to lie on. The cave was so warm we had no need of it to cover us, and I could not even feel its softness through the thick hard plate of my armour, but she had laid it out as a shared couch, natural and sweet, no thought of impropriety in her head, only the quiet, unspoken wish to lie as close to me as she could. She patted the spot on the cloak beside her, and when I lay down on my side, she knelt to kiss me soft and solemn on the lips, then curled up beside me. We were both asleep in less than a minute.

I woke twelve hours later to the faint fizz of water heating over our small thermal chemical pack, and found Naani already up, kneeling by our stores, preparing our rations. She laughed when she saw me blink awake, bright and tender, and came to kneel beside me to kiss me, so full of soft, motherly fondness I knew at once she had been awake for an hour or more, watching me sleep. I held my arms out to her, and she snuggled against my chest happily, unresisting, though the cold hard plate of my armour dug a little into her soft cheek. I did not squeeze too tight, for fear of hurting her, but she pressed closer anyway, as if the hard, unyielding metal of my protection felt like safety to her.

She fussed at me to lie back when I moved to get up and check the boulder at the cave mouth, chiding that she meant to spoil me for once. She had checked the lip a dozen times since waking, she said, the boulder was still balanced perfectly, and there was no sign of any creature moving along the gorge floor below. I obeyed, happy to let her tend to me, and she lifted my head to rest on her knees, brought me a cup of warm water, and kissed each of our ration tablets before she passed them to me to eat. We took our second ration, drank deep of the warm water, and laughed quiet, soft, giddy as children, our hearts light for the first time since we had fled the Redoubt.

When I stood up a few minutes later, I near cried out with the sharp, burning ache of bruises I had not even felt while I was running and fighting. The four-armed yellow beast man I had slain back in the dark lands had done far more damage to me than I had realized, and my whole body was stiff and sore as if I had been beaten with clubs. Naani had seen it coming, it turned out, and already had the jar of healing ointment unstoppered at the ready. She helped me unlatch my armour, peel off my tunic, and rubbed every bruise and sore muscle slow and gentle for a full hour, until the stiffness melted out of me, leaving me loose and pain free. Her eyes were soft and tender as she tended to me, and I felt so wrapped in her care I might have been a small child, held safe in the arms of someone who loved me more than anything in the world.

When she helped me to my feet again, I moved my limbs easy, no sharp pain left, and my courage surged back. I had been terrified I would be too sore to protect her when danger came. She brought me my spare clean tunic from the pouch, and then paused, holding it to her chest, her cheeks pink and soft as she stared at my arms, thick and corded with muscle from the daily strength exercises all folk of the Great Pyramid had practiced from childhood. Before I could tease her, she set the tunic aside, and held her arms out to me, that I might hold her.

I lifted her to my chest, my heart swelling so big I felt humbled, unworthy to hold something so soft and good. She rested her face against my breast for a long quiet minute, and I felt her press a secret, sly little kiss to the hard muscle of my chest where her face was hidden, before she pulled away, blushing bright, and helped me back into my tunic, then laced my armour shut for me.

When she was done, she stood back, half shy, half mischievous, then stepped forward, her eyes downcast, and sank to her knees in front of me, tears spilling over her cheeks. I did not ask what ailed her, for I knew: she was overflowing with love, and awe, and soft, strange worship, all tangled up in her heart, because to her I was the hero who had crossed the Night Land to save her. My own heart felt both proud and unutterably humble, and I knelt down to gather her up, and did not pretend to brush away her fond adoration. She was mine, and I would spend every rest of my days proving I was worthy of the way she looked at me.

She wiped her eyes after a minute, and nestled against my chest, quiet, until I slipped her little shoes off her feet one by one, chiding myself for not thinking of it earlier, for her soles were blistered and sore from all the miles we had walked. I tended to her feet, rubbed ointment into the blisters, tied her shoes back on, and then we climbed carefully down the steep cliff face from our high cave, setting back off down the gorge.

We traveled for six more careful, uneventful hours, the red glow of the fire vents lighting our path, the air warm, sharp with faint whiffs of sulfur, the distant low rumble of underground fires thrumming through the rock under our feet. We stopped to rest and eat, and I bathed her feet in a warm rock pool fed by the thermal springs, rubbing the ointment in deep to soothe the soreness. I knew we were no more than six or seven hours away from the long dark stretch of the gorge where the great slugs laired, and we would need to make that twelve hour stretch without pausing, no rest, no shelter, until we came out the other side. It would be wiser to rest and sleep now, fully refreshed, before we faced that horror.

I did not tell Naani about the slugs yet. She was happy now, charmed by the warm fire glow and the soft steam vents that hissed up through the rock, and felt quiet in her soul, sure that no evil thing would hunt us here. I would not spoil that peace until we had to.

Six more hours of easy walking, and we stumbled straight upon the very cave I had sheltered in on my outward journey, right beside the hot spring I had washed my wounds in, a stone’s throw from a small bubbling fire vent. Naani lit up with delight, eager to rest in the very spot where I had once hidden, half-dead and exhausted, after escaping the slugs. It was not set high up in the cliff like our last shelter, but it was dry and empty, no creeping things hiding inside, and we could block the mouth with boulders to make a barrier that would alert us if anything tried to break in while we slept.

We gathered all the heavy small boulders we could carry, and stacked them one by one across the cave mouth after we stepped inside, packing the gaps tight at the bottom so no small rat-thing or slug spawn could crawl through. It left us dim in the dark, but we felt utterly safe. Naani tucked our scrip and pouch under my head for a pillow, set the Diskos within arm’s reach beside me, even though the sharp, whirring edge of it made her shiver, then tucked the cloak around my shoulders and kissed me soft and solemn on the lips before she curled up on her own side of the small bed. She was asleep in minutes, breathing soft and steady.

I lay awake for a little while, watching her, until she snuggled over against me in her sleep, as if it was her right to press close to my side. I did not move to wrap my arms around her, or kiss her, for she trusted me utterly, and I would not take advantage of that soft, sleeping trust. Sleep claimed me soon after, and I did not stir for eight full hours.

I woke to the familiar fizz of Naani heating water, and saw her moving in the dim glow leaking through the gaps in our boulder wall. She noticed me wake, and gave a small, quiet cry of joy, hurrying over to throw her arms around my neck and kiss me three times quick on the lips. I realized then, half through the fuzz of waking, that I had felt a soft kiss on my lips in my dreams, moments before. The little mischief had snuck a kiss while I was still deep asleep, and said nothing of it. I said nothing either, only smiled, resolving that next time I would wake fast enough to catch her at it.

I climbed to my feet, pulled half the boulder wall down to let in the light, and peered out over the gorge floor. No monsters lurked there, no abominations crept through the red glow. The only living thing in sight was one of the fat, pale rat-things, gorged full of carrion, dozing peacefully on the edge of the small fire vent.

We emerged at last from the sad, shadow-choked mountain pass I had named the Upward Gorge, and paused to catch our breath at the foot of the peaks, far past the gorge’s dark, looming mouth.

Naani spun in giddy half-circles, her eyes darting every direction at once, breath coming in fast little gasps. Her irises glowed bright, lit up by equal parts wonder at all the new sights, and the sharp, dizzy relief of escaping the suffocating dread that had hung over us for days on end. She turned suddenly to stare back up into the gorge’s black depths, spotted its great shadowed opening, shivered, and broke into a run further down the sloping lit expanse of the Sea Country. She skidded to a halt again a short ways later, glancing back with a face that held equal awe and the quiet, loosening weight of released fear, before turning once more to gape at the wide rolling country and the vast stretch of the sea spread out before us. She was caught halfway between laughing and crying, overwhelmed by amazement, joy, and utter astonishment; she had never once in all her life stood in a wide, open stretch of soft, unconfined light, as any reader who knows her origin will already have guessed.

We both realized in that moment we no longer needed to whisper the way we had through the whole dark, narrow length of the gorge. Naani laughed out loud, and shouted as a child does to test an echo, her high clear voice drifting off pretty into the distance before it vanished across the empty country.

But an answer came back a moment later, rolling out of the dark mountains at our backs. We both spun around so fast our necks ached, unsure if the sound was just a natural echo, or some uncanny call luring us back down out of the gorge’s hidden horrors. We did not wait to find out, running further down the slope until we were gasping for air, halting only when we felt certain we had put all trace of the gorge’s strangeness far enough behind us.

We hunted for a flat, open rock to rest on, and soon found a perfect spot, elevated high enough above the low ground to give us a clear view of any approaching threat. We climbed atop it, sat close together to share our rations, glancing around constantly to make sure no Humpt Men or other dangerous creatures snuck up on us unawares.

Naani never stopped asking questions, never stopped staring out across the country at the sea. The vastness of it stirred her so deeply that faint, half-forgotten memories rose in her, vague and disjointed as old half-waking dreams, stinging her with a strange mix of pleasure and quiet grief. She broke suddenly into tears, burying her face in my chest until she had steadied herself, before bouncing back to her usual bright, easy joy.

Again and again she marveled at the clarity of the wide open air, a wonder I shared too, for I had spent my whole life trapped in the enclosed dark of the Night Land just as she had. Sometimes she would slip into speech that felt as old and distant as moonlight that fell ten thousand years prior, words tangled up in memories of a lost sunlit world no living soul in our dark age had any right to remember. Other times she would lean close against me, and we would sit in a soft, solemn silence, our spirits stirred by the great call of the sea that hummed somewhere in the back of both our minds, waking old dream-memories we had carried all our lives without ever knowing their source. I have no words to properly describe the soft, trembling chaos of feeling that wrapped around us in that quiet moment.

Miles away, past the foot of the mountains along the shore to our left, a great cloud of steam billowed up, the same mist I had tramped through on my outward journey. Naani asked about it, and I told her all I knew, explaining we would have to traverse it soon as part of our path home.

She stared awestruck at the glowing volcanoes burning out in the sea and scattered across the wide country, the grand scale of the landscape exalting her even as it made her feel small and soft, humbled by how much of the world existed outside the tiny cell she had been trapped in her whole life. I pulled her into my arms to kiss her, for she was so sweet and alive in that moment I could not help myself. She kissed me back immediately, peppering my face with pecks between questions, half eager to hear more of the world I had seen, half playfully teasing just to make me smile.

Presently she kissed me three slow, warm, deliberate times on the mouth, then set her small hands on my shoulders, their size absurdly precious against the broad metal of my armour, and shook me gently to make me answer her questions faster, full of bright, mischievous naughtiness that demanded I kiss her harder. I answered her between every kiss, and she laughed and slipped her hand between our lips to block me, so I kissed her small, soft palm instead. Before I could react she spread her fingers quick to kiss me through the gaps, then clamped her hand shut again to stop me from returning the gesture.

After that I made her stand up on the rock, brushed her hair loose over her shoulders, and pulled her boots off to show off her small, bare, pretty feet. She feigned protest at first, then stood soft and obedient, blushing so pink it made her even more lovely. I stepped back a pace to look at her, and she gave me a playful, naughty little glance before turning very grave, pretending to be a stiff, unyielding doll. When she turned back to face me, perfectly sedate, she suddenly stretched one foot out, pressing her pink toes to my lips before snatching them back fast, then launched herself gleefully into my arms begging to be hugged. I laughed, soft and tender, for I loved her more than words, and told her what any man in love would tell his beloved: that I wished I had a pocket big enough to tuck her inside, so she could stay pressed next to my heart forever. She laughed mischievously and told me she would tickle me and pinch me the whole time I carried her, and I shook her gently in mock exasperation, only for her to kiss me hard on the mouth halfway through, and I did not have it in me to pretend to be annoyed any longer.

She asked to be set down, then made me turn my back so she could reach the pouch strapped to my spine. She pulled out the small comb I kept there, and began to brush her hair as I sat and talked to her, my heart lighter than it had been for years. Though I still held quiet dread of the Humpt Men and the monstrous creatures that roamed the Sea Country, there was no lingering, crawling horror here like the evil that lurked in the dark of the Night Land. All the wonders here felt natural, untainted by the lurking malice of that old cursed place.

When she had finished brushing her hair, she reached to tie it back, but I asked her to leave it loose over her shoulders, for I thought it was impossibly pretty. She smiled and agreed, happy to indulge me.

We packed up our gear after that, I laced her boots back on for her, and we set off once more heading down the slope toward the shore. We kept a steady, easy pace that would not wear us out, for my plan was to make camp and sleep on this side of the great steam zone near the shore, just as I had done on my journey out. We tramped along the foot of the mountains for six full hours, and when the day’s travel clocked past the eighteenth hour, we were both ready to sleep.

We soon spotted a tall, steep rock that would be almost impossible for any climbing predator to scale, with a flat top twice my height across in every direction, perfect for our camp. We climbed safely to the top, ate our rations, and laid down the cloak to sleep on. The air was warm and soft, we did not need any covering at all.

We woke seven hours later, both sitting bolt upright at the same time to stare at each other, as if we were seeing one another for the first time all over again, flooded with fresh joy just from looking at each other. Naani threw herself into my arms, and we kissed until we were both breathless, hungry for each other even after we had woken pressed close. She laid out our morning rations, the fizzing mineral water bubbling over the edges of the cup to make us laugh, and we ate slowly, chattering about every new sight she spotted. I kept my gaze on the ground nearby, watching for any sign of approaching danger, while she stared up at the mountain range the gorge cut through. Seen in the soft light of day, the peaks loomed monstrous, a vast unbroken wall stretching upward forever until it vanished out of the lit zone, lost to the eternal dark of the cold upper sky. It made me realize how much calmer I was now, traveling with no desperate hurry, than I had been on my frantic outward journey.

We hurried ourselves a little once we noticed we were lingering too long, climbed down from our sleeping rock, and set off again at a good pace. Before long we heard the far-off hissing of steam, the roar of boiling water erupting up from the earth below, a sound Naani had never heard before. I reassured her, and she pressed close to my side as we stepped into the thick billowing mist. I carried her on my back the whole three hours we traversed the steam zone, leading the way so she would never accidentally step into a hidden boiling pool. I guided myself mostly by the feel of the sea shore to our left, since we could see nothing more than an arm’s length in front of us. The water boiled in patches all around us, the ground shook under our feet, and the hissing shrieks and whistles of the geysers sometimes sounded like the cries of great hidden monsters, other times fading to a dead silence broken only by a thin, lonesome piping from some distant steam vent.

By the fourth hour we stumbled out of the thick mist, the roars and hisses fading far behind us until the steam thinned to nothing more than a faint wisp of fog. Clear air opened around us, and Naani spotted the great forests stretching out to our right, while the sea shore stayed to our left. She was utterly awestruck by the trees, running to pluck branches, crumpling the leaves between her fingers and breathing in their scent, overwhelmed by those same faint, dreamlike old memories again; she had never seen a living tree in her whole life trapped in the pyramid.

When the sixth hour of travel after the steam zone came, we stopped at a sheltered spot to rest and eat our rations. Naani asked me to keep watch while she bathed in a shallow warm pool nearby, and I stood guard a short distance away, never far enough that I could not reach her in an instant if something came. When she was done she kept watch for me, and helped me clean up as best she could, soft and nurturing, half motherly in her care, even as her mischievous maidenly peeked through every few moments.

We set off again after that, chatting idly as I kept a sharp eye on our surroundings. After seven hours of walking we came within sight of the bright burning fire-hill volcano that jutted out of the sea, the very same one that cast warm golden light on me while I slept in the great tree during my outward journey. I had planned for us to stop to eat near that very tree, and when I brought Naani there and told her the story of how I had hidden there from the great horned beast, she begged me to climb up to the thick branch I had slept on, to eat our rations there.

I agreed, going below her the whole climb to catch her if she slipped, and we settled on the great wide branch. She made me show her exactly where I had laid, and traced the faint indent my heavy armour had left in the bark. She sat quiet for a long time, staring out at the glowing light of the sea volcano, and I did not disturb her. When she stood to climb back down, she kissed the spot where I had laid, then pulled her small knife out and cut a small chip of bark free, tucking it inside her tunic to keep as a souvenir. When we got back to the ground, she spotted the old tracks of the great horned beast I had outrun that day, the faint mark of its belly brushing the soil as it ran. It struck me then that only seventeen days had passed since I first stopped at that tree, though it felt like a lifetime of terror and joy compressed into that small span of time.

We set off once more, and I lifted Naani onto my back once she had walked twelve full hours, as was our usual custom. She insisted she could walk for six more hours to spare my strength, but I knew her fragile body, weakened after a month trapped alone and running for her life, would wear out far too fast if I let her. She pressed soft against my back as we traveled, marveling at how unshakable my strength and resolve had to be to make the whole journey out to rescue her. I smiled, happy she took such pride in my strength, just as any man in love would be glad to be strong enough to carry his beloved to safety.

I shifted her to make her more comfortable, and suddenly realized my hard metal armour must be digging into her bare shoulders the whole time. I asked her, and when she tried to lie I saw the dark purple bruises blooming where the metal had pressed into her soft skin. I was furious at myself for not noticing, and half-furious at her for hiding the pain from me. I set her down immediately, and she saw how cross I was, teasing me with little playful pouts and kisses to distract me. I pretended I was going to scold her properly, and she finally stopped teasing, tucking her two small fists into the palm of one of my huge hands, too stubborn to apologize properly but soft enough that I could not keep pretending to be stern. I wrapped my other arm around her, pulling her tight against my chest, and all my anger melted away, my whole being swearing to be her shield for the rest of our lives.

I drifted back to consciousness anchored only by throbbing, all memory scattered to static, every muscle too heavy to lift. For a dazed moment I thought some unknown, hostile force held me pinned, until I realized the weight was only my own ragged weakness, the aftermath of the fight I could not yet name.

I lay flat on my back, and a soft, ragged panting sounded just at my shoulder. I turned my head slow, every movement sending jolts of ache down my spine, and there she was: my Maid, still naked, heaving all her strength against the thick hardwood pole I had hewn back when the Humpt Men first descended on us. Her face was set hard, teeth bared with effort, her eyes fixed backward toward the far shore we were pulling away from. And in that instant, every memory flooded back. I was on our raft. She had dragged me here while I was unconscious, and was fighting to row us out of reach of the monsters that would have torn both of us apart.

I tried to make a sound, but my throat was too raw, and she did not hear. The far-off howl of the Humpt Men drifted across the water, thin and rabid, and I understood what had happened: she had woken first, to find me splayed across her chest, still and cold, soaked through with my own blood, and had not stopped to grieve. She had found a strength no ordinary woman could have summoned, hauled my limp, bleeding body all the way to the raft, and pushed us out into the current before the last of the Humpt Men could reach her. She never could explain how she did it, later, not even to me. She only ever said love gave her the power to carry me, that she would die before she let them tear the man she loved apart.

She told me the full tale later, when I was well enough to listen. When she first woke, my head was heavy on her breast, and she was certain I was dead. She slid out from under me as gentle as she could, laid me flat on the earth, and her heart nearly split when she saw how much blood I had spilled, staining the dirt all around us. But then she felt the faint, thready beat of my pulse, and hope exploded in her chest. She glanced around fast, and saw only the corpse of the Humpt Man that had attacked her, and the rest of their kind strewn dead across the flat-topped rock, just as I had left them.

She ran for the raft, filled my old headpiece with river water, and dashed it over my face to try and rouse me. When I still did not wake, a quiet, sharp certainty of coming danger pricked at the back of her mind, and she decided then she would save me or die trying. She dragged me all the weariful distance back to the raft, and then ran for the pole – and as she grabbed it, she spotted her own torn clothes, still clamped in the dead Humpt Man’s fist, the ones she had slipped out of to escape him. She wrenched them free in a single snatch, ran back to the raft, pushed it hard off the shore, and leaped aboard just as the two surviving Humpt Men burst out of the treeline. They raced down the bank, silent and intent on catching her, but she had already fought the raft far enough out that they could not reach it before they arrived. They must have been unable to swim, or knew some nameless dread lurked in the dark river water, for they never stepped past the shoreline. They only stood there, staring stupid after us, before throwing back their heads and howling – the very howling I heard the second I dragged myself back to wakefulness.

The howls grew fainter and fainter as she drove the pole through the water again and again, every muscle screaming with effort. I tried to push myself up to help her, but the blackness swallowed me whole before I could even lift my shoulders. I never knew a thing about the rest of that journey, not until I woke safe on the little island we had been aiming for, laid out on a bed of soft dry moss, and she had dragged me up the shore all alone, never stopping to rest for a second. I was inches from death, helpless to protect her, and she had saved me. I still burn bright with joy, even now, to remember I at least fought a good fight for her before I fell.

For a long stretch after that, nothing is clear to me. Only pain, and endless weariness, half-waking moments that dissolved back into fog the second I tried to hold onto them, when I had no sense of where I was or if the waking world was even real. But no matter how deep the black mists of my weakness dragged me, I could feel love hovering close, soft and unrelenting watchfulness, that chased the nameless, stealthy despairs creeping through my bones away before they could sink their teeth in.

Then one day I woke, and the haze lifted. The constant, searing ache had softened to a dull throb, the strange disorienting weight was gone from my limbs. I was laid soft on that bed of moss, the air around me still and sweet, and a warm, healing drowse seeped slow into my bones.

I turned my head, and there she was, kneeling right beside me, staring down at me with such overflowing love and gladness that it felt like I was drinking health straight from her gaze. She leaned down and kissed me, so gentle her lips barely brushed mine, and her warm tears fell soft on my cheek. I kissed her back, and there was no greater joy in all the world in that moment.

She cradled my head in the crook of her arm, lifted a cup to my lips, and made me drink a thin broth she had brewed from our nutrient tablets mixed with fresh water. When I was done, she kissed me again, light as a quiet summer wind brushing my mouth, and adjusted the moss bedding under my head. I fell asleep before she could even pull her hand away.

I woke three more times in the next two days, each time a little stronger, each time finding her right there holding my hand. The fourth time I woke, I whispered that I loved her, and she burst into sudden, unsteady weeping, pressing my hand tight to her breast over her heart.

The fifth time I woke properly, I noticed I was wrapped in my heavy cloak, my entire torso wrapped in soft bandages. She had torn the strips right from the remains of her own clothing, she told me later, to make dressings for my wounds. And she had mended the rest of her garments, too: she had pulled threads from the frayed edges of the cloth, pricked sharp thorns from the tiny island bushes to use as needles, broken dozens of them over the long days sitting by my side tending to me, until she had stitched every tear shut, and was dressed again in the clothes I had given her back when we first set out on our journey.

When I looked at her, taking in the neat mends, she remembered the last time I had seen her, bare on the raft, and blushed bright pink, leaning down to kiss me so she could tuck her face away from my gaze. I ached so badly to be strong enough to kneel before her, to honor her the way she deserved, the way anyone who has truly loved knows you would do for the person who would give the whole world for you.

My strength came back slow and steady. She tended to me every hour on the hour, checking the time against my old pocket watch to make sure she fed me broth and water on schedule, washed my wounds, changed the bandages, washed and dried the old ones to reuse again, since we had no spares to waste. By the fifth day, all the worst of the pain was gone, and I was so happy I could barely speak for the weight of it. She chattered soft and sweet to me the whole day, but kept me from moving a muscle, scolding me gentle every time I tried to sit up.

On the sixth day, she let me talk back. I told her every single way I loved her, every thought I had held of her while I lay silent, too weak to move. She told me she was perfectly well, fully recovered from the Humpt Man’s attack, but I could see she was thin, her eyes dark with circles from days of no sleep, even when they glowed bright with love every time she looked at me.

I made her bring all our remaining tablets over, kiss each one before she gave it to me to eat, and then bade her lie down beside me, rest her head on my good arm. I had her nestle into my side, told her to sleep, that the weight of her against me would rest me more than any quiet ever could. She worried at first that she was too heavy, but I pulled her gently closer to prove I had enough strength to hold her, and she settled in, and fell asleep instantly, so deep she barely stirred for twelve full hours. Once she made a soft little murmur in her sleep, and snuggled her face closer to my chest. I did not grow tired once, did not feel lonely at all. I just lay there, staring down at her, her face soft and peaceful, lit by the faint glow of our light stone, and a quiet, holy joy filled my chest, so full it felt like my heart would burst. I sipped our broth at the third hour, the sixth, the ninth, until it was all gone, and every so often I rested my hand on the hilt of my Diskos, that great old weapon, like it was a trusted comrade. It felt like it knew me, loved me back, the way all great weapons bond to the hands that wield them.

At the twelfth hour, she woke all of a sudden, jolting up in a sweet panic to check that I had not come to harm while she slept. She was horrified when she realized she had slept for half a day straight, no longer tending to me, but I pretended to scold her in mock sternness, forbade her from saying a single word more of apology, and told her the only thing that mattered was that I was happier than I had ever been in all my life, and she should be too. That impudent little thing then balled her tiny fist and prodded it against my nose, pretending to threaten me, and I laughed so hard she got scared I would tear my wounds open again and start bleeding. I assured her I was fine, no harm done at all.

Offhand, I asked her if she had any brothers, when she was little, for she played so naturally, so childlike in that moment. The second the words left my mouth I realized I had spoken thoughtlessly, for I knew full well almost no one in our dark age grew up with family. She nodded quiet, kissed my hand, and slipped away from me so she could weep out of sight. I called soft to her, no anger in my voice, and she came back soon, smiling and bright, though I could see she had been crying, and made me a fresh pot of broth. Before I would drink it, I pulled her into my arms, and she rested her weight light against me, careful not to press on my wounds. We ate together after that, talking soft and happy, until I fell asleep, and she stayed right by my side, keeping watch.

The seventh day was pure, unbroken joy. I woke to find her sleeping curled up right next to me, her face tucked against my shoulder, and she woke the second I stirred, used to rousing at the smallest sound to check on me. She washed me gentle, changed my bandages, and that day she let me eat full nutrient tablets instead of broth, the way I did when I was well. I ate as many as I could, trying to build my strength back fast, so I could protect her again, so we could finish our journey to the safety of the Mighty Pyramid. We kissed each other’s tablets before we ate, drank from the same cup, half children giddy with joy, half man and woman drunk on loving each other.

Later, she went off to bathe in a little hidden pool behind the island bushes, and came back with her hair hanging loose in a soft cloud down her back, her little bare feet still damp from the water. She teased me for being so impatient for her to come back, and sat right beside me, letting me watch her as she brushed out all that long, lovely hair. I plucked a single strand from her head when she was not looking, and when she held out her bare feet to me like I had asked, I bound her pretty toes together with that single hair, so she was my little captive. We laughed like fools, and she did not break the hair even once as she sat there beside me, trying very hard to pretend she had not noticed at all.

She started piling her hair on top of her head to pin it up, and I kept reaching up to tousle it back down again. She laughed, and draped all that abundance of hair over both sides of my face, so I was peeking out through a curtain of her hair, and kissed me soft on the mouth. Then she cut a lock of her hair, and a lock of mine, plaited them together, and tucked the braid into the neck of her dress, against her bosom. Then she made another braid, for me, and I tucked it under the big bandage wrapped over my heart. We pretended to quarrel over who had the braid that was kissed the most, and the only way to end the fight was to kiss until we were both laughing too hard to pretend to be mad. Everyone who has ever been in love knows that kind of silly, perfect foolishness, I think.

Then she held my wrists down, teased me that if I kept reaching up to mess her hair she would punish me, and her tiny little hands were so small she could barely hold my two wrists with one, only by hooking her thumbs over them. She covered my eyes with her free hand so I could not see, and kissed me light and impudent on the mouth, before letting go and pretending to be perfectly demure, like she had not done a single thing. We sat quiet for a while after that, and I held her two tiny fists in my one big, pale, shaking hand, the one that still had not recovered enough blood to stop trembling, and it was the sweetest pleasure I had ever known.

I told her a story then, of a young man who lived long ago, in the old sunlit world before the Night fell, who met the one woman in all the world made for him. They married, and she died, and the grief almost destroyed him, until he woke up in this dark far future, and learned his beloved was also alive here. He searched for her for years, and finally found her, and he loved her so deeply, so reverently, that his love felt like a soft, constant, sweet ache in his chest, made of memories of that old sunlit life, and the joy of being with her again. I did not get far into the tale before she broke down weeping, got on her knees beside me, and pressed my hand to her breast. A faint, glowing memory lit up in her eyes, like she was half dreaming, and I felt that same memory tug at my own mind, from the half-open gates of the forgotten past. We both remembered, for a second, the little child we had together, back in that old sunlit age. No words needed to be said. The silence hung soft and holy between us. She kissed me, pulled herself back from the haze of the old days, and went off to get our food ready.

That day was perfect. We laughed, we told old half-remembered jokes from that forgotten past, and one of us blurted out the answer to an old riddle no one in this dark age would ever know – why did the man die at the dinner party? Because he was a little hoarse. We stared at each other after we said it, confused, because neither of us could even remember what a horse was. But we could feel the memory of it, faint and soft, drifting up through millions of lost years.

I pulled us back from the haze of those old memories then, and told her all about the Mighty Pyramid, every little detail: the glowing spires, the endless libraries, the millions of people living safe behind its walls, the wonders she had never even dreamed of. She went quiet, eyes wide, drinking in every word, like I had come down from a star to tell her of worlds no one had ever seen. I knew right then, once my strength was fully back, we would make it there together. Nothing in all the Night Land could stop us.

It was the tenth hour of our day when we pushed the raft off from shore, and the paddles moved light and balanced in the carved rests I’d rigged earlier, just as I’d hoped. The craft cut through the smooth dark water with barely any effort, the Maid taking the fore paddle and I the stern, our steady, synchronized strokes carrying us at a pace almost as fast as we could walk the rough, broken shoreline of that sunken, light-dappled land.

By the twelfth hour we stopped to eat and drink, then took up our easy labor again. When we settled into the rhythm of the paddles, we scarcely noticed we moved at all, only the faint gentle rock of the raft under our feet. The hours slipped by soft and unrushed, our voices low as we spoke of small, dear things, the Maid glancing back over her shoulder often to smile at me with warm love, her lips curved in a little teasing pout that made me nearly drop my paddle every time to go to her, till she’d shake her head, laughing soft and bidding me keep to my work.

When the eighteenth hour arrived, we pulled our paddles aboard. The Maid spread our thick cloak out as a bed, we ate our rations, and we fell into a sound, happy slumber almost instantly, as if the soft lull of the water had rocked us both straight to sleep.

Eight full hours later we woke at the exact same moment, disoriented for a breath, not remembering at first where we were, before the full weight of our safe, shared journey settled over us. We laughed face to face, giddy with the joy of waking to each other, kissed long and slow, washed the grime of the voyage off in the cool sea water, and settled to our breakfast before shoving off to paddle onward. For the rest of that day we drifted along the coast, peaceful, content, no shadow of trouble touching us.

The entire voyage stretched across four full twenty-four hour days. We made no great haste, moved slow and easy, so I might take all the time I needed to rebuild my lost strength. Nothing untoward befell us in all that time, save once when we spotted a huge, lumbering beast heave itself up out of the sea far off on the shore, where it browsed slow on the thick herbage as if it belonged there. We were far too distant to make out its features clearly, but it filled us with no dread, only quiet relief we were not close enough to cross its path. It had no trace of that wrong, monstrous aura that clung to the abominations of the Night Land, it felt entirely natural, as if we had stumbled back into the earliest dawn of the world. That old saying about extremes meeting ran through my head then: the most ancient of natural life coexisting with the twisted, cursed half-human horrors of the land beyond the gorge. I did not waste long on the thought, only noted that the core spark of humanity could never be unmade, no matter how much the flesh might be modified by circumstance or cruel, twisted breeding — a truth that filled me with a quiet, unshakable hope for the bright, uplifted, holy world we had caught a glimpse of once, when we had stood pure together.

The only other thing of note we passed was a great Fire-Hill that rose straight out of the sea, so close we skirted its very edge. The water boiled hot around parts of its base, and two dozen great jets of steam and superheated gas shot hundreds of feet up into the dark air, their roar loud enough to reach us across the intervening water. Low, guttural grunts rumbled up from the depths of the sea at its foot, the sound of gasses fighting to escape the crust of the world below. We stared back at that seething hill of raw geologic power long after it had shrunk to a faint glowing dot behind us, aware for the first time of the titanic, sleeping energy coiled under every inch of that sunken land.

Beyond that, our path lay clear. We threaded the narrow, winding water passages that split the great inland sea into smaller, sheltered pools, and I pointed out the two spots where I had camped alone, starving and desperate, on my first journey through that sunken country. The Maid leaned in rapt, pestering me for every small, trivial detail of those lonely nights, her eyes bright with wonder.

Four full days to the hour from when we had pushed off, we beached the raft on a flat stretch of shore, where the land sloped upward to the dark mouth of the First Gorge I had traveled so long before. We dragged the craft up out of the tide line, and stood quiet for a moment, wondering if any soul would ever stumble across it for all the rest of eternity. The Maid cut a small, smooth splinter of its frame free, tucking it into the pocket of her tunic as a memento.

We laid all our gear out on the sand, and the Maid helped me strap back into my heavy armor, settle the scrip and the storage pouch firm on my back, and heft the Diskos into my hand, its weight familiar and reassuring. She hefted her own now much smaller pack, loosened her hunting knife in her belt, ready to walk. She knelt then, and pressed a soft kiss to the planks of the raft, all her old memories of the little island where she had nursed me back from the edge of death crowding up in her chest, one last gentle goodbye to that first chapter of our new life. I stooped to lift her to her feet, tucking her close against my side, and we set off, climbing the slow upward slope toward the dark mouth of the great gorge.

Miles off to our right loomed that unimaginably vast mountain, its four great fire vents glowing red far up near its peak, endless slopes of ash piled up below them that had been spewed out into the dark night for uncounted eons. The Maid stared at it for ages, awestruck, and I did not rush her — no human that had ever lived had seen a sight so grand, so utterly huge, as that.

At last we reached the high rim of the gorge, and paused at the sharp left bend where the path vanished into deeper, unlit gloom. We turned once more to look back over the whole sunken country of seas, glowing soft red far below us, spread out under the endless night of the world. We knew in that quiet moment we might well be the very last two olden humans ever to look on that lost land. I wondered, idly, if the brutish Humpt Men might one day, in some unimaginably distant eternity, nurture that soft, unbreakable human spirit hidden under their rough, crude flesh. For long minutes we stood silent, listening to the distant, low mutter of the great fire mountains, the faint hum of life drifting across the sunken land, only a few short paces away from the dead, absolute silence of the gorge that would lead us back to the Night Land. The Maid clung tight to my arm, silent tears rolling down her face, her heart heavy with equal parts sorrow and joy, already imagining the stories she would one day tell our children of this strange, wondrous land that no other soul would ever see.

We turned at last, her hand slipping firm into mine, and rounded the great sharp corner of the gorge, stumbling slow into the thick gloom.

We walked steady for sixteen hours, no trouble touching us, nothing but the deep dark to make us cautious, until we realized we had been awake for twenty-six hours straight — a foolish mistake, when I still needed every scrap of my full strength for the dangers that waited ahead. We found a sheltered nook between two great boulders, ate our rations, and consulted the notes I had scrawled on the pages of my journal to map our old outward path. We settled on a strict, unbreakable routine: sixteen hours of travel a day, eight full hours of unbroken sleep, no exceptions. It took us five full days to traverse the entire length of the great gorge. We cheered up every time we stepped out of the dark into the faint, dancing red light of a burning gas vent, holding our breath through pockets of choking, foul fumes that lingered in the rock crevices. My strength built back steadily, until I felt almost whole again. The Maid never once let me carry her, stepping light and sure over the broken rocks, growing used to the endless, steady march. At every camp spot I had rested at on my first lonely journey, she would stop and stand for a quiet moment exactly where I had once laid, heartbroken and certain I would never see her again, too tender with her affection for words.

She asked every few hours how much farther we had to the Night Land, when we would see the Mighty Redoubt, her excitement bright and thrumming, and my own heart beat just as fast as hers, every step making me more desperate to get her behind those walls, safe from all the monsters that roamed the dark. The gorge was utterly dead save for the occasional low moan or high thin whistle of a burning gas jet, the sound echoing for miles across the empty rock, making the vast loneliness of the place settle heavy in our bones. I felt a quiet, warm pride in my chest when I saw the way she looked at me, knowing she understood how much I had suffered that lonely journey out to find her, and that her love for me grew deeper with every step.

On the fifth day, around the seventh hour, I began to catch faint, odd sounds that seemed to whisper off the rocks all around us, as if the stone itself was muttering soft secrets. I pulled the Maid instantly tight to my side, drew the Diskos, and we moved forward slow, cautious. We passed three dancing gas fires, their moans mixing with the odd, whispering sounds, before I realized what we were hearing: they were just echoes, bouncing off the close rock faces, drifting all the way from a far greater, louder noise. I recognized it at once: the monstrous piping roar of the Great Gas Fountain I had encountered on my old journey. The Maid leaned forward instantly, bright with excitement — that landmark meant we were nearly out of the gorge, the long stretch of our travels almost at an end.

At first we could not pick the fountain out ahead of us, the dozens of smaller gas fires dotted along the length of the gorge blurring together into a sea of shifting light. But after we had walked a little further, we spotted it: the dark night far ahead flickered in constant shuddering bursts of blue light, as if the very darkness itself was gasping. Slowly that vague shimmer resolved into a vast, leaping pillar of brilliant blue flame, and the piping noise grew, rising into a shifting, thunderous roar that shook the very rocks under our feet. We walked past the last of the small, scattered gas fires, and the whole length of the gorge was lit in wavering, eerie blue light cast off by that giant fountain, so huge it loomed half the height of the gorge walls.

We came to a stop not far from the base of it, half deafened by the roar, two tiny, lonesome figures standing in that vast empty gorge, staring up at that impossible tower of flame. I wrapped my arm around the Maid, tucking her against my chest, and we stood silent for long minutes, unable to speak over the noise. When we turned to look at each other at last, we kissed soft and solemn, lit all over by that strange blue glow. We stared again at the fountain, watching its light spill out for miles across the distant gorge walls, turning the far dark peaks into the silhouette of some lost, long-forgotten world of empty mountains.

We pushed on a mile further to get far enough away that the roar stopped ringing in our ears, and stopped for our rest in a secure hollow among the boulders, the hour already well past the seventeenth. When we woke, we sat quiet a little while longer, watching the great flame dance, ringed round by towering dark rocks that looked like silent, giant sentinels standing guard over it for all eternity. We shouldered our packs, turned toward the utter black of the final steep slope that led up out of the gorge and back into the Night Land, and began the long, endless climb.

Every few steps at first we turned to glance back down the long height of the gorge, at the faint far glimmer of the fountain shuddering far below in the dark, its quaking light spilling through the blackness. Slowly it shrank to nothing behind us, vanished entirely, swallowed by the absolute, unbroken dark of the slope. We stumbled upward for sixteen straight hours, the thick dark wrapping so tight around us we began to feel half unreal, as if we had walked right out of the world we knew into some empty, eternal void.

For eight full days after that we crawled upward on hands and knees, I leading the way, the Diskos loose and ready at my hip. I tore two spare straps from the scrip and storage pouch, tying one end to my belt and the other to the Maid’s, so I would always know exactly where she was, no risk of her slipping away and getting lost in the total dark. We stuck to our routine unflinchingly: sixteen hours of crawling, stops to eat at the sixth and twelfth hours of travel, another meal before we settled to sleep, and one more on waking, with eight full unbroken hours of rest every cycle. Sometimes the endless upward grind wore me thin, sick of groping blind for handholds around boulders and gaping crevices, feeling as if we would never see light, never feel anything but that cold thick dark, for the rest of time. Every time that despair crept over me, I would stop, call soft to the Maid, and pull her into my arms in the pitch black, and we would hold each other tight, drawing all the comfort we could from each other, wrapped in the waiting silence of that endless night.

I drew at last within sight of the Mighty Pyramid, its bulk rearing vast out of the unending night like a forged mountain of life and safety. It would have stunned me afresh with its impossible scale, had not despair and bone-deep weariness clamped so tight to my heart I could not spare a thought for wonder—every fibre of my being burned for only one thing: to bear my Own Maid across the threshold of that great refuge. It still stood miles away, vast and glowing faint against the black sky.

I drove forward across the broken waste at a brutal, unrelenting pace. As I passed a hollow where a smoldering fire-hole cast wavering red light, something dragged itself up from the depression: a great shaggy, fur-covered man, who lifted his clawed, monstrous hands and stumbled eagerly toward me. His nails jutted hard and sharp as butchery blades, curving far past his fingertips, suited to tear through flesh and armor alike.

I set my burden flat to the earth in the same breath I drew my weapon. I cared nothing for my own life; I would not be delayed by any creature, not when my Maid’s life hung by a thread. I launched myself at the giant, cold, unbridled fury thrumming in my veins, and struck hard—but he twisted aside in a heartbeat, dodging the blow. His monstrous arm snaked out of the shadow cast by the fire-hole’s dancing flame, closed around my headpiece, and wrenched it from my skull with such brutal force he flung me a dozen feet backward onto the stony earth. I was unharmed save for bruises and the ringing shock of the blow, and I was on my feet in an instant, the Diskos roaring and blazing in my grip as I swung it. It bit through the giant’s torso as if he were made of smoke, glutted itself on his flesh even as his shoulders twisted in death. His upper half crumpled to the ground, leaving his legs and lower trunk standing upright in the fire-light, blood spouting upward in a dark fountain against the night.

I did not pause. I leaped to my Maid, swept her back into my arms, and ran on past the corpse, which collapsed behind me with a wet, horrid thud. The air thrumming around me hummed with the shared excitement of the Millions who lived in the Pyramid’s upper levels, their telepathic voices rising in a wave of love and delight—they had witnessed the fight, and poured their collective strength toward me across the dark miles.

I had not traveled a full mile more when two vague, shadowy shapes oozed out from behind a jumble of standing rocks. I cut them down with the Diskos without slowing, and never learned what they had been. After that, it felt as if I smote forever. Strange, slimy, fanged things poured out of the thorn thickets and crevices in the stone, as if the entire night land crawled with foul, ravenous life. I fought them in a daze of grim, mounting despair, pushing ever faster, certain the end of both our lives was close and I would not win my Maid to safety.

The land roared with the baying of giants, high and terrible, and lower, crawling sounds that curdled the blood in my veins. Once I heard the heavy, pounding footfall of a pack of giants running in the dark, and the whole of that endless night reeked of unnameable evil. I do not know what spared me from being torn apart a hundred times over, save that the brutal trials of my long journey had burned every last trace of softness and weakness from me, leaving no crack through which the malevolent forces of the Night could reach to break my mind.

Then the deep, dreadful baying of the Night-Hounds rose from the southeast, and drew nearer. I knew then no strength of my own could shield my Maid from them.

Out of the high dark where the Last Light hung, a sharp blue flash lanced downward, striking the earth far to the southeast. Then another, then a dozen more, accompanied by a sharp, crackling noise louder than any thunder I had ever known. The humans in the Pyramid were fighting for me, burning their power to clear a path.

It was as if all the life of the Night Land that had stirred before had been only half-asleep. The ground shook beneath my feet as hordes of monsters roared in confusion, great ancient forces roused from their aeons-long slumber, and that hideous, distant cackling Laughter from the hidden cursed country of the lost East boomed across the waste. The baying of the hounds grew louder by the second, a full massive pack now, no more than a mile off to my southeast. I was alone, save for my dying Maid in my arms, and I strained my eyes for the vanguard of the Hundred Thousand Prepared Warriors who had descended from the Pyramid’s levels to come to my aid. There was no sign of them, only shifting shadows, distant monstrous movement, and the hounds drawing closer every moment. Death was at my heels.

I did not slow my stride. I broke into a run, the glowing barrier of the Circle of Protection around the Pyramid clearly visible through the dark, peeking out between the hills. A desperate hope flared in my chest: I might yet carry my Maid across its threshold. The hounds’ baying was half a mile off now, to my left, their terrible hunting cries sharp with the scent of us. I screamed in useless despair, begging for aid, and the Millions’ collective anguish wrapped around me like a warm cloak—they felt my hopelessness, and poured every drop of their shared love and support into my spirit.

In that same moment, the deep thrumming vibration of the Earth-Current rose through the soles of my boots, and I saw the massive pack of hounds burst out of the dark to my left, great beasts the size of war horses, heads low as they ran full tilt for me. I knew we would be torn to shreds in less than sixty seconds if the Pyramid did not act. I stopped running, planted my feet, and glanced from the onrushing horde to the Mighty Pyramid, and back again. The hounds were scarce two hundred fathoms away, hundreds of them, closing fast.

As I stared toward the redoubt, a torrent of roaring, searing flame burst from its sealed lower levels, rushing down onto the path the hounds were running. The night vanished entirely in the blinding, terrible glory of that fire. A blast wave hit me, hot enough to singe my exposed skin even at that distance, and the great thunder of the Earth-Force tearing through the air and rending the stone beneath the hounds drowned out every other sound. The hounds were gone, swallowed by flame and ruptured earth, great boulders hurled in every direction by the force of the discharge. The Humans had loosed the full power of the Earth-Current to save me.

In a heartbeat, they cut the current off, reining the force back under their control. A vast silence fell over the waste, broken only by the crackle of residual flame where the Current had struck. I shook off my dazed stupor in seconds, and ran on, hope blazing hot in my chest that I would yet win us to safety.

My eyes adjusted to the dim dark quickly, and I scanned every direction, wary for any remaining threat. For a long stretch I saw nothing living, and heard no sound save that far-off, horrid Laughter from the dead East. As I ran, I glanced up often at the Mighty Pyramid, and soon realized its usual glow was far dimmer than it had ever been. The vast, unprecedented drain of the Earth-Current they had unleashed to save us had nearly depleted the redoubt’s core power. I knew at once what that meant: they could not risk firing the Current again until it recharged, or they would doom every last one of the Millions living inside. The Masters had no more great weapons to aid me.

The land soon began to fill with the low, hungry noises of roused monsters once more, shapes creeping between me and the dim glow of the Circle of Protection. I hefted the Diskos, tensed for the bitter final fight. A sudden unshakable warning pricked at the back of my spirit: new peril was close. I looked up toward the Pyramid, waiting for the Master Monstruwacan to flash a warning via the agreed telepathic signals, but no message came. The whole of the Pyramid’s machinery had failed, I later learned: every observation instrument, every great lift, every air pump had ceased running for nearly an hour while the Earth-Current’s flow stabilized. The entire civilization of mankind had come within a breath of total annihilation for my sake.

My spirit, wreathed in the shared awareness of the Millions, sensed the danger anyway, and I moved far more warily, scanning the dark every which way. Then I spotted it: a pale, steady glowing circle hanging motionless in the air, moving directly above my Maid and me. It was one of the holy, protective Powers of Light, standing between our souls and some nameless Evil Force that had crept near to destroy us. I did not fear it, placed my full trust in its guard, and ran on.

I drew within four hundred paces of the Circle, certain I would win my Maid to safety at last, when three shaggy beast-men rose up out of the dirt at my feet, growling and lurching for me. The first was so close I had no room to swing the Diskos, so I smashed its skull in with the weapon’s heavy haft. I twisted aside, swung the Diskos free, and cut through the other two in a cold, brutal frenzy, my Maid no heavier than a sleeping child in the crook of my arm. I slew them before they could land a single blow, unharmed.

A great shout of wonder and welcome rose from just inside the Circle, and I looked up to see thousands of men in gray armor lined up along the barrier. None of them stepped out to meet me, and in an instant I understood why. Monstrous, heaving Black Mounds of seething dark energy lined the entire stretch of no-man’s-land outside the Circle, writhing with a life that turned my stomach cold. Any human who dared step beyond the Circle’s threshold would have their soul torn apart instantly, devoured utterly by that Evil power. No aid could reach me, no matter how many of the Hundred Thousand were willing to die trying.

They shouted to me to run faster, and I poured every last scrap of my strength into my stride, watching that pale holy light hold steady above us, leading me on. I was no more than a hundred paces from the Circle’s glow when a horde of squat, brutish tusked men poured out of the shadows all around me, grabbing for me, trying to tear my Maid from my arms. I kicked out with my metal boots, wrenched free of their grasp, and leaped back, the Diskos spinning and roaring in my hands. I carved through them in a whirling blur of steel, holding my Maid high above their short, grasping reach so they could not harm her, even as they rained heavy stone blows down on my armor, denting and cracking it, leaving new wounds bleeding across my back and shoulders. The brutes seemed endless, but I fought ever forward toward the Circle, the shouts of the Hundred Thousand ringing in my ears as the men watched, many of them held back by their comrades from charging out to die uselessly.

I was scarce fifty paces from the barrier when the front rank of the Hundred Thousand hurled their spare Diskos weapons out into the horde around me, thinning the crowd of tusked brutes to clear a path. I summoned the last of my failing strength, charged through the gap, and stumbled over the glowing threshold of the Circle, its usual defensive resistance faint and weak as the drained current seeped back to full power. A thousand hands reached out to help me, then drew back, held off by an unspoken awe—my armor was caked head to foot in monster blood, the Diskos still dripping gore in my grip, and I was a stranger returned from a death no one had expected me to survive.

I stood swaying there, gasping, trying to form words to beg for doctors to save my Maid, who lay limp in my arms. Somewhere out in the dark, giants began to run. The men shouted around me, calling for doctors, warning each other of the approaching threat, pointing out that the holy light above us and the writhing Black Mounds outside the Circle had both vanished. The low, distant murmur of the Millions rose from the miles-high upper levels of the Pyramid, a soft, endless thunder of shared emotion drifting down through the dark.

When I found my voice, I asked the nearest man if any doctor was among the ranks. A Master of the Diskos, the highest ranking commander present, stepped forward, saluted me with his weapon, and made to take my Maid from my arms. I asked again, slow and hoarse, for a healer. He barked an order, and the hundred thousand armored men shifted ranks to form a long, silent lane stretching straight to the Great Gateway of the Pyramid. Two men from the upper cities ran forward, carrying a small Master Doctor slung between them on a stretcher. He gently helped me lay my Maid down on the stone, and the Master of the Diskos gestured for all the men to turn their backs, to give the doctor privacy to examine her.

A great, utter silence fell. The Hundred Thousand did not speak, no sound rose from the Pyramid. Everyone knew, by then, the terrible stake of this moment. The doctor looked up at me, his face quiet and pitying, and I knew at once. My Own Maid was dead. He gently drew a cloth over her face, stood, and signed to his men to support me, and carry her inside. I shook them off, my breath coming in ragged gasps, and when the Master Doctor saw that I still had the strength to stand, even with my heart broken, he waved his men back.

I stooped, and lifted my Own Maid back into my arms for the last short journey. I walked down the lane of the Hundred Thousand, every man I passed holding his Diskos reversed in the highest, most solemn honor the order knew. No one spoke. The world felt empty and still, my great task seemingly for nothing—until I remembered: I had saved her from the unspeakable torments of the outer dark. She had died warm, safe, wrapped in my arms, not screaming and mad in the clutches of the things that lurked in the far East. But the memory stabbed me, sharp as a blade: I would never get to wake to find her kissing me as I slept, the quiet little moment I had dreamed of for so long. A wave of blinding grief crashed over me, and I staggered, for a heartbeat ready to fall. The Master Doctor steadied my elbow for a moment, then stepped back, letting me find my footing on my own.

As I drew near the Great Gateway, the lights of the Pyramid blazed bright once more, the machinery of the lifts and air pumps whirring back to full life as the Earth-Current recharged to its usual strength. The great massive doors rumbled open, operated by their ancient power-driven mechanisms, and the full council of the Pyramid’s Masters stepped out to meet me. The dear old Master Monstruwacan led them, moving as fast as his old legs could carry him, like a father rushing to greet the son he had feared lost. When the men at the gateway told him the Maid was dead in my arms, every one of the Masters paused, and stood silent, reversing their own Diskos in tribute. It was an honor almost no living soul had ever earned.

When I finally drifted back into full consciousness, I knew I was ascending in the great lift, laid out upon the very bed I had once been certain I would never need again—never return to, never wake in, after vanishing forever into the silent, sunless vast of the Country of Silence. Vague, strange echoes rose up through miles of stone from the world’s deepest abysses: the low, rolling thunder of the Underground Organs, sounding like distant music that hummed even beyond the border of death. A deep chant followed, like the voices of uncounted multitudes singing far beyond the edge of the world’s farthest mountains, now dipping low as a wind soughing through the black Deep, then rising clear, bright, the ancient, holy melody of the Song of Honour. I understood, half in a dream, that the millions dwelling in that sunken realm were pouring out rejoicing over the impossible, unhoped-for joy that had come to pass.

All of it was faint, half-shrouded from my senses. My eyes would not lift to open, my whole self bobbing like a buoy on unsteady, unreal waters. Only the scents were solid: sweet, heady blooms drifting up from the vast flower-fields that lined the lift’s ascent path, as the car climbed mile after mile toward the upper levels of the Pyramid. I must have stirred, for the Master Doctor spoke to me, low and gentle, bidding me rest: the Maid was whole and well, all harm had passed.

I slipped then into a soft haze, drifting through long stretches where I half-lived, half-slept, untroubled by the quiet question of whether I was still among the living. For days after that I lay still, no thought for anything beyond the slow thud of my own heartbeat, and the Master Doctor leaned over me again and again, peering keenly into my slack face. At last, after long, uncounted hours, someone else bent over me: the dear, beloved face of Mine Own, her eyes spilling quiet, wordless love straight into my soul, her manner soft and still.

I fumbled weakly with my hands, and she took them in hers, pressing warmth and life into me through the palms. She did not speak, only held me, and slow, soft contentment bloomed in my chest until I drifted off into a deep, natural, healing sleep.

When the day came that I was allowed to rise, my attendants carried me to one of the Pyramid’s quiet, walled gardens, and left me there alone for a time. A moment later, someone stepped around a clump of flowering bushes, pausing to glance at me with half-shyness—though the love blazing in her eyes made that shyness small, trivial. It was Mine Own Maid, Naani, dressed in soft, frilled finery I had never seen on her before, more dainty and luminous than I had ever dared imagine. I struggled to stand to go to her, and she ran fast to stop my foolish exertion, sinking to sit beside me, tucking my head against her breast and pressing her lips to mine, half tender mother and half blushing maid in the same breath. She bade me lie still, and we sat wrapped in utter, wordless happiness, until my attendants returned with the Master Doctor, whose face softened with quiet satisfaction at the sight of us.

After that day, I saw Mine Own Maid every day, and my strength returned with impossible speed—for love was my truest medicine. Soon I was permitted to venture out into the vast fields, though we traveled only by private, hidden paths, for the Multitudes of the Pyramid would have crowded after us constantly, and we craved perfect quiet. The Master Monstruwacan and the Master Doctor gave their approval, and an officer of the marriage rites joined us in a small, private ceremony, far simpler than the grand public wedding we would hold later: that great public rite, where the Millions formed an eight-mile high Guard of Honour from the very peak of the Mighty Pyramid all the way to its lowest foundations, was a celebration the people insisted on giving, for they would not be denied the chance to honour what we had done.

But those grand days came later. For now, we were alone, husband and wife, my strength steadily building, and Naani’s own health fully restored at last. These were the Love Days, the most perfect, radiant stretch of existence possible, when love is true and unshakable. We wandered the vast fields as we pleased, treading the hidden Love Paths that ran near the small scattered villages that dotted the countryside, each field so large it might have passed for a whole country of old. We hid our names from strangers, lest curiosity and well-meaning kindness draw crowds around us, for we needed nothing but each other, uninterrupted. We chose our resting places in spots thick with the rarest, most beautiful blooms, carried small stores of food with us, and stopped to eat at the village inns dotted across the lands when our supplies ran low. Mine Own kept her promise to me a hundred times over, preparing huge, hearty feasts for me to devour, teasing me for being a glutton as I ate, then kissing me before I could ever get a word in to defend myself. She was every last thing my heart and my spirit had ever ached for: a companion wrapped in love, who had led my spirit straight into a state of perfect, unending joy.

Once we ventured down into the Country of Silence for a short visit, though we did not stay long at first, for the weight of all we had endured there rose over me fast. Later, we would wander those quiet sunken lands often, wrapped in shared memory, quiet reverence, and the love that holds all pain and all joy together. As we left that dark realm that first day, I told Naani that while she had been trapped in the death-like stasis the evil force of the ancient House had inflicted on her, I had lived with a terrible, lingering grief that I might never wake to catch her sneaking kisses to me while I lay asleep. She blushed so soft and lovely, for she had never guessed I had been half-aware of that sweet secret naughtiness, and her face fell as she remembered the agony of those days, when she had grieved for me thinking I was truly dead, before the great living vapour of the Earth-Force had pulled her free of the Silence. She curled into my side, full of tender understanding, and told me what the doctors had explained: she had been as if stunned, her spirit frozen, every thread of her life suspended, until the great Earth-Current’s life force had stirred her spirit awake, set her blood flowing warm again in her veins. The doctors had pored over the oldest surviving records of their craft recently, and found one faint, half-forgotten account of a similar miracle eons in the past—but nothing at all like ours, not in the whole span of the millions of years the Night Land had stood.

As we wandered and rested through the fields, I told Naani old stories of my adventures, and learned that she had grieved almost as deeply as I while I lay near death, refusing to leave my bedside until the Master Doctor had ordered her rest, for he had feared my spirit would slip away forever if she did not lend me her warmth and her will to live by my side. I can scarce put to words the depth of my love, when I thought of how brave and steady she had been, holding my hands all those days, when she could barely stand on her own feet from grief and exhaustion.

There is only one last thing left to tell, that happened some while after we held our grand public marriage ceremony. One day, my wife, my own Naani, led me with sweet, gentle cunning to the great Hall of Honour. When we stepped inside, I saw hundreds of the people of the Pyramid lined the great walls, standing in total, soft silence, as if they waited for something, and did not wish to disturb us. She led me forward to the centre of the hall, and I understood at last why she had hidden her plan from me: there, in the sacred Place of Honour reserved only for the Great Dead, stood a statue of a man clad in broken, dented armour, carrying a limp maid in his arms, striding through the dark. I was struck dumb. No one living had ever been granted this honour, not in all the long ages of the Night Land—and I was only a young man, no great hero, made great only by the fact that I had loved with every thread of my heart and soul, so that death had become a small, trivial thing to me next to that love.

Mine Own stood beside me, weeping quiet tears of joy and proud love for her man, and the whole great hall hummed with soft, dear sympathy. The people there let us leave in quiet, no cheering, no crowding, that gentle respect that is the sweetest mark of true honour. I walked back out into the light with my wife pressed close to my side, and I understood a simple truth: honour is no more than empty ash, if you do not have love to give it meaning. But to have true love is to have everything. For that perfect, true love gives birth to honour, and to faithfulness, and the three together build the House of Joy, where no shadow of the dark Night Land outside can ever reach.
