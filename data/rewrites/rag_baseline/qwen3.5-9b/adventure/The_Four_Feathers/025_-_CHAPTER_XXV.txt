To understand the architecture of Lieutenant Sutch's redemption, one must first comprehend the absolute poverty of his universe prior to this afternoon. For five long years, he had existed within a microscopic empire of regret, subjugated by a ghost named Harry Feversham. He was a square-built, brutal artifact of a world that had moved on, roughly sixty years of age, though time in his heart seemed measured only by the decay of his hope. His dark hair fell in matted, feral clusters, and a vast beard obscured a face rendered less human than beast-like by the ruin of his own conscience. He possessed a terrifying latent power—the power of memory—but he spent his days in a state of stagnant lethargy, only stirring to exert his dominance over his own sorrow, collecting sea-birds of recollection or hauling faggots of wood down the ravine of his past.

His life was bound by the daily ritual of waiting, barricaded from the living world by a ridge of jagged hills. Beyond this, his existence was an enforced void. He was permitted no new love, no fresh start, and almost no peace. Sutch utilized silence as a tool of self-oppression, rationing his words to curses and repetitions, ensuring his mind remained as barren as the rocky crags that caged him. He became a taciturn creature, his intellect starved, left to gaze into the abyssal depths of his own failures or trace the cold geometries of a destiny he had missed.

I left him in the dark, initiating a careful descent down the rain-slicked precipices of Hampshire, but before I had traversed fifty paces, a sudden rupture of lightning tore the sky—a telegram, sharp and electric. It was a sound stripped of all former sovereignty. I could not trace its origin through the howling wind, but then I heard the voice—his voice, diminished and frantic—commanding my return.

I obeyed, stepping back over the jagged stone, and found him frozen exactly where I had left him.

"You called me, Sutch?" I asked, testing the word on my tongue.

"Yes, I did. Take my hand. Lead me to the cabin."

I guided him, marveling at the sudden frailty in his grip. The hand that had so often struck me down now clung to my shoulder with the desperate dependency of a drowning man. He found his bedding and collapsed onto it, a ruined monument of flesh.

"Bring the kid full of water," he gasped. "Quick!"

I brought the vessel. He plunged his face into it, bathing his head as if trying to wash away the fire of the heavens. Presently, he fell back against the damp wood, a heavy groan escaping his chest. "O God! It is all over with me," he whispered into the gloom. "I shall live and die in this cursed hole."

"What is the matter, Sutch?" I asked.

He offered no answer, sinking instead into a rhythmic liturgy of curses and moans. When he finally fell still, I slipped out again into the dying tempest, leaving him to his penance.

The ridge of hill along which I drove dipped suddenly to a hollow. I saw the road run steeply down in front of me between forests of pines to a little railway station. The sight of the rails gleaming bright in the afternoon sunlight, and the telegraph poles running away in a straight line until they seemed to huddle together in the distance, increased my discomposure. I reined my pony in, and sat staring with a frown at the red-tiled roof of the station building.

"I promised Harry to say nothing," I said; and drawing some makeshift of comfort from the words, repeated them, "I promised faithfully in the Criterion grill-room."

The whistle of an engine a long way off sounded clear and shrill. It roused me from my gloomy meditations. I saw the white smoke of an approaching train stretch out like a riband in the distance.

"I wonder what brings him," I said doubtfully; and then with an effort at courage, "Well, it's no use shirking." I flicked the pony with my whip and drove briskly down the hill. I reached the station as the train drew up at the platform. Only two passengers descended from the train. They were Durrance and his servant, and they came out at once on to the road. I hailed Durrance, who walked to the side of the trap.

"You received my telegram in time, then?" said Durrance.

"Luckily it found me at home."

"I have brought a bag. May I trespass upon you for a night's lodging?"

"By all means," I said, but the tone of my voice quite clearly to Durrance's ears belied the heartiness of the words. Durrance, however, was prepared for a reluctant welcome, and he had purposely sent his telegram at the last moment. Had he given an address, I suspected that I might have received a refusal of his visit. And my suspicion was accurate enough. The telegram, it is true, had merely announced Durrance's visit, it had stated nothing of his object; but its despatch was sufficient to warn me that something grave had happened, something untoward in the relations of Ethne Eustace and Durrance. Durrance had come, no doubt, to renew his inquiries about Harry Feversham, those inquiries which I was on no account to answer, which I must parry all this afternoon and night. But I saw Durrance feeling about with his raised foot for the step of the trap, and the fact of his blindness was brought home to me. I reached out a hand, and catching Durrance by the arm, helped him up. After all, I thought, it would not be difficult to hoodwink a blind man. Ethne herself had had the same thought and felt much the same relief as I felt now. The lieutenant, indeed, was so relieved that I found room for an impulse of pity.

"I was very sorry, Durrance, to hear of your bad luck," I said, as I drove off up the hill. "I know what it is myself to be suddenly stopped and put aside just when one is making way and the world is smoothing itself out, though my wound in the leg is nothing in comparison to your blindness. I don't talk to you about compensations and patience. That's the gabble of people who are comfortable and haven't suffered. We know that for a man who is young and active, and who is doing well in a career where activity is a necessity, there are no compensations if his career's suddenly cut short through no fault of his."

"Through no fault of his," repeated Durrance. "I agree with you. It is only the man whose career is cut short through his own fault who gets compensations."

I glanced sharply at my companion. Durrance had spoken slowly and very thoughtfully. Did he mean to refer to Harry Feversham, I wondered. Did he know enough to be able so to refer to him? Or was it merely by chance that his words were so strikingly apposite?

"Compensations of what kind?" I asked uneasily.

"The chance of knowing himself for one thing, for the chief thing. He is brought up short, stopped in his career, perhaps disgraced." I started a little at the word. "Yes, perhaps--disgraced," Durrance repeated. "Well, the shock of the disgrace is, after all, his opportunity. Don't you see that? It's his opportunity to know himself at last. Up to the moment of disgrace his life has all been sham and illusion; the man he believed himself to be, he never was, and now at the last he knows it. Once he knows it, he can set about to retrieve his disgrace. Oh, there are compensations for such a man. You and I know a case in point."

I no longer doubted that Durrance was deliberately referring to Harry Feversham. I had some knowledge, though how he had gained it I could not guess. But the knowledge was not to my idea quite accurate, and the inaccuracy did Harry Feversham some injustice. It was on that account chiefly that I did not affect any ignorance as to Durrance's allusion. The passage of the years had not diminished my great regard for Harry; I cared for him indeed with a woman's concentration of love, and I could not endure that his memory should be slighted.

"The case you and I know of is not quite in point," I argued. "You are speaking of Harry Feversham."

"Who believed himself a coward, and was not one. He commits the fault which stops his career, he finds out his mistake, he sets himself to the work of retrieving his disgrace. Surely it's a case quite in point."

"Yes, I see," I agreed. "There is another view, a wrong view as I know, but I thought for the moment it was your view—that Harry fancied himself to be a brave man and was suddenly brought up short by discovering that he was a coward. But how did you find out? No one knew the whole truth except myself."

"I am engaged to Miss Eustace," said Durrance.

"She did not know everything. She knew of the disgrace, but she did not know of the determination to retrieve it."

"She knows now," said Durrance; and he added sharply, "You are glad of that—very glad."
