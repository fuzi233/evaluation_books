In the vernal season of 1849, I wandered the East, having recently severed the itinerary I had meticulously charted months prior—a plan duly communicated to my solicitor and banker in London. This abrupt deviation necessitated dispatching a servant to retrieve my correspondence and funds from the English Consul in a city now stricken from my revised itinerary. He was to rejoin me at a designated rendezvous. Fate, indifferent to his culpability, detained him on this errand. For a week, my retinue and I endured the limbo of encampment upon the desert's fringes. Only when the seventh day waned did the missing man emerge at my tent's threshold, bearing the money and letters intact.

"I fear I bring tidings of ill, sir," he intoned, gesturing to a missive adorned with a mourning border, addressed in the distinct hand of Mr. Bruff. Suspense, in matters of such gravity, is an agony beyond endurance. That letter with the blackened margin was the first to yield to my fingers. It bore the grim announcement of my father's death and proclaimed me heir to his vast fortune. Such wealth, cascading into my grasp, carried with it the weight of obligation; Mr. Bruff implored me to hasten my return to England.

By dawn's first light, I was already en route across the channel. Yet, the portrait painted of me by my old confidant, Betteredge, upon my departure, strikes me now as slightly exaggerated. In his peculiar dialect, he has solemnized one of my young mistress's satirical jibes regarding my continental upbringing, convincing himself that he witnessed genuine facets of French, German, and Italian character within me. These were illusions conjured solely by his fertile imagination, though my lively cousin claimed to detect them in jest. Barring this distortion, Betteredge speaks truth when he depicts me as heart-wounded by Rachel's treatment, fleeing England in the feverish heat of suffering born from life's bitterest disillusionment. I departed abroad, resolved that if change and distance could aid me, they should erase her. I am persuaded that no philosophy of human nature is truer than the assertion that alteration and separation *do* assuage a man in such extremities; they forcibly divert the mind from the exclusive contemplation of its own grief.

Yet, I never truly forgot her. The sting of memory merely dulled, incrementally, as time, distance, and novelty erected formidable barriers between Rachel and myself. Conversely, the moment I turned homeward, the remedy that had taken root so steadfastly began to recede with equal precision. As I drew nearer to the soil she inhabited, and closer to the prospect of reunion, her influence regained its dominion with irresistible force. Upon leaving England, she was the final name I would suffer to pass my lips; upon returning, she became the first inquiry I posed when Mr. Bruff and I reunited. Naturally, I was apprised of every event transpiring in my absence—that is, of all subsequent developments detailed herein, continuing Betteredge's chronicle, save for one crucial exception. Mr. Bruff, at that juncture, deemed himself lacking the liberty to disclose the private motivations influencing Rachel and Godfrey Ablewhite in reviving the marriage pledge, on either side. I spared him the embarrassment of probing this delicate terrain. Relief sufficed: after the jealous torment of learning she had once contemplated becoming Godfrey's wife, it was comfort enough to know that reflection had convinced her of her own rashness, allowing her to dissolve the engagement.

Having absorbed the history of the past, my inquiries, still fixated on Rachel, naturally pivoted to the present. Under whose guardianship had she been placed following her departure from Mr. Bruff's household? Where did she reside now? She lived under the care of a widowed sister of the late Sir John Verinder—Mrs. Merridew—who, commissioned by her mother's executors as guardian, had graciously accepted the role. Reports indicated they were progressing admirably, currently established for the season at Mrs. Merridew's residence in Portland Place.

Half an hour after receiving this intelligence, I was en route to Portland Place, lacking the fortitude to confess this urgency to Mr. Bruff. The man who answered the door hesitated, uncertain of Miss Verinder's whereabouts. I dispatched him upstairs with my card, the swiftest method to resolve the query. He descended with an impenetrable visage, informing me that Miss Verinder was absent. One might suspect others of deliberately withholding access, but it was impossible to doubt Rachel. I left word to return at six o'clock that evening.

At six, the second notification echoed: Miss Verinder was not at home. Had any message been left? None. Had she not received my card? The servant apologized profusely; Miss Verinder *had* received it. The inference was undeniable. Rachel declined to see me. Refusing to be thus treated without attempting to discern the cause, I summoned Mrs. Merridew, requesting a personal audience at her convenience. She granted immediate reception without hesitation. I was ushered into a cozy sitting-room, confronting a comfortable, elderly lady who expressed profound regret and surprise, entirely on my behalf. Yet, she confessed her inability to offer explanation or pressure Rachel on a matter seemingly rooted in private sentiment. This polite refrain, repeated with tireless patience, constituted my entire gain from Mrs. Merridew.

My final recourse was epistolary. My servant delivered a letter the following day, instructed to await a response. The reply arrived, concise and stinging: *"Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake."* Though fond of her, I felt the indignity of this rebuke acutely. Just as I composed myself, Mr. Bruff entered on business matters. I dismissed his concerns immediately, laying the entire case before him. He proved as illuminating as Mrs. Merridew. Did slander circulate concerning me in Rachel's hearing? Unaware of any. Had she referenced me while under his roof? Never. Had she not, during my prolonged absence, queried whether I lived or died? Not a single question had passed her lips.

From my pocket-book, I produced the letter written by poor Lady Verinder from Frizinghall on the day of my departure. Pointing to two specific sentences, I drew Mr. Bruff's attention: *"The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel's mind. Moving blindfold in this matter, you have added to the burden of anxiety which she has had to bear, by innocently threatening her secret with discovery through your exertions."*

"Is it possible," I asked, "that the sentiment described therein remains as bitter against me now?"

Mr. Bruff feigned distress. "If you demand an answer," he conceded, "I can place no other interpretation on her conduct than that."

Ringing the bell, I ordered my servant to pack my portmanteau and procure a railway guide. Astonished, Mr. Bruff inquired of my intentions.

"I am proceeding to Yorkshire," I declared, "by the next train."

"For what purpose?" he asked.

"Mr. Bruff, the assistance I innocently provided in the search for the Diamond was an unpardonable offense in Rachel's mind nearly a year ago; it remains so today. I refuse to accept that position! I am determined to unravel the secret of her silence toward her mother and her enmity toward *me*. If time, toil, and coin suffice, I shall lay my hand upon the thief who stole the Moonstone!"

The worthy old gentleman attempted remonstrance, urging reason and duty. I remained deaf to all his pleas. No earthly consideration could shake the resolution burning within me.

"I shall resume the inquiry at the precise point where I abandoned it," I continued, "following it step by step until I reach the present. There are missing links in the evidence, as I left it, which Gabriel Betteredge can supply—and to Gabriel Betteredge I go!"

Towards sunset that evening, I stood once more upon the well-remembered terrace, gazing at the tranquil old country house. The gardener was the first soul visible in the deserted grounds; he had left Betteredge an hour prior, basking in the customary corner of the backyard. Knowing him well, I resolved to seek him personally. Walking round the familiar paths, I peered through the open gate of the yard. There he was—the dear old friend of happier days never to return—seated on the old beehive chair in the usual corner, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* on his lap, flanked by his two canine companions dozing beside him.

From my vantage, my shadow stretched forward, cast by the last slanting rays of the sun. Whether the dogs perceived it or detected my approach by scent, they sprang up with a low growl. Startled in turn, the old man quieted them with a word, then shielding his failing eyes with his hand, looked inquiringly at the figure at the gate. Tears filled my own eyes. I was compelled to pause a moment before trusting myself to speak.
