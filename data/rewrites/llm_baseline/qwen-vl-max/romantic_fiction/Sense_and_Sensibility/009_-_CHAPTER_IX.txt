CHAPTER IX. The Dashwood family had now settled into their new life at Barton with a quiet contentment that suited their gentle ways. The cottage, the garden, and the surrounding landscape had become familiar and comforting, offering a peace they had not known since leaving Norland. Though the house lacked the grandeur of their former home, the simple pleasures of daily life—walking, reading, sketching—now brought them deeper joy than ever before. Sir John Middleton, eager to welcome them, called every day for the first two weeks, and was astonished to find the sisters always busy, absorbed in quiet pursuits. He urged them to join more social gatherings, assuring them his carriage was always at their disposal; but Mrs. Dashwood’s strong sense of independence made her resist such invitations. She preferred keeping within walking distance, and few families met that criterion. Among those who did, one stood out: an old, dignified mansion nestled along the winding valley of Allenham, which the girls had discovered on one of their early walks. It reminded them faintly of Norland and stirred their imaginations. But when they learned its owner—a kind, elderly lady—was too frail to receive guests, they resigned themselves to admiring it from afar.

The countryside around Barton was rich with scenic paths. The high downs rose invitingly beyond the cottage windows, offering fresh air and sweeping views, especially when the lower valleys were shrouded in mist or rain. One bright morning, after days of steady downpour, Marianne and Margaret set off toward one of these hills, drawn by patches of sunlight breaking through the clouds. They could no longer bear being cooped up indoors, though Elinor and their mother stayed behind, engrossed in books and drawings. Marianne insisted the weather would hold, and the girls climbed the slope with cheerful spirits, laughing as the brisk south-westerly wind swept across their faces. They mocked the caution that kept their elders inside, convinced they were missing something truly exquisite.

“Is there any happiness,” Marianne exclaimed, “greater than this? Margaret, let us stay here at least two hours.”

They walked on, braving the wind with playful defiance—until suddenly, the sky darkened, and a heavy rain began to fall, driving straight into their faces. Startled and disappointed, they turned back, knowing no shelter lay closer than their own home. Yet there was one small comfort: the chance to race down the steep hillside, a thrill they both welcomed. Marianne took the lead, but tripped and fell hard. Margaret, unable to stop herself, tumbled past her and reached the bottom safely. Just yards away, a gentleman with a gun and two pointers paused as he saw what had happened. He quickly laid down his weapon and rushed to help.

Marianne had managed to rise, but her ankle twisted in the fall left her unsteady. The man offered assistance, but she hesitated—modesty held her back. Without waiting, he gently lifted her into his arms and carried her down the hill. Through the open garden gate, he bore her straight into the cottage, where Margaret had just arrived. He did not set her down until she was seated in a chair in the parlour.

Elinor and Mrs. Dashwood looked up in surprise. Their eyes lingered on the stranger—not only because of his striking appearance, but because of the grace and sincerity with which he explained his intrusion. His voice was warm and courteous, and his manner both bold and respectful. Even if he had been plain or older, Mrs. Dashwood would have been grateful for his kindness toward her daughter. But his youth, his refined features, and his easy bearing made him all the more compelling. She thanked him warmly and, with her usual charm, invited him to sit. He declined, saying he was wet and muddy. When she asked his name, he replied simply: “Willoughby. I live at Allenham, and I hope I may call tomorrow to inquire about Miss Dashwood.” She readily agreed, and he left just as the rain intensified, adding to the impression he had already made.

At once, the household buzzed with admiration. His gallantry had been noticed, and the laughter over Marianne’s fall gained extra warmth from his good looks. Marianne herself had seen little of him after he lifted her—her face flushed with embarrassment, her thoughts overwhelmed. But what she had seen was enough. He seemed exactly as she had imagined a hero might be: noble, brave, and full of spirit. His swift action in carrying her without ceremony thrilled her. Everything about him pleased her—the sound of his name, his residence in their favorite village, even the practical elegance of his shooting jacket. Her mind wandered with pleasant dreams, and the pain in her ankle faded into insignificance.

Sir John visited again as soon as the weather improved. Hearing of the accident, he immediately asked if he knew any Mr. Willoughby at Allenham. “Willoughby?” he exclaimed. “Why, he’s here! That’s excellent news. I’ll ride over tomorrow and invite him to dinner Thursday.”
“You know him?” Mrs. Dashwood asked.
“Know him? Of course. He comes down every year.”
“And what sort of young man is he?”
“A fine fellow, I assure you—excellent shot, finest rider in England.”
“But that’s all you can say?” Marianne interrupted, annoyed. “What of his manners? His tastes? His talents?”
Sir John was taken aback. “Honestly, I don’t know much about those things. But he’s pleasant, good-natured, and has the most beautiful black pointer I’ve ever seen. Was she with him today?”
Marianne couldn’t tell him the dog’s color—but neither could he describe the depth of Willoughby’s character.

Elinor stepped in. “Who is he? Where is he from? Does he own a house at Allenham?”
Sir John clarified: Mr. Willoughby had no land of his own. He lived at Allenham Court only while visiting the elderly lady who owned it—his relative—and would inherit her estate. “Yes, yes,” he added with a wink, “he’s quite worth catching, Miss Dashwood. He has a modest estate in Somersetshire too. If I were you, I wouldn’t let your younger sister get him—especially after all this falling down hills. Miss Marianne mustn’t think she can claim all the men. Brandon will be jealous, mark my words.”

Mrs. Dashwood smiled gently. “I doubt Mr. Willoughby will be troubled by either of my daughters’ attentions. We haven’t raised them to pursue men, no matter how wealthy. Men are safe with us. Still, I’m glad to hear he’s respectable, and his company won’t be unwelcome.”

“Good fellow,” Sir John repeated. “Last Christmas, at a little dance at the park, he danced from eight till four—never sat down once.”
“Did he indeed?” Marianne said, her eyes shining. “And with grace? With fire?”
“Yes, and up again at eight to ride to covert.”
“That’s the way,” she declared. “A man should follow his passions with complete devotion—no fatigue, no limits.”

Sir John chuckled. “Ah, I see how it will be. You’ll be setting your cap at him now, and forget poor Brandon entirely.”
Marianne frowned. “That phrase disgusts me. I detest clichés meant to sound clever—‘setting one’s cap,’ ‘making a conquest.’ They’re vulgar and shallow. And even if they were once witty, time has long since worn away any charm.”
Sir John didn’t fully grasp her objection, but he laughed anyway. “Well, you’ll make conquests enough, one way or another. Poor Brandon—he’s already smitten. And he’s well worth chasing, I tell you, despite all this tumbling and sprained ankles.”
