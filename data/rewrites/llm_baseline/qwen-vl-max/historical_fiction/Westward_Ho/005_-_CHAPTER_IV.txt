“I could not love thee, dear, so much, Loved I not honor more.”—LOVELACE.

And what, all this while, has become of the fair one who broke so many hearts, yet whom I have not yet introduced to my readers?

She sat in the little farmhouse beside the mill, nestled deep within the green folds of Combe Valley, halfway between Stow and Chapel. There she brooded—not with anger, but with quiet melancholy—at being shut out from the grand affairs stirring in Bideford, forced into a kind of Martinmas Lent in this remote western glen. So solitary was her life that though she looked upon Eustace Leigh with no small measure of disfavor, and (being a devout Protestant) with considerable suspicion, she found herself unable to resist his company when he came down to the farm and its mill. He made it a point, on some pretext or other, to visit nearly every day.

Her uncle and aunt at first frowned upon these visits; the latter always taking care to be present whenever conversation turned. Yet Mr. Leigh was a gentleman’s son, a neighboring squire, and a good customer. Rose, too, was the daughter of wealth, while they were poor cousins—so quarreling with her would have been unseemly. And besides, the pretty maid, half by willfulness, half by charm, usually managed to get her own way wherever she went. She had even been wise enough to beg her aunt never to leave them alone—"for I could not bear the sight of Mr. Eustace," she said, "only I must have someone to talk with down here."

Her aunt, after pondering, concluded that she was only a simple countrywoman, that townsmen's ways were different, and that people knew their own business best. Thus, matters drifted along as they pleased.

Eustace, meanwhile, well aware that the difference in faith between him and Rose might prove the greatest obstacle to his affection, took great care to keep his private beliefs hidden. Instead of trying to convert the folk at the mill, he daily bought milk and flour from them, then gave it away to the old women of Moorwinstow, who remarked among themselves that, for a Papist, he was a remarkably godly young man. Eventually, after conferring with Campian and Parsons about certain political schemes then brewing, he decided they should all three attend church the following Sunday.

There, Messrs. Evan Morgans and Morgan Evans, having studied the rubrics beforehand, behaved with perfect orthodoxy. Poor Eustace, too, conducted himself so reverently that the pious folk were astonished. He returned home convinced he had fooled parson, clerk, and congregation alike—unaware, in his innocent cunning, that each good wife in the parish whispered to her neighbor: “He turned Protestant? The devil turned monk! He’s only after Mistress Salterne, the young hypocrite.”

Yet if the two Jesuits found it prudent, for the holy cause they served, to outwardly conform to the powers that be, they were no less active in secret, plotting their downfall.

Since April of the previous year, they had wandered across England like fugitives playing hide-and-seek. Now they lay quiet, awaiting news from Ireland to signal their next move—a great uprising in the West to sweep the throne from the stiff-necked, persecuting, excommunicated, illegitimate, profligate usurper who falsely called herself Queen of England.

They had long since convinced themselves—just as they do today, with a real Baconian disdain for sensible experience—that the heart of England still beat for Rome, and that the nation stood on the brink of returning to the Catholic fold, ready to drag Elizabeth in chains before the rightful Lord of Creation, the Old Man of the Seven Hills. This hope, ever just ahead of them, always just out of reach like the rainbow touching earth, they proclaimed in language that terrified old Mr. Leigh.

One day, as Eustace entered his father’s private room after his usual visit to the mill, he heard voices raised in debate—Parsons, as usual, blustering; Mr. Leigh peevishly protesting; and Campian, the gentlest of men, attempting to soothe the storm. Eustace, for the sake of the cause, paused outside and listened.

“My excellent sir,” said Mr. Leigh, “does not your very presence here show how I am affected toward the holy cause of the Catholic faith? But I cannot forget that I am an Englishman.”

“And what is England?” demanded Parsons. “A heretic and schismatic Babylon, whereof it is written, ‘Come out of her, my people, lest you be partaker of her plagues.’ Yea, what is a country? An arbitrary division of territory by princes who are naught and come to naught. They are created by the people’s will; their existence depends on the sanction of Him to whom all power is given in heaven and earth—our Holy Father the Pope. Take away the latter, and what is a king? The people who made him may unmake him.”

“My dear sir,” replied Mr. Leigh, “recollect that I have sworn allegiance to Queen Elizabeth!”

“Yes, sir, you have,” said Parsons. “And as I have shown at length in my writings, you were absolved from that allegiance the moment the bull of Pius the Fifth declared her a heretic and excommunicate, thereby forfeiting all dominion. I tell you, sir, what I thought you already knew—that since 1569, England has had no queen, no magistrates, no laws, no lawful authority whatsoever. To swear fealty to any English magistrate, or plead in an English court, is to disobey the apostolic precept: ‘How dare you go to law before the unbelievers?’ Rebellion is not merely permitted, sir—it is a duty.”

“Take care, sir!” cried Mr. Leigh. “For God’s sake, take care! Right or wrong, I cannot allow such words in my house. For the sake of my wife and children, I cannot!”

“My dear brother Parsons,” interposed Campian, “deal more gently with the flock. Your opinion, though probable in the eyes of most of our order, is hardly safe here. The opposite view is at least safe enough for Mr. Leigh to excuse his conscience. After all, are we not sent hither to proclaim precisely this—to relieve the souls of good Catholics from burdens they find too heavy?”

“Yes,” said Parsons, half-sulkily, “to allow all Balaams who will to sacrifice to Baal, while calling themselves by the name of the Lord.”

“My dear brother,” said Campian, “have I not often reminded you that Naaman was allowed to bow in the house of Rimmon? Can we then complain of the office to which the Holy Father has appointed us—to declare to such as Mr. Leigh his especial grace, whereby the bull of Pius the Fifth (on whose soul God have mercy!) shall bind the queen and heretics, but not the Catholics—at least as long as the present tyranny prevents the pious purposes of the bull?”

“Be it so,” said Parsons. “But observe, Mr. Leigh, that our brother Campian confesses this to be a tyranny. Observe that the bull still binds the so-called queen, and that she and her magistrates remain usurpers, nonentities, shadows of a shade. And observe this: when that which is lawful is excused to the weak, it remains no less lawful to the strong. The seven thousand who did not bow to Baal did not slay his priests; but Elijah did—and won a good reward. If the rest of Israel sinned not in sparing Eglon, yet Ehud’s deed was justified by all laws, human and divine.”

“For Heaven’s sake, do not speak thus!” cried Mr. Leigh. “Or I must leave the room. What have I to do with Ehud and Eglon, with slaughter and tyranny? Our queen is a good queen, if Heaven would grant her repentance and turn her to the true faith. I have never troubled myself about religion, nor anyone else I know in the West Country.”

“You forget Mr. Trudgeon of Launceston, father,” interposed Eustace, slipping in unnoticed. “And poor Father Mayne.”

“Yes,” added Campian softly, “your West of England has also known martyrs, as London has known Story.”

“What, young malapert?” roared poor Leigh, turning sharply on his son, glad to vent his irritation on someone. “Are you against me too, with a murrain on you? And pray, what brought Cuthbert Mayne to the gallows, or turned Mr. Trudgeron—always a foolish hot-head—out of house and home, but just such treasonable talk as Mr. Parsons holds in my house, to make a beggar of me and my children, as he will before he’s done?”

“The Blessed Virgin forbid!” said Campian.

“The Blessed Virgin forbid? But you must help her forbid it, Mr. Campian. We should never have had the law of 1571 against bulls, Agnus Deis, and blessed grains, if the Pope’s bull of 1569 hadn’t made them treason, preventing a poor soul from saving himself in the true Church without risking his neck by denying the queen’s authority.”

“What, sir?” almost roared Parsons. “Do you dare speak ill of the edicts of the Vicar of Christ?”

“I? No. I didn’t. Who says I did? All I meant was—I’m sure, Mr. Campian, you’re a reasonable man. Speak for me.”

“Mr. Leigh only meant,” said Campian, “that the Holy Father’s prudent intentions have been thwarted by the stubbornness and invincible misunderstanding of the heretics. What was meant for the good of oppressed Catholics has been perverted to their harm.”

“And thus, reverend sir,” said Eustace, eager to regain his father’s favor, “my father blames not the Pope—Heaven forbid—but the pravity of his enemies.”

“And it is for this very reason,” said Campian, “that we have brought with us the present merciful explanation of the bull.”

“I’ll tell you what, gentlemen,” said Mr. Leigh, growing bolder as his opponent seemed inclined to peace. “I don’t think the declaration was needed. After the new law of 1571, it wasn’t enforced until Mayne and Trudgeon made fools of themselves—six years later. A few offenders were warned and released. But even that didn’t happen here. It need not happen now—unless you put my son here (though you’ll never put me, I warrant) upon some deed better left undone, bringing us all to shame.”

“Your son, sir, if not publicly vowed to God, has, I hope, a due sense of that inward vocation we’ve seen in him. He reverences his spiritual fathers too well to listen to the temptations of his earthly father.”

“What, sir? Will you teach my son to disobey me?”

“Your son is ours also, sir. This is strange language from one who owes a debt to the Church—charitably believed to be paid through his child.”

These last words struck Mr. Leigh hard. He burst out, swearing fiercely at Parsons, who stood foaming with rage.

“A plague upon you, sir, and a black assizes for you—you’ll hang yet! Do you mean to taunt me in my own house with that Hartland land? You’d better go back and ask those who sent you where the dispensation is—the one they promised years ago, and kept putting off, till they’ve taken my money, my son, my conscience, and now, I vow before all saints, seem to want my head as well. God help me!”—and tears filled his eyes.

Now it was Eustace’s turn to rise. Though he was an Englishman and a gentleman, he spoke kindly but firmly:

“Courage, my dearest father. Remember I am still your son, not a Jesuit yet. Whether I ever become one will depend largely on how you are treated by these reverend gentlemen—for whom I, having brought them here, must stand surety.”

If a powder-barrel had exploded in their faces, they could not have been more stunned. Campian stared at Parsons, and Parsons at Campian. At last, the stouter-hearted recovered:

“Sir! Do you know the curse on those who, after putting their hand to the plough, look back?”

Eustace was one of those impulsive men—lacking moral courage—who could raise the devil but never fight him once roused. He tried to dismiss his words with winks and gestures toward his father, as if to say he was only calming the old man. But Campian was too startled, Parsons too furious, to notice. Eustace had to carry it through.

“All I read, Father Parsons,” he said, “is that such are not fit for the kingdom of God—of which high honor I’ve long felt myself unworthy. I have doubts about my vocation. In the meantime, I have not forgotten I am a citizen of a free country.”

And so saying, he took his father’s arm and walked out.

His final words struck the Jesuits hard. They had awakened the painful truth—daily thrust upon them since then, yet never learned—that all their scholastic cunning, plots, bulls, pardons, indulgences—here, across the Channel—were nothing but an enchanter’s cloud-castle, vanishing at the touch of the constable’s staff. “A citizen of a free country!” That was the rub. They exchanged glances, more perplexed than ever.

At last, Parsons spoke.

“There’s a woman in the wind. I’ll lay my life on it. I saw him blush crimson yesterday when his mother asked if some Rose Salterne was still nearby.”

“A woman? Well, the spirit may be willing, though the flesh be weak. We’ll inquire. The youth may serve us well as a layman. If anything happens to his elder brother—may the saints protect him—he inherits wealth. Meanwhile, perhaps dear brother Parsons sees the need to alter our tactics.”

And so began a long discussion between the two, sent together under the Jesuit rule: “Two are better than one”—so that Campian might temper Parsons’ fervor, and Parsons stir Campian’s gentleness, each complementing the other. And indeed, each often advised nearly the opposite of the other, “without the danger,” as their writers noted, “of seeming changeable or inconsistent.”

The result was that a day or two later—after Mr. Leigh and Eustace had made amends and things ran smoothly—Father Campian asked Father Francis, the household chaplain, as a special favor, to allow him to hear Eustace’s confession the following Friday.

Poor Father Francis dared not refuse. He consented with a sigh, knowing full well the intent: to extract family secrets that would weaken his influence and strengthen the Jesuits’. For throughout England, the regular clergy and the Jesuits lived in armed neutrality, barely short of open war—as it became during James I’s reign, when these gentle missionaries, by their moral torments, literally hunted to death the poor Popish bishop of Hippopotamus (that is, London).

Campion heard Eustace’s confession. Through subtle questions—easy to imagine to those familiar with the confessional—he discovered satisfactorily that Eustace was, as Campian would have said, “in love.” Though I question whether the term fits any facts poor prurient Campian uncovered—or indeed knew how to uncover—since a swine has no eye for pearls. Still, he had learned enough. He smiled, and set to work to discover who the lady was.

Had he simply said, “I understand your pain. If your desires are reasonable, lawful, possible, I will help you with all my heart,” he might have gained the young man’s confidence and saved himself hours of effort. But instinctively, he chose the crooked, suspicious path—assuming the worst, as one trained to see the basest motives as the engine of all human action. He began his moral probing with delicate questions, which Eustace dodged in every way, knowing the good father was too clever for him, and that he would eventually yield.

Yet like a rabbit squealing around the weasel, knowing it must leap into its jaws, Eustace parried, pretended ignorance, surprise, scrupulousness, even anger. Each question—about her marriage, creed, nationality—brought Campian closer to his goal. Finally, when Campian, finding the situation not entirely hopeless, asked about her worldly wealth, Eustace saw a way out and seized it.

“Even if she’s a heretic, she’s heiress to one of Devon’s wealthiest merchants.”

“Ah!” said Campian thoughtfully. “And she is but eighteen, you say?”

“Only eighteen.”

“Ah, well, my son, there is time. She may be reconciled to the Church. Or you may change.”

“I shall die first.”

“Ah, poor lad. Well, she may be reconciled, and her wealth may serve the cause of Heaven.”

“And it shall serve. Only absolve me, and let me be at peace. Let me have her,” he pleaded. “I do not want her wealth—not I! Let me have her, even for one year, one month, one day—and all the rest—money, fame, talents, yea, my life itself—are at the service of Holy Church. I shall glory in showing my devotion through some special sacrifice—some desperate deed. Prove me now, and see what I will not do!”

So Eustace was absolved. Then Campian added:

“This is well, my son. For there is a task now—one that may cost life.”

“Prove me!” Eustace exclaimed impatiently.

“Here is a letter brought me last night—no matter from whence. You can read it better than I. I longed to show it to you, but feared my son had become—”

“You feared wrongly, then, my dear Father Campian.”

So Campian translated the cipher:

“To Evan Morgans, gentleman, at Mr. Leigh’s house in Moorwinstow, Devonshire. News may be had by one who goes to Clovelly shore any evening after the 25th of November, at dead low tide, and watches for a boat rowed by one with a red beard, speaking Portuguese. If asked, 'How many?' answer, 'Eight hundred and one.' Take his letters and read. If the shore is watched, let the man show a light three times in a safe place under the cliff above the town; below is dangerous. Farewell, expect great things!”

“I will go,” said Eustace. “Tomorrow is the 25th. I know a sure and easy path. Your friend knows these shores well.”

“Ah! What is it we do not know?” said Campian, smiling mysteriously. “And now?”

“And now,” said Eustace, “to prove my trust in you, you shall come with me, and see—her, the lady I spoke of. Judge for yourself whether my fault is venial.”

“Ah, my son,” said Campian, “have I not absolved you already? What have I to do with fair faces? Nevertheless, I will come—to show you I trust you, and perhaps to reclaim a heretic, save a lost soul. Who knows?”

So they set out together. As fate ordained, they reached the top of the hill between Chapel and Stow Mill just as Mistress Rose Salterne appeared down the lane, radiant in a new scarlet hood. From beneath it, her large dark eyes flashed soft lightning straight into Eustace’s heart. She tripped up to them on delicate ankles and tiny feet—tall, lithe, graceful, a true West-country lass. As she passed with a blush and a curtsey, even Campian glanced back at the innocent creature, her long dark curls, in the fashion of the time, flowing down past her waist, ensnaring Eustace’s soul in their glossy nets.

“There!” whispered Eustace, trembling. “Can you excuse me now?”

“I had excused you long ago,” said the kindhearted father. “Alas, that so much fair red and white should have been created only as a feast for worms!”

“A feast for gods, you mean!” cried Eustace, struck by the absurdity. Then, as if to escape scolding for his heathenry:

“Will you let me return for a moment? I’ll follow you—let me go!”

Campian saw it was useless to object, and nodded. Eustace darted away, crossed a field, and met Rose at the next bend.

She started, giving a little shriek.

“Mr. Leigh! I thought you had gone on.”

“I came back to speak to you—Rose—Mistress Salterne, I mean.”

“To me?”

“To you I must speak—tell you all—or die!” He pressed close. She shrank back, frightened.

“Do not move! Do not go! I implore you! Rose, only hear me!” He seized her hand fiercely, pouring out his love, showering her with every extravagant compliment he could muster.

Much of what he said she had heard before. But the tremor in his voice, the fire in his eyes, stirred her instinctively.

“Let me go!” she said. “You are too rough, sir!”

“Ay!” he said, now gripping both her hands. “Rougher, perhaps, than the gay gallants of Bideford who serenade you, write sonnets, send posies. Rougher, but more loving, Rose! Do not turn away! I shall die if you look away! Tell me—now, here—this moment—before we part—if I may love you!”

“Go away!” she answered, struggling, bursting into tears. “This is too rude. If I am but a merchant’s daughter, I am God’s child. Remember I am alone. Leave me! Go! Or I will call for help!”

Eustace had read somewhere that such words were mere facons de parler—signs she meant no harm, intended no call for aid. So he gripped her hands tighter, gazed into her face with hungry eyes. But she was serious. A loud shriek made him realize—if he wished to preserve his reputation—he must flee. Yet there was one question for which he would risk his life.

He darted back, catching up with Campian just as the sun dipped behind the hills.
