I had just completed my studies at Oxford, stepping aside for a brief respite before formally assuming the stewardship of the estate. My father passed away while I was still a child, and my mother followed him within a year, leaving me nearly as isolated in the world as a man could possibly be. I possessed little knowledge of my ancestry's history; the only thread connecting me to my forebears was a noted propensity for scholarship. I had inherited this trait, dedicating significant portions of my time—though admittedly in a somewhat scattered manner—to the physical sciences. What drew me most was the sheer wonder of discovery. I was perpetually searching for strange analogies, linking facts across different scientific disciplines, bridging the gap between physical reality and metaphysical speculation, and drawing connections between current hypotheses and the faint glimmers emerging from the dreams I often indulged. I was also prone to the youthful error of mistaking a hypothesis for a proven theory. These were my mental peculiarities, requiring no further elaboration.

The house itself, like my family line, was steeped in antiquity, though describing its architecture serves no purpose here. It boasted a magnificent library, a collection begun before the invention of printing and expanded continuously through the ages, shaped inevitably by shifting tastes and intellectual pursuits. Nothing strikes a man more profoundly regarding the fleeting nature of ownership than inheriting an ancient legacy. Like a moving panorama, the estate had drifted past countless generations of eyes, and now it slowly slid before my own.

Although the library had undergone numerous structural changes and expansions over the centuries, it behaved like an encroaching tide, absorbing room after room until it claimed the majority of the ground floor. Its main chamber was vast, its walls lined with books almost to the ceiling. Branching off were smaller, irregularly shaped rooms connected by doors, open arches, short corridors, and flights of stairs ascending and descending. I spent most of my hours in the great room, immersed in volumes of science, both ancient and modern. My deepest fascination lay in tracing the history of the human mind's relationship with supposed knowledge. Figures like Ptolemy, Dante, the two Bacons, and Boyle held for me a greater allure than Darwin or Maxwell; they felt closer to the vanishing point where humanity broke through the darkness of ignorance.

On a gloomy August evening, I sat in my accustomed chair, my back to a window, engrossed in a book. Rain had battered the landscape throughout the morning and afternoon, but as sunset approached, the clouds parted, allowing the sun to pour its golden light into the room. I rose to gaze out the window. In the center of the expansive lawn, the ornate top of the fountain column was bathed in the sun's fiery glow. Turning back to my seat, my attention was immediately captured by that same brilliance reflecting off a single object in the room—a portrait set within a small, sunken niche amidst the endless rows of shelves.

I recognized it as the likeness of an ancestor, yet I had never questioned why it hung solitary in this obscure alcove rather than joining the others in the gallery or the grand halls. The direct sunlight rendered the painting vividly clear. For the first time, I truly saw it, and for the first time, it seemed to acknowledge my gaze. Filled with the light bouncing from the canvas, a sudden compulsion seized me. I turned toward the far end of the room and perceived—or so it seemed—a tall figure stretching a hand upward toward a bookshelf.

In the next instant, as my vision adjusted to the comparative dimness, the figure vanished. Concluding that my eyes had merely played a trick due to fatigue, I returned to my reading. I would undoubtedly have forgotten this fleeting hallucination had I not needed to consult a specific volume moments later. Upon locating the spot where the book should stand, I found a gap in the row. Simultaneously, the memory struck me: *just there* I had seen the old man searching for a tome. I scanned the area frantically, but found nothing.

The following morning, however, the book was precisely where I expected it to be. No one else in the household showed interest in such a volume.

Three days later, a stranger occurrence unfolded. On one of the library walls stood a low, narrow door leading to a closet housing some of the library's oldest and rarest treasures. It was a heavy door with a protruding frame, modified by an eccentric ancestor who had installed shallow shelves across it, displaying only book spines. This harmless deception was excusable, given that the titles on these fake covers were either whimsically invented or belonged to books lost forever. I harbored a fondness for this "masked" door. To heighten the illusion, a clever craftsman had inserted a fragment of a volume atop one of the rows—a piece thin enough to fit between the shelf above and the spine below. A diagonal section had been cut away, leaving a corner projecting outward. The binding was limp vellum, and opening the flap revealed manuscript written on parchment.

While seated and reading, my eyes wandered upward and landed on the door. Instantly, I realized the "book" described within the false cover was missing. Furious beyond measure, I rang the bell. The butler appeared. When I asked if he knew what had happened, he paled, assuring me he knew nothing. I found it harder to distrust his words than my own sight; he had served the family his entire life, renowned for his fidelity. Yet, he carried an air suggesting he held back information.

That afternoon, returning to my reading during a pause for reflection, I lowered my book and let my gaze drift. At that exact moment, I saw the back of a slender elderly man, clad in a long, dark coat worn smooth by age, slipping through the masked door into the closet beyond. I dashed across the room, threw the door open, and peered inside. The closet had no exit other than the door I had just opened. Seeing no one, I reluctantly concluded I was suffering another optical illusion and sat down again.

Naturally, a sense of unease lingered. Glancing up repeatedly to confirm I was alone, I eventually rose and rushed to the masked door. There, miraculously, was the mutilated volume in its proper place! I grabbed it and tugged; it remained firmly fixed as always.

Utterly bewildered, I summoned the butler again and recounted everything. His reaction mirrored my earlier encounter. He expressed relief that the "old gentleman" was being forgotten, noting that since I was the only witness, the secret remained safe. He admitted hearing tales of the figure early in his service but had carefully avoided mentioning him thereafter.

"The place was haunted by an old gentleman, was it?" I asked.

He replied that while everyone once believed it, the fact that I had never heard of it suggested the phenomenon had ended long ago. When pressed for details, he confessed he had never actually seen the apparition, despite arriving when my father was merely eight years old. My grandfather forbade any discussion of the subject, dismissing it as a pretext used by the maids to flirt with male staff. However, he recalled an ancient village woman who shared a legend concerning Mr. Raven, the longtime librarian to "Sir Upward"—the very man whose portrait hung in the niche. According to the tale, Sir Upward was a voracious reader of not only wholesome texts but also strange, forbidden, and evil books. Mr. Raven, whom the woman suspected was the devil himself, allegedly encouraged these pursuits. Suddenly, both men vanished, and Sir Upward was never seen again, though Mr. Raven reportedly continued to appear at irregular intervals in the library. Some believed he was immortal; others, including the old woman, found it more plausible that a dead man could return to the world he left than that a living man could endure for centuries. She claimed he meddled only with the books. Her description matched the figure I had witnessed perfectly.

"I hope it was but a friendly visit from the old gentleman!" he concluded, offering a troubled smile.

I assured him I welcomed whatever company Mr. Raven chose to bring, but urged him to maintain his silence regarding the matter with the servants. Finally, I asked if he had ever noticed the mutilated volume displaced. He insisted he never had, believing it to be a permanent fixture. He moved to the door and pulled; it remained immovable.

Nothing further occurred for several days. It was roughly a week later that the events I am about to recount finally unfolded. For some time, I had been haunted by thoughts of the manuscript fragment, attempting repeatedly to unlock whatever force held it captive, yet all my efforts proved futile. However, I had long harbored an intention to conduct a thorough inventory of the books stored in the closet; the peculiar atmosphere within had begun to unsettle me regarding their preservation.

One afternoon, this vague intention crystallized into a firm resolve. As I rose from my chair to begin the task, I caught a glimpse of movement near the closet's threshold. To be precise, I perceived a shadowy figure advancing from the doorway toward the far end of the room. It appeared to be a slight, stooping man clad in a shabby dress coat that trailed nearly to his heels. As he walked, the tails parted slightly, revealing thin legs encased in black stockings and large feet shod in wide, slipper-like shoes. Without hesitation, I followed. Whether I was pursuing a literal shadow or a tangible entity remained uncertain, yet my conviction that I was tracking *something* was absolute.

He exited the library, crossed the hall, and descended to the base of the grand staircase. Having grown up without siblings to encourage the kind of exploratory play that renders children intimately acquainted with every nook and cranny, and having been taken away while still a child by my guardian, I had not set eyes on this house since returning approximately a month prior to claim my inheritance.

We passed through successive passages until we arrived at a door situated beneath a winding wooden staircase. Though the space was thick with shadows, he was not among them.

I stood in the main garret, a cavernous expanse dominated by massive beams and rafters overhead. Great distances stretched out around me, punctuated only by occasional doors and long vistas whose gloom was pierced by a few cobweb-laden windows and small, dusky skylights. I gazed upon the scene with a strange mixture of awe and delight; this vast, uncharted territory was mine alone. The small chamber was bathed in light, yet it possessed the quality of sunlight in a desolate ruin—a dull, disconsolate radiance suggesting a place forgotten by utility, regretting its very existence. A few faint rays of sun cut through a suspended cloud of dust motes, illuminating a tall, narrow mirror. It was old-fashioned, appearing merely as an ordinary pane of glass, save for its ebony frame. I had been observing the mirror rather than peering into it when suddenly, reality shifted. I realized with jarring clarity that the reflection contained neither the chamber nor my own image. My memory is hazy regarding the transition; did the wall simply dissolve? What matters most is the aftermath, sufficient to explain any confusion: perhaps I mistook the glass protecting a wondrous painting for a reflective surface.

Before me lay a wild, broken landscape of heath and rock. In the middle distance rose desolate hills of unusual character, while the horizon was lined by the jagged peaks of a distant mountain range. Closest to me stretched a tract of moorland, flat and profoundly melancholy. Being short-sighted, I stepped forward to inspect the texture of a stone in the immediate foreground. In doing so, I noticed a large, ancient raven hopping toward me with solemn deliberation. Its plumage was a deep, purplish black, softened here and there with patches of gray. Unstartled by the presence of a living creature within what I believed to be a picture, I advanced another step to get a clearer view. Suddenly, I tripped—likely over the frame of the mirror—and found myself nose-to-beak with the bird. There was no glass between us. I was no longer in a room; I stood in the open air, upon a houseless heath.

I spun around, scanning the space behind me. Everything was a blur of indistinct shapes, a haze where fog merged with fields and clouds dissolved into mountainsides. There was only one certainty: I recognized nothing. Assuming I was trapped in a visual trickery that touch might unravel, I thrust my arms forward, groping blindly. Instinct drove me toward the only living presence nearby: the raven. It stood a short distance away, watching me with an expression that balanced respect and mockery. Had I stumbled into a realm where the physical and spiritual laws of our world had collapsed? Could a man vanish from the ordered universe at any moment, becoming prey to chaos? Then, a thought occurred to me: a bird capable of speaking to a man deserved a civilized response, perhaps even a greater claim to one than a human. As he spoke, he turned his back. Instantly, I recognized him. He was no longer a bird, but a man of medium height, stooped, very thin, clad in a long black tailcoat. He turned again, and I saw him as a raven once more.

He began to walk away, and once more I saw the librarian. He hadn't transformed; he simply seemed to have absorbed his shadow. "And my grandfather—is he also with you?" I asked.

"No; he remains in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead."

"Where is the Evil Wood? Let me find him."

"You will not find it, but you will hardly fail to encounter it. It is the place where those who refuse to sleep rise at night to kill their dead and bury them."

"I cannot comprehend you!"

"Naturally not. Nor do I understand you; I cannot read your heart or your face. When my wife and I fail to understand our children, it is simply because there is not enough of them to be understood. Only God can comprehend foolishness."

"Then," I said, feeling exposed and utterly worthless, "will you kindly show me the nearest way home? I know there are many paths, for I have taken two already."

"There are indeed many ways."

"Please tell me how to identify the nearest."

"I cannot," the raven replied. "You and I use the same words with entirely different meanings. We often cannot tell people what they *need* to know because they *want* to know something else, thereby misunderstanding everything we say. Home is always so far away in the palm of your hand, and telling you how to reach it serves no purpose. But you will get there; you must get there; you have to get there. Everyone who is not at home must go home. You thought you were at home where I found you; if that truly were your home, you could not have left it. Nobody can leave home. And nobody is, or ever will be, at home without having gone there."

"An enigma stepping on another enigma!" I exclaimed. "I did not come here to solve riddles."

"No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting for you! Indeed, you are the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, appearing so only because you are not true."

"Worse and worse!" I cried.

"And you *must* answer the riddles!" he insisted. "They will keep asking themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle striving to escape, and you are holding your door tightly shut against it."

"Will you not, in pity, tell me what I must do—where I must go?"

"How should I dictate *your* duty, or the path to it?"

"If I am not to go home, at least direct me toward my own kind."

"I know of none. The beings most like you lie in that direction." He pointed his beak. I saw nothing but the blinding setting sun.

"Well," I said bitterly, "I cannot help feeling ill-treated—snatched from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and denied instruction on where to go or what to do!"

"You forget," the raven said calmly, "that when I brought you and you rejected my hospitality, you reached what you call 'home' safely. Now you have come of your own accord. Good night."

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak lowered to the ground. I stood stunned. True, I had come of my own volition, but had I not intended to make amends? My heart ached, and in my mind, there was no quest, no purpose, no hope, no desire. Gazing after the raven, I wanted to follow, but felt it futile.

Suddenly, he lunged at a specific spot, driving the full weight of his body into his bill. For several moments, he dug vigorously. Then, with a flutter of his wings, he flung back his head, and something shot from his bill, soaring high into the air. Just then, the sun dipped below the horizon, and the air instantly grew dusky. Yet, the object transformed, opening into a soft radiance and drifting toward me like a firefly, though vastly larger and glowing with a brilliant yellow light. It flew overhead.

I turned and pursued it.

Here, I must interrupt my narrative to note the constant struggle involved in describing what cannot be stated with even approximate precision. The events recorded, along with the nature of the creatures involved, differ so profoundly from any conceivable occurrence in this world that I can only present them by translating their effects upon me into the forms and languages of earthly life—not the things themselves, but the feelings they awaken. Even this attempt is made with a persistent sense of failure, for I find it impossible to capture more than one facet of a multitude of complex significances, or one concentric layer of a graduated embodiment. A single event often seems to be and mean many things simultaneously, possessing an uncertain core that constantly alters their appearance. I am frequently driven to record

As the air thickened into an oppressive blackness and winter tightened its grip around me, the small fire I carried burned brighter, casting a stark, arresting glow. Hovering in mid-air, it waited patiently until I stepped beneath its radiance. Then, it drifted forward, pausing occasionally to circle patches of jagged rock. Each time I glanced upward, the flame appeared to swell, until finally, it cast a companion shadow beside me. It was clearly a bird-butterfly, gliding with a rhythmic, swallow-like double beat. Its wings were vast and nearly square, flashing through the entire spectrum of the rainbow.

Mesmerized by their splendor, I became so transfixed by their beauty that I tripped over a low rock and lay stunned. When consciousness returned, the creature hovered directly above my head, emitting a full chord of light filled with myriad shades and colors I had never witnessed before. I rose and continued, but unable to tear my gaze away from the shimmering entity, I struck my foot hard against a stone. Fearful of falling again, I sat down to watch this tiny glory, and a profound longing surged within me to possess it.

To my unspeakable delight, the creature began to descend. First slowly, then swiftly, it sank lower, growing larger as it approached. I felt as though the treasure of the universe was surrendering itself to me—I stretched out my hand and caught it. But the instant my fingers closed around it, its light vanished. Pitch darkness swallowed everything; a dead book with spread-out boards lay cold and heavy in my grasp. I tossed it into the air, hearing it crash among the heather. Burying my face in my hands, I sat in motionless misery. Yet, the cold grew so biting that, fearing frostbite, I forced myself to stand.

The moment my feet touched the ground, a faint sensation of light awakened within me. "Is it coming back to life?" I cried, a surge of hope piercing my despair. Alas, no! It was merely the edge of a moon rising sharply over a flat horizon. She offered light, but no guidance. This celestial body would not hover protectively over me, nor wait for my faltering steps. She could only present me with an ignorant choice.

With a full face, the moon ascended, and I began to discern my surroundings. To the west, not far from me, a range of low hills broke the skyline. I set off toward them. What a long, arduous night I endured before reaching them! The moon seemed to possess knowledge, for she stared at me with an odd intensity. Her gaze was indeed icy-cold, yet brimming with interest—or perhaps curiosity. She was not the familiar moon of Earth; her visage was strange, and her light even stranger. Perhaps it reflected an unknown sun! Every time I looked up, I found her staring at me with undiminished force.

At first, I was annoyed, viewing her stare as the rudeness of a fellow creature. Soon, however, I perceived—or imagined—a certain wondering pity in her gaze: *Why am I out in your night?* Only then did I truly comprehend the horror of being awake in the universe: I existed, and I could not stop it.

As I walked, my boots left the heather and tread upon bare, spongy soil, akin to dry, powdery peat. To my dismay, the ground heaved momentarily beneath me. Presently, I saw what appeared to be the ripple of an earthquake racing ahead of me, shadowy in the low moonlight. It faded into the distance, but while I watched, a single wave rose and crept slowly toward me. A yard or two away, it burst open, and from it scrambled and leaped forth an animal resembling a tiger. Clumps of mold hung from his mouth and ears, and his eyes winked and flamed as he charged, baring white teeth in a silent snarl.

I stood paralyzed, unaware of whether courage or fear held sway. He turned his head to the ground and plunged downward. "That moon is affecting my brain," I muttered, resuming my journey. "What life exists here but the phantasmagoric—the stuff of dreams? I am indeed walking in a vain show!"

Thus, I struggled to keep my heart above the waters of fear, unaware that the very entity I distrusted was my defense against the realities I mistook for phantoms. Her light controlled the monsters; without it, I would scarcely have taken a second step on this hideous terrain.

"I shall not be appalled by that which is merely seeming!" I told myself, yet it felt terrifying to traverse a sea where such monstrous fish swam below. Just then, a few paces away, the head of a worm emerged slowly from the earth. It was as large as a polar bear's head and bore a striking resemblance to one, complete with a white mane atop its red neck. The writhing contractions required to free its massive length were horrifying, yet I dared not look away. Once its tail cleared the surface, it lay seemingly exhausted, struggling weakly to burrow back underground.

"Does it feed on the dead," I wondered aloud, "and is it powerless to harm the living? If they scent their prey and emerge, why do they leave me untouched?" I now understood: the moon paralyzed them. Throughout the night, grotesque creatures, none identical to the others, threatened me. In some, vibrant beauty enhanced the loathsomeness of their shapes; one enormous serpent was draped from head to tail in feathers of glorious hue.

Eventually, I became so accustomed to these harmless threats that I began to entertain the path with inventions of monstrosity, never suspecting that I owed every moment of my life to the staring moon. Though her radiance was not primordial, it sufficiently hampered the evil things that I walked safely. After all, light remains light, even if it is but the final reflection in a countless chain. How swiftly my feet would have carried me across this restless soil had I known that, should her lamp cease to shine on this cursed spot while I remained within its reach, I would instantly fall at the mercy of beings devoid of mercy—becoming the center of a writhing heap of horrors, each individual as terrible as it had previously only seemed.

Foolish in my ignorance, I watched the weary, solemn, anxious moon descend the widening vault above me, feeling no greater unease than the dread of losing my way—even though, as yet, I had nowhere to lose it. Drawing near the hills I had chosen as my destination, and with the moon now close to their skyline, the soundless thrashing ceased, revealing a burrow lying motionless and bare.

Then, I saw the form of a woman walking slowly over the light soil. A white mist floated around her, constantly shifting between assuming and dissolving the shape of garments, gathering to her or blowing away by a wind that trailed her steps. She was beautiful, yet her countenance wore such a mixture of pride and misery that I could hardly believe my eyes. She paced up and down, vainly attempting to seize the mist and wrap it around herself. The eyes in her beautiful face were dead, and a dark spot marred her left side; she pressed her hand against it repeatedly, as if stifling pain or illness. Her hair hung nearly to her feet, and sometimes the wind mingled it so thoroughly with the mist that I could not distinguish one from the other. But when it settled, it shone a pale gold in the moonlight.

Suddenly, she pressed both hands to her heart and collapsed. The mist rose from her and dissolved into the air. I ran to her, but she began to writhe in such agony that I stood aghast. A moment later, her legs hurried away from her body, speeding off as serpents. From her shoulders fled her arms, fleeing also as serpents. Then, something flew up from her like a bat. When I looked again, she was gone.

The ground rose like a storm-tossed sea; terror seized me. I turned toward the hills and ran. Already on the slope of their base, the moon sank behind one of the summits, plunging me into its shadow. Behind me rose a waste and sickening cry, sounding like frustrated desire—the only sound I had heard since the death of the butterfly. It shook my heart like a flag in the wind. I turned, seeing many dark objects bounding after me, and sprinted for the crest of a ridge where the moon still shone.

She seemed to linger there, allowing me to see enough to defend myself. Soon, I spotted her and climbed faster. Crossing the shadow of a rock, I heard the creatures panting hotly at my heels. But just as the foremost threw himself upon me with a snarl of greedy hate, we rushed into the moon's beam together. She flashed out an angry light, and he fell from me, reduced to a bodiless blotch. Strength surged through me, and I turned on the rest. One by one, as they darted into the light, they dropped with a howl. I saw—or fancied I saw—a strange smile on the round face above me.

I climbed to the top of the ridge. Far away, the moon shone, sinking toward a low horizon. The air was pure and strong. I descended slightly, finding it warmer, and sat down to await the dawn. The moon dipped below the horizon, and the world once again fell into darkness.

I sank instantly into a deep slumber, and upon waking, the sun was just cresting the horizon. Climbing to the ridge once more, I cast a look backward. The hollow I had traversed under the pale moonlight was utterly empty; not a sign of life remained. Did the vast, tranquil plain stretching before me teem with creatures of insatiable hunger? Turning my gaze forward toward the path I must take, the landscape appeared as a boundless desert. In the far distance, a splotch of differing hue suggested a forest, though nothing else betrayed habitation—no smoke, no drifting dust, no shadow of cultivated fields. The sky was a flawless dome of blue, devoid of clouds or even the thinnest veil of haze.

Descending from the heights, I pushed onward toward that imagined grove. Something living surely waited within its bounds; certainly, nothing could exist on this barren side of it. When I finally reached the edge of the plain, my vision confirmed my fears: as far as the eye could pierce, the ground consisted solely of rock. Here it lay flat and channeled, there humped and pinnacled—it was clearly the dried-up bed of a mighty river, scarred by countless ancient waterways, yet entirely devoid of moisture. Some channels crusted with dry moss; others bore lichens hardened as stone. The air, once filled with the joyful clamor of flowing water, was now silent as death.

It took the entire day to reach the distant patch, which proved indeed to be a forest, though not a single trickle of stream or rivulet had I crossed! Yet, bathed in the blinding glare of midday, I felt pursued by an auditory hallucination, hearing with crystal clarity the roar of myriad waters, making the stark testimony of my eyes seem impossible. As the sun began its descent toward the western horizon, leaving the riverbed behind, I stepped into the woods. Sunken below the canopy, filtering its rays through pillar-like trunks, the light revealed a realm of blessed shadows eager to welcome me.

I had anticipated a pine forest, but instead, I encountered a riot of arboreal forms. Some bore striking similarities to trees from my home, while others defied description, presenting marvels unlike anything I had ever witnessed. Collapsing beneath the boughs of what resembled a blossoming eucalyptus, I gazed upward. Its flowers possessed a hard calyx akin to a skull, the top lifting like a lid to allow a frothy, brain-like bloom to overflow its cup. From the shade of its falchion-shaped leaves, my eyes drifted deeper into the gloom.

Soon, however, the forest's doors and windows began to close, sealing off aisles, corridors, and glades. Night descended instantly, bringing a coldness that was immediate and piercing. What a night it truly was! How can I convey its wild, spectral nature to you? The great tree beneath which I lay rose high before branching, yet its limbs drooped so low they seemed poised to cage me as I leaned against the smooth trunk. My gaze wandered through the fleeting twilight of the dying forest.

To my listless eyes, the varied outlines of the clumping foliage began to shift, assuming—or rather, suggesting—shapes other than their own. A gentle wind stirred, setting a neighboring tree rocking. Every branch swung, every twig and leaf merging its individual motion with the sway of its limb. Within the shifting leaf-patterns, I saw a pack of wolves struggling violently to break free from a wizard's invisible leash; greyhounds could not strain with such savage desperation. Watching them, my interest sharpened as the wind gained strength and their movements acquired life.

Another mass of foliage, denser and more compact, presented my imagination with a cluster of horse heads and forequarters, seemingly projecting caparisoned from stalls. Their necks bobbed up and down with impatient urgency, until the growing wind shattered their vertical rhythm with a wilder, side-to-side sway. What grotesque heads they were! So gaunt, so strange—some mere bare skulls, one with skin taut against its bones! Another had lost its lower jaw, hanging low in an expression of unutterable weariness, yet occasionally jerking high as if trying to ease a nonexistent bit. Hovering above them, at the tip of a branch, stood the form of a woman, waving her arms in an imperious command.

The distinctness of these leaf-masses first startled, then unsettled me. What if they overwhelmed my mind with deceptive reality? But the twilight dissolved into darkness; the wind died away; every shape vanished into the night. I slept.

When I awoke, it was still dark. Then, a distant, confused rushing sound emerged, mingled with faint cries. It swelled and grew until a tumult of gathering multitudes filled the wood. Suddenly, noises approached from all directions; the spot where I lay felt like the epicenter of a commotion spreading through the entire forest. I scarcely moved a finger or toe, terrified of betraying my presence to hostile entities.

At last, the moon crested the treeline and spilled slowly into the woods. With her first gleam, the noises erupted into a deafening uproar, and dim shapes materialized around me. As she climbed higher, brightening the scene, the cacophony intensified and the figures clarified. A furious battle raged all around me. Wild shrieks, roars of rage, the shock of collision, and prolonged struggles mixed with articulate words, surging in my ears. Curses and creeds, snarls and sneers, laughter and mockery, sacred names and howls of hate collided in a chaotic interpenetration.

Skeletons and phantoms fought in mad confusion. Swords swept through the ghosts; they merely shivered. Maces crashed upon the skeletons, shattering them hideously, yet not one fell or ceased fighting so long as a single joint held two bones together. Bones of men and horses lay scattered and heaped; grinding and crunching them underfoot, the skeletons continued to fight. Everywhere charged the bone-gaunt white steeds; everywhere, on foot or riding wind-blown misty horses, raged and ravaged the indestructible specters. Weapons and hooves clashed and crushed, while skeleton jaws and phantom throats swelled the deafening tumult with the war-cry of every opinion, good or evil, that had ever bred strife, injustice, or cruelty in any world.

Even the holiest words accompanied the most hateful blows. Truths distorted by lies hurled through the wind of javelins and bones. Moment by moment, someone would turn against their comrades, fighting more wildly than before, crying out, "THE TRUTH! THE TRUTH!" I noticed one figure wheeling endlessly in a circle, striking outward on all sides. Exhausted pairs would sit side by side for a minute, then rise to renew the fierce combat. No one stooped to comfort the fallen, nor did anyone step aside to spare him.

The moon shone until the sun rose, and all night long I glimpsed a woman moving at her will above the strife-tormented multitude, now on this front, now on that, one arm outstretched to urge the fight, the other pressed against her side. "Ye are men: slay one another!" she shouted. I saw her dead eyes and her dark spot, recalling vividly what I had witnessed the previous night. This was the battle of the dead, which I saw and heard lying beneath the tree.

Just before sunrise, a breeze swept through the forest, and a voice cried out, "Let the dead bury their dead!" At the word, the contending thousands dropped silently. When the sun peered in, it found not a single bone, but only here and there a withered branch.

I rose and resumed my journey through a wood as quiet as any that grows from the peaceful earth. The morning wind had ceased when the sun appeared, and the trees were silent. Not a bird sang, not a squirrel, mouse, or weasel showed itself, not a late-flying moth crossed my path. Yet, as I walked, I kept a vigilant watch over myself, daring not to let my eyes rest on any forest shape. Throughout, I seemed to hear faint sounds of mattocks and spades and hurling bones; any moment, my eyes might open to reveal things I would rather not see! Prudence whispered that perhaps, to manifest, ten thousand phantoms awaited only my consenting fancy.

By mid-afternoon, I emerged from the wood to find before me a second network of dry watercourses. At first, I feared I had wandered from my intended line and reversed direction, but soon realized otherwise. I concluded I had simply arrived at another branch of the same riverbed. Crossing it immediately, I found myself in the bottom of a wide channel just as the sun set.

Sitting down to wait for the moon, I grew sleepy and stretched out on the moss. The moment my head touched the ground, I heard the sounds of rushing streams—all manner of sweet, watery noises. The veiled melody of molten music lured me into a dreamless sleep. When I awoke, the sun was already high, casting the wrinkled countryside into wide visibility. Covered in shadows, the land lay striped and mottled like the skin of a wild beast. As the sun climbed, the shadows receded, as if the rocks were re-absorbing the darkness that had seeped forth during the night.

Hitherto, I had loved my Arab mare and my books more than any living man or woman. Now, at last, my soul thirsted for human presence, longing even for those inhabitants of this alien world

I knelt beside a channel, examining what appeared to be a modest shrub—the likely vanguard of an army hidden behind it. It bore a small fruit; unfamiliar and unrecognizable, I hesitated to touch it. Little did I know that hundreds of eyes, burning with curiosity, watched from the shadows of the rocks, waiting to see if I would succumb to temptation. Moving onward, I encountered plants of increasing size, eventually realizing they were not shrubs but dwarf-trees. By the time I reached the second branch of the riverbed, the banks were choked with them, forcing me to leap across gaps I otherwise couldn't cross. In one channel, a sound erupted—a rushing roar like a flock of birds fleeing an ivy-choked wall—yet I saw nothing.

Further along stood massive fruit-bearing trees, though their produce looked coarse. They lined the edge of a vast hollow, clearly the dried bed of an ancient lake. While the surrounding forest teemed with diverse species, the trees within the depression were almost exclusively fruit-laden. Exhausted, I descended a short slope of grass and moss to lie down. Just beyond rested a tiny tree heavy with rosy apples, no larger than cherries, hanging within reach. I plucked one and ate it. The flavor was exquisite. As I reached for a second, a sudden burst of childish shouting, mixed with laughter as clear and sweet as a mountain brook, shattered the silence.

"He likes our apples! He likes our apples!" cried a dozen tiny voices. "He's a good giant! A fine giant!"
"He *is* a giant," one child objected.
"He is rather big," another agreed wisely, "but size doesn't mean wisdom! You can grow tall and foolish unless you watch yourself!"

I propped myself up on my elbow to stare. Surrounding me was a motley assembly of children of every age, from toddlers learning to walk to youths nearing adolescence. A smaller group of three or four elders stood slightly apart, watching with less excitement than the frenzied majority. They chattered in clusters, debating and contradicting one another with the fervor of adults in a marketplace, yet possessing superior manners, sharper wit, and boundless joy. I realized immediately that my reaching for the second apple confirmed my enjoyment of the first. How they deduced my character from a single bite remained a mystery to me, though I wasn't surprised that at least one voice urged caution.

Silence fell as a movement rippled through the crowd. A sweet-faced boy, looking both innocent and mischievously rogue, stepped forward holding a huge green apple. All eyes fixed on me expectantly.
"Eat, good giant," he commanded softly.
I sat up, accepted the offering with a smile, and prepared to bite. But the moment my teeth touched the skin, I spat it out and hurled it far away.

A fresh shriek of delight erupted. The children swarmed me, nearly suffocating me in their embrace. They kissed my cheeks and hands, grabbed my legs, and climbed over my arms and shoulders, burying their faces in my neck. Overwhelmed by the affectionate little goblins, I collapsed onto the ground.
"Good, good giant!" they chorused. "We knew you'd come! Oh, you dear, strong giant!"

Their joyful babbling resumed, rising in waves of jubilation. Suddenly, the noise cut off. Those nearby recoiled; those climbing me scrambled down, helping me upright. Concern replaced their earlier mirth on their bright faces.
"Get up, good giant!" a little girl urged urgently. "Hurry! Hurry! He saw you toss his apple away!"

Before she finished, I was standing. She pointed upward toward the slope's crest. Perched there was a grotesque figure, a few inches taller than me, radiating hostility. Yet, seeing no weapon in his grasp, I felt no immediate fear. My little friends had vanished instantly. He began his descent, and I moved upward to gain a better vantage point. As he approached, he growled like a cornered beast. Once we were on level ground, he stopped and extended a hand. I offered mine in greeting, but he snatched it back, brandished a threat, and thrust it forward again. His intent became clear: he demanded the apple I had discarded. I made a face of disgust and waved my hand in refusal.

"Do you dare tell me your apple was unworthy?" he howled in rage.
"One rotten apple can spoil the finest tree," I replied.
Whether he grasped my metaphor, I could not say. He lunged forward, and I braced myself. However, he paused, allowing a second giant—identical in appearance—to creep up behind me before striking. I parried his initial blow to the face, but the newcomer struck me squarely on the back of the head. Together, they overpowered me easily.

They dragged me into the woods above the valley, home to their tribe. Here dwelled wretched huts constructed of fallen branches and scattered stones. They shoved me inside one, throwing me to the dirt and kicking me. A woman watched with cold indifference. During my captivity, I struggled to distinguish gender; the creatures differed so little in form and manner that I often wondered if I had stumbled upon a fungoid race, possessing just enough intelligence to mimic human emotion—anger, greed, and motion. Their diet of tubers, bulbs, and strange fruits repulsed me, yet nothing angered them more than witnessing my distaste. Women cuffed me and men kicked me whenever I refused to swallow their fare.

That night, I lay on the floor, barely able to move, though sleep brought some refreshment. At dawn, they dragged me back to the valley. Tying my feet to a tree with a rough rope, they placed a flat stone with a jagged, saw-like edge into my left hand. When I shifted it to my right, they kicked me and forced it back into my left. Their command was simple: strip the bark from every branch bearing no fruit. After one final kick, they abandoned me to the task.

I began the dreary labor, hoping that compliance might buy me solitude to observe and plan my escape. Fortunately, a dwarf-tree grew dangerously close. Every few minutes, I plucked a small fruit from it, eating it to sustain my strength against the relentless torment.

I had barely begun my labor when faint, high-pitched voices drifted toward me. Soon, the "Little Ones"—as they introduced themselves—crept forth from the dense undergrowth of tiny trees filling the gaps between the colossal trunks. Within minutes, dozens, then hundreds, swarmed around me. I signaled that the Giants had only just departed and remained nearby, but they erupted in laughter, claiming the air was pure and clear.

"They are too blind to see us!" they chimed, sounding like a flock of ringing sheep bells. "Do you like that rough hemp binding your ankles?" one asked.

"I pretend I cannot remove it," I explained.

"They can't even see their own toes!" he retorted, dancing with boundless joy. "Take short steps, and they'll believe the rope is secure."

As he spoke, a larger girl knelt to undo the clumsy knot. I smiled, assuming her delicate fingers would fail, but instantly the bond was free. They guided me to sit, offering me exquisite, miniature fruits. Then, the smallest began to play with me in wild, chaotic bursts, rendering my work impossible. As one grew weary, another took her place, continuing until the sun dipped below the horizon and the heavy, rhythmic thud of Giant footsteps approached.

The Little Ones scattered instantly. I hurried to rebind my ankles.

"We must be careful," warned the girl who had freed me. "A single crushing step from one of their stubby feet could kill a tiny one!"

"Can they truly not perceive you?" I asked.

"They might spot movement," she replied grimly. "If the children piled atop you as they did earlier, it would be dreadful. They despise every living thing except themselves—and yet, they aren't very alive either!" She whistled sharply like a bird, and in the blink of an eye, everyone vanished, including her.

My Master arrived, presumably to reclaim me. He unbound my feet, hauled me to the doorway of his hut, tossed me onto the dirt floor, re-tied my legs, delivered a sharp kick, and departed.

At that moment, escape was within my grasp. Yet, having found friends, I hesitated. They were enchanting, radiating such innocent charm that I felt compelled to learn more of them. *Tomorrow,* I promised myself with a surge of delight, *I shall see them again!*

However, from the moment silence settled over the huts until sleep claimed me, I heard their whispers drifting everywhere, knowing I was watched with affectionate intensity. From then on, solitude rarely touched me. I ceased to notice the Giants entirely; they were too obtuse to devise cruelty, merely stumbling blindly. Often, I evaded a blow by seizing the attacker's foot and toppling him, after which he never tried again.

But the Little Ones offered constant delight. Their presence transformed my monotonous toil into something bearable, stripping away both misery and fatigue. Very quickly, my love for them deepened beyond words. Though ignorant, they possessed profound wisdom, seemingly capable of absorbing any lesson. My bed was merely the bare earth, yet whenever I awoke, I found myself nestled in a cradle of children—one or another curled in my arms, the order of succession shifting imperceptibly until dawn revealed who held whom. Unconsciously, I clutched the new arrival, while the rest snuggled close, the tiniest nearest to my heart. The nightly chill meant nothing to me anymore.

Morning and evening rituals involved bringing me abundant food. One morning, I awoke alone. Sensing approaching footsteps, I saw Lona emerge from the woods—the tallest, most solemn of the group, revered as their mother. She was followed by the entire tribe, moving silently to avoid waking the Giant at our door. In her arms, she carried a newborn boy. Previously, a girl-infant had been the youngest member. Three older girls served as nurses, sharing the burden of caring for this treasure with the whole community.

Among the Little Ones, dolls were nonexistent; the elders cared for the young, who in turn tended the infants. Lona approached and placed the baby in my lap. The infant opened his eyes, fixed a gaze upon me, closed them, and drifted back to sleep.

"He loves you already!" Lona declared.

"Where did you find him?" I asked.

"In the wood, naturally," she beamed. "That is where we always discover them. Isn't he perfect? We searched all night. Sometimes it isn't easy!"

"How do you know when one appears?"

"I can't explain," she admitted. "Everyone rushes to tell the others, yet we never determine who spoke first. Perhaps someone murmured it in sleep, and another caught it half-awake. When a baby arrives in the wood, no one stops to inquire. By the time we locate it, it's too late."

"Do more boys or girls appear?"

She frowned slightly at the repetition of the query. "I don't know. You can certainly count them!"

"We never do that," she insisted. "We dislike being counted."

"Why?"

"It wouldn't feel smooth. We prefer not to know."

"Where do the babies originate initially?"

"From the wood. Always. There is nowhere else."

She knew their final destination perfectly but considered the origin complete knowledge.

"How frequently do you find one?"

"Such happiness drains all our joy, and we forget the previous discovery. Are you glad to have him, Good Giant?"

"Yes, indeed!"

"But how do you nourish him?"

"I will demonstrate," she said, vanishing briefly to return with two or three ripe plums. She placed one to the baby's lips.

From the gloom emerged a white figure, clutching a child tightly to her breast, moving with a frantic, stumbling gait. "She is being hunted!" I realized, the thought striking me with cold clarity. "A predator of this nightmare night stalks her!"

To follow her openly would only add to her panic. Instead, I stepped directly into her path, intending to intercept whatever shadow pursued her. For a heartbeat, I watched her flee into the dusk. Then, behind me, a soft, silent rush erupted. Before I could pivot, a blur of white leaped over my head, striking me squarely on the forehead. I fell hard.

I scrambled up instantly, but all I caught of my attacker was a streak of vanishing white. Ignoring the blood trickling down my face, I sprinted after the beast. Yet, within mere steps, a shriek of pure despair tore through the quivering air. My heart sank; it was likely too late.

Pushing forward, I spotted a low, white shape emerging from the mist-laced moonlight. Initially, I assumed it was another hunter, but it moved with agonizing slowness, making strange, floundering leaps. It hobbled on three legs, dragging its left forepaw high above the ground. Its skin was shining white, marred by dark, oval spots, accompanied by a low, rushing sound like water cascading over grass. As it passed, I saw a stream of red pouring from the raised limb.

"It's blood," I muttered. "Someone else has wounded this thing before I arrived."

Yet, a profound pity seized me at the sight of the suffering creature. Even if an axe had been in my hand, I could not bring myself to strike. With broken, hobbling leaps, it disappeared into the darkness, leaving a small torrent of blood to flow softly back through the grass beside me.

"If it bleeds like this," I thought grimly, "it will soon be harmless."

Driven by the hope that I might still aid the woman—or witness her savior—I pressed on. A short distance ahead, I found her sitting on the grass, the child cradled in her lap.

"Is there anything I can do?" I called out.

At my voice, she jumped violently, ready to rise. I threw myself onto the ground to appear less threatening.

"You need not fear," I said quickly. "I was chasing the beast just as you found a closer protector! It passed me moments ago, its leg bleeding so profusely that it must be dying."

"There is little hope of that," she whispered, trembling. "Do you not know whose beast this is?"

Suspicious thoughts flickered in my mind, but I feigned ignorance. "I know nothing of this brute. What happened to its champion?"

"What champion?" she replied, confused. "I saw no one."

"Then how did the monster suffer such injury?"

"I smashed its foot with a stone—hard as I could strike. Didn't you hear her cry?"

"Well, you are a brave woman!" I exclaimed. "I thought *you* were the one screaming!"

"It was the leopardess," she corrected.

"I've never heard such a sound from an animal! It sounded like a woman in torture!"

"My voice was gone. I couldn't have shrieked to save my baby! When I saw that horrid mouth at my darling's tiny white neck, I grabbed a stone and crushed its lame foot."

"Tell me about this creature," I urged. "I am a stranger here."

"You'll learn enough if you're heading to Bulika," she said. "But I must never return there!"

"Yes, I am going to Bulika—to see the Princess."

"Be careful! You'd better not go... unless perhaps you are? The Princess is a very good, kind woman!"

I noticed a slight shift in her posture. Thick clouds had gathered over the moon, obscuring our surroundings. I feared she was preparing to flee again.

"You are safe from me," I assured her. "What oath would you require?"

"I know by your speech that you are not of Bulika," she answered. "I trust you. Neither am I of them; otherwise, I wouldn't be here. They never trust anyone. I wish I could see your face, but I love your voice. There, my darling is asleep. The foul beast hasn't harmed her. Yes, it was my baby she sought! And then she would have torn her mother apart for carrying her off!"

"Some say the Princess keeps two white leopardesses," she continued, stroking the sleeping child. "I know only one—with spots. Everyone knows HER! If the Princess hears of a baby, she sends her immediately to drink its blood. Then the infant either dies or grows up an idiot. I tried to leave with my baby, but the Princess was away. I thought I could wait until I was stronger. But she must have brought the beast back while I rested, crossing my path. I heard the *sniff-snuff* of the leopardess behind me and ran—oh, how I ran! But my darling won't die. Look, there isn't even a mark on her!"

"Where are you taking her?"

"Somewhere no one will ever find."

"Why is the Princess so cruel?"

"An ancient prophecy states a child will be the death of her. That is why she refuses all marriage offers, they say."

"But what becomes of her country if she kills every newborn?"

"She cares not for her country. She dispatches witches to teach women spells that repel infants and force them to eat abhorrent things. Some whisper she is allied with the Shadows to extinguish the entire race. At night, we hear the hunting beast and lie awake, shivering. She can sense the birth of a child instantly, lying in wait at the door to enter. There are words powerful enough to drive her away, but they do not always work."

As she spoke, she began to rise hastily.

"I doubt she will ever make it home," I observed. "Let me carry the baby for you," I offered, standing up.

She gave no answer. When I reached for the child, she clutched it tighter against her chest.

"I cannot understand," I said, walking alongside her, "how that brute could be bleeding so heavily!"

"Take my advice and stay far from the palace," she warned. "There are sounds inside at night, as if the dead try to scream but cannot open their mouths!"

With an abrupt farewell, she turned away. Clearly, she desired no further companionship. I stood still, listening until her footsteps faded completely into the silence of the grass.

I had lost all sense of my bearings, wandering in a state of pure, helpless agitation, when suddenly I stepped directly into the path of a leopardess. Beneath her paws, a river of crimson blood surged forward, striking my ankles with the force of a mountain brook. Driven by a primal, formless dread regarding the origin of this gore, I hurried away from it. Yet, compelled by instinct, I stayed close to its rhythm, tracing the sound uphill toward Bulika.

Soon, reason cut through my panic. No leopardess, no elephant, not even the leviathans of our own world, could sustain such a torrent unless every vessel in their bodies burst open, draining the earth's lakes and forests faster than nature could refill them. This was not blood. I dipped a finger into the flow and confirmed it instantly: it was water. A soft, murmuring rivulet spilled aimlessly over the grass, singing a melody too sweet to ignore yet too dangerous to taste. I pressed onward, driven by the hope of dawn and the haunting familiarity of that alien sound—a stark contrast to the scorching heat of the day. The mere cooling of my feet revitalized me, allowing me to walk without fatigue until the encroaching twilight revealed the silhouette of a city against the pale aurora. These wall-towers seemed older than time itself.

Then, the brook vanished. For some time, I had noticed its volume fading, but I had stopped paying attention. Looking back, I saw the grass bent in the direction of its former course, dotted with small, shimmering pools. Beyond that point, heading toward the city, there was absolutely no trace of it. Near where I stood, the spring must have sputtered to a halt. Surrounding the fortress were vast gardens teeming with vegetables I did not recognize—no familiar flowers, no signs of animal life. The plots grew right up to the massive walls, separated only by towering mounds of gravel and refuse dumped from the ramparts.

I approached the nearest gate. It hung ajar, neither locked nor guarded, its hinges rusted in a state of perfect stasis, unable to swing further open or shut tighter. Stepping inside, I gazed down a long, ancient thoroughfare. Utter silence reigned; there was scarcely a sign of life. Was this a graveyard of a civilization? Turning back, I trudged over piles of dust, crossing several roads that all led to similar gates. I refused to return until I heard the stir of waking inhabitants. *What am I here for?* I asked myself. *What do I hope to find?* My purpose remained clouded, though one image burned clear: I needed to see the woman I had breathed life into once more. I desired no company from her; she had awakened terrifying doubts within me, making friendship—or worse, love—an impossibility. Yet, her presence exerted a strange, magnetic pull. I felt compelled to resist her influence while simultaneously dissecting it. I sought to pierce her inscrutable nature, to understand a mode of existence that defied imagination. How reckless! A man should not willingly court temptation for the sake of knowledge. Conversely, I had resurrected a malevolent force teetering on extinction. I was accountable for whatever chaos might follow. I had learned she was the enemy of children; perhaps the "Little Ones" were in peril. I had left them seeking answers, receiving only fragments of light. Now, I needed more. I needed to know how to shield them.

At last, a faint commotion drifted through the air. I moved through the next gate and down a narrow street lined with tall, looming houses, settling onto the base of a pillar crowned by a grotesque, bat-like statue. Soon, citizens began to saunter past. I addressed one, but he offered only a sneering glare and a harsh insult before moving on. Rising, I navigated alley after alley, which gradually filled with loiterers. As expected, I saw no children. Eventually, near a gate, a group of young men resembling the brutish giants I remembered surrounded me. They stared, shoved, hustled, and hurled debris at me. I endured it, desperate not to spark hostility where I intended to stay. I tried appealing to those who looked kinder, but no one paused to listen. I appeared destitute, and in Bulika, poverty was treated as a crime. Deformity and illness were taxed heavily, and nothing pleased their Princess more than laws designed to let poverty serve wealth. Finally, I fled, and no one pursued me beyond the threshold.

A burly figure sitting by the gate, chewing on a lump of bread, picked up a stone to hurl after me. In his clumsy zeal, however, he tossed the bread instead. I snatched it up; he dared not follow to retrieve it, for beyond the walls, everyone was a coward. I retreated a few hundred yards, collapsed into the grass, devoured the bread, and fell into a deep sleep. The hot sun restored my strength.

Night fell, and I awoke. The moon descended with a friendly gaze, reminding me of the companion that watched over me during my first terrifying night in this strange world. Its light was brilliant. A cold wind drifted from the gate, carrying a foul odor, yet it failed to chill me, for the sun's warmth still lingered in my bones. I slipped back into the city, joining the few souls crouching in corners to escape the biting blast.

Walking slowly down a long, narrow street, a huge white shape bounded across my path, flashing briefly in the moonlight before vanishing. Eager to catch a glimpse again, I turned down the next opening. It was a cramped lane, barely wide enough for passage, leading into a broader avenue. The moment I entered, I spotted the creature on the opposite side, lurking in the shadows. It was following me like a dog, mimicking a human figure. Every few moments, it glanced backward, but it said nothing and made no attempt to shoo away its pursuer.

Where it crossed a patch of moonlight, I realized it cast no shadow. It was itself a flat, two-dimensional shadow, yet an opaque one. It didn't just darken objects behind it; it rendered them invisible. Within the shade, it was darker than the darkness itself; in the moonlight, it appeared as if it had wrapped its own shadow around its form, leaving not a hint of gray beneath or beside it. Following closely at its heels was a leopardess, appearing as the white counterpoint to its blackness. As the pair moved from shadow into moonlight, the shadow-figure deepened into absolute void, while the beast flared into radiant brilliance.

I walked abreast of them on the opposite side, my bare feet clicking on the flat stones. The leopardess never turned its head or twitched an ear. The shadow-creature seemed to glance at me; for a split second, I lost its profile, seeing only a sharp, vertical line. In that instant, the wind caught me, blowing straight through my body. I shuddered from head to toe, and my heart hammered against my ribs like a pebble rattling in a child's toy.

I veered off the main thoroughfare, seeking refuge within the shadow of a crumbling stone archway. As I stepped inside, halting to gaze out into the alley bathed in silver moonlight, a figure emerged from the darkness behind me. A woman, her movements fluid yet frantic, skidded to a halt beside me. Her breath hitched, visible in the cool air, as she peered nervously back toward the entrance.

Seconds stretched into an eternity of silence until the sound of heavy, padded paws shattered the quiet. A massive leopard, its coat a stark canvas of white marbled with dark rosettes, surged through the archway like a living storm. The woman screamed, collapsing against my leg. Instinctively, I wrapped my arm around her trembling form.

"If that beast comes closer," I promised, my voice steady despite the adrenaline, "I will strike it down. But you must run."

"Please!" she whispered, her eyes wide with terror. "Have you encountered this before?"

"I have seen it several times," she confessed, shaking violently. "It belongs to the Princess. If you lived among us, you would recognize her mark."

"I am a stranger," I admitted. "Yet, is she permitted to roam freely?"

"She is caged," she explained, her voice dropping to a hushed tone, "her jaws bound by iron, her paws encased in gloves of thick crocodile hide. Chains drag behind her. Yet, she escapes often. She hunts children, drinking their blood. Fortunately, Bulika has very few mothers left."

She described a society frozen in time. Her people spent their days digging in subterranean cellars for precious stones, yet they possessed nothing else. Everything else—clothing, food, tools—was crafted for them in distant lands.

"Why do they not labor?" I asked.

"Work is a disgrace," she replied with a shrug that betrayed her own confusion. "Everyone in Bulika knows this truth."

"Poor people must exist somewhere," I countered.

"And when the mines are empty? When every gem is sold?"

"There are infinite treasures beneath our soil," she insisted confidently. "That day will never arrive."

"No foreigner dares approach," she declared, her chin lifting. "They tremble in fear of our Princess. She alone ensures our safety, our freedom, and our riches!"

"A stranger defiles the city," she stated flatly.

"How does that happen?"

"Because we are older, nobler, and superior to any other nation," she explained. "Therefore, we expel all strangers before the sun sets."

"So, how can you bring me into your home?"

"I shall make an exception for you," she said quickly.

"You mentioned no sanctuary for travelers in your city?"

"If such a place existed, it would be demolished, and its owner burned," she replied with a sneer. "How can purity be maintained unless the lowly are kept at a proper distance? Dignity is a fragile thing!"

She went on to describe a Princess who had ruled for millennia, wielding dominion over the air, the water, the earth, and perhaps even the fire itself. She was a capricious deity, answerable to no one, capable of doing exactly as she pleased.

Finally, she deemed it safe to proceed. We navigated a labyrinth of narrow alleys and winding passages, emerging onto a broader street flanked by towering, silent buildings. We ascended a steep, narrow staircase leading to her residence. No other soul stirred in the dead of night.

Halfway up the stairs, however, the woman froze. Fear seized her again. With a sudden burst of speed, she scrambled up the remaining steps, leaving me gasping for breath. Exhausted and unwilling to let my presence further pollute the sanctity of Bulika, I accepted the grim reality. I collapsed into the scant shelter offered by the wall, weary and alone.

Below the stairs, the street stretched out under a pale, indifferent moon, whipped by a wind that felt sickening and unwelcoming. Yet, within my small retreat, the air remained still. Just as I was settling into exhaustion, my eyes snapped open. Perched atop the highest step was the creature I had glimpsed trailing the Shadow—a being of shimmering, living light.

The instant our gazes met, she recoiled. With a fluid, unnatural grace, she reversed her stance, tail leading, and scrambled backward until she vanished down the flight of stairs. I rushed after her, scanning the dark thoroughfare, but she was gone. Defeated, I returned to my hard cot. Two predators now haunted this city: one marked with spots, the other pure white. Though I held little value in Bulika, the life of a child outweighed my own precarious existence. I resolved to guard the door until dawn.

Soon, the latch clicked—a slow, deliberate movement. Rising silently, I slipped inside. Standing there was not the woman I had aided, but the veiled figure from the desert. She said nothing, guiding me into an empty, stone-floored chamber and gesturing to a rug. Wrapped in its coarse weave, I lay down as she sealed the inner door. Outside, the outer door swung shut, leaving me alone in the silver gloom.

From a side room emerged the white leopardess, clutching a newborn infant in her jaws, cradling it as if it were her own cub.

I tackled her, forcing her to release the baby. The infant hit the stone floor with a pitiful wail. The veiled woman materialized, stepping over our tangled forms in the narrow passageway. She scooped up the child and vanished into the darkness. Moments later, she returned, lifting me from the beast's grasp and shoving me out the door. The leopardess trailed at my heels.

Instinct pulled me back. Instantly, she froze, resuming her position behind the Shadow. I advanced again; she attacked again, her eyes blazing like captured emeralds. Angry at my own foolishness, I turned away. I had wasted hours here. Night or day, I would march straight to the palace. High above the city's beating heart, surrounded by layers of defense, it loomed like a fortress rather than a residence. Its walls were crumbling, neglected for centuries, rendering them useless against time itself. Great gates stood open across a rocky chasm, though the water that once filled the moat was long dried. Silence hung so thick it seemed to seep into the stones, making even the moonlight feel uncomfortably awake.

To enter as a thief or to break the oppressive silence—I chose neither. Instead, I wandered the perimeter like an outcast dog until I found a small alcove with a stone bench. Seeking shelter from the biting wind, I collapsed, and despite the chill, sleep claimed me instantly.

Something leaped onto me, a rough tongue lapping at my face. "The white leopardess," I realized. "She comes to drink my blood—and surely I cannot afford to resist!" I lay still, bracing for agony. Conviction washed over me: her intent was kind. I rolled slightly, draped an arm over her, and surrendered to deep unconsciousness.

When I awoke, I imagined lying in a soft, familiar bed. Was I finally home? The scents of a beloved garden flooded my senses. Rubbing my eyes, I looked out. I was not in a bed, but on bare stone in the heart of a hostile city.

Had I truly slept with a leopardess, or was it a dream? She had just departed, for the lingering warmth of her body still clung to me. Leaving the alcove, I carried a hope both fragile and fierce. One truth remained absolute: I must find the princess. If I possessed any power over her, it stemmed from the debt she owed me—the life I had saved, paid for with my own vitality. That realization gave me the courage to face whatever awaited me.

Tracing a circular path around the fortress, I returned to the open gates, crossed the deep, ravine-like moat, and emerged onto a vast courtyard paved with stone. "I do not know the woman's name."

When consciousness returned, night had fallen. A single lamp hung from the ceiling, casting a soft, ethereal glow across the chamber. A heavy, dreamlike lethargy wrapped around me, lifting me far from solid ground and into a twilight sea. For a fleeting moment, existence was pure pleasure, devoid of pain. But then, a sudden, searing lance of agony pierced my heart—a mortal sting that made the previous bliss seem like a distant memory. It struck again, sharper, more sickening. I tried to raise my hand to clutch my chest, but something held me fast. Paralysis seized my entire body. An evil force pinned me down, hateful and immovable. My will screamed in protest, futile and broken. Forced into passivity, I waited.

Then, a gentle hand cradled my face, pressing my head deeper into the pillow, while a heavy weight settled across my torso. My breathing eased as the pressure lifted from my lungs. Opening my eyes, I found the princess standing over me, gazing out into the room with the vacant stare of a dreamer. Her great eyes were clear and unnervingly calm, her lips curved in a expression of satisfied passion. She wiped a smear of red from her mouth, locking eyes with me. Before I could react, she brought a handkerchief down hard across my face. It felt like a blade slicing through my vision, plunging me into blindness for several seconds.

A dull, heavy thud echoed nearby—the sound of a large, soft-padded beast landing after a short leap. Blinded, I heard the swish of a long tail disappearing through a partially open door. Instinct took over; I lunged forward. The creature was gone. I sprinted down the stairs and burst into the alabaster hall. The moon hung high outside, bathing the interior in a pale, ghostly light. The princess was nowhere to be seen. Panic flared within me. In her presence, I might survive; without her, I was prey. She treated me with favor only to make my consumption easier. My eyes remained defiant, refusing to close, knowing that if I did, she would strike again. Unable to see her, I felt her omnipresence, lurking in every shadow, perhaps waiting in some hidden corner of my own slumber. Safety existed only under her gaze.

Stepping outside, absolute darkness swallowed the world. Blindfolded by the void, I groped blindly until my fingers brushed a curtain. Pushing it aside, I entered a vast, black hall. Here, visibility defied logic. The walls, floor, and ceiling were draped in an infinite blackness, darker than any moonless night. Yet, my eyes strained to distinguish figures within the gloom. Shapes emerged vaguely from the mass—interpenetrating, dividing, and surrounding one another. My vision refused to stabilize. Pressing my eyeballs, staring harder, yielded nothing but a blur of blackness mingling with indistinct forms. Silence and undefined motion dominated the space, a dim, chaotic dance punctuated by recurring glimpses of familiar horrors: a woman with luminous eyes set in a skull, an armored rider atop a skeletal steed, grotesque burrowing phantoms. There was no order, no rhythm, only confusion. Solid shapes shifted color, accompanied by a swarm of independent shadows dancing to their own erratic wills.

I scanned the kaleidoscope of terror for the princess, but she was absent. No sign of her presence marred the wild shifting scene. Ignored completely as I wandered aimlessly, searching for her, despair began to take root. Turning away, I clung to a wall, using it as a guide, until I reached a curtained opening leading to a vestibule. Moonlight filtered weakly here, illuminating the cage of the leopardess. Inside, a desperate, silent struggle raged between two vastly different forms—human and beast. Their bodies and limbs tangled in a writhing embrace. The fight had lasted mere seconds when the leopardess suddenly burst from the cage, walking calmly toward the open door.

Hastening after her, I glanced back. The leopardess remained in the cage, crouching motionless as before. High in the sky, the moon shone bright and clear. A bodiless shadow walked through the trees toward the gate, followed closely by the leopardess, her tail swinging lazily. I trailed them silently, keeping my distance. Neither paused nor looked back. We passed through the open gate, descending toward the sleeping city, bathed in the stillness of the moonlight. The lunar face seemed frozen in anticipation.

The shadow moved directly to the staircase where I had lain the night prior. He ascended without hesitation, and the leopardess followed. I quickened my pace, but moments later, a cry of sheer horror tore through the air. Something soft and heavy crashed between me and the stairs. At my feet lay a body, frightfully blackened and crushed, yet unmistakably the woman who had guided me home and locked me out. Petrified, I watched as the spotted leopardess bounded down the remaining steps, a baby clamped firmly in her jaws.

I darted forward to snatch the infant before she could turn at the bottom of the stairs. In that same instant, from behind me, the white leopardess erupted through the moonlight like a bar of glowing silver. She seized the spotted leopardess by the neck. The spotted beast dropped the child; I scrambled to grab it, holding it tight as I watched the ensuing duel.

It was a terrifying spectacle. One fought, then the other gained the upper hand. Both were so focused that the only sounds were low growls, whimpering cries, and snarls of hatred, interspersed with the frantic scuttling of claws as they dragged and pushed for purchase on the pavement. The spotted leopardess was larger, and my heart ached for her friend. However, I quickly realized that while equal in strength and agility, the white leopardess possessed superior endurance. She never loosened her grip on the other's neck.

Finally, a howl of agony escaped the spotted leopardess's throat. It escalated rapidly, transforming into the long, drawn-out crescendo of a woman's final scream. The white leopardess released her jaws. The spotted beast recoiled, rising onto her hind legs. Standing erect in the moonlight was the princess. A swirling vortex of shadows raced over her pristine white fur—the leopard spots crowded, hurried, and fled toward the safety of her eyes, vanishing as they merged with her gaze. The last few stragglers, overtaken by speed, blended into the cloud of her flowing hair, leaving her radiant as the moon itself after a legion of tiny vapors has been scattered by the wind. Save for a thin trickle of blood running down the white column of her throat from the wounds of her adversary's teeth, she was perfect.

She turned, taking a few steps with the eerie, multi-legged gait of Hecate, then fell, instantly covered anew in spots. Covered, she broke into a long, stretching gallop and fled. The white leopardess turned as well, springing upon me. She tore my arms apart, snatched the falling baby, and launched herself down the street toward the gate, carrying our only hope into the night.

I turned and trailed the spotted leopardess, catching only a fleeting glimpse of her as she tore upward over the brow of the hill toward the palace gates. By the time I reached the entrance hall, the princess was just draping the discarded robe around her shoulders. The blood had already ceased to flow from her wounds, drying instantly in the chill wind of her escape.

When she saw me, a flash of irritation crossed her face, and she turned her head away. Then, forcing a smile, she looked back at me. "I've met with a small accident!" she exclaimed. "Hearing that the cat-woman was back in the city, I went down to drive her away. But she had one of those horrid creatures with her—it leaped upon me, driving its claws into my neck before I could strike!"

She shivered violently. I couldn't help but feel pity, even though I knew she was lying. Her wounds were genuine, and the pallor of her face reminded me too sharply of how she had appeared in the cave. My own heart began to reproach me for having allowed her to fight alone, and I suppose my expression betrayed the compassion rising within me.

"Child of folly!" she said, offering another strained smile. "--Not crying, surely?—Wait for me here. I'm going into the Black Hall for a moment. I need you to fetch something for these scratches."

But I followed her closely. Out of my sight, I feared her.

The instant the princess stepped inside, a buzzing sound filled the air, like the murmur of countless low voices. One section after another, the vast assembly began to shift and illuminate, as if struck by a traveling ray of light. Groups would flare brightly for a moment, then sink back into the general gloom, while another part of the crowd grew momentarily luminous.

Some of the scenes unfolding during these flashes were familiar to me; I had been there, or watched from afar, along with the princess. Now, present with every event, I saw her role in each.

In the Skull-Hall, where the headless dancers trod the grass, there she was, peering in through the doorway! In the Evil Wood, where the battle raged, there she was, urging the conflict forward! Yet throughout it all, I remained close behind her, watching her stand motionless, her head bowed against her chest.

The confused murmur continued—the chaotic commotion of colors and shapes—as the shifting ray moved on. Finally, it settled on the hollow in the heath. There, the princess walked up and down, vainly trying to wrap the vapor around herself.

Then, for the first time, I was truly startled. The old librarian approached her, standing for a moment regarding her figure. Suddenly, she collapsed. Her limbs abandoned her, fleeing from her form; her body simply vanished.

A wild shriek rang through the echoing chamber. With the fall of her *eidolon*, the princess herself—who had until that moment stood like a statue before me—fell heavily to the ground and lay still.

I turned immediately and ran outside. Not again would I seek to restore her!

As I trembled beside the cage, I realized with a jolt that within the black ellipsoid, I had been inhabiting the mind of the princess. I saw the tail of the leopardess quiver once more.

While I struggled to compose myself, I heard the princess's voice beside me. "Come now," she said. "I will tell you what I want you to do for me."

She led the way into the courtyard. I followed in a daze, compliant and numb. The moon hung near the zenith, its silver radiance seeming brighter than the gold of the absent sun. She guided me through the trees to the tallest of them, the solitary specimen in the center. It was unlike the others; its branches drew their tips together at the apex, forming a cluster that resembled a fir-cone when viewed from below.

The princess stood close beneath it, gazing upward. "On the summit of that tree grows a tiny blossom," she murmured, as if speaking to herself, "which would heal my scratches at once! I could become a dove for a moment and fetch it... but I see a little snake coiled in the leaves. Its bite would be far worse to a dove than a tiger's bite would be to me! How I hate that cat-woman!"

Turning quickly to me, she offered one of her sweetest smiles. "Can you climb?"

The smile vanished instantly with the question, replaced by a look of profound sadness and suffering. I should have left her to endure her pain, but the way she pressed her hand to her wounded neck pierced my heart.

I studied the tree. Running up the trunk were projections resembling the remnants of fallen palm leaves. "I can climb that tree," I answered.

"Not with bare feet!" she retorted.

In my haste to chase the leopardess earlier, I had forgotten my sandals in my room.

"It does not matter," I said. "I have long gone barefoot!"

Again, I looked up the trunk, my gaze wandering until it was lost among the high branches. The moonlight shimmered like silvery foam across the rugged bark, and a gentle breeze rustled through the treetops with a soft, murmuring sound, like water falling gently into water.

I stepped forward to begin my ascent, but the princess stopped me.

"I cannot let you attempt this with your feet bare!" she insisted. "A fall from that height would kill you!"

"So would a bite from the snake!" I replied—I must admit, I did not believe there was actually a snake.

"It would not hurt *you*!" she countered. "--Wait a moment."

She tore from her garment the two wide borders that met in the front. Kneeling on one knee, she made me place my left foot, then my right, onto the other strip, binding them tightly about my ankles with the thick, embroidered fabric.

"You've left the ends hanging, Princess!" I noted.

"I have nothing to cut them with," she replied calmly. "But they are not long enough to get entangled."

I turned to the tree and began to climb.

Now, in Bulika, the cold after sunset was rarely severe—not as much as in other regions, especially near the sexton's cottage. Yet, as I climbed a few feet higher, I began to feel a sharp chill. I grew colder with every ascent, becoming frigid entirely once I reached the upper branches.

Then came the shivers. I felt as though I had lost my hands and feet. There was hardly any wind, and the branches did not sway, yet as I neared the summit, a peculiar unsteadiness seized me. Every branch I touched or gripped seemed poised to give way.

When my head finally rose above the canopy into the open moonlight, searching for the blossom, I was instantly drenched from head to toe. The next moment, as if plunged into a violent storm, I was flung about wildly, feeling myself sink. Tossed up and down, thrown this way and that, rolling over and over, checked and spun again, I sank lower and lower. Gasping, gurgling, choking, I finally crashed onto a solid bottom.

"I told you so!" a voice croaked in my ear.

I wiped the moisture from my eyes and caught sight of the raven perched on the rim of a massive stone basin. The pale, cold light of dawn gleamed off his glossy feathers as he looked down upon me with calm detachment. I lay submerged in the water of the great fountain my father had built in the center of the lawn, propped up on my elbows, lifting my face just enough to meet his gaze. Above us, high in the sky, the thick, steel-like stalk of the fountain's mechanism shot upward, erupting in a torrent that soared a hundred feet before blooming into a crown of white foam.

Stinging from the chill of the bird's earlier words—"You told me nothing!"—I retorted, "I instructed you to do nothing anyone you distrusted asked you to do!"

"Tut! How was a mortal expected to remember that?"

"You will not forget the consequences of forgetting it," Mr. Raven replied. He leaned over the marble edge of the basin and extended a clawed hand toward me. I grasped it, and instantly, I was standing beside him on the dry grass, dripping and shivering.

"You must change your clothes immediately," he commanded. "A wetting matters little regarding your origins—though currently, such an accident is rare; here, it brings mere inconvenience."

He resumed his form as a raven, striding with dignified steps toward the house, whose doors stood wide open.

"I haven't much to change," I laughed, tossing aside my robe after climbing the tree. "It has been ages since I molted a feather!"

The raven chuckled. "In the house, no one seems awake."

I retreated to my chamber, donned a dressing gown, and descended to the library. As I crossed the threshold, the librarian emerged from a closet. Without a word, I collapsed onto a nearby couch. Mr. Raven pulled a chair close and sat beside me. For a moment, neither of us spoke.

Finally, I broke the silence. "What does it all mean?"

"A pertinent inquiry," he answered. "No one truly understands anything; a person can only grasp what a thing signifies. Whether they do depends entirely on how they utilize it."

"I have utilized nothing yet!"

"Not significantly, but you possess the awareness of that fact, which counts for something. Most individuals spend a lifetime realizing they have learned nothing and accomplished less. At least, you have harbored the desire to be useful."

"I wished to assist the children—the precious Little Ones, specifically."

"I am aware of your intent—and you chose the wrong path."

"I didn't know the correct path."

"That is accurate, yet you are culpable for failing to discover it."

"I am prepared to accept whatever truth you reveal, provided I comprehend its meaning."

"If you had accepted our invitation, you would have known the right way. When a man refuses to act within his own sphere, he must travel far to locate his work."

"Indeed, I have traveled far and arrived nowhere, for I still have not found my work! I abandoned the children to learn how to serve them, only to realize the peril they faced."

"When you were with them, you were exactly where you could aid them. By leaving, you sought your work elsewhere. It requires wisdom to know when to depart; folly lies in rushing back too soon."

"Do you imply, sir, that I could have aided the Little Ones by remaining with them?"

"Could you instruct them by abandoning them?"

"No. But how was I to begin teaching? Furthermore, they were far ahead of me!"

"That is true. Yet, you were not a ruler against which to measure them! If they possessed your knowledge—or rather, what you might have acquired—they would be ahead of you, utterly out of sight. However, you observed they were not growing, or growing so sluggishly that the concept of growth had not yet formed in their minds. They were even fearful of growing! You had never witnessed children simply *remain* children."

"But surely I lacked the power to force them to grow!"

"You could have eliminated certain obstacles preventing their growth!"

"What obstacles? I am unaware of them. I suspected perhaps it was a lack of water!"

"Certainly it was! They had no tears to shed!"

"I would have gladly prevented them from needing to weep!"

"No doubt you would—that is the hallmark of all foolish philanthropy! Why, Mr. Vane, were it not for the necessity of weeping, your world would never have been deemed worthy of salvation! You admitted you considered it might be thirst they felt; why did you not dig them a few wells?"

"That never occurred to me!"

"Not even when the sounds of subterranean waters reached your ears?"

"I believe it happened once. But I feared the giants for their sake. That was precisely why I endured so much abuse from those beasts myself!"

"In truth, you nearly conditioned these noble little creatures to dread the stupid Bags! While they fed, comforted, and worshipped you, you simultaneously allowed yourself to become a slave to bestial men. You offered the darlings a seeming coward in place of a hero! A graver injustice you could scarcely inflict. They surrendered their hearts; you owed them your soul! By now, you should have compelled the Bags to become woodcutters and water-carriers for the Little Ones!"

"I fear your assessment holds merit, Mr. Raven! Yet, I genuinely believed that imparting further knowledge might harm them, rendering them less innocent, less lovely."

"They afforded you no cause to harbor such fears!"

"Is not a modicum of knowledge a perilous thing?"

"That is one of the cherished falsehoods of your world! Is humanity's greatest knowledge merely 'a little'? Or because it is vast, does that render it dangerous? The notion that knowledge is inherently grand makes any degree of understanding seem more hazardous than any amount of ignorance. Knowing everything would not constitute greatness."

"At least, I acted out of love for them, not cowardice!"

"Granted. Nevertheless, you ought to have served the Little Ones, not the giants! You should have provided the Little Ones with water; then, they would swiftly have taught the giants their true station. Meanwhile, you could have ordered the giants to fell two-thirds of their coarse fruit trees to make space for the delicate ones! You squandered your opportunity with the Lovers, Mr. Vane. You speculated upon them instead of assisting them!"

Silence and shame settled heavy upon me. His words rang true: I had failed the Little Ones. Mr. Raven continued, his voice low but cutting. "You wronged them doubly. You harmed the foolish creatures themselves. For beings of such limited understanding, slavery would have been a step toward progress. A few harsh lessons, administered with a twig from their own trees, would have been worth a fortune to them."

"I didn't realize they were cowards!" I protested weakly.

"What relevance does that hold?" he countered sharply. "The man who justifies his actions upon another's cowardice is, in essence, a coward himself. Worse still, I fear consequences are brewing. By now, the Little Ones ought to possess the means to defend themselves from the Princess, let alone the Giants—they were always capable of such feats. As it stands, they mocked them! But now, due to your connection with her..."

"I loathe her!" I shouted, the emotion tearing from my chest.

"Have you told her of your hatred?"

Again, silence swallowed my reply.

"Not even to her have you remained loyal... But hush! We are being followed from the fountain, I suspect."

"No living soul did I witness," I whispered, scanning the shadows. "Except a shabby-looking cat that bolted into the bushes."

"It was a magnificent Persian," Mr. Raven corrected, his tone shifting to one of grim recognition. "Though soaked and ragged, appearing far worse than merely disreputable."

"What do you mean, Mr. Raven?" I cried, a fresh terror seizing my throat.

"There was a stunning blue Persian residing in the house, yet she fled at the mere sound of splashing water. Could she have been hunting the goldfish?"

"We shall find out," the librarian replied calmly. "I understand much about various breeds of felines, and there exists something within this room that will expose her nature, unless I am grievously mistaken."

He rose, moved to the door of the small closet, retrieved a mutilated volume, and sat back down beside me. I stared at the book in his grasp; miraculously, it was whole, intact and sound.

"Where is the other half?" I gasped.

"Embedded deep within my library," he answered simply.

I fell silent. Asking further questions would be akin to leaping into an abyss with no chance of return, and perhaps there was no time left.

"Listen," he commanded softly. "I intend to recite a stanza or two. There is someone present who, I imagine, will find little pleasure in hearing it."

He opened the vellum cover, turning a page or two. The parchment was yellowed with age, and one leaf bore a dark stain covering two-thirds of its surface. He slowly flipped this page as well, searching for a specific passage within what appeared to be a continuous poem. Somewhere near the midpoint, he began to read aloud. However, what follows is not the exact text he spoke, but rather the profound impression it left upon my mind.

The poem seemed written in a tongue I had never encountered before, yet I comprehended it perfectly, despite being unable to write the words or define their precise meanings without resorting to clumsy approximations. These fragments are the shapes those verses took as they passed through my consciousness once more:

*"But if I found a man who could believe / In what he saw not, felt not, and yet knew, / From him I'd take substance, and receive / Firmness and form related to touch and view; / Then should I clothe myself in the likeness true / Of that idea where his soul did cleave!"*

He turned the leaf and read again:

*"In me was every woman. I possessed power / Over the soul of every living man, / Such as no woman ever held in dower— / Could do what no woman ever could, or can; / All women, I, the woman, still outran, / Outsoared, outsank, outreigned, in hall or bower."*

*"For I, though he neither saw nor heard me, / Nor with his hand could touch the finger of mine, / Although not once my breath had stirred a hair / Upon his head, could bind his brain and spine / With roots so deep that Death could not untwine— / Or Life, though hope were evermore deferred."*

Once more he paused, turned the page, and began anew:

*"For by his side I lay, a bodiless thing; / I breathed not, saw not, felt not, only thought, / And made him love me—with a hungering / After he knew not what—if it was aught / Or but a nameless something wrought by him / Out of himself; for I did sing 'A song / That had no sound' into his soul; / I lay a heartless thing against his heart, / Giving him nothing where he gave his whole / Being to clothe me human, every part: / That I at last into his sense might dart, / Thus first into his living mind I stole."*

*"Ah, who was ever conquering Love but I! / Who else did ever throne in heart of man! / To visible being, with a gladsome cry, / Waking, life's tremor through me throbbing ran!"*

Suddenly, a strange, repulsive feline wail echoed from somewhere in the room. I jolted upright on my elbows, staring frantically around, seeing nothing. Mr. Raven flipped several leaves and continued reading:

*"Sudden I woke, nor knew the ghastly fear / That held me—not like a serpent coiled about, / But like a vapor moist, corrupt, and drear, / Filling heart, soul, and breast and brain throughout; / My being lay motionless in sickening doubt, / Nor dared to ask how came the horror here."*

*"My past entire I knew, but not my now; / I understood neither what I was, nor where; / I knew what I had been: still on my brow / I felt the touch of what no more was there! / I was a fainting, dead, yet live Despair; / A life that flouted life with mop and mow!"*

*"That I was a queen I knew right well, / And sometimes wore a splendour on my head / Whose flashing even dead darkness could not quell— / The like on neck and arms and girdle-stead; / And men declared a light my closed eyes shed / That killed the diamond in its silver cell."*

The ugly cry of feline anguish pierced the air again. I looked once more, finding neither shape nor movement. Mr. Raven seemed to listen for a moment, then turned several pages and resumed:

*"Hideously wet, my hair of golden hue / Fouled my fair hands; to have it swiftly shorn / I'd given my rubies, all for me dug new— / No eyes had seen, and such no waist had worn! / For a draught of water from a drinking horn, / For one blue breath, I'd given my sapphires blue!"*

*"Nay, I had given my opals for a smock, / A peasant-maiden's garment, coarse and clean: / My shroud was rotting! Once I heard a cock / Lustily crow upon the hillock green / Over my coffin. Dulled by space between, / Came back an answer like a ghostly mock."*

The beastly wail rose once more. "I thought some foul entity was in the room!" the librarian exclaimed, casting a wary glance around, but instantly he turned the leaves and continued:

*"For I had bathed in milk and honey-dew, / In rain from roses shaken, ne'er touching earth, / And ointed me with nard of amber hue; / Never had spot marked me from my birth, / Or mole, or scar of hurt, or fret of dearth; / Never one hair superfluous on me grew."*

*"Fleeing cold whiteness, I would sit alone— / Not in the sun—I feared his bronzing light, / But in his radiance back around me thrown / By fulgent mirrors tempering his might; / Thus bathing in a moon-bath not too bright, / My skin I tinted slow to ivory tone."*

*"But now, all round was dark, dark all within! / My eyes not even gave out a phantom-flash; / My fingers sank in pulp through pulpy skin; / My body lay death-weltered in a mash / Of slimy horrors—"*

With a fearsome yell, a massive white cat erupted from somewhere in the shadows. Its clammy fur hung in clumps, its tail thick as a cable, its eyes flashing green like chrysoprase, and its distended claws tangled as it floundered across the carpet, rushing straight for the fireplace. Instinctively, the librarian hurled the manuscript between the creature and the hearth. It crouched instantly, its eyes locked onto the book. Yet his voice continued as if reading uninterrupted, and his gaze seemed fixed solely upon the pages:

*"Ah, the two worlds! So strangely are they one, / And yet so measurelessly wide apart! / Oh, had I lived the bodiless alone / And from defiling sense held safe my heart, / Then had I escaped the canker and the smart, / Escaped life-in-death, escaped misery's endless moan!"*

At these words, a howling, a prolonged scream of agony burst forth from the cat, forcing us both to cover our ears. When the noise finally

"We must be vigilant," he said, his voice tight with urgency. "Or she will outwit us once more. She would deceive even the Elect!"

"How do we keep guard?" I asked.

"Every possible way," he answered. "She fears her child, and thus she hates her. She is in this house on a mission to destroy her. To her, the birth of children signifies the death of the parents; every new generation is the enemy of the last. Her daughter appears to her as an open channel through which her immortality—which she believes is inherent to herself—is rapidly draining away. To fill this void, she has pursued the girl with absolute enmity since her birth. Yet, the result of her schemes has been the appearance of a colony of children in the realm she claims as her own, a group led and protected by that very daughter.

"My Eve longed for the child and would have become a mother to her firstborn, but we were then unfit to raise her. She was carried into the wilderness, and for ages we knew nothing of her fate. However, she was divinely fostered, playing with young angels, knowing no care until she discovered a baby in the woods, awakening the mother within her. Since then, she has found many children, one by one, and that maternal heart remains unfulfilled. Her family is her total charge; she has never mothered children with greater devotion. Her authority over them is absolute, though unknown to herself, surfacing only in watchfulness and service. She has forgotten the time she lived without them, believing she emerged from the wood as the first of the family."

"You have saved the lives of her and theirs," he continued. "Therefore, your life now belongs to them. The princess was on her way to destroy them, but as she crossed that stream, vengeance overtook her. She would have perished had you not intervened. You did save her, and had she dared cross the stream again, she would have been ravaging the Little Ones. Yet, there remained another path to the blessed colony through the world of three dimensions. By slaying her former physical form, she had excluded herself from returning unless in direct contact with someone belonging to that reality. You provided the opportunity; never in her long years had she possessed one like it. Her hand rested lightly upon one or both of your muffled feet with every step you took climbing. In that small chamber, she watches now, waiting for the slightest chance to escape."

"She cannot know anything about the door!" I insisted, though my heart lacked the confidence of my words. "She certainly doesn't know how to open it!"

"Hush!" the librarian whispered, raising a hand. "She can hear through anything. You must go at once to my wife's cottage. I will stay here to guard her."

"Let me go to the Little Ones!" I cried.

"Beware, Mr. Vane. Go to my wife and do as she commands."

His counsel offered no comfort. Why hasten to face endless delay? If not to protect the children, why move at all? Even then, I found myself trusting him only enough to demand answers rather than obedience.

"First, Mr. Raven," I pressed, "tell me why you chose *this* place to imprison her. The night I fled your house, I ran straight into that closet!"

"The closet is neither closer to our cottage nor farther from it than any other location."

"But," I countered, struggling to grasp logic where none existed, "if that is true, how do you remove a whole book from that same door when I perceive only a fragment? The other part, you just told me, protrudes into your library. When you return it to the shelf, will it not again protrude into the closet? Do the two locations, where parts of the same volume coexist simultaneously, not lie adjacent? Or can one part of a book exist in space while the other exists nowhere?"

"I regret I cannot explain this to you," he replied. "There is simply no capacity within you to comprehend it. It is not merely that the phenomenon is inexplicable to you; its very nature is inapprehensible. Indeed, I only partially grasp it myself. Yet, you constantly experience things you do not—and cannot—understand. You think you understand them, but your comprehension is merely familiarity, preventing surprise. You accept them not because you grasp them, but because they are undeniable facts with unavoidable relations to you. The truth is, no man truly understands anything. When he realizes he does not understand, that is his first stumbling step—not toward understanding, but toward the potential to eventually understand. Regarding matters like these, you are unused, so you falsely assume you understand. Neither I nor anyone else can help you understand here, but perhaps I can assist you in learning to believe."

He moved to the closet door, gave a low whistle, and stood still, listening. Moments later, I heard—or seemed to hear—a soft whirring of wings. Looking up, I saw a white dove perch briefly atop the shelves above the portrait, then drop to Mr. Raven's shoulder, resting its head against his cheek. I could discern they were conversing only by the subtle movements of their heads; silence filled the air between them. Just as dawn began to bleed into the sky, I noticed the skeleton-princess standing in the doorway. Her eyes were open and glowing, a terrifying black mark staining her side. The next morning, as we departed, we spotted two skeletons moving through a nearby thicket. The Little Ones broke formation and ran to greet them. Seeing the skeletons gather handfuls of herbs—presumably to rub against their bones, for how else could such rudimentary systems find nourishment—the Little Ones carefully inspected the herbs, collected similar ones, and filled the empty hands the skeletons extended. They bid farewell, promising to visit again, and continued their journey, remarking that they had never known such kind people lived in the forest.

Upon reaching the nest-village, I stayed one night to oversee their resettlement. Lona still resembled the newly dead, and there was no rush. The princess had eaten nothing, her eyes remaining shut. Our journey included one terrible night—the third spent in the desert between the two branches of the dead river. Within moments, the Little Ones began to descend from the high foliage, buzzing with news. They claimed that scaling the apex of a tree—taller than any other—they had spotted something strange on the flank of a lone mountain. This mountain, they insisted, climbed endlessly toward the heavens until the sky itself grew dense enough to cap it, knocking its summit clean off.

"It might be a city," they declared, "yet it bears no resemblance to Bulika."

I ascended to verify their tale. What met my gaze was a vast metropolis rising into the azure clouds, a place where mountain and sky, rock and dwelling, dissolved into indistinguishable unity. Clouds, peaks, and palaces merged in a chaotic symphony of fractured shadows and blinding light. Descending once more, I joined the Little Ones, and together we hurried forward, our pace quickening. Their spirits lifted further with every step; they led the way, laughing and never glancing back.

The river transformed before our eyes, growing increasingly magnificent until I realized I had never witnessed true water before. Nothing in this world merely *resembled* it; it *was* perfection. Soon, the city emerged clearly against the blue backdrop, nestled among the clouds. Yet, darker clouds swirled around a towering spire—or perhaps a sheer cliff face—that pierced the skyline near the mountain's crest. These clouds shifted in hues of gray, charcoal, and violet, writhing in confused, contradictory motions, tossing up a vaporous foam while swirling spots danced within them like miniature whirlpools.

Suddenly, a dazzling flash erupted, momentarily illuminating the Little Ones ahead. It was followed instantly by blinding darkness, yet through the gloom, their voices rang out, hushed with awe.

"Did you see?"
"I saw!"
"What did you see?"
"The most beautiful man!"
"I heard him speak!"
"I didn't. What did he say?"

A tiny, childish voice cut through—the voice of Luva.
"He said, 'All mine, little ones: come along!'"

I had witnessed the lightning but heard no words; Lona, however, perceived both sight and sound with the children. A second flash struck, and though my ears remained deaf, my eyes were suddenly opened. The great trembling light coalesced into faces of angels. They flared into visibility and then vanished. A third flash arrived, its substance and radiance distinctly human.

"My mother!" I cried out.
"I see many mothers!" Luva exclaimed.

Once more the cloud flashed, revealing a kaleidoscope of creatures: horses and elephants, lions and dogs—oh, such beasts! And such birds! Great birds whose single wings shimmered with every color of the sunset or rainbow; small birds whose feathers sparkled as if dusted with the precious stones of the entire earth; silvery cranes; scarlet flamingos; opal pigeons; peacocks resplendent in gold, green, and blue; jewel-toned hummingbirds; great-winged butterflies; and lithe, voluminous creeping things—all revealed in one celestial instant.

"I see that serpents grow into birds here, just as caterpillars once became butterflies!" Lona observed.
"I saw my white pony, the one that died when I was a child," I replied softly. "I needn't have mourned so deeply; I simply needed to wait."

There was no thunder, no clap, no rumble. Instead, a gentle rain began to fall, filling the air with a caressing coolness. We breathed deeply and took longer strides. The falling drops reflected the colors of all awakened earthly gems, and a magnificent rainbow arched over the city. The blue clouds thickened; the rain poured in torrents; the children exulted, running wild, making it difficult to keep them in view.

With a silent, radiant rush, the river surged onward, swelling to fill its smooth, soft, yielding channel. For instead of flowing over rock, shingle, or sand, it moved across lush grass carpeted with primroses, daisies, crocuses, narcissi, pimpernels, and anemones—a starry multitude, large and bright, glowing through the brilliant water. The river bore no turbidity from the rain, not even a hint of yellow or brown; the delicate mass shone with a pale, beryl-like gleam rising from its deep, dainty bed.

Drawing closer to the mountain, we saw the river originate directly from its peak, rushing in full volume through the city's main thoroughfare. It cascaded down to the gate via a staircase of deep, wide steps crafted from porphyry and serpentine stone, which extended all the way to the mountain's foot. Upon arrival, we found shallower steps on either bank leading up to the gate and along the ascending street.

Without pausing, the Little Ones sprinted straight up the stairs to the gate, which stood wide open. Outside, on the landing, sat the portress—a woman-angel of dark visage, resting her shadowed brow on her idle hand. The children rushed toward her, burying her in affectionate embraces. Before she could comprehend their presence, they had seized heaven by surprise and were already inside the city, still climbing the stairs beside the descending torrent.

A great angel, accompanied by a host of shining beings, came down to greet and receive them. Yet, the children merrily evaded everyone, continuing upward. Then, in a joyful dance, a group of woman-angels descended upon them, and in an instant, they were embraced and bound in heavenly arms. The radiant figures carried them away, and I lost sight of them forever.

"Aha!" proclaimed the mighty angel as he continued his descent to meet us, who were now nearly at the gate and close enough to hear his words. "This is excellent! These are soldiers ready to storm heaven itself! I hear reports of a horde of black bats on the frontiers; these will put an end to them swiftly!"

Noticing the horses and elephants scrambling up behind us, he added, "Escort those animals to the royal stables. Tend to them, then release them into the King's forest."

Turning to us, he offered a low bow with the sweetest smile. "Welcome home!"

Immediately, he turned and led the way higher. The scales of his armor flashed like fragments of lightning. No thought can adequately describe what I felt, thus welcomed by the officers of Heaven. Everything I desired, everything I did not yet know, was surely on its way to me!

We paused briefly at the gate, where the radiant river roared forth. I could not identify the origin of the stones forming the archway, but among them, I recognized the prototypes of every gem I had cherished on Earth—far more beautiful than their earthly counterparts, for these were living stones. In them, I perceived not merely the intent, but the creator; not just the idea, but the embodied artist, the active sender. Nothing in this realm was dead; nothing was merely an object; nothing existed without purpose or spirit.

We proceeded upward through the city and exited onto the upper level. There was no wall here, only a massive pile of broken rocks sloping upward like the moraine of an eternal glacier. Through gaps between the rocks, the river bubbled out. Perched atop these rocks, I could faintly discern what appeared to be three or four great steps of a staircase, vanishing into a cloud white as snow. Above the steps, using only my mind's eye, I envisioned a grand, ancient chair—the Throne of the Ancient of Days. From over, under, and between those steps issued the River of Life, fresh and newborn, ceaselessly and abundantly.

The great angel could guide us no further; those final rocks we must ascend alone. My heart pounding with hope and longing, I gripped Lona's hand tighter, and we began to climb. Soon, however, we released each other's hands to use our limbs fully in the laborious ascent of the colossal stones.

At last, we approached the cloud hanging down the steps like the hem of a garment. Passing through its fringed edge, we entered its deep folds. A hand, warm and strong, grasped mine and pulled me to a small door secured with a golden lock. The door swung open; the hand released mine and gently pushed me through.

I turned quickly, expecting to see the angel, but instead, I saw the page of a large book closing behind me. Standing alone, I found myself in my own library.

I have not yet found Lona, though Mara remains close beside me, instructing me in endless layers of understanding. Is it possible that even this final awakening occurred within a dream? Am I still trapped in the chamber of death, suspended between slumber and reality, unripe for true waking? Or did I fail to surrender fully to sleep, rising prematurely before the hour was right? If this awakening was merely a dream, then it must have been a prophecy of a greater dawn to come—a sign that I was not deceived by a false vision. Even a shadow holds the promise of a brighter light at its core.

In moments of uncertainty, I cry out, "Could God Himself weave such exquisite realities?" And Hope replies, "From where then sprang your dream?" "From the depths of my darkness, emerging into the light of my conscious mind," I answer. "But how did you first descend into that darkness?" Hope asks. "My brain gave birth to it, and the fever coursing through my veins acted as its father."

"Speak rather," Hope suggests gently, "that your brain was the violin from which the music poured forth, and the fever the bow drawing the strings. Yet, who crafted the violin? Who guided the bow across those strings? Consider instead: who placed every songbird upon its branch in the Tree of Life, startling them one by one from their perches? Where did the fantasia originate? What life danced to its rhythm? Was it you, in the obscurity of your own unconsciousness, declaring, 'Let beauty exist; let truth appear,' and instantly bringing beauty into being while truth remained mere semblance?"

Man dreams and yearns; God broods, wills, and brings to life. When a man dreams his own dream, he is merely the plaything of his imagination; but when Another grants him the dream, that Other possesses the power to fulfill it. Since that day, I have never looked into a mirror again. The hand that returned me refused entry through that door once more. "For all the days of my allotted time, I shall wait until my transformation arrives."

Occasionally, gazing upon my books, they seem to tremble as if caught in a breeze, their solidity wavering as another world threatens to breach our reality. Sometimes, walking outdoors, the heavens and earth, the trees and grass, appear to shudder as though ready to dissolve; yet lo, they settle once more into their familiar forms. At other times, I hear faint whispers surrounding me, as if beloved ones speak of me, but whenever I strive to catch their words, silence falls heavy and absolute. I cannot tell if these sensations arise within my mind or invade it from without. I do not chase them; they arrive, and I release them.

Vague, indistinct memories—refusing any clear definition—often gaze upon me through the misted panes of the past, visible even in broad daylight. Yet I no longer dream. Perhaps, however, when I feel most awake, I am merely dreaming more deeply than ever. But when I finally awaken into that existence which, like a mother cradling her child, holds this life within its embrace, I shall truly know that I am awake, and doubt shall vanish forever. I wait. Whether sleeping or wide-eyed, I wait.
