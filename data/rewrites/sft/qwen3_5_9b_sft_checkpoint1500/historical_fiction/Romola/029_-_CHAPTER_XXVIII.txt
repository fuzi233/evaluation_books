Four days after the tumultuous arrival of the French, Romola set forth toward the dwelling of Piero di Cosimo on Via Gualfonda. The journey took her through arteries of the city thick with tension. On either side of the street, crowds gathered: French soldiers, arrogant and newly arrived, stared down at the citizens of Florence, while Florentines returned the gaze with a mixture of resentment and wary defiance. There was no warmth here, no mutual admiration. The invaders, unaccustomed to seeing themselves as anything less than the apex of European power, wore their superiority like armor. Yet the Florentines, having initially courted these "Most Christian" guests with feigned hospitality, now found their patience wearing thin. They resented the condescension, the "ogling" of their womenfolk, and the underlying message that Florence was merely a prize to be won.

Beneath the surface of polite ceremony lay a cold calculation. King Louis, having entered the city with lance in rest, spoke openly of leaving a viceroy behind and restoring the Medici family to power. To the Florentines, this was not the act of a humble servant of God, but of a conqueror treating a free city as a spoil. Though the official offense of Florence was limited to the unpopular policies of Piero de' Medici, the spirit of the republic refused to bow. This resolve was evident in the clandestine councils within the Old Palace and now manifested openly on the broad banners waving above the piazzas, mocking the insolence of the French. For a woman of high birth, the streets were treacherous terrain; yet Romola, veiled in deep black and accompanied by the faithful old Maso, moved with a quiet security.

Her destination was driven by a pressing need. A copy of her father's portrait, painted by Piero in the guise of Oedipus, remained unfinished. Piero was a man of erratic temperament; his dedication to a commission wavered with his whims. Sometimes, left without urgent pressure, he would abandon a canvas for months, hiding it away in a corner or chest until it seemed lost forever. Though Romola was a favorite of his and he often indulged her desires, she knew that affection alone could not guard against his capricious nature. Having promised completion weeks prior, he had likely forgotten the pledge. Romola's anxiety stemmed from a profound desire to preserve the memory of her blind father. She feared that without a physical likeness, his image would fade from her mind, leaving a void in her devotion. Love, she believed, required perfect loyalty; it could not rest content unless it achieved a completeness that included the faithful keeping of promises.

By special favor, she was permitted to enter Piero's studio without prior warning. Lifting the iron slide, she called out in a soft, flute-like voice, recalling the manner of the little maid who once summoned Titus. The response was immediate. When the door swung open, Piero explained his haste with a bluntness that offered no comfort.

"Ah, Madonna Romola, is it you?" he exclaimed, stepping back. "I thought my eggs were coming; I wanted them."

"I bring you something superior to hard-boiled eggs, Piero," Romola replied with a smile, removing her veil. From Maso, she retrieved a small basket brimming with cakes and *confetti*. "Maso has secured this for you."

Stepping past him into the interior, she continued gently, "I know you appreciate such treats when they arrive without trouble. Confess, do you?"

"Yes," Piero admitted, folding his arms and gazing down at the sweets as she displayed them. "They come to me as readily as the light does." His eyes then rose to meet hers, filled with a painter's acute appreciation as her heavy hood, pulled back by the weight of her veil, revealed her hair. "And they have arrived with the light today."

"But I understand why you are waiting for them," he observed, a knowing glint in his eye. "To keep your tongue from chiding me. Very well, step into the next chamber. I have altered the picture since you last saw it, though it remains incomplete. Be warned, however; I take great care never to make a promise I cannot keep."

"'Who promises and does not perform / Never sends his soul to heaven,'" he quoted, reciting the Tuscan proverb with a theatrical flair.

Beyond the threshold, the studio opened onto a wild, overgrown garden. Piero stood before an easel, working. However, he was not touching the portrait of Oedipus commissioned by Romola. That canvas stood propped against the far wall, neglected. As he reached to lift it, intending to move it into better light, he inadvertently obscured it and revealed another work hidden beneath—a fresh oil sketch of Tito.

Within recent days, Piero had added a startling detail to this smaller study. Because it hung recessed from the larger composition, the change had gone unnoticed until now. As he shifted the canvas to examine a specific area, the new element was fully exposed. Romola gasped, flushing with shock.

"That is Tito!"

Piero spun around, offering a silent shrug of vexation at his own lapse in organization. He was troubled by his forgetfulness.

Romola studied the sketch, her brow furrowed in puzzlement and alarm. "What a bizarre image! When did you paint this? What signifies it?"

"A mere whim," Piero said, removing his skull-cap and scratching the back of his neck with a gesture designed to mask deeper emotion. "I required a handsome countenance for this piece, and your husband's features were ideal."

Moving closer to the canvas, he bent down, presenting the back of the painting to Romola, and feigned a casual inspection before deciding to store it away as unworthy of display. Yet Romola, haunted by the memory of the chain-mail armor, struck by a terrifying coincidence linking Tito to images of dread, stepped forward.

"Do not hide it! Let me view it again. The figure with the rope around his neck... I saw him. I saw you approach him in the Duomo. What compelled you to include him alongside Tito?"

Seeing no path to evasion, Piero yielded a fraction of the truth. "It was pure chance. The man was fleeing, scrambling up the steps, and seized hold of your husband. He must have tripped. I happened to be present, saw the spectacle, and deemed the ragged old fellow a suitable subject. It is worthless, truly; merely a freakish daub." With an air of final dismissal, he pushed the sketch onto a high shelf. "Come, view the Oedipus."

In doing so, he had betrayed his caution. His eagerness to conceal the sketch had confirmed her deepest fears: there was indeed something unsettling, something detrimental to Tito's reputation, inherent in the scene's genesis. Stung by pride and delicacy, Romola recoiled from further inquiry. To question further might imply a suspicion she dared not harbor, lest it tarnish her husband's honor. In a voice barely above a whisper, she asked, "He was a strangely pitiful prisoner. Know you anything else of him?"

"No further knowledge," Piero replied quickly. "I merely guided him to the hospital. Observe now; the face of Oedipus is nearly complete. Tell me your thoughts."

Romola devoted herself entirely to the portrait, standing in a prolonged silence before it. Finally, she spoke. "Ah, you have accomplished what I desired. You have bestowed upon it the look of listening. My dear Piero..." Her eyes shimmered with gratitude. "...I am deeply thankful."

"Now there lies what I cannot endure in women," Piero muttered, turning impatiently and sweeping aside the clutter on the floor. "You pour forth emotions where none are needed. Why should you be grateful to me for a painting you paid for, especially when I have delayed it? If I paint, surely it is for my own pleasure and credit to execute it well, is it not? Must you thank a man for not being a rogue or a fool? 'Tis sufficient if he thanks Messer Domeneddio for granting him that distinction. Women imagine walls are held together with honey."

"You crusty Piero! I forgot how irritable you are," Romola laughed softly through her tears. Reaching into the basket, she offered him a particularly crisp sweetmeat.

Piero accepted it with the grace of a proverbial bear dreaming of pears; he genuinely relished the gift, though accustomed to masking his joys and sorrows behind a shaggy exterior. "It is excellent, Madonna Antigone," he remarked, reaching for another. He had subsisted solely on hard eggs for two weeks. Opposite him, Romola felt her mounting anxiety suspend momentarily, buoyed by the sight of this simple, naive delight.

"Adieu, Piero," she said eventually, placing the basket down. "I promise not to express thanks if you finalize the portrait swiftly and masterfully. I shall tell you then that you were bound to succeed for your own sake."

"Prithee," Piero said curtly, assisting her with practiced dexterity to wrap her mantle and veil securely around her form.

As soon as the door clicked shut behind her, a sigh escaped him. "Thank goodness she asked no further questions regarding that sketch," he murmured to himself. "I should be ill at ease if she surmised I considered her fine husband a fitting model for a coward. I played it light; she will not broach the matter again."
