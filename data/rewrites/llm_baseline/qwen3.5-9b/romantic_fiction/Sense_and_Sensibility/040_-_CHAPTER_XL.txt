"Well, Miss Dashwood," said Mrs. Jennings, flashing a sagacious smile the moment the gentleman had departed, "I needn't ask you what the Colonel has been saying to you. Though, upon my honor, I *tried* to stay out of earshot, I couldn't help catching enough to understand the nature of his business. And I assure you, I have never been happier in my life; I wish you joy of it with all my heart!"

"Thank you, ma'am," replied Elinor. "It *is* a matter of great joy to me, and I feel the goodness of Colonel Brandon most acutely. There are not many men who would act as he has. Few possess such a compassionate heart! I have never been more astonished in my life."

"Lord! My dear, you are very modest. I am not the least astonished in the world; indeed, I have often thought lately that nothing was more likely to happen."

"You judged from your knowledge of the Colonel's general benevolence; but at least you could not foresee that the opportunity would arise so very soon."

"Opportunity!" repeated Mrs. Jennings. "Oh! As to that, when a man has once made up his mind to such a thing, somehow or other he will soon find an opportunity. Well, my dear, I wish you joy of it again and again. If ever there was a happy couple in the world, I think I shall soon know exactly where to look for them."

"You mean to go to Delaford after them, I suppose?" said Elinor, with a faint smile.

"Aye, my dear, that I do, indeed. And as to the house being a bad one, I do not know what the Colonel would be at; it is as good a house as ever I saw."

"He spoke of its being out of repair."

"Well, and whose fault is that? Why doesn't he repair it? Who should do it but himself?"

Their conversation was interrupted by the servant entering to announce that the carriage was at the door. Mrs. Jennings immediately prepared to depart, remarking, "Well, my dear, I must be gone before I have had half my talk out. However, we may have it all over again in the evening, for we shall be quite alone. I do not ask you to come with me, for I dare say your mind is too full of the matter to care for company; and besides, you must long to tell your sister all about it."

Marianne had already left the room before the conversation began.

"Certainly, ma'am, I shall tell Marianne of it; but I shall not mention it to anyone else at present."

"Oh! Very well," said Mrs. Jennings, looking somewhat disappointed. "Then you wouldn't have me tell it to Lucy, for I intend to go as far as Holborn today?"

"No, ma'am, not even Lucy, if you please. One day's delay will not be very material; and until I have written to Mr. Ferrars, I think it ought not to be mentioned to anyone else. I shall do *that* directly. It is of importance that no time should be lost with him, for he will, of course, have much to attend to regarding his ordination."

This statement initially puzzled Mrs. Jennings exceedingly. She could not immediately comprehend why Mr. Ferrars was to be written to in such haste. After a few moments' reflection, however, a very happy idea struck her, and she exclaimed, "Oh, ho!—I understand you. Mr. Ferrars is to be the man. Well, so much the better for him. Ay, to be sure, he must be ordained in readiness; and I am very glad to find things are so forward between you. But, my dear, is this not rather out of character? Should not the Colonel write himself? Surely, he is the proper person."

Elinor did not quite grasp the beginning of Mrs. Jennings's speech, nor did she deem it worth inquiring into; consequently, she replied only to its conclusion.

"Colonel Brandon is so delicate a man that he rather wished someone else to announce his intentions to Mr. Ferrars than himself."

"And so *you* are forced to do it. Well, *that* is an odd kind of delicacy! However, I will not disturb you (seeing her prepare to write). You know your own concerns best. So goodbye, my dear. I haven't heard of anything to please me so well since Charlotte was brought to bed."

She departed, only to return a moment later. "I have just been thinking of Betty's sister, my dear. I should be very glad to secure her as a mistress. But whether she would do for a lady's maid, I am sure I can't tell. She is an excellent housemaid and works very well at her needle. However, you will think of all that at your leisure."

"Certainly, ma'am," replied Elinor, listening to little of what she said and feeling more anxious to be alone than to master the subject. How she should begin—how she should express herself in her note to Edward—was now her sole concern. The particular circumstances surrounding them made this task difficult, whereas for any other person it would have been the easiest thing in the world. Yet, she equally feared saying too much or too little, sitting deliberatively over her paper with the pen in her hand until she was interrupted by the entrance of Edward himself.

He had met Mrs. Jennings at the door on her way to the carriage as he came to leave his farewell card. She, after apologizing for not returning herself, had insisted that he enter by stating that Miss Dashwood was upstairs and wished to speak with him on very particular business.

Elinor had just been congratulating herself, amidst her perplexity, that however difficult it might be to express herself properly by letter, it was at least preferable to delivering the information verbally, when her visitor entered, forcing her into this greatest exertion of all. Her astonishment and confusion were profound upon his sudden appearance. She had not seen him since his engagement became public, and thus not since he knew she was aware of it; combined with the consciousness of what she had been thinking and what she had to tell him, this made her feel particularly uncomfortable for several minutes. He, too, was deeply distressed. They sat down together in a state of promising embarrassment. Whether he had asked her pardon for his intrusion upon first entering the room, he could not recall; but deciding to err on the side of caution, he offered a formal apology as soon as he could speak, after taking a seat.

"Mrs. Jennings told me," he said, "that you wished to speak with me, at least I understood her so—or I certainly should not have intruded in such a manner; though at the same time, I should have been extremely sorry to leave London without seeing you and your sister, especially as it will most likely be some time—it is not probable that I shall soon have the pleasure of meeting you again. I go to Oxford tomorrow."

"You would not have gone, however," said Elinor, recovering herself and determined to overcome what she so dreaded as quickly as possible, "without receiving our good wishes, even if we had not been able to give them in person. Mrs. Jennings was quite right in what she said. I have something of consequence to inform you of, which I was on the point of communicating by paper. I am charged with a most agreeable office..." (she breathed slightly faster than usual as she spoke) "...Colonel Brandon, who was here only ten minutes ago, has desired me to say that, understanding you mean to take orders, he has great pleasure in offering you the living of Delaford, now just vacant, and only wishes it were more valuable. Allow me to congratulate you on having so respectable and well-judging a friend, and to join in his wish that the living—it is about two hundred a year—were much more considerable, and such as might better enable you to... as might be more than a temporary accommodation to yourself—such, in short, as might establish all your views of happiness."

What Edward felt, as he could not say it himself, it cannot be expected that anyone else should articulate for him. He looked with all the astonishment that such unexpected, such unthought-of information could not fail to excite; but he said only these two words: "Colonel Brandon!"

"Yes," continued Elinor, gathering more resolution as some of the worst was over, "Colonel Brandon means it as a testimony of his concern for what has lately passed—for the cruel situation in which the unjustifiable conduct of your family has placed you—a concern which I am sure Marianne, myself, and all your friends, must share; and likewise as a proof of his high esteem for your general character, and his particular approbation of your behavior on the present occasion."

"Colonel Brandon gives *me* a living!—Can it be possible?"

"The unkindness of your own relations has made you astonished to find friendship anywhere."

"No," replied he, with sudden consciousness, "not to find it in *you*; for I cannot be ignorant that to you, to your goodness, I owe it all.—I feel it—I would express it if I could—but, as you well know, I am no orator."
