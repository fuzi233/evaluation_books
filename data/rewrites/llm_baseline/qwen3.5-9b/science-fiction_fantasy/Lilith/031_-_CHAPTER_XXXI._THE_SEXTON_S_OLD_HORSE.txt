I stood and watched the final shimmer of the white leopardess dissolve into the void before turning to follow my guide—though with heavy reluctance. Why should I succumb to sleep? Reason, surely, remained constant across all realities. What logic dictated sleeping while the dead slept, especially when the hour demanded vigilance for the living? Moreover, who would rouse me? And how could I guarantee awakening, or even attempt to rise, when those within that house allowed mornings to bleed indistinguishably into noons, and noons into eternal nights, stirring not a soul? I muttered these protests, yet I followed, for I possessed no other option.

The librarian moved ahead in silence, and I trailed behind, equally mute. Time and space slipped past us like water through fingers. The sun dipped below the horizon; shadows lengthened, and I sensed the chilling expansion of the Chamber of Death creeping through the air. My spirit sank deeper with every passing second. Soon, the lean, long-coated figure of the librarian vanished from my sight, his rhythmic stride lost among the heather. In its place emerged the slow, deliberate flap of a raven's wings. At intervals, a firefly or a luminous butterfly drifted upward into the starless sky. Gradually, the moon crept over the distant horizon, moving with agonizing slowness.

"You are weary, are you not, Mr. Vane?" the raven called out, landing heavily on a stone. "It is time you made acquaintance with the beast that will carry you tomorrow!" He emitted a peculiar whistle through his elongated black beak. Instantly, a dark blotch marred the face of the half-risen moon. To my ears came the drumming of swift, soft-galloping hooves. Within minutes, emerging directly from the lunar disc itself, thundered the terrible horse. Its mane streamed behind it like the crest of a storm-tossed wave, whipped seaward in frothy gray spray, while the lash of its tail blurred the moonlight into blindness. Standing nearly nineteen hands high, massive in bone, taut in skin, and dense with muscle, he was a steed worthy of Holy Death himself to ride forth and slaughter! Even the moon seemed to regard him with awe; in her eerie luminescence, he appeared a skeletal framework, loosely bound together. Yet, terrifyingly large, he moved with the effortless grace of a winged insect.

As he approached, his pace slowed, his flowing mane and tail settling around him. Though I was merely a lover of horses, claiming to cherish every equine I encountered, I had never spent coin on anything but steeds, nor had I ever parted with one. Seeing this majestic, fearsome creature ignited a raw longing within me—a pure greed, a rank covetousness, an evil impulse known across all worlds. I did not mean to steal him; rather, regardless of his sacred station, I would have purchased him if I could. I reached out, laying my hands upon him to stroke the protruding bones beneath a hide smooth, thin, and glossy as satin. So polished was it that the very silhouette of the moon reflected back at me. I caressed his sharp, pointed ears, whispering words into them, and breathed the warm air of humanity into his crimson nostrils. In return, he exhaled the breath of a horse's life into my lungs, and in that exchange, we loved one another.

What eyes he possessed! They were blue and filmy, reminiscent of the gaze of the departed, yet behind each pupil burned a glowing coal. The raven, wings half-spread, watched my courtship of the magnificent beast with evident pleasure. "That is well! Be friends with him," the bird advised. "He will bear you far better tomorrow! Now, we must hasten home."

My desire to ride this animal had surged into passion. "May I not mount him immediately, Mr. Raven?" I cried. "By all means!" he replied. "Mount, and ride him home." The horse bowed his head affectionately over my shoulder. Twisting my hands deep into his mane, I scrambled onto his back, aided by certain sturdy ridges of bone.

"He would outrun any leopard in creation!" I shouted triumphantly. "Not that way at night," the raven corrected. "The path is treacherous. But come; loss now becomes gain later. Waiting is harder than running, and its reward is fuller. Go on, my son—straight to the cottage. I shall arrive shortly. Your mother's heart will rejoice to see her son astride such a steed!"

I sat in silence. The horse stood motionless, rigid as a block of marble. "Why do you hesitate?" the raven asked. "I long so desperately to ride after the spotted leopardess," I confessed, "that I can scarcely hold back!" "You have promised!" "My debt to the Little Ones seems, I admit, a heavier burden than my bond to you." "Yield to this temptation, and you shall bring ruin upon them—and upon yourself." "What matters to me? I love them; and love commits no evil. I will go."

Truth be told, I had forgotten the children, intoxicated by the horse. Darkness flickered with unseen eyes, and I knew Adam stood beside me in his true form. I recognized the tone of his voice, suppressing an indignation barely contained. "Mr. Vane," he said, "do you not understand why you have accomplished nothing worthwhile thus far?" "Because I have been a fool," I admitted. "In what way?" "In everything." "Which counts as your most indiscreet act?" "Bringing the princess to life; I should have left her to her destined end." "Nonsense! You could not have acted differently, unaware of her malice—but you never brought anyone to life! How could you, being dead yourself?" "Dead?" I exclaimed. "Yes," he confirmed. "And you shall remain dead so long as you refuse to truly die."

"Back to the old riddles!" I retorted scornfully. "Be persuaded, and return home with me," he urged gently. "The greatest folly you ever committed was fleeing from our dead." I pressed the horse's ribs, and he launched forward like a sudden gust of wind. Patting his neck, I guided him in a sharp curve, close to the ground, mimicking a cat circling a mouse, leaning sideways until his mane brushed the tops of the heather. Through the gloom, I heard the raven's wings beating five rapid times before he perched atop the horse's head.

The horse halted instantly, digging his hooves into the earth. "Mr. Vane," the raven croaked, "consider your actions! Evil has struck you twice already—once born of fear, once of carelessness. Breaking a promise is far worse; it is a crime." "The Little Ones are in dire peril, and I caused it!" I screamed. "--Yet I cannot break my word to you. I will return and spend whatever nights, days, or years you require in your house." "I warn you again: going tonight will harm them further," he insisted.

A false sense of power, unrooted and vibrating solely from the sheer strength of the horse, had rendered me deaf to his warnings. "Will you deny me my last chance for redemption?" I cried. "This time, there shall be no evasion! It is my duty, and I will go—even if I perish trying!" "Then go, foolish boy!" he snapped, anger coloring his croak. "Take the horse and ride toward failure! May it lead to humility!" Spreading his wings, he took flight.

Once more, I gripped the lean ribs beneath me. "After the spotted leopardess!" I whispered into his ear. He turned his head this way and that, sniffing the air, then started forward in a slow, hesitant walk. Suddenly, he quickened to a trot, then broke into a gallop, his speed becoming tremendous in moments. He seemed to navigate the darkness perfectly, stumbling not once, faltering not once, hesitating not once. I sat as though balanced on the crest of a wave, feeling the distinct play of every muscle beneath me. His joints were elastic; each movement flowed seamlessly into the next, causing not a single jar. His increasing velocity carried him until he seemed to fly rather than run. The wind roared past us like a tornado. Across the cursed hollows, we sped like bolts fired from a giant crossbow. No monster raised its neck; all creatures knew the thunder of hooves sweeping overhead! We raced up the hills and shot down their far slopes; he did not swerve from the rocky chasms of the riverbed, holding his fierce, terrible gallop over the abysses.

The moon, halfway up the heavens, gazed with solemn distress upon our pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and the pride of my existence, I sat like a king. We neared the center of the many channels, the horse leaping clear of one, sometimes two, in every stride, gathering momentum for great bounding leaps whenever the moon reached the keystone of her arch. Then came wonder and terror: she began to descend, rolling like the nave of Fortune's wheel, pushed by the gods, accelerating rapidly. Like our own moon, this celestial body bore a human face, alternating between broad forehead and chin as it tumbled. I stared in horror. From the ravines came the howling of wolves. An ugly dread invaded the hollows of my heart; my confidence crumbled!
