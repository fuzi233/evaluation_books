He had not travelled far along the sun-dappled rural lane when he rounded a hedge and came upon a small curious group. Two men in plain, sober wool suits leaned lazily on either side of a grazing horse, chatting idly to each other, while their master, clad in a glossy silk doublet, bright English green jerkin and hose that shone as smooth as a mole’s pelt, lay flat on his stomach in the warm afternoon sun, looking for all the world like some overgrown iridescent lizard. His flaming yellow velvet cloak had been carefully spread out over the horse’s loins, as if the steed was far more precious than the man sprawled unguarded in the dust.

“Is aught amiss here?” Gerard called out, pausing beside them.
“None that I know of,” one of the servants replied without shifting his weight.
“But your master lies there as still as a corpse. Are you not ashamed to let him grovel in the bare dirt?”
“Go on now, hard ground is the best cure for his sort of disorder,” the man laughed. “Sober up in a soft bed and you wake with a splitting headache. But leap up off cold packed earth and you’re as spry as a lark in spring. Eh, Ulric?”
“He speaks true, young man,” Ulric agreed warmly.
The simplicity of Gerard’s next question made the two servants snort out hoarse, rough laughter. “What? The gentleman is drunk?”
Ulric suddenly stopped laughing, narrowed his eyes, and stared Gerard up and down from head to toe very gravely. “Who are you, and where were you born, that you don’t know the Count is always dead drunk at this hour?” In an instant, Gerard found himself eyed as a suspicious stranger.
“I am a traveller, new to these parts,” Gerard said quickly. “But an honest man, who loves to learn what he does not know. I ask out of curiosity, not prying.”
“If you’re an honest man,” Ulric said shrewdly, “then pay us trinkgeld for the knowledge we’ve just given you.”
Gerard’s face fell blank for a moment, but he put on a brave face. “You shall have what little my lean purse can spare, if you will first tell me why you’ve taken his fine cloak off the man and laid it across the horse’s back.”
At the promise of coming coin, the two men immediately offered two competing explanations. The first was practical: if the Count woke suddenly (and he was a seasoned toper, apt to come to his senses in a single sharp jolt) to find his horse standing out in the cold sweating, while a good cloak lay unused nearby, he’d fly into a cursing rage and likely lay about him with his staff. The second was far more grand: a horse was a soft milksop that drank nothing but cold water, so it needed to be warmed from the outside. But a nobleman, full of good beer, carried a fire of inward heat that kept his skin warm, making a cloak nothing but useless, silly vanity.

Each man grew fiercely attached to his own theory, and since both had shared more than a little of their master’s drink that day, their voices rose higher and higher until the green-clad Count on the ground stopped snoring and began to growl. Neither noticed. Soon the argument turned physical, as most arguments did in that rough age. Hans, holding the bridle in his right hand, cuffed Ulric hard across the ear with his left. Ulric returned the blow twice over, his own right hand free, and the two banged away at each other over the horse’s mane, jostling and poking the poor beast until it reared backwards and brought its iron-shod hoof down right on the fleshy backside of the prostrate nobleman. The Count started up howling like a toad pricked by an angel’s spear, one hand clamped to his smarting rear, the other fumbling to draw his sword. The terrified servants dropped the bridle, the horse galloped off whinnying down the lane, the two men pelted after it screaming, and the green-clad nobleman chased after all three, stark raving mad, naked sword in his hand, bouncing from hedge to hedge in his zigzag headlong rush down the narrow road.

Gerard turned his back on the whole ridiculous scene and walked calmly south, glad he had no need to hand over the four tin farthings he’d pulled out ready for trinkgeld. His heart was too heavy, though, to even smile at the absurd drunken chaos he’d left behind.

The sun was already dipping low, and Gerard had been hoping in vain for hours to spot an inn along the road. Black heavy clouds rolled across the sky, and he quickened his pace almost to a run. It was no use: the rain burst down in torrents, drenching him to the skin, seeming to snuff out the last fading rays of the sun. He trudged on through the dark, unfamiliar land, shivering. “Fool that I was, to leave Margaret,” he murmured to himself.

The dark thickened fast as he entered a vast, ancient wood. Huge gnarled branches stretched across the narrow track, turning the path into an endless, pitch-black cave with a rugged, stony floor that made him stumble again and again. On he went, shivering, his stomach empty, his heart faint, until the wolves roused from their lairs and bayed all around the edges of the wood. His hair bristled on his scalp, but he tightened his grip on his oak cudgel, ready to sell his life dearly. There was no wind, and his straining ears caught the soft patter of light paws across fallen leaves, and the rustle of low branches as unseen creatures glided past him in the dark.

Then, in that sea of inky black, he spotted a bright fiery star bobbing close to the ground. He hailed it like he would his patron saint. “A candle! A candle!” he shouted, trying to run towards it, though the rough uneven ground slowed him down. At last, deep in the heart of the forest, he found a low cottage, candles glowing behind the windows, loud voices ringing from inside. He looked for a signboard, but there was none. “Not an inn, after all,” he thought sadly. “But what Christian would turn a dog out into this wood on a night like this?” He pushed the door open slowly and stuck his head in, then jerked it back as if he’d been slapped in the face, recoiling back into the rain and dark.

He’d peeked into a large, low-ceilinged room, dominated by a huge round clay stove that stretched all the way up to the rafters. Wet clothes hung drying on lines strung across the room, and even more were draped straight over the backs of the rustics huddled around the heat. Their garments, soaked through from the day’s rain and caked with years of accumulated grime, steamed thickly, belching out rank, unnameable odours. In one corner a large travelling family huddled, giving off the faint sickly smell of neglected, unwashed children. Garlic hung thick in every gap of the air, all the windows were closed, the central furnace blazed hot, and the breath of at least forty people filled the small space to bursting. They had just finished supper.

Gerard, like most artists, had sensitive senses, and the potent stench filled him with dismay. But the rain lashed him outside, and the light and heat were too tempting to resist. He crept back to the door again and again, like a moth singed by flame, until he realized the different smells did not fully mix: the sour family stench ruled two corners, the reeking odour of unwashed peasants hung thickest around the stove, and the sharp tang of garlic clustered around the loud group by the window. He slipped into a small corner right beside the door, the farthest possible from the thickest clouds of stench, but even there the heat and damp vapours circulated until the walls dripped. The soft home-raised novice felt something like a cold snake wind around his legs, his head grew heavy as lead, and he felt half choked, half drifting off to faint, all at once. He nearly swooned, but recovered, and resolved to turn back for Holland at the first peep of dawn. Faint with hunger, he asked the garlic-eating group if the house was not, after all, an inn.

“Whence do you come that you do not know the Star of the Forest?” the man replied, bewildered.
“I am a stranger. In my country, every inn has a sign hanging out front.”
“Droll country yours! Who needs a sign for a public house, when every soul for twenty miles round knows it?”

Too tired and faint to argue, Gerard asked for the landlord. The native’s contempt at his ignorance rose too high for words, and he just pointed to a middle-aged woman sitting on the other side of the oven, before turning to his friends to tell them what a strange outlandish animal had wandered in. All the loud voices in the room fell silent one by one, and every eye turned to stare at Gerard, studying him like a new, exotic specimen. The landlady sat on a chair a few inches higher than everyone else, picking plumes out of a huge heap of feathers, littering the floor ankle-deep with loose quills that added a stuffy, drowsy haze to the already thick air.

Gerard asked if he could have something to eat. She stared at him in astonishment. “Supper ended more than an hour ago.”
“But I had no supper at all, good dame.”
“Is that my fault? You were welcome to show up for it same as anyone else.”
“I was lost in the dark, a stranger, forced to linger on the road against my will.”
“What have I to do with that? Everyone knows the Star of the Forest serves supper from six till eight. Show up before six, you eat well. Show up before eight, you get whatever Heaven sends. Show up after eight, you get a clean bed, a stirrup cup, or a horn of cow’s milk at dawn.”

Gerard’s face fell. “Then may I go to bed at once, dame?” he said grumpily. “It does no good to sit up wet and fasting, and the old saying goes ‘he who sleeps, sups well enough’.”
“The beds are not here yet,” she replied. “You will sleep when the rest do. Inns are not built for just one man.”

Gerard was stunned. What on earth did she mean, the beds were not here yet? He dared not ask, every word he had spoken so far had the whole room staring at him like a freak. He leaned against the wall and sighed loudly, and a ripple of quiet titter went around the watching company. “So this is Germany,” he thought. “A great big country right next to Holland. Give me small nations, any day.” He consoled himself that this would be his first and last night in the land.

An elbow jabbed sharply into his ribs. He turned, and saw a woman in the far corner beckoning him over. He walked towards her gingerly, as if her outstretched finger was pulling him across the floor with a gut-string. “Hold the child a moment,” she said cheerfully, and plopped her squalling bairn right into Gerard’s arms. He stood frozen, the child a heavy lump of lead in his hands, his face stretched in perfect comical horror. The whole room burst into loud, long laughter at his expression.

“Never mind them,” the woman said, still smiling. “They know no better, how should they, bred and born in the middle of a wood?” She rummaged through her bundle of clothes, and pulled out a small tin plate and a dried, pressed pudding, taking her child back with one arm and holding the food out to Gerard with the other. “Go set it by the stove to warm. You’re too young to go to bed fasting.”

Gerard thanked her warmly, but hesitated when he saw the group of unwashed rustics blocking the stove door. The landlady noticed, calmly set down her pile of feathers, and came over to move the men out of the way as easily as someone shifting furniture, her face completely impassive. “Turn about is fair play,” she said. “You’ve been dry for ten minutes now.” The men, who had gone from steaming to baking hot by the fire, trundled away grumbling, and Gerard set his pudding to warm, standing so close to the stove his damp clothes burst into steam.

The door flew open, and a farmhand hurled a bundle of straw into the room with his pitchfork. Another, and another, followed, until the whole floor was strewn like a clean farmyard. They layered the straw in rings around the stove, like seats in an arena, and in a moment every single person in the room had laid down to sleep. The beds had arrived.

Gerard pulled his warm pudding off the stove, and found it absolutely delicious. The woman who had given it to him, already curled up on her straw bed, beckoned him over again. He went to her side, and she whispered that the landlady had waited to let him warm his food. He hurried to finish the rest of his meal, then went over and knelt to help her pick up the loose feathers scattered all over the floor. “I do not know where you come from,” she said, her distrust fading into warmth, “but you were well brought up. I’ll wager you had a good mother.” She said a quick blessing over the whole house before she vanished to her own bed, and Gerard dragged his straw to the very edge of the room, right by the door, the best spot he could get, reserved for the last arrival. It was a blessing, not a punishment: he lay right on the shore of the thick stench and heat, instead of drowning in its middle by the stove.

He was just drifting off to sleep when the farmhand woke him, striding over all the sleeping bodies to press a huge oaken mug of beer under his nose. “She bade me say one good turn deserves another. Here is your nightcap.” Gerard took a deep drink, and made a wry face: the beer was muddy, with a strange sharp medicinal tang he had never tasted before in Holland. “Drink it all!” the farmhand reproached him.
“Enough is as good as a feast,” Gerard said. The farmhand pitied the strange traveller who would leave good liquor in his mug, took the mug from him, and drained the rest to the dregs.

Gerard turned his face to the wall, pulled up two handfuls of clean straw, bored a little hollow in it with his finger, and sheathed his nose deep inside to block out the smell. Soon the whole room was asleep: men, maids, wives, and children all higgledy-piggledy, snoring in a dozen different keys like an orchestra slowly tuning its instruments. His body lay on straw in the German forest, but his spirit was already miles away, back in Sevenbergen with Margaret.

When he woke at dawn, most of the guests had already left. He paid the landlady her asking price of two pfenning, barely an English halfpenny, and gave the farmhand a small extra trinkgeld. The man saw Gerard staring longingly at a foaming milk pail he had just brought fresh from the cow, and hoisted it straight to his lips. “Drink your fill, man,” he said, refusing Gerard’s offer of payment, saying no man should have to pay for a skinful of fresh milk. At the door, Gerard found his kind benefactress and her broad-chested artisan husband, and tried to press a small creutzer coin into her hand for the pudding. She pushed his hand away gently, colouring faintly. “What do you take me for? We are travellers and strangers, same as you. We owe kindness to anyone in the same plight.” Gerard blushed and stammered his apologies. The big husband grinned, and said “Give the vixen a kiss for her pudding, and call it even.” Gerard obeyed, kissed her cheek softly, and the two wished him God speed. He never saw them again for the rest of his life.

The sun had just risen, raindrops on the leaves glinting like diamonds, the air fresh and sharp. Gerard strode south, and completely forgot his half-hearted resolve to turn back for Holland. He walked eight leagues that day, and in the afternoon spotted a huge stone building with an enormous arched gateway and a smaller postern gate set in its side. “A monastery!” he cried joyfully. “I will go no further, lest I fare worse.” He knocked at the postern, stated where he came from and where he was bound, and was admitted at once, led to the spacious high-walled guest chamber where pilgrims and travellers were fed and lodged free of charge by the monks’ charity.

Soon the chapel bell tinkled for vespers. Gerard stepped into the convent church, and heard the service sung so exquisitely it sounded like the choir of heaven itself. The only thing that marred his rapture was the thought that Margaret was not there beside him to hear it, and he sighed bitterly mid-hymn. Supper was plain but wholesome, abundant food and good home-brewed convent beer laid out for him and his fellow travellers. At an early hour they were ushered into a large dormitory, and since the number of guests was small, each had a proper truckle bed, covered in soft sheepskins tanned with the fleece still on. A monk, struck by Gerard’s youth and earnest face, questioned him, and soon drew out all his plans to travel to Rome alone. When the monks learned he had been raised in a cloister himself, he became a favoured guest. The next morning they showed him the whole convent, made him stay for dinner in the refectory, pricked out a safe route for him on a slip of parchment, and the prior gave him a silver guilder to help him on his way. “Join the first honest group of travellers you meet,” he advised. “Do not face all the manifold perils of the road alone.”
“Perils?” Gerard thought to himself, surprised.

That evening he reached a small straggling town, and found its only inn. It had no sign hanging out, but he was now wise to local customs, and spotted it at once by the old coats of arms painted on its walls, left there by generations of distinguished nobles who had stopped to sleep there over the centuries. The place looked more like a mausoleum than an inn: nothing moved, no sound came from inside. Gerard hammered on the great oak main door, no answer. He shouted, no reply. He shouted louder, and at last a small round hole in the wall opened, a man’s head poked out cautiously like a tortoise from its shell, stared at Gerard stolidly, and said not a single word.
“Is this an inn?” Gerard asked, half smirking.
