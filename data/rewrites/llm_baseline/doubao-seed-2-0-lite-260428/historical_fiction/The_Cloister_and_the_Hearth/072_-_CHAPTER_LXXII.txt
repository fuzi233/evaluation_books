Once the most feared, most powerful order across all of Christendom, the Dominican friars – known far and wide as the Black Friars – were in slow, sharp decline by the 15th century. Their bitter rivals the Franciscans, with their raucous, populist preachers and humble, appealing vows of poverty, were outstripping them everywhere: even in wealthy, devout England, where the Black Friars had once been so powerful no noble would dare turn them away empty-handed from their gates.

So the order’s shrewd leadership, who guarded the Dominicans’ waning influence like smoldering embers they would not let die, grew desperate to recruit sharp, zealous new sons, hone them to a fine edge, and send them out to win back the flocks slipping from their grasp.

Brother Clement – the former scribe Gerard, living hidden under his new religious name – soon drew their notice. His rare gift for tongues, that allowed him to speak polished Latin for scholars, lyrical Italian for Roman crowds, formal French for northern courts, and both high Dutch for merchants and rough low dialect for peasant farmers, was a skill no other novice could match. The leadership set their plans: they would send him to travel and preach across England, reporting directly back to the Roman curia.

But Jerome, the gruff, battle-hardened senior friar who had the prior’s full confidence, blocked the order’s plan before it could move forward.
“Clement still carries the milk of the world in his veins,” he argued. “All its soft attachments, its untested weaknesses. Do not let his bright new-born zeal and gentle humility trick you into abandoning our old, tried wisdom. Test him first, temper him in the fire, lest one day we find ourselves leaning on a gilded reed for a staff, and it snaps clean in two.”
“Good counsel,” the prior agreed. “Take him in hand yourself, then, and prove his mettle.”

Jerome set to work, following centuries of Dominican custom. One day he led Clement to a public green where young men of the city played the popular games of the era: he knew this was a haunt of Clement’s old worldly friends. Sure enough, before long Pietro Vanucci and Andrea wandered past them, tossing a careless glance at the two shaven friars. They did not recognize their old lost friend, the scribe they thought dead, under the tonsure and black habit.
Clement flinched only for half a heartbeat, then cast his eyes firmly to the ground and murmured a quiet Paternoster.
“Will you not step forward and greet them, brother?” Jerome prodded, testing him.
“No, brother. Yet it does me good to see them. They remind me of the worldly sins I can never atone for deeply enough.”
“It is well,” Jerome said, and submitted a short, unenthusiastic but favourable report on Clement’s progress.

Next Jerome took Clement to a dozen plague-ridden deathbeds, and then down into Rome’s worst noisome dungeons: places where the dark was so thick you could not see your own hand in front of your face, the stench of sewage and rot so loathsome it made the stomach turn, where men who had been abandoned there rotted half-mad, coiled in rags and filth like feral beasts, all hope long dead. The work wore Clement’s body raw, but his soul gathered all its strength to lean close, hold their shaking hands, and speak to them of God’s unmerited mercy. He shone through the trial, and Jerome reported back: “His spirit is willing, but his flesh is weak.”
“Perfect,” the prior smiled. “A man whose flesh is weak but his spirit willing is far more reliable than the brute who never feels weariness or revulsion at all.”

But a far harsher trial still waited for Clement. It was witnessed by hundreds of onlookers on a crowded Roman main street, as described below:
One morning the principal thoroughfare leading out to the scaffold was packed shoulder to shoulder, side avenues blocked with heads craning to see. It was no ordinary execution. The crime had been unthinkable, the victim no common man: the governor of Rome had been found hacked to death in his bed at dawn. One hand, raised to fend off the blow, had been severed clean at the wrist and lay by his side, his throat slit ear to ear, his temples bashed in with a blunt club. The murder had been traced to his own household servant, and the sentence decreed retribution equal to the crime.
Italian executions were rarely gratuitously cruel in that era, but this butchery had enraged the whole city, and the crowd bayed for matching blood. The killer was first tied to the outer wall of the murdered governor’s house for half an hour, to taste the first bite of vengeance. Then his left hand was lopped off, just like his victim’s. A newly slaughtered fowl was cut open and bound around his bleeding stump, a bizarre folk remedy favoured by local doctors no one could quite explain the logic of, and the mutilated, bandaged man was hurried to the scaffold.
There, a young friar – Clement – knelt by his side, praying fiercely, speaking soft words of comfort, holding a crucifix pressed close to the condemned man’s eyes. Before long the executioner roughly shoved Clement out of the way, bashed the prisoner’s skull in with a heavy mallet, and fell on him to slit his throat. A collective cry of horror burst from the crowd. Clement swayed, and fainted dead away. The gigantic Jerome strode forward, slung him over his shoulder like a fainting child, and carried him back to the convent.
Clement returned to the cloister heavy with shame, weeping as he confessed his failure to the prior.
“Courage, son Clement,” the prior soothed him. “A Dominican is not forged in a single day. You shall have another trial, and I forbid you to go to it fasting.”
He did not have long to wait. A notorious robber was dragged to the scaffold, a monster of cruelty who had waylaid travellers, robbed them, and butchered them for sport, no provocation needed. Clement spent the last night with him in his prison cell, walked with him to the scaffold, and prayed so earnestly by his side that the hardened ruffian burst into tears, and threw his arms around the friar’s neck. Clement hugged him back, even as his flesh quivered with revulsion, and held the crucifix steady before his eyes. The man was garrotted, his soul leaving his body, and Clement drowned out the noise of the crowd with his own loud, passionate prayers. No sooner was the man dead than the hangman pulled out his axe, and quartered the corpse right there on the scaffold. The same crowd that had watched the robber die with indifference, almost satisfaction, wailed piteously at every stroke of the axe on the corpse that could feel no pain. Clement shuddered, but stood firm, like a rock that vibrates under a storm but cannot be toppled.
Then Jerome’s voice snapped in his ear: “Brother Clement, climb up on that cart and preach to the people. Quick! Strike while their hearts are hot, while there are souls here ripe for saving.”
Clement’s face flushed pale then red, he breathed hard, his hands shaking. But he obeyed, stumbled up the cart’s wobbly steps, and faced the largest crowd he had ever seen in his life: a sea of upturned heads. His throat was parched, his heart thudded against his ribs, his voice trembled as he began.
Slowly, the magnitude of the moment, the sight of all those faces hungry for words, and his own heart brimming with zeal lit a fire in him. He told them the robber’s own story, the small, trivial first sins that had led him step by slow step down the fatal ladder to murder, warned them all to never take that first tiny, seemingly harmless step off the path of virtue. One moment he thundered against the sin of bloodshed, the next he moved the crowd to soft pity and charity, and soon all his tremors fell away. He felt strung tight as a lute string, gifted with a power he never knew he had: he could feel the crowd’s very pulse, play on their hearts like he once played his psaltery. Sobs and groans rippled through the crowd, already raw from the tragedy they had just watched. Jerome stared, dumbfounded, like a man who strikes a tinder stick expecting a small flame, and accidentally sets off a rocket.
Clement caught Jerome’s shocked, unsmiling glance, broke off his sermon abruptly, and stepped down to join him.
“It was my first try,” he said apologetically. “Your command came on me like a thunderbolt. Did I do wrong? Tell me, correct me, lend me the benefit of your wisdom, Brother Jerome.”
“Humph,” Jerome grumbled, frowning thoughtfully for a long minute. Then he said, sullen and half-reluctant, “Give the glory to God, Brother Clement. My judgement is that you were born an orator.” He reported the same back to the order’s leadership, even though it galled him a little – for he was an honest friar, even if he was a dour one.

The next trial that came Clement’s way was the ordeal of Julio Antonelli, a man accused of sacrilege. Three witnesses swore on their lives they had seen him leaving the church the very night the golden candlesticks were stolen. Three other witnesses swore just as firmly he had been across the city at the time, with an unassailable alibi. No testimony could be shaken, so the court ruled he would submit to trial by ordeal: he could choose to plunge his bare arm to a depth of fourteen inches into boiling water to retrieve a pebble, or be bound hand and foot and lowered eight feet down into the Tiber. The clergy who believed him innocent pushed him to choose the hot water trial, which they secretly rigged to be far less painful for those they favoured. But the poor man was terrified of scalding, and picked the cold water ordeal – giving Jerome another chance to temper Clement’s nerve.
Antonelli took the sacrament, was stripped naked on the banks of the Tiber, and tied hand and foot to stop him thrashing, which would lift his torso above the water and falsely mark him guilty. He was lowered gently into the stream, floated for a split second with only his hair sticking above the surface, and a roar went up from the crowds on both banks: guilty! Then the new wet ropes slipped, and he sank entirely under the water. Another roar burst out: innocent! They left him at the bottom for the ordained half-minute, then hauled him up, gurgling, gasping, screaming for mercy. After the set prayers were recited, he was set free, formally cleared of all charges.
Clement had prayed earnestly on the riverbank the whole time. When it was over, he thanked God in a loud, only slightly trembling voice. After a while he turned to Jerome, and asked: “Should he not be compensated, brother? For the pain, the terror, the suffocation. He lives, but he tasted every drop of the bitterness of death, and he had done no wrong at all.”
“He is rewarded enough to have been cleared of the false charge,” Jerome said.
“But he was innocent of that fault, and yet he drank of death’s cup, even if he did not drain it. And his accusers, who bear far more guilt than he, suffer nothing at all.”
Jerome replied sternly, “The real punishment and reward of men is not meted out in this world, Brother Clement. Unhappy are they who sin and suffer no ill for it. Happy are the ones who bear the small ills this earth can inflict: all of it will be counted to them in heaven, a hundredfold over.”
Clement bowed his head, submissive. “May your good words take root in my heart, and never fall to the ground, Brother Jerome.”

But the severest trial Clement ever underwent at Jerome’s hands was entirely unplanned. It came to pass when Jerome, in a rare moment of good humour, brought Clement along to visit the scholar Fra Colonna. On Colonna’s table lay the brand new, perfectly fair copy he had just finished of his *The Dream of Polifilo*. The proud author was eager to show off his masterwork.
“For ages,” he ranted, “fools have lavished all their most poetic, amorous compliments on mortal women – mere creatures of earth, reeking of their fleshly origins. Roman women stand at their windows, carefully learned over a lifetime to show you their one good feature, be it a single ear or a fluttering eyelash, and hide all the rest. They are sirens at the window, magpies at the door, angels in the street, and devils in their own homes. But I? I ransack every line of the old minstrels’ love verses, not forgetting all the pretty lines Petrarch wasted on that little French coquette Laura, the slyest of them all – and I lay all that heap of sweet, amorous spice at the feet of the only females worthy of such devotion: the Nine Muses!”
“By that clever trick,” Jerome huffed, flipping roughly through the pages, “you, a Dominican friar, have written an obscene book.” He slammed *Polifilo* hard down on the table.
“Obscene? You discourteous monk!” Colonna ran around the table, snatched his manuscript away, locked it fast in a chest, trembled with mortification, and spluttered: “My Gerard – pshaw, what’s-his-name, the friar who was here before – never found *Polifilo* obscene. To the pure, all things are pure.”
“All those who read your *Polifilo* – heaven grant there are few of them – will find it exactly what I find it,” Jerome retorted.
Poor Colonna had to swallow the bitter insult. If he had not been in his own lodgings, a high-born gentleman as well as a famed scholar, a vulgar brawl would have broken out. Instead, he fought to steady himself, turned the conversation to a beautiful chrysolite the Cardinal Colonna had lent him, and as Clement turned it over in his fingers, he waxed lyrical on the supposed moral virtues of precious stones, a superstition his whole age devoutly believed in.
Jerome would have none of it, and argued against the folly of believing a dead stone could grant a man valour, chastity, immunity from poison, or temperance.
“Centuries of experience prove they do,” Colonna insisted. “As for that last virtue you named, there sits a living proof. This Gerard – I beg your pardon, Brother Thingemy – comes from the north, where men drink like fishes, and yet he was always the most abstemious soul alive. Why? Because he carried an amethyst, the clearest, richest coloured one I ever saw, on any hand but a noble’s. Where in heaven’s name is your amethyst now? Show it to this unbeliever!”
“And you think that little stone made the boy temperate?” Jerome asked, sneering.
“Of course! What is the very meaning of the word amethyst? *a-*, not, and *methuo*, to get drunk. Names are not assigned things for two thousand years for no reason at all.”
He went on to list all the great jewels, illustrating their powers with anecdotes from respected ancient historians.
“These are nothing but old wives’ fables,” Jerome said contemptuously. “Was there ever such credulity as yours?”
Colonna smarted under the jibe, and the insulted scholar, the man who had written the Dream of Polifilo, turned the full force of his vast, unruly learning on Jerome. He launched into a long, roaring tirade, of which only fragments are recorded here:
“The heathen blotted out? Why, they hold four fifths of the whole world. What have we Christians invented that they did not do first? Painting? Sculpture? These are heathen arts, and we are but pygmies beside them. What modern man can carve forms half so godlike as the great Athenian sculptors, or Dinocrates of Macedon, or the masters who carved the Laocoon out of a single block of marble? What prince has the genius to carve an entire mountain into a statue, as was done at Bagistan, and once planned for Mount Athos? What city has the soul to raise a colossus of brass in the sea, so tall the largest sailing ships could pass between its legs?
“Architecture? We are but children at it. Can any modern building match the pure perfect lines of the Parthenon? The grandeur of the Greek and Roman theatres, or the monstrous Egyptian temples that men walked to down a mile-long avenue of sphinxes, each as big as a Venetian palace? Their porphyry monuments are polished like crystal, not rough-hewn like our own shoddy structures. Even now their broken pillars lie overgrown with acanthus and myrtle, still sparkling, mocking the sloppy work of our modern masons. Roads? Aqueducts? Sewers? We have forgotten how to build a good road even with all the Roman models under our very feet. Tombs? No Christian nation has ever raised a tomb that does not make a learned scholar laugh when he sees it. Think of the Mausoleum, the Pyramids, the giant mountain tombs along the Indus and Ganges, stuffed inside with mines of precious stones. You have never seen the East, Jerome, or you would never dare decry the heathen.
“Even in the world of the mind, what have we invented? Geometry? Logic? We are all pupils of Euclid and Aristotle. Written letters? We did not invent them any more than Cadmus did. Poetry? No Christian has ever come close to Homer, or Virgil, or Horace. Tragedy? Comedy? The poets, the actors, the marble theatres all turned to dust at our touch, and we could never revive them. Would you compare our silly little morality plays, with their frigid personifications and dog Latin, to a Greek tragedy performed in a marble amphitheatre before a whole city of audience, written by Sophocles?
