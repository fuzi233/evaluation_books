Shirley proved her sincerity in desiring Caroline's company by actively seeking it, for Miss Helstone was naturally reticent about forming new acquaintances. Burdened by the belief that she was unwanted and incapable of entertaining, the shy governess viewed the vibrant, wealthy heiress of Fieldhead as too self-sufficient to appreciate her own modest society. Yet, Shirley, though brilliant and likely happy, found that no one exists entirely independent of warm social interaction. Within a month, she had cultivated friendly relations with the Sykes sisters, the Pearsons, and the formidable Misses Wynne of Walden Hall, yet she confessed to Mrs. Pryor that she felt no genuine geniality among them. She did not fraternize with any of them. Shirley mused that if she were truly the Earl of Briarfield, she would not wish to marry a single woman in the neighboring parishes, a sentiment Mrs. Pryor received with quiet amusement, warning her against adopting masculine mannerisms.

Shirley never mocked her former governess, respecting even her formalities as signs of quiet worth. Standing by the window, watching a bird on a grand cedar tree, Shirley began to chirrup, then whistle, executing a tune with surprising skill. When Mrs. Pryor protested, Shirley admitted she had forgotten her resolution not to whistle before her. Explaining that she had learned the habit by listening to a gentleman whistling in the fields, she identified him as Mr. Moore, the only non-gray-haired gentleman in the region, contrasting him favorably with the venerable Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke.

Mrs. Pryor remained silent until Shirley proposed taking Caroline for a walk on Nunnely Common. Persuading the diffident Caroline to join her, Shirley led her onto the quiet road. Once the initial shyness faded, Caroline found conversation with Miss Keeldar refreshing. Shirley spoke of the heath-covered ridges, recalling a sultry journey across Scottish moors where the landscape turned a livid purple-black, edged with brass and white, threatening with lightning. Caroline described the scene vividly, noting how the hills seemed rolled in a sullen mist before being washed from the world by rain. They paused on the green brow of the common to gaze down at Nunnely Dale, a valley robed in May's green, where the shadow of a cloud rested on Nunnwood—the last remnant of ancient British forest.

Shirley declared Yorkshire one of England's bonniest nooks, revealing she was born there, with five generations of her family sleeping in Briarfield Church. Caroline shook her hand, declaring them compatriots. Shirley described Nunnwood as an encampment of forest giants, a place of olden silence and ruin, specifically pointing out a dell containing the remains of a nunnery. She proposed an excursion there, alone with Caroline, to sketch and enjoy the solitude. Caroline eagerly agreed, listing her knowledge of the wood's hidden glades, mosses, and ancient trees, insisting that the presence of gentlemen would destroy the charm of such a retreat.

Reflecting on the nature of companionship, Caroline noted that while fools irritated, clever men caused a different, sharper pain: the insecurity of one's own worthiness in their company. Shirley dismissed this fear, asserting her confidence in associating with the best men. The conversation drifted to Caroline's uncle, the rector. Shirley praised his sensible face, but Caroline revealed a stark contrast: he was charming in society but stern and silent at home, locking his warmth away in his study. She described him as liberal rather than good-natured, brilliant rather than genial, and scrupulously equitable rather than truly just.

This led to a profound discussion on marriage. Shirley confessed that the thought of becoming a burden to a husband terrified her; she preferred the freedom to withdraw into solitude when her company was no longer welcome. Caroline agreed, citing her uncle's cynical view that marriage was a burden and that men inevitably grow sick of their wives. Shirley countered that they usually find an exception in the one they love, trusting in his kindness to animals and children as proof of his future fidelity. However, Caroline recalled her uncle's prediction that any man would be sick of her in a month.

Shirley then spoke of Robert Moore with enthusiasm, praising his handsome features and princely forehead. Caroline confirmed his nobility, adding that he possessed a mind that made him the lord of creation. They discussed the nature of true poetry, agreeing that it must stem from real feeling rather than intellectual display. Reciting Cowper's "The Castaway," they found solace in the poet's ability to transform anguish into art. Shirley noted that one could love Cowper only to comfort him, while Caroline insisted he was a nature too elevated for human love, comparing him to Rousseau—a being of clay and gold, repulsive in totality despite divine sparks.

The conversation turned to Caroline's relationship with Robert. Shirley questioned why he had cut a lock of her hair if he was so indifferent. Caroline admitted it was her own doing; she had asked for it, and he had traded a lock of his for one of hers. She confessed to feeling foolish and despising herself for this small, sharp recollection that lacerated her self-respect. Just as Shirley urged her to stop dwelling on her weaknesses, a tap at the door interrupted them. Shirley received a note from Mr. Moore. She declined to open it immediately, summoning candles instead, while Caroline waited expectantly. Soon after, the rector's niece, Fanny, arrived and departed, leaving the two women alone once more.
