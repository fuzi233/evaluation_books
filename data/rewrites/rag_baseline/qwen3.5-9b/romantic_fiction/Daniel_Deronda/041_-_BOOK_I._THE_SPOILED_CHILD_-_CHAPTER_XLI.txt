On the evening following his encounter with Mordecai, Daniel Deronda sat in a room where the air felt charged with the static of unresolved questions. For a mind of lesser susceptibility, the adventure might have been merely a curious diversion, a fleeting anomaly to be filed away; but for Deronda, the event had struck a chord so resonant that it demanded immediate scrutiny. His intellect, reacting to the intensity of his emotion, began to dissect the very foundations of his agitation, wrestling with the terrifying prospect of surrendering to its guidance. A cold alarm prickled beneath his skin: he realized he was being half-dominanted by Mordecai's energetic certainty, and far more so by the fervent, almost desperate trust radiating from the older man.

This realization triggered his characteristic defense mechanism: a recoil from the moral stupidity of undervaluing what had touched him closely, and a dread of missing the seismic shift occurring in his own life—a crisis he sensed was monumental in the grand, historic tapestry of humanity. Had he read of such an incident transpiring centuries prior in Rome, Greece, or the dusty streets of Cairo, involving a young man equally dissatisfied with neutrality and craving a specific, arduous duty, he would have deemed it entirely natural. Such a figure, cloaked in the garments of a bygone era, would have been understood as a product of an age defined by its weightier consequences. Yet, here he was, dressed for dinner in a white tie, surrounded by a circle where possessing a conscience was often mocked as the solemn folly of taking oneself too seriously. Deronda shrank from this cowardice before fashionable ignorance, yet he simultaneously recoiled from letting his course be dictated by mere contagion, devoid of reason's consent, or allowing a reverential pity for spiritual struggle to rush him down a dimly lit path.

"What, after all, had truly happened?" he asked himself, mentally rehearsing the clinical diagnosis Sir Hugo would surely offer. "A consumptive Jew, driven by a fanaticism intensified by obstacles and hastening death, has fixated on Deronda as the living embodiment of some visionary image born of wedded hope and despair: despair of his own life, coupled with an irrepressible hope in the propagation of his fanatical beliefs." The instance was perhaps odd, exceptional in its form, but substantively, it was not rare. Fanaticism was not uncommon as bankruptcy; taken in all its aspects, it was abundant. While Mordecai waited on the bridge for the fulfillment of his visions, another man was convinced he held the mathematical key to the universe, viewing all known physicists as conspirators; another claimed the metaphysical key, differing only by a hair's breadth from the old wards. Scattered everywhere were terrible souls, eyes either glittering or preternaturally dull, searching for the listener, armed with volumes difficult to print or impossible to read. Mordecai possessed a more pathetic aspect, a speech more passionate and penetrative than the usual monomaniac; he was more poetical than a social reformer mapping the new moral world in parallelograms or an enthusiast preaching about sewage. Still, he fell under the same classification. It would be right and kind to indulge him slightly, to offer such practical comfort as was feasible, but what likelihood existed that his notions held the value he ascribed to them? In such cases, a man of the world knows what to think beforehand. As for Mordecai's conviction that he had found a new executive self, it might be preparing him for the worst of disappointments—that which presents itself as final.

Deronda's ear caught these negative whispers, repeating them distinctly to himself. It was not the first, nor the most pressing, occasion on which he had faced this question of family likeness among the heirs of enthusiasm—whether prophets, dreamers, "great benefactors of mankind," or devotees of phantasmal discovery. From the first believer in his own unmanifested inspiration down to the last inventor of a perpetual motion machine, the kinship of human passion and the sameness of mortal scenery inevitably filled fact with burlesque and parody. Error and folly had their hecatombs of martyrs. Reduce the grandest type of man known to history to an abstract statement of his qualities and efforts, and he appears in dangerous company: say that, like Copernicus and Galileo, he was immovably convinced in the face of hissing incredulity; so too is the contriver of perpetual motion. One cannot fairly judge spirits by this test alone. To avoid administering the dose of hemlock or the sentence of banishment in the wrong case, nothing suffices but a capacity to understand the subject-matter on which the immovable man is convinced, and a fellowship with human travail, both near and afar, to prevent us from scanning any deep experience lightly. Should we say, "Let the ages try the spirits"? No, for we are the beginning of the ages, justified only by just judgments formed in separate human breasts—separate yet combined. Even steam engines could not have been made without that condition; they must have remained in the mind of James Watt.

This train of thought was familiar enough to Deronda to save him from any contemptuous prejudgment of Mordecai, even if their communication had been free from that peculiar claim on himself, strangely ushered in by some long-growing preparation in the Jew's agitated mind. Considered in a purely rational way, this claim might seem dismissible as illusory and preposterous; yet, it was precisely what transformed Mordecai's hold on him from an appeal to ready sympathy into a clutch on his struggling conscience. Consciences are not all cast in the same mold, delivering fixed laws; they are the voice of sensibilities as varied as our memories, which also possess their own kinships and likenesses. Deronda's conscience included sensibilities beyond the common, enlarged by his early habit of imagining himself into the experiences of others.

What was the claim this eager soul made upon him? "You must believe my beliefs—be moved by my reasons—hope my hopes—see the vision I point to—behold a glory where I behold it!" To accept such a demand as a direct obligation would have been preposterous, bordering on dishonesty. Looking on the agitation of those moments, Deronda felt thankful that amidst his compassion, he had preserved himself from the bondage of false concessions. The claim rested, too, on a supposition that might be—and probably was—in discordance with the full fact: the supposition that he, Deronda, was of Jewish blood. Was there ever a more hypothetical appeal?

Yet, since the age of thirteen, Deronda had associated the deepest experience of his affections with a pure supposition: that Sir Hugo was his father. That hypothesis had been the source of passionate struggle within him; by its light, he had learned to subdue feelings and cherish them. He was well-used to finding a motive in a conception that might be disproved, and accustomed to contemplating revelations that could alter his view of his particular duties. To exist in a state of suspense, one of emotive activity and scruple, was a familiar posture of his conscience.

Now, suppose that wish-begotten belief in his Jewish birth, and that extravagant demand of discipleship, were the foreshadowing of an actual discovery and a genuine spiritual result. Suppose Mordecai's ideas made a real conquest over Deronda's conviction? Nay, it was conceivable that as Mordecai needed and believed that connection to replenish himself, so Deronda might receive from Mordecai's mind the complete ideal shape of that personal duty and citizenship lying dormant in his own thought like sculptured fragments certifying some beauty yearned after but not traceable by divination.

As this possibility presented itself in his meditations, he became aware that it would be labeled dreamy, and he began to defend it. If the influence he imagined submitting to had been that of an honored professor, an authority in a seat of learning, or a philosopher accepted as the voice of the age, would thorough receptiveness toward direction have been ridiculed? Only by those who deem it a sign of weakness to be obliged for an idea, preferring to hint that they have implicitly held in a more correct form whatever others have stated with sad explicitness. After all, what was there but vulgarity in taking the fact that Mordecai was a poor Jewish workman, perhaps to be met on a sanded floor in the parlor of the *Hand and Banner*, as a reason for deciding beforehand that no spiritual force within him could determine the fate of a white-handed gentleman? There is a legend of Emperor Domitian, who, hearing of a Jewish family of the house of David from whom the ruler of the world was to spring, summoned them in alarm but quickly released them upon seeing they had the hands of workmen. Conversely, a Rabbi stood waiting at the gate of Rome, confident the Messiah would be found among the destitute who entered. Both Emperor and Rabbi were wrong in their trust of outward signs: poverty and poor clothes are no sign of inspiration, Deronda told his inward objector, yet they have accompanied it in remarkable cases. To regard discipleship as out of the question because of these factors would be mere dullness of imagination.

A more plausible reason for dismissing discipleship was the strain of visionary excitement in Mordecai, which turned his wishes into overmastering impressions and made him read outward facts as fulfillments. Was such a temperament likely to accompany the wise estimate of consequences, the only safeguard from fatal error even for an ennobling motive? It remained to be seen whether that rare conjunction existed in Mordecai: perhaps his nature was one where a wise estimate of consequences was fused in the fires of passionate belief, thereby determining the very consequences it believed in.
