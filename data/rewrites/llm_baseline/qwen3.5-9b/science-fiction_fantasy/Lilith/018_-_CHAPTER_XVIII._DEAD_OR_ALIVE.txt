I pressed onward, keeping my gaze fixed on the moon. It hung low in the sky, staring directly into the dense canopy of the forest ahead. I couldn't fathom what troubled her; she appeared dark and pitted, resembling a battered disc of tarnished copper. She looked utterly dispirited and exhausted. There was not a single cloud near enough to offer her companionship, and the brilliance of the stars seemed too harsh for her pale countenance.

"Will this endure forever?" she seemed to whisper, though no sound accompanied the thought.

She walked one way, I another, yet through the woods, our paths converged for a long distance. We shared little conversation, for my eyes were cast downward, but her sorrowful expression was fixed upon me; I felt its weight even without seeing it clearly. For a long time, the moon and I walked side by side—she a dull, fading luminescence, I a living shadow.

Then, something on the ground beneath a sprawling tree caught my attention with its stark whiteness. Turning toward it, I approached cautiously. Though vague in the dappled shade of the foliage, it suggested, as I drew nearer, a human form.

"Another skeleton!" I murmured to myself, dropping to my knees and placing a hand upon it.

It was a body, certainly, and not merely bone, though it resembled a skeleton as closely as flesh can mimic structure. Lying on its side, it was profoundly cold—not the inert chill of stone, but the specific, haunting cold of something that was once vibrant and is now silent. The closer I examined it, the more certain I became that it was truly dead. For a bewildered instant, I imagined it might be one of the wild dancers, a spectral Cinderella who had wandered astray and perished in the strange, outdoor night of this alien world.

She was completely naked, so emaciated that even in the shadows, peering closely, I could count every rib along her flank without needing to touch them. Her bones were visible as if encased only in thin, translucent leather. Her teeth, beautiful yet terrifying, were grotesquely exposed by retracted lips, gleaming ghastly in the gloom. Her hair cascaded longer than her own height, thick, incredibly fine to the touch, and black as the void of night.

It was the body of a tall woman, likely once graceful. How had she arrived here? She could not have come of her own volition in such a ravaged state. Her strength must have failed her; she had collapsed and lain there until starvation claimed her. Yet, how could she be reduced to this degree of waste? And why was she stripped naked? Which savages would dare to rob her, or what wild beast would tear away her garments? Leaving her body behind was understandable, but the question of her survival gnawed at me.

I rose, standing amidst the silence, and considered my options. I could not, and would not, allow her to remain exposed and abandoned. A natural reverence forbade it. Even the remains of a woman command respect; to leave her body uncovered was unthinkable. Irreverent eyes might feast upon her; brutal claws might toss her about; years might pass before gentle rains returned her to the earth.

But the ground was hard, almost solid with interlacing roots, and I possessed only my bare hands. Initially, it seemed clear she had not been dead long; there was no sign of decay. Yet, what slow-wasting life remained in her to rot? Could she still be alive? Might she not? In this strange world, everything defied logic. Even if she were alive, my chances of reviving her were slim, but I needed certainty before burying her.

As I had left the forest hall earlier, I had spotted a cluster of ripe grapes hanging in the doorway. I had carried them with me, eating sparingly as I traveled. A few remained on the stalk; their juice might possibly restore her. Regardless, it was all I had to attempt a rescue.

Her mouth was slightly agape, a fortunate detail, though her head rested in an awkward angle. To shift her body, I slid my arm under the shoulder supporting her weight and discovered the pine needles beneath were warm. She could not have been dead for long; she *might* still be alive, despite the absence of a heartbeat or breath I could detect.

One of her hands was tightly clenched, seemingly gripping something small. I forced a grape into her mouth, but no swallow followed. Desperate to do everything in my power, I spread a thick bed of pine needles and dry leaves, draped one of my garments over it—still warm from my own body—and lifted her onto it. I covered her with my clothes and a massive pile of leaves, intending to preserve whatever warmth lingered within her, hoping the returning sun would amplify it.

I attempted another grape, but perceived not the slightest movement of her mouth or throat.

"Doubt," I told myself, "is a poor incentive for action, yet a terrible excuse for inaction."

The skin was so taut against her bones that I dared not apply friction. Instead, I crawled into the heap of leaves, positioning myself as close to her as possible, and embraced her. My own reserves of heat were dwindling, but I would share what little I had left.

Thus, I spent the remainder of the night sleepless, yearning for the dawn. Her cold seemed to radiate outward, draining me, while none of my warmth penetrated her. If I had fled from the beautiful sleepers, each resting on their "dim, straight" silver couches, to lie alone with such a bedfellow, what madness drove me? I had refused a lovely privilege; I was condemned to an awful duty. Beneath the sad, slowly setting moon, I lay with the dead, waiting for the light.

Darkness finally yielded to the gray of early morning as the eastern horizon grew dimly clearer. Then, I noticed a motion—not of an object moving, but of movement itself—close to the ground and not far from me. It was the low, undulating glide of a large snake passing in a straight, unbroken line. Shortly after, a roe doe and her fawn appeared, heading for the same spot. Moments later, two creatures resembling bear cubs emerged, trailed by three or four smaller ones.

Light was increasing rapidly. A few minutes hence, when a troop of horses trotted past, I realized that while the largest were no bigger than the smallest Shetland pony, they must be fully grown. Their forms were perfect, their movements indistinguishable from those of great horses. They were of various breeds: some modeled after draft horses, others as chargers, hunters, or racers. Following them were dwarf cattle and tiny elephants.

"Why are the children not here?" I asked myself. "The moment I am free of this poor woman, I must return and fetch them!"

Where were these creatures bound? What drew them? Was this an exodus or a morning ritual? I waited for the sun. Until it rose, I could not abandon the woman. Placing my hand on her body, I noticed it felt marginally warmer. Perhaps it had absorbed some of the heat I had lost; it could hardly have generated any itself. Yet, the sliver of hope had not diminished.

The forehead of the day began to glow, and soon the sun peeked up, as if observing the bustling activity of this new world for the first time. Witnessing his great, innocent splendor, I rose, filled with renewed life and strength against death.

Removing the handkerchief I had placed to shield her mouth and eyes from the pine needles, I anxiously inspected her. Had I found a priceless jewel, or merely its empty casing? The body lay motionless, exactly as I had found it. It was then, in the morning light, that I truly saw how drawn and hollow her face had become, how sharp the bones protruded beneath the skin, and how every tooth defined itself through her lips.

Her clothing was worn to its very threads, yet the bird of heaven might still be nesting within, ready to awaken to motion and song. However, the sun beat down upon her face. I rearranged the handkerchief, laid a few leaves lightly over her features, and set out to follow the creatures.

Their main trail was well-beaten, evidently used for generations, merging with numerous side paths that broadened it further. As I progressed, the trees receded, and the grass grew denser. Soon, the forest vanished entirely, replaced by a vast expanse of lush green stretching to the horizon. Along the forest's edge, a small river flowed, and the track led directly to it.

At the sight of the water, a new, undefined hope surged within me. The stream appeared deep and full to the brim, though nowhere wider than a few yards. A bluish mist rose from it, dissipating as it ascended. On the opposite bank, amidst abundant grass, many small animals fed. They presumably slept in the forest and migrated to the plains each morning, swimming the river to cross.

I knelt to drink, but recoiled instantly. The water was scalding hot and tasted strangely metallic. Leaping to my feet, I realized this was the warmth I desperately sought—the first necessity of life! I raced back to my helpless charge. No one who has not experienced such profound isolation can comprehend what the redemption of this woman meant to me.

"Whatever she may prove," I reasoned, "at least I shall no longer be alone!"

I had found myself such poor company that, for the first time, I understood the true nature of hope
