A silence, thick and charged with unspoken dread, settled over the room following that final, desperate plea. While the scattered auditors of this harrowing chronicle whispered frantically among themselves and Thomas Adams turned toward his wife with a mixture of agonizing love and terror, Inspector Gryce returned the memorandum to Mr. Adams. The document, a testament to the detective's meticulous unraveling of chaos, bore a series of interrogations, each marked with the decisive stamp of resolution:

*   **Answered:** Why did a woman, composed enough to pause and fix her hair at the start of an encounter, descend into such frenzied exasperation that she took her husband's life with her own hand? (Recall the comb discovered on the floor of Mr. Adams's chamber.)
*   **Answered:** What significance lay in the words penned moments prior to the tragedy: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"*
*   **Answered:** Why was the singular pronoun "I" employed in this communication? What standing did Mr. Felix Adams possess vis-à-vis this young girl to warrant such language after her union with his brother?
*   **Answered:** Having invoked that authority, why, upon being assaulted, did he attempt to swallow the very paper bearing these words, perishing with the note clamped between his teeth?
*   **Answered:** If he met his end in rage, as monsters do, why did his visage reflect sorrow rather than hatred, and a resolve seemingly detached from the torrent of overwhelming emotion expected upon receiving a fatal blow from an unforeseen adversary?
*   **Answered:** Possessing the strength to grasp the aforementioned paper and guide it to his lips, why did he not utilize that vigor to ignite a light capable of summoning aid, instead leaving behind the blazing crimson glow which, under our current signal code, signifies: *"Nothing more required just now. Keep away?"*
*   **Answered:** What was the function of the massive steel plate discovered wedged between the door casings, and why did it remain motionless within its socket at this climactic juncture of his existence?
*   **Not Answered:** An elucidation of how Old Poindexter appeared on the scene so swiftly after the event. His overheard remark—"It is Amos' son, not Amos!"—demands scrutiny. Did he not know whom he awaited in this house? Was the state of the man lying before him, marked by a cross upon his bosom and a dagger in his heart, less startling to him than the identity of the victim?
*   **Answered:** Recall the deductions drawn from Bartow's pantomime. Mr. Adams was struck down by a left-handed thrust. We await confirmation that the young woman is indeed left-handed, and we must understand why, for so long, she held her other arm stretched rigidly behind her.
*   **Answered:** Why does the bird, whose primary cry is *"Remember Evelyn!"*, occasionally modulate its song to *"Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?"* For this tragedy to bear truth, it must reveal that Mr. Adams knew his brother's bride intimately, both in duration and depth.
*   **Answered:** If Bartow is, as we suspect, innocent of any complicity beyond witnessing, why does he exhibit such palpable joy at the outcome? Though this may not strictly fall within the purview of Thomas Adams's confession, it cannot be overlooked by us. This deaf-and-dumb servant was driven mad by the very fact that ignited his joy. Why? *(Note: The fragments of writing in Felix's hand had not yet been recovered by the police. Allusions therein suggest Bartow was consumed by a jealousy that likely curdled into delight upon seeing his master felled by the object of that master's affection and his own loathing. How he recognized in the bride of another the bearer of the name constantly hovering on his master's lips remains a puzzle for those more adept at the laws of perception than myself. Presumably, he spent considerable time at the stairway loophole, scrutinizing his master until he deciphered every gesture and expression.)*
*   **Answered:** Observe the following schedule, compiled after repeated trials with Bartow and the enigmatic lamp that casts myriad hues in Mr. Adams's study: *White light—Water needed. Green light—Coat and hat to be fetched. Blue light—Return books to shelves. Violet light—Prepare study for night. Yellow light—Await next signal. Red light—Nothing wanted; depart.* The final illumination was red at the moment of the climax. Can this be reconciled with Mr. Adams's account?

Only two paragraphs remained without full explanation. The first, Number Nine, carried significant weight. The description of the stroke delivered by Mr. Adams's wife failed to account for the peculiar constraint observed in Bartow's pantomime. Turning to the inspector, Mr. Gryce asked if Mr. Adams possessed the fortitude to reenact the blow exactly as witnessed by his wife. The startled youth regarded the inquiry with the hesitation of one daring to voice a silent fear. Like others present, he knew Bartow had performed characteristic gestures attempting to depict the crime, yet he remained ignorant of their specific nature, as this crucial detail had been deliberately withheld by the authorities. Consequently, he did not react impulsively to the suggestion. Instead, after a moment of intense contemplation, he extended his left hand, snatched an object from the inspector's table, and lunged across his body, striking an imaginary foe to his right. Then, he scanned the faces surrounding him with inexpressible anxiety, finding scant comfort in their expressions.

"You would conclude your wife is left-handed," Mr. Gryce proposed gently.

"Now I have watched her since her arrival here," Mr. Adams replied, his voice steady despite the tremor in his eyes. "I have seen no evidence of this."

"She is not left-handed," he continued, explaining with a clarity that cut through the fog of suspicion. "But she thrust with her left hand because her right was firmly grasped in mine. I had seized her instinctively as she leaped forward for the weapon, and the convulsive grip of our joined hands was not released until the sheer horror of her deed rendered her faint. She collapsed away from me onto the floor, sobbing: 'Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother's breast. I would at least see him die the death of a Christian.'"

Mr. Gryce exchanged a look of profound relief with the inspector. The mystery of the constrained posture of the right hand, which had baffled Bartow's pantomime, was now resolved. Taking up the blue pencil the inspector had set aside, he smiled and inscribed a firm, decisive "answered" across paragraph number nine.
