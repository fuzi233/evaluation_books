"There are briars besetting every path,
Which call for patient care;
There is a cross in every lot,
And an earnest need for prayer."

Margaret stepped out of the house with a heaviness in her heart that seemed to drag at her feet. Yet, as she walked the length of Milton Street, the familiar air of the city began to lift her spirits. By the time she reached her first turn, her step had lightened, and color returned to her cheeks. For the first time since arriving, she allowed her attention to drift outward from her internal turmoil.

The streets teemed with an unusual energy. Men with hands deep in their pockets sauntered with a casual swagger, while groups of girls laughed loudly, their voices ringing with a boisterous independence that startled her. The rougher elements of the population congregated on the steps of the beer-houses and gin-palaces, smoking and offering sharp commentary on every passerby. Margaret shuddered at the prospect of walking further through these crowded thoroughfares toward the fields she intended to visit. Instead, she decided to seek out Bessy Higgins. It would lack the serene refreshment of a country stroll, but it offered a kind of human connection she desperately needed.

She found Nicholas Higgins seated by the fire, smoking his pipe, while Bessy rocked gently beside him. Nicholas removed his pipe from his lips and stood, pushing his chair aside to make room for Margaret. Leaning casually against the chimney-piece, he greeted her warmly.

"How is Bessy?" Margaret asked.

"Hoo's rather down in the mouth regarding spirits," Nicholas replied, "but hoo's better in health. Hoo doesn't like this strike. Hoo's set on peace and quietness at any price."

"This is the third strike I've seen," Bessy sighed, her voice heavy.

"Well, the third time pays for all," Nicholas declared confidently. "See if we don't damn the masters this time. See if they won't come begging us to return at our own terms. We missed our chance before, I grant you; but this time, we've laid our plans deep."

"Why do you strike?" Margaret asked, genuinely puzzled. "Striking means stopping work until you secure your desired wage, isn't it? I shouldn't wonder at my ignorance; in my homeland, I've never heard of such a thing."

"I wish I were there," Bessy murmured wearily. "But it's not for me to grow weary of strikes. This is the last one I'll witness. Before it ends, I shall be in the Great City—the Holy Jerusalem."

"She's so filled with thoughts of the life to come that she cannot focus on the present," Nicholas explained. "Now, I must do the best I can here. I believe a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. Thus, we differ in our views on the strike."

"But," Margaret pressed, "if the people in my village struck, as most of them are field laborers, the seeds wouldn't be sown, the hay wouldn't be gathered, and the corn wouldn't be reaped."

"And what then?" Nicholas challenged, resuming his pipe and framing his 'well' as a question.

"Then," Margaret continued, "what becomes of the farmers?"

Nicholas puffed thoughtfully. "I reckon they'd either have to abandon their farms or agree to a fair wage."

"Suppose they couldn't, or wouldn't?" Margaret argued. "They couldn't give up their farms instantly, no matter how much they wished to. Yet, they would have no hay or corn to sell that year. Where would the money come from to pay the laborers' wages the following season?"

Still puffing, Nicholas finally spoke. "I know nothing of your ways down South. I've heard they are a race of spiritless, downtrodden men, utterly crushed by poverty, so dazed by their misery that they don't even realize when they are being exploited. Here, it is not so. We know when we are put upon, and we have too much blood in us to tolerate it. We simply take our hands from our looms and declare, 'You may crush us, but you shall not exploit us, my masters!' And be damned to them, they shall not succeed this time!"

"I wished I lived down South," Bessy interjected.

"There is much to endure there," Margaret admitted. "There are sorrows everywhere. There is grueling physical labor performed with scant nourishment to sustain the body."

"But it is outdoors," Bessy countered. "Away from the endless, suffocating noise and the sickening heat of the mills."

"It rains heavily, and it freezes bitterly," Margaret insisted. "A young person can withstand it, but an old man suffers terribly from rheumatism, becoming bent and withered prematurely. Yet, he must continue working, or he faces the workhouse."

"I thought you were so enamored with the customs of the South," Nicholas teased.

"So I am," Margaret smiled slightly, realizing she had been caught. "I only meant, Bessy, that there is both good and evil in everything. Since you feel the hardship here, I thought it fair that you learn of the hardships down there."

"Do you say they never strike down there?" Nicholas asked abruptly.

"No," Margaret replied. "I believe they possess too much sense."

"And I think," Nicholas retorted, blowing ash onto the floor with such force that his pipe shattered, "that it is not that they have too much sense, but that they lack spirit."

"Oh, Father!" Bessy cried out. "What have you gained by striking? Consider the first strike when Mother died. How we all had to stop work—you suffered the most of all. Yet, many continued working weekly at the same wage until everyone was employed, and some remained beggars for the rest of their lives."

"Aye," Nicholas conceded. "That strike was poorly managed. Fools and untrustworthy men took charge. But you will see; this time will be different."

"But you haven't told me what you are striking for," Margaret persisted.

"You see," Nicholas explained, "there are five or six masters who have maintained their current wages for the past two years, prospering and growing richer. Now, they approach us demanding lower pay. We refuse. We intend to crush them first and see who works for them afterward. They will have killed the goose that laid the golden eggs, I reckon."

"And so you plan to die in revenge against them?" Margaret asked.

"No," he corrected firmly. "I merely anticipate the possibility of dying at my post sooner than yielding. That is what people call noble in a soldier, and why not in a poor weaver?"

"But," Margaret pointed out, "a soldier dies for the Nation—for the sake of others."

He laughed grimly. "My lass, you are but a young woman, but do you not think I can support three people—Bessy, Mary, and myself—on sixteen shillings a week? Do you think I strike for myself? It is just as much for the sake of others as that soldier, only perhaps his cause involves strangers he has never met, whereas I champion the cause of John Boucher, who lives next door with a sickly wife and eight children, none of whom are of factory age. I do not champion only his case, though he is a poor soul who struggles to manage two looms at once. I champion the cause of justice. Why should we receive less now than we did two years ago?"

"Do not ask me," Margaret said, feeling her ignorance keenly. "Ask your masters. Surely they will provide a reason. It cannot be merely an arbitrary decision arrived at without cause."

"You are just a foreigner," Nicholas dismissed her contemptuously. "Know nothing about it. Ask the masters! They would tell us to mind our own business, and they would mind theirs. Our duty is to accept the reduced wage and be grateful; their duty is to drive us down to starvation levels to swell their profits. That is the reality."

"But," Margaret insisted, refusing to yield despite sensing she was irritating him, "perhaps the state of trade prevents them from offering the same remuneration."

"State of trade!" Nicholas exploded. "That is mere masterly humbug. I was speaking of the *rate* of wages. The masters control the state of trade; they wield it like a scarecrow to frighten naughty children into submission. Let me tell you, it is their role—their cue—to beat us down and enrich themselves. It is our duty to stand up and fight—not solely for ourselves, but for those around us, for justice and fairness. We contribute to their profits, and therefore, we deserve to share in spending them. It is not that we crave their coin as much as before; we have saved money. We are resolved to stand or fall together. Not a single man among us will accept less than the Union deems our due. So, I shout 'Hooray for the strike,' and let Thornton, Slickson, Hamper, and their ilk look out for themselves!"

"Thornton!" Margaret exclaimed. "Mr. Thornton of Marlborough Street?"

"Aye! Thornton of Marlborough Mill," he confirmed.

"He is one of the masters you oppose, is he not? What manner of man is he?"

"Have you ever seen a bulldog?" Nicholas asked. "Set a bulldog on its hind legs, dress it in a coat and breeches, and you have John Thornton."
