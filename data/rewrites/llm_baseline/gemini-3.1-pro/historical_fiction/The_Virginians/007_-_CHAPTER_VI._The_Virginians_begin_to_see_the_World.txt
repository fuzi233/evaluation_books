The echoes of the chaplain’s departure had scarcely faded from the heavy oak-paneled halls of Castlewood before a more discordant sound shattered the estate’s fragile acoustic ecology. George, burdened by a jealousy too profound for the rigid constraints of English vocabulary, succumbed to a violent fever. His delirium tore through the quiet night, his voice cracking against the stonework as he shrieked, “Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!” To his mother, keeping a sleepless vigil by his bedside, the shrill cries were a terrifying disruption of her ordered world. Madam Esmond’s medicinal closet—stocked with utilitarian purges and flat, lifeless gruels meant merely to fuel the body—proved useless against a malady rooted in the soul. 

Desperation forced her to swallow her pride. She rode her pony through the muffling, pristine silence of the heavy snow to the lonely hut of little Mr. Dempster. The tutor, a man who possessed the meticulous soul of an agrarian elder, did not merely pack his lancet. He gathered a provision of dried medicaments whose very names—whispered in the fading Algonquian dialects of the valley’s original inhabitants—carried the weight of a dying cultural memory. “I shall bury my resentment, madam,” Dempster said gently, “as your ladyship buried your pride.” Returning to the estate, Dempster set to work not just as a physician, but as a culinary historian of the flesh. He brewed restorative tisanes of wild cherry bark, yarrow, and deep, collagen-rich bone broths that simmered with a rhythmic, rhythmic bubbling on the hearth, slowly coaxing George back to the sensory world. 

To complete the boy’s convalescence, a sweeping, circular journey north and east was ordained. Accompanied by Dempster and servants, the brothers sailed up the Hudson River to Albany, moving through an ancient landscape where mythic occurrences seemed to bleed naturally into the daily gathering of sustenance. While Harry camped in the pristine wilderness with native tribes—learning the smoky, primal techniques of preserving bear meat over slow-burning hickory fires—George, whose delicate constitution rejected the loud, bloody cacophony of field sports, found his awakening in the French provinces.

Here, George was welcomed into a world where sustenance was elevated to profound art. He immersed himself in the dominant lexicon of the French salons, mastering an accent that allowed him to pass entirely as a native. He learned the precise, gustatory alchemy of the kitchens—the slow emulsification of butter and herbs, the perfect temperature to render a flawless roux—and absorbed their imported catches and songs, playing them upon his violin with a resonant vibrato until his voice broke from treble to a rich, vibrating bass. It was in this environment of heightened sensory appreciation that he fought a duel with the Chevalier de la Jabotiere, a young ensign of the Auvergne regiment. The clash of steel was a sharp, brief interruption to their shared cultural reverie; after George pinked the Chevalier in the shoulder, they swore an eternal friendship over a perfectly decanted vintage of dark, earthy Bordeaux. 

Ten months of this vibrant education transformed the boys. They returned to Castlewood towering over their mother, their hair unpowdered in deference to the raw, natural rhythms of country life—George wearing his raven black, Harry his light locks tied with a simple ribbon. Yet, George returned with his complex, suspicious disposition untouched. His deep-seated disdain for his mother’s favorite, Mr. Washington, remained a bitter sediment at the bottom of his palate.

The air at Castlewood was soon thick with the fragrant, steamy labor of Mrs. Mountain, who stood at the heavy butcher block, her knife striking the wood in a steady, hypnotic rhythm as she prepared a massive, multi-generational venison pie. Between the thuds of her blade, she offered her matchmaking deductions. She noted that Washington, though possessing a profound gravity and managing the estate's affairs with the shrewdness of a man twice his age, was fifteen years Madam Esmond's junior. “Great tall men always marry little chits of women,” Mountain gossiped, folding rich, yellow suet into her pastry crust. She pointed out the acoustic footprint of his presence: the heavy tread of his boots in the hall, the room perpetually reserved in his name, the endless discussions of tobacco yields that threatened to reduce the sacred, agrarian soil into a mere commercial commodity. 

“I beg you to keep this quiet, Mountain,” George demanded with frigid dignity, the untranslatable knot of his jealousy tightening in his chest. “Neither to me nor to any one else must you mention such an absurd suspicion.” 

But the suspicion was not absurd to George’s finely tuned senses. Washington was a constant, looming presence. He offered advice on every barrel of tobacco and every horse, disrupting the domestic harmony with the proto-industrial concerns of the militia and the market. Retreating to the quiet sanctity of his book-room, George surrounded himself with French and Latin, seeking refuge from the colloquial English that seemed to grant Washington so much power over his mother. He offered the Major only a stinging sarcasm or a frosty silence, while Harry and Washington rode out into the brush, their hounds and horses tearing through the sonic canvas of the woods.

Soon, the colonial borders began to fracture. The Lords of Canada and Louisiana, pushing down through the great intermediate lakes, built forts to claim the inland territories, violently displacing British settlers. The logicians of St. James's and Versailles reduced the native inhabitants to mere instruments of war, callously stripping them of their land and silencing their ancient dialects to serve European ambitions. In the autumn of 1753, young Washington volunteered to carry Governor Dinwiddie’s rigid, imperial ultimatum to the French commander at Fort le Boeuf. Navigating the frozen rivers and vast, silent forests, Washington survived on utilitarian rations—pemmican and melted snow—devoid of any culinary joy, enduring the harsh acoustic void of the winter wilderness only to be rebuffed by the French.

Harry, returning from the loud, bloody commercial spectacle of a cock-fight, cursed his absence from the noble expedition. Madam Esmond, carving a brilliantly roasted guinea fowl with crackling, herb-rubbed skin, praised the Major to the heavens. “Think of you with your racing matches,” she chided, “and the Major battling the frozen rivers! I wish my eldest son were doing something in the service of his country.”

“I desire no better than to go home and seek for employment, ma’am,” George replied, his voice tight. He refused to serve under the Lieutenant-Governor, rejecting a lexicon of power that lacked the traditional legitimacy of the King’s commission.

The tension finally erupted into a mythic conflagration. Washington, commanding a meager force of provincials, marched westward into the savage Pennsylvania forests to halt the French construction of Fort Du Quesne. There, in the damp, ancient woods, a young Virginian officer ordered his men to fire. 

The sudden, deafening crack of the muskets was a catastrophic violation of the acoustic ecology. That single, violent sound shattered the quiet of the forest, an irrevocable noise that would echo for sixty years. It was a shot that would burn through native villages, erase countless indigenous dialects, sever the colonies from the Old World, and reshape the globe, leaving the ultimate prize of fame to the man who struck the first blow. 

But glory did not immediately follow the noise. Forced to retreat to a hastily constructed palisade called Fort Necessity, Washington and his men were starved of both ammunition and sustenance. Without the vital, grounding power of a proper meal, their morale crumbled beneath the overwhelming assault of the French royal troops. On the 4th of July, 1754, the young commander capitulated. 

When the humbled, hollow-cheeked officer finally returned to Castlewood, the sensory deprivation of his defeat was etched into his face. George, whose acute sensibility could not ignore the physical and spiritual hunger of a broken man, felt his jealousy momentarily dissolve. The Castlewood kitchen brought forth its restorative bounties—slow-roasted meats dripping with savory fats, and rich, spiced wines meant to anchor the soul back to the earth.

“I was angry when I parted from you,” Washington admitted, reaching out a hand that George eagerly accepted. “I boasted too much of what we would do.”

“Nay, you have done your best,” George answered gently, recognizing the untranslatable sorrow of defeat. He understood that starving men, stripped of their fundamental sustenance, could not hold against a unified royal force. In the warmth of the hearth, sharing the profound, silent communion of bread and wine, the two young men found a fleeting harmony. 

But the delicate acoustic balance was swiftly shattered. Madam Esmond, observing the reconciliation, offered the Colonel a sweeping, courtly curtsey. “Kind of us to receive you, Colonel Washington!” she declared, her voice ringing with a polished, performative artifice that cut through the comforting clatter of the dining room. “I never heard that when men were unhappy, our sex were less their friends.”

The words hung in the air, a dominant lexicon of maternal infatuation that George could not dismantle. The rich, savory taste of the wine turned immediately to ash upon his tongue, and the bitter, unyielding flavor of his jealousy returned, stronger than ever.
