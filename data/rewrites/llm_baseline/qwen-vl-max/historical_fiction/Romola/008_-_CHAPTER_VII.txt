Bartolommeo Scala, secretary to the Florentine Republic—a man of considerable influence and ambition—lived in a grand palace near the Porta Pinti, now known as the Casa Gherardesca. His coat of arms, bearing a blue ladder across a golden field with the motto _Gradatim_ above the entrance, declared plainly that he had risen from humble origins by his own merit. Once the son of a miller, he had climbed steadily upward, not through favor or fortune alone, but through perseverance and pride. And though he was vain and prone to pomp, he was also honest in his belief that he deserved every honor he had received.

For twenty years, he had held the office of secretary—an office long associated with dignity and public trust. He had delivered orations on the platform of the Old Palace before visiting princes, while Marzocco, the republican lion, wore its golden crown in celebration, and the crowd roared, “Viva Messer Bartolommeo!” He had served on an embassy to Rome, where he had been named titular Senator, Apostolic Secretary, and Knight of the Golden Spur. Eight years prior, he had even become Gonfaloniere—the highest office a Florentine citizen could attain. With each step upward, however, came greater wealth—and greater gout. Now, on this May afternoon, he sat beneath the loggia of his palace, one foot propped up on a cushion, unable to move freely despite the elegance of his surroundings.

The sun bathed the garden beyond in golden light. The heavy silk lucco had been discarded, and a loose mantle draped over his tunic. Beside him sat his daughter Alessandra and her husband, the Greek poet-soldier Marullo. On the other side, two less illustrious friends—men of modest fame, therefore better listeners—occupied the conversation. Yet even in this moment of comfort, Scala’s contentment was shadowed. Before him lay a bundle of letters, the source of a quiet but bitter dispute: a correspondence between himself and Politian, the most renowned scholar of the age.

It was not uncommon in those times for men of learning to relish intellectual quarrels, especially when they could be shared with friends. Scala had done so before, seeking their judgment on whether he or Politian had the stronger claim to reason. This latest exchange began harmlessly enough—over a few playful Latin epigrams. But what started as jesting soon became a clash of egos, pride, and scholarly pretension.

The root of the matter lay in Politian’s unrequited affection for Alessandra, Scala’s daughter. Though rejected, he harbored a lingering resentment toward the secretary—one who had chosen a soldier-poet over the greatest mind of the era. Scala, meanwhile, was a man of achievement, both in public service and in self-congratulation. He was lucky, successful, and increasingly arrogant—qualities that grated on a scholar who valued intellect above status. Yet there was irony in his situation: though he wielded power, he craved recognition as a writer. And so, like many men of rank who dabbled in literature, he composed verses—not for publication, but for private amusement, sending them to friends in letters that served as informal literary journals.

His friends were generous with praise. Ficino and Landino admired his Latin prose as elegant and vigorous. Even Joseph Scaliger, though far removed in time, would later dismiss him as ignorant of classical grammar. But such admiration was never enough. The need to impress someone more critical—someone who might truly judge his work—was irresistible. That someone was Politian.

And thus, the conflict ignited.

Scala sent Politian a Latin epigram, written in a light tone, featuring a mosquito—_culex_, a creature familiar along the banks of the Arno. But in his verse, he assigned it the feminine gender. Politian, ever attentive to detail, corrected him in a polite epigram of his own, noting the error with scholarly precision. Scala responded with a witty retort, subtly mocking Politian’s failed courtship. Politian found the joke charming—yet deeply flawed in Latin form. He decided, out of friendship, to send Scala a Greek epigram of his own, on the same subject. It was a clever piece, weaving myth into zoology: Venus, born from the sea, and the mosquito, likewise emerging from water—both creatures of watery origin.

Scala, in reply, claimed his verses were never meant for a scholar so refined as Politian, whose taste was so delicate that even sturgeon must seem bland. He defended his work, admitting it was hastily written, meant only as a summer pastime for those satisfied with mediocrity—unlike others who sought fame through booksellers. As for the Greek epigram, he confessed he barely understood it, let alone appreciated its subtleties. Still, he ventured a critique: comparing the mosquito to Venus might offend the goddess herself. Moreover, wasn’t Thales’ doctrine—that all things originated from water—more applicable to fish than insects? Perhaps a bat or owl, given their nocturnal habits, would be more fitting metaphors.

Politian seized the opportunity. He dismissed Scala’s defense as trivial. Virgil himself, he reminded him, called the mosquito “the nursling of waters”—an ancient authority that settled the matter. As for the comparisons to birds and beasts, they were “nihil ad rem” (nothing to the point). The eagle flew higher, but that did not mean the mosquito could not fly at all. He added, with measured scorn, that he had no deceit in his nature and would not be fooled by flattery in his absence—quoting Homer to underscore his integrity.

The quarrel escalated. Scala grew more defensive, insisting on his reverence for antiquity and lamenting the decline of his age. He accused Politian of arrogance, of pretending to be a Hercules purging literary monsters, yet failing to recognize his own excesses. In return, Politian returned fire with biting wit. He called Scala a “branny monster,” born in the refuse of a mill, fit only to turn the family grindstones—“in pistrini sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus.” The insult was both personal and profound.

It was against this backdrop that Tito Melema arrived. The papers detailing this feud had been brought forth specifically to test the young Greek scholar’s judgment. Scala wanted an impartial opinion—especially on Politian’s Greek epigram. After exchanging pleasantries about Tito’s travels and examining a collection of ancient gems—among them an agate showing Cupid riding a lion, and a “Jew’s stone” with a serpent crowned by a lion’s head—conversation turned naturally to classical studies and the state of learning in Florence.

Politian, they discussed, was brilliant but overbearing. He styled himself a guardian of purity in literature, writing letters without signing them as if they were divine revelations. Yet his own critiques were often questionable. He mocked those who imitated Cicero too closely, ending every sentence with “esse videtur,” yet didn’t he himself fall into another trap—favoring rare words and affected phrases?

Tito, having just read Politian’s ‘Miscellanea,’ offered thoughtful observations. He praised some passages, critiqued others—always with care and balance. His insights pleased Scala immensely. Here was a man who could see both strength and flaw in scholarship, who could appreciate satire even in vulgar speech.

Encouraged, Scala produced a few of his own sonnets—light-hearted verses poking fun at Politian’s quirks—though he hesitated to share them fully. But Tito listened with interest, laughing gently at the barbs.

As the interview concluded, Tito was warmly invited to return. His gems were intriguing, yes—but he himself was more so. He had come recommended by Bardo de’ Bardi, a respected scholar and old friend of Scala’s. But now, he had earned his place through his own merits.

Tito left the palace under a bright sky, walking through the Porta Pinti with a smile. The day had gone well. He had passed the test, won the approval of a powerful man, and seen the absurdity of a learned quarrel over a mosquito. Fortune, it seemed, had taken his hand once more. And as he walked, he allowed himself a quiet laugh—knowing full well that the real battle had only just begun.
