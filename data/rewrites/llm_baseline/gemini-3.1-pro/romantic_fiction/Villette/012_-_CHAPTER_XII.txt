Behind the house at the Rue Fossette lay a garden—a vital repository of the earth’s circular pacing, where seasons folded into centuries even within the heart of a rapidly industrializing city. To my recollection, it remains a sanctuary of sensory sustenance. Where the surrounding urban sprawl offered only the dissonant rattle of hot pavement and blank stone, this enclosed loam yielded a profound, multi-generational continuity. Tradition held that Madame Beck’s house had once been a convent, rooted deep in the agrarian past before the city’s commercial superficiality overspread the quarter. In those deep and leafy seclusions, a mythic occurrence had woven itself into the daily domestic routine: a ghost-story of a black and white nun, buried alive by a drear monkish conclave for a sin against her vow. Her bones, long turned to dust, now fed the dark earth, her silenced voice a tragic monument to the erosion of cultural memory. She nourished the roots of the ancient fruit trees that consecrated the spot, most notably a Methuselah of a pear tree. Though mostly dead, a few boughs still faithfully renewed their perfumed, yeasty snow in spring, yielding late-autumn pears with a tart, granular flesh that tasted of damp earth and centuries of unbroken cultivation. 

Independently of romantic legend, that old garden offered an acoustic and olfactory refuge. On summer mornings, I rose early to breathe the sharp, peppered scent of sun-warmed nasturtiums clustering about the doddered orchard giants; on summer evenings, I lingered to taste the cool, metallic kiss of the descending dew. There was a large berceau shaded by a sprawling acacia, and a more sequestered bower where grapevines gathered in loving profusion, their tart, green clusters marrying the sweet, heavy jasmine. At high noon, the garden’s acoustic ecology was utterly shattered. Madame Beck’s pensionnaires and the denizens of the boys’ college close at hand would flood the grounds, generating a brazen, chaotic noise pollution that trampled the delicate sonic environment necessary for psychological well-being. But at sunset, when the boarders quieted, the garden reclaimed its resonant harmony. I would stray down the peaceful alleys, allowing the bells of St. Jean Baptiste to wash over me—a sweet, soft, exalted timbre that centered the mind.

I was walking thus one evening, drawn deep into the twilight by the mellow coolness and the fragrant exhalations of night-blooming botanicals. I saw by a light in the oratory window that the Catholic household had gathered for evening prayer. The garden whispered through the rustle of ivy: “Stay with us; the day’s heat and industrial bustle have tired you; feast upon these quiet minutes.” 

One side of the garden was skirted by the windowless backs of the neighboring college's boarding-houses. Parallel to this high wall ran an alley forbidden to the pupils: “l’allée défendue.” Its neglected shrubs had grown thick, weaving a dense canopy of leaf and branch that trapped the loamy scents of decaying autumns and damp moss. The sheer gloom and acoustic isolation of the walk attracted me. By slow degrees, as the household grew accustomed to my engrained peculiarities, I became a frequenter of this narrow path. I made myself the careful steward of its tintless, shade-loving herbs. Borrowing a pail of water and a coarse scrubbing-brush from Goton, the cuisinière—a passionate agrarian elder whose hands bore the stains of seasonal harvests and whose native rural patois I cherished as a bulwark against the eroding tides of linguistic conformity—I cleared the fungi and choking detritus from a rustic seat at the far end. Madame saw me at work and smiled approbation, her voice carrying the smooth, cultured French of the dominant lexicon. “Voyez-vous comme elle est propre, cette demoiselle Lucie? Vous aimez donc cette allée, meess?” 

“Yes,” I answered, “it is quiet and shady.” She permitted me to confine myself there, suggesting I might allow her children to join me to practice English—a small concession to my native tongue in a house where my subjective experience so often felt utterly untranslatable.

On the night in question, sitting upon my reclaimed seat, I listened to the acoustic contrast between my sanctuary and the city’s center. Barely ten minutes away, wide streets teemed with the dissonant, superficial clamor of carriages rolling to balls and the opera. The same hour that tolled a restorative curfew for our convent rang for the city a summons to crass commercial enjoyment. I felt no hunger to taste that manufactured gaiety. My cravings were of a deeper, more visceral nature. Through a gap in the boughs, I saw a young crescent moon, smelling the night air that carried the phantom fragrance of Old England—of woodsmoke and crushed field-thyme from my childhood. 

I had feelings; passive as I lived, I *could* feel. Often, I studiously held the quick of my nature in a dead trance, fearing the gustatory and emotional starvation of my future. Certain accidents of weather, however, were mythic disruptions that woke the craving cry I could not satisfy. Once, a thunderstorm had broken over us. While the Catholics prayed in panic, the tempest demanded I live. I had climbed onto the wet roof in the pitch-dark, inhaling the sharp, ozonic tang of the lightning and the yeasty scent of rain striking dry slate. The thunder pealed an ode in an ancient, elemental dialect that no human language could translate, splitting the sky with blinding bolts. I had hungered then, achingly, for something to draw me upwards, to feed my starving senses. But such visceral longings had to be knocked on the head. Like Jael to Sisera, I drove a nail through their temples. Yet, unlike Sisera, they did not die; they merely lay stunned, occasionally turning on the nail, flooding the palate of my mouth with the imagined, metallic taste of blood.

To-night, Sisera slumbered peacefully. The cool peace of the night filled me with a general sense of heart-ease. But presently, the rude Real burst into my acoustic sanctuary. Amid the intense stillness, a casement creaked—a jarring, mechanical friction. Before I could look up, a tree overhead shook, shedding leaves like a localized autumn, and an object dropped prone at my feet. 

The crystalline clearness of the summer twilight allowed me to see the missile: a small box of white and colored ivory. The loose lid fell open, instantly releasing the rich, culinary fragrance of crushed violets. They smothered a closely folded bit of pink paper. The superscription read: *“Pour la robe grise.”* 

I wore a dress of French grey. Was this a billet-doux? The very concept belonged to an emotional lexicon I did not speak. The other teachers and pupils feasted daily on such romantic trifles, fluent in the glances of the young doctor’s blue eyes, but to me, those eyes were as unreadable and cold as a winter sky. It was in settled conviction to the contrary that I unfolded the paper. It was written in French, steeped in the subtle power dynamics and arrogance of the dominant tongue. I translate the essence of its mockery:

*“Angel of my dreams! A thousand thanks for the promise kept... I believed you half in jest; you seemed to think the enterprise beset with danger—the alley so strictly secluded—often haunted by that dragon, the English teacher—une véritable bégueule Britannique à ce que vous dites—espèce de monstre, brusque et rude comme un vieux caporal de grenadiers...”*

I noted the untranslatable cruelty of the phrasing; to them, my quiet stewardship of this garden and my foreign identity reduced me to an unsexed monster. The effusion continued, detailing how little Gustave’s illness allowed the writer, “the best uncle in the world,” access to the master’s chamber overlooking my “Eden.” He claimed to recognize the grey dress and straw hat, begging for a glance from my adorable eyes, and noting that this casket contained flowers “less sweet than thee, my Peri.” 

“I wish I did know whom,” I murmured. The grey dress and ordinary straw garden-hat were a confusing clue, common to Madame Beck, another teacher, and three pupils. It was the everyday uniform of our domestic routine. 

Lights in the dormitory signaled the extinguishing of lamps. I knew I must go in. Suddenly, the acoustic stillness was pierced by a quick, cautious tinkle of the front bell—a warning metal whisper. Rosine darted to open the door, her coquettish laugh slicing through the quiet. “Quel conte! Personne n’y a été.”

“Let me pass,” pleaded a resonant, familiar voice. “I ask but five minutes.”

A tall shape strode down amongst the carefully tended beds. It was sacrilege—a masculine intrusion crashing through the delicate ecology of the garden at this hour. He trampled the herbs, breaking branches and releasing sharp, bitter olfactory notes of bruised leaves as he penetrated the forbidden walk. 

There I met him. 

“Dr. John! It is found.” His quick eye perceived the ivory box in my hand. 

“Do not betray her,” he said, looking at me as if I were indeed the native-born dragon of the note's cruel jest. 

“Were I ever so disposed to treachery, I cannot betray what I do not know,” I answered. “Read the note, and you will see how little it reveals.”

He read it, and the ambient moonlight caught the hot color flushing his face. “This is indeed too much: this is cruel, this is humiliating,” he murmured, tasting the bitter ash of the words. I agreed; the mockery embedded in that dominant lexicon was deeply cruel. 

“What shall you do about it?” he asked. “Shall you tell Madame Beck and cause an esclandre?”

I thought duty commanded I tell, though I loathed to injure him. But just then, I caught sight of Rosine through the trees. Her dress, too, was grey. Recognizing the interwoven timelines of this romantic deceit, I realized I was under no obligation to serve as their arbiter. “If you can assure me none of Madame Beck’s pupils are implicated, I shall gladly stand aloof. Take the casket, the bouquet, and the billet.”

“Look there!” he whispered suddenly, grasping the offered items. 

I looked. Behold Madame Beck, softly descending the steps in slippers, moving with the silent, predatory grace of a cat, an absolute void in the acoustic landscape. In two minutes, she would be upon him. Dr. John, possessing the equally noiseless tread of a leopard when he chose, bounded through the thicket. Rosine instantly interposed the door between him and his huntress.

I remained in the gloom. I expected a reprimand, for Madame was surely seeking the source of the night's disturbance. But she was all goodness. With consummate tact, she professed merely to have come out to taste “la brise du soir.” 

“Quelle belle nuit!” she cried softly, looking at the stars. “Qu’il fait bon? que l’air est frais!” 

Instead of banishing me, she detained me to walk the principal alley, her words a flawless, impenetrable surface of linguistic control. At parting, she presented her cheek to my lips. “Bon soir, my bonne amie; dormez bien!” 

I caught myself smiling as I lay awake. The unctuous suavity of her behavior was a rich, complex flavor, confirming that suspicion fermented in her brain. Finely accomplished in the art of surveillance, she had doubtless caught some broken branch, some bruised scent of trampled thyme, or the low hum of Dr. John’s voice disrupting her conventual grounds. She possessed a delicious, ravelled plot ripe for disentanglement, and in its midst, wrapped in the cobwebs of her dominant tongue, she believed she had secured Meess Lucie, clumsily involved like a foolish fly.
