The air turned black as winter closed in around me, sharp and unrelenting. Above, a flickering fire blazed with sudden brilliance—no ordinary flame, but something alive, hovering like a breath caught between worlds. It paused, suspended, watching. As I stepped into its glow, it drifted forward, slow and deliberate, lingering over rocky patches of ground. Each time I looked up, it seemed to grow larger—its form shifting, luminous, until it cast a shadow beside me.

It was no bird, nor insect, but something beyond both: a creature of winged light, broad and nearly square, shimmering with every hue the spectrum could hold—and some that defied naming. I stood transfixed, captivated by its radiant beauty, so entranced that I tripped over a low stone and fell hard, stunned.

When I stirred again, it hovered above me, pulsing with color, its radiance weaving through the darkness like a symphony of unseen light. I rose, but my eyes remained fixed on it, unable to tear away. My foot struck another rock—this time, I sat down, not from injury, but from longing. I wanted it. I needed it. To have it in my hands, to feel its warmth, its magic.

And then—it descended.

Slowly at first, then faster, growing immense as it drew near. I felt the universe offering itself, a treasure too vast for words. I reached out—and grasped it.

But the moment my fingers closed around it, the light vanished. The world plunged into absolute dark. In my hand lay only a cold, lifeless book, its cover cracked open, pages blank. I hurled it into the air—heard it fall among the heather with a dull thud.

I buried my face in my palms, heart crushed. Then, the cold became unbearable. Fear of freezing drove me back to my feet.

Instantly, a faint sensation returned—a whisper of light. “Is it waking?” I whispered, hope flaring like a spark in the void.

No. It was the moon—just beginning to rise, sharp and pale, cutting through the horizon like a blade of silver ice. She gave me sight—but no direction. She did not hover. She did not wait. She offered only choice, blind and cruel.

Her face was full, her gaze piercing. But she was not Earth’s moon. Her light felt alien, strange—perhaps born of a sun I had never known. Every glance upward found her staring, unblinking, as if studying me. At first, I resented it—the intrusion of a silent watcher. Then, slowly, I sensed something deeper: not judgment, but sorrow. A quiet pity. Why was I here? Awake in this endless night?

That was when I truly understood: to be conscious in the cosmos is to be alone—and helpless.

My feet left the heather behind, stepping onto bare, spongy earth—like dry peat, yet unnaturally soft. Beneath me, the ground trembled. Then, a ripple passed ahead, shadowy under the moonlight, like a wave through water. It faded into distance—then, a single swell rose, crested, and broke.

From within burst a beast—tiger-like, but twisted, covered in clots of soil. Its eyes flared, teeth bared in a silent snarl. I froze. Not from fear—not yet. From shock. Then, without warning, it turned and vanished beneath the surface, as though swallowed by the land itself.

“That moon,” I muttered, “is playing tricks on my mind.” I walked on, muttering to myself. “What kind of life exists here? Only dreams? Phantoms? I am walking through a nightmare made real.”

But I didn’t know—then—that the moon was my shield. That her light held these things at bay. That without it, I would have been torn apart long before I took a second step.

“I will not fear what only seems,” I told myself. Yet even as I said it, dread curled in my gut. For the ground beneath me was not solid—it was a sea, and monsters swam below.

A few steps ahead, something began to emerge from the earth. A head—massive, like a polar bear’s—rose slowly, crowned with a mane of white fur, neck red as blood. The body followed, writhing, grotesque, each inch of its length dragging free with a sickening twist. When it was whole, it collapsed, panting, struggling to burrow again.

“Does it feed on the dead?” I wondered. “Why does it not touch me?”

I didn’t know then that the moon paralyzed them. That their power was bound by her gaze.

All night, they came—creatures unlike any other. Some were beautiful in horror: a serpent coiled with feathers of emerald, sapphire, and gold, its scales flashing like stained glass. Others were pure nightmare—limbs too many, faces split, bodies fused. And still, they did not attack. They watched. They lurked. They threatened—but never touched.

I grew numb to their presence. I began to imagine worse horrors than any real one could be. I conjured beasts from shadows, laughing at my own fears—unaware that every breath I took was granted by the moon’s watchful eye.

Her light was not divine. It was not primal. But it was enough. Light, even reflected, even borrowed, still holds dominion over darkness. Even the last echo of a dying sun can bind evil.

Had I known—had I understood—that if the moon set while I was still within her reach, I would be devoured in an instant, I might have run faster. I might have fought harder. But I did not. I only watched her sink, slow and solemn, toward the edge of the hills I had chosen as my goal.

Then—silence.

The ground ceased its wallowing. The burrows lay still.

And there, walking across the pale soil, was a woman.

She moved slowly, wrapped in a mist that shifted like cloth—now forming a robe, now dissolving into vapor. The wind tugged at her, swirling the fog around her limbs. She was beautiful—yet her face bore a terrible duality: pride and pain, dignity and despair. Her eyes were hollow, dead. On her left side, a dark stain pulsed faintly, as if infected. She pressed her hand against it, as though trying to silence a scream.

Her hair fell to her feet, pale gold in the moonlight. Sometimes the wind tangled it with the mist, and I could not tell where one ended and the other began.

Then—she doubled over, clutching her chest. The mist lifted, rising into the air like smoke from a dying fire.

I ran to her.

She convulsed. Her legs detached—snakes, swift and sinuous—slithering away. Her arms fled from her shoulders, also serpentine, vanishing into the dark. Something flew from her back—a bat-like shape—then she was gone.

The ground surged like a storm-tossed sea. Terror seized me. I turned and ran—toward the hills.

I reached the slope just as the moon dipped behind a peak, casting me into shadow.

Behind me, a sound erupted—raw, agonizing, like a cry of thwarted hunger. The first voice I’d heard since the butterfly died.

I turned. Shapes bounded after me—dark, swift, hungry. I sprinted for the ridge, where the moon still shone.

She lingered there—just long enough for me to see.

I climbed, gasping. The creatures panted behind me. One lunged—snarling, jaws wide.

We crashed into the moonlight together.

She flashed—bright, furious—and the beast dissolved into a smear on the ground.

Strength flooded me. I turned, facing the rest.

One by one, they darted into her light—and dropped, howling, crumbling into nothing.

And high above, I saw it: a smile upon the moon’s face—strange, knowing, almost mocking.

I reached the top of the ridge. The moon was sinking, low and distant, fading into the horizon.

The air was clear, crisp, alive.

I descended slightly, found warmth, and sat to wait.

The moon slipped beneath the world.

And once more, darkness claimed everything.
