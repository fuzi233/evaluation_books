The rain had kept them indoors at Offendene, and Grandcourt, lounging on the sofa, watched Gwendolen with a lazy satisfaction. She had taken up embroidery since their engagement, though her stitches were more haphazard than precise.

“The carriage will come for your mother tomorrow,” he said. “You can see Diplow and decide what changes you’d like before we leave for Ryelands.”

Gwendolen let the needle pause. “Why must it be tomorrow?”

“Because the day after, the hunt begins.”

“And after that?”

“I’ll be away for two days.” His fingers brushed hers. “Do you mind?”

“It hardly matters if I do,” she said coolly, resisting the urge to demand if he was visiting *her*—the woman whose shadow lurked behind their engagement.

Grandcourt smiled, indulgent. “I could delay. Or travel by night and return sooner.”

“Then don’t delay,” she replied, enjoying her power even as irritation prickled beneath it.

He laughed softly. “How cruel you are to us poor men.”

“*Are* we so cruel?” She met his gaze, half-hoping he’d confess some vulnerability, some fault that might justify her own doubts.

“Always.” His voice dropped. “Are you as kind to me as I am to you?”

The question struck her. She had taken so much—his wealth, his name—and now she was bound to the chariot she’d chosen, its reins in his hands. There was no turning back.

She forced a smile. “If I were as kind as you, your generosity would mean less.”

He lifted her hand to his lips, leaving her oddly unsettled.

---

At Diplow the next day, Gwendolen moved through the rooms with deliberate distraction. She had resolved to ignore Deronda—until she saw him. Then, despite herself, her attention sharpened.

“You remember me, I hope?” he said when introduced.

“Too well,” she replied lightly. “You disapproved of my roulette.”

“Did I?” His steady gaze unsettled her.

“You brought me bad luck.”

Grandcourt scoffed. “Roulette is a bore.”

“It was, once I started losing.” She glanced at Deronda, catching an expression that pierced her more than any critique. There was no mockery in it—only a quiet intensity that made her feel *seen*.

Later, she found herself drifting toward him. “Do you hunt tomorrow?”

“If the weather allows.”

“And you don’t object to it?”

“Only when I can’t boat or cricket instead.”

“Do you object to *me* hunting?” she challenged.

“I’ve no right to object to anything you choose.”

“Yet you judged my gambling.”

The words slipped out, and color rose in her cheeks. Deronda’s own face flushed—whether from the reference to the necklace or something else, she couldn’t tell. The silence between them thickened, charged with something unspoken.

---

That night, Gwendolen lay awake, her thoughts circling Deronda. Who was he, really? A ward, a bastard, a man with no name yet an air of quiet command. She wondered if he pitied her, if he guessed at the shadows in her marriage.

Her mother’s offhand gossip about his birth gnawed at her. An unknown mother, a life of borrowed privilege—had he, too, been cheated of something? The idea twisted inside her, sharp as regret.

By morning, the thrill of the hunt pushed aside her unease. She would see him again, and this time, she wouldn’t falter.

But the chase swept them apart, and as twilight fell, frustration mounted. Then, on the road home, she reined in her horse and looked back.

“Go on,” she ordered Grandcourt. Then, with a flick of her whip toward Deronda: “I wish to speak with *him*.”

The words were defiance, a claiming of her own will. What she would say, she didn’t yet know. But for the first time, she meant to find out.
