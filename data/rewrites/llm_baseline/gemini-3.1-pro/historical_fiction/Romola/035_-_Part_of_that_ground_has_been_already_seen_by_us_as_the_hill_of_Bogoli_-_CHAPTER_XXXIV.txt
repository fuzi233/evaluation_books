Messer Naldo returned to the hill of San Giorgio sooner than expected, ascending the winding paths on the evening of the twenty-eighth of November. As he climbed, the harsh, unnatural dissonance of the Florentine streets—the jarring clamor of mercenaries in the Prato and the metallic clink of commercial ambition—faded into the rhythmic, grounding acoustic ecology of the agrarian heights. Here, the sonic environment was a balm to his agitated psyche. Time did not march in the linear, sterile progression of the city below; it moved in sweeping, circular rhythms dictated by the season, the harvest, and the hearth. 

More than a year and a half had passed since Tito first wove this sanctuary for Tessa. Initially born of a soft-hearted, self-indulgent impulse to protect a trusting creature, this domestic refuge had become profoundly necessary to his sensory and emotional sustenance. Romola’s world, while stirring his highest ideal passions, was built on an abstract, morally rigorous architecture that often neglected the physical senses. Her unyielding Florentine lexicon and high-minded expectations felt increasingly like a starvation of his immediate, tactile desires. Tessa’s world, by contrast, was a rich, olfactory landscape of roasting chestnuts, crushed wild fennel, and the deep, yeasty warmth of agrarian life. 

Tito was meticulous in preserving this dynamic. He consciously weaponized the subtle power dynamics embedded in their language, keeping Tessa firmly rooted in her native *contadina* dialect. To introduce her to the dominant, educated lexicon of his world would be to shatter the untranslatable subjective innocence he relied upon. If she were to learn the broader language of the city, her world would widen, her childish pleasures would cease, and the mythic, pastoral charm of her existence would be polluted. By keeping her illiterate and bound to the sensory joys of a squirrel-like hoarding of fresh walnuts and wild honey, Tito maintained supreme control, relishing the sweet, grounding sustenance her rustic life provided while keeping his own exhausting secrets safe.

It was just past eight o’clock when he stepped onto the stone threshold of her room. The acoustic sanctuary of the house was entirely undisturbed, save for a singular, rhythmic sound: the soft, percussive *click-clack* of olive-wood rosary beads slipping through fingers. Tito opened the door to a space thick with the scent of dried sage, sweet milk, and the lingering, rich smoke of the dying hearth fire. A single dim light illuminated Tessa, kneeling beside the bed where her baby slept. Her head rested sideways on the pillow, her brown rosary dangling from a loose grip beneath the golden palm-branches and the icon of the Madonna. She had fallen asleep to the cadence of her own prayers.

Tito moved silently across the terracotta floor and sat beside her. She opened her blue eyes with the slow, languid grace of one emerging from a deep, cyclical dream, looking at him without starting.

“I dreamed it,” she whispered in her rustic tongue, the dialect rounding the edges of her words like worn river stones, “and then I said it was dreaming—and then I awoke, and it was true.”

“Little sinner,” Tito murmured, pinching her chin, his smooth, dominant Italian contrasting sharply with her rural phrasing. “You have not said half your prayers. I will punish you by not looking at your baby; it is ugly.”

Tessa pouted, her brow furrowing with genuine distress as she bent over the sleeping infant. “Ah, it is not true! He is prettier than anything. You will look at him. He is even prettier than when you saw him before. Only he is asleep, and you cannot see his eyes or his tongue, and I cannot show you his hair—and it grows—isn’t that wonderful? If you kiss him very gently, he won’t wake. You want to kiss him, is it not true?”

Tito appeased her by brushing a soft, moth-like kiss against the infant’s forehead, breathing in the sweet, milky fragrance of new life. “You like looking at the baby better than looking at your husband, you false one,” he teased, turning her face toward his.

She rested her hands on his knee, looking up at him with the wide, adoring devotion of a painted angel. “No,” she said, shaking her head. “I love you always best, only I want you to look at the bambino and love him. I used only to want you to love me. Did you come back sooner because of the beautiful chain-coat Messer San Michele gave you? You have it on now.” She peaked through the opening of his tunic, her fingers grazing the hidden metal. 

“Perhaps it did, Tessa,” Tito replied, needing to steer away from the jarring reminder of his armor. “But tell me of your days. Did the noise of the soldiers reach you here? Did you hear the drums and trumpets?”

“Yes, and the sounds frightened me,” she said, shrinking a little at the memory of the industrial, military noise pollution that had threatened to pierce their quiet hill. “Monna Lisa was afraid they would carry off our kids. But the Holy Madonna protected us. Yet, something else has happened, and that is what I was saying more Aves for.”

Tito’s stomach tightened. “Make haste and tell me.”

Tessa climbed onto his knee, needing the tactile reassurance of his embrace. “At first I didn’t mean to tell you,” she whispered, as if speaking softly could mitigate the transgression. “We thought he would be gone before you came. But he is still here. I wanted to show him my baby, and he was a stranger with nowhere to sleep, so I made Monna Lisa say he could rest on the straw next to the goats. And when he returns in the evening, I talk to him, and I bring him sustenance.”

“Some beggar, I suppose,” Tito said, his voice clipped. “I must have him sent away.”

“No, not a beggar,” Tessa insisted, her culinary pride showing. “He works for his keep, and I feed him well—a thick, nourishing *ribollita* of dark winter kale, white beans, and oil pressed from the ancient groves. He eats as one who has been starved of all goodness. But sometimes, I think his mind is broken. He looks as if he doesn’t know where he is.”

“What sort of face has he?” Tito’s heart began a frantic, arrhythmic pounding, a violent disruption of his inner calm. 

“His face is yellow, with deep marks, and his hair is white. He looks at me from under black eyebrows and says, ‘Poor thing!’ as if I were still beaten by my stepfather. I asked his name, but he couldn’t tell me. It is as if he has forgotten the words for everything. He has a book, and he stares at it, but his lips never move.”

Tito ceased to see the warmly lit, herb-scented room. The subjective terror that gripped him was wholly untranslatable; a profound gulf opened beneath him, dropping him back to the sun-baked steps of the Duomo. Destiny had collapsed the timelines, bringing the mythic figure of the betrayed father directly into the heart of his agrarian sanctuary. Tito realized, with a desperate, calculating rush, that there were no witnesses here. He had gold from the sale of Bardo’s library. If he could bridge the gap, if he could use his honeyed words to extract forgiveness, he could restore the acoustic and emotional peace of his life. He could replace the bitter venom of Baldassarre’s hatred with the sweet, restorative taste of reconciliation.

“My Tessa, get me a lantern,” Tito said, standing abruptly. “Don’t cry, little pigeon. I am not angry.” 

When they reached the bottom of the stairs, he stopped her. “Stay here. I want no lantern. Keep quiet.”

In half a minute, Tito stood before the outhouse. The moon washed the unpainted, weathered wood in a pale, ghostly light. The night was profoundly quiet, save for the rustle of dry straw and the breath of the goats within. For a second, Tito hesitated, a primal instinct shrinking from the tiger-glance he knew waited inside. 

Within the barn, Baldassarre was not sleeping. He sat in the silvery stream of moonlight, plunging through the most wretched depths of language death. He had spent the daylight hours staring at the open book beside him, fighting the tragic erosion of his own cultural memory. The rich, nuanced lexicons of ancient Greek, the precise names of the chalcedony and sardonyx symbols engraved on his stolen jewels—all were slipping away, dissolving into an impenetrable darkness. This loss of language left him paralyzed, unable to articulate his own history, reducing his entire existence to a solitary pulse of untranslatable rage. He sat clutching the hilt of his unsheathed dagger, his mind narrowed to a single, sensory dream: the physical, yielding resistance of plunging that blade into a base heart.

Tito pulled the heavy door. It dragged against the uneven earth with a rough, grating scrape—an abrasive sound that startled Baldassarre from his agonizing void. The old man rose to one knee, disoriented in the hay-scented dark. As the door swung wide, the moonlight fell upon Tito’s dark curls and smooth olive cheek. 

It was an apparition of water offered to a man dying of thirst. Before Tito could even draw a breath, the mythic fury of a wronged generation exploded. Baldassarre sprang forward with preternatural force, the dagger flashing in the lunar light. 

A violent, unnatural *CRACK* shattered the acoustic sanctity of the barn. 

The dagger had snapped cleanly in two against the hidden rings of Tito’s chainmail. Under the parrying force of Tito’s arm, Baldassarre was thrown backward, collapsing into the sweet-smelling straw, clutching nothing but a broken hilt. The shattered blade lay gleaming at Tito’s feet.

A great leap of terror gave way to the intoxicating rush of deliverance. His armor had held; his vengeance-obsessed father lay helpless in the hay. Yet Tito felt no devilish urge to strike. Instead, looking down at the broken elder, the prospect of an easy reconciliation beckoned—a way to cleanse his palate of this lingering, sour dread. He sought to impose his own narrative using the language of their shared past. 

“*Padre mio*!” Tito’s voice was soft, resonant, carrying the exact cadence it had on the shores of Greece. 

There was no sound but the uneven, ragged breathing of the old man. 

“I came to ask your forgiveness!” Tito paused, allowing the honeyed, dominant words to sink in. “I was taken by surprise that morning. I wish now to be a son to you again. I wish to make the rest of your life happy, that you may forget what you have suffered.”

He waited. Perhaps Baldassarre’s mind was too shattered by the language death he was undergoing to even comprehend the offer. But slowly, trembling from the physical shock of the fall, the old man tossed aside the useless hilt and began to push himself up from the agrarian earth. Tito extended a hand to aid him, a flicker of irritation already crossing his mind at the irksome burden of maintaining this restored bond. 

Baldassarre clutched the offered hand. He pulled himself up, drawing so close that their faces were mere inches apart, their breaths mingling in the cold air. When Baldassarre finally spoke, he rejected Tito’s manipulative, abstract lexicon entirely. His deep, trembling voice was rooted only in the primal, undeniable truths of sustenance and theft.

“I saved you—I nurtured you—I loved you. You forsook me—you robbed me—you denied me. What can you give me?” Baldassarre’s eyes burned with a singular, unquenchable fire. “You have made the world bitterness to me; but there is one draught of sweetness left—*that you shall know agony*.”

He dropped Tito’s hand, retreating into the shadows of the barn, sinking back onto the straw, utterly exhausted by the final, failed outleap of his physical and linguistic power.
