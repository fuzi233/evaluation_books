I had been at my monotonous scraping for but a few moments when the heavy, oppressive silence of the forest floor was broken by a delicate shift in the acoustic ecology. Small voices, harmonious and resonant like wind through dry reeds, murmured near me. Presently, the Little Ones—as I would soon come to know them—emerged from the fragrant underbrush of tiny, spice-scented saplings that filled the gaps between the ancient, monolithic trees. In a minute, scores of them surrounded me, their presence an immediate balm to the fractured soundscape left by the giants.

I gestured frantically, trying to communicate that the massive brutes had only just departed and might return, but the children merely laughed. It was a cascading sound, a multitude of silver sheep-bells that cleared the stagnant air. 

“They are too blind to see us,” they sang out, their voices carrying the lilting cadence of a deeply rooted, native dialect. “The wind is quite clean of them.”

“Do you like that rough binding about your ankles?” asked one boy, his tone carrying the melodic curiosity of their tongue. 

“I need them to believe I am still bound,” I replied, my own voice feeling thick and unrefined compared to theirs. 

“They can scarcely see their own feet!” he rejoined, dancing with a rhythmic merriment that perfectly complemented the rustling leaves. “Walk with short steps and they will think the rope is undisturbed.”

A larger girl knelt beside me, her hands smelling faintly of crushed wild mint and damp loam. I assumed her delicate fingers could do nothing with the crude, industrial knot, but with a few deft, practiced movements—like a baker turning a fragile dough—the fibrous rope fell away. 

They coaxed me to sit, instantly transforming the grim earth into a vibrant site of communal sustenance. From woven pouches, they produced a mosaic of wild woodland fruits. They fed me berries that burst on the palate with tart, complex notes of dark honey, petrichor, and sun-warmed citrus. It was a profound offering, the sharing of a terroir that the giants wholly ignored. As I savored the lingering finish of the juices, the smaller children engaged me in wild, joyous play, pulling me into their ancient, domestic rhythm until the late afternoon sun began to cast long, amber shadows.

Suddenly, the harmonious woodland symphony was shattered. Heavy, arrhythmic thuds approached—a violent, industrial noise pollution that shook the very soil and disrupted our psychological sanctuary. The children scattered. I hastily pulled the coarse rope back around my ankles.

“We must have a care,” whispered the girl who had freed me, her voice a hushed rustle of warning. “A crush of one of their horrid, stumpy feet might kill a very little one!”

“Can they not perceive you at all?” I asked.

“They might see a blur of movement. If the children were piled upon you as they were, it would be terrible. They possess no ear for the forest’s song; they hate every living thing but themselves—not that they are truly alive, existing only to consume.” She emitted a sharp, trilling whistle, perfectly mimicking a local nightjar. In a blink, the visual and sonic landscape was utterly devoid of them.

My master, deaf to the nuanced vibrations of the wood, lumbered into the clearing to claim me. He freed my ankles, dragged me to the threshold of his utilitarian hovel, threw me to the dirt, retied my feet with brutal efficiency, and delivered a hollow kick before abandoning me to the silence. 

I could have slipped my bonds and fled into the twilight, but I was tethered by a sudden, fierce devotion. I had found a community that understood the profound meaning of shared sustenance and acoustic harmony. I resolved to stay, to fold myself into the generational cycles of these children. As the heavy, lifeless sleep of the giants settled over the huts, the air around me hummed with harmonic, protective whispers. I was being lovingly watched.

From that day forward, the Little Ones wove their timeline seamlessly into mine. The giants, with their sterile, fuel-driven existence, offered nothing to know; they were too dull to invent cruelties, and their crude attempts at kicks were easily thwarted. The children, however, transformed my monotonous toil. They were an agrarian society in miniature, profoundly wise in the old ways of the earth. My bed was the bare ground, yet I awoke each dawn in a living, breathing nest of children. Their overlapping breaths created a rhythmic, therapeutic soundscape, insulating me from the night's chill. Their first domestic duty of the dawn, and the last before dusk, was to bring me a feast of meticulously foraged provisions.

One morning, the natural cycle shifted. I awoke to a subdued, reverent procession. The tall girl, Lona—recognized by the community as a traditional elder despite her youth—stepped from the deep woods. The multitude followed in jubilant, breathless silence, careful not to pollute the quiet and wake the giant snoring violently nearby. 

In her arms, Lona cradled a boy-baby. Until now, a yearling girl had been their youngest. Among this society, the concept of a lifeless doll was unknown; caretaking was an unbroken, generational chain, the older tending the younger in an endless, circular rhythm of domestic life.

Lona approached and gently laid the infant in my arms. He opened eyes like polished acorns, stared at me, and fell back into a tranquil sleep. 

“He loves you already!” she whispered, her voice a soothing cadence.

“Where did you find him?” I asked.

“In the wood, of course,” she smiled, her eyes reflecting the deep canopy. “Where we always gather them. Isn’t he a beauty? We’ve been out all night listening for him. Sometimes, their quietest cries are swallowed by the wind.”

“How do you know when there is one to find?” 

“The knowledge passes among us like a changing breeze,” she replied. “We never find out who spoke the first word. Sometimes I think one murmurs it in their sleep, and another catches the sound half-awake. The moment we know, we drop all tasks. Once found, the origin is already lost.”

“Do more boys or girls come to the wood?”

“They do not come to the wood; we go to the wood and harvest them.”

“Are there more boys or girls among you now?” I asked, attempting to quantify them—a habit of my own rigid lexicon. To ask precisely the same question twice made them knit their brows, their native dialect resisting such sterile categorization.

“I do not know,” she answered.

“You can count them, surely!”

“We never do that. To count is to sever the whole into pieces. It wouldn’t be smooth to the ear. We would rather not know.”

“Where do the babies come from first?”

“From the wood—always. There is no other soil they can spring from.” Her cultural memory stopped there, treating their advent as an untranslatable, mythic occurrence seamlessly integrated into their daily foraging.

“How often do you find one?”

“Such a harvest takes all the gladness we possess, and we forget the time before. You too are glad to hold him, are you not, good giant?”

“Yes, I am,” I answered warmly. “But how do you nourish him?”

“I will show you the old way,” she said. She departed and returned with three flawlessly ripe, indigo plums, their skins dusted with a natural bloom. Lona’s culinary technique was a marvel of meticulous preservation. She did not merely feed the child; she engaged in an intimate act of extraction. Pinching the fruit gently, her fingers worked the skin, macerating the soft flesh inside without breaking the surface. She brought it to the baby’s lips.

“He would open his mouth if he were awake,” she explained. She squeezed with measured, precise pressure, forcing a single, golden drop of nectar—rich with the scent of wild orchids and summer rain—to the surface. The sleeping infant instantly began to suckle. Lona continued her slow, deliberate extraction until only the bitter stone and fibrous skin remained, ensuring the child received the pure essence while discarding the indigestible chaff.

“There!” she murmured in gentle triumph. “What a sterile, big-apple world it would be if there were nothing for the babies to taste! We wouldn’t remain in it. We would leave it to the bad giants.”

“But what if the stone slipped into the baby’s mouth during the pressing?” I asked.

“No mother would be so careless with her craft,” she replied. “I shouldn’t be fit to tend a baby.”

I marveled at the profound meaning she found in this simple sustenance. It led me back to the mystery of their lineage. “Will you tell me where you lived before?”

“Here,” she replied.

“Have you never lived anywhere else?”

“Never. We all emerged from the wood. Some believe we ripened and dropped from the branches.”

“Why are there so many of you who remain quite little?” 

“I do not understand your words. Some are smaller, some are bigger. I am very big.”

“Baby will grow bigger, won't he?”

“Of course he will!”

“And will you grow bigger?”

“I hope not. I am the biggest. The thought terrifies me.”

“Why should it?”

She offered no answer. I pressed on, utilizing the dominant lexicon of my own world. “How old are you?”

“I do not know what you mean. We are all just exactly what we are.”

“How big will the baby grow?”

“I cannot tell.” A sudden acoustic tremor of anxiety entered her voice. “Some... some begin to grow after we think they have settled. That is a frightful thing. We lack the words to talk about it.”

“What makes it frightful?”

She paused, the silence heavy. “We fear they may be forgetting the language. We fear they are growing into giants.”

Before long, I began to observe the erosion of cultural memory Lona feared. Among two or three of the smaller children, traces of greed surfaced. They hoarded berries, refusing to participate in the communal meals. The older girls watched them with acute, silent anxiety. Yet, their loving ministrations toward me never ceased. They sang to me for hours, their voices interweaving in complex, polyphonic melodies, climbing my tree to pop meticulously peeled fruits into my mouth. 

One afternoon, a moody little boy sang me a strange, crooning song. Its refrain was deeply melodic and rich in sorrow. Though the words belonged to a dead dialect I could not translate, the sheer acoustic emotion of the performance resonated within my chest, causing hot tears to run down my face. 

This physical reaction plunged the assembly into profound perplexity. In this world, they had never witnessed water—falling, lying, or running. Moisture was known only through the sap of vines or the juice of fruits. They possessed a dim, instinctive perception of weeping, but interpreted it through their gustatory lens. A very small child marched up to the singer, shook a clenched fist, and scolded him in their melodic tongue: 

“‘Ou skeeze ze juice out of ze good giant’s seeberries! Bad giant!”

Later, as Lona sat at the foot of my tree, feeding the baby, I asked, “Why do I never see any children among the giants?”

She looked at me as if my syntax had shattered. “They are giants; their lexicon has no word for little ones.”

“Do they never have children?”

“No. The wood yields nothing for them. They have no palate for love. If they heard our songs, they would stamp us into the mud.”

“Are there always the same number of giants, then? I once thought they were your fathers and mothers.”

She burst into a cascading, bell-like laughter. “No, good giant; we are their ancestors.” 

But the laughter died in her throat, leaving a chilling acoustic void. I stopped my scraping. “How can that be?”

“The translation is difficult,” she murmured. “We were here, speaking the old words, and they were not. They fade from us. They could have chosen to remember, but they forgot.”

“How long have you been here?” I asked, attempting to map this circular timeline.

“Always. The song of us has always been sung. But the giants were not made always. If a Little One stops listening, he grows greedy for heavy food. Then lazy, then large, then deaf, and finally, bad. The dull creatures forget they were once us. Very few believe our language even exists. They call it nonsense! Look at little Blunty over there. He is eating one of their apples! He will be the next. He will soon be bloated and deaf, and he will not even know he has forgotten us.”

I looked. Blunty stood apart, gorging himself on a massive giant’s apple. The fruit was a monolithic, commercialized mass of sustenance—devoid of complex flavor, terroir, or sensory joy, serving only as utilitarian fuel. It was an offensive sight. 

“I will take the flavorless thing from him!” I declared.

“It is useless,” Lona said, her voice carrying the mournful tone of a dying language. “We have offered our finest fruits, but it is too late. When he refused to share his harvest, claiming the yield solely for himself, we knew his palate was corrupted. He is a glutton, lost to the giants' grammar of consumption.”

“Could the boys not guard him from the poisonous crop?”

“It is the desire for the apple that changes him. To eat the apple is to speak the word that makes you a giant. He belongs to their vocabulary now. Look at him—he has swelled since yesterday.”

“He looks as hideous and green as the lump in his hand!”

“It suits the mold he is pouring himself into. He hates the giants, yet he consumes their fuel. Oh, baby,” she crooned to the infant, “he was just such a perfect verse as you when we found him!”

“Will he hate the Little Ones when he changes?”

“He will not remember us. He will lose the capacity to perceive our voices. He will eat his mass-produced apples in silence.”

“I do not understand your world,” I sighed. “I come from a place where everything is governed by different rules.”

“I do not know this word, ‘world.’ What is it? Just a heavy sound in your beautiful big mouth. You speak it, and it claims space.”

“Never mind the word. Tell me what happens next to Blunty.”

“He will wake and find himself a giant. Not a mindful one like you, but a deaf, consuming brute. He will believe he has always been massive. Peony says the giants have lost their own origin story, and that is why they never sing. They have no babies, only hunger. I wonder what the word ‘bad’ truly tastes like, good giant!”

“I wish I knew no more of it than you,” I replied softly. “But I try to preserve the good.”

A long pause settled, filled only by the wind in the canopy. 

“Then you truly do not know how the babies arrive in the wood?” I asked one last time, probing the edges of their cultural memory.

“There is no knowledge there,” she insisted. “They are in the wood; they ripen there.”

“Then why do you never find one before it is fully formed?”

She frowned, the dialect of her ancestors meeting a logical wall. “They are not there until the recipe is finished. Little Tolma, the one before this baby, looked as if she carried a grand story when I found her under a beech tree, sucking her thumb. But she lacked the words. She only looked at me. She will never forget the old tongue! When they begin to bloat, they care for nothing but dominance and mass. The bad giants measure their worth only by their fat.”

“In my world,” I noted bitterly, “they do not use the word ‘fat.’ They say ‘rich.’”

“In their central hovel,” Lona continued, critiquing the power dynamics of their oppressors, “sits the largest of them. He hoards so much that no one can see him. The giants make pilgrimages to his door, chanting a degraded lexicon of praise, begging him to grant them the strength to consume more and grow fat like him.”

A few days later, a subtle shift in the acoustic environment signaled a profound loss: Blunty had vanished. The older children wore faces of quiet mourning, recognizing the death of a native speaker, though the younger ones barely registered the absence. 

The following dawn, Lona touched my arm and pointed toward a twisted quince tree. “Look there. That is the giant that was Blunty. Would you have recognized him?”

“Never,” I breathed. “Though, now that you say it, I can see Blunty’s features buried beneath that blank, stupid fog.”

“He does nothing but chew those flavorless apples now,” she said, her voice trembling. “That is the fate of Little Ones who refuse the nuance of the small.”

*They call it growing up in my world,* I thought to myself, the realization souring my stomach. If only she could teach me to reverse the rigid progression of my own timeline. Could I ever learn to laugh with the pure, resonant chime of sheep-bells? I had been given the chance to savor the profound meaning of a shared, sensory existence, and I had nearly discarded it for the utilitarian fuel of my past. Blunty and I were tragic mirrors. He did not know the language he had just lost, and I was only just beginning to realize the vocabulary I had to relearn.
