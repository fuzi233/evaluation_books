A ragged sigh escaped the gasping chest of Amos Cadwalader, a sound that spoke louder than words of a sorrow raw and unhealed, despite three decades having passed since the calamity Felix recounted.

Felix, mirroring the sentiment, pressed forward swiftly. "It was twilight when I laid bare my tale, and from shadow until dawn we sat, eyes locked upon one another's face, sleep and rest denied to us. Then we sought John Poindexter. Had he fled, we might have found mercy, but he met us openly, quietly, with the detached indifference of a man incapable of feeling because he knows none exists. His opening sentence proved this point: 'Save yourselves, spare me all fruitless recrimination. The girl is dead; I cannot summon her back. Live your days, feast and drink, acquire and squander what little remains; it is but a fleeting span. Why cling to ancient sorrows?' His final word rang like a bell of triumph: 'When a man loves nothing and no one, it is futile to curse him.'"

"That was the key," Felix whispered, his voice trembling with suppressed rage. "That was the secret of his dominion. He loved nothing, no one—not even himself. We felt the blow, and we bowed beneath it. Yet, before we departed him and the town, you and I, Amos, swore that we would eventually make that frozen heart bleed. Someday, somehow, we would force that impassible nature to suffer as he had torn us apart, regardless of how prosperous or content he appeared. That was thirty years ago, and that vow remains unfulfilled."

Felix paused. Thomas lifted his head, but the old man silenced him with a shake of his frail finger. "Some men forget in a month, others in a year. I have never forgotten, nor has Felix. When you were born—having remarried, hoping for renewed joy—I felt, though I know not why, that Evelyn's avenger had arrived. A year hence, when news reached us that Providence had seemingly overlooked John Poindexter's sins—or perhaps remembered them—and blessed him with a child after eighteen barren years, I looked upon your beautiful face. In that gaze, I saw, or believed I saw, the instrument destined to execute the vengeance to which Felix and I had consecrated our lives.

"You grew. Your ardent nature, generous temper, and quick mind promised a brilliant adulthood. When your mother died, leaving me once more a widower, I hesitated no longer to dedicate you to the purpose for which you seemed born. Thomas, do you recall the start of that pilgrimage that ultimately severed you from me? Remember how I carried you on my shoulders along a dust-choked road, and when we neared his home, I lifted you up, pointing to those distant gables looming black against the golden twilight, dedicating you to the ruin of whatever happiness might bloom under that innocent infant's smile? You do? I thought you could not forget; and now that the hour for fulfilling that promise draws near, I call upon you again, Thomas. Avenge our griefs. Avenge your sister. *Poindexter's girl* has become a woman."

At the weight of these words, Thomas recoiled in sheer horror. But the old man failed to read the depth of his terror, clutching his arm and speaking with a feverish passion. "Courage! Woo her! Conquer her! They know you not. To them, you shall be Thomas Adams, not Thomas Cadwalader. Enfold this blossoming flower in your embrace, and then—Oh, he must love his child! Through her, we seize hold of his heart. Make her suffer—she is but a country girl, and you have basked in the lights of Paris. Make her suffer, and if, in the process, you cause him to flinch, then believe me as I speak from the grave I am hastening toward: be happy, for you will not have lived, nor will I have died, in vain."

He paused to draw a ragged breath, but his iron will conquered mortality, casting a spell over Thomas that defied instinct and turned him into a puppet of emotions that had swelled to a crescendo of hatred in a single hour—a hate that his father and brother had cultivated over twenty-five years.

"I shall die; I am dying now," the old man gasped, continuing. "I shall never live to witness your triumph; I shall never see John Poindexter's eyes cloud with those agonies that rend the entrails and make a man question if God reigns in heaven. But I shall know it where I am. No mound of earth can bury my spirit when John Poindexter feels his doom. I shall be conscious of his anguish and I shall rejoice; and when, in the abyss of darkness to which I descend, he comes stumbling along my path..."

"Boy, boy, you were reared for this," Amos panted, his voice rising. "God forged you handsome; men forged you strong; you have fashioned yourself intelligent and accomplished. You need only present yourself to this country girl to become the master of her will and affection. Once these are yours, *remember me!* *Remember Evelyn!*"

Thomas had never witnessed such a torrent of passion. It swept him along in a burning river against which he struggled in vain. Raising his hand in what he intended as a response to the appeal, he opened his mouth to speak but choked. His father, misinterpreting his silence, cried out bitterly, "You are mute! You dislike the task; you are virtuous, perhaps—you who have lived for years alone and unencumbered in Paris. Or do you possess instincts of honor, habits of generosity that blind you to wrongs that have screamed for vengeance longer than your entire lifetime? Thomas, Thomas, if you should fail me now—"

"He will not fail you," cut in the voice of Felix, calm, suave, and insinuating. "I have observed him; I know him; he will not fail you."

Thomas shuddered. He had momentarily forgotten Felix, but hearing these words, he could no longer delay turning to the man who had offered to stand surety for the unholy deed his father demanded. As he turned, he saw a figure who, in any setting, would attract attention, awaken admiration, and yes, fear. He was not large, not so large as himself, but the frenzy expressed on his father's lips manifested as quiet, inflexible resolve in the gray eye resting upon Thomas with a power he could never hope to escape. As he looked and understood, a steel band seemed to compress his heart; yet simultaneously, he was aware that the personality before whom he thus succumbed was as elegant as his own and as perfectly trained in the ways of men and life. Even the aura of poverty that had shocked him regarding his father's person and surroundings was invisible here. Felix was both well and handsomely clad, capable of holding his own as the elder brother in every aspect most insisted upon by the Parisian gentleman. Behind him stretched a long, mysterious curtain of dark-green serge from floor to ceiling, throwing out his pale features with remarkable distinctness, and for an instant, Thomas wondered if it had been hung there specifically to produce this effect. But the demand in his brother's face drew his attention, and bowing his head, he stammered, "I am at your command, Felix. I am at your command, father. I cannot say more. Only remember that I never saw Evelyn, that she died before I was born, and that I—"

Here, Felix's voice broke in, kind but measured. "Perhaps there is some obstacle we have not accounted for. You may already love some woman and desire to marry her. If so, it need be no impediment—"

But here Thomas's indignation found voice. "No," he said sharply. "My heart is whole, save for a few lingering fancies that are rapidly fading into vanishing dreams."

It seemed he had lived many years since stepping into this room. "Your heart will not be disturbed now," Felix commented, his voice clear and cutting. "I have seen the girl. I went there expressly a year ago. She is as pale as a snow-drop and as listless. You will not be obliged to recall the gay smiles of Parisian ladies to remain proof against her charms."

Thomas shrugged his shoulders. "She must be made to know the full intoxication of hope," Felix proceeded, his tone sharpening. "To realize despair, she must first experience every delight that accompanies satisfied love. Do you possess the skill, as well as the heart, to play to the end a role requiring patience as well as dissimulation, courage as well as subtlety, and that union of will and implacability which feeds on tears and is strengthened, rather than diminished, by the suffering of its victim?"

"I have the skill," Thomas murmured, "but—"

"You lack the incentive," Felix finished. "Well, well, we must exercise patience with your doubts and hesitations. Our hate has been nurtured by memories of her whom, as you say, you have never seen. Look, then, Thomas. Look at your sister as she was, as she is for us. Look at her, and think of her as despoiled, killed, forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever gazed upon a more moving countenance, or one in which beauty contends with a keener prophecy of woe?"
