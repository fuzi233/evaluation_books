A quiet, unlooked-for change settled over Margaret Brandt, soft as the mist that clings to Tergou’s cobblestones after dusk. She moved through her days of churning butter, carding wool, and tending the cottage hearth like a soul half lost in waking dreams. Even her old father Peter, slow and unobservant as he was, soon marked how she startled at the clink of a tankard or a sharp word from his lips, her great dark eyes flying wide with a terror that had no name. She visited her kind patroness Margaret Van Eyck far less often than of old, and when she did sit in that warm, tapestried chamber of the old painter’s home, she no longer leaned into the old woman’s caresses. She would stand passive, trembling, sometimes shrinking back as if the softest touch stung.

Strangest of all, she would not set foot outside her own threshold while the sun hung high. Any errand to the town of Tergou was made only after dark had wrapped the streets in shadow, and she slipped back home again long before the first rooster crowed at dawn. She would not even walk to the village church for the early matins. At last Peter, bewildered, asked her what ailed her. Her voice was thin as thread when she answered: “Methinks every soul I pass on the street stares right through my skin.”

One day Margaret Van Eyck drew her aside, gentle as she would handle a skittish fawn, and pressed her to speak of what grieved her. All the girl gave her was a wild, scared glance, then a flood of hot, unstopping tears. “What, sweetheart?” the old lady murmured, stroking her hair. “Are you afraid to lay your sorrows at my feet, who have loved you as my own?”
“I have no sorrows that I did not bring on myself,” Margaret whispered, shaking. “I am treated far kinder than I deserve—most of all in this house.”
“Then why do you not come see me more often, my dear?”
“I come far oftener than I deserve,” she sighed so deeply her whole frame shook, just as Reicht, the Van Eycks’ sharp but kind serving maid, called out for her from the yard. The old lady sent her off, and stood frowning softly, turning the puzzle over in her mind. At last she decided Margaret must be wounded that Gerard’s family, the hosier’s people, had treated her with cold contempt. “I will go speak to them,” she resolved, “call the women of that household to account for their churlishness.”

The very next morning she wrapped herself in her fur-lined cloak, pulled her hood low over her silver hair, and set out with Reicht at her heels for the hosier’s house. Catherine, Gerard’s mother, welcomed her with deep respect, wiping her hands on her apron and weeping as she thanked her for all the kindness she had shown her faraway son Gerard. But the moment Margaret Van Eyck turned the conversation to Margaret Brandt, Catherine’s tears dried up instantly, her lips pressed thin and stubborn as unworked clay, and her face set hard the way only ignorant, obstinate peasant women’s faces can set. You might as well try to argue with a brick wall as shift that look once it settled. Margaret Van Eyck pleaded with her, argued, tried every gentle persuasion she knew, and all to no avail. Proud and unused to being thwarted, she snapped at last, turned on her heel, and stormed out of the house, tossing a hasty, half-hearted curtsey over her shoulder that Catherine returned with a sharp, defiant nod, no obeisance in it at all.

Outside the door, Margaret Van Eyck found Reicht chatting with little Kate, Gerard’s gentle lame sister, perched on her crutches. The old painter was all flushed with anger, about to push past them with a scornful toss of her head meant for every last member of that stubborn family, when a small, soft hand slipped timidly into hers. She looked down, and saw the girl’s dove-like eyes swimming with tears, staring up at her, full of gratitude and a desperate, quiet plea all tangled together. The old woman’s anger melted away like snow in spring. She stopped, and kissed Kate’s soft forehead. “I see now,” she said, quiet. “Never fear, little one. I leave the matter to you.”
When she got back home, she sat down by her easel and sighed to Reicht: “I saw two things today, one very common, one very rare. I saw a stupid, stubborn woman as hard as any stone, and I saw an angel in the flesh. If I could paint that face of hers just right, I’d pick up my brushes again, old as I am, and never stop till I got it down on panel.”

Little Kate did not fail to live up to that good opinion. She waited a few quiet days, then told her mother everything she had heard from Reicht Heynes: how Margaret had spilled her own blood, cutting her arm open to drag the wounded, feverish Gerard out of the wolf-infested woods and save his life. “See, mother? No one on earth could love him more than she does.”
“Who wouldn’t love our Gerard?” Catherine said gruffly, but her voice was softening.
“Oh, mother, think what that must have cost her! The poor soul.”
“Ay, wench. She’s got her own troubles, sure enough, same as the rest of us. I can’t abide the sight of blood, let alone shed it for someone else.”
That was a small victory won, but the moment Kate tried to push her mother further on the subject, Catherine shut down hard and would not speak of it more.

About a month later, one evening, a soldier of the Dalgetty clan tramped up to the hosier’s door, just back from fighting in the Duke of Burgundy’s wars. He set a letter down on the supper table where Catherine and the rest of the family were seated, Peter being off on a business trip. He refused every penny they offered him as a reward for bringing it, tipped his cap, and tramped off for the tavern in Sevenbergen.
They spread the letter flat out on the table, and though none of them could read a word of writing, every one of them recognized Gerard’s scrawl at a single glance.
“And your father’s gone away, of all times!” Catherine wailed. “Are you all not ashamed? Not one of my brood can read a word of your own brother’s letter!”

But even though the inked words were hieroglyphics to them, there was something on that page they could read perfectly. Art has a tongue that needs no words, unshackled by the pen’s limits, that slips straight through the eye to the heart, for learned and unlearned alike. It crosses seas and borders, loses nothing on the way, needs no translator, for it speaks the one universal language all souls understand.
When they saw the little drawing Gerard had scribbled with his pencil, right between the two short paragraphs of his letter—two hands clasped tight together—they understood its meaning immediately, and it went straight to every heart in the room. Gerard was bidding them farewell. They stared at that simple sketch, every line of it so full of his old familiar way of drawing, that it felt like Gerard was standing right there in the candlelight, looking at them, saying goodbye. The women wept over it till their eyes were too blurred to make out the lines. Even Giles, the quietest of the boys, muttered “Poor Gerard!” in a softer voice than anyone had ever heard him use. Even Cornelis and Sybrandt, the two greedy brothers who had done their best to drive Gerard away, felt a sharp twinge of remorse, and sat silent and dark-faced, staring at the table.

But none of them dared bring a stranger in to read the letter out loud. They did not want anyone outside their family to see their ignorance, or see how broken-hearted they were.
“What if we go to Dame Margaret Van Eyck?” Kate suggested, small and timid.
“Ay, that’s a good thought, Kate,” Catherine said at once. “She’s got a kind heart, and she loves Gerard too. She’ll be glad to hear word from him. I was short with her when she came to the house, that’s the truth, but I’ll go apologize, and then she’ll read me every word my poor boy wrote to me.”

She was at the Van Eyck house in no time. Reicht led her into the parlour, and told her to wait a moment: the old dame was at her prayers. There was a young woman seated pensively by the stove, and she rose at once, moving politely out of the way so Catherine could warm her hands at the fire.
“Thank you, lass,” Catherine said, holding her palms out to the glowing coals. “Winter nights are cruel cold, and your stove here is a blessing.” She glanced the girl up and down, curious. The girl wore a plain linen wimple, but her gown was trimmed with soft sable fur, a sign of wealth and gentle birth in those days. But what struck Catherine most was the girl’s face, open, candid, and soft with a quiet modesty. She felt certain she would find sympathy there, and began to chatter.
“Do you know what brings me here, my dear? A letter! A letter from my poor boy, who’s off in some faraway savage land, God knows where. I’m shamed to say none of us in the family can read a word of it. I wonder if you can read writing?”
“Yes,” the young woman said, her voice trembling a little.
“Can you now? That’s such credit to you, lass. The old dame won’t be long, I’m sure—but every minute feels like an hour to a poor mother, pining to know what her boy says.”
“I will read it to you,” the girl said, holding out her hand for the letter.

Catherine was so eager, she did not notice the suppressed, wild eagerness in the girl’s slow outstretched hand, did not see the way her fingers shook as they closed around the crumpled paper. “Go on then, read it to me, for pity’s sake. I’m half wild waiting for it.”
“The first words are ‘To my honoured parents,’” the girl read, her voice thin and wobbly.
“Ay! He always was a good, respectful boy, the poor soul.”
“‘God and the saints have you in their holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.’”
Catherine pressed a hand to her chest, and sank back in her chair, heaving one long, ragged sob.
“Then comes this,” the girl went on, “ ‘A long farewell.’”
“Ay, go on, bless you, lass. It’s sad comfort you give me, but it’s still comfort, all the same.”
“‘To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more.’”
Catherine gasped. “What can that mean? Ah!”
“‘To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father’s house. Be kind to her—’” The girl’s voice broke.
“That’s Margaret Brandt,” Catherine said, tears running down her face. “His sweetheart, poor soul. I’ve not been kind to her, my dear. Forgive me, Gerard.”
“‘—for poor Gerard’s sake: since grief to her is death to me—’”

The girl could fight no more to keep her composure. All her strength gave way, and she slipped right off her chair, falling insensible to the floor, the letter still clutched tight in her hand, her head resting soft on Catherine’s knees.
