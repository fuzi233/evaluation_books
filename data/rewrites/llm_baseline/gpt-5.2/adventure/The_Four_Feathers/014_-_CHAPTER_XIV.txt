July wore itself out into August, and the days at Mrs. Adair’s house by the Salcombe creek went on with that deceptive quiet that belongs to places where water keeps time more faithfully than clocks. The estuary had its own kitchen-music: the sip of tide against mud, the dry whisk of reeds, the occasional click of a halyard on a mast down-creek, a sound like a spoon tapping porcelain. Even indoors, with the windows thrown wide, you could tell whether the breeze came off the sea or over the fields by what it carried—salt that sharpened the nostrils, or warm hay and crushed leaf that softened the mind.

Ethne stood at an open window in the sunlight, her hands resting on the sill as if it were a board on which she might steady herself. Behind her Mrs. Adair sat with a book on her lap, her posture composed, her stillness so complete that it seemed another sort of listening.

“I am afraid,” Ethne said.

Mrs. Adair’s mouth curved, the smallest movement, as though the line on the page had pleased her. “Of what? That some accident happened to Colonel Durrance yesterday in London?”

“No. Not of that.” Ethne spoke slowly, as if tasting each word to be sure it did not betray her. “For he is at this moment crossing the lawn towards us.”

Mrs. Adair did not lift her head. “I thought so,” she murmured, so low that it was almost a sound meant only for herself.

Ethne watched the figure moving over the grass, and the fear that had no proper name rose again. “Do you think he has any hope whatever that he will recover his sight?”

The question seemed to startle Mrs. Adair more than any mention of danger ever could. She considered it with the air of one weighing a new ingredient. “Would he travel up to town so often to see his oculist if he had none? Of course he hopes.”

“I am afraid,” Ethne said again, and turned sharply, the words no longer general but pointed like a pin. “Haven’t you noticed how quick he has grown—and is growing? Quick to interpret your silences, to infer what you do not say from what you do, to fill out your sentences, to make your movements the commentary of your words? Laura, haven’t you noticed? At times I think the very corners of my mind are revealed to him. He reads me like a child’s lesson book.”

Mrs. Adair’s voice was smooth. “Yes. You are at a disadvantage. You no longer have your face to screen your thoughts.”

“And his eyes no longer tell me anything at all,” Ethne added, and in the cool simplicity of the sentence was the whole reversal of their power. Where once Durrance had been bound to what her visible honesty offered him, now he was free—free to listen, to weigh, to catch the smallest shift in breath as though it were a spoken confession. And she, who had never doubted what he meant, often now stood in darkness before him.

Mrs. Adair’s interest sharpened. “There is something, then, to be concealed from him?” she asked, with that quietness that made a question feel like the turning of a key.

“Yes.”

“Something rather important?”

“Something which at all costs I must conceal,” Ethne exclaimed; and even as she said it, dread ran through her that he had already found it out.

She stepped out on to the stone-flagged terrace. The lawn fell away to the hedge; beyond it the grass fields undulated, and beyond them, among trees, the smoke from Colonel Durrance’s chimneys rose in a thin, domestic thread. To the left the creek lay framed by beeches and oaks, a gap cut in the fringe so that sunlight on water flashed like a knife’s edge, and a small sailing-boat down the creek worked slowly against the light wind.

Ethne hesitated, as if mustering her courage the way a cook gathers her ingredients before a difficult dish—choosing, measuring, discarding what might spoil the whole. Then she descended the broad shallow steps to the lawn and walked out to meet Durrance.

If Ethne moved as though toward an ordeal, Durrance came steadily, without a pause. His stick switched at the grass blades—not searching, not groping, but making a soft, habitual rhythm, as though his feet remembered the path and his hand merely kept company with them. There was something almost intolerable in the ease with which he approached: the calm of a man who had learned to replace sight with certainty.

Behind the curtain, Mrs. Adair let the book slip from her hands. She ran to the window and hid herself in the drapery’s fold, watching with a smile that did not warm her eyes. In them a fierce brightness kindled, and her face drew tight with a hunger that had nothing to do with food.

“Something which at all costs she must conceal,” she whispered, exulting. She had contempt, too, for Ethne—the girl of open air and open roads, now afraid of a blind man’s claim, shrinking from a marriage that would bind her freedoms by compassion and obligation. Mrs. Adair was fiercely glad of Durrance’s blindness. If her opportunity came—and each day seemed to promise it more—his dependence would be a possession no rival could contest. She watched Ethne and Durrance meet, turn, and walk side by side toward the creek. Ethne’s hands moved once, as if pleading, and Mrs. Adair’s whole being leaned toward the imagined sound.

Ethne was pleading.

“You saw your oculist yesterday?” she asked quickly when they met. “What did he say?”

Durrance shrugged. “That one must wait. Only time can show whether a cure is possible or not.”

Ethne bent forward, scrutinising his face as if she distrusted what it could no longer show her. “But must you and I wait?” she asked.

“Surely,” he replied. “It would be wiser on all counts.”

Then, with a suddenness that made her heart jump, he asked, “It was Mrs. Adair, I imagine, who proposed this plan—that I should come home to Guessens and that you should stay with her here across the fields?”

Ethne did not see why he asked, but she answered at once. “I was in great distress when I heard of your accident. So distressed that at the first I could not think what to do. I came to London and told Laura, since she is my friend, and this was her plan. Of course I welcomed it with all my heart.” The pleading note rang again; she was asking him to accept her sincerity as he used to accept the look in her eyes.

He turned his face toward her and smiled gently. “I know that very well, Ethne.”

Relief loosened her breath.

“It was kind of Mrs. Adair,” he resumed, “but rather hard on you, who would like to be back in your own country. I remember very well a sentence which Harry Feversham—”

He spoke the name carelessly, as if it were merely a name, and paused a fraction after it, listening. Ethne felt the pause like a hand hovering near a bruise. She would not move; she would not flinch. Not even a tightening of her fingers on her skirt.

“A sentence which Harry Feversham spoke a long while since,” Durrance continued, “in London, just before I left for Egypt. He was speaking of you, and he said: ‘She is of her country and more of her county. I do not think she could be happy in any place which was not within reach of Donegal.’ When I remember that, it seems selfish that I should claim to keep you here at so much cost to you.”

“I was not thinking of that,” Ethne exclaimed, “when I asked why we must wait. That makes me out most selfish. I was merely wondering why you preferred to wait—why you insist upon it. For, of course, although one hopes and prays with all one’s soul that you will get your sight back, the fact of a cure can make no difference.” She spoke with deliberate slowness, as though laying each word down carefully, like slices on a platter that must not topple. “It can make no difference.”

Durrance started, as if roused from a deep, inward listening. “I beg your pardon, Ethne. I was thinking at the moment of Harry Feversham. There is something which I want you to tell me. You said a long time ago at Glenalla that you might one day bring yourself to tell it me—and I should rather like to know now. You see, Harry Feversham was my friend. I want you to tell me what happened that night at Lennon House to break off your engagement—to send him away an outcast.”

Ethne’s silence was long enough for the creek’s small sounds to fill it: a distant gull, the low shush of water in reeds. At last she said gently, “I would rather not. It is all over and done with. I don’t want you to ask me ever.”

He did not press her. “Very well,” he said cheerily. “I won’t ask you. It might hurt you to answer, and I don’t want, of course, to cause you pain.”

“It’s not on that account that I wish to say nothing,” Ethne answered quickly, earnestly. She paused, choosing words as one chooses the right salt—not too much, not too little, for either would ruin. “It isn’t that I am afraid of any pain. But what took place, took place such a long while ago—I look upon Mr. Feversham as a man whom one has known well, and who is now dead.”

They were nearing the opening in the trees that showed the water bright beyond. Ethne lifted her eyes and saw that the little sailing-boat she had watched earlier had nosed into the shore. The sail had been lowered; the short mast stood up above the bank. A man had climbed out and stood uncertainly, staring toward the house as if the place refused to match his expectations.

“A stranger has landed from the creek,” she said. “He looks as if he had lost his way. I will go on and put him right.”

She ran forward, seizing on the interruption as on a sudden reprieve—like a witness in court when the judge rises for luncheon and the cross-examination stops mid-sentence. For each talk with Durrance left her feeling she had been questioned with a skill she could not fully trace, though she had suspected from the first that it was there.

The stranger advanced to meet her. He was of middle size, with a short snub nose, protruding brown eyes that stared vacantly, and a moustache of aggressive shape. He lifted his hat and showed a round forehead already going bald.

“I have sailed down from Kingsbridge,” he said, “but I have never been in this part of the world before. Can you tell me if this house is called The Pool?”

“Yes; you will find Mrs. Adair if you go up the steps on to the terrace,” Ethne replied.

“I came to see Miss Eustace.”

Ethne stared. “I am Miss Eustace.”

He looked at her in silence, his eyes taking their time. “So I thought.” He twirled one moustache, then the other. “I have had some trouble to find you, Miss Eustace. I went all the way to Glenalla—for nothing. Rather hard on a man whose leave is short!”

“I am very sorry,” Ethne said, forcing a smile, “but why have you been put to this trouble?”

Again he played with his moustache, as if winding himself up to speech. “You have forgotten my name, no doubt, by this time.”

“I do not think that I have ever heard it,” she answered.

“Oh, yes, you have, believe me. You heard it five years ago. I am Captain Willoughby.”

The name struck like a slap. Ethne drew sharply back; the colour dropped from her cheeks. Her lips set; her eyes hardened into a glare she could not soften even if she would.

Captain Willoughby was not discomposed. If anything, he spoke like a man indulgent of another’s rudeness. “I can quite understand that you do not welcome me, Miss Eustace, but none of us could foresee that you would be present when the three white feathers came into Feversham’s hands.”

Ethne swept the explanation away. “How do you know that I was present?” she demanded.

“Feversham told me.”

“You have seen him?” The cry leaped from her before she could hold it back, pure as a heartbeat made audible. It startled her as much as it surprised Willoughby. She had trained herself to omit Harry Feversham—out of thought, out of tenderness, out of the daily language of her life—yet the name, touched by the fact of his living speech, broke through every discipline.

“You have actually seen him?” she repeated, wonder shaking her voice. For a moment she envied this dull man his privilege. “You have spoken to him? And he to you? When?”

“A year ago, at Suakin. Else why should I be here?”

The answer came as a shock. Ethne could not yet find the shape of the reason, but she feared it in advance.

“Yes,” she said slowly, almost against her will. “After all—why are you here?”

Willoughby took a letter-case from his breast pocket and opened it with deliberate care. From one compartment he shook out into his palm a tiny white feather—soiled, limp with age and handling. He held it toward her.

“I have come to give you this.”

Ethne did not take it. She shrank as if it might stain her skin. “Why?” she asked, unsteady.

“Three white feathers, three separate accusations of cowardice, were sent to Feversham by three separate men. This is actually one of those feathers which were forwarded from his lodgings to Ramelton five years ago. I am one of the three men who sent them. I have come to tell you that I withdraw my accusation. I take my feather back.”

“And you bring it to me?”

“He asked me to.”

Ethne took the feather at last. It was almost weightless—nothing, a scrap from a bird—yet it filled her palm like a stone. The garden and trees seemed to tilt and turn; the sunlight on the creek went dizzy. She was aware of Willoughby speaking, but his voice had thinned and receded as if she stood underwater and he called to her from a far bank. A coldness spread through her despite the August warmth.

But Willoughby was one of the three. She would not show weakness before any of them. Pride steadied her as surely as a hand on a shoulder. With an effort she mastered herself, on the very brink of faintness.

“Come,” she said. “I will hear your story. Your news was rather a shock to me. Even now I do not quite understand.”

She led him away from the open space into a small plot of grass above the creek, enclosed on three sides by thick hedges. Tall elms and poplars rose behind; before them the water flashed and broke into ripples, and beyond it the opposite trees climbed toward sloping meadows. A gap in the hedge served as an entrance; a garden seat stood at the centre, sun-warmed wood, the faint smell of sap and old varnish rising when one sat.

“Now,” Ethne said, motioning him to sit beside her. “You will take your time, perhaps. You will forget nothing. Even his words, if you remember them! I shall thank you for his words.”

She held the feather clenched tight. Somehow, impossibly, Harry Feversham had redeemed his honour. Somehow she had been unjust. She was to learn how. The knowledge that she had been unjust should, later, call remorse; but not now. For the moment it was too great a happiness to allow any bitterness. She opened her hand and stared at the feather as though it were a relic and an indictment both.

Memories she had kept under lock rose with the speed of a tide that has turned: the spring in Dublin five years ago, the season before Ramelton received those feathers, the sound of voices speaking with the authority of a dominant tongue, naming cowardice as if the word itself were a sentence that could not be appealed. She had tried to make Harry Feversham “dead” in her mind by refusing him a place in her daily speech, by denying him the simple human grammar of presence—yet here was a proof that language could not do everything it claimed. There were experiences for which her carefully chosen words had been only a poor translation.

Willoughby began his story, and almost at once the Devonshire scene dissolved. This coast—Plymouth and Dartmouth and Brixham and the Start—was a country where red cliffs carried the memory of dead centuries, where even a quiet creek seemed haunted by the long-ago ring of shipwrights’ hammers and the brave departures of small barques on the Spanish Main. But Ethne had no mind for those echoes. The meadow-sweet air and salt tang were pushed aside by the scent of another world she had learned through Durrance’s confidences: dry dust, sun-scorched stone, the thin breath of desert night.

Before her inner sight—more vivid than the creek’s glitter—stood stunted mimosas, the yellow spread of sand like a sea without water, and sharp black rocks rising pyramidal and abrupt. It was part of the irony of her life that she could so much more completely imagine what one lover had endured because the other lover had given her the details.

And as Captain Willoughby spoke on, the quiet domestic creek with its gentle, healing sounds was only a shifting mist at the edge of her hearing.
