Baldassarre wandered through Florence, searching for a place to rest—any sheltered corner where he might sleep without spending much. He had grown used to open skies and wide horizons over the years, and now the narrow streets of the city, with their crowded rooftops and limited views, pressed in on him like a cage. His thoughts were heavy, his memory faint, and the silence of the alleys deepened his loneliness.

His steps led him toward a rare spot within the city walls—a hill that rose above the buildings, offering a glimpse beyond the stone and tile into the distant hills and valley. This was the slope behind the Via de’ Bardi, known locally as the hill of San Giorgio. Bratti had told him Tito lived there, and though he hadn’t yet found him, the hill’s quiet appeal drew him upward.

It was sparsely settled, mostly gardens and patches of rough earth. Near the edge of a broken stretch of land—once ruined by a landslide centuries before—stood a squat, square building with fluted tiles, looking like a truncated tower. Beside it was a small outhouse, half-built against a crumbling wall. Under a large mulberry tree, its leaves turning yellow, an old woman was untying a goat with two kids. The outhouse door stood open, revealing only straw and tools inside.

This was exactly what Baldassarre needed.

He approached her, but she was deaf, speaking in low murmurs and shaking her head. Only when he shouted close to her ear did she understand. He explained he was a poor traveler from far away, unable to afford a hostel. All he wanted was a place to lie down, and he’d pay her a few quattrini a week.

She remained doubtful, muttering to herself. Then, as if struck by an idea, she fetched a hatchet and pointed to a thick log half-buried in litter. “Chop this,” she said, “and you can stay one night.”

He agreed.

Monna Lisa watched with arms crossed, a smile playing on her lips. “It’s been there since my husband died,” she whispered. “I might as well have thrown a stone on the fire. He chops well enough, though he speaks oddly and looks strange. I couldn’t have paid less. And if he only wants straw to sleep on, maybe he’ll run errands for me. Who will know? And sin hidden is half forgiven.” She glanced toward the upper window, where a child’s eyes peered through a round hole in the shutter.

Tessa had seen everything. At first drawn by the loud voice, she had stayed at the opening, watching quietly. Now, as Baldassarre entered the outhouse and sat on the straw under the gathering dusk, a sudden urge stirred in her heart. She would take him part of her supper. He wasn’t deaf like Monna Lisa, and she could tell him things she couldn’t say aloud to the old woman.

It was forbidden. Her duty was obedience, and she knew it. But it was also exciting. And she had nothing else to confess—only sometimes dozing over her prayers or being cross with Monna Lisa for being so hard of hearing. There was no fear now. She was safe, free, and idle.

She stepped back from the shutter, her face bright with mischief. She wore her dark-green serge dress, red girdle, silver hairpin, and red glass beads. Around her neck was a new gold ring, a treasure she wore proudly. She sat on her rickety stool, knees tucked close, her mind fluttering between fear and delight.

She had been warned: make no friends, or her new life would vanish like a dream turned to lead. She had obeyed—going to church with her hood pulled low, lips tightly pursed. But this man was not a friend. He was just a stranger, sleeping in the outhouse. And if she brought him food, he wouldn’t betray her.

Monna Lisa would forbid it, so Monna Lisa must not be consulted. It didn’t matter what came after.

Supper was ready—macaroni with cheese, fragrant and warm. Tessa hurried downstairs, pretending innocence. “What was that noise?” she asked, then, before the old woman could answer, gave her a sharp look and sent her upstairs. “I’ll eat down here.”

Three minutes later, she stood at the outhouse door, lantern in one hand, wooden bowl in the other. She kicked gently, and Baldassarre opened the door, startled awake.

“Supper!” she shouted, lifting her mouth to his ear. “Sit down and eat. I’ll stay with you.”

Surprise held him still. Distrust warred with something softer. But he could not push her away. He sank back onto the straw, and Tessa placed the bowl in his lap, set the lantern between them, and smiled as if to say, *You may really eat it.*

He had been gnawing dry bread. The warmth of the macaroni was welcome.

She watched him eat a few bites, then leaned in again. “I like my supper. Don’t you?”

Baldassarre looked at her—not with a smile, but with a quiet tenderness, like a dog touched by kindness. “Yes,” he said. “But I can hear. I’m not deaf.”

“Oh!” she said, hands rising in surprise. “I forgot. Monna Lisa is deaf. I live with her. She’s kind. We have plenty. Nuts, if I want. No work. I used to work—I didn’t like it. But I liked feeding mules. I miss little Giannetta. Now we have a goat and two kids. I talk to the goat. But now… I’ve got something else. Can you guess?”

She tilted her head, challenging him with a grin.

“No,” he said, putting down his bowl. “I don’t know.”

“You like talking to me?” she asked. “But you mustn’t tell anyone. Shall I bring you sausage?”

He shook his head, but his face softened. She felt at ease.

“Well,” she said, “I’ve got a baby. A pretty bambinetto. Little fingers, little nails. Born at Nativita, Monna Lisa says. I was married at Nativita, long ago. Nobody knew. Oh, Santa Madonna! I didn’t mean to tell you!”

She bit her lip, shoulders lifted, eyes wide—expecting him to be shocked.

But Baldassarre seemed untouched.

“You’re a stranger,” she said. “You don’t live anywhere. You don’t know anyone?”

“No,” he replied, more to himself than to her. “Only one man.”

“His name isn’t Nofri, is it?” she asked, suddenly afraid.

“No,” he said, seeing her fear.

“Is that your husband’s name?”

Tessa laughed, clapping her hands. “No! But I can’t tell you about him. You wouldn’t believe what he is. Not like Nofri at all!”

She laughed again, delighted by the contrast—the cruel stepfather and her gentle husband.

“But I don’t see him often,” she said more quietly. “And sometimes I pray the Holy Madonna to send him sooner. Once she did.”

She paused. “But I must go back to my bimbo. I’ll bring it tomorrow. You’ll like to see it. It cries only when it’s hungry. Monna Lisa says it’s fine. She had babies once—they’re all dead men now. My husband says she’ll never die, because she’s so well dried. I’m glad. I love her. You’ll stay tomorrow, won’t you?”

“I’d like to come here to rest,” he said. “I’ll pay. I won’t hurt anyone.”

“No,” she said, “I think you’re not a bad old man. But you look sad. Will you cry when I leave?”

“I think not,” he said.

“That’s right,” she said, smiling. “I’ll bring breakfast. And the baby. Good-night.”

She took her bowl and lantern, closed the door.

To Baldassarre, the visit had been like a fleeting rainbow across a stormy sky. He barely thought of her again until sleep took hold, dreams weaving through his waking mind.

But Tessa thought of him constantly.

As soon as she returned, she told Monna Lisa everything. “Let him stay,” she insisted. “He’ll help. He knows no one. He’s not dangerous.”

The old woman pretended reluctance. “Messer Naldo will be angry,” she muttered.

Tessa didn’t believe it. Naldo had never objected to strangers who had nowhere to go. And this man only knew one person—someone who wasn’t Nofri.

“Well,” Monna Lisa finally said, “if he stays and helps me carry things up the hill, you must keep silent. Tell no one.”

“I’ll only tell the bimbo,” Tessa promised.

“And God may love us enough to keep Messer Naldo from finding out,” Monna Lisa added. “He comes only at night. He was here two days ago. Likely he won’t return till the old man has gone.”

“Oh me,” Tessa sighed, clasping her hands. “I wish Naldo didn’t have to go so far.”

“Ah, child,” Monna Lisa said, “the world is big. Behind the mountains, people walk night and day to reach Rome, to see the Holy Father.”

Tessa nodded, solemn. Then she began rocking her baby, humming soft, meaningless syllables like a chime.

The next morning, she was eager. She prepared breakfast quickly, wanting to show him her baby and speak again.

But Monna Lisa had already made Baldassarre fetch water. Tessa hurried down, insisting he sit on his straw. “I’ll stay with you,” she said, placing steaming broth and soft bread in his hands.

“I won’t let Monna Lisa give you too much work,” she said. “I don’t like work. I like sitting in the sun, feeding things. Monna Lisa says work is good—but she does it all. So I don’t mind. She’s not cross. You needn’t fear her.”

Then she went to get her baby.

She returned with the tiny bundle, wrapped snugly. The infant had large, dark eyes and a tiny nose. “Look,” she said, seating herself beside him. “This is my baby. You didn’t think it was so pretty?”

“It’s like the little Gesu,” she added. “I think the Holy Madonna will be kinder to me now. I have everything—except seeing my husband more often.”

“You may hold him,” she said, “but don’t kiss him. You might hurt him.”

She spoke gently, almost pleading. Baldassarre took the baby.

“Poor thing,” he murmured, his voice deep and strange. “Poor thing.”

He did not feel comforted. Instead, he felt a fierce protectiveness rise within him. This child, this innocent girl—she was not a solace, but a cause for anger. A reason to fight.

“Oh, don’t be sorry for me,” Tessa said. “Even if I don’t see him often, he is more beautiful and good than anyone in the world. I pray to him when he’s away. You couldn’t imagine what he is.”

She looked at him with wide, mysterious eyes, longing for him to ask.

“Yes,” he said, bitter. “I could.”

“No,” she said firmly. “You couldn’t. You thought he might be Nofri.”

She smiled triumphantly. “But never mind. You couldn’t know. What is your name?”

He rubbed his brow, stared blankly. “Ah, child,” he said slowly, “what is it?”

He did not forget his name. But the question had stunned him. Memory failed him in that moment.

Tessa saw his pain. “Never mind,” she said softly. “You’re a stranger. Names don’t matter. Good-bye. I need my breakfast. Come whenever you like. Monna Lisa says you may. And don’t be unhappy. We’ll be good to you.”

“Poor thing,” Baldassarre said again, holding the baby close, his voice heavy with sorrow.
