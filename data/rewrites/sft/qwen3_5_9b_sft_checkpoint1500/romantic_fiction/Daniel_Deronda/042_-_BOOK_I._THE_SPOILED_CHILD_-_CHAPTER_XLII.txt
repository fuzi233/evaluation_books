If there are hierarchies of suffering, the Jewish people occupy the summit; if endurance elevates the spirit, they stand alongside the nobility of every land. Consider literature: if a few classical tragedies constitute richness, what of a national tragedy sustained for fifteen hundred years, performed by the heroes themselves? This reflection, drawn from Zunz, lingered in Daniel Deronda's mind as he approached the Cohen household. To him, the Cohens presented a jarring contrast to that sublime archetype of the Jewish martyr. Ezra Cohen, a pawnbroker, wore no pallid vestments of penitence, nor did he embody the tragic weight of his ancestry. Instead, he was animated by a voracious appetite for profit, a trait that seemed to defy the historical stoicism of his race. Yet, as Deronda watched, he sensed a deeper irony: nestled within this "self-gratulating" prosperity was Mordecai, a fragile vessel of the national conscience, struggling to breathe amidst the mundane.

When Deronda returned, the gloom lifted instantly. The Cohens' faces glowed with a radiant, almost theatrical joy. Ezra declared that the diamond ring, however lucrative, mattered less than the sheer delight of the women and children in welcoming a young gentleman whose visit had become the topic of endless chatter. Young Mrs. Cohen expressed regret that the infant was asleep but delighted that Adelaide remained awake, urging Deronda to bypass the shop entirely and proceed directly to the parlors to meet "mother and the children." Deronda accepted, carrying a set of paper figures for Adelaide and an ivory cup and ball for Jacob.

Inside, the grandmother presided over a game of "plates," tossing cards that miraculously remained whole mid-air. Jacob, eager to impress, ran to Deronda, begging him not to step on his card before watching it return to perfection. The game eventually collapsed into fragments, and Deronda sat down, noting quietly that the door leading to Mordecai's workroom was now sealed. He wished to establish a rapport with the family before revealing his urgent concern for their troubled lodger.

Only after settling Adelaide on his knees and arranging her toys did Deronda ask, "Is Mordecai in?"

Ezra Cohen, seizing a brief lull in business, explained the situation. Mordecai had recently been "a little touched," lacking the sharp rationality of the others, yet industrious and devoted to the boy. Lately, however, he had moved like a somnambulist or sat statue-still, staring into space. The grandmother whispered gently of illness, fearing he could not withstand it much longer, while the younger Mrs. Cohen attributed his condition to a preoccupation with writing, noting his delayed responses. Despite acknowledging their own limitations, Ezra insisted on their principle: they would never abandon Mordecai, regardless of his burden, distinguishing themselves from those who profited from ignorance. "Mordecai carries a blessing inside him," the grandmother affirmed, a sentiment Jacob quickly corrected, citing Mordecai's refusal to eat a bun.

Deronda, however, felt a resonance with Jacob's concern. He revealed that he had visited Mordecai at Ram's bookshop and promised to return, an assurance that seemed to stir something within the lodger. As Jacob offered to fetch Mordecai, his mother silenced him, asking only for the door to be opened quietly. Jacob obeyed with practiced stealth, and Deronda followed him into the dim workroom, lit by a dying fire and a single shaded candle. Jewelry and books cluttered the boards, but Mordecai sat alone at a high chair, hands clasped over a watch on a stand, vibrating with the agonizing suspense of a prisoner awaiting release.

"I am come for you. Are you ready?"

Mordecai turned instantly, grabbing his fur cap and joining Deronda. As they emerged into the sitting room, Jacob attempted to cheer him with a toss of the ivory ball, which elicited a startled smile and a "Fine, fine!" from Mordecai. Young Mrs. Cohen retrieved his greatcoat, and Ezra, returning to an undertone, admitted he had predicted Mordecai's reaction.

Outside, Deronda remarked on the Cohens' apparent affection. Mordecai replied swiftly, "And I for them," describing them as possessing the heart of Israel despite lacking the understanding of the broader world. Apologizing for his delay, Deronda explained his previous absence. Mordecai, agitated by the stirring of his youth, described his physical weakness as a cage for a bird seeking flight, his joy threatening to shatter his frail frame. Deronda advised caution regarding the cold air, suggesting they proceed to *The Hand and Banner*, assuming privacy there. Mordecai hesitated, fearing the crowds of the club, but Deronda assured him he would accompany him.

They arrived at "The Philosophers," a modest gathering of impoverished men who met to discuss profound ideas. The room, barely fifteen feet square, was filled with smoke and the hazy glow of gaslights. Several men, shabbily dressed, listened intently to a speaker quoting Shelley. Upon entering, the attention shifted to Mordecai, who introduced Deronda as a traveler and student of their subjects.

"My name is Daniel Deronda," he announced simply, earning a murmur of approval. Seated opposite Mordecai, Deronda observed the group with quiet respect. Among them were Miller, a second-hand bookseller of German extraction; Buchan, a Scottish saddler; Pash, a vivacious Jewish watchmaker; and Gideon, a rational Jew who advocated for assimilation. The conversation, initially focused on the laws of progress and statistics, quickly turned to the nature of ideas and social change.

Miller argued that ideas acted as a parliament distinct from the "commonwealth" of material actions, while Goodwin countered that ideas gained power only when intimately mixed with the realities of life. Pash, with a mischievous grin, suggested that the idea of nationality was spreading like a scent among wild asses, a notion Mordecai gently refuted by emphasizing nationality as a feeling rather than a concept.

The debate deepened when Pash claimed that the nationalist sentiment was destined to die out in Europe, replaced by a universal progress. Buchan challenged the definition of progress itself, questioning whether change inherently implied betterment. Deronda intervened, arguing against the fatalism of accepting trends as inevitable, warning against mistaking dangerous tendencies for laws of nature.

Mordecai, however, seized the floor. He spoke of the "seed of fire" within the Jewish soul, a growth knit together by joy and sorrow, absorbing the thoughts of other nations while enriching the world. He painted a picture of a people whose spirit, though scattered and oppressed, refused to dry up. "Who shall say the fountain of their life is dried up?" he asked passionately. "Not he who feels the life of his people stirring within his own."

Gideon, the rational voice, pushed back, advocating for the shedding of superstition and exclusivity, suggesting that Jews should merge into the surrounding populations and adopt the maxim, "A man's country is where he's well off." Pash supported this, mocking the idea of a Jewish homeland as impractical. But Mordecai rose, his voice gaining a feverish brilliance. He rejected the notion of assimilation, declaring that to lose the specific "sap of special kindred" was to lose the essence of humanity.

"For the same reason that the cleverest men in the country don't get themselves into Parliament," Pash quipped, dismissing the Jewish contribution to the world. Mordecai countered, "Each nation has its own work... Israel is the heart of mankind." He described the history of his people not as a static relic but as a dynamic, living organism that had preserved its identity through millennia of persecution, turning scattered misery into a reservoir of wisdom.

"The night is unto them, that they have no vision," Mordecai proclaimed, his eyes burning with a hectic light. "But the sun is gone down over the prophets, and the day is dark above them." He envisioned a revival, a "living warmth" spreading to the weak extremities of the race, transforming superstition through the illumination of great facts. He spoke of a future where the Jewish spirit would manifest in a new order, a republic of justice and equality, serving as a neutral ground between East and West.

"And the world will gain as Israel gains," he concluded, his voice sinking to a whisper as exhaustion overtook him. "The vision is there; it will be fulfilled."

Silence fell over the room. Mordecai sank his chin onto his breast, his eyelids falling as if in a trance. The others, including Gideon and Pash, looked at him with a mixture of awe and unease. Deronda, however, felt a profound connection to the man's vision. He saw in Mordecai not merely a suffering individual, but a conduit for a vast, invisible current of hope and memory. The talk had ended, but the impact lingered, leaving the room empty save for the two friends, bound by a shared, silent understanding of the struggle and the dream.
