It was one of those bright, bustling mornings in London’s high season when the city seemed to have polished itself up for a grand performance—every cab horse shined, every gentleman’s hat sat at just the right angle, and even the pigeons looked as if they’d been dressed by a French tailor. Into this glittering world strode Major Arthur Pendennis, the very picture of military elegance in retirement. He came from his lodgings like a man who knew exactly where he belonged—and that place was not merely at the Club in Pall Mall, but *on* it.

Now, the Club wasn’t just any club. It was a temple of taste, wit, and fine wine—where only the most distinguished men could be seen without drawing suspicion. And Pendennis? He wasn’t just a member. He was its unofficial headmaster, chief tastemaker, and occasional disciplinarian. The waiters bowed to him like courtiers before a king. The stewards tiptoed around him as if he might sneeze and bring down the entire establishment. His presence was so perfectly timed—always arriving at ten minutes past ten—that you could set your watch by his boots, which were blacker than midnight and shone with the intensity of a newly minted penny.

He wore a checked cravat that never frayed until dinner (a feat even the gods envied), a buff waistcoat adorned with buttons bearing the crown of England (because why wear a button unless it has royal endorsement?), and linen so crisp and white that Mr. Brummel himself—had he still been allowed into England—would’ve offered his last guinea for the name of her laundress.

His coat? Flawless. His gloves? Whiter than snow on a winter morning. His cane? A walking stick that probably had a pedigree. Even his whiskers were arranged with military precision, like two well-drilled battalions of facial hair.

From afar, he looked about thirty. Up close? Ah, there was a little deception—a few crow’s-feet near the eyes, a hint of dye in the hair, and the faintest whisper of age beneath the perfect surface. But who cared? He carried himself like a man who had never lost a battle—or perhaps just hadn’t bothered to fight one yet.

And then there was the table. Not just *a* table. *The* table. By the fire, near the window, facing the door—his personal throne. No one dared sit there. Once or twice, some reckless young fool tried to claim it, but the moment Pendennis took the next seat and fixed them with a look that said, “I know what you did last summer,” they fled faster than a hare chased by a pack of angry dukes.

His letters awaited him, neatly stacked like soldiers at attention. There were invitations—dinner cards thick as bricks, promising three courses and endless conversation about politics, poetry, and whether Lady So-and-So really *was* engaged to Lord Something-or-Other. There were notes from marchionesses, bishops, and marquises—all seeking advice, company, or simply a word of wisdom from the man whose opinion mattered more than a coronation speech.

Pendennis read them all with the calm dignity of a man who had mastered both fashion and fate. He accepted the Bishop of Ealing (slow dinners were acceptable when they involved ecclesiastical robes). He declined Cutler, the East India Director (too much talk of tea and tariffs). He accepted Steyne’s invitation—French-speaking party at the Star and Garter, no less—but only after checking that Glowry, the Scotch surgeon across the room, was watching with jealous eyes.

Glowry, incidentally, had no invitations. None. Zero. Zilch. And while he sipped his weak tea and stared at Pendennis’s pile of correspondence, he muttered under his breath: “Is it another _Jook_?”—by which he meant, “Is it another woman writing to him in distress?” Because clearly, Pendennis’s life was nothing but a series of dramatic love letters, social obligations, and minor crises involving hats.

But among all these grand missives, one letter stood apart. Small. Plain. With a country postmark and a seal that looked like it had been stamped by a chicken. It bore the address: *Fairoaks, Clavering St. Mary’s*. And the handwriting—oh, the handwriting! Delicate, feminine, trembling slightly, as if the writer had been weeping over the inkwell.

“Immediate,” it said, underlined with a frantic dash.

Pendennis ignored it. For now. He had more important things to do—like deciding whether to wear his gold pocket-watch chain or his silver one today. But eventually, even the most powerful men must answer their rural relatives.

He broke the seal. “Fairoaks” was engraved upon it. And inside, he found two letters—one from his sister, Helen, and one from his nephew, Arthur Pendennis Jr.

The first began:
“My dear Major Pendennis, I beg and implore you to come to me immediately.”

Immediately? Oh, yes. Immediately was the word of the day.

“I am in the greatest grief and perplexity,” she wrote. “My dearest boy—our darling Arthur—has formed… I can hardly write it… a passion, an infatuation… for an actress performing here.”

Pendennis blinked. Then blinked again. Then dropped his toast.

An *actress*?

A *passion*?

For a woman who was *twelve years older*?

And the boy wanted to marry her?

“Hay!” muttered Glowry, half out loud. “What’s got into Pendennis now?”

The Major’s face went from composed to crimson to something resembling volcanic fury. He read on.

“She insists upon marrying her,” the letter continued. “Do come instantly. As Arthur’s guardian, entreat, command, the wretched child to give up this most deplorable resolution.”

And then, signed: *Your unhappy affectionate sister, Helen Pendennis.*

Pendennis put down the letter. Took a deep breath. Then picked up the second one—the one written in a shaky hand, sealed with wax that looked like it had been applied during a seizure.

It began:
“FAIROAKS, Monday, Midnight.”

Ah. Midnight. That explained the urgency.

“My dear Uncle,” the boy wrote, “In informing you of my engagement with Miss Costigan…”

Miss Costigan? Of course. The famous Miss Fotheringay of Drury Lane and Crow Street. The tragedian whose voice could make angels weep and critics sigh.

“And though I know society may not approve,” the letter continued, “her family is as ancient and noble as our own!”

Yes, because nothing says nobility like being descended from kings of Ireland. Especially when your ancestor was a king *before* the British Empire existed. Useful fact, that.

“Although Miss Costigan is some years older than myself,” the boy added, “that does not operate as a barrier to my affection.”

No, indeed. Nothing stops true love—not age, not class, not common sense.

“I have loved once,” he declared, “and I shall die without ever knowing another passion.”

Oh, the drama. The pathos. The sheer *audacity* of it all.

Pendennis read the rest—how the boy begged him to come down, reconcile the mother, bless the union, and generally save the family from ruin. And then, the kicker:
“I press for a speedy marriage with my Emily—for why, in truth, should it be delayed? A delay implies a doubt, which I cast from me as unworthy.”

Unworthy? Unworthy of whom? Of *himself*, apparently. Or maybe of the entire concept of rational thought.

When Pendennis finished reading, he didn’t speak. He didn’t move. He just sat there, staring at the letter like it had just bit him.

Glowry, seeing this, reached into his card-case and felt for his lancet. Just in case. Just in case the Major needed medical intervention.

Because honestly? This was worse than a broken leg. This was a shattered reputation.

The head of the Pendennises—going to marry an actress? At *eighteen*? Who was twelve years older? And who happened to be a *tragedy queen*?

“Oh, mercy,” Pendennis groaned inwardly. “People will laugh. They’ll say, ‘There goes Pendennis—once the finest man in London, now reduced to escorting his nephew to a nuptial ceremony in a barn!’”

And worse—he’d have to cancel Steyne’s dinner. The Bishop’s. The Marquis’s. All of it. He’d have to take the mail-coach—tight, uncomfortable, smelling of damp wool and regret—instead of enjoying a leisurely evening with the cream of English society.

He rose from his table, left his breakfast untouched, and marched into the writing-room. There, with the solemnity of a man signing his own death warrant, he penned refusal after refusal.

To the Marquis: “Regretfully unable to attend.”
To the Earl: “Duty calls.”
To the Bishop: “Family matters of the utmost gravity.”

Then he sent his servant to book seats on the evening coach—charging the cost to the account of the widow and the young scapegrace he was supposed to be guarding.

As he walked back to his table, Glowry watched him with pity.

“Poor devil,” he murmured. “First love. Never good for breakfast.”

And indeed, it wasn’t. The toast had gone cold. The coffee had turned sour. And the future—well, the future looked like a long journey through the countryside, ending in a house full of emotional wreckage and a boy who believed in love like it was divine revelation.

And somewhere, in the depths of Fairoaks, Miss Fotheringay probably practiced her lines—because she was going to need them.
