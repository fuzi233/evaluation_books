I found myself smiling—no, laughing—at Madame Beck once more, less than a day after the little encounter in the garden. Villette’s climate was as changeable as any English town’s, though not as damp. The gentle sunset had given way to a blustering night, and the following day was stormy—dark, clouded, yet dry, the streets hazy with dust swept from the boulevards.

Even if the weather had been fair, I doubt I would have returned to the garden that evening. My quiet alley, the shaded walks, the shrubs—all had lost their charm. Their solitude now felt uncertain, their peace fragile. That window, raining down notes, had stripped the place of its innocence; the flowers seemed to watch, the trees to listen. Some plants had been trampled in Dr. John’s haste, and I took care to mend them early, smoothing over his footprints before anyone else could notice.

With a quiet resolve, I settled at my desk to study German as the pupils began their evening lessons. The other teachers took up their needlework. The refectory, smaller than the classrooms, was reserved for the boarders—no more than twenty. At dusk, two lamps were lit, signaling the end of schoolbooks and the start of *la lecture pieuse*.

This "pious reading" was, I soon realized, less about devotion than mortification—a deliberate humbling of reason. The book, ancient as the Hôtel de Ville, was a collection of saints' legends. *Good God*—I say it reverently—what absurdities they were! Tales of boastful miracles and monkish excesses, interwoven with darker threads: stories of priestly tyranny, of highborn women broken by confessors. Conrad and Elizabeth of Hungary’s twisted tale returned again and again, a grotesque blend of oppression and impiety.

Night after night, I endured it, though my hands clenched once, snapping the tips of my scissors into the worn table. But at last, the heat in my cheeks, the pounding in my temples, became unbearable. Prudence dictated escape—the moment that wretched book appeared, I slipped away.

No candles were allowed outside the refectory, so I vanished into darkness—pacing the cold halls in winter, or in summer, retreating to the dormitory. There, I leaned from my casement, listening to distant music, lost in my own thoughts.

This evening, fleeing once more, I climbed the stairs and eased open the dormitory door. The room, usually empty, *felt* occupied—not by sound, but by the absence of solitude. Every white-draped bed lay bare. Then came the faint scrape of a drawer. Shifting silently, I saw her—Madame Beck, dumpy and efficient, rifling through my belongings with methodical care. My work-box, my drawers, even my folded dresses—all inspected, yet left perfectly undisturbed.

I should have been furious. Instead, I watched, amused. Had I been a man, I might have admired her neatness. But it was time to retreat. If she turned and saw me, all pretense would shatter. I had no wish to leave—her yoke was light, her expectations manageable. And so I fled, swift as a spider down the banister.

Laughter seized me in the schoolroom. She *had* seen Dr. John in the garden—her suspicions were absurd. Yet as my mirth faded, a sharper emotion rose—bitterness, like Meribah’s waters. I wept, not for her mistrust, but for reasons tangled and unspoken.

By morning, equilibrium returned. I was Lucy Snowe again.

Inspecting my drawers, I found every lock secure, every item untouched—even the dried violets hidden in my best dress. Had she disturbed even one, forgiveness might have been harder. But all was pristine. *Let bygones be bygones.*

One mystery lingered: How had Dr. John known of the dropped casket? I resolved to ask him—if I ever found the courage.

Days passed. Little Georgette recovered, and Dr. John’s visits grew rare. Then one evening, Madame announced she was going out—an odd hour for errands. “The child is flushed,” she declared. “I will send the doctor. You will receive him.”

*Ah, a scheme.* But I was unconcerned.

Madame left, adorned in a daring green hat. I amused Georgette until drowsiness crept over her. Then—the doorbell. Rosine ushered in Dr. John, lingering shamelessly.

“The brat’s fine, yes?” she asked, chin jutting toward Georgette.

“Quite,” he said, scribbling a harmless prescription.

Rosine edged closer. “And the box—did you find it?”

My pulse jumped. *Ask him!* But she did it for me.

“Yes.”

“Who threw it?”

“I saw it dropped from a window,” he said simply.

Rosine pouted. “No intrigue? No romance?”

“None whatsoever.”

Disappointed, she flounced off, rewarded with a coin for her nosiness.

I turned to the window. Then—movement. The same lattice opened; a handkerchief waved; something white fluttered down.

“There!” I exclaimed.

Dr. John spun toward me. “What?”

“Another note.”

He ordered me to fetch it. I did, retrieving a folded paper from the shrubs. He tore it unread.

“It’s not her fault,” he said.

“Whose?”

“You truly don’t know?”

I shook my head.

“If I knew you better, I might confide in you,” he mused. “She’s innocent—unsuspecting. I’d protect her if I could.”

*Madame?* I nearly laughed. But I offered my help.

He hesitated. “You *must* know. So flawless, so good—”

A click. A stifled sneeze. Madame stood in the doorway, impeccably timed.

“A cold,” she declared, launching into tales of her outing.

The prayer-bell rang. I left them, the mystery unresolved—but the game far from over.
