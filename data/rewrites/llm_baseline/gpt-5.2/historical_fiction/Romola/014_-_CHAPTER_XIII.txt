The afternoon of the seventh of September had that languor Florence knows so well: heat stored in stone, light thickened to honey, the air almost edible with dust and late-summer fig. Two months had gone since Romola and Tito had let their voices—shy at first, then certain—name what their hearts had already rehearsed. And now Tito, stepping down from the street into Nello’s shop, brought with him the outer city: the faint clatter of hooves on paving, a distant vendor’s cry, the muffled chorus of a place that never wholly falls silent.

Inside, the barber’s room smelled as it always did—citrus oils, warm skin, wetted linen—layered today with the drowsy paper-scent of a book left open like a loaf gone stale. Nello lay stretched on the bench with his cap over his eyes, one leg drawn up, the other slipping toward the ground as if it had tired of holding its own weight. A manuscript volume had fallen with him and lay crushed, its leaves dog-eared and flattened under the dull tyranny of sleep.

In the corner, Sandro sat solemn as an altar boy, playing _mora_ against himself: right hand demanding numbers, left hand answering slowly, as though the fingers were old peasants reluctant to leave the furrow. The boy watched his own hands as if they were a small, private miracle.

Tito moved as softly as one who steals a pinch of salt from the jar so the cook won’t hear. He snatched up the lute, bent over the sleeping barber, and touched the strings with a lightness that was almost a breath. Then, as if the notes themselves had drawn him into their current, he sang under his voice:

“Quant’ è bella giovinezza,
Che si fugge tuttavia!
Chi vuol esser lieto sia,
Di doman non c’è certezza.”

Nello woke as a bird wakes—no heavy hauling of consciousness, only a sudden flutter into wakefulness. The cap flew off; he sprang upright, blinking once, and the next moment his whole face was alive with jest.

“Ah, my Apollino! Late with my siesta, it seems. That comes of not falling asleep in a natural way, but swallowing a potion of potent poesy. Listen how I begin to match words by the first letter, like some trovatore—bad symptom, that. I fear the good wine of my understanding is running away at the spigot of authorship. I shall be left an empty cask with only the smell of dregs, like many another incomparable genius I could name.”

He stretched both arms long as a man measuring cloth, then brought them round to seize Tito’s curls and tug them playfully, as if the boy’s bright head were a fruit he meant to test for ripeness.

“What is it, my Orpheus? There’s coaxing in that soft strain. Show me the very needle’s eye of your desire, as the sublime poet says, and I’ll thread it.”

“That is a tailor’s image for your sublime poet,” Tito answered, still letting his fingers fall in a gentle, dropping way on the strings, like water from a thin spout. “But you have divined the reason of my affectionate impatience. I want you to give me an extra touch of your art—not on my chin; no—but on the _zazzera_, which is as tangled as Florentine politics. You slip your comb in so adroitly it flatters the skin and stirs the animal spirits in that region; and a little of your most delicate orange-scent will not lie amiss. I am bound to the Scala palace. I must present myself in radiant company. The young cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici is to be there, and he brings with him a certain Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena, whose wit runs so fast I see no way to out-rival it save by smelling of orange-blossoms.”

Nello had already seized his comb and flourished it like a conductor’s baton. He pushed Tito backward into the chair with the easy authority of a man who has guided a hundred necks into linen, wrapped the cloth around him, and began that practiced work: the teeth of the comb finding their path through the thick hair, tugging just enough to make the scalp quicken, then easing off, smoothing, parting, taming. His fingers were nimble, scented faintly of soap and bitter peel.

“Never talk of rivalry, bel giovane,” he said. “Bernardo Dovizi is sharp, yes, but he won’t cast a net to catch the wind. And he has the same weasel look as his brother Ser Piero—the creature Piero de’ Medici keeps to slip through small holes for him. No. You distance all rivals. Soon you’ll touch the sky with your forefinger. They say you have carried enough honey to sweeten sour Messer Angelo himself. He has pronounced you less of an ass than might have been expected, considering there is such a good understanding between you and the Secretary.”

Tito smiled—one of those smiles that made even self-interest look like charm—and let his head incline under the comb. “Between ourselves, Nello mio, Messer Angelo has more genius and erudition than I find in all the other Florentine scholars together. It answers well enough for them to cry me up now, when Poliziano is beaten down with grief, or illness, or something else. I can try a flight with such a sparrow-hawk as Pietro Crinito; but Poliziano—he is a large-beaked eagle. He would swallow me, feathers and all, and not feel the difference.”

Nello clicked his tongue and worked in a little oil, rubbing it between his palms before pressing it into Tito’s hair. The scent rose immediately—orange with a thread of something resinous—bright as the first cut into rind.

“I won’t contradict your modesty if you insist on it,” he said. “But we clever Florentines can’t keep saying the same things every day of our lives, as we must if we always told the truth. We cry down Dante and cry up Francesco Cei, just for variety. And if we cry you up as a new Poliziano, heaven has taken care the lie shall not be so big as it might. And are you not a pattern of virtue in this wicked city? Ears double-waxed against every siren invitation that would lure you from the Via de’ Bardi—and from the great work that is to astonish posterity?”

“Posterity,” Tito said, letting the word slide out as if it were a coin of doubtful stamp, “whom it will probably astonish as the universe does, by the impossibility of seeing what the plan of it was.”

“Yes—something of that sort was prophesied here the other day,” Nello replied. “Cristoforo Landino said the excellent Bardo is one of those scholars who lie overthrown in their learning, like cavaliers in heavy armour, and then get angry because they are over-ridden. A pithy remark, and not, I think, a herb from his own garden; for of all men to feed one with an empty spoon and gag one with vain expectation by long discourse, Messer Cristoforo is the pearl.”

He stepped back, surveyed his handiwork with the satisfaction of a cook who has coaxed a sauce to the right sheen, and whisked away the cloth.

“Ecco! Perfect. Impossible to add a grace more! But love is not always fed on learning, eh? I shall have to dress the _zazzera_ for the betrothal before long—is it not true?”

“Perhaps,” Tito said, smiling, “unless Messer Bernardo should next recommend Bardo to require that I yoke a lion and a wild boar to the car of the Zecca before I can win my Alcestis. But I confess he is right to hold me unworthy of Romola. She is a Pleiad—she may grow dim by marrying any mortal.”

“_Gnaffè_, your modesty is in the right place there,” Nello said, and his hands—still smelling of orange—made a quick, dismissive wave, as if batting away flies. “Yet fate seems to have measured and chiselled you for the niche left empty by the old man’s son—who, by the way, Cronaca was telling me, is now at San Marco. Did you know?”

Something went through Tito like a thin wire struck by lightning: no cry, no visible start, only a sudden inward jolt that made his breath change. He hid it as he had learned to hide so many things—by bending at once to a practical action. He stooped, picked up the fallen manuscript, and began smoothing the crushed leaves with careful fingers, pressing them flat as though neatness could keep danger at bay.

“No,” he said, and his voice sounded natural enough. “He was at Fiesole, I thought. Are you sure he has come back to San Marco?”

“Cronaca is my authority.” Nello shrugged. “I don’t frequent that sanctuary, but he does. Ah!” He took the book from Tito, peered at its abused pages, and sighed in mock tenderness. “My poor _Nencia da Barberino_! It jars your scholarly feelings to see pages dog’s-eared. I was lulled to sleep by that rustic maiden—‘prettier than the turnip-flower,’ ‘with a cheek more savoury than cheese.’ But to get such a well-scented notion of the _contadina_, one must lie on velvet cushions in the Via Larga—not go to look at the Fierucoloni stumping in to the Piazza della Nunziata this evening after sundown.”

“And who are the Fierucoloni?” Tito asked, as if it were nothing to him—settling his cap, touching the edge as though testing its fit.

“The _contadine_,” Nello replied, “come down from the mountains of Pistoia, the Casentino, heaven knows where, to keep their vigil at the Nunziata, and sell their yarn and dried mushrooms at the _Fierucola_—the little Fair. They make a queer show with paper lanterns, howling their hymns to the Virgin on this eve of her nativity. Wild work in the piazza. One may get a stone or two about one’s ears or shins without asking. I was never fond of that pressing attention. Addio.”

Tito left with the orange-blossom scent clinging to him like a promise meant for an evening that might never arrive. The Scala palace received him with its usual murmur of fine voices and rustle of garments; the air inside was cooler, scented faintly with wax and the stored sweetness of old wood. Yet the visit ended earlier than he had expected. The boy-cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici—youngest of red-hatted fathers, already practiced in all the indulgences that make a man’s face broad and memorable—was detained at his favourite pastime of the chase and failed to appear.

Half an hour before sunset Tito came out again, intending to go straight to the Via de’ Bardi. The city, however, had begun to change its note, as it always did toward evening: footsteps quickened, voices rose, the soundscape thickened with the anticipation of crowds. He had gone only a little way when, to his astonishment, he saw Romola advancing toward him along the Borgo Pinti.

She was wrapped in a thick black veil and mantle, a darkness moving through the amber of late light; but her figure and her walk were unmistakable. By her side was a short, stout form—Monna Brigida—whom he recognized despite the unusual plainness of her dress. Romola had not been bred to devotional observances; her going out beyond the familiar roof-loggia was rare and always arranged in advance because of Bardo’s jealousy of being left alone. Tito felt at once that something urgent had driven her into the street without warning him.

She saw him through the veil and hurried.

“Romola—has anything happened?” Tito asked, turning to walk beside her.

Romola did not answer immediately, and Monna Brigida’s voice burst in, practical and breathless, as if she were scolding a kettle for boiling too slowly.

“Ah, Messer Tito, you do well to turn round, for we are in haste. And is it not a misfortune? We must go round by the walls and turn up the Via del Maglio because of the Fair. The _contadine_ coming in block up the way by the Nunziata, which would have taken us to San Marco in half the time.”

Tito’s heart leapt hard—so hard it seemed to strike his ribs and rebound. “Romola,” he said, lowering his voice, “are you going to San Marco?”

They had passed out of Borgo Pinti and now walked under the city walls. On their left lay wide gardens, green and hushful, their leaves holding the day’s heat like cupped hands. Here the noise of Florence fell away. The city’s clamour became a distant wash; nearer were the small, living sounds—the whisper of wind in vines, a bird’s brief call, the faint scrape of a hoe far off. Romola lifted her veil a little to breathe, and Tito saw the subdued agitation in her face.

“Yes, Tito mio,” she said, looking at him with sad, steady eyes. “For the first time I am doing something unknown to my father. It comforts me that I have met you, for at least I can tell you. But if you are going to him, it will be well for you not to say you met me. He thinks I am only gone to my cousin, because she sent for me. I left my godfather with him: he knows where I am going, and why.”

Tito’s mind, which usually ran like a smooth mill-wheel, began to race and grind all at once. He tried to keep his face composed while his thoughts flew forward, striking sparks from fear: what would be asked, what would be said, what would be revealed.

“You remember that evening,” Romola went on, “when my brother’s name was mentioned, and my father spoke of him to you?”

“Yes,” Tito answered. His own voice sounded as if it came from farther away.

“The _cugina_ had heard he was come back. The evening before—the evening of San Giovanni—as I afterwards found, he had been seen by our good Maso near the door of our house. But when Maso went to inquire at San Marco, Dino—my brother—he was christened Bernardino, after our godfather, but now he calls himself Fra Luca—had been taken to the monastery at Fiesole because he was ill.

“But this morning a message came to Maso, saying he was come back to San Marco; and Maso went to him there. He is very ill, and he has adjured me to go and see him. I cannot refuse, though I hold him guilty. I still remember how I loved him when I was a little girl, before I knew he would forsake my father. And perhaps he has some word of penitence to send by me.”

Her words moved as quietly as water, yet each one seemed to press on Tito’s chest. She spoke again, and her voice trembled—not with fear, but with the strain of acting against years of obedience.

“It cost me a struggle to act in opposition to my father’s feeling, which I have always held to be just. I am almost sure you will think I have chosen rightly, Tito, because I have noticed your nature is less rigid than mine, and nothing makes you angry. It would cost you less to be forgiving; though, if you had seen your father forsaken by one to whom he had given his chief love—by one in whom he had planted his labour and his hopes—forsaken when his need was becoming greatest—even you, Tito, would find it hard to forgive.”

What could he say? The truth—his own truth—was a twisted thing, full of hidden bones. He could not tell Romola that such offences ought never to be pardoned; he could not find the courage to dissuade her from going. He felt as if the air under the walls had thinned; even the birds and leaves seemed to listen, and the city beyond, with its coming fair and its hymns, was only a distant, indifferent roar.

“You are right, my Romola,” he said at last. “You are always right—except in thinking too well of me.”

There was something genuine in it, and the words made him momentarily pale, his lip quivering as though he had tasted something too sharp. In the orange-scent on his hair there was suddenly a bitter edge, as if the blossom had been bruised.

Romola, who interpreted all things largely—her mind prepossessed with high beliefs—looked at him with tearful brightness, touched with keen joy that he felt so strongly whatever she felt. Without pausing in her walk, she said:

“And now, Tito, I wish you to leave me, for the _cugina_ and I shall be less noticed if we enter the piazza alone.”

“Yes, it were better you should leave us,” Monna Brigida said, blunt as always. “For to say the truth, Messer Tito, all eyes follow you; and let Romola muffle herself as she will, every one wants to see what is under her veil, for she has that way of walking like a procession. Not that I find fault with her, only it doesn’t suit my steps. And indeed I would rather not have us seen going to San Marco, and that’s why I am dressed as if I were one of the Piagnoni themselves, and as old as Sant’ Anna; for if it had been anybody but poor Dino—who ought to be forgiven if he’s dying, for what use is a grudge against dead people? Make them feel while they live, say I—”

No one made any scruple of interrupting Monna Brigida. Tito raised Romola’s hand to his lips—warm through the thin glove, real as bread in a man’s palm—and murmured, “I understand. I obey you.” Then he turned away, lifting his cap in a sign of reverence that native Florentines rarely made. Bernardo del Nero would have sneered at it as the fawning of a Greek; Romola, loving homage, had always found in it an added grace.

He was half glad to be dismissed, half aching to cling to her for the last moments of her unquestioning love. For in his mind the future had already begun to unroll like a scroll whose ink he dreaded: this brother—Fra Luca—would, before all things, want to know; and Romola, before all things, would confide. She would tell her father’s position, her own; she would speak of the coming betrothal, of the young scholar chosen to fill the place left vacant long ago by a wandering son. And then the name. Tito could hear it spoken in his imagination with Romola’s sincere softness—Tito—and he could hear what would answer it from a man who had renounced the world and yet carried the world’s knowledge like a wound.

Fra Luca would tell all he knew and all he guessed. Tito saw no net of lies that could now catch this wind. The worst consequences of his earlier dissimulation would walk straight into daylight.

It was all over with his prospects in Florence. He pictured Messer Bernardo del Nero, satisfied as a farmer whose warning about late frosts has come true; the talk under every loggia—the young Greek professor, his benefactor in slavery. The words would pass mouth to mouth like a bitter herb, each person crushing it to taste its sharpness.

For the first time in his life, Tito felt too fevered and agitated to trust his usual self-command. The city’s sounds struck him differently: each call, each laugh, each footfall seemed too loud, too near, like the beginning of a storm. He gave up his intended visit to Bardo and walked up and down under the walls until the yellow light in the west had quite faded.

Then, without any distinct purpose, he took the first turning—Via San Sebastiano—which led him directly toward the Piazza dell’Annunziata.

He was in one of those lawless moments that come when we have no guide but desire, and the path desire had promised seems suddenly closed. He was ready to follow any beckoning that offered an immediate purpose—any sound in the darkening city that would drown, for a little while, the beating of his own heart.
