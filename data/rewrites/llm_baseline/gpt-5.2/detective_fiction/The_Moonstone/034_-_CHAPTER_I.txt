Miss Clack has laid down her pen; and, in the order of these depositions, it falls to me to take it up.

There are two plain reasons why my hand is wanted next. First, because certain matters have been hinted at, and left to ferment in the reader’s mind without being properly brought to the light. Miss Verinder had her private cause for breaking her engagement—and I was the man who forced the truth to the surface. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite had his private cause for withdrawing his claim—and I was the man who found the telltale seam in it.

Second, because (for good or ill, I am still uncertain which) my own life at this period touched the mystery of the Indian Diamond. I received, at my office, an Oriental stranger of distinguished manners, whom I take to have been—unquestionably—the chief of the three Indians; and I met, on the following day, the traveller Murthwaite, and spoke with him of the Moonstone in a way that bears upon later events. But these things are not yet. The first link in the chain—chronologically, and therefore narratively—is the broken engagement; and the first scene, oddly enough, opens not in a drawing-room, nor at a railway-station, but at the bedside of my late client and friend, Sir John Verinder.

There is a sort of hush that belongs to sickrooms of the old English kind: thick curtains that swallow street-noise; a coal-fire that breathes and settles; the faint, domestic ticking of a clock whose small industry seems indecently confident in the presence of mortality. In such a hush, you hear the very scratch of a pen as if it were a saw. Sir John, who had always been a kindly man with a stubborn distaste for troublesome duties, had put off making his will as he had put off other unpleasantnesses—until illness, like a bailiff, had taken him by the shoulder and said, “Now.”

Lady Verinder had pressed him; I had pressed him. He admitted we were right—then smiled, changed the subject, and escaped into health again. It was only when the sentence was pronounced in his body that the message came to me at last, and I went to take his instructions.

He was dozing when I entered. He roused at the sound of my step on the carpet, looked at me with the mild impatience of a man interrupted in comfort, and said:

“How do you do, Mr. Bruff? I sha’n’t be very long about this. And then I’ll go to sleep again.”

I took out my papers and pen with the practised economy of a man who has done the thing a hundred times and has no wish to do it a hundred and one. The inkstand gave off that cold metallic smell which, in certain houses, clings to the very idea of legal business—like sealing-wax and dust.

“Are you ready?” he asked.

I dipped my pen.

“I leave everything to my wife,” said Sir John. “That’s all.” And he rolled, with the satisfied air of a child who has said his lesson, and composed himself for sleep.

I was obliged, in my office as his solicitor, to be cruel.

“Am I to understand,” I said, “that you leave the whole of the property, of every sort and description of which you die possessed, absolutely to Lady Verinder?”

“Yes,” he answered, with a faint frown. “Only, I put it shorter. Why can’t you put it shorter, and let me go to sleep again? Everything to my wife. That’s my Will.”

In ten minutes, the simplest will I ever drew was written and executed. Sir John’s estate—land and money—was entirely within his disposal; and if, in other cases, such a sweeping gift might have made me uneasy, I knew Lady Verinder to be not only worthy of trust (as all good wives are), but capable of administering it—a rarer gift, in my experience, than people like to admit. When I left, the old gentleman was finishing his interrupted nap, and the fire made that soft collapsing sound as a coal gives in.

Lady Verinder justified her husband’s confidence without delay. In the first days of widowhood—those days when the very house seems to mourn with a peculiar quietness, and the simplest domestic actions sound louder than they ought—she sent for me and made her own will. I had not to coax her into prudence. Her mind took the measure of her position with a clearness that was almost bracing. My responsibility was merely to give legal body to her instructions. Before Sir John had been a fortnight in his grave, the future of their daughter had been provided for with wisdom and tenderness.

That will lay for years in its fireproof box at my office—years that, for my own comfort, I do not count too closely. It was not until the summer of 1848 that I had occasion to look at it again, and then under circumstances which no lawyer ever welcomes—circumstances in which parchment and ink are forced to stand as the last servants of a life that is slipping away.

At the date I name, the doctors pronounced upon Lady Verinder what was, in plain terms, a sentence of death. She told me first; and she asked to go over her will with me again.

Rachel’s provisions could not be improved: there was nothing to add, nothing to take away. But some minor legacies—those small bequests by which family feeling expresses itself in shillings and guineas—had altered with time; and three or four codicils were needed. After securing her wishes immediately, for fear of accident, I asked permission to embody the new instructions in a second will, clean and coherent, so that repetitions and confusions should not disfigure the instrument. I own, frankly, that such disfigurements offend my professional sense as sour wine offends a careful palate.

The execution of this second will Miss Clack has already described, she having been obliging enough to witness it. As far as Rachel’s pecuniary interest went, the second will was word for word the counterpart of the first. The only changes related to the appointment of a guardian and certain provisions attendant on that appointment, made under my advice. After Lady Verinder’s death the will went, in the usual way, into the hands of my proctor, to be proved.

In about three weeks—so far as my memory serves—the first faint sound reached me of something moving beneath the surface: the sort of sound you catch, at night, in a quiet country house, when a shutter has come loose and is tapping softly, softly, as if trying not to be heard.

I happened to be looking in at my proctor’s office, and saw, at once, by his manner that he had something more than routine to tell.

“I have some news for you,” he said. “What do you think I heard at Doctors’ Commons this morning? Lady Verinder’s will has been asked for, and examined, already!”

The words fell oddly in my ear. There was nothing contestable in the will, and no person, to my knowledge, with the slightest interest in examining it. (For the benefit of those who do not know the practice: any will may be examined at Doctors’ Commons by anyone who applies, on payment of a shilling.)

“Did you hear who asked for it?” I said.

“Yes. The clerk told me readily enough. Mr. Smalley—of Skipp and Smalley. The will has not been copied into the Folio Registers yet; so they had to let him see the original. He went over it carefully, and made a note in his pocket-book. Have you any idea what he wanted with it?”

“None,” I answered. “But I shall find out before I am a day older.”

I returned at once to my office. If another firm had been concerned, discovery might have cost me more trouble. But I possessed an easy handle in this case: my common-law clerk—an excellent man—was Mr. Smalley’s brother. Moreover, through this indirect connection, Skipp and Smalley had, for years, enjoyed something of my professional patronage: cases that fell, like crumbs, from my table when I declined them. It is surprising how clearly a man hears his duty when his bread is in another’s hand. I intended, if necessary, to remind them of that.

I spoke to my clerk immediately, told him what had happened, and sent him to his brother’s office with my compliments and a plain question: why had Messrs. Skipp and Smalley found it necessary to examine Lady Verinder’s will?

The message brought Mr. Smalley back to me, accompanied by his brother. He admitted, at once, that he had acted under instructions received from a client. Then he asked me whether it would not be a breach of professional confidence to say more.

We had a smart discussion. He was right; I was wrong. I was angry and suspicious; and I insisted. Worse than that, I refused to receive any further information as a secret in my keeping. I claimed perfect freedom to use my discretion. Worst of all, I took an advantage of my position.

“Choose, sir,” I said, “between the risk of losing your client’s business and the risk of losing mine.”

It was indefensible—a tyranny, pure and simple. Like other tyrants, I carried my point. Mr. Smalley smiled with resigned politeness and gave me the name.

Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That name was enough. I wanted to know no more.

And now, so far as Lady Verinder’s will concerns this matter, the reader must stand, from this point, on the same level of knowledge as myself.

Rachel Verinder had nothing but a life-interest in the property. Her mother’s excellent sense, joined to my experience, had guarded her against the danger—always possible to a young woman of fortune—of being made the victim of a needy and unscrupulous man. Neither Rachel, nor any husband she might take, could raise a sixpence on the land or on the money. They would have the London house and the Yorkshire house to live in; they would have the handsome income—and that was all.

When I considered what I had discovered, I was perplexed as to my next step.

Hardly a week had passed since I had heard, with surprise and distress, of Miss Verinder’s intended marriage. I admired her sincerely, and was attached to her as I had been attached to her mother. I had been inexpressibly grieved to hear she was about to throw herself away on Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. And now the man—whom I had always believed to be a smooth-tongued impostor—plainly revealed the mercenary object on his side. Many marriages are made on motives no better, I know. But if the girl were your sister, you would not shrug your shoulders quite so easily.

One practical question presented itself at once. Would Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite hold to his engagement after his solicitor had discovered the truth?

That depended on his pecuniary position, of which I knew nothing. If he was not desperately pressed, Rachel’s income alone might be worth the bargain to him. But if he needed a large sum, and needed it by a fixed time, then Lady Verinder’s will met the case precisely and saved Rachel—quietly, almost mechanically—from falling into a scoundrel’s hands.

In the latter event, there would be no need for me to distress Miss Rachel in the first days of mourning by an immediate disclosure. In the former, if I kept silent, I should be conniving at a marriage that would make her miserable for life.

My doubts ended in action. I called at the London hotel where Mrs. Ablewhite and Miss Verinder were staying. They told me they were going to Brighton the next day, and that an unexpected obstacle prevented Mr. Godfrey from accompanying them. I proposed, on the spot, to take his place. While I reasoned at a distance, I could hesitate. When I saw Rachel, the hesitation ended. Whatever the cost, I would tell her the truth.

I found my opportunity the day after my arrival at Brighton, when we were out walking together.

“May I speak to you,” I asked, “about your marriage engagement?”

“Yes,” she said indifferently, “if you have nothing more interesting to talk about.”

“Will you forgive an old friend and servant of your family, Miss Rachel, if I venture to ask whether your heart is set on this marriage?”

“I am marrying in despair, Mr. Bruff—on the chance of dropping into some sort of stagnant happiness which may reconcile me to my life.”

The words were startling; they suggested a story below the surface—some secret romance, some wound not named. But my purpose was limited, and I declined (as we say in my profession) to pursue the question into its side issues.

“Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite can hardly be of your way of thinking,” I said. “His heart must be set on the marriage, at any rate?”

“He says so, and I suppose I ought to believe him. He would hardly marry me, after what I have owned to him, unless he was fond of me.”

It struck me then, sharply, how little the most obvious danger presents itself to a generous mind. The notion of a man marrying her for selfish ends had never entered her head. The task I had taken on began to look harder.

“It sounds strangely,” I went on, “in my old-fashioned ears—”

“What sounds strangely?” she asked.

“To hear you speak of your future husband as if you were not quite sure of the sincerity of his attachment. Are you conscious of any reason for doubting him?”

Her quickness of perception—quick as a hand to a hot dish before the mind has named the heat—caught something in my voice, or my manner. She stopped, withdrew her arm from mine, and looked at me straight.

“Mr. Bruff,” she said, “you have something to tell me about Godfrey Ablewhite. Tell it.”

I knew her well enough to obey her. I told her what I had discovered: that his solicitor had examined Lady Verinder’s will; what that implied; and what the will, in fact, allowed and forbade.

She put her arm into mine again and walked on slowly. Her hand tightened, not consciously, but as if her nerves had taken hold of my sleeve. Her face grew paler as I spoke. She did not interrupt me once. When I had finished, she still said nothing. Her head drooped a little; she walked on beside me as if she had forgotten where she was, or that I existed—lost in her own thoughts.

I did not disturb her. I have learned, through long observation, that Rachel Verinder’s nature did not run to questions and confidences as other girls’ natures do. Many young women, told of anything that moves them, question you at once, then fly to a friend to talk it over. Rachel’s instinct was to shut herself up within her own mind and think. Such self-dependence is praised as a virtue in men. In women it has the drawback of morally separating them from their sex, and so exposing them to misconstruction. I dare say I share the world’s prejudice in that matter—except where Rachel is concerned. In her, that solitary strength had always seemed to me one of the virtues of her character; and it had bearing, too, on my view of her connexion—whatever it might be—with the loss of the Moonstone. Bad as appearances looked; shocking as it was to find her associated in any way with an undiscovered theft; I was still satisfied that she had done nothing unworthy of herself, because I was satisfied she did nothing—good or ill—without that inward retirement and consideration first.

We had walked, I should say, nearly a mile before she roused. She looked up at me, and on her face there came a faint reflection of the smile I had seen there in happier times—the most irresistible smile I have ever known in a woman.

“I owe much already to your kindness,” she said. “And I feel more deeply indebted to it now than ever. If you hear any rumours of my marriage when you get back to London, contradict them at once, on my authority.”

“Have you resolved to break your engagement?” I asked.

“Can you doubt it?” she returned, with a proud quietness, “after what you have told me!”

“My dear Miss Rachel,” I said, “you are very young—and you may find more difficulty in withdrawing than you anticipate. Have you no one—a lady, of course—whom you can consult?”

“No one,” she answered.

It distressed me to hear it. She was young; she was lonely; and she bore it as if loneliness were merely another piece of furniture in her room, to be dusted and endured. The impulse to help her overcame any sense I might have had of my own unfitness, and I offered, as well as I could, such advice as occurred to me on the moment. I have advised countless clients through awkward troubles, but I had never before advised a young lady how to extricate herself from a marriage engagement.

In brief, I recommended this: that she should tell Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, at a private interview, that he had betrayed to her certain knowledge the mercenary motive on his side; that marriage after that discovery was impossible; and that he must choose—either to fall in with her wish and secure her silence, or to force her, by opposition, to let the world know why she withdrew. If he denied or defended himself, she was to refer him to me.

She listened attentively, thanked me with that graceful courtesy which sat so naturally upon her, and then said quietly that she could not follow my advice.

“May I ask,” I said, “what objection you see to it?”

She hesitated; then met me with a question.

“Suppose you were asked to express your opinion of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s conduct,” she began.

“Yes?”

“What would you call it?”

“I should call it the conduct of a meanly deceitful man.”

“Mr. Bruff! I have believed in that man. I have promised to marry that man. How can I tell him he is mean, how can I tell him he has deceived me, how can I disgrace him in the eyes of the world after that? I have degraded myself by ever thinking of him as my husband. If I say what you tell me to say to him—I am owning that I have degraded myself to his face. I can’t do that. After what has passed between us, I can’t do that! The shame of it would be nothing to him. But the shame of it would be unendurable to me.”

There, without reserve, was one of the peculiarities of her nature: a sensitive horror of contact with anything base, so intense that it blinded her to what she owed to herself and placed her, by her own nobleness, in danger of a false position. Until then I had been a little uncertain whether my advice had been proper. After those words I felt no doubt it was the best that could be given, and I pressed it again.

She shook her head.

“He has been intimate enough with me to ask me to be his wife. He has stood high enough in my estimation to obtain my consent. I can’t tell him to his face that he is the most contemptible of living creatures, after that!”

“But, my dear Miss Rachel,” I remonstrated, “it’s equally impossible for you to withdraw without giving some reason.”

“I shall say that I have thought it over, and that I am satisfied it will be best for both of us if we part.”

“No more than that?”

“No more.”

“Have you thought what he may say?”

“He may say what he pleases.”

I could not but admire her delicacy and her resolution; and I could not but see she was putting herself in the wrong. I entreated her to consider her position. I reminded her she would expose herself to odious misconstruction.

“You can’t brave public opinion,” I said, “at the command of private feeling.”

“I can,” she answered. “I have done it already.”

“What do you mean?”

“You have forgotten the Moonstone, Mr. Bruff. Have I not braved public opinion there, with my own private reasons for it?”

That answer silenced me. It set me trying—vainly—to trace, out of that strange avowal, the explanation of her conduct at the time of the Diamond’s disappearance. Perhaps I might have managed it when I was younger. I could not manage it now.

I made a last remonstrance before we returned to the house. She was immovable. When I left her that day my feelings were in conflict. She was obstinate; she was wrong. She was interesting; she was admirable; she was deeply to be pitied. I made her promise to write to me the moment she had any news, and I returned to my business in London with a mind ill at ease.

On the evening of my return—before her promised letter could possibly reach me—I was surprised by a visit from Mr. Ablewhite the elder. He informed me that Mr. Godfrey had received his dismissal—and had accepted it—that very day.

With the view I already took, the fact stated in those words made the motive plain. He needed a large sum of money, and needed it by a given time. Rachel’s income might have helped him to live; it could not help him to raise capital. Lady Verinder’s will had saved Rachel from him by the mere legal impossibility of his profiting as he intended; and Rachel had released herself without encountering serious opposition.

Any exultation I might have felt at this lucky turn was checked by what passed at my interview with old Mr. Ablewhite.

He came, of course, to ask whether I could explain Miss Verinder’s extraordinary conduct. I could not. The annoyance of my refusal, added to irritation produced by some recent interview with his son, threw him off his guard. His looks and language convinced me that Miss Verinder would find him a merciless man to deal with when he joined the ladies at Brighton the next day.
