I mentioned my lady earlier. Consider this: the Diamond could never have vanished from our household if it hadn't first been gifted to my lady's daughter. And that daughter would never have existed to receive such a gift if my lady hadn't brought her into the world through pain and labor. Therefore, if we trace our investigation backward to my lady, we are certain to reach the root of the matter. For anyone engaged in a task of this magnitude, knowing exactly where to begin is a profound relief.

If you are familiar with the fashionable world, you likely know of the three renowned Miss Herncastle sisters: Adelaide, Caroline, and Julia—the youngest and, in my humble opinion, the finest of the trio. I had ample opportunity to judge their character, as you shall soon discover. I entered the service of their father, the old Lord, at the tender age of fifteen as a page-boy, tasked with attending to these three honorable young ladies. I remained in that capacity until Miss Julia wed the late Sir John Verinder. He was an excellent man, though one who required management; fortunately, he found someone willing to provide it. Indeed, he flourished under her care, growing prosperous and content from the day she married him until the moment she breathed her last sigh and closed his eyes forever.

It bears mentioning that I accompanied the bride to her husband's estate upon their marriage. "Sir John," my lady declared, "I cannot function without Gabriel Betteredge." To which Sir John replied, "Nor can I, my lady." Thus was my fate sealed. It mattered little to me where I served, provided my mistress and I remained united. Observing that my lady took a keen interest in outdoor affairs, farming, and estates, I naturally developed a similar passion—a sentiment fueled by the fact that I was the seventh son of a small farmer myself. My lady placed me under the supervision of the bailiff, where I performed diligently, earned satisfaction, and secured promotion.

Years later, on a Monday, my lady announced, "Sir John, your current bailiff is a senile old fool. Pension him generously and appoint Gabriel Betteredge to his post." By Tuesday, Sir John confirmed, "Your wish is my command; the bailiff is pensioned, and Gabriel Betteredge takes his place." You often hear tales of couples living wretchedly together; here is a testament to the contrary. May it serve as a warning to some and encouragement to others.

So, I found myself in a position of trust and honor, residing in a modest cottage of my own. My mornings were spent patrolling the estate, my afternoons managing accounts, and my evenings enjoying a pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. What more could a man desire? Recall what Adam sought when alone in Eden; if you do not fault him, do not fault me.

The woman who caught my eye was the housekeeper at my cottage, named Selina Goby. I share the sentiments of the late William Cobbett regarding the selection of a spouse: ensure she chews her food thoroughly and places her feet firmly on the ground when walking, and you need fear no further complications. Selina excelled in both regards, which was one reason for my choice. Another, purely practical motive also influenced me. As a single woman, she charged me a weekly fee for her board and services. As my wife, however, she could not demand payment, rendering her labor gratis. This perspective, blending economy with affection, guided my decision.

I presented this logic to my mistress as a matter of duty. "I have been contemplating Selina Goby," I stated, "and I believe, my lady, it would be more economical to marry her than to retain her services." My lady erupted in laughter, expressing equal shock at my phrasing and my principles. Presumably, a jest tickled her fancy—one reserved for those of high station. Unaware of its deeper meaning, I proceeded anyway. Naturally, Selina agreed.

As the wedding date approached, and discussions arose regarding a new coat for the ceremony, doubts crept into my mind. Having consulted with other men in similar predicaments, I learned that nearly all privately wished to escape the ordeal about a week prior. I went even further; I attempted to withdraw entirely. However, justice dictates compensation when a man renounces a contract. Adhering to English law and careful deliberation, I offered Selina Goby a featherbed and fifty shillings to dissolve the engagement. You may find this incredulous, yet it is true: she foolishly refused. From that moment, my resolve crumbled. I procured the cheapest coat available and endured the remainder of the process with minimal cost.

We were neither a blissful nor a miserable union; we were simply incompatible. With the best intentions, we constantly obstructed one another. When I ascended the stairs, she descended; when she sought to descend, I ascended. Such is the nature of married life, based on my experience. After five years of such stairway misunderstandings, divine providence intervened, relieving us of each other by taking my wife. I was left with my daughter, Penelope, as my sole child.

Shortly thereafter, Sir John passed away, leaving my lady with her only remaining child, Miss Rachel. I have spoken poorly of my lady if you require confirmation that Penelope was cared for under her direct supervision, educated diligently, and eventually promoted to become Miss Rachel's personal maid. As for me, I continued my duties as bailiff year after year until Christmas 1847, when a significant change occurred.

On that fateful day, my lady invited herself to tea in my cottage. She noted that, counting from the year I began as a page-boy under the old lord, I had served her for over fifty years. She then handed me a magnificent woolen waistcoat, woven by her own hands, intended to keep me warm during the biting winter. I accepted this grand gesture, utterly speechless at the honor bestowed upon me. Yet, to my astonishment, the waistcoat was not merely a token of esteem but a subtle bribe. My lady had discerned that I was aging faster than I realized and sought to persuade me—through gentle coaxing—to relinquish my arduous outdoor role as bailiff and assume the leisurely position of house steward within the mansion.

I resisted this indignity as fiercely as possible. However, my mistress understood my vulnerabilities perfectly; she framed the offer as a favor to herself. Our dispute concluded with me wiping my eyes, behaving like a foolish elder, clutching my new woolen vest, and agreeing to consider the proposal.

The turmoil in my mind following her departure was immense. Seeking solace, I employed the remedy that has never failed me in times of doubt: smoking a pipe and reading *Robinson Crusoe*. Within five minutes of engaging with that extraordinary tale, I stumbled upon a comforting passage on page 158: "Today we love, what tomorrow we hate." Clarity instantly emerged. Today, I desired to remain the farm-bailiff; tomorrow, armed with the wisdom of *Robinson Crusoe*, I would embrace the alternative. By projecting myself into tomorrow's mindset, the decision was made. Thus, I slept soundly as Lady Verinder's farm-bailiff and awoke the next morning as her house-steward. All resolved comfortably, thanks to *Robinson Crusoe*!

My daughter Penelope recently glanced over my shoulder, observing my progress. She commended the beauty of my writing and the truthfulness of every word. However, she raised a valid objection: the narrative thus far bears no resemblance to the story I was commissioned to tell. Instead of recounting the history of the Diamond, I have chronicled my own life. This curiosity escapes me entirely. Do authors who write for a living ever find their own lives intruding upon their subjects as I have? If so, I sympathize deeply.

For now, this constitutes another false start and a waste of fine paper. What remains? Only for you to maintain your patience and for me to commence anew—for the third time.
