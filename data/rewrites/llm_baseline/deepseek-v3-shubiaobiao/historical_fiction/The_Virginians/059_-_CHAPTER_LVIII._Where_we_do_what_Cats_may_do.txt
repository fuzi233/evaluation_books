Our Virginians, like many young men of their time, had embraced staunch Jacobite sympathies, professing fervent loyalty to the exiled Stuart line. The Old Pretender himself had once granted Madam Esmond’s father the title of Marquis, a distinction she could not entirely dismiss, however fallen the family’s fortunes. As for the whispers about her sister, Madame de Bernstein, and the Chevalier—well, such scandals paled beside the domestic irregularities of the Hanoverian kings. Yet, king *de facto* or *de jure*, a sovereign was a sovereign, and Madam Esmond was adamant: her sons must present themselves at court. Who knew? The King might even bestow some mark of favor—a post, a ribbon—upon them. After all, the Virginia Esmonds were as noble as any in England.

Thus commanded, the young men prepared to make their bows at Kensington. Had their mother known George borrowed Harry’s best laced coat for the occasion, her displeasure would have been severe. In those days, the King’s drawing-room welcomed his loyal subjects almost daily, and with war brewing, patriotic fervor ensured a steady stream of visitors. The monarch himself, though longing for Hanover, remained prudently in England—his ministers wisely deterring any risky voyages. A certain German countess, elevated to British peerage, provided consolation, her native tongue soothing his homesick heart.

It was in this setting that George Warrington, accompanied by his uncle and General Lambert, approached Kensington Palace. They arrived by hackney-coach—Lord Wrotham’s carriage having been reclaimed—and entered through the grand portico, passing through tapestry-lined galleries adorned with masterpieces. Titian’s *Venus*, Rubens’ *St. Francis*, Van Dyck’s royal portraits—George, wide-eyed, absorbed it all, barely heeding Lambert’s whispered commentary on the assembled courtiers.

“There’s Admiral Boscawen, who fired the first shot in your colonies,” murmured Lambert. “That gold-laden figure is Mr. Fox, once Minister, now content with a lucrative sinecure. And yonder—ah, the Duke’s approach cuts short my lecture.”

The royal party soon emerged: first the King, a keen-eyed little man in plain snuff-colored suit and Garter ribbon; then the Duke of Cumberland, broad and scarlet-faced; and finally, towering above them all, the formidable Mr. Pitt. Even George, awestruck, felt an instinctive urge to doff his hat as the great statesman passed, his thunderous presence dwarfing the monarch himself.

His Majesty moved through the room with brisk familiarity, pausing here and there. He recognized Lambert—a veteran of Dettingen—and praised his promotion warmly. Then his gaze fell upon George.

“This is the other brother?” the King remarked dryly. “Timely arrival, else the first might have spent the whole fortune.”

Sir Miles, bursting with pride, nearly wept at this royal notice. “The greatest monarch! The finest gentleman!” he exclaimed afterward, as they departed in his coach, where he insisted on hosting Lambert and George for a modest collation—six-year-old mutton, no less, and a questionable cordial of Lady Warrington’s own making.

The evening dissolved into a blur of flattery. Sir Miles extolled George’s (largely imagined) heroic reception at court; Flora and Dora gazed adoringly; even young Miles piped in with an inconvenient truth. Lambert, observing the spectacle, could only marvel.

Later, recounting the day to his wife, the General was succinct.

“Humbugs,” he declared.

“Hypocrites!” she agreed.

“Bo,” said Lambert.

“Why ‘Bo’?”

“To a goose, my dear.”

And with that enigmatic close, the chapter of courtly farce ended.
