The reverberations of Fra Girolamo’s voice still clung to the cool, porous limestone of the Duomo, a mythic frequency settling into the quiet dust of everyday life. Baldassarre stood paralyzed within this acoustic sanctuary, his internal tempest utterly untranslatable into the dominant Florentine dialect echoing around him. Possessed by the fading cadence of the Frate’s sermon, he had not noticed the subtle disruption in the sonic environment—the soft, deliberate footfalls of another man who had entered through the rear portal. 

It was Piero di Cosimo, who had paid no mind to the preaching, drawn instead by the mere visual commodity of an escaped prisoner. During the heavy, resonant pause in which the preacher and his audience had surrendered to an inarticulate, collective emotion, Piero advanced. The scent of oxidized linseed oil and sharp turpentine cut through the cathedral’s ancient aroma of beeswax and damp earth as he touched Baldassarre on the arm.

The old man looked round, tears still tracking slowly through the deep, agrarian furrows of his face. He released a vigorous sigh, a sound like wind rushing through winter-barren vines, signaling an end to his emotional harvest.

“Shall I cut your cords for you?” the painter asked in a low murmur, careful not to shatter the church's delicate acoustic ecology. “I have heard how you were made prisoner.”

Baldassarre did not reply immediately. He tasted the metallic tang of suspicion, observing the officious stranger. At last, he offered a quiet, “If you will.”

“Better come outside,” Piero said.

Again, that guarded, untrusting stare. Piero, partly guessing the old man's silent dialect of fear, smiled faintly, drew a small knife, and sliced through the thick hemp. As the cords parted with a sharp *snick*, Piero noted the peculiar, fermented intensity in the prisoner's expression, deciding the rumors of his madness were perhaps a misinterpretation of a mind too deeply steeped in trauma to speak the language of the sane. *If he does any mischief,* Piero thought, *he’ll soon get tied up again. The poor devil shall have a chance to taste freedom, at least.*

“You are afraid of me,” Piero murmured, his tone a flat contrast to the echoing vaults. “You don’t want to tell me anything about yourself.”

Baldassarre was folding his arms, savoring the slow, peppery rush of blood returning to his starved muscles—a physical reawakening as profound as the first taste of salted bread after a long famine. He answered with a less suspicious gaze and a tone of quiet, unyielding decision, protecting the borders of his private history. “No, I have nothing to tell.”

“As you please,” Piero said. “But perhaps you want shelter, and may not know how hospitable we Florentines are to visitors with torn doublets and empty stomachs. Sustenance is the first duty of the living. There’s an hospital for poor travelers outside all our gates. They serve a thick, restorative *ribollita* simmered with winter cavolo nero, and crusty, unsalted bread soaked in the sharp, grassy bite of fresh-pressed olive oil. If you liked, I could put you in the way to one. There’s no danger from your French soldier. He has been sent off.”

Baldassarre nodded, accepting the offer of sustenance in his native tongue of silence, and together they left the vaulted resonance of the church.

As they stepped into the Via dell’ Oriuolo, the sacred quiet was violently overthrown by the harsh, industrial clatter of the city—the jarring dissonance of iron-rimmed cartwheels and the aggressive, sharp consonants of street merchants. This modern noise pollution assaulted the senses, a stark erosion of the spiritual calm. 

“You wouldn’t like to sit to me for your portrait, should you?” Piero asked as they navigated toward the gate of Santa Croce. “I am a painter: I would give you money to get your portrait.”

Baldassarre’s suspicion flared, hot and bitter as raw garlic. He recognized the superficial commercialization of art in the painter’s request—the desire to commodify his untranslatable suffering into a palatable visual lexicon for the elite. “No,” he said, with absolute finality.

“Ah!” Piero said curtly. “Well, go straight on, and you’ll find the Porta Santa Croce, and outside it the hospital for travelers. So you’ll not accept any service from me?”

“I give you thanks for what you have done already. I need no more.”

“It is well,” said Piero with a shrug, and they turned away from each other, their timelines diverging back into the city's chaotic flow. 

*A mysterious old tiger,* the artist mused, turning the encounter over in his mind. *Ugly—with deep lines—looking as if the heavy iron plough and the harrow had tilled the very soil of his heart. A fine, earthy contrast to my bland and smiling Messer Greco—my Bacco trionfante, who has married the fair Antigone in contradiction to all history and fitness.* Tito, with his polished, dominant Greek lexicon, consumed the world without ever truly tasting its sorrow; he viewed life as utilitarian fuel, whereas this old man was a living, breathing archive of eroded cultural memory. *Aha! No wonder the young scholar’s blood curdled uncomfortably, like milk turned by a thunderstorm, at the old fellow’s clutch!*

When Piero re-entered the Piazza del Duomo, the multitude that had been listening to Fra Girolamo was pouring out from all the doors. The haste of their departure proved how fiercely they viewed the Frate's sermon as a vital ingredient for their day, a necessary spiritual sustenance before returning to their trades. The artist leaned against the Baptistery, delighting in the rich olfactory and visual tapestry of the crowd—faces etched with the character of Masaccio's peasants from fifty years prior, complex and weathered as aged pecorino.

This morning’s congregation was a profound cross-section of Florence’s dietary and political strata. There were men of high birth—the fervent Piagnoni—who carried the scent of roasted squab and imported saffron in their heavy wools. Men like Francesco Valori, handsome and arrogant, whose palates craved the immediate, divine authority to reshape freedom to their own liking. Beside them walked men like Piero Capponi and Soderini, wise and cautious, who consumed the Frate's ideas not as a spiritual feast, but as utilitarian fuel to keep the Medici expelled and the Republic fed. At their elbows were doctors of law, men whose native dialects had been largely overwritten by the dominant lexicon of Accursius, yet who still found their blood stirred by the rhythmic, acoustic power of the Frate’s voice—men like Messer Luca Corsini, who would one day hurl paving stones in the street, driven by the intoxicating fermentation of religion and liberty.

Among the dignities in black *lucco* and furred mantles were scholars of the Strozzi and Acciajoli lines, sensitive men who tasted the ash in the city's wealth and sought the austere, monastic diet of San Marco. There were artists, too, elevated by Savonarola’s teachings, like the young painter who had recently frescoed the divine child on the bare walls of the Frate’s cell, unawares that he would soon trade his earthly pigments for the tonsure, becoming Fra Bartolommeo. The mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni hurried past, carrying the tidings of the sermon like a rare, perishable spice to his friend Pico della Mirandola, whose own life was a flame sputtering in its final hours. 

There were well-born women in scrupulous, undyed linens, and a vast proportion of the *popolani*, the middle-class artisans smelling of tanned leather, woodsmoke, and raw wool. But the most striking element of the crowd was the long stream of poorer tradesmen. To them, the Frate’s biblical interpretations were not abstract theology but a slow-simmered, nourishing broth. In the dim, quiet hours over their stitching boards, the memory of his resonant voice provided the acoustic harmony necessary for their survival, illuminating their grueling, repetitive labor with the sustenance of divine science.

Yet, scattered among this faithful harvest were those who found the Frate’s recipe wholly unpalatable. Feigned Mediceans, terrified of the popular party, offered artificial smiles like cheap, adulterated wine. There were pragmatic republicans who saw Savonarola merely as an ambitious monk—a man who had mastered the city's political lexicon and must be managed as a social fact. And there were the bitter enemies, the licentious young men who detested the Frate for stripping the city of its rich, decadent banquets. Men of ideas, like young Niccolò Macchiavelli, observed the crowd with cold precision, seeking only the raw data to export to country villas. Meanwhile, men of crude appetites, like Dolfo Spini, hunted the Frate as a public nuisance who had made the game scarce, seeking to feed their own starved hatreds.

Perhaps no preacher had ever drawn such heterogeneous ingredients into a single pot. Savonarola’s massive influence lay in the highly complex, heavily spiced character of his preaching. Baldassarre, lost in an ecstasy of self-martyring revenge, had digested only one bitter herb from that vast spiritual garden. The Frate’s voice offered strains that appealed to the finest, most delicate sensibilities of the soul, yet it also contained heavy, coarse elements that gratified low egoism, fed gossiping curiosity, and sustained timorous superstition. 

His imperious need for predominance, his labyrinthine allegorical recipes of Scripture, and his enigmatic visions never ceased, in his own large soul, to be ennobled by a passionate sense of the infinite. He possessed a profound agrarian sympathy for the common good, demanding the subjection of selfish, rich appetites so that the entire community might be fed. But for the mass of his audience, whose palates had been ruined by the harsh, commercialized flavors of Florentine politics, the true sustenance of his preaching lay only in his sensational, denunciatory visions. Having once nourished that audience and held them in his mastery, it became necessary to his nature—and, he believed, to their welfare—that he should keep them at his table.

The effect on his internal ecology was inevitable. No man ever struggled to retain power over a mixed, cacophonous multitude without suffering a vitiation of his own spirit; his recipe must inevitably be altered to suit their baser hungers, substituting the pure, subtle flavors of his best insight for the heavy salts of their lower needs. The mysteries of human character are seldom presented in a way more fitted to rebuke the facile knowingness of historians than in Girolamo Savonarola. We can offer him a reverence that requires no blinding of the palate to the truth, if we regard his life as a great, generational saga—a slow, inward fermentation that accompanied his outward political changes. 

Up to this period, his need for ascendancy had burned undiscernibly in the pure, clarifying flame of his zeal for God and man. It was the fashion of the ancients, when an ox was led out for sacrifice, to chalk over its dark spots, offering the gods a false, unblemished whiteness. Let us fling away the chalk. The victim is spotted; the meat is bruised by the heavy, dissonant world. But it is not in vain that his mighty heart is laid upon the altar, a profound offering meant to nourish the highest, most ancient hopes of mankind.
