As the days passed, our thoughts turned to a long-overdue visit to Virginia, where the Young Rachel's cabin accommodations awaited us. Our mother's fervent appeal had touched our hearts, and we envisioned a joyous reunion with our family. However, fate had other plans. Our child fell ill, and the prospect of a perilous journey was too daunting for the mother to bear. The Young Rachel set sail without us that year, leaving us to anxiously await her safe return.

The following year, a new challenge arose when my worship suffered a debilitating bout of gout, which confined him to his bed for several weeks. Though it ultimately cleared his mind and revitalized his spirits, it delayed our plans once more. Then, just as we thought the stars were aligning in our favor, we received disheartening news from Jamaica. The General, who had returned from his governorship a little wealthier but much sorrowful, shared the devastating news of his beloved wife's passing. She had succumbed to the fever, leaving behind a legacy of love and a deep sense of loss.

As we tried to comfort the grieving widower, my wife's presence was a balm to his wounded heart. Her gentle nature and kind words brought solace to his sorrow, and it was clear that she had become a source of strength for him. Little Hetty, with her unwavering devotion, proved to be his greatest comfort. She had turned down several advantageous matches in the island, including one from Lord Wrotham's heir, choosing instead to remain by her father's side. When the suggestion was made for her to marry and live at Oakhurst, she flatly refused, stating that as long as her father needed her, she would stay with him.

We proposed that the General join them at the Manor for six months, and then they could move to Oakhurst for the remaining six. However, Hetty insisted that her father could not bear the thought of returning to Oakhurst without her mother, and she would not marry for material gain. The General, sensing her resolve, attributed her reluctance to a past disappointment that she had never spoken about, and we chose to respect her wishes. Meanwhile, my brother Lambert had established himself as a parson near Winchester, and his wife, Mrs. Jack, had begun to make her presence known at our home. While we welcomed her warmly, we couldn't help but notice her peculiar views on Hetty's decision to remain single.
