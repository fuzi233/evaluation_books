Buck carried within him something older than the warm lawns of Judge Miller’s place—something that had slept under kitchen floors, under hearthstones, under the very language of home, until the North’s hard weather woke it and fed it day by day. Trail life was a lean cook: it gave no garnish, no second helpings, no mercy. Yet what it did give—cold, hunger, labor, danger—was the kind of fare that seasons bone and spirit. The change in Buck was not loud. It rose like a slow boil in a covered pot. Newborn cunning kept him steady. He learned to waste no motion, no breath, no anger. Even with Spitz, the white dog who carried his dominance like a knife between his teeth, Buck did not rush. He kept his appetite for the final bite hidden.

Spitz, for his part, could not leave Buck alone. Perhaps he smelled the threat the way a baker smells yeast waking in dough—silent, inevitable, swelling. Spitz bared teeth whenever he could, shouldered him at harnessing, stole inches of space, stared too long. He wanted the fight that ended everything.

The chance nearly came on a night so bitter the air seemed to crack when you inhaled it.

They made camp on the shore of Lake Le Barge, and everything about it was wrong. Snow drove sideways. The wind cut like a blade heated white, not by flame but by cruelty. Darkness pressed in so close the men groped like blind cooks at a cold stove, searching for anything that would light. Behind them stood a wall of rock—sheer and black—and so Perrault and François were forced to build their fire on the ice itself. They had thrown away the tent back at Dyea to travel light. Now the lightness cost them. A handful of driftwood sticks, stingy and damp, gave a fire that gnawed down through the ice and left them crouching to eat supper by a hole of thin warmth, their food swallowed more than tasted.

Buck found shelter where the rock broke the wind. He dug and turned and pressed snow into a nest the way his body remembered without being taught, packing it until it held heat like a covered dish. It was snug—so snug that when François thawed the fish over the fire and began handing out rations, Buck did not want to leave his hollow. The fish smelled sharp and oily, sea-cold turned to smoke-cold, and he came because hunger called louder than comfort. He ate, then returned quickly to the nest that was his.

It was not his anymore.

A low, possessive snarl met him in the dark. Spitz lay in Buck’s warmth, in Buck’s place, his pale coat a smear of arrogance against the snow.

Buck had avoided trouble. He had turned his shoulder to insults. He had swallowed anger the way a starving dog swallows bones—whole, quick, no chewing. But this was theft of the most intimate kind: the stealing of heat, of shelter, of the small domestic center a creature makes in a wilderness. Something in him—older than training, older than the club—rose up with a roar.

He sprang.

They burst from the ruined nest in a knot of fur and teeth, rolling and snapping, their breath puffing hard, their bodies striking the ice-packed snow with dull thuds. Spitz, who had read Buck as heavy and timid, was stunned by the sudden ferocity. François, seeing them shoot out, understood at once.

“A-a-ah!” he shouted, voice cracking like kindling. “Gif it to heem, by Gar! Gif it to heem, the dirty t’eef!”

Spitz gave nothing away. He circled, crying in rage, quick-footed, seeking an opening. Buck circled too—equally eager, but measuring, saving himself, watching for the advantage like a hunter watching wind and cover.

And then the night split open.

Perrault swore—a harsh sound that struck the air like iron. A club landed with a flat, resounding blow on ribs that were too close to the skin. A sharp yelp followed, and in that instant the camp was no longer camp. It was a pantry discovered by famine.

Shapes flowed out of the dark: skulking huskies—four or five score—skeleton dogs from some Indian village, drawn by the smell of food the way smoke draws wolves. They had crept in while Buck and Spitz fought. Now they rushed the fire, the packs, the men. When Perrault and François sprang among them with clubs, the invaders fought back, crazed by scent: bread, bacon, fish—anything. One had its head buried in the grub-box. Perrault’s club crashed on its gaunt ribs; the box toppled, spilled its precious insides onto the snow like a careless hand dumping flour into ash. In a blink, a score of starving mouths were on it. The clubs rained down, but hunger was armor. They yelped and howled under blows, jaws working as if the pain itself were something to chew. They ate with the frenzy of a people whose language for “tomorrow” has died.

The team dogs erupted from their nests, only to be met by the invaders’ teeth. Buck had never seen dogs like these—so thin it looked as though their bones might punch through their hides, eyes blazing in loose, draggled skins. They were terrible in their hunger, and their hunger made them irresistible. The team was driven back toward the cliff at the first onset. Buck was jumped by three at once; in moments his head and shoulders were ripped open, skin scored, blood warming then freezing. The noise was a maddened orchestra—howls, snarls, clubs thudding, men shouting, harness bells clinking as bodies slammed together.

Billee cried as he always cried, a high, pleading sound. Dave and Sol-leks, blood streaking their coats, fought shoulder to shoulder, grim as oxen. Joe snapped like a demon; once he caught a husky’s foreleg and crunched through bone, the sound like breaking dry sticks. Pike, the shameless malingerer, leaped onto the crippled animal and finished it with a quick twist, neck snapping. Buck got one by the throat; his teeth found the jugular, and blood surged hot into his mouth—warmth with a metallic tang that made him fiercer, made him forget pain. He sprang on another—

—and felt teeth sink into his own throat.

Spitz. Not as enemy in open battle, but as thief of advantage, slicing in from the side while Buck was occupied. It was a treacherous bite, meant not simply to wound but to topple. Under the crush of those starving bodies, one fall could mean death.

Perrault and François, having beaten the worst of the raiders away from the grub, rushed to the dogs. The wave of famished beasts rolled back before their clubs; Buck shook free. But only for a heartbeat. The men were forced again to protect what food remained, and the huskies surged back toward the team.

Panic became strategy. Billee, frightened into a sudden courage, broke through the savage circle and fled over the lake ice. Pike and Dub followed. The others streamed after, the whole team spilling out into the open, bells jangling like frantic cutlery.

Buck gathered himself to follow—and saw Spitz rush him, aiming to knock him down into the mob. Buck braced for the impact, held his ground just long enough to keep his feet, then turned and fled with the rest, the ice beneath them groaning with cold.

Later the nine gathered in the forest, huddled under trees that offered little but the illusion of shelter. No one pursued them. The invaders had come for food, not sport. Still, the team was wrecked. Each dog wore four or five wounds, some deep and ugly. Dub’s hind leg hung wrong. Dolly—newest to the team—had a throat torn ragged. Joe’s eye was gone, leaving a dark, wet hollow. Billee’s ear was shredded, and he whimpered through the night, a thin, broken sound that made sleep hard.

At daybreak they limped back to camp. The marauders were gone. The two men were not in the forgiving mood of people who have eaten well. Half the grub was gone. The huskies had chewed through lashings and canvas coverings. Anything remotely eatable had been tested by teeth: Perrault’s moose-hide moccasins eaten down, chunks gnawed from leather traces, even two feet of lash bitten clean from François’s whip, as if hunger had decided to sample the very means of discipline. François stared at the frayed end, mourning it the way a cook mourns a lost knife.

Then he turned to his dogs and spoke softly, voice worn thin. “Ah, my frien’s… mebbe it mek you mad dog, dose many bites. Mebbe all mad dog, sacredam! Wot you t’ink, eh, Perrault?”

Perrault shook his head, uneasy. Four hundred miles still lay between them and Dawson; madness in the team would be a disaster they could not afford.

Two hours of cursing and raw work got the harnesses into some shape. The team, stiff with wounds, was put back on the trail. Ahead waited the hardest stretch yet—the Thirty Mile River.

It ran wide open, wild water shouldering aside the frost. Ice held only in eddies and quiet corners, thin and treacherous as sugar crust. Six days of exhausting toil they spent on those thirty miles, and every foot was paid for in risk—dog and man.

A dozen times Perrault, probing ahead with his pole, broke through ice bridges. Each time, he saved himself by the way he held the pole so it fell across the hole, giving him purchase like a plank. But a cold snap had tightened the world; the thermometer fell to fifty below. Whenever he went in, he had to build a fire and dry his clothes, for wet at that cold meant death in minutes. The fire was their only hearth, their only kitchen; they fed it as carefully as they fed themselves, coaxing flame from mean wood, breathing smoke that smelled of wet bark and desperation.

Nothing daunted Perrault. It was why he had been chosen. He pushed his small, weazened face into the frost and drove on from dim dawn to dark, skirting frowning shores on rim ice that bent and crackled beneath them. They dared not halt on it. Once, the sled broke through with Dave and Buck. They were hauled out half-frozen, water clinging to them in sheets that turned instantly to ice. The men built the usual fire and ran them close around it, forcing them to move, to sweat, to thaw. The flames licked so near their fur they were singed, the smell of burnt hair mixing with fish grease and smoke.

Another time Spitz went through and dragged the whole team after him as if he meant to drown them all in his pride. Buck, at the rear, braced backward with every muscle, forepaws on the slippery edge, ice quivering and snapping around him. Behind him Dave strained too, grim and silent, and behind the sled François pulled until tendons stood out like rope.

Once the rim ice broke away both in front and behind, leaving them trapped between cliff and water. There was no escape except up. Perrault scaled the cliff by what felt like a miracle. François prayed for the same miracle, and then made it with work: every thong, every lashing, every bit of harness was made into a long rope. The dogs were hoisted one by one, swinging above the river like sacks of grain. François came up last, after sled and load. Then began the search for a way down. By rope they descended, inch by inch, and night found them back on the river with only a quarter mile gained.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua and good ice, Buck was played out. The others were scarcely better. But Perrault, driven by time and pride, pushed them late and early as if he could knead strength into them by sheer will. One day they ran thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon; the next, thirty-five to the Little Salmon; the third, forty, bringing them toward the Five Fingers.

Buck’s feet were the weak point. The huskies’ pads were tight and hard as cured hide, shaped by generations of ice and stone. Buck’s had softened in the many generations since his last wild ancestor had been tamed to fire and roof. Now the packed trail cut him like a rasp. All day he limped in agony. When camp was made he dropped like a dead dog, ribs heaving. Hunger gnawed him, but he would not rise even for fish. François brought the ration to his mouth.

Each night after supper François sat with Buck’s paws between his hands, rubbing them as carefully as if he were working dough, trying to drive warmth back into the flesh. Then he sacrificed the tops of his own moccasins, cutting and shaping them into four little coverings for Buck’s feet. The leather smelled of smoke and sweat and old trail. Those moccasins were relief—softening the cruelty of the snow the way a good broth softens a hard crust.

One morning François forgot them, and Perrault’s weazened face cracked into a grin, because Buck lay on his back, four feet waving helplessly in the air, refusing to move without his foot-gear, as though he had learned not only pain but the language of protest.

Later his feet hardened. The moccasins wore through, and were thrown away.

At the Pelly one morning, as they harnessed up, Dolly—never notable before—went suddenly mad.

It began with a long wolf howl, so full of heartbreak it made every dog bristle. The sound sliced through the morning’s hush, through the little domestic noises of camp—snapping straps, muttered oaths, the soft thump of fish being handled—and turned them all into one raw, frightened silence. Then Dolly sprang straight for Buck, frothing, eyes gone wrong.

Buck had never seen madness, but his body understood it instantly as horror. He fled in a blind panic. Dolly followed one leap behind, panting and snarling, foam flying. He could not lose her; terror drove him, and her madness drove her. He plunged through the wooded breast of an island, crashed down to the lower end, crossed a back channel filled with rough ice to another island, then a third. He curved back toward the main river, desperate, and started to cross it.

All the while he did not look back, yet he heard her—always one leap behind, her breathing a harsh rasp, her snarl like a saw.

François called from far off, a quarter mile away. Buck doubled back toward that voice the way a starving creature runs toward the smell of food. He put all his faith in François. The dog-driver stood with his axe poised, a clean tool for a terrible job. Buck shot past him, and the axe came down on Dolly’s head. The sound was final.

Buck staggered against the sled, exhausted, sobbing for air, legs shaking. He was helpless.

Spitz saw it as opportunity.

He sprang upon Buck and, twice, his teeth sank in and ripped to the bone, taking payment while Buck could not answer. Then François’s lash whistled. It fell on Spitz with a fury that tasted of justice. Buck, gasping, had the grim satisfaction of watching Spitz receive the worst whipping yet given.

“One devil, dat Spitz,” Perrault said. “Some dam day heem keel dat Buck.”

“Dat Buck two devils,” François replied, eyes narrowed. “All de tam I watch dat Buck I know for sure. Lissen: some dam fine day heem get mad lak hell an’ den heem chew dat Spitz all up an’ spit heem out on de snow. Sure. I know.”

From then on, it was war.

Spitz, as lead-dog and acknowledged master, felt his supremacy threatened by this Southland dog who did not die, did not soften, did not fade the way the others from warm places had. Buck endured and even prospered, growing stronger in that harsh way meat toughens and sweetens under smoke. What made him more dangerous was not just strength and savagery—it was the club that had beaten blind rashness out of him. He was cunning. He could wait. He could hide his hunger for power until the moment it could be eaten cleanly.

And Buck wanted leadership. He wanted it with a pride as old as the traces. It was a pride that made dogs labor beyond sense, that lured them to die in harness rather than be cut loose. It was in Dave at the wheel, in Sol-leks pulling with all he had, in the way the whole team would change at break of camp—sour brutes becoming straining, eager creatures once the work began. It held Spitz too, making him thrash shirkers and blunderers. It made him fear Buck.

Buck began to threaten his rule openly. He stepped between Spitz and the dogs Spitz meant to punish. He did it deliberately, not from fairness—fair play had frozen out of him long ago—but from strategy.

One night a heavy snowfall buried the camp. In the morning Pike, the malingerer, did not appear. He was hidden in his nest beneath a foot of snow like a thief under a table after supper. François called. He searched. Nothing.

Spitz raged, sniffing, digging, snarling so horribly that Pike trembled in his hiding place, hearing the sound as if it were teeth already in his flesh.

When Pike was finally unearthed and Spitz flew at him to punish him, Buck launched himself between them with equal rage. The move was sudden and shrewd. Spitz was hurled backward, knocked off his feet. Pike, heartened by this mutiny, sprang on his fallen leader. Buck joined in—no code, no honor, only advantage.

François, chuckling even as he kept justice, brought his lash down on Buck with all his might. Buck did not yield. François struck again, then used the butt of the whip. The blow half-stunned Buck, knocked him back. The lash fell again and again until Buck was driven off, while Spitz rose and punished Pike soundly for his many offenses.

After that, Buck continued his interference—but craftily, when François was not near. Under Buck’s covert rebellion the team’s solidarity began to crumble. Dave and Sol-leks stayed what they were, but the others slid into insubordination. Bickering grew constant. Camp became a bedlam of snarls and quarrels that tore at the night’s quiet. François lived in dread of waking to the final fight between Buck and Spitz, and more than once he burst from his sleeping robe at sounds of strife, lash in hand, only to find the others tangled in their own feuds.

Still the great fight did not come. They pulled into Dawson one dreary afternoon with it hanging ahead like a storm that refuses to break.

Dawson was noise and work. Men everywhere, dogs everywhere, and every creature moving as if labor were the only law. Teams swung up and down the main street all day. At night the bells still passed, jingling through darkness like thin music. Dogs hauled cabin logs and firewood and freight to the mines, doing the work that horses did back in the Santa Clara Valley. Buck saw a few Southland dogs, out of place and soft-eyed; mostly he saw huskies, wolf-blooded and sharp.

And every night, regularly—at nine, at twelve, at three—the huskies sang.

It was not a song meant for the market stalls or the painted stage. It was older than commerce and too wild for applause. It rose from throats shaped by cold into a weird chant, pitched in a minor key with long-drawn wails and half-sobs. In that sound lived the acoustic world of the North: the far crack of trees in frost, the whisper of snow, the deep silence that made every howl a kind of truth. Buck delighted to join, his voice finding a place in the chorus as if he were remembering words his mouth had never learned.

Under the cold flame of the aurora borealis, under stars that seemed to leap and dance in the brittle air, the song carried the woe of unnumbered generations. It was the pleading of life in a land that answered with hunger. When Buck moaned with them, it was not philosophy; it was sensation and memory intertwined—pain of living, fear of dark, mystery of cold. It was as if some part of him spoke a dialect older than Judge Miller’s commands, a tongue that could not be translated into any warm-house vocabulary. He did not have words for it, only sound.

Seven days after reaching Dawson, they dropped down the steep bank by the Barracks to the Yukon Trail and turned back toward Dyea and salt water. Perrault carried urgent despatches—and more than that, he carried the fierce pride of travel. He meant to make the record trip of the year. Luck favored him: the week’s rest had put the dogs in trim; the trail was packed hard by later travelers; the police had laid grub deposits in places; and he traveled light.

They made Sixty Mile—a fifty-mile run—in the first day. The second day they boomed up the Yukon, well on their way to Pelly.

But fine running did not mean peace.

Buck’s quiet revolt had ruined the team’s unity. No longer did they leap in the traces as one creature. The encouragement Buck gave the rebels turned into petty crimes. Spitz was still lead-dog, but he was no longer feared as before. Pike stole half a fish one night and gulped it down under Buck’s protection. Another night Dub and Joe stood up to Spitz and forced him to forego the punishment they deserved. Even Billee whined less placatingly. Buck never came near Spitz without bristling and snarling. At times he swaggered openly before Spitz’s nose, the way a young cook might walk too close to an old master’s station just to claim the space.

The breakdown spread. The dogs fought among themselves more than ever until camp sounded like a kitchen where every pot boils over at once. Dave and Sol-leks remained unchanged, but the constant squabbling made them irritable. François swore strange barbarous oaths and stamped the snow and tore his hair, lash singing uselessly. When his back turned, the fighting started again. He backed up Spitz with his whip. Buck backed up the rest. François knew Buck was at the root of it, and Buck knew François knew—but Buck was too clever to be caught again in the act. In harness he worked faithfully; toil had become delight. Yet it was a greater delight to tangle traces and precipitate fights with a sly nudge here, a calculated shoulder there.

At the mouth of the Tahkeena one night after supper, Dub turned up a snowshoe rabbit. He blundered and missed. In an instant the whole team was in full cry, the sudden burst of sound ripping through the night’s hush like a knife through skin. A hundred yards away lay a camp of the Northwest Police with fifty huskies. They heard the chase and joined, the pack swelling until it was sixty dogs, a moving storm of paws and breath and bell-jingle.

The rabbit fled downriver, then turned into a small creek, running up its frozen bed. It skimmed lightly over the snow’s crust while the dogs plowed through, chest-deep at times, driving forward by brute force. Buck led the pack, his body flashing in the moonlight, low to the run, whining with eagerness. Bend after bend he drove, but he could not gain. Ahead the rabbit flitted like a pale frost wraith, quick and unreal.

In Buck, the old instincts surged—blood lust, joy of the kill—not as borrowed sport, not as distant entertainment bought and sold, but as something intimate and necessary. He ran not to shoot, not to pose, but to take living meat with his own teeth and feel warm blood wash his muzzle.

He was alive with every muscle, every joint, every sinew singing. He forgot himself in the pure rush of motion, flying under the stars over the dead matter of snow and ice that did not move.

But Spitz, cold and calculating even in that ecstasy, cut away from the pack. He crossed a narrow neck of land where the creek looped in a long bend, taking a shortcut as sharp as a thief’s hand.

Buck did not see him go. He rounded the bend, rabbit still ahead—and then another larger frost wraith dropped from the overhanging bank into the rabbit’s path.

Spitz.

The rabbit could not turn. Spitz’s white teeth caught it midair and broke its back. The rabbit shrieked—a humanlike, terrible sound—and at that cry, the whole pack raised a chorus of delight, a savage music that rolled through the cold night and made the wilderness itself seem to listen.
