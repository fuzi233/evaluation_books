The ink on the page dried with the precision of a final verdict. January twenty-first. London. The address was Regent Street, a thoroughfare of commerce and noise, yet the writer's voice sought the quietude of the cellar.

Dear Ginger,

I am recovering. The timeline suggests three months should suffice for such a restoration; the arithmetic of resilience dictates that any wound heals within a quarter-year. Yet, the body remembers what the mind attempts to bury. I have not healed completely, but I have achieved a tactical victory: I have stored my troubles in the cellar and ceased the futile practice of dragging them into the light.

One ought to document the impressions of London. However, familiarity has rendered the city invisible. I have resided here for decades, though the calendar claims otherwise.

Observe the address. Mr. Faucitt retains his inheritance. He anticipates liquidating it shortly. A man with a beard and a predatory gaze lunches with him constantly; significant transactions are rumored. Poor Faucitt. He is desperate to flee the metropolis, to retreat to the countryside and cultivate ducks. London has failed him. It is not the entity of his memory. For years, he wandered in a state of mournful displacement, searching for landmarks of a youth spent nearly thirty years prior. The tragedy of the returned exile is cyclical: every thirty years, London undergoes a metamorphosis. A shopfront shifts from blue to red. To the exiled consciousness, this is catastrophic. Faucitt resembles Rip Van Winkle. His initial shock occurred when the Empire Theatre became a music-hall. Subsequent devastation followed when the Tivoli vanished entirely. Finally, seeking solace in the baker's shop of Rupert Street—the site of his lodging in the eighteen-eighties—he discovered it converted into a dressmaker's establishment. Despair seized him until a magenta fog rolled in, confirming that certain atmospheric constants remain unchanged.

At Laurette et Cie., I am occupied. Not being a French scholar like you—do you recall Jules?—I initially mistook "Cie" for a junior partner's name, anticipating a handshake with Mr. Cie, a figure of admiration. I serve as the female counterpart to your position at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.; essentially, a right-hand woman. My duties involve hovering near the entrance, offering falsehoods regarding the weather—"Chawming weather, moddom!"—and directing clients to the staff who execute the labor. I could endure this indefinitely, provided Mr. Faucitt did not sell. Every Englishman I encounter possesses an obsession with property ownership in Loamshire, Hants, or Salop. Their singular objective is capital accumulation to repurchase ancestral estates, properties invariably sold in Act One to settle gambling debts.

Faucitt, as a child, inhabited a village in Gloucestershire near Cirencester—or rather, Cissister, a fact unknown to you. After fifty years of oblivion, he yearns to die there, amidst pigs and poultry. I accompanied him recently. Oh, Ginger, the English countryside! I cannot comprehend why any inhabit it. Consider the architecture: ancient grey stone, golden haystacks, squelching mud, massive trees, distant blue hills. The tranquility is absolute. Should I ever sell my soul, I demand forty years in an English parish as compensation.

My concern for your existence remains paramount. Last week, scanning the newspaper, I encountered the headline "International Match." Initially meaningless, the phrase triggered a recollection. This was the sport you once pursued as a professional snip. I traveled to Twickenham to witness the event, enduring a crush that threatened suffocation solely for your sake. The English wore white; the Welsh, red. After a spectator screamed himself hoarse, I inquired politely for the location of the English scrum-half. Immediately, a dozen assailants in red descended upon a diminutive figure who had secured the ball. Ginger, you are fortunate to remain seated behind your desk at Fillmore's outer office, protected by the law. Those were the scrum-half's duties. Stopping a rush. A routine occupation. How could you engage in such brutality? You possess depths I never suspected.

Descending Piccadilly on a bus, I observed a familiar silhouette: Mr. Carmyle. He has returned. Fortunately, he did not notice me. I require no reminders of New York at this juncture.

Regarding your news: cease sending it. It induces memories I wish to suppress. I write to relieve my own burden, but do not reply. Please understand.

Fillmore and Gladys Winch are wed. Based on my observation, this is the optimal outcome for Brother F. She is exceptional. I intend to correspond with him.

Laurette et Cie.
London
March 12th

Dear Ginger,

The Sunday paper announced the production of "The Primrose Way" in New York, noting its success. I am pleased, though newspapers publishing such trivia unsettle me.

Feeling isolated and distant from humanity, I performed a peculiar act: I visited your club and requested you. A uniformed attendant, paternal in tone, informed me you had not appeared recently, speculating you were abroad. He summoned a small boy, also in uniform, who skipped away chanting, "Mister Kemp! Mister Kemp!" in a piercing treble. Hearing your name echo in the distance evoked a strange sensation. I felt shame for causing inconvenience. When the boy returned, I placed twopence in his palm—a transgression of protocol, though he accepted it gladly.

Mr. Faucitt has sold the business and retired. I am adrift.

Monk's Crofton, Much Middleford, Salop.
April 18th

Dear Ginger,

What is the utility of escape? I attempt to flee New York, yet it pursues me to my sanctuary. Recently, walking aimlessly down the Strand, I encountered Fillmore, arm-in-arm with Mr. Carmyle. Evasion was impossible. First, Carmyle recognized me; second, the distance to evade Fillmore is negligible. I blushed for him. In the Strand itself. In my darkest nightmares, I never imagined him so colossal. Upon what sustenance does this Fillmore feed to attain such magnitude? Poor Gladys! Viewing him, she must feel akin to a bigamist.

Fillmore remains ambitious, discussing the acquisition of English plays. He has arrived to stage "The Primrose Way," residing at the Savoy. They escorted me there for lunch, celebrating as if reunited with a lost lamb. This was calamitous. Fillmore spoke of Broadway incessantly. By the time he finished digesting French pastry, lolling back with labored breath awaiting coffee and liqueurs, I was consumed by homesickness. Had I not feared a public breakdown, I would have wept. The Savoy is merely an annex to Broadway. Americans populated every table. I might as well have attended the Astor.

If fate insists on importing New York to England for my distress, I must endure it. Events drift. Two days ago, I drifted here. Mr. Carmyle, seemingly fond of Fillmore, extended an invitation to Monk's Crofton. Refusal offered no plausible pretext. Thus, I arrived, currently composing this in a cavernous bedroom featuring an open fire, armchairs, and luxuries. Fillmore is golfing. He departs for New York on Saturday via the Mauretania. Horror grips me upon learning he promotes a light-weight boxing championship in Jersey City, guaranteeing substantial sums. Argument is futile; he cites figures demonstrating fortune-making potential. Moreover, the fight occurs in a fortnight. The knowledge makes my flesh crawl.

Worry serves no purpose. Change the subject. Are you acquainted with Monk's Crofton? Likely not, as I recall it being a recent acquisition purchased by Mr. Carmyle from a lord suffering losses on the Stock Exchange. I hope you have not seen it, for I intend to describe it exhaustively. I wish to pour my soul onto the page. Ginger, what crime has England committed to merit such paradises? I considered Faucitt's Cissister estate adequate, yet it pales in comparison. His was a standard country house; this is a Seat. Monk's Crofton belongs to the genre of English novels. "The sunset fell on the walls of G---- Castle..." I envision Tennyson's Maud inhabiting this domain. These stately homes stand beautifully, and I am intoxicated by them.

From the station, drive three miles, then turn into a grand iron gateway flanked by stone posts and beasts. Adjacent stands a quaint lodge where an elderly man emerges to tip his hat. You assume arrival, prepare to alight, only for the vehicle to continue fifty miles through beech forests teeming with rabbits and meadows hosting deer. Suddenly, rounding a bend, the house reveals itself, obscured previously by dense foliage.

It is vast, low, and square, featuring a tower and a battlemented upper porch reminiscent of medieval fortifications. Lawns stretch endlessly, bordered by shrubberies and a lake visible beyond the fields. To the left, a future rose garden promises a million blooms in June, enclosed by a high red-brick wall shielding the kitchen garden. I explored today. Enormous greenhouses, a farmyard with puppies tearing at one's heartstrings due to their sweetness, and a large, somnolent cat blinking in the sun while puppies traverse her fur. A serene stillness prevails; one hears growth. Thrushes and blackbirds sing. Oh, Ginger, it is heaven.

However, a caveat exists. "Where every prospect pleases and only man is vile." Or perhaps merely stodgy. I now understand your incompatibility with the Family. They are present. Uncle Donald and the rest.
