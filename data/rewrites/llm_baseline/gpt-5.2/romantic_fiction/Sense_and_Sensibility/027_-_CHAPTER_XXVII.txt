“If this open weather holds much longer,” Mrs. Jennings declared at breakfast, buttering her toast with the generous hand of one who believed good fare a moral duty, “Sir John will not like leaving Barton next week. ’Tis a sad thing for sportsmen to lose a day’s pleasure. Poor souls! I always pity them; they take it so to heart.”

“That is true,” Marianne cried, with a brightness that startled Elinor by its suddenness, and she crossed at once to the window, as if the whole question were to be settled by the look of the sky. The panes were cold under her fingers; beyond them, London breathed its damp, mild breath—no sting of winter yet, only that soft, indecisive warmth that left mud in the streets and a languor in the air. “I had not thought of that. This weather will keep many sportsmen in the country.”

The recollection was like a spoonful of cordial. Colour came back to her face; her eyes warmed. She returned to the table as if the very scent of tea—sharp, tannic, with the faint bergamot Mrs. Jennings liked—had restored her.

“It is charming weather for them indeed,” she went on, tasting her words as if they had flavour. “How much they must enjoy it! But—” and the sweetness soured, just perceptibly, like milk threatened by heat, “it cannot be expected to last long. At this time of year, and after such a series of rain, we shall certainly have very little more of it. Frosts will soon set in, and in all probability with severity. In another day or two perhaps; this extreme mildness can hardly last longer—nay, perhaps it may freeze tonight!”

“At any rate,” Elinor said quickly—because she heard, beneath Marianne’s talk of weather, the other name that weather carried for her sister—“I dare say we shall have Sir John and Lady Middleton in town by the end of next week.”

“Ay, my dear,” Mrs. Jennings answered, nodding, crumbs dropping cheerfully upon her napkin, “I’ll warrant you we do. Mary always has her own way.”

And Elinor, who understood that “Mary’s own way” often travelled by post, thought, with a silent sinking, Now she will write to Combe by this day’s letter. Yet if such a letter was written, it was done with a secrecy that escaped even Elinor’s careful watchfulness; no blotting-sand betrayed it, no hurried fold, no lingering over sealing-wax. And though Elinor could not feel content, she could not feel wholly miserable while Marianne looked even for a moment as she looked now—alive again, strung to expectation.

Marianne’s spirits, indeed, were fed all day—not by society, nor by any common amusement, but by the air itself. The morning was spent in making calls with Mrs. Jennings, leaving cards at the houses of her acquaintance to announce her being in town. In every doorway the world offered its little domestic signs: coal-smoke that clung to the curtains in the entry, a footman’s polished shoes creaking on stone, the thin chime of a distant musical clock competing with the raw scrape of wheels on wet pavement outside. London’s soundscape pressed upon Elinor like a constant hand—street cries and carriage-hubs, the hammering of a far-off builder, a sudden metallic shout as iron met iron—so different from Barton’s wind in trees and the hush that could settle like flour over a kitchen at dusk. Marianne, however, moved through all of it as if she were listening only to the sky.

She watched the direction of the wind between one house and another, lifting her face as they stepped from a warm parlour into the street’s cooler breath; she studied clouds as others studied faces.

“Don’t you find it colder than it was in the morning, Elinor?” she asked, drawing her muff up as if it could keep not only her hands but her heart warm. “There seems to me a very decided difference. I can hardly keep my hands warm even in my muff. It was not so yesterday, I think. The clouds seem parting too; the sun will be out in a moment, and we shall have a clear afternoon.”

Elinor was alternately diverted and pained. Marianne persevered, and in everything she met—the brightness of the fire at night, the clearer look of the atmosphere at morning—she tasted, with desperate faith, “certain symptoms of approaching frost,” as if frost were an ally, as if winter’s hard clean hand could force some long-delayed truth to the surface.

In the arrangement of Mrs. Jennings’s household there was nothing stingy, nothing pinched. Meals were plentiful, the tea-table always ready, the sideboard never bare; she managed her kitchen like a benevolent sovereign who thought hospitality part of good government. Elinor could not dislike that liberality, even when she found its forms loud. And Mrs. Jennings, though she carried about with her a few old city friends whom Lady Middleton regretted and would gladly have seen dropped, had sense enough not to drag Elinor and Marianne into any company that might truly distress them. Elinor, pleased to find herself more comfortably placed in that particular than she had expected, made her peace with the rest—though their evenings, whether at home or abroad, were generally those industrious gatherings formed only for cards, where the shuffling of hands and the smack of laid-down counters became the chief music.

Colonel Brandon had a general invitation and came almost every day. His entrance always altered the room’s tone, as if a deeper instrument had joined a thin tune. He came to look at Marianne and to talk with Elinor. Elinor often derived more satisfaction from conversing with him than from any other daily occurrence, for his mind had a steadiness that felt, in London’s racket, like stepping into a pantry where all is clean, ordered, and quietly necessary. Yet she saw, with real concern, his continued regard for her sister. She feared it was a strengthening regard. There was an earnestness in the way his eyes followed Marianne—as if he listened for her in the room the way one listens for a familiar voice in a crowded street—and his spirits were certainly worse than when at Barton.

About a week after their arrival, it became certain that Willoughby was also arrived. His card lay on the table when they came in from their morning drive, stiff and white against the polished wood, like a small thing that could cut.

“Good God!” Marianne cried. “He has been here while we were out.”

Elinor’s heart sprang—not with the same wild leap as Marianne’s, but with relief, as if a knot might at last be loosened. “Depend upon it, he will call again tomorrow,” she ventured.

Marianne scarcely heard her. When Mrs. Jennings came in, Marianne slipped away with the precious card as if it were food to be hidden, a morsel to be hoarded in famine.

From that moment, her mind never rested. The expectation of seeing him every hour made her unfit for anything. It was like waiting for a dish that has been promised—your appetite sharpened until it is pain, every sound from the street making you lift your head, certain that now, now the thing will arrive that will restore you. She insisted on being left behind the next morning when the others went out.

Elinor’s thoughts, during the drive, stayed fixed on Berkeley Street, on what might be passing there in their absence—the knocker struck, the door opened, the voice asking for Miss Dashwood. But the moment she returned, one glance at Marianne told her all. Willoughby had not come.

A note was brought in just then, and laid upon the table.

“For me!” Marianne cried, stepping forward with such haste that the paper seemed already hers by right.

“No, ma’am, for my mistress,” the servant answered.

Marianne snatched it up nevertheless. “It is indeed for Mrs. Jennings; how provoking!”

“You are expecting a letter, then?” Elinor said, no longer able to keep silence.

“Yes, a little—not much.”

There was a pause, in which the fire snapped and the street outside sent up its constant, wearying rumble, the world always moving, always loud, indifferent to one girl’s waiting.

“You have no confidence in me, Marianne.”

“Nay, Elinor, this reproach from you—you who have confidence in no one!”

“Me!” Elinor returned, colouring with the confusion of a person accused where she cannot defend herself. “Indeed, Marianne, I have nothing to tell.”

“Nor I,” Marianne answered, with a kind of fierce purity, “our situations then are alike. We have neither of us anything to tell; you, because you do not communicate, and I, because I conceal nothing.”

The injustice of it stung Elinor; yet she could not wholly repel it. There were things—one thing—she could not speak of, and Marianne’s sharp sincerity found the tender spot. She knew not, under such circumstances, how to press her sister to greater openness.

Mrs. Jennings appeared, and taking the note, read it aloud. It was from Lady Middleton, announcing their arrival in Conduit Street the night before, and requesting the company of her mother and cousins the following evening. Business on Sir John’s part, and a violent cold on her own, had prevented their calling in Berkeley Street.

The invitation was accepted. But when the hour drew near, necessary as it was in common civility that both should attend Mrs. Jennings, Elinor had difficulty in persuading Marianne to go. She had still seen nothing of Willoughby; and she was not only indisposed for any amusement abroad, but unwilling to risk his calling in her absence, as if the mere act of stepping out might betray her into missing the only moment that mattered.

Elinor found, when the evening was over, that disposition is not materially altered by a change of abode. Sir John, scarcely settled in town, had contrived to gather nearly twenty young people and to amuse them with a ball. The rooms were bright, warmed by crowded bodies and many candles; the air carried the mingled scents of wax, pomatum, damp woollen cloaks drying by the heat, and the thin sharpness of wine from the sideboard. Two violins scraped their way through country-dance measures, the sound trying hard to make itself merry against the thick London hush that exists even inside gaiety—the constant awareness of streets outside, the coach-wheels always waiting.

It was, however, an affair of which Lady Middleton did not approve. In the country, an unpremeditated dance was very allowable; but in London, where the reputation of elegance was more important and less easily attained, it was risking too much, for the gratification of a few girls, to have it known that Lady Middleton had given a small dance of eight or nine couple, with two violins, and a mere side-board collation.

Mr. and Mrs. Palmer were among the party. From Mr. Palmer, whom they had not seen since their arrival in town, they received no mark of recognition on entering; careful to avoid the appearance of attention to his mother-in-law, he looked at them slightly, as if they were strangers, and merely nodded to Mrs. Jennings from the other side of the room.

Marianne, the instant she entered, gave one swift glance around the apartment. It was enough—he was not there. She sat down, equally ill-disposed to receive or to communicate pleasure. The music might have been water poured upon stone; the laughter, the talk, the clapping of hands, all seemed to pass her without wetting her.

After they had been assembled about an hour, Mr. Palmer sauntered toward the Miss Dashwoods to express surprise on seeing them in town—though Colonel Brandon had been first informed of their arrival at his house, and Mr. Palmer himself had said something “very droll” on hearing they were to come.

“I thought you were both in Devonshire,” said he.

“Did you?” Elinor replied.

“When do you go back again?”

“I do not know.”

And thus ended their discourse.

Never had Marianne been so unwilling to dance, and never so fatigued by the exercise when at last she did. The very act of moving through the figures, of turning and stepping while her mind stood still, seemed to exhaust her. She complained of it as they returned to Berkeley Street, her voice low and tight, as if the cold she feared had taken hold of her from within.

“Aye, aye,” said Mrs. Jennings, who could not resist the comfort of her own conjectures, “we know the reason of all that very well; if a certain person who shall be nameless had been there, you would not have been a bit tired. And to say the truth, it was not very pretty of him not to give you the meeting when he was invited.”

“Invited!” Marianne cried, stopping short on the word as if it were something bitter in her mouth.

“So my daughter Middleton told me; for it seems Sir John met him somewhere in the street this morning.”

Marianne said no more; but her face, in the street-light, looked as if it had been struck.

Elinor, impatient to do anything that might lead to her sister’s relief, resolved to write the next morning to her mother. If she could not command events, she might at least summon inquiry. She hoped, by awakening her mother’s fears for Marianne’s health, to procure those questions which had been so long delayed. And she was the more eager in this measure because, after breakfast, she perceived Marianne writing again—writing with that hurried absorption which belonged now to only one name. Elinor could not suppose it was to any other person than Willoughby.

About the middle of the day, Mrs. Jennings went out alone on business. The house fell quieter. Even London’s noise seemed, by contrast, more insistent—cart-wheels grinding, a hawker’s call rising and fading, the hard knock of a neighbour’s door. Elinor began her letter directly. Marianne, too restless for employment and too anxious for conversation, walked from one window to another, or sat by the fire in melancholy meditation, staring into the coals as if she could read her fate in their shifting red.

Elinor wrote earnestly. She related what had passed, her suspicions of Willoughby’s inconstancy, and urged her mother by every plea of duty and affection to demand from Marianne an account of her real situation with respect to him. She chose her words as carefully as if she were seasoning a dish that must be strong enough to rouse, but not so strong as to offend; yet her hand trembled all the same.

The letter was scarcely finished when a rap sounded below—a sharp interruption, the house answering it with the quick echo of footsteps on stairs. Colonel Brandon was announced.

Marianne, who had seen him from the window and who hated company of any kind in her present state, left the room before he entered.

He looked more than usually grave; and though he expressed satisfaction at finding Miss Dashwood alone, as if he had something in particular to tell her, he sat for some time without speaking. The silence was filled with small noises that become loud when hearts are strained: the settling of the fire, the faint creak of leather as he moved, the distant thud of London beyond the walls.

Elinor, persuaded he had some communication to make in which Marianne was concerned, waited with impatience. It was not the first time she had felt such a conviction; for more than once before, beginning with “your sister looks unwell today,” or “your sister seems out of spirits,” he had appeared on the point either of disclosing or inquiring something particular.

After several minutes, he broke the silence, and in a voice of some agitation asked her when he was to congratulate her on the acquisition of a brother.

Elinor, struck and unprepared, could only ask what he meant.

He tried to smile. “Your sister’s engagement to Mr. Willoughby is very generally known.”

“It cannot be generally known,” Elinor returned quickly, “for her own family do not know it.”

He looked surprised. “I beg your pardon. I am afraid my inquiry has been impertinent; but I had not supposed any secrecy intended, as they openly correspond, and their marriage is universally talked of.”

“How can that be? By whom can you have heard it mentioned?”

“By many—by some of whom you know nothing, by others with whom you are most intimate: Mrs. Jennings, Mrs. Palmer, and the Middletons. But still I might not have believed it—for where the mind is perhaps rather unwilling to be convinced, it will always find something to support its doubts—if I had not, when the servant let me in today, accidentally seen a letter in his hand, directed to Mr. Willoughby, in your sister’s writing. I came to inquire; but I was convinced before I could ask the question. Is everything finally settled? Is it impossible to—? But I have no right, and I could have no chance of succeeding. Excuse me, Miss Dashwood. I believe I have been wrong in saying so much, but I hardly know what to do, and on your prudence I have the strongest dependence. Tell me that it is all absolutely resolved on; that any attempt—that in short concealment, if concealment be possible—is all that remains.”

The words, with their broken restraint and their helpless honour, conveyed to Elinor a direct avowal of his love for Marianne, and they affected her deeply. For a moment she could not speak. When she recovered, she debated what answer it would be most proper to give. The real state of things between Willoughby and her sister was so little known even to herself that in explaining it she might say too much or too little. Yet she was convinced that Marianne’s affection for Willoughby left no hope of Colonel Brandon’s success, whatever the event might be; and she wished also, tenderly, to shield Marianne from censure.

She therefore said more than she truly knew or believed. She acknowledged that, though she had never been informed by themselves of the terms on which they stood, of their mutual affection she had no doubt; and of their correspondence she was not astonished to hear.

He listened in silence. Then he rose directly, and after saying, in a voice shaken with emotion, “To your sister I wish all imaginable happiness; to Willoughby that he may endeavour to deserve her,” he took leave and went away.

Elinor was left with no comfort from the conversation to lessen her other uneasiness. Instead, a melancholy impression of Colonel Brandon’s unhappiness settled upon her like the lingering smell of extinguished candle-wick—sad, clinging, impossible to sweeten. And she was prevented even from wishing it removed by the very anxiety that must confirm it: the dread and hope bound together, tightening around Marianne’s name whenever the street below sent up the sound of another arriving carriage.
