On the fourteenth day of April Romola passed again beneath the gates of Florence, and the very air that met her seemed thickened with breath and rumor, as if the city had been boiling a bitter broth through the night and could not help sending up its steam. She had meant to halt longer at Pistoja; but no one report there would keep faith with the next—Savonarola had walked through fire; Savonarola had failed; Savonarola had fled; Savonarola had been taken. Rest was impossible while the story shifted under her feet like loose stones.

At Prato, when she was already feeling herself drawn, step by step, toward the city she dreaded, she met an Augustinian monk of San Spirito—the very man who had once taken her godfather’s confessions. His cowl was dusted as if he had been pushed in a crowd; his voice carried the hoarseness of a throat that had shouted or wept. He told her what had happened in Florence, not as a tale brought from afar, but as something still ringing in his ears.

Savonarola had been arrested. And Tito—Tito was dead.

The monk had stood in the living stream that followed the waggon into the Piazza, that slow heaving tide of bodies and breath, curses and prayers. What Florence “generally knew,” he gave her: Tito had escaped an assaulting mob by leaping into the Arno, but had been murdered on the bank by an old man who had long borne him enmity.

Romola heard as one hears the clack of a mill-wheel one has lived beside all one’s life—mechanical, inevitable, dreadful in its continuance. Yet she understood the catastrophe as no other listener in Florence could understand it; for to them Tito’s death was a public rumor with a shape like any other; to her it was the last act of a long deceit whose taste still lay on her tongue, sharp as spoiled oil.

Of Fra Girolamo, the monk spoke with that unfavorable bias usual among the Black Brethren toward the brother who showed white beneath his black: Savonarola, he said, had confessed himself a deceiver of the people. Even before the words were fully spent Romola felt the lash of them—confession wrested by torture, truth and untruth kneaded together like dough under brutal hands until nothing could be distinguished but submission.

She paused no longer. That evening she was within Florence, in Monna Brigida’s house, seated under a storm of exclamations—joy and wailing mingled, narrative pouring over her like water from a tipped jar. Brigida had backslidden in Romola’s absence into false hair, and at the first sight of her had dragged it off as if it scorched her head.

“I’ll not mind being grey,” she cried, shaking her own thin locks. “Not if my dear child will stay with me.”

Romola, under her long widow’s veil, sat in an agitated silence. Brigida’s gossip—this neighbor said that, that one swore the other—fell on her dulled sense as rain falls on stone. The two great facts she had come to Florence to meet—Tito’s death and Fra Girolamo’s confession of duplicity under coercion—pressed on her like a weight laid across her ribs; they left her hardly any power for lesser matters. All the mind she could command, under that awe-stricken grief, gathered itself to two purposes that would not be put aside: to try and see Savonarola, and to learn what had become of Tessa and the children.

“Tell me, cousin,” she said abruptly, cutting across Brigida’s plans for their living together as one might cut a thread, “has anything been seen or said since Tito’s death of a young woman with two little children?”

Monna Brigida started, rounded her eyes, and lifted both hands. “Cristo! no. What—was he so bad as that, my poor child? Ah, then, that was why you went away and left me word only that you went of your own free will. Well, well; if I’d known that, I shouldn’t have thought you so strange and flighty. For I did say to myself—though I didn’t tell anybody else—‘What was she to go away from her husband for, leaving him to mischief, only because they cut poor Bernardo’s head off? She’s got her father’s temper,’ I said, ‘that’s what it is.’ Well, well; never scold me, child: Bardo was fierce, you can’t deny it. But if you had only told me the truth, that there was a young hussey and children, I should have understood it all.”

Romola’s fingers tightened under her veil as if they had found a rough edge. “Has anything been seen or said of her?” she urged.

“No; and the less the better,” Brigida answered, lowering her voice as if the walls were full of ears. “They say enough of ill about him without that. But since that was the reason you went—”

“No, dear cousin,” Romola broke in, her earnestness sounding like the clear ring of a knife on stone, “pray do not talk so. I wish above all things to find that young woman and her children, and to take care of them. They are quite helpless. Say nothing against it; that is the thing I shall do first of all.”

Brigida drew back, baffled; her mouth puckered in a discomfited shrug. “Well,” she muttered, “if that’s being a Piagnone, I’ve been taking peas for paternosters. Why, Fra Girolamo said as good as that widows ought not to marry again. Step in at the door and it’s a sin and a shame, it seems; but come down the chimney and you’re welcome. Two children—Santiddio!”

“Cousin, the poor thing has done no conscious wrong,” Romola said, and her voice softened, as if she were crumbling bread into a bowl to make it easy to swallow. “She is ignorant of everything. I will tell you—but not now.”

Early the next morning Romola went beyond San Ambrogio to the house where she had once found Tessa. The street had its usual life—women bargaining over greens that still held a night’s damp, a boy carrying warm flat loaves in a cloth that let out their faint sour-sweet steam, the chatter of vendors striking and falling like sparrows’ calls. But to Romola every sound seemed set too loud, too close; Florence’s noise pressed on her like an argument that would not allow silence for thought.

At the house she sought, she found only emptiness. Tessa was gone.

Romola’s mind leapt at once to the likeliest thread: Tito had sent her away beforehand to some place where he meant to join her; he would not willingly have torn himself from those children. The conjecture cut her with an extra pain, because it carried with it an image she could not drive away—Tessa, that childish creature, waiting by some roadside, waiting and waiting, hungry, bewildered, with the children tugging at her and no understanding why the promised arms did not come.

Those living near could tell her nothing, except that old deaf Lisa had gone away a week ago with her goods, and no one knew where Tessa had gone. Romola found no further active search open to her. She had no starting-point for inquiry, and not only her innate reserve but a nobler sensitiveness made her shrink from parading Tessa’s relation to Tito, and her own purpose, before the eyes of Florence—shrinking from an ostentation of generosity that would be another kind of cruelty.

Days passed in anxious inaction, as if time itself had become a hard loaf she could not break. Even when her thoughts were driven toward other necessities, her heart would begin to throb if she caught sight of a pair of round brown legs beneath a short skirt, or a short woman in contadina dress. She never told herself that it was heroism, or exalted charity, to seek these beings. She needed something she was bound specially to care for. She yearned—almost with a physical hunger—to clasp the children and make them love her. That at least would be some sweet result, for others as well as herself, out of her long sorrow.

There was property of Tito’s to which she could lay claim; but she distrusted the cleanness of that money, as one distrusts water drawn from a tainted well. She had determined to make it all over to the State, except so much as equaled the price of her father’s library. That would be enough for the modest support of Tessa and the children.

Monna Brigida, however, pushed all plans into the background with clamorous insistence: Romola must live with her and never forsake her till Brigida had seen herself safe in Paradise—else why had she been persuaded to turn Piagnone?—and if Romola wanted to rear other people’s children, then Brigida must rear them too.

“Only,” Brigida would add, with a meaningful lift of brow, “they must be found first.”

Romola felt the full force of that little sting. Yet strong feeling, unsatisfied, breeds its own superstition—of hope or despair. Romola’s was the superstition of hope: somehow she was to find that mother and the children.

At last, as if Providence had tired of her pacing in place, a new direction for inquiry offered itself. She learned that Tito had provided horses and mules to await him at San Gallo; therefore he meant to leave Florence by the gate of San Gallo. She determined—without much confidence, but with the stubbornness of one who will at least touch every door-handle—to question the gatekeepers whether any one answering to Tessa’s description, with two little children, had passed out before the morning of the ninth of April.

She walked along the Via San Gallo, watchful under her long veil lest she miss any sign. In the city’s soundscape she could pick out the particular textures: the iron-shod hooves striking cobble, the watery slap where a cartwheel found mud, the shrill thinness of a child’s cry ricocheting off stone, the deeper human murmur that was Florence’s perpetual hearth-fire. To Romola, worn by grief, the city’s roar felt like a violence; it was not the honest noise of field and wind, where each sound had room to die, but a crowded clamor that flattened every note into the same harsh coin. Even art here was cried in the street like cheap fruit.

In that very street she descried Bratti, chaffering with a customer—Bratti, the roaming man who knew the ways of small bargains and smaller secrets. She thought he might aid her; she would not mind speaking of Tessa to him.

But as she put aside her veil and crossed toward him, she saw something hanging from the corner of his basket, and her heart sprang up with a hope so sudden it seemed to lift her by the throat.

“Bratti, my friend,” she said, so abruptly that her words struck like a hand on a table, “where did you get that necklace?”

“Your servant, madonna,” Bratti replied, looking at her with his usual deliberate coolness, as if surprise were a luxury he never bought. “It’s a necklace worth money, but I shall get little by it, for my heart’s too tender for a trader’s. I have promised to keep it in pledge.”

“Pray tell me where you got it,” Romola said; and now her voice trembled in spite of her effort. “From a little woman named Tessa—is it not true?”

“Ah! if you know her,” said Bratti, “and would redeem it of me at a small profit, and give it her again, you’d be doing a charity. For she cried at parting with it—you’d have thought she was running into a brook. It’s a small profit I’ll charge you. You shall have it for a florin, for I don’t like to be hard-hearted.”

“Where is she?” Romola demanded, already drawing out the coin, already unclasping the beads from his basket with hands that could scarcely obey her.

“Outside the gate there,” Bratti said, nodding toward San Gallo, “at the other end of the Borgo, at old Sibilla Manetti’s. Anybody will tell you which is the house.”

Romola went as if her feet had learned a new swiftness. She blessed—without knowing why she blessed it—the Carnival incident that had burned the look of this necklace into her memory: each bead a small dark seed of recognition, each one now a breadcrumb leading her out of bewilderment.

Soon she stood at the house. The poor widow who opened to her talked at once, with that needful eagerness of poverty to explain itself: the young woman and the children were in the inner room—had been promised fetching-away a fortnight ago and more—had no money, only their clothes, to pay for food and lodging. But since madonna knew them—

Romola waited for no more. She opened the door.

Tessa sat on the low bed. Her crying had passed into tearless sobs; her face had the look of something bruised and left too long in the air. She stared with sad blank eyes at the two children playing in the corner—Lillo with his skirt pulled over his head, roaring at Ninna to frighten her, then peeping out again to see whether his thunder had struck. Ninna, smaller and solemn, half-laughed, half-wondered, as if even play had become uncertain without a grown hand to steady it.

The door was behind Tessa; she did not turn when it opened, thinking it only the old woman. Expectation was no longer alive in her—like a fire gone down to ash.

Romola had thrown aside her veil. She paused a moment, holding the necklace where it could catch the light. Then she spoke, and the sound of her voice—pure, firm, the same voice that used to cheer her father in his darkened study—fell into that poor room like a clear note in a muddied stream.

“Tessa!”

Tessa started to her feet and wheeled round. Her eyes, wide and incredulous, fastened on Romola as if on an apparition that might vanish if she blinked.

“See,” Romola said, and with a tenderness that was also an authority she clasped the beads again on Tessa’s neck, fastening them as one fastens a child’s cloak against cold. “God has sent me to you again.”

The poor thing gave a cry that broke into sobbing, and clung to the arms that had fastened the necklace, clung as if those arms were the only solid thing left in the world. She could not speak.

The children, startled by the cry, toddled from their corner and caught at their mother’s skirts. They looked up at Romola with wide eyes—two small faces in which the same blood that had wronged Romola now pleaded without words. Romola felt their gaze like a touch on her skin, and the hunger in her—hunger for some duty that would not mock her—answered.

That day they all went home to Monna Brigida’s, in the Borgo degli Albizzi.

On the way, by gentle degrees, Romola made known to Tessa that Naldo could never come to her again—not because he had grown cruel, but because he was dead. The words had to be given as one gives a child bitter medicine: slowly, with pauses, with the hand ready to soothe the choke that follows.

Tessa’s grief was bewilderment more than rebellion; she clutched at the children as if to count them and be certain they were still there, as if the world were a basket from which things slipped unseen.

“But be comforted, my Tessa,” Romola said, drawing her nearer. “I am come to take care of you always. And we have got Lillo and Ninna.”

Monna Brigida’s mouth twitched in the struggle between awe of Romola and the desire to speak too soon. “Let be, for the present,” she thought. “But it seems to me a thousand years till I tell this little contadina—who seems not to know how many fingers she’s got on her hand—who Romola is. And I will tell her some day, else she’ll never know her place. It’s all very well for Romola; nobody will call their souls their own when she’s by. But if I’m to have this puss-faced minx living in my house she must be humble to me.”

Yet even Brigida’s resentment softened when supper came. The children’s small mouths, opening trustfully for sweetmeats, touched that old domestic place in her where grief and pride and piety all ended at the table. She wanted to give them too many sweets, and Romola had to check her, as one checks a cook’s over-generous hand with sugar when children’s bellies are at stake.

At the last, as Brigida was going to bed, she confessed in a lower, reluctant tenderness, “It would be a shame not to take care of such cherubs. But you must give up to me a little, Romola, about their eating, and those things. For you have never had a baby, and I had twins—only they died as soon as they were born.”
