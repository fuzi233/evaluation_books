Madame de Bernstein was no less delighted than her Virginian nephews by the outcome of Harry’s final meeting with Lady Maria. George sent her a letter that very evening, detailing what had transpired; and shortly after, her nephew Lord Castlewood—whose visits to his aunt were few and far between—arrived to pay his respects and speak openly about the affair. No man understood the value of candour better than my Lord Castlewood, and now that the matter between Maria and Harry was settled, there was no need for pretense or reserve. The game was played, the cards laid bare: he saw no reason not to recount every move, every trick, every subtle maneuver.

“She is my own sister,” said the lord affectionately. “She won’t have many more chances—such chances as this—to marry and secure her future. I may not have approved of the match in every way, and I may have pitied your ladyship’s young Virginian favourite; but such an opportunity could not be wasted. I was bound to stand by my own flesh and blood.”

“Your honesty does you credit, my lord,” replied Madame de Bernstein, “and your devotion to your sister is truly admirable.”

“Nay,” said the nobleman, bowing slightly, “we lost the game, and I speak without rancour. It is not for you, who have triumphed, to bear ill will.”

The Baroness protested she had never been in better spirits. “Now, confess, Eugene—was it your idea for Maria to visit Harry at the spunging-house? That touching gesture of returning all his gifts—was that your doing?”

“It was pity for the young man,” replied Lord Castlewood demurely, “and a sense of duty to Maria’s affianced lover in his hour of misfortune. Surely those were motives enough.”

“But you advised her, my good nephew?” pressed the Baroness.

With a shrug, Castlewood admitted he had. “Yet we would have won, despite your ladyship—had not the elder brother made his appearance. And I’ve been trying to console poor Maria by showing her how fortunate it was, after all, that we lost.”

“Suppose she had married Harry, and then George arrived?” mused the Baroness.

“Effectivement,” exclaimed Eugene, taking snuff. “As the grave was to give up its dead, let us thank it for disgorging in time! I must say, Mr. George Warrington seems a man of sound judgment, no more selfish than any other eldest son or worldly gentleman. My poor Molly fancied he might be—how shall I put it?—a greenhorn, like his younger brother. She hoped he’d be inclined to share everything equally with Twin junior. So infatuated was she with the lad that I believe she would have taken him anyway. ‘Harry Warrington, with half a loaf, might do very well,’ I told her, ‘but Harry Warrington with no bread at all, my dear?’”

“And no bread?” asked the Baroness.

“Well,” said Castlewood, “no bread except at his brother’s side-table. The elder brother said as much.”

“What a heartless creature!” cried Madame de Bernstein.

“Oh, bah!” retorted the lord, playing his cards openly. “Mr. George only did what anyone else would have done. We have no right to resent him. Indeed, Molly herself came around once her initial grief passed, and I brought her to reason. The silly old thing—to be so smitten with a young man at her age!”

“It was real passion, I almost believe,” said the Baroness. “You should have heard her say farewell to him. C’etait touchant, ma parole d’honneur! I wept. Before George appeared, I couldn’t help myself. There he stood, muddy stockings, hair falling into his eyes, bursting into our dinner party, making his offer to Molly in frank, noble terms—and she answered him. Begad, it reminded me of Mrs. Woffington in that new Scotch play Lord Bute’s man wrote—Douglas, was it? She clung to the boy, bid him adieu in heart-rending tones, stepped from the room in stately despair—no more chocolate, thank you. If she’d made a faux pas, no one could have withdrawn with greater dignity. A masterful retreat after defeat. We were driven from our position, but we left with all the honours of war.”

“Molly won’t die of disappointment,” observed the Baroness, sipping her tea.

Lord Castlewood gave a snarl of a grin, revealing his yellow teeth. “He, he! She’s had the malady before—twice or thrice—and recovered perfectly. It doesn’t kill, as you know, at Molly’s age.”

How could she know? She didn’t marry Doctor Tusher until late in life, and became Madame de Bernstein even later. Old Dido, as poets say, knew sorrow well—and thus learned compassion for others in distress. In the little world, people quarrel, fight, abuse each other, and remain estranged for years. But in the great world, wisdom prevails. Differences arise, meetings cease—but then reconciliation comes, unspoken, unexamined. A wandering prodigal—or a stray puppy-dog—is welcomed back under amnesty, though he may have roamed with unsavoury company.

For six months, since the Castlewoods and Madame de Bernstein had vied for control over poor Harry Warrington, these two branches of the Esmond family had kept apart. Now, with the matter resolved, they were free to reunite as if no rift had ever existed. Madame de Bernstein drove her grand coach to Lady Castlewood’s gathering, and the Esmond ladies smiled brightly at Madame de Bernstein’s festivities, loving each other just as fervently as before.

“So, sir,” said the Baroness, delighted and tapping George with her walking stick, “I hear you’ve behaved like a heartless monster toward your poor brother Harry!”

“I acted on your ladyship’s suggestion,” replied George, turning red. “I wanted to see whether your kin sought him for himself—or for his supposed fortune.”

“Maria could not marry a man utterly penniless,” countered the Baroness, “especially when his elder brother declared he’d give him nothing!”

“I did it for the best, madam,” George insisted, still blushing.

“And so you did, O thou hypocrite!” laughed the old lady.

“Hypocrite, madam? Why?” demanded Mr. Warrington, straightening himself with dignity.

“I know all, my infant!” said the Baroness in French. “Thou art very like thy grandfather. Come—let me embrace thee! Harry has told me everything, including how thou hast shared thy small inheritance with him.”

“It was natural,” said George, his voice softening. “We’ve had one heart and one purse since birth. I merely pretended harshness to test those people yonder.”

“And thou wilt divide Virginia with him too?” asked Madame de Bernstein.

“I don’t say so,” replied George. “It wouldn’t be fair. The land belongs to the eldest born, and Harry would not claim otherwise. Perhaps I shall die, or my mother live longer than both of us. But half of what is mine is his. And remember—he was extravagant only because he believed he was heir.”

“Ah!” exclaimed Madame de Bernstein, turning to her attendant as she prepared for bed. “It is a knight of old, a Bayard, the grandfather reborn!”

That evening, when the young men left her, she bestowed a tender kiss upon Harry’s hand—and a rouged cheek to George, who blushed nearly as deeply as the rose often painted on her face, touched by such favour from his ancient kinswoman.

Though Harry Warrington was among the least envious of men, and held his brother in the highest regard as chief, guide, and superior, it was natural that a deep sense of humiliation and sorrow weighed upon him after being stripped of his title as the Fortunate Youth and heir to vast Virginian lands. His friends at Kensington promised they would love him all the more for his fall. Harry bowed low and thanked them graciously—but he could not ignore the reality. When he accompanied George to the grand reception hosted by Lord Castlewood for his newly discovered kinsman, he saw plainly that George was the centre of attention: receiving all the conversation, compliments, and attentions, while Harry was scarcely noticed—except by poor Maria, whose eyes followed him with wistful longing, reproachful glances, as though blaming him for accepting her freedom too readily.

“Ah!” her eyes seemed to say. “It’s very generous of you, Harry, to accept the liberty I granted you—but I never meant for you to be so pleased at being released.”

She gave him up, yet did not quite forgive him for taking her at her word. She refused him, yet could not fully let go. Oh, my dear young readers, how sweet the beginning of love is—and how undignified the end can be! What a romantic vista lies ahead for young Damon and Phillis (or their middle-aged counterparts) when, their innocent affection revealed, they entwine their arms and gaze upon the lovely pays du tendre stretching beneath them! Together, they’ll wander through life, facing rocks and roaring rivers—yet Damon’s steadfast love will carry Sweetheart across them all. Dragons and dangers may lie in wait—but his brave sword will cut them down. At dusk, they’ll rest close together, like two children in the wood, moss for a bed, stars for a canopy, arms for pillows. This is the wise plan youth sets forth when embarking on the journey of love—and yet—O me!—they’ve barely walked a mile when they meet a towering wall and must turn back.

They’re squabbling with the post-boy at Barnet (the first stage on the Gretna Road), and lo—Strephon has no money, or Papa arrives with a horsewhip, takes Miss back, and locks her in the schoolroom, weeping. Heartbreaking, yes—but years later, when Strephon has become a prosperous barrister or a broken-down raff, and Delia a banker’s wife with eight children, and they meet again—what a ridiculous reunion!

Still, I hope no young man falls in love unless he believes his passion eternal. Doubt creeps in only after second or third loves. But some women remain romantic to the end—from eighteen to eighty-five, always expecting their hearts to break.

In short, when you’ve loved and no longer do, when the King of France, with twenty thousand men, colours flying, music blaring, marches up the hill—only to march down again—you find yourselves in an absurd position. This, no doubt, was Harry Warrington’s feeling when he returned to Kensington and met Maria’s melancholy, accusing eyes.

Yes—it is foolish, indeed. But how melancholy to return to a house once lived in, and see blackened windows and emptiness where once fires burned warm. Melancholy? Yes—but how bitter, how absurd, to pass sentimentally by No. 13 and see someone else grinning from the window, clearly on excellent terms with the landlady.

I always feel wounded—even at a familiar inn—if I see others’ trunks and boots outside rooms once mine. Did those boots rest on the sofa I once reclined upon? I kick you from before me, you muddy, vulgar highlows!

Thus, knowing his time of occupation was over, and Maria’s rooms—though perhaps not yet occupied by another—were certainly available for rent, Harry felt uneasy in her presence, and she likely in his. He found either nothing to say, or what she said was dull and commonplace. And now, the red lip of a white-necked pipe of Virginia tobacco was far more agreeable to him than Maria’s softest words or most sorrowful expression.

When George went to Kensington, Harry cared little to follow, citing other engagements. At his uncle’s house in Hill Street, the poor lad fared no better. He was barely acknowledged by the virtuous household. The ladies did not hesitate to deny themselves when he visited. He could scarcely believe such insincerity after their earlier embraces, welcomes, and repeated declarations of affection. Yet, sitting with the Lamberts for an hour after calling on his aunt, he saw her chairmen arrive with an empty carriage. His aunt stepped out, entered the vehicle, and did not even blush when he bowed from the opposite window.

To be denied by one’s own relations—to have a door that once opened wide slammed shut in his face! Poor simple Harry could not have believed such a thing possible.

Perhaps he thought the knocker had a kind heart, and was not made of brass. But the change in his Aunt Lambert’s face—just as cold and stern as the knocker itself—was no less shocking.

“My father’s own brother’s wife! What have I done to offend her? Oh, Aunt Lambert, Aunt Lambert—have you ever seen such cold-heartedness?” Harry burst out, as usual, with impulsive emotion.

“Do we make any difference to you, my dear Harry?” asked Aunt Lambert, casting a sideward glance at her youngest daughter.

“The world may look coldly upon you,” she continued, “but we do not belong to it. You may come to us in safety.”

“In this house, you are different from others,” replied Harry. “I don’t know how, but I always feel quiet and happy when I come here.”

“Quis me uno vivit felicior? aut magis hac est Optandum vita dicere quis potuit?” called out General Lambert.

“Do you know where I got these lines, Mr. Gownsman?” he asked his son from college, who had come to spend Easter with his parents.

“You took them from Catullus, sir,” replied the scholar.

“I took them from no such place,” said the General. “I took them from my beloved Democritus Junior—from old Burton, who has supplied many indifferent scholars with learning.” And Montaigne, too, were favourite authors of the good General.
