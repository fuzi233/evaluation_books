The Young Wife.

As Tito hurried across the bridge, the new armor hidden beneath his cloak, Romola paced the length of the ancient library, her thoughts fixed entirely on his return. It was a day when almost every window in Florence gaped open to witness the arrival of the French King and his nobility, yet Romola's shutters remained drawn. Since her father's sudden and terrifying death three months prior, she had not attended a single festivity.

"He asked if Tito were coming to write," Bardo had murmured earlier that evening, his voice trembling with a mixture of hope and exhaustion. "I told him yes, though he was at a supper at the Cardinal's. Perhaps he brings news of the library?"

But the hope had curdled into a frantic urgency. Bardo, flushed and pale in turns, had declared he must proceed without his son-in-law. "Get the pen, Romola!" he had cried, his voice cracking. "He has brought me no new text, yet I must speak on the New Platonists. If I die today, nothing will be done."

Romola had knelt instantly, pen in hand, expecting his voice. Instead, the room fell into a silence so profound it became a physical weight. When she finally spoke, offering encouragement, he did not answer. The silence stretched, unbroken, until it seemed to swallow the air itself. Later, looking back on that hour, Romola felt a sting of shame for her own thoughts. In the first shock of grief, she had secretly whispered, *Perhaps my life with Tito will be more perfect now.*

That dream of a triple existence, united with her father and her husband, had never fully materialized. It was as if a rainbow shower of sweets contained invisible seeds of bitterness. She had realized with a cruel clarity that no human could endure her father's monotonous, exacting demands forever. Her own patience, forged by deep sympathy, had eventually cracked under the strain. Before their marriage, Tito's efforts had seemed effortless, buoyed by the novelty of the task. But now, the irksomeness of the burden had grown unbearable.

Yet, she refused to blame Tito. To do so would be unjust. He remained gentle, tender, and sweet in his excuses, lingering with playful fondness before departing for whatever social engagements called him. His nature was different from hers; he craved the vivid gaiety of the world, the admiration of crowds, which she, absorbed in her father's legacy, had willingly renounced. Their natures diverged widely—he lighter, less proud, quicker to forgive—while she found her affections becoming increasingly singular and consuming. She told herself this was the natural order of man and woman, that her capacity for absorption was greater, and that she must accept his inevitable flight from the gloom of study.

A sense of guilt, however, gnawed at her as she contemplated her father's death. Was it not sinful to feel a glimmer of relief that his importunate claims on her heart were silenced? Now, free from the division of her duties, their young lives should flow together in one pure current. Yet, the promise to fulfill her father's supreme wish regarding the library weighed heavily upon her soul. That piety was the only atonement she could offer for the shadowy joy she felt at his passing. The laborious, dark existence—the "seed-time without a harvest"—must end. What remained of it was the collection of manuscripts and antiquities, the fruit of fifty years of toil, now secured from creditors thanks to Bernardo del Nero's advance of a thousand florins.

But the political landscape had shifted violently. Within ten days, all hope in the Medici circle had evaporated. The famous collections in the Via Larga faced dispersal; French agents eyed the antique gems as spoils of war, and the Florentine State, drowning in debts and preparing for conflict over Pisa, was likely to sell rather than preserve. For Romola, educated in a secluded world of books, these turbulent shifts mattered less than the fate of her father's dream. Her newfound interest in the public sphere, sparked by the French invasion and the fall of the Medici, burned hot and focused. She needed to know the truth, not through Tito, whose insights into the machinery of state often softened the edges of reality, but for herself.

This resolve led her to the Duomo that morning. She had heard Fra Girolamo preach. While the sermon's fiery rhetoric and denunciations of sin had initially struck her like distant thunder, a strange, piercing emotion had seized her when Savonarola invoked martyrdom. She had wept, feeling a resonance with a spiritual fervor that transcended her daily life, akin to hearing actual vibrations of music rather than a mere memory of sound. Yet, that emotion, powerful as it was, lay outside the sanctuary of her domestic world. As Tito returned, she suppressed the impulse to discuss it, fearing it might isolate her from him.

Waiting for hours, her heart pounding, she finally heard the great door of the courtyard creak open. Hastening to the stone steps, she caught sight of him ascending the staircase. Eighteen months had altered her face, softening the lines, lending it a pleading, beseeching quality that made her appear infinitely more lovely than on the day of their wedding. Tito, too, seemed changed, his features more defined, his demeanor heavier.

"My Tito, you are tired," she greeted him gently, noting the fatigue etched around his eyes. "A fatiguing day?"

Maso followed them into the ante-chamber, and soon they were alone in the library. The fire crackled warmly in the great dogs, a welcome sight to the weary traveler. Tito sat down heavily, throwing aside his cap, and shivered.

"Romola, I implore you," he said, his voice lacking its usual lilt, "stop sitting in this library. Our rooms are warmer, pleasanter."

Romola felt a sharp pang of hurt. He had never been so indifferent, so cold. She watched him anxiously, searching for an explanation in the shadows of his expression. "You know I am cataloguing the manuscripts on the new plan Father wished for," she reminded him softly. "You have no time to assist, so I must work closely."

Instead of engaging, Tito closed his eyes, rubbing his hands over his face and hair. He felt a terrible alienation rising within him, a repulsion toward the mind of the woman he loved, though he could not name it. He was vexed with himself for behaving so poorly, unable to summon the affectionate warmth he desired. "I am not well, Romola," he muttered, "do not be surprised if I am peevish."

Kneeling beside him, Romola laid her arm across his chest, smoothing back his hair. Suddenly, she recoiled with a start, her gaze sharpening with alarm. "What is that under your tunic, Tito? It feels as hard as iron."

"It is iron," he admitted quietly, revealing the chain-mail armor he wore beneath his clothes. "I bought it unexpectedly. I must wear it constantly for some time."

"What threatens you?" Romola clutched his arm, her voice trembling. "Did you encounter danger today?"

"Everyone is threatened unless they are enemies of the Medici," Tito said, attempting a smile to break the tension. "My armor protects me from covert attacks."

"But my godfather," Romola insisted, "he is in far greater danger than you. Why does he take no precautions?"

"It is because I am of lesser importance," Tito explained, his smile fading. "Men like Bernardo are shielded by wealth and influence. I am a Greek, unavenged by anyone, and constantly suspected of being an envoy."

Romola shrank from the thought of a specific threat, dreading the degradation of such a fear in her husband. "Have you received special threats?" she whispered.

"Yes," Tito replied firmly, cutting short her inquiry. "Do not mention it to your godfather, or you will have broken my confidence."

"I will not," Romola promised, her face flushing with shame. "But it makes you so wretched, Tito. Seeing you in this heavy shell... it seems like a story of enchantment, as if a malignant fiend has hardened your skin."

"And you would rather see me exposed to peril?" Tito teased gently, trying to restore the thread of their intimacy. "If you do not mind my being stabbed, why should I mind? I will give up the armor."

"No, Tito, no!" she cried, shaking her head. "I am merely fanciful. Do not listen to me. Are crimes like this becoming common in Florence? My father and Godfather always said otherwise."

"They will become frequent," Tito said grimly, "bred by bitter hatreds."

Silence fell between them again. Romola, unwilling to press the subject further, forced a cheerful tone. "Tell me, what happened today? Did everything go well?"

"Tremendously well," Tito laughed, the sound hollow. "First, the rain ruined Luca Corsini's tedious oration. Then, a quick-witted speaker gave the French King the briefest possible welcome in broken French. After that, a storm of gifts, jewels, and trappings poured in. There was strutting, prancing, confusion, and shouting. The King smiled from ear to ear. I was at the Tornabuonis; there was feasting and playing. I shall tell you all about it tomorrow."

"But is there any hope?" Romola asked, her face falling. "Will Florence avoid fresh trouble? Will the Republic remain peaceful?"
