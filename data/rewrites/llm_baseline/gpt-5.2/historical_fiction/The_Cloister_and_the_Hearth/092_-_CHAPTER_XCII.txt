Yes: the hermit of Gouda had been the vicar of Gouda, and never knew it—so thick had he walled himself from tidings, so absolute his withdrawal.

When the blaze of Clement’s rage went out, it left him in a cold ash of remorse. Not the sort that pricks and passes, but the kind that makes a man afraid of his own hands, as if his very soul had slipped and only by miracle had not plunged. “Ah, good Jerome,” he cried into the dim of his cell, “how much better you knew me than I knew myself! How bitter—yet how wholesome—was your admonition!”

He did what he had been trained to do: he searched himself like a careful cook tasting a sauce after it has turned—probing for the one hidden bitterness that has soured the whole pot. He found it at once. Margaret.

“I love her then better than God,” he said, and the words were a choking mouthful. “Better than the Church. From such a love what can spring to me—or to her?” He shuddered, as at rich meat eaten on a day of fast. “Let the strong battle temptation; ’tis for the weak to flee. And who is weaker than I have shown myself? What is my penitence, my religion? A pack of cards built patiently into a fair-seeming house; and lo—one breath of earthly love, and it lies in the dust.”

So he resolved to begin again, and on a surer foundation: to leave Holland at once; to hide himself for years in some distant convent, where the very air would be strange to his desire. Time would blunt the edge of passion; and he, older and better disciplined, might come back with mastery. Meanwhile Margaret—abandoned—would marry, and love another. The anguish that thought cost him was the hard proof he trusted: it showed him where duty cut against nature. He clung to that pain as a penitent clings to nettles.

Yet even while he planned to turn his back on her without a kind word or look—lest pity undo resolve—he would not neglect her temporal welfare. The thought that he could make her comfortable for life, rich enough in the world’s goods she was not bound to despise, was like a small crust offered to a starving man: not feast, but endurance.

“Oh, what will she think of me?” he groaned. “Shall I not seem to her, of all creatures, the most heartless—inhuman? But so best. Ay, better she should hate me. Heaven is merciful, and giveth my broken heart this comfort: I can make that villain restore her own, and she shall never lose another true lover by poverty. Another? Ah me! God and the saints to mine aid!”

How he fared on that errand has been told elsewhere; but first—before all bargains and reckonings—he went by night to shrive the hermit of Gouda.

He found him dying and stayed, never quitting the low chamber until the breath had gone and the eyelids were gently closed. Then he buried him beneath the floor of the little oratory that clung to the cell like a swallow’s nest to a wall. It was the peaceful end of a stormy life. The man had been a soldier; even now, under his rough wool, he wore a steel corslet against his skin, saying that he was Christ’s soldier now as he had been Satan’s.

When Clement had shriven him and prayed by him, he in turn sought counsel of one who lay with death already in his mouth and yet spoke with a strange simplicity, as if the world had fallen silent at last. The hermit urged him to be his successor in that retreat.

“It was a hard fight,” the dying man told him, “against the world, the flesh, and the devil; and I never thoroughly baffled them till I retired into the citadel of Solitude.”

Those words, and the seal set upon them by the hermit’s pious, untroubled death, sank deep in Clement. In that age the solitary recluse was still revered: whether immured in a cell fastened to a monastery, or hidden in the harsher, freer seclusion of an independent hermitage. And Clement knew more of hermits than most divines so young. At Tergou he had read their lives; at Rome he had devoured manuscripts; he had questioned mendicant friars—those great carriers of local memory before print—until names and places clung in his mind like well-worn prayers.

Jerome himself had once been anchorite on the heights of Camaldoli, where the Thebaid had been revived for centuries; and Jerome—cold on many themes—warmed into a grim delight on this. He had told Clement of nobles and famous warriors who ended as hermits; of bishops and archbishops who passed from the see to the cell and sometimes back again; of an Archbishop of Ravenna among the former, and Pope Victor the Ninth among the latter. He had relished the austerities: how each solitary hunted out his own weakest part and set upon it without pity until weakness was turned, if not to strength, then at least to obedience. He had described the sevenfold rhythm of their days: matins, prime, tierce, sexte, nones, vespers, compline—no excuse of weather, no bargaining with the body. In thunder and snow, in moonlight and torchlight, they would converge from scattered caves to the convent church, and the very soundscape of that holy mountain was their discipline: wind in firs, bell-stroke, chant, and the crunch of sandals on rock.

Under Clement’s eager questioning, Jerome had even drawn the faces of men he had prayed beside—Moncata, Duke of Moncata and Cardova, who fled youth’s pomp and pleasure; Father John Baptist of Novara, once leader of armies and now private soldier of Christ; Cornelius, Samuel, Sylvanus. Of Sylvanus he told, with relish, how he met the great Duchess de’ Medici at the first wooden cross when at last she had wrung permission to visit Camaldoli; how he rebuked her, warned her, and persuaded her to retreat, though she stood surrounded by courtiers and captains—so that a whole glittering troop withdrew before one hoary hermit.

At Basle Clement had gathered more—German and English anchorites—and had even begun, as a scholar hoards spices, a “Catena Eremitarum” from the year of our Lord 250, when Paul of Thebes commenced his ninety years’ solitude, down to 1470. He called them Angelorum amici et animalium—Friends of Angels and Animals. So though he had never then thought to become one, the path had been laid, stone by stone, in his mind.

And now the dying hermit of Gouda blessed solitude as a citadel where a man might win at last; and invited Clement to take up, as it were, the breastplate of faith that fell from his own shrunken body. Clement felt an inward certainty: Heaven had led his foot to this place for that end. It struck him, too, as no small coincidence that his patron, St. Bavon, was himself a hermit—an austere one, a cuirassier of the solitary cell.

So, once reconciled with Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, Clement went eagerly to the hermit’s abode, praying it had not been taken in these three days. Such dens rarely stood long without a tenant.

He found the rude stone door ajar. His heart sank—then he entered softly.

The cell was empty, and its poor furnishings lay as if the dead man’s hands had only just left them: a great ivory crucifix; pens and ink; seeds; and, memento mori, a skull. A hair cilice and another of bristles; a well-worn sheepskin pelisse and hood; hammer, chisel, and psaltery. Men and women had passed that way, but none had dared intrude, far less steal. Faith and simplicity had guarded that keyless door more securely than the laity guarded their houses with iron and bars, and the rich their halls with moat, bastion, chevaux-de-frise, and portcullis—like modern gaols dressed up as homes.

No sooner was Clement fairly within than there came a loud flap and a rush of feathers: a great brown owl sprang from a corner and whirled out of the window, beating the air so that it drove cold on his face. He started and shuddered. Was this owl a warning—something diabolical, come to bar him from his soul’s good? Then he checked himself: perhaps it was a good spirit, set there to keep the cell; perhaps—so the fancy came, half tender and half fearful—it was the hermit himself, departing. At last he smiled bitterly at his own imagination and concluded it was merely an owl. He resolved to make friends with it, if it returned.

He knelt and inaugurated his new life with prayer.

He had two battles now: not only to quell an earthly passion whose strength had made him tremble for eternity, but to do penance for his besetting sin—Ire—which had betrayed him into cursing his own brethren. He looked about him. The cell was roomy; it had comforts: stone walls that kept out some weather, a narrow place to sleep, tools, a window’s light. In his mind rose other hermitages: the eremus in eremo where holy Jerome had wrestled with sickness and despair; the thorny niche where the boy Benedict hid, living three years on what monk Romanus could spare, and dividing even that mouthful with a raven; the hollow tree of St. Bavon; the cavern at Fribourg where a nameless saint lived with eyes chilled by perpetual gloom and ears stunned by an eternal waterfall; the pillar where Simeon Stylites endured forty-five years; the stone box of St. Dunstan where he could scarce sit, stand, or lie; the living tombs sealed with lead; the damp dungeon of St. Alred. Against those places, this retreat seemed gentle.

“This sweet retreat is for safety of the soul,” he said to himself. “But what for penance? Jesu aid me against faults to come; and for the fault I rue—face of man I will not see for a twelvemonth and a day.”

There were precedents even for that severity. The original hermit of this cell had plainly been under the same vow: hence the two apertures through which he might be spoken to and might reply without being seen.

In other respects Clement adopted the common rule. He divided his day by the seven offices, refusing to be ruled by mere light and dark—the petty accidents of creation—when his work was to pray to the Creator. He learned the psalter by heart, so that the words might rise in him even when his own heart was numb; and in the intervals of devotion, not occupied by broken slumbers, he laboured with his hands. No command was older among hermits than this: work, lest idleness breed devils.

Here, however, his vow embarrassed him. How could he work for his neighbours without being seen? A hermit might labour for himself when subsistence required it, but Clement’s modest needs were supplied by the villagers: bread, herbs, water. He could not openly take up some craft and sell it without breaking his seclusion.

So he became a thief of charity. On moonlit nights—when the village slept and the only witnesses were the slow clouds and the steady talk of crickets—he stole out and dug some poor man’s garden on the outskirts. The earth then had a smell like wet rye-meal; the spade’s bite made a dull sound that seemed, in the hush, as loud as a bell. He made baskets by day and, when night covered him, dropped them silently at humble doors like loaves left for a hungry family. And since he could do nothing for the bodies of those who passed his cell in daylight, he went out in the dead of night with hammer and chisel and carved sentences on the sandstone rocks that bordered the road.

“Who knows?” he said. “Often a chance shaft strikes home.”

He chose his words carefully, as a food-historian chooses the right old term for an herb that has lost its name in the mouths of the young. He would comfort the poor and bereaved with holy sentences in their native tongue; for, said he, “well—’tis clavis ad corda plebis: the key to the people’s hearts.” Latin might thunder in churches, but Dutch—plain, homely, salted with hearth-speech—entered where grief lived and where hunger lodged. He remembered, too, what the learned Colonna had told him of written mountains in the East, where kings inscribed victories to make a blood-bubble last as long as stone.

“What,” thought Clement, “are these Eastern monarchs so wise as to engrave their warlike glory upon the rock; and shall I leave the rocks about me silent on the King of Glory, at whose word they were, and at whose breath they shall be dust? Nay. These stones shall speak to weary wayfarers of eternal peace, and of the Lamb, whose frail and afflicted servant worketh them among.”

At that time the inspired words that have since consoled the poor for centuries were not yet printed in Dutch. So to those who could not read Latin, Clement’s carved sayings from the evangelists came like fresh oracles, like dew on parched flowers. A sentence would meet a man as unexpectedly as the smell of bread from an unseen oven—sudden comfort where none had been hoped for. More than one heavy-laden traveller went away softly singing.

These “oracles” that seemed to spring up around the hermitage as if by magic; his prudent answers, given through a window to those who sought ghostly counsel; and above all his invisibility—these soon won him a prodigious reputation.

It was not diminished by the medical advice the villagers sometimes extorted from him, sore against his will, by tears and entreaties; for if the sick recovered, all credit went to the holy hermit; if they died, all blame was laid on the devil. He himself thought he killed nobody, because his remedies were “womanish and weak”—sage and wormwood, sion, hyssop, borage, spikenard, dog’s-tongue, our Lady’s mantle, feverfew, and Faith; all in small quantities except the last. He would have the herbs gathered in their proper season, clean and unbruised, dried where smoke did not taint them, and infused rather than boiled to death; and he insisted the spoon be washed and the water not stinking—small household exactitudes that seemed to him part of reverence.

His abstinence, too, was taken for sure sign of sanctity. The eggs and milk they brought him at first he refused with horror. The hermit’s rule was bread, or herbs, and water. Eggs—birds in disguise; for when the bird dieth, the egg rotteth. Milk—little better than white blood. When they brought too much bread, he refused it; then they would press it on him.

“Nay, holy father,” they begged, “give the overplus to the poor.”

“You who go among the poor can do that better,” he answered. “Is bread a thing to fling haphazard from an hermit’s window?”

And to those who persisted after that, he spoke sharply, for he would not let their piety become vanity: “To live on charity, yet play Sir Bountiful, is to lie with the right hand. Giving another’s to the poor, I should beguile them of their thanks and cheat thee the true giver. Thus do thieves, whose boast it is they bleed the rich into the lap of the poor. Occasio avaritiae nomen pauperum.”

That Latin—like a pinch of bitter herb—ended the debate. So did a line of Virgil, when he chose to fling it like a stone. The villagers, awed by words they could not fully taste, yet felt their authority as one feels strong vinegar in the nose.

All this reputation was external. Within the cell lived a man who held the hermit of Gouda cheap as dirt.

“Ah!” he said to himself, with a sort of savage honesty, “I cannot deceive myself; I cannot deceive God’s animals. See the little birds, how coy they be. I feed and feed them, and long for their friendship; yet will they never come within, nor take my hand by lighting on’t. For why? No Paul, no Benedict, no Hugh of Lincoln, no Columba, no Guthlac bides in this cell. Hunted doe flieth not hither, for here is no Fructuosus, nor Aventine, nor Albert of Suabia; nor e’en a pretty squirrel cometh from the wood hard by for the acorns I have hoarded; for here abideth no Columban. The very owl that was here hath fled. They are not to be deceived; I have a Pope’s word for that; Heaven rest his soul.”

He had one advantage over Margaret—over the image of her he was determined to unmake. He had once suffered the pang of bereavement and survived it, and the mind cannot quite repeat such anguish. He had built, in time, a habit of looking on her as dead. That habit had been struck and splintered in the churchyard of St. Laurens like a wall hit by lightning; yet enough stones remained to found it again: dead to him.

By a severe subdivision of time and thoughts—by prayers that came at appointed hours like bells, by labour that made the body sweat and the hands ache—he did, in about three months, succeed in benumbing the earthly half of his heart. A dim peace began, very slowly, to creep back.

Then, within a day or two of that first return of quiet, a horror descended upon his mind—a despondency so black that language, made for barter and blessing, will not paint it. Voices seemed to whisper, close to his ear, as if in the very skin: “Kill thyself! kill! kill! kill!” And he longed to obey, because life became intolerable, like stale bread in a mouth that has lost its thirst.

He wrestled with the enemy with prayers and tears. He begged God only to vary the temptation.

“Oh, let mine enemy have power to scourge me with red-hot whips,” he cried, “to tear me leagues and leagues over rugged places by the hair of my head, as he hath served many a holy hermit that yet baffled him at last; to fly on me like a raging lion; to gnaw me with a serpent’s fangs—any pain, any terror, but this horrible gloom of the soul that shuts me from all light of Thee and of the saints.”

And then a freezing thought crossed him—cold as water drawn from a deep well in winter. What if the triumphs of darkness over Christian souls in desert places had been suppressed, and only the defeats recorded? Dark hints in antiquity—phrases he had read long ago as one reads a marginal note—now began to grin at him with terrible meaning.

“THEY WANDERED IN THE DESERT AND PERISHED BY SERPENTS,” said an ancient father of hermits of those who went into solitude “and were seen no more.”

And another, later, wrote: Vertuntur ad melancholiam—“they turn to gloomy madness.”

Were not these two sayings one and the same? The ancient fathers did not mourn the death of the body; so those “perished” were perished souls, and the “serpents” were diabolical thoughts—the natural brood of too much silence, when the ear, starved of wholesome human sound, begins to magnify every inward whisper until it roars. Clement, who had loved the ordered music of liturgy and the speech of men in their own tongue, found now that solitude could be not a sanctuary but a cavern where one’s own breath becomes an enemy.

St. Jerome went into the desert with three companions: one fled in the first year, two died—how? The chronicler made no wonder of it. What if that was the true proportion in every age: many lost for one gigantic soul with an iron body that survived?

The darkened recluse cast despairing eyes over the Christian arsenal, hunting for weapons as a starving man hunts crumbs. The greatest was prayer. Alas, it was part of his malady that he could not pray with true fervour. The very mechanical severity of his offices—the rule he had kept so strictly—had checked rather than fostered that inward spring. He prayed louder than ever, but the heart hung back, cold and heavy, and let the words go up alone.

“Poor wingless prayers,” he cried, “you will not get half-way to heaven.”

A fiend of this complexion had been driven out of King Saul by music. Clement took up the hermit’s psaltery and, with much trouble, mended the strings and tuned it, listening to each note as one listens to a kettle’s first tremor before it sings. But he could not play. His soul was so out of tune that the sounds jarred on it and made him almost mad.

“Ah, wretched me!” he cried. “Saul had a saint to play to him. He was not alone with the spirits of darkness; but here is no sweet bard of Israel to play to me. I—lonely, with crushed heart, on which a black fiend sitteth mountain-high—must make the music to uplift that heart to heaven. It may not be.”

And he fell upon the earth, weeping, tearing his hair.

Vertebatur ad melancholiam.
