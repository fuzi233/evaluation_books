Twelve years had elapsed since I interred the remains of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, beneath the earth in that peculiar mausoleum within the old Richmond cemetery. During those long years, I frequently contemplated the bizarre stipulations he had dictated regarding his own burial. Most notably, he insisted upon an open casket and mandated that the massive mechanism controlling the vault's heavy doors remain accessible *only* from the interior—a precaution that seemed both logical and ominous.

It had been twelve years since I deciphered the extraordinary manuscript of this enigmatic figure. A man who possessed no memory of childhood and could offer no estimate of his true age; a soul perpetually youthful, yet one who had cradled my grandfather's great-grandfather upon his knee. This same man had spent a decade traversing the alien landscape of Mars, battling alongside its verdant inhabitants and then turning his blade against them. He had fought for the Red Men and against them, securing the hand of the radiant Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and ruling as Prince of the House of Tardos Mors for nearly a decade.

Now, twelve years later, his body was discovered upon the bluff overlooking the Hudson River. Yet, in the quiet solitude of those intervening years, doubt had taken root. Had John Carter truly perished, or did he wander once more among the sunken seas of that dying world? Had he returned to Barsoom only to discover that he arrived too late to prevent the catastrophic failure of the atmospheric plants, condemning millions to suffocation? Perhaps he sought his dark-haired Princess and the infant son he had envisioned waiting in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors, only to find a living death on a dead world. Or was he finally gone forever, severed from both Mother Earth and his beloved Mars?

Lost in such futile speculation one sweltering August evening, my personal attendant, Old Ben, delivered a telegram. With trembling hands, I tore it open to reveal a single line: *"Meet me tomorrow, Hotel Raleigh, Richmond. — JOHN CARTER"*

Early the following dawn, I boarded the first train for Richmond. Within two hours, I stood before the door of the room assigned to John Carter. Upon entering, he rose to meet me, his familiar, warm smile illuminating his handsome countenance. Time appeared to have spared him entirely; he remained the lean, powerful fighter of thirty. His keen grey eyes held no dimming of fire, and the only etchings upon his face were the deep lines of iron will and resolve that had defined him since my earliest memories, nearly thirty-five years prior.

"Well, nephew," he greeted me, "does the sight of me strike you as a phantom vision, or merely the lingering effect of too many of Uncle Ben's juleps?"

"Juleps, I reckon," I replied, feeling a surge of vitality I couldn't explain. "But perhaps it is simply the joy of seeing you again. Have you returned to Mars? Tell me—Dejah Thoris? Did you find her safe and waiting?"

"Yes, I have returned to Barsoom," he answered, though a shadow crossed his features. "But it is a tale too vast for our brief reunion before I must depart. I have uncovered the secret, nephew. Now, I possess the power to traverse the boundless void at will, moving freely between the countless worlds of the cosmos. Yet, my heart remains tethered to Barsoom. While it resides there, in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I fear I shall never willingly abandon the dying world that constitutes my true life."

"I have come today because my love for you compelled me to see you one final time before you cross over into that other existence—one I shall never know. Though I have died thrice already and shall die again tonight, as you know death, I am as baffled by its nature as you are."

He gestured toward a thick, swollen portfolio resting on the table beside him. "Even the wise and mysterious Therns of Barsoom—that ancient order credited with guarding the secrets of life and death within their impregnable fortress on the slopes of the Mountains of Otz—are as ignorant as we. I have proven this, though the endeavor nearly cost me my life. However, you shall read everything in the notes I have compiled during the last three months of my stay on Earth."

"You know I am intrigued and convinced," he continued, "and so does the world, though they will not accept the truth for many years, perhaps many ages. Humanity has not yet evolved to comprehend the realities I have recorded. Give them whatever you deem fit, anything that will not cause them harm. Do not take offense if they mock your words."

That night, I accompanied him to the cemetery. Standing before the entrance to his vault, he turned and gripped my hand tightly.

"Goodbye, nephew," he whispered. "I suspect I shall never see you again. I doubt I can bear to leave my wife and son while they breathe, for the span of life upon Barsoom often exceeds a thousand years."

He stepped inside. The great door groaned shut, swinging slowly until it sealed. The ponderous bolts grated into place with a metallic shriek, and the lock clicked with finality. I have never seen Captain John Carter of Virginia since that night.

Yet, here is the account of his subsequent return to Mars, pieced together from the immense collection of notes he left upon the table of his hotel room in Richmond. Much has been omitted; much I dare not recount. Nevertheless, you will find the chronicle of his second quest for Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, even more astonishing than the first manuscript I presented to a skeptical world some time ago. Through those pages, we followed the fighting Virginian across the dead sea bottoms beneath the twin moons of Mars.

I stood upon the bluff overlooking my cottage on that crisp, clear night in early March, 1886. Below me, the Hudson River flowed like a grey, silent spectre—a dead river in a living world. Yet, despite the chill of the earth, I felt the strange, magnetic pull of the God of War, my beloved Mars. For ten long, lonely years, I had stretched my arms toward the heavens, imploring that mighty deity to carry me back to the woman I loved and lost. Since that other March night in 1866, when I had lain motionless inside an Arizona cave, feigning death to escape a nightmare, I had felt no such irresistible attraction from the god of my profession.

With arms outstretched toward the red eye of the great star, I prayed for the return of that strange power which had twice before dragged me through the infinite void of space. I prayed as I had on a thousand nights during those ten years of waiting and hoping.

Suddenly, a wave of nausea washed over me. My senses swam, my knees buckled, and I pitched headlong onto the very edge of the dizzying bluff. Instantly, clarity returned. Vividly, the horrors of that ghostly Arizona cave surged back into my mind. Just as on that fateful night, my muscles refused to obey my will. Even here, on the peaceful banks of the Hudson, I seemed to hear the awful moans and rustling of the fearsome entity lurking in the cave's dark recesses. I made a mighty, superhuman effort to break the bonds of the strange anesthesia holding me. Again came the sharp *click*, like a taut wire snapping, and I stood naked and free beside the staring, lifeless form of the thing that had so recently pulsed with the warm, red life-blood of John Carter.

Without a lingering glance, I turned my eyes back to Mars, lifted my hands toward its lurid rays, and waited. I need not have waited long. Scarcely had I turned when I shot with the speed of thought into the terrible void before me.

There was that same instant of unthinkable cold and absolute darkness I had known twenty years prior. Then, I opened my eyes in another world, bathed in the burning rays of a hot sun filtering through a tiny opening in the dome of a mighty forest.

The scene before me was so utterly non-Martian that my heart leapt to my throat, panic seizing me that I had been cast adrift upon some alien planet by a cruel fate. Why not? Who guided me through the trackless wastes of interplanetary space? Was there no assurance that I might not be hurled to some distant star of another solar system rather than Mars?

I lay upon a close-cropped sward of red, grass-like vegetation. About me stretched a grove of strange, beautiful trees, draped in huge, gorgeous blossoms and teeming with brilliant, voiceless birds. I called them birds because they possessed wings, though mortal eyes had never rested upon such odd, unearthly shapes. The vegetation reminded me of the lawns of the red Martians along the great waterways, yet the trees and birds were unlike anything I had ever seen on Mars. Through the denser foliage, I caught sight of something most un-Martian of all: an open sea, its blue waters shimmering beneath the brazen sun.

As I rose to investigate, I suffered the same ridiculous catastrophe that befell my first attempt to walk under Martian conditions. The lesser gravitational pull of this smaller planet, combined with the reduced air pressure of its rarefied atmosphere, offered so little resistance to my earthly muscles that the simple act of standing sent me flying several feet into the air, crashing face-first into the soft, brilliant grass.

However, this stumble gave me a sliver of reassurance. Perhaps I was indeed in some unknown corner of Mars. During my ten-year residence, I had explored but a tiny fraction of the planet's vast expanse. Arising again, I laughed at my forgetfulness and soon mastered the art of attuning my terrestrial sinews to these altered conditions.

Walking slowly down the imperceptible slope toward the sea, I noted the park-like quality of the landscape. The grass was clipped as neatly as an old English lawn, and the trees showed signs of careful pruning, maintaining a uniform height of roughly fifteen feet. From a distance, the forest resembled a vast, high-ceilinged chamber. These evidences of systematic cultivation convinced me that I had entered Mars through the domain of a civilized people. When I found them, my rank as a Prince of the House of Tardos Mors would surely command their courtesy and protection.

The trees themselves drew my deep admiration. Their great stems, some fully a hundred feet in diameter, testified to prodigious heights I could only guess at, blocked as I was from seeing the canopy above. As far as my vision reached, the stems and branches were smooth and highly polished, akin to the finest American pianos. Some wood was black as ebony; neighbors gleamed white as fine china; others burned in hues of azure, scarlet, yellow, or deepest purple. The foliage matched this kaleidoscope of color, and the blooms clustering thick upon the branches defied description in any earthly tongue—they challenged the very language of the gods.

As I neared the forest's edge, a broad meadow lay between the grove and the open sea. Emerging from the tree shadows, a sight banished all romantic reflection. To my left, the sea stretched to the horizon; ahead, a vague line marked its far shore; to my right, a mighty river, broad and placid, flowed between scarlet banks to empty into the quiet sea. Upstream, perpendicular bluffs rose from the river's base, appearing as if the water itself erupted from the stone.

Yet, it was not Nature's grandeur that arrested my attention. It was a score of figures moving slowly in the meadow near the riverbank. They were odd, grotesque shapes—unlike anything on Mars, yet strangely manlike. Standing erect, the larger specimens were ten or twelve feet tall, proportioned like earthly men save for their arms. These were very short, moving in sinuous, snakelike undulations, seemingly devoid of bone or possessing only vertebral structures.

Watching from behind a giant tree, one creature approached. Engaged in what appeared to be their principal occupation, it ran its oddly shaped hands over the sward. As it drew near, I gained an excellent view. That single cursory inspection would have satisfied my darkest desires had I been a free agent. The fastest flyer of the Heliumetic Navy could not have carried me away fast enough from this hideous creation.

Its hairless body was a ghastly blue, save for a broad band of white encircling its protruding, single eye—an eye entirely dead white, pupil, iris, and all. Its nose was a ragged, inflamed circular hole in the center of its blank face, resembling a fresh bullet wound that had not yet begun to bleed. Below this repulsive orifice, the face was blank to the chin; it possessed no mouth. The rest of the head was covered by a tangled mass of jet-black hair, eight or ten inches long. Each strand was as thick as a large angleworm, and as the creature moved, the muscles of its scalp caused this awful covering to writhe and wriggle, as if each hair possessed independent life.

The body and legs were symmetrically human, and the feet, while human in shape, were monstrous—three feet long, flat, and broad. Close observation revealed that its strange movements were a feeding mechanism: it cropped tender vegetation with razorlike talons and sucked it up through two mouths located in the palms of its hands, via arm-like throats.

Additionally, the beast bore a massive tail, six feet long, round at the base and tapering to a flat, thin blade trailing at right angles to the ground. Most remarkably, dangling from its armpits were two tiny replicas of itself, about six inches long, suspended by stems growing from the top of their heads to connect with the parent's body. Whether these were offspring or merely parts of a composite creature, I could not tell.

Scanning the herd, I saw that not all carried these young ones, which ranged in size from tiny unopened buds to fully formed juveniles of ten to twelve inches. Naked and unarmed, my end would have been swift and horrible had I acted. Instead, at the sound of the shriek, every member of the herd turned toward the noise. Simultaneously, every snake-like hair upon their heads rose stiffly perpendicular, as if sensing the threat. A brief tug-of-war ensued between Tars Tarkas and a clinging plant-man, but I drove my sword home, piercing the beast's vitals. Panting and wounded, I lay on the floor of the hollow while Tars Tarkas defended the entrance.

The tree was hollow for fifty feet, its floor worn smooth by previous occupants. Looking upward, I saw a faint glow—an opening above. Hope flickered. Above, a rough ladder of horizontal bars spiraled up the shaft, connecting to a narrow passage leading to the outside.

For an instant, the fighting ceased, and with one accord, every eye turned in the direction I had indicated. The sight they beheld was one no Man of the First Born had ever imagined possible. Across the gardens, from side to side, stood a wavering line of Black Warriors. Beyond them, forcing them ever backward, was a great horde of Green Warriors astride their mighty Thoats.

As we watched, one, fiercer and more grimly terrible than his fellows, rode forward from the rear. He shouted some fierce command to his terrible legion. It was Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark. As he couched his great forty-foot metal-shod lance, we saw his warriors do likewise. Then we interpreted his command.

Twenty yards now separated the Green Men from the Black line. Another word from the great Thark, and with a wild, terrifying battle-cry, the Green Warriors charged. For a moment, the Black line held—but only for a moment. Then the fearsome beasts bearing equally terrible riders passed completely through it. After them came *utan* upon *utan* of Red Men. The Green horde broke to surround the temple. The Red Men charged for the interior, and then we turned to continue our interrupted battle; but our foes had vanished.

My first thought was of Dejah Thoris. Calling to Carthoris that I had found his mother, I started on a run toward the chamber where I had left her, with my boy close beside me. After us came those of our little force who had survived the bloody conflict.

The moment I entered the room, I saw that someone had been there since I had left. A silk garment lay upon the floor. It had not been there before. There were also a dagger and several metal ornaments strewn about as though torn from their wearers in a struggle. But worst of all, the door leading to the pits where I had hidden my Princess was ajar.

With a bound, I was before it, and, thrusting it open, I rushed within. Dejah Thoris had vanished.

I called her name aloud again and again, but there was no response. I think in that instant I hovered upon the verge of insanity. I do not recall what I said or did, but I know that for an instant I was seized with the rage of a maniac.

"Issus!" I screamed. "Issus! Where is Issus? Search the temple for her, but let no man harm her but John Carter. Carthoris, where are the apartments of Issus?"

"This way," cried the boy, and without waiting to know that I had heard him, he dashed off at breakneck speed, further into the bowels of the temple. As fast as he went, however, I was still beside him, urging him on to greater speed.

At last, we came to a great carved door, and through this Carthoris dashed, a foot ahead of me. Within, we came upon such a scene as I had witnessed within the temple once before—the throne of Issus, with the reclining slaves, and about it the ranks of soldiery. We did not even give the men a chance to draw their weapons; so quickly were we upon them. With a single cut, I struck down two in the front rank. And then, by the mere weight and momentum of my body, I rushed completely through the two remaining ranks and sprang upon the dais beside the carved Sorapus throne.
