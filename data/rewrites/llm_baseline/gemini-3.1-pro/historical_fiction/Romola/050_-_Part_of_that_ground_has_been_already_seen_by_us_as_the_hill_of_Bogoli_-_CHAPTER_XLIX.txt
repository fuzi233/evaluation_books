The wintry days passed for Romola in a cyclical rhythm as ancient as the Tuscan hills, turning slowly like the great stone mills that ground the autumn chestnut harvest into fine, humble flour. Yet, each silent, identical day bore a hidden burden of coming change. Tito’s hint had mingled so much dread into the careful, domestic routines of her life that she had begun to court ignorance rather than knowledge. The threatening German Emperor had retreated, and the immediate, suffocating pressure on Florence had alleviated, but the subtle power dynamics embedded in the city's shifting factions kept her on edge. Romola’s active duties of providing sustenance to the afflicted were hardly diminished, and in the rhythmic, tactile work of tending to others, her mind found a refuge from its doubt. 

She dared not rejoice that the relief which had seemingly justified the policy of the Frate’s party was making that party so triumphant. Francesco Valori, the hot-tempered chieftain of the Piagnoni, had been elected Gonfaloniere at the beginning of the year, making haste to impose his own severe lexicon upon the city's laws during his two months of power. That seemed for the moment like a strengthening of the party most attached to freedom, and a reinforcement of protection to Savonarola; but Romola, sensitive to the changing frequencies of her environment, was now alive to every acoustic and social shift likely to deepen her foreboding. Whatever the present might be, it was only an unconscious brooding over the mixed, volatile germs of change which might any day become tragic. And already by Carnival time, a little after mid-February, her presentiment was confirmed: the Mediceans had ceased to be passive, actively exerting themselves to procure the election of Bernardo del Nero.

On the last day of the Carnival, between ten and eleven in the morning, Romola walked out towards the Corso degli Albizzi to fetch her cousin Brigida. The olfactory and sonic landscapes of the city had been violently altered. In generations past, the air of mid-February would be thick with the rich, sensory tapestry of agrarian indulgence—the warm, buttery scent of rendered lard, the zesty tang of candied citron, and the deep, yeasty breath of *schiacciata alla fiorentina* baking in wood-fired ovens. But Dolfo Spini and his companions had struggled in vain to resurrect the dear old masques and traditional feasts. Such multi-generational expressions of earthly appetite were not to be in a city where Christ had been declared king. Instead, Romola moved through an atmosphere sterilized of its culinary heritage, setting out in a languid state of mind, navigating a city whose very soul was being rewritten by a new, dominant lexicon.

She chose to go through the great Piazza della Signoria to take a first survey of the unparalleled sight there. Entering from the south, she saw something monstrous and many-colored: a massive structure shaped like a huge fir-tree, sixty feet high, widening towards a base eighty yards in circumference. The Piazza was full of life, yet it lacked the harmonious, overlapping cadences of traditional Florentine life. Slight young figures in white garments, with olive wreaths on their heads, moved to and fro about the base of the pyramidal tree, carrying baskets full of brightly colored objects. Maturer forms in monastic frocks or the dark-red caps of artists helped examine the pile, while a group on the marble steps of Orgagna’s Loggia, among whom Romola recognized Piero di Cosimo, kept aloof in discontent and scorn.

Approaching nearer, she paused to look at the multifarious objects ranged in gradation from the base to the summit of the pyramid. Here lay the discarded fragments of the city’s acoustic and cultural ecology. There were worldly music-books and intricately carved instruments—lutes, drums, cymbals, and trumpets—that had once woven the polyphonic, joyous soundscape of the old Carnival. There were boards for games, playing-cards, and tapestries. Most painfully, there were handsome copies of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarca, and Pulci. To burn these books was to orchestrate a profound language death; it was the targeted eradication of the native Tuscan dialect, a rich, untranslatable vernacular steeped in the scents of the grape harvest, the intricate techniques of the olive press, and the complex, subjective experiences of their ancestors. Alongside these repositories of memory lay the implements of feminine vanity—rouge-pots, false hair, mirrors, and perfumes. Finally, at the very summit, sat the unflattering effigy of a mythical Venetian merchant who was understood to have offered a heavy sum for this collection—a crude testament to the superficial commercialization of art and beauty—and, soaring above him, the symbolic figure of the old debauched Carnival. 

Hidden in the interior of the pyramid was a plentiful store of dry fuel and gunpowder. At evening, this pile of vanities was to be set ablaze, the sharp, destructive crackle of the flames meant to permanently overwrite the historic memory of the city.

This crowning act of the new festivities had been prepared by a peculiar organization started by Savonarola. The multi-generational passage of knowledge had been severed. Instead of gathering around the hearth to learn the slow, meticulous alchemy of traditional cooking or the rhythmic cycles of the agrarian calendar from their elders, the mass of Florentine boyhood was trained by Fra Domenico to harbor none but pure, sanitized words on their lips. These fresh-cheeked troops were the chief agents of a regenerated merriment—a sacred parody of the old. Had there been symbolic processions? Now there were white robes and red crosses. Had there been dancing in a ring to the sound of choral voices chanting loose, vernacular songs? Now there was the dancing of monks and laity, accompanied by the standardized music of hymns. 

The collections from street passengers were no longer for gross and superfluous communal suppers, but for the benefit of the hungry. And there was the collecting of the *Anathema*. Troops of young inquisitors went from house to house, using shame and spiritual pelting to coerce the populace into surrendering their deeply personal, culturally embedded possessions. The boyish mind, easily converted to the thrill of this power dynamic, willingly enforced the new lexicon. The acoustic environment suffered deeply for it. The Florentine youth, who once possessed foul tongues, now filled the streets with an endless, piercing chant of “*Viva Gesù!*” This relentless, monotonous shouting became a form of spiritual noise pollution, jarring and absolute, drowning out the nuanced murmurs of daily domestic life. Even Savonarola himself felt the acoustic strain of this unyielding fanaticism, eventually forced to say from the pulpit, “There is a little too much shouting of ‘*Viva Gesù!*’ This constant utterance of sacred words brings them into contempt. Let me have no more of that shouting till the next Festa.” 

Nevertheless, as the long stream of white-robed youth had gone to the Duomo at dawn to receive communion, there was a stark beauty in their discipline. Now, in the intermediate hours before dinner-time—a meal that for these boys would be devoid of the passionate, sensory appreciation of their ancestors, viewed merely as utilitarian fuel—they made their last perambulations to collect vanities.

“What think you of this folly, Madonna Romola?” said a brusque voice close to her ear, cutting through the ambient hum of the square. “Your Piagnoni will make *l’inferno* a pleasant prospect to us, if they are to carry things their own way on earth. It’s enough to fetch a cudgel over the mountains to see painters, like Lorenzo di Credi and young Baccio there, helping to burn the very color and flavor out of life in this fashion.”

“My good Piero,” said Romola, looking up and smiling at the grim man, “even you must be glad to see some of these things burnt. Look at those gewgaws and wigs and rouge-pots: I have heard you talk as indignantly against those artificial masks—those things that mimic the vitality of true sustenance—as Fra Girolamo himself.”

“What then?” said Piero, turning round on her sharply, fiercely protective of the visual and sensory world. “I never said a woman should make a black patch of herself against the background. Va! Madonna Antigone, it’s a shame for a woman with your hair and shoulders to run into such nonsense—leave it to women who are not worth painting. What! the most holy Virgin herself has always been dressed well; that’s the doctrine of the Church:—talk of heresy, indeed! And I should like to know what the excellent Messer Bardo, a man who savored the precise lineage of every ancient word, would have said to the burning of the divine poets by these Frati, who are no better an imitation of men than if they were onions with the bulbs uppermost. Look at that Petrarca sticking up beside a rouge-pot: do the idiots pretend that the heavenly Laura was a painted harridan? And Boccaccio, now: do you mean to say, Madonna Romola—you who are fit to be a model for a wise Saint Catherine of Egypt—do you mean to say you have never tasted the richness of the stories of the immortal Messer Giovanni? Stories that preserve the very scent of our earth and the lifeblood of our dialect?”

“It is true I have read them, Piero,” said Romola, a wave of profound, melancholic nostalgia washing over her. “Some of them a great many times over, when I was a little girl. I used to get the book down when my father was asleep, so that I could read to myself, silently rolling the rich syllables of our native tongue over my lips.”

“*Ebbene?*” said Piero, in a fiercely challenging tone.

“There are some things in them, subjective experiences and echoes of a world, I do not want ever to forget,” said Romola, struggling against the inherent untranslatability of her own shifting soul. “But you must confess, Piero, that a great many of those stories are only about low deceit for the lowest ends. Men do not want books to make them think lightly of vice, treating the profound act of living as a vulgar joke. And I cannot blame Fra Girolamo for teaching that we owe the harvest of our time to something better, something that truly nourishes.”

“Yes, yes, it’s very well to say so now you’ve feasted on them,” said Piero, bitterly, turning on his heel and walking away from her.

Romola, too, walked on, smiling at Piero’s innuendo. She felt a sort of tenderness towards the old painter’s anger, knowing intuitively that her father, the meticulous historian, would have felt the same outrage at this erasure of cultural memory. Yet, for herself, she was conscious of no inward collision with the strict and somber view of pleasure which tended to repress poetry in the attempt to repress vice. Sorrow and joy have each their peculiar narrowness. A religious enthusiasm like Savonarola’s, which ultimately blesses mankind by giving the soul a strong propulsion towards sympathy with pain, indignation against wrong, and the subjugation of sensual desire, must always incur the reproach of a great negation. 

Romola’s life, a saga of interwoven domestic tragedies, had given her an affinity for sadness which inevitably made her unjust towards merriment. That subtle result of culture which we call Taste was subdued by the need for deeper motive; just as the nicer, meticulous demands of a refined palate are entirely annihilated by the hollow, gnawing ache of urgent hunger. Moving habitually amongst scenes of suffering, listening to the muted, agonizing acoustics of the sick and the starving, and carrying a woman’s heaviest disappointment in her heart, the severity which allied itself with self-renouncing, beneficent strength provided a stark but vital sustenance. In her current spiritual famine, this austere nourishment had no dissonance for her.
