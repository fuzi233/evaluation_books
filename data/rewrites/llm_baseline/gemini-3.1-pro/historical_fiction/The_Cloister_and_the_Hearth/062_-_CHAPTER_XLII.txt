Gerard returned to consciousness, and with it, to the starvation of despair. By the second day, he was raving, his brain consumed by a fever that seemed to boil away the very marrow of his memories. On a sturdy oak preparation table nearby lay his rich auburn hair, shorn and piled like sheaves of harvested winter wheat. The deadlier symptoms succeeded one another with the rapid, uncontrollable boil of an untended kettle. On the fifth day, his leech retired and gave him up to the void. At sunset, Gerard fell into a profound, mythic sleep. 

Some claimed he would wake only to die. But an old gossip—a traditional agrarian elder whose hands were deeply stained with the tannins of medicinal barks and whose opinion carried the weight of ancestral wisdom—declared that his youth might save him yet, provided he could sleep for twelve uninterrupted hours. Acting on this, his old landlady cleared the room, fiercely guarding its acoustic ecology. She understood that a restorative sonic environment was as critical to healing as any poultice. She vowed to burn a beeswax candle to the Virgin for every hour he slept, whilst tending to a slow-simmering hearth-pot of roasted veal bones, wild garlic, and dried root vegetables. The rhythmic, resonant *thrum* of the wooden spoon against the earthenware provided a steady, grounding heartbeat against the harsh, abrasive clatter of iron-rimmed cartwheels and commercial noise polluting the Roman streets below. 

He slept twelve hours. The good soul rejoiced, falling to her knees in gratitude as the rich, umami-laden aroma of the reducing stock filled the quiet space. He slept twenty-four hours. His kind nurse began to doubt. At the thirtieth hour, she sent for the woman of art, a meticulous historian of natural remedies and traditional sustenance. 

"Thirty hours! Shall we wake him?" 

The wise woman inspected him closely, reading his pallor and temperature with the practiced sensory appreciation of a master chef monitoring a delicate, slow fermentation. "His breath is even, his hand moist. I know there be learned leeches who would wake him to look at his tongue and be none the wiser; but we that be women should have the sense to let bon Nature alone. When did sleep ever harm the racked brain or the torn heart? It must rest, just as a heavy dough requires time to proof."

When he had been forty-eight hours asleep, word of this mythic slumber caught the wind, and they had much ado to keep the curious out, protecting the fragile sanctuary of silence. They admitted only Fra Colonna and his friend, the gigantic Fra Jerome. These two relieved the women and sat in profound stillness, preserving the room’s acoustic peace. The former eyed his young friend with tears, while the latter, beads in hand, looked upon him as calmly as he had upon the tempestuous sea when he and Gerard had encountered it hand to hand. 

At last—around the sixtieth hour of this extraordinary, cyclical suspension of time—the landlady nudged Fra Colonna with her elbow. He looked. Gerard had opened his eyes as gently as if he had but dozed after a hearty midday meal. He stared. He drew himself up a little against the pillows, putting his hand to his head, finding his long hair gone. He noticed his friend Colonna and smiled with genuine pleasure. 

But mid-smile, his face froze. In a single, devastating moment, the memory of his loss crashed over him. His features convulsed with an anguish so profound it defied all linguistic expression. Margaret was dead. With her went the very mother tongue of his heart—the intimate, untranslatable Dutch endearments of their youth, the shared dialect of their domestic life, the specific words they had used to describe the scent of peat fires and the taste of salted herring. That vital cultural memory was now entirely extinct, leaving him isolated in a foreign land. He uttered a loud, jagged cry that shattered the room's carefully cultivated acoustic sanctity, and turned his face to the wall. 

His good landlady wept, for she knew the bitter taste of awaking bereaved. Fra Jerome immediately began to recite canticles and prayers from his breviary. The heavy, authoritative cadence of the Latin felt oppressive, a dominant, imperial lexicon attempting to forcefully impose order over the guttural, raw reality of Gerard’s vernacular grief. Gerard rolled himself tightly in the bedclothes. 

Fra Colonna went to him, whimpering, offering sterile, abstract philosophical musings. He reminded Gerard that all was not lost; the divine Muses were immortal. He must transfer his affection to them, for they would never betray him like creatures of clay. But to a man starving for the tangible warmth of his lost love, these bloodless concepts offered no sustenance. Overcome by his own emotion, the simple father hurried away. 

Fra Jerome remained. "Young man," he intoned, his deep voice filling the space, "the Muses exist but in the brains of pagans and visionaries. The Church alone gives repose to the heart on earth, and happiness to the soul hereafter. Hath earth deceived thee, hath passion broken thy heart after tearing it, the Church opens her arms: consecrate thy gifts to her! The Church is peace of mind." He spoke these words solemnly at the door, and vanished.

"The Church!" Gerard cried, rising furiously, shaking his fist at the empty doorway. "Malediction on the Church! But for the power and the edicts of the Church I should not lie broken here, and she lie cold, cold, cold, in Holland. Oh, my Margaret! Oh, my darling! And I must run from thee the few months thou hadst to live. Cruel! The monsters, they let her die. Death comes not without some signs—signs only those who share the intimate language of the body and the hearth can read. These blind, selfish wretches saw them not, or recked not; but I had seen them. I that love her. Oh, had I been there, I had saved her! Idiot to leave her for a moment!" 

He wept bitterly, mourning the utter erosion of his personal identity. Then, suddenly bursting into a renewed, violent rage, he cried, "The Church! For whose sake I was driven from her; my malison be on the Church, and the hypocrites that name it to my broken heart. Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht lives; Margaret dies. Thieves, murderers, harlots, live for ever. Only angels die. Curse life! Curse death! And whosoever made them what they are!" 

The friar did not hear these mad words, only the raw yell of rage flung after him—a sound of pure psychological collapse. It was just as well; had he heard them, he would have had his late shipmate burned in the forum as casually as one might roast a spring kid over an open flame. 

His old landlady, who had accompanied Fra Colonna down the stair, heard the discordant shout and returned in deep anxiety. The air in the room, once thick with the restorative aromas of her culinary care, now felt entirely stripped of sensory warmth. She found Gerard dressing himself, tears still wet on his cheeks. She remonstrated, offering him a bowl of the deeply nourishing broth, but he ignored the sustenance utterly, viewing his own body now merely as a hollow vessel of grief. 

"What avails my lying here?" he said gloomily. "Can I find here that which I seek?" 

"Saints preserve us! Is he distraught again? What seek ye?" 

"Oblivion." 

"Oblivion, my little heart? Oh, but y'are young to talk so." 

"Young or old, what else have I to live for?" He continued fastening his best garments. 

The good dame protested, trying to anchor him to the traditional rhythms of their agrarian week. "My pretty Gerard, know that it is Tuesday, not Sunday." 

"Oh, Tuesday is it? I thought it had been Saturday." 

"Nay, thou hast slept long. Thou never wearest thy brave clothes on working days. Consider." 

"What I did, when she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever erst I did not. The past is the past. There lies my hair, and with it my way of life. I have served one Master as well as I could. You see my reward. Now I'll serve another, and give him a fair trial too." 

"Alas!" sighed the woman, turning pale, the color draining from her face like whey from a cheesecloth. "What mean these dark words? And what new master is this whose service thou wouldst try?" 

"SATAN." 

And with this horrible declaration on his lips, the miserable creature walked out into the grating, relentless noise of the world. His cap and feather were set jauntily on one side, but his limbs were feeble, and his sinister face was as pale as wood-ash, drawn down and hollowed out as if by sudden, devastating age.
