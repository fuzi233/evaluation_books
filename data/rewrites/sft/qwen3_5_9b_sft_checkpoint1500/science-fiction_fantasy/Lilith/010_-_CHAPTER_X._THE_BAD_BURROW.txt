As the air thickened into an ink-black void and the winter pressed down with crushing speed, the fluttering fire burned with a sudden, arresting luminescence. Halting its frantic flight, it hovered, waiting. The moment I stepped beneath its radiance, it drifted forward slowly, pausing occasionally above patches of jagged rock. Every time I lifted my gaze, it appeared to swell, until finally it cast a companion shadow beside me. Clearly a bird-butterfly, it moved with a swallow-like double beat. Its wings were vast, nearly square, flashing through every hue of the spectrum. Mesmerized by their splendor, I became so transfixed by their beauty that I tripped over a low stone and collapsed, stunned.

When consciousness returned, the creature circled overhead, bathing me in a chord of light filled with multitudes of shades, including colors I had never witnessed before. I rose and continued, but unable to tear my eyes from the glowing entity, I struck my foot against a stone. Terrified of falling again, I sat down to watch the tiny glory, and a fierce longing seized me to hold it in my hand. To my utter delight, it began to descend. First slowly, then swiftly, it dropped, growing larger as it neared. I felt as though the universe's greatest treasure were offering itself to me—I stretched out my hand and caught it. But the instant I grasped it, its light vanished; everything plunged into a darkness as deep as pitch. A dead book with spread-out boards lay cold and heavy in my grip. I tossed it upward, hearing it crash into the heather below. Burying my face in my hands, I sat in a paralysis of misery.

Yet the cold bit so fiercely that, fearing freezing, I forced myself upright. The moment my feet touched the ground, a faint sensation of light ignited within me. "Is it waking?" I cried, a surge of hope piercing through me. Alas, no! It was merely the edge of a moon peering up sharply over the flat horizon. It offered me illumination, but no direction. It refused to hover over me, to wait for my stumbling steps. All it could give me was an ignorant choice.

Her face swelled to fill the sky, and I began to discern my surroundings. Westward, not far away, a range of low hills cut across the skyline. I headed for them.

What a night I endured before reaching those slopes! The moon seemed to possess knowledge, for she gazed at me with an odd intensity. Her countenance was truly ice-cold, yet brimming with interest, or perhaps curiosity. This was not the moon I knew from Earth; her features were alien, and her light even stranger. Perhaps it emanated from an unknown sun! Every time I looked up, I found her staring at me with every ounce of her power. Initially, I was annoyed, chafed by the rudeness of a fellow creature, but soon I perceived—or imagined—a certain wondering pity in her gaze. Why was I wandering in her night? There, first, did I understand the horror of being awake in the cosmos: I WAS, and I could not stop it!

As I walked, my boots left the heather, treading a bare, spongy soil that resembled dry, powdery peat. To my dismay, it gave a sudden, jarring heave beneath me. Presently, I saw what appeared to be the ripple of an earthquake moving ahead of me, shadowy in the dim moonlight. It faded into the distance, but as I stared after it, a single wave rose and crept slowly toward me. A yard or two away, it burst, and from it, scrambling and leaping, emerged an animal resembling a tiger. Clumps of mold hung from his mouth and ears, and his eyes winked and flamed as he charged, baring white teeth in a silent snarl. I stood paralyzed, conscious of neither courage nor fear. He turned his head to the earth and plunged back into it.

"That moon is affecting my brain," I muttered, resuming my journey. "What life exists here but the phantasmic—the substance of dreams? I am indeed walking in a vain show!"

So I struggled to keep my heart above the waters of fear, unaware that the being I distrusted was actually my shield from the realities I mistook for illusions. Her light restrained the monsters; without it, I would scarcely have taken a second step on that hideous ground. "I will not be appalled by that which is merely seeming!" I told myself, yet it felt like a nightmare to walk upon a sea where such grotesque fish played below. Suddenly, a few paces from me, the head of a worm began to emerge slowly from the earth, massive as a polar bear's and bearing a striking resemblance to one, complete with a white mane atop its red neck. The wriggling draws with which its colossal length freed itself were horrifying, yet I dared not look away. As soon as its tail cleared the surface, it lay as if exhausted, thrashing weakly in a futile attempt to burrow again.

"Does it feed on the dead," I wondered, "and is it powerless to harm the living? If they smell their prey and emerge, why do they leave me unharmed?"

I now know it was the moon that paralyzed them.

Throughout the long night, as I walked, hideous creatures, none exactly alike, threatened me. In some, the beauty of color heightened the loathsomeness of their shapes; one large serpent was draped from head to distant tail with feathers of glorious, vibrant hues.

Eventually, I became so accustomed to these harmless threats that I began to entertain the road with inventions of monstrosities, never suspecting that I owed every breath of life to the staring moon. Though hers was not a primal radiance, it so hindered the evil things that I walked in safety. For light remains light, however faint the reflection. How quickly would not my feet have carried me across this restless soil if I had known that, should her lamp cease to shine on this cursed spot, I would instantly fall to the mercy of those who knew no mercy, becoming the center of a writhing heap of horrors, each individual as terrifying as it had merely seemed! A fool of ignorance, I watched the descent of the weary, solemn, anxious moon across the widening vault above me, troubled only by the dread of losing my way—where, as yet, I had no path to lose.

I drew near the hills I had chosen as my goal, and she was now close to their skyline, when the soundless wallowing ceased, revealing the burrow lying motionless and bare. Then I saw, walking slowly across the light soil, the form of a woman. A white mist floated around her, constantly shifting, dissolving, and reforming into garments as it gathered to her or blew away by a wind that trailed her steps.

She was beautiful, yet her countenance held such a mixture of pride and misery that I could hardly believe what I saw. Up and down she walked, vainly trying to seize the mist and wrap it around herself. The eyes in her beautiful face were dead, and on her left side was a dark spot against which she would now and then press her hand, as if stifling pain or sickness. Her hair hung nearly to her feet, and sometimes the wind mixed it so thoroughly with the mist that I could not distinguish one from the other; but when it fell and gathered again, it shone a pale gold in the moonlight.

Suddenly, pressing both hands to her heart, she collapsed to the ground, and the mist rose from her, melting into the air. I ran to her. But she began to writhe in such agony that I stood aghast. A moment later, her legs, tearing free from her body, sped away like serpents. From her shoulders fled her arms, also transforming into serpents in terror. Then something flew up from her like a bat, and when I looked again, she was gone. The ground rose like the sea in a storm; terror seized me. I turned toward the hills and ran.

I was already on the slope of their base when the moon sank behind one of the summits, plunging me into shadow. Behind me rose a wasting, sickening cry, like the lament of frustrated desire—it was the only sound I had heard since the fall of the dead butterfly. It shook my heart like a flag in the wind. I turned, seeing many dark objects bounding after me, and scrambled for the crest of a ridge where the moon still shone. She seemed to linger there, allowing me to see enough to defend myself. Soon I gained sight of her and climbed faster.

Crossing the shadow of a rock, I heard the creatures panting at my heels. Just as the foremost threw himself upon me with a snarl of greedy hate, we rushed directly into the moon. She flashed out an angry light, and he fell from me, reduced to a bodiless blotch. Strength surged through me, and I turned on the rest. One by one, as they darted into the light, they dropped with a howl. And I saw—or fancied I saw—a strange smile upon the round face above me.

I climbed to the top of the ridge; far away, the moon shone, sinking toward a low horizon. The air was pure and strong. I descended a little way, found it warmer, and sat down to wait for the dawn.

The moon dipped below, and the world fell dark once more.
