Our reader must consent—if only for a few miles—to shut the door upon the west, upon Clavering chatter and Fairoaks quiet, and to be tumbled, like a pat of butter in a churn, into London; that great kitchen of smoke and noise where reputations are stewed down, fortunes are whisked up, and young gentlemen are served, hot, to the public.

Arthur Pendennis took his seat on the “Alacrity,” bound once for all (so he vowed) to face the world and make his fortune. The coach went rattling away from the friendly gates of home, wheels drumming a steady tattoo on the road; and in that travelling music—hoof, harness, horn, and axle—Pen heard the beginning of his campaign. He lay awake in the night, seasoning his future with a hundred plans: prudence sliced thin, ambition laid on thick, repentance sprinkled like salt, and vanity—well, vanity was the sauce he always had within reach.

He had failed once, and the taste of it still lingered sharp on the tongue; yet it had not broken his appetite. He knew, as young men know, that he was better than many who had got the first servings. A year’s thought and mishap had made him feel older than when, twelve months ago, he had travelled this same road to and from Oxbridge, light as a cork and as empty.

His mind went back, with a tenderness that would have softened even a coffee-house chop, to the mother who had blessed him at parting: who trusted him still, loved him still, though he had given her small cause. Blessings on her, he prayed, looking up at the cold, bright stars—those quiet lamps in Heaven’s ceiling, burning without fuss, without smoke, without the clatter of London. O give him strength, he begged, to work, to endure, to be honest, to avoid temptation—to be worthy of that love which asked no questions and kept the hearth warm for him though he strayed.

And Laura’s love? He would have liked, in his best moments, to pack it into his portmanteau like a white handkerchief and carry it always. But she had refused it, because he was not worthy; and he confessed it with shame, and yet—since we are dealing with a young gentleman who can be sincere and selfish in one breath—he was glad to be free. “I am not good enough for such a creature,” he owned; and drew back from her spotless innocence as a prodigal draws back from a church he once entered without fear: not hating it, only feeling he has no right to that clean air.

So, while the coach flew through silent commons and by dark woods under calm skies, Pen vowed, and planned, and reproached himself, and congratulated himself, until the nipping dawn of October found him still awake. He slept at last, and woke with a traveller’s hungry refreshment when they stopped at the old breakfasting place at B——, where he had eaten a score of merry meals in boyhood—meals whose very memory brought back the clink of plates, the steam of coffee, the smell of toasted bread and damp greatcoats drying by the fire.

How a man’s courage rises with a warm room and a hot dish! The sun broke out brightly as they left; the pace was rapid; the horn blew; milestones slipped away like crumbs brushed from a table. Pen smoked, joked with the guard, exchanged merry words with passengers and roadside folk along that familiar ribbon of road. And as the traffic thickened, as the air grew heavier with the breath of the approaching city—coal smoke, wet stone, and that sour-sweet odour of crowds—his spirits rose until the last team of greys came out at H—— and the coach drove into London.

What young fellow has not felt that thrill—half hunger, half fear—on first entering the vast place? Hundreds of other carriages, stuffed with their thousands of men, hurried along as if the whole nation had overslept and was running for its breakfast. “Here is my place,” thought Pen; “here is my battle beginning. I must fight and conquer, or fall. I have been a boy and a dawdler. Oh, I long to show I can be a man!” And from the coach-roof he looked down upon the city with the eager longing of a young soldier hearing the first distant guns.

On the road he had made acquaintance with a cheery fellow-passenger in a shabby cloak, who talked incessantly of men of letters as though he had them all in his pantry and could bring them out at pleasure—Tom Campbell, Tom Hood, Sydney Smith—this one and that—each served up with a flourish, as if the speaker had carved their reputations himself. The gentleman was indeed a reporter for a London newspaper, returning from a great wrestling-match in the west; and his name, set down boldly on a card, was Mr. Doolan of the Star.

As they passed by Brompton, Mr. Doolan pointed out Mr. Hurtle the reviewer, walking with his umbrella. Pen craned over the coach like a boy trying to spy the cook through the kitchen door. He had not yet learned to distrust reviewers, editors, and authors; he believed in them as country people believe in London—thinking all its bread whiter and all its wit sharper. Even Wagg, whose books did not strike him as masterpieces, he secretly revered as a successful writer; and when Doolan told him that Wagg received three hundred pounds a volume, Pen began at once to reckon how quickly five thousand a year might be boiled out of paper and ink.

The very first acquaintance Pen met, as the coach pulled up at the Gloster Coffee-house, was his old friend Harry Foker, prancing down Arlington Street behind an enormous cab-horse. Foker had white kid gloves, white reins, and, as nature had lately taken to decoration, a considerable tuft on his chin. A very small cab-boy (Stoopid being retired from office) swung on behind the vehicle—knock-kneed, and imprisoned in the tightest leather breeches that ever took the joy out of a child.

Foker stared at the dusty coach and its smoking horses as if he were watching the roast come in. “What, Foker!” cried Pendennis.

“Hullo! Pen, my boy!” Foker waved his whip in salutation; and Pen’s heart warmed at the queer, kind face. Mr. Doolan, too, instantly enlarged his respect for Pen upon seeing that he had an acquaintance who commanded so grand a cab-horse. Pen, excited to be free and in London, asked Doolan to dine with him at the Covent Garden Coffee-house, where he put up. Off he rattled in a cab in the highest spirits, delighted to see the bustling waiter again, the polite bowing landlord, missing the old Boots as if he were a kinsman, and wishing to shake hands with the very furniture.

He had a hundred pounds in his pocket; which is an amount that makes a young man feel virtuous in advance. He dressed in his best, dined in the coffee-room with a modest pint of sherry—being determined, he told himself, to be very economical—and went to the theatre adjoining.

The lights, the music, the crowd, the gaiety—London serving up its first course—charmed and intoxicated him. The fiddles scraped, the audience buzzed, the painted scenery smelled faintly of lamp-oil and damp canvas; and beneath it all ran that great urban sound—laughter, coughing, foot-shuffling—like a tide against a sea-wall. Pen laughed at the jokes, applauded the songs with such honest freshness that the dreary old habitues in the boxes—those men who had long ago ceased to taste anything unless it were over-seasoned—looked at him almost with gratitude, as one looks at a young stomach enjoying what one’s own has grown tired of digesting.

At the end of the first piece he strutted about the lobbies, fancying himself in the very centre of fashion. What tired frequenter of London stones cannot remember his early delusions, and would not, if he could, bring them back—those first bright bites of the town, before the palate is blunted?

There was Foker again, ardent votary of pleasure; and with him Grandy Tiptoff of the Household Brigade (Lord Tiptoff’s brother) and Lord Colchicum, Captain Tiptoff’s uncle—a venerable peer who had been a man of pleasure ever since the first French Revolution and seemed resolved to outlive all reforms. Foker rushed upon Pen, insisted he should come into his private box, and there, like the chief dish in a supper-party, sat a lady with the longest ringlets and the fairest shoulders. This was Miss Blenkinsop, the eminent actress of high comedy; and in the back of the box, snoozing in a wig, sat old Blenkinsop, her papa—“the veteran Blenkinsop,” “the useful Blenkinsop,” “that old favourite of the public,”—he who played heavy fathers on the stage and, by all accounts, did not lay the part aside at home.

And while Pen, on his first London night, sat blinking at gaslight and beauty, it was about eleven o’clock at Fairoaks; Mrs. Pendennis had gone to bed, wondering whether her dearest Arthur was at rest after his journey. Laura, too, was awake. Yesterday night, as the coach rolled over silent commons and by cottage windows twinkling like little hearth-fires in the dark, Pen had been vowing to reform. Now the farce was going on very successfully, and Mrs. Leary, in a hussar jacket and braided pantaloons, was enchanting the audience with her archness, her figure, and her ballads—while London’s constant roar outside, wheels and cries and endless human stir, throbbed like a drum under the music, never stopping for anyone’s prayer.

Pen would have liked to listen; but the other people in the box did not care about her song or her pantaloons, and kept up an incessant chattering, as if talk were the only art worth paying for. Tiptoff knew where her maillots came from. Colchicum declared he had seen her when she came out in ’14. Miss Blenkinsop pronounced that she sang out of tune; which pained and astonished Pen, who thought Mrs. Leary as beautiful as an angel and as musical as a nightingale. When Hoppus came on as Sir Harcourt Featherby, the young man of the piece, the gentlemen declared Hoppus was getting stale; and Tiptoff proposed flinging Miss Blenkinsop’s bouquet to him.

“Not for the world,” cried Miss Blenkinsop. “Lord Colchicum gave it to me.”

Pen remembered that nobleman’s name; and, with bow and blush, said he believed he had to thank Lord Colchicum for proposing him at the Megatherium Club, at the request of his uncle Major Pendennis.

“What, you’re Wigsby’s nephew, are you?” said the peer. “I beg your pardon—we always call him Wigsby.” Pen blushed to hear his venerable uncle treated like a nickname. “We balloted you in last week, didn’t we? Yes, last Wednesday night. Your uncle wasn’t there.”

Delightful news! Pen poured out handsome thanks, which Lord Colchicum listened to with his double opera-glass up, as though examining a picture for flaws. Pen’s mind danced at the thought of being a member of that polite club—of belonging, at last, to the city’s inner dining-room.

“Don’t be always looking at that box, you naughty creature,” cried Miss Blenkinsop to one of the gentlemen.

“She’s a dev’lish fine woman, that Mirabel,” said Tiptoff; “though Mirabel was a d——d fool to marry her.”

“A stupid old spooney,” said the peer.

“Mirabel!” cried Pendennis.

“Ha! ha!” laughed Foker. “We’ve heard of her before, haven’t we, Pen?”

It was Pen’s first love. It was Miss Fotheringay—Emily—who, the year before, had been led to the altar by Sir Charles Mirabel, G.C.B., formerly envoy to the Court of Pumpernickel, busy at the Congress of Swammerdam, signer of the Peace of Pultusk, and wearer of a star and other decorations which glitter bravely even when sense does not.

“Emily was always as stupid as an owl,” said Miss Blenkinsop.

“Eh! Eh! pas si bête,” the old peer murmured.

“Oh, for shame!” cried the actress, who did not in the least know what he meant.

Pen looked out, beheld his first love again, and wondered—as a man who once burned his mouth on a foolishly hot pie wonders afterwards—how he ever could have been so eager.

Thus, on the very first night of his arrival in London, Mr. Arthur Pendennis found himself introduced to a club, to an actress of genteel comedy and a heavy father of the stage, and to a dashing society of jovial blades, old and young. For my Lord Colchicum, though bald, enfeebled, and stricken in years, was still indefatigable in the pursuit of enjoyment; and it was his boast that he could drink as much claret as the youngest member of any society he frequented. He lived with youth about town, gave them countless dinners at Richmond and Greenwich, and, being an enlightened patron of the drama in all languages and of the Terpsichorean art, entertained dramatic professors of all nations at his banquets—English from Covent Garden and the Strand, Italians from the Haymarket, French from their own pretty theatre, or from the Opera boards where they danced. And at his villa on the Thames, this pillar of the State gave sumptuous entertainments to scores of young men of fashion, who consorted affably with the greenroom—chiefly with the ladies, for Viscount Colchicum preferred their society as more polished and gay than that of their male brethren.

The next day Pen went and paid his entrance-money at the club—an operation which carried off exactly one-third of his hundred pounds, as neatly as a practised carver removes the choicest slice. He took possession of the edifice, ate luncheon there with immense satisfaction, plunged into an easy-chair in the library and tried to read all the magazines at once—like a glutton attacking a whole sideboard. He wondered whether the members were looking at him; and marvelled that they could dare to keep on their hats in such fine rooms. He sat down and wrote to Fairoaks on club paper, describing what a comfort the place would be after his day’s work was over.

Then, in compliance with his mother’s earnest desire, he went over to his uncle’s lodgings in Bury Street with some tremor. He was relieved to find the Major not yet returned to town. The apartments were blank; brown hollands covered the library-table; bills and letters lay on the mantelpiece, grimly awaiting their master. The Major, the landlady said, was on the Continent, at Badnbadn, with the Marquis of Steyne. Pen left his card upon the shelf with the rest—Fairoaks still written on it, as if he were a boy asking permission to come in.

When Major Pendennis returned to London—coming back in time for the November fogs, after which he proposed to spend Christmas with friends in the country—he found another card of Arthur’s; this one engraved, with an air of business, “Lamb Court, Temple,” and a note from Arthur and from Mrs. Pendennis stating that the young gentleman had come to town, entered a member of the Upper Temple, and was reading hard for the bar.

Lamb Court, Temple—where was it? The Major remembered ladies of fashion talking of dining with Mr. Ayliffe the barrister, who was “in society,” and lived there in the King’s Bench—of which prison there was probably a branch in the Temple; and Ayliffe might very likely be an officer. Mr. Deuceace, Lord Crabs’s son, had also lived there, he recollected. He despatched Morgan to find Lamb Court and report upon the lodging selected by Mr. Arthur. Morgan, who had tracked game much harder to find than a young gentleman with his name on a door, had little difficulty.

“What sort of a place is it, Morgan?” asked the Major next morning from out of the bed-curtains in Bury Street, while the valet arranged his toilette in the thick yellow fog.

“I should say rayther a shy place,” said Mr. Morgan. “The lawyers lives there and has their names on the doors. Mr. Harthur lives three pair high, sir. Mr. Warrington lives there too, sir.”

“Suffolk Warringtons! I shouldn’t wonder: a good family,” thought the Major. “Cadets of good families follow the robe. Comfortable rooms, eh?”

“Honly saw the outside of the door, sir—Mr. Warrington’s name and Mr. Arthur’s painted up, and a piece of paper with ‘Back at 6;’ but I couldn’t see no servant, sir.”

“Economical at any rate,” said the Major.

“Very, sir. Three pair, sir. Nasty black staircase as ever I see. Wonder how a gentleman can live in such a place.”

“Pray, who taught you where gentlemen should or should not live, Morgan? Mr. Arthur is going to study for the bar,” the Major said with dignity; and closed the subject, as one snaps down a dish-cover.

Left to himself, he mused more kindly. “Boys will be boys. He has written me a devilish good letter. Colchicum says he has dined with him and thinks him a gentlemanlike lad. His mother is one of the best creatures in the world. If he has sown his wild oats and will stick to business, he may do well yet. Think of Charley Mirabel, the old fool, marrying that flame of his! that Fotheringay! Gad, I’ll go and see him—hang me if I don’t.”

So, having learned from Morgan that a city omnibus would put him down at the gate, the Major one day after breakfast at his club—not the Polyanthus, where Mr. Pen had just been elected (the Major was too wise to have a nephew constantly under his own club roof), but another—entered one of those public vehicles and bade the conductor set him down at the Upper Temple.

It was about twelve when Major Pendennis reached that dingy portal. A civil personage with a badge and white apron directed him through dark alleys and under melancholy archways into courts each more dismal than the last, until at length he reached Lamb Court. If Pall Mall was dark, what was Lamb Court? Candles were burning in many rooms: in Mr. Hodgeman the special pleader’s pupil-room, where six pupils scribbled declarations under tallow; in Sir Hokey Walker’s clerk’s room, where the clerk—far more cheerful and gentlemanlike than the celebrated counsel—conversed patronisingly with an attorney’s managing clerk at the door; and in Curling the wigmaker’s melancholy shop, where, behind the feeble glimmer of two lights, large serpents’ wigs and judges’ wigs loomed drearily, blank blocks staring out at the lamp-post in the court as if waiting to be given faces and voices.

Two little clerks were playing toss-halfpenny under that lamp. A laundress in pattens clacked in at one door. A newspaper boy issued from another with a sharp cry that cut the fog like a knife through suet. A porter, his white apron faintly visible, paced up and down. Sound here did not sing; it dripped—muffled footsteps, distant coughs, the scrape of pens—everything smothered by old stone and stale air. The Major, who liked a world where conversation was crisp and claret clear, shuddered. “Good Ged!” he said. “The poor boy mustn’t live on here.”

The filthy oil-lamps that lit the Temple staircases by night were, of course, not lit by day; and the Major, having read with difficulty his nephew’s name under Mr. Warrington’s on the wall of No. 6, found still greater difficulty climbing the abominable black stairs. His gloved hand groped along banisters that contributed damp exudations to his kid. At last he reached the third story. A candle burned in the passage of one of the two sets of rooms; doors stood open; and the names of Mr. Warrington and Mr. A. Pendennis were clearly visible as he went in. An Irish charwoman with pail and broom opened to him.

“Is that the beer?” cried a great voice. “Give us hold of it.”

The gentleman speaking was seated on a table, unshorn and smoking a short pipe; in a farther chair sat Pen with a cigar, his legs near the fire. A little boy who acted as clerk grinned at the Major’s face, delighted at the notion of a gentleman of his appearance being mistaken for beer.

“Pen, my boy—it’s I—it’s your uncle,” the Major said, choking a little with the smoke; but as most young men of fashion used the weed, he pardoned it easily enough.

Mr. Warrington rose from the table; Pen rose too, perturbed from his chair.

“Beg your pardon for mistaking you,” said Warrington in a frank loud voice. “Will you take a cigar, sir? Clear those things off the chair, Pidgeon, and pull it round to the fire.”

Pen flung his cigar into the grate; and was pleased—more pleased than he cared to show—by the cordiality with which his uncle shook his hand. As soon as he could speak for the stairs and the smoke, the Major began to ask Pen kindly after himself and his mother; for blood is blood, and the sight of the boy softened him.

Pen gave his news, and then introduced Mr. Warrington—an old Boniface man—whose chambers he shared.

The Major was quite satisfied to hear that Mr. Warrington was a younger son of Sir Miles Warrington of Suffolk. He had served with an uncle of the Major’s in India and in New South Wales years ago.

“Took a sheep-farm there, sir, made a fortune—better thing than law or soldiering,” Warrington said. “Think I shall go there too.” And here the expected beer arrived in a tankard with a glass bottom; whereupon Warrington, laughing, supposed the Major would have none, and took a long deep draught himself, then wiped his wrist across his beard with immense satisfaction. He wore a ragged old shooting-jacket; his beard was bristly and blue; he drank beer like a coalheaver; and yet you could not but perceive he was a gentleman.

After sitting a minute or two, Warrington went out, leaving uncle and nephew together.

“Rough and ready, your chum seems,” the Major said. “Somewhat different from your dandy friends at Oxbridge.”

“Times are altered,” Arthur replied, blushing. “Warrington is only just called, and has no business, but he knows law pretty well; and until I can afford to read with a pleader, I use his books and get his help.”

“Is that one of the books?” the Major asked with a smile; for a French novel lay at the foot of Pen’s chair.

“This is not a working day, sir,” Pen said. “We were out very late at a party last night—at Lady Whiston’s,” he added, knowing his uncle’s weakness for the seasoning of great names. “Everybody in town was there except you, sir; Counts, Ambassadors, Turks, Stars and Garters—I don’t know who—it’s all in the paper—and my name too,” he said with great glee. “I met an old flame of mine there, sir,” he added with a laugh. “You know whom I mean—Lady Mirabel—to whom I was introduced over again. She shook hands and was gracious enough. I may thank you for being out of that scrape, sir. She presented me to the husband too—an old beau in a star and a blonde wig. He does not seem very wise. She has asked me to call on her, sir: and I may go now without any fear of losing my heart.”

“What, we have had some new loves, have we?” the Major asked in high good-humour.

“Some two or three,” Mr. Pen said, laughing. “But I don’t put on my grand sérieux any more, sir. That goes off after the first flame.”
