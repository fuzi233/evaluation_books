A purpose, forged in the silent heat of the afternoon and swelling within her like a tributary joining a mighty flood, drove Romola forward. It was not merely a resolve, but a compulsion of spirit. Ignoring the encroaching shadows of the streets and refusing the cautious escort of Maso, she crossed the bridge where the river lay black beneath the fading crimson of the sunset. With unerring speed, she sought the Old Palace. Whether she met her husband there mattered not; probabilities held no sway over her urgent need to unburden her soul. Blind to the corridors ahead, unaware of the specific turns she took through the pillared courts or up the broad staircases, she demanded an usher for the Gonfaloniere. She gave her name, begged for a private audience, and pressed onward.

Soon, the chamber opened to reveal the frescoed saints and the flicker of newly lit tapers. Before solitude could take hold, the door swung open and Bernardo del Nero entered. His white hair stood erect atop his silken *lucco*, a crown of dignity undimmed.

"Romola, my child, what is this?" he exclaimed, his voice thick with anxious surprise as he shut the door behind them.

She uncovered her head and moved toward him in silence. He laid a heavy hand upon her shoulder, pulling her slightly away so he might inspect her features. Her countenance was ravaged by exhaustion and prolonged torment; her hair hung loose and disordered. Yet, a fierce excitement burned in her eyes, a fire that had conquered physical weakness.

"What has he done?" Bernardo asked abruptly. "Speak, child. Tell me everything. Cast aside pride. I am your father."

"It is not concerning myself—it is nothing about me," Romola replied quickly. "Dearest godfather, it concerns you. I have heard whispers... some I dare not speak aloud. But you stand in peril within these walls, and in peril everywhere. Fanatics seek to harm you, and there are traitors among us. Trust no one. To trust is to invite betrayal."

Bernardo smiled, a faint, weary curve of his lips.

"Have you driven yourself to this feverish state, my poor child," he said, lifting a hand to stroke her hair gently, "to repeat such ancient, worn truths to an old man like me?"

"Oh, no! They are not old truths," she insisted, pressing her clasped hands together with painful intensity, as if the gesture could seal the forbidden words inside. "These are fresh horrors I have known, yet I cannot articulate them. Dearest godfather, you know I am no fool. Why should I come to you without cause? Is it too late to warn you against anyone, *everyone* who appears to serve your interests? Is it too late to cry out, 'Flee to your villa, hide in the countryside before these final three days of office end'? Oh, God! Perhaps it is too late! And if any harm falls upon you, it will seem as though I have struck the blow!"

The final words escaped her involuntarily, a long-suppressed emotion bursting forth in a spasmodic gasp. Immediately, she was startled by her own vehemence and arrested herself.

"I mean," she stammered, "I know nothing concrete. I only know what fills me with terror."

"Poor child!" Bernardo murmured, gazing at her with a quiet, piercing depth for a fleeting moment. Then he spoke softly. "Go, Romola—return home and rest. These fears may be but large, ugly shadows cast by something trivial and harmless. Even traitors require motive; the rats will run only where they smell the cheese, and we know not yet from which direction the scent drifts."

He paused, turning his gaze away with an air of abstraction, until, with a slow shrug, he added, "As for warnings, they avail me little, child. I enter into no plots, nor do I ever forsake my colors. If I march alongside obstinate men who charge blindly at guns and pikes, I must share their consequences. Let us say no more. I have not many years left in my sack for them to steal away. Go, child; return and find peace."

Once more he placed his hand upon her head, caressing her. She could not resist clinging to his arm, pressing her brow against his shoulder. This godfather's affection felt like the final remnant of that youthful filial life which now appeared so radiant to her, even amidst its troubles, for those struggles remained unstained by malice.

"Is silence best, my Romola?" the old man asked.

"Yes, for now; yet I cannot know if it will remain so forever," she answered, hesitating, her eyes lifting in an appeal.

"Well, you possess a father's ear while I breathe, and a father's home," he said, lifting the black drapery to fold it tenderly around her head. "Remember that." Opening the door, he gestured for her departure. "Hasten away. You resemble a black specter, but you will be safe enough."

That night, when Romola finally succumbed to sleep, it was profound and deep. Agitation had reached its zenith; she required strength before she could endure further suffering. Despite her rigorous habits, she slumbered well past sunrise.

Awakening, she was greeted by the thunderous roar of cannon. Piero de' Medici, backed by thirteen hundred men, stood before the gate facing Rome.

Maso, returning with many details of dubious reliability, informed her of the situation. A countryman had arrived before dawn to alarm the Signoria; otherwise, the city would have fallen to surprise. His master was absent, having been summoned to the Palazzo long ago. Romola dispatched the old man once more to gather intelligence while she climbed repeatedly to the loggia, straining her eyes to detect either the dread advance or signs of a successful repulse. Maso reported that the great Piazza swarmed with armed men, and that many leading citizens, suspected of sympathy with the Medici, had been summoned and detained. Some of the populace seemed indifferent to Piero's entry, even whispering that the Signoria itself had invited him; others claimed the soldiers from Pisa were arrayed against him.

In the memory of those morning hours, few things stood out to Romola as distinct external realities rising clearly above the tumultuous waves of retrospect and anticipation. She knew she had walked to the Badia at the appointed hour despite the street alarms, and she knew she had waited there in vain. However, the scene she had witnessed emerging from the church, standing on the steps as the doors closed behind her for the afternoon recess, returned to her mind with the vivid clarity of a remembered dream.

A transformation had passed over the faces and tones of both armed and unarmed folk lingering or hurrying through the streets. Though the guns fired again, the noise now provoked laughter rather than fear. She soon grasped the cause of this shift. Piero de' Medici and his horsemen had turned their backs on Florence, galloping with desperate speed along the road to Siena. This truth came from a substantial shopkeeper, a *Piagnone* who had not yet lowered his pike.

"It is true," he concluded, his emphasis carrying a bitter edge. "Piero is gone, but those who remained behind were privy to his coming—we all know that. If the new Signoria fulfills its duty, we shall soon learn who they were."

Like a sharp spasm, these words pierced through Romola. Yet the evil they foreshadowed was not yet immediate. As she entered her home again, her most pressing anxiety shifted to the terrifying possibility that she had lost sight of Baldassarre for a long time.
