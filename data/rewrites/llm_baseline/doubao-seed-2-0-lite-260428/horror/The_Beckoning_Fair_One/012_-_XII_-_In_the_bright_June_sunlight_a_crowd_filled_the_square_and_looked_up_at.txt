The June sun blazed white-hot over the cobblestone square, gilding the tops of the crowd’s bowed, craning heads as every pair of eyes fixed on the old red brick house at the alley’s end. Its facade was pocked with antique dented fire-insurance marks that looked like old, faded bruises, and the wooden “To Let” notice boards hung over the top of the surrounding paling like sharp, axe-edged choppers, glinting threateningly in the light. Two constables planted their boots in front of the broken alley gate, chests puffed to hold the gawkers back.
The women clustered at the throng’s fringes, shifting every few steps to angle for a better view of the house’s drawn, blood-red blinds, their voices low, breathless whispers that never carried far enough for anyone else to overhear. Not a single child was out in the square; every front door stood shut, and you could hear faint, muffled curious cries from behind them, smothered by mothers’ hands over small mouths.
A sharp-nosed man named Barrett had a knot of listeners huddled around him, reeling off his story for the dozenth time, his tone thick with self-righteous drama, while a tiny, wide-eyed fat man darted from group to group trying to steal his audience, rambling about a key no one would stop to listen to him about.
“Revealed to me that something was very wrong that very afternoon, it was,” Barrett was saying, chest puffed. “I was standing right where Constable Saunders is now, minding my own business, when they came out that gate. No fooling me, no. I saw her face… white as bleached writing paper, just like the woman our Lord told to go and sin no more. No lying to me! I marched straight home to Jane, and I said ‘Jane, this stops right now. We’re commanded to shun evil, and this ends today. You don’t set foot in that house again.’ And what did she say? ‘John, it’s four-and-sixpence a week for the cleaning.’ I told her if it was forty-six thousand pounds it wouldn’t be worth it. She hasn’t crossed that gate from that day to this.”
A short, strained silence fell. “Did your Jane… ever see anything queer, then?” someone asked, half-hesitant.
Barrett rounded on him, face austere. “What Mrs. Barrett did or did not see stays with her. The Good Book says to keep thy tongue from speaking ill, and I abide by that.”
Another man cut in before anyone could press him further. “That poor actor fellow Oleron was three sheets to the wind in the Waggon and Horses the night before last, weren’t he?”
“Right miserable state he was in. Sat in the bar alone, wouldn’t stand anyone a drink. Ranting to himself about walls breathing.”
The fat, jittery man lunged forward again, hands waving. “She got that key from me! Knew the number off by heart, she did, came into my shop on a Tuesday evening—”
No one paid him any mind.
“Shut your traps,” a gruff labourer rumbled, nodding towards two men in peaked inspector caps pushing through the crowd. “She still ain’t turned up. Now we’ll get the truth.”
The inspectors conferred briefly with the two constables posted at the gate, while the fat man scrambled to their side, spitting his story so fast the words tripped over each other. “I remember the key number! Three ones and three threes, 111333! She bought it off me, I swear it!” One of the inspectors brushed him gently aside.
“Nobody’s gone in the house?”
“No, sir.”
“Right. Brackley, you come with us. Smith, you stay on the gate. The rest of the squad’s five minutes out.”
The three men tramped down the narrow, sun-dappled alley and pushed through the house’s front door. They climbed the wide oak staircase, its carvings crusted with decades of grime, and the lead inspector kicked aside a drift of dead brown leaves and yellowed half-written manuscript pages piled right outside Oleron’s bedroom door.
“Looks like he’s not been sweeping up much lately. Don’t bother knocking. Break one of the glass panels, Brackley.”
A sharp crack of breaking glass echoed through the empty halls. Brackley thrust his arm through the shattered pane, fumbled the latch open, and the second they stepped across the threshold both men choked.
“Christ, that stench—like a closed hearse that’s been sitting in the sun. Flung all the windows open, quick, let the light in.”
Out in the square, the crowd saw the red blinds go whipping up and every sash of the old house’s windows thrown wide. The inspector leaned out, gasping for fresh air. “Simms, you check the rest of the house, I’ll tend to the man on the bed.”
They hauled the bedroom blind up next, and Oleron, gaunt, waxy-white, half starved, threw a shaking hand over his eyes to shield them from the brutal June sunlight that felt like needles stabbing straight into his skull. His hearing felt stuffed with cotton; he could barely process the two uniformed men leaning over him, asking over and over where Elsie Bengough was, the woman everyone knew as Miss Fair One. He only shook his head, too foggy to speak.
“Not getting anything out of him right now. Get him on his feet, easy, careful with him. I’m going to go check the kitchen.”
The second inspector tramped through Oleron’s study, found nothing out of the ordinary at first, then pushed open the kitchen door. His boot sank into ankle-deep muck of rotting vegetable scraps and slimy, blackened dead flower petals that covered the whole floor. The kitchen window had no blind, blocked out almost entirely by the blank brick wall of the house across the alley, leaving the room dim even in full midday sun. At first glance it looked empty—until he spotted a shuffling, smeared track through the refuse, leading straight to a corner cupboard.
Set high in the cupboard door was a small square sliding hatch, running on old wooden runners. The door itself was pulled shut. He reached for the tiny brass knob, slid the hatch open, and stumbled back a full step, his breath catching in his throat.
Framed in the little square opening, bulging so large it jammed halfway out, was a thing that looked exactly like a misshapen, overstuffed plum pudding, wrapped tight in a bag of faded, browny-red frieze. A dark, sticky stain seeped through the cloth. He couldn’t bring himself to touch it, to shove it back through the hatch with his bare hands. Instead he twisted the main cupboard door’s handle and pulled. The weight behind it was impossible, so heavy he could only heave it open four inches before he had to shove his whole shoulder to the wood to force it closed again. A triangle of Elsie’s old black-and-white check apron skirt caught in the door crack, sticking out a few inches above the floor.
He stumbled out into the front hall, calling out. “We’ve got her.”
Oleron was half dressed by then, still holding his hands over his eyes, his brain too muddled to make sense of any of it. He couldn’t understand the piles of dead flowers everywhere, couldn’t understand why policemen were tramping through his house, why one of them was yelling for a four-wheeler cab and a stretcher to be sent for. He could hear heavy thuds from the kitchen, like something big was being dragged across the floor.
“What’s happening?” he mumbled, half asleep.
A murmur rose up from the square outside, and the clatter of cab wheels on the cobblestones. An officer leaned over him, whispering something about his statement being used as evidence, and before he knew it they were lifting him to his feet, guiding him down the stairs. The crowd outside roared, angry, baying for blood, and he caught sight of the little fat key-seller babbling his story into a constable’s notebook, repeating the key number 111333 over and over.
The two inspectors flanked him as they walked him to the cab at the alley gate, trying to block his view of what was being carried out behind him—but they were too slow. Oleron caught a flash of a hooded canvas stretcher, bulging far too lumpy, far too big for a normal human body, being trundled out of the house. A stone whizzed through the air and thudded against the back of his cab.
The inspector climbed in after him, pressing his back to the window facing the crowd to shield Oleron from the flying debris. Through the open cab door, Oleron could see the edges of those wooden chopper-sharp “To Let” boards sticking up through the privet hedge, one of them still painted with the notice that the key was kept at Number Six.
All at once the roar of the crowd went dead quiet. Slow, shuffling footsteps echoed down the alley, and the second inspector climbed into the cab, slamming the door shut and pressing his back to the other window to block it off. Trapped between the two uniformed men, Oleron’s eyelids slid shut, and he drifted off into a heavy, dreamless sleep as the cab trundled out of the square. The other wagon, the one carrying the stretcher, turned and trundled up the hill, towards the mortuary.
