The rebels outnumbered us fifteen to one, but numbers alone do not win battles. They were untrained, poorly armed, and leaderless—a rabble bound by ideals but shattered by panic. When the first wave of their assault faltered, they broke like driftwood against stone.

We did not give them time to regroup. My men and I struck fast, turning flanks, carving through their disordered ranks. Fear spread like wildfire. Those on the edges fled first, scattering into the forests, and soon the rest followed, their defiance crumbling. It was not a glorious fight, nor a fair one—but it was decisive.

By nightfall, the field was ours. We camped among ruined houses, turning them into a makeshift fort. The rebels had left behind more than their dead; a herd of panicked horses blundered into our lines, and we butchered five for supper. Fires crackled, wounded men tended their injuries, and my officers dissected the day’s maneuvers with the relish of soldiers who had cheated death once more.

But I dreaded the silence that would come when the talk died. Sleep brought dreams I did not wish to face. So I kept the conversation alive—until a sentry interrupted.

“My lord,” he said, “a woman approaches. She refuses to give her name or business. Only demands to see you.”

Suspicion prickled. This was no place for a lone traveler. I followed the sentry to the perimeter, where torchlight revealed a cloaked figure struggling against the guards.

“Release her,” I ordered.

The moment they obeyed, I knew her. Even swathed in fabric, the curve of her stance was unmistakable.

“Ylga,” I said.

She exhaled sharply. “You recognize me so easily? Perhaps there is hope yet.”

I led her beyond earshot. “What brings you here?”

“News you won’t relish.” Her voice was bitter. “Phorenice has cast you aside. She knows of Nais—knows you used your priest’s arts to preserve her in slumber. And now her jealousy has fangs.”

A cold weight settled in my gut. “What does she plan?”

Ylga’s laugh was jagged. “Nothing quick. She wants you crippled, not dead. A hand and foot lopped off, then cast into the wilds to be hunted like a beast. And all while Nais remains her plaything, locked in stone.”

I clenched my fists. “Then I will not return.”

“Wise,” she said. “But you’ll need more than wisdom to survive. Tob—the old sea captain—sails tonight. He’s gathering what’s left of Atlantis’ desperate and disillusioned. I struck a bargain: passage for you, if you reach his ship before dawn.”

“And where does Tob think he’s going?”

“Some forgotten islands north of Europe. He calls them the Tin Isles. Thinks he’ll be king there.” She hesitated. “I could come with you.”

I shook my head. “It’s no place for you.”

Her eyes flashed. “You think I fear hardship? I’ve shot bears and sewn hides. I’m not some pampered courtier.”

“It’s not fear that stops me. It’s loyalty.”

“To a woman who sleeps a century?” She stepped close, her breath warm against my cheek. “Then take this as farewell.”

Her lips pressed to mine—fierce, fleeting. Before I could react, she wrenched away and vanished into the trees.

---

The ship was a creaking relic, its hull worm-eaten, its crew a mix of seasoned sailors and hollow-eyed refugees. Tob greeted me with a sailor’s bluntness.

“We’re short on time and long on risk,” he said. “Phorenice’s patrols won’t stay blind forever.”

As the anchors lifted, I stared back at the fading lights of Atlantis. The city that had birthed empires was now a pyre of tyranny.

Tob clapped my shoulder. “Cheer up. The Tin Isles may be raw, but they’re ours. No Phorenice there.”

I said nothing. My path was set—but not toward his promised kingdom.

When the coast was a smudge on the horizon, I found him at the helm. “Drop me on the European shore,” I said.

He scowled. “You’d rather face barbarians than be king?”

“I have unfinished business.”

Tob spat over the rail. “Stubborn as ever. Fine. But if you die, don’t haunt me.”

The ship turned north. Behind us, Atlantis burned. Ahead lay the unknown.

And I would meet it on my own terms.
