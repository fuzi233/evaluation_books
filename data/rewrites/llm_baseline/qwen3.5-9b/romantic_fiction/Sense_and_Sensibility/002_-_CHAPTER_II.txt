Mrs. John Dashwood now settled into her role as the mistress of Norland Park, relegating her mother and her sisters-in-law to the status of mere visitors. Yet, despite this shift in hierarchy, she treated them with a quiet, surface-level civility, while her husband offered them a kindness reserved for anyone other than himself, his wife, or their infant son. He earnestly urged them to regard Norland as their permanent home. Since no alternative seemed more suitable to Mrs. Dashwood than staying put until she could find a residence nearby, she graciously accepted his invitation. Remaining in a place saturated with memories of past joy was precisely what soothed her spirit. In moments of mirth, her temperament was as buoyant as any, brimming with that sanguine anticipation of happiness that often *is* happiness itself. Conversely, in times of sorrow, she was swept away by her imagination just as easily, finding herself as distant from consolation as she was from alloy in her pleasures.

However, Mrs. John Dashwood harbored deep reservations regarding her husband's intention to aid his sisters. To withdraw three thousand pounds from the inheritance of their beloved little boy would, in her view, render him destitute to a terrifying degree. She implored him to reconsider. How could he reconcile robbing his sole offspring of such a substantial sum? Furthermore, what conceivable claim did the Miss Dashwoods hold against his generosity? They were related to him only by half-blood, a connection she deemed virtually non-existent. It was common knowledge that no affection was presumed to exist between children born of different marriages; thus, why should he ruin himself and their poor little Harry by squandering all his wealth on his "half-sisters"?

"It was my father's final request," her husband replied solemnly, "that I should assist his widow and daughters."

"He was likely speaking in a fog," she countered sharply. "Ten to one he was light-headed at the time. Had he possessed his faculties, he could not possibly have imagined asking you to give away half your fortune from your own child."

"He made no specific stipulation for a sum, my dear Fanny," he explained gently. "He merely asked, in general terms, that I assist them and improve their circumstances as far as my ability allowed. Perhaps it would have been wiser had he left the matter entirely to my discretion; he could hardly have anticipated that I would neglect them. Nevertheless, since he secured my promise, I felt bound to fulfill it. Something must be arranged for them once they depart Norland and establish a new household."

"Very well, then," she conceded reluctantly, "let something be done. But let us agree that 'something' does not necessitate three thousand pounds. Consider, my dear, that once money is parted with, it returns to no one. Your sisters will inevitably marry, and the funds will vanish forever. Unless..."

"Why, certainly," interrupted her husband, with grave deliberation, "the outcome would differ vastly if the sum remained with our poor little boy. There may come a time when Harry regrets having surrendered such a large portion. Should he eventually raise a numerous family, for instance, it would prove a remarkably convenient addition to their resources."

"To be sure, it would."

"Perhaps, then, it would serve all parties better if we reduced the sum by half. Five hundred pounds would constitute a prodigious windfall for their fortunes!"

"Oh! Beyond anything great!" she exclaimed. "What brother on earth would offer half so much to his sisters, even if they were truly his sisters? And considering they are only half-related—"

"But you possess such a generous spirit!" he interjected warmly.

"I would not wish to act meanly," he continued. "On such matters, one prefers to err on the side of excess rather than deficiency. Surely no one believes I have fallen short; indeed, even they could hardly expect more."

"There is no predicting what *they* might expect," the lady remarked coolly. "Yet, we are not concerned with their hopes, but with what you can realistically afford."

"Certainly. And I believe I can afford to grant them five hundred pounds apiece. As it stands, without my contribution, they will each inherit approximately three thousand pounds upon their mother's passing—a very respectable fortune for any young woman."

"To be sure," she agreed. "In fact, it strikes me that they require no augmentation whatsoever. With ten thousand pounds divided among them, they will assuredly fare well if married, or live comfortably together on the interest if unmarried."

"That is accurate," Mr. Dashwood admitted. "Therefore, I fail to see why it would be more prudent to provide for their mother during her lifetime rather than for the daughters directly—I refer specifically to an annuity. A hundred pounds a year would render them all perfectly content."

His wife paused, weighing the proposal. "Admittedly," she said slowly, "it is preferable to parting with fifteen hundred pounds outright. However, if Mrs. Dashwood survives fifteen years, we shall be thoroughly ensnared."

"Fifteen years! My dear Fanny, her life is unlikely to extend even half that duration."

"Certainly not; yet, observe, people invariably seem immortal when an annuity is involved. She is robust, healthy, and barely forty. An annuity is a binding commitment; it recurs annually, offering no escape. You underestimate the gravity of such an arrangement. I have witnessed firsthand the turmoil annuities can cause. My mother was burdened by similar obligations to three elderly servants via my father's will. It was exhausting. Payments were due twice yearly, requiring constant travel to deliver the funds. Then came the deception: one servant would be reported dead, only to resurface later. My mother grew utterly weary of it. She lamented that her income was no longer her own, shackled by perpetual claims. It was particularly cruel, she noted, because otherwise, the funds would have been entirely at her disposal without restriction. Consequently, I harbor such an aversion to annuities that I would not accept one under any circumstance."

"It is undeniably distressing," Mr. Dashwood concurred, "to face such annual drains on one's income. As your mother rightly observed, one's fortune ceases to be truly one's own. Being tethered to the rigid schedule of payments, devoid of flexibility, strips away one's independence."

"Exactly. Moreover, there is rarely gratitude to be gained. They assume security; you perform merely what is expected, eliciting no real thankfulness. If I were you, I would exercise complete discretion. I would not bind myself to a yearly allowance. It may become inconvenient in certain years to spare a hundred, or even fifty pounds from our own necessities."

"I believe you are correct, my love," he yielded. "It is superior to provide occasional gifts rather than a fixed annuity. Were they assured of a steady income, they would simply elevate their standard of living, gaining no actual financial advantage by year's end. A sporadic gift of fifty pounds prevents immediate distress and, I trust, sufficiently honors my father's wish."

"To be sure," she affirmed. "Frankly, I am convinced your father never envisioned you providing significant sums. The assistance he contemplated was likely limited to reasonable neighborly duties: securing a modest cottage, aiding with moving, and sending seasonal provisions like fish and game. I wager my life he intended nothing further; it would indeed be bizarre and unreasonable if he did. Consider, my dear Mr. Dashwood, how comfortably your mother-in-law and her daughters can subsist on the interest of seven thousand pounds, plus the five hundred pounds generated by the individual legacies of the girls (fifty pounds each). Altogether, they command five hundred pounds annually. What earthly necessity could four women have beyond that? They will live frugally. No carriages, no horses, perhaps few servants, no social engagements, and consequently, no expenses. Imagine their comfort! Five hundred a year! I cannot fathom how they would spend half of it. To suggest you give more is absurd. They will be far more capable of providing for *you*."

"Upon my word," Mr. Dashwood declared, "I believe you are entirely correct. My father surely meant nothing more than you describe. I now fully comprehend his intent, and I shall strictly fulfill my engagement through the acts of kindness you have outlined. Once your mother relocates, I shall gladly assist in accommodating her to the best of my ability. Additionally, a modest gift of furniture may prove welcome."

"Certainly," Mrs. John Dashwood replied. "However, one crucial detail must be addressed. When your parents moved to Norland, although the Stanhill furniture was sold, all the china, silverware, and linens were preserved and left to your mother. Thus, her new home will be nearly fully furnished immediately upon her arrival."

"That is a significant factor," he acknowledged. "A valuable legacy indeed! Though, some of the silverware would have been a delightful addition to our own collection here."

"Yes; and the breakfast service alone is twice as exquisite as ours. Far too ornate, in my humble opinion, for any establishment they can ever afford. Nevertheless, such is the case. Your father thought solely of *them*. I must add, however, that you owe neither special gratitude nor strict adherence to his wishes. We both know that if he had possessed the means, he would have bequeathed almost everything in the world to them."

This argument proved irrefutable. It supplied the decisive resolve lacking previously, leading him to conclude that it would be not only unnecessary but potentially indecorous to provide the widow and her children with more than the neighborly gestures suggested by his wife.
