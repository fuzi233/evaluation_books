Supper time had fallen soft over Eli’s cottage, the hearth fire spitting golden light across the scrubbed oak table heaped with smoking earthen dishes. Every soul of the household was gathered round the board save Margaret, and to Catherine’s utter surprise, Eli leaned back in his oaken settle and announced he would wait a while for her.
“Why, I told you not to linger for the duke himself, no less,” Catherine fussed, tucking a stray strand of hair back under her cap.
“Ah, she is no duke,” Eli rumbled. “She is a good lass, poor thing, that hath waited not mere minutes, but long years, for that graceless son of mine. You can set all the meat out just the same—then we may fall to without wasting a second more the moment she steps through the door.”
The savour of spiced barley stew and roasted pork wrapped in herbs drifted thick across the room, and at last Eli’s good intentions crumbled. “She will come running the moment we set knives to board, you mark my word—they always do,” he said, waving a hand to their guest. “Come, sit ye down, Mistress Joan! You are no servant here, but an honoured guest. There—hark, that’s a quick step on the cobbles! Off with the covers, and fall to.”
The silver lids were whisked off the dishes, every man’s knife raised to carve, and the figure that burst through the door was no soft-faced Margaret. It was a Dominican friar, his black habit flapping, his face livid with unholy rage.
He cleared the distance to the table in one long stride, leaned his tall frame across its narrow width, and hovered both hands over the cowering heads of Cornelis and Sybrandt like eagles swooping down on a trapped quarry. He cursed them by name, soul and body, in this world and the world to come. That was an age when men wove curses as vivid as any sermon, but this one was so full, so minute, so blighting and searing in its wrath, no full transcript could be set to paper without turning the reader’s blood cold.
“Cursed be the lips,” he shrieked, “that spoke the lie that Margaret was dead! May they rot on your faces before the grave claims you, and burn on kisses of white-hot iron in hell for all eternity! Doubly cursed be the hands that tampered with my letters, that hid the truth from two hearts that loved true—may the hangman’s knife strike them off at the wrist, that they may handle nothing but hell’s unquenchable fire forever! Thrice accursed be the cruel, coward hearts that conceived that damned lie to tear true love apart! May you sicken and wither on this earth, joyless, loveless, hopeless, waste away to dust long before your allotted years, and burn for all time in the fires below!”
He cursed the food at their very mouths, cursed every hair on their heads, every bone, every drop of blood, down to the soles of their trembling feet. The two brothers cringed so far down they were half hidden beneath the table, and the friar tore a crumpled letter from the bosom of his habit and flung it hard at Eli’s chest.
“Read that, hard old man—you that locked your own son away, read it and see what monsters you have reared under your own roof! The memory of my wrongs, and hers, shall hang heavy on all of you for every day you draw breath. I will meet you all again on judgment day. On this earth, you shall never see my face again.”
He was gone as swiftly as he had come, vanishing out the door and down the lane before a single person could move to speak, leaving the whole family stiff and cold and white as carved statues huddled around their untouched, smoking supper.
It was this frozen, eerie sight that greeted Margaret and Jorian as they stepped over the threshold. Pale, motionless figures clustered around untasted food, exactly as old Eastern poets wrote of folks turned to stone mid-meal by a curse. Margaret’s eyes darted round the room, and she gasped, pressing a hand to her chest.
“Oh joy! All are whole, no blood has been spilled. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he did not strike you dead.”
Catherine let out a sharp, sobbing scream the moment she saw her, then turned her face away. But Eli had just scanned the false, tampered letter the friar had thrown down, every thread of the long, cruel deceit clicking sharp in his mind. Seeing Margaret, the other wronged victim, standing there pale and weary with all her years of grief written on her face, he roared out in a white heat of rage.
“Stand clear! Let me get at those traitors. I will hang for them, I swear I will!”
He whipped out the short iron sword he kept at his belt, and lunged for the two brothers.
“Fly!” Margaret shrieked. “Fly for your lives!”
Cornelis and Sybrandt scrambled howling across the floor, slipped under the table, and crawled out the far side. But before they could reach the door, the furious old man circled the table and cut off their escape. Catherine could only scream and wring her hands, quite useless in the face of such violence, and blood would surely have spilled that night if Margaret and Jorian had not thrown their full weight against Eli’s arms, holding him fast till the two rascals stumbled out the open door and fled down the lane. Only then did they release him, and he stormed out after them, raging and shaking his sword at the dark empty street long after they had vanished over the horizon.
When he trundled back home, he hacked down the wooden board nailed to his outer wall that bore the two sons’ names, and flung it straight into the roaring hearth. Catherine sat huddled in her chair, rocking back and forth, her apron pulled over her face. Joan had hurried to her husband’s side. Margaret had her arms wrapped tight round Catherine’s neck, pale and panting, doing all she could to soothe her.
But there was no soothing her that night. “Oh, my poor, lost children!” Catherine wailed between sobs. “Oh, miserable mother that I am! It is a mercy our Kate is laid ill upstairs. I thank God for that now, for it would have killed her to see all this. He would have done far better to stay in Italy, than come home to curse his own flesh and blood and turn this whole house upside down.”
“Hold your silly chatter, woman!” Eli snapped at her, sharp with anger. “You still take the side of the evil-doers! You got exactly what you deserved—your soft foolishness is what made those rogues what they are. I was all for correcting them hard when they were lads: sore ills need sharp remedies, but you always took their part, undermined me, and baffled all my wise severity. And you, Margaret, leave off comforting her—she ought to be comforting you, for what is her hurt compared to yours? She never had a grain of true justice in her bones, and never will. Come to me now. From this hour, I am your father.”
It was no request, it was a gentle command. Margaret kissed Catherine’s wet cheek, tottered over to him, and he set her down on a chair right beside his own. She laid her weary, feeble head on his broad, honest breast, and not a tear fell—her grief was too deep, too long held back, for tears. “Poor lamb,” he murmured, stroking her hair.
After a little while, Eli cleared his throat, and spoke in a rough, broken voice to Jorian and Joan. “Good folks, you can see we are in a bit of trouble here. But that is no reason you should go hungry. For our Lady’s sake, sit and fall to your supper! Do not add to my grief the shame of being thought a churlish host.” He gave a sudden, ghastly attempt at a hearty laugh. “The more knaves I have the luck to be rid of, the more I need good, true folk like you to help me clear these dishes, and cheer my eyes with honest faces where there ought to be nothing but gaps. Fall to, I entreat you.”
Catherine, still sobbing, added her own plea to his. These were poor, simple, hospitable old souls, and Jorian—whose appetite had been ravenous ever since his recent illness—was all ready to tuck in, till Joan leaned over and whispered a word in his ear. He drew back at once.
“Nay,” he said, shaking his head. “I will touch no meat that a man of Holy Church has laid his curse upon.”
“Ah, that is right, I forgot,” Eli said, apologetic. “My own son, that was reared at my very table, has cursed my victuals. A strange thing, that. Well, what God wills, man must bow to.”
The whole untouched supper was carried out and dumped in the yard for the strays and the pigs. Jorian and Joan walked home through the gathering dark, and heavy, unbroken sadness hung over Eli’s cottage for the rest of that night.
Where was Clement, meanwhile? He lay stretched full length on the cold stone floor of the convent church, his lips pressed to the lowest step of the altar, caught in a tangle of terror, utter misery, penitence, and self-abasement—with tiny, bright threads of joy shining through it all, the knowledge that Margaret was alive, after all those years of grief.
Night fell, and found him still lying there, weeping and praying. Morning would have found him there too, if he had not jolted suddenly awake, horrified to realise that in the heat of his own rage, and his own grief, he had committed yet another sin: he had neglected a dying parishioner that had sent for him hours earlier.
He stumbled to his feet, groaning at his own accumulated wickedness, and set out at once to repair his mistake. The weather had turned, rain lashing hard at his face, and when he had left the town behind him, he heard the wolves baying, out hunting on the moor.
