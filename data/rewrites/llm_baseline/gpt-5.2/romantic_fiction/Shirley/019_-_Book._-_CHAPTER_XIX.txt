Dusk had come softly down on Briarfield, as if laid like a cool cloth over a heated brow. The air, washed clean by no rain but by the day’s own sinking, grew so clear that the first stars pricked through it easily—pin-bright points in a sky still faintly pearl where the west would not quite let go.

“There will be just light enough to show me the way home,” said Miss Keeldar at the rectory garden door, settling her shawl as one might settle courage on the shoulders.

“You must not go alone, Shirley; Fanny shall accompany you.”

“That she shall not. In my own parish—what am I to fear? I would walk from Fieldhead to the church any fine midsummer night, three hours later than this, for the mere pleasure of seeing the stars—and the chance of meeting a fairy.”

“But only wait till the crowd is cleared away.”

“Agreed. There go the five Misses Armitage, streaming like a flock. And here comes Mrs. Sykes’s phaeton—Mr. Wynne’s close carriage—Mrs. Birtwhistle’s. I do not wish to go through the ceremony of bidding them all good-bye. Let us step into the garden and take shelter amongst the laburnums for an instant.”

They slipped back among blossoms and drooping gold, letting the polite commotion spend itself. The church porch emptied: rectors, curates, churchwardens; handshakes, praises for speeches, anxious cautions against the night air. Wheels crunched, harness jingled, voices receded. The village, that had been so full of words, began to empty into distance.

Miss Keeldar was just leaving her flowery refuge when Mr. Helstone came briskly into the garden and met her, as though he had been hunting her among the shrubs.

“Oh, I want you!” he said. “I was afraid you were already gone.—Caroline, come here.”

Caroline came quickly, both she and Shirley expecting rebuke for her invisibility in church. But the rector’s mind had flown to other matters.

“I shall not sleep at home to-night,” he began. “I have just met with an old friend, and promised to accompany him. I shall return probably about noon to-morrow. Thomas, the clerk, is engaged, and I cannot get him to sleep in the house, as I usually do when I am absent for a night. Now—”

“Now,” broke in Shirley, with that lightness that would not wholly hide her quick intelligence, “you want me as a gentleman—the first gentleman in Briarfield, in short—to supply your place, be master of the rectory and guardian of your niece and maids while you are away?”

“Exactly, captain. I thought the post would suit you. Will you favour Caroline so far as to be her guest for one night? Will you stay here instead of going back to Fieldhead?”

“And what will Mrs. Pryor do? She expects me home.”

“I will send her word. Come, make up your mind to stay. It grows late; the dew falls heavily. You and Caroline will enjoy each other’s society, I doubt not.”

“I promise you, then, to stay with Caroline,” said Shirley. “As you say, we shall enjoy each other’s society. We will not be separated to-night. Now, rejoin your old friend, and fear nothing for us.”

Mr. Helstone’s eye—half fatherly, half keen—rested on her as if measuring the steel under the ribbon.

“If there should chance to be any disturbance in the night, captain; if you should hear the picking of a lock, the cutting out of a pane of glass, a stealthy tread of steps about the house (and I need not fear to tell you, who bear a well-tempered, mettlesome heart under your girl’s ribbon sash, that such little incidents are very possible in the present time), what would you do?”

“Don’t know; faint, perhaps—fall down, and have to be picked up again. But, doctor, if you assign me the post of honour, you must give me arms. What weapons are there in your stronghold?”

“You could not wield a sword?”

“No; I could manage the carving-knife better.”

“You will find a good one in the dining-room sideboard—a lady’s knife, light to handle, and as sharp-pointed as a poniard.”

“It will suit Caroline. But you must give me a brace of pistols. I know you have pistols.”

“I have two pairs. One pair I can place at your disposal. You will find them suspended over the mantelpiece of my study in cloth cases.”

“Loaded?”

“Yes, but not on the cock. Cock them before you go to bed. It is paying you a great compliment, captain, to lend you these. Were you one of the awkward squad you should not have them.”

“I will take care. You need delay no longer, Mr. Helstone. You may go now.”

He went out at the garden gate with the haste of a man who has timed his own absence to some darker clock than the church’s.

“He is gracious to me to lend me his pistols,” Shirley remarked, watching him go. Then, turning, as if to lock away thought by opening another cupboard: “But come, Lina, let us go in and have some supper. I was too much vexed at tea with the vicinage of Mr. Sam Wynne to be able to eat, and now I am really hungry.”

Within, the dining-room lay dim and warm, as though it had kept the breath of the day. The windows were open, and the evening air stole in with the garden’s perfumes—laburnum and late roses, damp box, the faintly acrid sweetness of crushed leaves. From far down the road came the last thinning fall of footsteps. Under it all—distinct in the stillness, like a thread of sound that did not break—ran the beck in the Hollow, fretting on its stones.

“Shirley,” Caroline said, pausing at the casement, listening as if she were learning a language the daylight drowned, “I hear the beck in the Hollow.”

Then she rang for a candle and asked for bread and milk—Shirley’s common supper, and her own. When Fanny brought the tray, she would have shut the windows and fastened the shutters, but was told to leave them.

The twilight was too calm; its breath too balmy to be excluded yet. They ate in silence: the soft dip of bread, the small clink of china, the faint sweet animal warmth of milk newly brought—comforting and homely, and therefore, in a time like this, strangely precious. Caroline rose once to move a glass of flowers from the sideboard to the window-sill; the room was sultry, and the blossoms exhaled too richly, as if their scent pressed on the lungs.

When she returned, she half opened a drawer and drew out something that caught what little light there was and gave it back coldly—steel, bright and narrow in her hand.

“You assigned this to me, then, Shirley, did you? It is bright, keen-edged, finely tapered; it is dangerous-looking. I never yet felt the impulse which could move me to direct this against a fellow-creature. It is difficult to fancy that circumstances could nerve my arm to strike home with this long knife.”

“I should hate to do it,” Shirley answered; and the words were quiet, but not light now. “But I think I could do it, if goaded by certain exigencies which I can imagine.”

She sipped her milk thoughtfully, a little pale—though, indeed, she was rarely anything else than pale; her bloom was not in colour but in energy.

When they had eaten, Fanny was called again, and she and Eliza were told to go to bed. Both were weary—there had been much cutting of currant-buns, filling of urns and teapots, carrying of trays; domestic labour, like kneading, had its own rhythm, and it leaves the arms aching with honest fatigue. Presently the maids’ chamber door shut.

Caroline took a candle and went quietly through the house, fastening windows, barring doors. She did not avoid the haunted back kitchen nor the vault-like cellars; she traversed them as if fear were a thing to be checked off like a latch.

When she returned she said, trying to smile and failing: “There is neither spirit nor flesh in the house at present which should not be there. It is now near eleven o’clock, fully bedtime; yet I would rather sit up a little longer, if you do not object, Shirley. Here—I have brought the brace of pistols from my uncle’s study. You may examine them at your leisure.”

She laid the pistols on the table as one lays down a fact.

“Why would you rather sit up longer?” Shirley asked, taking them up, looking them over with a composed hand, and laying them down again.

“Because I have a strange, excited feeling in my heart.”

“So have I.”

“Is this state of sleeplessness and restlessness caused by something electrical in the air, I wonder?”

“No; the sky is clear, the stars numberless. It is a fine night.”

“But very still. I hear the water fret over its stony bed in Hollow’s Copse as distinctly as if it ran below the churchyard wall.”

“I am glad it is so still a night. A moaning wind or rushing rain would vex me to fever just now.”

“Why, Shirley?”

“Because it would baffle my efforts to listen.”

“Do you listen towards the Hollow?”

“Yes; it is the only quarter whence we can hear a sound just now.”

“The only one, Shirley.”

They sat near the open lattice, both leaning their arms on the sill, both inclining their heads as though the ear were a kind of mouth and silence could be tasted. They could see each other’s young faces by starlight and that dim June remainder which does not wholly leave the west till the east begins to promise morning.

“Mr. Helstone thinks we have no idea which way he is gone,” Shirley murmured, “nor on what errand, nor with what expectations, nor how prepared. But I guess much; do not you?”

“I guess something.”

“All those gentlemen—your cousin Moore included—think that you and I are now asleep in our beds, unconscious.”

“Caring nothing about them—hoping and fearing nothing for them,” Caroline added.

They said no more for a long half-hour. The night said little too; only the church clock measured it, dividing time into quarters as a careful cook divides dough—without passion, without pity. Once they drew their scarves closer and put on their bonnets again, because the air cooled and the dew thickened, and their vigil had the stillness of people who listen not for pleasure but for meaning.

Towards midnight the house-dog began that teasing, monotonous bark which wears on the nerves like a dripping tap. Caroline rose and went noiselessly through dark passages to the kitchen, carrying herself as if every board had ears. She appeased him with a piece of bread and returned.

The dining-room was now dark; Shirley had put out the candle. Her outline showed near the open window, leaning out. Caroline asked no question; she went to her side. The dog recommenced barking, then stopped abruptly, as if some nearer authority had silenced him. The two at the window listened—and now it was not merely the beck in the Hollow.

A muffled sound came up the road below the churchyard: measured, beating, approaching. A dull tramp of marching feet.

It drew nearer, and the extent of it grew on the ear. It was not two men, nor a dozen, nor a score. It was the tread of hundreds. The garden shrubs made a leafy screen, so that sight was denied them; but hearing, sharpened by stillness, brought everything close. The troop seemed passing the rectory, and then a human voice, speaking one word only, broke the hush:

“Halt!”

The march stopped. A low conference followed, indistinct, yet charged with intent.

“We must hear this,” Shirley said.

She turned, took the pistols from the table, and passed out through the middle window—indeed a glass door—into the garden. She stole down the walk and stood listening beneath the lilacs. Caroline, who would not have quitted the house alone, followed because Shirley went; she glanced at the long knife on the sideboard, left it, and soon stood at Shirley’s shoulder. They did not dare look over the wall; they crouched behind it and heard words that seemed to come from mouths close to the bricks, rough and low, with that dialect that carries a whole parish’s history in its consonants.

“It looks a rambling old building. Who lives in it besides the damned parson?”

“Only three women—his niece and two servants.”

“Do you know where they sleep?”

“The lasses behind; the niece in a front room.”

“And Helstone?”

“Yonder is his chamber. He was burning a light, but I see none now.”

“Where would you get in?”

“If I were ordered to do his job—and he desarves it—I’d try yond’ long window; it opens to the dining-room. I could grope my way upstairs, and I know his chamber.”

“How would you manage about the women folk?”

“Let ’em alone except they shrieked, and then I’d soon quieten ’em. I could wish to find the old chap asleep. If he waked, he’d be dangerous.”

“Has he arms?”

“Firearms, allus—and allus loadened.”

“Then you’re a fool to stop us here. A shot would give the alarm. Moore would be on us before we could turn round. We should miss our main object.”

“You might go on, I tell you. I’d engage Helstone alone.”

There was a pause. Someone dropped a weapon; it rang sharply on stone. At that ring the rectory dog burst into furious barking again, as though the sound had struck his instinct like a hand on a drum.

“That spoils all!” said the voice. “He’ll awake. A noise like that might rouse the dead. You did not say there was a dog. Damn you! Forward!”

Forward they went—tramp, tramp—slow-filing, manifold. The night swallowed them.

Shirley rose, looked over the wall along the road.

“Not a soul remains,” she said. After a moment: “Thank God!”

Caroline repeated it, but her voice shook. Her heart beat thickly; her face was cold, her forehead damp with a sweat that felt like dew fallen inward.

“Thank God for us!” she whispered. “But what will happen elsewhere? They have passed us by that they may make sure of others.”

“They have done well,” Shirley returned with composure that had iron under it. “The others will defend themselves. They can do it. They are prepared for them. With us it is otherwise. My finger was on the trigger of this pistol. I was quite ready to give that man, if he had entered, such a greeting as he little calculated on; but behind him followed three hundred. I had neither three hundred hands nor three hundred weapons. I could not have effectually protected either you, myself, or the two poor women asleep under that roof. Therefore I again earnestly thank God for insult and peril escaped.”

She stood a second, thinking as quickly as she had acted.

“What is it my duty and wisdom to do next? Not to stay here inactive, I am glad to say, but, of course, to walk over to the Hollow.”

“To the Hollow, Shirley?”

“To the Hollow. Will you go with me?”

“Where those men are gone?”

“They have taken the highway; we should not encounter them. The road over the fields is as safe, silent, and solitary as a path through the air would be. Will you go?”

“Yes,” said Caroline mechanically—not from readiness, but because she could not bear to let Shirley go alone.

“Then we must fasten up these windows, and leave all as secure as we can behind us. Do you know what we are going for, Cary?”

“Yes—no—because you wish it.”

“Is that all? And are you so obedient to a mere caprice of mine? What a docile wife you would make to a stern husband! The moon’s face is not whiter than yours at this moment, and the aspen at the gate does not tremble more than your busy fingers; and so, tractable and terror-struck, and dismayed and devoted, you would follow me into the thick of real danger! Cary, let me give your fidelity a motive. We are going for Moore’s sake—to see if we can be of use to him, to make an effort to warn him of what is coming.”

“To be sure!” Caroline cried, shame and love meeting in her voice. “I am a blind, weak fool, and you are acute and sensible, Shirley. I will go with you; I will gladly go with you!”

“I do not doubt it. You would die blindly and meekly for me, but you would intelligently and gladly die for Moore. But, in truth, there is no question of death to-night; we run no risk at all.”

Caroline barred and shuttered the dining-room windows with hands that, for all their trembling, were quick and capable. Then they were out, running, taking the fields as if the earth belonged to them in that hour. Walls checked them but did not baffle them. Shirley was surefooted, springing like a deer when she chose. Caroline, more timid and less practised, fell once or twice and bruised herself; she rose instantly each time, insisting she was not hurt, as if pain were a luxury she could not afford.

A quickset hedge bounded the last field. They lost time hunting a gap; when found, it was narrow, and they worked through it, snagging hair and tearing tender skin and catching silks and muslins on thorns. What they regretted most was not the damage but the delay; every impediment felt like a hand thrust between them and Robert Moore.

Beyond the hedge the beck met them, deeper than it sounded, its bed rough with stones. A narrow plank was the only bridge. Shirley had crossed it often without thought; Caroline had never yet dared.

“I will carry you across,” Shirley said. “You are light, and I am not weak. Let me try.”

“If I fall in, you may fish me out,” Caroline answered, and squeezed her hand. Then, without pausing to bargain with fear, she stepped onto the trembling plank as if it were firm turf. Shirley followed, no more resolute than Caroline, no safer. Their blood was too hot for caution; on this errand, fire and water were not masters.

But a sound stopped them. Hardly had they reached the opposite bank when a shot split the air from the north. A second later another burst from the south. Within three minutes, like harsh birds calling across the valley, similar signals boomed from east and west.

“I thought we were dead at the first explosion,” Shirley said, drawing a long breath. “I felt myself hit in the temples, and I concluded your heart was pierced; but the reiterated voice is an explanation. Those are signals—it is their way—the attack must be near. We should have had wings. Our feet have not borne us swiftly enough.”

They cleared a portion of copse, branches whispering overhead with the small nocturnal rustle that belongs to leaves, to nesting birds, to insects—yet beneath those natural sounds lay another layer now: the human multitude moving like a tide. When they emerged, the mill lay below them. They could see the buildings and yard, and the road beyond.

The first glance told Shirley she had guessed rightly. They were too late to warn.

The road, which should have shown pale, was dark with a moving mass. The rioters were gathered in front of the closed yard gates, and within stood a single figure, apparently addressing them. The mill itself was black and still; no light burned, no movement showed at lattice or door.

“Surely he is prepared,” Shirley whispered. “Surely that is not Moore meeting them alone?”

“It is,” Caroline breathed, and her whole body leaned forward as if longing could make a path. “We must go to him. I will go to him.”

“That you will not.”

“Why did I come, then? I came only for him. I shall join him.”

“Fortunately it is out of your power. There is no entrance to the yard.”

“There is a small entrance at the back besides the gates in front. It opens by a secret method which I know. I will try it.”

“Not with my leave.”

Shirley clasped her round the waist with both arms and held her back with an authority that was tenderness armed. “Not one step shall you stir. At this moment Moore would be both shocked and embarrassed if he saw either you or me. Men never want women near them in time of real danger.”

“I would not trouble—I would help him.”

“How?—by inspiring him with heroism? Pooh! These are not the days of chivalry. It is not a tilt at a tournament we are going to behold, but a struggle about money, and food, and life.”

“It is natural that I should be at his side.”

“As queen of his heart? His mill is his lady-love, Cary! Backed by his factory and his frames, he has all the encouragement he wants or can know. It is not for love or beauty, but for ledger and broadcloth, he is going to break a spear. Don’t be sentimental; Robert is not so.”

“I could help him; I will seek him.”

“Off then—I let you go—seek Moore. You’ll not find him.”

She loosened her hold. Caroline darted forward; behind her rang Shirley’s short, gibing laugh, with a warning flung after it: “Look well there is no mistake!”

But there was a mistake. Caroline stopped, stared; the figure within the gate had suddenly retreated and was running back towards the mill.

“Make haste, Lina!” Shirley called. “Meet him before he enters!”

Caroline returned slowly, her face changing as certainty replaced hope. “It is not Robert,” she said. “It has neither his height, form, nor bearing.”

“I saw it was not Robert when I let you go,” Shirley replied, and there was relief now in her tone, almost mirth at her own quick deception. “How could you imagine it? It is a shabby little figure of a private soldier; they had posted him as sentinel. He is safe in the mill now. I saw the door open and admit him. My mind grows easier. Robert is prepared. Our warning would have been superfluous; and now I am thankful we came too late to give it. It has saved us the trouble of a scene. How fine to have entered the counting-house toute éperdue, and to have found oneself in presence of Messrs. Armitage and Ramsden smoking, Malone swaggering, your uncle sneering, Mr. Sykes sipping a cordial, and Moore himself in his cold man-of-business vein! I am glad we missed it all.”

“I wonder if there are many in the mill, Shirley!”

“Plenty to defend it. The soldiers we have twice seen to-day were going there, no doubt, and the group we noticed surrounding your cousin in the fields will be with him.”

“What are they doing now, Shirley? What is that noise?”

“Hatchets and crowbars against the yard gates. They are forcing them. Are you afraid?”

“No; but my heart throbs fast. I have a difficulty in standing. I will sit down. Do you feel unmoved?”

“Hardly that; but I am glad I came. We shall see what transpires with our own eyes. We are here on the spot, and none know it. Instead of amazing the curate, the clothier, and the corn-dealer with a romantic rush on the stage, we stand alone with the friendly night—its mute stars—and these whispering trees, whose report our friends will not come to gather.”

“Shirley, Shirley, the gates are down!” Caroline cried. “That crash was like the felling of great trees. Now they are pouring through. They will break down the mill doors as they have broken the gate. What can Robert do against so many? Would to God I were a little nearer him—could hear him speak—could speak to him! With my will—my longing to serve him—I could not be a useless burden in his way; I could be turned to some account.”

“They come on!” Shirley said, and her voice sank, as if instinctively she would not waste sound. “How steadily they march in! There is discipline in their ranks. I will not say there is courage—hundreds against tens are no proof of that quality—but” (she spoke lower still) “there is suffering and desperation enough amongst them. These goads will urge them forwards.”

“Forwards against Robert; and they hate him. Shirley, is there much danger they will win the day?”

“We shall see. Moore and Helstone are of earth’s first blood—no bunglers—no cravens—”

A crash—smash—shiver—cut her off. A volley of stones, hurled together, struck the broad front of the mill. All its windows answered at once, not with light but with ruin: every pane in every lattice shattered and fell, pounded into fragments that glittered for an instant and then were gone.

A yell followed—a rioters’ yell: harsh, long, packed with hunger, anger, and the thick accent of the West-Riding clothing district. It poured into the Hollow and filled it, drowning the beck’s fretting voice as surely as a flood would drown a shallow stream.

Caroline rose; Shirley put her arm round her, and they stood together as still as the straight stems of two trees. When the yell ended, the night was not quiet; it was full of the swaying murmur of a crowd, like wind in a heavy crop.

“What next?” they asked each other without words. The mill remained mute as a tomb.

“He cannot be alone,” Caroline whispered.

“I would stake all I have that he is as little alone as he is alarmed,” Shirley answered.

Shots were discharged by the rioters; small flashes, sharp reports. Had the defenders waited for that signal? It seemed so. The hitherto inert mill woke: fire leaped from its empty window-frames; a volley of musketry pealed through the Hollow.

“Moore speaks at last!” said Shirley. “And he seems to have the gift of tongues. That was not a single voice.”
