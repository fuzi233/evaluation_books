A grave, white-haired seneschal approached their board with a softness of step that seemed to hush the very air around him. Over the clatter of trenchers and the thin music of knives on pewter, he asked, courteous as a priest at the altar, whether Gerard Eliassoen sat among them.

“I am he,” said Gerard; and the seneschal inclined his head.

“The Princess Marie would confer with you, young sir. I am charged to conduct you.”

At that, every face within earshot swung round as if tugged by one string—eyes sharp with curiosity, and sharper with envy—fastening on the young man who was to be summoned like a dish from the common table to a princess’s private board. Gerard rose at once.

“I wager we shall not see you again,” said Margaret, very calm; but the colour crept, despite herself, into her cheek.

“That you will,” he answered; and then, bending to her ear where the warm smell of bread and spiced wine hung about her hair, he whispered, “This is my good princess; but you are my queen.” Aloud he added, “Wait for me, I pray you. I will presently return.”

“Ay, ay!” said Peter, waking and speaking in the same breath, as if the words had knocked him awake.

Gerard went with the seneschal, and, the instant he was gone, those two—plain in dress, strangers in a rich hall—found themselves stared at as though their homely wool had suddenly turned to cloth of gold merely by sitting near the man a princess had sent for.

William Johnson, seeing the eyes, seized his moment. He came forward, put on surprise as one puts on a cloak, and claimed them loudly for kin.

“And to think there was I at your backs, and you saw me not!”

“Nay, cousin Johnson,” said Margaret, chill as well-water drawn, “I saw you long syne.”

“You saw me, and spoke not?”

“Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is for us to welcome you at Sevenbergen. Your servant denied us a seat in your house.”

“The idiot!”

“And I had a mind to see whether it was ‘like maid like master;’ for there is sooth in bywords.”

The blood rushed to William Johnson’s face so quickly it was as if a hot kettle had been clapped upon it. He read something in her keen steadiness that made him wary of defending himself in words; so he took the wiser road and tried deeds instead. He insisted they must come home with him at once—home, where bread was cut under his own roof and a welcome could be proved by fire on the hearth and honest ale in the jug.

“Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?” said the scholar, smoothing the thing with scholarship as one smooths a rough crust with a knife.

Margaret thanked him with due grace, yet would not rise. She said she wished to hear the minstrels again; and indeed the strains—thin as threads of silver—were winding through the hall, sliding between laughter and talk like smoke.

A quarter of an hour passed. Many guests had left; the din had loosened a little, like a market when the best buyers are gone. Johnson renewed his urging, and then Margaret’s true reason came out, the way the heart’s truth will show itself even when wrapped in manners.

“It were ill manners to our friend; and he will lose us. He knows not where we lodge in Rotterdam, and the city is large, and we have parted company once already.”

“Oh!” said Johnson, quick as a man finding an easy stitch for a tear he feared. “We will provide for that. My young man— ahem! I mean my secretary—shall sit here and wait, and bring him on to my house. He shall lodge with me and with no other.”

“Cousin, we shall be too burdensome.”

“Nay, nay. You shall see whether you are welcome or not, you and your friends, and your friends’ friends, if need be; and I shall hear what the Princess would with him.”

At the word lodge—under the same roof—joy leapt in Margaret so suddenly she felt it in her throat; then a small misgiving pricked, like a bone hidden in stew.

“But if your young man should be thoughtless, and go play, and Gerard miss him?”

“He go play? He leave that spot where I put him, and bid him stay?” Johnson’s voice rose with injured certainty. “Ho! Stand forth, Hans Cloterman!”

A figure in black serge and dark violet hose rose as if drawn up by a cord. He took two steps and halted before them. Not a muscle twitched. His face was long and solemn, his bearing starched to the very soul, as if duty had been baked into him like salt into cured meat. Seeing him, Margaret—being very happy and therefore dangerously near laughter—could hardly keep her countenance.

She leaned and whispered to Johnson, “I would put my hand in the fire for him.”

“We are at your command, cousin,” she added, “as soon as you have given him his orders.”

Hans was instructed to sit at the table, wait for Gerard, and conduct him to Ooster-Waagen Straet. He answered not with words, but by calmly taking the seat indicated, as though the chair itself were a post of honour. Then Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson went away.

“And, indeed, it is time you were abed, father, after all your travel,” said Margaret; for that had been simmering in her mind all along, like broth kept for the weak.

Hans Cloterman sat. The minutes ran past him like water past a mill-stone, and stirred no impatience in that perfect image of propriety. Johnson had laughed to scorn the thought of his secretary forsaking duty for sport. Yet the eye that enforces steadiness was gone now; and time, like a sly cook, began to add an ingredient no one had ordered.

Hans, solemn and businesslike, improved the waiting by quaffing—gravely, silently, and at short but accurately measured intervals—goblets of Corsican wine. It was strong; so was his head. But after Gerard had been absent near an hour, the model secretary, still maintaining a face as rigid as a chapel door, conceived that Creation itself expected Hans Cloterman to drink the health of all good fellows, and by name of the Duke of Burgundy there present. With this lofty purpose he filled bumper nine, rose gingerly, and, having attained his full height, instantly rolled upon the grass with the goblet still in hand. The cold liquor spilled over more than one ankle; their owners frisked and cursed; but Hans, in the total eclipse of reason, never disturbed one solemn muscle.

Meanwhile the seneschal had led Gerard through passages where torchlight wavered like flame seen through wine. They came to the pavilion-door. There sat certain young noblemen—embroidered, feathered—keeping watch on the heir-apparent and playing cards by the red glare of torches held by servants whose arms must have ached with the stillness.

A whisper from the seneschal; one of the nobles rose as though forced, stared at Gerard with haughty surprise, and disappeared within. He returned and beckoned; and so, through another passage or two, they were brought to an antechamber where three more young gentlemen sat—furred, feathered, glittering like bright birds—deep in dice as though salvation depended on pips.

“You can’t see the Princess—it is too late,” said one, without lifting much more than his eyelids.

“She passed this way but now with her nurse,” said another. “Gone to bed, doll and all. Deuce—ace again!”

Gerard, crestfallen, prepared to retire. But the seneschal, with an incredulous smile, replied, “The young man is here by the Countess’s orders; be so good as conduct him to her ladies.”

A superb Adonis rose with the injured air of one made to wait upon a commoner, and led Gerard into a room where eleven ladies sat or lolled, chattering like magpies. Two, more industrious than the rest, played cat’s-cradle; their fingers moved as deftly as their tongues. At sight of a stranger, all tongues stopped at once, as if a single peg had been pulled from a mechanism; and all eyes turned, simultaneously, to fix him like pins.

Gerard had been ill at ease before; but this sudden battery of eyes drove his gaze to the ground. The ladies, finding there was a creature they could startle, giggled—delighted with their own power, as children delight in making a timid dog shrink. Then a duenna said severely, “Mesdames!” and the giggling died as if modesty were a cord drawn tight.

This same duenna took Gerard and marched before him in solemn silence. His heart sank; for a moment he half longed to bolt like a hare.

“What must princes be,” he thought, “when their courtiers are so freezing? Doubtless they take their breeding from him they serve.”

These thoughts were cut short: the duenna opened into another room. Three ladies sat working, the quick whisper of thread through cloth making its small, domestic music amid all this grandeur; and a pretty little girl was tuning a lute, testing each string until it sat true, like a cook tasting broth until the salt is right.

The ladies were richly but not showily dressed. The duenna went to one who was hemming a kerchief, spoke low; and that lady turned with a smile and beckoned Gerard near. She did not rise, yet the very motion of laying aside her work was full of ease and grace, the sort that makes ceremony feel like kindness.

“Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend of mine, sir,” she said at once, “and I am right glad to have a letter from her hand, and thankful to you for bringing it safely. Marie, my love, this is the gentleman who brought you that pretty miniature.”

“I thank you a thousand times,” said the young Princess with eager warmth.

“I am glad you feel her debtor, sweetheart,” said the Countess of Charolois, “for our friend would have us do him a little service in return.”

“I will do anything on earth for him,” the girl burst out.

“Anything on earth is nothing in the world,” replied her mother quietly—soft, yet with a weight beneath, as if she knew what “anything” can cost. Then to Gerard: “Well, then—what would you have me to do, sir?”

Gerard understood now what company he stood in; the air itself seemed finer, like flour sifted twice. He spoke gently, a little tremulous.

“My sovereign demoiselle—where there have been no pains, there needs no reward.”

“But we must obey mamma,” said the Princess, quick as a bird. “All the world must obey.”

“That is true,” said Gerard; and then, finding courage in a request that harmed no one, “Then reward me, if you will, by letting me hear the stave you were going to sing and I did interrupt it.”

“What! you love music, sir?” the Princess cried.

“I adore it.”

She glanced to her mother, who smiled assent. Then the child took her lute and sang a romaunt of the day. She was but twelve, yet well taught and painstaking. Her small hand swept the chords with courage and precision; the notes of the arpeggio came clear and bright as if someone had scattered little stars over dark water. But the chief charm was her voice: not mighty, but round and ringing, clear as a bell heard across a frosty field. There was modest eloquence in her singing; she knew none of the tricks by which older throats counterfeit feeling. She was too young to be theatrical, even to be sentimental; so nothing was forced. It all seemed to gush, as if the song had been stored in her like scent in a bruised herb.

When she ceased, and the clink of glasses in far rooms resumed, Gerard’s eyes were wet. The Countess watched him; it was common to applaud a princess loudly, but not to be moved in cheek and eye.

When the last vibration of the lute died, she asked demurely, “Was he content?”

Gerard started; speech broke the spell like a hand clapped on water.

“Oh, madam,” he cried, “surely it is thus that cherubs and seraphs sing, and charm the saints in heaven.”

“I am somewhat of your opinion, my young friend,” said the Countess, with emotion; and she looked on her child with love and gentle pride, the kind of look that seems to acknowledge both the sweetness and the briefness of youth.

Then she resumed, business returning as softly as a lid set on a pot.

“My old friend requests me to be serviceable to you. It is the first favour she has done us the honour of asking, and the request is sacred. You are in holy orders, sir?”

Gerard bowed.

“I fear you are not a priest—you look too young.”

“Oh no, madam. I am not even a sub-deacon. I am only a lector; but next month I shall be an exorcist, and before long an acolyte.”

“Well, Monsieur Gerard, with your accomplishments you can soon pass through the inferior orders. And let me beg you to do so. For the day after you have said your first mass I shall have the pleasure of appointing you to a benefice.”

“Oh, madam—”

“And, Marie, remember I make this promise in your name as well as my own.”

“Fear not, mamma. I will not forget,” cried the Princess; then, with a rush of bright fancy, “But if he will take my advice, what he will be is Bishop of Liège. The Bishop of Liège is a beautiful bishop. What! do you not remember him, mamma, that day we were at Liège? He was braver than grandpapa himself. He had on a crown—a high one—and it was cut in the middle, and it was full of such beautiful jewels; and his gown stiff with gold; and his mantle too; and it had a broad border, all pictures; but above all, his gloves—you have no such gloves, mamma. They were embroidered and covered with jewels, and scented with such lovely scent; I smelt them all the time he was giving me his blessing on my head with them. Dear old man! I dare say he will die soon—most old people do—and then, sir, you can be bishop, you know, and wear—”

“Gently, Marie, gently,” said her mother. “Bishoprics are for old gentlemen; and this is a young gentleman.”

“Mamma! he is not so very young.”

“Not compared with you, Marie, eh?”

“He is of good birth, dear mamma; and I am sure he is good enough for a bishop.”

“Alas! mademoiselle, you are mistaken,” said Gerard, low.

“I know not that, Monsieur Gerard,” said the Countess, and there was a mild amusement in her gaze. “But I am a little puzzled to know on what grounds mademoiselle pronounces your character so boldly.”

“Alas! mamma,” said the Princess, lifting her brows as if the question were wilful blindness, “you have not looked at his face, then.”

“I beg your pardon,” said the Countess. “I have. Well, sir, if I cannot go quite so fast as my daughter, attribute it to my age, not to want of interest in your welfare. A benefice will do to begin your career with; and I must take care it is not too far from—what call you the place?”

“Tergou, madam.”

“A priest gives up much,” the Countess continued; “often, I fear, he learns too late how much.” And her woman’s eye rested a moment on Gerard with a pity that surprised him, as if she looked past his cassock-to-be and saw, beneath, the ordinary human hungers and the domestic joys he would be asked to renounce. “At least you shall be near your friends. Have you a mother?”

“Yes, madam, thanks be to God!”

“Good. You shall have a church near Tergou. She will thank me. And now, sir, we must not detain you too long from those who have a better claim on your society than we have. Duchess, oblige me by bidding one of the pages conduct him to the hall of banquet; the way is hard to find.”

Gerard bowed low, and backed towards the door.

“I hope it will be a nice benefice,” the Princess called after him with a pretty smile; then shook her head with a solemn little doubt, as if prophesying weather: “but you had better have been Bishop of Liège.”

He followed his conductor, heart warm with gratitude. Yet before he reached the banqueting hall a chill stole over him, the way cool air creeps under a door when the fire has been high. A mind bred to quiet days cannot hold contrary feelings evenly; it is seized by each in turn. With the Countess, novelty and honour had filled him—this promise, this royal favour, the pride it would bring at home. But now passion found its voice again.

Give up Margaret—whose soft hand still seemed to lie in his palm, whose deep eyes had lodged in his breast like a relic? Resign her, and that whole world of joy she had opened upon him this day, for a benefice and a priest’s lonely title?

The revulsion came so strong he resolved, hastily and fiercely, to say nothing at home of the offered living.

“The Countess is so good,” he told himself, clinging to hope as a hungry man clings to crust. “She has a hundred ways to aid a young man’s fortune. She will not compel me to be a priest when she learns I love one of her sex. One would almost think she knows it; she cast a strange look, and said, ‘A priest gives up much, too much.’ She will give me a place about the palace.”

That thought eased him. He reached the banqueting hall, thanked his guide, and ran with bright, eager eyes to Margaret.

He came in sight of the table.

She was gone.

Peter was gone too.

No one of their party remained—only a citizen in sober garments had just tumbled beneath the board, dead drunk, and several were hauling him up to carry him off, his limp weight sliding like a sack of wet grain. Gerard never guessed how important that solemn drunkard was to him. He looked only for Beauty, and let the Beast lie.

He ran wildly through the hall, now grown emptier; the laughter thinned, the air stale with spilled wine and trampled rushes. She was not there. He left the palace, and outside found a crowd gaping at two great fan-lights newly kindled over the gate—two bright openings of flame that made the night seem almost a cheated thing.

He asked, urgent, whether they had seen an old man in a gown, and a lovely girl, pass out.

They laughed. They were staring at those new lights that turned night into day; they would not trouble their heads about old men and young wenches—everyday sights.

From another knot he learned a Mystery was being played under canvas hard by, and half Rotterdam had gone to see it. That lifted his hopes; he hurried there, listening for any voice he knew amid the murmuring, the fife’s thin cry, the thud of feet on turf.

The Mystery was in full course. Holy and unholy alike were dragged into the same rude daylight: divine personages—too sacred to name with ease—came clumsily down from heaven to bandy sophistry with the cardinal Virtues, the nine Muses, and the seven deadly sins, all in human shape and, to Gerard’s eye, too much alike in their painted solemnity. To hearten the weary matter in rattled the Prince of the power of the air, and an imp kept molesting him, buffeting him with a bladder; at each thwack the crowd roared as if the sound itself were their supper.

When the Vices had delivered a good store of obscenity and the Virtues had answered with twaddle, the celestials—Muses included—went gingerly back to heaven one by one; for there was but one cloud, and two artisans worked it up with its supernatural freight, then worked it down with a winch, all in full sight. When that was done, the bottomless pit opened and flamed in the centre of the stage; carpenters and Virtues shoved the Vices in, and then the Virtues, and Beelzebub, and his tormentor danced merrily round the place of eternal torture to fife and tabor, as if damnation were a harvest-home.

This entertainment had been writ by the Bishop of Ghent to diffuse religious sentiment by the aid of the senses—eyes to see, ears to be struck, bodies to jolt with laughter. Yet Gerard, whose ear had lately been filled with a child’s pure singing, found no comfort in this harsh din, no health in this riot of noise. The crowd’s merriment beat against him like a smith’s hammer; it seemed to drive his own thoughts into a smaller, darker corner. He watched faces lit by flare and torch, and could not find the one face he sought. He could not even stay to the end. He went away in the second act, in the midst of a much-admired passage wherein Justice out-quibbled Satan, and walked street after street, listening for the particular cadence of Margaret’s speech, the country turn of her words that carried home in them like the taste of milk in fresh bread.

But Rotterdam was wide, and the night had many mouths and none spoke her name.

At last, worn quite out, he went to a hostelry and slept till daybreak. All that day, heavy and heartsick, he sought her again—market, quay, church-porch, lanes where fish-smell and peat-smoke clung together, broader streets where hoofbeats rang and voices clattered like loose pans—yet he could never fall in with her nor her father, nor obtain the slightest clue.

Then suspicion, like sourness in milk, began to rise. He told himself she was false, or had changed her mind. Irritation joined sorrow; and the good fortune that had fallen on him—the angels in his purse, the medal on his breast, the Countess’s promise—seemed now only to mock him, like rich sauce set before a man who cannot swallow.

On the third day, after he had once more gone through every street he could think of, he stopped as if struck by a sudden certainty.

“She is not in the town,” he said aloud, “and I shall never see her again. I will go home.”

So he started for Tergou with royal favour promised, with fifteen golden angels in his purse, a golden medal on his bosom, and a heart heavy as lead.
