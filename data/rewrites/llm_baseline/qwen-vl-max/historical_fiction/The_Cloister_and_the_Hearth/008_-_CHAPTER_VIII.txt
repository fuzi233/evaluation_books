While the burgomaster was exposing Gerard at Tergou, Margaret faced her own trial in Sevenbergen. It was a quiet sorrow, born of a woman’s daily care, yet deeper than most could fathom. She came to Martin Wittenhaagen, the old soldier, her eyes brimming with tears.

“Martin,” she said, “there is nothing in the house. Gerard is coming home, and he never thinks to eat before he reaches us. When his work is done, he comes straight to me—poor soul—and often he arrives weak from hunger. And now, I have nothing to set before my servant who loves me so dearly.”

Martin scratched his head, puzzled. “What can I do?”

“It is Thursday—the day you go hunting,” she replied. “I counted on you today.”

“Nay,” he said, “I may not hunt when the Duke or his friends are out. You know that well enough.” He paused, then added, “I am no scholar.” With that, he pulled from his pouch a parchment sealed with a grand wax stamp. It bore the mark of Philip, Duke of Burgundy, and granted Martin Wittenhaagen, one of his archers, a yearly stipend of four merks and a licence to shoot three arrows once a week—on Thursdays only—in any of the Duke’s forests in Holland. But there were strict conditions: he might not hunt a seven-year-old buck or a doe carrying young; nor might he enter the woods if the Duke or his companions were afield. To do so risked forfeiture of salary, fine, and even death.

Margaret sighed. “Then what shall I do?”

“Come, cheer up, mistress,” Martin said, his voice firm. “For your sake, I’ll take the risk. I’ve done it for others less worthy than your little finger. It’s not such a great danger. I’ll just slip into the edge of the forest. Odds are they’ll drive a hare or fawn within range.”

“If you go,” she said, “you must promise not to go far, and not to be seen. Better Gerard goes supperless than harm come to you, faithful Martin.”

He gave his word. Then, taking his bow and three arrows, he crept into the wood—not more than a furlong away. The distant sound of horns echoed through the trees, signaling the chase. All the game was stirring.

“Soon,” thought Martin, “I shall fill the pot, and no one will be the wiser.”

He took position behind a thick oak that overlooked an open glade. His bow—a fearsome English yew, six feet two inches long and proportionately strong—was strung with care. Martin, broad-shouldered and powerful, had drawn such bows since childhood. He could pull a three-foot arrow back to his ear, and when released, the shaft flew like lightning, the string singing as clear as a harp. This weapon had laid low many a man in the wars between the Hoecks and Cabbeljaws. In those days, battles were not shrouded in smoke; men saw their enemies clearly, and fewer bullets meant more precise kills.

A hare bounded across the clearing, then stopped, ears pricked upward in a perfect V. Martin raised his bow. The arrow left the string with a sharp twang—but he’d been too hasty. The shaft struck the ground beside the hare, slid beneath her belly, and vanished into the grass. She leapt three feet straight up and fled.

“Bungler!” Martin muttered. A true marksman would never blame the prey.

He no sooner fitted another arrow than a wood-pigeon landed on the very branch above him. “Aha!” he thought. “You’re small, but dainty.” This time he took his time. He drew slowly, released smoothly, and watched the arrow pierce the bird—feathers flying like dust. But instead of falling, the pigeon fluttered away, wounded but alive. It rose above the trees, flew fifty yards, and died unseen among the branches.

“Luck is against me,” Martin muttered, disheartened.

But he readied a third arrow, scanning the glade. Suddenly, a rustling behind him made him turn. Too late—he saw a noble deer cross the open space, but could not draw his bow. He slammed his weapon down in frustration.

Then, swift as shadow, a long-spotted animal darted past—its belly brushing the earth. Martin froze. It was the Duke’s leopard.

“The hunters won’t be far,” he said. “And I must not be seen. Gerard will go supperless tonight.”

He plunged into the woods, following the trail of buck and leopard—his usual path home. But soon he heard something strange: violent rustling, heavy trampling ahead. He hurried forward.

There, in the dim light, the leopard was atop the buck, tearing at its flesh with teeth and claws. The deer ran in a frantic circle, blood streaming from its hide.

Martin made a desperate decision. He drew his arrow to the head and loosed it. The shaft buried itself deep in the buck’s side. Though the leopard clung to its back, the deer bounded high and fell dead.

The leopard continued feeding, unaware. Martin hoped it would gorge itself and leave. He waited. Then, stepping forward, he placed a hand on the buck’s leg.

The leopard snarled—a sound like thunder in the stillness—and stopped sucking. She turned her fierce gaze upon him.

Martin knew a tale: wild beasts cannot endure the human eye. So he stood tall, fixed his stare upon her. The leopard met his gaze, unblinking, furious.

Still he held her look. Slowly, she charged—yelling, eyes blazing, jaws wide. Martin barely caught her by the throat before her teeth reached his face. One claw ripped his shoulder; the other, aimed at his cheek, snagged in the leather hood he wore over his head.

He fought to keep her mouth from his face, gripping her throat with iron hands. She tore at his shoulder—like blunt reaping hooks grinding through flesh. The pain was terrible. But instead of breaking his spirit, it fired his rage. He gnashed his teeth, squeezing harder. Their eyes burned into each other—his nearly as wild as hers.

She struggled wildly, dragging his hood over his face, blinding him. Her claw tore free, leaving a wound. Yet he held fast.

Then—her tail, raised high, began to fall.

“Aha!” Martin shouted, tightening his grip. Soon, her body lost strength. He held her until all motion ceased. Then he threw her to the ground, gasping, and removed his cowl.

The leopard lay silent, tongue lolling, paw bloody. For the first time, fear seized Martin.

“I am a dead man,” he whispered. “I have slain the Duke’s leopard.”

He quickly gathered leaves and covered her body. Then he shouldered the buck and staggered home, leaving a trail of blood—his own and the deer’s.

He burst into Peter’s house, a figure of horror—bleeding, stained with gore—and flung the carcass down.

“There,” he said. “No questions. Just broil me a steak. I’m faint.”

Margaret did not see his wound. She thought the blood was all from the deer. She rushed to the fire, while the soldier bound his own injury. Soon, Gerard joined them, and they sat down to a feast of broiled venison.

They laughed and talked, merry under the influence of Schiedam wine that Gerard had brought. Martin, warmed by drink, told them how he’d killed the deer—though he left out the leopard. They all laughed at the tale.

Then—silence.

Margaret’s face went pale. Her eyes locked onto the window, trembling. She pointed, unable to speak.

At the glass, crouched in the twilight, was a dark shape—eyes like green fire.

It was the leopard.

They stood frozen, transfixed by those glowing eyes. Then, from the woods, came a single deep bay.

Martin trembled. “They’ve lost her. They’ve sent muzzled hounds. They’ll find her here. And the venison. Good-bye, friends. Martin Wittenhaagen ends here.”

Gerard grabbed his bow and handed it to the soldier. “Be a man,” he cried. “Shoot her. Throw her into the woods before they arrive. Who will know?”

More hounds barked—closer now.

“Curse her!” Martin roared. “I spared her once. Now she dies—or I do—or both.” He raised his bow, drew the arrow to his ear.

“Nay! Nay!” cried Margaret, seizing the arrow.

It snapped in half. The pieces fell to either side of the bow.

At once, the air filled with the baying of dogs—they were hot on the scent.

“What have you done, wench?” Martin cried. “You’ve put the halter around my neck.”

“No!” she shouted. “I’ve saved you! Stand back from the window—both of you! Your knife—quick!”

She tore the long-pointed blade from his belt and darted from the room.

Now the house was surrounded—dogs barking, men shouting. The leopard remained motionless, her eyes still burning in the dusk.
