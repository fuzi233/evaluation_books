When I had done with my visit to my brother, and my wife with our child were again safe under my mother’s vigilant roof at Richmond, I rode across to his Excellency at his country quarters near Williamsburg. The roads already had that charged hush which comes before storms: not silent, but listening. You could hear the frogs in the ditches and the saw of cicadas in the pines as plainly as a sermon—nature keeping her own order while men prepared to unmake theirs.

Everywhere in the colonies, as far as our couriers and newspapers could carry tidings, there were open drills, secret meetings, lists taken, powder counted, talk sharpened into purpose. Virginia had appointed her Delegates for the General Congress to sit at Philadelphia—names which, since then, have gone abroad like stamped coin. In Massachusetts, people and King’s troops stood nearly muzzle to muzzle. In Maryland and Pennsylvania we chose to believe the temper more loyal; in the Carolinas and Georgia, they said, the Crown might still reckon on a stout majority. And for our own province—how could one suppose the Old Dominion would fling off the ancient habit of allegiance? Had we not gentry proud of Cavalier blood, and old houses where the King’s health was pledged as dutifully as bread was broken?

Round the Governor the Loyalists swarmed, loud as locusts, full of those brave words that come so readily when a man’s boots are clean and his sword unblooded. At their tables the air was thick with claret and candle-grease, roast venison, pepper, and the salt edge of ham—those domestic perfumes that seem to promise stability, even while the talk at the board promises destruction. They drank his Majesty’s health again and again, the glasses clinking like little bells of certainty, and all the while they thundered that, when the time came, they would draw steel and scatter the rascally rebels like chaff.

In such company I was forced to hear my brother Harry’s name handled as if it were some tainted meat set among wholesome victuals. His wife, they said—too often with that relish men take in blaming women—had led him into these extreme opinions. He was infatuated by his friendship for the gentleman of Mount Vernon; he followed that man’s judgement in all things; he was committing himself day by day farther in a desperate course.

“This is your friend,” they cried to me; “this is the man your Excellency favoured—he that ate at your board, that had your special confidence!”

Much of it was true, and yet what they called treachery was, in those first months, very often a desperate loyalty of another kind: a longing to remain faithful, to strain every last sinew of reconciliation before the dreadful alternative—revolt and separation—became the only road left. But my companions had no palate for such distinctions. They wanted their politics like their punch: strong, sweet, and unquestioned.

They urged me to intercede. Was it yet too late? Would my influence reclaim Harry, if the Government offered him a command? They spoke, too, with a kind of patronage toward my mother and me—our loyalty, our service, should purchase forgiveness for my brother’s “benighted conduct.”

Williamsburg was all alternation: triumph in the morning, gloom by supper; fury at the rebels, then anxious waiting for aid from home; boasts that England’s power would soon crush the provinces, then whispers of doubt that slid along the table like spilled oil. I promised what I could. I wrote to Hal, though with little hope, setting forth again the arguments I had used before.

My mother wrote too.

In truth I expected less from her letter than from mine; she had a habit, in any dispute, of driving her Scripture like a stake. She cited the powers that be, threatened punishments, and—worst of all—hinted at his “subjection” to his wife, which is the one accusation sure to make an honest husband more stubborn. She did not show me what she wrote, perhaps wisely; but she did show me his answer.

To my surprise, Hal replied with texts of his own—scores of them—laid down with neatness, temper, and a kind of polish I should once have doubted him capable of. It had his cadence, but not always his hand. I learned soon enough that he had taken counsel of Mr. Belman, the New Richmond clergyman, who had declared for the Whig side and preached and printed against Hagan—our Hagan—so that, I fear, Belman carried the better of the controversy, as an able cook carries a dish by knowing when to salt and when to let the meat speak.

My own letters he did not answer.

Pressed yet again by the friends of Government, I wrote—most imprudently—that I would visit him by week’s end at Fanny’s Mount. I rode there and found only my sister. She received me with perfect cordiality, bright as a hearth when you come in from rain, and told me that Hal had gone far toward the Blue Mountains to look at horses and would be away—she did not know how long.

There are evasions as plain as closed shutters at noon. I understood. There was no hope.

“My dear,” I said, “as far as I can judge from the signs of the times, the train that has been laid these years must have a match put to it before long. Harry is riding away. God knows to what end.”

“The Lord prosper the righteous cause, Sir George,” she said, without wavering.

“Amen, with all my heart,” I answered. “You and he speak as Americans; I as an Englishman. Tell him from me, that when anything in the course of nature shall happen to our mother, I have enough for me and mine in England, and shall resign all our land here in Virginia to him.”

“You don’t mean that, George?” she cried; and her eyes brightened as if I had set a dish before her she had long desired.

“Well, to be sure, it is but right and fair,” she added presently. “Why should you, who are the eldest but by an hour, have everything? a palace and lands in England—the plantation here—the title—and children—and my poor Harry none? But ’tis generous all the same—leastways handsome and proper, and I didn’t expect it of you; and you don’t take after your mother in this, Sir George, that you don’t, nohow. Give my love to sister Theo!”

She offered her cheek to kiss, and I rode away with a heaviness I could not put off. With such a woman beside him—so quick in feeling, so sure in judgement—how was I to hope to convert my brother?

I went back to his Excellency, and we agreed it was time to arm in earnest. The Court and officials were agitated and excited; and, to my mind, needlessly savage in their abuse of the “wicked Whigs,” shouting Old England for ever as if volume could conjure ships across an ocean. But they were eager for the day when parties might meet hand to hand and they could ride the rebels down.

In Council his Excellency thanked me, and I engaged to raise a body of men for the Crown. Thus began the corps afterwards called the Westmoreland Defenders, of which I had the honour to be appointed Colonel.

The announcement was printed in the Government Gazette. We had no trouble filling the officer lists; gentlemen are never scarce where titles are to be had. But recruits—plain men with hoes in their hands and families at table—came in slowly, and vanished quickly, like dew when the sun comes high. Still, friend Hagan stepped forward at once to offer himself chaplain. My mother gave us our colours, and went about the country with a zeal that made her small figure seem larger than life. And the most indefatigable recruiter of all was my old tutor, little Mr. Dempster—who had been out as a boy on the Jacobite side in Scotland, and now travelled even into the Carolinas among the children of his banished comrades who had once worn Prince Charles’s white cockade, and who, in this quarrel too, showed themselves stubbornly loyal.

Meanwhile Hal’s “horse journey” carried him far beyond the Blue Mountains—on to Annapolis and Baltimore, and from Baltimore to Philadelphia, where a second General Congress was sitting. The very route of his going seemed like a thread being drawn through the colonies, stitching them into something new.

And then, as all the almanacs now record, Lexington happened. The first shots were fired in the war that would end in the independence of our native country.

We still protested loyalty to his Majesty; we still spoke of petitions and rights; yet men assembled with arms round about Boston—twenty thousand of these “loyal petitioners,” cannon among them, helped out of Government stores. Mr. Arnold began his career with boldness—daring and burglarious—forcing fort doors as if the King’s locks were mere inconveniences. Three generals from Bond Street, with reinforcements, sailed to help Mr. Gage out of his ugly place. Presently the armies were engaged, and our British generals commenced their conquest and pacification with the “glorious blunder” of Breed’s Hill, after which they fortified and waited while Congress deliberated who should command the confederated forces.

We all know on whom their choice fell.

Of the Virginian regiment that marched to join the new General-in-Chief, one was commanded by Henry Esmond Warrington, late a Captain in his Majesty’s service; and by his side rode his little wife, of whose bravery we were to hear much. I confess I was glad she had quitted Virginia; had she remained after her husband’s departure, my mother would infallibly have gone over to give her battle, and I was thankful that such a domestic civil war was spared our history.

The rush of our farmers and country-folk went northward. Virginia itself was flattered that a Virginian gentleman had been chosen for the principal command. Across the provinces the news of blood drawn at Lexington ran like fire through dry corn. Men denounced British cruelty with a passion that did not pause to weigh words. The soldier, indeed, was but doing his duty; he was met and resisted by armed men who meant to prevent him from taking what he deemed his own. But anger has its own language, and soon all men had chosen their side. Breed’s Hill grew in the imagination into a mountain visible from every colony, with Liberty, Victory, Glory blazing upon its summit. If farmers and ploughmen could stand against these dreaded troops, if doctors and lawyers could out-general famous officers, then—so men boasted—Britons might conquer the world, but their children across the Atlantic could match and conquer Britons.

For bravery and for vainglory I know not which deserves the palm. We laugh at our French neighbours for gasconade; but for steady self-esteem and indomitable confidence, who rivals Britons save their own descendants?

In our parts, the people took the people’s side. My Westmoreland Defenders—never robust in numbers—thinned further after the news of battle, and thinned worst of all because of the conduct of our Governor, which enraged no one more than his own partisans. For months in meeting after meeting, over thousands of bumpers, it had been agreed and applauded that his lordship would plant the King’s standard and summon loyal gentlemen to rally round it. I could name many a man—now a snug officer under the United States—whom I have heard hiccup out a prayer to perish under the folds of his country’s flag, or roar challenges to absent traitors with the rebel army.

But let bygones be bygones. Public history has recorded this: that his lordship—peer of Scotland, the King’s representative—who invited all lieges to join the standard, was the first to put it in his pocket and flee to his ships out of danger. He would not leave them save as a pirate at midnight to burn and destroy. We loyal gentry were left ashore, committed, exposed to greater peril because of the weakness and cruelty of him who ought to have led us.

It was early June. Orchards and gardens bloomed with their plenty; the smell of apple blossom, wet earth, and young leaves lay over the fields, mingled with kitchen smoke and the yeasty breath of bread. A week before I had been in Williamsburg, exchanging compliments with his Excellency, planning future movements, and parting with hands shaken hard and vincere aut mori—conquer or die—on our lips as if words could serve for powder.

Our small family sat in Richmond, as we did daily, talking of affairs in the north and of the quarrels between our Assembly and his Excellency, when Hagan burst into the parlour—Hagan, always as if he carried a whole bell-tower in his chest—and cried, “Have you heard the news of the Governor?”

“Has he dissolved the Assembly again, and put that scoundrel Patrick Henry in irons?” my mother demanded, her voice sharp enough to slice bread.

“No such thing. His lordship with his lady and family have left their palace privately at night. They are on board a man-of-war off York, whence my lord has sent a despatch to the Assembly, begging them to continue their sitting, and announcing that he himself had only quitted his Government House out of fear of the fury of the people.”

What was to become of sheep when the shepherd ran?

The House of Assembly sent entreaties as pathetic as any prayer: they guaranteed him safety if he would but land; they implored him to appear among them, if only to pass bills and transact necessary business. No. The man-of-war was his seat of government, and he desired his House of Commons to wait upon him there. This was planting the King’s standard with a vengeance.

He had left us. The Assembly ruled in his stead. A rabble followed the fugitive viceroy to his ships, and negroes deserted from plantations to join this other deserter. He landed here and there in darkness, seizing and burning with an alacrity that matched the most lawless of our opponents. He invited runaway negroes to his standard; he sent an ambassador to Indians begging them to join him. When he came ashore it was to burn and destroy; when people resisted, as at Norfolk and Hampton, he retreated to his ships again.

Even my mother, after that flight, was scared at the shape of affairs and doubted the speedy putting down of rebellion. The arming of negroes was, in her judgement, the most cowardly blow of all. Loyal gentry were ruined, robbed of what property they had. From Richmond and Castlewood a score of our worst hands deserted to his lordship’s fleet; some were slain; a couple were hung by the enemy for plunder and robbery committed while with that precious “army.”

Because her property was injured, and the King’s chief officer proved an imbecile, would Madam Esmond desert Royalty and Honour? Never. She was never so prodigiously dignified, so enthusiastically loyal, as after she heard of his lordship’s defection. Neighbours who thought quite otherwise listened without unkindness. Her oddities were known through the province; young men, I fear, would “smoke” her—draw her out to tell stories of her Marquis father, the splendour of her family, the old France that lived in her tongue. Yet along with the pomp, her charities were remembered; and many a “rebel,” as she called them, had a sneaking regard for the little Tory lady.

As for me—the Colonel of Westmoreland Defenders, a colonel without much of a regiment—I escaped some serious danger in consequence of disputes I was known to have had with Lord Dunmore.

After he had burned the stores at Hampton and issued his proclamation calling the negroes to his standard, I went on board his ship and took the liberty to remonstrate regarding both measures. I implored him to return to Williamsburg, where hundreds—thousands, I hoped—would be ready to defend him to the last extremity. In my remonstrance I spoke freely; or rather, I suspect my contempt showed itself in my manner as plainly as rancid butter shows itself on the tongue.

His lordship flew into a rage, declared I was a rebel like all the rest, and ordered me under arrest on board his own ship.

In my quality of militia officer—I commonly wore a red coat then, to show I bore the King’s colour—I begged for a court-martial immediately. Turning to two officers who had witnessed our altercation, I desired them to remember all that had passed. These gentlemen, no doubt, thought as I did about our chief’s behaviour; and the interview ended in my going ashore without a guard.

The story travelled, and improved in the telling, as stories do when they pass from mouth to mouth like a dish being seasoned anew at every table. Among the Whig gentry it was said I had spoken my mind manfully; no Whig, they declared, could have uttered sentiments more liberal. When riots broke out in Richmond and many Loyalists were in peril, fleeing to the ships, my mother’s house and property were never endangered, nor her family insulted. We were still in that earlier stage when reconciliation was thought possible.

“Ah! if all the Tories were like you,” a distinguished Whig once said to me, “we and the people at home should soon come together again.”

That was before the Fourth of July—before the Declaration which made reconcilement impossible.

Afterwards, when rancour thickened, motives less creditable were assigned. It was said I chose to be a Liberal Tory because I was a cunning fox and meant to keep my estate whichever way events went. And I must add that this opinion of me has obtained in high quarters at home too, where a profound regard for my own interest has been supposed to have guided my conduct during the unhappy troubles.

There were, however, two or three people in the world—since I had not told my mother how I meant to cede to my brother all my life-interest in our American property—who knew I had no mercenary motive. It was not worth undeceiving others. What is life worth, if a man must feel himself forever on the track—la piste—of calumnies?

To this day I do not quite know how it happened that my mother, that notorious Loyalist, remained for several years undisturbed at Castlewood, save that now and then a stray troop of Continentals was quartered upon her. I cannot say for certain, though I can guess shrewdly.

Madam Fanny, after a campaign before Boston, came back to Fanny’s Mount, leaving her Colonel. My modest Hal, until the end of the war, would accept no higher rank, believing he could be more useful commanding a regiment than a division. And after Fanny’s return, it was remarkable how her old asperity toward my mother seemed to melt away, and what an affection she showed for her—and for all the property. She was great friends with the Governor and with influential gentlemen of the new Assembly. Madam Esmond was harmless, they said; and for her son’s sake, who was bravely battling for his country, her errors should be lightly visited.

Thus my mother remained unharmed for years—except for heavy requisitions, which of course she had to pay—until the day when redcoats appeared about our house, and then, at last, serious evil came near enough to be heard in the night like distant drums.
