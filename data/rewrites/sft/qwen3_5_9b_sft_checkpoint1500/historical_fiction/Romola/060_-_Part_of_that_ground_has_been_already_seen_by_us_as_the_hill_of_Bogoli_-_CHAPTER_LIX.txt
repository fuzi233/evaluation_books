The morning burned with a crisp, early clarity as Romola resumed her journey toward San Marco. The night prior, through the mediation of Fra Salvestro, she had secured a rare audience with Fra Girolamo within the convent's chapter house. Such meetings were the exception rather than the rule; Savonarola guarded his secluded life with iron resolve, fearing even the slightest pretext for slander, and when permission was finally granted, it was stripped of all mystique. Hence, the hour chosen was deliberate—a time when the outer cloisters bustled with ordinary visitors, ensuring the meeting appeared as mundane as any other administrative duty.

Romola resolved to traverse the beating heart of the city first, seeking to gauge the feverish pulse of public sentiment. Everywhere she looked, the air vibrated with the clamor of gratuitous debate. Every loggia, every shadowed corner of the piazza, every shopfront serving as a gossip station, hummed with a languorous trade that only made political discourse more frantic. It was evident that the factions supporting and opposing the execution of the conspirators were racing against the clock, determined to harness the three-day interval to crystallize the popular mood. Handbills fluttered like black birds overhead; some shouted in massive, bold letters the stark choice between justice and the Republic's ruin, while others, equally enlarged, screamed for the observance of the law and the granting of the appeal. Surrounding these jagged islands of capital letters were vast lakes of smaller script, offering arguments deemed unnecessary to read aloud. At that time, in the first intoxicating flush of triumph following the invention of printing, the prevailing belief was that no persuasion could match the convincing power of a question-begging phrase printed in giant type.

Yet, Romola craved the arguments hidden in the smaller print. Though compelled to hurry, her gaze darted anxiously around her, desperate to snatch a copy before the wind scattered them or the *sbirri* tore them from the walls. For miles, she found only flyers clutched by eager hands or pinned high above reach, until finally, slipping behind San Giovanni with a quickened stride to evade the crowds, she spotted Bratti. He held a stack of pamphlets, peddling them for small coins to the passersby. She knew the humble rhythms of Florence too well for Bratti to be a stranger, and turning to him, she commanded, "Have you two sorts of handbills, Bratti? Let me have them quickly."

"Two sorts," Bratti replied, slowly separating the damp sheets with a deliberation that tested Romola's patience. "'There's 'Law,' and there's 'Justice.'"

"And which sells faster?" she pressed.

"'Justice,'" Bratti admitted. "'Justice' flies off the shelves, so I hiked the price to two danari. But then I recalled that the 'Law' is just as fine merchandise and deserves a fair charge; folk do not value cheap things. If I sold the 'Law' for one danaro, I'd be wronging it. And I'm a fair trader. 'Law' or 'Justice,' it makes no difference to me; they're both good wares. I acquired them gratis, and I'll sell them at a proper profit. But you'll need more than a single pair?"

"No, no," Romola interrupted, unfolding a white *quattrino*. "Here for both." She folded the bills and fled.

Soon she reached the outer cloisters of San Marco, where Fra Salvestro awaited beneath the arcade, oblivious to her approach as he chatted with lay visitors. Under the current regime, which favored the Frate, the timidity that had gripped the faithful after the initial shock of excommunication was slowly dissolving. Among the listeners sat Andrea Cambini, a known satellite of Francesco Valori, gesturing emphatically as he expounded some point. Fra Salvestro listened with an air of trivial curiosity that betrayed how deeply he cared for the news and how lightly for its substance—a trait that always grated on Romola. Yet now, the disjointed fragments reaching her ears suggested Cambini was discussing the fate of the conspirators. Choosing not to interrupt, she waited until she had captured Fra Salvestro's attention, then turned sharply toward the chapter-house door. A lay brother opened it for her, closing it softly behind her.

Inside, the room was adorned with those sad frescoes that had once seemed to weep with her over the death of her brother Dino. Inevitably, a trace of that sorrow returned, but the intensity of her present dread rendered the memory less a recollection than a vague recurrence of impressions, blending with her agitating fears. Her anxiety felt like a resurrection of the fierce longing she had once brought to this spot—to find solace only to be met by marble rigidity. She gave no space to reflection, immediately unrolling the handbills, hoping to read them before Fra Girolamo's arrival. By the time she finished the tract advocating for the observance of the law, the door swung open, and she doubled the papers, standing expectant.

When the Friar entered, she knelt, adhering to custom, but rose instantly as he offered a benedictory greeting, standing opposite him at a respectful distance. Seclusion since his excommunication meant this was the longest interval since she had last seen him. Late months had visibly etched deeper lines into his face, marking the toll of overtaxed intellect and physical austerity. Yet, Romola sensed a shift more profound than mere weariness. Was the serene elevation and pure human fellowship that once moved her spirit absent? Or was it the gnawing sense of his division from her regarding her godfather that stirred dormant wells of alienation, blurring her vision? Perhaps both currents ran deep. Human relations are often dictated by such coincident flows; the unforgivable word or deed rarely strikes until affection or reverence has been eroded by the strain of repeated apologies.

Indeed, Savonarola's gaze held a hardness born of egotistical preoccupation. He divined that her visit concerned the conspirators, a subject on which he had already silenced inner voices that threatened to roar again if encouraged from without. Seated in his cell, correcting the sheets of his *Triumph of the Cross*, it was far easier to rest upon a resolution of neutrality.

"It is a matter of grave import, doubtless, for which you sought见我, my daughter," he began, his tone gentle primarily due to self-control rather than immediate inclination. "I know you do not usually burden yourself with trifles."

"Father, you know the weight before I speak," Romola replied, pouring forth her plea as if forgetting all else. "You know what I cherish—it is the life of the old man I love best in the world. The thought of him has traveled with the thought of my father since I can recall daylight. That is my warrant for coming, even if my journey proved superfluous. Perhaps it does: perhaps you have already decided that your sway over human hearts shall be used to deny the Florentines a right you yourselves helped secure."

"I meddle not with the functions of the State, my daughter," Fra Girolamo stated, strongly disinclined to reignite a debate he had exhausted internally. "I have preached and labored that Florence possess a good government, for sound governance is requisite for the perfection of Christian life; but I keep my hands from specific affairs which are the office of seasoned citizens to administer."

"Surely, father—" Romola broke off. The word escaped almost impulsively, but she halted, struck by the counter-agitation of feeling herself standing in accusation toward the man who had been her guide and strength. In rebelling, she bruised her own reverence.

Savonarola was too sharp not to divine the conflict arresting her spirit—too noble, deliberately, to assume in calm speech the self-justifying evasion into which he was often hurried in public by the crowding impulses of the orator.

"Speak what is in your heart; proceed, my daughter," he said, standing with arms crossed, looking at her with quiet expectation.

"I was about to say, father, that this matter surpasses in gravity many issues about which I have heard you preach fervently. If it were yours to urge that men condemned for offenses against the State retain the right to appeal to the Great Council—and if counting it a glory to have won that right for them—you cannot fail to declare yourself against its denial to the first men who need it? Surely this touches the Christian life more intimately than whether you foresaw the Dauphin's death or Pisa's conquest."

A subtle movement, like a subdued sign of pain, twisted Savonarola's strong lips before he spoke.

"My daughter, I speak as I am permitted to speak. I am not master of the times when I may become the vessel of knowledge beyond common lights. In this instance, I possess no illumination beyond what wisdom grants to those charged with state safety. Regarding the law of Appeal against the Six Votes, I labored to ensure its passage so no Florentine might suffer loss of life or goods through the private hatred of a few momentarily in power. However, these five men, who sought to overthrow a free government and restore a corrupt tyrant, have been condemned with the assent of a large assembly of their fellow-citizens. They initially refused to bring their cause before the Great Council. Thus, they have forfeited the right to appeal."

"How can they have lost it?" Romola cried. "It is the right to appeal against condemnation, and they have never been condemned until now! Forgive me, father, but _this_ is private hatred that denies them the appeal; _this_ is the violence of the few that frightens others.
