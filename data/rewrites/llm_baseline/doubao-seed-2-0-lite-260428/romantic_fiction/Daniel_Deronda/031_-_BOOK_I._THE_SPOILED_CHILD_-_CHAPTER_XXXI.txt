The day Gwendolen Harleth became Mrs. Grandcourt dawned soft and gilded, the low sun kissing a thin, sharp frost that crisped the edges of every leaf along the Pennicote lanes. Half the village had turned out to line the winding path to the church, craning their necks to catch a glimpse of the bridal party, and even the air hummed with the thrill of a match far grander than any the little parish had seen in a generation. The rector’s old friend had traveled in to perform the ceremony, while the rector himself walked Gwendolen down the aisle, stepping in for the father she had never known.

Every onlooker remarked that only two faces in the procession bore any trace of tears: Mrs. Davilow’s, her delicate lids pink and puffy as if she had sobbed half the night long, and quiet Anna’s, fidgeting with the lace of her bridesmaid’s gown. No one found the mother’s grief odd — to lose the daughter who had been the bright, unchallenged center of her whole world, especially after all the recent hardship of their near ruin, was a parting that would wring any heart. But no one could quite puzzle out why Anna looked so pale, when Gwendolen’s good fortune was lifting all their families’ worries at a stroke.

Everyone else glowed in the light of the occasion, none more so than the bride herself. The village agreed unanimously that her tall, proud carriage was fit for a lady of title, even if her cheeks lacked the soft rose bloom most folks expected of a wedding day. But the groom himself was pale, too — as the miller’s wife, Mrs. Girdle, pointed out to her 14-year-old daughter, he had the same sallow, quiet complexion as her own husband, so their match was all the more perfect. “He must be daft about her,” the village whispered. “Only hope he never flings it in her face how close she came to going off to work as a governess, and her ma moving into that tiny hovel at Sawyer’s Cottage — that’s a story we’ve all gossiped over for months.”

Her wide-eyed daughter protested that no high-born gentleman would ever treat his wife badly, but Mrs. Girdle huffed and set her straight. “Oh, child, men’s men, gentle or simple, they’re all much of a muchness. My own mother was lady’s maid up to Squire Pelton’s, and she saw him drag hounds and a long whip into his wife’s chambers just to frighten her half out of her wits, back in her day.”
“That’s ill luck to talk of on a wedding day, Mrs. Girdle,” the tailor cut in. “Quarrels might end with the whip, but they start with the tongue, and we all know the women have the lion’s share of that.”
“Lord gave it to us to use, I reckon,” Mrs. Girdle sniffed. “Never meant the men to have it all their own way.”
“Word from the groom’s groom up at Offendene,” the tailor went on, “is that Mr. Grandcourt barely speaks a word. Everything gets done just the way he wants it without him ever having to order a soul.”
“Then all the more whip, mark my words,” Mrs. Girdle grunted. “Our Gwendolen’s got plenty of tongue, that one. Look — there they are, coming out the church steps together!”

Gwendolen’s long brown eyes glowed that day with a strange, electric lustre, her step lighter, her bearing more erect than any of the villagers had ever seen it. It was the same sharp, heightened thrill she had felt that night at the gambling table, when Daniel Deronda had stood watching her and she had begun to lose, every nerve strung tight to a point where even uneasiness felt like a kind of euphoria. The quiet pricks of her conscience, the faint, nagging memory of the promise she had broken, she brushed them off as one might swat at a gnat mid-triumph — nothing was going to stand in the way of the ambition she had nurtured through all those humiliated weeks when she had been forced to hunt for work to feed her family.
She could not, that morning, have said she regretted marrying Grandcourt. No hazy fear of the future could dim the bright, glowing fact that she was the center of every eye, the object of every envy, about to step into a life of luxury and power she had dreamed of since she was a small girl. She did not want for quiet, half-suppressed dread: the knowledge she was doing something wrong, the certainty that the woman whose life she had upended was right now burning with resentment against her, the sharp intuition that Deronda — who had seen right through her greed at the roulette table — must despise her now for selling herself to this man, the creeping terror that the tie she had once held loosely in her hand was now being slung around her neck like a noose. All that roiled in her chest like yeasty, frothing wine, but she crushed it down under her boot with a giddy, exultant defiance. She was playing for the highest stakes at the game of life, and even if she lost, she would lose with grandeur, not the small, shabby, penurious fate that had been waiting for her if she had said no. She was not the tearful, trembling bride of storybooks — she was a girl drunk on the heady fumes of her own ego, walking blind through a garden of illusions, with only a faint, unnameable under-tug of awareness that she was a little unsteady, a little too intoxicated to be walking straight.

“Thank God you bear it so well, my darling,” Mrs. Davilow whispered, trembling all over, once she had helped Gwendolen step out of her white bridal gown and into her soft traveling dress. All the mother’s strength was spent on holding back her sobs, which only made Gwendolen lean into the performance of triumph harder.
“Why, you’d have fussed just as much if I was leaving to go be governess to Mrs. Mompert’s brats, you dear, sad old thing,” Gwendolen laughed, cupping her mother’s pale cheeks in her hands, then stepping back to spread out her arms and show off her new outfit. “Look at me! I’m Mrs. Grandcourt now! What else could I be but everything you ever wanted me to be? You were half mad with vexation a few months back, convinced I’d throw this all away, remember?”
“Hush, hush, child, for heaven’s sake,” Mrs. Davilow hissed, clutching her wrists. “It just tears me apart to part with you. But I will bear anything, anything at all, if only you are happy.”
“Not gladly, mamma,” Gwendolen teased, shaking her head with a bright, wobbly smile. “Sorrow’s your favorite sauce — you can’t take any good thing without a dash of it. But you’ll sit at home and gush over all my grand things, you’ll see: the big houses, the horses, the diamonds, the trips to court, all the titles we’ll climb to. And I’ll love you more than anyone else in the whole wide world, always.”
“My sweet girl — I won’t even be jealous if you love your husband more than me. He ought to be first in your life, now.”
Gwendolen made a playful, pouty grimace. “That’s a pretty ridiculous expectation. But I won’t treat him badly, not unless he deserves it.”
They clung to each other then, and for a second Gwendolen couldn’t hold back a stray tear, her voice cracking as she whispered, “I wish you were coming with me, mamma.” The faint damp on her long lashes only made her look more glowing when she stepped out to take Grandcourt’s hand and climb into the waiting carriage.

The rector stopped by to give her a final, solemn blessing, then turned back to Mrs. Davilow and said, half reassuring, half reverent: “Count your blessings, Fanny. She’s in a position far better than any of us ever dared hope for. Few women are ever chosen so wholly for their own sake. You are a happy mother.”

The train carried the newlyweds fifty miles west, and the bright morning sky had clouded over completely by the time they pulled into the little station near Ryelands. It was nearly twilight when their carriage turned through the park gates, and Gwendolen, leaning out the window, drank in every inch of the grand estate she now called hers: the long, winding drive lined with dark evergreens, backed by gnarled, ancient grey oak trunks, wide rolling grassy hills dotted with dark clumps of trees, until at last she spotted the grand white house, set against a wooded hill, the line of its terrace balustrade curving gently in the dim light.
She had chattered nonstop the whole train ride, bright and vivacious as a frisky kitten, ignoring every unspoken shift in their dynamic, turning every soft attempt Grandcourt made to take her hand into a playful, two-handed squeeze, too wired to sit still. Now, as they neared the house, her chatter died away unbidden, her heart thudding hard against her ribs. She could almost see the girl she had been at 14, daydreaming about living in a place like this, walking down long, grand corridors under painted ceilings, with servants who bowed and did exactly what she told them to, no one ever daring to point out that she wasn’t born a great lady. Was that all really hers now? The thrill tangled with a nameless, cold prickle of dread, the kind that comes when you’re about to open a door you’ve spent years dreaming of, and you can’t shake the feeling something terrible is waiting on the other side.
“Here we are, home at last,” Grandcourt said, and leaned in to kiss her softly on the lips. She hardly noticed, numb with the flood of new sensations, after the long, overexcited day, a soft, foggy numbness settling over her.

The hall blazed with lamplight, warm and full of thick carpets, full length portraits, marble statues, quiet attentive servants. Gwendolen’s new maid was led off by the housekeeper, and Grandcourt led her down a corridor scented with jasmine wax, to a little sitting room glowing with soft firelight.
“These are our private dens,” he told her. “Rest here a little while before dinner. We’ll dine early tonight.” He pressed her hand to his lips and left her, more hopelessly, tenderly in love than he had ever imagined he could be.

Gwendolen tossed off her hat and mantle, sank into a chair by the hearth, and saw her own reflection multiplied in the glass panels lining the walls, against soft pale green satin upholstery. The housekeeper hovered in the doorway for a moment, and Gwendolen, impatient to be alone, told her to send her maid Hudson in when she’d laid out her dinner gown, and that she needn’t wait around.
“Madam, there is a sealed packet here, Mr. Grandcourt’s orders that no one’s hands but yours should touch it, when you were alone,” the housekeeper said, stepping forward to set a small wrapped parcel on her lap. “The man who brought it said it was a wedding gift he’d arranged for you to get as a surprise, and that he wasn’t to know it had arrived till you wore it for dinner. I hope you’ll forgive me for lingering, madam, I wanted to make sure I handed it to you properly.”

The doors clicked shut behind her, and Gwendolen’s fingers curled around the packet. Of course — it was the diamonds Grandcourt had promised her, the set he had said was locked away for their wedding day. She’d been half hoping he’d give them to her, a little treat to play with, a bright, glittering diversion to break the strange, soft languor stealing over her.
She tore off the layers of paper, the velvet jewel case inside, and flipped it open. The diamonds blazed up at her, catching the firelight — but there was a letter, tucked right on top of the gems, in a handwriting she recognized instantly.
It was like an adder had risen up out of the velvet to strike her. Her heart gave a wild, hard leap that left her breathless, her hands shaking so hard the thin paper rattled as she unfolded it. The words burned into her eyes, sharp as if they had been printed in ink of fire:
*These diamonds, once given with all my young love to Lydia Glasher, are passed on now to you. You broke your word to me, to steal the life that was mine. You think you will be happy now, as I once was, think you will bear his beautiful children to push mine out of his sight. But God is too just for that. The heart of the man you married died a long time ago. His only real young love was mine. You cannot take that from me, even if you have stolen everything else. I am the grave where your chance of happiness is buried, right next to mine. You had your warning, and you chose to throw it all away to hurt me, to hurt my children. He was going to marry me, at last. He would have, if you had not broken your promise. You will get your punishment. I wish for it with every part of my soul. Will you run to show him this letter, to turn him against me, to ruin my children and me further? Will you stand before him tonight with these diamonds glinting on your neck, and carry my words in both your hearts? Do you think he will let you complain when he makes you miserable? You took him with your eyes wide open. The wrong you chose to do to me will be your curse.*

Gwendolen read the words over and over, as if trapped in a penance spell, until a wave of wild, terrified panic crashed over her. She lunged forward, hurling the letter straight into the roaring hearth. It caught fire instantly, curling up into ash before she could think twice, but the movement sent the jewel case slipping from her lap, the diamonds rolling across the rug. She didn’t even glance at them. She fell back into her chair, limp, helpless, her lips and hands trembling so hard she could not move. She sat there for what felt like hours, the words of the letter repeating in her head, over and over, the diamonds glinting like shards of broken ice scattered at her feet. The gems she had dreamed of wearing were poisoned, and the poison had already seeped deep into her bones.

A soft tap at the door cut through the silence, and Grandcourt stepped in, dressed for dinner. The sight of him jolted every frayed nerve in her body past breaking point. Gwendolen opened her mouth and screamed, again and again, high, sharp, hysterical sobs tearing out of her throat. He had expected to find her laughing, all dressed and ready for dinner. Instead he saw her, deathly pale, screaming as if a monster stood right before her, the scattered diamonds glinting all over the floor at her feet.
Somewhere, the Furies had followed them across the threshold, and stepped straight over the line of their new home.
