A month after the Carnival, near the close of March, Tito descended the marble steps of the Old Palace upon an urgent mission to San Marco. Yet, driven by some unspoken instinct, he eschewed the direct path—a mere slight bend—and chose instead a circuitous route through the Piazza di Santa Croce. There, he anticipated a throng emerging from the church following the early sermon.

Indeed, the great church of Santa Croce had lately become the arena for the largest audiences of the Lenten season. Savonarola's voice, once thunderous within San Marco, had been silenced by a hostile Signoria obeying a fresh mandate from the Pope. A threat of immediate interdict hung over the city unless the "wretched worm" and "monstrous idol" were forbidden to preach and summoned to Rome to beg pardon. Thus, while the friar himself fell silent, his detractors flourished. Next to listening to Fra Girolamo, the most stirring Lenten pastime was witnessing him torn apart and vilified. This fervor peaked at Santa Croce, where the appointed Franciscan preacher had proposed a dramatic climax: walking through the fire alongside Fra Girolamo. The Dominican had scoffed at the notion, claiming his prophetic doctrine would only be proven by a miracle at the proper time; yet here, the Franciscan declared the time fit. Let Savonarola traverse the flames; if emerged unscathed, the divine origin of his teaching would be demonstrated, proving his prophecy fulfilled. If consumed, his falsehood would be laid bare. To leave no room for evasion, the Franciscan offered himself as a willing victim to secure the necessary premise for this high logic.

Savonarola, as per his custom, had ignored these pulpit assaults. However, the zealous preacher of Santa Croce was no ordinary friar, but Fra Francesco di Puglia. The previous year at Prato, he had challenged Savonarola's devoted follower, Fra Domenico, to a similar ordeal, only to be recalled by superiors before the contest became physical. Consequently, honest Fra Domenico, currently preaching Lenten sermons to the women of the Via del Cocomero, seized the gauntlet the moment he heard of the new challenge, declaring his readiness to walk through the fire with Fra Francesco. The populace, viewing this as a swift and facile method of settling the debate, grew deeply engrossed. Yet, when Tito arrived in the square, finding the crowds pouring forth in vast numbers, he noted that instead of scattering, many converged toward the entrance of the Franciscan monastery. Threading the crowd with a careless, leisurely gait, Tito kept a vigilant eye on that monastic door, expecting some object of interest to emerge.

What occupied the multitude was not a figure, but a large placard affixed by order of the Signoria, covered in legible official handwriting. Curiosity was somewhat dampened by the fact that the document was chiefly in Latin. Though nearly every man knew roughly what the placard contained, an appetite for precise knowledge gnawed at them, creating an irritating sense of their neighbors' ignorance regarding the learned tongue. An ear trained to Latin phrases from pulpit quotations offered little aid when confronted with written script; moreover, the spelling of the vernacular was in a chaotic state for the literate masses, and most gathered nearest the board lacked even that fragmentary knowledge.

"It is the Frate's doctrines he must prove by being burned," declared Goro, a prominent figure among the gazers. "The Signoria has taken charge, and the writing informs us. It is what the Padre has been preaching."

"Nay, Goro," countered a sleek shopkeeper with compassion. "Thou hast thy legs entangled in twisted hose. The Frate must prove his doctrines by *not* being burned; he walks through the fire and emerges sound and whole."

"Yes, yes," chimed in a young sculptor, wearing a white-streaked cap and tunic with jaunty pride. "But Fra Girolamo objects to walking through the fire. Already sound and whole, he sees no reason to undergo the ordeal to remain in that same condition. He leaves such trifling odds and ends to Fra Domenico."

"Then I say he flinches like a coward!" cried Goro in a wheezy treble. "Suffocation! That was what befell him at the Carnival. He had us all in the Piazza watching lightning strike him, and naught came of it."

"Hold thy bleating," retorted a tall shoemaker, stepping forward with bunches of slippers draped over his shoulders. "Seemeth to me, friend, that thou art as wise as a calf with water on its brain. The Frate will flinch from naught: perchance he saith nothing beforehand, but when the hour cometh, he shall walk through the fire without asking any grey-frock to keep him company. Yet, I would give a shoestring to know wherein this Latin lieth."

"There is so much thereof," sighed the shopkeeper, "save that I am fairly adept at guessing. Is there no scholar to be seen?" he added with a trace of disgust.

A general turning of heads revealed Tito approaching in their rear.

"Here is one," smiled the young sculptor, raising his cap.

"It is the secretary of the Ten," announced the shopkeeper, bowing low, though such marks of respect were rarely bestowed save upon the highest officials. "He goes to the convent, doubtless; make way." The reverence Tito elicited stemmed from the splendor and grace of his appearance; his black mantle, fastened with a gold fibula, resembled a royal robe, and his velvet cap seemed an entirely exceptional headgear. The hardening of his cheeks and mouth, the principal alteration in his visage since arriving in Florence, lent a sterner masculinity to his beauty. He raised his own cap instantly and addressed them—

"Thanks, my friend. I merely wished, as ye did, to behold what rests beneath this placard—ah, 'tis as I expected. I had been apprised that the government permits any who will to subscribe their name as a candidate to enter the fire—an act of liberality worthy of the magnificent Signoria, reserving, of course, the right to select. And doubtless many faithful souls will be eager to sign. What is it to enter the fire, to one whose faith is firm? A man fears the fire because he believes it will burn him; but if he believes the contrary?" Here Tito lifted his shoulders and paused oratorically—"for which reason I have never been wont to disbelieve the Frate when he hath said he would enter the fire to prove his doctrine. For in his place, if ye believed the fire would not burn ye, which of ye, my friends, would not step therein as readily as ye would walk along the dry bed of the Mugnone?"

As Tito scanned the assembly, a transformation swept over some of them, akin to the change in an eager dog invited to sniff something pungent. As the matter of burning became practical, few rashly committed themselves to a general view of the relation between faith and flame. The scene might have overwhelmed a gravity less under command than Tito's.

"Then, Messer Segretario," ventured the young sculptor, "it seemeth to me Fra Francesco is the greater hero, for he offereth to enter the fire for the truth, though he is certain the fire will consume him."

"I do not deny it," replied Tito, blandly. "Yet if it transpires that Fra Francesco is mistaken, he will have been burned for the wrong side, and the Church hath never reckoned such victims as martyrs. We must suspend our judgment until the trial hath truly taken place."

'Tis true, Messer Segretario,' murmured the shopkeeper with subdued impatience. 'But wilt thou favor us by interpreting the Latin?'

"Assuredly," said Tito, smiling. "It doth but express the conclusions or doctrines which the Frate specially teacheth, and which the trial by fire is to prove true or false. They are doubtless familiar to thee. First, that Florence—"

"Let us have the Latin bit by bit, and then tell us what it meaneth," urged the shoemaker, a frequent hearer of Fra Girolamo.

"Willingly," said Tito. "Ye shall then judge if I render the right meaning."

"Yes, yes; that's fair," assented Goro.

"_Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione_," recited Tito. "That is, the Church of God needeth purifying or regenerating."

"It is true," chorused several voices.

"That meaneth the priests ought to lead better lives; there needeth no miracle to prove that. That's what the Frate hath always been saying," observed the shoemaker.

"_Flagellabitur_," continued Tito. "That is, it shall be scourged. _Renovabitur_: it shall be purified. _Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur_: Florence also, after the scourging, shall be purified and shall prosper."

"That meaneth we shall get Pisa again," remarked the shopkeeper.

"And recover the wool from England as we used to do, I should hope," added an elderly gentleman in an old-fashioned beretta, who had remained silent until now. "There hath been scourging enough with the sinking of the trade."
