Aristide Valentin, head of the entire Paris Police force, was late to his own dinner party. His earliest arriving guests were reassured at once by Ivan, Valentin’s long-serving confidential servant: an old man with a jagged scar across one cheek, his face nearly the same ashen gray as his heavy moustache, who sat permanently at a small desk in the front entrance hall. The walls of that hall were lined from floor to ceiling with antique blades, pistols, and even crossbows—no mere decor, for Valentin’s house was as peculiar as his reputation.

It was a weathered old stone structure tucked behind high walls, tall poplars nearly brushing the Seine’s edge a few paces behind the garden. Its most notable quirk, one Valentin had designed for his own safety, was this: there was no way to step out onto the public street except through that single guarded front door. The sprawling, meticulously tended garden had a dozen doors opening out from the house’s back rooms and conservatory, but its perimeter was ringed by a sheer, unclimbable smooth stone wall topped with iron spikes. It was a perfectly enclosed space, ideal for a man whose life’s work hunting criminals had earned him hundreds of sworn enemies.

Ivan informed the guests his master had phoned moments earlier, delayed no more than ten minutes tying off final formalities for a scheduled execution. Valentin hated the harsh end of criminal justice, but carried out his duties with unflinching precision: he was a ruthless hunter of guilty men, but spent far more of his considerable political influence lobbying for lighter sentences and better prison conditions across France and much of Europe. A prominent free-thinking humanitarian, he only ever fell short of warmth by holding mercy to the same cold, unforgiving standard he applied to justice itself.

When Valentin finally arrived home, he was already in tailored black evening wear with the red rosette of his Legion of Honour pinned to his lapel, dark beard streaked clean through with silver. He walked straight to his ground-floor study, which opened directly onto the back garden, carefully locked away his official case files in their iron safe, then paused a minute at the open French doors staring out at the grounds. A sharp, silver moon was tearing through the tattered remaining rags of a passing storm, and Valentin stared at it with a soft, uncharacteristic wistfulness—an odd flicker of premonition, for the greatest mystery of his entire career was about to land right on his own lawn. He shook the strange mood off almost at once, remembering his guests were waiting.

When he stepped into the drawing room, he spotted every member of his small party at once. There was Lord Galloway, the choleric British ambassador, his face round and russet like a ripe apple, wearing the blue sash of the Order of the Garter. Beside him was his slim, silver-haired wife Lady Galloway, and their daughter Lady Margaret Graham: a pale, sharp-featured pretty girl with elfin features and coppery red hair. The opulent, dark-eyed Duchess of Mont St. Michel was there too, with her two equally full-figured, dark-eyed daughters in tow. Dr. Simon, the eminent French scientist, sat polishing his glasses, his forehead crisscrossed with permanent parallel wrinkles from a lifetime of haughty eyebrow raises. Next to him was Father Brown, the quiet little Catholic priest from the small Essex parish of Cobhole, whom Valentin had met briefly on a recent trip to England. And standing a little apart from the rest, waiting to greet his host, was a tall man in a Foreign Legion uniform: Commandant O’Brien, an Irishman who had fled his country after a catastrophic run of bad debts, and who had known the Galloway family—especially Margaret—since they were children. The Galloways had greeted him stiffly, refusing to meet his eye.

None of them, though, was the guest Valentin had been most eager to welcome: Julius K. Brayne, the American multi-millionaire famous across two continents for dumping colossal sums of money on every untested new religious and philosophical movement that called itself “progressive.” Nobody could say for sure if Brayne was an atheist, a Mormon, or a Christian Scientist, but he would fund any weird new intellectual project under the sun, even a prize for the next great unrecognized American Shakespeare. He’d decided Valentin was “progressive” too, a compliment the great detective privately found deeply insulting.

Brayne himself lumbered into the room minutes later, as unmissable and decisive as a dinner bell: a huge broad-shouldered man, equally tall and fat, in unadorned black evening wear with not a single ring or watch chain for decoration, his white hair slicked back like a German professor’s, his round cherubic red face marked only by a single tuft of dark hair under his lower lip that gave him an oddly Mephistophelean air. He was immediately escorted to the dining room on Lady Galloway’s arm.

The rest of the dinner passed uneventful enough, save for Lord Galloway’s obvious simmering displeasure at O’Brien’s presence. His temper only boiled over after the women had left the table, and the three younger men—Dr. Simon, Father Brown, and O’Brien—drifted off to join the ladies or smoke in the conservatory. Left alone with Brayne (the credulous American who believed in every new religion) and Valentin (the hardened skeptic who believed in none), Lord Galloway grew more and more irritated by their endless tedious debate over “progressive” reform, until he finally stood up to storm back to the drawing room.

He got lost in the long twisty back passages for nearly ten minutes, until he heard the doctor’s didactic voice, then Father Brown’s soft murmur, followed by general laughter. When he pushed open the drawing room door, he noticed two empty spots at once: O’Brien was gone, and his daughter Margaret was gone too.

Panicked and furious, he stamped back down the passages, his paranoia that the disgraced Irish soldier was going to elope with his daughter rising to a fever pitch. He rounded the corner toward the back of the house and nearly ran straight into Margaret, who swept past him with a white, scornful face, too angry to even speak. If she wasn’t with O’Brien right now, where was he? He stumbled toward the back servant’s door leading to the garden, and stepped out under the now fully cleared moon, its sharp silver light flooding every corner of the lawn. There he saw O’Brien, his blue uniform glowing in the moonlight, striding fast toward the study French windows, before vanishing back inside the house.

Lord Galloway seethed. The moonlit garden, all soft silver grass and dark shadowed poplar trunks, felt like some taunting fairy-tale Watteau scene mocking his attempts to keep the lovers apart. He stormed forward to follow O’Brien, and tripped hard over something half-buried in the long grass. He glared down to snap at whatever stone or tree root had tripped him, then froze.

A minute later, the quiet garden was shocked to see the elderly British diplomat running as fast as his legs could carry him, bellowing at the top of his hoarse voice.

His shouts drew Dr. Simon out of the study, his glasses askew and his face pale. “There’s a corpse in the grass!” Galloway shrieked. “A blood-covered corpse!”

Valentin was already right behind the doctor, drawn by the noise. The second he heard what had happened, his face shifted instantly from polite host to sharp, focused professional detective: this was his trade, right on his own back lawn. The three of them followed Galloway back to the spot, where a thin river of mist was now curling up across the lawn from the Seine. The body lay face down in the deep grass: a very tall, broad-shouldered man, bald save for a few strands of brown hair clinging to his skull like wet seaweed, blood pooling dark under his face.

“He’s not one of our party,” Dr. Simon said, his voice low and strange. He knelt to check for a pulse, then gestured for Valentin to help him lift the body an inch off the grass. The severed head lolled loose, completely detached from the neck. Even Valentin flinched. “Whoever killed him must have been as strong as a gorilla,” he muttered.

Dr. Simon lifted the head gently. The face was unmarked save for a few shallow cuts around the jaw: heavy-jowled, yellow-skinned, hawk-nosed, looking like a wicked Roman emperor with a faint dash of Chinese royal brutality. None of the four men recognized him. The dead man was wearing formal evening clothes, his starched white shirtfront stained through with blood, as if he had been on his way to join a party. Valentin crawled on his hands and knees for twenty yards around the body, searching the grass for clues, but all he found were a handful of small, snapped twigs. Nothing else.

A sharp shout from Lord Galloway cut through the heavy silence: “Who’s that over by the garden wall!”

A small, slightly round figure wobbled out of the mist, looking for a second like a mischievous goblin before they realized it was Father Brown, the little priest they had left in the drawing room minutes earlier. “I say,” he said meekly, “you do realize there are no other gates leading out of this garden at all, right? No other exits, at all.”

Valentin’s dark brows drew together—he usually regarded priests with instinctive skepticism—but he couldn’t argue the point. “You’re right. Before we figure out who killed him, we first have to figure out how a total stranger even got into this walled, locked garden in the first place.” He turned to the others, his tone firm and authoritative. “As head of the Paris police, I’m invoking my discretion here. We have distinguished guests, a foreign ambassador, ladies in the other room. No one calls in the public police before we clear every single one of my own house guests of suspicion first. No one leaves this house before noon tomorrow. We have more than enough bedrooms for everyone. Simon, go get Ivan—he’s completely trustworthy, tell him to leave one servant on the front door and come meet us at once. Lord Galloway, go break the news gently to the ladies, make sure they don’t panic, and tell them they are all to stay put. Father Brown, you and I will stand guard over the body for now.”

His orders were obeyed instantly. Dr. Simon fetched Ivan, Galloway returned to the drawing room to break the news softly, and the priest and the atheist detective stood still at either end of the dead man, lit by the cold moon, like two opposing statues of death’s different philosophies.

Ivan came racing across the lawn, nearly giddy with the excitement of the case, and bent to look at the severed head. He almost dropped it. “Sir—do you know who this is?” he gasped. Valentin shook his head, and the two men carried the heavy corpse back to lay on a sofa in the study before the whole party gathered in the drawing room.

Valentin sat at the desk, pen in hand, his eyes as hard as a judge’s at a criminal assize. “Is everyone present?” he asked.

“Where’s Mr. Brayne?” the Duchess said, glancing around the room.

“And where’s Commandant O’Brien?” Lord Galloway added gruffly. “I saw him walking in the garden right before I found that body.”

“Ivan,” Valentin said, “go find them. Brayne is finishing a cigar in the dining room, I think. O’Brien was pacing in the conservatory.”

Ivan vanished. Valentin turned to Dr. Simon. “The man’s head was cut clean off. Could that have been done with an ordinary knife?”

“Not a chance,” the pale doctor said. “To make a cut that clean, severing the entire neck in one pass? You’d need a battle axe, a headsman’s old blade, or a two-handed medieval sword.”

“But there are no battle axes here!” the Duchess nearly shrieked, half hysterical.

“Could it have been done with a standard French cavalry sabre?” Valentin asked, still scribbling notes on his paper.

A soft knock rapped at the door, and the whole room went cold, as if they’d heard the knock in Macbeth. “A sabre—yes, that could do it,” Dr. Simon said quietly.

“Come in, Ivan,” Valentin said. The servant stepped in, followed by Commandant O’Brien, his uniform rumpled, his jaw set in defiant anger.

“What do you want with me?” O’Brien snapped.

“Please sit down,” Valentin said evenly. “You aren’t wearing your sword. Where is it?”

“I left it on the library table,” O’Brien said, his Irish brog thickening with irritation. “It was digging into my side, getting in the way.”

“Ivan, go fetch the Commandant’s sword from the library,” Valentin said. The servant vanished. “Lord Galloway says he saw you leaving the garden right before the body was found. What were you doing out there?”

O’Brien laughed sharply, flinging himself into a chair. “Admiring the moon! Communing with nature, if you like.”

Another knock came at the door. Ivan stepped back in, holding an empty steel scabbard. “This is all I could find, sir.”

A dead, heavy silence fell over the room. Then Lady Margaret stood up, her face pale but determined. “I can tell you what he was doing in the garden, since he won’t say. He asked me to marry him. I turned him down. I told him all I could offer him was my respect. He walked off alone to cool his head. I will swear on any Bible you like that he could not have killed that man.”

Lord Galloway hissed at her to be quiet, but she rounded on him, her eyes blazing. “You old fool. If he was in that garden murdering someone, where was I? Do you hate him so much you’d accuse your own daughter of covering for a killer?”

Before anyone could speak, a soft, confused little voice piped up from the corner. “Wait,” Father Brown said. “That cigar Mr. Brayne was smoking before we left the dining room. It was awfully long. Longer than a walking stick, almost. Shouldn’t he have finished it hours ago?”

Valentin’s head snapped up, a sharp look in his eye. “You’re absolutely right. Ivan, go check on Mr. Brayne. Bring him here at once, now.”

Ivan was back less than a minute later, his scarred face flushed. “Beg pardon, sir, but Mr. Brayne’s gone. Left the house. His hat and coat are missing too. I ran out to search the street, and I found something fifty yards up the road to Paris, thrown in the bushes.” He held up a long, glinting naked cavalry sabre, its edge and tip streaked dark with dried blood. “This is it. The Commandant’s sword. Right where Brayne dumped it when he ran away.”
