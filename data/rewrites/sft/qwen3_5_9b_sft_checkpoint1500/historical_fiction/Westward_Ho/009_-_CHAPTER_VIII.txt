"It is virtue, gentlemen, that maketh gentlemen; that maketh the poor rich, the base-born noble, the subject a sovereign, the deformed beautiful, the sick whole, the weak strong, the most miserable most happy. There are two principal and peculiar gifts in the nature of man, knowledge and reason; the one commandeth, and the other obeyeth: these things neither the whirling wheel of fortune can change, neither the deceitful cavillings of worldlings separate, neither sickness abate, neither age abolish." —LILLY'S *Euphues*, 1586.

Now it falls to my lot to chronicle the founding of that most chivalrous brotherhood of the Rose. Though its fame would shortly spread far beyond Devon, becoming formidable in Ireland, the Netherlands, and across the Spanish Main, I am aware that this narrative may seem to modern ears Quixotic and fantastical. Yet consider this generation: they were men who spoke of love and honor with a fervor that belied their reality as practiced soldiers, prudent politicians, and diplomats. The author of the *Arcadia* was, in his youth, a subtle statesman; the poet of the *Faerie Queene* penned serious treatises on the governance of Ireland. If critics sneer at Lilly's *Euphues*, I ask them: Have they read it? To those who have, I offer pity if they fail to find in it a brave, righteous, and pious testament to the Elizabethan spirit. While Sir Walter Scott later ridiculed the "Euphuists" in *Sir Piercie Shafton*, creating an anachronistic caricature that ignored the historical reality—their genuine reverence for law, their commercial daring, and their deep-seated national pride—we must recognize these old gallants not as absurdities, but as the ancestors of true Englishmen. Their chivalry was merely another garment for the beautiful tenderness and mercy that remains the twin sister of English valor today. Even their extravagance in Continental manners reveals a wide-hearted teachableness, allowing us to borrow wisdom from all ages without losing our distinct character.

Thus, I proceed to my story. If any reader finds it wanting, I suggest turning the page to find pastures better suited to their taste.

Amyas could not sail immediately, for a southerner had freshened, blowing a gale directly into the bay. Having lowered the *Mary Grenville* downriver to Appledore Pool, ready for departure at the first shift of wind, Amyas returned quietly home. His mother, mounting a pillion behind an elderly serving-man, rode toward Clovelly to visit Frank, who lay wounded there. Amyas accompanied her as far as Bideford, where he encountered a procession of horsemen descending the High Street. At its head rode Will Cary, clad cap-a-pie in shining armor, sword on thigh, helmet at saddle-bow—a vision of gallantry that drew admiring glances from the townsfolk. Following him on sturdy country ponies were several serving-men bearing lances, baggage, and arms. Bringing up the rear, in a horse-litter to Mrs. Leigh's delight, was Master Frank himself.

Frank reported that his injuries were mere flesh-wounds, the dagger having turned aside from his ribs. He insisted on seeing Amyas one last time before departing. Declining to return home to Burrough, he resolved to take up residence with Cary at the Ship Tavern, near the Bridge-foot. He did so forthwith, establishing a court there in state, surrounded by the town's gossipers, who spun white fibs regarding the circumstances of his injury.

Meanwhile, Frank and Amyas devised a scheme, executed the following market day. The innkeeper commenced a frenzy of roasting, boiling, and frying unparalleled in the tavern's history. Amyas, venturing into the market, personally invited numerous schoolfellows—those Frank had identified—to a merry supper and subsequent revelry. By this stratagem, each of Rose Salterne's admirers arrived expecting a tête-à-tête, only to find themselves seated at the same table with six rivals, none of whom they had spoken to in half a year. All were too well-bred to allow the Leighs to perceive the awkwardness, though they certainly understood it. They arranged the seating with Frank lying in state at the head of the table and Amyas at the bottom. Through an abundance of good food, they endeavored to spare their guests the pain of interaction until the wine should loosen their tongues and warm their hearts. During this interlude, Amyas and Frank, ignoring the silence of their guests with provoking good humor, chatted, joked, and told stories. Will Cary, finding merriment infectious, melted into jest after jest, attempting to coax laughter from Mr. Coffin and Mr. Fortescue, who reacted with bows and frowns respectively. Unabashed, Cary played his "light artillery" upon the waiters until they fled the room, bursting with laughter.

When the cloth was drawn and sack and sugar became the order of the day, having duly consumed the "Queen and Bible," Frank attempted a fresh maneuver.

"I have a toast, gentlemen—" he declared. "'The gentlemen of the Irish wars; and may Ireland never be without a St. Leger to stand by a Fortescue, a Fortescue to stand by a St. Leger, and a Chichester to stand by both.'"

Of course, this necessitated the drinking of the healths of the three representatives, followed by mutual compliments and returns. The ice cracked slightly further. Young Fortescue proposed the health of "Amyas Leigh and all bold mariners," to which Amyas replied with blunt kindness, expressing a wish to sail the world again with the company as fellow-adventurers, thereby giving the Spaniards another taste of the men of Devon.

As the wine flowed sweetly, the lips of those previously silent began to speak. The ice broke up entirely, and every man conversed like a rational Englishman with his neighbor.

"And now, gentlemen," said Frank, sensing the moment for his grand assault, "let me propose a health which I doubt any of you will refuse to drink with heart and soul as well as lips. I speak of one whom beauty and virtue have so ennobled that in their light the shadow of lowly birth is unseen. I would proclaim her peerless in loveliness, were it not that every gentleman here has sisters who might well challenge her for the girdle of Venus. Yet, what else dare I say? Those same lovely ladies, who, if they consulted their mirrors, must be far better judges of beauty than I, have in my own hearing repeatedly assigned the palm to her. Surely, if the goddesses decide among themselves the question of the golden apple, Paris himself must vacate the judgment seat. Gentlemen, your hearts have already bid you, as my unworthy lips do now, to drink 'The Rose of Torridge.'"

Had the Rose of Torridge herself entered the room, she could scarcely have caused greater blank astonishment. Every guest flushed red, then pale, then red again, exchanging looks that whispered, "What right has anyone but I to drink her health?" Yet Frank, with sweet effrontery, drank "The health of the Rose of Torridge, and a double health to that worthy gentleman, whosoever he may be, whom she is fated to honor with her love!"

"Well done, cunning Frank Leigh!" cried blunt Will Cary. "None of us dare quarrel with you now, however much we may sulk at each other. For there's none of us, I'll warrant, but thinks that she likes him the best of all; and so we are bound to believe that you have drunk our healths all round."

"And so I have," Frank agreed. "And what better thing can you do, gentlemen, than to drink each other's healths all round likewise? Show yourselves true gentlemen, true Christians, ay, and true lovers? For what is love, let me speak freely, but the very inspiration of that Deity whose name is Love? Be sure that not without reason did the ancients feign Eros to be the eldest of the gods, by whom the jarring elements of chaos were attuned into harmony and order. How, then, shall lovers make him the father of strife? Shall Psyche wed with Cupid to bring forth a cockatrice's egg? Or shall the soul filled with love, the likeness of the immortals, burn with envy and jealousy? True, the rose has its thorn; but it leaves poison and stings to the nettle. Cupid has his arrow; but he hurls no scorpions. Venus is awful when despised, as the daughters of Proetus found; but her handmaids are the Graces, not the Furies. Surely he who loves aright will not only find love lovely, but become himself lovely also. I speak not to reprehend you, gentlemen; for to you (as your piercing wits have already perceived by your honorable blushes) my discourse tends; but to point you, if you will but permit me, to that rock which I myself have, I know not by what Divine good hap, attained. If indeed I have attained it, and am not about to be washed off again by the next tide."

Frank's rapid and fantastic oratory, utterly unexpected, had left their wits no time to set their tempers on fire.
