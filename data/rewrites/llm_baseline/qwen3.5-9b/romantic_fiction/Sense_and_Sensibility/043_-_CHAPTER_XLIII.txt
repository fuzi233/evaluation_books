The next morning, Marianne rose at her accustomed hour. When asked how she felt, she insisted she was recovering, attempting to prove it by resuming her usual activities. Yet, a day spent shivering beside the fire, clutching a book she could not read, or lying listlessly upon a sofa, offered little evidence of improvement. By the time she finally retreated to bed, increasingly indisposed, Colonel Brandon was merely astonished by her sister's composure. Elinor, despite tending to Marianne all day against her wishes and forcing medicine upon her at night, shared Marianne's faith in the healing power of sleep and felt no genuine alarm.

However, a restless, feverish night dashed their expectations. When Marianne, after stubbornly insisting on rising, eventually confessed her inability to sit up and returned willingly to bed, Elinor readily accepted Mrs. Jennings's suggestion to summon the Palmers' apothecary. Mr. Harris arrived, examined the patient, and while encouraging Miss Dashwood to anticipate a swift recovery within a few days, his diagnosis carried a darker tone. He described the disorder as having a "putrid tendency" and casually uttered the word "infection." This single phrase sent Mrs. Palmer into an instant panic regarding her infant. Mrs. Jennings, who had suspected from the outset that Marianne's condition was more serious than Elinor realized, grew grave upon hearing Mr. Harris's report. Confirming Charlotte's fears, she urgently advocated for the immediate removal of the child and her mother. Although Mr. Palmer dismissed their anxieties as unfounded, he found his wife's anxiety and persistence insurmountable. Consequently, her departure was arranged. Within an hour of Mr. Harris's arrival, she departed with her son and nurse for the home of a relative living several miles outside Bath. Mr. Palmer promised to follow shortly, as urged by his wife, and she pressed her mother just as fervently to accompany them.

Mrs. Jennings, however, declared with heartfelt kindness that she would not stir from Cleveland so long as Marianne remained ill. Determined to replace the absent mother through her own attentive care, she proved to be a most willing and active helper to Elinor, eager to share the burden and offering valuable nursing experience. Poor Marianne, drained by the severity of her malady and feeling universally unwell, could no longer hope for recovery by the next day. The thought of what that day would have brought without this illness—the start of their journey home, accompanied by a servant, surprising their mother the following morning—made every ailment seem more acute. Her few words were filled with lamentation over this unavoidable delay, though Elinor strove to lift her spirits, genuinely believing at the time that the postponement would be brief.

The following day brought little change; Marianne was neither better nor seemingly worse. The household was further diminished when Mr. Palmer, though reluctant due to his natural goodness and dislike of appearing cowardly, was finally persuaded by Colonel Brandon to fulfill his promise. As he prepared to depart, Colonel Brandon himself began to speak of leaving as well. Here, Mrs. Jennings intervened with perfect tact. She argued that sending the Colonel away while his heart was so troubled by concern for his sister would deprive them both of comfort. Urging him to stay, citing her need for his company in playing piquet while Miss Dashwood cared for Marianne, she won him over. Gratified by the opportunity to remain, Colonel Brandon could not long feign reluctance, especially as Mr. Palmer also expressed relief at having someone capable of advising Elinor in any emergency. Naturally, Marianne was kept ignorant of these arrangements. She did not realize she had inadvertently caused the owners of Cleveland to depart within a week of their arrival. Seeing nothing of Mrs. Palmer caused her no surprise, nor did it trouble her enough to mention her name.

Two days passed since Mr. Palmer's departure, and Marianne's condition remained largely unchanged. Mr. Harris, attending daily, still spoke confidently of a speedy recovery, and Elinor matched his optimism, even writing lighter accounts to her mother than her friend had suggested. However, the day ended less auspiciously than it began. Toward evening, Marianne grew heavier, more restless, and uncomfortable. Elinor, still hopeful, attributed the shift to the fatigue of sitting up for her bed to be made. Administering the prescribed cordials, she watched with satisfaction as Marianne sank into a slumber, expecting beneficial results. Though not perfectly quiet, the sleep lasted a considerable time. Anxious to witness the outcome, Elinor resolved to stay by her side throughout.

Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the change, retired unusually early. One of the principal nurses was resting in the housekeeper's room, leaving Elinor alone with Marianne. The patient's repose became increasingly disturbed. Watching her constant shifting and hearing the frequent, inarticulate murmurs of distress, Elinor almost wished to wake her from such painful dreams until Marianne was suddenly startled awake by a noise in the house. Rising hastily with feverish wildness, she cried out, "Is Mama coming?"

"Not yet," Elinor answered, concealing her own terror and helping Marianne lie down again. "But she will be here, I hope, before long. It is a great distance from here to Barton."

"But she must not go round by London!" Marianne exclaimed in the same hurried tone. "I shall never see her if she goes by London."

Elinor perceived with alarm that Marianne was not herself. While attempting to soothe her, she eagerly checked her pulse. It was lower and quicker than ever. Marianne's frantic talk of her mother caused Elinor's alarm to escalate so rapidly that she decided instantly to send for Mr. Harris and dispatch a messenger to Barton for her mother. Consulting Colonel Brandon on the best method to achieve this was the immediate next step. Having rung for the maid to replace her, Elinor hastened downstairs to the drawing-room, where she knew he usually stayed later. There was no time for hesitation. Her fears and difficulties were laid bare before him. Lacking the courage or confidence to dispel her fears, he listened in silent despondence; yet her practical difficulties were instantly solved. With a readiness that seemed pre-arranged, he offered himself as the messenger to fetch Mrs. Dashwood.

Elinor made no resistance that was not easily overcome. Thanking him with brief, fervent gratitude, she watched as he hurried off his servant with orders for post-horses. Meanwhile, she wrote a few lines to her mother. How gratefully she felt the comfort of such a friend at that critical moment! A companion whose judgment would guide, whose presence would relieve, and whose friendship would soothe. Whatever he felt internally, Colonel Brandon acted with the firmness of a collected mind, arranging everything with despatch and calculating exactly when she might expect his return. No moment was wasted. The horses arrived sooner than anticipated. After pressing her hand with a look of solemnity and speaking words too low for her to hear, he hurried into the carriage.

It was nearly twelve o'clock. Elinor returned to Marianne's room to await the apothecary and watch through the remaining hours. It was a night of nearly equal suffering for both: sleepless pain and delirium for Marianne, and cruel anxiety for Elinor, before Mr. Harris appeared. Once her apprehensions were raised, they demanded payment for all her earlier complacency. The servant who sat with her, refusing to disturb Mrs. Jennings, only tormented her more by hinting at what her mistress had always feared. Marianne's thoughts remained fixated incoherently on her mother; every mention of her name pierced Elinor's heart. Reproaching herself for trifling with days of illness and desperate for relief, Elinor feared that all efforts might be in vain, that everything had been delayed too long, and imagined her suffering mother arriving too late to see her daughter alive or sane.

On the verge of calling Mr. Harris again—or seeking other counsel if he could not come—he finally arrived, albeit not until after five o'clock. His opinion offered some solace for his tardiness. Acknowledging an unexpected and unpleasant alteration in his patient, he refused to deem the danger material, speaking confidently of the relief a new treatment would bring. He promised to return in three or four hours, leaving both patient and attendant somewhat more composed than he had found them.

In the morning, Mrs. Jennings heard the account with deep concern. Her former apprehensions, now justified, left her no doubt of the gravity of the situation. Though she tried to offer comfort to Elinor, her conviction of Marianne's peril prevented her from expressing false hope. Her heart truly grieved at the prospect of such a young, lovely girl fading away quickly. Beyond professional duty, she felt personal sorrow; Marianne had been her companion for three months, under her care, and known to have suffered greatly. Furthermore, considering that Marianne might be to Mrs. Dashwood what Charlotte was to herself, her sympathy for Elinor's distress was profound.

Mr. Harris was punctual for his second visit, only to disappoint his own hopes. His medicines had failed; the fever persisted unabated. Marianne remained in a heavy stupor, quieter but no more herself. Elinor, seizing every sign of fear in his voice, proposed calling in further advice. He deemed it unnecessary, claiming he had one final remedy to try, of which he was as confident as before. His visit concluded with reassuring assurances that reached Elinor's ears but could not penetrate her heart.
