Elinor stood where he had left her, as though the air still carried the imprint of his voice. Even when the last wheel of his carriage had thinned into silence down the avenue—first a rumble, then a soft diminishing hiss upon gravel, then nothing but the house’s own breathing—she could not move with ease. Her thoughts came like dishes set down in haste upon a crowded table: some scalding, some bitter, some too sweet to bear. The prevailing flavour was sadness.

Willoughby—Willoughby, whom, half an hour earlier, she had held in her mind as unworthy of any feeling but abhorrence—had contrived, by the very sufferings that sprang from his faults, to draw from her a reluctant pity. It was not pity proportioned to his deserts, she knew; it was pity measured against his wishes, his visible regret, his still-ardent attachment to Marianne—an attachment that had no right to live and yet lived, like a stubborn plant kept green by a careless hand.

She reproached herself for it. What merit was there in a countenance the world called attractive, in an open affectionate manner that seemed to promise warmth and honesty as readily as a well-laid hearth promises comfort? These were gifts, not virtues. And yet, as the house’s smaller sounds returned to her—footsteps on the landing, the faint clink of a spoon somewhere below, the steady tick that the clock insisted on keeping—she felt his influence linger. It would be long, long before it faded.

At last she went back to Marianne.

She found her sister just stirring from sleep, that long, sweet, restoring sleep which had been prayed for with such desperation that its arrival felt like a miracle accepted with trembling hands. Marianne’s lashes fluttered; colour, though faint, had returned enough to soften the sharpness of her face. Elinor’s heart, already full, swelled painfully at the sight. Everything pressed upon her at once—the past and its mistakes, the present and its fragile safety, the future and all it might yet demand; Willoughby’s sudden visit; Marianne’s recovery; their mother’s expected arrival. Her spirits were in such agitation that fatigue could not touch her; she feared only that her own countenance might betray too much to Marianne’s newly awakened eyes.

That fear had scarcely time to settle, for within half an hour of Willoughby’s departure, another carriage announced itself. The sound came first as a distant, urgent drumming, then rose and thickened as it approached, scattering the quiet of Cleveland like birds startled from a hedge. Elinor, catching the meaning before any servant could speak, ran downstairs. She reached the outer door just as it opened, just in time to receive her mother into her arms.

Mrs. Dashwood had been driven toward the house in such terror that she had almost convinced herself Marianne was already lost. There was no voice left in her for questions—no voice even for Elinor. Her breath came in broken pulls, as if each was fought for. Elinor did not wait for greeting or inquiry; she gave the relief at once, as one might press a cup of warm broth into shaking hands.

“Marianne is better.”

It was enough. Her mother took the words as the thirsty take water, with all her natural warmth and a force that undid her. In the same instant she was as overwhelmed by happiness as she had been by fear. Between Elinor and their friend she was supported into the drawing-room, shedding tears of joy and still unable to speak, embracing Elinor again and again; and each time she turned, she pressed Colonel Brandon’s hand with a look that said gratitude and recognition—recognition that he too had suffered, that his heart had been bound to theirs in this danger.

He returned that look with silence, deeper even than hers. The room itself seemed hushed in deference to it; Elinor noticed, as if for the first time, how the house sounded when a strong feeling ruled it—how even the crackle of the fire and the faint stir of curtains seemed to soften.

As soon as Mrs. Dashwood could command herself, her first desire was to see Marianne; and in two minutes she was beside her child. The meeting was wordless at first—kiss, touch, that desperate laying of a palm against a forehead as if warmth could be measured and life confirmed by the skin. Marianne, made dearer by absence, unhappiness, and danger, looked up with a weak, grateful calm that stabbed Elinor with tenderness. Elinor’s joy, in witnessing what each felt, was checked only by the fear that such emotion might steal Marianne’s strength and rob her of further rest.

But Mrs. Dashwood could be prudent when a child’s life depended upon it. She learned quiet with the same passion she had spent on despair. Marianne, satisfied merely to know her mother was near, and conscious of being too weak for talk, submitted readily to the silence and stillness that every nurse around her prescribed.

Mrs. Dashwood would sit up all night. Elinor, urged by her mother’s entreaty and by reason itself, went at last to bed; but rest would not come. A night entirely sleepless, and many hours of wearing anxiety, ought to have made sleep a mercy easily accepted; instead, her spirits were irritated into wakefulness. The house at night offered a different music—wind against panes, the sigh of a settling board, a distant cough, then the soft tread of someone pacing; but none of it quieted her.

Willoughby—poor Willoughby, as she now allowed herself to name him—would not leave her thoughts. She could not but have heard his vindication, and now she blamed herself for judging him so harshly, and now acquitted herself again. Yet the promise she had given—of relating his explanation to Marianne—lay upon her like a weight. She dreaded it: dreaded the telling, dreaded the effect; doubted whether Marianne, once armed with such knowledge, could ever give her whole heart elsewhere. For a moment—only a moment—she wished him a widower; and the instant that wish formed, she felt ashamed, remembering Colonel Brandon: his constancy, his sufferings borne in quiet, his honour that asked nothing and yet gave everything. To him, far more than to his rival, any reward from Marianne’s future was due. Elinor wished anything rather than the death of Mrs. Willoughby.

Colonel Brandon’s errand to Barton had been softened, in its effect upon Mrs. Dashwood, by her own previous alarm. Her uneasiness for Marianne had already driven her to determine, even before his arrival, to set out for Cleveland that very day without waiting for further intelligence; she had gone so far as to make arrangements that the Careys were expected every moment to fetch Margaret away, her mother unwilling to take the child where there might be infection.

Marianne mended with each day. Strength returned, at first in barely perceptible degrees—the steadier colour on her cheek, the lessening of that harsh, tight cough, the ability to take nourishment without exhaustion. The household’s sounds changed with her improvement: fewer hurried whispers, fewer doors closed in haste, more ordinary movements permitted. Mrs. Dashwood’s face grew brilliant with cheerfulness; her spirits rose so high that she repeatedly declared herself one of the happiest women in the world.

Elinor heard these declarations and watched their proofs with a strange inward question. Did her mother ever recollect Edward? But Mrs. Dashwood, trusting to the temperate account of Elinor’s own disappointment that had been sent to her, was carried away by the exuberance of her present joy. Marianne restored to her from danger—danger to which she could not help feeling her own mistaken judgement had contributed, by encouraging that unfortunate attachment—was blessing enough. Yet in Marianne’s recovery there was another source of happiness, one that Elinor had not anticipated.

It came out the first time they were alone together, when Marianne slept and the house, for once, seemed to hold its breath for them.

“At last we are alone,” Mrs. Dashwood said, taking Elinor’s hands with a fervour that would not be denied. “My Elinor—you do not yet know all my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself.”

Elinor, moved by turns to pleasure and pain, surprised and yet not surprised, listened in silence.

“You are never like me, dear Elinor, or I should wonder at your composure now. Had I sat down to wish for any possible good to my family, I should have fixed on Colonel Brandon’s marrying one of you as the object most desirable. And I believe Marianne will be the most happy with him of the two.”

Elinor was half inclined to ask why—because she was certain no reason, founded on an impartial comparison of age, character, and feelings, could be given—but her mother’s imagination always ran ahead on any subject that interested her. Elinor passed it off with a smile.

“He opened his whole heart to me yesterday as we travelled,” Mrs. Dashwood continued. “It came out quite unawares, quite undesignedly. I— you may well believe—could talk of nothing but my child. He could not conceal his distress. I saw it equalled my own, and he perhaps—thinking that mere friendship, as the world now goes, would not justify so warm a sympathy—or rather not thinking at all, I suppose—giving way to irresistible feelings, made me acquainted with his earnest, tender, constant affection for Marianne. He has loved her, my Elinor, ever since the first moment of seeing her.”

Elinor could hear, beneath the statement, the shaping hand of her mother’s fancy—not in the Colonel’s language, perhaps, but in the way Mrs. Dashwood embroidered it, as if to make it more delightful to taste.

“His regard for her,” Mrs. Dashwood went on, “infinitely surpassing anything that Willoughby ever felt—or feigned—as much more warm as more sincere or constant, whichever we are to call it, has subsisted through all the knowledge of dear Marianne’s unhappy prepossession for that worthless young man! And without selfishness—without encouraging a hope! Could he have seen her happy with another—such a noble mind! such openness, such sincerity! no one can be deceived in him.”

“Colonel Brandon’s character,” said Elinor, steady and sincere, “as an excellent man, is well established.”

“I know it is,” her mother replied more seriously, “or after such a warning, I should be the last to encourage such affection, or even to be pleased by it. But his coming for me as he did—with such active, such ready friendship—is enough to prove him one of the worthiest of men.”

“His character,” Elinor answered, “does not rest on one act of kindness, to which his affection for Marianne—were humanity out of the case—would have prompted him. To Mrs. Jennings, to the Middletons, he has been long and intimately known; they equally love and respect him; and even my own knowledge of him, though lately acquired, is very considerable; and so highly do I value and esteem him, that if Marianne can be happy with him, I shall be as ready as yourself to think our connection the greatest blessing to us in the world. What answer did you give him? Did you allow him to hope?”

“Oh, my love, I could not then talk of hope to him—or to myself. Marianne might at that moment be dying. But he did not ask for hope or encouragement. His was an involuntary confidence, an irrepressible effusion to a soothing friend, not an application to a parent. Yet after a time I did say—for at first I was quite overcome—that if she lived, as I trusted she might, my greatest happiness would lie in promoting their marriage; and since our arrival, since our delightful security, I have repeated it to him more fully, have given him every encouragement in my power. Time—a very little time, I tell him—will do everything. Marianne’s heart is not to be wasted for ever on such a man as Willoughby. His own merits must soon secure it.”

“To judge from the Colonel’s spirits, however,” Elinor observed, “you have not yet made him equally sanguine.”

“No. He thinks Marianne’s affection too deeply rooted for any change in it under a great length of time; and even supposing her heart again free, he is too diffident of himself to believe that, with such a difference of age and disposition, he could ever attach her. There, however, he is quite mistaken. His age is only so much beyond hers as to be an advantage—to make his character and principles fixed; and his disposition, I am well convinced, is exactly the very one to make your sister happy. And his person—his manners too—are all in his favour. My partiality does not blind me. He certainly is not so handsome as Willoughby; but at the same time, there is something much more pleasing in his countenance. There was always a something—if you remember—in Willoughby’s eyes at times, which I did not like.”

Elinor could not remember it; but her mother, without pausing for assent, continued:

“And his manners, the Colonel’s manners are not only more pleasing to me than Willoughby’s ever were, but they are of a kind I well know to be more solidly attaching to Marianne—their gentleness, their genuine attention to other people, and their manly unstudied simplicity. That is much more accordant with her real disposition than the liveliness—often artificial, and often ill-timed—of the other. I am very sure myself, that had Willoughby turned out as really amiable as he has proved himself the contrary, Marianne would yet never have been so happy with him as she will be with Colonel Brandon.”

She paused only long enough to let the picture settle.

“At Delaford, she will be within an easy distance of me,” Mrs. Dashwood added, the practical dream already laying out rooms and roads. “Even if I remain at Barton; and in all probability—for I hear it is a large village—indeed there certainly must be some small house or cottage close by, that would suit us quite as well as our present situation.”

Poor Elinor—there was the familiar current beneath the words, a new scheme carrying them all toward Delaford. But her spirit, quiet as it was, remained stubborn.

“His fortune too!” Mrs. Dashwood continued, as if tasting yet another agreeable possibility. “For at my time of life you know, everybody cares about that; and though I neither know nor desire to know what it really is, I am sure it must be a good one.”

At that moment they were interrupted by the entrance of a third person. Elinor withdrew, glad of solitude, to turn everything over in private: to wish success with her whole heart to a man she esteemed—and yet, even in wishing it, to feel an involuntary pang for Willoughby.
