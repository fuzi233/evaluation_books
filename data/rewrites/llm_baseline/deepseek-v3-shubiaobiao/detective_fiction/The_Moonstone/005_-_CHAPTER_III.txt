The problem of how to begin this tale properly vexed me until I sought the counsel of my daughter Penelope. Her suggestion was simple: start at the beginning, the day we received word that Mr. Franklin Blake was returning to the house. Dates, she argued, have a way of sharpening memory—and she, ever dutiful with her diary, could supply them. When I proposed (rather cleverly, I thought) that she might narrate the tale herself from those very pages, she flushed crimson and declared her journal private, punctuating her refusal with a sharp, *"Fiddlesticks!"* I, in turn, murmured, *"Sweethearts."*

And so, guided by Penelope’s method, I begin with the morning of Wednesday, the twenty-fourth of May, 1848, when my lady summoned me to her sitting room.

*"Gabriel,"* she said, *"prepare yourself for a surprise. Franklin Blake has returned from abroad. He stays with his father in London now, but tomorrow he joins us—remaining through Rachel’s birthday next month."*

Had I held a hat at that moment, sheer propriety alone would have kept me from hurling it to the ceiling in delight. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since his boyhood, and in my recollection, he was the finest lad ever to spin a top or shatter a windowpane. Miss Rachel, present for this exchange, countered with her own memory: *"I recall him as the most insufferable tyrant who ever tormented a doll, and the cruelest taskmaster to harness a weary little girl."* She concluded with theatrical fervor, *"Even now, I burn with indignation and ache with fatigue at the mere thought of Franklin Blake!"*

You may wonder why Mr. Franklin spent his youth abroad. The answer lies in his father’s misfortunes. The elder Mr. Blake, a man of vast wealth and litigious appetite, spent years—and a fortune—battling in court to claim a dukedom he believed his by right. After his wife and two children perished, the courts finally dismissed his claim, leaving him embittered. He resolved that England, having failed him, would not educate his son. *"How can I trust my native institutions,"* he declared, *"when they have treated me thus?"* And so, young Franklin was dispatched to Germany, then France, then Italy—returning a polymath of sorts, dabbling in writing, painting, and music, though I suspected his talents, like his childhood debts to me (a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven shillings sixpence still unpaid), were borrowed. His inheritance, seven hundred a year, vanished swiftly, for money slipped through Mr. Franklin’s fingers like water. Yet his charm made him welcome everywhere; his address, as he jested, was simply *"Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for."* Twice he resolved to visit us, and twice, a lady (of unmentionable character) detained him. The third attempt, as you know, succeeded.

On Thursday, the twenty-fifth, we would see what manner of man our boy had become. The day dawned splendid, and my lady and Miss Rachel, not expecting Mr. Franklin until dinner, departed for a luncheon with neighbors. I inspected his prepared quarters, then—as butler and steward (the latter role my own insistence, for I would tolerate no other hand on Sir John’s cellar key)—I fetched a bottle of our finest Latour claret, setting it to breathe in the sun. Deciding the same warmth might benefit my aged bones, I carried my beehive chair to the courtyard—only to halt at an odd, rhythmic tapping from the terrace.

Rounding the corner, I discovered three Indians in white linen, small drums at their waists, accompanied by a fair-haired English boy bearing a bag. The leader, a man of striking courtesy, introduced them as traveling performers seeking an audience with the lady of the house. Now, I am no miser with amusement, nor do I judge a man by his complexion—but let a stranger of refined manners linger near my pantry, and I think instantly of the plate basket. I informed him my lady was absent and saw them off the premises.

I had scarcely settled into my chair when Penelope descended upon me in a fury, insisting the Indians be apprehended at once. Why? Because they knew of Mr. Franklin’s arrival and meant him mischief.

At this, I sat straighter. *"Explain."*

Penelope, aflame with conviction, demanded I see the gravity of this. Particularly the third question: *"Has the English gentleman got It about him?"*

*"What is 'It'?"* she pressed.

I teased that we might ask Mr. Franklin—though I meant it lightly. She did not.

*"Ask him,"* she retorted. *"See if he laughs."*

Later, I did ask. And to my astonishment, Mr. Franklin did not laugh at all.

For he believed *"It"* meant the Moonstone.
