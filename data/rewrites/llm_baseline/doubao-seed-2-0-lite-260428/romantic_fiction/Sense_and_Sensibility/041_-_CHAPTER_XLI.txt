Edward barely remembered the walk from Colonel Brandon’s lodgings to Bartlett’s Buildings, his chest so swollen with unaccustomed joy it felt as if he might float off the pavement entirely. When he burst through Lucy’s door, his grin so wide it ached, she could tell at a glance his dearest hopes were finally realized. The next day, when Mrs. Jennings trundled round with her congratulations, Lucy was still so giddy she swore she had never seen Edward in such high spirits in all the years of their secret, hard-won engagement.

Her own happiness was no less unshakable. She laughed and nodded along to Mrs. Jennings’ giddy plans, already certain the two of them would be settled cozily in Delaford Parsonage long before Michaelmas came. Far from holding back her gratitude to Elinor, she sang her praises with unreserved warmth, declaring she believed Miss Dashwood would move heaven and earth for anyone she truly cared for, and no kindness from her now or in future would ever surprise her. As for Colonel Brandon? She already worshipped him like a living saint, and resolved he should be treated as one in all worldly matters too, his tithes raised to the very highest possible sum, and that at Delaford she would make free use of his carriage, his best dairy cows, and his plumpest, finest poultry whenever she pleased.

A full week had passed since John Dashwood called at Berkeley Street, and in all that time neither he nor Fanny had sent more than one passing verbal enquiry after her “indisposition” following the discovery of Edward’s secret engagement. Elinor knew social courtesy demanded she pay Fanny a formal visit. It was an obligation she loathed, and none of her companions offered her any encouragement to go: Marianne not only refused to come along herself, she begged Elinor not to go at all, too proud to put on polite airs for the woman who had so cruelly slighted them. Mrs. Jennings, who would have placed her own carriage at Elinor’s disposal at a moment’s notice, disliked Fanny so heartily that not even her curiosity to see Fanny’s mortified face, nor her giddy urge to side openly with Edward and affront her, could overcome her reluctance to share a room with her. So Elinor set out alone, dreading every step, at risk of being left tête-à-tête with a woman her friends had far less cause to despise than she.

The maid told her Mrs. Dashwood was not at home, but before the carriage could turn away from the house, John Dashwood happened to step out the front door. He lit up with feigned delight at the sight of her, declaring he had been just on his way to call at Berkeley Street, and insisted on leading her back upstairs to the drawing room, promising Fanny would be overjoyed to see her.

The room was empty.
“Fanny must be resting in her chamber,” he said. “I’ll fetch her in a moment. She will not be the least bit displeased to see you, far from it—you and Marianne have always been such favourites of hers. Why could Marianne not come today?”
Elinor offered a polite excuse for her sister.
“I am glad to see you alone,” he replied, lowering himself into an armchair. “I have so much to ask you. This living of Colonel Brandon’s—can it possibly be true? Has he really given it to Edward? I heard of it only yesterday by chance, and was coming straight to you to confirm the story.”
“It is perfectly true. Colonel Brandon has presented Edward to the Delaford living.”
“Good heavens, that is astonishing! No relation between them, no connection at all! And livings fetch such a price these days—what is the value of this one, exactly?”
“About two hundred pounds a year.”
“Two hundred a year! Why, if the late incumbent had been old and frail, likely to die any day, he could have sold the next presentation for fourteen hundred pounds at the very least! What a fool he was not to sell it before the man passed! A man of his sense, too! I cannot fathom such improvidence. Though, come to think of it, I am sure the truth is this: Edward is only holding the living temporarily, until the young man the Colonel really sold the presentation to comes of age. Aye, that is it, you may depend on it.”
Elinor contradicted him flatly, explaining she had personally carried the Colonel’s offer to Edward, and knew the full terms of the gift. He was forced to concede she could not be mistaken.
“It is truly astonishing,” he cried, when she finished. “What on earth could the Colonel’s motive possibly be?”
“A very simple one: to be of use to Mr. Ferrars.”
“Well, well. Edward is the luckiest man alive, no doubt of it. But you must not mention this to Fanny, will you? I have broken the news to her already, and she bears it very well, but she would hate for it to be gossiped about beyond the family.”
Elinor bit her tongue to keep from pointing out that a secure income for her own brother, which would never take a single penny from her or her little boy Harry, ought to be a thing Fanny could bear with perfect composure.
“Mrs. Ferrars does not know a word of this yet,” John went on, dropping his voice to a sombre, confidential hush, “and I think we ought to keep it hidden from her for as long as we possibly can. When the marriage takes place, I fear she must hear the whole truth.”
“But why such caution?” Elinor asked. “It is not as if Mrs. Ferrars could ever take pleasure in knowing her son finally has enough money to live on, of course—no one could expect that. But after the way she behaved, after she cast him off for ever and bade everyone around her shun him too, how can she possibly care anything that happens to him? She cannot be so weak as to throw away the comfort of a child, yet still cling to the anxieties of a parent.”
“Ah, Elinor, your reasoning is sound enough, but it ignores human nature,” John said, shaking his head. “When Edward’s wretched match finally happens, his mother will grieve for him just as much as if she never discarded him at all. We must hide any news that might speed that dreadful day. Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers who ever lived.”
Elinor said no more, and fell silent.
“We are thinking now,” John said after a short pause, his tone grave and deliberate, “of arranging for Robert to marry Miss Morton instead.”
Elinor smiled faintly at his self-important tone, and replied calmly, “I suppose the lady herself has little choice in the matter.”
“Choice? What can you possibly mean by that?”
“Only that from your manner of speaking, it seems it makes no difference to Miss Morton whether she marries Edward or Robert.”
“Of course there is no difference. Robert will be treated as the eldest son in every practical respect now, and both are perfectly agreeable young men. I do not know that one is superior to the other by any measure.”
John fell quiet for a moment, then leaned over to take Elinor’s hand, his voice dropping to a dramatic whisper.
“One thing I may assure you, my dear sister, and it will gratify you, I know. I have it on the best authority, straight from Fanny, who heard it from Mrs. Ferrars herself, that of the two possible engagements Edward might have made—you understand me—the one to you would have been infinitely preferable. It would have given her less than half the vexation Lucy’s attachment has caused. She said it would have been the least of two evils, and she would gladly settle for nothing worse than that now. But all that is quite out of the question, of course, not to be mentioned or thought of. But I wanted to tell you, because I knew it would please you. Not that you have any reason to regret, mind you. You will do exceedingly well, no doubt of it. Colonel Brandon has been calling on you a great deal lately, has he not?”
Elinor’s head was spinning, her nerves taut enough to snap, and she was more than relieved when the door opened and Robert Ferrars sauntered into the room. After a few idle moments of small talk, John Dashwood remembered he had not yet told Fanny Elinor was there, and slipped out of the room to fetch her. Elinor was left alone with Robert, whose bright, careless self-complacency as he reaped all the benefits of his mother’s favour at his disinherited brother’s expense, earned only her deepest disdain.
Barely two minutes had passed when Robert began to speak of Edward. He had heard of the living too, and was bursting with curiosity. Elinor repeated the details just as she had told them to John, and the effect on Robert, while very different, was no less striking. He laughed immoderately, the idea of the stiff, awkward Edward being a clergyman and living in a tiny drafty parsonage diverting him past all bounds. When he added the silly image of Edward in a white surplice, reading banns for a pair of random village common folk, he thought there could be nothing more ridiculous on earth.
Elinor sat perfectly still, her face grave and unsmiling, staring at him with a look of quiet contempt he was far too vain to notice. When he finally wiped the tears of laughter from his eyes, he grew serious.
“We may treat it as a joke, but upon my soul it is a most grave business. Poor Edward! He is ruined for ever. I am very sorry for him, he is a good-hearted fellow, as well-meaning as any man alive. Poor Edward, his manners never were the best. But we are not all born with the same ease and address, are we? I was absolutely thunderstruck when the engagement came out. I told my mother straight away, ‘Madam, if Edward marries that girl, I shall never see him again.’ That is exactly what I said. Poor Edward! He has shut himself out of all decent society for ever. If I had known of the attachment a few hours sooner, I could have talked him out of it easily, told him exactly what a disgrace he was bringing on himself. But it was too late, I was not in the way to interfere. Now it is all over. He will be half-starved in that little parsonage for the rest of his days, you may be sure of it.”
The door opened before Elinor could reply, and Fanny Dashwood stepped into the room. Though she never spoke of Edward’s misfortune outside her immediate family, Elinor could see the embarrassment on her face, and the forced, stilted cordiality of her greeting. She even went so far as to feign sorrow that Elinor and her sister would be leaving town so soon, that she had hoped to spend more time with them. John Dashwood followed her in, gazing at her with adoring pride as if every fake, gracious word she spoke to Elinor was the height of kindness and grace.
