Early the following morning, Willoughby arrived at the cottage. Though Margaret had styled him "Marianne's preserver" with a flair that lacked precision, the fact remained that he came to inquire personally. Mrs. Dashwood received him with a warmth that blended genuine politeness with the gratitude Sir John had spoken of, creating an atmosphere where every word confirmed the family's sense, elegance, and domestic harmony. Of course, Willoughby needed no further evidence of their charm; he had already seen enough.

The sisters were a study in contrasts, yet both possessed undeniable allure. Miss Dashwood, Elinor, had a complexion of delicate fairness, features arranged with mathematical regularity, and a figure of remarkable poise. Marianne, however, was a different sort of beauty entirely. Taller and more striking, her form was less perfectly proportioned than her sister's but infinitely more commanding. Her skin, though deeply tanned, glowed with a brilliant transparency, revealing a vitality beneath. Her features were excellent, framed by a sweet, engaging smile, but it was her eyes that captivated—the deep, dark pools within them burned with a restless spirit, an eager life force that demanded attention.

At first, Willoughby hesitated, his gaze faltering under the weight of his recent indebtedness. Yet, as the initial embarrassment faded, replaced by the realization that this gentleman combined impeccable breeding with a frank, vivacious nature, his demeanor shifted completely. It was when Marianne declared her passion for music and dancing that the transformation was sealed. With a look of pure approbation, she surrendered the floor to him, and for the remainder of his stay, her conversation flowed exclusively toward these shared interests.

To speak of a favorite amusement was to unlock Marianne's tongue. She possessed no shyness, no reserve; she could not remain silent when the topic turned to dance or melody. Soon, it became clear that their enjoyment was not merely parallel but identical, born of a profound agreement in judgment. Encouraged by this harmony, Marianne pressed further, questioning him on literature. As she listed her favorite authors, glowing with rapture, it became impossible for a man of twenty-five to remain unmoved. His defenses crumbled instantly. Wherever a difference in taste might have arisen, Marianne's arguments, delivered with the brilliance of her eyes, prevailed immediately. He acquiesced to every decision, catching her enthusiasm like a flame, and before his visit ended, they spoke with the intimacy of old acquaintances.

"Elinor," Marianne exclaimed later, as soon as Willoughby had departed, "how fair! How just! Are my ideas so scanty?" She paused, realizing the truth behind her sister's teasing. "But I see what you mean. I have been too easy, too happy, too frank. I have violated every rule of decorum, speaking openly and sincerely where I should have been reserved, spiritless, and deceitful—if I had spoken only of the weather and the roads, offering a comment once every ten minutes, this reproach would not exist."

"My dear love," their mother interjected gently, "do not take offense at Elinor; she was only joking. I would scold her myself if she wished to dampen the joy of your conversation." At those words, Marianne's spirits lifted instantly.

Willoughby, for his part, offered every sign of delight in their growing friendship. He visited daily. Initially, he claimed to come to inquire after Marianne's health, but as her welcome grew warmer with each passing day, that excuse became superfluous, especially since she was now fully recovered from her confinement. For several days she had been bedridden, yet those days felt far less irksome than usual. Willoughby was a youth of considerable ability, quick imagination, and lively spirits, coupled with an open, affectionate manner. Above all, he possessed a natural ardor of mind, a quality now ignited and amplified by Marianne's own enthusiasm, making him irresistible to her heart.

Their society became her highest delight. They read, talked, and sang together, his musical talents proving considerable. He read aloud with a sensibility and spirit that Edward, unfortunately, lacked. In Mrs. Dashwood's eyes, he was flawless. Elinor, however, saw a flaw that troubled her: a propensity to speak too freely on every occasion, regardless of the audience or circumstance. In hastily voicing his opinions of others, in sacrificing general politeness for the sake of undivided attention when his heart was engaged, and in lightly dismissing the forms of worldly propriety, he revealed a lack of caution that Elinor could not endorse, despite Marianne's vigorous defense.

Marianne began to realize that the desperate conviction she had held at sixteen—that no man could ever satisfy her ideal of perfection—had been rash and unjust. Willoughby embodied everything her fantasy had sketched in those unhappy moments and every brighter interval thereafter. His behavior spoke loudly of his earnest wishes, matching the strength of his abilities. Even Mrs. Dashwood, who had previously harbored no speculative thoughts of marriage, found herself hoping and expecting union within a week, secretly congratulating herself on securing two such sons-in-law as Edward and Willoughby.

Meanwhile, Colonel Brandon's partiality for Marianne, which his friends had noted long ago, finally became apparent to Elinor, precisely because it was ignored by everyone else. Attention and wit had been diverted to Willoughby's more fortunate rivalry; the mockery formerly directed at Brandon for his sensitivity had vanished when his feelings truly began to demand it. Reluctantly, Elinor had to accept that the sentiments Mrs. Jennings had attributed to Brandon were now genuinely stirred by her sister. While a general resemblance of disposition might hasten Willoughby's affection, a striking opposition of character did not hinder Colonel Brandon's regard. Elinor viewed this with concern. What hope could a silent man of fifty-three years hold against a lively twenty-year-old? Since she could not wish him success, she heartily wished for indifference. Yet, she liked him. Despite his gravity and reserve, she saw an object of genuine interest. His serious manners were mild, and his reserve seemed the result of suppressed sorrow rather than natural gloom. Sir John's hints of past injuries and disappointments had justified Elinor's belief that he was an unfortunate man, earning her respect and compassion. Perhaps she pitied him the more because he was slighted by Willoughby and Marianne, who, prejudiced against his lack of liveliness and youth, seemed determined to undervalue his merits.

One day, while discussing him, Willoughby remarked, "Brandon is just the kind of man whom everybody speaks well of, and nobody cares about; whom all are delighted to see, and nobody remembers to talk to."

"That is exactly what I think of him," Marianne agreed eagerly.

"Do not boast of it, however," Elinor cautioned. "It is injustice in both of you. He is highly esteemed by the whole family at the Park, and I never fail to make an effort to converse with him whenever I see him."

"That he is patronized by *you*," Willoughby retorted, "certainly counts in his favor. But regarding the esteem of the others, it is a reproach in itself. Who would submit to the indignity of being approved by such women as Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings, if they could command the indifference of anyone else?"

"But perhaps the abuse of people like yourself and Marianne makes amends for the regard of Lady Middleton and her mother. If their praise is mere censure, then your censure may be praise, for they are no more discerning than you are prejudiced and unjust."

"In defense of your protégé, you can even be saucy," Willoughby observed.

"My protégé, as you call him, is a sensible man," Marianne countered, "and sense will always have attractions for me. Yes, Marianne, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has seen the world, traveled abroad, and possesses a thinking mind. I have found him capable of providing vast information on many subjects, answering my inquiries with readiness, good breeding, and good nature."

"That is to say," Marianne sneered, "he has told you that the climate in the East Indies is hot and the mosquitoes troublesome."

"He *would* have told me so, I doubt not, had I asked, but I was previously informed on those points."

"Perhaps," Willoughby suggested, "his observations extend to the existence of nabobs, gold mohurs, and palanquins."

"I venture to say that *his* observations stretch far beyond *your* candour. But tell me, why do you dislike him?"

"I do not dislike him," Willoughby insisted. "On the contrary, I consider him a very respectable man, who has everyone's good word and nobody's notice; who has more money than he spends and more time than he employs wisely, and acquires two new coats every year."

"And add to this," Marianne cried, "that he lacks genius, taste, and spirit. His understanding holds no brilliance, his feelings no ardour, and his voice no expression."

"You judge his imperfections so broadly," Elinor replied calmly, "and rely so heavily on the strength of your own imagination that my commendation of him appears cold and insipid by comparison. I can only pronounce him to be a sensible man, well-bred, well-informed, of gentle address, and, I believe, possessing an amiable heart."
