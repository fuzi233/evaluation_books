Mrs. Dashwood was taken aback for a fleeting instant upon seeing him, yet in her estimation, his arrival at Barton was the most natural occurrence imaginable. Any initial surprise vanished instantly, replaced by a radiant joy and a warm expression of affection that far outweighed her wonder. She greeted him with the utmost kindness, and any trace of shyness, coldness, or reserve quickly melted away under the weight of her welcoming demeanor. Even before he crossed the threshold, these barriers had begun to crumble, completely overwhelmed by Mrs. Dashwood's captivating nature. Indeed, it was nearly impossible for a man to harbor deep feelings for either of her daughters without those sentiments inevitably extending to her mother. To Elinor's quiet satisfaction, Edward soon began to resemble his former self; his affections seemed to reawaken toward them all, and his genuine interest in their welfare became evident once more.

However, a certain heaviness lingered about him. Though he praised their home, marveled at its view, and attended to every courtesy with kindness, he was plainly not in high spirits. The entire family sensed this discrepancy. Mrs. Dashwood, attributing his gloom to some perceived lack of generosity on the part of his mother, sat down to dinner with a heart indignant against all selfish parents.

"What are Mrs. Ferrars's designs for you just now, Edward?" she asked after the meal, as they gathered around the fire. "Are you still destined to be a great orator, whether you wish it or not?"

"No, indeed," he replied. "I trust my mother is finally convinced that I possess neither the talent nor the inclination for public life!"

"But how will you establish a reputation? For surely you must be famous to satisfy your family. With no desire for expense, no affection for strangers, no profession, and no assurance, you might find it a difficult path."

"I shall make no attempt at it. I have no wish to be distinguished, and I have every reason to believe I never shall. Thank heaven, I cannot be forced into genius or eloquence."

"You have no ambition, I know well. Your wishes are all moderate."

"As moderate as those of the rest of the world, I imagine," he said. "I wish, as everyone else does, to be perfectly happy; but, like everyone else, it must be in my own way. Greatness will not secure that for me."

"Strange that it wouldn't!" cried Marianne, her voice ringing with enthusiasm. "What do wealth or grandeur have to do with happiness?"

"Grandeur has very little to do with it," said Elinor gently, "but wealth has much."

"Elinor, for shame!" Marianne exclaimed, turning sharply to her sister. "Money can only grant happiness where there is nothing else to provide it. Beyond a comfortable competency, it offers no real satisfaction regarding mere self."

"Perhaps," Elinor smiled, "we may arrive at the same conclusion. *Your* competence and *my* wealth are, I dare say, remarkably similar; and without them, as the world currently stands, we would both agree that every form of external comfort must be lacking. Your ideas are simply more noble than mine. Come, tell me exactly what constitutes your competence?"

"About eighteen hundred or two thousand a year; not more than that."

Elinor burst into laughter. "*Two* thousand a year! *One* is my wealth! I knew exactly how this conversation would end."

"And yet two thousand a year is a truly moderate income," Marianne insisted, her face flushing slightly. "A family cannot possibly be maintained on less. I am certain I am not extravagant in my demands. A proper establishment of servants, a carriage—or perhaps two—and hunting hounds cannot be sustained on anything smaller."

Elinor smiled again, listening to her sister describe their future expenses at Combe Magna with such vivid accuracy.

"Hunting hounds!" Edward repeated, leaning forward. "But why must you have hounds? Not everyone hunts."

"But most people do," Marianne replied, her cheeks glowing with animation.

"I wish," said Margaret, striking out with a sudden, novel idea, "that someone would give us all a large fortune apiece!"

"Oh, that they would!" cried Marianne, her eyes sparkling with delight at the prospect of such imaginary bliss.

"We are all unanimous in that wish, I suppose," Elinor remarked, "despite the apparent insufficiency of wealth."

"Oh, dear!" sighed Margaret. "How happy I should be! I wonder what I would do with it!"

Marianne looked as though she already knew the answer perfectly.

"I should be utterly puzzled to spend such a large fortune myself," Mrs. Dashwood interjected, "if my children were all to be rich without my assistance."

"You must begin your improvements on this house," Elinor suggested lightly, "and your difficulties will soon vanish."

"What magnificent orders would travel from this household to London in such an event!" Edward mused, his tone lightening considerably. "What a happy day for booksellers, music-sellers, and print shops! You, Miss Dashwood, would issue a general commission for every new print of merit to be sent to you. And as for Marianne—I know the greatness of her soul—there would not be enough music in all of London to content her. And books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott... she would buy them all over and over again. I believe she would purchase every copy available to prevent them from falling into unworthy hands. She would acquire every volume that teaches her how to admire an ancient, twisted tree. Would you not, Marianne? Forgive me if I am being rather saucy. But I was willing to demonstrate that I have not forgotten our old disputes."

"I love to be reminded of the past, Edward," Marianne answered softly, her gaze steady. "Whether it be melancholy or gay, I cherish recalling it. And you will never offend me by speaking of former times."

"You are quite right in supposing how I would spend my money—at least some of my loose cash would certainly be employed in improving my collection of music and books."

"And the bulk of your fortune would be laid out in annuities on the authors or their heirs."

"No, Edward, I should have something else to do with it."

"Perhaps, then, you would bestow it as a reward upon that person who wrote the ablest defense of your favorite maxim—that no one can ever fall in love more than once in their life? Your opinion on that point remains unchanged, I presume?"

"Undoubtedly. At my age, opinions are tolerably fixed. It is unlikely that I should now see or hear anything capable of changing them."

"Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see," Elinor observed, glancing warmly at her sister. "She is not at all altered."

"She has only grown a little more grave than she was," Edward noted.

"Nay, Edward," Marianne interjected, "you need not reproach me. You are not very gay yourself."

"Why should you think so!" he replied, letting out a soft sigh. "But gaiety has never been a part of my character."

"Nor do I consider it a part of Marianne's," Elinor said quietly. "I should hardly call her a lively girl. She is very earnest, very eager in everything she undertakes. She sometimes talks a great deal, and always with animation, but she is not often truly merry."

"I believe you are right," Edward admitted, "and yet I have always considered her a lively girl."

"I have frequently detected myself in such errors," Elinor confessed, a thoughtful look crossing her face. "In a total misapprehension of character in some particular. I fancied people so much more gay or grave, or ingenious or stupid, than they truly are, and I can hardly tell why or in what the deception originates. Sometimes one is guided by what they say of themselves, and very frequently by what others say of them, without taking time to deliberate and judge."

"But I thought it was right, Elinor," Marianne said, her tone earnest. "To be guided wholly by the opinion of others. I thought our judgments were given merely to be subservient to those of our neighbors. This has always been your doctrine, I am sure."

"No, Marianne, never. My doctrine has never aimed at the subjugation of the understanding. All I have ever attempted to influence has been behavior. You must not confuse my meaning. I confess, I have often wished you to treat our acquaintances in general with greater attention; but when have I advised you to adopt their sentiments or to conform to their judgment in serious matters?"

"You have not been able to bring your sister over to your plan of general civility," Edward turned to Elinor. "Do you gain no ground?"

"On the contrary," Elinor replied, looking expressively at Marianne.

"My judgment," Edward returned, "is entirely on your side of the question; but I am afraid my practice lies much more on my sister's side. I never wish to offend, but I am so foolishly shy that I often appear negligent when I am merely held back by my natural awkwardness.
