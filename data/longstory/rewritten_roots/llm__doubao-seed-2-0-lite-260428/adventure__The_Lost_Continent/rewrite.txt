We were both stiff as fence posts after a full night sleeping rough out in the Gran Canaria highlands: even here, the thick mountain dew and the sharp post-sunset chill bite right through unlined hiking clothes, no matter how many layers you pile on. Normally I’d shake that ache out with a quick jog first thing, but the ground here is all jagged lava scree, not three straight yards of level ground for miles around. So when Coppinger started doing calisthenics he’d dreamed up, lifting two chunks of bristly, craggy lava like they were gym dumbbells, I followed suit.

Coppinger’s got more degrees than a university prospectus, for one thing — he picks up a new obscure qualification on average every other year — and he’s fanatical about his fitness routines, treats them like a religious observance. Two days of prior rain had left a thin trickle of water running down the bottom of the deep barranca gully nearby, so we trundled down there to wash the grime off and brush our teeth. You have no idea how heavenly a fresh toothbrush feels on a weeks-long backcountry expedition.

“Look at this,” Coppinger said, tipping out his pockets onto a flat rock. “We’ve got barely a crumb of grub left, and it’s all been wrapped in that scratchy local Spanish newspaper till it’s half ink.”
“Yours is mostly pipe tobacco ash.”
“Worse if we leave it. We’ve got hours of nasty scrambling ahead of us before we get a hot meal.”

That much was obvious, so we sat right there by the gully stream and ate every last crumb. We still had a ten-mile trudge ahead to the little roadside fonda at Santa Brigida where we’d left our heavy packs, and Coppinger was dead set on taking a hundred more photos and measurements of the cliffside caves before we left the site. We’d be ravenous long before we got to eat, and long before we got to taste the fonda patron’s legendary aged country wine — the kind no tourist holed up in the fancy Las Palmas hotels even knows exists, the stuff that’d vanish off the shelves in a week if word ever leaked out.

To be perfectly honest, the two crumbly Guanche mummies we’d already dug up were more than enough adventure for me. Their goatskin wrappings were brittle as old tissue paper, and the poor ancient things puffed out dust like a ripe puffball the second you so much as tapped them. But Coppinger was convinced he’d stumbled on the ruins of some lost sacred Guanche college hidden in the cliff face, and he wouldn’t rest until he’d ransacked every last cave pocket in that whole rock wall. He had 28 rolls of film left in his kodak and a full stock of flashlight powder, and insisted we clear the whole site out now instead of wasting weeks of time on a return trip. So he hefted the crowbar, I slung the knotted hemp rope over my shoulder, and we trundled up to the cliff ridge that had baked us to a crisp under the midday sun the day before.

It was obvious the caves had been left untouched for centuries: the old Guanche had used ladders woven from goathide rope back in the day, Coppinger claimed, yanking them up behind them to lock out trespassers, and the cave mouths sat in an even row 30 feet below the ridge top, 50 feet above the bouldered cliff base. No casual local hiker was going to climb vertical rock to poke around, that much was certain. Instead of hauling clunky ladders up from below, our plan was to rappel straight down the cliff face from the ridge top to each cave mouth, then scramble down to the base and hike back around to the next gap in the rock.

Coppinger was brave, and never got vertigo, but he was portly, pushing fifty, and the fact that he insisted on climbing down every single cave himself told me just how obsessed he was with the site. I went first every time, climbing into the cave mouth to reach down for him, but when you’re hanging off a 90-degree cliff face on a frayed thin rope, there’s not much real help you can offer the climber behind you besides yelling encouragement. The first three caves were nothing but empty old store chambers, and I told him he could save himself the climb, but he waved me off, insisting my untrained eye couldn’t tell a prison cell from a young priest’s classroom from a grain silo. Classic thorough, over-imaginative archaeologist, that.

By mid-afternoon the sun was blistering hot, and Coppinger was clearly at the end of his strength, but he was stubborn as a mule, refusing to quit even with an 80-foot drop below the ridge. Finally he had to pause, but there were three more unopened cave openings left, and he sweet-talked me into checking them, promising full legal ownership of whatever find I pulled out of them. I was sun-struck, my palms raw and oozing from the rough hemp rope, and sick to death of dusty mummies — but he was so dead set on it I caved.

I scrambled over loose scree, wedged the crowbar into a solid fissure at the cliff top, and rappelled down the rock face, swinging hard through an overhang to land with a thump on my back in the cave mouth, never letting go of the rope. This cave was nothing like the rough, hacked-out dens we’d explored before: it had been cut clean with sharp tools, walls smooth and flat, angling inwards towards the roof to make a whole connected warren of chambers. The three unmarked gaps Coppinger had spotted? They were just doorways and windows leading into this single massive complex. No wall inscriptions, just dust and bats, so I smoked a full cigarette (Coppinger hated rushing “official archaeological surveys”) before yelling down my report.

He panicked, yelled for me to hang on, and tied his kodak and flashlight gear to the end of the rope for me to haul up. I snapped photos of four of the chambers, then set the camera on a wide, high ledge by a fifth doorway to steady it — and the second I put my weight on that ledge to recharge the flash, there was a sharp crackle, and the whole slab dropped half a foot. It was a hidden compartment, hermetically sealed with cement. I dug out the rubble, pried at the contents with my pocket knife and the heel of my boot, and pulled out a solid 20x15x15 inch block of thin stacked sheets: each one made of shiny talc, coated in a dark toffee-coloured waxy substance, scored with tiny patterns I wrote off as decorative carvings — Coppinger had drilled it into my head a hundred times that the ancient Guanche people had no written language, so I never guessed those marks were letters in a lost, unknown script. I lowered the whole block down to Coppinger on the rope.

By the time I climbed back down to the base of the cliff, he was on all fours in the dirt, nearly mad with excitement. It wasn’t Guanche, he hissed. The talc wasn’t even native to the Canary Islands, the script was a variant no modern scholar had ever documented, the waxy coating was some unknown mineral compound, likely asphalt. This wasn’t a Guanche relic — this was Atlantean. The lost continent of Atlantis, the very land Plato wrote of that sank beneath the waves just north of the Canary Islands, the cataclysm that spawned every ancient flood myth around the world, including the old Greek story of Deucalion. He fumed that I’d smashed the top and bottom layers prying the block loose, claiming those lost pages held a full, unique account of ancient Yucatan, a far-flung Atlantean colony.

By then it was already four o’clock, and I was ravenous, so we trundled the ten miles back to the Santa Brigida fonda. Coppinger wrapped the precious manuscript tenderly in his coat, refusing to let it be tied to the pack for fear of chipping the edges, and carried it the whole way himself until he was nearly dropping from exhaustion. Back at the fonda, I ate the biggest dinner I could find, cracked open that legendary aged Canary wine, smoked a final pipe, and crashed straight into bed. Coppinger ate a meal too, but he never slept that night: he pored over the manuscript by candlelight, bleary-eyed but bouncing with excitement the next morning.

The author, he told me, was none other than Deucalion himself: an Atlantean priest-general, one of the very few survivors of the continent’s sinking, who’d carried this full history of his people to this hidden cave to save it from the waves. The pages were crammed with illustrations of long-extinct beasts: giant cave tigers, massive predatory bats, mammoths, creatures no living human had laid eyes on in ten thousand years. He claimed it would take him a full year of hard work to translate the full, damaged text.

That same afternoon I got an urgent cable ordering me back to England for critical business. I raced to Las Palmas, caught the Liverpool steamer just as it was pulling out of the harbour, and buried myself under work for a full year, barely thinking of Coppinger or the manuscript at all. Then I spotted a small paragraph in a national newspaper: he’d donated the talc manuscript to the British Museum, valued at no less than ten thousand pounds.

I wrote him a pointed note reminding him he’d explicitly promised the full find belonged to me. He sent back a stiff formal reply, enclosed the full English translation of the manuscript, and claimed the profits of publication would make full reimbursement. When I sent an angry follow-up letter, his solicitors wrote back to state all future correspondence had to go through them.

I’m not here to drag out old arguments. This is the full, unredacted history of Deucalion, the last survivor of Atlantis, the story of a lost world swallowed whole by the sea. Dr. Coppinger takes full credit — and full responsibility — for the translation work. All proceeds from this publication go to me, the man who pried those talc sheets out of that hidden cliffside compartment by pure luck. Any changes to this arrangement will be announced publicly if they ever come to pass. I don’t expect they ever will.

The grand viceregal installation ceremony was done, but the thunder of the drums and bray of trumpets still hummed in my bones. The imperial proclamation had been read, Empress Phorenice’s name roared in adoration by every throat in the gilded pyramid hall, and Tatho—my old brother-in-arms, my once humble comrade from the Priests’ Clan—had been formally seated on the snake-throne, the 300-gemmed chain of supreme office draped over his shoulders. For twenty years I’d held that throne. Today I’d handed the reins of the Yucatan colony to him, no protest, no fuss, as ritual demanded.

Tatho rose for his first state procession, I walked behind him, arms folded, head bowed as custom dictated, between ranks of burnished copper guards, courtiers in iridescent feathered robes, tribal chiefs draped in jaguar pelts. The roof timbers shook with the cries: “Long live Tatho! Empress Phorenice forever!” He inclined his head slow, stately, acknowledging each cheer as the ritual required, moving to the three lesser thrones of the East, North and South provincial governors to receive their homage. I followed in his wake, humbled, deposed, and I will admit the iron mask of a passionless court face was hard to hold in place. Twenty years earlier, all these same bows, all these same obeisances, had been offered to me. The gods had turned the wheel of fortune, and I was the one beneath it now.

But etiquette could not forbid the soft, warm glances. Every guard, every courtier, every chief lining that hall looked at me not with the scorn reserved for failed rulers, but with quiet gratitude, with unfeigned regret. It is a rare thing, to step down from power and find no hand raised to trip you. The old, unbreakable custom of installation days allowed every attendee to level accusations against the outgoing viceroy with full amnesty, to push for policy changes, to air every grievance unpunished. We circled the hall three full times, as law required. Not a single voice spoke against my twenty years of rule. Not a single person called for a shift in how the colony was run. The silence was a greater tribute than all the cheering for Tatho.

We marched across tiles inlaid solid with beaten gold, the great officers of state falling in behind us, until we reached the heavy stone doors of the private viceregal quarters—quarters that had been mine, an hour before. I had no home left in Yucatan’s ziggurat cities, no right to the palaces I’d built, the roads I’d carved through the impenetrable jungle. I should have counted myself lucky, of course: any other ousted viceroy would have lost his head the second the new ruler’s seal hit the proclamation paper. But the bitter tang of displacement stung all the same.

Tatho called out the required ritual phrase: “Open to the Viceroy!” The slave attendants hauled the two massive stone valves wide. He stepped inside first, I at his heels, the rest of the procession halting at the threshold to call their formal farewells. The stone doors clanged shut behind us, the heavy lock slamming home, and for the first time all the court pomp, all the masks of office, fell away. Tatho folded his arms the same humble way he used to when we were lads running errands for the lowliest village priest, and bowed low to me.

“Deucalion,” he said, quiet, “I never sought this post. It was forced on me. If I’d refused, my head would be on a spike outside the Atlantis palace gates by now, and some creature who hates you with every bone in his body would have been sent out here to replace you. Empress Phorenice does not suffer her will to be questioned.”

“My friend,” I said, and clapped him on the shoulder, “there is no man alive in all of Atlantis or its overseas territories I would have handed this government to more gladly. For twenty years I’ve ruled here, from the rainforest coasts of Yucatan to the high sierras of Mexico, first under the old King, then as minister to this new Empress. I know this land like the lines on my own palm. I’ve hunted jaguars and roving savage tribes in the deepest jungle. I built roads that cut travel time across the province by half, dammed the rivers so ocean-going galleons could sail three hundred miles inland. I nurtured the metalworking arts, the jade carvers, the astronomers, and said the daily rituals to the Sun God three times a day myself. Through famines, through hurricanes, through border skirmishes, I ruled only to make this people prosperous, to tie them close to the motherland Atlantis. I love them like a father. And now I hand them to you, Tatho, and I beg you to guard their welfare as your own.”

“I will never fill your boots, Deucalion,” he said, and his voice was thick. “But I swear to you, I will follow every step of your rule exactly, no new stupid changes, no greedy taxes to line imperial pockets. I came out here kicking and screaming, I’d never have agreed to take your place if I’d known how much this deposition would hurt you.”

“We’re alone here,” I said, “no need for fine words. The formality of the court is shut outside that stone door. And this came out of nowhere. Till an hour ago, when you requested audience, I thought I had another ten years of rule ahead of me. I still don’t know why they’re pulling me back to Atlantis.”

“The official proclamation says they need your skills back home, to serve the empire,” Tatho said, and then he glanced over his shoulder at the tapestries, pulled me further into the center of the room, and dropped his voice so low I almost had to lean in to hear. “But that’s just the cover story. I think there’s trouble brewing. Phorenice is pulling every able, experienced governor back to the capital, because her throne is shaking.”

“You don’t have to whisper,” I told him. “This whole wing is carved out of solid bedrock, ten feet of stone between us and any other chamber. I oversaw every course of masonry laid here, no hidden nooks for eavesdroppers. Besides, we don’t do that kind of slimy court trickery out here in the colonies. We consider listening in on private conversations a disgrace fit for a slave.”

Tatho huffed a bitter laugh. “That’s the innocence of the colonies, right? Back home in Atlantis, a loose tongue gets you beheaded before sundown. Half the court’s full of spies paid to carry every private word straight to the palace. But fine, since you’re sure we’re safe: the throne’s wobbling bad. Phorenice is scrambling for every strong, loyal man she can find to prop it up.”

“I haven’t been back to Atlantis in twenty years,” I said. “I don’t know the first thing about their modern politics. The few bits of news that drift across the ocean on trading galleons never felt relevant to us. Yucatan’s its own world, you’ll learn that soon enough, full of new problems, new people, new priorities. Atlantis feels like a half-remembered dream, a shadow far across the water. I poured every last bit of my energy into this place.”

“Deucalion, you don’t know how famous you are back home,” Tatho said, grinning a little. “When we were lads in the priest college, they used to tell us the great heroes of the past were the greatest men who ever lived. Now? They teach your name as the model. Mothers name their newborn sons Deucalion, it’s the most popular name in the whole empire. You’re a household word. Only Phorenice’s name is spoken as often.”

I frowned. That news irked me. “I did my duty for duty’s sake, not for the crowd’s cheap praise. And besides, the people’s mouths should be full of the names of the gods, not mortal men.”

Tatho shrugged. “The gods haven’t been very popular lately. Modern science, new inventions, people have outgrown the old stories, and no new god has popped up to replace them. If your only competition was the gods, your name would be ten times bigger than it is now.”

“Of mortal names,” I said, “Empress Phorenice’s should be first. The old King’s dead, she’s the ruler.”

“Oh, she’s determined to make sure that’s the case,” Tatho said, and there was a sharp, dark edge to his tone. I pulled him over to a carved marble bench, sat down beside him.

“I’m not talking to the new Viceroy of Yucatan right now,” I said. “I’m talking to Tatho, my old classmate from the priest college, the man who fought beside me in a dozen border skirmishes, who spent three straight weeks with me sorting out a hay farmer’s land dispute when we were just rookie low-level administrators, long before any of us got famous. This room was mine two hours ago, and that doesn’t change the fact that between us, we never used anything but plain, unvarnished truth. Spit it out. What’s happening with Phorenice? What has she done to make the ancient, unshakable Atlantis throne wobble like a loose tooth?”

Tatho sighed. “Man, I’ve gotten so used to polished, empty court words, I almost forgot how to speak plain. But for the sake of those old days, when we cared more about a farmer’s stolen goat than a whole rebellious city, I’ll tell you everything. Just know this: if anyone overhears me, we both get tortured to death and hung on the palace walls as traitors. You know the old law of Atlantis, right? The throne was supposed to pass only to a man born of the Priestly Clan. Phorenice is neither. She’s a swineherd’s daughter, from the poorest mountain village in the north. She’s declared herself a god’s child, born of a miracle, and anyone who even whispers that they remember her real parents gets burned alive. The whole story’s officially a secret now, and half the people believe the fable. Zaemon, the provincial governor of that mountain region, his wife adopted her, raised her as their own. The swineherd and his wife tried to protest. They turned up dead two weeks later.

“She grew up sharp as a blade. Before she was fifteen, she ruled the whole Zaemon household, and half the province along with it. Remember Zaemon? The old fool, all wrapped up in occult mysteries, no backbone at all, couldn’t lead a patrol to catch a stray dog, let alone an army. She learned to fight with spear and sword, trained with the royal guards, and when a rebellion blew up in the province, no one expected her to march out with the troops. But she did. Crushed the rebellion in a week. Gave every surviving rebel a choice: die by the sword, or join her army. Every last one of them joined. She’s got a magnetism about her, Deucalion. You’ve never seen anything like it.”

Tatho leaned forward, his eyes bright, like he was half lost in the memory. “The Priests’ Clan got furious, of course. They sent two neighbouring governors with their full armies to arrest her. She wiped the floor with both of them. Killed them both in battle, gave their troops the same choice—die, or join her. Her army tripled in size. Then the Council of Priests got desperate. They gathered the largest, best-equipped army Atlantis had ever raised. And they put me in command. They even promised me the throne, if I won. Told me the old king was dying, that a conquering hero would be the obvious pick for new emperor. I swore I’d capture her alive, drag her back to the capital in chains. I had every advantage. Our army outnumbered hers eight to one. But she’d invented something no one had ever seen before: fire-tubes, that hurl a sharp iron dart further than two bowshots could reach. She used tactics none of us had ever studied. Her men would harry our flanks, hit us and run, never let us get close for the hand-to-hand battle we were trained for. My whole army melted away like sand in the tide. Panic broke out. I tried to die fighting, rather than go back to the capital and get beheaded for failure. But she found me, surrounded by three of my last loyal men, fighting off a whole squad of her troops. She walked right up, looked me dead in the eye, gave me the same choice: die by her sword, or swear service to her. And just then, a runner came galloping up with news: the old King was dead.

“I looked at her, Deucalion. Fairest woman I ever saw, sharpest mind I ever met, the greatest general in the history of our people. And I knelt. I swore fealty to her, right there on the battlefield. I’ve been her man ever since. You can judge me all you want, but wait till you see her with your own eyes, feel that magnetism. You’ll understand. Every man who meets her does.”

Tatho’s words were no sleeping draught that night. I lay awake staring at the pyramid’s stone ceiling, realizing I’d made a fool’s bargain: for twenty whole years, I’d buried myself so deep in building the colony of Yucatan that I’d completely lost touch with the machinations back home in Atlantis. I’d known for years that transocean mariners spoke in guarded half-truths, that no news leaked out of the imperial borders unless the Empress herself allowed it – but I’d had no patience to peel back the veil. I refused to torture men who held orders of silence from their superiors, not even for my own curiosity.

The iron discipline of our Priestly Clan left me no choice in the end. My office was stripped from me without a single moment’s warning. All my seals, all my state authority, every document that marked me as ruler of Yucatan was handed over to Tatho before the last sun of my tenure had even set. I was allowed the courtesy of one more night in the pyramid that I’d designed and raised stone by stone, but at first light I was commanded to ride for the harbour at full speed and report to Atlantis at once.

Tatho, for all his newfound vice-regal arrogance, was true to our old comrade’s bond. He was at my chambers an hour before dawn, his whole staff of servants standing behind him ready to put every resource he commanded at my disposal. I asked for almost nothing. “A single ship to take me home,” I told him. “That’s all I need. I’ll owe you the debt.”

He blinked, surprised. “You can have any ship in my fleet, but they’re fouled from the long crossing, all due for a careen. You’d crawl across the ocean at half speed. Why not take Yucatan’s own state navy? They’re anchored in the harbour right now, fine fighting galleons, built for the open sea.”

“They aren’t mine to take. They belong to the colony.”
“Deucalion, you *were* the colony. You’ve ruled it for twenty years.”

I understood what he was implying, and I rejected it stiffly. The ships were funded by public tax or owned by merchant syndicates, I told him, and I had no more claim to them than the lowest labourer in the quays. Tatho shrugged. “You know your own policies best, but it’s madness for a man in our position not to keep a private navy stashed away. You never know when a recall will drop out of nowhere. Without precautions, a lifetime of work can be looted out from under you in a dozen hours.”

“I have no fear of that,” I said coldly.
“Of course not – because you know *I’m* your friend. But if some other man had been sent to replace you? You’d have left here penniless, stripped of every coin you ever earned. Few men can resist the pull of a full treasury sitting under their hand, waiting to be emptied.”

“My Lord Tatho,” I said, “it’s clear our tastes have grown worlds apart. Every possession I’ve earned in twenty years of governing would make no man covetous. I have the rough wool robe on my back, a box of medicinal herbs, three servants: two scribes, and a burly European savage who cooks my meals and draws my bath. I drew only a common soldier’s ration from the state for all my years of service, nothing more. If any man in my name stole so much as an ounce of bronze from a Yucatan farmer, I ask you as one last favour: hang the thief for me before the week is out.”

Tatho stared at me, half amused, half bewildered. “I can’t decide if I admire you or pity you. Atlantis heard tales of your uprightness, your strict justice, but no soul guessed you’d taken it this far. Man, money is power. With gold you could outmaneuver anyone who tried to trip you up. Without it? One tiny, petty betrayal will break you before you see it coming.”

“The Gods will choose my fate.”
“Maybe. But I choose to hedge my bets. I’m not here to copy your model of governance. I’ll rule Yucatan well enough, but I’ll rule it for Tatho first. I brought my own eight private warships, my household guard, my wife and her retinue. All of them need to be provided for. Why shouldn’t the people we govern pay a fair price for the peace we give them?”

“We will never agree on that,” I said. “You hold the power now, do with it what you will. But I beg you – go easy on these people when you raise your new taxes. They’ve grown very dear to me.”

“I hate that I’ve disgusted you,” he said, and there was real regret in his voice. “But I can’t live the way you did. I have a wife and children to care for, even if you have no ties to hold you. You have no idea how to feast, man. I spent the whole long voyage across the ocean daydreaming of the grand welcome banquet you’d throw for me – and what did I get? A meal worse than what a backcountry farmer eats every night. Your European cook is a devil who burns every cut of meat. Your robes are rough as a herdsman’s, you don’t wear a single jewel to mark your rank.”

“I eat when I’m hungry,” I said. “I wear a robe until it wears through. Gluttony and silken softness never appealed to me. But we’ve talked too long. We’ve both changed over twenty years. Let’s part now, forget these differences, and hold on to the memory of the friendship we shared when we were both young officers in Atlantis.”

Tatho bowed his head. “So be it. But I will not let you leave here on a scruffy merchant galley that will get you halfway across the ocean before it falls apart. You sail on *the Bear* – my own personal flagship – and the rest of my fleet will escort you all the way home. I won’t have the man who built Yucatan walking the roads of the harbour afoot at dawn, for the crowds to see that their former ruler was sent away like a disgraced slave.”

The fleet needed a month to refit, first. They hauled out of the inner harbour, anchored in the entrance basin, and crews worked day and night to caulk the hulls, restock fresh water and salted meat, and recharge their solar drives – the secret Atlantean mariners’ tech that pulled power directly from the unclouded sun, sucking water in at the bow and ejecting it at high pressure from the stern to push the ships faster than any oared galley could ever sail, even against the wind. No more hugging coastlines now. Every ship carried a small, weighted idol balanced on the foredeck, its outstretched arm always pointing steady towards the Southern Cross, so no mariner ever lost his way even when the stars were hidden.

For the first time in twenty years, I had no work to do. I sat on the deck of the *Bear* for hours, staring back at the city I’d raised, the fields we’d cleared from the dense jungle, the pastures that fed a hundred thousand families. I hoped Tatho would not ruin all I’d built.

When we finally raised anchor and sailed east, the voyage was nothing like the slow, cautious crossings of my youth. We cut straight across the open gulf, slipped through the Carib Islands as if they were signposts on a highway, and stopped only twice to resupply with fruit and fresh water. The island natives brought their goods to the docks willingly, no fights, no skirmishes – a quiet proof of just how far Atlantis’s power stretched across the seas.

We hit brutal storms that the mariners fought through with stubborn, quiet endurance. Twice, blazing meteorites hissed down from the sky and slammed into the waves a hundred yards from our hull, sending up fountains of steam. The great sea lizards that ruled the open deeps hunted us for days, and most of the time the fleet outran them – until three of the biggest, a giant male and two smaller females, came at us in a coordinated pack, the way they’d learned to hunt human ships in recent years.

Captain Tob, master of the *Bear*, paled and ordered me to lock myself in the reinforced aftercastle. He said Tatho had sworn he would feed him to the lizards alive if anything happened to me, and he was not about to risk that. But I refused. I wanted to see the fight for myself.

Arrows bounced off the lizards’ thick, bull-like hides. Thrown fire didn’t even singe their scales. Only twenty axes hitting the same exact spot at once could force their heads back, with barely a scratch to show for the effort. The lizards reared up, their necks as tall as the ship’s masts, their jaws foaming, their stench choking the whole deck. I grabbed a sharp-tipped lance from a sailor’s hand, shouldered past Tob’s guards, and baited the nearest of the females. I dodged as she lunged for me, and drove the full length of the lance straight into her small, deep-set, vulnerable eye. The shaft snapped off in my hand. She screamed so loud the waves rippled, thrashed in circles, and fled for the open horizon, blind and bleeding.

Tob, desperate to get the other two beasts off our hull, deliberately shoved two strong sailors into their waiting jaws. The monsters feasted for a few minutes, then turned and swam after their wounded pack mate.

I leaned against the taffrail, breathing hard, the taste of salt and lizard slime still in my mouth. Somewhere ahead of us, hidden just over the curve of the horizon, lay Atlantis. The old intrigues, the court politics, the reason the Priests had recalled me all the way home from the colony I’d loved – all of it was waiting, and I knew the dangers ahead would be far deadlier than any sea lizard. But I smiled. The Gods had kept me alive across the ocean. I would not go down easily.

When we finally hove in sight of Atlantis’s coast, Tob’s strange new navigational tools had carried us to our target with a precision that bordered on the miraculous—but we still faced ten days of backtracking along the shoreline to reach the broad, sheltered inlet that led straight to the continent’s glittering capital.

That first sight of green hills, and the wind that blew off them thick with the scent of wild thyme, turned earth, and sun-warmed pine, saved every last one of us. Weeks of brutal open-ocean tossing had eaten through our crews: dozens were dead, more were bedridden with scurvy, their gums black and swollen, their limbs too weak to heave a line even at the crack of a mate’s lash. But the moment they smelled that land air, the sick dragged themselves upright, color seeped back into their cheeks, and within days they were hauling rigging, patching sails, and spoiling for a fight to defend the voyage they’d all nearly died to finish.

I’d had doubts about my reception in Atlantis ever since I was deposed in Yucatan, no matter what Tatho had promised. But I’d never wasted breath fretting over it. It was all in the Sun God’s hands. Empress Phorenice might rule every inch of mortal land, might order my head struck from my shoulders the second my foot touched her docks—but the Sun reigned above even her. If my death was written, it would come because He willed it, not because I feared it, and so I’d spent the whole long voyage deep in calm study of the higher mysteries, no weight of worry on my mind.

But the day we rounded the two vast, craggy headlands that marked the inlet’s mouth, barely forty-eight hours’ sail from the capital, we spotted it: a whole foreign navy, anchored in a sheltered cove, waiting for us. Lookouts posted on the cliff tops screamed the alarm the second we came into view, and by the time we cleared the headland, every one of their ships was under sail, moving to block our path.

We had only five ships left out of the original fleet—all the rest had sunk in storms, or drifted derelict with every last man dead of scurvy. The enemy had three sleek, gleaming black warships, and three long galleys packed with oars. Our hulls were crusted thick with trailing ocean weed, our sails patched so many times you could barely tell the original canvas. Theirs were bright, fresh, immaculate. We flew Tatho and Deucalion’s royal colours high, unashamed of who we were and what errand brought us home, but the rival fleet carried no banners, no crests, as if they were ashamed to show their faces.

It was no surprise they meant to fight. No law held out on the open seas, miles out of sight of any shore: any crew was free to plunder any other that crossed their path, especially if they came home heavy from a raid or a trading run. Tob set us to scrambling into fighting trim in minutes, and our four remaining captains fell into tight formation beside us. None of them meant to end this long voyage in slavery.

The Sun blazed overhead, feeding full power to our solar engines, and the two fleets surged toward each other, the enemy’s three galleys hanging back to hold line beside their warships. When barely a hundred ship-lengths separated us, the strangers hauled to a halt. One galley pulled ahead, green palm branches flapping from its mast: they wanted a parley.

It was an unusual move, and sea-battered as we were, we had no reason to pick a fight we didn’t have to. We passed word down the line, hauled to a stop too, and Tob tied a dry, withered old sea grass frond to a spear and hoisted it to show we’d respect their ambassador.

The galley glided up, backed till its stern scraped against our shield rail, and a young, sharp-dressed crewman hopped over our gunwales. He was plump, healthy, soft from months of eating land food, and he looked around at our rags and gaunt scurvy-ridden men with a sneer of easy confidence. When he spotted Tob, he nodded like he was greeting an old drinking buddy.

“Old pot-mate,” he called, “your wife’s waiting for you on the quay in Atlantis, four little brats hanging off her skirts. Saw her less than two weeks ago.”
“You didn’t sail all the way out here to pass along home gossip,” Tob grunted. “We’ve drunk more than our share of tankards together, Dason. I know your tricks.”
“I’m just pointing out your home’s still there, all ready to welcome you back.”
“I never forgot it,” Tob said grimly. “It’s why I rammed and sank three separate pirate ships out on the deep, when a less careful man might have just surrendered and taken slavery. I’ve got a family to come home to.”
“I know you’re a stubborn bastard,” Dason laughed. “I’ll admit, if we fight, we’ll lose a few good men before we sink all your crazy rafts and your half-dead crew. But we’ve got orders, and my offer’s simple: hand over what we want, and we’ll let you sail home whole, no slit throats, no trouble.”
“Came to the wrong ship to pluck,” Tob rumbled with a heavy laugh. “We picked up no treasure in Yucatan, only dried food and fresh water. Our holds are full of nothing but half-dead scurvy sailors. You can search them from stem to stern if you don’t believe me. All you’re going to get here is blows, and we’ll hand those out for free.”
“Funny you value your cargo that little,” Dason said, grinning. “Because your cargo is exactly what I was sent to take, if you want that safe conduct home. We all know you’re sailing Deucalion back to Atlantis to prop up Phorenice. We don’t want her getting any more power to fight us. We’re taking Deucalion into our own custody, to deal with him our own way.”
“And if I refuse to hand him over?”
Dason glanced around at our splintered hull, our ragged little fleet. “Then we sink every last one of you in ten minutes. Instead of Deucalion going to stand before the Gods alone, he’ll be dragging a whole ragged crowd of you fools down to the bottom with him.”
“I doubt that,” Tob said, “but we’ll see. Handing over my Lord Deucalion is out of the question, anyway. First: if I showed up in Atlantis without him, Tatho would hunt me down and kill me slow, foot by foot. Second: I saw Deucalion kill a 40-foot sea lizard single handed out on the deep, and he’s too good a man for me to hand over against his will, even to Tatho. Third: you still owe me for your share of that wine cask we drank through on our last shore leave, and I’ll see you in the nether hells before I give you so much as a crumb till you pay that debt.”
“Hope you drown easy,” Dason sneered. “And as for that wife of yours? I’ve had my eye on her for years. I’ll find a very good use for her once you’re fish food.”

Tob’s face went purple with rage. “I’ll snap your neck for that, you spawn of a European brute. Get off my deck before I do it right now, you father of monkey children! Get out, or I’ll stove your ribs in and string your guts around your silly crew’s necks for necklaces.”

Dason didn’t wait for a second warning. He scrambled back over the rail to his galley, and Tob immediately cranked the solar engine of our flagship *The Bear* to full power, roaring orders to the rest of our little fleet. Even the scurvy-weak men jumped to obey. All five of our ships surged forward, picked one enemy warship out of the line, and slammed into its broadside all at once with their underwater rams, tearing its planks open so wide it sank before we could even back clear.

That brought the enemy’s numbers down to five, same as ours—but the advantage didn’t last. Their three fast galleys cracked their whips over their chained oar slaves, surged forward, rammed one of our ships clean through the hull, and cut down every man that tried to swim clear. Then the melee erupted, a tight, savage brawl that would have made the fiercest old warrior grin. The ships ground against each other on the swells like fighting wild beasts, men hacking with axes, thrusting with swords, hurling that sticky burning fire that sears through flesh and wood alike. Man-eating gulls circled overhead, and schools of sharks swarmed the water, fighting over every man that fell overboard.

Every part of the fight converged on *The Bear*. The enemy poured all their rage on us, and every surviving man from our other four ships rallied to us when their own vessels burned or sank. I’m of the Priestly Clan, I’ve fought a dozen wars to carve out new colonies for Atlantis, but that sea battle outdid any fight I’d ever seen. The swaying decks under our feet, the burning fire that clung to every surface, the sharks snapping below, the gulls screaming above—all of it was enough to break the bravest warrior. But those hard-bitten sailors faced it all without a single whimper of fear. Life on the open sea was already a brutal, death-haunted thing; they faced man-eaters and storms every week, and they had no fear left for a fight. Our battle cry was not my royal name, it was *TOB*, the name of that rough, unpolished old captain, and it rang out loud enough to drown out the half-dozen confused rallying cries the enemy shouted at each other.

By the end of it, every ship on both sides was either sunk or a smoldering wreck, their crews all dead—only *The Bear* and Dason’s galley were left. We had barely twenty men left standing out of all the crews that sailed from Yucatan. The enemy had boarded us, and were pushing us back across our blood-slick decks, when we smelled smoke: that burning fire had caught in *The Bear’s* timbers, and the whole ship was going up in flames around us.

Tob just laughed, loud and boisterous. “They can stay here and roast if they’re fools enough!” he roared. “We’re taking their galley. Guard Deucalion, lads, and follow me!”

The whole crew screamed *TOB* at the top of their lungs, and I found myself shouting the name right along with them. It was a strange, glorious thing, fighting not as a leader but in the wake of that big axe-swinging sailor. He cut a path right through the enemy line, his axe red to the hilt, and we charged after him, raging like madmen. Only ten of us made it across to the galley, the flames and smoke of our dying ship spitting at our heels. We stabbed down anyone that tried to follow, hacked through the grapple ropes, and let the burning *Bear* drift away to its death. The chained oar slaves stared at the fight with dull, unconcerned faces, only flinching when a stray arrow nicked one of them. We killed the three last enemy fighters who’d scrambled aboard after us, tossed their bodies to the sharks, chained five surrendered enemy men to the empty oar benches where dead slaves had sat, and left only Dason alive.

He’d dropped his sword, kneeling on the deck waiting for his fate, and Tob marched over to him grinning. “Well, old pot-mate,” he said, “you talked a big game about my wife and kids back on the quay. I’m giving you a choice. I can drag you home, parade you through the docks in front of the whole city, and let my wife do whatever she pleases to your body for that insult. Or I can kill you right here, quick and clean.”
Dason bowed his head, baring his neck. “I’ll take the quick way.”

One swing of Tob’s axe, and Dason’s head was off. He stuck it right on the galley’s bowsprit, as a warning for every other pirate that might try to block the path of Tatho’s fleet. The body he tossed over the rail, and one of the big man-eating gulls swooped down, snatched it up, and flew off to its nest in the cliff crags.

We feasted that night on fresh figs, warm bread, and dried lamb we found in the galley’s stores, and passed a skin of wine around the remaining ten of us. I filled a horn, poured a libation out at Tob’s feet, and clapped a hand on his blood-smeared arm. “You fought a grand fight today. I don’t know if I’ll be given my old power back the second we step ashore, or if Phorenice will order my head struck off on the docks, but the Sun God is my witness: I will never forget how you served me this voyage. Your wife and your children will never want for anything for the rest of their lives.”

Tob laughed, filled his own horn to the brim, and splashed half the wine at my boots. “That’s all the promise I need. The lads have earned their homecoming. And I guarantee you, when I sail into that quay with Deucalion still whole, Empress Phorenice will be so happy to see us, she’ll forget all about the six ships I lost to that trash.”

The oars dipped in smooth, steady strokes as we sailed onward. Ahead of us, over the blue water, the distant white spires of Atlantis began to glow in the sunset.

It was only when enemy war galleys ambushed us at the gulf’s narrow mouth, fighting like feral beasts just to drag me into their custody, that I finally grasped how high my stakes ran as a new recruit for Empress Phorenice. Up to that point I’d barely spared my own significance a second thought. But that carefully laid trap, that unhinged ferocity to get their hands on me? Even the most blind fool could read the writing on the waves: I was meant to be the linchpin of Atlantis’s fate.
If enemies had been watching for our arrival, Phorenice’s scouts had beaten them to the punch. Perched high on the coastal mountain ridgeline, they spotted our galley first, then bolted overland for the capital. That twisting arm of the sea leading to Atlantis’s gates narrows to river width in spots, choked by old volcanic lava flows, then yawns open for half a day’s sail into glassy lake basins where far shores blur into haze. A courier who knew the safe paths across the flats, reeking swamps, and smoking volcanic hills could outrun even the fastest, most brutally whipped oar galley—if he avoided lava streams, roving predators, and flash floods.
Phorenice didn’t skimp on speed. She sent twenty runners to that headland overlooking the gulf mouth, each taking a separate overland route, ordered to prioritize speed over every last precaution. Seven of them made it back to the capital completely unharmed, three more confirmed to have survived their perils, meaning the entire city knew we were coming long before we sighted the sacred peak. We, though, had no clue we were expected, chugging along desperate to end the voyage as fast as possible. The rowing slaves were mostly savage captives from Europe, their unwashed stench thick enough to choke on, and a constant fine grit pouring off an erupting inland volcano bit at our eyes and throats the whole way. Even with all my priest-learned stoicism, plain human curiosity burned hot in me: what kind of reception would I get? Formal arrest and a public trial for defying the old mad priesthood of the Yucatan colony? Quiet anonymity, followed by a quiet palace summons a few weeks later? I never could have guessed the reality.
Dawn broke one morning, and there it was: the sacred mountain’s jagged peak, rimmed with the eternal glow of its summit fires, rising straight behind Atlantis. The boatswains’ whips cracked twice as loud, driving the gasping slaves to heave on their oars for a final sprint. The wind was dead against us, no sails could be raised, but we cut through the water fast, the mountain’s slopes growing longer, until the tips of the city’s hundred pyramids and the spires of its tallest towers began to peek over the horizon, bobbing like they were floating on the sunlit water. It had been twenty years since I’d last seen Atlantis, and my throat tightened. Those oar strokes didn’t just move the galley forward—they were pulling me home.
I picked out landmarks one by one as the city grew clear: the temple where I’d been initiated into manhood, the pyramid’s inner chamber where I’d been sworn to the lesser mysteries, then the small house where my father and mother had raised me, and my eyes blurred with old, soft memories. We pulled up outside the harbour’s white sea walls as the law required, the slaves slumping over their oars, heaving for air. Usually you’d wait hours for the port captain to drag himself out just to prove his dignity, but today a ten-oared launch was already waiting, the harbour boom chain dropped, and it shot out to meet us the second we hove to.
“That’s Dason’s captured galley, right?” the captain called up.
“Right,” Tob yelled back. “We saw his head nailed to the bowsprit. Tatho’s fleet sent all our other ships to the bottom, this stinking hulk was the only one left floating.”
The port captain’s eyes swept over us on the afterdeck, his face going pale. “Where’s Lord Deucalion? If you let him get killed under your charge, the Empress will flay you alive inch by inch.”
Tob jerked his thumb at me, grinning. “The man’s right here. He’ll save my hide himself.”
The captain stared, then dropped to a deep ceremonial bow. “My lord, forgive me for not recognizing you at once—truth be told, I expected you in royal robes, not rough sea clothes.”
“Spare me the formalities,” I said. “I don’t parade my rank with shiny baubles. Just get us into the harbour, will you?”
He all but grovelled, leading us in, the castle guards at the harbour mouth blowing trumpets to announce our arrival. He’d run my royal banner up his launch’s masthead, and by the time we glided across the harbour basin, we could hear music blaring from the quays, see the warm flicker of welcome fires dancing in the sunlight. I was half awestruck—this whole huge city, the Empress herself, making this much fuss over a regular returning subject?
We moored to the royal quay’s gold rings, sacred only to the imperial house, and Tob cackled: “If Dason saw us tying up with plain hemp rope instead of silk, his head would roll right off that bowsprit for real.” He squeezed my hand in his huge calloused fist, all gruff sincerity. “You’re the best fighter this world’s ever seen, Deucalion. That spear throw of yours through the giant lizard? Tavern bards are going to sing of that for a hundred years.”
Three gorgeously robed chamberlains escorted me to a crimson silk pavilion set up right on the quay, then laid out piles of bejewelled robes, insisting I change before the Empress arrived. I flat refused. When one stammered that it was what Phorenice wanted, I asked him point blank if he had a written direct order from her about my clothes. He had to admit he didn’t. “She summoned Deucalion the man, not some glittering popinjay,” I told him. The three of them immediately drew their swords and held the hilts out to me, begging me to kill them now instead of turning them over to the imperial torturers for the insult. I waved them off, but that small moment told me everything: this new Atlantis was a place where men feared torment over nothing more than a misstep about a coat.
I bathed quickly in the pavilion’s over-scented tub, then sat waiting. Phorenice didn’t make me play the petty waiting game that most rulers use to puff up their dignity. The second our galley tied to the quay, she left the palace, the whole grand procession already assembled and ready to march. I watched from the pavilion door as the regiments of soldiers marched out of the broad main street, lined the square, followed by the Mariners’ Guild, the merchant guilds, tanners, spear-makers, all parading in their finest, some riding tamed great beasts that would have torn them to pieces in the wild.
Then the crowds parted, and the first wonder rolled into view: a giant bull mammoth, shaggy as a storm cloud, its tusks gilded, its neck draped in flowers, carrying a gilded half-castle on its back carved with the imperial outstretched hand and silver snake emblems. I’d hunted mammoths all my life, killed dozens that raided village cornfields—they were the fiercest wild thing on the continent short of the giant lizards. And here this one was, tame as a house dog, plodding along like it was born to march in parades.
And then I saw the Empress.
She was small, lithe, perfectly formed, her fiery red hair cut in the new short fashion, bunching soft on her shoulders. Her face was sharp, clever, and breathtakingly fair, but her eyes—no man could plumb the depth of those eyes. She was studying me just as hard as I was studying her. The chamberlains hissed behind me to kneel, to bow my head at the very least, but I walked straight across the marble square, head held high, gaze meeting hers unflinchingly.
She smiled when I got close. “Others kneel to me, because they are all beneath me. But you are Deucalion. I decree this day: no man in Atlantis is above you. Any who disobeys you disobeys me, and will feel my wrath.”
A stair was wheeled over, I climbed up to sit beside her on the golden half-castle, and the mammoth turned, carrying us back toward the royal pyramid. The whole procession fell in around us, heavy-armed troops flanking the giant beast on both sides. Phorenice laughed, soft. “You slighted me at first. You looked at my mammoth before you looked at me. A woman never forgives that, usually.”
“I wasn’t slighting you,” I said. “I was envying you the greatest conquest any man or woman ever made. I’ve fought mammoths, killed them, never once dared to try to tame one.”
“Your flattery’s not hollow, that’s a relief,” she said, her tone turning sharp. “Everyone else around me fawns till I’m sick of it. They all want to be my lover, or stuff their own treasuries full. I sent for you because you’re the only strong man left in the world who doesn’t care about my looks, my title, or my favours. I need you as my chief minister, to rule this empire with me.”
I told her straight I’d be her honest minister, and that I had no interest in the silly game of love. She smiled, approving, and that’s when I noticed it: the gorgeous palace spires, the new temples, all that imperial grandeur, right next to gaunt, hungry-faced crowds, half-naked paupers, even the rich traders looking over their shoulders like they were waiting for a knife in the back.
“Rebels,” Phorenice said, following my gaze. “They’re besieging Atlantis, pounding at the gates, building new siege engines to break the walls. They gave us a holiday today for your welcome. Tomorrow, if you want, you can lead the sally and break their engines for sport.”
I was startled. I’d had no idea the city was under siege. Then she pulled down the shoulder of her silk robe, showing a bloodstained linen bandage just below her left breast. “An arrow got me yesterday,” she said, grinning a little. “If it had hit an inch to the right, you’d be riding to my funeral pyre today instead of this parade. That’s how serious I am about those rebels, Deucalion.”
The girl behind us, Ylga, fanned her robes closed again. My welcome to Atlantis, and to the service of Empress Phorenice, was complete.

It looked, for the time being, like I was to take up residence inside the royal pyramid. The glittering, gold-cloaked cavalcade drew up in the vast paved square before the monolith, hooves stamping, trumpets blaring, and massed into clusters that glinted like scattered coins under the midday sun. The mammoth carrying us rumbled to a halt directly before the pyramid’s grand entrance, attendants hauled a broad oak stair up to its howdah, the horns wailed again, and the three of us who’d ridden beneath the emerald-encrusted canopy of carved snakes – Phorenice, her fan-girl, and I – stepped down to the stone.

Before I even thought to check myself, the decades-deep muscle memory of my priestly upbringing kicked in. I turned unthinking toward the craggy, smoke-wreathed heights of the Sacred Mountain that loomed over the city, dropped to my knees, pressed my forehead to the paving stones, and whispered the age-old prescribed prayer of thanks to the Sun and the old Gods for the safe air that let men draw breath. It was nothing but habit, a ritual I’d performed every day for forty years. But when I stood back up, I could have sworn I heard a snickering titter ripple through the ranks of the courtiers watching us.

I scowled in the direction of the scoffers, half-ready to demand Phorenice punish them for that open disrespect, and that was when I spotted the oddity. I’d expected to see her kneeling right there beside me, bowing to the Mountain as every rightful ruler of Atlantis had done for a thousand years. Instead she was standing perfectly straight, not a crease in her silk robes, never having bent her head at all. She was staring at me, that half-smirk on her face I couldn’t quite parse, like I’d just done something ridiculous.

Whatever she was thinking, she didn’t air it in front of the crowd. “Come,” she said, and turned to the great stone doorway, calling out the secret daily passphrase that swung the hundred-ton granite portal open on hidden, greased hinges. She swept past into the cool, dim interior, her fan-girl gliding demurely at her heels, and a cold heaviness settled in my chest as I stepped after them. The doors thudded shut sullenly behind us, sealing the three of us inside the maze of solid stone.

We didn’t go far at first. Phorenice stopped in the Hall of Waiting, the same hall I remembered so clearly from twenty years prior, when the old king had bade me farewell before I sailed off to take up my viceroyalty in distant Yucatan. Red walls plastered with carvings of long-dead kings lined the space, and the central jet of burning earth-breath blazed on its bronze stand, casting amber light over everything. The air here was nothing like the crisp, clean air I remembered, though: it was thick with cloying, sweet perfumes, heavy enough to make my head swim.

“Deucalion, my old minister,” Phorenice said, “I do not think you meant any insult. But two decades of that colonial life have turned you dreadfully simple. That obeisance you just made to the Mountain? No one has done that since I took the throne.”

“Your Majesty,” I said, “I was raised from boyhood in the Priests’ Clan, taught to revere the Gods. I’ve been chased through volcanic passes by searing fire flows and choking toxic fumes three separate times, in the old days patrolling my Yucatan territory. That prayer isn’t empty ritual to me – it comes straight from the gratitude of a man who knows how lucky he is just to be breathing.”

“Atlantis changed while you were gone,” she said. “When we give thanks now, we do not waste them on those old, forgotten Gods.”

The impiety of it hit me like a blow to the gut. If that was the new order, I wanted no part of it. I’d sworn to serve my rightful monarch faithfully, but I would not bow to sacrilege, would not treat a swineherd’s daughter who’d clawed her way to the throne as a living goddess worthy of prayers. I chose to call that bluff right then.

“Phorenice,” I said, “I’ve been a priest my whole life. Until I see proof that the old ways are false, I will hold to that oath. If that faith costs me, I will pay the price.”

She smiled slow, showing no anger. “You are a stubborn, strong man, Deucalion.”

I bowed.

“I’ve known plenty of stubborn men,” she said, shaking out her full mass of ruddy hair so the earth-breath light glowed over the curves of her face. “They all converted, in the end. I never even had to burn the hard ones. They threw themselves at my feet, begging to worship me. But it seems my charms fall short today.”

“You are Empress,” I said, stolid. “I am your servant. Give me leave tomorrow, and I will march out and crush the rebel rabble camped outside your walls, prove my worth to you that way.”

“They say you are one of the best fighters in the kingdom,” she said. “I hold a little skill with a sword myself, I know a good warrior when I see one. Tomorrow we will ride out and taste battle side by side. But today, the welcome feast I planned for you will be ready soon. There are private chambers set aside for your use, stocked with everything you could need. Ylga, my fan-girl here, will show you the way.”

We waited until Phorenice vanished down a shadowed passage at the far end of the hall, then Ylga picked up an oil lamp and led me off through the twisting, narrow stone corridors. The passages doubled back on themselves over and over, built so deliberately convoluted a stranger could get lost for days in the maze, no intruder ever reaching the royal quarters unannounced. Old tales said the pyramid’s builder had killed every last mason who’d laid its inner stones, to make sure no one but the royal line ever learned the layout.

Ylga moved like she’d walked those halls a thousand times, no hesitation. She turned sharp corners, pushed a hidden indent in a blank wall to swing a secret stone door open, even stamped her foot on a loose flagstone that reared up to reveal a steep, narrow hidden stair that led down, then up again through another inclined tunnel. Finally she stopped before a heavy granite slab, pushed it open, and gestured me inside a broad, richly furnished chamber.

“All the chests along the walls hold fine new robes,” she said, “jewels and gold trinkets in the bronze coffer there. Phorenice sent them as her first gift, she says, and there will be far more if you play along. You can drop that old colonial simplicity now, dress in the finery the rest of the court wears.”

“Watch your tongue, girl,” I snapped. “I do not need advice on how to dress.”

“If you think that rude, you can tell the Empress, and she’ll have me whipped,” she shot back, her dark eyes glowering up at me from under her thick brows. “You could even ask to see it done yourself. Phorenice would grant you almost anything right now.”

“You are closer to that whipping than you think,” I said.

“I have a name,” she retorted. “Ylga. You’d have heard it on the mammoth ride here if you hadn’t been so wrapped up in staring at the Sacred Mountain.”

I stared at her, curious. “You’ve never met me before. Why speak to me like that? There’s some reason for it.”

She slid the heavy stone door shut behind us, then grabbed my wrist, pulling me away from the airshaft and into the far, shadowed corner of the room, where no hidden listener could hear. “I am Zaemon’s daughter,” she said, quiet. “The Zaemon you called your oldest friend, back before you sailed to Yucatan.”

“Did he send you with a message?” I whispered, shocked.

“How could he? He lives up on the Sacred Mountain, in the priests’ compound. I haven’t seen him in two years, not since Phorenice dragged me down to the court to be her personal attendant. But the second I saw you step off that red pavilion at the harbour, I felt sorry for you, Deucalion. I knew exactly what she’s been plotting these last two months.”

“I cannot hear you speak ill of the Empress,” I said, automatically.

“Even when she is planning to destroy you?” she snapped, stamping her sandal on the stone floor. “You are either very blind or very brave. I won’t interfere more right now, but I’ll be watching. If you ever need a friend, I will help you. I am second highest ranking lady in Atlantis now, do not forget that. Women hold power here, under Phorenice. My friendship is a thing men have begged for, for years.”

“Thank you,” I said, and she left, after showing me how to call the chamber slaves. I stood there for a full minute, still reeling, realizing I’d just promised my gratitude to the daughter of the man who’d taught me my first priestly rites decades before.

The slaves came running when I called, immediately trying to smother me in thick perfumes, lace my hair with gold dust, dress me in layers of frilly silk finery I had no use for. Irritated by their fussy impertinence, I set them to beating each other with their own palms, promising if they didn’t do it hard enough I’d send them to the brander to mark their skins with permanent stripes. Even lowly servants can get under your skin, when they’re fawning over you like you’re some pretty toy.

But nothing I saw that day shocked me half as much as how Phorenice summoned the feast. I sat waiting in my chamber, when a low, rumbling hum started, growing so slowly I barely noticed it at first, until it swelled into a clangorous, ear-splitting din that made the whole massive pyramid shake like a child’s wooden toy, groaning and trembling under my feet.

It was the sacred Earth-Roar, the ritual the Priests’ Clan only performed once a year on the peak of the Sacred Mountain, to remind all the people that fire and tremor had birthed them, and fire and tremor would swallow them in the end. And Phorenice was prostituting that most holy of signs, turning it into nothing more than a dinner bell to call her guests to gluttony and debauchery.

How had she gotten control of that secret power? Had some disloyal priest on the Mountain betrayed the most sacred mysteries of our order to her? Or had her sharp, ungodly mind cracked the secrets of the earth’s fire all on her own? If she’d worked it out herself, I could still serve her faithfully, as my Empress. But if a priest had betrayed the order? That was a crime we hounded to the end of the earth, the traitor and anyone he’d told.

My head spinning, I followed the slaves through the maze of passages to the great banqueting hall. The sight was dazzling: the vast carved chamber was packed with courtiers draped in every possible shade of silk and gem, all painted and perfumed to within an inch of their lives. No one spared a thought for the sacred roar that had just summoned them, no one cared that rebels besieged the city walls outside, no one mourned the starving poor dying in the streets, no one thought of the old Gods on the Mountain. They only cared about the next sip of wine, the next bite of spiced meat.

The burning earth-breath jets lit the hall as bright as midday, and trumpets blared the second I stepped through the doors. No one roared out a hearty welcome, though. They all lisped my name, bowing mincingly to the floor so their silks swished and their jewels jangled, no heart to it at all. When Phorenice stepped out onto the dais even their cheers were fake and soft, hearty loud welcome was considered barbaric, by this new shallow culture. Two smirking chamberlains with gilded wands led me to the dais, to seat me right beside her on her own private divan, eat from her own plate.

“Honour could not run higher than this,” I said, as I knelt to sit.

“Deucalion could get far more than that, if he asked nicely,” she said, that sidelong smirk back on her face. “All the men in the known world have dreamed of favours I could give you, favours no one else will ever get. But you have to ask for them warmly.”

“First favour,” I said, “give me leave to ride out and wipe that rebel rabble from the walls tomorrow.”

“Pah,” she said, frowning. “Are you only interested in grim, blood-drenched fun? They must have been terribly rustic in that Yucatan colony of yours. For now, eat. Tell me if you like the feast.”

The dishes were piled high, over-spiced, over-sweet, wines so thick they clung to the glass. I had no appetite for any of it, but the rest of the court revelled in every bite, treating each sip and morsel like a gift from the gods, caressing their palates like it was sacred. Phorenice smiled, calling favoured courtiers up to her to give them a scrap of meat from her own plate, which the lucky recipients ate with over-the-top dramatic gestures, and two of them even tucked the bits of food into their clothing to keep as relics. I kept catching glances at Ylga, standing behind Phorenice’s divan, her face sharp and honest, no simpering fake grace like the rest of the court. She was a thousand times easier to look at than even Phorenice, for all the Empress’s legendary beauty.

Halfway through the feast, a foppish outlying governor who’d fled his rebellious province for the capital stumbled up to the dais, drunk, bowing low. “Greatest of all Empresses, fairest of Goddesses, queen of my adoring heart!” he cried, and before anyone could stop him he grabbed Phorenice’s full cup, drained every last drop of wine, and yelled “May all your troubles pass as easily, and leave as sweet a taste!”

Phorenice looked over at me, amused. “You don’t approve of this new custom?”

“It was once the highest mark of respect to pour a libation of wine at a ruler’s feet,” I said blunt. “Drinking it yourself is just greedy self-indulgence, you can do that anywhere.”

“You still cling to those old, austere rules,” she said. “Our new code says enjoy every pleasure first, and don’t let anything get in the way of that.”

The feast dragged on, the courtiers growing more and more ridiculous, the guards lining the walls standing as still and stern as the stone statues carved into the outer walls. Then, all of a sudden, a shout went up at the far doorway. Two guards crossed their spears to block a ragged intruder, but he shoved right past them, unharmed, and walked straight up the centre of the banqueting hall between the rows of feasters. The crowd jeered at him, then went quiet, the women shuddering like a cold winter wind had blown through the room. He stopped right in front of Phorenice’s divan, stared her dead in the eye, and did not bow at all.

He was a frail old wanderer, wild white hair tumbling over his shoulders, ragged white beard caked in volcanic dust, mud clotted on his bare feet. A single rough cloth was wrapped around his waist and shoulders, and in his hand he carried a priest’s sun-wand, the symbol of the Sun glowing bright red at its tip. I recognized him instantly as one of the wandering ascetics, priests who’d renounced the comforts of the Mountain to live out in the dangerous volcanic badlands, facing fire flows and venomous lizards and toxic fumes every day, gaining mastery over earth mysteries no normal priest could ever touch.

The whole hall went dead silent. Phorenice spoke first, her voice sharp and clear. “Those two guards at the door failed me. Remove them.”

The next rank of guards drove their spears straight through the two men’s chests, so fast one didn’t even make a sound as he fell dead to the floor.

The old priest’s voice was thin, high, and cracked, but every word carried to the far corners of the hall. “You have added another sin to your tally, Phorenice. Not half your army could have stopped me entering if I’d wanted to come. I bring you your final warning. The Gods favoured you, gave you enough power and cunning to climb above every other mortal, take the throne of Atlantis for your own. We on the Sacred Mountain even let you have that last ambition, thought you might rule well. But you have failed.

“Undo all the evil you have done. Give bread to the starving people of the city, even if you take it out of the gluttons sitting in this very hall. Restore Atlantis to the honourable, orderly state the old kings built it to be. If you do not? You will be removed. We will not let this country sink back into barbarism. Give me your answer, true.”

“Speak him fair! For your own sake, speak him fair!” Ylga hissed in a desperate, urgent whisper from behind us. Phorenice did not turn to her. She leaned forward on the cushions of the divan, her brows knitted in cold, furious anger.

“Do you dare to threaten me, old man?” she snarled. “Knowing exactly who I am?”

This was how I was welcomed back to Atlantis’ capital, and my first look at the new order Phorenice had carved out for herself. I make no excuses for my deliberate stillness in those first hours. All the old, simple lines of duty had frayed to nothing. Once, to serve the king was to serve the ruling Priest Clan that had anointed him, whose very head he was. But Phorenice had clawed her way to power alone, answering to no one but herself. Zaemon, the priests’ mouthpiece, had defied her openly. What was a plain, honest soldier supposed to do, pick a side in that mess on the spot?

Cold logic settled the question for me. Governments exist for the good of all the realm, don’t they? I’d find out which faction actually promised Atlantis a future, and throw my full weight behind it. That line of thought led me straight to the other half of the puzzle: the rebels I’d heard mutterings about, the group strong enough to have the whole capital under siege. Before I pledged loyalty to anyone, I owed it to the country to weigh their claims too. So that very same night, hours after I’d set foot on Atlantean soil again, I slipped away from the royal pyramid to see the state of things with my own eyes.

No one inside that gilded tomb dared stop me. The banquet had shattered into chaos the second Tarca collapsed on the stone floor, his leprosy scars bulging and glistening for every feaster to see. Even the greediest glutton there lost his appetite fast. Phorenice glowered at the crowd in silent fury for a long minute, said not a word, then turned her gaze on me, her hard face softening just a little.
“You may be honest, Deucalion,” she said at last, with a sharp, amused smile, “but you are the coldest man I’ve ever met. I wonder when you’ll thaw? Soon, I think. For now, farewell. At dawn we’ll take this whole country by the shoulders and wrench it into something new.”
She swept out with Ylga at her heels, and I went next, stepping ahead of my rank, while every lord in the hall stood and bowed. I stopped first at Tarca’s side, laid my hand on his diseased flesh. “You’re an unlucky man,” I told him, “but I know a brave soldier when I see one. If there’s any cure for this plague to be found, I’ll pull every string I have to get it for you.”
He mumbled his thanks through his wrecked, rotting mouth, and the nobles around him shuddered in fake disgust. I rounded on them, my face dark. “Your charity runs as thin as your courage. You all put on a grand show of scoffing at Zaemon and his priests, but the second you have to stand up to anything ugly, you hide like sniveling children. I’d rather be Tarca, all unclean, than any one of you right now.”
I waited cold as ice until they’d all given me the proper salute due my rank, then turned and walked out of the banqueting hall without a second glance. I headed straight for the pyramid’s grand main gate, called for the guard officer, and demanded to leave. The man was obsequious, but he hesitated.
“My lord’s attendants haven’t arrived yet.”
“I have none.”
“Does my lord know what the streets are like after dark?”
“I knew them well twenty years ago. I’ll find my way.”
“The city is under siege, my lord,” he pressed on. “The people are starving. They prowl in packs once the sun goes down, and I don’t doubt you could win any fight, no matter the odds, but—”
“Correct. I’ve no desire to brawl in the gutters tonight. Lend me enough men to make sure no one tries. You’ll know how many we need. I’m used to city streets that don’t try to eat you.”
He assigned twenty broad, armed guards to my escort, and with them flanking me two deep, I marched out of the thick, cloying perfume of the pyramid into the cool, silver glow of Atlantean moonlight. My plan was to circuit the city walls and get a clear sense of the besiegers’ strength.

The Gods, though, had a far rougher lesson waiting for me first. The city I walked that night was nothing like the capital I’d remembered: the one built slow, over centuries, balanced with comfortable homes for the many and grand monuments for the few. The grandeur was still there, bigger than ever. Entire working-class wards had been bulldozed flat to make space for new palaces and new pyramids for the empress’s inner circle. I couldn’t help but admire the skill and raw ambition that built those soaring, moon-glowing structures. Gazing up at them, I almost understood why those barbarian Europeans worship giant buildings as gods, convinced no ordinary human could raise something so majestic.
For all their beauty, though, the cost of those monuments blared at you from every corner. For every grand new palace rising where a hundred small homes had stood, a hundred families had been thrown out into the streets with nowhere to go. Now every alley niche slept three or four homeless wretches, every shadow hid a pack of feral, hungry men ready to rob or kill anyone who wandered past without enough guards. I’ve never been soft on the common folk: a man who chooses to starve instead of work deserves what he gets, and the state is better off without him. But what I saw that night was blind, pointless cruelty. Starved corpses, their bones poking through paper-thin skin, sprawled in the gutters. It was plain as day: outside the tiny circle of the empress’s favorites, every last soul in the capital was being ground under a boot.
Even that, though, I couldn’t complain too hard about. By old Atlantean law, every person, high or low, was the property of the king, to be put to whatever use he saw fit. Phorenice sat on the throne now, after all. I tried to push the anger down, not let treason creep into my thoughts, sighed, and kept walking, staring up at the purple night sky and the stars, hoping the High Gods would send me some guidance for what to do next.
It wasn’t long before the winding streets brought us to the great outer walls. We marched beside them, answering the sentries’ drum calls as we passed, until we reached the main western gate, where the garrison captain kept post.

Something was very wrong there. Laughter boomed up into the quiet night, mixed with the snarl and roar of some enormous beast, and the sharp, high shriek of a man in agony. The old builders had designed the city’s main gates to be as unbreakable as the walls themselves: four sets of stone valves, each two men tall, one man thick, set in the doubled thickness of the wall around an open, circular stone circus. Hidden gears linked the valves so one could never open until the other was fully shut. Without siege engines, you’d have better luck trying to push over the royal pyramid with your bare hands than forcing those gates.
My escort blew the horn hung on the wall for summoning, and a warder peered out through an arrow slit, going through the long, tedious old forms of the gate check. Half the time we couldn’t hear each other over the racket coming from the circus inside. At last he’d finished all the formalities, shot back the massive stone bolts, and cracked open one heavy valve to let me and my men crowd into the antechamber between the two gate sets. He pulled the door shut behind us, bowed low to the ground at my feet.
“I’ve no coin for you,” I said bluntly. “Get on with it. Open the other outer gate.”
“With all respect, my lord, it would be far wiser if I announced you first. There’s a baiting going on in the circus, and the tigers there are untamed beasts, no respecters of rank.”
“The what?”
“Tigers, for lack of a better name. They’re tearing through prisoners the Empress (blessed be her name) sent us to guard the gate. But if you’d like, the ward rooms lead to old archer galleries that overlook the whole circus. You can watch the sport from there, perfectly safe.”
I’ve never gotten any thrill out of mindless killing, but I was curious what Phorenice had dreamed up for her new gate defenses. So I followed him through the lodge to the galleries, built by the old architects as a second line of defense: if invaders ever forced their way into the circus, the archers could pick them off from the slits without ever being struck back, turning the whole gate complex into a perfect death trap.
The moon lit the circus bright, and the air drifting in reeked thick of blood. There was no “sport” to what was happening – just cold, cruel slaughter. I’m no prude: give a condemned man a sharp blade, toss him in a pit with a beast of equal strength, let him fight to his last breath, and I’ll cheer every good strike. A war prisoner earns his death, and he’d far rather go out fighting hot blood than under a headsman’s axe. But this baiting was no fair fight.
The “tigers” weren’t ordinary big cats. They were cave tigers: the biggest, most terrifying land predators alive, so huge and strong they’ll drag down a full mammoth alone if they catch it away from its herd. I’ve hunted beasts my whole life, and I’ll not be ashamed to say I always stalked cave tigers with every trick and bit of caution I could muster. To trap one alive, chain it, and drag it all the way to the city? That was a feat I’d never even dared imagine. Phorenice was more of a genius than I’d given her credit for.
Their purpose was obvious. Loose in the circus, no living soul could cross through the gates. Each wore a thick bronze collar attached to a massive iron chain that ran back to a windlass hidden in the galleries. On normal days, the tigers would be hauled tight to either end of the circus, leaving a clear safe path between the gates. But right now, the chains were slack, and the two striped brutes prowled the paving, links clinking behind them. Eight dead men already lay sprawled on the stone, and the tigers nuzzled them now and then, swatting their bodies with heavy paws, but made no move to eat. They knew the game. They had more victims coming.
Sure enough, the outer gate valve cracked open a crack, and a fighting prisoner was shoved out into the circus. He fell flat on his face, then lay perfectly still, eyes on the ground, waiting for death. The stone gate clanged shut. The onlookers behind the arrow slits chattered and yelled, betting on how long he’d last.
The windlass clanked, the chains were pulled in twenty links. One tiger crouched, lashed its tail, and launched itself in a terrifying spring. The chain went taut, singing with strain, and the beast roared so loud the walls shook. It missed the prone man by a hand’s breadth. The watchers shrieked with delight. The second tiger sprung, missed by even less, and the crowd howled louder. The prisoner never moved an inch.
The tigers soon grew bored of that. They lay down, purred, stretched, and slowly crept their chains out, reaching for the man with their huge forepaws. The male couldn’t quite reach. The female’s claw tip just barely grazed his bare side, raking bright red scratches across his skin. Still he didn’t stir. Then the male tiger executed a brutal trick: he turned his back, backed against the chain, and kicked out with his hind paw as hard as he could.
I heard the crunch of the prisoner’s ribs. His body went flying like a tossed fruit. The tigress knew the move. She caught him mid-fall, tossed him 10 feet in the air, and leaped up after him, catching him on her paws. The crowd screamed their approval, but my gorge rose. Toying with a man’s dead body like he was a cheap doll was an insult to every human’s pride. I held my peace, though. I was there to learn the shape of Atlantis’s new order, not throw a scene.
The tigers got bored of the bodies, started prowling again, waiting for more fun. The windlass clanked once more, the beasts were hauled clear of the outer gate, and another prisoner was shoved out. My stomach turned. It was a woman.
I’ve seen my share of death in battle, I’ve fought women who picked up blades and fought as hard as any man, earned their fatal blows fair. But this was wanton, cruel, unnecessary. I had to clamp down hard on the urge to leap down into the circus and end the game myself. I was supposed to be thinking of the good of the whole country, not one single life. But my heart was hammering. This woman was no sullen, resigned victim. She was going to fight.
The guards had stripped her naked, to make sure she had no hidden weapons. She planted her back to the stone gate she’d been pushed out of, eyes glowing bright, facing the tigers. She snatched up a sharp bone from the litter of bodies on the paving, broke its thick end against the wall, then bit the tip to a razor point with her teeth. The tigers paced closer, noiseless, playing their usual torment game, and the woman didn’t wait for them to strike. She lunged first, driving that sharpened bone straight for the tigress’s eye. The crowd roared. She didn’t blind it, but she cut a deep, stinging gash across its eyelid, and the huge beast bellowed in surprise and pain, scampering back to get a running start to charge her.
She didn’t wait for the charge. She ran full tilt across the moonlit circus straight for the wall where I stood, threw every muscle in her body into a leap I’d have thought impossible, flying so high her feet dangled a man and a half above the paving. Her fingers curled tight around the sill of my arrow slit. She would have slipped right back down if I hadn’t reached out, grabbed her wrist, and hauled her halfway through the slit.
I expected her to beg for mercy. Instead, she panted, savage and wild, tightened her grip on that sharp bone, and lunged it straight for my throat, using the hand she had wrapped around my wrist to pull me closer. I barely dodged the first blow. I grappled her as gently as I could, the thick stone wall between us, pried the weapon out of her hand, and held her still until she stopped fighting.
“That was an ungrateful strike,” I said. “Without my hand on you, you’d be down there getting torn apart by the tigers right now.”
“I have to kill someone,” she panted, “before they kill me.”
“Then you’ll have plenty of chances another day. You’re safe now.”
“You’re lying. You’ll throw me to the beasts the second I let go. I know your kind. You won’t give up your cruel sport.”
“Then I’ll prove it to you.” I called for the warder. “Wind those tigers’ chains in short, then go down into the circus and help this lady down to the ground gently.”
The order was carried out. I climbed down to the circus too, and joined the woman standing there in the moonlight. But the men behind the arrow slits who’d been watching the sport were furious. One of the outer gate valves was thrown open hard, and a red-faced armed captain stormed out, sword drawn.
“By all the Gods!” he shouted. “Who dares get between me and my pastime?”

I’d not had a full stretch of unbroken rest in three days, so I’ll own I slept heavy through most of that long daylight stretch, Nais huddled quiet at the temple entrance keeping watch. Night fell thick, and we stayed holed up in the abandoned, dust-choked shrine. Our plan was simple: hunker down until the whole rebel camp slipped into deep sleep, then slip for the shore where my waiting boat lay, no soul the wiser.

The night turned cold and damp, a fine drizzle seeping through every crack in the rough-hewn temple walls. Peering out the narrow stone slits, we spotted the camp’s dozens of well-tended fires glowing orange, ragged folk wrapped in tattered animal skins huddled shoulder to shoulder for warmth, steam curling thick off their rain-soaked clothes. Wet, miserable people are notoriously short-tempered, sharp-eyed, and quick to snap at the smallest irritation. Nais frowned as she watched the drizzle lash down on the huddled crowds.
“This was meant to be the perfect night,” she muttered. “I could have slipped you back to the city before anyone even realized we were gone. But in this chill, no one sleeps deep. We’ll have to wait a few more hours till the cold drugs them into real unconsciousness.”

So we sat side by side on the cold stone pavement, a floor no worshipper’s knee had touched in centuries, barely speaking a single word out loud. Good companionship never needed constant chatter anyway.
As the small hours dragged on, the night’s quiet shattered. From the distance, someone blew on a giant conch shell, belting out a harsh, raucous non-stop note that drew nearer by the second. The camp’s night owls started yelling insults at the noisemaker for disturbing their rest. Soon the horn fell silent, and we stood to peek out the slits: half the camp had roused from their uneasy rest, clustering in a tight throng around a man who stood preaching from the stone pedestal of a long-broken war engine.
We didn’t care what he was yelling about, at first. Nais whispered that if every last awake campfolk trundled off to listen to this stranger, we could slip out the temple’s far side and head for the sea completely unspotted.

But we hadn’t counted on the camp’s nosiest busybodies. A dozen pairs of feet splashed through the mud right up to the temple door, yelling loud for Nais to come join the crowd. She was famed for her sharp, persuasive tongue, they said, they needed her to stand at their side and counter the stranger’s divisive nonsense before he split their whole rebel band apart.
I thought we’d be forced to brawl our way out then and there, but Nais squeezed my wrist tight, whispering fast: “Come with me. Stay well back from the firelight. Don’t say a word.”
“How do I explain what I’m doing hiding out here?” I hissed.
“No one’s going to ask for an explanation,” she said, a bitter little laugh tugging at her mouth. “They’ll just assume you’re my lover. That’s the fashion out here, no one bats an eye. Though why the gods couldn’t make you grow one of those silly curled beards all these men wear, I’ll never know—you’d have been halfway to the sea safe two hours ago.”
“A smooth chin suits me better,” I said.
“It suits me too,” she murmured so quiet I almost missed it, as she heaved the heavy stone door ajar. “Your chin.”
The ragged crowd outside barely glanced at me, tossed off a few crude jests I ached to run through with my sword, then trundled off ahead, glancing back only to make sure we followed. The whole camp was on the move now, dark silhouettes against the drizzling sky, rain spattering hissing on the fire pits. There wasn’t a single sleeper left, everyone was heading to hear this mountain preacher, and if we’d tried to duck off course, we’d have been spotted instantly.

We pushed through the throng until we were close enough to make out the shouting. “Who are your chiefs?” the preacher boomed at the end of a fiery harangue, and the whole crowd roared back in a deafening wave: “We have no chiefs! We’re done with all rulers! We’re all equal! Kill us with your magic if you want, but you won’t rule us. Not the old priests, not Phorenice, not anyone!”
The press of bodies shoved us right up near the preacher’s platform. He was ancient, wild white hair tumbling down his shoulders, a long unkempt white beard, nothing but a scrap of rag around his hips, his skin glistening wet in the firelight. In his hand, he held the Sun Symbol—our sacred priestly wand, the tip burning with unquenchable holy fire, glowing bright like a fallen star. No mortal could forge that. The crowd knew instantly he was one of the old priest clan, the line that had ruled Atlantis since time out of mind. “You can kill us with your magic,” they screamed, “we don’t care. We’re done with rulers, we’ve suffered too much.”

Then my blood ran cold. That old man was Zaemon. Nais’s father. The very man I’d sat next to at Phorenice’s banquet the day before. He could denounce me as Deucalion right here, right now, and every single rebel in this crowd that had already burned an entire navy trying to kill me would tear me apart. Nais twined her fingers in mine, a quiet goodbye squeeze, and I squeezed back. I was sorry I wouldn’t get to see more of her, but a grim old fighter like me couldn’t help already mentally counting how many of these fools I’d cut down before they pulled me down— that final glorious last battle I’d daydreamed of for years.

But Zaemon didn’t see me yet, he was too wrapped up in his sermon. He told them the old, sacred story: how the priest clan had ruled justly for millennia, fended off invaders, held back hordes of wild beasts, warned of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, built Atlantis into the greatest, most prosperous land on earth. Until the people got soft, he said, forgot the gods, and the gods sent Phorenice as a scourge to punish them. But the old order would be restored, he promised, if they submitted, humbled themselves before the gods.
A hot young rebel jumped up onto the platform next to him, yelling that they’d never take a ruler back, even if he used magic to kill him on the spot. Zaemon just gave him a sharp, stern look, and the kid went completely mute, face white with terror, stumbling back down into the crowd. The mob roared for a public miracle, furious. Zaemon roared back that he would never cheapen the holy mysteries to put on a mummer’s show for an ungrateful crowd: the choice was theirs, humble themselves and earn the gods’ pardon, or Phorenice would grind them all to dust before the gods struck her down.

The crowd surged restive around us, weapons raised. A grizzled rebel right next to me spun around, squinting hard at my bare, uncurled chin. “Hey, friend, no beard, no scar? What’s your story skulking here in the dark?”
I joked back that with all their loud talk of freedom, he ought to let me wear my chin any way I liked. He didn’t buy it, drew breath to yell an alarm. So I grabbed him quick, twisted his neck with that old fighter’s quiet, practiced trick, heard the faint crack, and let him slump against the packed bodies of the crowd so he looked like he’d just fainted from the press. No one noticed. I didn’t want my last fight starting here, no room to swing a sword, no space to carve out a battle ring.

Zaemon led the whole throng in the ancient sacred ritual then, the old responses rolling out through the rain, their voices booming in wild unison:
“WHO STILLED THE BURNING OF THE MOUNTAINS, AND MADE COOL PLACES ON THE EARTH FOR US TO LIVE!--PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS.
“WHO GAVE US MASTERY OVER THE LESSER BEASTS AND SKILL OF TEN TIMES TO PREVAIL!--PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS.”
It was eerie, these wild, irreligious rebels chanting prayers they’d abandoned for years, but I knew their enthusiasm would burn out fast. An hour from now they’d be cold and stubborn all over again.

Zaemon stepped down off the war engine pedestal, the glowing Sun Symbol held straight out before him. The crowd crushed back automatically, clearing a wide empty path for him, no one daring to block his way. And he was walking straight for me. My gut tightened. So this was how it went. He’d seen me at Phorenice’s feast, he was going to denounce me as her right-hand man, smite me with the Symbol—no glorious last battle, no pile of dead around me, no sword drawn. But I was a priest too. If the council on the Sacred Mountain had ordered my death, I’d take it, no fight. I just couldn’t help a little twinge of regret that I wouldn’t get to draw my sword one last time.

I stood straight, gave him the traditional sacred salutation between ranking priests. He returned it perfectly. The crowd fell back further, the Symbol’s unearthly holy light driving them away, leaving just the two of us in a big empty ring. Even Nais couldn’t get close, the mystery power of the Symbol pushing her back. The crowd roared instantly: “A SPY! ZAEMON GREETS HIM AS A PRIEST!”
Zaemon turned to them, nodded grim. “Aye. This is the Lord Deucalion.”
The roar that went up was deafening. They’d spent a whole navy trying to kill me a few days back, now they were frothing to tear me limb from limb. But the Symbol’s blazing light held them back, like they were staring at a river of molten lava. Zaemon spoke to me in the old sacred tongue no commoner understood. “Brother Deucalion—who do you serve? Deucalion, or Atlantis?”
I told him he already knew my whole record, every report the council had of all my years governing Yucatan, every good thing I’d done for the land. “We know your past is spotless,” he said. “But you’ve never faced a temptation like Phorenice. All other attempts to bring her down have failed. You’re our last hope, the strongest, most skilled man we have. You’re our champion. If you fail, we’ll have to open the Ark of the Mysteries, and unleash powers no mortal dares even name.”
I laughed bitter. “Wouldn’t you save a lot of trouble just throwing me to this mob right now?”
He scowled, told me to stop shirking my duty. I reminded him of the curse he’d yelled at Phorenice at the banquet, that her royal pyramid would be torn apart stone by stone. He shuddered, said he couldn’t see the full future, but before they unleashed the unnameable powers sealed in the Ark, I had to try. The council had arranged for me to marry Phorenice, he said. As her husband, not an obvious enemy, I could persuade her to see the futility of fighting the gods, talk her into submission before it was too late.

My chest ached. I’d barely thought of women all my busy, duty-bound life, but the last two days I’d fallen for Nais, hard. Marrying Phorenice would mean losing her forever. But I bowed my head, pressed his hand to my forehead, the traditional gesture of a priest accepting a sacred order. “It is an order. I obey.”

We turned to leave, the Symbol blazing a clear path through the howling mob. I didn’t get to say a word to Nais, just caught that one fiery, heartbroken glance from her eyes as we passed. No one dared touch us. When the crowd started to follow us out of curiosity and bloodlust, Zaemon whirled the Symbol behind us, a burst of searing holy light, and the whole crowd dropped to their knees, grovelling in the mud, and didn’t dare move another step.
The rain still fell, glinting off Zaemon’s thin, wet skin, and far off through the dark, the great Sacred Mountain loomed, its ring of eternal fires glowing bright on the crest. I sighed, thinking of the old quiet peaceful days I’d spent in its temple groves, knowing my new, terrible duty lay straight ahead.

The hidden passage had been cunningly sealed from outside, but it was no mere man-made tunnel: it was one of the ancient lava veins where Earth’s fiery core once ran red hot eons prior. The molten rock had long since cooled, but the air still hung thick, warm, and acrid with sulphur, burning the back of my throat the second I stepped inside. I fished a tallow lamp from its carved niche in the stone wall and marched fast, coughing so hard my ribs ached, cursing the fact my entire pack of healing herbs, the only treasure I’d brought back from the Yucatan vice-royalty, had gone to the bottom of the sea during that naval fight against Dason’s raiders. Since I’d landed back in Atlantis, there’d been no spare time to restock simple remedies for a wracking catarrh.

The network of old lava veins branched like a madman’s map. Any soul who stumbled in by accident would have gotten hopelessly lost, or plunged straight down one of the unmarked pits that dropped miles to the still-boiling magma below. But the 20-year-old initiation rite I’d taken when I was named to the Seven Priests had seared the correct path into my memory, no map needed. The floor jutted with jagged cooled rock, the air grew fouler the further I went, and I knew full well the sun would be high overhead long before I crawled out on the other side of the city walls, inside Atlantis’ inner precincts.

The vow of the Seven slowed me to a crawl, though: we were strictly forbidden from stepping out of this secret passage in front of any mortal eye, no matter how urgent the emergency. One exit after another, I found crowds loitering, chattering too loud to slip past unseen. I’d planned to emerge inside the royal pyramid, but the place hummed like a kicked beehive, no chance to sneak out unobserved. The next five outlets were all the same: the whole city was roiling with some wild, unnameable excitement.

At last I found an exit behind the crumbling statue of a minor, all-but-forgotten god, and paused to listen. No sound. The floors, the worshipper’s stone pews, were thick with undisturbed dust—this temple had sat abandoned for months. I shifted the stone covering the exit, stepped out, slid it back into place, then grabbed the frayed broom propped against a pew and swept away every trace of my footprints. But when I got to the main temple door, it was sealed tight. Even as a high priest, I didn’t know the secret mechanism for the massive stone slab that blocked the way. Trapped.

No one but this temple’s own attendant priests could open that door. If every last one of them had run off to gawk at whatever spectacle was whipping the city into a frenzy, I’d be stuck here forever. I followed the dark corridor back to the priests’ quarters built into the temple roof, half expecting every room to be empty. I already knew the old order had been shattered, but I was only just starting to grasp how ruthlessly the old traditions had been torn down to make way for the new.

I rapped the sacred call on the first door I found, and it swung open. The man peering at me through the dimness was Ro, my old classmate from the sacred college. Everyone had called him the dullard back then: he could barely memorize the god rites, knew nothing of statecraft, was useless with a weapon. The only reason he’d climbed to second custodian of this forgotten temple was that every single one of his superiors had abandoned their posts. His beard was badly curled in the latest fashion, his robes unbrushed and ill-fitting, just as slovenly as I remembered.

“Phorenice, that impious tyrant, has declared herself a living goddess,” he mumbled, when I asked what had the whole city so riled up. “She says she’ll light the public sacrifice with her own divine fire, and she’ll do it too. She gets everything she wants. The old cult of the true gods is bleeding dry. No one brings offerings anymore. And yesterday? The high priest here tried to do the weekly sun sacrifice at the great stone circle. One of those giant featherless man-eating birds from the northern mountains swooped down right in the middle of the rite. The priests were unarmed, per the rubric, trusting the Sun God to guard them. The bird tore the sacrifice to pieces, mauled my superior so bad he’s lying half-dead on my bed right now. The crowd screamed that the Sun God was weak, that no old god was left to protect them. Then someone shouted Phorenice was the new goddess, and the whole circle took up the cry. Today she’s leading the sacrifice herself to prove it.”

I tensed. The city gates were under siege by rebel forces, I could hear the faint, constant crash of rams against stone even in this remote temple, and no one was paying it a single mind. The law had dissolved completely, Ro added: people were murdering each other in the streets for scraps of food, no one buried the dead, five months of no offerings had left him surviving on his own hidden jars of corn.

“Open the doors,” I told him. “I have to see this for myself.”

He hesitated, fumbled with the levers, and the massive stone slab rumbled ajar. I stepped out onto the street, and the surging, weapon-carrying crowd swept me along with them, all of us heading straight for the ancient Sun God’s stone circle. The once-clean paved streets were ankle-deep in mud, scattered with the bones of people who’d starved and been left to rot where they fell. Savage chaos lapped at the feet of Atlantis’ grand pyramids and gilded palaces, a sick, jarring contrast.

When we reached the sun circle, I stared up at the 49 massive uncut stones, set in a perfect ring, lintel stones balancing across their tops so heavy no modern Atlantean engineer could have moved them even with a hundred teams of men. The crowd packed around the outer perimeter, no common soul allowed past the line on pain of death, and we waited for hours in the brutal midday sun. Dozens fainted from heat and hunger, several died where they stood, no one paused to help the fallen. The crash of the rebel siege engines boomed louder and louder from the walls, and no one turned their head.

At last the drums blared. The procession rounded the corner, glittering with gold, the lords and new court priests draped in silk while the crowd starved at their feet. Then Phorenice rode into view, perched in the golden howdah I’d once sat in myself, on the back of her giant shaggy mammoth. The sullen, hungry faces of the crowd lit up like they’d all been handed a full meal, and they roared her name unbidden, no guards forcing them to cheer. Even from a distance I could see Ylga, the fan girl, Zaemon’s own daughter, standing stiff and pained in her place beside Phorenice, her face heavy with dread. She knew what sacrilege was about to unfold.

Phorenice stepped down the ivory stair from the mammoth’s back, and strode straight across the sacred sun circle, no old high priest leading her. The men trailing behind her, a mix of her loyal laymen and traitor priests from our own sacred clan, dragged a live cloven-hoofed plains horse to the altar stone, bound it, and laid it on the stacked pyre. They stepped back, leaving Phorenice standing alone before the carved altar, the image of the snake and outstretched hand of the Sun God’s life glowing in the sun on its face.

She called out in the common tongue, so every last person could hear her, raising her arms to the clear, bright blue sky. No one noticed the tiny, whirling ball of mist that appeared out of nowhere, growing darker and denser by the second, until it cast a dark shadow over the whole circle, full of tiny stabs of silent, dancing flame. She cried out to the Sun, claiming she was his daughter, that she was the new goddess of Atlantis, that he send down divine fire to light the sacrifice as proof.

A blinding flash spat down from the cloud. The horse neighed once, convulsed, and fell dead. Fire crackled across the pyre, the smell of burning meat curling through the air. The dark cloud dissolved instantly, the flames burned bright against the clear blue sky, and a thin spiral of blue smoke drifted straight upward.

Phorenice stood before the altar, flushed, exultant, radiantly beautiful. The priests inside the circle, the thousands of people outside, all threw themselves to the ground, foreheads pressed to the stone. “Phorenice! Goddess!” they screamed, the roar echoing off the great standing stones.

I did not kneel. I refused to bow to this apostasy. I stood straight, in the middle of the screaming, prostrate crowd, and waited for whatever fate would come for me next.

A low murmur rippled through the throng packed tight around the stone altar, and swelled fast into a rabid roar. “Kneel,” a man breathed in my ear, his palm digging into my shoulder, “kneel now, or they’ll tear you apart.” Another shrieked over the din: “Bow your head, beardless foreign dog, worship the one true Goddess, or by the old sun gods I’ll claim priesthood of her rites and butcher you on these flagstones!” The shouts climbed higher, thick with fanatic fury, and spears were lifted point-first in my direction.

But then the name “Deucalion” passed from lip to lip, and the frenzy drained out of them like water through cracked clay. They knew the name: the exile who’d left Atlantis twenty years prior to rule the rough colonial holdings of Yucatan, the man who’d ridden only days before at the Empress’s side under the gold-canopied litter on the back of her war mammoth. When they weighed the vengeance I could call down, the roars died limply away, every last zealot dropping his gaze to the dust.

Silence pooled around the circle then. Phorenice turned from the altar where she’d been receiving her people’s worship, and saw me standing tall, the only soul in the throng not prostrate in the dirt. I stepped between the two great weathered megaliths that marked the ritual circle’s edge, crossed the trampled grass to her side of the altar, and did not bow. At the exact formal distance her own edicts had set for her chief minister, I gave the crisp salute she herself had ordained, and hailed her plainly by her imperial title, no line of god-worship on my lips.

“Deucalion, man of ice,” she purred, eyes glinting with that sharp, half-amused edge only she carried.

“I still give my fealty to the old Gods.”

“I did not speak of that,” she said, and flicked me a sidelong, knowing smile.

Ylga, her favourite handmaiden, came hurrying over then, face bleached bone-white, hands shaking so hard her ceremonial fan slipped from her fingers. “If he will not turn yet,” she pleaded, bobbing a deep curtsy, “surely you can pardon him? He is a strong man, slow to renounce the beliefs he has held all his life. You may win him over in time. But Phorenice—he is Deucalion. Slay him here over this one slight, and there is no man left in all the marches of Atlantis worthy of serving you half so well—”

The Empress cut her off, voice cracking like a whip. “You little slut. I kept you near me to tend my wardrobe and wave my fan, not to meddle in matters of state. Get out of this circle, now, or I’ll have you flogged within an inch of your life. And more than that: I’ll do to you what Zaemon did to my former captain Tarca. Shall I point my finger, and smite that pretty skin of yours with a creeping, disfiguring leprosy?”

Ylga bowed her shoulders, cowed, and scurried past the stone markers out of sight. Phorenice turned back to me, the cold venom melting out of her face into something soft, bright, reckless. “My lord,” she said, “I am like a fledgling that hatches and suddenly finds it has full-grown wings. There are a hundred flights I want to take, before I even know what they are.”

“May every one of them bring prosperity to Atlantis,” I said formally.

She laughed, eyes flashing. “Oh, I know what you care for most. We will ride back to the pyramid, and talk this all through at leisure. For now, my lord, give me your arm, and conduct me back to my mount.”

It was clear then I would face no punishment for refusing to worship her, no public execution to cast doubt on her new claim to divinity. Ylga’s ill-timed interference had earned her only a lashing that was yet to come. I did not waste time untangling all the whys of it; I accepted the reprieve, for it pushed forward the secret mission Zaemon and the high priest council had laid on my shoulders.

When she rested her fingers in my palm—warm, delicate, fingers that had still wielded a war axe with deadly skill a few days prior—I led her gravely out of the ritual circle, the acrid stench of the goat sacrifice clinging thick behind us. I had expected the old Sun God to strike her down for her sacrilege right there, but no vengeance fell. I knew He would choose His own time, His own instrument, and my puny mortal arm did not need to be the one to defend His honour.

I led that lovely, dangerous woman to the great red war mammoth, standing placidly at the edge of the crowd, its shaggy hide glinting red in the sun. She climbed the carved steps up to the golden litter lashed to its back, the canopy woven with live royal snakes, and bade me sit beside her on the plush silk cushion. Her fan-girl’s empty spot sat at our backs; there was no one else within earshot to hear what we said as the mammoth rumbled slowly toward the city streets.

She was hungry to hear how I had fared after the rebel attack on the north gate. I told her the full tale, making sure to speak of Nais, the rebel girl I had saved from the circus tigers, as sharp, loyal, and someone who could be won back to the empire’s side with gentle handling. Only the secret rites Zaemon had shared with me in the sacred priest tongue did I hold back—those words are never spoken outside the High Council, gathered before the Ark of Mysteries.

“You speak very warmly of this rebel Nais,” Phorenice observed, tilting her head.

“She is far sharper, far more thoughtful than the common run of foot soldiers,” I said.

She sighed, and it sounded genuine. “An Empress loses all the small joys that even the poorest woman takes for granted.”

“What joys?”

“Honest wooing, from a man who is her equal, not a toady bowing and scraping for favours.”

“If you insist on styling yourself a Goddess to the people,” I said, “that is a hard line to walk.”

“Pah! I have no wish to be a Goddess to you, Deucalion. That trick of calling down fire from the skies? All seven high priests know how to do it. I filched the ritual from your stores to give me more power over the common folk. Cannot you be generous enough to admit a woman can figure out those natural laws just as well as your stuffy old elder priests?”

“Even so, you are still Empress of Atlantis.”

“Then do not treat me like one, not right now. Think of me only as a woman sitting here beside you on this cushion. Drop all the ceremonies, all the statecraft. Do you want to sit around until all your manhood has withered away for the sake of formalities? No one is saying we need to rush. But only a fool waits for the fruit to ripen, then leaves it hanging until it rots.”

I looked at her, at that stunning, famous beauty, and by all the old Gods it left me cold. But I remembered the command laid on me by the council, and forced a soft smile. “I have been fastidious all these years away in Yucatan,” I said, “but I do not regret waiting for this long.”

“Nor I. I have played the innocent maid long enough. I am a woman now, full-blooded, ready for more than empty ceremony.”

I closed my hand gently over hers. “Take me as your husband then. I will be a good, loyal man to you. But I will speak plainly, as one equal to another: I will never be your puppet.”

She flicked me that sidelong smile again, amused. “I have been the one giving orders for so long, the idea of being mastered sometimes sounds like a delight. You will never be a puppet, Deucalion. No one could force you to dance to anyone else’s tune, if you did not want to step.”

“Then we will rule Atlantis together, side by side. All these stupid divides will be swept aside: the rebels will go back to their farms and their hunting grounds, the priest clan will be pacified, and we will build an empire that outshines every other nation on the face of the earth, peaceful and prosperous for all.”

She pulled her hand away from mine, petulant, her pretty face souring. “Ugh. Your conditions, your endless Atlantis talk. That rough colonial bluntness of yours grows tiresome very fast. Am I the one doing all the wooing? Is there not a single spark of passion under all that ice of yours?”

“I have spent twenty years fighting border wars and administering colonies. I had barely any time to speak to women, let alone learn what ‘love’ is supposed to be.”

“We were both fools when you first landed, then,” she said, laughing. “Every bearded lord in Atlantis has sighed and grovelled at my feet. Some of them were fun for an hour, but none of them ever touched me. I never knew what that grand passion felt like, either. But you are going to teach me, Deucalion—if you keep being this stubborn and cold, I will make you.”

“From what I have seen, love is for the poor, the weak, the flighty.”

She flung her head back, red curls flying, voice thick with longing. “I wish I was not Empress. I wish I was some ragged gutter girl, so a strong man could pick me up, throw me over his shoulder, and carry me home for pure, unthinking passion. I would revel in that. I would give everything I have just to feel that, for once.” She snickered. “Though I would make his life a living hell if he did not please me, of course.”

“We cannot change the bones of who we are,” I said.

Her face darkened, sullen. “If I cannot melt that ice of yours, Deucalion, there is going to be so much trouble for this Atlantis you love so much. Our whole household will burn, if I grow to love you, and your heart stays cold and dead.”

I could see right then she would have had me kiss her, hold her, right there on the mammoth’s back, in full view of the crowded streets thronging with gawking citizens. She never cared for privacy. But I could not force that familiarity. The Gods know I was dead serious about my mission, and the whole charade made my skin crawl. I was a priest, first and last, and I had set aside all personal desire to fulfil the council’s orders. I was trained in diplomacy, sharp enough of tongue on all matters of state, but I had never wasted time learning to trifle with women. If I had tried to spout the flowery, fake romantic lines the rest of her court tossed at her, she would have seen through the fraud in a heartbeat. She had lived on that empty flattery for years, and could weigh the worth of a pretty speech down to a single copper coin. Honesty would have ruined everything. So I said nothing, and let her dismiss me as a clumsy colonial oaf.

“I always heard the Yucatan colonists were far more polished than the lot stuck in Egypt,” she jeered. “You do your viceroyalty no credit. Even the exiled Egyptian lords that come through my court know how to speak to a woman.”

“They must have improved drastically in twenty years,” I said. “The last time I was sent to report on Egypt, the native black tribes ruled the whole land, and our people lived there only on their sufferance. Their pyramids were tiny, their cities no more than mud forts.”

“Oh, they are still exiles, but they never forgot their manners. They all raved about my face, loud enough to make the palace walls ring. One of them struck a deal with me: ten pretty, flattering words, and he cut off his own right hand to give me as a gift. It sits dried, on a shelf in my private chambers to this day.”

A streak of good luck saved me from her teasing right then. The rebels besieging the outer walls had stepped up their attacks, and switched from throwing stones to lobbing fire-hardened wood carcasses stuffed with throwing oil. The missiles bounced off stone buildings harmless enough, though a few unlucky passers-by were scorched by the splattering flames. But when those fire bombs hit the rough wooden booths and rush huts in the poor quarters, they exploded into roaring walls of flame that no bucket of water could put out.

Neither Phorenice nor I would have cared for that small siege chaos—until the great red mammoth caught the scent of smoke on the wind. The huge beast lifted its trunk high, swaying its great shaggy head uneasily. It had been trained to ignore shouting, drums, arrows, stones, but the ancient, racial terror of fire ran in its blood. Since the first days of man and mammoth hunting each other in the wild northern steppes, fire had been the great mammoth’s only unbeatable enemy.

The guards at its haulers’ rings wrenched at the heavy iron chains bolted to its spiked neck collar, driving the sharp metal points deep into its flesh. Blood dripped down its red fur, but the beast’s panic overpowered every lesson it had ever learned. It gave a deafening, screaming trumpet, and bolted backward down the street it had come, scattering the spearmen like straw, mowing a swath through the crowd so thick we could hear the screams of the trampled civilians over the din. The golden litter swayed so hard the snake canopy whipped back and forth, and we were nearly flung off like pebbles from a slingshot. I braced my feet against the front rail, wrapped one arm around the central wooden pillar, and pulled Phorenice tight against my chest to cushion her from the jolts.

She settled there, her soft cheek resting just under my chin, the scent of her jasmine hair filling my nose, as the mammoth charged on blindly. The smoke and fire were far behind us now, but the beast was too far gone in panic to care. It left the entire royal procession far in the dust, thundering through the artisan quarters, the merchant districts, down a long open lane that ended at the glint of the city harbour.

I braced for the beast to skid to a halt, then spin around and charge right back into the city. But it never slowed. It trumpeted loud enough to rattle the dock pilings, and ran straight off the shore into the dark water of the harbour. The ancient city sewers emptied their waste right there, and the bottom was thick with soft, sucking black sludge. The mammoth kept running until its feet sank deep into that mire, and then it stopped, bellowing in terror, unable to pull itself free no matter how hard it strained. The whole litter lurched sideways, and we were both flung far out into the cold harbour water.

Phorenice laughed as she surfaced, her soaked red curls sticking to her face, her fine silk gown heavy with water. “You move well for a clumsy colonial, Deucalion. You made a very fine cushion for me back there. You have more gallant in you when the chips are down than you let on. How was that ride? Better than any boring royal procession, I wager.”

“I would rather have walked,” I said, treading water beside her.

She snickered. “You will never make a courtier. You are supposed to say you wished the ride had gone on forever, so you could hold me in your arms longer. Ho! The boat there! Hold your arrows! Deucalion, hail them, tell them not to hurt my mammoth. Let it wear itself out thrashing for a day or so, to teach it a lesson, then we will haul it out with windlasses and break it all over again. I would never feel like Phorenice again if I did not have my great red shaggy mammoth to carry me around.”

A ten-oar galley, belonging to the port captain, was rowing as fast as it could toward us. When I hailed them, the soldiers on the foredeck lowered their bows, and the steersman turned to pick us up. The port captain himself, a simpering, fawning little man who lived only to lick the boots of his superiors, knelt grovelling on the deck the second we were hauled over the side, wailing that he wished he had gone blind rather than see the great Empress put in such peril.

“Peril?” Phorenice said, flicking water off her sleeve. “Nothing mortal will ever kill me. I am only annoyed my favourite gown is ruined, and I lost a few pretty jewels.”

The fool immediately offered to blind himself to spare her future discomfort, and she snapped at him that if he was that eager to lose his eyes, she would have them plucked out right then and there, for daring to suggest he kill her beloved mammoth over a little “frolic.” The port captain grovelled lower, and said he had two prisoners locked in his cabin, caught sneaking into the harbour under cover of night, and he would sacrifice both of them at once to the trapped mammoth, to appease its rage.

“Prisoners?” Phorenice said, tilting her head. The galley had bumped against the royal quay now, and she was stepping onto the stone docks, water still dripping from her hem. “Bring the woman out first. I am in the mood for romance today. She was caught sneaking into the city to find the man she loves, if your story is true.”

The port captain scurried into the tiny cabin, white-faced, and dragged the bound female prisoner out into the sunlight.

It was Nais. The same rebel girl I had saved from the cave tigers in the circus, the one who had sat with me all night in the woods and told me stories of the free rebel lands outside the city walls. Phorenice knew her at once. She did not even bother hiding the recognition, a sharp, mocking smile spreading across her face as she called Nais by name, and taunted her loudly about her rebel friends, and the lover she had snuck into the city to find.

The Sun God of Atlantis hung low, his golden rays gilding the roiling black smoke that belched from the burning volcanic mountain far inland. Phorenice twisted the seawater out of her sodden tunic, her short red hair plastered to her sharp, delighted face, and shivered in the lagoon wind.
“Put your back into those paddles, Deucalion,” she ordered, grinning, “and get us through the water-gate before I catch my death. That fat merchant we stole these clothes from would weep to see his fine silks ruined, and I weep a little too—what woman does not grieve to spoil good finery, when there are no other pretty garments to steal nearby?” She glanced at me sidelong, flicking wet hair out of her eyes. “What do you make of my cleverness, to ride out to that fishing village with no guard but you?”
“The Empress can do no wrong,” I quoted the old royal formula with a dry smile.
“And I proved I can swing a blade, too,” she laughed. “I caught you watching, you know, approving how I carved through those rebel fishermen. You were such a stiff, hard man to thaw, Deucalion, but you’re warming fast the more time you spend near me. If this keeps up, we’ll be a pair of simpering rustic sweethearts yet. I’m glad I thought of the trick of luring us out to fight those smelly coastal oafs.”
So that was her whole plan: the unsupervised litter ride, the surprise scrap, all to show off her fighting skill, and test how I carried myself under pressure too. If we were to rule Atlantis side by side as husband and wife, it was only sensible we each knew the full measure of the other’s strength. As a veteran of a hundred battlefields who lived for the clean, brutal joy of a fair fight, I couldn’t even find it in me to be angry. But unbidden, my mind turned to Nais: given the same chance, Nais would have spared those squalid rebel fishermen out of simple pity, while Phorenice had carved through forty of her own hardiest subjects just to satisfy a playful whim.
I pushed the disloyal comparison away, clinging to the vows I had sworn to the High Council on the Sacred Mountain. As I pulled at the oars, driving our small fishing skiff toward the harbour forts, I prayed to the High Gods to grant me the smooth, honeyed speech Phorenice adored, for the good of all Atlantis. The Gods answered. By the time we neared the reef barrier, Phorenice’s eyes were glowing on me, bright as embers.
That stretch of fishing beach was cut off from the open sea by a jagged wall of submerged rock, a maze of reefs only local fishermen could navigate. I had no knowledge of the hidden passes, no skill to leap overboard and heft the skiff over the shallow stones, and I waited for the omen: would the Gods let this impious, brilliant Empress reign, and let me stand at her side as her consort?
The answer came without ambiguity. An earthquake tremor rippled through the lagoon, and a great curling swell rose under our hull, hurling us forward clean over the entire reef barrier. We never scraped a single stone, not even brushing the weed-slick rock edges. I bowed my head over the oars, rowing hard for the harbour. It was clear the High Gods would not take their vengeance on Phorenice, not yet.
The fort sentries were half-asleep in the growing dusk, their drums thudding through the routine watch, ready to let our anonymous fishing craft drift unchallenged. But two senior officers were taking the evening air on a stone parapet turret, recognized our shouts, and before long torches were lowered to confirm our faces, the massive harbour chain was splashed into the water, and a royal galley pulled alongside to tow us in.
Dry, warm clothes were brought for us to change into in the galley’s cabin, to ward off the fevers that fester in wet fabric after sunset. I knew Phorenice well enough now to see she had no divine delusions when it came to mortal sickness, shivering even as she pulled on the soft fresh linen.
The galley glided across the calm inner harbour to tie up at the golden-runged royal quay. As we waited for our litters, I spotted Phorenice’s captured mammoth half-submerged in the harbour mud, its red bulk glinting in the guard boat’s lantern light, trunk tilted up as it snuffled nervously. I made no move to beg for its safety. That ride on its swaying back had left me humiliated and helpless, a sensation I loathed more than any battle wound. If the beast sank deeper into the mire that night, I would have borne the loss with perfect equanimity.
Phorenice did not waste time asking after Nais, either. When the port captain bowed before us, she demanded immediately to know where her rival was held, and learned the girl was locked in the deepest dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, the guard captain having sworn his life for her safe keeping.
“Let us hope he knows his work,” Phorenice purred. “That pretty Nais knows every secret tunnel in that pyramid better than most of the royal masons. I’d hate to have him sent to the tormentors for sticking her in a cell with a hidden exit. You’d be pleased if she escaped, wouldn’t you, Deucalion?”
“I would,” I said frankly, making no secret of it. “I bear no Nais ill will.”
“But I bear her every ill will,” she snapped viciously. “And I still mean to lock your loyalty to me for good with that wedding gift we spoke of.”
“All for the Empress of Atlantis,” I said, bowing.
“Pah, Deucalion, you are far too stiff,” she laughed, tugging my hand to help her climb into the litter. “You should be flattered I am jealous enough to fight over you. Climb in beside me, keep me warm against the night wind. Guards, move back against the walls! That cheap incense you’re burning chokes me.”
That night the royal banqueting hall held another feast, and I sat at Phorenice’s side on the raised dais beneath the carved symbols of the sacred serpent and the outstretched hand of royal power. This time our coming marriage was not a private rumor, but proclaimed openly to the court: Phorenice herself named me her future husband, and every jewel-draped lord and lady of the realm hailed me as their superior. The courtiers had already scented out my preference for simple, old-fashioned custom. No one drank loud, toasting speeches over my health; instead, every guest stepped forward in turn to spill a goblet of wine at my feet. When I invited any of them to take a bite from my platter, they ate it simply and gratefully, no absurd bowing or fawning gestures.
The flaring torches of burning natural gas showed their dress had grown plainer too, their manner far more sober than the usual decadent court frivolity. Phorenice leaned against me, her red curls brushing my cheek, and teased me in a whisper that I had already stolen all her royal authority before we were even wed.
“Sir,” she mocked, “you will make the realm far too ascetic to be fun. I gave no orders for this. There are no perfumes in the hall tonight, the food is plain enough to make me burn the cook half way through, and look at their clothes! They look like they stepped straight out of my dead father’s boring old reign, before I introduced bright dyes and pretty jewels to the court. Even their beards! Half the men have trimmed off those ridiculous fancy curls they spent three hours twisting into shape yesterday. By my crown, I swear they’d shave their faces smooth to match yours tomorrow if I didn’t put a stop to it.”
“Why stop them, if they prefer it?”
“Because there is only one man in all of Atlantis who gets to be clean-shaven,” she said firmly, tapping my jaw with her finger. “And that man is my husband. Rulers must keep small, exclusive marks of their rank, Deucalion. If we let everyone copy our every hairstyle and robe fold, they will stop admiring us and start resenting our arrogance. But if we reserve a few small, pretty privileges only for ourselves, the people will admire them, and envy gently, and never turn restive.”
“Ruling is far more clever than I ever guessed,” I said.
“And yet everyone tells me you ruled your own northern lands very well,” she laughed, smacking my arm playfully. “You are turning into as fine a courtier as any of them, you know. No fan-girl here to cool my blushing cheeks tonight, either. I need to pick a new one—but it won’t be Ylga. Ylga looks at you far too fondly for my taste. If she’s smart, she’ll go live in her estate on the far side of the city, busy herself with her embroidery and her slaves. I won’t be cruel to her, but no other woman in this kingdom will treat you with undue favour.”
“Then how am I to object,” I teased, “when every man in Atlantis stares at you as if they are hopelessly in love?”
“You cannot ask me to do the impossible,” she said. “I rule their bodies, but not their silly hearts. Though if you like, you can make proclamation that I am yours, body and soul, and any man who dares to look at me too long gets handed over to the tormentors. It would be a very pretty show of jealousy, Deucalion. I hate that I am the only one jealous here.”
“A man who has spent a lifetime far from soft feelings cannot learn all the little tricks of love in a single day,” I told her. “I think I am doing very well, considering.”
“Then you are easily satisfied,” she pouted, but her eyes were soft.
The feast did not drag on late. Phorenice had fought, ridden, and schemed from dawn to dusk, and for all her claims to be a living goddess, her body was still mortal. Dark rings of weariness pooled under her bright eyes, and she leaned back against the cushions, limp and exhausted. When the dancers filed in to perform for us, she tossed them a jewel and waved them away halfway through their routine. The poet who arrived to sing of that day’s battles got no hearing at all.
“Come back tomorrow,” she told him, voice thick with fatigue. “I will pay you well if your poem is short. But if you fill it with useless long-winded verses, I will pay you with a whipping, not gold.”
She rose to her feet, the courtiers bowed their farewells, and I led her down the maze of secret, stone-door lined hallways that led to her private chambers. She leaned heavy on my arm, and when we paused to let a massive sliding stone block grind slowly open, she rested her head on my shoulder. Her warm breath fanned my cheek, her red lips were inches from mine, and she waited, clearly expecting me to kiss her. But the part I had to play weighed heavy on my chest, and I could not bring myself to cross that line. When the stone slid fully open, she drew back with a soft, disappointed sigh, and we walked the rest of the way in silence.
“May the High Gods guard you well,” I said, when we reached the door to her bedchamber.
“I am my own goddess,” she snapped, “except for this one thing. You are the most tardy wooer in all Atlantis, Deucalion. Where do you think you are going?”
“To my own chambers, to sleep.”
“Go then, go!” she huffed. “Nothing you could do would suit your stiff, old-fashioned honour, anyway. You are a terrible wooer. Don’t you know that women value things all the more when they are taken by a little rough force?”
“I admit I know very little of women,” I said.
“You have never spoken a truer word,” she said, laughing bitterly. “And your coldness only makes everyone respect you more, doesn’t it? Go on then, run off. Let the High Gods keep you, if you truly believe they can. Good night.”
I had copied the full map of the royal pyramid’s secret tunnels out of the archives before the feast, and I knew every twist by heart, threading my way through the maze without pause. I was bone-weary, but I could not waste a minute on sleep. Nais was marked for death, I knew—but a plan had come to me that might let me save her life, if I moved fast.
I rounded a dark corner and nearly ran into Ylga, who stepped out of the shadows to block my path, sullen and angry.
“I know I’ve lost my place as your favourite,” she said. “Phorenice never intended to let me near you, not from the start. I was a fool to ever think otherwise. You probably think I’m a silly, meddling old woman.”
“I never forget a kindness,” I said.
“Oh, you will learn all the court tricks soon enough,” she snapped. “Everyone in the city is talking about you marrying her for the throne. I thought you were different. I thought you were the kind of man who didn’t chase pretty red hair and power just because it was handed to you.”
“Ylga,” I said, “do not speak ill of the Empress. But I need your help. Go wait for me in my private chambers. I will come to you shortly.”
Her eyes widened. “At this hour? It is not proper for a maiden to come to a man’s rooms alone.”
“I am Deucalion,” I said. “If you are afraid, do not come.”
She snorted. “Everyone knows you are as cold as a northern mountain. I was a fool to doubt that. Fine. I will come.”
I waited till she was out of sight, then turned toward the hidden tunnel that linked the royal pyramid straight to the Temple of Our Lady the Moon. The passage was known only to the seven highest priest initiates, and almost no one had trod it for generations. I fumbled my way through the dark, recalling every twist and turn from my initiation lessons, till I reached the secret spy hatch that opened onto the main temple. The great hall was empty, dust lay thick on the altars, no worshippers had come to honour the Moon Goddess in years.
I spoke the required initiation prayer, pulled the hidden metal stair from its nook in the wall, and climbed it up to rest against the giant stone face of the Moon Goddess. My heart hammered in my chest. I was a veteran of a thousand battles, but tampering with the ancient sacred drug was a risk no man had dared take in centuries. I reached up, pressed the jewel set in the Goddess’s forehead, then pushed at the corner of her stone eyebrow. With a soft creak, the massive stone eyeball swung inward, revealing the hollow chamber hidden inside her skull.
I had only ever heard of the drugs in lessons, never seen them before. Three small, rough, nut-sized balls of grass-green, beeswax-soft substance lay in the hidden nook. No one had ever told me what they were made of, only that their power could put a human being into a death-like sleep that would last for years, unaging, unhurt, till they woke again. I took one, tucked it in my pouch, left the other two exactly as I found them, and climbed back out, sealing the eyeball hatch shut behind me. I spared one last look at the vast, calm stone statue of the Moon Goddess, glowing in the eternal gas torchlight, then slipped back through the secret tunnel to the pyramid.
When I walked into my private chambers, Ylga was hiding behind the curtain, peering out to make sure I was alone. She stepped out, still sullen, still ready to snap at me for being a fraud who chased Phorenice’s crown.
“Enough of your complaints,” I said, cutting her off. “You are not here to judge me. The choice is yours now. Tomorrow, Nais will either die by the Empress’s order—or I can give her this sacred drug, and she will fall asleep, and wake on a day far happier than this one. All Atlantis will think she is dead. No one will harm her, no one will torment her. And it is you who will help me pull this off.”

In Atlantis, when Phorenice spoke, her will moved faster than a wildfire through dry grass. The empress had long since decided the whole of her realm, every soul, every stone, every coin, existed solely to serve her whims, and no cost, no toil, no number of shattered lives was too great to see those whims fulfilled.

Her edict that Nais be buried alive beneath the stone throne Deucalion was to build went out at dawn, and before the sun cleared the palace pyramids, every mason, stonecutter, labourer, sculptor, engineer and architect in the city had dropped whatever work they held, summoned by heralds blaring the command through every ward. They seized every material they wanted, no payment, no negotiation, and tore down whole streets of buildings to haul the monstrous multi-ton stone blocks they’d selected to the open square before the royal pyramid, even as rebel war rams thudded unceasingly against the outer walls. The city’s craft guilds, boosted by Phorenice’s clever new lifting machines, could not heft the god-sized blocks of the ancient Sun Temple, but they handled weights of twenty men apiece as easily as a child moves a pebble.

The throne rose in the square through the night, lit by guttering torches. Seven tiers of stone formed the base, each knee-high, three steps cut into their front faces, and a hollow cavity carved into the topmost tier to hold Nais’s living body. Suspended high above that cavity, held steady by massive wooden lifting rams, hung the throne’s massive seat block, carved all around with the coiled shape of Atlantis’s royal cobra, its great hooded head looming over where Phorenice would sit. Relays of workers sweated through the dark, whips cracking over their backs, men fainting dead from exhaustion only to be dragged aside so the next labourer could take their place. No delay was tolerated when the empress had given her word. By sunrise, the last scaffolding and stone chippings were cleared away, along with the half-dozen workmen who’d died from falls or overexertion, and the full crowd of thousands had begun to pour into the square to gawk at the spectacle.

It was the cool of the morning when chamberlains summoned Deucalion out of the royal pyramid. Great nobles paraded in with their trumpeters and banner bearers, commoners wore their finest silk and coloured turbans, even starved rag-wrapped paupers had twisted scraps of bright cloth around their heads to mark the supposed “festivity” of the day. The trumpets blared his arrival, the crowd roared a welcome, and he walked stiff-backed to the scarlet silk awning set up for the guests, his face an impassive mask of iron.

Then Phorenice emerged, carried on an open litter of gold and ivory by brutish, tattooed savages imported from northern Europe, her sharp, glowing beauty made all the more striking by their ugly, feral faces. A collective gasp rippled through the crowd, every man there seething with envy that Deucalion, the warrior prince, had won her as his bride that very day. No one, save perhaps the hidden, veiled handmaiden Ylga, had the faintest clue the man beneath the mask was half-dead with grief. Deucalion had drilled his expression to total stillness: no shaking hands, no twitching cheek, no flicker of pain that would tip Phorenice off to their secret. He would carry his beloved Nais down into that dark hollow as calmly as a priest laying a stranger to rest, and she would play her part too, because for both of them, nothing came before the safety of Atlantis.

There was no cheap mockery in the ceremony, none of Phorenice’s usual frivolity. She’d ordered the full, rigid, sombre ritual of old Atlantis, and the crowd fell quiet as she was carried to the awning, Deucalion stepping forward to help her down to the cushions. She twined her fingers with his, the traditional pose for Atlantis’s rulers and their consorts, as a squad of soldiers marched forward to reveal Nais in their centre. A heavy iron collar locked around her neck, two guards holding the chains tight to make sure she could not throw herself on the spears of the escort to escape the slow, lingering death that had been sentenced to her. To Deucalion’s quiet, profound gratitude, she was still dressed in her fine linen robes – condemned prisoners normally had their clothes seized as plunder by their guards, and he knew without a doubt Ylga had paid them off to spare her that last humiliation.

She walked calmly to the edge of the awning’s shade, bowed low before Phorenice without being forced, her face pale but unflinching, no trace of fear or defiance in her steady eyes.

“Nais,” Phorenice purred, “you once ate from my platter, drank from my cup, I gave you every good thing you ever had. You should have known better than to steal what I have set aside for myself. I offered you pardon, once, on easy terms, and you rejected it. But today is my wedding day, and I feel generous. Kneel, tell me you are sorry, name any man in the city you wish to marry, and you shall go free, wed before we rise from these cushions.”

“I will not wed,” Nais said, her voice soft and unshaking.

“Think one last time of the alternative,” Phorenice said, her tone turning sharp. “You will be laid warm, alive, beautiful as you stand now, in that hollow beneath the throne. Deucalion, my new husband, will place you there as a symbol that all my enemies shall share your fate. Then he will release the rams, lower that granite block, and no eye in Atlantis will ever see your face again. Breathe the sweet air, look at the sun, girl. It does not have to end this way.”

“I will not wed,” Nais said again.

Phorenice nodded, pulling her fingers from Deucalion’s arm. “Show her I do not jest, Deucalion.”

“The Empress does not jest,” he called out loud for the crowd to hear. “This is the fate of all who defy her. Lead the prisoner to the throne.”

The guards turned, chains taut to drag Nais up the steps, but she walked willingly, her colour never shifting, and Deucalion followed behind her, ten thousand eyes fixed on his every movement. He reached the top step, ordered the guards to unclip her collar, then wrapped his arms around her roughly, as if he was restraining a struggling, violent captive. A surge of raw, overwhelming love and agony crashed through him so hard he nearly fainted, and it was only every scrap of the iron will he’d trained for battle that kept his grip firm, his face cold. He half-dragged her across the stone platform to the open hollow, and she did not fight him even once. Brass clamps had been set into the stone around the cavity to lock down over the opening, so no prisoner could wriggle free and escape by being crushed instantly under the falling block. He slammed the clamps home one by one, his other hand pressed hard to her throat hard enough to leave purple, obvious bruise marks on her pale skin, to sell the illusion of brutality. Sweat dripped off his chin onto the thirsty granite, and when he finally pulled his hand away, Nais never met his eye. She stared straight up at the massive stone block hanging above her, unblinking, and he turned and walked away before he could break.

He gave the signal to the engineers manning the rams. The heated metal in their mechanisms cooled, contracted, and the ton-weight throne block began to creep slowly, so slowly, down toward its bed. Deucalion stood there, face impassive, watching the work like any architect supervising his project, until the block thudded home, sealed tight, and the engineers dragged the rams away. He walked a circuit with the chief architect and his plumb line and level to confirm the stone sat perfectly in place, the useless, punctilious ritual of the masons’ guild, his chest hollow as if half his own flesh had been left trapped in the dark with Nais.

He walked down the steps to report to Phorenice that her order was fulfilled, following the ancient formal words of the ceremony.

“Then we are wed,” she said, grinning, rising to her feet. “We do not need priests, not for us. No profane hand will officiate for the Empress of Atlantis and her consort. We have the whole of this crowd for witnesses, and later we will cut our union deep into the face of this throne, letter it in gold, so it will shine for ten thousand years.”

Deucalion took her outstretched hand, helped her stand, and they stepped out into the bright sun, Phorenice leaning against his arm. The crowd roared, screaming that she was a living goddess, and no one spared a cheer for Deucalion, whose grim, set face scared and repelled them. They climbed the steps to the throne, Deucalion seating her under the shadow of the cobra’s hood, and she pulled him down to sit beside her. The crowd fell dead silent as she raised her jewelled hand.

“Hear me, my people, hear me the old High Gods of Atlantis!” she proclaimed. “I take Deucalion as my husband, to rule with me, to guard our great realm. May every last enemy of mine perish, as the one beneath us has perished!” She wrapped her arms around his neck, and kissed him hard on the mouth.

Deucalion spoke his own lines by rote, as the old rites demanded, and kissed her back, the first woman’s lips he had ever touched, when all the while his heart lay buried under the granite at his feet. Then came the blood rite: Phorenice pricked his forearm with her dagger, lapped the bead of blood that welled up, and Deucalion pricked the soft white skin of her shoulder and drank her blood in turn. The metallic taste clung to his dry, parched throat, and he thought he would choke. Phorenice laughed, glowing, drunk on the thrill of their union, gushing that she could feel his strength flowing into her, that they were bound now, no force in the world could tear them apart. Deucalion could not bring himself to speak soft, false lover’s words, and mumbled a hasty excuse that he was a warrior unused to pretty speeches, that his heart spoke too loud to put its feelings to words.

The gods spared him the need to pretend any longer. A wounded soldier stumbled screaming into the square, an arrow sticking out of his shoulder, and the roar of his cry rippled through the whole throng: “The rebels are in! They tunnelled under the walls! They killed the cave tiger guards and took the east post gate! They’re sweeping through the lower city!”

Panic exploded across the square. The distant siege that everyone had dismissed as a nuisance was suddenly at their throats. Phorenice’s soft, glowing look vanished in an instant, replaced by the sharp, bright glare of a warrior empress, and she grabbed for her sword. “I fight at your side,” she said. “We are newly wed, we do not split in battle.”

Deucalion stood, stretching his cramped chest, desperate for air, for action, anything to stop the screaming grief in his head. “Grant me a wedding gift, then,” he said.

“Anything you name, it is yours,” she answered at once.

“Give me five hundred picked troops, and order the west gate a mile from the rebel breach thrown open wide.”

“You can take two hundred of the guards here right now,” she said, nodding. “Pull the rest from the barracks along the south wall on your way. But what is your plan?”

I won’t waste breath recounting every charge we ran that day, me and my handful of crack fighting men tearing through a rebel horde that outnumbered us fifteen to one. Their ranks were already rotting from the inside out with panic: every last fighter they’d sent creeping through the secret mine tunnel under our walls, the one they’d bet the whole siege on, had never come back. The shouts of terror they’d first heard echoing from inside the city had curdled, then given way to roars of triumph from our garrison holding the inner walls. Those wretched rebels had no real combat training worth naming, most carried farm tools and sharpened fire-hardened sticks instead of proper iron arms, and their stupid egalitarian nonsense had gutted every last chain of command—there was no one to rally the fleeing, no one to point where to stand when the first blow fell.

Panic spread through their ragged lines faster than a plague murrain. My attack plan was brutally simple: we never stopped. No pausing to form a defensive line, no looting the dead. We flanked their left, charged through their centre, then wheeled to harry their right, hacking a new path through the packed mass every few minutes. We kept them running, kept them guessing where the next blow would land, until the outer ranks broke entirely and scattered for the forests and trampled cornfields, while the rebels trapped in the middle only stared around desperate for a gap to follow.

This rout was no feat of legendary arms, and no professional soldier would brag of it. It was nothing more than seizing the moment, and it worked. Give those same rebels an open battle on ground they’d chosen, and they’d have fought until every last man was dead, sheer ferocious numbers dragging down any trained phalanx the city could have fielded. They were no cowards—every last one was Atlantean, same as the people they were besieging, spurred to wild desperation by the generations of oppression and wrongs they’d endured. But scattered they were, the siege broken in a single afternoon, and the rebellion was all but done if we avoided stupid, brutal over-punishment that would only drive them to even more suicidal acts later.

Chasing the last of the fugitives far from the walls to hammer the lesson of their panic home left us drained. We’d fought through the full blazing heat of the Atlantean midday, every man of my band bone-weary, so I called a halt for the night in a cluster of half-ruined stone huts that made a ready makeshift fort. Luck was with us: a herd of skittish little cloven-hoofed wild horses, spooked by fleeing rebels, blundered straight into our lines. We dropped five of them before the rest could bolt, and soon campfires were glowing, the meat roasting to feed every man in the bivouac.

Sentries paced the perimeter, calling their check-ins to one another through the dark. Wounded men huddled by the flames patching their cuts and gashes. I sat with my officers going over the day’s charges, the small missteps and unexpected strokes of luck, the kind of tedious post-mortem that only soldiers ever care about. The hour dragged on, snores rising from the dark edges of camp, every man clearly exhausted, only staying awake and talking out of deference to their commander. I dragged the conversation on, though—feverishly, because I could not bear to be left alone with my own thoughts.

The awkward impasse broke when a sentry stepped up to the fire, saluting. “My lord, we caught a woman slipping through the perimeter. She won’t give her name, won’t explain her business, only says she has to see you.”

“Bring her here,” I ordered, then thought better of it. If she’d trekked all the way out to our rough bivouac, she had private, urgent news. I got to my feet and followed him to the outer lines. “Your lads seem jumpy.”

“Begging your pardon, my lord—she could be a rebel assassin come to stick a knife in your ribs. The Empress sets high store on you right now, and we all know how ruthless her torturers are.” His frank concern made me grin, and then he stepped aside to show me the prisoner.

She was swathed head to toe in a thick hooded cloak, but I’d know the line of that slim frame anywhere: it was Ylga, Nais’s twin sister. I told the sentries to stand down, no questions, and led her far out of earshot of the camp, down a narrow game trail. “This can’t be good news that brought you all the way out here through the dark, Ylga.”

“You recognized me? There’s more warmth between us than you ever let show, Deucalion, if you can pick me out under all these wrappings. But you never ask after the carrier, do you? Only the message. Well. You aren’t husband to Empress Phorenice anymore. She’s cast you off.”

I went cold. “That’s impossible.”

“It’s true. She found out about you and Nais. There’s a second airshaft right next to the cell where you talked Nais into drinking the stasis drug that’ll keep her asleep for a hundred years, waiting for you to come wake her. That shaft led to another prisoner, locked in a neighbouring cell, who heard every word you two said. He tried to trade that story for his freedom, but by the time his gaoler remembered to feed him, Phorenice had already publicly married you. They beheaded the fool before he could talk, but not before she’d gotten the whole story out of other scraps of testimony. She’s mad with jealousy. She isn’t just going to kill you.”

Ylga quoted Phorenice’s words, low and bitter, and I felt every hair on the back of my neck stand up: “‘Shall I hew off his head? Pah. Shall I hand him to my torturers? He wouldn’t wrinkle his brow at their worst. No. He gets a punishment that lasts. I’ll cut off his right hand and his left foot, so he can never fight again, then hurl him crippled into the dangerous wild lands outside the city. The great beasts will hunt him, the volcanic ground fires will burn him while he sleeps. He’ll know hunger, he’ll breathe the poisonous fumes from the rifts. And all the while he’ll remember I have Nais locked safe in her stone sleep, right here in the palace, to toy with and abuse however my fancy takes me.’ That’s what she said. Are you still stupid enough to march back into the city?”

I shook my head. Mutilation and a slow death in the wastes was not on my agenda.

“Good,” Ylga said, plucking at my sleeve. “I took the liberty of making your escape plan for you. You remember Tob? The ex-captain of Tatho’s old navy?”

“Hard-drinking tar-stained sailor who fought the great sea leviathans with me off the coast of Yucatan? Of course I do.”

“He’s sailing tonight. He’s sick of the corruption in Atlantis, sick of his wife and kids going hungry while the court hoards all the grain. He’s planning to sail west to found his own kingdom. I struck a deal with him. His ship is anchored at the cove three hours hard march west, waiting for you. He was so happy when I told him you’d be coming, he said he’d sail straight through the harbour forts before the night chain went up, no questions asked. Come on. We have to go, before someone in camp notices you’re missing.”

I thanked her, but she brushed the words off like they were worthless, hurrying me down the trail. We moved fast, our weapons drawn to fend off any stray predatory beast or rebel straggler. The way was rough: the dark swallowed the path, and once we came to a narrow r spanned only by a rotting log, over which scalding hot water from a volcanic spring bubbled down toward the sea. Slip, and the water would peel the flesh clean from your bones. “My footing’s no good here,” Ylga said, and reached out to take my hand. I helped her across, then let go, and she sighed softly before pulling her fingers away.

We walked for hours in silence. When I offered to help her over a patch of thorny brambles, she shoved my hand away roughly. “I’m no soft weakling, not if that’s the only reason you’re touching me.”

At last we crested a rise, and the great gleam of Atlantis’s estuary spread out below us through the trees. A single old, weathered ship rode at anchor a mile off shore, her hull dark in the faint starlight. We stumbled down the slope to the beach, and a hail shot out at us from the decks the second we got within arrow range. It was Tob, sharp and watchful. He rowed a dinghy in to meet us, grinning through his thick salt-and-pepper beard.

I turned to Ylga, holding out my hands. “Come back to the city with a guard I’ll send with you to the gates. You’ll be safe. Phorenice has no reason to hurt you.”

She shook her head. “I’m not going back.”

“Ylga, this voyage is no pleasure trip. I’ve carved out colonies before, and this will be ten times worse. We have no proper supplies, half the crew are raw landsmen with no survival experience. Most of us will get scars, most of us will go hungry, the first year will break anyone who isn’t tough as forged iron.”

“I can cook. I can shoot a bow better than half your soldiers. I can sew clothes out of beast hide stitched with sinew. You only ever saw me as Phorenice’s fan-girl, didn’t you? You think I’m soft and useless. I don’t care. I’m not going back to that court where every man kisses my boots but none of them see me.”

“I can’t bring you with me,” I said, heavy. “My heart belongs to Nais. I couldn’t ever—”

She laughed, sharp and bitter. “Go on, kick my pride. I knew I’d only ever be second best to her. But I still can’t make myself hate you. I’d rather die out here on this voyage than go back to the palace and watch you die slow at Phorenice’s hands.” Before I could speak, she flung her arms around my neck and kissed me hard on the mouth, her lips warm and salty. “That’s for goodbye. You’ll never forget that, will you?” Then she turned and ran, vanishing into the dark treeline before I could call after her.

Tob yelled from the water that the tide was turning, and we didn’t have time to go looking for her. I waded out to the dinghy, clambered up the ship’s side, and the crew hauled anchor immediately.

“Greeting, my lord,” Tob rumbled, pointing out the rickety old hull. “She’s a rotten old tub, no fancy machines, no automatons to steer her. Got worm rot in the bottom from decades in the Egypt trade. But she’s the best I could afford. We’ve got fifty fighting lads, eight women, all ready to carve out a new home. We’re not heading for savage Europe, either. I found a chain of islands far north once, full of tin ore strewn all over the beaches, no Atlantean knows they exist but me. The savages there are blue-painted, naked, easy to beat into submission. No giant lizards, barely any big dangerous beasts. Perfect for a new kingdom.”

He’d dreamed of being king there for years, he admitted, but he’d long decided he’d serve under me, if I was willing to lead them. For two months we sailed slow along the coast, stopping at every river mouth and sheltered cove to hunt wild game, catch fish, forage for grain, stock up our larders so we wouldn’t starve on the long open crossing to the Tin Islands. No patrol ships from Phorenice ever chased us down the estuary. The crew relaxed, started laughing, started making plans for their new homes.

It was then I turned to Tob, leaning against the rail watching the waves roll by. “Tob,” I said. “I’ve got a favour to ask. I don’t want to sail all the way to the Tin Islands. Drop me off on this shore here, the part of the Atlantean main continent that faces out toward Europe. I don’t have gold or supplies to pay you with, but I’ll give you every weapon and charm I carry, the ones that kept me alive through Yucatan. They’ll keep your crew safe on the crossing. I don’t want a kingdom far out at sea. I’ve got unfinished business here on the main land.”

Tob blinked, then grinned so wide his beard crinkled. “Aye, my lord. Whatever you say. That shore’s full of wild game, no guards, no patrols. I’ll drop you at the next cove at high tide. Fair winds to you.”

Since the first gods walked the earth and shaped man from clay, there have always been stretches of land too cruel, too hungry, for human life to take root. But none are half so brutal as that wild western edge of Atlantis, facing the unbroken savagery of ancient Europe and Africa.

Common folk shun it entirely. Their old superstition swears the spirits of the most wicked dead roam there in broad daylight, tearing apart anyone the land’s natural horrors spare. Even murderers and traitors, running from the capital’s justice, will turn tail of their own free will and drag themselves back to face execution rather than spend a single night lost in that wilderness’s unnameable terrors. The educated laugh many of those old myths off… but no one steps over the boundary cairns without a cold prickle down their spine. The mountains rumble nonstop, earth tremors shake the ground under your boots every single day, invisible clouds of bitter poison gas slip through the trees so fast you can taste the sulphur before you have time to run. Rivers run crystal clear but brim with deadly neurotoxin, great cracks split the earth open to the molten fires below, and when cold rain pours down into them the whole land detonates in scalding steam and mud. Boiling mud geysers and flows of glowing molten rock can swallow a camp before you hear the warning rumble.

But for all that, it holds vast, shadowed forests of black ironwood, trees so old no living man remembers a time they weren’t standing. Under the canopy, every inch of ground sits submerged in warm, steaming water, the air thick with perpetual damp that saps the strength right out of your bones. And it is the undisputed domain of the lizards: scaled beasts of every size, most of them large enough to make a man think twice, some so colossal no human blade or spear could leave a mark. I saw one once, heaving itself out of a deep lake after it had finished feeding for the day, and the whole marsh thudded and pulsed under its tons of weight. That beast could have swallowed a fully grown war mammoth whole, and even a mammoth’s full charge would not have scratched the thick, horny plates that cased its skull and spine. Great spines ran along its back, tail, and limbs, and it sheared through 10-foot-wide tree trunks like they were dry twigs as it trundled off to its wallow. Millennia later, a paleontologist digging in Wyoming would unearth its kind’s skeleton: 130 feet long, 50 tons, 8 feet of solid thigh bone, enough empty space in its ribcage to seat 40 grown men.

These colossi rarely get caught in the land’s fissures. They move faster than you would ever believe, and they are far cleverer than most men give them credit for: they can feel the mountains rumbling hours before an eruption, and they will retreat to deep, cool lakes or thick mud wallows to wait out the danger. Their hides can stand heat no human could survive. I watched one lie completely peaceful in a pool where steam bursts shot boiling water 20 feet into the air, and another sprint straight across a slow flow of molten rock without so much as a singe on its scales.

That was the world I was dumped into, exiled by Empress Phorenice to die a slow, horrible death. For the first months I came closer to knowing raw, unthinking fear than I ever had in my life, and only my unshakable faith that the High Gods still had a purpose for me kept me from breaking entirely. My clothes rotted off my back until I was as naked as a wandering European savage. Ague wracked my limbs day and night, and I could find no healing herbs to ease the fever. For weeks on end I survived only on fat tree grubs and bitter leaves, and more than once I ate unfamiliar jungle game that left me vomiting and writhing in pain for hours.

But a man adapts, or he dies. As the months dragged on, I learned the land’s tricks. I found a massive hollow ironwood, cut handholds up its inner trunk to a perch 10 man-heights off the ground, and built a rough floor of branches for a shelter. Later I carved arrow slits into the wood for light and air, and dragged bucket after bucket of sand up to make a hearth, so I could cook my food safely above the ground where no wandering lizard could smell the smoke. I learned to trap the giant scaled fish in the rivers and snare the smaller freshwater turtles, and soon my ribs stopped sticking through my skin. The bitter, tar-tasting wild greenery I never could force myself to eat, but I no longer starved.

Years slipped by. The brute, survival part of me was content enough, but the ache for Nais never faded. I carved her face over and over out of a hippo’s ivory tusk, my fingers clumsy, my art terrible, but even the lumpy, crude little figurine felt like a piece of her when I traced the curve of her jaw. It did not sate my longing, though. It only made me hungrier for news of her, for a single glimpse of the tomb where I had sealed her beneath the granite throne of the Sacred Mountain to keep her safe from Phorenice’s wrath.

Every night, from my arrow slit, I watched the distant snow-capped peak far to the east burn blood red as the sun set behind it. I remembered, back when I was a new initiate to the priesthood, that the sun rose every morning right over that very summit. And I knew: if I climbed to that peak’s crest, I could see all the way across the steaming, swampy lowlands to the Sacred Mountain, to the city where my love lay.

I set out at dawn the next day. The thick, clinging mists of the lowlands swallowed my path, I wandered blind for weeks, covering three times the direct distance. A hundred times I rounded a tree and came face to face with a 30-foot lizard, and only the grace of the Gods made the beast turn away without seeing me. Twice I had to hew whole rafts out of driftwood to cross rivers of scalding, boiling water that blocked my path. I did not care. I had waited years for this, and every step carried me closer.

As I climbed higher, the trees thinned out and the air turned cold. I wove myself buskins and a thick coat out of tough alpine grass to fend off the chill. Higher still, the permanent snow started, then the glacial ice, its mazes of crevasses trying to swallow me with every step. A giant man-eating vulture followed me up the slopes for days, diving at my head until I lunged at it with my spear and it veered off, waiting for me to collapse. I pushed on, sick with altitude, gasping for thin air, until finally I hauled myself over the summit.

Far, far below, glinting through the clear, biting wind, was the Sacred Mountain. Its eternal ring of temple fires burned faint and pale in the midday sun. I could not see the city at its foot, but I knew exactly where it lay, and my whole heart ached for Nais. I was so lost in the memory of her face that I did not hear my name being called until the third time.

I spun, spear in hand. An old man was tramping toward me over the snow: white hair, white beard, bent and frail, but moving faster than any healthy young soldier could ever run, the glowing sun symbol of our order burning bright in his fist. It was Zaemon. He traced the sacred sign of the Seven, and I returned it with all the ritual reverence due him. He was the head of the High Council of Priests, the highest authority left on Atlantis.

“Your summons comes from the full Council,” he said, his voice sharp over the mountain wind. “Phorenice’s madness has no bounds. She has laid siege to the Sacred Mountain, swears every last soul that will not bow to her will dies. The Council has no general left alive who can lead our defenses. You will return at once. Atlantis will fall if you do not.”

My grip on the spear tightened. “I obey. But first: what of Nais?”

Zaemon’s face did not soften. “Phorenice has stolen most of our ancient magic. She knows you still live. She has left Nais unharmed in that tomb, waiting to use her as a weapon against you. Do not be soft, brother. Even I, her own father, would sacrifice her in a heartbeat if it saved Atlantis.”

I stepped forward, my voice cold. “I will lead your armies. I will fight every soldier Phorenice throws at us. I will die in the last ditch if I have to. But you swear to me, right now, that you will get Nais out of that tomb unharmed, and give her to me. If you do not, I will not lift a finger to save your sacred city. I will burn every last stone of Atlantis to the ground to make Phorenice’s funeral pyre.”

Zaemon snarled, his old face twisting with rage. He called me faithless, called me traitor to my oaths, said the Council never thought they would have to bargain with one of their own. But there was no one else. He had no choice. At last he spat out the oath, swearing by the sun itself that Nais would be mine, unharmed, the second we retook the Sacred Mountain.

He turned without another word, and set off down the slope. He moved faster and faster, his footprints in the deep snow growing shallower and shallower, until they vanished entirely. The whole flank of the mountain lay before me, trackless, untouched by any other human foot. He was already hundreds of miles away, back at the Sacred Mountain, by the time I slung my spear over my shoulder and started down the slope after him.

I had taken oaths to Atlantis once. I had put the good of the nation before all else. But those oaths were dead now. Nothing mattered. Not the temples, not the council, not the whole fate of the lost continent. I would win this war, and I would walk away with Nais. Everything else could burn.

My cross-continent trek across the breadth of ancient Atlantis was stitched with hidden danger at every turn, but there is no need to drag through every tedious mile. Only one forced stop broke my pace, and it very nearly became my last.
I’d run a stag to ground across miles of sunbaked savannah, driven my spear home, and scented the air carefully for sign of lion, cave bear, or any other predator that might claim the kill before I could butcher it. Convinced the clearing was safe, I knelt to dress the meat and tie a heavy bundle to my shoulders—until my heel brushed something coiling in the long grass.
It was a monstrous green venomous centipede, its hundred legs glistening with slime, its fangs dripping a poison I’d learned to fear from old campfire tales. It struck faster than a dart of lightning, sinking its jaws deep into the flesh of my lower thigh. I hacked its head off with my hunting knife mid-strike, watched its writhing body twist its remaining legs into mad knots on the dirt, then drove my blade deep around the bite in a ring of cuts, forcing the tainted blood to pour out and wash the poison clean before it could course higher. I thought I’d cheated a terrible death. I slung the venison over my shoulder and set off, pleased with the quick escape.
By the third hour of walking, my leg throbbed so hard I could feel the pulse hammering in my toes. The skin swelled tight as stretched parchment, the ache crawling up my hip until a violent, roiling sickness wracked my whole frame. I could barely stand. Any half-starved predator that stumbled on me then would have made a meal of me without a single fight. I dragged myself up into the crook of a thick oak at full dark, and huddled there awake through the whole long night, sweating through waves of agony.
At dawn, when the night hunters had slunk back to their lairs, I clambered down, leaned my whole weight on my spear, and limped onward through the shadowed forest. The moss on the north side of trees was my compass, but soon I was seeing moss creeping around every trunk, my vision swimming from fever and the iron-hard swelling in my leg. My last remaining clear thought was of the High Council’s promise: defeat Phorenice, and Nais, my lost love drugged into eight years of enchanted sleep, would be woken. I would not stop before I did it. That was the last thing I remembered before a dark shape stepped out of the trees and caught me as I fell.
I woke to the feel of soft moss and dried ferns under my back, laid out in a narrow cleft between two boulders, its entrance barred with sharp stakes driven into the dirt to keep out prowling beasts. My thigh was wrapped in a thick, warm herbal poultice, and across the cave, a woman squatted tending a fire of sticks, stirring a pot of roasted wood grubs and wild honey.
“Who brought me here?” I croaked.
“I did,” she said, not looking up. “Folk call me the nymph of the western woods. I collect herbs, cure fevers, tell fortunes, lay curses on the folks who skip paying for my services. Lean work these days, what with wars and pestilence wiping out half the population round here.”
“Do you get news of the city, Atlantis?”
She snorted. “Not me. Empress Phorenice would boil me alive in brine if she caught me. That vain tyrant don’t tolerate no magic but her own. Word is she’s marching her whole army on the Sacred Mountain now, to drag the old high priests out of their sanctum.”
The news spilled fast: Phorenice still sat on the throne, and had taken Tatho as her husband three years prior, bearing him twin sons. When I asked about the distant colony of Yucatan, the story made my blood run cold: hordes of squat, hairy cannibal men had poured over the western sea, armed with nothing but stone-tipped clubs, driven by no hunger for plunder or slaves, only a ravenous lust to eat Atlantean flesh. They’d overrun every Yucatan city, swarming over the walls by the thousands even as the colonists slaughtered them by the tens of thousands, eating every last inhabitant, then moving on to the next settlement. Tatho and the last handful of survivors had barely escaped on a fishing boat, leaving the whole colony I’d worked years to build abandoned to the jungle. I said nothing to the half-mad old healer of my ties to that lost land, only thanked her, stayed six days in her cave while her herbs drew the last of the venom out of my leg, then hunted down a fat wild horse to leave her as payment before I set off again.
The lands east of her cave were a graveyard. Once, homesteaders had built log houses heavy and dovetailed tight enough to keep cave bears from breaking through, strung tree-perched huts across the forest, built walled villages to fend off predators. Now nine out of ten of them stood empty. Hearth stones were choked with weeds, vegetable patches reclaimed by scrub, village log gates rotting into the undergrowth, no trace left of the people who’d lived there but piles of old kitchen midden. Phorenice’s tyranny had spread over the land like a rotting blight, sucking the life out of every corner.
I avoided every village I could, slipping off into the forest to circle their garbage stench rather than waste time fighting the half-starved, desperate locals who rushed out to seize unvetted travelers and press them into forced labor. I had no wish to kill them; I could see they were only trying to survive under the heel of a mad empress.
By the time I was within half a day’s march of Atlantis itself, the Sacred Mountain loomed ahead, its ring of eternal sacred fires glowing red against the dusk. Every fighting man for miles had been conscripted into the siege army ringed around the mountain’s only accessible approach. The whole plain was patrolled end to end. A single man could never fight his way through that host. I hid in the edge of a wood, beating my brains out for a trick to get past the lines, when a man came running toward me, his hands up.
I recognized Ro instantly: a low-ranking priest I’d never trusted, greedy, cowardly, eager to save his own skin above all else. He told me Zaemon, the highest of the Three High Priests, had found him in his temple, paralyzed his right hand to force him to track me down, and given me my orders: light a massive grass fire on the plain as a signal that I’d arrived, then sneak around to the sheer, unguarded sea cliff on the far side of the Sacred Mountain at midnight, where the priests above would lower a rope to haul me up. The last thing Ro mumbled before he scurried off, begging me to ask Zaemon to heal his hand and slip him a few coins to feed his wife, was a piece of news that shocked me cold: Phorenice had stolen the Secret of Life, the most guarded holy secret only the Three High Priests were supposed to know, and made herself functionally immortal.
I set the dry grass of the plain alight before Ro was even out of sight. The fire roared up, bright as a bonfire, and that midnight I picked my way across the dark, beast-haunted plain to the bare, sheer cliff face. A thin rope snaked down over the rock not long after I arrived, I tied it tight around my waist, and the priests above hauled me up hand over hand. The men dragging me over the crest were old, their lungs wheezing with the effort, every last young priest-warrior having already fallen fighting the siege below.
They led me straight to Zaemon, who greeted me with the royal sign of the priesthood’s highest office. “All hail Deucalion, King of Atlantis, duly elected by the High Council,” he said.
I did not waste words. “Is Phorenice dead yet?”
“Not yet,” he said, his voice grim. “It is your duty to kill her. She has broken every law of the Gods, defiled every temple, set her own face up on altars across the empire to demand worship, and stolen the Secret of Life to make herself eternal. The High Gods have given the order: we will destroy her, even if we have to unleash every power locked in the Ark of the Mysteries and shatter the entire continent to do it. We have endured her evil for years, but now she has gone too far.”
I bowed, accepting the charge. “As the Gods command, I obey. But first, I must have my queen Nais woken. I cannot rule this realm half-hearted, not with her still asleep under that eight-year drug.”
Zaemon nodded. “She shall be brought to you at once.”
I stepped out of the cave then, the old high priest at my heels. It was still night, but the top of the Sacred Mountain never saw full dark. The ring of eternal fires, fed by natural gas venting up from the heart of the earth, burned bright around the whole crest, lighting the rock face as bright as midday, a wall of flame that no mortal army could ever hope to climb. Far below, Phorenice’s vast siege camp stretched across the plain, thousands of campfires twinkling like stars, the fight for the last stronghold of the old order just a breath away.

A stooped, white-haired priest stumbled through the press of armed defenders clustered at the pass gate, and tapped me hard on the shoulder.
I snapped my head around, hand already half-drawn to my sword at the interruption. We were moments out from traitor Empress Phorenice’s hordes breaching our outer defences, and every man left at my back was a veteran ready to die where he stood. “What is it?”
“Word has been carried to the Three, my King, of the treachery unfolding here. They’ve sent a command.”
I laughed, breathless, already tasting the fight to come. “Good. Tell them I’m here to see the finest bloodbath of my life before I fall. When I die, I’ll stand guard at the gates of the afterlife behind the stars to welcome the rest of them when their turn comes. My apologies that they’re too old and feeble to pick up a blade and get a taste of the killing themselves.”
The old priest’s face went solemn. “They forbid you to die here, my King. They say they still have use for you. You will quit the gate at once.”
I rounded on him, hot with rage. “I will take no such order. You’ve misheard them. If I am a man, a priest, a soldier, a king, my honour demands no enemy passes this gate while my heart still beats. Go back and throw that lie right in their teeth.”
He smiled, and the line of it held the ghost of the warrior he’d been in his youth. “I knew you’d never believe it. I begged them for a sign, and they gave me this.” He held out a glint of carved gold, the serpent bracelet I’d fastened around Nais’s elbow the day I’d crowned her my queen.
My anger died in my throat. “They stripped that from her?”
“They gave no explanation for how they came by it. Their word is this: the owner waits for you inside the Ark of the Mysteries. The purpose they have for you is tied directly to her.”
I’ll own it freely, every soldier reading this will understand: the lure of standing with my men, sword in hand, and going down fighting, was almost too strong to resist. But Nais’s face rose unbidden before me, and my love for her won out in the end. I gave the formal salute that marked I accepted their order.
I turned to the knot of priests who’d stayed to hold the gate, my throat tight. “You’ll get the fight I’m forced to miss, brothers. I envy you it. But though I am King of Atlantis, I am still one of the Seven, a servant of the Three, and their word is the will of the Highest Gods. Do your worst. I’ll ask you to save me a spot at that star-side guard post you’ll be heading for soon, since I can’t march in there with you.”
They grinned, clashed their weapons together in salute, and I turned and walked up the path before I could change my mind and run back to stand with them.
I never saw that fight, but I knew it was brutal. Long, long minutes dragged by before the first of Phorenice’s hordes crested the far side of the pass, screaming and blood-soaked. That tiny remnant of priests sold their lives for every inch of ground, for every enemy they could cut down before they fell.
The climb up the sacred mountain was long. I passed groves of cypress, half-empty temples, colleges where scholars had studied for centuries, houses that had belonged to the greatest families of the continent. The no-man’s-land that only the highest initiates could cross stood before me, but no sentries barred my path. All the old rules were already burning away with the world below.
Old Zaemon was waiting for me at the boundary temple, his voice trembling as he chanted through a full ancient ritual begging dispensation for my trespass, slow as molasses. Below us, we could already hear the clash of steel at the last gate. The handful of old priests manning the ramparts were bartering their lives for every invader they could kill, but they couldn’t hold out forever. The storm was coming for the sacred mount any minute now. I itched to run, but Zaemon wouldn’t budge until the last line of the rite was spoken.
He led me to the great Ark of the Mysteries at the peak, chiding me for my impatience, and made me kiss its unscratchable obsidian surface in reverence. A second old, emaciated priest I’d never seen before climbed slowly down the nailed battens running up the Ark’s curved side. These two were the last two living initiates of the highest order, the only men who knew all the secrets of the Gods.
Zaemon lifted a shaking finger and swept it over the land spread far below the peak. “Look.”
I followed his gesture, and a cold chill crawled down my spine. The sun hung high in a cloudless blue sky, the air warm and still — but the coasts far out were being hammered by walls of surf hundreds of feet high, waves that rolled inland and never receded. The wide estuary leading to Atlantis’s main city was choked with churning flood, swallowing the harbour walls whole, creeping up into the lower wards of the great city itself. The lower farmlands were already gone, blotted out under roiling grey water. The mountain ranges dotted across the continent were spouting smoke and gouts of flame, their slopes hissing where the floodwaters hit hot rock.
“You see it?” Zaemon asked.
“Part of it.”
“Then hear the rest. The Highest Gods’ patience has run out. The whole of Atlantis, the vast continent stretching far out west past our coasts, every man, woman, child, beast, and temple — all of it is doomed to drown and shatter. This was foreseen thousands of years ago. That Ark you just kissed? The Gods ordered it built for exactly this day. No blade can scratch it, no rock can crack it, no wave can sink it. Inside, all the forbidden knowledge of our people is carved on stone sheets, there’s grain and fresh water enough for two people to survive four years, seeds, weapons, every supply we could possibly need to build a new life far away. The Gods have decreed only two people will survive. A man, a woman, chosen to carry that knowledge across the waves, to raise a new people that will honour the Sun God and the rest of the divinities once Atlantis is gone. The woman is already inside. The Gods have chosen you, Deucalion, to be the man.”
I went numb. “I don’t know if I want to be the last one left.”
“The Gods don’t ask for your wants.” His voice was sharp. “They command you, their faithful servant.”
Habit kicked in, hard. I saluted, and told him I would obey.
The time we had left was vanishing fast. Below us, the flood rose higher, the volcanoes rumbled so hard the ground under our feet shook. The last gate fell. Phorenice’s troops poured through, screaming, swarming up the slopes. But the two old priests didn’t hurry a single line of the initiation ritual that would raise me to their rank, one of the Three. A nearby volcano burst with a roar that shook the mountain when seawater hit its magma chamber. They didn’t flinch. Thousands of panicking soldiers ran through the temple groves below us. They didn’t skip a single chant. They went through every majestic, age-old line of the rite, with nothing but the sky for a ceiling and the Gods for witnesses, and I forced my reeling, distracted mind to memorise every secret, every key to the powers they gave me, even as I watched the sea swallow more and more of the land.
By the time the ritual drew to a close, Phorenice’s troops had flooded onto the peak, wild with panic, knowing the whole world was ending. They were hunting every priest they could find to tear them apart, convinced the old men had called the destruction down on their heads.
“Kneel,” Zaemon said, his voice steady as ever. “Bow to the Earth, your mother, and swear you will never misuse the powers the Gods have granted you over her.”
I knelt, swore, and stood up the third of the highest order of initiates, the third of the Three.
“Brother,” Zaemon said, clapping me on the shoulder, “the two of us will not be coming with you. We will stay here, to give the Gods our final account of our stewardship, to stand beside them behind the stars. Go. Climb into the Ark. Shut its roof door the second anyone tries to break in. You are forbidden to fight, forbidden to take any provocation, no matter how much it stings. The Gods’ will is that you and Nais survive. But linger a moment at the top, if you can. You are one of only two people who will ever carry the story of Atlantis’s death. You should see it all.”
I climbed the battens up the Ark’s side, slipped down the inner stair, and found Nais waiting for me in a small, lamp-lit chamber, her face pale but calm. “Zaemon dragged me here,” she said, reaching for my hand. “Threatened me, said you’d be here soon. He’s my father, gruff as he is, I know he loves us. Can’t we save him?”
I shook my head. “The Ark was never meant for more than two. His mind is made up. He’d rather go face the Gods than hide.”
I kissed her, and climbed back up to the Ark’s roof.
The whole of Atlantis was gone now. The great city had vanished under the waves, the plains were all foaming white flood, the only land left above water was the very peak of the sacred mountain we stood on. A handful of oarless galleys were tossed across what had once been inland forests, until a great wave swallowed them whole. The last of the giant lizards that had roamed the lowlands were drowning in the churning, boiling sea.
Then I spotted a tight, armed force marching straight for us through the panicking mob. Phorenice’s personal guards, their blades still wet with blood, had cut their way through their own terrified troops to make a path for their Empress. Behind them, carried in a gold and ivory litter borne by her European slaves, rode Phorenice herself, her hair cascading red over her shoulders, her face calm, lovely, no trace of fear. Her two small children lay at her feet. Tatho, her scheming chief advisor, marched at her right, his beard curled into a thousand neat ringlets. Ylga, the fan girl who’d once been my friend, walked at her left, her fan in her hand, unruffled.
They halted ten paces from the Ark.
Phorenice lifted a perfectly plucked eyebrow and looked up at me, and laughed. “Well, well. Deucalion. The man I once thought was sweet enough to keep, until he bored me. They made you king, did they? All the little smelly old priests on this peak crowned you their leader?”
“Nais is inside this Ark with me,” I called back. “We two will be the only ones left of Atlantis before long.”
She threw her head back and laughed. “She can have you, dear. I am a Goddess, remember? My arts let me live forever. When all this mess is over, I’ll just go back to the star-side realm I came from. You’re welcome to your little stone box and your rustic queen.”
Zaemon stepped forward, holding the blazing Sun Symbol high in his hand, and roared a curse so loud it cut through the thunder of the rising flood. Phorenice’s face twisted with rage. She screamed for her guards to kill him, tried to leap from the litter to kill him herself — but the power of the Sun Symbol blazed, and her dark arts couldn’t touch him. She sank back against her cushions, beaten, sullen, for the first time I’d ever seen her broken.
Only Ylga broke away from the group, running to the base of the Ark, and looked up at me. The roar of the waves was getting so loud it almost swallowed her words. “Deucalion! I wouldn’t come with you even if you begged me. I don’t take half a man. But I’ll die happy knowing you almost loved me once, and you’ll never forget me.”
A wall of green, churning seawater crested the edge of the peak behind them, swallowing half the panicking soldiery in one single rush. Steam from the flooded volcanoes billowed up in a thick golden mist, turning the whole world hazy. I looked down and saw Zaemon wave the Sun Symbol at me, pointing to the roof door of the Ark, ordering me to shut it.
I slammed the heavy obsidian door shut, shot home the iron bolts, and stumbled back down the stair to Nais’s side, wrapping my arms tight around her. Outside, I heard men battering at the Ark’s sides with axes, screaming, climbing the battens to wrench at the roof door.
A great surge hit, and the whole Ark lurched upward, as light as a feather, torn free of the rock of the peak. The noise of blows and screaming faded, muffled through the Ark’s thick stone walls. The lamp tipped over and went out, and we were left in total darkness, tossed on the heaving peaks of the endless ocean, the only two survivors of a whole lost world.

Our ancient ark was rudderless, oarless, stripped of all mechanism that might steer it, free to drift only where the high powers of the deep chose to carry it. Its interior was wrapped in total, unyielding darkness, thick with a resinous, centuries-old scent that clung to every breath, thrumming with the low groan of strained timber and distant, muffled echoes of the world we had lost. I fumbled blindly for the firebox of the fallen lantern for what felt like hours, but could not find it, and so we were forced to endure the chaos unfolding around us sightless, adrift in the black. The waves toyed with our hull in merciless, giddy sport, heaving it high before slamming it down into troughs that made our teeth rattle, and I clung tight to Nais, holding her pinned to the only solid berth in the dark belly of the craft. We spoke no more than a handful of words to each other those first days, but our shared grief at the loss of every person and every home we had ever known bound us closer than any conversation could.

When Atlantis tore free of the crust and plunged to form new ocean bed, it left roiling, ravenous whirlpools and scalding plumes of steam spouting from its drowned volcanic fires as a grim, fleeting gift to the sea gods. Trapped as we were in the ark’s pitch-black hull, we could not see any of that devastation, but we soon felt the next, far worse blow: a vast, screaming hurricane rolled in to scour the last traces of the broken continent from the face of the waters. That apocalyptic turmoil raged for seven nights and seven full days, counted later on the old water clock nailed to the ark’s inner wall, until at last the howl of the wind died away, leaving only the slow, heavy roll of an endless, oily swell.

Feeling the shift in the motion of the hull, I dared at last to climb the rough-hewn stair leading to the hatch set in the ark’s roof. When I pried that heavy panel open, sweet, sun-warmed salt air gushed down to chase the foul, stale air out of the hull. The ark rode high and dry over the unbroken swells, so I ducked back below to carry Nais up to bathe in the healing light of the sun. We knelt side by side on the warm deck planks, faces turned up to that golden light, and gave thanks for the mercy that had pulled us alive through the ruin of our world, before laying down to slip into the deep, bone-deep rest we had not dared to seek since the ground had split open beneath our feet.

Though I was exhausted past all words, sleep would not come for a long while. I stared out over the sun-dappled, glassy blue water, and not a single speck of land broke the unbroken ring of the horizon. The vault of the sky stretched endless overhead, and the bosom of the deep was littered with the broken wreckage of Atlantis: snapped great cedar trees, hewn house timbers, a caved-in upturned hide boat, and here and there the rounded forms of men and cattle drifting over the swells, keeping pace with our ark like a grim, silent funeral convoy to prove all that had happened was no nightmare. At last I slipped into unconsciousness, still holding Nais’s hand, and when I woke hours later I found her already watching me, her gaze soft and tender.

We were both fully rested, and that returned strength softened the edge of our sharp grief, left us ready to accept the fate the high gods had laid out for us without complaint. We climbed back down into the ark’s hull to eat and drink, to rebuild our strength for the unknown new life stretching ahead of us. It was only then, at leisure, that we discovered what a marvel this ancient craft truly was. Though this was the first time it had ever touched open water in all the centuries since its builders had finished it, it did not leak a single drop, not a single suncrack marred its timber, and its floor was bone dry. You could see the grain of the wood in the faint light that filtered down from the hatch, but there was not a single seam, joint, or nail to be found anywhere. The ancients had grown whole timbers into one seamless hull via a craft our generation has long lost, then treated the entire structure with a secret forgotten preservative that made the wood harder than metal, completely impervious to rot, storm, or salt.

Stowed in its cave-like inner hull were provisions enough for years. Great sealed tanks lined the central passage, one set holding a mountain of dry grain, another brimming with cool, fresh drinking water. Shelves lined other walls, stocked with herbal medicines, preserved essences, and extracts of hunted beasts, all the supplies the old builders had planned for a craft roaming at the sea’s mercy. Every wall, every tank partition, every surface of the seamless timber was carved in the rough, ancient art of a forgotten age, depicting every single beast that had ever lived in old Atlantis. As a hunter, I traced those carvings with fascination, many of them showing massive, long-extinct creatures I had only ever heard old storytellers whisper of, frozen in the wood before they vanished forever along with the rest of our world. A full store of sharp bronze weapons and fine carpentry tools sat stacked in a side nook too.

For weeks after that, our quiet life unfolded on the drifting ark, as the currents and the will of the gods carried us far across the empty expanse of ocean. We had no way to steer, no way to nudge the hull forward even an inch faster than the current allowed. The high gods who had plucked only the two of us from the tens of millions of lives that had died in Atlantis held full control over our fate, and we handed our future over to Them gladly. I will speak in time of the new shore we eventually reached, of the land we built our new home on, of the children born to Nais and me there. But this chronicle is written in the order of events as they unfolded, so first I must tell of how I came by the wax sheets I use to write it.

In a great iron-bound coffer set dead in the center of the ark’s floor, we found the sum of all Atlantis’s accumulated secret knowledge, written in precise, careful script: every hidden ritual, every forbidden power, every dark trick the scholars of ten generations had hoarded. I read through a portion of those scrolls in the long, quiet days of drifting, and the awful, world-breaking power they granted control over appalled me. I had seen a fraction of those powers unleashed on our homeland, watched it tear the continent apart. But these scrolls held secrets far greater: the lost mystery of life and death that the mad queen Phorenice had chased to her own destruction, and other, darker things that I will never, ever put pen to. The burden of being custodian to that terrible knowledge was too heavy to bear, the thought of it falling one day into greedy, reckless hands too horrible to face.

At last I took two hot iron pokers, and seared every wax sheet until every last character of that old forbidden writing melted away to nothing. If I did wrong, the high gods will mete out their justice in their infinite wisdom. If those great secrets were meant to endure on the earth, They can dictate them afresh to some worthy scribe of their choosing. But I burned every last trace of them there, as the ark swayed gently over the long, oily swells. Later, once we landed on new shores, I would overwrite these blank sheets only with the true story of the greed and folly that dragged great Atlantis down to its death. Nais, that I love so tenderly—
