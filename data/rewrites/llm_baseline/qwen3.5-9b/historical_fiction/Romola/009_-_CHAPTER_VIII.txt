For a Northerner, rising on Midsummer morning is a simple affair: one sees the dew clinging to the grassy verge of a dusty path, notices the tender shoots pushing through the dark green of oaks and firs in the coppice, and looks over the gate at the freshly shorn meadow, all without recalling that it is the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist.

Not so for the Florentine—and certainly not for the Florentine of the fifteenth century. To him, that specific morning held a distinct brilliance; the eastern sun striking the Arno possessed a special quality, and the ringing of the bells was articulate, proclaiming the great summer festival of Florence, the Day of San Giovanni.

San Giovanni had served as Florence's patron saint for at least eight centuries, ever since Lombard Queen Theodolinda commanded her subjects to honor him peculiarly. Indeed, according to the old chronicler Villani, this devotion stretched back to the days of Constantine the Great and Pope Sylvester, when the Florentines deposed their idol, Mars. They did so carefully, however, avoiding any insult; while they consecrated their beautiful temple to God and "Blessed Master Saint John," they placed the old god of war respectfully upon a high tower near the River Arno. Ancient memorials claimed Mars had been elected their tutelary deity under specific astral influences, warning that if he were broken or treated with indignity, the city would suffer great damage and upheaval. But by the fifteenth century, this discreet regard for the feelings of the Man-destroyer had long vanished. The god of spear and shield ceased to frown beside the Arno, and the defenses of the Republic were believed to lie in its craft and its coffers. For spears and shields could be hired with gold florins, and on those very gold florins, there had always been the image of San Giovanni.

Much good had come to Florence since the dim times of struggle between the old patron and the new: undoubtedly, there had been quarrels and bloodshed between Guelf and Ghibelline, Black and White, between orthodox sons of the Church and heretical Paterini; there had been floods, famines, and pestilences. Yet, amidst this, there had been immense wealth and glory. Florence had achieved conquests over walled cities once mightier than itself, particularly over hated Pisa, whose marble buildings were too high and beautiful, and whose masts were too honored on the Greek and Italian coasts. The name of Florence grew prouder in every court of Europe, even in Africa, bolstered by the purest gold coinage, finest dyes and textiles, pre-eminent scholarship, poetic genius, and wits of the most serviceable sort for statesmanship and banking. It was a name so omnipresent that a Pope with a turn for epigrams called Florentines "the fifth element." And for this high destiny, though it might partly depend on the stars and the Madonna dell'Impruneta, and certainly on other higher powers less often named, the praise was largely due to San Giovanni, whose image graced the fair gold florins.

Therefore, it was fitting that the Day of San Giovanni—an ancient Church festival already venerable in the days of Saint Augustine—should be a day of peculiar rejoicing for Florence. It was ushered in by a vigil kept in strict old Florentine fashion, filled with dancing, street jesting, and perhaps not a little stone-throwing and window-breaking, but emphatically with sights unique to a city employing a clever engineer and architect named Cecca. With Cecca's help, the very saints, surrounded by almond-shaped glory and floating on clouds with their joyful retinue of winged cherubs (as seen today in the works of Perugino), seemed to bring a piece of heaven down into the narrow streets, passing slowly through them. More wonderfully still, saints of gigantic size, accompanied by attendant angels, moved in a slow, mysterious manner along the thoroughfares, like a procession of colossal figures descended from the high domes and tribunes of the churches. The clouds were made of fine woven stuff; the saints and cherubs were unglorified mortals supported by firm bars; and those mysterious giants were actually men of steady brains, balancing themselves on stilts and enlarged, like Greek tragedians, by huge masks and stuffed shoulders. One would be a miserably unimaginative Florentine to think only of this mechanical trick—indeed, somewhat impious, for in the images of sacred things lay some of the virtue of the sacred things themselves. Following this came a company of merry black demons, well-armed with claws and thongs and other implements of sport, ready to perform impromptu farces of bastinadoing and clothes-tearing. Why, that was simply the demons' way of keeping vigil, and they, too, might have descended from the domes. The Tuscan mind slipped from the devout to the burlesque as readily as water rounds an angle. The saints had already done their turn, paused before the gates of San Giovanni to honor him on the eve of his *festa*, and departed. On the morrow, the great day thus inaugurated, it was fitting that the tributary symbols paid by Florence's dependent cities, districts, and villages—whether conquered, protected, or held by immemorial possession—should be offered at the shrine of San Giovanni in the old octagonal church, once the cathedral and now the baptistery, where every Florentine had received the sign of the Cross with anointing chrism on his brow. From the white-haired elder to the stripling, from the matron to the lisping child, all were clothed in their best to honor the great day and witness the great sight. When the sun began to slope and the streets cooled, there would follow the glorious race or *Corso*: unsaddled horses, clad in rich trappings, running right across the city from the Porta al Prato on the northwest, through the Mercato Vecchio, to the Porta Santa Croce on the southeast, where the richest *Palii*—velvet and brocade banners with silk linings and fringes of gold, suitable for a city that half-clothed the well-dressed world—awaited the winner on a triumphal car.

Thereafter followed more dancing. Nay, throughout the entire day, as an old chronicler noted at the beginning of that century, there were weddings, grandest gatherings, piping, music, song, balls, feasts, gladness, and ornament, such that this earth might have been mistaken for Paradise!

Yet in this year of 1492, it was perhaps a little less easy to make that mistake. Lorenzo the Magnificent and Subtle was dead, replaced by an arrogant and incautious Piero, an evil change for Florence unless, indeed, the wise horse prefers the bad rider, as he is more easily thrown from the saddle. Already, regrets for Lorenzo were yielding to murmured desires for government on a broader basis, where corruption might be arrested and there might exist that free play for everyone's jealousy and ambition which characterized the ideal liberty of the good old quarrelsome, struggling times when Florence raised her great buildings, reared her own soldiers, drove out would-be tyrants at the sword's point, and was proud to keep faith at her own loss. Lorenzo was dead; Pope Innocent was dying; and a troublesome Neapolitan succession, coupled with an intriguing, ambitious Milan, threatened to set Italy by the ears shortly. The times were likely to be difficult. Still, there was all the more reason for the Republic to maintain its religious festivals.

Midsummer morning in this year of 1492 was no less bright than usual. Early in the morning, the symbolic offerings destined for the grand procession were assembled at their starting point in the Piazza della Signoria—that famous square where stood the massive, turreted Palace of the People, the Palazzo Vecchio, and the spacious Loggia built by Orcagna, the scene of all grand state ceremony. The sky formed the fairest blue tent, beneath which the bells swung so vigorously that every evil spirit with sense enough to be formidable must long since have fled. Windows and terraced roofs were alive with human faces; sombre stone houses glowed with hanging draperies. The boldly soaring palace tower, the yet older square tower of the Bargello, and the spire of the neighboring Badia seemed to keep watch above. Below, on the broad polygonal flags of the piazza, unfolded the glorious display of banners, horses with rich trappings, and gigantic *ceri*, or tapers—fitly called towers—strangely aggrandized descendants of those torches by whose faint light the Church worshipped in the Catacombs. Early in the morning, all processions needed to move under the Midsummer sky of Florence, where the shelter of narrow streets had to be periodically exchanged for the glare of wide spaces; the sun would be high in the heavens before the long pomp completed its pilgrimage in the Piazza di San Giovanni.

But here, where the procession was to pause, the magnificent city, with its ingenious Cecca, had provided another tent than the sky.
