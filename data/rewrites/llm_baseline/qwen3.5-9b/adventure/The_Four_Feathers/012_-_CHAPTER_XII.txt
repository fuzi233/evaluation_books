It was a May night, and atop a grassy knoll overlooking the Nile near Wadi Halfa, three officers sat in silence, their cigars glowing against the darkness. Above them, the moon hung full and fierce, stripping the planets of their glow and washing the sky into a pale, luminous curve. Below, the sluggish river mirrored the silver light, while the opposite bank stretched away into an endless, frost-white desert where stones glittered like scattered jewels. Behind them, the shadow of the mess-room verandah anchored the scene in solid blackness.

One officer struck a match, the flame illuminating a face etched with anxiety.

"I hope no harm has come to him," he murmured, tossing the match aside. "Though I wish I dared believe it."

The speaker was Colonel Dawson, a man of middle years commanding a Sudanese battalion. He was joined by Lieutenant Calder of the Engineers, whose hair had indeed turned grey—a common sight in the Sudan—but whose unlined face betrayed a youth untouched by cynicism. Yet, in this instance, youth offered no optimism to counter Dawson's dread.

"He left Halfa eight weeks ago," Calder said, his voice heavy.

"Exactly eight weeks today," Dawson replied.

A third figure, Major Walters of the Army Service Corps—tall, spare, and long-necked—offered a cheerful dissent.

"It's premature to declare Durrance lost," Walters argued. "Know Durrance? Give him a campfire in the desert with a couple of sheikhs circling him, and he'd bounce for a month without boredom. Here, he has letters, an office, a desk—he hates it all. He'll turn up eventually, just not soon."

"He is three weeks overdue," Dawson countered grimly. "He is methodical, in his own way. I am afraid."

Walters gestured toward the empty white expanse across the river. "If he traveled west, I might concede. But Durrance headed east, through the mountains toward Berenice and the Red Sea. Those tribes were quiet, even when Osman Digna threatened Suakin."

Dawson offered no comfort, tugging nervously at his moustache. "Three weeks overdue."

Lieutenant Calder tapped ash from his pipe and leaned forward, pressing the tobacco down with his thumb. "I wonder. Perhaps a trap was set for Durrance. I didn't mention it before because I hadn't suspected it, but now... you recall the night before he departed?"

"Yes," Dawson shifted closer. Calder was the only man in Wadi Halfa truly intimate with Durrance. Despite the rank gap, they were similar in age; Calder, fresh from England with an ardent thirst for experience, had found a reticent but generous friend in Durrance.

"I remember that night," Walters interjected. "Durrance dined at the mess and left early to prepare."

"His preparations were complete," Calder corrected. "He didn't go to his quarters. He walked down to Tewfikieh."

Tewfikieh was a frontier town separated from Halfa by a mile of riverbank. It was a chaotic mix of Greek stores, dirty cafes, native cookshops, and tobacconists, where the dialects clashed as sharply as the cultures. Yet, despite the noise, there was a strange, brooding silence underlying the clamor—the silence of the desert and the East.

"Durrance arrived in Tewfikieh at ten," Calder recounted. "I checked his quarters at eleven; he wasn't there. He was due to start east at four, and I needed to speak to him regarding some business details. I waited. He returned about fifteen minutes later, speaking rapidly and restlessly. He seemed deeply agitated, oscillating between anger and a forced laughter. He barely listened to me, answering randomly, before bundling me out. He told his servant to sleep, saying he would admit his own visitor personally."

"And who was the visitor?" Dawson asked.

"I don't know," Calder admitted. "All I know is that when the servant woke him at four, Durrance was still sitting on the verandah, waiting. The visitor never came."

"No message left?"

"None. I was awake before he left. I thought he was worried, intending to explain. Even as he mounted his camel, he looked back at me, hesitating, before striking the animal's flank and riding off into the desert, head bowed. I wonder if he rode into a trap. Who was this secret visitor? What business brought them to Wadi Halfa? Important, troubling business—evidently. And he didn't meet them. Was the whole thing a lure?"

Silence fell again until Walters broke it with his characteristic cheerfulness. "I don't think Durrance has got scuppered."

"I know what I'll do," Dawson declared. "Tomorrow, I dispatch a strong search party."

The next morning, over breakfast on the verandah, Dawson outlined his plan. Walters, despite his earlier optimism, had clearly pondered Calder's tale. Leaning across the table, he asked, "Did you never inquire who Durrance spoke to in Tewfikieh that night?"

"I did," Calder replied, turning from the Nile toward the glass doors of the mess-room. "It puzzles me. He spoke to only one person, and that person couldn't possibly be the visitor he awaited. Durrance stopped at a café where wandering musicians played for their nightly lodging. A Greek musician came out, took his hat, and they conversed briefly. Durrance tossed a sovereign into the hat."

As Calder spoke, a look of sudden recognition flashed across his face. He gripped the table edge, leaning forward as if to leap up, then froze. Bewilderment replaced recognition. Glancing around, he saw Colonel Dawson pouring cocoa and Walters staring at his plate; no one else had noticed his sudden tension. Calmer now, he stared ahead, continuing, "But I heard nothing else. He seemed to stroll through the village and return to Halfa."

"That offers little aid," Walters noted.

"And that is all you know?" Dawson pressed.

"No," Calder said slowly. "I know, for instance, that the man we are discussing is staring straight at me."

Every eye turned toward the mess-room entrance.

"Durrance!" Dawson shouted, rising instantly.

"When did you arrive?" Walters asked.

Durrance stood in the doorway, dust powdering his clothes, his skin burned to the color of red brick. He listened intently to the voices within. Notably, Calder, his friend, did not rise or greet him. Instead, he watched with curiosity and confusion. As Durrance descended the steps onto the verandah, a look of troubled comprehension settled over his features.

"We expected you three weeks ago," Dawson said, pulling a chair for him.

"The delay was unavoidable," Durrance replied, taking the seat.

"Does my story explain it?" Calder asked.

"Not at all. It was the Greek musician I expected that night," Durrance laughed softly. "Curiosity drove me to ask why such ill luck had cast him out to play the zither for a night's lodging in a Tewfikieh café. That was all," he added, his voice dropping. "Yes, that was all."

"Meanwhile, you're neglecting your breakfast," Dawson said, standing up. "What will you have?"

Calder leaned slightly forward, eyes fixed on Durrance. Durrance scanned the table, then summoned the waiter. "Moussa, get me something cold," he ordered. As the waiter retreated, Calder nodded with a faint smile, recognizing a cleverly surmounted difficulty.

"There is tea, cocoa, and coffee," Calder offered.

"Thank you," Durrance said. "But I think I'll have a brandy-and-soda." Another nod from Calder confirmed his understanding.

Durrance ate and drank, recounting his journey. He had ventured further east than planned, finding the Ababdeh Arabs peaceful among their mountains. Though they acknowledged no Egyptian allegiance, they paid no tribute to Mohammed Achmet either. The weather had been fine, ibex and antelope abundant. On the surface, Durrance seemed content. But Calder watched him, believing not a single word. The others attended to their duties, leaving Calder to wait. Durrance lingered endlessly, asking questions about the last eight weeks and boasting enthusiastically of his travels.

Finally, he stopped abruptly, looking suspiciously at his companion. "You are taking life too easily this morning."

"I haven't eight weeks' backlog of letters to clear, as you have, Colonel," Calder laughed. He saw Durrance's face darken and his brow furrow.

"True," Durrance muttered after a pause. "I forgot my letters." Rising, he climbed the steps and entered the mess-room.

Immediately, Calder sprang up, following Durrance's gaze. Durrance approached a nail near the glass doors, retrieved the key to his office, and exited through the far door. Leaving it ajar, Calder watched him walk down the path, unlatch the gate, and cross the sandy yard toward his office. Once Durrance vanished inside, Calder collapsed into his chair, elbows on the table, face in his hands.
