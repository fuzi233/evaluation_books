The next morning, the boys and I set off with the cart loaded with bundles of bamboo, heading toward our fruit tree avenue. We left the buffalo behind—his services weren’t needed just yet, and I wanted his nose wound to heal completely before putting him back to work.

We hadn’t arrived a moment too soon. Many of the young trees that had looked weak earlier were now fully toppled over, others bent dangerously, and only a few still stood upright. We quickly lifted the fallen ones, dug deep around their roots, and propped them up with sturdy bamboo stakes, securing each firmly with strong, broad fibres.

“Papa,” Franz said as he handed me fibres one by one, “are these trees wild or tame?”

“Oh, they’re wild—ferocious ones!” Jack laughed. “We’re tying them up so they don’t run away. Soon we’ll untie them, and they’ll trot after us, giving us fruit wherever we go. We’ll tame them like the buffalo—put rings in their noses!”

“That’s not true,” Franz replied seriously. “Wild trees grow in forests, like crab-apples. Tame ones grow in gardens, like pears and peaches at home. Which are these, Papa?”

“They’re not wild,” I answered. “They’re cultivated—what you’d call tame. No European tree bears good fruit unless it’s grafted.”

I saw confusion flicker across Franz’s face at the word *grafted*, so I explained.

“Grafting means taking a small branch—or slip—from one tree and inserting it into a knot or hole in another tree. That slip then grows and produces fruit from its own kind, not the original tree. For example, if you have a sour crab apple tree but want sweet apples, you take a slip from a ribston pippin tree, insert it into the crab tree, and in a year or two, you’ll have branches full of delicious apples.”

“But where did those good slips come from?” Ernest asked. “If no good fruit grows without grafting?”

“From foreign lands,” I said. “In colder climates like ours, trees need grafting to produce good fruit. But in warmer regions—like parts of Africa, Asia, or South America—many fruit trees grow naturally, bearing sweet, healthy fruit without any care at all. In England, Germany, and even France, those same trees require expert gardening just to survive.”

“Like when the Romans came to Britain,” Fritz added. “They found only crab apples, nut bushes, and brambles. But by grafting, they grew fine apples, hazelnuts, and raspberries. It was the same in Switzerland—all our fruit trees were brought from elsewhere.”

“Even cherries?” asked Jack. “Can’t we call cherries Swiss? I always thought they only grew here.”

“I’m afraid not,” I said. “Cherries come from Cerasus, a region in ancient Asia Minor. A Roman general named Lucullus brought them to Europe about seventy years before Christ. Hazelnuts also came from Pontus. Walnuts originated in Persia. As for grapes—they’re among the oldest cultivated plants. Remember Noah planting vines? Grapes have been moved from place to place and now grow in most civilized parts of the world.”

As we crossed Jackal River and entered our plantation at Tentholm, Fritz asked, “Do you think all these trees will grow? We’ve got lemons, pomegranates, pistachios, and mulberries.”

“I’m confident they will,” I replied. “We’re clearly in the tropics, where such trees thrive.”

“These pines come from France, Spain, and Italy; olives from Armenia and Palestine; figs originally from Chios; peaches and apricots from Persia; plums from Damascus; and pears from Greece. Our land may not have blessed us with natural fruit, but it gave us wisdom and skill—enough to bring these trees from other lands and grow them well.”

We worked and talked until every tree was secured with a bamboo support. Then, hungry as hunters after a long chase, we returned to Falconhurst.

My wife nearly gasped at how eagerly we devoured the corned beef and palm cabbage she’d prepared. But soon enough, our appetites were satisfied, and we sat back, digesting our meal and discussing our plans.

“I wish you could find another way to get to the nest,” my wife said. “It’s perfect, really—it’s wonderful. But climbing that ladder every time is exhausting. Could you make steps instead?”

I thought carefully. “Stairs outside would be impossible, but inside the trunk… perhaps. I’ve suspected this tree might be hollow or partly so. You told me the other day bees were coming out of a hole in it?”

“Oh yes!” little Franz exclaimed. “I went to look, and one flew right into my face and stung me! I almost cried—but I didn’t.”

“Brave boy,” I said. “Now, if the trunk is big enough to hold a swarm of bees, it might be hollow all the way up. Like a willow back home—it can stay healthy even if the core is rotten.”

Jack, ever practical, jumped up to test my theory. The others followed, scrambling up like squirrels, peering into the hole and tapping the wood to hear how far the cavity extended.

They forgot who lived there—until the bees, disturbed by the noise, erupted with a furious buzz and attacked them. They swarmed around the boys, stinging hands, faces, necks, settling in hair, chasing them screaming to me for help.

We struggled to clear the angry insects and tend to the wounded. Jack, who’d reached the hole first, suffered the worst—his face swollen grotesquely. Only cold earth applied constantly eased the pain. They all wanted to attack the bees immediately, but their pain made them too weak to help.

While they recovered, I began preparing. I took a large calabash gourd and turned it into a beehive. I flattened the bottom half, cut an arched doorway, and added a straw roof to protect it from rain and sun. The hive was ready.

But the bees were still furious, so I waited till dark. When all had returned to the tree, Fritz and I sealed every hole with wet clay so the bees couldn’t escape the next morning.

At dawn, we started. I inserted a hollow cane through the clay into the tree and smoked tobacco down it, using a pipe. The buzzing inside became deafening—the bees confused, frightened.

After finishing the first pipe, I blocked the end with my thumb and passed it to Fritz to refill. I smoked again. The sound softened, then faded into silence. The bees were stunned.

“Now, Fritz,” I said. “Quick—hammer and chisel. Stand beside me.”

He was ready. Together, we cut a small door near the hole, leaving it attached at one corner so we could remove it fast. After one last burst of smoke, we opened it.

Carefully, we removed the bees—clinging in clusters—and placed them in the new hive. Then I scraped every bit of wax and honey from the tree and stored it in a cask.

But I worried: would they return to the old hive when they woke? To prevent that, I lit a board of tobacco inside the trunk, letting the fumes fill the space.

It worked. When the bees regained consciousness, they flew back to the tree—only to find it empty and smoky. Confused, they buzzed between the hive and the tree, finally choosing the new home. By evening, they were calm. We opened the cask.

We separated the honey from the comb and poured it into jars. The rest we boiled in water, strained through a canvas bag under pressure, and collected pure honey. The leftover wax I saved—for candles.

That night, exhausted, we slept.

The next day, we began transforming the tree’s interior. At the base, we carved an opening just wide enough for the door from the captain’s cabin—hinges and all, ready to hang.

We cleared out the rotten wood from the trunk’s center. It took time, but eventually, standing below, we could see straight up the smooth, funnel-like shaft to the sky above.

Now came the staircase. First, we planted a sturdy sapling in the middle as a central pillar. We cut notches into it and into the tree wall to support the steps. The steps themselves were made from wrecked ship planks, nailed securely in place.

We built upward, adding windows along the way for light and air, until we reached the top of the central pole. Then we added a second pole to reach the nest floor, building more stairs around it.

For safety, we ran handrails on both sides—one around the central pillar, the other following the curve of the trunk.

This task took a full month. By the end, we were used to having a major project underway and began wondering what to tackle next.

But we hadn’t ignored daily duties. The goats and sheep had given birth—new lambs and kids bouncing around. To keep them close, we tied small bells around their necks, salvaged from the wreck. The jingle would help us track them.

Juno, our dog, had a litter of puppies. Though the children begged to keep them all, I allowed only two. The rest vanished—just as puppies often do. To comfort her, Jack placed his jackal pup beside the remaining puppies. To everyone’s surprise, the jackal accepted them and cared for them like his own.

Our other animals thrived. The buffalo, once stubborn, was now tamed—strong and reliable. He carried loads and served as a riding beast for the boys. We guided him with a bar through his healed nose, like a horse’s bit. I trained him slowly—first with a hide belt and weights, then with Master Knips riding him, and finally with Fritz himself.

The eagle was also being trained. Fritz hunted small birds daily and placed them near the buffalo or goat, teaching the bird to swoop and catch prey. Soon, the eagle obeyed his whistle and could strike flying birds with precision. We kept him far from the poultry yard—no risk of him attacking our chickens.

Master Knips, the monkey, wasn’t idle either. Ernest, with Jack’s help, made a basket of rushes, strapped it to the monkey’s back, and taught him to climb coconut palms and retrieve fruit.

Jack’s jackal, Fangs, was less cooperative. He caught animals but ate them instantly—leaving only scraps. Still, Jack refused to give up. He believed Fangs could be trained, and he would train him.

These tasks kept us busy while we built the stairs.

I also worked on improving our candles. The first batch had been exciting, but the light was dim and the wicks—made from cotton neckbands—looked rough. My wife urged me to find a better alternative.

I decided to use bamboo moulds. At first, I planned to pour wax in and pull the candle out, but that failed. So I split the moulds lengthwise, greased them, and filled them with wax while tightly bound. Once cooled, the candles came out cleanly.

Next came the wick. My wife refused to let us use our cloth bands. Instead, I used thin strips of flammable wood from an Antillean tree, placing them in the mould centers. She made wicks from karata tree fibers, claiming hers would win.

We tested both. We melted a mix of beeswax and candleberry wax, poured it into the moulds—half with wooden wicks, half with karata—set them in cold water, and let them cool.

When night fell, we lit them. The verdict? Neither matched the cotton wicks. But mine burned bright and steady, while hers flickered weakly—almost useless.

Then I turned shoemaker. I wanted waterproof boots. I filled socks with sand, coated them with clay, dried them in the sun, and layered caoutchouc over them. After drying, I broke out the clay, nailed buffalo-hide soles to the boots, and coated them again. The result? Comfortable, durable, weatherproof boots.

Everyone wanted a pair. Soon, the whole family wore them.

One issue remained: no spring near Falconhurst. The boys had to fetch water daily from the stream—a chore. So we built a dam upstream to raise the water level. From the reservoir, we laid pipes leading to a turtle shell near our home. Excess water drained through the hole Fritz had made with his harpoon.

It was a great convenience. We celebrated by washing a whole sack of potatoes in the trough.

Days passed, each bringing new work, each ending with accomplishment. We had no time to waste or mourn our isolation.

One morning, as we finished the spiral staircase, a terrifying roar echoed through the forest. It was unlike any animal I knew. Jack guessed lion. Fritz thought gorilla. Ernest said hyena—possibly right.

“Whatever it is,” I said, “we must prepare. Up to the nest—quick! I’ll secure the door.”

I armed the dogs, sent them to guard the animals, closed the door, and joined my family. Guns were loaded. Eyes watched.

The sound drew closer—then stopped. Nothing was seen. I decided to investigate. Fritz and I crept down quietly, guns cocked, moving silently through the trees.

Suddenly, the roar sounded again—close by. Fritz raised his gun—then dropped it, bursting into laughter.

There was no mistaking it: *hee-haw, hee-haw!* Our donkey was braying merrily—followed by another animal. Slender and graceful, it looked like a horse, but unmistakably a wild ass—an onager.

“Fritz,” I whispered, “that’s an onager. Go back to Falconhurst—quietly—and bring me a rope.”

While he went, I split a bamboo into pincers—useful if I got close. Fritz returned with rope, oats, and salt.

We tied one end of the rope to a tree, made a running noose at the other. Watching silently, Fritz rose, holding the noose in one hand, oats in the other.

The donkey approached, munching happily. The onager hesitated—then, seeing no danger, stepped forward, sniffing the food.

In a flash, the noose snapped around her neck. She leapt back, the rope tightening—she fell, half-strangled.

I rushed forward, loosened the rope, replaced it with a halter, and secured her with cords between two trees. I left her to recover.

Everyone hurried up to see. She rose, eyes blazing, kicking wildly, snorting in rage. But the cords held. After struggling, she collapsed, exhausted.

I approached calmly. She let me lead her to the base of our tree—our temporary stable—where I tied her beside the donkey, preventing both from wandering.

She was beautiful—wild, fierce, but now under our care. And so, another creature joined our island family.
