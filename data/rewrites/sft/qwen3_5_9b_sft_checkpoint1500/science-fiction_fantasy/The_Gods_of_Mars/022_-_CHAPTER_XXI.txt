Yersted's intelligence had confirmed what I had feared most: there was no time to lose. The assault by Tars Tarkas's forces was imminent, scheduled for the break of dawn. Yet, if I could slip into the Temple of Issus in secret before the battle began, I was certain I could breach its defenses and secure Princess Dejah Thoris. Behind me, I knew, stood a legion of warriors ready to hold the line.

Carthoris and the others arrived swiftly, and immediately we began ferrying our troops through the submerged passageways. Our goal was to emerge from the submarine pool at the temple end of the watery tunnel and converge upon the pits of Issus. It required numerous trips, but eventually, all five thousand of us—the finest fighting stock of the Red Men—stood assembled once more at the threshold of our final objective.

Since Carthoris alone knew the labyrinthine secrets of the tunnels, we could not split our forces to strike simultaneously from multiple angles. Instead, he led us in a single, relentless column toward a point near the temple's center.

Just as we prepared to leave the pool and enter the corridor, an officer drew my attention to the water surrounding the submarine. Initially, the agitation suggested a large vessel surfacing beneath us in pursuit. However, the truth soon revealed itself: the water level was rising steadily, inexorably, threatening to overflow the pool's banks and submerge the chamber floor.

For a fleeting moment, the gravity of the situation escaped me. It was Carthoris who grasped the catastrophe—the sabotage of Omean's pumps meant we would be trapped like rats in a drowning trap unless we reached the upper pits before the waters breached them.

"Haste!" he roared. "Delay is death! The pumps are cut off. If we do not reach the upper levels now, we perish. Move!"

"Lead on, Carthoris," I commanded. "Follow me."

The youth leaped into the corridor, and the soldiers formed a double-column behind him, marching in disciplined ranks. Each company entered only upon the signal of its dwarf-captain. Before the final unit cleared the chamber, the water had risen to ankle-deep, and the men's nerves were visibly frayed. Unaccustomed to vast bodies of moving water, the Martians instinctively recoiled from the formidable depths, though their discipline held firm as the water swirled and eddied around their legs.

I was the last to exit the submarine chamber, wading through water that rose to my knees. The corridor, sharing the same floor level, was equally flooded. Our progress was swift given the narrow confines, but it could not keep pace with the pursuing tide. As the passage narrowed toward the dome's apex, the water's rise accelerated in inverse proportion to the shrinking space, swelling with terrifying speed.

I watched the column from the rear, convinced that long before the last men could reach the safe upper pits, the flood would overwhelm us in a crushing deluge, claiming half our expedition. Panic began to grip the men ahead of me. Without intervention, they would stampede blindly into one another, trampling hundreds in a chaotic rout.

"I will find a way!" I shouted, raising my voice above the roar of the water. "Call back the last twenty-five units! This is the path to escape! Turn and follow me!"

Thirty dwar obeyed, wheeling their men back into the teeth of the rising torrent. As the first dwarf passed, I grabbed his arm, whispering urgently, "Listen closely for my command. Under no circumstances leave the pits for the temple proper until I am with you. Remember what I said: I would rather die before reaching you."

He saluted and vanished into the current. Thousands of men filed past me, rushing into the diverging corridor I had spotted—a steep upward slope promising salvation. The water surged to my chest, knocking men down, sweeping others away. I struggled to keep the terrified troops from chaos, holding back the stampede and dragging drowning souls back to safety. When the dwarf of the tenth unit, the valiant Gur Tus, stood beside me, we formed a human barrier, keeping the frightened ranks in semblance of order. Soon after, Djor Kantos joined us, ensuring that not a single soul perished during the transition to the branch passage.

By the time the last unit emerged, the waters lapped at our necks. Hand in hand, we stood our ground until the final man had climbed the steep grade to relative safety. Within a hundred yards, we ascended above the floodline.

Yet, just as I believed the peril of drowning had passed, disaster struck anew. A cry of "Fire!" echoed from ahead, followed by screams of terror and the frantic shouts of officers directing men away from a fresh menace.

"They've burned the pits ahead!" someone screamed. "Flames surround us in front, floods behind!"

"We're suffocating!"

A wall of dense, choking smoke swept backward from the fires, blinding us and driving us into a staggered retreat. There was no choice but to flee again. Fire and smoke were a thousandfold worse than the water, so I seized the first gallery leading upward and away from the smoldering inferno.

Two thousand men rushed through the new passage, but I hesitated. Was anyone left behind? Driven by a desperate need to ensure no soul died alone in the dark, I sprinted up the gallery toward the source of the flames, which glowed with a dull, malevolent heat.

The air was stifling, heavy with toxic fumes. Finally, I reached a vantage point where the fire illuminated the corridor. There was no soldier of Helium remaining between me and the blaze. Beyond that seething hell of chemicals, no one could survive. Satisfied in my grim duty, I turned to return to the corridor where my men had fled.

Horror froze me in place. The exit was barred. Across the mouth of the corridor stood a massive steel grating, lowered deliberately from above to seal my retreat.

The enemy knew. Of course they did. After the fleet's attack the previous day, after the deliberate failure of the pumps, and now this calculated chemical combustion—it was all a masterstroke of design. Invisible eyes watched us at every turn. If I were forced to fight foes who never revealed themselves, how could I save Dejah Thoris? A thousand times I cursed myself for falling into this trap, realizing now that a direct assault from the valley might have been safer.

The smoke pushed me relentlessly back toward the corridor's depths, where the water roared through the darkness. The last torch had gone out with the fleeing troops, and unlike the lower levels, this corridor lacked the comforting radiance of phosphorescent rock. I was close to the upper pits, beneath the temple, but the air grew thick, the smoke suffocating.

I felt the cold water lap at my feet. The smoke was a physical weight, pressing down. My suffering was acute. In this nightmare, death offered an easy release. So, I walked down the corridor, surrendering to the inevitable, until the waters of Omean closed over me. I swam on into utter blackness, waiting for the ceiling to crush me.

Self-preservation is a powerful instinct, even when reason dictates doom. I swam slowly, waiting for the corridor to capsize above me.

Instead, I struck a blank wall. Had I miscalculated? I reached out. No, I had returned to the main corridor, and miraculously, a sliver of breathing space remained between the water's surface and the rocky ceiling. Turning upstream, in the direction Carthoris had taken hours ago, I swam on. Hope, fragile as glass, began to flutter in my chest. I was nearing the point where the water would thin, where I could stand again.

Then, the shock. My head struck the rocks above. The worst had happened. I had reached a rare geological dip, a section of the tunnel plunging deep below the surface before rising again. How far did it stay submerged? Who knew?

A single, forlorn hope remained. I filled my lungs, diving into the inky, freezing void. I swam along the submerged gallery, rising repeatedly only to find the ceiling closing in, denying me air. My strength ebbed, my lungs burning. Retreating was impossible; death stared me in the face, his icy breath chilling my brow.

With a final, frantic surge of failing strength, I broke the surface. My tortured lungs gasped, expecting nothing but the strangling water, but instead, the revivifying breath of life surged into me. I was saved.

A few more strokes brought me to solid footing. Above the water, I raced down the corridor, seeking the first door to Issus. If I could not reclaim Dejah Thoris, I vowed to exact vengeance upon her murderer, whatever life I chose next.

Sooner than expected, I found what looked like an exit to the temple above. It branched off to the right, likely leading to other chambers. It didn't matter where it led. I climbed the short, steep incline and threw open the door.

The portal groaned inward. Before it could slam shut, I sprang inside. The room was brilliantly lit, though dawn had not yet broken. Its sole occupant lay prone on a low couch, seemingly asleep. The sumptuous furniture and rich hangings marked this as the private quarters of a priestess, perhaps of Issus herself.
