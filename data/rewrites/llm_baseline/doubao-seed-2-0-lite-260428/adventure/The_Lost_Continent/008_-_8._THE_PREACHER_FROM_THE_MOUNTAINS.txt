I’d not had a full stretch of unbroken rest in three days, so I’ll own I slept heavy through most of that long daylight stretch, Nais huddled quiet at the temple entrance keeping watch. Night fell thick, and we stayed holed up in the abandoned, dust-choked shrine. Our plan was simple: hunker down until the whole rebel camp slipped into deep sleep, then slip for the shore where my waiting boat lay, no soul the wiser.

The night turned cold and damp, a fine drizzle seeping through every crack in the rough-hewn temple walls. Peering out the narrow stone slits, we spotted the camp’s dozens of well-tended fires glowing orange, ragged folk wrapped in tattered animal skins huddled shoulder to shoulder for warmth, steam curling thick off their rain-soaked clothes. Wet, miserable people are notoriously short-tempered, sharp-eyed, and quick to snap at the smallest irritation. Nais frowned as she watched the drizzle lash down on the huddled crowds.
“This was meant to be the perfect night,” she muttered. “I could have slipped you back to the city before anyone even realized we were gone. But in this chill, no one sleeps deep. We’ll have to wait a few more hours till the cold drugs them into real unconsciousness.”

So we sat side by side on the cold stone pavement, a floor no worshipper’s knee had touched in centuries, barely speaking a single word out loud. Good companionship never needed constant chatter anyway.
As the small hours dragged on, the night’s quiet shattered. From the distance, someone blew on a giant conch shell, belting out a harsh, raucous non-stop note that drew nearer by the second. The camp’s night owls started yelling insults at the noisemaker for disturbing their rest. Soon the horn fell silent, and we stood to peek out the slits: half the camp had roused from their uneasy rest, clustering in a tight throng around a man who stood preaching from the stone pedestal of a long-broken war engine.
We didn’t care what he was yelling about, at first. Nais whispered that if every last awake campfolk trundled off to listen to this stranger, we could slip out the temple’s far side and head for the sea completely unspotted.

But we hadn’t counted on the camp’s nosiest busybodies. A dozen pairs of feet splashed through the mud right up to the temple door, yelling loud for Nais to come join the crowd. She was famed for her sharp, persuasive tongue, they said, they needed her to stand at their side and counter the stranger’s divisive nonsense before he split their whole rebel band apart.
I thought we’d be forced to brawl our way out then and there, but Nais squeezed my wrist tight, whispering fast: “Come with me. Stay well back from the firelight. Don’t say a word.”
“How do I explain what I’m doing hiding out here?” I hissed.
“No one’s going to ask for an explanation,” she said, a bitter little laugh tugging at her mouth. “They’ll just assume you’re my lover. That’s the fashion out here, no one bats an eye. Though why the gods couldn’t make you grow one of those silly curled beards all these men wear, I’ll never know—you’d have been halfway to the sea safe two hours ago.”
“A smooth chin suits me better,” I said.
“It suits me too,” she murmured so quiet I almost missed it, as she heaved the heavy stone door ajar. “Your chin.”
The ragged crowd outside barely glanced at me, tossed off a few crude jests I ached to run through with my sword, then trundled off ahead, glancing back only to make sure we followed. The whole camp was on the move now, dark silhouettes against the drizzling sky, rain spattering hissing on the fire pits. There wasn’t a single sleeper left, everyone was heading to hear this mountain preacher, and if we’d tried to duck off course, we’d have been spotted instantly.

We pushed through the throng until we were close enough to make out the shouting. “Who are your chiefs?” the preacher boomed at the end of a fiery harangue, and the whole crowd roared back in a deafening wave: “We have no chiefs! We’re done with all rulers! We’re all equal! Kill us with your magic if you want, but you won’t rule us. Not the old priests, not Phorenice, not anyone!”
The press of bodies shoved us right up near the preacher’s platform. He was ancient, wild white hair tumbling down his shoulders, a long unkempt white beard, nothing but a scrap of rag around his hips, his skin glistening wet in the firelight. In his hand, he held the Sun Symbol—our sacred priestly wand, the tip burning with unquenchable holy fire, glowing bright like a fallen star. No mortal could forge that. The crowd knew instantly he was one of the old priest clan, the line that had ruled Atlantis since time out of mind. “You can kill us with your magic,” they screamed, “we don’t care. We’re done with rulers, we’ve suffered too much.”

Then my blood ran cold. That old man was Zaemon. Nais’s father. The very man I’d sat next to at Phorenice’s banquet the day before. He could denounce me as Deucalion right here, right now, and every single rebel in this crowd that had already burned an entire navy trying to kill me would tear me apart. Nais twined her fingers in mine, a quiet goodbye squeeze, and I squeezed back. I was sorry I wouldn’t get to see more of her, but a grim old fighter like me couldn’t help already mentally counting how many of these fools I’d cut down before they pulled me down— that final glorious last battle I’d daydreamed of for years.

But Zaemon didn’t see me yet, he was too wrapped up in his sermon. He told them the old, sacred story: how the priest clan had ruled justly for millennia, fended off invaders, held back hordes of wild beasts, warned of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, built Atlantis into the greatest, most prosperous land on earth. Until the people got soft, he said, forgot the gods, and the gods sent Phorenice as a scourge to punish them. But the old order would be restored, he promised, if they submitted, humbled themselves before the gods.
A hot young rebel jumped up onto the platform next to him, yelling that they’d never take a ruler back, even if he used magic to kill him on the spot. Zaemon just gave him a sharp, stern look, and the kid went completely mute, face white with terror, stumbling back down into the crowd. The mob roared for a public miracle, furious. Zaemon roared back that he would never cheapen the holy mysteries to put on a mummer’s show for an ungrateful crowd: the choice was theirs, humble themselves and earn the gods’ pardon, or Phorenice would grind them all to dust before the gods struck her down.

The crowd surged restive around us, weapons raised. A grizzled rebel right next to me spun around, squinting hard at my bare, uncurled chin. “Hey, friend, no beard, no scar? What’s your story skulking here in the dark?”
I joked back that with all their loud talk of freedom, he ought to let me wear my chin any way I liked. He didn’t buy it, drew breath to yell an alarm. So I grabbed him quick, twisted his neck with that old fighter’s quiet, practiced trick, heard the faint crack, and let him slump against the packed bodies of the crowd so he looked like he’d just fainted from the press. No one noticed. I didn’t want my last fight starting here, no room to swing a sword, no space to carve out a battle ring.

Zaemon led the whole throng in the ancient sacred ritual then, the old responses rolling out through the rain, their voices booming in wild unison:
“WHO STILLED THE BURNING OF THE MOUNTAINS, AND MADE COOL PLACES ON THE EARTH FOR US TO LIVE!--PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS.
“WHO GAVE US MASTERY OVER THE LESSER BEASTS AND SKILL OF TEN TIMES TO PREVAIL!--PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS.”
It was eerie, these wild, irreligious rebels chanting prayers they’d abandoned for years, but I knew their enthusiasm would burn out fast. An hour from now they’d be cold and stubborn all over again.

Zaemon stepped down off the war engine pedestal, the glowing Sun Symbol held straight out before him. The crowd crushed back automatically, clearing a wide empty path for him, no one daring to block his way. And he was walking straight for me. My gut tightened. So this was how it went. He’d seen me at Phorenice’s feast, he was going to denounce me as her right-hand man, smite me with the Symbol—no glorious last battle, no pile of dead around me, no sword drawn. But I was a priest too. If the council on the Sacred Mountain had ordered my death, I’d take it, no fight. I just couldn’t help a little twinge of regret that I wouldn’t get to draw my sword one last time.

I stood straight, gave him the traditional sacred salutation between ranking priests. He returned it perfectly. The crowd fell back further, the Symbol’s unearthly holy light driving them away, leaving just the two of us in a big empty ring. Even Nais couldn’t get close, the mystery power of the Symbol pushing her back. The crowd roared instantly: “A SPY! ZAEMON GREETS HIM AS A PRIEST!”
Zaemon turned to them, nodded grim. “Aye. This is the Lord Deucalion.”
The roar that went up was deafening. They’d spent a whole navy trying to kill me a few days back, now they were frothing to tear me limb from limb. But the Symbol’s blazing light held them back, like they were staring at a river of molten lava. Zaemon spoke to me in the old sacred tongue no commoner understood. “Brother Deucalion—who do you serve? Deucalion, or Atlantis?”
I told him he already knew my whole record, every report the council had of all my years governing Yucatan, every good thing I’d done for the land. “We know your past is spotless,” he said. “But you’ve never faced a temptation like Phorenice. All other attempts to bring her down have failed. You’re our last hope, the strongest, most skilled man we have. You’re our champion. If you fail, we’ll have to open the Ark of the Mysteries, and unleash powers no mortal dares even name.”
I laughed bitter. “Wouldn’t you save a lot of trouble just throwing me to this mob right now?”
He scowled, told me to stop shirking my duty. I reminded him of the curse he’d yelled at Phorenice at the banquet, that her royal pyramid would be torn apart stone by stone. He shuddered, said he couldn’t see the full future, but before they unleashed the unnameable powers sealed in the Ark, I had to try. The council had arranged for me to marry Phorenice, he said. As her husband, not an obvious enemy, I could persuade her to see the futility of fighting the gods, talk her into submission before it was too late.

My chest ached. I’d barely thought of women all my busy, duty-bound life, but the last two days I’d fallen for Nais, hard. Marrying Phorenice would mean losing her forever. But I bowed my head, pressed his hand to my forehead, the traditional gesture of a priest accepting a sacred order. “It is an order. I obey.”

We turned to leave, the Symbol blazing a clear path through the howling mob. I didn’t get to say a word to Nais, just caught that one fiery, heartbroken glance from her eyes as we passed. No one dared touch us. When the crowd started to follow us out of curiosity and bloodlust, Zaemon whirled the Symbol behind us, a burst of searing holy light, and the whole crowd dropped to their knees, grovelling in the mud, and didn’t dare move another step.
The rain still fell, glinting off Zaemon’s thin, wet skin, and far off through the dark, the great Sacred Mountain loomed, its ring of eternal fires glowing bright on the crest. I sighed, thinking of the old quiet peaceful days I’d spent in its temple groves, knowing my new, terrible duty lay straight ahead.
