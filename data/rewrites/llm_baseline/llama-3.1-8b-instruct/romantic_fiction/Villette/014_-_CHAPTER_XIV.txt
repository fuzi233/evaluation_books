The Fête


As soon as Georgette's health improved, Madame sent her off to the countryside, and I felt a pang of loss. I had grown fond of the child, and her departure left me feeling poorer than before. But I shouldn't complain; our household was abuzz with vitality, and I could have easily found companionship among the other teachers. Still, I preferred solitude, finding solace in the quiet rhythms of my own thoughts.

Each of the instructors, in turn, attempted to form a closer bond with me, but I tested their sincerity. One proved to be a straightforward woman, though her thinking was narrow and her emotions stifled by self-absorption. Another, a Parisian, presented herself as refined, yet beneath her polished exterior lay a void of principles, devoid of genuine affection. Her fascination with gifts was almost comical, and her counterpart, a teacher lacking distinctiveness, shared this peculiarity, albeit in a more subtle manner. This latter individual, however, had another defining trait – an insatiable hunger for wealth. At the mere sight of gold coins, her eyes would gleam with a peculiar intensity, a spectacle both captivating and unsettling. She would often take me on tours of her quarters, showcasing her secret stash, a trove of coarse, heavy coins worth around fifteen guineas. Her fixation on these savings bordered on an almost feral devotion, as if they represented the very essence of her existence.

In stark contrast, the Parisian instructor embodied excess and profligacy in her nature, though her actions remained largely contained. Her true character revealed itself to me only once, when a fleeting glimpse of her baser instincts briefly surfaced. It was a curious sight, like beholding a serpent's head poking out from the underbrush – an unexpected novelty that piqued my curiosity. Had she been bold enough to expose herself fully, I might have stood my ground, observing her with a detached air, analyzing the intricate patterns of her psyche. However, she retreated upon encountering a show of displeasure, leaving behind a trail of disappointment and disillusionment.

This Parisian was perpetually in debt, her salary being spent long before it was earned, on everything from fine clothes to perfumes and sweets. Her approach to life was that of a shallow epicure, devoid of passion, empathy, or intellectual curiosity.
