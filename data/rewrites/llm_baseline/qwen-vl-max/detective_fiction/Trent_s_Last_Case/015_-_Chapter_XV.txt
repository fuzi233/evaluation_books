A heavy oak desk stood by the window in a spacious room overlooking St. James’s Park from an elevated position. The space was large and tastefully furnished—evidence of refined taste—but carried the unmistakable air of a bachelor’s retreat, untouched by domestic warmth. John Marlowe unlocked the desk and pulled out a thick, sturdy envelope from the back compartment.

“I understand,” he said to Mr. Cupples, “that you’ve read this.”

“I read it for the first time two days ago,” replied Mr. Cupples, seated on a sofa, his face gentle as he scanned the room with quiet interest. “We’ve discussed it thoroughly.”

Marlowe turned to Trent. “Here is your manuscript,” he said, placing the envelope on the table. “I’ve gone over it three times. I don’t believe there’s another man who could have uncovered as much of the truth as you’ve laid bare here.”

Trent ignored the compliment. He sat beside the table, staring into the fire, his long legs crossed beneath him. “You mean,” he said, pulling the envelope closer, “that there’s still more to reveal. We’re ready to hear it whenever you are. I expect it will be a long story—and the longer, the better, so far as I’m concerned. I want to understand everything. What we’d both like, I think, is some background on Manderson—your relationship with him. From the beginning, it seemed to me that the dead man’s character had to be central to what happened.”

“You were right,” Marlowe replied grimly. He crossed the room and settled onto the corner of a tall, cushioned fender. “I’ll begin where you suggest.”

“I should tell you first,” Trent said, meeting his eyes directly, “that while I’m here listening, I haven’t yet abandoned the conclusions I’ve drawn in this report.” He tapped the envelope. “This is a defense you’re about to make—I assume you understand that?”

“Perfectly.” Marlowe remained calm, composed, a man transformed from the worn, nervous figure Trent had known at Marlstone eighteen months earlier. His frame was lean and strong, held with the precision of physical discipline. His brow was open, his blue eyes clear—though when he paused, gathering his thoughts, they still carried the same unsettling look Trent had noticed during their first encounter. Only the lines around his mouth betrayed tension, a sign he was aware of his peril and prepared to face it.

“Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal mind,” Marlowe began quietly. “Most of the wealthy Americans I met had risen through abnormal greed, relentless industry, overpowering drive, or sheer luck. None possessed exceptional intellects. Manderson shared those traits—he loved accumulating wealth, worked ceaselessly, wielded a dominant will, and enjoyed his share of fortune. But what made him unique was his mental power. In America, people might say his defining trait was ruthlessness—but many others would have done just as little for others if they’d been capable of plotting such schemes.

“I’m not saying Americans aren’t clever—they’re ten times sharper than we English, as a nation. But I never met anyone else who combined such foresight, memory, and intellectual stamina as Manderson did in his business dealings. They called him the ‘Napoleon of Wall Street’ often enough in the papers. But few understood how accurate the title was. He never forgot a fact that might serve him. He studied business data systematically—just as Napoleon once digested military intelligence—reviewing summaries prepared for him at regular intervals, always within reach. He could assess coal, wheat, railroads, or any market at a moment’s notice. And then he would devise plans bolder and more ingenious than anyone else. People learned not to expect the obvious from Manderson; what he actually did was almost always a surprise. That unpredictability was key to his success. When the Street knew the old man was out with his gun, it shook. Opponents often surrendered without a fight—like Colonel Crockett’s coon in the old tale. The scheme I’m about to describe would take most men weeks. Manderson could plot it, down to the last detail, while shaving.

“I used to wonder if his distant Indian blood—the line so faint it was nearly lost—might explain his cunning and cruelty. Strangely, no one but himself and I knew it existed. It came to light when he asked me to research his family history. In tracing it, I discovered he descended from the Iroquois chief Montour and his French wife—a woman of fierce reputation who ruled tribal politics in the wilderness two centuries ago. The Mandersons had been active in the fur trade along Pennsylvania’s frontier, and several had married Native women. Other strains may have flowed into Manderson’s bloodline through earlier or later unions—some ancestors were untraceable, and generations passed before civilization reached the region. My research led me to believe that American bloodlines contain a great deal of indigenous ancestry, widely dispersed. Newer families intermarried with older ones, and many took pride in their native heritage. But Manderson saw mixed blood as a disgrace—a belief, I suspect, intensified after the Civil War and the rise of racial tensions. He was stunned when I told him, and desperate to hide it. Naturally, I never revealed it while he lived—I don’t think he believed I would. But since then, I’ve wondered if that discovery marked a turning point in his attitude toward me. It happened about a year before his death.”

“Did Manderson,” Mr. Cupples asked suddenly, catching both men off guard, “have any clear religious beliefs?”

Marlowe hesitated. “None I ever heard of,” he said. “Worship or prayer were foreign to him, as far as I could tell. I never heard him mention religion. I doubt he truly sensed God, or could experience Him emotionally. But as a child, he’d received a strong moral upbringing rooted in faith. His private life, in conventional terms, was blameless. He was almost ascetic—except for smoking. I lived with him four years and never heard him utter a direct lie, even though he constantly practiced deception in other ways. Can you imagine someone who freely used every trick of the market to mislead others, yet refused to speak a falsehood—even on trivial matters? Manderson was like that. And he wasn’t alone. You might compare it to a soldier who is personally honest but stops at nothing to deceive the enemy. The rules of war allow it—and so, in their minds, do the rules of business. For them, it’s always wartime.”

“It’s a sad world,” Mr. Cupples observed.

“As you say,” Marlowe agreed. “Now, I was saying that one could trust Manderson’s word when he gave it plainly. The first time I ever heard him lie outright was the night he died—and hearing it, I believe, saved me from being hanged for his murder.”

Marlowe stared at the ceiling light. Trent shifted uneasily in his chair. “Before we get to that,” he said, “will you tell us exactly what your relationship with Manderson was during the years you worked for him?”

“We were on excellent terms throughout,” Marlowe replied. “Not friendship—he wasn’t the kind to form close bonds—but a solid, trusting relationship between employer and secretary. I joined him shortly after graduating from Oxford. I was meant to enter my father’s business, but he suggested I travel for a few years. So I took this post, which promised variety and experience. The year stretched into four before it ended. The job came through something I’d never have thought a qualification: chess.”

At the word, Trent clapped his hands together with a low exclamation. The others looked at him in surprise.

“Chess!” repeated Trent, rising and stepping toward Marlowe. “Do you know what struck me the first time we met? Your eyes, Mr. Marlowe. I couldn’t place them then—but now I know. They were the eyes of Nikolay Korchagin, the great Russian master, whom I once shared a railway carriage with for two days. I thought I’d never forget the look of a chess player’s gaze after that—but I couldn’t name it when I saw it in you. I beg your pardon,” he added abruptly, resuming his reserved posture.

“I’ve played since childhood,” Marlowe said simply. “With good players. It’s hereditary, if you can call it a gift. At Oxford, I was nearly as strong as anyone. I devoted most of my mind to chess, the O.U.D.S., and general mischief. Oxford, as you probably know, offers endless distractions—and authorities encourage them. Near the end of my final term, Dr. Munro of Queen’s—whom I’d never beaten—summoned me. He said I played a decent game. I thanked him. Then he asked, ‘They say you hunt too.’ I said, ‘Now and then.’ He asked, ‘Is there anything else you can do?’ I said, ‘Not really,’ not liking the tone—he had a way of making people defensive. He grunted, then mentioned inquiries were underway for a wealthy American businessman seeking an English secretary. Manderson was the name. He’d never heard it—understandable, since he never read newspapers and hadn’t slept outside the college in thirty years. If I could improve my spelling—‘rub up your spelling,’ he put it—I might have a chance. Chess, riding, and an Oxford education were the only essentials.”

“And so I became Manderson’s secretary. For a long time, I enjoyed it. Serving an energetic American tycoon in his prime meant few dull moments. It also gave me independence. Around that time, my father faced serious financial setbacks, and I was glad to avoid relying on him. After the first year, Manderson doubled my salary. ‘It’s big money,’ he said, ‘but I guess I don’t lose.’ By then, I was doing far more than riding with him in the mornings or playing chess at night. I managed his houses, his Ohio farm, his Maine hunting lodge, his horses, his cars, his yacht. I became a walking railroad guide and a connoisseur of cigars. I was always learning.

“So now you understand my role in the final years of my time with Manderson. It was, overall, a satisfying life. Busy, varied, interesting—time for leisure, and money to spend. Once, I made a fool of myself over a girl. That was unpleasant—but it taught me to appreciate Mrs. Manderson.” He nodded respectfully toward Mr. Cupples. “She may choose to tell you about it. As for her husband, he never changed his stance toward me, despite the shift in his behavior in his final months. He treated me well—generously, though coldly—and I never felt he regretted our arrangement. That consistency made the revelation all the more shocking when, on the night of his death, I realized how deeply Manderson hated me.”

Trent and Mr. Cupples exchanged a glance.

“You never suspected he disliked you before?” Trent asked.

“And to what did you attribute this change?” Mr. Cupples added simultaneously.

“I never guessed until that night,” Marlowe answered. “How long it had existed, I don’t know. I can’t imagine why it was there. After his death, I considered it must have been madness—a delusion where he believed I was plotting against him, as lunatics sometimes do. Some insane conviction must have driven it. But who can grasp the depths of a madman’s mind? Can you picture a man condemning himself to death to deliver someone he hates to the gallows?”

Mr. Cupples stiffened. “So you’re saying Manderson caused his own death?”

Trent glanced at him impatiently and returned his focus to Marlowe, whose face had relaxed slightly in the act of speaking.

“I do,” Marlowe said firmly, meeting the questioner’s gaze. Mr. Cupples nodded slowly.

“Before we go further,” the old gentleman said, adopting a tone of academic detachment, “one might note that the state of mind you attribute to Manderson—”

“Let’s hear the story first,” Trent interrupted gently, laying a hand on Mr. Cupples’s arm. “You were telling us,” he continued, turning to Marlowe, “how things stood between you and Manderson. Now, will you tell us what happened that night?”

Marlowe flushed slightly at the subtle emphasis on “facts.” He straightened.

“Bunner and I dined with Mr. and Mrs. Manderson that Sunday evening,” he began, speaking carefully. “It was like any other dinner among the four of us. Manderson was quiet and dark, as we’d grown accustomed. We kept the conversation going. We left the table around nine. Mrs. Manderson went to the drawing-room, Bunner went to the hotel to visit an acquaintance. Manderson asked me to join him in the orchard behind the house, saying he wanted to talk. We walked the path, out of earshot. As he smoked, he spoke in his usual cool, deliberate manner. He’d never seemed more rational—or more amiable. He said he needed me to do him an important service. A major operation was underway. It was secret. Bunner knew nothing. The less I knew, the better. He wanted me to follow his orders exactly, without questioning.”

“This was typical of Manderson’s method,” Marlowe continued. “When he needed a tool, he’d say so. He’d used me in that way a dozen times. I assured him I could be trusted. ‘Right now?’ he asked. I said, of course.”

“He nodded. ‘There’s a man in England involved in this with me. He was supposed to leave tomorrow for Paris by the noon boat from Southampton to Havre. His name is George Harris—at least, that’s the alias he’s using. Do you remember that name?’ ‘Yes,’ I said. ‘Last week, when I went to London, you asked me to book a cabin under that name. I gave you the ticket.’ ‘Here it is,’ he said, pulling it from his pocket.”

“Then,” Marlowe continued, “he said—word for word, as best I recall—‘George Harris cannot leave England tomorrow. I need him here. I need Bunner here. But someone must go by that boat and carry certain papers to Paris. Otherwise, the whole plan collapses. Will you go?’ I said, ‘Certainly. I’m here to obey orders.’”

“He bit his cigar. ‘That’s good—but these aren’t ordinary orders. Not something you’d ask of a man in routine duty. Here’s the point: the deal I’m working on requires that neither I nor anyone connected to me appear publicly yet. That’s vital. But the people I’m opposing know your face as well as mine. If your identity is linked to crossing to Paris at this time—if it’s known you met certain people—then the game is over.’”

He threw away his cigar and looked at me.

“I didn’t like it, but I liked failing Manderson even less. I said lightly, ‘I suppose I’ll have to disguise myself. I’ll do my best.’ I mentioned I used to be good at makeup.”

“He nodded. ‘Good. I thought you wouldn’t let me down.’ Then he gave instructions. ‘Take the car now and head for Southampton—no train fits. You’ll drive all night. You should arrive by six a.m., barring accidents. But when you do, go straight to the Bedford Hotel and ask for George Harris. If he’s there, tell him you’re replacing him, and ask him to phone me immediately. It’s critical he knows as soon as possible. But if he’s not there, that means he got my wire today and didn’t come to Southampton. In that case, forget him. Just wait for the boat. Leave the car at a garage under a false name—mine mustn’t be used. Change your appearance—however you like, as long as it’s convincing. Travel as George Harris. Pretend to be anything you wish, but stay silent. Upon arrival, check into the Hotel St Petersbourg. You’ll receive a message addressed to George Harris—telling you where to deliver the wallet I’ll give you. The wallet is locked. Guard it closely. Have you got it all?’”

“I repeated it. I asked if I’d return from Paris after delivering the wallet. ‘As soon as you like,’ he said. ‘But whatever happens—don’t contact me at any stage. If you don’t get the message right away, wait—days, if necessary. No communication. Understand? Now get ready. I’ll drive with you partway. Hurry.’”

“That’s the substance of what he said,” Marlowe concluded. “I went to my room, changed clothes, packed a few essentials in a kit-bag. My mind was racing—not from the task itself, but from its suddenness. I remember telling you last time,” he said, turning to Trent, “that Manderson loved theatricality. He enjoyed mystery and drama. I told myself this was pure Manderson.”

“I hurried downstairs, retrieved the car from the garage behind the house. As I brought it round, I remembered something troubling—I had only a few shillings in my pocket.”

“For weeks, I’d been short on cash—living temporarily on borrowed money. While with Manderson, I’d been careless with finances. I was sociable, made many friends—some from a New York set with idle fortunes. Though I was well paid, I hadn’t indulged much. Until I started speculating—out of curiosity. An old story, especially on Wall Street. I thought it easy. I was lucky at first. I’d always be prudent. Then came the week I drowned. In one stretch, I lost everything—as Bunner put it when I confessed. I owed money. I’d learned my lesson. I went to Manderson, told him what I’d done. He listened with a grim smile, then advanced me a sum to cover my debts—his nearest approach to sympathy. ‘Don’t play the markets again,’ he said.”

“So on that Sunday night, Manderson knew I had no money. He knew Bunner knew it too. He may have known I’d borrowed extra from Bunner for pocket money, since my next paycheck would be small due to my advance. Keep that in mind.”

“I returned to the library and told him about the expense issue.”

“What followed gave me my first hint something was wrong. As soon as I mentioned ‘expenses,’ his hand instinctively moved to his left hip-pocket—where he always kept a case with about a hundred pounds in notes. This habit was so ingrained that I was startled when he suddenly stopped himself. Then, to my shock, he swore under his breath. I’d never heard him do that before—but Bunner had told me he’d recently shown irritation like this when alone. I wondered: Had he misplaced his note-case? But it didn’t matter—because the week before, when I’d gone to London, I’d withdrawn a thousand pounds from his bankers—all in small denominations, as he requested. I didn’t know why he needed so much cash, but I knew the packets were in his locked desk in the library—or had been earlier that day, when I saw him handling them.”
