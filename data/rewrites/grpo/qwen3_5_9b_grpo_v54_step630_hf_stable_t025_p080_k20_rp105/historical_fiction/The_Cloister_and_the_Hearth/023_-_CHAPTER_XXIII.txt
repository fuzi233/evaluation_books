Life and freedom, when unthreatened, pass unnoticed, taken for granted as the midday sun that blinds rather than warms. It is only when the shadow lengthens, when the light fractures into shards of topaz beneath the elm, that men truly perceive its value. Yet in that twilight, the light is dimmer, overshadowed by the encroaching gloom. It was this very contrast that ignited a fierce, trembling gratitude within Gerard and Margaret. Though their hearts still echoed the barking of hounds and the brutality of voices that had nearly claimed them, they moved now through a sanctuary of trees, bathed in a joy so profound it made their spirits glow.

Then, Gerard's gaze fell upon the dark stains blooming on Margaret's ankles.

"Martin! Martin, help! They have wounded her! The crossbow!" he cried, his voice cracking with panic.

"No, no!" Margaret laughed softly, a sound meant to soothe his terror. "I am unhurt, my love. Not a scratch."

"But what is this, then?" Gerard demanded, agitation seizing his throat. "In Heaven's name, explain!"

"Do not scold me!" she blushed, her eyes dropping.

"Have I ever scolded you?"

"Not once, dear Gerard." She paused, her expression shifting to one of desperate explanation. "Well, Martin told me the dogs followed the scent of blood. So, thinking if I could but offer them a little red upon my shoes, they might chase me instead of you... I scratched my arm with Martin's knife. Forgive me! Whose else could I use? Yours, Gerard? Ah, no. You forgive me?" she pleaded, her face a mask of loving submission.

"Let me see this wound," Gerard choked out, his composure fracturing. "There. I knew it. A scratch? I call it a gash—a deep, cruel, terrible cut." He shuddered, unable to look away from the crimson mark.

"She could have used her bodkin," the soldier muttered, his tone sharp with irritation. "A milksop, sickened by the sight of a mere drop of blood."

"No, no. I could stare upon a sea of blood, but not upon hers," Gerard insisted, his voice rising in anguish. "Oh, Margaret! How could you be so cruel?"

Margaret smiled, a radiant expression of ineffable love. "Foolish Gerard," she murmured, wrapping her guilty arm around his neck. "To make such a fuss over nothing." She pressed herself against him. "As if I would not give every drop of blood in my heart for you, let alone a few from my arm." Under the weight of his recent peril, tears streamed down her face, mingling with his own as they wept together for the pain they had inflicted upon one another.

"I must leave her," he sobbed, the reality crashing down. "We who love so deeply—one in Holland, one in Italy. Ah, me! Ah, me!"

Margaret wept anew, but her tears were silent, patient, born of an instinct that knew her words would only deepen his sorrow. To speak her thoughts would be to add to his grief, so she remained in quiet mourning.

Suddenly, they broke from the trees onto a beaten path. Martin halted, hanging his head.

"This is the bridle-road I spoke of," he said, pointing toward the distant inn. "And there lies the hostelry."

Gerard and Margaret exchanged a terrified glance.

"Come a step with me, Martin," Gerard whispered, pulling the soldier aside. Once they were hidden, he spoke in a broken voice. "Good Martin, watch over her for me! She is my wife, yet I leave her. Look, Martin! Here is gold—it was for my journey. I cannot ask her to take it; she would refuse. But you will take it for her, will you not? Oh, Heaven! Is this all I can offer? Money? But poverty is a curse. You will ensure she wants for nothing, dear Martin? The burgomaster's silver is enough for me."

"You are a good lad, Gerard," the soldier replied, his rough demeanor softening. "Neither want nor harm shall touch her. I care for her little finger more than for all the world; and were she nothing to me, even for your sake, I would be a father to her. Go with a stout heart, and may God be with you, going and returning." The rough warrior squeezed Gerard's hand, turning his head away with an emotion he rarely showed.

For a moment, he hesitated to return to the couple, but Gerard stopped him. "No, good Martin; pray, stay here behind this thicket. Turn your head away from us, while I—oh, Martin! Martin!"

Thus, Gerard escaped the witness of his agony in leaving the woman he loved, and Martin spared himself the sight of their despair. He saw nothing of the young pair kneeling before Heaven to renew the holy vows cruel men had interrupted. He saw nothing of them clinging together as one, then failing to part, returning to each other like drowning souls, holding fast in mutual desperation. He heard only the raw sobbing of Gerard and the mournful moans of Margaret.

Finally, a hoarse cry pierced the air, followed by the patter of feet on the hard road.

Martin sprang up. There stood Gerard, running wildly, hands clasped above his head in frantic prayer. Beside him, Margaret tottered backward, palms extended in a pitiful plea for aid, her cheeks ashen, her eyes fixed on empty space.

He caught her in his arms, speaking words of comfort, but her mind could not grasp them. Only the sound of his voice caused her to moan, to hold him tightly, and to tremble violently.

He lifted her onto the mule, his arm wrapped securely around her frame. What had once been strong as a boy's, she was now relaxed and powerless. Supporting her, he guided her slowly and sadly home. She shed not a single tear, spoke not a single word.

At the edge of the wood, he dismounted her and bade her cross to her father's house. She obeyed without question.

Martin rode on to Rotterdam; Sevenbergen had become too hot for him.

Gerard, severed from the love of his life, moved through the world like a dreamer. At the inn, he hired a horse and a guide, riding swiftly toward the German frontier. Yet everything was mechanical; his senses felt dulled. Trees, houses, and men drifted past him like objects viewed through a thick veil. His companions spoke to him twice, receiving no answer. Only once did he cry out savagely, "Shall we never be out of this hateful country?"

Hours later, they reached the brow of a steep hill, with a small brook running at the bottom.

"Halt!" the guide cried, pointing across the valley. "Here is Germany."

"Where?"

"On the other side of the stream. No need to ride down the hill, I trow."

Gerard dismounted without a word, drawing the burgomaster's purse from his girdle. As he opened it, the guide said half-sulkily, "You will soon be out of this hateful country. Perhaps the one you seek will like you no better; but once across that stream, though it be a church you have robbed, they cannot take you."

Such words, at another time, would have earned the speaker a rebuke or a blow. Now, they fell on Gerard like idle air. He paid the lad in silence and descended the hill alone. The brook was silvery, murmuring over pebbles varnished by clear water. He sat and stared stupidly at them, then drank from the stream. He washed his hot feet and hands in the icy water; it woke him.

Rising, he took a run and leaped across the stream into Germany. Even as his foot touched the strange soil, he turned suddenly and looked back.

"Farewell, ungrateful country!" he cried. "But for her, it would cost me nothing to leave you forever, and all my kin, and—the mother who bore me, and my playmates, and my little native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome the wide world! *Omne solum fortipatratra*." With these brave words on his lips, he suddenly drooped, arms and legs weak, sitting down to sob bitterly upon the foreign earth.

When the young exile had sat for a while bowed in grief, he rose and wiped the tears from his eyes like a man. Casting not a single glance behind him to weaken his resolve, he stepped out into the wide world.

His love and heavy sorrow left no room for vulgar misgivings. Compared with the tearing of himself from Margaret, walking to Italy in that rude age seemed a small price to pay.

"All nations meet in a convent," he thought, recalling the monks who had aided him. "Thanks to them and my thirst for knowledge, I can speak most of the tongues needed on this long road." He told himself, "I will soon be at Rome; the sooner the better."

After walking a good league, he arrived where four serpentine country roads met, a junction that had puzzled many a neighbor. Gerard took out the little dial Peter had given him, set it in the autumn sun, and steered unhesitatingly for Rome. He was inexperienced as a young swallow flying south, yet unlike the swallow, he wandered south alone.
