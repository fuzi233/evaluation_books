After a few days at Tunbridge Wells, our young Virginian had ascended to the pinnacle of local celebrity. In that bustling watering-place, no figure commanded such scrutiny as Harry Warrington. Even the Bishop of Salisbury received less deference than the lad, whose every step drew the gaze of the populace. Market-goers paused to stare; matrons at the assembly rooms subtly maneuvered to isolate him with their daughters, whose smiles were plentiful. The town possessed an uncanny, microscopic knowledge of his affairs: they knew the exact acreage of his Virginia estate, the precise sum of his income, and the nightly tally of his gambling winnings. This collective curiosity extended even to his servant, Gumbo, who was treated to ale and cold meats by strangers eager to glean news of their master. Gumbo, fattened by this sudden demand for information, rose to prominence within the Society of Valets, spinning tales of such grandeur that they bordered on the mythical.

Harry found this adulation somewhat bewildering yet bearable. Born in the colonies with a healthy dose of self-regard, he accepted his newfound status without arrogance. He adhered strictly to a personal code of honor; when challenged by the gentlemen of the town to races, drinking bouts, or card games in the name of Old Virginia, he felt compelled to accept. He possessed a sharp mind, a steady hand, a secure seat, and nerves of steel. Thus, in his friendly rivalry with the mother country, the representative of Virginia acquitted himself with distinction.

Madame de Bernstein, who had previously suffered a string of bad luck at the tables, found solace and delight in her nephew's triumphs. He had bested Jack Morris at shooting, outpaced Mr. Scamper in a riding match, and held his own remarkably well against Captain Batts at tennis, despite having never touched a racket before. Even the celebrated gamblers, the Lords of Chesterfield and March, testified to his coolness and breeding. While Harry was not a literary genius, his handwriting was competent enough to satisfy the fashionable set, and his innocent ignorance served as a source of amusement for the Baroness. She, a woman who preferred the lively world to the quiet library, took a maternal pride in curating his appearance, selecting fine laces for his ruffles and presenting him with a diamond brooch. Consequently, Harry became the favorite of all her kin. Lady Maria, though perhaps secretly pleased to see her brothers overshadowed by her nephew's success, watched with a mixture of envy and fear as Harry's reputation soared in her wealthy aunt's favor.

Two weeks into his stay, Harry had become a central figure in the social sphere. He had mastered the art of navigating good company, though he admitted it was difficult to avoid the bad as well. Was it his fault that he drank from the sparkling cup of pleasure offered to him? His aunt, far from being a rigid moralist, encouraged his enjoyment of life. Yet, Harry retained a reserve from his colonial upbringing. Though the vices of London were rampant, the rough towns of America had kept him largely untouched by libertinism. He blushed at the bold talk of his European peers and was often baffled by his aunt's worldly jests, earning her affectionate nickname of "Joseph" or "simpleton."

However, innocence is rarely credited where suspicion abounds. The world assumed Harry was as depraved as his surroundings suggested. He had seen Lord March driving Madame Cattarina in her carriage and, believing there was no harm, had escorted her through the public walks. When she desired a trinket from the toy shop, Harry, flush with cash, had happily purchased it for her. He repeated this generosity with lace and other small gifts, until finally, he laughed off a further request, refusing to buy diamond drops for her ears. He was generous but not reckless.

Yet, who was to believe his modesty? Baroness Bernstein, particularly in the presence of her niece Maria, insisted that Harry was playing the role of Don Juan, supplanting Lord March. She reveled in Maria's silent distress, enjoying the sting of her words: "Boys will be boys, and I don't want Harry to be the first milksop in his family!" The bread Maria ate at her aunt's expense seemed suddenly indigestible.

Meanwhile, Colonel Wolfe, the object of Maria's affection, visited her constantly. Knowing the Colonel was fully occupied with his courtship, Harry expected little interaction, despite valuing the officer's company. Wolfe's conversation stood in stark contrast to the ribald chatter surrounding Harry; he spoke of nothing but duty and avoided the scandals of the age. Indeed, the era was one of open dissoluteness, with kings keeping mistresses and nobles chasing pleasure recklessly. Harry had unwittingly stepped into this whirlpool, finding himself surrounded by wild characters and living with an aunt who thrived in the midst of the rout.

Harry failed to notice the shift in Colonel Wolfe's demeanor. When they met after a tennis match, the Colonel's greeting was icy. While Wolfe bowed politely to Captain Batts, he barely acknowledged Harry with a mere touch of his forefinger. Harry, confused by the coldness, approached the Colonel as they walked.

"I have not seen you these many days," Harry remarked.

"You have had other companions," Wolfe replied curtly.

"But I would rather be with you than any of them," Harry insisted.

"Indeed, I might be better company for you than some of them," Wolfe said grimly.

"Is it Captain Batts you mean?" Harry asked.

"He bears a rascally reputation from the army, and I doubt he has improved since his dismissal. You could find better friends," Wolfe stated bluntly.

"Friend? He is no friend; he merely teaches me tennis. He is hand-in-glove with my lord and the fashionable set."

"I am not a man of fashion," Wolfe corrected.

"My dear Colonel, what is the matter? Have I angered you?" Harry asked, genuinely concerned.

"I will be frank," Wolfe said gravely. "I dislike some of your friends."

"They are men of the highest rank in England," Harry protested, unwilling to take offense.

"Exactly. They are of too high a rank and too great a fashion for a hard-working soldier like me. If you continue to live with such, you will find that humdrum people cannot afford such company. I am here paying my addresses to an honorable lady. Yesterday, I saw you walking openly with a French ballet-dancer and removing your hat. I must tell you, I would prefer you did not remove your hat in such company."

Harry flushed crimson. "Do you mean I am to forgo the honor of your acquaintance altogether?"

"Certainly, when you are in the company of that person," Wolfe said angrily, using a word too vulgar for print, reminiscent of Othello's rage.

"Great heavens! What a shame to speak so of any woman!" Harry cried. "How dare anyone say she is not honest?"

"You ought to know best, sir," Wolfe said, looking at him with surprise, "or the world belies you very much."

"What ought I to know best?" Harry demanded. "I see a poor little French dancer ordered by doctors to drink the waters. I know a gentleman of my rank does not usually keep such company, but are you so squeamish? I can speak a little French, and she is welcome to it. If Colonel Wolfe does not choose to touch his hat to me, he may leave it alone."

"Do you not know the woman's character?" Wolfe pressed.

"Of course, she is a dancer, and no better or worse than her neighbors. But had she been a duchess or your grandmother, I could not have respected her more."

"Were you not to win her at dice from Lord March?" Wolfe countered.

"At what?"

"At dice. Everyone at the Wells knows the story. I heard it just now from Mr. Richardson; the ladies are calling you a colonial Lovelace."

Harry asked what else they had said, and the Colonel laid before him the most alarming accounts of his own wickedness: a corrupter of virtue, an habitual drunkard, a notorious blasphemer, and a fitting companion for the debauched. Wolfe explained that he shared these rumors because Harry's reaction suggested innocence, and he felt a debt to clear the air.

Harry readily accepted the apology, shaking hands with renewed cordiality. He easily refuted most charges, admitting only to the gambling. He believed a gentleman should accept a fair challenge if he could afford it, and since he was playing with other people's money, he felt no guilt. Play was fair, he reasoned, and pleasant; why should England, save for the Methodists, abstain?

Wolfe, however, did not comment on Harry's assessment of the "best company" at the Wells. Instead, he warned the young man that while his life was pleasant, it was not profitable. "Every man was made to do some work," Wolfe urged. "A gentleman, if he has no profession, must make one. Do you know the laws of your country? You will one day be a magistrate. Should you not study the trades and manufactures of the nation? Is there not honor in our profession? Honor is the aim of life. Idle bread is the most dangerous of all foods. Cards and pleasure may be pastimes after work, but never instead of work."

Wolfe concluded with a poignant observation: "Instead of being the Fortunate Youth, as the world calls you, I think you are Warrington the Unlucky, followed by daily idleness, flattery, and temptation. May the Lord grant you deliverance from your good fortune."

Harry returned to the house, unable to explain to his aunt why he looked so grave. He resolved not to drink, yet jolly fellows at the ordinary passed the bottle around, and he could not resist. He refused to play in the evening, but a fourth was needed at his aunt's table, and how could he decline? He became her partner several times that night, relying on his own luck, and once more saw the dawn, feasting on chickens and champagne, blissfully unaware of the storm brewing in the minds of those who loved him.
