If actors and actresses are like children, prone to weeping over spilled milk, they possess the equally childish talent of forgetting the spill the moment a new ice cream truck appears. It baffled Sally that a single mortal could generate such a tidal wave of joy merely by lending her brother Fillmore twenty thousand dollars. If the Second Coming had descended upon the Primrose Way Company, the cast couldn't have looked more radiant. The heavy fog of lethargy that had settled over them during their week of doing nothing evaporated instantly, shed like a wet coat. The sudden promotion of the assistant stage manager—a creature previously known only for hiding in the wings—to the dizzying heights of "Proprietor" struck them as the most dramatic plot twist imaginable. Most of them had starred in tragedies where the heroine was similarly exiled around eleven o'clock; seeing it happen to the villain was theatrically satisfying. Furthermore, now that Miss Hobson was gone, everyone finally admitted she had been a blight on the scenery.

Optimism became the official religion of the theater. Cheerful rumors circulated with the speed of gossip. Bowler-hatted Teddy claimed the lift-boy at his hotel whispered that the theater ban would be lifted on Tuesday at the latest. Meanwhile, the girl running the cigar stand at the Pontchartrain told the man playing the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were opening tomorrow. The general consensus was that the sun was bursting through the clouds and that Fate had given up on its hopeless campaign to keep good men down.

Fillmore was restored to his former self. Everyone has their own peculiar way of expressing elation; Fillmore's involved buying a new waistcoat, purchasing a hundred half-dollar cigars, and fussing excessively over his lunch. It may have been an optical illusion, but to Sally, he seemed to have gained at least six pounds in a single day. As a serf polishing paper knives, he had been almost gaunt. Now, as a manager, he bloomed into soft, billowy curves. Standing on the sidewalk before the theater, gloating over the new posters bearing the legend:

he was so wide that the populace had to perform a delicate detour just to pass him.

In this era of bubbling joy, it was difficult that Sally, the fairy godmother responsible for the miracle, should not be completely happy too. It puzzled her why a shadow lingered at the back of her mind, refusing to step into the light. It wasn't until she was driving in a hired car with Gerald one afternoon on Belle Isle that the truth revealed itself.

Since Miss Hobson's departure, Gerald had been at his absolute best. Like Fillmore, he was a man who thrived in the sunshine of prosperity. His moodiness had vanished, replaced by his old charm. Yet, as the car slid smoothly through the pleasant woods and fields by the river, something jarred.

Gerald was cheerful and talkative. He found nothing wrong with life. He spoke expansively of the grand plans he intended to execute.

"If this play gets over—and it's going to—I'll show 'em!" His jaw was squared, his eyes glowing as they stared into an inviting future. "One success—that's all I need—then watch me! I haven't had a chance yet, but..."

His voice rolled on, but Sally had stopped listening. It was the time of year when the chill of evening follows swiftly on the mellow warmth of the afternoon. The sun had dipped behind the trees, and a cold wind was blowing up from the river. Suddenly, as if the wind had cleared her mind, she understood what had been lurking in the shadows. For an instant, it stood out naked and unhidden, and the world seemed to turn forlorn.

She realized the fundamental difference between a man's outlook on life and a woman's. Success! How men worshipped it, and how little of themselves they had left to give to anything else. Ironically, it was the theme of Gerald's very play—the play she had saved from destruction—that highlighted this truth. Of all the men she knew, how many viewed life solely as a race they must strain every nerve to win, regardless of what they missed along the way? Fillmore. Gerald. All of them. There might be a woman in each of their lives, but she was always second—an afterthought, a hobby for their spare time. Gerald was everything to her. His success would never be more than a side issue to her; he himself, without any of the trappings of fame, was enough. But she was not enough for him. A spasm of futile jealousy shook her. She shivered.

"Cold?" said Gerald. "I'll tell the man to drive back... I don't see any reason why this play shouldn't run a year in New York. Everybody says it's good... if it does get over, they'll all be after me. I..."

Sally stared out into a bleak world. The sky was a leaden grey, and the wind from the river blew with a dismal chill.
