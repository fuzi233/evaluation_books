Between the realities that shape our fate and the illusions that merely decorate them, how must a discerning mind judge? When the calculating, unyielding intellect of Sigsbee Manderson was shattered by a bullet from an unseen hand, the world lost nothing of consequence worthy of a tear; indeed, it gained a stark, memorable lesson in the vanity of amassed wealth, for the dead man had accumulated fortunes without securing a single loyal friend to mourn him or perform an act to honor his memory. Yet, when word of his demise spread, it seemed to those inhabiting the great vortices of commerce that the very earth shuddered under the blow.

Throughout the lurid commercial annals of his nation, no figure had so dominated the collective consciousness of the trading world. He occupied a niche apart in its temples. While financial giants, powerful in directing capital and charging millions for their labor, had existed before, Manderson possessed a singularity: a pale halo of piratical romance clung to his head, a sentiment especially cherished by his countrymen, despite his role as the unquestioned guardian of stability, the crusher of manipulated crises, and the scourge of the raiding chieftains plaguing the borders of Wall Street.

His fortune, inherited from a grandfather who had been a buccaneer of modest scale, passed to him through a father who quietly lent money without ever margining stocks. Manderson, always possessing vast sums at his disposal, should have belonged entirely to that newer American plutocracy steadied by the traditions of great wealth. It was not so. Though his upbringing and education instilled in him European notions of a rich man's proper exterior—the quiet magnificence that does not shriek for attention—he nonetheless carried the spirit of the Forty-Niner and the financial rover. During what was termed his "early bad manner," he was little more than a gambler of genius, a prodigy whose hand was raised against every man's, bringing a brain superior to any opposition to the enthralling pursuit of speculation. It was at St Helena that war was defined as *une belle occupation*; thus, the young Manderson found his battlefield in the multitudinous, complex dog-fights of the New York Stock Exchange.

Then came his transformation. Upon his father's death, when Manderson was thirty, some new revelation regarding the power and glory of the deity he served appeared to strike him. With the sudden, elastic adaptability characteristic of his nation, he turned to steady labor in his father's banking business, closing his ears to the roar of street battles. In a few short years, he came to control the entire activity of the great firm whose unimpeachable conservatism, safety, and financial weight lifted it like a cliff above the angry sea of the markets. All mistrust founded on his youthful performances vanished. He was plainly a different man. No authority could explain how the change occurred, though stories circulated of final words spoken by his father, the sole figure Manderson had truly respected or perhaps loved.

He began to tower above the financial landscape. Soon, his name echoed in the bourses of the globe. To speak Manderson's name conjured a vision of all that was broad-based and firm in the vast wealth of the United States. He planned grand combinations of capital, centralizing industries of continental scope, financing the large designs of states and private enterprises with unerring judgment. Often, when he "took hold" to crush a strike or federate the ownership of great fields of labor, he sent ruin upon countless tiny homes; if miners, steelworkers, or cattlemen defied him and invoked disorder, he proved more lawless and ruthless than they. Yet, these acts were pursued in the name of legitimate business. Tens of thousands of the poor might curse his name, but the financier and the speculator execrated him no longer. Stretching a hand to protect or manipulate the power of wealth in every corner of the country, forcible, cold, and unerring, he ministered to the national lust for magnitude, and a grateful country surnamed him the Colossus.

Yet, there was an aspect of Manderson in this later period that lay long hidden, known only to his secretaries, lieutenants, and certain associates of his former hurling days. This small circle knew that Manderson, the pillar of sound business and market stability, harbored hours of nostalgia for the lively times when the Street trembled at his whisper. As one confidant noted, it was as if Blackbeard had settled down as a decent merchant in Bristol on the spoils of the Main. Now and then, the pirate would glare suddenly outward, the knife in his teeth, sulphur matches sputtering in his hatband. During these spasms of reverting to type, scores of tempestuous raids upon the market were plotted in the inner rooms of Manderson, Colefax and Company. They were never executed. Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old self within him and descend soberly to his counting-house—perhaps humming a stanza or two of "Spanish Ladies" under his breath. Manderson would permit himself the harmless satisfaction, once the window for action had closed, of pointing out to some Rupert of the markets a coup worth a million to a depredator might have been made. "Seems to me," he would say with a wistful sigh, "the Street is getting to be a mighty dull place since I quit." By slow degrees, this amiable weakness of the Colossus became known to the business world, which exulted greatly in the knowledge.

At the news of his death, panic swept through the markets like a hurricane, for it fell at a luckless hour. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two days, Wall Street burned as a clamorous inferno of pale despair. Across the United States, wherever speculation had its devotees, drifted a waft of ruin, a plague of suicide. Even in Europe, not a few ended the lives that had become pitiably linked to the destiny of a financier most of them had never seen. In Paris, a well-known banker walked quietly from the Bourse and fell dead upon the broad steps amidst a raving crowd of Jews, a phial crushed in his hand. In Frankfort, one leapt from the Cathedral top, staining the red tower with a deeper crimson. Men stabbed, shot, and strangled themselves, drinking death or breathing it as the air, because in a lonely corner of England, life had departed from one cold heart vowed to the service of greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous moment. It arrived when Wall Street was in a condition of suppressed "scare"—suppressed, because for a week past the great interests acting under or actually controlled by the Colossus had been desperately combatiing the effects of the sudden arrest of Lucas Hahn and the exposure of his plundering of the Hahn banks. This bombshell, in turn, had fallen when the market had been "boosted" beyond its real strength. In the jargon of the place, a slump was imminent. Reports from the corn-lands had been poor, and two or three railway statements had fallen short of expectations. But at whatever point in the vast area of speculation the shudder of the threatened break was felt, "the Manderson crowd" had stepped in to hold the market aloft. Throughout the week, the speculator's mind, shallow yet quick-witted, sentimental yet greedy, saw in this the hand of the giant stretching out in protection from afar. Manderson, according to the newspapers in chorus, was in hourly communication with his lieutenants in the Street. One journal reported the exact sum spent on cabling between New York and Marlstone in the previous twenty-four hours, noting how a small staff of expert operators had been dispatched by the Post Office authorities to Marlstone to handle the flood of messages. Another revealed that Manderson, upon the first news of the Hahn crash, had arranged to abandon his holiday and return home via the *Lusitania*, only to determine shortly thereafter that he need not return, having the situation well in hand.

All this was falsehood, more or less consciously elaborated by the "finance editors," consciously initiated and encouraged by the shrewd businessmen of the Manderson group. They knew that nothing could better aid their plans than this illusion of hero-worship—they knew equally well that no word had come from Manderson answering their messages, and that Howard B. Jeffrey, of Steel and Iron fame, was the true organizer of victory. Thus, they fought down apprehension through four feverish days, and minds grew calmer. On Saturday, though the ground beneath the feet of Mr. Jeffrey yet rumbled occasionally with Etna-like mutterings of disquiet, he deemed his task nearly complete. The market was firm, slowly advancing. Wall Street turned to its Sunday sleep, worn out but thankfully at peace.

In the first trading hour of Monday, a hideous rumor flew round the sixty acres of the financial district. It came into being as lightning does—a blink seeming to start nowhere, though it is suspected it was first whispered over a telephone, accompanied by an urgent selling order from some employee in the cable service. A sharp spasm convulsed the convalescent share-list. In five minutes, the dull noise of the kerbstone market in Broad Street leaped to a high note of frantic interrogation. From within the hive of the Exchange itself rose a droning hubbub of fear, and men rushed hatless in and out. "Is it true?" asked every man; and every man replied, with trembling lips, that it was a lie concocted by some unscrupulous "short" interest seeking to cover itself.
