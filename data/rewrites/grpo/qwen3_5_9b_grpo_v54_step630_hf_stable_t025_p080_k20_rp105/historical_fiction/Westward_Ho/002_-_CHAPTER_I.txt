"The hollow oak our palace is,
Our heritage the sea."

Any traveler who has wandered through the lush, undulating landscape of North Devon must inevitably arrive at the little white town of Bideford. It climbs gently from its broad river, paved with golden sands, past the many-arched bridge where salmon await the autumn floods, rising toward the pleasant uplands to the west. Above the town, the hills close in, cushioned with deep oak forests, through which jagged crags of fern-fringed slate jut defiantly; below, they soften into rounded knolls and fertile squares of red and green, sinking finally into the wide, hazy expanse of salt marshes and rolling sand-dunes where the Torridge meets the Taw. Together, they flow quietly toward the great Atlantic swell, an everlasting thunder that defines the coast.

Here, the old town stands beneath a soft, Italian sky, fanned perpetually by the fresh ocean breeze that banishes both the biting winter frosts and the stifling heat of the midlands. It has stood thus for perhaps eight hundred years, since the first Grenville, a cousin of the Conqueror, returned from the conquest of South Wales. He gathered around him trusty Saxon serfs, free Norse rovers with their golden curls, and dark Silurian Britons from the Swansea shore, blending their blood into the unique strength and beauty that still characterizes the seaward folk of the region.

Yet at the time of this writing, Bideford was more than merely a pleasant country town. It was one of England's chief ports, furnishing seven ships to fight the Armada. Even a century later, chroniclers noted that it sent more vessels to the northern trade than any port in England save London and Topsham. It was the center of a local civilization and enterprise, small perhaps compared to the vast efforts of today, but who dares despise the day of small things if it proves to be the dawn of mighty ones? England owes the foundation of her naval and commercial glory to the sea-life and labor of Bideford, Dartmouth, Topsham, and Plymouth—then a petty place—and many another little western town. It was the men of Devon, the Drakes, Hawkins, Gilberts, Raleighs, Grenvilles, and Oxenhams, and a host of "forgotten worthies," to whom she owes her commerce, her colonies, and her very existence. Had they not first crippled the ill-gotten resources of the Spaniard through West Indian raids, and then crushed his last huge effort in Britain's Salamis in 1588, what would we be but a popish appanage of a world-tyranny as cruel as heathen Rome?

It is in memory of these men—their voyages, battles, faith, valor, heroic lives, and no less heroic deaths—that I write this book. If I seem to warm into a style somewhat too stilted and pompous, let me be excused for my subject's sake. It is a tale fit rather to be sung than said, a proclamation to all true English hearts, not as a mere novel but as an epic, echoing the great messages of Troy, the Persian Wars, and the trophies of Marathon and Salamis.

One bright summer's afternoon in the year of grace 1575, a tall and fair boy came lingering along the Bideford quay. Clad in a scholar's gown, carrying a satchel and slate, he watched wistfully the shipping and the sailors until, just after passing the bottom of the High Street, he found himself opposite a tavern overlooking the river. In the open bay window sat merchants and gentlemen discoursing over their afternoon draught of sack; outside the door, a group of sailors gathered, listening earnestly to a speaker standing in the midst. The boy, all alive for any sea-news, stepped up to join the sailor-lads peeping and whispering under the men's elbows.

The speech that followed was delivered in a loud, bold voice, accented strongly with Devonshire and sprinkled liberally with oaths.

"If you don't believe me, go and see, or stay here and grow all over blue mould. I tell you, as I am a gentleman, I saw it with these eyes, and so did Salvation Yeo there through a window in the lower room. We measured the heap, as I am a christened man, seventy feet long, ten feet broad, and twelve feet high, of silver bars, each weighing between thirty and forty pounds. And says Captain Drake: 'There, my lads of Devon, I've brought you to the mouth of the world's treasure-house, and it's your own fault now if you don't sweep it out as empty as a stock-fish.'"

"Why didn't you bring some of them home, then, Mr. Oxenham?"

"Why weren't you there to help carry them? We would have brought them away, safe enough, and young Drake and I had already broken the door abroad. But Captain Drake goes off in a dead faint. When we came to look, he had a wound in his leg you might have laid three fingers in, and his boots were full of blood, having been so for an hour or more. But the heart of him was that he never knew it till he dropped. Then his brother and I got him away to the boats, him kicking and struggling, bidding us let him go on with the fight though every step he took in the sand was in a pool of blood. So we got off. Tell me, ye sons of shotten herrings, wasn't it worth more to save him than the dirty silver? For silver we can get again, brave boys; there's more fish in the sea than ever came out of it, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave all the streets in the west country. But of such captains as Franky Drake, Heaven never makes but one at a time. And if we lose him, good-bye to England's luck, say I. And who don't agree, let him choose his weapons, and I'm his man."

The speaker was a tall, sturdy personage with a florid black-bearded face and bold, restless dark eyes. Leaning against the wall with crossed legs and arms akimbo, he seemed to the schoolboy a very magnifico, some prince or duke at least. He was dressed, contrary to all sumptuary laws of the time, in a suit of crimson velvet, a little the worse for wear. By his side lay a long Spanish rapier and a brace of daggers, gaudy enough about the hilts; his fingers sparkled with rings, and he wore two or three gold chains about his neck and large earrings. Behind one of these, a red rose was stuck jauntily among his glossy black curls. On his head sat a broad velvet Spanish hat, in which, instead of a feather, a great gold clasp held a whole Quetzal bird, whose gorgeous plumage of fretted golden-green shone like one entire precious stone.

As he finished his speech, he removed the hat and looked at the bird.

"Look ye, my lads, did you ever see such a fowl as that before? That is the bird which the old Indian kings of Mexico let no one wear but their own selves. And therefore I wear it—I, John Oxenham of South Tawton—as a sign to all brave lads of Devon that, as the Spaniards are the masters of the Indians, we are the masters of the Spaniards." He replaced his hat.

A murmur of applause followed, though one hinted that he doubted the Spaniards were too many for them.

"Too many? How many men did we take Nombre de Dios with? Seventy-three were we, and no more when we sailed out of Plymouth Sound. Before we saw the Spanish Main, half were *gastados*, used up, as the Dons say, with the scurvy. In Port Pheasant, Captain Rawse of Cowes fell in with us, and that gave us some thirty hands more. With that handful, my lads, only fifty-three in all, we picked the lock of the new world! And whom did we lose but our trumpeter, who stood braying like an ass in the middle of the square instead of taking care of his neck like a Christian? I tell you, those Spaniards are rank cowards, as all bullies are. They pray to a woman, the idolatrous rascals! And no wonder they fight like women."

"You're right, captain," sang out a tall, gaunt fellow standing close to him. "One west-country-man can fight two easterlings, and an easterling can beat three Dons any day. Eh! my lads of Devon?"

"For O! it's the herrings and the good brown beef,
And the cider and the cream so white;
O! they are the making of the jolly Devon lads,
For to play, and eke to fight."

"Come," said Oxenham. "Come along! Who lists? Who lists? Who'll make his fortune?"

"Oh, who will join, jolly mariners all?
And who will join, says he, O!
To fill his pockets with the good red gold,
By sailing on the sea, O!"

"Who'll list?" cried the gaunt man again. "Now's your time! We've got forty men to Plymouth now, ready to sail the minute we get back. We want a dozen out of you Bideford men, and just a boy or two, and then we're off and away, and make our fortunes, or go to heaven."

"Our bodies in the sea so deep,
Our souls in heaven to rest!
Where valiant seamen, one and all,
Hereafter shall be blest!"

"Now," said Oxenham, "you won't let the Plymouth men say that the Bideford men daren't follow them? North Devon against South, it is. Who'll join? Who'll join? It is but a step of a way, after all, and sailing as smooth as a duck-pond as soon as you're past Cape Finisterre. I'll run a Clovelly herring-boat there and back for a wager of twenty pounds, and never ship a bucketful all the way. Who'll join? Don't think you're buying a pig in a poke. I know the road, and Salvation Yeo here, too, who was the gunner's mate, as well as I do the narrow seas, and better. You ask him to show you the chart of it now, and see if he don't tell you over the ruttier as well as Drake himself."

On this, the gaunt man pulled from under his arm a great white buffalo horn covered with rough etchings of land and sea, and held it up to the admiring ring.

"See here, boys all, and behold the picture of the place, drawn out so natural as ever was life. I got it from a Portuguese, down to the Azores. He'd pricked it out, pricked it out, wheresoever he'd sailed, and whatsoever he'd seen. Take it in your hands now, Simon Evans, take it in your hands; look it over, and I'll warrant you'll know the way in five minutes so well as ever a shark in the seas."

The horn was passed from hand to hand, while Oxenham, seeing his hearers becoming moved, called through the open window for a great tankard of sack and passed that from hand to hand after the horn.

The schoolboy, who had been devouring everything with his eyes and ears and had contrived to edge himself into the inner ring, now stood face to face with the hero of the emerald crest, getting as many peeps as he could at the wonder. But when he saw the sailors, one after another, turn it over and offer to join Mr. Oxenham, his soul burned within him for a nearer view of that wondrous horn, as magical in its effects as that of Tristram or the enchanter's in Ariosto. When the group had somewhat broken up and Oxenham was going into the tavern with his recruits, the boy asked boldly for a nearer sight of the marvel, which was granted at once.

To his astonished gaze, cities and harbors, dragons and elephants, whales fighting sharks, plate ships of Spain, islands with apes and palm-trees, each with its name overwritten, appeared. Here and there, "Here is gold"; again, "Much gold and silver"—inserted most probably, as the words were in English, by the hands of Mr. Oxenham himself. Lingeringly and longingly, the boy turned it round and round, thinking the owner more fortunate than Khan or Kaiser. Oh, if he could but possess that horn, what needed he on earth beside to make him blest?

"I say, will you sell this?"

"Yea, marry, or my own soul, if I can get the worth of it."

"I want the horn—I don't want your soul; it's somewhat of a stale sole, for aught I know; and there are plenty of fresh ones in the bay."

And therewith, after much fumbling, he pulled out a tester—the only one he had—and asked if that would buy it?

"That! No, nor twenty of them."

The boy thought over what a good knight-errant would do in such a case, and then answered, "Tell you what: I'll fight you for it."

"Thank 'ee, sir!"

"Break the jackanapes's head for him, Yeo," said Oxenham.

"Call me jackanapes again, and I break yours, sir." And the boy lifted his fist fiercely.

Oxenham looked at him a minute, smilingly. "Tut! Tut! My man, hit one of your own size, if you will, and spare little folk like me!"

"If I have a boy's age, sir, I have a man's fist. I shall be fifteen years old this month, and know how to answer any one who insults me."
