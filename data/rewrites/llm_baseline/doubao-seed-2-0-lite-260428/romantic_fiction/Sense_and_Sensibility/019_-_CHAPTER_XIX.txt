Edward lingered seven sweet, tangled days at the Barton cottage, every hour stitched through with quiet joy he seemed determined to burn out before it could settle. Mrs. Dashwood pressed him again and again to stay longer, but it was as though he had set his own heart to a small, sharp penance: he would tear himself away the very moment his happiness with them swelled to its brightest peak.

His spirits shifted like the valley mists those last few days, uneven but steadily lighter—he wandered the surrounding lanes with Elinor till he knew every wild rose thicket by name, leaned in to laugh at Marianne’s piano playing till his eyes crinkled, and never once spoke of leaving without a soft, unmistakeable sigh. He swore he had no engagements anywhere else, no plans at all for the days ahead, and even hesitated to name the place he would go once he turned their lane. Yet go he insisted he must. No week had ever slipped by so fast; he repeated the marvel of it over and over, and every other word he spoke gave the lie to his own stubborn resolve. There was no pleasure for him at Norland, he said. He detested the noise and cold formality of London. Their kindness was worth more to him than anything in the world, and his greatest happiness was here at Barton. Still, he would leave them at the end of the week, against their wishes and against his own, with no actual tie binding his time to anyone else.

Elinor laid all this strange, contradictory behaviour at the feet of his mother, and it was a mercy for her own frayed feelings that Mrs. Ferrars was little known to her—so easy to make her the general excuse for every oddity in her son’s actions. Disappointed, a little vexed, and sometimes stung by his uncertain, half-hearted attention to her, she still chose to view his choices with all the gentle allowance she had been forced to fight so hard to extend to Willoughby not long before. His low spirits, his secrecy, his inconsistency—all of it she blamed on his lack of independence, on his quiet dread of Mrs. Ferrars’s sharp temper and rigid plans for his future. The shortness of his visit, his iron resolve to leave, all came back to the same old, tired grief: a man forced to temporize with a parent who held every thread of his fortune and his freedom in her fists. She daydreamed of the day that struggle would end, when Mrs. Ferrars would soften, and Edward would be free to chase his own happiness. But when those wishes felt too empty, she turned back to the small, vivid proofs of his affection: every lingering glance he sent her way, every half-stammered compliment, and above all the thin, plain silver ring he never once took off his finger, the tiny, unspoken promise he wore against his skin.

“I think, Edward,” Mrs. Dashwood said at their breakfast table on his last morning, “you would be a far happier man if you had a profession to fill your days and give purpose to your plans. It might inconvenience your friends, of course—you would not have so much time to spend with us. But” she added with a soft smile, “it would benefit you greatly in at least one small way: you would never have to stand wondering where on earth to go once you left our door.”

“I assure you,” Edward replied, his voice heavy, “I have thought that very same thing for years. It has always been, and will always be, the greatest misfortune of my life that I have no necessary work to occupy me, no profession to give me purpose, or any sort of true independence. My own silly scruples, and those of my family, turned me into this idle, helpless thing. We could never agree on what path I ought to take. I always loved the church, and I still do—but that was not nearly grand enough for them. They pushed me toward the army, and that was far too loud and flashy for me. The law was deemed genteel enough—plenty of young men with chambers in the Temple shine in high society, driving flashy gigs around town—but I had no taste for it, even the shallow, performative version my family approved. The navy was fashionable, but by the time they thought of it I was too old to enter. At last, because there was never any real need for me to work at all, because I could be as frivolous and expensive without a uniform as I could be in one, they decided idleness was the most honourable and advantageous choice of all. And an eighteen-year-old boy is hardly ever stubborn enough to resist his family begging him to do nothing at all. So I was sent up to Oxford, and I have been properly idle ever since.”

“Then I suppose the consequence will be,” Mrs. Dashwood laughed, “that since leisure has not brought you happiness, your own sons will be raised to a dozen different pursuits, professions, and trades, just as Columella’s were.”

“They will be raised,” he said, very serious, “to be as unlike me as it is possible for human beings to be. In feeling, in action, in every circumstance of their lives.”

“Come now, that is just the low spirits speaking,” she chided gently. “You are melancholy this morning, and imagine anyone unlike yourself must be happier. But remember that the pain of parting from friends touches everyone at times, no matter their station or work. Your happiness is already almost yours—you only need patience, or to give it a prettier name: hope. Your mother will give you that independence you crave in time. It is her duty, and soon it must become her happiness too, to not let your whole youth waste away in discontent. A few months can change so very much.”

“I think,” Edward said, quiet and bitter, “that a great many months could pass without bringing me a single good thing.”

That despondent note hung over their parting, which came not long after, and left a heavy, uncomfortable weight on all of them—most of all Elinor, who found the ache slow to soften, even when she set her whole will to subduing it. She refused to follow the path Marianne had taken after Willoughby left, moping in solitude, hiding away from the family, wasting hours indulging grief until it grew sharper and deeper. The moment Edward’s figure vanished down the lane, Elinor sat herself at her drawing table, and worked busily the whole day. She neither sought out nor avoided talk of him, kept up her usual interest in the household’s small affairs, and if this discipline did not lessen her own grief, it at least kept it from swelling unnecessarily, so her mother and sisters never fussed over her or worried she was breaking.

To Marianne, this behaviour was no more admirable than Marianne’s own indulgence had seemed to Elinor. She had already decided that perfect self-command was impossible for anyone with real, strong feelings, and worthless for anyone who had feelings so calm they never troubled them. She could not deny her sister loved Edward, even if the admission stung—but she proved the strength of her own soft heart by loving and respecting Elinor all the same, even through that petty, mortifying conviction.

Elinor never shut herself in her room, never wandered off alone for hours, never lay awake half the night to ruminate—but she still found plenty of quiet moments to think of Edward, his words, his strange contradictory behaviour, all the shifts of her own feelings: tender one minute, pitying the next, then approving, then doubting, then softening again. When the rest of the family were busy, when no one was talking, every bit of solitude she needed wrapped around her. Her thoughts could not be chained down to her drawing. The past and the future, tied up in the man she loved, forced their way to the front of her mind, engrossing all her memory, her reflection, her fancy.

She was pulled out of one such reverie one morning, not long after Edward’s departure, by the sound of new visitors arriving. She was entirely alone in the parlour, and the click of the small wicket gate at the edge of their green front court drew her eyes to the window. A whole party was walking up the path: Sir John, Lady Middleton, Mrs. Jennings, and two strangers, a gentleman and a lady she had never seen before. Sir John spotted her at once, left the rest of the party to knock at the door, and tramped across the lawn to make her open the casement to speak to him, the space between the door and window so small they could not talk without the rest of the party overhearing.

“Well!” he grinned. “We’ve brought you new strangers. What do you think of them?”

“Hush, they will hear you!” Elinor whispered.

“Never mind if they do. It’s only the Palmers. Charlotte’s very pretty, I promise you. Look this way and you’ll see her.”

Elinor said she would be perfectly able to greet them in two minutes without craning out the window. “Where is Marianne? Did she run off to hide when she saw us coming? I saw her pianoforte is open.”

“I believe she’s out walking.”

Mrs. Jennings came bustling up before Sir John could say more, too impatient to wait to come inside before telling her story. She hollered through the open casement, “My dear, how do you do? How is Mrs. Dashwood, and where are all your sisters? What, all alone? You’ll be glad of a bit of company! I’ve brought my other son and daughter to call on you—only think how sudden! I heard a carriage last night when we were drinking tea, and never thought it could be them. I was convinced it was Colonel Brandon coming back, and I said to Sir John, ‘I do believe that’s a carriage, it must be Colonel Brandon—’”

Elinor had to turn away from her to let the rest of the party in. Lady Middleton formally introduced the two strangers, just as Mrs. Dashwood and Margaret came downstairs. They all settled in the parlour to look one another over, while Mrs. Jennings kept right on telling her story as she walked through the front hall.

Mrs. Palmer was several years younger than Lady Middleton, and entirely unlike her sister in every possible way. Short and plump, with a bright, pretty face, she carried the most unreserved, sunny good humour imaginable. Her manners were less polished than Lady Middleton’s, but far more warm and endearing. She walked in with a smile on her face, smiled through her whole visit, and was still smiling when she left, only breaking into full peals of laughter in between. Her husband was a grave, distant young man of twenty-five or six, more fashionable and seemingly sharper than his wife, but with no inclination whatsoever to be polite or make anyone feel welcome. He stepped into the room with an air of great self-importance, bowed barely to the ladies without saying a word, glanced briefly over the whole room, grabbed a newspaper off the table, and proceeded to read it for the entire rest of his visit.

Mrs. Palmer, by contrast, was full of natural, unforced cheer, and hardly sat down before she burst out gushing about how perfect their little cottage was. “Oh! What a delightful room! I never saw anything half so charming! Mama, just think how much nicer it is than the last time I was here! I always thought this such a sweet little place,” she said, turning to Mrs. Dashwood, “but you’ve made it absolutely magical! Look, sister, how lovely everything is! I want a house just like this for myself, don’t you, Mr. Palmer?”

Mr. Palmer did not answer her, and never even lifted his eyes from his newspaper.

“Mr. Palmer never hears me sometimes,” she laughed. “It’s so ridiculous!”

Mrs. Dashwood had never before thought inattention qualified as wit, and stared at them both in faint surprise. Meanwhile Mrs. Jennings kept talking at the top of her voice, recounting every detail of how shocked they had been the night before when the Palmers turned up, till every last part of the story was told. Mrs. Palmer laughed again and again at the memory, and the whole room agreed two or three times over that it was a most pleasant surprise.

“You can imagine how glad we were to see them,” Mrs. Jennings added, leaning in to Elinor and lowering her voice like it was a great secret, even though they sat on opposite sides of the parlour. “But I do wish they hadn’t rushed their journey so much, coming all the way around London on some silly business—you know,” she nodded significantly at her daughter, “it’s very bad for her in her condition. I told her she ought to stay home and rest this morning, but she insisted on coming with us, she was so desperate to see you all!”

Mrs. Palmer laughed and said it would do her no harm at all.

“She’s due to have the baby in February,” Mrs. Jennings confided further.

Lady Middleton could not bear that line of conversation, so she forced herself to ask Mr. Palmer if there was any interesting news in the paper.

“No, nothing at all,” he replied, and kept right on reading.

“Here comes Marianne!” Sir John shouted. “Now Palmer, you’re about to see the most beautiful girl you ever laid eyes on.”

He went straight out to the hall, wrenched the front door open, and ushered Marianne inside himself. Mrs. Jennings asked her the second she stepped in if she had been walking to Allenham, and Mrs. Palmer laughed so hard it was plain she knew exactly what the reference meant. Even Mr. Palmer looked up from his newspaper to stare at Marianne for several long minutes, before dropping his gaze back to his pages.

Mrs. Palmer’s eye then caught the collection of drawings hanging on the parlour walls. She jumped up to examine them. “Oh dear, these are so beautiful! Mama, look how lovely they are! I could stare at them forever!” She sat back down a minute later, and promptly forgot all about the sketches.

When Lady Middleton stood up to leave, Mr. Palmer rose too, set down his newspaper, stretched, and glanced around at all of them. “My love, have you been asleep?” his wife giggled. He did not answer her, only observed after a final look around, that the room was very low-ceilinged, and the ceiling was crooked. Then he bowed, and left with the rest of the party.

Sir John had been badgering them all the while to come dine at the park the next day. Mrs. Dashwood, who did not wish to accept more dinners at the park than she could invite the Middletons back to the cottage, refused for herself, and said her daughters could do as they pleased. But the girls had no great curiosity to watch Mr. and Mrs. Palmer eat their dinner, and no expectation of enjoying the evening, so they tried to excuse themselves too, saying the weather looked uncertain. But Sir John would not take no for an answer—he would send the carriage for them, they must come. Lady Middleton pressed them too, and Mrs. Jennings and Mrs. Palmer added their own pleas, till the young ladies had no choice but to give in.

“Why do they insist on dragging us over there?” Marianne huffed the second the door shut behind their guests. “They say the rent on this cottage is very low—but it feels a very harsh bargain, if we are expected to dine at the park every single time anyone comes to stay with them, or with us.”

“They are not being unkind by all these invitations,” Elinor said softly. “They are just as eager to be civil to us now as they were when we first arrived. The parties only feel tedious and dull because we carry a little weight in our own hearts these days. The change is not in them—it is in us.”
