One afternoon, Mr. Warrington repaired to his usual dinner at the White Horse ordinary. To his delight, he spotted the jovial, handsome face of Parson Sampson seated among the diners. The clergyman was currently regaling the company with tales and witty remarks that drew roaring laughter from the crowd. Although Harry had not been in London for several months, the parson possessed the latest gossip—what passed for news among the townsfolk. He spoke of the intrigues at the King's house in Kensington and the Duke's residence in Pall Mall; of Mr. Byng's behavior in prison and his visitors; of the odds at Newmarket and the current favorite toast in Covent Garden.

Mr. Sampson, a jolly chaplain, offered information on every conceivable topic. While the accuracy of his reports might be questionable, the content was perfect for the country gentlemen listening. If Lord Viscount Squanderfield was ruining himself for Mrs. Polly, Sampson might call her Mrs. Lucy; if Lady Jane loved the actor rather than Lady Mary, or if Harry Hilton of the Horse Grenadiers fought Chevalier Solingen instead of Tommy Ruffler of the Foot Guards, it mattered little. Names and dates were secondary to the liveliness and wickedness of the stories. Mr. Sampson laughed and chatted endlessly, charming the assembly with his spirit and conversation, draining bottle after bottle while his delighted audience insisted on paying for them.

A century prior, the "Abbe Parson"—the clergyman frequenting theaters, taverns, racecourses, and high society—was a familiar figure in English life. His voice could be heard loudest in the hunting field; he could sing the catchiest songs at the Rose or the Bedford Head after a performance at Covent Garden, and call a bet as well as anyone at a gaming table. Whether modesty or claret caused his reverend face to flush a deeper crimson, when he saw Mr. Warrington enter, he leaned toward the laughing country squire beside him—a man in a drab coat and gold-laced red waistcoat—and whispered, *"Maxima debetur."* Rising abruptly, the chaplain nearly stumbled forward in his haste to greet the Virginian.

"My dear sir, my very dear sir, my conqueror of spades, clubs, and hearts!" cried the chaplain. "I am delighted to see your honor looking so fresh and well."

Harry returned the greeting with genuine pleasure, equally happy to see Mr. Sampson and to compliment the clergyman on his cheerful countenance and rosy cheeks. The squire in the drab coat recognized Mr. Warrington immediately, making space beside himself and urging the parson to resume his seat and continue his tale of Lord Ogle and the grocer's wife. Before he could finish the location, however, a shout and an oath erupted, directed at the parson for stepping on a gouty toe.

Apologizing hastily, the chaplain turned to Mr. Warrington and announced, to the amusement of the entire company, that Lord Castlewood sent his affectionate regards to his cousin and had specifically ordered Mr. Sampson to travel to Tunbridge Wells to oversee the young gentleman's morals. He reported that Lady Viscountess and Lady Fanny had departed for Harrogate, that Mr. Will had won his money at Newmarket and was visiting the Duke, and that Molly, the housemaid, was weeping over Gumbo, Mr. Warrington's valet. In short, he delivered all the news from Castlewood and its vicinity.

As Mr. Sampson boasted that all of Hampshire had heard of Harry's successes at Tunbridge, the guests began to drift away, leaving the young Virginian alone with his wine and the chaplain. "Though I have had plenty," declared the jolly priest, "that is no reason why I should not have plenty more." He proceeded to drink toast after toast and bumper after bumper, much to Harry's amusement, who always enjoyed his company. By the time Sampson had satisfied his appetite, Harry had become notably generous, warm-hearted, and friendly.

"Why should Mr. Sampson incur the expense of an inn when there is a room at Harry's quarters?" Harry asked. The chaplain's trunk was dispatched to his lodgings, and Gumbo was instructed to ensure his comfort. Nothing would satisfy Mr. Warrington except that Sampson visit the stables to see his horses. Harry now owned several fine mounts. When Sampson arrived, he recognized his own horse—the one Harry had recently won from him. The animal whinnied with delight, rubbing its nose against his old master's coat. Harry exclaimed energetically, vowing by Jupiter that Sampson should have his horse back. He gave it to the chaplain as a gift, an offer the priest accepted by seizing Harry's hand, blessing him, throwing his arms around the horse's neck, and weeping tears of Bordeaux wine and gratitude.

Arm in arm, the friends walked to Madame Bernstein's, carrying the scents of the stables into her ladyship's apartment. Their flushed cheeks and brightened eyes testified to the day's amusement. Many gentlemen's cheeks naturally flushed in those days for similar reasons. Madame Bernstein received her nephew's chaplain kindly. She relished Sampson's broad jokes and rapid chatter, much as she enjoyed a highly spiced dish or a novel entree from her cook, provided it appeared only twice or thrice. The only activity she never grew tired of, she confessed, was cards.

"The cards don't cheat," she often remarked. "A bad hand tells you the truth to your face; and there is nothing so flattering in the world as a good suit of trumps." When in a good humor, sitting down to her favorite pastime, she would laughingly bid her nephew's chaplain say grace before the meal.

At first, honest Sampson hesitated to join the game at Tunbridge Wells. Her ladyship's stakes were too high, he admitted with a comical, pitiful slap to his pocket, noting that his funds had already been transferred to the fortunate youth at Castlewood. Like most of her generation, Madame Bernstein was not prodigal with money. Presumably, it was from Harry Warrington, whose heart overflowed with generosity as much as his purse with guineas, that the chaplain secured a small sum of ready coin, enabling him soon to sit at the card table.

Our young gentleman welcomed Mr. Sampson to his coins as readily as he shared all the other good things he had gathered. It was remarkable how quickly the young Virginian adapted to the customs of the people among whom he lived. His suits remained black, yet of the finest cut and quality. "With a star and ribbon, stockings pulled down, and hair flowing over his shoulders, he would make a pretty Hamlet," observed the gay old Duchess Queensberry. "And I make no doubt he has been the death of a dozen Ophelias already, here and amongst the Indians," she added, thinking no less of Harry for his alleged success with the fair sex.

Harry's lace and linen were as exquisite as his aunt could desire. He purchased fine shaving plates from toy-shop women and a pair of magnificent brocade bedgowns in which he lounged at ease, sipping his morning chocolate. He owned swords, walking canes, French watches with painted backs and diamond settings, and snuffboxes enamelled by artists of that cunning nation. A levee of grooms, jockeys, and tradesmen waited daily in his anteroom, admitted one by one to him and Parson Sampson over their chocolate by Gumbo, the groom of the chambers. We have no record of exactly how many men Mr. Gumbo now served, but it is certain that no single Negro could have managed all the fine possessions Mr. Warrington now held, let alone the horses and the postchaise his honor had purchased.

Furthermore, Harry instructed himself in the arts proper to a gentleman of the era. A French fencing-master and a dancing-master of the same nationality resided at Tunbridge during that season. The young Virginian attended their classes assiduously, acquiring considerable skill and grace in both peaceful and martial accomplishments. Within weeks, he could handle foils against his master or any frequent visitor of the fencing school. With a sigh, Lady Maria—who danced very elegantly herself—confessed that no gentleman at court could perform a minuet more gracefully than Mr. Warrington.

Regarding riding, although Mr. Warrington took a few lessons on a large horse from a master who visited Tunbridge, he declared that their native Virginian style was sufficient. He claimed to see no one among the fine folk and jockeys who could ride better than his friend, Colonel George Washington of Mount Vernon.

The obsequious Sampson found himself in better quarters than he had known for a long time. He knew much of the world and told even more, and Harry was delighted with his stories, whether true or fabricated. The man of twenty looked up to the man of thirty, admiring the latter's old jokes, stale puns, and tarnished anecdotes, seasoned with the wine of a hundred dinner tables. Sampson's urban and collegiate witticisms were all new and charming to the young Virginian. A hundred years ago—no doubt such people do not exist today—there were grown men in London who loved to associate with fashionable youths entering life, tickling their young fancies with merry stories, acting as mentors at Covent Garden, accompanying lads to the gaming table, drinking lemonade to replace Master Hopeful's Burgundy, and staggering through the streets with perfectly cool heads while young lords reeled out to beat the watch.
