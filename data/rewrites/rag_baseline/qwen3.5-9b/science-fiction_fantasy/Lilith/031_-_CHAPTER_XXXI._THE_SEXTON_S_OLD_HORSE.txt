I stood and watched the last gleam of the white leopardess melt into the void, then turned to follow my guide—reluctantly. What purpose served sleep when the hour demanded vigilance? Reason was constant across the cosmos; to succumb to unconsciousness while the living called was illogical. Yet, no one would rouse me, and certainty of awakening was a fantasy. In that house, morning bled indistinguishably into noon, and noon into eternal night; the inhabitants were statues of suspended animation. I murmured my protest, but compliance was the only variable remaining.

The librarian advanced in silence, a lean, long-coated silhouette against the encroaching gloom. I matched his cadence, moving through a landscape where time and space dissolved into a uniform gray. Sunset descended, casting the air thick with the chilling radiance of the Chamber of Death. My resolve eroded with every step. The figure ahead vanished into the heather, his swishing stride swallowed by the dark. Soon, only the slow, rhythmic flapping of a raven's wings pierced the silence, punctuated occasionally by the erratic rise of a firefly or a luminous butterfly drifting into the rayless atmosphere.

Then, the moon ascended, a slow, deliberate crossing of the distant horizon.

"You are weary, Mr. Vane?" the raven asked, landing heavily on a stone. "You must acquaint yourself with the beast that will bear you tomorrow!"

A strange whistle tore from his long black beak. Simultaneously, a blemish marred the lunar surface. Instantly, the sound of swift, soft-galloping hooves drummed against the ether. Within minutes, the terrible steed erupted from the very disc of the moon. His mane streamed behind him like the crest of a wind-defying wave, torn seaward in hoary spray, while the lash of his tail blinded the lunar gaze. Nineteen hands high, massive in bone, taut in skin, dense in muscle—he was a mount worthy of Holy Death himself to ride into slaughter. Even the moon regarded him with awe; in her spectral light, he appeared a skeletal framework, loosely lashed together by gravity. Yet, terrifyingly large, he moved with the deceptive lightness of a winged insect.

As he closed the distance, his velocity moderated, his flowing mane and tail settling into a chaotic drift. I was no mere admirer of equine form; I possessed every horse I encountered. My currency had always been spent solely on steeds, never withdrawn. This creature, monstrous in appearance, ignited a primal craving within me. It was pure greed, rank covetousness—an evil impulse transcending worlds. I did not intend theft, but possession regardless of propriety; I would have purchased him if the market allowed.

I placed my hands upon him, stroking the protuberant bones that humped a hide smooth, thin, and glossy as satin. So polished was the coat that the moon's reflection warped within it. I caressed his sharp, pointed ears, whispering endearments, and breathed the warm air of humanity into his red nostrils. In response, he exhaled the raw, musk-laden breath of a horse's life into my lungs. We locked gazes. What eyes he possessed! Blue-filmy, like the sockets of the dead, yet behind each orb burned a glowing coal. The raven, wings half-extended, observed our bonding with evident satisfaction.

"That is well," the bird croaked. "Forge a friendship; he will carry you further tomorrow. Now, haste toward the cottage!"

My desire to mount grew feverish. "May I not ascend at once, Mr. Raven?" I cried.

"By all means!" he answered. "Ride him home."

The horse bowed his head over my shoulder with affectionate submission. I twisted my fingers into his mane, scrambling onto his back, aided by the very ridges of his spine. "He would outrun any leopard in creation!" I shouted.

"Not that path at night," the raven corrected sharply. "The terrain is treacherous. Loss incurred now becomes gain later; waiting is harder than running, and its reward is fuller. Proceed, my son—straight to the cottage. Your mother's heart will rejoice to see her son astride such a beast!"

I remained motionless. The horse stood rigid as a block of marble. "Why do you hesitate?" the raven demanded.

"I yearn so desperately to pursue the spotted leopardess," I admitted, "that I cannot restrain myself."

"You have pledged your word!"

"My debt to the Little Ones outweighs my bond to you."

"Yield to this temptation, and you shall bring ruin upon them—and upon yourself."

"What consequence matters to me? I love them; love commits no evil. I shall proceed."

The truth was, I had forgotten the children, intoxicated by the animal. Shadows flickered in the darkness, and I recognized Adam standing in his own form beside me. His voice carried a suppressed indignation, barely contained.

"Mr. Vane," Adam said, "do you not comprehend why you have accomplished nothing of value thus far?"

"Because I have been a fool."

"In what respect?"

"In every aspect."

"What counts as your most indiscreet act?"

"Awakening the Princess. I should have permitted her to meet her destined end."

"Nonsense! You could not have known she was malevolent. Furthermore, you never resurrected anyone! How could you, given that you are already dead?"

"I dead?" I exclaimed.

"Yes," he replied calmly. "And you shall remain dead so long as you refuse to accept it."

"Back to the riddles!" I retorted scornfully.

"Be persuaded and return with me," Adam continued gently. "The single, nearly sole folly you committed was fleeing from our dead."

I pressed the horse's ribs, and he surged forward like a sudden gust of wind. I pat the side of his neck as he executed a sharp, driven curve—"close to the ground, like a cat wheeling after a mouse"—leaning sideways until his mane brushed the tops of the heather. Through the obscurity, I heard the raven's wings beating five rapid times before he settled atop the horse's head.

The horse halted instantly, digging his hooves into the earth.

"Mr. Vane," the raven croaked, "consider your actions. Evil has struck you twice—once from fear, once from negligence. Breach of promise is far worse; it is a crime."

"The Little Ones face imminent peril, and I caused it!" I cried. "Yet I cannot break my vow to you. I shall return and endure whatever nights, days, or years you require."

"I warn you once more: going tonight brings harm to them," the raven insisted.

A false sense of power, vibrating from the sheer strength of the steed, had rendered me deaf to reason. "Will you deny me my last opportunity for reparation?" I demanded. "This time, there shall be no evasion! It is my duty, and I shall go—even if I perish!"

"Go then, foolish boy!" the raven returned, anger coloring his croak. "Take the horse and ride to failure! May it lead to humility!"

With a beat of his wings, he took flight. I pressed the lean ribs beneath me. "After the spotted leopardess!" I whispered in his ear.

He turned his head, sniffing the air, then paused in a slow, indecisive walk. Suddenly, he quickened to a trot, breaking into a gallop whose speed became tremendous. He navigated the darkness with uncanny vision, stumbling not once, faltering not once, hesitating not once. I sat balanced on the ridge of a wave, feeling the distinct play of every muscle beneath me. His joints were elastic; his movements glided seamlessly into one another, creating zero jar. His accelerating swiftness transformed him into something flying rather than running. The wind roared past us like a tornado.

Across the evil hollow, we sped like a bolt from an arbalest. No monster dared lift its neck; all sensed the thunder of hooves overhead. We rushed up the hills, shot down their far slopes, and traversed the rocky chasms of the riverbed without swerving, holding a fierce, terrible gallop over the abyss. The moon, halfway up the heavens, gazed with solemn trouble in her pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and the pride of my existence, I sat like a king.

We neared the middle of the many channels; the horse cleared one, sometimes two, in every stride, gathering momentum for a great bounding leap as the moon reached the keystone of her arch. Then came wonder and terror: she began to descend, rolling like the nave of Fortune's wheel, propelled by unseen gods, spinning faster and faster. Like our own moon, this celestial body possessed a human face, alternating between broad forehead and chin as it rotated. I gazed in horror. From the ravines below came the howling of wolves. An ugly dread invaded the hollows of my heart; my confidence crumbled.

The horse maintained his headlong swiftness, ears pricked forward, nostrils thirsting for the wind of his career. But above, the moon jolted like an ancient chariot-wheel down the hill of heaven, radiating awful omen. Finally, she rolled over the horizon's edge and vanished, extinguishing all her light.
