The church wall bore letters of fire—a celestial demand questioning why no masses were said for the duke in these troubled times. Many witnessed the apparition, which lingered before fading, leaving the townsfolk gaping in awe. The town fathers, unwilling to be outdone in loyalty by heavenly messengers, promptly funded a flood of masses. Our old acquaintance, the cure, lined his pockets handsomely—for he had a fine appreciation of such windfalls. Were it not for this profitable miracle, I would not trouble you with so common a spectacle.

Denys pleaded for the return of his weapons. *"Must you shame him as well as break his heart?"* he argued. The answer came: *"Swear upon the cross of your sword never to abandon the bastard’s service until this rebellion is crushed."* Reluctantly, he yielded, loosing a volley of oaths before reclaiming his arms and his freedom.

The company halted at *The Three Fish*, where Marion, spotting Denys, exclaimed, *"Bad luck for me! Who’d have thought to see you again?"* Noting his wounded expression, she softened. *"What’s amiss?"* He told her of Gerard’s flight. Ever practical, Marion brightened. *"Gerard’s too handsome and well-mannered to come to harm. Women will see him safe. If fortune favors him, he’ll join some decent company bound for Rome. If not, he’ll turn home—perhaps even beat you there, given the fighting ahead in Flanders."* She then returned his gold, steadfastly refusing a single coin despite his insistence. Her honesty lifted his spirits somewhat, offering a glimmer of hope in a world where his friend now wandered alone.

Scattered bands of foot soldiers, some penniless, trudged toward Flanders, sustained by town bailiffs who ordered innkeepers to feed and lodge them. Anthony of Burgundy swept them up as he passed, swelling his ranks until he crossed the frontier at the head of six hundred men. There, his brother Baldwyn met him with reinforcements—arms, provisions, and fresh recruits. They marched in battle array through loyal towns, greeted not with resistance but cheers.

*"This rebellion’s a farce,"* grumbled Simon, a soldier who had latched onto Denys. Denys said nothing but silently vowed to Saint Mars that if this rising—which had cost him Gerard—proved hollow, he would put a bolt through Anthony of Burgundy himself.

That afternoon, they sighted a fortified town—rumored disloyal. Yet its gates stood open, the flanking towers manned by lone sentinels. The army relaxed, breaking formation as they neared. Then, a furlong away, the drawbridge rose with a groan, the portcullis crashed down, and the walls bristled with lances. A low, fierce murmur ran through the ranks.

*"Halt!"* roared Anthony.

A herald spurred forward, sounding his bugle. A tall figure in gleaming brass armor appeared above the gate. A brief, heated exchange—inaudible but unmistakable—ended with the herald sounding a defiant note before galloping back, face dark with war.

*"Pioneers, to the front!"* Anthony commanded.

Hedges fell, the army fanned out, and the siege began.

For Denys, the clang of the portcullis was a tonic. His eyes gleamed; his heart quickened. As the herald returned, battle in his gaze, Denys laughed aloud for the first time in days. *"Courage, all! The devil’s dead!"*

The besiegers encamped just beyond arrow range. Sappers, pioneers, and crossbowmen advanced, laboring under a hail of missiles to raise a stockade at the moat’s edge. Denys, behind his mantelet, traded shots with an English archer whose arrows thudded perilously close. *"You shoot well, friend!"* he called. *"Step out and take your praise!"*

Among the defenders, one figure stood out—a tall knight in brass armor, directing the resistance with a long lance. Arrows clattered harmlessly off his mail. He strode the battlements, impervious, as if the shafts were mere rain. The besiegers, irked, cursed his invulnerability.

Gunpowder would later rob war of its artistry. But here, in the age of visible valor, the siege unfolded like a grim ballet. Catapults hurled stones; trebuchets flung fire. A *cat*—a covered ramp—crept toward the wall, filling the moat with fascines until the knight, with a flaming tar-barrel, forced it back.

Then came the wooden tower—rolling forward on hidden wheels, a monstrous siege engine bristling with armored men. The knight fired the mine beneath it. The tower lurched, tilted, and collapsed, spilling soldiers like ants from a crushed hill.

Amid the chaos, Denys spotted the knight balancing on a beam, thrusting fire-bags into the wreckage with his lance. Seizing his chance, Denys darted from cover, loosed a bolt—and missed the knight but struck the pikeman steadying him. The knight toppled, armor flashing, and plunged into the moat.

A moment later, Denys hissed as an English arrow pinned his arm. He retreated, clutching the wound, just as the knight—miraculously alive—was dragged from the water and hauled before Anthony.

*"A rebel knight?"* Anthony sneered.

Anthony scoffed. *"Then you’ll die at a stake, riddled with arrows."*

The hosier stammered, *"H-hang your b-b-brother Baldwyn first."*

A letter changed everything—a royal summons for Anthony to escort an English princess to Burgundy. The hosier, sensing advantage, offered a bribe: *"A th-th-thousand angels to end this."*

By dinner, under a flag of truce, the two dined and struck a deal.

*"But why rebel?"* Anthony asked.

Denys, nursing his wound, received a purse from the Comte de Jarnac for his service.

*"Your grief has passed?"* the comte asked.

The end came swifter than he expected. Released from duty the next day, Denys set out, wounded but resolute, to find his friend.
