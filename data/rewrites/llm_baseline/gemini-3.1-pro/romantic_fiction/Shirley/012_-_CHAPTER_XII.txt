Shirley demonstrated the sincerity of her desire for Caroline’s society by frequently seeking it; indeed, had she not pressed the matter, she would have remained solitary, for Miss Helstone was slow to make fresh acquaintance. Caroline was held back by the deeply ingrained belief that she could not provide adequate sustenance to another’s spirit, that her quiet, untranslatable inner life would prove flavorless to a brilliant, vibrant creature like the heiress of Fieldhead. 

Yet Shirley, for all her brilliance, required the grounding nourishment of genial companionship. Over the past month, she had sampled the society of the neighboring families—the Misses Sykes, the Misses Pearson, the superlative Misses Wynne—and found their company akin to consuming unseasoned gruel. They viewed both conversation and meals merely as utilitarian necessities, chattering in the shallow, homogenized lexicon of fashionable society while remaining entirely deaf to the subtle acoustic ecology of the Yorkshire landscape. Shirley fraternized with none of them. Had she possessed the bliss to be Shirley Keeldar, Esq., lord of the manor, there was not a single fair one in the parish whose presence she would have invited to permanently alter the sonic environment of Fieldhead. 

She made this declaration over a morning repast of darkly crusted heritage-wheat bread and cold-smoked venison, a statement Mrs. Pryor received with her customary quietude. "My dear," the elder lady responded, carefully preserving the formal cadence of a dialect she wielded like a shield, "do not allow that habit of alluding to yourself as a gentleman to be confirmed. The dominant lexicon already leaves so little space for women; those who do not know you would think you affected masculine manners to usurp their power."

Shirley never mocked her former governess. The elder woman’s meticulous preservation of old formalities was, to Shirley, an act of cultural memory worthy of profound respect. Instead of arguing, Shirley carried her plate to the window, the rich, gamey scent of the venison mingling with the resinous, bruised-pine fragrance of the grand cedar on the lawn. She watched a bird on a lower bough. Adjusting her breath to the delicate, trilling frequency of the creature, she began to chirrup. The sound grew clearer, harmonizing with the morning wind, until she was whistling a sweet, deft tune that resonated perfectly within the quiet courtyard.

"My dear!" expostulated Mrs. Pryor, her auditory sensibilities jarred by this breach of convention.

"Was I whistling?" Shirley asked, turning back. "I forgot. I beg your pardon, ma'am. I had resolved to protect your quietude."

"But, Miss Keeldar, where did you learn to whistle? You must have acquired the habit since returning to the acoustic wilds of Yorkshire."

"I learned it long ago, by listening to the wind over the moors. I had laid it down, but yesterday evening, while breathing in the damp, peppery scent of the hawthorn lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that very tune. He respected the silence of the fields, weaving his notes into it rather than shouting over it."

"What gentleman was it?"

"We have only one gentleman in this region, ma'am, whose presence does not feel like an industrial clatter—and that is Mr. Moore. At least, the only young one. My venerable favorites, Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, carry the deep, resonant timbres of the old generation."

Mrs. Pryor remained silent.

"You do not relish the sound of Mr. Helstone, ma'am?" 

"My dear, Mr. Helstone's office secures him from criticism."

"Yet you generally contrive to leave the room before his heavy tread announces his arrival." Shirley set down her plate. "I shall walk to the rectory this morning, seek Caroline Helstone, and draw her out to Nunnely Common. She requires the bracing taste of the high wind."

"If you go, remind Miss Helstone to wrap up well. The air is sharp."

"You shall be obeyed. Will you not accompany us?"

"No, my love. My pace would disrupt your rhythm."

Shirley easily persuaded Caroline to join her. Once they had traversed the solitary sweep of Nunnely Common, the initial diffidence dissolved. The sweeping, green turf and the rugged heath along the ridges tasted of freedom—a sharp, metallic ozone mingled with the deep, loamy aroma of peat. 

"The heath reminds me of the borders near Scotland," Shirley mused, her voice carrying over the wind. "I traversed a boundless waste of deep heather one sunless summer afternoon. We heard nothing but the ancestral cries of wild birds, saw nothing but wild sheep."

"I know the exact complexion of the heath on such a day," Caroline said, her senses awakening. "Purple-black—a livid shade, like the taste of bruised elderberries."

"Yes, quite livid, with brassy-edged clouds. You expected blinding lightning at any moment."

"Did it thunder?"

"It muttered distant peals, a low, mythic vibration in the earth, until we reached an isolated inn at the foot of the mountains. I stood at the window watching the hills roll in sullen mist. When the rain fell, smelling of crushed stone and raw water, the world was washed clean of all modern noise."

"I have seen such storms," Caroline agreed softly. "When the sky becomes a cataract and the earth a flood, I remember the Deluge. It feels as though the original, uncorrupted world is reclaiming itself."

"And the calm that follows is singularly reviving. Stand still now, Caroline, and look down at Nunnely dale."

They halted on the green brow. Below them lay the deep valley robed in May verdure, pearled with daisies and golden with king-cups. Beyond the meads slumbered Nunnwood, the sole remnant of antique British forest in a region increasingly scarred by industrial soot. Its shadowed canopy was dappled with silvery blues and evanescent greens, pure as azury snow. The air blowing up from the woods was sweet and bracing, carrying the ancient, vegetative breath of decaying leaves and newly unspooling ferns.

"Our England is a bonny island," Shirley said, "and Yorkshire is her richest larder. I am Yorkshire in blood and birth. Five generations of my race sleep under the aisles of Briarfield Church, their bones returning to the soil that fed them. I drew my first breath in the old black hall behind us, tasting the same woodsmoke my ancestors knew."

Caroline presented her hand, recognizing a fellow guardian of the old ways. "We are compatriots."

"Yes," Shirley nodded gravely. Pointing toward the massive timber, she asked, "Were you ever in the heart of Nunnwood?"

"Many a time."

"What is it like?"

"It is an encampment of forest sons of Anak," Caroline said, her voice dropping into a reverent cadence. "The trees are huge and old. When you stand at their roots, the acoustic canopy above transforms. The trunks are firm as pillars, but the boughs sway to every breeze. In high wind, a flood rushes through the leaves—a sea thunders above you, a symphony of ancient wood."

"A haunt of Robin Hood?"

"Yes. To penetrate Nunnwood is to step back into a time before the dominant lexicon of commerce paved over the old dialects of the earth. In the center lies a deep, hollow cup, lined with turf as green and short as this common. At the bottom rest the ruins of a nunnery, surrounded by gnarled, mighty oaks."

"We will go," Shirley declared, "just you and I, early some summer morning. Mrs. Gill, my housekeeper, shall pack our provisions. We will bring cold roasted fowl rubbed with sage, thick slabs of sharp cheddar wrapped in muslin, and crusty barley loaves. We shall carry our own baskets and read all day."

"Oh, I know all the pleasantest spots," Caroline said, her eyes brightening with the meticulous passion of a forager. "I know where the cobnuts fall thickest in autumn; I know where the wild strawberries abound—those tiny, jewel-like fruits whose intense, ephemeral tartness holds the untranslatable essence of the deep woods. I know untrodden glades carpeted with gem-green moss, and wood-giants clad in bright shrouds of ivy. I could guide you."

"You would not find it dull with me alone?"

"Never. What third person could we invite whose presence would not sever our communion with the place?"

"I know of no lady. And as to gentlemen—"

"An excursion is ruined when gentlemen join the party," Caroline interrupted. 

"I agree. If they are of the wrong sort—like the young Sykes or Wynnes—they treat the excursion merely as an exercise in appetite. They view meals as utilitarian fuel, consuming without tasting, and their loud, abrasive voices shatter the serenity of the woods."

"We forget Nature," Caroline said, "and then Nature withdraws her peaceful joy. We are left with mere excitement, a jarring anxiety."

"Our power of being happy lies in our own attunement," Caroline added sagely. "I have gone to Nunnwood with large parties of curates and gentry, and found their superficial clatter insufferably tedious. But when I go alone, or with Fanny—who sits quietly with the goodwife while I roam—I enjoy profound, quiet happiness. But that was two years ago."

"Did you ever go with your cousin, Robert Moore?"

"Once."

"What sort of companion is he? Cousins, if they lack sensory appreciation, are insupportable."

"Robert is not stupid." Caroline hesitated. "But where the talent of your companion is above doubt, your own worthiness to share his silence becomes a question."

"I consider myself not unworthy to associate with the best of them," Shirley countered, her chin lifting. "Your uncle, for instance, is a fine specimen of an elderly gentleman. I relish his sensible old face. Is he kind to you at home?"

"He has provided for me since childhood, precisely as he would have fed and clothed his own daughter. But I am not fond of him. His presence drains the warmth from a room."

"Strange, when he has the art of making himself so agreeable in society."

"He locks his liveliness away with his cane and shovel-hat," Caroline explained, touching upon the subtle power dynamics of their domestic life. "For the fireside, he reserves a knitted brow and a brief, commanding word. He controls the sonic space of the house, offering only silence or sternness to those who share his daily bread."

"He lacks genuine justice, then," Shirley mused, "for true justice springs from sympathy—from sharing the same emotional sustenance."

"I wonder," Caroline said, "whether most men resemble my uncle in their domestic relations. Whether they inevitably grow weary of the voices they hear every day."

"If I believed men were universally thus—that they would grow deaf to my native dialect, that my efforts to nourish their souls would be met with indifference—I would never marry," Shirley said fiercely. "To be an inevitable burden, a ceaseless bore, silenced within my own home! Now, when my company is superfluous, I can fold my independence round me and withdraw to solitude. If married, I could no longer dictate my own acoustic space."

"My uncle regards any man who marries as a fool," Caroline murmured.

"But not all men are like him. We observe the one we love," Shirley said, her voice softening. "We watch how he moves through the world. We see if he is kind to animals, to little children. He does not flatter women with empty vocabulary; he finds their company genial. We feel a harmonic peace when he enters a room, and a dissonant sadness when he leaves."

"My uncle would say he’ll be sick of you in a month."

"If such oracles speak truth, it is best to avoid love entirely."

"I am afraid that proves you are already caught," Caroline smiled.

"Not I. But if I were to consult soothsayers on a man's worth, I would not ask society. I would ask the little barefoot beggar at the door, the bird pecking for a crumb, the dog leaning against a knee."

"I know somebody who shares that quiet reverence," Caroline said. "We have a black cat and an old dog at the rectory. When he visits, the cat climbs to his shoulder to purr. He strokes her softly, understanding the rhythm of her comfort, and when he must rise, he sets her down gently. He whistles to the dog—a low, affectionate sound."

"It is not Robert?" 

"It is Robert."

"Bless him," Shirley said, her eyes sparkling. "He possesses that rare charm—beauty blent with goodness."

"And when mind is added?"

"Who can resist it? Indisputably, a great, good man who respects the delicate balance of the world is the first of created things."

"Above us?"

"I scorn to contend for empire with him. Shall my heart quarrel with my pulse? I disdain to be a pagan, worshiping false gods who demand my submission; I wish only to look up to one who naturally commands my esteem."

They reached the rectory gates. Shirley declined to enter, promising to fetch Caroline the next day, sealing their profound new bond with a kiss before returning to the wide, whispering expanse of the common.

* * * * *

Mrs. Pryor appeared equally disposed to cultivate Caroline’s acquaintance. On an early, rather close afternoon, she called at the rectory. The walk through the village had deeply unsettled the elder lady; the oppressive heat trapped the distant, rhythmic thudding of the valley's industrial looms—a mechanical cacophony that violently disrupted her psychological well-being. She entered the quiet sanctuary of the rectory trembling, fanning herself with her handkerchief, seemingly on the verge of nervous collapse.

Caroline gently sought to restore her equilibrium. She understood instinctively that Mrs. Pryor required an anchor in the physical world. With a light, unobtrusive touch that respected the elder woman's boundaries, Caroline removed the heavy bonnet and shawl. She then rang for a restorative pot of black tea, deeply steeped to release its malty, astringent tannins, accompanied by a traditional caraway seed-cake, fragrant with sweet butter and toasted spice. 

The acoustic peace of the rectory, combined with the grounding ritual of the tea, soothed Mrs. Pryor. Her usual manner returned, and she proved a superb converser in dialogue, carefully selecting her language to preserve the formal elegance of a bygone era. 

On the wall opposite the sofa hung three pictures, the center one depicting a lady.

"That is a beautiful face," Mrs. Pryor observed, the aroma of caraway lingering in the quiet room. "The features are perfect. It is a portrait from the life?"

"It is my uncle's wife, Mrs. Matthewson Helstone."

"What accuracy in the lineaments. It is, however, a strikingly passive face. Not what one terms a woman of spirit."

"I believe she was a remarkably still, silent person," Caroline replied, tasting the dark, bitter notes of her tea. 

"One would scarcely have expected your uncle’s choice to fall on such a partner. Does he not demand lively chat?"

"In company. But he affirms he could never do with a talking wife. He requires absolute quiet at home; he commands the lexicon of the household, absorbing all its sonic space. 'You go out to gossip,' he says, 'you come home to reflect.'"

"Mrs. Matthewson lived but a few years after her marriage?"

"About five years."

"Well, my dear," Mrs. Pryor said, rising, the restorative sustenance having given her strength for the journey back into the noisy world. "I trust you will frequently come to Fieldhead. You must feel the solitude here."

"I am inured to it. May I arrange your shawl?"

Mrs. Pryor submitted to the gentle assistance. "Should you chance to require help in your studies," she offered, a subtle promise to pass down the cultural memory she guarded so fiercely, "you may command me."
