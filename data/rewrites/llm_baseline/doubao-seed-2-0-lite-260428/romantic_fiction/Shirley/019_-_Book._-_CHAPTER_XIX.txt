Dusk draped the Briarfield lanes soft as gossamer, and the crisp, clean midsummer air seemed to coax the first faint points of starlight out to glimmer across the wide, cloudless sky.
“There’s just enough glow left to light my path all the way home,” Shirley Keeldar said, pausing at the rectory garden gate where Caroline Helstone lingered to see her off.
“You cannot go wandering alone after dark, Shirley. I’ll send Fanny to walk you back to Fieldhead,” Caroline protested, her fingers brushing the sleeve of Shirley’s light muslin gown.
“That you will not. What on earth have I to fear in my own parish?” Shirley laughed, her bright voice carrying on the warm breeze. “I’d stroll the three miles from Fieldhead to the church at three hours past midnight on a fine midsummer evening, just to watch the stars wheel and catch a glimpse of a fairy dancing in the fern fronds at the lane edge.”
“At least wait until all the crowds from the service have scattered,” Caroline urged.
“Fair enough. Look, the five Misses Armitage are drifting by now, gossiping arm in arm. There’s Mrs. Sykes’s phaeton trotting past, Mr. Wynne’s closed carriage, Mrs. Birtwhistle’s little cart. I’ve no desire to go through the tedious formality of waving goodbye to every last one of them, so let’s duck back into the garden for a minute and hide under the laburnum boughs.”
The rector, his two curates, and the churchwardens soon stepped out of the church porch in a babble of chatter, shaking hands, clapping each other on the back, congratulating one another on well-delivered speeches, and warning every passerby to wrap up warm against the night dew. Bit by bit the throng dispersed, the last carriage wheels rumbled away down the road, and Shirley was just stepping out from behind the golden dripping laburnum blooms when Mr. Helstone rounded the garden path and almost ran right into her.
“Ah, there you are! I was afraid you’d already set off for home,” he said, grinning. “Caroline, come here, will you?”
Caroline hurried over, both young women half expecting a scolding for vanishing mid-church service earlier that evening, but the rector’s mind was fixed on far more urgent matters.
“I won’t be sleeping at the rectory tonight,” he explained. “I ran into an old army friend of mine only ten minutes ago, and promised to ride back to his inn with him. I’ll be back around noon tomorrow. Thomas the parish clerk is otherwise engaged, so I can’t get him to stay here to watch the house like I usually do when I’m gone for a night. Now—”
“Now you need me, the self-appointed first gentleman of Briarfield, to step into your post,” Shirley cut in, eyes sparkling. “Be master of the rectory, and stand guard over your niece and your two maids while you’re off gallivanting, isn’t that it?”
“Exactly, Captain. Knew the post would suit you,” the rector chuckled. “Will you do Caroline the favour of staying here as her guest for the night, instead of trekking all the way back to Fieldhead after dark?”
“But Mrs. Pryor will be waiting up for me, expecting me home,” Shirley protested gently.
“I’ll send a boy over at once to leave her a note explaining the whole situation. Come, make up your mind. The dew’s falling heavy, and it’s getting far too late to traipse across the fields. You and Caroline will have far more fun catching up with each other than you would alone at Fieldhead, I’d wager.”
“Then I promise to stay,” Shirley said at once, turning to squeeze Caroline’s hand. “You’re right, we won’t waste a single minute of the night apart. Go on now, meet your old friend, and don’t worry about a single thing here.”
“If there happens to be any trouble in the dark hours, Captain—if you hear a lock being jimmied, or a pane of glass being cut, or soft stealthy steps creeping around the house—and I don’t mind telling you, what with that sharp, brave heart of yours under all that lace and ribbon, those little incidents are all too common these days—what would you do?”
“Can’t say for sure. Might faint dead away and make Caroline carry me to the sofa,” Shirley joked, “but if you’re posting me on sentry duty, you’ve got to give me weapons. What’s stowed in your little stronghold, doctor?”
“You don’t know how to wield a sword, I suppose?”
“Not a chance. I’d do far better with a carving knife.”
“There’s a lovely sharp one in the dining room sideboard,” he told her. “It’s a lady’s knife, light as a feather in your hand, and pointed as a tiny poniard.”
“That one will suit Caroline. But you’ve got pistols, I know you do. Give me a brace of those.”
“I’ve two pairs total. I’ll lend you one pair. They’re hanging over my study mantelpiece in their cloth cases.”
“Loaded?”
“Aye, but not cocked. Remember to cock them both before you turn in for the night. This is a great compliment, you know. If you were one of those clumsy lads who can barely hold a gun without shooting their own foot off, I’d never hand them over.”
“I’ll be careful. You don’t need to hang around here any longer, Mr. Helstone. Off you go,” Shirley laughed, waving him through the garden gate. “He’s awfully gracious to lend me his pistols, isn’t he?” she murmured to Caroline as the rector vanished down the lane. “Come on, Lina, let’s go in and get some supper. I was so thoroughly annoyed sitting next to Mr. Sam Wynne at tea that I couldn’t eat a single bite, and now I’m absolutely ravenous.”
They stepped inside, making their way to the dim dining room, where the wide open windows let the soft night air drift in, heavy with rose and jasmine scent from the garden, the faint far echo of last stragglers’ footsteps on the road, and a low, thrumming murmur Caroline paused to listen to, leaning her elbows on the windowsill.
“Shirley, I can hear the beck rushing over the stones down in the Hollow,” she whispered.
She rang the bell, asking Fanny to bring a candle and two plates of bread and milk, the simple supper both of them preferred. When Fanny arrived with the tray, she moved to close the windows and shutter them, but both girls asked her to leave them propped open. The twilight was too calm, its breath too sweet, to lock out just yet. They ate their quiet meal in soft, easy silence, until Caroline rose to move the overscented vase of carnations from the sideboard to the windowsill, where their perfume wouldn’t cling thick in the sultry room. On her way back, she tugged a side drawer half open, and drew out the sharp, glinting carving knife the rector had mentioned.
“So you assigned this one to me, Shirley?” she said, turning the blade over in her fingers. “It’s bright, sharp-edged, tapering to a fine point. Dangerous-looking. I can’t even imagine what it would feel like to point this at another human being. I don’t think I could ever summon the strength to drive it home.”
“I’d hate to do it too,” Shirley replied, sipping her glass of cold fresh milk, her face thoughtful and pale as ever, “but I know I would, if there was no other way to protect the people I care for.”
When they’d finished their supper, they called Fanny and Eliza back in, urging the two tired maids to head straight to bed after a long day of baking currant buns, filling teapots, and running up and down stairs with trays. Within minutes, they heard the maids’ bedroom door click shut down the hall. Caroline took a candle and wandered through every room in the house, making sure every window was latched tight and every door barred, even the spooky back kitchen and the vault-like cellars. When she returned to the dining room, it was nearly eleven o’clock.
“Every door and window’s locked, no strangers hiding anywhere inside,” she said. “It’s almost bedtime, but I don’t want to turn in just yet, if you don’t mind, Shirley. I brought the two pistols out of uncle’s study for you.” She set them on the table in front of Shirley.
“Why don’t you want to sleep?” Shirley asked, picking up the firearms one by one, checking the mechanisms gently before setting them back down.
“I’ve got this strange, jittery, wide-awake feeling in my chest.”
“So do I.”
“Is it something electrical in the air, do you think?”
“No, look, the sky’s perfectly clear, there are more stars than I’ve ever seen. It’s a perfect night.”
“But it’s so still. I can hear the water in the Hollow chattering over its stony bed as clearly as if it was running right under the churchyard wall.”
“I’m glad it’s quiet,” Shirley said. “If there was wind howling or rain lashing down, I think it would drive me half feverish right now.”
“Why is that, Shirley?”
“Because I wouldn’t be able to hear properly.”
“You’re listening towards the Hollow, aren’t you?”
“Yes. It’s the only direction any sound could possibly come from right now.”
They settled side by side by the window, leaning their arms on the sill, their faces lit soft by starlight and that faint lingering June twilight that never fully fades from the western sky until the first hint of dawn bleeds pink in the east.
“Mr. Helstone thinks we have no idea where he’s gone, or what he’s planning to do,” Shirley murmured, “but I think I’ve guessed most of it. Haven’t you?”
“I’ve guessed some of it,” Caroline whispered back.
“All those men out there—your cousin Robert Moore included—think we’re fast asleep in our beds, none the wiser.”
“That we don’t care a thing about them, that we’ve no hopes or fears for them at all,” Caroline added.
They fell quiet for half an hour, only the church clock chiming the quarters marking the slow passage of the night. When the air grew chill, they wrapped their silk scarves tighter around their shoulders, tucked their bonnets back on, and kept watching.
Just before midnight, the rectory’s guard dog started barking, sharp and nonstop. Caroline slipped noiselessly through the dark kitchen passages to give him a piece of bread to quiet him, and when she came back to the dining room, the candle was out. Shirley was leaning far out the open window, her dark hair catching the faint starlight. Caroline didn’t say a word, just slipped to her side. The dog started barking again, furious, then fell abruptly silent.
They both strained their ears. It wasn’t just the rush of the mill stream they could hear now. There was a nearer, muffled, rhythmic tramp of feet on the road below the churchyard—hundreds of feet, marching slow and steady, drawing closer.
They couldn’t see a thing, the thick leafy hedge in the garden blocking their view of the road, but the tramp grew louder, until a sharp, rough voice cut the silence.
“Halt!”
The march stopped dead. There was a low huddle of whispering voices, no single word clear from where they stood.
“We have to hear what they’re saying,” Shirley breathed.
She grabbed the two pistols off the table, slipped silently through the glass dining room door that opened right onto the garden path, and stole down to the high garden wall, pressing herself against it under the bloom-heavy lilac bushes. Caroline would never have dared leave the house alone, but she would follow Shirley anywhere. She glanced at the sharp carving knife on the sideboard, but left it there, and joined her friend at the wall. They didn’t dare peek over the top for fear of being spotted, so they huddled down behind it, and the words drifted clear to them.
“Looks like a rambling old dump. Who lives here besides that damn parson?”
“Just three women—his niece, and two maids.”
“Where do they sleep?”
“The maids are in the back rooms. The niece has a bedroom at the front.”
“What about Helstone?”
“That room up yonder’s his. He had a light burning a little while ago, but it’s out now.”
“Best way to get in?”
“If they told me to do for him—and he’s more than earned it—I’d try that long window down there, the one leading to the dining room. I could creep my way up the stairs easy, I know exactly where his bed is.”
“What about the women? They’ll scream.”
“Leave ‘em be unless they make a noise, then I’ll shut ‘em up quick. Wish I could find the old sod asleep. If he wakes up, he’s dangerous.”
“Does he have guns?”
“Firearms, aye, and they’re always loaded.”
“Then you’re a fool to hang around here. One shot and the whole neighbourhood’s alerted. Moore’s men would be on us before we could turn around. We’d blow our whole plan.”
“Go on ahead then, I tell you. I can take care of Helstone all on my own.”
There was a pause. One of the men dropped a heavy tool, and it clanged loud on the stone causeway. The guard dog burst into wild, furious barking again.
“Damnation! That’s ruined it,” the lead man snarled. “He’d be woken up by that racket even if he was dead. You never said there was a dog. Forward, move out!”
The tramp of hundreds of feet picked up again, rumbling down the road, until the last echo of it faded away.
Shirley stood up straight, and peeked over the top of the wall. “Not a single one left,” she said, quiet. Then she breathed out, soft and shaky, “Thank God.”
Caroline echoed the words, her voice far less steady. She was trembling all over, her heart hammering so hard she could feel it in her ears, her face cold, her forehead damp with sweat.
“Thank God they left us alone,” she whispered. “But where are they going? They passed right by us to go somewhere far more important.”
“They did the smart thing,” Shirley said, calm as ever. “The men they’re heading for can defend themselves. They’re prepared. If those rioters had broken in here, I had my finger on the trigger of my pistol, ready to greet the first one through the window with a bullet he’d never expect—but three hundred of them behind him? I haven’t got three hundred hands, or three hundred guns. I couldn’t have kept you, or the two poor maids asleep upstairs, safe. Thank the Lord we didn’t have to find out.”
She paused a second, then went on. “We can’t just stand here doing nothing. We’ve got to go to the Hollow.”
“The Hollow, Shirley?”
“Where the rioters are heading. They took the high road, so we won’t run into them. The path across the fields is all quiet, no one on it at all. Will you come with me?”
“Yes,” Caroline said at once, even though her knees felt wobbly, even though every instinct was screaming to hide behind a locked door, because she couldn’t possibly leave Shirley to go alone.
“Then let’s lock all the windows up tight, make sure the house is as secure as we can leave it,” Shirley said. “You know why we’re going, don’t you, Cary?”
“I… because you want to go.”
“You’d follow me straight into the middle of a fight just because I wanted you to? What a gentle, loyal wife you’d make for a stern husband!” Shirley laughed soft, but her voice was warm. “Your face is whiter than the moon right now, and your fingers are trembling like the aspen tree by the gate. Let me give you a better reason. We’re going for Robert Moore’s sake. We’re going to try to warn him the rioters are on their way.”
“Of course!” Caroline said, lighting up at once. “I was being such a silly blind fool. I’ll go with you, Shirley, I’ll go gladly.”
“I know you will. You’d follow me to my death meekly, but you’d run straight to Robert’s side without a second thought. But there’s no dying tonight, I promise. We’re not in any real danger.”
Caroline ran around the house, latching every shutter and every window fast. “Don’t worry, I can run as fast as you need me to,” she said, grabbing Shirley’s hand tight. “Let’s cut straight across the fields.”
“You think you can climb the stone walls?”
“Tonight I can.”
“Aren’t you afraid of the hedges, and the beck we’ll have to cross?”
“I can cross it.”
They set off, running, every now and then skidding to a halt to climb over a stone wall. Shirley was light on her feet, quick as a deer, leaping over gaps without even breaking stride. Caroline, less sure-footed, stumbled twice, scraped her palms raw, but jumped straight back up, laughing that she wasn’t hurt. They reached a thick hawthorn hedge, fumbled for ten minutes finding a narrow gap to squeeze through, and their hair got tangled, their muslin gowns tore, but they didn’t care about anything but speed. On the other side, they reached the beck, rushing deep in its stony bed, with only a single narrow plank strung across it for a bridge. Shirley had run across it a hundred times before, but Caroline had never dared step foot on it.
“I’ll carry you over,” Shirley said. “You’re light, I’m strong. Let me.”
“If I fall in, you can fish me out,” Caroline said, squeezing her hand tight, and she stepped onto the wobbly plank like it was solid grass, not a thin strip of wood over rushing dark water. Shirley stepped right behind her, and neither of them so much as wobbled. They were so far past caring about small dangers that even a raging flood wouldn’t have stopped them. They’d barely set foot on the opposite bank when a gunshot cracked the air from the north. A second later, another shot boomed far off to the south. Within three minutes, two more reports echoed from the east and west.
