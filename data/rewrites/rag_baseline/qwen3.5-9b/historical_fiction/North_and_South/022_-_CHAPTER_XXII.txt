The air in the drawing-room tasted of dust and suppressed panic. Though the windows hung half-open to combat the stifling heat, the Venetian blinds cast a gray, distorted gloom across the floorboards, reflecting the chaotic energy of the street below. This unnatural light, mingled with the sickly green hue filtering from above, rendered even Margaret Hale's own reflection in the mirror ghostly and wan. She sat, waiting, the silence of the room pressing against her ears until it became deafening. Occasionally, a phantom gust seemed to carry the distant, multitudinous roar closer, only to die away into a profound, terrifying stillness.

Finally, Fanny burst in, her face a mask of hysteria.

"Mamma will be with you directly, Miss Hale," she gasped, her voice trembling. "She begged me to apologize for the delay. You must understand, my brother has imported hands from Ireland, and it has inflamed the Milton populace to a frenzy—as if he lacked the right to secure labor wherever he could! These fools refused to work for him, and now, terrified by their own threats, they dare not let the poor Irish starvelings out. You can see them huddled in the attic of the mill, kept there for safety from the brutes who will neither work nor allow them to. Mamma is arranging their food, and John is attempting to calm the women, some of whom are weeping to return. Oh! Here comes Mamma!"

Mrs. Thornton entered, her expression carved from black stone, a look that made Margaret feel acutely guilty for intruding during such a crisis. Yet, she remained, compelled by Mrs. Thornton's earlier instruction to attend to her mother's needs. Mrs. Thornton's brow furrowed deeper, her mouth tightening into a rigid line as Margaret spoke softly of her mother's restlessness and Dr. Donaldson's urgent recommendation for a water-bed. Silence stretched between them, taut and brittle.

Then, Mrs. Thornton sprang upright, her composure shattering.

"They're at the gates! Call John, Fanny—bring him in from the mill! They're battering the gates! They'll tear them down! Call John, I say!"

Simultaneously, the gathering thrum she had ignored while listening to Margaret erupted just outside the wall. An escalating cacophony of angry voices roared behind the wooden barrier, shaking it as if the unseen, maddened mob had transformed their bodies into living battering rams. They recoiled briefly, only to surge forward with renewed, unified momentum, their collective weight making the sturdy gates quiver like reeds before a hurricane.

Women flocked to the windows, transfixed by the terrifying spectacle. Mrs. Thornton, the maids, and Margaret stood frozen. Fanny had fled upstairs, screaming as if pursued by demons, collapsing onto the sofa in hysterical sobs. Mrs. Thornton scanned the room for her son, who remained in the mill. He emerged, looking up at the pale cluster of faces, offering a smile of grim encouragement before locking the factory door. He signaled to a servant to unlock his own entrance, which Fanny had hastily barred in her panic. Mrs. Thornton stepped forward herself.

The sound of Mr. Thornton's familiar, commanding voice seemed to strike the infuriated multitude like a physical blow. Until now, they had been voiceless, breath spent in their futile attempts to breach the defenses. Now, hearing him speak from within, they unleashed a fierce, unearthly groan that turned Mrs. Thornton's face deathly pale as she led him into the room. He appeared slightly flushed, but his eyes gleamed with the fire of a warrior answering a trumpet call. With a proud, defiant set to his jaw, he looked less like a man and more like a noble figure forged in conflict. Margaret, who had always feared her own courage might crumble in an emergency, found herself forgotten in the intensity of the moment. She felt only a piercing, painful sympathy for the unfolding tragedy.

Mr. Thornton stepped forward with frank authority.

"I am sorry, Miss Hale, that you have visited us at this ill-fated hour, when you may be drawn into our peril. Mother, would you not retire to the back rooms? I suspect they may have breached the path from Pinner's Lane into the stable-yard; if not, you will be safer there. Go, Jane!"

He commanded the upper servant, who obeyed, followed closely by the others.

"I remain here," Mr. Thornton declared firmly.

His mother refused to move. "Where you stand, I shall stay." Indeed, retreat was futile; the crowd had already surrounded the outbuildings at the rear, unleashing a terrifying roar from behind. The servants scrambled into the garrets, their cries and shrieks echoing in the chaos. Mr. Thornton watched them with a sneer of disdain. He glanced at Margaret, who stood alone by the window nearest the factory. Her eyes glittered, her cheeks flushed with a deep, feverish color. Sensing his gaze, she turned to him, voicing a question that had been brewing in her mind:

"Where are the poor imported workers? Are they still in the factory?"

"Yes," he replied. "I left them cowering in a small room at the head of the back staircase, instructing them to flee downward if they sensed an assault on the mill doors. But it is not them—they seek me."

"When can the soldiers arrive?" his mother asked, her voice low but steady.

He consulted his watch with the same composed precision he applied to all things, performing a quick mental calculation.

"If Williams departed immediately upon my command and avoided the crush, it will be twenty minutes yet."

"Twenty minutes!" his mother exclaimed, her terror finally breaking through in her tone.

"Close the windows instantly, Mother! The gates cannot withstand another shock. Close that window, Miss Hale."

Margaret hurried to shut hers, then moved to support Mrs. Thornton's trembling fingers. For several minutes, the unseen street held its breath. Mrs. Thornton stared wildly at her son's face, searching for an interpretation of the sudden silence. His features were set in rigid lines of contemptuous defiance; neither hope nor fear could be deciphered there.

Fanny stirred, raising herself weakly. "Are they gone?" she whispered.

"Gone!" he answered sharply. "Listen!"

They all heard it: the single, great strain of held breath, the slow creak of wood surrendering, the wrench of iron, and the thunderous crash of the ponderous gates falling inward. Fanny staggered, took a step toward her mother, and collapsed into her arms, overcome by faintness. Mrs. Thornton lifted her with a strength born of sheer will, carrying her away.

"Thank God!" Mr. Thornton breathed as he watched her exit. "Would you not prefer to go upstairs, Miss Hale?"

Margaret's lips formed a silent "No," but her words were lost beneath the tramp of innumerable footsteps pounding against the very foundation of the house and the fierce, low growl of angry voices that possessed a ferocious, satisfied murmur, far more dreadful than their frantic cries moments prior.

"Never mind!" he said, attempting to reassure her. "I regret that you were trapped in this alarm, but it cannot endure long. A few more minutes, and the soldiers will be here."

"Oh, God!" Margaret cried suddenly. "There is Boucher! I recognize his face, though he is livid with rage—he is fighting desperately to reach the front! Look! Look!"

"Who is Boucher?" Mr. Thornton asked coolly, stepping closer to the window to identify the man who captured Margaret's attention. Instantly, the crowd erupted in a yell so primal it bordered on the demonic—a ravenous hunger for the sustenance being denied them. Even Mr. Thornton drew back momentarily, dismayed by the intensity of the hatred radiating from the mob.

"Let them yell!" he shouted back. "In five minutes more... I only pray my poor Irishmen are not terrified out of their wits by such fiendish noise. Hold your courage for five minutes, Miss Hale."

"Do not fear for me," she said hastily. "But what happens in five minutes? Can you do nothing to soothe these poor creatures? It is horrifying to witness them."

"The soldiers will arrive directly, and that will restore their reason."

"To reason!" Margaret snapped, her voice rising. "What kind of reason?"

"The only reason that governs men who transform themselves into wild beasts. By heavens! They have turned toward the mill-door!"

"Mr. Thornton," Margaret pleaded, her whole body shaking with passion, "go down this instant, if you possess any shred of courage. Do not be a coward. Go down and face them like a man. Save these poor strangers whom you have lured here. Speak to your workmen as if they were human beings. Speak kindly. Do not wait for the soldiers to slaughter these mad creatures. I see one there who is already broken. If you have any nobility left in you, go out and address them, man to man!"

He turned to regard her as she spoke. A dark cloud settled over his face as he listened. He gritted his teeth, absorbing her words.

"I will go. Perhaps I may ask you to accompany me downstairs and bar the door behind me; my mother and sister require that protection."

"Oh! Mr. Thornton! I do not know—I may be mistaken—only—"

But he was already gone. Downstairs in the hall, he had unbolted the front door. All Margaret could do was follow him swiftly, latch it shut behind him, and climb the stairs again with a sick heart and a dizzy head
