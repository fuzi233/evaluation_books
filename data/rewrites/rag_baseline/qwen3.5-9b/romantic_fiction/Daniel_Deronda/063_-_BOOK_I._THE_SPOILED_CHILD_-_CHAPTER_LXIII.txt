The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Mirah, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of London itself, utterly deaf to the quiet suffocations of its women. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax and damp wool, heavy with the residue of a day that had ended in silence and shared grief. Mordecai sat motionless, his head thrown back, eyes sealed shut, breathing with the labored difficulty of a man whose soul seemed to be straining against the walls of a prison cell. Beside him, Mirah remained frozen, her hand interlocked with his, her face pale as moonlight, her curls disheveled as if she had just awakened from a fever dream. The scattered papers on the floor lay in chaotic heaps, forgotten artifacts of a sudden collapse, while the ticking of the mantel-clock marked the slow erosion of twilight into a darkness that felt less like night and more like a final curtain falling.

In this suspended moment of despair, the door swung open, and the sound of a familiar voice cut through the gloom like a sudden ray of sunlight breaking through storm clouds. "Daniel Deronda—may he come in?"

"Come! Come!" Mordecai cried, his voice transforming instantly from a dying whisper to a radiant command. He rose, his face illuminated by an inner fire that banished the shadows of his exhaustion, his eyes wide and expectant as if he had awaited this visitor since dawn. Mirah startled, a flush of confused alarm rising to her cheeks, but the tension in the room shattered the moment Deronda stepped forward.

As he approached, he extended his right hand to Mirah, standing close to her brother's left, while his other hand settled firmly on Mordecai's broad shoulder. For a heartbeat, he held them both, silent and steady, reading the turmoil on their faces until he spoke, his voice tight with concern. "Has anything happened? Any trouble?"

"Speak not of trouble now," Mordecai replied, shielding Mirah from the necessity of answering. "There is joy in your face—let the joy be ours."

Mirah thought, *It is for something he cannot tell us.* Yet, as they took their seats, the atmosphere shifted with the weight of a revelation. Deronda drew a chair close before Mordecai, his demeanor carrying the gravity of a man stepping onto a stage where destiny awaited.

"That is true," he said, his tone emphatic, resonating with a newfound certainty. "I have a joy which will remain to us even in the worst trouble. I did not tell you the reason of my journey abroad, Mordecai, because... never mind. I went to learn my parentage. And you were right. I am a Jew."

The declaration hung in the air, a spark igniting dry tinder. The two men clasped hands in a gesture that seemed to carry the static charge of lightning, passing through Mirah like an electric shock that rewired her understanding of the world. Deronda continued, his voice speaking from Mordecai's mind as much as his own, bridging the gap between their souls. "We have the same people. Our souls have the same vocation. We shall not be separated by life or by death."

Mordecai's response emerged in Hebrew, barely a whisper yet vibrating with liturgical power: "Our God and the God of our fathers."

The weight of emotion pressed down on the room, rendering speech unnecessary. Mirah sank to her knees beside her brother, her gaze fixed on his face, which had transformed from deathly stillness to glowing vitality. The movement was an involuntary eruption of relief, a physical release from the crushing burden of despondency, solemn as a religious rite. In that instant, her own future receded; she lived solely through the restoration of her brother's spirit.

"And it is not only that I am a Jew," Deronda went on, basking in the clarity of a truth finally aligned with his actions. "But I come of a strain that has ardently maintained the fellowship of our race—a line of Spanish Jews that has borne many students and men of practical power. And I possess what will give us a sort of communion with them. My grandfather, Daniel Charisi, preserved manuscripts, family records stretching far back, in the hope that they would pass into the hands of his grandson. And now his hope is fulfilled, in spite of attempts to thwart it by hiding my parentage from me. I possess the chest containing them, with his own papers, and it is down below in this house. I mean to leave it with you, Mordecai, that you may help me to study the manuscripts. Some of them I can read easily enough—those in Spanish and Italian. Others are in Hebrew, and, I think, Arabic; but there seem to be Latin translations. I was only able to look at them cursorily while I stayed at Mainz. We will study them together."

Deronda concluded with a bright smile, a beacon cutting through the habitual gravity of his countenance, revealing a man fully awake to his own potential. But when this warm glance drifted from Mordecai to rest upon Mirah, it carried the intensity of too much sunshine, forcing her to shift her posture. She had knelt under an impulse so pure that any personal embarrassment felt incongruous, yet the sudden awareness of Mrs. Grandcourt's looming presence stirred unease within her. Thoughts of how her guardian might react to this new aspect of things flushed her cheeks, compelling her to rise and resume her usual pose of crossed hands and feet, striving for a quietude that masked the turbulence beneath.

Deronda, equally perceptive, sensed that the raw emotion flooding his eyes might be repugnant to her, fearing that any unexpected manifestation of passion could spoil the delicate balance of their relationship. He worried that if Mirah could harbor no love for him, any advance on his part would render their inevitable proximity a source of mutual misery. While these currents pulsed rapidly between them, Mordecai, seeing only a blessed fulfillment in his friend's presence, began to speak with his characteristic expansiveness.

"Daniel, from the first, I have said to you, we know not all the pathways. Has there not been a meeting among them, as of the operations in one soul, where an idea being born and breathing draws the elements toward it, and is fed and glows? For all things are bound together in that Omnipresence which is the place and habitation of the world, and events are of a glass wherethrough our eyes see some of the pathways. And if it seems that the erring and unloving wills of men have helped to prepare you, as Moses was prepared, to serve your people the better, that depends on another order than the law which must guide our footsteps. For the evil will of man makes not a people's good except by stirring the righteous will of man; and beneath all the clouds with which our thought encompasses the Eternal, this is clear—that a people can be blessed only by having counsellors and a multitude whose will moves in obedience to the laws of justice and love. For see, now, it was your loving will that made a chief pathway, and resisted the effect of evil; for, by performing the duties of brotherhood to my sister, and seeking out her brother in the flesh, your soul has been prepared to receive with gladness this message of the Eternal, 'behold the multitude of your brethren.'"

"It is quite true that you and Mirah have been my teachers," Deronda replied, his voice steady. "If this revelation had been made to me before I knew you both, I think my mind would have rebelled against it. Perhaps I should have felt then—'If I could have chosen, I would not have been a Jew.' What I feel now is—that my whole being is a consent to the fact. But it has been the gradual accord between your mind and mine which has brought about that full consent."

As he spoke, the memory of their first evening in the bookshop surfaced vividly, recalling the aloofness he had then felt from Mordecai's prophetic confidence. Yet now, driven by a nature that delighted in satisfying the eagerly expectant soul, he leaned forward, his eyes alight with the fervor of a man who had finally found his place in the grand design.
