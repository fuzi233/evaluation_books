The refectory hummed with quiet conversation when Gerard took his seat among the monks. At his table, the talk turned to the sudden passing of Brother Werter, who had been tasked with transcribing the monastery’s charter and rule. Gerard hesitated, then ventured timidly, "My lords, if it please you, I might take up the work—not for payment, but in gratitude for the shelter your order has often given a weary traveler."

A flicker of doubt crossed the monks' faces. One smiled kindly but murmured, "Brother Werter was a master scribe. His hand cannot be matched by any but the most skilled."

Gerard, undeterred, drew from his pouch a scrap of vellum—a deed he had cleaned and inscribed as a sample. The monk who took it stiffened in surprise, then hurried to the high table, genuflecting at the fifth and tenth steps before presenting it to the prior. A murmur rose among the elders; fingers pointed, voices buzzed. Soon the monk returned, beaming, bearing a silver cup of spiced pimentum and a savory game pie for Gerard.

The young scribe bowed deeply, first to the prior, then to his tablemates, and drank. The monks exchanged knowing glances. "You were convent-bred," one declared. "Deny it not!"

Gerard admitted it gladly. "Yet how did you guess?"

"You drank with both hands on the cup," two answered in unison.

Further down the hall, raucous laughter erupted—a burst so loud the prior sent the sub-prior to quell it. Gerard’s cheeks burned, for amid the noise he had caught Denys’s booming voice and the word *courage*.

Soon, Gerard was installed in the late Werter’s cell, surrounded by wax lights, inks, and a scribe’s frame. His work stretched beyond a single night, and he feared Denys—ever disdainful of monks—would refuse to linger. But to his relief, the soldier shrugged. "A day’s rest won’t kill us. Write, and I’ll amuse myself."

When Gerard’s calligraphy won praise, the monks declared their records improved by Werter’s death. The sub-prior pressed a rix-dollar into his hand, along with brushes and pigments from the abbey’s stores. Heartened, Gerard resumed his journey, hopeful his skill might yet win back his lost love.

"Come, Denys," he said cheerfully, displaying his gifts. "See what kindness the brothers have shown me? Now, in fairness, you must admit they are not all hypocrites."

Denys grinned. "I recant! I was a scurrilous fool. These men are sinners, perhaps—but hypocrites? No. They own their vices like honest rogues."

Gerard frowned. "Then you’ve seen nothing to repent of?"

Denys launched into a tale of midnight revels—of monks feasting in secret, of a rope-ladder tryst upon the wall, of raucous songs and flagons disguised as breviaries. Gerard recoiled in disgust. "Wretched men! Corrupters of innocence! Would that the church had crushed them all!"

Denys blinked. "Hypocrites? Nay, they never pretended to virtue. But—" He lowered his voice at Gerard’s warning. The crypt they’d defiled, it was said, was haunted. Voices of the dead whispered there; ghostly music echoed through the stones.

Denys paled. "Had I known, not even mules could have dragged me down!" Then, with a wistful sigh: "And what a merry night I’d have missed."

Their debate was cut short by a lay brother galloping up on a mule, waving a vellum deed—the contract between Ghysbrecht and Floris Brandt. Gerard, overcome, kissed the mule’s nose in gratitude before carefully stitching the parchment into his doublet, his thoughts flying to Sevenbergen.

At last, they reached the promised Burgundian inn—*Les Trois Poissons*. Denys frowned at a sprawling new inscription:

Gerard stared. Denys, after a laborious deciphering, puffed up with pride. "Ah! ‘Here they lodge man and horse.’ A fine welcome indeed!"

And with that, they stepped inside.
