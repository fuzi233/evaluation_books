“Mist clogs the sunshine,
Smoky dwarf houses
Hem me round everywhere.”

The next afternoon, some twenty miles from Milton-Northern, the train pulled into the narrow branch line that led to Heston. The town stretched out in a long, scattered street running parallel to the sea—a place with a character all its own, distinct from the genteel seaside resorts of southern England and even more so from those on the continent. It had a certain purposefulness, a Scottish word might say—*purpose-like*. Carts here were built with more iron, less wood and leather; their harnesses sleek and functional. Even the people, though come for pleasure, moved with a quiet urgency, as if every moment counted. The colours were duller—gray, enduring, not bright or decorative. There were no smock-frocks among the country folk; they had fallen out of use, impractical near machinery, too slow for a world that valued speed.

In southern towns, Margaret had often seen shopkeepers lounging at their doors, watching the passersby, enjoying the air. Here, if there was a lull in customers, they busied themselves inside—rolling and unrolling ribbons, polishing glass, tidying shelves. It was as if idleness was a sin.

These contrasts struck her deeply as she and her mother stepped out the next morning to search for lodgings. Their two nights at hotels had cost more than Mr. Hale had expected, and so they were eager to settle quickly. They took the first clean, cheerful rooms available—simple but comfortable, with a view of the sea.

For the first time in days, Margaret felt truly at ease. A soft dreaminess settled over her, making rest feel like luxury. The distant sea lapped the shore with a steady rhythm. Closer, the cries of donkey-boys echoed through the streets. Scenes passed before her eyes like fleeting pictures—she didn’t care to understand them fully, only to let them drift by. She walked down to the beach, breathing in the warm, salt-scented air, even at the end of November. The sea stretched endlessly, misty and pale, meeting a sky tinged with tender hues. A white sail glinted silver in a weak sunbeam. In that stillness, she could almost imagine living forever in this peaceful reverie—where the present was everything, and past and future alike faded away.

But the future could not be ignored, however harsh it might appear.

One evening, plans were made: the next day, Margaret and her father would travel to Milton-Northern to find a house. Mr. Hale had received letters from Mr. Bell and one or two from Mr. Thornton. He wished to speak directly with the latter to clarify his position and prospects in the town. Margaret understood they needed to move, yet she hesitated. The idea of a manufacturing town filled her with unease. She believed her mother was benefiting from the fresh air of Heston and would have gladly delayed the journey.

But the journey came.

Long before reaching Milton, a heavy, leaden cloud hung over the horizon. It stood out sharply against the pale gray-blue of the winter sky. Frost had already begun to touch the fields around Heston. As they neared the town, the air carried a faint tinge of smoke—not strong, perhaps, but unmistakable. It wasn’t so much the smell of fire as the absence of green things, the loss of grass and herbs beneath the breath of industry.

They were swept through endless, straight streets lined with small brick houses, identical and stark. Here and there rose a large, rectangular factory—many-windowed, towering over the homes like a hen among her chicks—spewing black, unparliamentary smoke into the sky. This, Margaret realized, explained the cloud she had thought might herald rain.

From station to hotel, the roads were choked. Heavy wagons and carts blocked the narrow thoroughfares. Every vehicle seemed burdened with cotton—raw in sacks, woven in bales of calico. People crowded the sidewalks, dressed neatly enough in fabric, but slovenly in posture and manner—different from the threadbare elegance of London’s working class.

“This is New Street,” Mr. Hale said. “I believe it’s the main artery of Milton. Bell has told me about it. Thirty years ago, when this lane was widened into a great road, his property soared in value. Mr. Thornton’s mill must be nearby—he’s Bell’s tenant. I suspect he began with the warehouse.”

“Where’s our hotel, Papa?”

“Near the end of this street, I think. Shall we have lunch before or after we look at the houses listed in the *Milton Times*?”

“Oh, let’s finish the work first.”

“Very well. I’ll just check for any notes from Mr. Thornton—he said he’d send word about the houses. Then we’ll go. We’ll keep the cab. It’s safer than getting lost and missing the train.”

There were no messages waiting.

They began their search. Thirty pounds a year was all they could afford. In Hampshire, such a sum might buy a spacious home with a garden. Here, even basic accommodations—two sitting rooms, four bedrooms—seemed impossible. They visited each house on their list, rejecting every one.

At last, they looked at each other, dismayed.

“We must return to the second choice,” Mr. Hale said. “The one in Crampton, isn’t it? That suburb? Three sitting rooms—remember how we laughed at that compared to three bedrooms?”

“I’ve worked it out,” Margaret replied. “The front room downstairs will be your study and our dining room—poor Papa! You know we decided Mamma should have the cheeriest sitting room possible. And the front room upstairs—despite the dreadful blue-and-pink paper and that heavy cornice—has a lovely view. It overlooks the plain, with a bend of river or canal below. I’ll take the little bedroom behind, in that projection above the kitchen. You and Mamma can share the room behind the drawing-room. And that closet in the attic—perfect for your dressing room.”

“But what about Dixon and the maid?”

“Wait—let me think. I’m overwhelmed by my own genius for planning. Dixon gets the back sitting room—I think she’ll like that. She complained so much about the stairs in Heston. And the girl can have the sloping attic over your room and Mamma’s. Won’t that do?”

“I suppose so. But the papers… such taste! And those enormous cornices!”

“Never mind, Papa. Surely you can persuade the landlord to re-paper the drawing-room and your bedroom—Mamma will spend most time there. Your bookshelves will hide much of the pattern in the dining room.”

“So you think it’s best? Then I’ll go at once to see Mr. Donkin—the landlord mentioned in the ad. I’ll take you back to the hotel. You can order lunch and rest. By the time it’s ready, I’ll be back. I hope I can get new paper.”

Margaret hoped so too, though she said nothing. She had never understood the love for ornament, however poor, over simplicity—something she believed to be the true foundation of elegance.

Her father guided her into the hotel, left her at the foot of the stairs, and hurried off to Canute Street.

Just as Margaret reached for the door of their room, a waiter approached.

“I beg your pardon, ma’am. The gentleman left so quickly, I couldn’t tell him. Mr. Thornton called right after you left. I told him you’d be back in an hour, and he returned five minutes ago. He said he’d wait for Mr. Hale. He’s in your room now.”

“Thank you. My father will return soon. You can inform him then.”

She opened the door and entered with her usual calm dignity. No awkwardness stirred within her—she was too accustomed to society for that. This was a man visiting on business. Since he had shown kindness, she meant to treat him with full courtesy.

Mr. Thornton, however, was taken aback. He had expected a quiet, middle-aged clergyman. Instead, a young woman stepped forward—tall, composed, radiant in her plainness. Her bonnet was of fine straw, trimmed simply with white ribbon. A large Indian shawl draped her shoulders like royal robes. Her expression was open, direct—unfazed by his presence. He did not know who she was, but saw at once that she regarded him with no alarm, no flush of surprise. Her face remained serene, pale ivory under the light.

He had heard Mr. Hale had a daughter—but imagined her a child.

“Mr. Thornton, I believe?” she said, pausing briefly while he struggled for words. “Please sit. My father brought me to the door just now, but he wasn’t told you were here. He’s gone on errands. He’ll return shortly. I’m sorry you’ve had to call twice.”

Though used to command himself, Thornton found himself yielding to her quiet authority. Moments earlier, he had been annoyed at the delay. Now, he sat at her request, calm.

“Do you know where your father went? Perhaps I could find him.”

“He’s gone to Mr. Donkin in Canute Street—the landlord of the house we’re considering in Crampton.”

Thornton knew the house. He had seen the advertisement, and inspected it at Mr. Bell’s request—and also out of personal interest in the case of a clergyman who had resigned his living under such circumstances as Mr. Hale’s. He had thought the house suitable. But now, seeing Margaret—her grace, her stillness, her way of holding herself—he felt ashamed of ever thinking it adequate for such a family. The house had flaws, yes, but now they seemed vulgar beside her.

She could not help her beauty. Yet her features—her short curled upper lip, the firm, upturned chin, the way she carried her head—gave strangers the impression of pride. She was tired, and wished only to rest, but she maintained her composure. She spoke courteously, though she longed for him to leave. He answered curtly, never expanding on a topic. She had removed her shawl and hung it over the chair. She sat facing him, facing the light. Her full beauty met his gaze—her white throat rising from a lithe figure, her lips moving gently, never breaking the cool serenity of her face. Her eyes, soft and shadowed, met his with quiet freedom.

Almost, he told himself, he didn’t like her. He resented her indifference, misreading it as contempt. He felt insulted, even angry—almost tempted to rise and walk out. He saw himself in her eyes as a rough man, lacking refinement. Her calmness he took as scorn.

Just as she ran out of things to say—conversation reduced to brief exchanges—her father entered.

With his gentle, courteous manner, Mr. Hale apologized and restored the balance. He and Thornton discussed their mutual friend, Mr. Bell. Margaret, relieved her role was over, moved to the window to observe the unfamiliar street.

She became so absorbed she barely heard her father.

“Margaret! The landlord insists on keeping that dreadful paper. I fear we must let it stay.”

“Oh dear!” she sighed. “I’m sorry.” She considered hiding it with sketches, but gave up—afraid it would only make matters worse.

Meanwhile, Mr. Hale, with his old-fashioned hospitality, invited Thornton to stay for lunch. It would have been inconvenient, but he felt he should accept—if only Margaret had encouraged it. She did not. He was glad, yet irritated.

She bowed low and gravely when he left. He felt more self-conscious than he ever had in his life.

“Well, Margaret,” said Mr. Hale, “to luncheon as fast as we can. Have you ordered?”

“No, Papa. That man was here when I arrived. I haven’t had a chance.”

“Then we’ll take whatever’s available. He must have waited a long time.”

“It seemed endless,” she said. “I was nearly speechless when you came in. He never expanded on anything—just short, abrupt answers.”

“Very to the point, I’d say. He’s clear-headed. He mentioned—did you hear—that Crampton is on gravelly soil, the healthiest suburb near Milton.”

Back in Heston, they recounted the day to Mrs. Hale, who questioned them eagerly between sips of tea.

“And what is Mr. Thornton like?” she asked.

“Ask Margaret,” said her husband.

“Oh, I hardly know,” Margaret replied lazily, too weary to describe. Then she roused herself. “He’s tall, broad-shouldered—about thirty, I’d say.”

“Thirty,” confirmed her father. “And his face—neither handsome nor plain. Nothing remarkable. Not quite a gentleman—but that wasn’t to be expected.”

“Not vulgar,” added Mr. Hale, slightly defensive. “Not common.”

“Oh, no,” said Margaret. “But with such strength and resolve, no face could be vulgar. I wouldn’t want to bargain with him—he looks inflexible. A man made for his place, Mamma—strong, sagacious, as becomes a great manufacturer.”

“Don’t call them tradesmen, Margaret,” said her father. “They’re very different.”

“Are they? I use the word for anyone who sells something tangible. But if you prefer, I wonI use it. Still—oh, Mamma—prepare yourself for our drawing-room paper. Pink and blue roses with yellow leaves! And that monstrous cornice!”

When they finally moved into their new house in Milton, the offensive papers were gone. The landlord accepted their thanks calmly. He let them believe he had changed his mind. But in truth, he had done nothing for a Reverend Mr. Hale unknown in Milton. Only one sharp word from Mr. Thornton—the wealthy manufacturer—had been enough.
