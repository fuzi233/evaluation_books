While Tito hurried across the bridge, the newly purchased armor hidden beneath his cloak, Romola paced the floor of the old library, her thoughts fixed on him and her heart longing for his return. Few faces had remained shut within their windows that day to watch the entry of the French King and his nobility; Romola's was one of the few. Since her father's sudden death three months prior, she had attended no festivities.

"Will Tito not come to write?" he had asked earlier that evening, long after the bell had tolled the customary hour. He had hesitated to ask, fearing a refusal, yet Romola had seen in his attentive gaze and restless movements that nothing else occupied his mind. "No, father," she had replied gently, offering a soft excuse. "He must attend a supper at the Cardinal's. Everyone desires his company so greatly."

"Aha!" Bardo had seemed momentarily pacified by this hope. "Perhaps he will bring some definite news regarding the library; the Cardinal promised last week..." He fell silent for a moment, then flushed suddenly. "I must proceed without him, Romola. Fetch the pen. He brings me no new text to annotate, yet I must speak on the New Platonists. I shall die, and nothing will remain undone. Haste yourself, my Romola."

"I am ready, father," she had said instantly, holding the pen aloft. Yet, silence followed. Romola ignored it for a while, accustomed to pauses in dictation, but when she finally glanced around inquiringly, her father's posture had not shifted. "Father, I am entirely ready!" Still, Bardo remained mute, and his silence was never broken again.

Looking back on that hour, Romola felt a sting of indignation toward herself. Even amidst the first surge of grief, an irrepressible thought had mingled: *Perhaps my life with Tito will be more perfect now.* The dream of a triple existence, united in undivided happiness, had not been fully realized. Ideally, a rainbow shower of sweets should contain invisible seeds of bitterness; the crowned Ariadne, beneath falling roses, had increasingly sensed unexpected thorns.

"It was not Tito's fault," Romola told herself repeatedly. He remained gentle to both her and her father. However, she now saw clearly that the nature of things dictated that no one but herself could endure, month after month, year after year, the monotony of her father's exacting demands. Even she, whose youthful passion and faith stemmed from her deep sympathy with him, had not always been patient; inward rebellion had often stirred within her. True, before their marriage—and for some time thereafter—Tito had appeared more tireless than she. But naturally, the burden felt lighter when novel. One assumes a load with confident readiness, and the growing weight remains tolerable until the desire for relief becomes irresistible.

She chastised her younger self for ignorance, vowing now to make no unfair demands on the man to whom she had offered her whole womanhood. The sadness that clung to her as she watched her father slowly sink from elation into fresh disappointment—while Tito offered bland excuses for withholding his share of their joint work—was not Tito's fault. It was their inevitable destiny. If he stayed away, it was because they could rarely be truly alone. His caresses remained tender; if she timidly pleaded for him to stay with her father instead of accepting non-peremptory engagements, he excused himself with charming gaiety, lingering with fond playfulness before departing. Thus, she felt only a small ache of heartbreak amidst her love, then turned to soothe her father's vexation.

Yet, inwardly, her imagination struggled to reconcile Tito's goodness with his inability to sacrifice the vibrant social pleasures that appealed more intensely to a bright spirit like his than to ordinary men. She, too, craved gaiety and admiration; she willingly surrendered them for her father's sake, and would have sacrificed much more for even a slight wish of Tito's. Their natures differed widely, perhaps merely reflecting the inherent gap between man and woman, wherein her affections proved more absorbing. If there were other differences, she persuaded herself the inferiority lay with her. Tito was kinder, better tempered, less proud and resentful. He held no angry retorts, met all complaints with perfect sweetness, and slipped quietly away from unpleasant duties.

Large natures, when not gripped by strong, unquestioning emotion, tend to suspect themselves, doubting the truth of their own impressions, aware of horizons beyond their reach. Romola was driven to doubt herself all the more by the necessity of interpreting her disappointment in her marriage in a way that satisfied both her love and her pride. Was there no milder word than "disappointment"? Perhaps all women suffered the disillusionment of ignorant hopes, if only they knew the experience.

Still, her lot held something peculiar: her relationship with her father demanded unusual sacrifices from her husband. Tito had once believed his love would render these sacrifices easy; his love had not been sufficient. She was not justified in resenting a self-deception. Resentment must not arise; all endurance seemed easier to Romola than admitting that Tito acted unworthily. Any new heartache she felt during her solitary hours with her father in his final months was not her husband's fault. Now, however, a hope arose—one that would manifest even in the first moments after her father's place became empty—that there was no longer any pressing claim to divide her from Tito. Their young lives would merge into one current, and their true marriage could finally begin.

Yet, a sense of guilt toward her father colored this hope, born from his death, intensifying her anxiety to fulfill his supreme wish. This piety toward his memory served as the sole atonement for thoughts that bordered on joy at his passing. The laborious, simple life, pure from vulgar corruption but embittered by frustrated hopes, imprisoned in total darkness—a long seedtime yielding no harvest—had ended. What remained, aside from the tablet in Santa Croce and the unfinished commentary on Tito's text, was the collection of manuscripts and antiquities, the fruit of fifty years of toil and frugality. Fulfilling her father's lifelong ambition for this library was a sacramental obligation for Romola.

The precious relics were safe from creditors. When the deficit was calculated, Bernardo del Nero, though not among Florence's wealthiest, had advanced nearly a thousand florins—a vast sum then—accepting a lien on the collection as security. "The State will repay me," he had told Romola, treating the service lightly despite the inconvenience it cost him. "If the Cardinal finds a building, as he seems to promise, our Signoria may complete the rest. I have no children; I can afford the risk."

But within the last ten days, all hopes tied to the Medici had evaporated. The famous Medicean collections in the Via Larga faced dispersal. French agents already argued that such exquisite antique gems, gathered by Lorenzo, rightfully belonged to the first nation in Europe. The Florentine State, having seized the Medicean library, was likely to seek a buyer. With a war to reclaim Pisa looming and the certainty of paying heavy subsidies to the French King, the State preferred coin to manuscripts.

To Romola, these grave political shifts held significance primarily through their impact on her father's legacy. Raised in learned seclusion, she had viewed heroic deeds and great principles as antithetical to the vulgar present, considering the Pnyx and Forum more worthy than the councils of living Florentines. Now, the expulsion of the Medici mattered to her little more than the extinguishing of her best hope for the library. Though she knew the times were perilous for friends of the Medici, like her godfather and Tito, and that superstitious shopkeepers and the rabble brimmed with suspicion, her newfound keen interest in public events—the outbreak of war, the French King's visit, the impending state changes—was ignited solely by love and duty to her father's memory.

All of Romola's ardor had concentrated in her affections. Her participation in her father's scholarly pursuits had been little more than a toil endured for his sake; Tito's airy brilliance held no attraction for her that did not merge into the deeper sympathies of young love and trust. She had touched no mind capable of stirring the larger possibilities of her nature; they lay folded and crushed like embryonic wings, contributing nothing to her consciousness except an occasional vague unease.

However, this new personal stake in public affairs compelled her to understand precisely how Fra Girolamo's preaching might sway events. Discussions of state transformation filled the air, and everything she learned from Tito, whose secretaryship immersed him in the heart of public business, only fueled her eagerness to hear for herself what was currently tearing Florence apart. That morning, for the first time, she had attended one of Fra Girolamo's Advent sermons in the Duomo.

After Tito departed, she had formed a sudden resolve. Visiting the spot where her father lay buried in Santa Croce, she had walked on to the Duomo. The memory of her last scene with Dino remained vivid, yet it had receded behind the experiences and anxieties of her married life. The new sensibilities and questions it had half-awakened were quieted again by that submission to her husband's mind characteristic of a wife who loves with passionate devotion and full reliance.

She remembered the effect of Fra Girolamo's voice and presence, expecting his sermon to move her despite his narrow-mindedness. Indeed, it deepened her previous impression that this fanatical preacher of tribulation was a man deserving of personal regard and reverence.
