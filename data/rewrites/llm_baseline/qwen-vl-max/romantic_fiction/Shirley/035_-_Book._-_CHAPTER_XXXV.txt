Martin had planned well. He had woven a clever scheme for his own quiet amusement. Yet even the most cunning of schemers—older and wiser than he—often find their most carefully spun designs swept away by the sudden, sweeping hand of Fate, that relentless housewife whose red arm no one can resist. In this case, the broom that scattered his plans was forged from the unyielding fibers of Moore’s own stubborn will, bound tight with resolve. He was recovering his strength, and now pushing back against Mrs. Horsfall with astonishing force. Each morning brought a new surprise to that devoted matron. First, he dismissed her from her duties as valet—he would dress himself. Then he refused the coffee she offered—he would breakfast with the family. Finally, he barred her from his room. On the same day, amid the outcry of every woman in the village, he stepped outside into the open air. The next morning, he followed Mr. Yorke to the counting-house and sent word to fetch a chaise from the Red House Inn. He declared he meant to return home to Hollow Cottage that very afternoon. Mr. Yorke, instead of opposing him, quietly supported the decision. The chaise arrived, though Mrs. Yorke insisted it would be fatal. Moore, barely speaking, let his purse speak for him. He paid each servant and Mrs. Horsfall with generous coins. The latter understood perfectly; money made up for all past disobedience. They parted on the best of terms.

Having calmed the kitchen, Moore turned to the parlour. There was Mrs. Yorke, sunk in silent anger, lost in dark thoughts about human ingratitude. He approached, bending low. She could not help but look up—even if only to tell him to go away. Still, there was beauty in his pale, thin face. His hollow eyes held a quiet earnestness, a sweetness—especially when he smiled.

"Good-bye," he said, and as the words left his lips, the smile flickered and faded. He no longer had full control over his emotions; even the smallest feeling stirred visibly in his weakened state.

"And where are you going?" she asked. "We’ll keep you here, do anything in the world for you—if only you’d stay until you’re stronger."

"Good-bye," he repeated, then added softly, "You’ve been like a mother to me. Let your wilful son have one last embrace."

As a foreigner might, he offered first one cheek, then the other. She kissed him.

"What a burden I’ve been," he murmured.

"You’re the worst kind of trouble now, headstrong boy!" she replied. "Who will nurse you at Hollow’s Cottage? Your sister Hortense knows nothing about such things—she’s no more capable than a child."

"Thank God," he said, "for I’ve had enough nursing to last a lifetime."

The little girls came in—Jessie weeping, Rose quiet but solemn. Moore took them into the hall, soothing them, petting them, kissing them gently. He knew his sister-in-law could not bear to see anyone else cherished in her presence. She would have felt affronted if he had cuddled a kitten while she watched.

The boys stood near the chaise as Moore climbed in—but he gave them no farewell. To Mr. Yorke, he simply said, "You're rid of me. That was an unlucky shot, Yorke—it turned Briarmains into a hospital. Come visit me soon at the cottage."

He pulled down the windowpane. The chaise rolled off. Within half an hour, he stepped through the gate of his own garden. After paying the driver and dismissing the carriage, he leaned on the wicket for a moment—resting, reflecting.

"Six months ago," he said aloud, "I walked out of this gate—a proud, angry, disappointed man. Now I return sadder, wiser—weak, yes, but not broken. A cold, gray, yet peaceful world lies around me—one where I hope little, but fear nothing. All petty fears of shame or embarrassment have vanished. If the worst comes, I can work, like Joe Scott, for an honest living. Such a fate holds hardship, but no disgrace. Once, financial ruin meant personal dishonor to me. Not anymore. I know the difference. Ruin *is* an evil—but I am ready for it. I know the day it will come, because I've calculated it. I can delay it six months—no longer. If things change by then—which is unlikely—if the chains binding our trade were somehow loosened (the most improbable thing imaginable)—perhaps I could still win this long battle. Perhaps—I could do wonders! But that thought is madness. Let me see clearly. Ruin will come. It will strike at the roots of my fortune and cut them down. I’ll take a sapling, cross the sea, and plant it in American woods. Louis will go with me. Will anyone else join us? I don’t know. I cannot ask."

He entered the house.

It was late afternoon. Outside, twilight stretched starless and moonless—though the sky wore a mask of frozen clouds, locked fast. The mill-dam was ice-bound. The Hollow was hushed. Inside, darkness had already fallen. Sarah had lit a strong fire in the parlour; she was preparing tea in the kitchen.

"Hortense," Moore said as his sister hurried forward to help him with his cloak, "I’m glad to be home."

Hortense did not grasp the unusual weight of those words—coming from a brother who had never before called the cottage *home*, who had always seen its narrow walls as limiting rather than comforting. Still, anything that brought him joy pleased her, and she expressed her delight.

He sat, then rose again. He walked to the window, then returned to the fire.

"Hortense?"

"Mon frère?"

"This little parlour looks remarkably clean and cheerful—brighter than usual."

"It is true, brother," she said. "I’ve had the whole house cleaned thoroughly while you were away."

"Sister," he said, "on your first day back, you ought to have a guest or two for tea—just so they can see how fresh and neat you’ve made the place."

"True, brother. If it weren’t too late, I could send for Miss Mann."

"You might—but it’s too late to disturb her, and the evening is far too cold for her to venture out."

"How thoughtful of you, dear Gérard! We’ll wait till another day."

"I want someone tonight—someone quiet, who won’t tire either of us."

"Miss Ainley?"

"A good choice, they say—but she lives too far. Tell Harry Scott to run to the rectory and ask Caroline Helstone to come spend the evening with you."

"Wouldn’t tomorrow be better, dear brother?"

"I’d like her to see the house just as it is now—its brightness and order are proof of your care."

"It might set a good example."

"It must. She should come."

He went to the kitchen.

"Sarah, delay tea by half an hour." Then he instructed her to send Harry Scott to the rectory, giving her a hastily scribbled note in pencil, addressed to "Miss Helstone."

Scarcely had Sarah begun to worry about her toast when the messenger returned—with the guest in tow.

She entered through the kitchen, slipped quietly up the stairs to remove her bonnet and furs, and descended just as calmly—her curls neatly arranged, her graceful merino dress and delicate collar spotless, her bright work-bag in hand. She paused to exchange a few warm words with Sarah, admired the new tortoise-shell kitten basking by the hearth, spoke gently to the startled canary, and then made her way to the parlour.

A soft greeting passed between them—cousins meeting after time apart. A quiet pleasure, subtle as perfume, filled the room. The newly lit lamp burned brightly. The tea tray and singing urn were brought in.

"I’m glad to be home," Moore repeated.

They gathered round the table. Hortense did most of the talking. She praised Caroline for her evident improvement in health—her colour returning, her cheeks plump and rosy. It was true. Miss Helstone looked different. Everything about her seemed alive. The heaviness, fear, and sorrow had lifted. No longer drooping or dull, she appeared lightened—like one who had tasted healing and flown on wings of hope.

After tea, Hortense went upstairs. She hadn’t sorted through her drawers in weeks—and now the urge was irresistible. While she was gone, the conversation fell naturally into Caroline’s hands. She carried it effortlessly, slipping into her finest tone. Her speech flowed with grace and elegance, adding charm to familiar topics. Her voice, always soft, now held a new music—gentle, surprising, captivating. Unusual expressions lit her face with character and warmth.

"Caroline," Moore said after watching her intently, "you look as if you’ve heard good news."

"Do I?"

"I invited you tonight to cheer myself—but you cheer me more than I expected."

"I’m glad. And I truly cheer you?"

"You shine—your eyes sparkle, your movements are light, your voice sings."

"It’s pleasant to be here again."

"Indeed it is. And seeing health on your face and hope in your eyes is a joy, Cary. But what is this hope? What has brought this light I see around you?"

"For one thing," she said, "I’m happy with mamma. I love her so much—and she loves me. She nursed me long and tenderly. Now that her care has restored me, I can give back to her all day. I say it’s my turn to serve her—and I do. I’m her waiting-woman as well as her daughter. I enjoy—oh, you’d laugh if you knew how much pleasure I take in sewing dresses for her. She looks lovely now, Robert. I won’t let her be old-fashioned. And she’s wonderful to talk to—wise, thoughtful, rich in knowledge, endlessly observant. Every day I live with her, I love her more, respect her more, cherish her deeper."

"That’s one thing, then, Cary. You speak of 'mamma' in such a way it makes one jealous of the old lady."

"She’s not old, Robert."

"Of the young lady, then."

"She doesn’t pretend to be young."

"Well, of the matron. But you said 'mamma’s' affection was just one reason for your happiness. What’s the other?"

"I’m glad you’re better."

"What else?"

"I’m glad we’re friends."

"You and I?"

"Yes. I once thought we never would be."

"Cary, some day I mean to tell you something about myself—not to my credit, and so it won’t please you."

"Oh, don’t! I can’t bear to think ill of you."

"And I can’t bear that you think better of me than I deserve."

"But I already half know your secret—I believe I know all of it."

"You don’t."

"I believe I do."

"Whom does it concern besides me?"

She blushed, hesitated, stayed silent.

"Speak, Cary. Whom does it concern?"

She tried to whisper a name—but couldn’t.

"Tell me. Only we’re here. Be honest."

"But if I guess wrong?"

"I’ll forgive. Whisper, Cary."

He leaned close. Still, she wouldn’t—or couldn’t—speak plainly. Seeing his patience waning, she finally said, "Miss Keeldar stayed at the rectory about a week ago. The evening grew bitterly cold, and we persuaded her to stay overnight."

"And you curled your hair together?"

"How do you know?"

"And then you talked—she told you—"

"It wasn’t during curling. So you’re not as wise as you think. And she didn’t tell me."

"You slept together afterwards?"

"We shared the same room and bed. We didn’t sleep much—we talked all night."

"I’ll swear you did! And then it all came out—*tant pis*. I’d rather you’d heard it from me."

"You’re mistaken. She didn’t tell me what you suspect—she’s not the kind to reveal such things. But I guessed—partly from what she said, partly from rumors, mostly from instinct."

"But if she didn’t confess that I wanted to marry her for her money, and she refused me with scorn—(you needn’t start nor blush, nor prick your fingers with your needle. That’s the truth, whether you like it or not)—then what did she talk about? You said you talked all night. About what?"

"About things we’d never discussed before, even though we’ve been close friends. But you don’t expect me to tell you?"

"Yes, yes, Cary—you will tell me. You said we were friends. Friends confide in each other."

"But you promise not to repeat it?"

"Absolutely."

"Not even to Louis?"

"Not even to Louis. What does Louis care about young ladies’ secrets?"

"Robert," she said, "Shirley is a remarkable, noble creature."

"I suppose. I can imagine she has strange and grand qualities."

"I’ve found her guarded with her feelings. But when they burst forth—like a river rushing past you without warning—you stare, wonder, admire… and perhaps… love her."

"You saw that?"

"Yes. At midnight, when the house was silent, and starlight and snow-reflected glow shone in our room—that’s when I saw Shirley’s heart."

"Her heart’s core? Do you think she showed you that?"

"Her heart’s core."

"And how was it?"

"Like a shrine—for it was holy. Like snow—for it was pure. Like flame—for it was warm. Like death—for it was strong."

"Can she love? Tell me that."

"What do you think?"

"She has loved none who loved her."

"Who have loved her?"

He named several gentlemen, ending with Sir Philip Nunnely.

"She has loved none of them."

"Yet some were worthy of a woman’s affection."

"Of some women—yes. But not of Shirley’s."

"Is she better than others of her sex?"

"She is unique—and dangerous to take as a wife… rashly."

"I can imagine that."

"She spoke of you—"

"Oh, she did! I thought you denied it."

"She didn’t speak as you imagine. But I asked her—insisted she tell me what she thought of you, or rather, how she felt toward you. I needed to know. I’d long wanted to know."

"So had I. But let’s hear. She thinks poorly of you, feels contempt—doubtless?"

"She thinks of you almost as highly as a woman can think of a man. You know she can be eloquent. I still feel the warmth of her words in my mind."

"But how does she feel?"

"Till you shocked her—she said you shocked her, but wouldn’t explain how—she felt as a sister might toward a brother she both loves and admires."

"I’ll shock her no more, Cary—for the shock rebounded on me, and I staggered. But that comparison is nonsense. She’s too proud and wealthy to feel fraternal feelings toward me."

"You don’t know her, Robert. And somehow, I think now—though I once believed otherwise—that you never will. You and she are built differently—unable to truly understand each other."

"Perhaps. I admire her, esteem her. Yet my impressions are harsh—perhaps unjust. I believe, for instance, that she is incapable of love—"

"Shirley incapable of love!"

"That she will never marry. I think she fears compromising her pride, surrendering her power, sharing her wealth."

"Shirley has wounded your *amour propre*."

"She did. Though I had no tenderness, no passion for her."

"Then, Robert, it was wicked of you to want to marry her."

"And very base, my little pastor, my sweet priestess. I never wanted to kiss Miss Keeldar—though she has fine lips, red and round as cherries. Or if I did, it was only the eye’s desire."

"Now I doubt whether you’re telling the truth. The fruit is sour—'hung too high.'"

"She has a lovely figure, a lovely face, beautiful hair. I acknowledge her charms—but feel nothing for them, or only in a way she’d despise. I suppose I was tempted by the gold of the bait. Caroline, what a noble fellow your Robert is—great, good, selfless—and so pure!"

"But not perfect. He made a great mistake once—and we’ll hear no more about it."

"And shall we forget it, Cary? Shall we not despise him in our hearts—kind but just, compassionate but upright?"

"Never. We’ll remember: 'With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you.' So we’ll give no scorn—only love."

"Which won’t satisfy," he warned. "Something stronger, sweeter, warmer will be demanded one day. Is it there to give?"

Caroline was deeply moved.

"Be calm, Lina," Moore said gently. "I have no intention—nor right—to disturb you now, nor for months. Don’t look as if you’re leaving. Let’s drop this. Resume our chatter. Don’t tremble. Look at me. See how pale, how weak, how ghost-like I am—more pitiable than frightening."

She looked shyly. "There’s still something formidable," she whispered, her gaze falling under his.

"To return to Shirley," Moore continued. "Do you think she’ll ever marry?"

"She loves."

"Platonically—hypocritically—all humbug!"

"She loves—sincerely."

"Did she say so?"

"I can’t say she confessed it. No words like 'I love this man' passed her lips."

"I thought not."

"But the feeling broke through anyway. I saw it. She spoke of one man in a tone impossible to misunderstand. Her voice alone was testimony. After I forced her to judge your character, I asked for a second opinion—about someone I suspected, though my suspicions were tangled beyond belief. I *made* her speak. I shook her, scolded her, pinched her fingers when she tried to deflect me with jokes. And finally, it came. The voice—barely above a whisper—yet full of soft intensity. No confession, no confidence. These things she refuses. But I know that man’s happiness is as dear to her as her own life."

"Who is it?"

"I accused her. She didn’t deny it. Didn’t admit it. Just looked at me. I saw her eyes in the glimmer of snow-light. That was enough. I triumphed over her mercilessly."

"What right had you to triumph? Are you free yourself?"

"Whatever I am, Shirley is a prisoner. Lioness, she has found her captor. Mistress of all around her, yet not her own master."

"So you rejoice in recognizing a fellow slave—in one so fair and regal?"

"I do. Robert, you’re right—so fair and regal."

"You admit it—a *fellow*-slave?"

"I admit nothing. But I say haughty Shirley is no freer than Hagar."

"And who, pray, is the Abraham—the hero who conquered her?"

"You still speak with scorn. But I’ll make you change your tone before I’m done."

"We’ll see. Can she marry this Cupidon?"

"Cupidon? He’s just as much a Cupidon as you are a Cyclops."

"Can she marry him?"

"You’ll see."

"I want to know his name, Cary."

"Guess."

"Is it someone nearby?"

"Yes—within Briarfield parish."

"Then it’s someone unworthy of her. I know no one in Briarland equal to her."

"Guess."

"Impossible. I suppose she’s deluded—will plunge into absurdity."

Caroline smiled.

"Do you approve?" Moore asked.

"Completely. Completely."

"Then I’m puzzled. For the mind that owns this cascade of hazel curls is a brilliant little machine—accurate, steady, inherited from 'mamma,' I suppose."

"And I *fully* approve. Mamma was charmed."

"'Mamma' charmed—Mrs. Pryor! Then it can’t be romantic?"

"It *is* romantic. But it’s also right."

"Tell me, Cary—out of pity. I’m too weak to be teased."

"You’ll be teased—it won’t hurt you. You’re not as weak as you claim."

"I’ve twice tonight thought of falling at your feet."

"You’d better not. I won’t help you up."

"And worshipping you outright. My mother was a Roman Catholic. You look like one of her images of the Virgin. I’ll convert—kneel and adore."

"Robert, Robert, sit still. Don’t be absurd. I’ll go to Hortense if you carry on."

"You’ve stolen my senses. Now all I can think of is *les litanies de la sainte Vièrge. Rose céleste, reine des anges*!"

"*Tour d’ivoire, maison d’or*—isn’t that the jargon? Sit quietly and solve your riddle."

"But 'mamma' charmed—there’s the puzzle."

"I’ll tell you what mamma said when I told her. 'Depend upon it, my dear, such a choice will bring Miss Keeldar lasting happiness.'"

"I’ll guess once—and no more. It’s old Helstone. She’s going to be your aunt."

"I’ll tell my uncle! I’ll tell Shirley!" Caroline laughed delightedly. "Guess again, Robert—your mistakes are delightful."

"It’s the parson—Hall."

"No indeed. He’s mine, if you please."

"Yours! The whole female generation in Briarfield seems to worship that priest. I wonder why—he’s bald, sand-blind, gray-haired."

"Fanny will be here to fetch me before you solve it, if you don’t hurry."

"I’ll guess no more—I’m tired. And I don’t care. Miss Keeldar may marry *le grand Turc* for all I care."

"Must I whisper?"

"That you must—and quickly. Here comes Hortense. Come closer, my dear Lina. I care more for the whisper than the words."

She whispered. Robert gave a start, a flash in his eye, a brief laugh. Miss Moore entered, and Sarah followed with news that Fanny had arrived. The hour of conversation was over.

Robert managed a final whisper as Caroline descended the stairs, putting on her shawl.

"Must I call Shirley a noble creature now?" he asked.

"If you wish to speak the truth—certainly."

"Must I forgive her?"

"Forgive her? Naughty Robert! Was she wrong, or were you?"

"Must I, at last, love her—truly, deeply?"

Caroline looked up sharply, moving toward him—half loving, half teasing.

"Only give the word, and I’ll try to obey."

"Indeed, you must not love her. The very idea is perverse."
