Let us revisit the little town of Clavering, that cheerful hamlet nestled near Pen's ancestral home of Fairoaks. While the society there was hardly a source of amusement, it did possess its own peculiar brand of theatricality. Among its most spirited inhabitants was Mr. Samuel Huxter, a man whose vacation visits to the village transformed dull tea-tables into stages for the wit of Bartholomew's and the gossip of high London society.

Huxter's circle included Mr. Hobnell, the young gentleman Pen had once thrashed during the Fotheringay affair. At the time, Hobnell was a Grammar School pupil who knew exactly how to navigate Mrs. Huxter's tea-tables and the local surgery's tamarind-pots, often scenting his handkerchiefs with rose-water to impress. It was during these formative years that Hobnell fell for Miss Sophy Huxter. Upon his father's death, he married her and moved them to the Warren, a few miles from Clavering.

The Hobnells had long been yeomen, but Mr. Hobnell Sr. had a vision. He demolished the old farmhouse, erected a glaring white mansion with capacious stables, installed a piano in the drawing-room, and acquired a pack of harriers. Thus, the family ascended from farming to county gentry. When the elder Hobnell passed, his son inherited the title of Squire. Sam Huxter, back in London, took great pride in boasting to his comrades at Bartholomew's about his brother-in-law's hounds, horses, and hospitality.

Every year, when Mrs. Hobnell was too busy with her nursery duties to travel, Squire Hobnell would descend upon London for a "lark." He secured rooms at the Tavistock and, alongside his learned brother-in-law Sam, indulged in the city's pleasures. They toured Ascot, the theatres, Vauxhall, and the convivial taverns of Covent Garden. As Squire Hobnell declared, "When I'm in London, I like to do as London does, and 'go it a bit.'" Upon returning to the West Country, he would present Mrs. Hobnell with a new bonnet and shawl, trading elegant metropolitan amusements for country sports for the next eleven months.

Sam and Hobnell maintained a lively correspondence. Sam sent news of the metropolis in exchange for baskets of hares, partridges, and clouted cream from the Warren. To the Huxters, Sam was the life and soul of the household; his songs and jokes kept the Warren in a perpetual roar. He had even once saved their eldest child's life by extracting a fishbone from her throat. He was, simply put, the delight of their circle.

However, fate, that mischievous playwright, arranged for Pen to encounter Mr. Huxter just three days after their altercation at Vauxhall. True to his vow, Pen had not visited little Fanny. He was attempting to drown his thoughts of her in relentless work, laboring incessantly in his rooms and delivering savage critiques of poems and romances for the *Pall Mall Gazette*. Having slain these literary foes, he retreated to the lonely club of the Polyanthus, where the vast silence only deepened his gloom. He sought relief in the theaters, but the roaring laughter of the crowds only highlighted the ignoble farces he despised, leaving him with a dismal face that would have dampened even the buffoon on stage.

Exhausted from walking twenty miles over Hampstead Common and Hendon lanes in search of rest, Pen decided to visit the Back Kitchen, his old haunt with Warrington. He wasn't sleepy yet, but the thought of seeing Bows offered a comfort. Bows was presiding calmly over the old piano while tremendous comic songs cracked the room with laughter. To Pen, these songs seemed alien; amidst the merriment, he could only focus on Bows. It was a marvel, he mused, that a breast he claimed was an extinct volcano could still feel such a flame. Two days of indulgence had kindled it; two days of abstinence had set it burning in fury.

Drinking glass after glass, Arthur's gaze fell upon Mr. Huxter, who had attended the theater with two or three comrades. Huxter whispered something to his friends that clearly annoyed Pen. Arthur sensed he was the subject of conversation. Huxter then worked his way through the room, followed by his cronies, until he reached Pen's table. He nodded familiarly and extended a dirty hand for a shake.

Pen accepted the handshake, feeling a pang of regret for his savagery the previous night. Huxter, however, was in perfect good humor with himself and the world; the idea that he could be disagreeable never crossed his mind. The "chaff" of Vauxhall was a triviality he barely registered.

The group ordered four stouts and began searching for a topic that would amuse Pen, landing precisely on the one most painful to him.

"A jolly night at Vauxhall, wasn't it?" Huxter asked, winking with knowing familiarity.

"I'm glad you liked it," groaned Pen in spirit.

"I was dev'lish cut—uncommonly so," Huxter continued, feigning offense. "I've been dining with some chaps at Greenwich. That was a pretty bit of muslin hanging on your arm—who was she?"

The question broke Pen. "Have I asked you any questions about yourself, Mr. Huxter?"

"I didn't mean any offence—beg pardon—hang it, you cut up quite savage," Huxter exclaimed, astonished.

"Do you remember what took place between us the other night?" Pen asked, his wrath gathering. "You forget? Very probably. You were tipsy, as you observed just now, and very rude."

"Hang it, sir, I asked your pardon," Huxter said, turning red.

"You did certainly, and it was granted with all my heart," Pen replied sharply. "But if you recollect, I begged that you would omit me from the list of your acquaintance for the future. And when we meet in public, that you would not take the trouble to recognize me. Will you please remember this henceforth? And since the song is beginning, permit me to leave you to your unrestrained enjoyment of the music."

Taking his hat and bowing to the amazed Mr. Huxter, Pen departed. As Huxter's comrades paused in wonder, they erupted into a roar of laughter at Huxter's expense, prompting the room's president to bawl, "Silence, gentlemen; do have silence for the Body Snatcher!" as the popular song began. Pen flattered himself that he had commanded his temper perfectly. He actually wished Huxter had been pugnacious; he would have preferred to fight someone. He went home, but the day's work, the dinner, the play, the whisky-and-water, and the quarrel offered no solace. He slept no better than the previous night.

A few days later, Mr. Sam Huxter wrote home to Mr. Hobnell, making Arthur Pendennis the principal subject. Sam described Arthur's London pursuits and his "confounded insolence" toward old friends. He labeled him an abandoned criminal, a regular Don Juan, and a fellow who, if he ever returned to the country, ought to be barred from honest households. He claimed to have seen Arthur dancing with an innocent girl from the lower ranks, making her a victim. Huxter had identified the girl through an Irish gentleman in the army and intended to warn her father. The letter concluded with thanks for the last parcel of game and a hint of readiness for further favors.

Once a year, a christening occurred at the Warren. This ceremony took place the day after Hobnell received Sam's letter. The infant, a darling little girl, was named Myra Lucretia after her godmothers, Miss Portman and Mrs. Pybus of Clavering. Naturally, Hobnell showed the letter to his wife, and Mrs. Hobnell, in turn, imparted its horrid contents to her gossips.

By evening, the story of Arthur's alleged depravity was circulating through every corner of Clavering society. Myra was too shocked to speak, but Mrs. Pybus had no such reserve. She discussed the matter with Mrs. Portman, the Honorable Mrs. Simcoe, Mrs. Glanders (ordering her daughters out of the room), Madame Fribsby, and everyone else. Madame Fribsby, glancing furtively at her picture of a dragoon and inwardly at her own wounded memories, remarked that men were deceivers by nature and quoted lines from *Marmion* about where deceiving lovers should rest. Mrs. Pybus, however, possessed words of hatred and horror strong enough for such a villain. She attributed the behavior to early indulgence, insolence, extravagance, and aristocratic airs—specifically noting that Pen had refused to drink tea with her—and the corrupt parties of modern Babylon.

Mrs. Portman feared that her own fatal partiality had spoiled the boy, that his literary success had turned his head and his passions made him forget the principles Doctor Portman had instilled. Captain Glanders, the atrocious Dragoon, whistled and made jocular allusions to the affair at dinner, earning a rebuke from Mrs. Glanders and another order to remove the girls. Mr. Simcoe remained calm, merely confirming his low opinion of the "wretched young man" without having read a single line of his "dangerous and poisonous works."

Pen became the subject of a second sermon at the Clavering chapel-of-ease, where the dangers of London and the crime of writing novels were preached to a large congregation. They did not wait to hear if he was guilty; they took his wickedness for granted and eagerly threw stones at poor Pen.

The next day, Mrs. Pendennis, alone and nearly fainting from emotion and fatigue, ran to Dr. Portman's house. She had received an anonymous letter—a "Christian" thought it their duty to stab the good soul who had never harmed anyone. The letter referenced Scripture, pointed out the doom of sinners, and detailed Arthur's crime. Two hours of this agony had aged her visibly. In her first moment of agitation, she dropped the letter, and Laura read it. Laura blushed, her whole frame trembling with anger. "The cowards," she said. "It isn't true. No, mother, it isn't true."

"It is true, and you've done it, Laura!" cried Helen fiercely. "Why did you refuse him when he asked you? Why did you break my heart? It is you who led him into crime. It is you who flung him into the arms of this—this woman. Don't speak to me. Don't answer me. I will never forgive you, never. Martha, bring me my bonnet and shawl. I'll go out. I won't have you come with me. Go away. Leave me, cruel girl; why have you brought this shame on me?"

Bidding her daughter and servants away, she ran down the road to Clavering.

Dr. Portman glanced at the letter, recognized the handwriting, and knew the charge against poor Pen. Against his own conscience, perhaps (for the worthy Doctor, like most, had a natural aptitude for believing reports unfavorable to his neighbors), he strove to console Helen. He pointed out that the slander came from an anonymous quarter and must be the work of a rascal; that the charge was likely untrue; that Pen must be heard before condemnation; that the son of such a mother was unlikely to commit such a crime.

Helen saw through his feint immediately. "You think he has done it," she said. "You know you think he has done it. Oh, why did I ever leave him, Doctor Portman, or suffer him away from me? But he can't be dishonest—pray God, not dishonest—you don't think that, do you? Remember his conduct about that other person—how madly he was attached to her. He was an honest boy then—he is now. And I thank God—yes, I fall down on my knees and thank God he paid Laura. You said he was good—you did yourself. And now, if this woman loves him—and you know they must—if he has taken her from her home, or she tempted him, which is most likely—why still, she must be his wife and my daughter. And he must leave the dreadful world and come back to me—to his mother, Doctor Portman. Let us go away and bring him back—yes—bring him back—and there shall be joy for the sinner that repenteth. Let us go now, directly, dear friend—this very—"

Helen could say no more. She fell back and fainted. She was carried to a bed in the Doctor's house, and the surgeon was summoned. She lay all night in an alarming state. Laura came to the rectory, though Helen refused to see her. Doctor Portman, still beseeching her to be tranquil and growing bolder in his confidence of Arthur's innocence as he witnessed the terrible grief of the poor mother, wrote a letter to Pen. He warned him of the rumors against him and earnestly prayed that he would break off and repent of a connection so fatal to his best interests and his soul's welfare.

And what of Laura? Was her heart not wrung by the thought of Arthur's crime and Helen's estrangement? Was it not a bitter blow for the innocent girl to realize that in one stroke she might lose all the love she cared for in the world?
