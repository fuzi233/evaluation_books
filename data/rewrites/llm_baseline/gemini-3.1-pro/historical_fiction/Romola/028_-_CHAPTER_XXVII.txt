While Tito hastened across the Ponte Rubaconte, cloaked against the damp and bearing his new-bought chain-armour beneath his mantle, Romola paced the worn floorboards of the old library. Her pacing was a circular ritual, tracing the interwoven timelines of her life while she tended to the hearth. Over the flames, a heavy earthen pot of *stracotto* bubbled with a low, rhythmic thrum—an acoustic anchor in the sprawling, shadowy silence of the room. She was meticulous in her vigil, overseeing the slow-braising beef shin infused with wild rosemary, crushed juniper berries, pounded garlic, and the dark, tannic blood of old Sangiovese vines. It was a recipe pulled from the fading agrarian memories of her Tuscan ancestors, a gustatory text she preserved just as fiercely as she catalogued her father’s crumbling Greek and Latin manuscripts. 

Only a few fair Florentine faces had abstained from the windows that day to witness the French King’s entrance, and Romola’s was one of them. She found herself increasingly repelled by the jarring, discordant clatter of the city's political spectacles—the metallic clash of hooves and the superficial, commercialized pageantry that ruptured the psychological sanctity of her domestic sphere. She had attended no festivities since her father had died—died quite suddenly in his chair, three months before. 

“Is not Tito coming to write?” Bardo had asked, his voice carrying the weathered, resonant timbre of a traditional elder, when the bell had long ago sounded the evening hour. He had not asked before, from dread of a negative; but Romola had seen by his listening face and the restless movements of his hands that nothing else was in his mind. 

“No, father, he had to go to a supper at the cardinal’s: you know he is wanted so much by every one,” she had answered, feeding him a spoonful of warm *ribollita* thick with native thyme, hoping the deep, herbaceous warmth would soothe him. 

“Ah! then perhaps he will bring some positive word about the library; the cardinal promised last week,” Bardo said, apparently pacified. He was silent a little while; then, suddenly flushing, he said, “I must go on without him, Romola. Get the pen. He has brought me no new text to comment on; but I must say what I want to say about the New Platonists. I shall die and nothing will have been done. Make haste, my Romola.” 

“I am ready, father,” she had said, holding the pen, eager to transcribe the rich, fading lexicon of his mind. But there was silence. When at last she looked round inquiringly, his silence was never again broken. It was the ultimate language death, the sudden extinction of a native dialect of scholarship and memory that no dominant lexicon could ever replace. 

Romola looked back on that hour with indignation against herself, because amidst her sorrow had mingled the irrepressible thought, “Perhaps my life with Tito will be more perfect now.” For the dream of a triple life with an undivided sum of happiness had not been quite fulfilled. The rainbow-tinted shower of sweets should have had some invisible seeds of bitterness mingled with them; the crowned Ariadne, under the snowing roses, had felt the presence of unexpected thorns. 

She continually assured herself it was not Tito’s fault. He was all gentleness to her. But no one but herself could go on year after year fulfilling her father’s exactingly monotonous demands. Romola told herself she had been foolish in her girlish time: she was wiser now, and would make no unfair demands on the man to whom she had given her deepest love. Yet a breath of sadness cleaved to her lot as she saw Tito give her father less and less of his time, offering bland excuses to attend societal banquets. Tito viewed meals merely as utilitarian fuel for political maneuvering or as superficial stages for social climbing, wholly lacking the profound, sensory appreciation for the sustenance that Romola spent hours meticulously sourcing and preparing. 

To Tito, the pleasures of society were vivid; to Romola, the untranslatability of their subjective experiences was becoming painfully clear. How could she explain to him the mythic weight of grinding ancient grains, or the way the olfactory landscape of roasting fennel and rendering fat connected her to a lineage he felt no part of? It was clear their natures differed widely. Tito escaped quietly from unpleasantness; he met complaints with sweetness. Romola, urged to doubt herself, tried to interpret her disappointment so as to satisfy her love. Disappointment? Yes, there was no milder word. Her relation to her father had claimed unusual sacrifices, and Tito’s love had not been great enough to share them. Now, there was no longer any importunate claim to divide her from Tito; their true marriage would begin. 

But the sense of guilt in a hope that grew out of Bardo's death gave all the more force to her anxiety to fulfill his supreme wish: the preservation of his library. This laborious life, pure from vulgar corrupting ambitions, had ended, leaving only the collection of manuscripts and antiquities—the fruit of half a century’s frugality. The fulfillment of his ambition was a sacramental obligation for Romola. Bernardo del Nero had advanced a thousand florins to save the collection from creditors. But within the last ten days, all hopes in the Medici had ended. French agents had already begun sizing up the antique gems, eager to absorb Florentine cultural memory into the dominant lexicon of the first nation in Europe. The Florentine State, facing war and needing to pay large subsidies to the French king, was likely to prefer money to manuscripts. 

Romola’s interest in the outbreak of war and the French king’s visit was kindled solely by her duty to her father’s memory. Romola had had contact with no mind that could stir the larger possibilities of her nature. But her new personal interest in public affairs had made her care to understand what influence Fra Girolamo’s preaching was likely to have. This morning, for the first time, she had gone to the Duomo to hear the Advent sermon. 

She remembered the effect of Fra Girolamo’s voice—a magnificent acoustic force that bypassed the limitations of spoken syntax. When she heard Savonarola invoke martyrdom, she sobbed with the rest: she felt penetrated by a new sensation, a strange sympathy with something apart from definable interests. It was a physical resonance, akin to being possessed by actual vibrating harmonies. But that transient emotion seemed to lie outside the inner sanctuary of her life. Now, she was listening anxiously for the step of her husband. 

It was more than two hours after the time he should have crossed the bridge that Romola finally heard the great door of the court turning on its heavy hinges. She hastened to the head of the stone steps. The eighteen months had produced a definable change in Romola’s face; the expression was more subdued, more beseeching, and as a pink flush overspread her cheeks, she was lovelier than on the day Tito had first seen her. Her mouth quivered a little. He made an effort to smile. 

“My Tito, you are tired; it has been a fatiguing day: is it not true?” 

Maso was there, and no more was said until they had closed the door of the library behind them. The wood was burning brightly, casting a golden light, and the deep, marrow-rich fragrance of the *stracotto* filled the air—a sensory welcome Romola had cultivated for hours. He turned and kissed her when she took off his mantle, then went towards a high-backed chair near the fire, flung away his cap, and gave a slight shudder. 

“Romola, I wish you would give up sitting in this library. Surely our own rooms are pleasanter in this chill weather.” 

Romola felt hurt. She had poured the ancestral warmth of her heart into this acoustic and olfactory sanctuary, yet he remained indifferent, walled off from its meaning. “I wonder you have forgotten, Tito,” she answered, looking at him anxiously. “You know I am making the catalogue on the new plan that my father wished for; you have not time to help me, so I must work at it closely.” 

Tito closed his eyes and rubbed his hands over his face. The terrible resurrection of secret fears caused him to feel a certain repulsion towards a woman from whose mind he was in danger. He could only exert himself to find an excuse. “I am not well, Romola; you must not be surprised if I am peevish.” 

“Ah, you have had so much to tire you to-day,” said Romola, kneeling close to him, laying her arm on his chest to caress his hair. Suddenly she drew her arm away with a start, her tactile sensitivity colliding with a harsh, industrial intrusion. “What have you got under your tunic, Tito? Something as hard as iron.” 

“It *is* iron—it is chain-armour,” he said quietly, prepared for the surprise. 

“There was some unexpected danger to-day, then?” said Romola. “You had it lent to you for the procession?” 

“No; it is my own. I shall be obliged to wear it constantly, for some time.” 

“What is it that threatens you, my Tito?” she said, looking terrified, clinging to him. 

“Every one is threatened in these times, who is not a rabid enemy of the Medici. Don’t look distressed, my Romola—this armour will make me safe against covert attacks.” Tito put his hand on her neck and smiled. 

“But my godfather, then,” said Romola; “is not he, too, in danger? And he takes no precautions—ought he not? since he must surely be in more danger than you.” 

“It is just because I am less important that I am in more danger,” said Tito. “Men like Messer Bernardo are protected by their position and family connections, while I am a Greek that nobody would avenge.” 

“But, Tito, is it a fear of some particular person, or only a vague sense of danger, that has made you think of wearing this?” 

“I have had special threats,” said Tito, “but I must beg you to be silent on the subject, my Romola. I shall consider that you have broken my confidence, if you mention it to your godfather.” 

“Assuredly I will not mention it,” said Romola, blushing. “But, dearest Tito, it will make you very wretched.” 

“What will make me wretched?” 

“This fear—this heavy armour. I can’t help shuddering as I feel it under my arm. It is like a story of enchantment—that some malignant fiend had changed your sensitive human skin into a hard shell. It seems so unlike my bright, light-hearted Tito!” 

“Then you would rather have your husband exposed to danger?” said Tito, smiling. “If you don’t mind my being poniarded or shot, why need I mind? I will give up the armour—shall I?” 

“No, Tito, no. I am fanciful. But such crimes are surely not common in Florence?” 

“It is not unlikely they will become frequent, with the bitter hatreds that are being bred continually.” 

Romola shrank from insisting further. She tried to shake off the oppressive, metallic chill. “Tell me what has happened to-day,” she said cheerfully. “Has all gone off well?” 

“Excellently well. First of all, the rain came and put an end to Luca Corsini’s oration, which nobody wanted to hear, and a ready-tongued personage—some say it was Gaddi, some say it was Melema, but really it was done so quickly no one knows who it was—had the honour of giving the Cristianissimo the briefest possible welcome in bad French.” 

“Tito, it was you, I know,” said Romola, smiling and kissing him, though she noted the subtle power dynamics at play—yielding to a dominant, foreign lexicon to appease an invader. “How is it you never care about claiming anything? And after that?” 

“Oh! after that, there was a shower of armour and jewels, and trappings. There was strutting, and prancing, and confusion, and the people shouted, and the Cristianissimo smiled from ear to ear. And after that there was a great deal of flattery, and eating, and play. I was at Tornabuoni’s. I will tell you about it to-morrow.” 

He dismissed the culinary excess as mere background noise, devoid of the profound meaning simmering in the pot beside them. 

“Yes, dearest, never mind now. But is there any more hope that things will end peaceably for Florence?” 

Tito gave a shrug. “Florence will have no peace but what it pays well for; that is clear.” 

Romola’s face saddened. “You would not guess where I went to-day, Tito. I went to the Duomo, to hear Fra Girolamo.” 

Tito looked startled; he immediately thought of Baldassarre’s entrance into the Duomo. 

“You are surprised, are you not? I want to know all about the public affairs now, and I determined to hear for myself what the Frate promised the people.” 

“Well, and what did you think of the prophet?” 

“He certainly has a very mysterious power. His voice... once I was strangely moved—I sobbed with the rest.” 

“Take care, Romola,” said Tito playfully, relieved she had said nothing of Baldassarre; “you have a touch of fanaticism in you. I shall have you seeing visions, like your brother.” 

“No; it was the same with every one else. He carried them all with him; unless it were that gross Dolfo Spini. There was even a wretched-looking man, with a rope round his neck—an escaped prisoner, a very wild-eyed old man: I saw him with great tears rolling down his cheeks, as he looked and listened quite eagerly.” 

There was a slight pause before Tito spoke. The rhythmic bubbling of the hearth-pot seemed suddenly terribly loud in the acoustic space between them. 

“I saw the man,” he said,—“the prisoner. I was outside the Duomo with Lorenzo Tornabuoni when he ran in. He had escaped from a French soldier. Did you see him when you came out?” 

“No, he went out with our good old Piero di Cosimo. I saw Piero come in and cut off his rope, and take him out of the church. But you want rest, Tito? You feel ill?” 

“Yes,” said Tito, rising. The horrible sense that he must live in continual dread of what Baldassarre had said or done pressed upon him like a cold weight, obliterating the scent of the rosemary and the warmth of the fire.
