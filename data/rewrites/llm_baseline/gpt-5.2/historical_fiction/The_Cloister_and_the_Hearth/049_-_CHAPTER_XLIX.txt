Margaret Brandt had never belonged to Sevenbergen the way other women did.

She lived among them, bargained with their merchants, nodded to them at church doors and market-stalls; yet she carried herself as if some thin pane of glass stood between her and their breath. They called it pride. She had heard the word passed like a dry crust from mouth to mouth, and she knew what hunger sat beneath it: jealousy, and that relish people have for another’s fall—especially when it can be served hot and public.

Now the fall was ripening within her, day by day, and could not be kept under cloak much longer.

It was not that she was blind to her own case. Margaret’s mind, though unlettered in the fine wire-drawn distinctions of law, had the plain, hard sense that in those times often sat more firmly in women’s judgment than in men’s. A betrothal, made solemnly, written and witnessed, was no child’s ribbon to be untied at whim; it was a bond. She knew, too, what old custom and both Church and State had long held: that a woman betrothed might rightly look on her troth as marriage; that children born of such union were not bastards in God’s sight nor always in man’s.

But law and conscience were not what tormented her. It was the world.

Her written contract—her “marriage lines,” the one stout paper that could have stood like a trencher before the barking of tongues—was not in her chest, not under her key. In a moment of foolish trust—trust that had felt as natural as taking bread from a hand she loved—she had let Gerard carry it away. And Gerard was far, far off; and the paper, like a spice locked in some distant cupboard, might scent nothing here until it was too late.

She hated herself for that oversight with an honest ferocity. If she could have boiled it out of her like bitterness from an over-strong herb, she would have done it. Yet this was the one thing that could not be strained away.

For what would the tale sound like, once her condition could no longer be hidden under dark gowns and hurried steps?

“I have my marriage lines, but I cannot show them.”

It was a story fit to be laughed into crumbs. Women would not believe it; and it was women’s belief she craved, not men’s easy pardon. Men might shrug and call it nature; women would measure her, taste the scandal on their tongues, and decide she had been wanton. A bare slander is bad enough; but here was slander glazed with the shine of visible fact.

In those days there was no fashion of a woman standing up and calling herself “strong-minded,” no neat word to varnish endurance. Margaret had been mistress of her father’s house since she was sixteen; she could rule servants, keep accounts, portion flour and salted fish through a hard winter without waste. Yet in this she was weak as water: not because her conscience accused her, but because she foresaw the pitch of public shame laid ready to smear her name.

A virtuous woman who foresees herself paraded as a frail one knows a torment that needs few words. It is like smelling smoke in one’s own kitchen while the door is shut and the baby sleeps: the dread is constant, and the mind runs in frantic circles—bucket, cloth, prayer—yet the flames are not in sight, only the certainty they will be.

So Margaret hid. She stayed indoors; she shunned daylight; when she must go out she went as if the air itself could point at her. She would sit for long stretches, hands idle in her lap, hearing nothing yet everything: the scrape of a neighbor’s shoe on cobbles beyond the shutters, the caw of a crow, the water’s faint slap against a wharf-pile—each sound seemed an omen, each laugh in the street a knife.

Often her father would talk to her, and she would answer properly, as one answers while kneading dough: the hands work, the mind is elsewhere. Then without warning her cheek would burn as if a coal had been set beneath the skin; and old Peter would pause and peer at her, wondering what word of his had stung her. It was no word. It was the ever-present fear rising like heat from an oven she could not cool.

But the fear leapt into panic after her visit to Margaret Van Eyck’s.

There, among women, her secret had been seen. The old artist, secluded in his own quiet, might keep counsel; his house was like a larder with the door shut—what was within stayed within. But Catherine—Catherine was in the middle of a large household and a tight neighborhood, where news traveled faster than yeast in warm milk. She had the habit of speech, the bright-eyed appetite for telling and being told.

One spark of hope remained: Catherine had spoken kindly, almost lovingly. Such a woman, once roused, must become either the warmest friend or the sharpest enemy; there was no lukewarmness in her.

Margaret waited as one waits for the kettle to boil when there is no firewood left—listening, listening. No word came. The silence itself began to taste like betrayal. Catherine was not going to shelter her; Catherine was going to talk. And then, in Margaret, the wish to flee began to grow—at first a small stirring, like a mouse in the thatch; then a gnawing that would not stop; then a wild, passionate craving to be gone and hidden somewhere no one knew her name.

Yet how could she wrench her father from his place? Old people cling to their hearthstones as ivy clings to a wall. Peter was very old, and infirm. To ask him to change his lodging was to ask a tree to walk.

There seemed only one course: to make him her confidant. Better to tell him than to run away without him. And still she could not do it. The thought of speaking it out to a man—even to her father—made her throat close and her hands go cold. Between the urge to fly and the dread to confess, she hung in a torment that vibrated every hour.

Then, one day, between those two pressures came a third thought, sudden and fatal, slipping into her mind like a knife into a sheath:

End all.

There were canals in this land, calm and deep, threading through daily life as naturally as a pot-hook hangs above the fire. People cursed them in winter, praised them in summer, crossed them, hauled goods along them, washed in them, drowned in them. Catherine had her superstitions about them, her fears of water’s quiet treachery; and now that same quiet tempted Margaret.

One afternoon she stood on a bank and looked down. The water lay smooth and dark, smelling of reeds and cold mud, a smell like shut cellars. It did not roar. It did not threaten. It offered repose. In the distance she heard the faint clop of a cart, the call of a child, the creak of a boat’s rope against a post. The ordinary sounds of living seemed far away from that still surface.

No more trouble. No more waiting for shame. No more imagining women’s eyes.

If Gerard had not loved her—if she had not been able to taste his love as surely as she could taste salt—she might have ended there. But love, even absent, has weight.

She sank to her knees by the water and prayed, breathless and fervent, as if her words could build a railing between her and the brink.

“Oh, keep such wicked thoughts from me,” she whispered. “Selfish wretch—to leave my father. Wicked wretch—to kill my child, and make my poor Gerard lose all his pain and peril undertaken for my sight.”

And then, as if the prayer itself had given her a new backbone, she said aloud, “I will tell father all—ay, ere this sun shall set.”

She rose and hurried home as if chased, fearing that her good resolve might ooze away like melted butter before she reached the door.

In the house, domestic matters had always been her realm. Learned Peter could read the world’s secrets in plants and stars, yet in household ordering he was as simple as a child. Margaret had governed gently but absolutely; her word had been the pinch of salt that makes a whole pot right.

So it was strange—almost unnatural—to see her now, standing before him with the faltering hesitation of a servant asking leave, while the true master of the house sat less important, in that moment, than Martin Wittenhaagen or even the little girl who came each morning for a pittance to scour vessels and went home at night.

“Father,” she began, and her voice was small, “I would speak to thee.”

“Speak on, girl,” said Peter, without lifting his eyes from the simple work before him.

“Wilt listen to me? And—and—not—and try to excuse my faults?”

He looked up then, mild in the face, and said, “We have all our faults, Margaret—thou no more than the rest of us; but fewer, unless parental feeling blinds me.”

“Alas, no, father.” Her hands twisted together. “I am a poor foolish girl, that would fain do well, but have done ill—most ill, most unwisely—and now must bear the shame. But, father, I love you, with all my faults; and will not you forgive my folly, and still love your motherless girl?”

“That ye may count on,” said Peter cheerfully.

“Oh—well—smile not.” The plea came out sharp with pain. “For then how can I speak and make you sad?”

“Why, what is the matter?”

“Father—disgrace is coming on this house; it is at the door. And I the culprit.” Her breath came quick. “Oh, father, turn your head away. I—I—father, I have let Gerard take away my marriage lines.”

Peter’s brows lifted. “Is that all? ’Twas an oversight.”

“’Twas the deed of a mad woman. But woe is me—that is not the worst.”

He cut in, comforting by habit. “The youth is honest, and loves you dear. You are young. What is a year or two to you? Gerard will assuredly come back and keep troth.”

“And meantime know you what is coming?” Her voice broke.

“Not I, except that I shall be gone first for one.”

“Worse than that. There is worse pain than death.” She shut her eyes as if bracing for a blow. “Nay, for pity’s sake turn away your head, father.”

“Foolish wench!” muttered Peter; yet he turned his head aside.

She trembled so hard her bones seemed to click. Her cheeks were aflame; it felt as if all the heat of the hearth had leapt into her face. She forced the words out, each one catching.

“I did look on Gerard as my husband—we being betrothed—and he was in so sore danger, and I thought I had killed him, and I—oh, if you were but my mother I might find courage: you would question me.” She made a helpless little motion with her hands. “But you say not a word.”

He turned his head back, astonishment and impatience mingled. “Why, Margaret, what is all this coil about? And why are thy cheeks crimson, speaking to no stranger, but to thy old father?”

“Why are my cheeks on fire?” A sob strangled her; the truth would not come shaped into decent speech. “Because—because—father, kill me; send me to heaven! Bid Martin shoot me with his arrow! And then the gossips will come and tell you why I blush so this day. And then, when I am dead, I hope you will love your girl again for her mother’s sake.”

Peter’s face hardened—not in cruelty, but in alarm. “Give me thy hand, mistress,” said he.

She held it out, trembling.

He took it gently, and, more physician than father in that instant, began to feel her pulse, his eyes intent, his mouth set. Anxiety crept into his features.

“Alas, nay,” she cried, snatching at breath. “’Tis my soul that burns, not my body, with fever. I cannot, will not, bide in Sevenbergen.” And she wrung her hands with an impatience that was half despair.

“Be calm now,” said the old man, softening at once, “nor torment thyself for nought. Not bide in Sevenbergen? What need to bide a day, as it vexes thee and puts thee in a fever? For fevered thou art, deny it not.”

“What!” Margaret stared at him as if a door had opened where she expected a wall. “Would you yield to go hence, and—and ask no reason but my longing to be gone?”

And suddenly she dropped to her knees beside him, seized his sleeve, then his arm, then clung to his shoulder, pouring out supplication with the fierce gratitude of the reprieved.

“Oh, go—go, father! Quit this place, and ask me not why. Alas, what needs it? You will soon see it. And I could never say it. I would liever die.”

“Foolish child,” he answered, and there was tenderness under the grumble, “who seeks thy girlish secrets? Is it I, whose life hath been spent in searching Nature’s?” He sighed, and his gaze wandered, as if the room itself had grown strange to him. “And for leaving Sevenbergen—what is there to keep me in it, thee unwilling? Is there respect for me here, or gratitude? Am I not yclept quacksalver by those that come not near me, and wizard by those I heal? And give they not the guerdon and the honour they deny me to the empirics that slaughter them? Besides, what is’t to me where we sojourn? Choose thou that, as did thy mother before thee.”

At that, Margaret’s strength ran out. She embraced him, and wept against his shoulder, her tears warm through his worn cloth. The reprieve was like cool milk after thirst: she drank it greedily, yet even as she wept she almost wished she had found the courage to tell him everything—had laid the whole truth on the table like a loaf sliced open, so it could not be misnamed by anyone.

After a while Peter would not be content until she took a medicine. He rose, went to fetch it with the briskness of one who can mend bodies if not reputations, and brought her a composing draught. She swallowed it obediently—if only to please him, for what else could she offer? Though given under a common error, it did her more good than harm. The heaviness of sleep came down on her like a clean linen cloth laid over fevered brow and thoughts.

She woke calmer after a long rest. That very day she began to prepare.

There were small domestic tasks that had to be done as if nothing world-shaking were at stake: linen counted and folded, jars checked, the good knife wrapped, little store of spices tied up—pepper that had cost dear, dried herbs from summer, a knob of nutmeg treasured like a relic. In such work there is a kind of prayer; the hands can obey when the heart cannot. The house made its accustomed noises: the scrape of a chest-lid, the thump of a bundle set down, the low hiss of embers. Outside, the town’s soundscape went on—voices, carts, bells—indifferent as ever. Margaret listened to it with a new hunger: soon those particular noises would no longer define her days.

The next week they went to Rotterdam, bag and baggage, and lodged above a tailor’s shop in the Brede-Kirk Straet, where the air smelled of cloth, chalk, and hot irons, and the street below stitched together many tongues into one restless murmur.

Only one person in Tergou knew whither they were gone: the Burgomaster. He locked the knowledge in his own breast. What use he would make of it ere long could not easily be divined—for he did not divine it himself. But time, which kneads all scattered things into consequence, would show.
