The estate of Castlewood was a place of plenty—its halls filled with the laughter of dutiful servants, its lands yielding abundance, its name respected throughout Virginia. Madam Esmond Warrington presided over it all with the authority of a sovereign, her rule unquestioned by neighbors, her children obedient to her will. Yet, for all her blessings—her wealth, her station, her devout piety—there was a thorn in her contentment.

She had been a dutiful daughter, obedient to her parents’ wishes, even in marriage. But obedience had not tempered her spirit. She was a woman who demanded submission, and woe to those who resisted. Her father, the old Colonel, had watched her imperious nature with amusement, but now that he was gone, there was none left to check her.

The English Warringtons, her late husband’s family, found little favor in her eyes. She had married beneath her, she often reminded herself—a mere younger son of a Norfolk baronet. The Warringtons, in turn, thought little of this proud colonial widow who spoke of them with such disdain. Once, formal letters had passed between them, but when word reached England of her sharp tongue, the correspondence ceased.

Nor did her sons escape her dominion. George, her eldest, inherited his grandfather’s thoughtful nature, and this often set him at odds with her. When the matter of his inheritance arose—six thousand pounds left in trust, which she believed should be hers to command—George sided with the lawyers against her. She could not forgive him for it.

"You would see your brother left with nothing!" she cried. "While you hoard your fortune, must Harry beg for scraps?"

George, wounded, could only reply: "I have no power over the money, mother. Would that I did, if only to give you peace."

But peace was not in her nature. She took to saving fiercely for Harry, cutting luxuries, halting improvements, even regretting the grand carriage she had imported from England.

"Three hundred and eighty guineas!" she lamented. "Oh, Harry, if only I had that now for you!"

Harry, ever cheerful, laughed. "You’ll provide for me while you live, and George will after. I’ll not starve."

She turned a cold eye upon George. "Not unless he learns charity—which I pray for daily."

At this, George’s composure broke. "Would that you could bless me as well, mother!" he exclaimed, tears choking his voice.

Harry flung an arm around him. "Pay no mind, George. I know your heart."

But the wound festered.

---

It was in this uneasy household that Harry first met the lady who would reveal a long-buried truth.

Seated beneath a portrait of a radiant young woman—painted by Kneller in Queen Anne’s day—the stranger studied Harry with keen interest.

"Are you my cousin too?" Harry asked, sensing a kindness in her that his other relatives lacked.

She smiled faintly, gesturing to the painting above her. "Do you not recognize that face?"

Harry shook his head.

"It was mine once," she said softly. "I was Beatrix Esmond."

A silence fell.

"Your mother never spoke of me?"

"Never."

The lady’s lips tightened. "Then let this be your first lesson in family secrets, child. Your mother is my half-sister—and she has erased me from her history."
