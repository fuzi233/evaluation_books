The next morning, the boys and I loaded the cart with bundles of bamboo and set off to tend the avenue of fruit trees near Tentholm. We left the buffalo behind, wanting his wounded nostrils to heal fully before putting him to work again.

Many of the young trees had toppled, while others leaned precariously. We carefully straightened them, digging deep to secure their roots before propping them up with sturdy bamboo stakes, lashing them firmly with broad fibers.

As we worked, Franz, handing me the fibers, asked, *"Papa, are these wild trees or tame ones?"*

Jack burst out laughing. *"Oh, these are wild trees—ferocious ones! We’re tying them up so they don’t run off. Soon we’ll tame them, put rings in their noses like the buffalo, and they’ll follow us around dropping fruit at our feet!"*

Franz frowned. *"That’s not true. But some trees *are* wild, like crab-apples, and some are tame, like pears and peaches. Which are these, Papa?"*

*"They’re grafted trees,"* I explained. *"In Europe, fruit trees must be cultivated to bear well."* Seeing his puzzled look, I added, *"Grafting means taking a twig from a good fruit tree and joining it to another. The new branch grows and bears the same fruit as the tree it came from."*

Ernest, ever curious, asked, *"But where did the first good fruit twigs come from?"*

*"From warmer lands,"* I said. *"In the south, fine fruits grow wild. But in colder places like ours, they must be carefully tended."* I told them how the Romans had brought apples and cherries to Europe, and how even our beloved Swiss fruits had come from far away.

Fritz, surveying our work, asked, *"Will all these trees grow here?"* He pointed to the pomegranates, lemons, and pistachios.

*"I believe so,"* I said. *"We’re in the tropics, where such trees thrive."*

By the time we finished, hunger gnawed at us, and we returned to Falconhurst, devouring my wife’s corned beef and palm-cabbage with gusto. As we rested, she mentioned her wish for an easier way to reach the eagle’s nest.

*"That ladder is dreadful,"* she said. *"Could we have stairs inside the tree?"*

I recalled the bees we’d seen entering a hole in the trunk. *"If it’s hollow, we might carve steps within."*

Jack, always eager, scrambled up to investigate—until the bees, disturbed, swarmed out in fury. The boys fled, stung and yelping, while I quickly fashioned a clay plug to seal the hive overnight.

At dawn, we smoked the bees into drowsiness with tobacco, carefully transferring them to a gourd hive I’d prepared. Once they settled, we claimed their old home. Clearing the rotten core, we built a spiral staircase inside the trunk, notching steps into a central pole and lining the walls with handrails.

A month later, the stairs stood complete, and we turned to other tasks: training the buffalo as a pack animal, teaching the eagle to hunt, and even outfitting Knips the monkey with a basket to harvest coconuts.

One morning, as we put finishing touches on the stairs, a terrifying roar echoed through the forest. We armed ourselves, fearing a wild beast—only to discover our donkey trotting happily toward us, followed by a sleek, spirited onager!

With a noose and some oats, Fritz captured the wild ass. After a fierce struggle, she calmed, and we led her to join our donkey in the makeshift stable.

As ever, our days were full—building, taming, and adapting. But with each challenge conquered, our little colony thrived.
