"Moses, despite his opposition to art, was himself a great artist and possessed the true artistic spirit. Yet this spirit in him, as in his Egyptian countrymen, was directed solely toward the colossal and the imperishable. But unlike the Egyptians, who shaped their works from brick and granite, he built human pyramids, hewed human obelisks; he took a poor shepherd tribe and forged it into a people destined to withstand the centuries... he created Israel!" — HEINE: *Confessions*.

Consider the shift in Daniel Deronda's inner landscape between the moment he departed England and his return. He had sailed for Genoa shrouded in total uncertainty regarding how his deepest desires and affections would be received, fearing that the claims newly revealed to him might pull him onto paths diverging sharply from the ones his thoughts had recently followed with a hesitant, dangerous consent. He returned, however, bearing something akin to a discovered charter validating the inherited right his ambition had long yearned for. More than mere freedom, he carried a dutiful bond—an obligation his experiences had prepared him to welcome gladly, even if it offered no guarantee of satisfying a secret, passionate longing that had never yet grown into hope. Now, he dared to acknowledge to himself the specific object of his love.

Since the hour he left the Chelsea house in silent devotion, stirred by the exquisite appeal of Mirah's farewell gaze and words, her hold on his heart had fortified his conduct, keeping him blameless amidst difficult circumstances. Though he sensed no likelihood of ever courting a creature who had become dear amidst associations that forbade courtship, she had secured a place in his soul as a beloved archetype. Her presence diminished the power of other fascinations, creating a void where they once stood. This influence grew steadily stronger. It was fate that Gwendolen's dependence on Deronda aroused in him an enthusiasm of self-sacrificing pity rather than personal love, allowing his tenderer feelings to flow freely toward an internalized image entirely unlike Gwendolen. Furthermore, his growing closeness to Mordecai drew him nearer to Mirah, a connection that remained agitating precisely because his outward position toward her appeared unchanged. She was inevitably woven into every thought that made him recoil from outcomes disappointing to her brother.

This transformation was not unconscious in Deronda; he was aware of it, much as one recognizes a covetousness better nullified by distraction than confessed. Yet, jealousy flared at Hans's pretensions, and when his mother accused him of loving a Jewess, every evasion suddenly felt like betrayal. His mother forced a decisive acknowledgment of his love, just as Joseph Kalonymos had compelled a definite statement of his resolve. This new state of decision struck Deronda with a force that surprised him. All the energy previously spent on self-restraint and suppression due to doubtful conditions was released. As he neared England from Mainz, the remaining distance felt increasingly like an obstruction, prompting laughter at his own impetuosity. It was as if discovering his ancestry had granted him an additional soul. His judgment ceased wandering in the labyrinths of impartial sympathy, choosing instead the partiality that is humanity's greatest strength: the closer fellowship that makes sympathy practical. He exchanged the "bird's-eye" reasonableness that soars to avoid preference for the generous rationality of standing shoulder-to-shoulder with men of shared heritage. He craved to be with Mordecai again, to pour forth rather than restrain his feelings, to agree and dissent alike, all while encountering Mirah without the embarrassment of obvious pursuit, interpreting her looks and words from a new foundation. While he was not greatly alarmed by Hans's attentions, he suspected Mirah's feelings had flowed from the start in a channel unlikely to divert into love. To astonish a woman by becoming her lover when she viewed him merely as a Lord Chancellor was a transition men naturally seek to smooth.

No wonder, then, that Deronda saw only one path: to proceed directly from the London railway station to the lodgings in the small square in Brompton. Every argument urged haste. He had promised to visit Lady Mallinger at the Abbey the following day, and it was already sunset. He wished to deposit the precious chest with Mordecai, who would study its contents both alone and in his company; doing so without delay would please Mordecai's heart, and thus, Deronda's. The strongest currents of his nature rushed in one direction—the fervent affectionate delight in fulfilling the wishes of those near him, and the imaginative need for a far-reaching relation to broaden the horizon of his daily acts. In this classical, romantic, world-historic position, bringing forth his hereditary armor from hiding, he wore, as surely as the most ancient heroes did, the summer costume of his contemporaries. He did not reflect that the drab tones suited him, for he rarely indulged in such introspection; yet his own depth of coloring lent the attire an added radiance, a fleeting, returning glow in his skin and eyes, as he entered the house wondering what he would find. He made his entrance as silently as possible.

It was the evening of the very afternoon Mirah had spoken with her father. Mordecai, pierced by her grief and haunted by the sad memories the incident had awakened, had not resumed his task of sorting papers. Some lay scattered on the floor in the initial moments of anxiety, neither he nor Mirah thinking to arrange them again. They sat perfectly still, unaware of the passage of time, while the clock ticked on the mantelpiece and the light faded. Unable to think of the food she should have eaten, Mirah had not moved since throwing off her dust-cloak to sit beside Mordecai, her hand in his. He leaned his head back, eyes closed, breathing with difficulty, appearing, Mirah thought, as one whose soul could no longer inhabit its cramped dwelling. The thought of his imminent death visited her constantly when she saw his face thus devoid of vivid animation. To her existing grief was added the regret that she had failed to curb the violent outburst that had shaken him. She watched him—oval cheeks pale, eyes shining with the sorrowful brilliance of recent tears, curls disordered like a child's just waking—gazing at that emaciated face, where it seemed a veil had been drawn forever, as if it were her dead joy that sustained her strength in sorrow. Life stretched before Mirah with more than a repetition of past sadness; the shadow of the father loomed large, compounded by a double bereavement—one living, one dead.

Then the door opened. No one entered, but a familiar voice asked, "Daniel Deronda—may he come in?"

"Come! Come!" cried Mordecai, rising instantly with an irradiated face and open eyes, seemingly unsurprised as if expecting the visitor in the morning; Mirah jumped up, blushing with confused, half-alarm.

Yet when Deronda entered, his sight was like clarity after rain; no coming clouds could hinder the cherishing beam of that moment. Holding out his right hand to Mirah, who stood near her brother's left, he placed his other hand on Mordecai's right shoulder, standing thus for a moment, holding them both, silent, reading their faces until he asked anxiously of Mirah, "Has anything happened? Any trouble?"

"Speak not of trouble now," said Mordecai, sparing her the need to answer. "There is joy in your face—let the joy be ours."

Mirah thought, *It is for something he cannot tell us.* They all sat down, Deronda pulling a chair close before Mordecai.

"That is true," he said emphatically. "I have a joy that will remain with us even in the worst trouble. I did not tell you the reason for my journey abroad, Mordecai, because—never mind. I went to learn my parentage. And you were right. I am a Jew."

The two men clasped hands in a movement that seemed part of a flash from Mordecai's eyes, passing through Mirah like an electric shock. Deronda continued without pause, speaking from Mordecai's mind as much as his own: "We share the same people. Our souls share the same vocation. Life or death shall not separate us."

Mordecai's reply was uttered in Hebrew, in no more than a loud whisper, using the liturgical words expressing the religious bond: "Our God and the God of our fathers."

The weight of feeling pressed too heavily on that ready-winged speech, which usually adapted itself swiftly to every stir of his fervor.

Mirah fell to her knees beside her brother, gazing at his now illuminated face, which had moments prior been deathly pale. The action was an inevitable outlet for the violent reversal from despair to a gladness that swept over her as solemnly as if witnessing a religious rite. For the moment, she considered the effect on her own life only through its impact on her brother.

"And it is not only that I am a Jew," Deronda continued, enjoying one of those rare moments when yearnings and actions align perfectly, revealing the ideal good as reality; "but I descend from a strain that has ardently maintained our race's fellowship—a line of Spanish Jews that produced many scholars and men of practical power. I possess what will grant us a communion with them. My grandfather, Daniel Charisi, preserved manuscripts and family records stretching far back, hoping they would reach his grandson. Now his hope is fulfilled, despite attempts to thwart it by concealing my parentage. I possess the chest containing them, along with his own papers, located downstairs in this house.
