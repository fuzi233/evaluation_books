Scarcely had Margaret departed before Mr. Thornton burst into the dining-room, his countenance aflame with agitation. "I could not arrive sooner; the superintendent detained me!" He swept the room with a searching gaze, his eyes finally locking onto his mother with an intensity that bordered on fierceness. "Where is she?"

His mother, quietly rearranging the displaced chairs, offered no immediate answer. "Where is Miss Hale?" he repeated, his tone sharpening.

"Gone home," she replied, her voice curt.

"Gone home!" The exclamation rang out, filled with disbelief.

"Yes," she continued, dismissing the gravity of the moment. "She was considerably improved. I do not believe it was any serious injury at all; some simply faint at the slightest shock."

"I am distressed that she has returned home," Mr. Thornton muttered, pacing the floor with restless energy. "She was scarcely fit for travel."

"She insisted on it," Mrs. Thornton stated firmly. "And Mr. Lowe confirmed her condition. I fetched him personally."

"Thank you, Mother." He paused, extending his hand in a gesture of gratitude, only to withdraw it as she failed to register the motion. "What disposition have you made of the Irish workers?"

"Dispatched them to the Dragon Inn for a substantial meal, though they are pitiable wretches," she answered. "Fortunately, I encountered Father Grady and engaged him to address them, preventing a mass departure." Her attention shifted back to the crisis. "How did Miss Hale manage to reach home? I am certain she could not have walked."

"A carriage was procured," Mrs. Thornton noted, her composure returning. "Every arrangement was handled with propriety, including the payment. Let us discuss matters less disruptive."

"I confess I am unsure where I would have stood without her intervention."

Mrs. Thornton's expression hardened. "Have you become so inept that you require a young lady for protection?"

A flush of indignation colored Mr. Thornton's cheeks. "Few women would have accepted the blows intended for me with such fortitude—" he added, emphasizing the sincerity behind her actions, "and certainly not with the genuine goodwill displayed."

"A girl under the influence of affection performs remarkable feats," Mrs. Thornton observed, her tone dismissive.

"Mother!" He stepped forward, halting abruptly as he struggled to contain a surge of emotion. He breathed heavily, his frame taut, his eyes burning with a mixture of triumph, rage, pride, and bewildered uncertainty. The raw power of these conflicting feelings unsettled his mother. Unable to decipher the storm within him, she moved to the sideboard, retrieving a duster from a drawer. Noticing a stray drop of eau de Cologne on the polished sofa arm, she instinctively reached for it, turning her back to her son long past necessity. When she finally spoke, her voice sounded strained and artificial.

"You have addressed the matter of the rioters, I presume? Do you anticipate further violence? Where were the constables when needed most?"

"On the contrary," he replied, regaining his composure. "I witnessed several officers struggling effectively near the gate, and more arrived precisely as the yard cleared. I could have detained several individuals if I had maintained my focus. However, identification will pose no difficulty; numerous witnesses remain."

"But will they not return tonight?"

"I intend to arrange a robust guard for the premises. I have agreed to meet Captain Hanbury at the station in half an hour."

"You ought to have some tea first."

"Tea? Prudence dictates it. It is half-past six, and I may be absent for some duration. Do not wait up for me, Mother."

"Do you expect me to retire before ensuring your safety?"

"Well, perhaps not." He hesitated. "However, if time permits, I shall visit Crampton after settling affairs with the police and Hamper and Clarkson." Their gazes met, holding an unspoken weight for a minute before she questioned him.

"Why the detour to Crampton?"

"To inquire after Miss Hale."

"I shall dispatch Williams. He must provide the water-bed she requested and ascertain her well-being."

"I must attend to it myself."

"Not solely to inquire on her health?"

"No, not merely that. I wish to express my gratitude for standing between me and the mob."

"What compelled you to descend at all? You placed yourself directly in harm's way!"

He cast a sharp glance at her, sensing she remained ignorant of the private exchange in the drawing-room, and countered with another query. "Will you be frightened to remain alone until I secure the police? Or shall I send Williams immediately, ensuring they arrive before tea concludes? Time is of the essence; I must depart within fifteen minutes."

Mrs. Thornton withdrew from the room. Her servants, accustomed to her sharp directives, found her instructions today confused and hesitant. Mr. Thornton lingered in the dining-room, ostensibly planning his duties at the police office, yet his thoughts were entirely consumed by Margaret. The memory of her arms encircling his neck, the soft, clinging pressure that sent a flush through his skin, rendered everything else dim and vague.

The tea service was unnervingly quiet, broken only by Fanny's incessant recounting of her alarm, her subsequent relief, and her physical tremors. "There, that suffices," Mr. Thornton interrupted, rising. "The reality itself was ample." As he prepared to leave, his mother laid a hand upon his arm.

"You will return here before proceeding to the Hales'," she urged in a hushed, anxious tone.

"I comprehend what lies ahead," Fanny murmured to herself.

"Why?" Mrs. Thornton pressed gently. "Will it prove too late to disturb them?"

"John, return to me for this single evening," she pleaded, a rare vulnerability breaking through her pride. "It will be late for Mrs. Hale. Yet, that is not the sole concern. Tomorrow, you will..." She stopped, her eyes glistening. "Come back tonight, John!"

Though rarely one to plead, she had never failed in her entreaties. "I shall return directly after concluding my business. You will ensure they are inquired after? Specifically her?"

Mrs. Thornton was typically taciturn and a poor listener when her son was absent, yet upon his return, her senses were acutely attuned to every detail of his endeavors to protect himself and his chosen allies from future outrage. He perceived his objective clearly: punishment and suffering were the inevitable consequences for those who participated in the riot. All that was required to safeguard property and assert the proprietor's authority was a decisive, sword-like execution of justice.

"Mother! Are you aware of what I must convey to Miss Hale tomorrow?" The question struck her unexpectedly amidst a lull where she had momentarily forgotten Margaret.

She lifted her gaze. "Yes, John. You can hardly avoid it."

"Give me alternative instruction? I fail to grasp your meaning."

"I imply that, having allowed her emotions to overwhelm you, you are bound by honor—"

"Honor!" He scoffed. "I fear honor plays no role here. 'Her emotions overwhelmed me!' Which emotions do you refer to?"

"Nay, John, there is no cause for anger. Did she not rush to cling to you to shield you from peril?"

"She did!" he admitted. "Yet, Mother," he halted in his stride, facing her squarely. "I dare not harbor hope. I have never been faint-hearted before, but I cannot conceive that a creature of her sort would care for me."

"Don't indulge in folly, John. A creature of that description! She might be the daughter of a duke, judging by your tone." She smiled slowly, tears gathering in her eyes. "And what further proof of her affection could you desire? I suspect she has wrestled with her aristocratic prejudices, but I find her more commendable for seeing clearly at last. It is a considerable concession on my part to admit this," she whispered, her voice trembling. "For after tonight, I stand second."

"Dearest Mother!" (Love remains inherently selfish, and instantly he reverted to his own hopes and fears, casting a chilling shadow over Mrs. Thornton's heart.) "But I know she does not care for me. I shall place myself at her feet—I must. Were it but a sliver of chance in a thousand, or a million, I would do it."

"Don't tremble!" she commanded, suppressing her own mortification regarding his lack of attention to her fleeting maternal warmth and the sting of jealous neglect. "As far as love extends, she may prove worthy of you. It required immense effort to subdue her pride. Do not fear, John," she said, kissing him as she bade him goodnight. She exited the room with slow, majestic dignity. Once inside her chamber, however, she locked the door and sank into a chair to weep tears she had never before shed.
