Once my visit to my brother concluded, and my wife and young child had safely returned to our maternal home in Richmond, I turned my attention to the Governor, who was residing at his country estate near Williamsburg. My purpose was to discuss the ominous preparations for war that were unfolding not merely within our province, but across the entire continent.

Gentlemen whose names history has since immortalized were being appointed as delegates to the General Congress convening in Philadelphia. In Massachusetts, the populace and royal troops stood face-to-face in open hostility. In Maryland and Pennsylvania, we naively believed a loyal spirit still prevailed. In the Carolinas and Georgia, the Mother Country could count on steadfast adherence, with the vast majority of inhabitants firmly behind the Crown. It was inconceivable to us that Virginia, the cradle of loyalty, would forsake its ancient allegiance. Though our provincial forces were scant, our gentry prided themselves on their lineage tracing back to the Cavaliers of old. Surrounding the Governor were swarms of loud, confident Loyalists, eager for the moment to draw their swords and sweep aside the "rascally rebels."

Naturally, in these gatherings, I was subjected to harsh criticism of my poor brother, Harry. Everyone agreed—perhaps justifiably—that his wife had led him toward these extreme anti-British views, which he had recently espoused. Furthermore, it was whispered that he was infatuated with the gentleman of Mount Vernon, whose radical opinions Harry invariably followed, pushing him daily deeper into a course of desperate resistance.

"This is your friend," the circle around His Excellency remarked, gesturing toward me. "This is the man you favored, who enjoyed your special confidence and repeatedly shared your hospitality!" Much of this was true. Yet, what some eager Loyalists termed treachery was, in reality, evidence of a profound desire by Mr. Washington and other gentlemen to remain faithful to the Crown. They sought to exhaust every final chance for reconciliation before risking the terrifying alternative of revolt and separation.

"Let traitors arm, and villains draw the parricidal sword!" cried the Loyalists. "We, at least, will remain faithful. The unconquerable power of England shall be exerted, and the misguided, ungrateful provinces punished until they return to obedience." With what fervor we toasted His Majesty's health after our banquets! We vowed to die in defense of his rights and welcomed the prospect of a prince from his Royal house returning to govern our ancient dominions.

In light of my own and my mother's unwavering loyalty, my brother's benighted conduct should surely be forgiven. Could it not be too late to secure him by offering a commanding post? Should I not intercede with the Governor, known for his influence over Harry? Thus, in our Williamsburg councils, we swung wildly between exaltation, triumph, fury against the rebels, anxious expectation of reinforcements, and deep-seated doubt and gloom.

I promised to plead with my brother and wrote to him, albeit with little hope, reiterating arguments I had used countless times in conversation. My mother also employed her authority, though I feared little gain from her efforts. True to her habits, she assailed him with Scripture verses supporting her view, threatening divine punishment for disobedience to constituted authorities. She cited the example of his elder brother and, I fear, hinted at his subservience to his wife—the weakest possible argument in such a debate. While she did not show me her letter, fearing I might criticize its tone, she displayed his reply. To my astonishment, Mr. Hal had countered her scriptural barrage with dozens of texts of his own, composing a response that was neat, temperate, and elegantly written—a feat I suspected even his wife could not have achieved. Indeed, he had enlisted Mr. Belman, the clergyman of New Richmond, a zealous Whig who preached and printed sermons against Hagan (a figure of our faction). In this intellectual skirmish, Belman undoubtedly emerged victorious.

My exhortations proved as futile as my mother's. Harry ignored my letters entirely. Pressed further by government friends, I wrote imprudently promising to visit him at Fanny's Mount within the week. Upon arrival, however, I found only my sister. She greeted me warmly but informed me that Harry had departed for the countryside, traveling far toward the Blue Mountains to inspect horses, with no indication of when—or if—he would return. At that moment, hope vanished.

"My dear," I said, "judging by the signs of the times, the fuse lit over these years must soon ignite. Harry is riding away. God knows to what end."

"The Lord prosper the righteous cause, Sir George," she replied.

"Amen, with all my heart. You and he speak as Americans; I as an Englishman. Tell him from me that should anything happen to our mother, I possess sufficient means in England for myself and mine. I shall resign all our lands here in Virginia to him."

"You don't mean that, George?" she exclaimed, her eyes brightening.

"Well, to be sure, it is but right and fair," she added shortly thereafter. "Why should you, the eldest by mere hours, hold everything—a palace and estates in England, this plantation here, the title, and children—while my poor Harry gets nothing? Yet, it is generous of you. Leastways, it is handsome and proper. I didn't expect it of you; and unlike your mother, you do not act thus. Give my love to sister Theo!" She offered me her cheek to kiss before I rode away.

With a woman like Fanny guiding him, how could I possibly hope to convert my brother? Disappointed in my mission, I returned to the Governor. We agreed it was time to arm earnestly and prepare for the inevitable shock. His Excellency and his court officials were agitated, nearly savage in their denunciation of the "wicked Whigs," shouting praises of Old England. Yet, beneath the rhetoric, they all hungered for the day when opposing factions would meet hand-to-hand, allowing them to ride down these "wicked Whigs."

Leaving the Governor, I accepted his thanks in council and pledged my utmost effort to raise a body of men to defend the Crown. Thus began the corps later known as the Westmoreland Defenders, of which I was honored to be appointed Colonel, tasked with leading them once they appeared in the field. Naturally, the announcement in the Government Gazette drew officers quickly, but recruits were slow to enlist and swift to vanish. Nevertheless, friend Hagan eagerly volunteered as chaplain. Madam Esmond presented our colors and traveled the countryside recruiting volunteers. Most diligent of all was my old tutor, little Mr. Dempster, a former Jacobite exile from Scotland who had fought for Prince Charles. He ventured specifically into the Carolinas, among the descendants of his banished comrades who still wore the white cockade of the Pretender, finding them fiercely loyal to the Crown.

Meanwhile, Harry's horse-hunting expedition carried him far beyond the Blue Mountains, eventually leading him to Annapolis, Baltimore, and finally Philadelphia, where a second General Congress was seated alongside our Virginian delegates from the previous year.

As the almanacs recorded, history unfolded rapidly. Lexington had fallen; the first shots of the war that would culminate in our nation's independence had been fired. We continued to protest our loyalty to His Majesty, declaring our resolve to "die or be free." Some twenty thousand loyal petitioners assembled around Boston with arms and cannons, seized from government stores. Benedict Arnold had launched his infamous career with the daring, burglary-like capture of two forts. Three generals from Bond Street, accompanied by a large reinforcement, sailed to assist General Gage in his precarious position at Boston. Soon, the armies clashed. Our British generals began their careers of conquest and pacification with the glorious blunder of Breed's Hill. Fortifying themselves, acknowledging they lacked the strength for another decisive victory, the two armies watched one another while Congress deliberated in Philadelphia on who should command the confederated colonial forces.

History records the fortunate choice that fell upon the nation. Among the Virginian regiments marching to join the new General-in-Chief was one commanded by Henry Esmond Warrington, Esq., formerly a Captain in His Majesty's service. Riding beside him was his little wife, whose bravery we would later hear much of. I rejoiced that she had already left Virginia; had she remained after her husband's departure, our mother would inevitably have joined the fray to fight alongside them. I was grateful, at least, that this terrifying episode of civil war was spared to our family's direct experience.

The rush of farmers and country folk flowed almost exclusively toward the new northern army. Our people felt flattered by the selection of a Virginian gentleman for the principal command. With a surge of wrath and fury, the provinces learned of the blood spilled at Lexington. Men shouted denunciations against the cruelty and wantonness of the "bloody British invader." The invader was merely doing his duty, met by armed men wishing to prevent him from seizing what was theirs. Yet, anger clouds judgment; the colonists had chosen their side. Driven by a natural spirit and ardor, they were determined to test their strength against the arrogant Mother Country. Breed's Hill became a symbolic mountain visible to all the American continent, crowned with Liberty, Victory, and Glory on its flaming summit.

These dreaded troops could be withstood by farmers and ploughmen; these famous officers could be outgeneraled by doctors, lawyers, and civilians!
