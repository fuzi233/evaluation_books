To truly grasp the architecture of Mr. Moore's silence, one must first witness the absolute desolation of his universe following the dismissal of the deputation. He returned to Messrs. Helstone and Sykes not as a triumphant leader, but as a figure carved from obsidian, wearing a countenance so devoid of beam or breeze that it resembled a stormless, sunless day. The rector, perceiving the profound exhaustion beneath the surface, prudently buttoned up his congratulations, signaling the end of the performance. "Come, sir," he murmured to Sykes, whose senses were too blunt to detect the subtle friction of his presence, "your road and mine diverge. Let us bear each other company and leave Moore to the solitary reveries he seems compelled to indulge."

"And where is Sugden?" Moore demanded abruptly, breaking the spell.

"Ah, ha!" cried Helstone, his voice dripping with theatrical self-importance. "I have not been idle while you were occupied. I have assisted you, I flatter myself, without judgment. While you parleyed with that down-cast gentleman—Farren, I believe—I opened this rear window, summoned Murgatroyd from the stables to wheel Mr. Sykes's gig around, and personally smuggled Sugden and Brother Moses—complete with his wooden leg—through the aperture. With the benevolent consent of our good friend Sykes, of course, I watched them mount. Sugden took the reins; he drives with the ferocity of Jehu. Within a quarter-hour, Barraclough shall be safely incarcerated in Stilbro' jail."

"Very good; thank you," said Moore, his tone flat as a ledger page. "And good-morning, gentlemen," he added, politely ushering them to the threshold until they vanished from his sight.

For the remainder of the day, Moore remained a statue of taciturnity. He refused to engage in banter with Joe Scott, who, for his part, offered only the barest necessities required for the machinery of business to function. Yet, Joe's eyes tracked his master constantly, darting from the corners of his vision. He approached to tend the counting-house fire, and once, as the mill slowed due to the ebb of trade, he ventured a suggestion: "It is a grand evening, sir. I could wish Mr. Moore to take a stroll up th' Hollow. It would do him good."

At this, Mr. Moore erupted in a sharp, incredulous laugh. "What solicitude is this?" he demanded, seizing the keys from Joe's hand and shoving him bodily aside. "Do you take me for a woman or a child?" But before Joe could retreat fully to the yard-gate, Moore called him back.

"Do you know those Farrens? Are they not well-off, I suppose?"

"They cannot be well-off, sir," Joe replied, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Not having worked for three months. You'd see for yourself that William is sorely changed—fair paired. They've sold most of the goods from the house."

"He was not a bad workman?"

"Never had a better, sir, since you began trade."

"And decent people—the whole family?"

"Niver dacenter," Joe corrected gently. "The wife is a straight canting body, yet as clean—you might eat your porridge off the house floor. They are sorely come down. I wish William could find work as a gardener; he understands the craft well. Once he lived with a Scot who taught him the mysteries of that trade."

"Now, then, you can go, Joe. You need not stand there staring at me."

"Ye've no orders to give, sir?"

"None, but for you to take yourself off."

Which Joe did accordingly, retreating into the twilight.

Spring evenings possess a cruel duality, often cold and raw despite the preceding warmth. Though the day had been fine, glowing with morning and midday sun, the air turned brittle at sunset. By dusk, a hoar frost was insidiously creeping over the unfurling buds and green grass, whitening the pavement before Briarmains—Mr. Yorke's residence—and wreaking silent havoc upon the tender plants of his garden and the mossy lawn. Even the great tree, strong-trunked and broad-armed, guarding the gable nearest the road, seemed to defy the chill, its bare boughs standing defiant against the night. So too did the leafless grove of walnut-trees rising tall behind the house.

In the moonless, star-dusted darkness, lights flickered vividly from the windows. This was no scene of isolation; Briarmains stood near the highway, an ancient edifice predating the road itself, accessible only by a winding lane through fields. Yet, the hum of Briarfield, barely a mile away, pierced the silence, its glare distinct. Closer still, Briar Chapel—a large, new, raw Wesleyan sanctuary—rose within a hundred yards. Inside, a prayer-meeting raged. The illumination of its windows cast a bright reflection onto the road, while a hymn of extraordinary fervor, one that might move a Quaker to dance, roused the echoes of the neighborhood. The words drifted audibly, snatched from the chorus:

"Oh! who can explain This struggle for life, This travail and pain, This trembling and strife? Plague, earthquake, and famine, And tumult and war, The wonderful coming Of Jesus declare!"

"For every fight Is dreadful and loud: The warrior's delight Is slaughter and blood, His foes overturning, Till all shall expire: And this is with burning, And fuel, and fire!"

An interval of clamorous prayer followed, accompanied by fearful groans. Then, a shout erupted: "I've found liberty!" "Doad o' Bill's has fun' liberty!" The assembly broke forth again, a tidal wave of sound.

"What a mercy is this! What a heaven of bliss! How unspeakably happy am I! Gathered into the fold, With Thy people enrolled, With Thy people to live and to die!"

"Oh, the goodness of God In employing a clod His tribute of glory to raise; His standard to bear, And with triumph declare His unspeakable riches of grace!"

"Oh, the fathomless love That has deigned to approve And prosper the work of my hands. With my pastoral crook I went over the brook, And behold I am spread into bands!"

"Who, I ask in amaze, Hath begotten me these? And inquire from what quarter they came. My full heart it replies, They are born from the skies, And gives glory to God and the Lamb!"

Following another interregnum of shouts, yells, and frantic cries, the final stanza capped the climax of zeal.

"Sleeping on the brink of sin, Tophet gaped to take us in; Mercy to our rescue flew, Broke the snare, and brought us through.

"Here, as in a lion's den, Undevoured we still remain, Pass secure the watery flood, Hanging on the arm of God.

"Here----"

(The terrible, most distracting shout in which the last stanza was given.)

"Here we raise our voices higher, Shout in the refiner's fire, Clap our hands amidst the flame, Glory give to Jesus' name!"

The roof of the chapel did not fly off, a testament to its solid slating.

Yet, if Briar Chapel seemed alive, so too did Briarmains, though the mansion enjoyed a quieter phase of existence than the temple. Windows glowed; lower casements opened onto the lawn. Curtains concealed the interior, partially obscuring the rays of candles, yet they failed to muffle the sounds of voice and laughter. One is privileged to enter that front door and penetrate to the domestic sanctum.

It is not the presence of guests that animates Mr. Yorke's habitation, for there is none within save his own family, assembled in the farthest room to the right, the back parlour. This is the usual evening gathering. By daylight, those windows reveal brilliantly stained glass, purple and amber dominating the hues, glittering around a gravely tinted medallion in the center of each, depicting the suave head of William Shakespeare and the serene visage of John Milton. Canadian views hang on the walls—green forests and blue waters—and amidst them blazes a night eruption of Vesuvius, glowing ardently against the cool foam of cataracts and the dusky depths of woods.

The fire illuminating this room is such as a southerner rarely sees burning in a private apartment: a clear, hot coal fire, heaped high in the ample chimney. Mr. Yorke insists upon such fires even in warm summer weather. He sits beside it with a book in hand, a little round stand at his elbow supporting a candle; yet he reads nothing. He watches his children.

Opposite him sits his lady—a personage I might describe minutely, yet I feel no vocation for the task. I see her clearly nonetheless: a large woman of the gravest aspect, care etched upon her forehead and shoulders. It is not overwhelming, inevitable care, but rather the voluntary, exemplary cloud and burden carried by those who deem it their duty to be gloomy. Ah, well-a-day! Mrs. Yorke held that notion. Grave as Saturn, she was morning, noon, and night. Hard things she thought of any unhappy wight—especially of the female sex—who dared display the light of a gay heart in her presence.
