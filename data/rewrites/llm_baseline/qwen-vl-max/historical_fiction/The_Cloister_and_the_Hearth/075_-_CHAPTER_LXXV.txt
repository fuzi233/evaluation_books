Clement sighed. He began to wonder if he had acted wisely with a woman so deeply stirred by passion. Yet though still young, he had already absorbed many lessons in the quiet wisdom of the Church. One such lesson was patience—knowing when to remain silent, to say nothing and do nothing, until the turmoil of the soul might settle into clarity. So he said nothing. Instead, he prayed for guidance.

All he did was gently withdraw his foot from where it rested. But the penitent clutched it at once, her arms winding tightly around it as she sobbed aloud, her body trembling beneath the weight of her sorrow. A deep flush rose up her neck and spread across her face, betraying not only shame but the fierce struggle within. She lay before him, quivering like a leaf in the wind.

“Daughter,” he said softly, “take heart. Do not torment yourself further over this Gerard, who is no more. I am Brother Clement—sent by Heaven today to comfort you, to help you save your soul. You made me your confessor; therefore, I claim your obedience.”

“Oh, yes,” she wept, her voice breaking. “Leave this pilgrimage, and return at once to Rome. Penitence far from home is little worth. The true trials are those we must face where we live—not fleeing to distant lands in search of grander sins, but enduring the daily temptations that surround us. It is easy to wash the feet of strangers when we wear masks. But how hard it is to be gentle, truly humble, with those we know and love.”

“I will never touch her again,” she vowed.

“No,” he replied, “I speak not only of servants, but of all who depend on you—kinsmen, friends, even family. This shall be your penance: each night before sleep, and each morning after matins, remember your sin and God’s mercy. Let that memory soften your heart and make you kinder to others’ faults. The world honors wealth, but seek instead the poor—not beggars, for most are neither honest nor truly destitute. Rather, find those who blush to ask for help, yet need it dearly. Give to them, and you lend to Heaven itself. Marry a good son of the Church.”

“Me?” she whispered. “I will never marry.”

“You will marry within the year,” he declared firmly. “And I command you—to wed one who fears God. For you are fragile clay. A bad match will break you. But if you choose well, through God’s grace, you may live as a pious princess—and die a saint.”

“I?” she murmured.

“You.”

He then asked her to rise and go forth to fulfill the task he had set before her. She knelt slowly, removed her veil, and looked up at him with eyes full of unspoken emotion. Then, lowering her gaze in humility, she said, “I will obey you as I would an angel. How happy I am—and yet how sad! For my heart tells me I shall never see you again. I will not leave until I have dried your feet.”

“It is unnecessary,” he said gently. “I have absolved you of this useless act.”

“But it is no burden to me,” she insisted. “Ah, you do not forgive me—if you refuse to let me tend to your feet.”

“Very well,” Clement said, yielding with quiet resignation. And inwardly he thought, *Levius quid foemina.* Yet even such frail beings, drawn to small things as stars are drawn to the sun, sometimes shine with sudden flashes of divine insight.

When she had finished drying his feet, she looked up at him again, tears glistening in her eyes, and whispered with tender compassion, “I will have masses said for her soul. May I?”

The words struck him like a soft blow. A faint blush touched his cheek, and a single tear moistened his cold blue eye. It came from one whom he had just spoken to with sternness, yet now offered such grace.

“It is a noble thought,” he replied. “Do as you wish. Such deeds often return to bless the giver like dew upon dry earth. I am your confessor, not hers. My duty is to save your soul—or else suffer the consequences. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see now angels fighting for your soul—good and evil—this very moment. Stand firm on your side. Do you remember all I have told you?”

“Remember?” she echoed. “Sweet saint, every word you spoke is carved into my heart.”

“One last thing,” he said. “Pray much to Christ, and little to His saints.”

“I will.”

“And that,” he added, “is the best counsel I can give you. So let us part on this.” He paused. “You to the place most fitting—your father’s house. I to my holy mother’s work.”

“Adieu,” she breathed, barely audible.

“Adieu,” he answered, “you whom I loved too deeply, hated too fiercely, known and honored too late. Forgiving angel, adieu—for ever.”

He heard her words, though spoken only in a sigh. “For ever?” he called out, his voice rising with sudden fervor. “Christians live for ever, and love for ever—but they never part for ever. They separate, as earth and sun do, only to meet again in greater light. We part here for life. And what is life? One line in the great story of the Church—of which we are both children. One grain in the sand of time, one drop in the ocean of eternity. Adieu—for this brief moment called ‘a life.’ We part in sorrow, we shall meet in peace. We part as mortal creatures, we shall meet as immortal spirits. We part in a world of sin and pain, we shall meet in a realm of purity and divine love, where no darkness remains, only Christ, and His saints robed in white. There—in the turn of an hourglass, in the burst of a bubble, in the passing of a cloud—we shall meet again. And sit at the feet of angels and archangels, apostles and saints, radiant with joy beyond words, bathed in the shadow of God upon His throne—FOR EVER—AND EVER—AND EVER.”

And so they parted.

The monk stood tall, his gaze lifted heavenward, burning with sacred fire. The princess walked away slowly, glancing back again and again—each look filled with awe and quiet regret. She returned home subdued, purified, transformed.

Clement reached Basel in due time, and took up his duties: teaching in the university, preaching throughout the town and surrounding villages. His life became defined by two qualities: profound study and rigorous self-denial. The reader has already glimpsed the depth of his soul. In Basel, he grew ever deeper in spiritual zeal and learning. The brothers of his order began to see in him a true heir to the saints and martyrs.
