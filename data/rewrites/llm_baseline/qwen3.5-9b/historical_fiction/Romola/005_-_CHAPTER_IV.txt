"Good day, Messer Domenico," Nello greeted the first of the two men entering the shop, nodding respectfully to the second. "You arrive at the perfect moment, like cheese destined for macaroni. Ah, you are in a hurry—eager to be shaved without delay—*ecco!* Indeed, it is a morning when everyone carries heavy burdens upon their minds. Florence lies orphaned; the very pivot of Italy has been snatched away, leaving heaven itself unsure of its next move. *Oimè!* Very well then; yet the sun continues its journey toward dinner-time. As I mentioned, you arrive precisely like freshly grated cheese.

For this young stranger seeks an honorable merchant willing to advance funds against a ring of considerable value. If I were to count every goldsmith and moneylender in Florence on my fingers, I could find no finer name than Menico Cennini. Moreover, he deals in other wares relevant to your trade: Greek learning and youthful eyes—a dual commodity that printers are perpetually in need of."

The grave elderly man, son of Bernardo Cennini—who, twenty years prior, had heard of the German printing innovations and cast his own typefaces in Florence—remained silent and passive, covered in shaving lather, while Nello poured forth this torrent of conversation. However, he did cast a slow, sideways glance at the stranger.

"This fine youth offers unlimited services in Greek, Latin, or Italian," Nello continued, delighting in expansive interpretation. "He is a marvel of juvenile learning, comparable to Francesco Filelfo or our own incomparable Poliziano. He is, in fact, a second Guarino, though fate dealt him a cruel blow in the form of a shipwreck, likely causing him to lose a vast collection of precious manuscripts that could have corrected even your most accurate editions, Messer Domenico. Fortunately, he salvaged several gems of rare worth. His name is... you stated your name, sir, was—"

"Tito Melema," the stranger replied smoothly. He slipped the ring from his finger and presented it to Cennini, whose shave Nello had completed with equal speed and dexterity.

Meanwhile, the companion who had entered with the goldsmith—a tall figure around fifty years of age, sporting a neatly trimmed beard, an old felt hat, and a threadbare mantle—had kept his gaze fixed intently on the Greek youth. Now, he spoke abruptly.

"Young man, I am currently painting a depiction of Sinon deceiving old Priam. I would be most grateful if you would serve as my model for Sinon, provided you are willing to sit."

Tito Melema startled, turning his head with a pale expression of astonishment, as if struck by a sudden accusation. Yet, Nello offered him no respite to formulate a defense.

"Piero," the barber declared, "you are the most extraordinary mixture of moods and caprices ever confined within human skin. What trickery do you intend to employ with this distinguished scholar's visage to suit your traitor? Instead, ask him to lift his eyes upward; you might transform him into a Saint Sebastian capable of drawing crowds of pious women. Or, if you prefer a classical tone, drape myrtle around his curls and fashion him into a young Bacchus—or rather, a Phoebus Apollo, for his face shines as warm and bright as a summer morning. It won me as a friend within the span of a single 'Credo.'"

"Ay, Nello," the painter responded, speaking in abrupt pauses. "If your tongue could cease its endless chirping long enough for your understanding to reflect, you would realize you have just revealed the very reason why this gentleman's face suits my traitor perfectly. A true traitor must possess a face untouched by the marks of vice—lips capable of lying with a dimpled smile, eyes of such agate-like brilliance and depth that no infamy can cloud them, cheeks resilient enough to rise after murder without appearing haggard. I do not claim this young man is a traitor; I merely suggest he possesses the features of a perfect one should he harbor the corresponding heart. To state otherwise is simply to acknowledge that he has a beautiful face, filled with rich, vibrant blood, sustained sufficiently by nourishment to maintain its color with minimal reliance on virtue. He may possess the heart of a hero alongside it; I assert nothing to the contrary. Ask Domenico here if lapidaries can always identify a gem solely by sight. And now, I must stuff tow into my ears, for your chatter combined with the church bells exceeds my endurance. Say no further, but trim my beard."

With these final words, Piero (known as "di Cosimo" after his master, Cosimo Rosselli) extracted two pieces of tow, stuffed them firmly into his ears, and settled into the chair before Nello. The barber shrugged, casting a grimace of shared intelligence at the Greek, signaling, *"As you can see, a whimsical fellow! Everyone treats his words as mere jests."*

Tito, standing transfixed with his long dark eyes fixed on the enigmatic man who had addressed him so ambiguously, seemed to regain his composure only when Piero shifted positions. Satisfied with the explanation, he redirected his full attention to Cennini, who remarked shortly thereafter:

"This is a curious and valuable ring, young man. This intaglio of the fish bearing the crested serpent above it, set within the black stratum of the onyx—or rather, the niello—is beautifully highlighted by the surrounding blue of the upper layer. Does this ring, undoubtedly, carry a history?" Cennini asked, regarding the stranger with keen intensity.

"Yes, indeed," Tito replied, meeting the scrutiny openly. "The ring was discovered in Sicily. From those who specialize in gems and seals, I understand that both the stone and the carving possess virtues to bring fortune to the wearer, particularly at sea, and to restore whatever one may have lost. However," he continued, smiling faintly, "although I have worn it constantly since departing Greece, it has not rendered me entirely fortunate at sea, as you observe, unless escaping drowning counts as sufficient proof of its efficacy. Whether my lost chests will surface remains to be seen. Nevertheless, to ensure no opportunity is missed, Messer, I beg you to accept the ring as a pledge for a modest sum far below its true value. I shall redeem it as soon as I can liquidate certain other gems secured within my doublet, or indeed as soon as I can earn something through scholarly employment, should fortune allow me to secure such work."

"That can be arranged, young man, if you will accompany me," Cennini said. "My brother Pietro, who judges scholarship more competently than I, may be able to provide a task testing your abilities. In the meantime, take back your ring until I can deliver the necessary florins. If you wish, follow me."

"Yes, yes," Nello interjected eagerly. "Go with Messer Domenico; you could not be in better company. He was born under the constellation that bestows skill, wealth, and integrity upon its subjects—whatever that constellation may be, which matters little since infants cannot choose their own horoscopes. Indeed, if they could, there might be an inconvenient surge of babies during specific epochs. Furthermore, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has demonstrated that horoscopes are nothing but nonsensical dreams—a view that causes fewer troubles. *Addio, bel giovane!* Do not fail to return to me."

"No fear of that," Tito replied, offering a wave of farewell as he turned his radiant face toward the door. "You are to render me a significant service; that serves as the most absolute guarantee of our future meetings."

"Say what you will, Piero," Nello muttered as the young stranger vanished. "I shall never behold such an exterior without assuming it signifies a lovable nature. You will surely argue next that Leonardo, whom you rave about incessantly, ought to have painted his Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But you are as deaf as the summit of Mount Morello with that cursed tow in your ears. Very well; I shall gather a bit more of this young man's history before escorting him to Bardo Bardi."
