The morning’s quiet was abruptly shattered by the abrasive, metallic dissonance of Zat Arras’s Zodangan guards, a jarring industrial clatter that polluted the delicate acoustic ecology of our quarters. We were gathered to be marched to the great hall of the temple. In pairs, Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I entered the cavernous chamber, moving down the broad Aisle of Hope. The space had been designed centuries ago by meticulous architects to amplify the resonant, harmonic frequencies of Helium’s native dialects, yet today it was choked by the sterile, oppressive hum of Zodangan soldiery lining our path.

Reaching the raised enclosure in the center, I beheld our judges. By Barsoomian tradition, thirty-one men of the noble class were to be drawn by lot, a sacred geometry meant to ensure a balance of voices. Instead, I saw a monolithic wall of Zodangans—men whose very presence felt like an aggressive commercialization of justice, lacking any sensory appreciation for the truth. It was I who had dismantled Zodanga’s brutal machinery alongside the green hordes, and here, wrapped in the dominant lexicon of the victors, they sought to erase the cultural memory of that liberation. 

The vast circular coliseum was packed, a sweeping, multi-generational tapestry of Helium’s citizens spanning all ages and classes. As we stepped upon the Throne of Righteousness, the subdued, organic murmur of the crowd was instantly swallowed by a breathless, heavy silence. We were seated with our backs to a smaller central platform, facing the judges and the ten thousand spectators. 

Zat Arras took the golden chair of the presiding magistrate. When he rose and called my name, his voice was a harsh, unmusical bark that sought to enforce a rigid, untranslatable power dynamic. 

“John Carter,” he cried, ordering me to the Pedestal of Truth. He turned to the audience, wielding his words like blunt instruments, attempting to flatten the mythic complexity of our recent survival into a sterile list of transgressions. He claimed I had blasphemed against the Sacred Iss, the Valley Dor, the Holy Therns, and Issus herself. He wielded their ancient religion as a weapon of linguistic death, arguing that he who is dead may not live again, and that by returning, I had violated the sanctity of their most rigid definitions of existence.

“What reward shall be meted to John Carter?” Zat Arras demanded.

“Death!” shouted a Zodangan judge, the word slicing through the air like a cold, flavorless blade. 

But the acoustic void was suddenly filled by a pure, resonant chord of native defiance. “Justice! Justice! Justice!” 

It was Kantos Kan. Leaping past the Zodangan guards, his movements carrying the rhythmic grace of Helium’s ancestral martial dances, he sprang upon the platform. “What manner of justice be this?” he demanded, his native dialect cutting through the dominant Zodangan lexicon. He appealed directly to the people, and the coliseum responded—a great, thunderous chorus demanding fair treatment that Zat Arras dared not silence.

Turned over to me, Zat Arras snarled his permission for me to speak. I addressed not the sterile judges, but the people of Helium. I spoke of the visceral, subjective horror of the Valley Dor, an experience entirely untranslatable to those who had not tasted its bitter reality. I described the cloying, metallic scent of the plant men’s blood, the cold and rotting breath of the great white apes, and the cruel, hollow hunger of the Holy Therns. I sought to save their daughters and wives from a false hereafter devoid of the rich, agrarian warmth of true Barsoomian life—a place where the deeply sustaining rituals of love, sustenance, and domestic harmony were violently consumed by the cold, dead Iss.

I reminded them of my history, a sweeping timeline that had seen me rise from a captive of the green men to a Prince of Helium, driven always by a profound devotion to the life and breath of this world. “It is to you of Helium that I speak now,” I concluded. 

A great noble from the audience roared for me to continue, and the building rocked with the magnificent, deafening symphony of their approval. For two hours, I wove the tale of our mythic struggles into the everyday understanding of the people, merging the grand with the intimate. Yet when I finished, Zat Arras turned to his judges, his tone a low, dismissive drone, and asked for their verdict.

“Death to the blasphemer!” they cried in unison, thirty-one swords raised in a sterile, choreographed display of tyranny. 

The crowd’s sullen murmur swelled into a furious roar. Kantos Kan raised his hand, orchestrating a moment of breathless quiet. “Let each man act according to his own heart,” he spoke, his voice cool and level. “Here is the answer of Kantos Kan, head of the navy of Helium, to Zat Arras and his judges.” With a profound, percussive clang that echoed perfectly off the temple’s ancient stonework, he unbuckled his scabbard and threw his sword at my feet.

Instantly, the dam broke. A hundred men surged the platform, and a hundred blades rattled and clanked in a metallic symphony of loyalty. Zat Arras was furious but helpless against this raw expression of cultural memory. I raised each sword to my lips, tasting the faint, coppery tang of the metal—a sensory oath—and buckled them back upon their owners.

When Zat Arras ordered his organized Zodangan troops to halt our departure, the coliseum erupted. Swords flashed from every quarter. A cry rose out, intertwining the mythic past with the present: “Tardos Mors is dead—a thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium!”

Sensing that this beautifully chaotic acoustic ecology was moments away from degrading into the tragic, deafening noise of civil war, I leapt back to the Pedestal. I refused to let our city’s rich generational tapestry be torn apart by bloodshed that would pit brother against brother. I proposed a compromise: a one-year respite, resting the matter until Tardos Mors or Mors Kajak returned. I turned to Zat Arras, warning him that the multitude of drawn swords before him was an undeniable force of nature. 

Cornered by the overwhelming resonance of the crowd, the Jed of Zodanga yielded, his voice trembling with impotent rage as he granted the delay. When he ordered the temple cleared, I guided the people out with a gesture, and they filed past the scowling Zodangans in a silent, threatening tide.

Escorted by Kantos Kan, Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and a hundred loyal souls, we made our triumphal return to my palace on the outskirts of the city. As we entered the gates, the oppressive noise of the city gave way to the deeply comforting, olfactory landscape of home. The air was rich with the scent of ancient mosses, the earthy aroma of roasted root vegetables, and the fragrant smoke of hearth-fires. My old slaves fell to their knees, weeping and kissing my hands. Among them was my eldest culinary historian, a passionate guardian of our domestic traditions, who cried out that the day would be perfect if only our divine Princess were here to share in the harvest. Tears stung my eyes, the bitter salt of profound loss. Carthoris wept openly among the slaves, their shared grief a testament to the emotional sustenance Dejah Thoris had provided us all. 

It was here that Tars Tarkas finally learned that his daughter, Sola, had accompanied Dejah Thoris on that last long pilgrimage. True to the stoic nature of the green Martian, he offered no loud lamentation, but the sudden, heavy silence that emanated from his massive frame was an acoustic void that spoke volumes of his agony. He, more than any of his kind, understood the profound, agrarian roots of love and kinship.

The feast of welcome held that day in the great dining hall was a somber, deeply ritualistic affair. We were over a hundred strong. The grand table was triangular, honoring the sacred geometry of our family. Carthoris and I presided at the centers of our respective sides, while the third side centered upon Dejah Thoris’s high-backed, carven chair. It stood vacant, draped in her gorgeous wedding trappings and jewels, a profound visual and emotional anchor. Behind it stood a slave, perfectly still, maintaining the physical memory of her presence.

The meal itself had been prepared by our master chefs with meticulous devotion, an attempt to conjure meaning through sustenance. There were tenderized cuts of *thoat* flank, slow-braised over fragrant *pimalia* wood until they yielded entirely to the palate; complex reductions of sweet *sorak* fruit designed to evoke the bittersweet untranslatability of our current sorrow; and ancient grains harvested from the terraced gardens of Helium, carrying the very terroir of our survival. Yet, for all its gustatory brilliance, the feast was a melancholy communion. Eating was not a utilitarian act of fueling the body, but a deliberate, almost painful ingestion of memory. 

At my right sat Kantos Kan, and to the right of Dejah Thoris’s empty place sat Tars Tarkas, accommodated by a raised section of the board I had constructed years ago to integrate his mighty bulk into our domestic routine. Hor Vastus took the seat of honor beside Carthoris. The room, usually ringing with the crystalline, merry laughter of my vivacious Princess, was instead filled with a muted, reverent quiet—an acoustic ecology mourning its lost melody. We were haunted by her absence, by the uncertain fate of Tardos Mors, and by the looming threat to Helium’s cultural soul.

Suddenly, the heavy quiet was pierced by a distant, organic clamor—a chaotic mingling of voices outside the palace. A kitchen slave, his garments redolent with the smell of crushed desert herbs and hearth-ash, rushed into the dining hall, laughing and shrieking in a manic dissonance. 

“Dejah Thoris is found!” he cried. “A messenger from Dejah Thoris!”

I waited for nothing more. The great windows of the dining hall overlooked the avenue leading to the main gates. Without breaking stride, I cleared the triangular board with a single leap, passing over the meticulously prepared dishes, and sprang through the balcony doors. Thirty feet below lay the scarlet sward of the lawn, where a crowd had gathered around a massive, panting thoat. The beast smelled of ozone, exhausted sweat, and the red dust of the deep plains. 

I vaulted to the ground below and sprinted toward the rider. As I closed the distance, the figure slid from the mighty mount. It was Sola.

“Where is the Princess of Helium?” I cried, my voice raw.

The green girl ran to me, bringing with her the desperate, untranslatable grief of the wastes. “O my Prince! My Prince!” she cried. “She is gone for ever. Even now she may be a captive upon the lesser moon. The black pirates of Barsoom have stolen her.”
