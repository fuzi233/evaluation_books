The evening and the morning arrived with the velocity of a collapsing siege. Romola possessed a singular, driving purpose that had coalesced from the afternoon's anxieties, swelling like a tributary merging into a flood. It was less a decision than a physiological imperative. Ignoring the encroaching gloom of the streets and discarding the thought of Maso's cautious escort, she sprinted toward the bridge. Below, the Arno churned black against the fading crimson of the horizon. She chose the shortest route to the Old Palace, accepting the risk of encountering her husband as a variable she could not calculate; she needed only to purge her heart.

She navigated the pillared court and ascended the broad stairs in a trance, her destination defined solely by the urgent need to speak. At the threshold of the audience chamber, she demanded an usher for the Gonfaloniere, citing her name and insisting on privacy. She was not kept waiting long amidst the silent vigil of frescoed saints and flickering tapers. The door swung open, and Bernardo del Nero entered, his white hair stiff and upright within the folds of his silk *lucco*.

"Romola, my child, what is this?" he asked, his voice tight with alarm as he shut the door against the corridor.

She removed her veil and moved toward him, speechless. He placed a firm hand on her shoulder, pulling her slightly back to inspect her features. Her face was gaunt, carved by exhaustion and prolonged tension; her hair had fallen in chaotic disarray. Yet, her eyes burned with a feverish intensity that seemed to transcend her physical collapse.

"What has he done?" Bernardo demanded abruptly. "Speak, child. Discard your pride. I am your father."

"It concerns not myself," Romola replied quickly. "Nothing concerns me personally. Dearest godfather, it concerns you. I have gathered intelligence—some of which I dare not articulate. But you are under threat within these walls, and everywhere. Fanatics seek your blood, and traitors lurk in the shadows. Trust no one. If you place faith in others, you will be betrayed."

Bernardo offered a weary smile. "Have you driven yourself to this hysterical state, my poor child," he said, lifting a hand to stroke her hair, "to recite such ancient platitudes to an old man like me?"

"Oh, no! These are not old truths," she insisted, clutching her hands together until her knuckles whitened, as if the gesture could contain the words she dared not speak. "These are fresh revelations I possess but cannot vocalize. Dearest godfather, you know I am not foolish. Why did I come to you without cause? Is it too late to warn you against every single individual currently operating in your favor? Is it too late to command, 'Retreat to your villa and remain hidden in the countryside once these final three days of office conclude'?"

Her voice cracked. "Oh God! Perhaps it is too late! And if harm befalls you, it will feel as though I have delivered the blow!"

The confession erupted involuntarily, a spasmodic release of a long-suppressed dread. She recoiled, startled by her own vehemence.

"I mean," she stammered, regaining composure, "I hold no concrete evidence. Only a terror that consumes me."

"Poor child!" Bernardo observed, regarding her with a gaze of quiet penetration for several moments. Then he spoke softly. "Return home, Romola. Rest. These fears may merely be monstrous shadows cast by something trivial and harmless. Even traitors require motive; they follow the scent of profit like rats to cheese. We do not yet know the direction of the wind."

He paused, averting his eyes with an air of abstract resignation, then shrugged slowly. "Warnings serve no purpose for me, child. I engage in no conspiracies, nor do I abandon my banner. Should I march alongside stubborn men charging with pikes and muskets, I must share the consequence. Let us cease this discussion. I have few years remaining in my sack for them to plunder. Go, child; go home and rest."

He touched her head again, affectionately. She could not resist leaning into his arm, pressing her forehead against his shoulder. This caress represented the final remnant of that youthful filial bond, which now appeared radiant even amid its tribulations, untouched by malice.

"Is silence the best path, my Romola?" the elder asked.

"Yes, for now," she answered hesitantly, lifting her head with a pleading expression. "But I cannot foresee if it will endure."

"Well, you possess a father's ear while I draw breath," he declared, lifting the heavy black drapery of his cloak to wrap it securely around her head. "And a father's home. Remember that."

Opening the door, he gestured urgently. "Hasten away. You resemble a specter of darkness; you will find safety elsewhere."

That night, Romola sank into a profound sleep. Her agitation had reached its zenith; she required reserves of strength before facing further suffering. Against her usual discipline, she remained unconscious well past sunrise.

When consciousness returned, it was shattered by the roar of artillery. Piero de' Medici, backed by thirteen hundred men, stood before the gate facing Rome. This was the report Maso delivered, embellished with numerous circumstantial details of questionable reliability. A peasant had rushed into the Signoria before dawn, preventing a surprise attack. His master had already departed, summoned to the Palazzo earlier.

Romola dispatched the old servant to gather further intelligence while she climbed to the loggia, scanning the skyline for signs of the dreaded entry or its repulsion. Maso confirmed that the great Piazza teemed with armed troops and that many prominent citizens suspected of Medician sympathies had been detained within the palace. Public sentiment was fractured; some appeared indifferent to Piero's arrival, whispering that the Signoria itself had invited him, while others noted the hostile reception from Pisan soldiers.

In the swirling chaos of those morning hours, few external events registered clearly in Romola's mind, obscured by the tumult of retrospection and anticipation. She recalled walking to the Badia precisely as scheduled, despite street alarms, and waiting in vain. However, the scene unfolding outside the church doors as they were barred for the afternoon recess remained vividly etched in her memory.

A palpable shift had occurred in the expressions and cadences of the crowd, both armed and unarmed. The gunfire resumed, yet this time it elicited laughter rather than fear. She soon understood the cause. Piero de' Medici and his cavalry had abandoned Florence, galloping furiously down the road to Siena.

This confirmation came from a sturdy shopkeeper among the *Piagnoni*, who had not yet lowered his pike.

"It is true," he concluded, his tone bitter. "Piero has fled, but those who conspired with his arrival remain. All know their identities; if the new Signoria fulfills its duty, we shall soon expose them."

The words struck Romola like a sudden, sharp convulsion. Yet, the impending evil they hinted at was not yet immediate. As she stepped back into her home, her primary anxiety shifted to the terrifying possibility that she had lost sight of Baldassarre for an extended period.
