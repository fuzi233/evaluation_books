Baldassarre wandered the uneven terrain outside Florence's walls, seeking the cheapest shelter available. His path led him toward the hill of San Giorgio, a jagged scar of land where a landslide had shattered homes centuries prior, leaving behind rough ground strewn with great stones. Above this broken earth stood a peculiar, truncated tower capped with fluted tiles, and nestled against its ruins was a small outhouse. Here, under the skeletal branches of a dying mulberry tree, a shriveled old woman untied a goat with two kids. One section of the outbuilding housed livestock; the other, open to the air, contained only straw and tools—the perfect refuge Baldassarre sought.

He approached the woman, shouting his request for lodging and offering payment. Initially, she responded only with muffled denials, her head shaking in the rhythmic cadence of the deaf. Persistence eventually pierced her silence. He explained he was a destitute stranger from overseas, unable to afford hostels, needing only a place to sleep on straw for a few coins a week. She remained dubious, muttering to herself, until a pragmatic thought struck her. Fetching a hatchet, she pointed to a log half-buried in litter in the corner. "Chop that," she demanded. "If you do, you may sleep here tonight."

Baldassarre agreed. Monna Lisa watched him with arms akimbo, a smile of cunning satisfaction playing on her lips. *'It's lain there since my old man died,'* she muttered, observing his efficient strokes. *"What then? Might as well throw a stone on the fire. He chops well, speaks a foreign tongue, looks odd. Cheaper than anyone else. If he only needs straw, I'll make him run errands up and down the hill. Who cares? Hidden sins are half-forgiven. Peccato celato e mezzo perdonato. He's a stranger; he won't notice her. I'll tell her to keep quiet."*

Her thoughts drifted to a pair of bright blue eyes fixed on a four-inch hole in the upper window shutter. The shutter was closed not out of malice, but necessity; the opposite window held glass, while this one remained a crude aperture for ventilation and surveillance. The hole was too high, requiring the small observer to stand on a rickety stool, yet Tessa endured the discomfort willingly. Drawn by the strange voice, she had darted across her room repeatedly, finally settling on the stool to watch until the wood was split and Baldassarre entered the darkness, seating himself on the straw as dusk gathered.

A powerful urge seized Tessa. She wanted to share her supper, to converse with this silent man. Unlike Monna Lisa, he wasn't deaf, and she could whisper truths to him that would be wasted on the old woman. Strangers were transient figures, drifting through lives that mattered less to her than the safety of her current arrangement. Though obedience formed the bedrock of her duties, the temptation was overwhelming. She could confess this transgression to the Padre next Easter alongside her minor faults—falling asleep during prayers, her occasional temper with the deaf woman. With idle hands and no fear of fabrication, she turned from the shutter, her childish face flushed with excitement. Her attire was that of a peasant girl prepared for a festival: a clean dark-green serge gown cinched with a red girdle, a string of red glass beads at her throat, and brown hair knotted tight with a silver pin. A single new ornament, a fine gold ring, caught the fading light.

For a moment, she sat nursing her knees, her soul trembling on the precipice of forbidden joy. She had been strictly warned that forming acquaintances would cause her happiness to vanish like a treasure turning to lead upon exposure. Yet, her compliance had been driven partly by terror of her stepfather, Nofri, whose shadow loomed even here. This old man posed no threat; he was a stranger sleeping in the outhouse, likely unaware of Nofri's existence. Furthermore, if she fed him, he would be grateful and silent. Monna Lisa's prohibition would be irrelevant once the act was committed.

Downstairs, supper was being prepared—a mountain of cheesy macaroni, fragrant enough to tempt any wanderer. Tripping lightly, Tessa descended with a mind full of schemes. Pretending innocence, she asked what the noise was, then furrowed her brow with a peremptory air reminiscent of a kitten attempting intimidation. She ordered the old woman upstairs, declaring her intention to dine below. Within moments, Tessa emerged, lantern in one hand, a wooden bowl of macaroni in the other, kicking gently at the outhouse door.

Baldassarre, lost in somber reverie, blinked in confusion as the door opened to reveal a surprising little maid. Delight sparkled in her wide eyes, breaking his isolation. "I've brought you supper!" she shouted, leaning close as if addressing the deaf. "Eat while I stay with you."

Distrust warred with surprise, but Baldassarre found no impulse to reject her. He sank onto the straw passively as Tessa positioned herself beside him, placing the bowl on his lap and setting down the lantern. She crossed her hands, nodding significantly at the food, signaling permission to eat. In her fervor, she had momentarily forgotten his hearing ability, defaulting to her usual pantomime and volume.

The invitation was welcome; he had been gnawing dry bread, his appetite keen for warmth. Tessa watched silently as he consumed several mouthfuls, noting how his initial fierce gaze softened. Then, she leaned in again. "I like my supper, don't you?"

Baldassarre returned a look of gentle affection, akin to a dog touched by kindness but unable to smile. "Yes," he said softly. "But I hear well. I am not deaf."

"It is true; I forgot," Tessa admitted, clasping her hands. "But Monna Lisa is deaf. She's kind, and I'm not afraid. We live well; we have plenty. I can have nuts whenever I want. I used to work, but I hated it. I loved feeding the mules, especially poor Giannetta. We have a goat and two kids now. I used to talk to the goat because Monna Lisa was the only one there. But now I have something else—can you guess?"

She pulled back, challenging him with a mischievous smile.

"No," Baldassarre replied dreamily, setting aside his bowl. The girl seemed a fragment of his own distant past.

"You like me to talk to you, don't you?" Tessa pressed. "But you mustn't tell anyone. Should I fetch some cold sausage?"

He shook his head, appearing mild and harmless.

"Well, then, I have a little baby. Such a pretty *bambinetto*, with tiny fingers and nails! Not old yet; born at the Nativity, Monna Lisa says. I was married one Nativity, a long time ago, and nobody knew. Oh, Santa Madonna! I didn't mean to tell you that!"

Tessa stiffened, biting her lip, watching him as if expecting a dramatic reaction. But he seemed indifferent, perhaps due to the nature of strangers.

"Yes," she continued aloud, "you're a stranger. You don't live anywhere or know anyone, do you?"

"No," Baldassarre murmured, thinking aloud rather than answering. "I only know one man."

"His name isn't Nofri, is it?" Tessa asked, anxiety tightening her features.

"No," Baldassarre noticed her fear. "Is that your husband's name?"

The assumption amused Tessa. She laughed, clapping her hands. "No, indeed! But I can't tell you about my husband. You'd never guess what he's like—he's nothing like Nofri!"

She laughed again at the absurd contrast between the name Nofri, synonymous with a cross-grained stepfather, and the image of her actual spouse.

"But I don't see him often," she said more seriously. "Sometimes I pray to the Holy Madonna to send him more frequently, and once she did. But I must return to my *bimbo*. I'll bring it tomorrow to show you. You'd like to see it. Sometimes it cries and makes faces, but only when hungry, Monna Lisa says. You wouldn't believe it, but Monna Lisa had babies once, and they're all dead old men. My husband says she'll never die now because she's so well-dried. I'm glad, for I love her. Would you like to stay here tomorrow?"

"I'd like to have this place to rest in," Baldassarre said. "I'd pay for it and harm no one."

"No, indeed. You're not a bad old man. But you look troubled. Is there something you'll cry about when I leave you alone? *I* used to cry once."

"No, child. I think I shall cry no more."

"That's right. I'll bring breakfast and show you the *bimbo*. Goodnight."

Tessa collected her bowl and lantern, closing the door behind her. To Baldassarre, the girl was no more than a faint rainbow against the blackness of a man wrestling in deep waters. He barely thought of her again until his dreamless wakefulness gave way to disturbed dreams.
