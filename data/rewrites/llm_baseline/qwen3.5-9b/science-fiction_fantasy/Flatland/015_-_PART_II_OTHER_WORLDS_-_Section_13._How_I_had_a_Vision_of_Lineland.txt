It was the penultimate day of the 1999th year of our era, marking the dawn of the Great Holiday. After indulging until late hours in my beloved pastime of geometry, I retired to sleep, burdened by an unsolved mathematical puzzle. That night, I dreamed.

Before me lay a vast throng of tiny straight lines—whom I naturally presumed to be women—interspersed with even smaller entities, luminous points. All moved back and forth within a single, identical straight line, maintaining roughly the same velocity. At intervals, a chaotic chorus of chirping and twittering erupted from them during their movement; yet whenever they halted, absolute silence fell.

Approaching one of the largest figures, whom I took to be a woman, I attempted to address her, but received no response. Two further appeals yielded the same result. Impatient at what seemed intolerable rudeness, I maneuvered my mouth directly in front of hers to block her path and shouted, "Woman, what does this gathering signify? What is this strange, confused chirping, and this monotonous oscillation along this single straight line?"

"I am no woman," replied the diminutive line. "I am the Monarch of this world. But tell me, whence do you intrude into my realm of Lineland?"

Taken aback by this abrupt rebuke, I apologized for startling his Royal Highness. Describing myself as a stranger, I implored him to explain the nature of his dominion. However, extracting meaningful information proved exceedingly difficult. The Monarch could not resist the assumption that because something was familiar to him, it must be known to me, interpreting my queries as playful feigned ignorance. Through persistent questioning, however, I eventually coaxed the following truths from him:

This poor, self-proclaimed ignorant monarch believed that the straight line he termed his kingdom—and wherein he spent his entire existence—constituted the entirety of the world, and indeed, all of space itself. Incapable of moving or seeing outside this line, he possessed no concept of anything beyond it. Although he had heard my initial voice, the sound arrived in a manner entirely alien to his experience. As he later explained, he saw no person and perceived a voice emanating strangely from "my own intestines."

Until I physically obstructed his path, he had neither seen me nor understood anything but confused vibrations striking what I called his side, but which he termed his "inside" or "stomach." Even then, he held no notion of my origin. To him, everything outside his world—or line—was not merely empty, but non-existent. A blank implies space; for him, there was simply nothing.

His subjects—the small lines who were men, and the points who were women—all shared this confinement. Their motion and sight were restricted strictly to that single straight line, which served as their universe. Consequently, their entire horizon was limited to a mere point. To a Linelander, every man, woman, child, or object appeared only as a point. Sex and age could be discerned solely by the timbre of the voice. Furthermore, since each individual occupied the entirety of the narrow path constituting their universe, and no one could shift right or left to accommodate others, it followed that no Linelander could ever pass another. Once neighbors, forever neighbors. For them, neighborhood was akin to marriage; neighbors remained neighbors until death separated them.

Such a life, with vision reduced to a point and motion confined to a line, struck me as inexpressibly dreary. I was surprised, therefore, to observe the King's evident vivacity and cheerfulness. Curious whether joy was possible under conditions so hostile to domestic intimacy, I hesitated to inquire about his family. Finally, driven by curiosity, I bluntly asked about the health of his household.

"My wives and children," he replied, "are well and happy."

Staggered by this answer—for immediately surrounding the Monarch, as I had noted in my dream, were only men—I dared to respond, "Pardon me, but I cannot conceive how Your Royal Highness can ever see or approach their Majesties when at least six individuals stand between you. You cannot see through them, nor pass them. Is it possible that in Lineland, proximity is unnecessary for marriage and procreation?"

"How dare you ask such an absurd question!" retorted the Monarch. "If things were as you suggest, the universe would quickly become uninhabitable. No, no; proximity is irrelevant to the union of hearts, and the birth of children is too sacred to rely on such chance accidents as physical closeness. Surely you are not unaware of this. Yet, since you choose to pretend ignorance, I shall instruct you as if you were the youngest infant in Lineland."

"Know, then, that marriages are consummated through the faculty of sound and the sense of hearing."

"You must understand," continued the King, "that every man possesses two mouths or voices, just as he has two eyes—a bass at one end and a tenor at the other. I ought not to mention this unless I failed to distinguish your tenor during our conversation."

I admitted I had only one voice and was unaware that his Majesty possessed two.

"That confirms my suspicion," said the King. "You are not a man, but a feminine monstrosity possessing a bass voice and an utterly uneducated ear. Nevertheless, let us proceed."

"Since Nature ordains that every man must wed two wives—"

"Why two?" I asked.

"You push your pretended simplicity too far," he cried. "How can a complete harmonic union exist without the combination of the four-in-one: the bass and tenor of the man, plus the soprano and contralto of the two women?"

"But suppose," I interjected, "that a man prefers only one wife, or perhaps three?"

"It is impossible," he declared. "As inconceivable as two and one making five, or as the human eye perceiving a straight line."

I wished to interrupt, but he pressed on: "Once a week, a Law of Nature compels us to engage in a rhythmic motion of unusual intensity, lasting approximately the time it takes to count one hundred and one beats. During this grand choral dance, at the fifty-first pulse, all inhabitants of the universe halt in mid-stride. At this decisive moment, every individual emits their richest, fullest, sweetest tone. It is here that all our marriages occur. So exquisite is the adaptation of bass to treble, and tenor to contralto, that often lovers separated by twenty thousand leagues instantly recognize the responsive note of their destined partner. Penetrating the trivial barriers of distance, Love unites the trio. The marriage thus consummated yields a triple offspring—one male and two females—which enters the lineage of Lineland."

"What! Always triple?" I exclaimed. "Does every wife then always bear twins?"

"Bass-voiced monstrosity! Yes," replied the King. "How else could the balance of the sexes be maintained if two girls were not born for every boy? Would you deny the very alphabet of Nature?"

He fell silent, overcome by fury, and considerable time passed before I could coax him to resume his tale.

"You will not, of course, assume that every bachelor finds his mate at the first attempt in this Universal Marriage Chorus. On the contrary, the process is repeated by most of us many times. Few are the fortunate souls who immediately recognize in each other's voices the partner ordained by Providence and rush into a reciprocal, perfectly harmonious embrace. For most, courtship is prolonged. The wooer's voice may align with one future wife but not the other; or match neither initially; or the soprano and contralto may lack perfect harmony. In such cases, Nature provides that every weekly chorus brings the three lovers into closer alignment. Each trial of voice, each fresh discovery of discord, subtly induces the imperfect party to adjust their vocal output to approximate the superior tone. After numerous trials and gradual approximations, success finally arrives. There comes a day when, amidst the customary Marriage Chorus echoing across Lineland, the three distant lovers suddenly achieve exact harmony. Before they realize it, the wedded triplet is embraced vocally in a duplicate union, and Nature rejoices in another marriage and three new lives."
