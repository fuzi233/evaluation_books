In the shadowed heart of the marketplace, justice was performed not with the solemnity of law, but with the brutality of fear. Four figures were condemned to stand pinioned, exposed to the prying eyes of the populace for two hours—a grotesque spectacle designed to ensure that any witness to their guilt, or any knowledge of their other crimes, might be extracted during the trial. Yet, despite the weight of their exposure, the crowd remained silent. No new accusations arose. This passivity was hardly surprising; these were creatures of the night, vampires who fed on the exhaustion of weary travelers, preying upon strangers in the dim recesses of the city.

But as the officers prepared to release them, a sound shattered the air—a scream of such primal terror that it seemed to tear the fabric of reality itself. A woman in the throng pointed a trembling finger at one of the accused, her eyes wide and bulging with a horror that defied human comprehension. She spoke not a word before collapsing, her spirit fleeing her body in sheer revulsion.

Men and women surged forward, torn between the instinct to aid the fallen and the morbid curiosity of the mob. As consciousness slowly returned to the woman, a cacophony of speculation erupted among the bystanders.

"'Twas at him she pointed!" someone cried.

"Nay, 'twas at this one!" countered another.

"No, no," a third insisted, gesturing wildly, "'twas at yon hangdog with the hair hanging loose around his neck."

Before these conjectures could spiral into chaos, the woman regained her wits entirely. With the ferocity of a lioness denied her kill, she lunged at the nearest officer—the landlord of the tavern—and screamed, "My child! Man! Man! Give me back my child!" Her fingers clawed at the glossy golden hair the officers had tied around the accused's neck, tearing it free with a violent snap. She buried her face in the strands, kissing them with a delirious desperation. Only then did the fog of grief clear from her mind. In that terrible clarity, she realized the truth: her lost daughter was dead. Overcome by the weight of this revelation, she sank to the ground, shrieking and sobbing over the stolen lock of hair until the entire crowd rose in a savage roar, turning their fury upon the accused.

His life ended swiftly and brutally. In those days, every citizen carried the means of instant execution at their girdle. But Denys, ever vigilant, drew his sword and shouted, "A moi, camarades!" rallying his companions to form a wall of steel between the mob and the victim. "Who lays a finger on him dies!" he commanded, his voice cutting through the din. Other archers fanned out, backing him up. With great difficulty, they held the line, keeping the assassins at bay while Denys appealed to the rioters.

"What sort of vengeance is this?" he roared, his face grim. "Would you be so mad as to rob the wheel of justice and grant this vermin an easy death?"

The mob, though growling with flashing eyes, was held passive not by Denys's words, but by the cold certainty of the archers' steel. Seeing the situation spiraling, the municipal officers gathered, forming a protective ring with the soldiers, and prudently escorted the accused back to the dungeon.

As the mob hooted at both the prisoners and the guards indiscriminately, Denys ensured the detainees were safely lodged. He then turned his steps toward "The White Hart," expecting to find Gerard waiting inside. On his path, however, he paused beneath a first-floor window where two girls were working. He saluted them, and they smiled back, their manners easy, their complexions radiant. Intrigued, he engaged them in conversation, inviting them to a meal at the inn. They initially objected, but after a brief exchange, they relented. In this fleeting moment of charming society, Denys momentarily forgot the plight of Gerard, who was currently being dragged away to prison.

Yet, fate intervened. Just as Gerard was led off, he suddenly halted, his presence of mind returning amidst the confusion. Demanding to know by whose authority he was arrested, he confronted his captors.

"By the vice-baillie's," the constable replied flatly.

"The vice-baillie?" Gerard exclaimed, his voice rising in indignation. "Alas! What have I, a stranger, done to offend a vice-baillie? This charge of sorcery must be a blind. No sorcerer am I; I am merely a poor, true lad far from my home."

Such vagueness disgusted the officer. "Show him the capias, Jacques," he ordered.

Jacques stepped forward, holding the writ aloft in both hands, extending it nearly a yard and a half from Gerard's face. Simultaneously, the large constable pinned Gerard down. Both officers stood on tenterhooks, fearing the prisoner might snatch the document—an act they attributed with superstitious dread.

But the poor prisoner had no such treacherous thoughts. One might ask if he would have touched it even with tongs. Instead, he simply craned his neck, reading the parchment with wide, startled eyes. To his infinite astonishment, he discovered that the vice-bailiff who had signed the writ was none other than the friendly alderman. Courage surged within him. "There is some error here," he assured his captor. Finding no response, he demanded to be taken immediately before the alderman.

"What say you to that, Jacques?" the officer asked.

"Impossible. We have no orders to take him before his worship. Read the writ!"

"Nay, but good kind fellows, what harm can it be? I will give you each an ecu."

"Jacques, what say you to that?"

"Humph!" Jacques grunted. "I say we have no orders *not* to take him to his worship. Read the writ!"

"Then say we take him to prison round by his worship."

Agreement was reached. They accepted the money, bidding Gerard observe that they were doing him a favor. He saw clearly that they desired gratitude as much as silver. He attempted to satisfy their cupidity, but the words stuck in his throat; feigning was never his forte.

Entering the alderman's presence, Gerard felt his heart hammering against his ribs. Begging with a faltering voice, he pleaded to know what offense he had committed since leaving that very room with Manon and Denys.

"Nought that I know of," the alderman replied calmly.

When the writ was produced, the alderman admitted signing it at daybreak. "I grow old, and my memory faileth me," he confessed. "A discussion regarding the girl... I quite forgot your own offense. But I remember now. All is well. You are the one I committed for sorcery. Wait! Before you go to gaol, you shall hear what your accuser says. Run and fetch him, you."

The man could not locate the accuser immediately. Growing impatient, the alderman turned to Gerard. "The main charge is that you set a dead body ablaze with diabolical fire—that flame which burns yet consumes nothing. And if 'tis true, young man, I am sorry for thee, for thou wilt assuredly burn with the fire of good pine logs in the marketplace of Neufchâteau."

"Oh, sir, for pity's sake let me have speech with his reverence the cure!" Gerard implored.

The alderman advised against it. "The Church was harder upon sorcerers than was the corporation."

"But, sir, I am innocent," Gerard protested, his voice shifting between a snarl and a whimper.

"Oh, if you think you are innocent—" the alderman interrupted sharply. "Officer, go with him to the cure; but see he escape you not. 'Innocent,' quotha?"

They located the cure in his doublet, repairing a wheelbarrow. Gerard recounted everything, appealing piteously to the priest. "Just for using a little phosphorus in self-defense against cut-throats, they intend to hang me."

It proved fortunate for the magician that he had already detailed his tale fully to the cure. Thus, that shrewd personage held the stick at the right end, while the corporation gripped it by the ferule. His reverence assumed an exceedingly grave demeanor. "I must question you privately on this untoward business," he declared. He ushered Gerard into a private room, ordering the officer to stand outside and guard the door, ready to enter if summoned. The big constable stood sentinel, quaking, expecting the room to vanish in a puff of brimstone.

Instantly alone, the cure's countenance unlocked, and he returned to himself. "Show me the trick on't," he said, his curiosity piqued.

"I cannot, sir, unless the room be darkened."

The cure swiftly closed out the light with a wooden shutter. "Now, then."

"But on what shall I put it?" Gerard asked. "Here is no dead face. 'Twas that which made it look so dire."

The cure groped about the room. "Good; here is an image: 'tis my patron saint."

"Heaven forbid! That were profanation," Gerard objected.

"Pshaw! 'Twill rub off, will't not?"

"Ay, but it goes against me to take such liberty with a saint," the sorcerer hesitated.

"Fiddlestick!" the divine dismissed him.
