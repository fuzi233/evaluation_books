The Major's epistle, a document of dire import, acted as a lightning rod for Doctor Portman. With the alacrity of a man rushing to administer a painful but necessary extraction, he hastened to Fairoaks. He viewed the situation not with malice, but with the grim efficiency of a dentist pulling a bad tooth; the wisdom required was simply to get it out, and quickly. While he lamented the youth's descent into perdition, he maintained a professional demeanor, comparing his own vigorous application of the forceps to a necessary duty. After reading the letter aloud to Myra and Mrs. Portman, accompanied by a torrent of damnable commentary regarding the "young scapegrace" spiraling further into sin, the Doctor departed. The ladies, armed with their usual precision and speed, immediately disseminated the intelligence through the Clavering society before striding purposefully toward Fairoaks to deliver the final blow to the widow.

However, Helen Pendennis had already received the news via Pen's own correspondence, and strangely, it brought relief rather than devastation. A heavy, gloomy presentiment had hung over her for months, whispering of inevitable ruin. Now, knowing the worst, she felt a strange sense of closure. Her darling boy had returned, repentant and tender-hearted. Could she desire more? When the Rector attempted to lecture her, his words grounded in common sense yet bolstered by ancient tradition, failed to stir any true indignation within her heart. She harbored no anger, save for a singular sorrow that *he* might be unhappy. To her mind, the academic degree was a meaningless fuss; why did the Rector and the Doctor insist on shipping the boy to a den of temptation with so little hope of redemption? Why not keep him at home, safe with his mother? Regarding his debts, she dismissed them as trivialities—"his debts!" she cried internally. Wasn't his father's money essentially his own? Didn't he possess the inherent right to spend it? Thus, the widow confronted the virtuous Doctor, and remarkably, none of his righteous arrows pierced her gentle bosom.

Meanwhile, a peculiar shift in domestic dynamics had occurred between Pen and his younger sister, Laura. For years, following a childish custom of brotherly affection, Pen had greeted Laura with a familiar kiss. Upon returning from college after months away, however, he found not the simple girl he remembered, but a tall, slim, and striking young lady. The old ritual was impossible; he could not proffer the customary kiss, and she received him with a gracious curtsy, a proffered hand, and a flush that climbed from her neck to her cheek, precisely where Pen had once saluted her.

The narrator, admitting a lack of skill—and perhaps inclination—to describe female beauty, insists that virtue outweighs fleeting charms. Yet, Miss Laura Bell, at sixteen, possessed a distinct presence. Standing five feet four inches, she was deemed "gawky" or a "Maypole" by smaller peers, though adorned with beautiful roses around her head. Pale usually, with a faint rose tint, her cheeks bloomed violently into deep crimson when emotion demanded, leaving lingering stains long after the feeling had passed. Her eyes, large since childhood, shone with an innate brilliance that defied description, requiring two pairs of veils in the form of the finest black lashes to mitigate their glare. Critics claimed she "ogled" everyone, but the truth lay in Nature's design; they simply shone. Her complexion rivaled Lady Mantrap's, unassisted by powder. Her nose remained to the reader's imagination, but her smile revealed pearly teeth, and her voice was a low, sweet music. Despite rumors of large feet due to her long dresses, her overall grace suggested a harmony of proportion.

Though outwardly sweet, Laura's internal compass had shifted. Having matured significantly in mind and person over the last two years, she was only half-pleased with Pen's recent conduct. His letters to home had grown sparse and brief. When Helen pleaded for constancy, citing his studies and engagements, Laura countered sharply. "It is better that he loses a prize than forget his mother," she argued, insisting he stay home rather than vacation at his friends' grand estates where no one loved him as deeply as she did. "As I do?" Helen sighed, feigning offense. Laura, however, stood firm, denying any personal jealousy, yet refusing to accept Helen's defenses. She insisted that Pen must forge his own path, cultivate influential acquaintances, and navigate the complex webs of male ambition that women could not comprehend. Whether Helen truly believed these excuses or merely endured them for Pen's sake, the result was a comforting maternal delusion: the certainty that a woman's love, once given, remains an untiring fortress of forgiveness.

Furthermore, Laura found Pen's recent behavior shocking. He spoke lightly and laxly of women, neglecting the courtesies expected of a gentleman. Smoking his horrid pipes indoors, refusing to accompany his mother to church or social calls, and lounging in dressing-gowns yawning over novels while she returned from her duties, he was a stranger to the hero of her infancy. The boy she had spent countless nights praising for his virtues, love, and bravery was replaced by a bold, brilliant, and sarcastic young man who seemingly scorned the simple devotions and pleasures of the women around him.

The catalyst for her outrage, however, was the Fotheringay affair. Learning of it first through the Major's sarcasm and then from neighbors at Clavering, Laura was thunderstruck. A Pendennis throwing himself away on an actress! Helen's boy galloping daily to kneel before a stage performer and drink with her infamous father! "I would have run away," Laura declared passionately, "if I had to walk barefoot through the snow." When Helen asked if she would abandon her mother, Laura immediately retracted her statement, and the two women embraced in a display of warmth typical of their natures.

Yet, where did Laura's indignation stem from? Was it ignorance of the reciprocal nature of human weakness, or misinformation from the bitter Claverings and the vengeful Mrs. Portman? Or perhaps, as often alleged, a hidden jealousy? Regardless of the cause, her fury toward Pen did not extend to Helen. Instead, she devoted herself to the widow with a fierce, girlish affection—a passion bordering on folly. She showered Helen with caresses and tender epithets, a scene too intimate for sober historians to recount with propriety.

But when Laura learned of Pen's actual misery and shame, her wrath evaporated instantly, replaced by a tender, unreasonable compassion. He was the Pen of old days again—the frank, generous, and tender-hearted boy. She sided immediately with Helen against the Doctor, dismissing the "enormity" of Pen's transgressions. Debts? They were trifles; he had been thrust into expensive society by his uncle's orders and was forced to emulate his companions. Failed to obtain his degree? The poor boy was ill during the exams, distracted by financial woes, perhaps bullied by jealous tutors. Others, she reasoned, must have been cruel and unfair. With flushing cheeks and eyes bright with righteous anger, she stood before the Doctor, seizing Helen's hand and kissing it openly, daring him to speak a word against her "darling mother's Pen."

Discomfited and amazed by the obstinacy of the women, the Doctor eventually departed. Laura renewed her embraces and arguments with tenfold fervor. Helen, sensing the cogency of her daughter's points, suspected foul play among the examiners. Together, the announcement of misfortune vexed them very little. Pen, plunged in shame and grief in London, tormented by remorse for his mother's sorrow, would have marveled at how lightly they bore the calamity. Calamity, after all, is welcome to a woman if it promises to bring a truant lover home. If one reduces their mistress to a crust, they will happily share it, provided the partner eats the remainder in their company.

Once the Doctor left, Laura ordered fires lit in Mr. Arthur's rooms and his bedding aired. By the time Helen finished a tender, affectionate letter to Pen, Laura had prepared the apartments. Smiling fondly, she led her mother into the warm room where fires blazed, and the two sat on the bed, talking endlessly of Pen. Laura added a postscript to Helen's letter, addressing him as "my dearest Pen," urging him to come home instantly with two emphatic dashes under the command, promising happiness with his mother and affectionate sister.

Late that night, after reading Bibles and casting a glance into Pen's room, Laura, whose head occasionally occupied the pillow once pressed by the late Pendennis, suddenly cried, "Mamma, are you awake?" Helen stirred, confirming she had not slept a wink, her gaze fixed on the night-lamp in the chimney, her thoughts consumed by Pen for hours. Laura, similarly awake and plotting, revealed a notable scheme born in her busy brain. She proposed that, having lived with Helen for ten years without touching her purse, she owed the widow a sum equal to ten years' schooling costs—five hundred pounds. They would visit the banker, Mr. Rowdy, retrieve the funds, secure two additional sums to repay, and send everything to Pen. This would clear his debts without harming anyone, allowing them to live happily ever after. Helen responded with a jumble of incoherent ejaculations and embraces, and the two women slept soundly until dawn.
