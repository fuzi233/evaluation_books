The church stood hushed and still, its stone floor patterned with shafts of golden light that pierced through the stained glass. Above, the vaulted ceiling seemed to glow with a soft, celestial purple, as though the heavens themselves had drawn near. Father Clement felt his soul stirred by the quiet beauty—just as Margaret had once done. This sacred space did not merely please the eye; it lifted the spirit toward the source of all goodness and purity.

His gaze swept across the nave, filled with villagers gathered for worship. A thousand white caps, each delicately edged with gold thread, shimmered like frost on a winter field. He had wandered far in his years—through cities, forests, and foreign lands—but never had he seen anything so strikingly pure and peaceful as this sea of heads bowed in reverence. Earlier that day, he had thundered from the pulpit with fire and judgment, but now, beneath the gentle afternoon sun, his heart softened. The harshness of his words seemed out of place. Instead, compassion surged within him. These were souls who rarely heard of mercy, whose ears were deafened by law and fear rather than love.

He began to speak—not with anger, but with tenderness, pouring forth the message of salvation. His voice was calm, yet deeply felt, carrying the weight of truth without the showiness of those preachers who spun endlessly like weathercocks in the wind. He moved not just their minds, but their hearts. Yet he did not forget those burdened by sin or sorrow. As he spoke of Christ’s boundless grace, he turned toward the southern aisle—where sunlight streamed through a high window, casting a radiant pool upon the floor.

And there she stood.

Margaret Brandt.

Her face glowed in the light, her auburn hair catching the sun like molten fire, framing a countenance both familiar and transformed. She leaned lightly against a marble column, her lashes lowered in quiet attention. Her expression was one he knew well—soft, listening, open-hearted. It was the same Margaret he had left behind: only more mature, more radiant, as if time had polished her instead of fading her.

Clement froze.

No emotion at first—only a sudden numbness, as if the world had stopped breathing. Then, slowly, his throat tightened. Words failed him. He trembled, staring at her as though seeing a vision from beyond death. There she was—his lost love, returned. Her presence stole every other thought. The congregation faded into silence, their forms blurring into shadows. All he could hear was a distant rustle, a low murmur of movement, like a dream half-remembered.

She looked up.

Full into his eyes.

Violet eyes—her own—staring back at him with a look neither recognition nor ignorance, but something deeper: a fleeting moment of startled wonder, the instant before understanding dawns.

In that breath, Clement lost himself. His lips parted, ready to cry her name—then she turned away, swift as a bird, vanishing into the crowd. But not before giving him one last glance—quick, sharp, electric. That look struck him like lightning. It left her trembling, her mind reeling, and him gasping, clutching the pulpit as if to anchor himself to reality.

He forced himself to speak again, muttering words no one could grasp. Then, haltingly, he resumed his sermon, stammering at first, then gathering strength. As she disappeared from sight, he convinced himself it had been a vision—a glimpse sent from heaven. And in that delusion, his voice rose, swelling with fervor, nearly slipping into rhapsody. The people listened, stunned, as if touched by divine fire.

When the service ended, he sat down heavily on the pulpit stool, drained and shaken. But soon, a notion took hold—one common enough in those times: if he had found no grave for her in Sevenbergen, perhaps God had brought her back to him here, in this very church. She must be buried nearby—perhaps right beside that pillar where she had appeared.

The idea settled in his mind like truth itself.

He would ask the sexton.

The church was empty now. Clement stepped through an opening in the south wall onto the grassy ground beyond. There, digging a grave, was the man he once knew as Jorian—the lad whose life he had saved long ago. But Jorian did not recognize him. Without his beard, the friar’s face was unrecognizable—tonsure, grey-streaked hair, pale cheeks hollowed by fasting.

“Son,” Clement said softly, “if you remember those you have laid to rest, tell me—has any Christian been buried inside the church, near a pillar?”

Jorian paused, wiping sweat from his brow. “No, father. All burials are in the churchyard. Why?”

“Never mind. Tell me—where lies Margaret Brandt?”

Jorian blinked. “Margaret Brandt? She died three years past. Buried here.”

“Oh,” Clement whispered. “Then I must know—was it near this pillar?”

“No,” Jorian replied, frowning. “That was before my time. The vicar might know. If she was noble—or rich enough to pay him.”

Clement sighed. “Alas, son, she was poor. And paid dearly for it. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician. She came from Sevenbergen—to die.”

As he spoke, his head drooped. He seemed no longer aware of where he stood, or whom he addressed. His mind had slipped back into memory, into grief.

Jorian stared, then spat into the dirt. “Are you making sport of me, holy sir? Or has some fool played a trick on you?”

He resumed digging with sudden force, clearly annoyed. Clement watched him, puzzled, but with a quiet sorrow in his eyes.

But Jorian, though gruff, was not cruel. After three shovels of earth, he stopped. “By the saints,” he muttered, “what a boor I am, speaking so to a holy man. You stand there like a lamb, and I growl like a dog.” He set down his spade, pulled on his doublet, and said, “Wait—I’ve it now. You’re not looking for Margaret. You want Peter Brandt’s grave. He does lie here—near the west door. Come, I’ll show you.”

He didn’t know that someone waited on the tombstone—someone who had been watching, hoping, for this very moment.

Pietro Vanucci and Andrea did not recognize the friar without his beard. For indeed, a man’s beard—especially one grown wild and untouched—can become part of his face, shaping it, defining it. Remove it, and he may vanish from memory entirely.

And so, as the sexton led the way, the friar walked forward, unaware that the past was waiting just ahead—waiting to reclaim what had been lost.
