Deronda did not undress that night. The room allotted him had the faint, stale warmth of a place where other travellers had slept badly and risen early, and from the corridor there came at intervals the soft scuff of a slipper, a door-latch tried and let go, the hush of voices that meant to be mercifully inaudible. Beyond all that—the faintest undertone, as if the sea were still speaking through stone and timber, a long breathing that would not cease. He kept his coat on, as if any loosening of his own habit might admit the day’s calamity more freely into him.

Gwendolen had insisted on seeing him again before she would let her women undress her; and when he came she had been perfectly still—too still, as a flame is still when the air has been withdrawn from it. She had only whispered, with that eager control which was worse than sobbing, that he must promise to come when she sent in the morning. He had promised. And yet he lay down in his clothes with a foreboding that did not belong to imagination but to the trained dread of what follows a shock: fever, delirium, words flung out beyond recall. He spoke to her attendant, making it understood he would be within call, and that he was in communication with her friends in England—an authority he could assume the more readily because Grandcourt’s valet, the one old servant from the voyage, knew him and accepted his presence.

When at last weariness overcame the day’s various emotions, his sleep was broken by morning dreams—events knotted together, faces rising and sinking like things seen through water. He woke with his heart held in a grip, an image pressed on him by anxiety, and lay listening. No messenger. No quick steps. Only the hotel’s morning—somewhere a chair moved, somewhere a shutter tapped in the wind, and always the distant, tireless sea.

It was still early; he could not go asking questions. Later he learned she had been wakeful through most of the night, but without violent agitation, and had fallen asleep toward morning. He wondered at the force that dwelt in her—so alive to dread, and yet mastering herself as if concealment were a last necessity. For himself, he felt curiously dulled, as if what had passed between him and his mother had blunted the edge of immediate sensibility. He seemed to be living by claim and duty rather than by any overflow of tenderness. He had lately been absorbed in an experience far apart from Gwendolen’s lot; and now, though his care for her was urgent, it had the strange air of revisiting a former scene where he had once suffered, and not yet recovering the full inward response.

He set himself, with the practical steadiness that comes when the mind cannot rest, to obtain a formal statement from the fishermen who had rescued Gwendolen—something legally recognized, to guard her from conjecture and accusation. Few details emerged. The boat in which Grandcourt had gone out had been found drifting, the sail loose, and had been towed in. They thought it likely he had been struck overboard by the sail flapping while he put about, and that he could not swim. They had been near, but their attention was first arrested by a cry that seemed like a man in distress; then, as they hastened with their oars, they heard a shriek from the lady and saw her leap in.

When Deronda returned to the hotel, he was told Gwendolen had risen and desired to see him.

He was led into a room darkened by blinds and curtains, where the daylight was not allowed to enter frankly, but seeped in as a dim, chastened presence. The air carried that mingled odour of warmed cloth, extinguished candles, and the faintest medicinal sharpness—lavender and camphor—such scents as women summon about pain when they have no better defence. Outside, filtered through the curtains and the building’s thickness, the sea’s low thunder was like an acoustic memory that would not be silenced: a world insisting on its own continuance.

Gwendolen sat wrapped in a white shawl, facing the door as if she had been waiting and listening for his step. Her long hair was gathered up and coiled with care; the blue stars in her ears held their place with a strange pertinacity, as if vanity were a pin fastening her to the last shred of her former self. When he entered she rose impulsively to her full height, sheathed in white, her face and neck scarcely less white, save for a purple line beneath her eyes. Her lips were a little apart with the helpless look of one accused. She was like the unhappy ghost of that Gwendolen Harleth he had once seen at the gaming table—turning away with firm lips and proud self-possession from her losses. The sight pierced him; pity, and with it all their past, sprang back into living force.

“I beseech you to rest—not to stand,” he said, moving toward her.

She obeyed at once, dropping back into the chair as if her strength were only borrowed.

“Will you sit down near me?” she said. “I want to speak very low.”

He drew a small chair close beside her. The nearness, the quiet act of placing himself within the circle of her breath, seemed to touch her more than any words. She turned her pale face full upon his—so close now that he could see the slight trembling in her eyelids—and said, in the lowest audible tone, “You know I am a guilty woman?”

He himself grew paler. “I know nothing,” he answered, and dared not add more.

“He is dead.” The words came with the same undertoned decision.

“Yes,” Deronda said, suspended in mournful reluctance, as if speech might make the fact more final.

“His face will not be seen above the water again,” she whispered, her hands clenched. “Not by any one else—only by me. A dead face. I shall never get away from it.”

She spoke with desperate self-repression, looking away toward some point on the floor as if the scene lay there, fixed like a stain. Deronda’s mind caught at a hope—that she was speaking through an exaggerating medium of excitement and horror, that delirium might be shaping this confession. Yet he felt the conflict that held him silent: she was bent on confessing, and he dreaded the confession. Against his better will he shrank from the task laid on him. He was not a priest. He dreaded the weight of her soul flung upon his own with imploring dependence. Cowardly as the wish seemed, he wished she could bury her secret in herself.

She spoke again, hurriedly, fixing him with her glance.

“You will not say that I ought to tell the world? you will not say that I ought to be disgraced? I could not do it. I could not bear it. I cannot have my mother know. Not if I were dead. I could not have her know. I must tell you; but you will not say that any one else should know.”

“I can say nothing in my ignorance,” Deronda answered, in a low, mournful voice, “except that I desire to help you.”

“I told you from the beginning—as soon as I could—I told you I was afraid of myself.” The murmur was piteous, and he had to bend his ear to catch it. Her face afflicted him too much to look at steadily. “I felt a hatred in me that was always working like an evil spirit—contriving things. Everything I could do to free myself came into my mind; and it got worse—all things got worse. That is why I asked you to come to me in town. I thought then I would tell you the worst about myself. I tried. But I could not tell everything. And he came in.”

She paused; a shudder ran through her as if cold water had passed over her skin. Then she went on.

“I will tell you everything now. Do you think a woman who cried, and prayed, and struggled to be saved from herself, could be a murderess?”

“Great God!” Deronda’s voice broke, deep and shaken. “Don’t torture me needlessly. You have not murdered him. You threw yourself into the water with the impulse to save him. Tell me the rest afterward. This death was an accident that you could not have hindered.”

“Don’t be impatient with me.” The childlike beseeching in the words compelled him to look. Her poor quivering lips went on. “You said—you used to say—you felt more for those who had done something wicked and were miserable; you said they might get better—they might be scourged into something better. If you had not spoken in that way, everything would have been worse. I did remember all you said to me. It came to me always. It came to me at the very last—that was the reason why I—But now, if you cannot bear with me when I tell you everything—if you turn away from me and forsake me, what shall I do? Am I worse than I was when you found me and wanted to make me better? All the wrong I have done was in me then—and more—and more—if you had not come and been patient with me. And now—will you forsake me?”

Her hands, once clenched, lay now helplessly relaxed and trembling on the chair’s arms. Her lips stayed parted after she ceased speaking, as if she had no power to close them upon what she had uttered. Deronda could not answer; he was forced to look away. He took one of her hands and clasped it—an instinctive grasp, like that of two children who must walk through dark together. It was the only way he could say, I will not forsake you. And even as he did it he felt as if he were signing his name to a blank paper that might be filled with something dreadful.

That grasp was new to her: she had never had from any man a sign of tenderness that answered a need in her own being. She translated its powerful effect into a promise of inexhaustible patience and constancy. A stream of renewed strength came over her; she could go on, not in orderly narrative, but in that fitful, wandering confession where time collapses and all moments of guilt seem one.

“All sorts of contrivances in my mind—but all so difficult. And I fought against them—I was terrified at them—I saw his dead face”—her voice sank to a breath close to his ear—“ever so long ago I saw it, and I wished him to be dead. And yet it terrified me. I was like two creatures. I could not speak—I wanted to kill—it was as strong as thirst—and then directly I felt beforehand I had done something dreadful, unalterable—that would make me like an evil spirit. And it came—it came.”

She stopped, as if memory had entangled itself and could not find the strand to pull.

“It had all been in my mind when I first spoke to you—when we were at the Abbey. I had done something then. I could not tell you that. It was the only thing I did toward carrying out my thoughts. They went about over everything; but they all remained like dreadful dreams—all but one. I did one act—and I never undid it—it is there still—as long ago as when we were at Ryelands. There it was—something my fingers longed for among the beautiful toys in the cabinet in my boudoir—small and sharp like a long willow leaf in a silver sheath. I locked it in the drawer of my dressing-case. I was continually haunted with it and how I should use it. I fancied myself putting it under my pillow. But I never did. I never looked at it again. I dared not unlock the drawer: it had a key all to itself; and not long ago, when we were in the yacht, I dropped the key into the deep water. It was my wish to drop it and deliver myself. After that I began to think how I could open the drawer without the key: and when I found we were to stay at Genoa, it came into my mind that I could get it opened privately at the hotel. But then, when we were going up the stairs, I met you; and I thought I should talk to you alone and tell you this—everything I could not tell you in town; and then I was forced to go out in the boat.”

At the last words a sob rose for the first time, and she sank back. The recollection of that sharp disappointment—being shut out from speech with him—seemed for a moment to efface all that had followed. Deronda, not looking at her, spoke insistently, as if he could press her into safety by the sheer force of a question.

“And it has all remained in your imagination. It has gone on only in your thought. To the last the evil temptation has been resisted?”

Silence answered him. Tears rolled down her cheeks; she pressed her handkerchief to them and sat upright, summoning resolution. Then, leaning toward him again, she whispered:

“No, no; I will tell you everything as God knows it. I will tell you no falsehood; I will tell you the exact truth. What should I do else? I used to think I could never be wicked. I thought of wicked people as if they were a long way off me. Since then I have been wicked. I have felt wicked. And everything has been a punishment to me—all the things I used to wish for—it is as if they had been made red-hot. The very daylight has often been a punishment to me. Because—you know—I ought not to have married. That was the beginning of it. I wronged some one else. I broke my promise. I meant to get pleasure for myself, and it all turned to misery. I wanted to make my gain out of another’s loss—you remember?—it was like roulette—and the money burned into me. And I could not complain. It was as if I had prayed that another should lose and I should win. And I had won, I knew it all—I knew I was guilty. When we were on the sea, and I lay awake at night in the cabin, I sometimes felt that everything I had done lay open without excuse—nothing was hidden—how could anything be known to me only?—it was not my own knowledge, it was God’s that had entered into me, and even the stillness—everything held a punishment for me—everything but you. I always thought that you would not want me to be punished—you would have tried and helped me to be better. And only thinking of that helped me. You will not change—you will not want to punish me now?”

Deronda groaned, “God forbid!” but he sat motionless, as if movement would betray him into some wrong act—either too much comfort or too much recoil.

He dared not urge her again. He must let her mind go where it must. She left intervals unconsciously, not always distinguishing what she had spoken from what she had only seen inwardly. When she began again, it was as if she took up the thread from a different knot.

“That all made it so hard when I was forced to go in the boat. Because when I saw you it was an unexpected joy, and I thought I could tell you everything—about the locked-up drawer and what I had not told you before. And if I had told you, and knew it was in your mind, it would have less power over me. I hoped and trusted in that. For after all my struggles and my crying, the hatred and rage, the temptation that frightened me, the longing, the thirst for what I dreaded, always came back. And that disappointment—when I was quite shut out from speaking to you, and was driven to go in the boat—brought all the evil back, as if I had been locked in a prison with it and no escape. Oh, it seems so long ago now since I stepped into that boat! I could have given up everything in that moment, to have the forked lightning for a weapon to strike him dead.”

The fierceness she recalled crept into her undertone, like a knife’s cold edge in the voice. After a silence, she hurried on:

“If he were here again, what should I do? I cannot wish him here—and yet I cannot bear his dead face. I was a coward. I ought to have borne contempt. I ought to have gone away—gone and wandered like a beggar rather than stay to feel like a fiend. But turn where I would there was something I could not bear. Sometimes I thought he would kill me if I resisted his will. But now—his dead face is there, and I cannot bear it.”

Suddenly she loosed Deronda’s hand and sprang up, stretching her arms upward to their full length, and moaned as if the sound were torn from her body:

“I have been a cruel woman! What can I do but cry for help? I am sinking. Die—die—you are forsaken—go down, go down into darkness. Forsaken—no pity—I shall be forsaken.”

She dropped back into her chair and broke into sobs. For that moment Deronda had no place in her consciousness; she was alone with a vision that filled her. He was unmanned—more than he had been prepared for. Instead of his late experience having dulled him, it seemed to have made him more penetrable: her swift travel from bright rash girlhood into this agony of remorse—an agony he had watched grow while he stood helpless—pierced him the deeper because it came close upon another sad revelation of spiritual conflict. He rose, and as he watched that terrible outburst—made more awful by her instinct to keep the voice low, as if secrecy were still her only shelter—he felt that passionate pity which makes a man ready to renounce pleasure and live only for the stricken.

He turned his back and walked away a few paces, as if distance might give him a breath.

Presently there was stillness. Her mind opened to the fact that he had moved from her. When he turned and approached again, he saw her face lifted toward him, eyes dilated, lips parted: timid, forlorn beseeching—too timid to frame itself into words while he remained aloof. Was she forsaken—now—already? His eyes met hers fully for the first time since her first low question. His look, intense with mournfulness, seemed to say: I know it, and therefore I will not forsake you. He sat down beside her again, in the same attitude, without turning his face toward her and without taking her hand.

Once more she was pierced—not only by fear for herself, but by a compunction that reached beyond it. In a tone of loving regret she said, “I make you very unhappy.”

Deronda gave an indistinct “Oh,” shrinking together, shifting as if the words struck him physically. Then he gathered firmness enough to answer clearly: “There is no question of being happy or unhappy. What I most desire at this moment is what will most help you. Tell me all you feel it a relief to tell.”

Devotion lay in the words; yet they widened a spiritual distance between them—compassion that seemed to regard her from a height. She felt it, and speaking grew harder. An impulse rose in her to humble herself more, to kneel before him; but something in her—pride, fear, the strange checks of her mixed consciousness—held her motionless and silent.

Her stillness made him say at last, “Perhaps you are too weary. Shall I go away, and come again whenever you wish it?”

“No, no,” she said quickly, dread of his leaving bringing back her power. She leaned a little nearer, and her low-toned eagerness returned. “I want to tell you what it was that came over me in that boat. I was full of rage at being obliged to go—full of rage—and I could do nothing but sit there like a galley slave. And then we got away—out of the port—into the deep—and everything was still—and we never looked at each other, only he spoke to order me—and the very light about me seemed to hold me a prisoner and force me to sit as I did. It came over me that when I was a child I used to fancy sailing away into a world where people were not forced to live with any one they did not like—I did not like my father-in-law to come home. And now, I thought, just the opposite had come to me. I had stepped into a boat, and my life was a sailing and sailing away—gliding on and no help—always into solitude with him, away from deliverance. And because I felt more helpless than ever, my thoughts went out over worse things—I longed for worse things—I had cruel wishes—I fancied impossible ways of—I did not want to die myself; I was afraid of our being drowned together. If it had been any use I should have prayed—I should have prayed that something might befall him. I should have prayed that he might sink out of my sight and leave me alone. I knew no way of killing him there, but I did, I did kill him in my thoughts.”

She fell silent, overwhelmed by what no words could truly carry. The sea’s distant sound seemed to swell in the pause, a vast, indifferent ear.

“But yet, all the while I felt that I was getting more wicked. And what had been with me so much, came to me just then—what you once said—about dreading to increase my wrong-doing and my remorse—I should hope for nothing then. It was all like a writing of fire within me. Getting wicked was misery—being shut out forever from knowing what you—what better lives were. That had always been coming back to me then—but yet with a despair—a feeling that it was no use—evil wishes were too strong. I remember then letting go the tiller and saying ‘God help me!’ But then I was forced to take it again and go on; and the evil longings, the evil prayers came again and blotted everything else dim, till, in the midst of them—I don’t know how it was—he was turning the sail—there was a gust—he was struck—I know nothing—I only know that I saw my wish outside me.”

Her voice grew quicker, and sank even lower, as if shame pressed her down to secrecy.

“I saw him sink, and my heart gave a leap as if it were going out of me. I think I did not move. I kept my hands tight. It was long enough for me to be glad, and yet to think it was no use—he would come up again. And he was come—farther off—the boat had moved. It was all like lightning. ‘The rope!’ he called out in a voice—not his own—I hear it now—and I stooped for the rope—I felt I must—I felt sure he could swim, and he would come back whether or not, and I dreaded him. That was in my mind—he would come back. But he was gone down again, and I had the rope in my hand—no, there he was again—his face above the water—and he cried again—and I held my hand, and my heart said, ‘Die!’—and he sank; and I felt ‘It is done—I am wicked, I am lost!’—and I had the rope in my hand—I don’t know what I thought—I was leaping away from myself—I would have saved him then. I was leaping from my crime, and there it was—close to me as I fell—there was the dead face—dead, dead. It can never be altered. That was what happened. That was what I did. You know it all. It can never be altered.”
