News came on a thin, wind-scraped morning, when the street outside sounded like a broom dragged over cobbles and the canal carried its small, constant lapping: Margaret van Eyck was dead, and already laid in earth.

It did not arrive with ceremony—only with the plain, official voice of a messenger, and then, later, the rustle of paper: a will made the year before. After funeral charges, and certain keepsakes to Reicht Heynes, the whole of Margaret van Eyck’s property was left to her dear daughter, Margaret Brandt, with one request folded like a last stitch in a seam: keep Reicht in the house, so long as she remained unmarried.

So Margaret inherited a furnished house, and pictures and studies that would one day feed the hungers of collectors the way bread feeds a family. But there was one painting she prized above all the rest, as if it were a loaf still warm from the oven, as if it could be broken and shared.

It was a betrothal.

The man’s face held the grave, braced solemnity of a vow; the woman’s, a demure composure that seemed almost to listen. Margaret van Eyck had painted her almost wholly; the rest Jan had touched—his hand in the accessories, in the hard bright truth of a jewel, in the sheen of cloth, in the tiny, obedient reflections that make a room feel inhabited. It was finished with such patient cunning that you could nearly hear the hush of witnesses in it, the faint creak of a bench, the soft tick of a ring against a cup.

Margaret Brandt sent word to Reicht: stay in the house until she herself could bear to step across its threshold and not be met by the face and voice that used to meet her there. And—above all—take especial care of the picture “in the little cubboord”: the diptych.

Not many days after, Luke Peterson came home.

He came in as men come back from roads—boots loud on boards, skin wind-chapped, and the whole body carrying the remembered clatter of other towns’ bells. He heard, almost at once, that Gerard was a monk.

Joy cracked in him like a nut under a hammer. He hurried to Margaret as if the words burned his mouth.

“Heed, mistress,” he burst out. “If he cannot marry you, I can.”

“You?” said Margaret, staring. “Why, I have seen him.”

“But he is a friar.”

“He was my husband, and my boy’s father, long ere he was a friar. And I have seen him—I've seen him.”

Luke stood bewildered, as if she had spoken in a tongue he could not parse. Then, with the stubbornness of a man who trusts ink and seals more than a woman’s heart, he said, “I'll tell you what: I have got a cousin a lawyer. I'll go and ask him whether you are married or single.”

“Nay. I shall ask my own heart, not a lawyer. So that is your regard for me—to go making me the town talk, oh, fie!”

“That is done already without a word from me.”

“But not by such as seek my respect. And if you do it, never come nigh me again.”

“Ay,” said Luke, and the sound of his breath was like a bellows that has been pulled and let go, “you are like a dove to all the rest; but you are a hardhearted tyrant to me.”

“’Tis your own fault, dear Luke, for wooing me. That is what lets me from being as kind to you as I desire. Luke, my bonny lad, listen. I am rich now; I can make my friends happy, though not myself. Look round the street—look round the parish. There is many a quean in it fairer than I twice told, and not spoiled with weeping. Look high, and take your choice. Speak you to the lass herself, and I'll speak to the mother; they shall not say thee nay. Take my word for't.”

“I see what ye mean,” said Luke, flushing to the ears. He swallowed once, hard. “But if I can't have your liking, I will none o’ your money. I was your servant when you were poor as I; and poorer. No. If you would liever be a friar’s leman than an honest man’s wife, you are not the woman I took you for. So part we withouten malice. Seek you your comfort on yon road, where never a she did find it yet; and for me, I’ll live and die a bachelor. Good even, mistress.”

“Farewell, dear Luke; and God forgive you for saying that to me.”

After he was gone, the house seemed to listen, as if a pot left too long on the hearth still whispered from heat though the fire was out. For some days Margaret dreaded—almost as much as she desired—the coming interview with Gerard. She told herself he would hang back. So should she, had she been he. Yet he would hear he was a father; and the pull of their child would draw him, like the smell of new bread draws a hungry man along a street.

“And if so be that meeting does not kill me,” she said to herself, pressing her boy’s small warm head to her cheek, “I feel I shall be better after it than I am now.”

But day after day went by, and no word. No footfall. No shadow across her doorway.

A cold suspicion began to crawl toward her mind, slow as frost creeping along a windowpane. What if his absence were chosen? What if he had gone to his fellow monks—men with smooth rules and hard hands—and they had told him never to see her more? The convent had already shown itself as merciless to true lovers as the grave itself.

At that thought, something in her went numb, as if life had been steeped too long in winter water.

And indignation—hot, sudden—mingled with grief.

“Can he have ever loved me?” she whispered aloud once, in the kitchen where Reicht had been rubbing a pan with sand and ash until it shone. “To run from me and his boy without a word! Why, this poor Luke thinks more of me than he does.”

She was in that fevered state when Giles came roaring in, his voice filling the room the way a storm fills a sail.

“I’ve hit the clout,” he bellowed. “Our Gerard is vicar of Gouda.”

A very brief sketch of the dwarf’s rise will serve; for the thing that made him dangerous at court was the same thing that made him useful in a kitchen: a sharp eye, a sharper tongue, and hands that could do work quickly while other folk were still talking about it.

Some months before he went to court his wit had budded sudden, like yeast taking hold. He dated the change from a certain eighth of June, when he had been swinging by one hand along the week’s washing on a tight rope in the drying ground; something went crack inside his head, and—lo—his thoughts came loose as birds from a cage. At court, shrewdness and blunt speech, coupled with a voice that could drown a trumpet and a body small enough to slip away from consequences, made him a Power. Without the small body, his great voice and his impudence might have led him to the gallows.

The young Duchess of Burgundy, and Marie the heiress apparent, petted him as great ladies have petted dwarfs in all ages, feeding him sweetmeats and laughter and using him, too, as a kind of living bell to ring when their rooms grew dull. And the court poet—paid to turn common life into sugared paste—took Giles’s rough sayings and melted butter by the six-foot rule, pouring it over them until they ran long and soft and shining, fit to serve to fine mouths. A letter even went to Eli, grandly titled with the princess’s name and the dwarf’s complaint and the poet’s elaborate crawling.

But Giles’s true climax came by a mixture of mind and muscle.

The day before a great court joust, he challenged the Duke’s giant to a trial of strength. Even grave men smiled; expectation hummed like a lute string.

Giles had a lofty pole planted. At the hour, he went up it like a squirrel, and by strength of arm made his body into a right angle and held it there—stiff as a spit, steady as a pot-hook. Then he slid down so fast that the high and puissant princess squeaked and hid her face in her hands, fearing the end of her pocket-Hercules.

The giant managed perhaps ten feet, then looked ruefully up and down and climbed down sweating to argue.

“It was not the dwarf’s greater strength,” he panted, “but his smaller body.”

The company took that excuse with loud derision: in their eyes, the fact was what mattered. The dwarf mounted; the giant excused.

“Come,” said Giles, “an ye go to that, I’ll wrestle ye, my lad—if so be you will let me blindfold your eyne.”

The giant, smarting, agreed.

“Madam,” said Giles, “see you yon blind Samson? At a signal from me he shall make me a low obeisance, and unbonnet to me.”

“How may that be, being blinded?” asked a maid of honour.

“I’ll wager on Giles for one,” said the princess.

“That is my affair,” said Giles; and when wagers were laid, he stepped in and struck the giant clean in the bread-basket. The man doubled—obeisance made—and his bonnet tumbled off.

The room screamed with delight. Giles ran. The giant followed as soon as he could breathe and tear away the bandage, but Giles had prepared a little door in the wall—painted so cunningly it looked like solid stone—which would pass a dwarf and deny a giant. He flung it wide and shot through, leaving behind on the back of the trick door a posy written large:

Long limbs, big body, panting wit  
By wee and wise is bet and bit

After that, they could not ignore him.

Now he stood before Margaret, stout with triumph, and seeing she would not swallow the good news whole, he began to tell his tale.

“When the princess sent for me to her bedchamber as of custom, to keep her out of languor, I came not mirthful nor full of country dicts, as is my wont, but dull as lead.

“‘Why, what aileth thee?’ quo’ she. ‘Art sick?’

“‘At heart,’ quo’ I.

“‘Alas, he is in love,’ quo’ she; whereat five brazen hussies—maids of honour, they call them—did giggle loud.

“‘Not so mad as that,’ said I, ‘seeing what I see at court of women folk.’

“‘There, ladies,’ quo’ the princess, ‘best let him a be. ’Tis a liberal mannikin, and still giveth more than he taketh of saucy words.’

“‘In all sadness,’ quo’ she, ‘what is the matter?’

“I told her I was meditating, and what perplexed me was: other folk can now and then keep their word, but princes never.

“‘Heyday,’ says she, ‘thy shafts fly high this morn.’

“I told her, ‘Ay, for they hit the Truth.’

“She said I was as keen as keen; but it became not me to put riddles to her, nor her to answer them. ‘Stand aloof a bit, mesdames,’ said she, ‘and thou speak withouten fear.’ For she saw I was in sad earnest.

“I began to quake a bit; for mind ye, she can doff freedom and don dignity quicker than she can slip out of her dressing-gown into kirtle of state. But I made my voice soft as honey”—and here he shot Margaret a look, for he loved to be admired—“and I said, ‘Madam, one evening, a matter of five years agone, as ye sat with your mother, the Countess of Charolois, who is now in heaven, worse luck—you wi’ your lute, and she wi’ her tapestry—do ye mind there came into ye a fair youth with a letter from a painter body, one Margaret van Eyck?’

“She said she thought she did. ‘Was it not a tall youth, exceeding comely?’

“‘Ay, madam,’ said I. ‘He was my brother.’

“‘Your brother?’ says she, and did eye me all over.

“So I told her what passed between her and Gerard, and how she was for giving him a bishopric; but the good countess said, ‘Gently, Marie! he is too young;’ and with that they did both promise him a living. ‘Yet,’ said I, ‘he hath been a priest a long while, and no living. Hence my bile.’

“‘Alas!’ said she. ‘’Tis not by my good will; for all this thou hast said is sooth, and more. I do remember my dear mother said to me, “See thou to it if I be not here.”’ So she cried out, ‘Ay, dear mother, no word of thine shall ever fall to the ground.’

“I, seeing her so ripe, said quick, ‘Madam, the vicar of Gouda died last week.’ For when ye seek favours of the great, behoves ye know the very thing ye aim at.

“‘Then thy brother is vicar of Gouda,’ quo’ she, ‘so sure as I am heiress of Burgundy and the Netherlands. Nay, thank me not, good Giles, but my good mother. And I do thank thee for giving of me somewhat to do for her memory.’ And doesn’t she fall a-weeping for her mother? And doesn’t that set me off a-snivelling for my good brother that I love so dear—and to think that a poor little elf like me could yet speak in the ear of princes and make my beautiful brother vicar of Gouda.

“Eh, lass, it is a bonny place, and a bonny manse; hawthorn in every bush at spring-tide, and dog-roses and eglantine in every summer hedge. I know what the poor fool affects. Leave that to me.”

He had strutted at the beginning—parading his court triumph as if it were a roasted capon carried high through a hall. But he ended in Margaret’s arms. She could not contain herself; she caught the little body up and hugged him, and kissed him, blushing at her own abandon.

“Oh, Giles,” she cried, “I cannot keep my hands off thee, thy body it is so little, and thy heart so great. Thou art his true friend. Bless thee! bless thee! bless thee! Now we shall see him again. We have not set eyes on him since that terrible day.”

“Gramercy, but that is strange,” said Giles. “Maybe he is ashamed of having cursed those two vagabones, being our own flesh and blood—worse luck.”

“Think you that is why he hides?” said Margaret, clutching at the notion as if it were a handhold on a slippery bank.

“Ay—if he is hiding at all. However, I’ll cry him by bellman.”

“Nay, that might much offend him.”

“What care I?” cried Giles. “Is Gouda to go vicarless and the manse in nettles?”

And, to Margaret’s secret satisfaction, Giles had the new vicar cried in Rotterdam and in the neighbouring towns: the bellman’s call rolling out over markets and quays, mixing with the cries of fishwives, the clop of hooves, the slap of oars, and the tolling of church bells that shaped the day’s hours like a cook’s rhythm shapes a meal.

Giles persuaded her that in a day or two Gerard would hear, and come to his benefice.

Margaret went to look at the manse. She walked its rooms slowly, hearing how each wall returned her own breath. She imagined the place warmed: a pot set to simmer; herbs hung to dry; clean rushes underfoot; a loaf cooling; the small domestic sounds that are kinder than any sermon—the knife on board, the kettle’s first singing, the child’s patter. She thought how comfortable it might be made for him, and how dearly she should love to do it.

But the days rolled on, and Gerard came neither to Rotterdam nor to Gouda.

Giles grew mortified, as if he had promised a feast and served an empty dish. Margaret grew indignant—and then, beneath indignation, wretched with a wretchedness that made food tasteless and mornings heavy.

“Thinking me dead, he comes home,” she told herself, “and now, because I am alive, he goes back to Italy—for that is where he has gone.”

Joan advised her, as women do when they have no power but counsel, “Consult the hermit of Gouda.”

“Why, sure he is dead by this time.”

“Yon one, belike. But the cave is never long void; Gouda ne’er wants a hermit.”

Margaret would not go again on such an errand. “What can he know, shut up in a cave? Less than I, belike. Gerard hath gone back t’ Italy. He hates me for not being dead.”

Presently a man from Tergou came in with a word from Catherine: Ghysbrecht van Swieten had seen Gerard later than any one else.

On that, Margaret resolved to go to the house and goods left her, and to bring Reicht Heynes home to Rotterdam. And as might be guessed, her feet carried her first to Ghysbrecht’s.

She found him in his garden, seated in a chair with wheels, diminished by illness, yet still holding himself as a man used to rule. The garden was full of low sounds: bees in the herbs, a spade set down, a sparrow’s sharp note. He greeted her feebly but with cordiality. When she asked if it were true he had seen Gerard since the fifth of August, he said, “Gerard no more, but Friar Clement. Ay, I saw him; and blessed be the day he entered my house.”

And then, in his own words, he told her.

He told how the friar had come to Tergou with the missing deed in his bosom, meaning to make him disgorge her land by force of law. Yet finding him disposed toward penitence, the friar had turned aside from vengeance to mercy and worked upon his soul instead.

“Was not this a saint,” said the burgomaster hoarsely, “who came to right thee, but must needs save his enemy’s soul in the doing it?”

When Margaret asked if he had recognized him, he shook his head. “I ne’er suspected such a thing. ’Twas only when he had been three days with me that he revealed himself. Listen while I speak my shame and his praise.

“I said to him, ‘The land is gone home, and my stomach feels lighter; but there is another fault that clingeth to me still.’ Then told I him of the letter I had writ at request of his brethren—I whose place it was to check them. Said I, ‘Yon letter was writ to part two lovers, and the devil aiding, it hath done the foul work. Land and houses I can give back, but yon mischief is done for ever.’

“‘Nay,’ quoth he, ‘not for ever, but for life. Repent it then while thou livest.’

“‘I shall,’ said I, ‘but how can God forgive it? I would not, were I He.’

“‘Yet will He certainly forgive it,’ quoth he; ‘for He is ten times more forgiving than I am, and I forgive thee.’

“I stared at him; and then he said soft, but quavering like, ‘Ghysbrecht, look at me closer. I am Gerard, the son of Eli.’

“And I looked, and looked, and at last—lo—it was Gerard. Verily I had fallen at his feet with shame and contrition, but he would not suffer me. He said it became not mine years and his, for a particular fault. ‘I say not I forgive thee without a struggle,’ said he, ‘not being a saint. But these three days thou hast spent in penitence, I have worn under thy roof in prayer; and I do forgive thee.’ Those were his very words.”

Tears began to run down Margaret’s face as she heard it—tears hot with love and pain and pride. The old man’s voice broke as he continued.

“And even with that he bade me farewell. ‘My work here is done now,’ said he. I had not the heart to stay him; for let him forgive me ever so, the sight of me must be wormwood to him. He left me in peace, and may a dying man’s blessing wait on him, go where he will. Oh, girl, when I think of his wrongs, and thine, and how he hath avenged himself by saving this stained soul of mine—my heart is broken with remorse, and these old eyes shed tears by night and day.”

“Ghysbrecht,” said Margaret, weeping, “since he hath forgiven thee, I forgive thee too. What is done, is done; and thou hast let me know this day that which I had walked the world to hear. But oh, burgomaster, thou art an understanding man—now help a poor woman, which hath forgiven thee her misery.”

She told him all that had befallen: her sight of Gerard, her fear, her waiting, and how the living could not be held open for ever. “He bids fair to lose that,” she said, “as well as break all our hearts.”

“Call my servant,” cried the burgomaster, and for a moment vigour flared in him like a coal stirred under ash.

A table was brought, and writing tools. He dictated letters to burgomasters in all the chief towns of Holland, and one to a Prussian authority—his friend. His clerk wrote, and Margaret wrote too, her hand moving fast, ink scratching like mice in a wall. Ghysbrecht signed each with the steadiness of a man spending his last authority like coin.

“There,” said he. “The matter shall be despatched throughout Holland by trusty couriers, and as far as Basle in Switzerland; and fear not, but we will soon have the vicar of Gouda to his village.”

Margaret went home with fresh hope, and with a new shame at having accused Gerard in her heart.

“I value my wealth now,” she told Joan that night, measuring out flour for the morning loaf with hands that trembled less than they had. For money, used well, could buy messengers and time and chances. It could buy, perhaps, a path back to a man lost in the world’s wide din.

And she resolved—hard as it was—to blame his conduct no more until she should hear, from his own lips, his reason.

Not long after her return from Tergou, a fresh disaster fell—quiet at first, like a knife slid into a sheath, and then loud with consequence.

Catherine, it appeared, had secret meetings with the black sheep the very day after they were expelled. Cornelis followed her to Tergou and lived there on secret contributions; but Sybrandt chose to remain in Rotterdam.

Before Catherine left, she asked Margaret for the loan of two gold angels. “For,” said she, “all mine are spent.”

Margaret was glad to lend them, glad even to give. But the words were scarce out when she caught a look—regret and distress—crossing Catherine’s face, and she saw, in a flash, where the money would go. Still she gave it, and then shut herself up with her boy, holding him close, smelling the clean milk-breath of him and the faint sourness of flour on her own fingers, as if small, homely scents could keep sorrow from crossing the threshold.

The money was meant to last Sybrandt until his mother could contrive an excuse to visit Rotterdam again, and then bring him more from her industrious savings.

But Sybrandt, with gold in his pocket, believed it inexhaustible. Freed from restraint, he sank into the life of a sot, drinking like a man trying to drown a voice inside him. One afternoon, in a drunken frolic, he climbed onto the stable roof at the inn where he caroused, and began to walk along it—a trick he had done often sober.

Now his unsteady brain made his legs unsteady. He rolled, slid, and fell with a loud thwack onto a horizontal paling, where he hung a moment in a half-circle like a broken doll, then toppled and lay silent in the yard amid roars of laughter. When they went to pick him up he could not stand, but kept giggling as they hauled him.

So they staggered and shouted down the street with him, carrying him—at risk of another fall—to the shop in the Hoog Straet, for he had babbled out his own shame and his lodging-place to all.

As soon as he saw Margaret, he hiccupped, “Here is the doctor that cures all hurts—a bonny lass.” He said he bore her no malice, though he paid her a visit sore against his will. “Wherefore, prithee send away these drunkards, and let you and me have t’other glass, to drown all unkindness.”

Margaret went pale and then red, the way a hearth flares and dies when damp wood is thrown on. His insolence struck her like smoke in the eyes. But one of the men whispered what had happened, and she saw something streaky and strange in Sybrandt’s face that was not mere drink.

“And he cannot stand up, say you?”

“A couldn’t just now. Try, comrade! Be a man now!”

“I am a better man than thou,” roared Sybrandt. “I’ll stand up and fight ye all for a crown.”

He heaved himself upright—and instantly folded, rolling into his companion’s arms with a groan that emptied the laughter from the room like a bucket of water on fire. Then he began to curse them, declaring they had stolen his legs away.

“He can feel nothing below the waist,” said one, suddenly sobered.

“Alas, poor wretch,” said Margaret, and her voice changed, as if pity were a new language forcing itself through her mouth.

She turned on the men, grave as judgment. “Leave him here. And if you have brought him to this, go on your knees; for you have spoiled him for life. He will never walk again. His back is broken.”

Sybrandt heard. The foolish drunken look fled; anguish took its place, sharp and naked. “The curse,” he groaned. “The curse!”

Margaret and Reicht Heynes carried him carefully and laid him on the softest bed, moving him as gently as one lifts a scalded child from a pot too near the fire.

“I must do as he would do,” Margaret whispered to Reicht, her voice low. “He was kind to Ghysbrecht.”

Her opinion proved true. Sybrandt’s spine was fatally injured; he lay helpless, groaning, fed and tended by the very woman he had most wronged.

Word was sent to Tergou, and Catherine came over.

The blow shattered something in her. She accused herself as the cause, as if her own hands had pushed him from the roof.

“Oh, false wife; oh, weak mother,” she cried, “I am rightly punished for my treason to my poor Eli.”

She sat for hours by Sybrandt’s bedside rocking herself in silence, the way women rock cradles when there is nothing else to do; only now it was grief she rocked, not a child. She was never quite herself again. From that hour the first grey hairs showed in her head, thin as ash on a black pot.

Sybrandt’s cry became a constant moan for Gerard. He would whine to Margaret like a suffering hound, “Oh, sweet Margaret—oh, bonny Margaret—for our Lady’s sake find Gerard, and bid him take his curse off me. Thou art gentle, thou art good; thou wilt entreat for me, and he will refuse thee nought.”

Catherine shared the belief that Gerard could cure him; she added her entreaties, desperate as prayer whispered over a feverish child.

Margaret hardly needed urging. Ghysbrecht’s letters and agents had failed; now she employed her own, and spent money like water, buying questions, buying rides, buying ears in taverns and at ferry-steps, buying the chance that a name spoken in the right dialect might unlock another man’s memory.

Among these agents, poor Luke enrolled himself.

She met him one day looking very thin, as if love had eaten him as sickness eats. She spoke to him compassionately. At that he began to blubber, saying he was more miserable than ever; he would like to be good friends again upon almost any terms.

“Dear heart,” said Margaret sorrowfully, “why can you not say to yourself: now I am her little brother, and she is my old, married sister, worn down with care? Say so, and I will indulge thee, and pet thee, and make thee happier than a prince.”

“Well, I will,” said Luke savagely, wiping his nose on his sleeve, “sooner than keep away from you altogether. But above all, give me something to do. Perchance I may have better luck this time.”

“Get me my marriage lines,” said Margaret; and in a moment the light went out of her face, leaving it sad and set.

“That is as much as to say, get me him—for where they are, he is.”

“Not so. He may refuse to come nigh me; but certes he will not deny a poor woman, who loved him once, her lines of betrothal. How can she go without them into any honest man’s house?”

“I’ll get them you if they are in Holland,” said Luke.

“They are as like to be in Rome,” replied Margaret.

“Let us begin with Holland,” said Luke, prudent even in passion.

The slave of love took money from the soft tyrant he could not resist and wandered hither and thither, coopering and carpentering for his bread, listening for Gerard’s name in the music of marketplaces—where every town had its own cadence, its own vowels and clipped endings that carried old memory like salt carries meat.

“I can’t be worse if I find the vagabone,” he told himself, “and I may be a hantle better.”

Months rolled on.

Sybrandt improved in spirit but not in body; he became Margaret’s pensioner for life, lying like a living reproach and a living lesson under her roof. A long-expected sorrow fell upon poor Catherine, bowing her further: she lost her fine, hearty, bustling way and went no more about the house singing. Her nerves shook; she lived in dread that some terrible misfortune might fall on Cornelis too. The curse, she believed, lay on him as well as Sybrandt.

She begged Eli—sturdy but not unkind—that if she had been a faithful partner all these years he would take Cornelis back into his house again, and let her live awhile at Rotterdam.

“I have good daughters here,” she said. “But Margaret is so tender and thoughtful, and the little Gerard—he is my joy. He grows liker his father every day, and his prattle cheers my heavy heart. I do love children.”

And Eli consented, sorrowfully, as a man consents to a necessary loss.

Meanwhile the people of Gouda petitioned the duke for a vicar—a real vicar.

“Ours cometh never nigh us,” they complained. “This six months past. Our children die unchristened, and our folk unburied, except by some chance comer.”

Giles’s influence baffled this just complaint once; but a second petition was prepared. And now even Giles, for all his roaring voice and court cunning, could give Margaret little hope the present state could be held one day longer.
