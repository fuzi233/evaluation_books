To claim that Daniel Deronda was merely a romantic figure would be to misunderstand the very core of his nature. Beneath his composed, slightly restrained demeanor burned a fire of intense feeling, allowing him to discover profound poetry and drama within the mundane currents of daily existence. Perhaps such beauty is as abundant today as it ever was, obscured only by those indifferent souls who, regardless of the era, dismiss wonder as a foolish error. Poetry does not require isolation; it thrives alongside microscopes and rattles inside train cars, vanishing only when the human spirit fails to perceive it. Why should the vast machinery of the universe, from the furthest stars down to the nurturing embrace of a mother, fail to inspire awe and tenderness in a heart devoid of fellowship? For Deronda, the chance encounter with Mirah stirred emotions comparable to the legendary quests of Orestes or Rinaldo. He spent half the night awake, reliving the moments since he first spotted her by the riverbank with a vividness that only deep emotion can grant.

When he attempted to read to quiet this inner turmoil, the printed words dissolved into a transparent lattice through which he still saw and heard every detail of the recent hours. He perceived not just what happened, but the infinite possibilities of what might have been and what could yet come, fueled by the warm, trembling energy of hope and fear. A specific resonance in his own life caused Mirah's desperate search for her mother to grip his imagination with unusual force. His initial instinct was pure sympathy: if missing persons existed in London, there were methods to locate them, akin to scientific inquiry, requiring only the right tools to be activated. Yet, the complex shadows of his own family history soon colored his concern for Mirah. The desire to uncover his own mother was perpetually shadowed by dread; projecting this onto Mirah, he feared that finding the mother and brother from whom she had been torn away as a child might prove to be a catastrophe. In the boat, she had described them as "good," but that assessment likely stemmed from her innocent ignorance and longing. Twelve years were a lifetime capable of altering character drastically.

Despite his natural inclination to champion the oppressed, Deronda had never felt a practical draw toward the Jewish community. The facts he knew of them—whether they appeared in fine clothes or hid in alleyways—were mostly repellent. He assumed that educated Jews had abandoned their faith to assimilate into their host nations. While scorn directed at a specific individual might evoke his deepest empathy, the blanket hatred cast upon an entire race could sometimes justify the punishment of a single guilty member. Even those unfamiliar with theology, like the Caribs, might logically associate theft with certain moral frameworks based on observation. Deronda could not avoid hearing the ugly stories surrounding Jewish customs and trades. Though he protested against severing the past from the present, he rarely ventured beyond the general notion that Jews possessed the accumulated virtues and vices of a long-suffering people. Now, however, Mirah's urgent longing forced him to scrutinize the details, conjuring disturbing images of what it might mean to meet this middle-aged woman and her son.

He acknowledged the exquisite refinement and charm of Mirah herself, which suggested nobility in her kin, but he decided to wait for more information. Perhaps Mrs. Meyrick could offer clues directly from Mirah. Her voice, her accent, her very appearance—all wrapped in a purity that seemed sacred—made him recoil even further from associating her with anything base or corrupt. Yet, these comforting words are often mere smoke used to obscure harsh realities; true thought operates differently. Deronda's mind raced through grim scenarios: being led by an official guide into a squalid street, entering a dark doorway to see a shrewd, unkempt woman haggling over a starving girl's last dress; or finding himself in a slicker district, breathing the air around a young Jew eager to please gentlemen and unashamed of shady dealings. He excused himself from indulging in such insults, noting that his mind did not naturally equate prophets with advertisements, but currently, he was driven by terror. If Mirah's parents had been Christian, his fears would simply be broader; instead, his dread was rooted in the specific stigma attached to unknown parentage, a connection that felt tragically reasonable in both his own case and hers.

So, what was to be done for Mirah? She required shelter and protection above all else. Every chivalrous impulse within him demanded that he secure the interest of others immediately, believing that sharing her burden was the highest fulfillment of his duty. He felt no right to provide for her completely, despite his ability to do so; the depth of her impact on him made him wish she understood herself as independent of him. Vague, fantastical visions of the future lingered, leaving behind an anxiety sharper than any logical reason. He feared that those observing his actions closely must know the full history of his relationship with Mirah from the start. He had vowed never to let the truth become a disgrace to another person, especially since many truths are not of our own making. He lived in constant terror of breaking this promise, falling into the cynical trap of explaining the world solely through one's own behavior.

At one point, he resolved to confess the entire adventure to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger at breakfast the following morning. However, the possibility that his next visit to Mrs. Meyrick might yield new revelations checked this impulse. Ultimately, he fell asleep deciding to wait until he had spoken with Mrs. Meyrick before taking any further steps.
