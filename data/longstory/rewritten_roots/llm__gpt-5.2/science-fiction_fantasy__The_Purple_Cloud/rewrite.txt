Toward the end of May, in this year 1900, there came to the man whose name stands on the title-page a letter and a packet of papers so strange that, even now, the touch of them seems to leave a grit upon the fingertips—graphite dust and the uneasy sense of other people’s breath caught in paper.

They were sent by a friend of mine whose name I have no reason to veil: Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, M.A. (Oxon.), F.R.C.P.—a Norfolk physician, exact of habit, and, when he chose, tender in ways that were almost brusque. For two years I had lived chiefly in France; and because Browne held to his country rounds, and because we were both scandalously indolent correspondents, our friendship had subsisted in silence with only two brief notes to show for it. Then, at last, this packet arrived.

It consisted of four note-books, each crowded from cover to cover with Pitman shorthand, those giddy hookings and strokes that, at a glance, resemble startled swarms of midges hovering above a summer pan. They were pencilled with little care for the niceties of thick and thin, with vowels miserly withheld; to decipher them has been like trying to cook from a recipe written in half-remembered dialect—something essential omitted, and the heat of one’s own conjecture forced to supply the missing measure. The letter itself was also in shorthand, and I print it here with the second of the note-books I have made out (it was marked “III.”). In perhaps five places I have been obliged to crutch a sentence with my best guess; in two, the characters were so perversely mystical that I abandoned them with a headache. None of this, I think, touches the current of the narrative.

Browne wrote:

“DEAR OLD SHIEL,—I have just been lying thinking of you, and wishing you were here to give one a last squeeze of the hand before I—‘go’: for, by all appearance, ‘going’ I am.

“Four days ago I began with a soreness in the throat; passing old Johnson’s surgery at Selbridge I went in and asked him to look at me. He muttered ‘membranous laryngitis,’ which made me smile; but by the time I reached home I was hoarse, and not smiling. Before night I had dyspnoea and laryngeal stridor. I telegraphed at once to London for Morgan, and between him and Johnson they have been opening my trachea, and burning my inside with chromic acid and the galvanic cautery.

“The difficulty as to breathing has subsided, and it is wonderful how little I suffer; but I am much too old a hand not to know what’s what. The bronchi are involved—too far involved—and as a matter of absolute fact there isn’t any hope. Morgan is still, I believe, fondly dwelling on the possibility of adding me to his successful-tracheotomy statistics; but prognosis was always my strong point, and I say No. The very small consolation of my death will be the beating of a specialist in his own line.

“So we shall see.

“I have been arranging some affairs this morning, and remembered these notebooks. I meant to send them months ago; but my habit of putting things off, and the fact that the lady was alive from whom I took down the words, prevented me. Now she is dead, and as a literary man, and a student of life, you should be interested—if you can manage to read them. You may even find them valuable.

“I am under a little morphia at present, propped up in a comfortable languor; and as I can write without much effort I will set down in the old Pitman something about her.

“Her name was Miss Mary Wilson. She was about thirty when I met her, forty-five when she died, and I knew her intimately all those fifteen years. Do you know anything of the philosophy of the hypnotic trance? That was the relation between us—hypnotist and subject. She had been under another man before my time, but no one was ever so successful with her as I.

“She suffered from tic douloureux of the fifth nerve. Most of her teeth had been drawn before I saw her, and an attempt made to wrench out the nerve on the left side by external incision. It made no difference. All the clocks in hell tick-tacked in that poor woman’s jaw; and it was the mercy of Providence she ever came across me. My organisation was found to have almost complete, and quite easy, control over hers; and with a few passes I could expel her Legion.

“You never saw anyone so singular in appearance as my friend Miss Wilson. Medicine-man as I am, I could not behold her suddenly without a shock—she suggested what we call ‘the other world,’ with some odour of the worm about her, as though she were rather ghost than woman. I cannot give you the why except in dry detail: lofty brow, meagre lips, pointed chin, ashen cheeks. She was tall and deplorably emaciated, her whole skeleton, save the thigh-bones, visible. Her eyes were the bluish hue of cigarette smoke and held the strangest feeble unearthly gaze; and at thirty-five her paltry wisp of hair was quite white.

“She was well-to-do, and lived alone in old Wooding Manor-house, five miles from Ash Thomas. As you know, I was ‘beginning’ in those parts, and soon took up my residence at the manor. She insisted I should devote myself to her alone; and that one patient constituted the most lucrative practice I ever had.

“I quickly found that in trance Miss Wilson possessed very remarkable powers—remarkable not because peculiar to herself in kind, but because so constant, reliable, exact, and far-reaching in degree. The veriest fledgling in psychical science will now discourse finically about the reporting powers of the mind in trance, as though it were new; yet every old crone has known it since the Middle Ages, and, I suppose, long before. The Psychical Research Society, after endless investigation, admits the fact as scientific; but in establishing it beyond doubt they have not explained it. They reveal nothing that many of us knew already with absolute assurance.

“But to return. Her powers were remarkable in quantity. I believe, in general, trance powers manifest more particularly with regard to space than time: the spirit roams in the present, travels over a plain; it does not usually attract observers by great ascents or descents. Yet Miss Wilson travelled in every direction, easily in all but one—north and south, up and down, in the past, the present, and the future.

“I discovered this gradually. In trance she would emit a stream of sounds—I can hardly call it speech, so murmurous yet guttural, mixed with puffy breath-sounds at the languid lips. The state came with intense contraction of the pupils, absence of knee-jerk, considerable rigor, and a rapt and arrant expression. I grew used to sitting long hours at her bed-side, fascinated, trying to catch the import of that opiate visionary language that came puffing and fluttering in deliberate monotone from her lips. Gradually my ear learned to detect the words; the veil was rent for me also; and I could follow somewhat the course of her wandering spirit.

“At the end of six months I heard her repeat words familiar to me:

“‘Such were the arts by which the Romans extended their conquests, and attained the palm of victory; and the concurring testimony of different authors enables us to describe them with precision—’

“I was startled. They are from Gibbon’s Decline and Fall, which I guessed she had never read. I said sternly: ‘Where are you?’ She replied: ‘Us are in a room, eight hundred and eleven miles above. A man is writing. Us are reading.’

“Two things I tell you. First: in trance she never spoke of herself as ‘I,’ nor even ‘we,’ but always in the objective way as ‘us’: ‘us are,’ ‘us will,’ ‘us went’—though she was educated, and I don’t think ever lived in the West where ‘us’ is used so. Second: when she wandered in the past she always represented herself as ‘above’ (the earth?), and higher the further back; in describing present events she felt herself on the earth; and as regards the future she invariably declared ‘us’ were so many miles ‘within’ the earth.

“To her excursions into the future there appeared fixed limits. I cannot be sure; I only mean that despite my efforts she never went far in that direction. Three, four thousand ‘miles’ were common figures for her distance ‘above’; but her distance ‘within’ never went beyond sixty-three. Usually she would say twenty, twenty-five. In relation to the future she resembled a diver who, the deeper he strives, meets a more resistant pressure until, at no great depth, resistance becomes prohibition.

“I am afraid I cannot go on, though I had much to tell you. During fifteen years, off and on, I sat listening by her dim bed-side to her murmuring trances. At last my expert ear could detect the sense of her faintest sigh. I heard the Decline and Fall from beginning to end. Some reports were frivolous nonsense; over others I have hung in a horror of interest. Certainly I have heard amazing words proceed from the wan lips of Mary Wilson.

“Sometimes I could hitch her repeatedly to any scene or subject I chose by the exercise of my will; at others the flighty waywardness of her spirit baffled me—she resisted, she disobeyed—else I might have sent you not four note-books but twenty or forty. About the fifth year it struck me well to jot down her more connected utterances, since I know shorthand.

“The note-book marked ‘I.’ (which seems to me the most curious) belongs to the seventh year. Its history, like the others, is this: I heard her one afternoon murmuring in the intonation used when reading; the matter interested me; I asked where she was. She replied: ‘Us are forty-five miles within: us read, and another writes’; from which I concluded she was some fifteen to thirty years in the future, perusing an as yet unpublished work. After that, during some weeks, I managed to keep her to the same subject, and finally I fancy won pretty well the whole work. I believe you would find it striking, and hope you will be able to read my notes.

“But no more of Mary Wilson now. Rather let us think a little of A.L. Browne, F.R.C.P.—with a breathing-tube in his trachea, and Eternity under his pillow…”

Here his letter turns to matters that do not concern us.

Dr. Browne’s prognosis proved correct: he died two days after writing. I now give, without comment, my transcription of the shorthand book marked “III.” I remind the reader only that these words purport to be the substance of a book or document to be written—or to be motived, as Miss Wilson had it—in that Future which, no less than the Past, exists substantively in the Present though, like the Past, we do not see it. The title and division into paragraphs, &c., I have contrived for convenience.

Here begins the note-book marked “III.”

Well: memory seems to be getting rather impaired now—weak at the edges, like a sauce left too long on the hob until the first bright scent goes, and what remains is thickness without freshness. What, for instance, was the name of that parson who preached, just before the Boreal set out, about the wickedness of any further attempt to reach the North Pole? I have forgotten. Yet four years ago it was as familiar to me as my own.

Things before the voyage grow cloudy. I sit here in the loggia of this Cornish villa to write down some account of what has happened—God knows why, since no eye can ever read it—and at the very threshold I cannot recall that name.

He was a strange man, surely: a Scotchman from Ayrshire, big and gaunt, tawny-haired; he went about London in shough rough-spun clothes, a plaid flung from one shoulder. Once I saw him in Holborn with his wild stalk, frowning and muttering to himself, and even among the clatter of wheels and the metallic bark of modern streets his presence made a kind of silence, as when a storm presses down the birds and the city’s own din seems suddenly cheap.

No sooner had he opened his chapel—some little place, I think, in Fetter Lane—than it began to be crowded; and when later he moved to a larger establishment in Kensington, men came to hear him even from America and Australia, though this was not an age apt to kindle into enthusiasm over pulpit prophets. Yet he did wake the strong dark feelings that sleep in the heart. His eyes were singular and powerful; his voice ran from a whisper, gathering like snow-balls, and then crashed, as I have heard pack-ice in commotion far yonder in the North; his gestures were uncouth and gawky as some wild man’s from the primitive ages.

What was his name? Macintosh? Mackay? Yes—Mackay.

Mackay took offence at the new attempt to reach the Pole in the Boreal, and for three Sundays, as her preparations neared completion, stormed against it at Kensington.

The world’s excitement about the North Pole had then reached a pitch that can only be called fevered—though fever is too small and bodily a word for the strange ecstasy and unrest that prevailed. For the old abstract desire to know had been kindled to a thousandfold blaze by something concrete: money. Mammon had put his mouth to the trumpet. In the ten years before the Boreal expedition no fewer than twenty-seven attempts had set out and failed; and the secret spring of this new rage lay in the will of Mr. Charles P. Stickney of Chicago—king of faddists, and, by repute, the richest man who ever lived—who had died ten years earlier leaving one hundred and seventy-five million dollars to the man, of whatever nationality, who first reached the Pole.

The wording—“the man who first reached”—had loosed a long hot controversy on both sides of the Atlantic: did he mean the chief of the first successful expedition? Or the actual individual whose foot first touched the 90th degree? In the end the highest legal authority decided that the words must rule: it would be the person—whatever his station—whose foot first reached 90° N who had title to the fortune.

And so the public ferment rose to a kind of delirium. The daily progress of the Boreal’s fitting was discussed minutely in the newspapers; everyone became an authority; she was in every mouth a bet, a hope, a jest, or a sneer. It was felt, at last, that success was probable.

Thus Mackay had an audience acutely interested—if startled, if cynical. There was something lion-hearted in him: one man setting himself against the spirit of his age, one voice standing in the teeth of four hundred million bent the other way, telling them they were wrong, all wrong. People called him John the Baptist Redivivus, and indeed he suggested something of that sort. At the time he denounced the Boreal there was not a sovereign in Europe who—were it not for shame—would not have been glad of a subordinate post on board.

On the third Sunday night I was there in the Kensington chapel and heard him.

And the wild talk he talked. He seemed delirious with inspiration. People sat spell-bound while his prophesying voice ranged up and down through every thunder-modulation, from the hurrying mutter to the reverberant shock and climax. Those who came to scoff remained to wonder.

Put simply, he said this: that there was some Fate or Doom connected with the Poles of the earth in reference to the human race; that man’s continued failure, in spite of continual efforts, proved it super-abundantly; and that this failure was a lesson—and a warning—which the race disregarded at its peril.

The North Pole, he said, was not so very far away; the difficulties were not, on the face of them, so very great. Human ingenuity had achieved a thousand things a thousand times more difficult; yet after half-a-dozen well-planned efforts in the nineteenth century and thirty-one in the twentieth, man had never reached. Always he had been baulked—baulked—by some seeming chance, some restraining Hand. Herein lay the lesson, herein the warning.

Wonderfully—really wonderfully—like the Tree of Knowledge in Eden was that Pole: all the rest of earth open and offered, but that persistently veiled and forbidden. It was as when a father lays a hand upon his son: Not here, my child; wheresoever you will—but not here.

But, said he, human beings were free agents; they could stop their ears and harden their consciousness against Heaven’s whispers and warning signs. The time was now come, he believed, when man would find it absolutely in his power to stand on that 90th of latitude and plant an impious right foot on the head of the earth—just as it had been given into Adam’s power to stretch an impious right hand and pluck the Fruit. Yet—his voice now pealing into one long proclamation of awful augury—just as abuse of that power had been followed in the one case by catastrophe swift and universal, so in the other he warned the entire race to look thenceforth for nothing from God but a lowering sky and thundery weather.

His frantic earnestness, his authoritative voice, his savage gestures could not but work on all. As for me, I sat as though a messenger from Heaven addressed me.

But I believe I had not reached home before the whole impression had passed from me like water from a duck’s back. The Prophet in the twentieth century was not a success. John Baptist himself, camel-skin and all, would have met with tolerant shrugs. I dismissed Mackay with the thought: He is behind his age, I suppose.

But haven’t I thought differently of Mackay since, my God…

It was perhaps three weeks before that Sunday night at Kensington, before the loud-throated man with his prophecy and his thundery weather, that Clark came to see me—no summons of business, merely the easy social call of an acquaintance who believes the world will always make room for his plans.

By that time I had been established a year in Harley Street—No. II; and though I was not yet five-and-twenty, I had contrived, by a certain promptitude and polish, to gather a practice as select as any young physician in Europe might reasonably covet.

Select—but not large. I could keep my rooms in that correct order which soothes the rich—silver polished, coal scuttles filled, the very ticking of the clock made genteel—and I could move among men who spoke of rents and titles as lightly as of the weather. Yet now and again the quiet pinch came: the moment when one calculates, with a coldness that feels like shame, whether the month will end before the fees do. Just then I was saved—saved is not too big a word—by my book, *Applications of Science to the Arts*, which did well enough to keep the wolf from sniffing too near my door.

Clark, in his off-hand way, sat down as if he owned my chair, took in the room with the quick eye of a man who has watched pack-ice from a crow’s-nest, and said, almost laughing:

“Do you know what I dreamed about you last night, Adam Jeffson? I dreamed you were with us on the expedition.”

He must have seen me start. The truth was, that on that very night I too had dreamed it—so precisely that, for a second, his words had the clang of something repeated, not invented. But I said nothing of that. I answered, with a little stumble, as if my tongue had been iced:

“Who? I?—on the expedition? I would not go, if I were asked.”

“Oh, you would.”

“I wouldn’t. You forget I am about to be married.”

“Well, we needn’t discuss the point, as Peters is not going to die,” he returned. “Still—if anything did happen—you know it is you I should come straight to, Adam Jeffson.”

“Clark, you jest,” I said. “I know really very little of astronomy, or magnetic phenomena. Besides, I am about to be married—”

“But what about your botany, my friend? There’s what we should be wanting from you; and as for nautical astronomy—poh! A man with your scientific habit would pick it up in no time.”

“You talk as gravely as if it were a possibility,” I said, smiling as one smiles to shut a door. “Such a thought would never enter my head. There is, first of all, my fiancée—”

“Ah, the all-important Countess,” he said. “Well—but she, as far as I know the lady, would be the first to force you to go. The chance of stamping one’s foot on the North Pole does not occur every day, my son.”

“Talk of something else,” I said. “There is Peters.”

“Of course there is Peters. But believe me, the dream I had was so clear—”

“Let me alone with your dreams and your Poles!” I laughed.

Yes—I remember it. I laughed loud, as if laughter could drown the scrape of fate. Yet even then, in the secret room of me where I do not pretend, I knew that one of those crises was at hand which has made my life, from my youth, the strangest any earth-born creature ever lived. I knew it because of the two dreams; and I knew it because, when Clark went out and I was drawing on my gloves to go to see Clodagh, I heard distinctly the old Two Voices begin again their contention.

One said: Go not to see her now.
The other cried: Yes—go, go!

An ordinary reader, reading this, will call it metaphor—two impulses, two moods, the cheap dialectic of a hesitating man. Or he will suppose me disordered. But I mean what I say: I heard them with a nearness nearer than breathing, closer than hands and feet; not thoughts, but presences; not vague leanings, but commands that came with their own sound, their own pressure, as if the skull were a chamber in which two speakers stood.

It began when I was about seven. I was playing one summer evening in a pine-wood of my father’s—resin in the warm air, needles underfoot, the slow hush that pines make when the wind moves through them like a long, green breath. Half a mile away was a quarry cliff—thirty feet of blunt stone. As I played, it suddenly seemed as if someone said inside me: *Just take a walk toward the cliff*; and as if someone else whispered: *Don’t go that way at all.* Mere whispers then—thin as the squeak of a bat—but they swelled, as I grew, into cries of wrathful contention. I went toward the cliff. I fell. Some weeks later, when speech came back to me, I told my astonished mother that “someone had pushed me” over the edge, and that someone else “had caught me” at the bottom.

Soon after my eleventh birthday, lying in bed, I was struck by a thought as cold and certain as the touch of metal: that my life must be of importance to some unseen thing or things; that two Powers, hating one another, were continually after me—one wishing for some reason to kill me, the other for some reason to keep me alive; one driving me one way, the other dragging me back; that I was not like other boys, but marked for—something. Already I had those touches of mood, those primitive instincts, which I believe were as old as the first man who stepped; so that when I read in the Bible, “The Lord spake unto so-and-so, saying,” I never stumbled at the mechanism of the Voice. It did not seem absurd that man might once have had more ears than two; nor would I have been surprised to learn that I, in this late age, resembled in some poor degree those primeval ones.

Yet no creature—except perhaps my mother—ever guessed the truth I write here. I seemed the ordinary youth: pulling in my ‘Varsity eight, cramming for examinations, wasting hours in clubs, talking idly of women and money. When it came time to choose a profession, who could have suspected the conflict that transacted itself in me while my brain seemed indifferent? The brawling voices shouted: *Be a scientist—a doctor*; and the other: *Be a lawyer, an engineer, an artist—be anything but a doctor!*

A doctor I became. I went to what had grown into the greatest of medical schools—Cambridge. And there, in Trinity, in rooms in New Court where a certain set of us foregathered, I came across Scotland—a man with a view of the universe as odd, to others, as it was dreadful to me.

Scotland was a gentle soul—short, Roman-nosed, with that continual effort to straighten his neck and draw in his paunch, as if politeness were a physical strain. He had a passion for cats, for Sappho, for the Anthology; and he used to talk of “Black” and “White” Powers until the men made a joke of it, calling him “black-and-white-mystery-man.” One day, when someone spoke of “the black mystery of the universe,” Scotland interrupted, mild as milk but obstinate as fate:

“The black-and-white mystery.”

His doctrine—repeated until it became, for my companions, a tiresome refrain—was that the universe was being frantically contended for by two Powers: a White and a Black. The White was stronger, he said, but did not find the conditions of our planet favourable; the White had held the advantage up to the Middle Ages in Europe, and since then had been slowly, stubbornly giving way; and finally the Black would win—not everywhere perhaps, but here—and would carry off, if no other earth, at least this one, for his prize.

While others listened with tolerant smiles, I drank in his words with agony, sitting cynically among them. I knew too well what it was to be a battleground.

And now—after Clark’s visit—I was drawing on my gloves to go to Clodagh, when the Two Voices were as clear as if they spoke into my physical ear. Sometimes one impulse is so urgent that it carries the body like a tide carries a boat. So it was then with the Voice that bade me go.

From Harley Street to Hanover Square is no pilgrimage; yet in those minutes it seemed as if my whole being were stretched between two cries. One shouted: *Since you go, breathe no word of the Boreal, and Clark’s visit.* The other shouted: *Tell, tell—hide nothing!*

The distance was crossed; the door opened; and Clodagh was in my arms.

She was, to my eyes then, the most superb of creatures: that haughty neck which always seemed to scorn something just behind her left shoulder; the sharp-cut florid face; the air of command that made even an ordinary room seem a stage. Superb—and I know now, know with a sick certainty, a godless woman, Clodagh, with a bitter heart.

Once she confessed, lightly, that her favourite character in history was Lucrezia Borgia; and when she saw my horror she added at once, laughing, “Well, no—I am only joking!” Such was her duplicity. I see now that she lived in constant effort to hide her heinous heart from me. Yet how completely she enthralled me.

Our proposed marriage was opposed by both families—mine, because her father and grandfather had died in lunatic asylums; hers, because I was neither rich nor noble. Her sister, much older than herself, had married a common country doctor—Peters of Taunton—and that so-called mésalliance made the proposed match with me doubly detestable to her people. But Clodagh’s passion for me was not to be stemmed by threats or prayers. She burned. At times she frightened me.

She was not young—five years older than I; and also five years older than her nephew, born of that sister’s marriage with Peters of Taunton. The nephew was Peter Peters, who was to accompany the *Boreal* expedition as doctor, botanist, and meteorological assistant.

That very day—Clark’s day—I had been seated with Clodagh scarcely five minutes before, driven by that wicked compulsion in me, I spoke.

“Dr. Clark—ha! ha! ha!—has been talking to me about the Expedition. He says that if anything happened to Peters, I should be the first man he would run to. He has had an absurd dream—”

Even as I said it, a consciousness filled me of the crooked wickedness of it—as if some part of me were watching another part lay a trap. But I could no more help it than I could fly.

Clodagh stood at the window, holding a rose to her face. For a full minute she did not answer. I saw her in profile, steadily bent, smelling—breathing in the scent as if it were information. At last she said, cold and rapid:

“The man who first plants his foot on the North Pole will certainly be ennobled. I say nothing of the many millions. I only wish I was a man!”

“I don’t know that I have any special ambition that way,” I said. “I am very happy in my warm Eden with my Clodagh. I don’t like the outer cold.”

“Don’t let me think little of you!” she returned, petulant.

“Why should you? I am not bound to desire to go to the North Pole, am I?”

“But you would go, I suppose, if you could?”

“I might—I doubt it. There is our marriage—”

“Marriage indeed! It is the one thing to transform our marriage from a sneaking difficulty to a ten times triumphant event.”

“You mean if I personally were the first to stand at the Pole. But there are many in an expedition. It is very unlikely that I, personally—”

“For me you will, Adam—” she began.

“‘Will,’ Clodagh?” I cried. “You say ‘will’? There is not even the slightest shadow of probability—”

“But why? There are still three weeks before the start. They say—”

She stopped.

“They say what?”

Her voice dropped, and the words came out as if they were something she tasted first:

“That Peter takes atropine.”

I started then. She moved from the window, sat in a rocking-chair, and turned the leaves of a book without reading. The room seemed to fill with the small sounds of paper, of the chair’s faint creak; and under them my own blood made a louder surf. We were silent. I stood looking at her while she drew her thumb across the edges of the pages, beginning again, contemplative. Then she laughed—dryly, a little; a dry, mad laugh.

“Why did you start when I said that?” she asked, reading now at random.

“I—I did not start, Clodagh! What made you think I started? I did not start! Who told you that Peters takes atropine?”

“He is my nephew; I should know,” she said. “But don’t look dumbfoundered in that absurd fashion. I have no intention of poisoning him in order to see you a multimillionaire, and a Peer of the Realm—”

“My dearest Clodagh!”

“I easily might, however. He will be here presently. He is bringing Mr. Wilson for the evening.” Wilson was going as electrician of the expedition.

“Clodagh,” I said, “believe me, you jest in a manner which does not please me.”

“Do I really?” She gave that stiff half-turn of her throat. “Then I must be more exquisite. But, thank Heaven, it is only a jest. Women are no longer admired for doing such things.”

“Ha! ha! ha!—no—no longer admired, Clodagh! Oh, my good Lord! Let us change this talk—”

But she could talk of nothing else. That afternoon she got from me the history of Polar expeditions: the latitudes reached, the aids used, the reasons for failure. Her eyes shone; she listened with an eagerness like hunger. Before that day she had been interested in the *Boreal*, knew details of her outfitting, was acquainted with several of the men. Now her mind seemed wholly possessed, as if my mention of Clark’s visit had set a match to some stored-up tinder in her.

The passion of her kiss when I tore myself from her embrace I shall not forget. I went home with a heavy heart.

Dr. Peter Peters lived three doors from me, across the street. Toward one that night his footman ran to knock me up with news that Peters was very ill. I hurried over; and at the first glance—delirium, that wild staring of the pupils—I knew he was poisoned with atropine. Wilson was there; he had passed the evening with him at Clodagh’s in Hanover Square.

“What on earth is the matter?” Wilson cried.

“Poisoned,” I said.

“Good God! With what?”

“Atropine.”

“Good Heavens!”

“Don’t be frightened. I think he will recover.”

“Is that certain?”

“Yes—I think—if he leaves off taking the drug, Wilson.”

“What! It is he who has poisoned himself?”

I hesitated; I felt the hesitation like a hand on my throat. But I said:

“He is in the habit of taking atropine, Wilson.”

Three hours I remained, toiling hard for his life. When I left him in the dark of fore-day my mind was at rest: he would recover.

I slept until eleven, then hurried back. In the room were my two nurses—and Clodagh.

She put her forefinger to her lips and whispered:

“Sh-h-h! He is asleep.”

She came close to my ear.

“I heard the news early. I am come to stay with him till—the last—”

We looked at one another some time, eye to eye, steady she and I; but my eyes dropped first. A word lay on my mouth, heavy as lead. I did not speak it.

The recovery was not as steady as I expected. At the end of the first week he was still prostrate. Then I said to Clodagh:

“Your presence at the bedside here somehow does not please me. It is so unnecessary.”

“Unnecessary certainly,” she replied. “But I always had a genius for nursing, and a passion for watching the battles of the body. Since no one objects, why should you?”

“Ah—I don’t know. This is a case I dislike. I have half a mind to throw it to the devil.”

“Then do so.”

“And you, too—go home, go home, Clodagh.”

“But why?—if one does no harm. In these days of ‘the corruption of the upper classes,’ and Roman decadence of everything, shouldn’t every innocent whim be encouraged by you upright ones who strive against the tide? Whims are the brakes of crimes—and this is mine. I find a sensuous pleasure, almost a sensual, in dabbling in delicate drugs—like Helen, and Medea, and Calypso, and the great antique women who were all excellent chymists. To study the human ship in a gale, and the slow drama of its foundering—isn’t that a quite thrilling distraction? And I want you to get into the habit at once of letting me have my little way—”

She touched my hair with a lofty playfulness that soothed me—yet even then my eyes went to the rumpled bed and I saw what she pretended to ornament: the man was truly sick.

I have nausea still to write of it. Lucrezia Borgia in her own age may have been heroic; but Lucrezia in this late century—one could retch up the heart.

The second week passed; and only ten days remained before the start.

At the end of that second week, I entered one evening. Wilson was sitting by Peters’ bedside. Clodagh, at the moment, was about to administer a dose; but seeing me she set down the medicine-glass on the night table and came toward me. As she came, I saw a sight that stabbed me: Wilson, with a hurried light-fingered stealth, took up the medicine-glass, elevated it, looked at it, smelled into it—an action made furtive by the under-look he gave it, the meaningness of his expression. It was mistrust made visible.

Meanwhile Clark came daily. He himself had a medical degree; and about this time I called him in professionally, with Alleyne of Cavendish Square, to consult. Peters lay in semi-coma broken by passionate vomitings; his condition puzzled us all. I stated formally that he took atropine, that he had been originally poisoned by atropine. Yet we saw that the present symptoms were not atropine symptoms; it seemed rather some other vegetable poison—unnamed, slipping through our classifications like a fish through the hand.

“Mysterious thing,” said Clark when we were alone.

“I don’t understand it,” I answered.

“Who are the two nurses?”

“Highly recommended—people of my own.”

“At any rate,” he said, “my dream about you comes true, Jeffson. It is clear Peters is out of the running now.”

I shrugged.

“I now formally invite you to join the expedition,” said Clark. “Do you consent?”

I shrugged again.

“Well, if that means consent,” he said, “let me remind you that you have only eight days—and all the world to do in them.”

This was in the dining-room. As we passed through the door, I saw Clodagh gliding down the passage outside—rapidly—away from us.

I said nothing to her that day about Clark’s invitation. Yet the question worried me like grit under a lid: Did she not know? Had she not listened and heard?

However that was, about midnight Peters opened his eyes and smiled. By noon the next day his fine vitality—so fitted for the Arctic—had reasserted itself. He leaned on an elbow, talking to Wilson; and except pallor and strong stomach-pains there was hardly a trace of the late approach to death. For the pains I prescribed quarter-grain tablets of sulphate of morphia and went away.

Now Wilson and I never greatly loved one another. That very day he brought about a painful situation between Peters and me by telling Peters that I had taken his place in the expedition. Peters, touchy, dictated at once a letter of protest to Clark; and Clark sent that letter to me marked with a large blue interrogation.

Peters’ preparations were complete; mine were not. He had six days in which to recover. I therefore wrote to Clark that the changed circumstances of course annulled my acceptance of his offer, though I had already incurred the inconvenience of negotiating with a locum tenens.

It decided it. Peters was to go; I was to stay.

The fifth day before departure dawned—Friday, the 15th of June. Peters sat in an arm-chair now. He was cheerful, but fevered in pulse, and still with stomach-pains. I was giving him three quarter-grains of morphia a day.

That night at eleven I visited him and found Clodagh there, talking to him. Peters was smoking a cigar; the room had that mingled smell of tobacco and sickroom—stale smoke, warmed linen, the faint sweetness of drugs. Clodagh said:

“Ah. I was waiting for you, Adam. I didn’t know whether I was to inject anything to-night. Is it Yes or No?”

“What do you think, Peters? Any more pains?” I asked.

“Well—perhaps you had better give us another quarter,” he said. “There’s still some trouble in the tummy off and on.”

“A quarter-grain, then, Clodagh,” I said.

As she opened the syringe-box she remarked, with a pout:

“Our patient has been naughty. He has taken some more atropine.”

Anger sprang up in me at once.

“Peters,” I cried, “you know you have no right to do things like that without consulting me! Do that once more, and I swear I have nothing further to do with you!”

“Rubbish,” said Peters. “Why all this unnecessary heat? It was a mere flea-bite. I felt I needed it.”

“He injected it with his own hand,” Clodagh added.

She had lifted the syringe-box from the night-table, taken from its velvet lining both the syringe and the vial containing the morphia tabloids, and gone to the mantel-piece to melt one tabloid in distilled water. Her back was to us. She was a long time. I stood; Peters sat smoking. She began to talk, while she lingered there, about a charity bazaar she had visited that afternoon.

She was long—too long. From some dim region in me the crazy thought passed: *Why is she so long?*

“Ah—that was a pain!” Peters exclaimed. “Never mind the bazaar, aunt—think of the morphia.”

Suddenly an irresistible impulse seized me—to rush upon her, to dash syringe, tabloids, glass, and all from her hands. I must have obeyed it; I was on the very brink, my body already leaning—when a voice from the opened door behind me said:

“Well—how is everything?”

Wilson stood there.

With lightning swiftness I remembered that under-look of mistrust I had once seen on his face. Oh, well—I would not, could not—she was my love; I stood like marble.

Clodagh went to meet Wilson, offering him her frank right hand; in her left she held the fragile glass with the injection. My eyes were fastened on her face. It was full of reassurance, of free innocence. I said to myself: *I must surely be mad.*

An ordinary chat began. Clodagh turned up Peters’ sleeve; kneeling there, she injected his fore-arm. As she rose, laughing at something Wilson said, the drug-glass dropped from her hand; her heel, by an apparent accident, trod on it. She put the syringe among a number of others on the mantel-piece.

“Your friend has been naughty, Mr. Wilson,” she said again with the same pout. “He has been taking more atropine.”

“Not really?” said Wilson.

“Let me alone, the whole of you,” Peters answered. “I ain’t a child.”

These were the last intelligible words he ever spoke.

Shortly before one in the morning he died. He had been poisoned by a powerful dose of atropine.

From that moment until the moment when the *Boreal* bore me down the Thames, the world was a tumbling nightmare in which details would not keep their shape. Only fragments remain, as if memory itself were frost-bitten.

I remember the inquest, and how I was called upon to prove that Peters had himself injected himself with atropine. Wilson corroborated it. Clodagh corroborated it. The verdict was in accordance.

And in that chaotic hurry of preparation three other things—only three—stand now with a cruel distinctness.

The first—and chief—is that tempest of words I heard at Kensington from big-mouthed Mackay on the Sunday night. What led me, busy as I was, to that chapel then? Perhaps I know.

There I sat and heard him; and most strangely the words of his peroration have planted themselves in my brain, for when he rose to a passion of prophecy he shouted: “And as in the one case transgression was followed by catastrophe swift and universal, so, in the other, I warn the entire race to look out thenceforth for nothing from God but a lowering sky, and thundery weather.”
