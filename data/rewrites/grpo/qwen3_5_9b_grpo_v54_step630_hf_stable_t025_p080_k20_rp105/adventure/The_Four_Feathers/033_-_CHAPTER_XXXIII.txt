The words arrived that evening, spoken with a quiet finality that shattered the silence of the room. Ethne retreated to her farmhouse, the summer heat having long since given way to a chill that seeped through the floorboards. She lit the fire, watching the coals glow with a stillness that betrayed the storm raging within her. The moment she had waited for, the half-hour of Harry Feversham's presence, had come and gone. Now, she sat alone in her remote hill village, more isolated than she had been since Willoughby sailed away that August morning. For months, she had lived for those fleeting minutes of his company; now that they were past, the emptiness of the world pressed in on her. Yet, a part of her nature had always anticipated this hollow ache, knowing that sending Harry away would trigger a desperate urge to recall him. She forced herself to remember that she had seen this coming, that she must look beyond the immediate pain. Tomorrow, she would realize they had not parted forever; tomorrow, she would weigh this separation against the tragic parting at Lennon House and see how small it truly was. As she shivered by the fire, wondering what Harry would do now that he had returned, she felt the warm weight of Dermod's old collie nuzzling her hand. The dog sensed her distress with an instinctive certainty. Ethne rose, cupping the animal's head in her hands and kissing its fur. He was ancient, she thought, destined to die soon and leave her alone, leaving her to wait years before her own great moment arrived.

A knock at the door broke her reverie. A servant announced that Colonel Durrance was waiting.

"Yes," Ethne replied, rising to meet him. She did not shy away from the role she had assigned herself; she stepped out from the secret chamber of her grief the moment she was summoned. She conversed with her visitor as though nothing extraordinary had occurred an hour prior, discussing their marriage and the reconstruction of Lennon House. It was difficult, but she had grown accustomed to hardship. Only tonight did Durrance make her path steeper. After the maid served tea, he asked her to play the Musoline Overture on her violin.

"Not tonight," Ethne said, feigning fatigue. "I am rather tired." But the words slipped out before she could retract them. Ethne resolved that in the small matters as well as the great, she must not evade responsibility. "Still, I believe I can manage the overture," she said with a forced smile, retrieving her instrument. She played from start to finish. Durrance stood at the window, his back to her, until the final note faded. Then he approached her side.

"I was rather a brute," he said softly, "to ask you to play that piece tonight."

"I wasn't eager to play," she answered, setting the violin down.

"I know. But I needed to find out something, and I knew no other way."

Ethne turned a startled face toward him.

"What do you mean?" she asked, her voice tight with suspense.

"You are rarely off your guard," Durrance continued calmly. "Only when you play. Once before, when you performed that overture, you were vulnerable. I thought if I could get you to play it again—the one once strummed in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—I might catch you off balance."

His words stole her breath and drained the color from her cheeks. She rose slowly, staring at him with wide eyes. He could not possibly know. It was impossible. He did not know.

Yet Durrance pressed on.

"Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?"

These were the incredible words. Durrance spoke them with a faint smile. It took Ethne a long moment to comprehend that he had actually uttered them. Her senses felt overstrained, as if reality itself were bending; but he repeated the question, and she could no longer deny or misinterpret what she heard.

"Who told you of any fourth feather?" she asked.

"Trench," Durrance answered. "I met him at Dover. He only mentioned the fourth feather," he clarified. "I already knew of the three. Trench would never have spoken of the fourth unless I had known of the three beforehand. Otherwise, I would not have met him as he disembarked at Dover. I would not have asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' Knowing of the three was sufficient."

"How do you know?" Ethne cried, a note of despair in her voice. As she moved closer, he gently took her arm.

"But since I know," he protested, "what difference does it make how I know? I have known for a long time, ever since Captain Willoughby brought the first feather to The Pool. I waited to tell you until Harry Feversham returned, and he came today."

Ethne sat back down, stunned by this unexpected revelation. She had guarded her secret so meticulously that realizing it had been open knowledge for a year struck her like a blow. Yet, amidst her confusion, she understood she needed time to regain her composure. She spoke of trivialities to buy moments.

"You were in the church, then? Or did you hear us on the steps? Or did you meet him as he rode away?"

"Not one of your guesses is correct," Durrance smiled. Ethne had chosen the right subject to delay the inevitable. Durrance possessed vanities like any other man, particularly one that had blossomed since his blindness: he prided himself on the acuteness of his perception. It delighted him to make discoveries that no one expected a blind man to achieve, and to announce them unexpectedly. It was an added pleasure to explain the steps of his deduction to a puzzled audience.

"Not one of your conjectures is right, Ethne," he said, practically inviting her to question him further.

"Then how did you find out?" she asked.

"I knew from Trench that Harry Feversham would return soon. This afternoon, I passed the church. Your collie dog barked at me, so I knew you were inside. But a saddled horse was tied beside the gate. Someone else was with you, and not someone from the village. Then I had you play, and that told me exactly who rode the horse."

"Yes," Ethne murmured vaguely, barely listening. "Yes, I see." Then, in a firm voice that signaled her regained self-control, she asked: "You went away to Wiesbaden for a year. You left just after Captain Willoughby arrived. Was that the reason?"

"I went because neither you nor I could sustain the game of pretences we were playing," Durrance explained. "You pretended you had no thought for Harry Feversham, that you hardly cared if he lived or died. I pretended not to have discovered that, above all else, you cared for him. Eventually, one of us would have failed, each in turn. I dared not fail, nor did you dare. I could not let you, who said 'Two lives must not be spoilt because of me,' live through a year believing two lives had been ruined. And you, on your side, dared not let me, who said 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship on both sides,' know that on one side there existed only friendship. We were so close to failing that I had to act."

"You did not fail," Ethne said quietly. "It was only I who failed."

She blamed herself bitterly. She had set herself the highest task—to shield this man from knowledge that would crown his calamities—and she had failed. He had tried to protect her from understanding that she had failed, and he had succeeded. It was not merely humiliation that troubled her, the realization that the man she thought she had deceived had deceived her. Rather, she felt she ought to have succeeded, for her failure had robbed him of his last chance at happiness. That was the sting.

"But it was not your fault," he said. "Once or twice, as I mentioned, you were off your guard, but the convincing facts were not revealed that way. When you played the Musoline Overture before, on the night Willoughby brought you such good news, I took that happiness of yours as inspiration for your playing. You must not blame yourself. On the contrary, you should be glad I have found out."

"Glad?" she exclaimed.

"Yes, for my sake, glad." Looking at him in wonder, he continued, "Two lives should not be spoilt because of you. Had you had your way, had I not found out, not two but three lives would have been ruined because of you—because of your loyalty."

"Three?"

"Yours. Yes—yours, Feversham's, and mine. It was hard enough to keep the pretence during our few weeks in Devonshire. Admit it, Ethne! When I went to London to see my oculist, it was a relief; it gave you a pause, a rest to drop the pretence and be yourself. It could not have lasted long even in Devonshire. But what when we lived under the same roof, seeing each other every hour of every day, with no visits to the oculist? Sooner or later the truth must have come to me. It might have crept up gradually, suspicion adding to suspicion until doubt vanished. Or it might have flashed out in one terrible moment. But it would have been clear. And then, Ethne? What then? You aimed at a compensation; you wanted to make up to me for the loss of what I love—my career, the army, the special service in strange lands. A fine compensation to sit before you knowing you married a cripple out of pity, thereby crippling yourself and forfeiting the happiness that is yours by right. Whereas now—"

"Whereas now?" she repeated.

"I remain your friend, which I would rather be than your unloved husband," he said very gently.

Ethne made no reply. The decision had been wrested from her hands.

"You sent Harry away this afternoon," Durrance noted. "You said goodbye to him twice."

At the word "twice," Ethne lifted her head, but before she could speak, Durrance explained: "Once in the church, again upon your violin." He picked up the instrument from the chair where she had laid it. "It has been a very good friend, your violin," he said. "A good friend to me, to us all. You will understand that, Ethne, very soon. I stood at the window while you played. I had never heard anything in my life half so sad as your farewell to Harry Feversham, and yet it was nobly sad. It was true music; it did not complain." He laid the violin back on the chair.

"I am going to send a messenger to Rathmullen. Harry cannot cross Lough Swilly tonight. The messenger will bring him back tomorrow."

It had been a day of profound emotion and surprise for Ethne. As Durrance bent toward her, he noticed she was weeping silently. For once, tears had their way with her. He took his cap and walked noiselessly to the door. As he opened it, Ethne rose.

"Don't go for a moment," she said, leaving the fireplace to stand in the center of the room.

"The oculist at Wiesbaden?" she asked. "Did he give you hope?"

Durrance paused, meditating on whether to lie or speak the truth.

"No," he said finally. "There is no hope. But I am not so helpless as I feared I might be. I can get about, can't I? Perhaps one of these days I shall go on a journey, one of the long journeys among the strange people in the East."

He left the house to carry out his errand. He had learned his lesson long ago, and the violin had taught it to him. It had spoken again that afternoon, though with a different voice, offering him the same message: true music cannot complain.
