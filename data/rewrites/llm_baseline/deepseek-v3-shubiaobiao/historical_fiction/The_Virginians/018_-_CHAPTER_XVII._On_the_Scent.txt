The suddenness of young Harry Warrington’s defiance left Madame de Bernstein momentarily stunned, her fury flashing like lightning as she swept from the room. Her parting glare at Lady Fanny and her mother spoke volumes, while Lady Maria, bent over her tambour-frame, scarcely lifted her eyes—her needle darting in and out with practiced indifference.

“So, despite everything, *you* have—” Lady Castlewood’s sharp gaze seemed to accuse.

“Have what?” Fanny’s wide, innocent eyes blinked back. But innocence was a performance, and Fanny’s was less convincing than Maria’s. Had she been guilty, she would have rehearsed her role better.

Lady Castlewood’s lips thinned. She could not claw her daughter’s eyes out here—not in the open. Such discipline required privacy, a smiling arm around the waist before the gouging began.

“You’ve no desire to trail after the old lady to Tunbridge Wells, then?” she inquired of Harry, her tone honeyed.

Lord Castlewood intervened smoothly. “No blame to him. A man ought to mix duty with pleasure. Let him learn the workings of an English estate before he idles at pump-rooms. My brother has shown him the kennels, the stables, the games—and he plays them as well as his tutors. Come harvest, we’ll show him shooting; in winter, hunting. Though our Virginia kin and we have been estranged, blood is blood. Before he returns to his mother, let him see how an English gentleman lives.”

The frankness of his speech left the others wary. What game was he playing? Castlewood’s schemes often unfolded months later, his meanings opaque until the final move.

“What’s his aim now?” muttered William as they retreated to the corridor.

“By God, I see it!”

“What?”

“He’ll lure the boy to the tables and win back the Virginia lands!”

“But the lad hasn’t the estate to stake,” protested his mother.

William scoffed. “If my brother hasn’t a scheme, I’ll eat my hat.”

“He can’t mean Maria—she’s old enough to be his mother.”

Fanny sighed. “With her freckles and sandy hair? Impossible.”

Mamma’s eyes narrowed. “Oh? Had *you* designs on the Iroquois?”

“I know my station,” Fanny retorted. “Unlike some, I’d not marry where I didn’t love.”

A bitter laugh from William. “Our parents scarcely loved, yet they wed. Remember their battles, Fan? And Esmond playing peacemaker.”

“Spare me those memories,” Lady Castlewood snapped.

Fanny’s voice turned plaintive. “What good is a coronet without coin? Tradesmen’s widows dress finer than we do!”

William smirked. “Glad I dine at Court, then.”

“Happiness needs no title,” Fanny insisted.

“Even in America? With war-whoops and scalping knives?”

“I’d skip church,” Fanny muttered.

William roared. “You’d go with *any* suitor—just like Maria!”

Madame Bernstein’s voice cut through. “What merry war is this?”

William gleefully recounted the spat—Fanny’s supposed infatuation, their mother’s veto. Fanny protested, cheeks aflame.

Bernstein’s brow furrowed. “If not for Fanny, why does he stay? You’ve shown him no beauty but frights and nursery-maids.”

William grinned. “Tastes differ. The kitchen’s fair game.”

“Not for *this* boy.” Her mind raced. Maria—*Maria?* Impossible. Yet—

She recalled Harry’s absences, Maria’s coinciding walks. Garden whispers, lingering touches. A sudden suspicion took root.

“Impossible?” William scoffed. “Lady Suffolk wed young Berkeley at sixty. And didn’t Harry Estridge pine for *you* at twenty?”

The jab struck home—not with anger, but amusement. “Age doesn’t quench folly,” she mused.

“But Maria?” William persisted. “Unless—” He laughed. “By Jove, we’ve flushed the fox!”

How far had it gone? Threats would only harden Harry’s resolve. Opposition fed obsession—she knew that well.

“Leave him to it,” William suggested. “An earl’s daughter for a planter’s son isn’t ill-matched.”

Outrage erupted. Bernstein silenced them. “Remember what you owe his family! His grandfather surrendered title, lands—*this very castle*—to yours. And you’d repay him by palming off a withered spinster?”

Lady Castlewood bristled. “Our name is honor enough for any Virginian.”

Bernstein’s laugh was sharp. “Fanny belongs at Court, not in a wilderness. Let him take a wife fit for a plantation.”

The others demurred, but the Baroness saw through them. She knew the game now—Maria’s desperation, Harry’s infatuation. How deep did it run?

As she withdrew, her mind turned over the puzzle. Women’s secrets were buried deep—some from modesty, some from cunning. And Maria, the family joke, had outplayed them all.

*Let her try,* Bernstein thought. *But not at Harry’s expense.*
