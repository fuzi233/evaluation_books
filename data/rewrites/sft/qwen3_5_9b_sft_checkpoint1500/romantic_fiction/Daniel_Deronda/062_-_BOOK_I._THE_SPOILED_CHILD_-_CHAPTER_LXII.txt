"Fortune is a fickle maid,
Who loves not to abide;
She brushes from thy brow the cloud,
And kisses thee aside.

But Misfortune holds thee fast,
And presses thee to heart;
She saith she hath no haste to pass,
And sits beside thy part."

A shadow that Mirah had watched approaching like the fulfillment of a warning now lingered, heavy with its accustomed baggage of sorrow. Turning from Knightsbridge, where she had just sung at a charity concert in a wealthy home, she felt footsteps matching her stride with unsettling precision. Her simple black concert dress, draped in a dust-cloak, offered no obvious pretext for alarm, nor did the crowd outside the venue suggest danger. Yet, a different fear gripped her: she imagined herself trailed by a ghost. To turn and confront the pursuer would be to invite a flood of emotion she deemed intolerable. If it were her father, he would demand recognition, forcing her to face him. She resolved to wait for that inevitable compulsion. Walking on, she did not hasten, but her mind raced ahead, rehearsing the encounter with grim certainty. A pang of regret struck her for having promised Mrs. Meyrick no concealment regarding her parentage. Driven by a desperate need to shield her brother from a sudden shock, she decided to turn before reaching her own door, choosing to summon the confrontation rather than submit to it passively.

As she approached the entrance of her small square, her intuition grew sharper, slipping from behind to her side until a hand grasped her wrist. "Mirah!" the voice called, soft and persuasive.

Mirah froze instantly. It was the voice she dreaded, yet it met her eyes with an intention to soothe. Her countenance remained grave, akin to facing an executioner, while his face wore a mask of feigned tenderness. Once handsome, with vibrant color, his features were now sallow and deeply lined, bearing a certain impudent smoothness born of seeking favor while accepting disdain. Light-built and active, he possessed a deceptive youthfulness that masked his nearing fifty-seventh year. His attire remained shabby, as she remembered it. This unworthy father, appearing now in a world where she lacked the circle of friends to buffer him from scorn, stirred within her a tumultuous mix of shame, grief, repulsion, and pity.

"It is you, father," she whispered, her voice tremulous.

"Why did you flee from me, child?" he began, his speech rapid, punctuated by jerky, illustrative gestures. "What terrified you? You knew I never compelled you against your will. I broke your engagement in the Vorstadt solely for your sake, seeing it ill-suited, yet you repaid me by abandoning me to the hardships that followed. I had secured you a position at the Dresden theater; I withheld the news to surprise you. And so you left me stranded, forced to vanish to avoid breach of contract. Think of the hardship, after I sacrificed everything to educate you, hoping it would become your fortune. What father devoted himself more fully? You know how I bore the sting of your silence, making the best of it. When I had no one but you, and was breaking under the strain of fighting with my wits alone, you chose to leave. To whom did you owe everything but me? Where was your gratitude? For what I cared, I might have perished in a ditch."

Lapidoth halted abruptly, not for lack of words, but having reached a pathos-laden climax. He produced a yellow silk handkerchief and gave a sudden, feminine sob. He genuinely felt betrayed by his daughter—a sentiment naturally potent in those unscrupulous souls who calculate debts without acknowledging counter-claims. Despite the tear, Mirah possessed enough resolve not to let him deceive her completely.

"You know why I left, father," she replied, her voice firmer than before, the first time she spoke accusations to him. "I had cause to distrust you, for I was certain you had deceived my mother. Had I trusted you, I would have stayed and labored for you."

"I never intended to deceive your mother, Mirah!" he protested, wiping his eyes, his voice struggling against further weeping. "I planned to take you back to her, but chances thwarted me at the critical moment. Then came news of her death. It was wiser for you that I remain where I was, allowing your brother to care for himself. Only you held any claim upon me. A particular friend managed affairs for me; I sent him funds to cover expenses. There remains one possibility—" a flicker of cunning crossed his mind—"he may have lied to extract money from me."

Mirah offered no reply. She could not voice the truth—that she believed not a single word of his fabrication—and merely signaled a desire to move forward, fearing that standing still might attract unwanted scrutiny. As they walked, the contrast between Mirah, radiant in the quiet dignity of an English lady, and this shabby, foreign, gesticulating man, whose jauntiness belied his plight, would surely arrest the gaze of passersby.

"You appear to have prospered, Mirah?" Lapidoth observed, examining her with emphatic scrutiny. "You know no want."

"Kind friends who found me in distress assisted me in finding work," Mirah answered, barely aware of her words as her thoughts turned to what she must soon reveal. "I teach lessons. I sing in private homes. I have just performed at a concert." She paused, adding with gravity, "I have very good friends who know all about me."

"And would you shame them by showing your father in this state? No wonder." Lapidoth's tone shifted to self-pity. "I came to England with no prospects save finding you. It was a mad quest, yet a father's heart is superstitious, drawn like a loadstone. I might have flourished abroad, free from the burden of you, rolling along like a ball. But loneliness breaks a spirit when one lacks purpose. I hoped my little Mirah would repent her departure when she looked back. I have suffered a sharp pinch working my way; I know not what depths await me next. Talents like mine are useless here. When a man wears rags, belief dies. I have been forced to sink lower for a shilling."

Mirah's anxiety surged at the thought of his further degradation, compelling her to intervene. Before she could respond to his fluent, rehearsed sentences, he asked promptly, "Where do you live, Mirah?"

"In this square. Not far from the house," she replied.

"In lodgings?"

"Yes."

"Anyone to care for you?"

"Yes," Mirah said, fixing her gaze on his keen, searching eyes. "My brother."

Lapidoth's lids fluttered as if lightning had struck them; his shoulders twitched. After a brief, perceptible pause, he asked, "Ezra? How did you know? How did you find him?"

"That requires telling later. Here we are at the door. My brother would not wish me to shut you out."

Mirah stepped onto the doorstep, her face turned downward toward her father standing on the pavement. Her heart hammered with the prospect of meeting Ezra. In granting leave to the father she had once obeyed, seeing him shrink from the direct admission he sought, she felt a unique, sympathetic humiliation—the stinging heart of reverence belonging to a nature intensely filial.

"Stay a moment, *Liebchen*," Lapidoth murmured, lowering his voice. "Tell me, what sort of man has Ezra become?"

"A good man—a wonderful man," Mirah answered slowly, striving to master the tremor in her throat. "Yet, twelve years ago, when friends found him, he was a poor worker. Once, strong and happy, he journeyed eastward, a land he loved. My mother called him back because... because she had lost me. He went to her, tending her through great tribulation, working until she died in grief. Ezra, too, lost his health and strength. The cold seized him returning, for she was forsaken. For years he has weakened, always poor, always laboring, yet filled with knowledge and greatness. All who approach honor him. To stand before him is like standing before a prophet of God." Her voice faltered, her heart throbbing. "Falsehoods serve no purpose."

She cast down her eyes, unable to bear the ignoble glimmer of frustration gathering in his face. Yet, he was swift in invention and decision.

"Mirah, *Liebchen*," he cooed in his old, caressing manner, "should I not seek to appear more respectable before my son? With a modest sum, I could dress properly and return home as a father ought. I could seek a decent post. With a clean shirt and coat, men would welcome me. I could serve as a courier, if I did not resemble a broken mountebank. I wish to be with my children, to forget and forgive. But you have never seen your father thus. Ten pounds, or perhaps you could bring it tomorrow..." He gestured vaguely. "I could be fitted out by tomorrow afternoon."

Mirah felt a temptation she struggled to resist. Obliging herself to look at him again, she replied, "I cannot deny you what you ask, father; but I have pledged not to act secretly toward you. It is hard to see you in need, yet we shall endure this briefly. Soon, you shall have new clothes, and we shall pay for them." Her practical sense recognized the wisdom of Mrs. Meyrick's condition.
