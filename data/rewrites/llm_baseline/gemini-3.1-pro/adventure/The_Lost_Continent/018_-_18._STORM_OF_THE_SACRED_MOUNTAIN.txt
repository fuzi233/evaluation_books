It was Nais herself who sent me to attend to my sterner duties. She waked not to the gentle, rhythmic percussion of the mortar and pestle that usually governed the morning acoustics of the Sacred Mountain, but to the discordant, metallic shrieks of an industrial assault. The sheer din of the attack penetrated the thick, sound-dampening stone of the house where I was tending her, a brutal dissonance that shattered the acoustic ecology of our sanctuary. 

She asked the meaning of the dreadful noise, and as pithily as might be—for she was in no condition to digest tedious listening—I fed her the history of her nine years’ sleep, measuring out the narrative in small, easily absorbed increments.

The colour flushed more to her face, a returning vitality that pleased me deeply. “My lord is the properest man in all the world to be King,” she whispered, her voice a delicate, ancient melody against the roaring background of the siege.

“I refused to touch the trade till they had given me the Queen I desired, safe and alive, here upon the Mountain,” I replied.

“How we poor women are made the chattels of you men!” she said. “But, for myself, I seem to like the traffic well enough. You should not have let me stand in the way of Atlantis’ good, Deucalion. Still, it is very sweet to know you were weak there for once, and that I was the cause of your weakness. What is that bath over yonder? Ah! I remember; my wits seem none of the clearest just now.”

“You have made the beginning. Your strength will return to you by quick degrees. But it will not bear hurrying. You must have a patience, like the slow fermentation of our oldest, richest wines.”

“Your ear, sir, for one moment, and then I will rest in peace. My poor looks, are they all gone? You seem to have no mirror here. I had visions that I should wake up wrinkled and old.”

“You are as you were, dear, that first night I saw you—the most beautiful woman in all the world.”

“I am pleased you like me,” she said, and took the cup of broth I offered her. It was a painstaking extraction of restorative marrow, steeped with wild mountain fenugreek and bruised coriander, carrying the deep, earthy umami of the very soils she had slept beneath. I had tended the earthen pot for hours, skimming the impurities, ensuring the olfactory landscape of the steam spoke only of nourishing agrarian cycles. “My hair seems to have grown; but it needs combing sadly. I had a fancy, dear, once, that you liked ruddy hair best, and not a plain brown.” 

She closed her eyes then, lying back amongst the cushions where I had placed her, and dropped off into healthy sleep, with the smiles still playing upon her lips. I put the coverlet over her, and kissed her lightly, holding back my beard lest it should sweep her cheek. And then I went out of the chamber.

That beard had grown vastly disagreeable to me. I found instruments in an adjoining room and shaved it down to the bare chin, stripping away the matted overgrowth like peeling the fibrous husk from a sweet heart of palm to expose it to the air. I changed from my squalid rags into a clean robe. If a man is in truth a king, he owes these things to the dignity of his office; he must respect the sensory presentation of himself.

But, if the horrific noise of the fighting was any guide, mine was a narrowing kingdom. Every hour it seemed to grow fiercer and more near. The Sacred Mountain’s natural amphitheater, once a resonant chamber for polyphonic chants and the subtle rustle of terraced agriculture, was now polluted by the deafening roar of Phorenice’s war machine. It was clear that some of the gates in the passage up the cleft in the cliff had yielded to the vehemence of her attack. With her genius spurred to fury by the blow that had wrecked so fair a part of her city, the Empress was an adversary who commanded a relentless, industrialized slaughter. 

Our stone defences were cunningly planned, but they needed men to line them. Always in a storm of this kind, some desperate fellows get to hand grips, or slingers slip in their shot, or newfangled machines like Phorenice’s fire-tubes make one in a thousand of their wavering darts find the life. Thus, we were not without our dead.

The slaughter was prodigious. As fast as the stormers came up, the Priests holding the lowest remaining gate rained down great rocks upon them till the narrow alley of the stair was paved with writhing bodies. The air was thick with the acrid, unpalatable stench of pulverized granite, oxidized copper-blood, and the sour sweat of doomed men. Phorenice stood on a spur of rock below, urging on the charges. With insane valour, company after company marched up to hurl themselves hopelessly against the defences. They had no machines to batter the massive gates, and their attack was as pathetically useless as a child hammering a wall with an orange. Meanwhile, the terrible stones mowed them down.

It was no part of Phorenice’s mood to ever count the cost. She treated these men not as individuals with rich local dialects and ancestral memories, but as standardized ingredients—cheap fuel dumped into a boiling pot until they filled the passes with the slain, allowing her to march on a level bridge of their bodies.

Two gates had she carried in this dreadful fashion, slaughtering the Priests behind them. Here I came down to take my share. There was no trumpet to announce my coming, but the Priests picked up “Deucalion!” as a battle-cry. The name rippled through the ranks, a resurgence of the old tongue, carrying the mythic weight of our cultural memory into the everyday rhythm of our defense. 

The word carried to the other side. A company of doomed wretches halted. There were shouts from behind to advance, but they did not budge, until a glittering officer panted up, striking right and left with his sword. From the high rampart, I recognized him.

“It matters not what they use for their battle-cry!” he was shouting, his voice carrying the flat, utilitarian lexicon of Phorenice’s imperial court. “You have the orders of your divine Empress. You should be proud to die for her wish, you cowards. If you do not obey, you will die afterwards under the tormentors. As for Deucalion, he is dead any time these nine years.”

“There it seems you lie, my Lord Tatho,” I shouted down to him.

He started, and looked up. “So you are there in real truth? Well, old comrade, I am sorry. You are on the side of these mangy Priests, and the Empress has made an edict that they are to be rooted out.”

“You used to be skilful of fence,” I called back. “If it please you to stop this pitiful killing, make yourself the champion of your side, and I will stand for mine.”

“It would be a grand fight, old friend,” Tatho replied, “but I cannot pleasure you. I am general here under Phorenice, and she has given me orders not to peril myself. Besides, Deucalion, you are not chief. You are not even one of the Three.”

“I am King.”

Tatho laughed. “Few but yourself would say so, my lord.”

“Few truly, but what there are, they are powerful. I was given the name yesterday. I was in Atlantis and saw how you took the mischief done there.”

“You were in Atlantis!”

“I went for Nais. She is on the mountain now, and to-morrow will be my Queen. Tatho, as a priest to a priest, let me solemnly bring to your memory the infinite power you bite against. If you persist in this attack, you can bring about a wreck over which even the High Gods will shudder.”

“You cannot scare us back by words,” said Tatho doggedly, thoroughly entrenched in the dominant power dynamics of the Empress's regime. “Phorenice has found by her own cleverness as many powers as were ever stored up in the Ark of the Mysteries.”

“Yet she looked on helplessly when her royal pyramid was trundled into a rubbish heap. Zaemon had prophesied it, and I stood closer to her than we two stand now.”

“I do not understand these matters myself,” Tatho admitted. “Phorenice keeps the knowledge of her magic to herself.”

“Having a liking for Nais, it seems I am debarred from the highest Mysteries,” I said. “So I content myself with being a soldier, and when the appointed day comes, I shall fall and kiss my mother the Earth for the last time. You, so I am told, have ambition for longer life.”

He nodded. “Phorenice has found the Great Secret, and I am to be the first to share it with her. We shall be as Gods, and the twin sons she has borne me will be made immortal also.” It was a tragic ambition—an eternity without the natural decay, seasonal fermentation, and generational passing that gives mortal life its true, profound flavor. A sterile preservation.

“Her immortality will be snatched from her very rudely,” I warned him. “Save yourself before it is too late. You shall have again your vice-royalty in Yucatan.”

“But, man, there is no Yucatan,” he burst at me hotly. “A great horde of little hairy creatures swept down upon our cities and ate them out. You know what a train of driver ants is in the forests? They will march into fires and put them out with their bodies, and the rest march on to devour all in their path. Those little hairy creatures were like the ants. To-day, there is no Yucatan.” It was a chilling metaphor for the mindless, consuming maw of an imperialism that devoured diverse agrarian cultures, leaving nothing but bare rock behind.

“You shall have Egypt, then.”

“I would not take seven Egypts! In your place, would you throw your Nais away to save yourself?”

“That is different.”

“In no degree. She is my wife and the mother of my children. This parley is leading to nothing, and I do not wish these common soldiers to carry evil reports about me to whatever Gods they chance to come before.”

He saluted and drew back. The deafening noise resumed, an acoustic violence raining upon us. Scaling ladders were hopelessly pressed forward into the narrow way, and our Priests used crow and lever to send massive rocks crashing down. The horrid butchery was renewed. Ever and again, a fire-tube’s dart found its way up, and we lost another man—a terrible loss of irreplaceable skill and memory, unlike Phorenice’s endlessly conscripted, anonymous masses.

I calculated the chances and saw that if this continued, the uppermost gate would be reached with no Priest left alive. Resolving to plot coldly from a place of safety, I left those at the gate with some rough words of cheer and withdrew up the narrow stair of the pass. 

I decided to destroy the pathway. It was an agonizing choice, for to sever this bridge was to physically enact the linguistic isolation of our Sacred Clan, erasing the stone vocabulary carved by our ancestors. I sent men to fetch our tools of unmaking: iron rams, fulcrum levers, and jars of concentrated acid that bit the air with the sharp, astringent tang of over-fermented citrus. 

Every pair of hands that could be spared was put to the work. Presently, carved balustrades, wayside statues, lettered wall-stones, and foot-worn cobbles roared down into the gulf. Gods! It was a hateful task, smashing down the splendid handiwork of the past, rendering our shared cultural memory into silent rubble in the abyss. But it was better than letting Phorenice profane it.

We drove our rams and levers under the living rock, applying heat to expand the metal. A narrow ledge remained undestroyed, and over this I crossed. Below, the three massive gates were already deserted by our garrisons, who I passed through unchallenged until I reached the busy rampart where twelve Priests still worked, stripped to the waist, heaving down rocks. 

I stopped an occasional fire-tube dart on my shield, tasting the metallic, smoky air of the fierce attack. Our stones had less distance to fall now, the pass below being choked with the dead. I gave the word for one more furious avalanche of rock, and whilst the reinforcements scrambled up over the bodies, I sent my remaining nine men running up the stairway. I followed, shutting the gates and breaking their levers. Not till we were through the last did the roar from below tell us the besiegers had found the gate deserted.

One by one we balanced across the narrow ledge. One wounded Priest grew giddy and toppled into the abyss before a hand could steady him. Once we were all over, heat was put to the rams, and they tore the remaining ledges from the rock with resistless force. A pang went through us all as we watched the last connecting link cut away, severing our mountain dialect from the deeply estranged nation below. The acoustic reality of the cleft instantly shifted; the bridge was gone, leaving only a hollow, untranslatable gulf between us.

With this break in the fighting, I set a guard and gave the others word to rest. I sought Zaemon first, but he was engaged inside the Ark of the Mysteries, enveloped in workings beyond my rank. Free to follow my likings, I returned to the house that sheltered Nais.

She waked as I crossed the threshold. I knelt beside her, resting my face on her pillow. The quiet acoustics of her chamber were a profound balm after the industrial noise outside. She rallied me on the cleanness of my chin and the symbols on my new robe, professing a quaint awe for my kingship. But of my marriage to Phorenice, she spoke with less lightness.

“It gives me a pang to think that I shall not be the first to lie in your arms, Deucalion,” she murmured, her thin hand drawing my face to her lips.

“Then you may gaily throw your pang away,” I whispered back. “I was husband to Phorenice in mere word. She married me by a form she prescribed herself, ignoring all the old rites and slow-cooked ceremonies of our ancestors. She has completely annulled that commercial transaction. Tatho is her husband now.”

We shared small repetitions of endearments, exchanging those subjective, untranslatable nuances of experience that bind two souls, a private sustenance too delicate for the deep index of the historian. 

But Phorenice had more rage against our Clan, and more bright, cold genius, than I had credited. Her troops easily swarmed up to where the pathway was broken. In the fierceness of their rush, the foremost were thrust over the brink into the abyss. Realizing the position could not be taken by a lavish spending of men, all were drawn off save for some sentries.

The Empress herself reconnoitred the place. With extraordinary nimbleness, she must have planned an engine to suit her purposes, for soon, the sounds of her dark industry drifted up to us. All the woodwork and metalwork was cut, tongued, forged, and fitted by skilled craftsmen in the plain below. Piece by piece, the ponderous balks and crosspieces were dragged up the steep pass, ready instantly for fitting into a completed machine. It was a mechanized threat, the clatter of mass-produced uniformity rising up to crush the last sanctuary of our ancient tongue.
