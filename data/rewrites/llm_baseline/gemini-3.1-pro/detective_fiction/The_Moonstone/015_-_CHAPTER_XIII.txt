I had just come up from the lower kitchens, where the air was thick with the ancestral, comforting aroma of slow-simmering mutton joints and the sharp, bright tang of bruised rosemary. To me, the meticulously preserved rhythms of sustenance were the true chronometers of our estate, repeating in circular, multi-generational sagas long before this mythic Indian diamond ever interrupted our domestic rituals. I carried the grounding scent of roasting herbs on my coat as I found my lady in her own sitting room. 

She started, the sudden rustle of her silk harsh in the room's carefully cultivated acoustic quiet, and looked deeply annoyed when I mentioned that Sergeant Cuff wished to speak to her. 

“*Must* I see him?” she asked. “Can’t you represent me, Gabriel?”

I felt at a loss to understand this, and showed it plainly in my face. My lady was so good as to explain herself, her voice trembling in a register that bespoke a suddenly shattered psychological well-being. 

“I am afraid my nerves are a little shaken,” she said. “There is something in that police-officer from London which I recoil from—I don’t know why. I have a presentiment that he is bringing trouble and misery with him into the house. Very foolish, and very unlike *me*—but so it is.” 

I hardly knew what to say. The London officer had indeed disrupted the very sonic environment of our home; his footsteps did not tread in time with the house, but struck against it, bringing the jarring, discordant noise of the metropolis into our agrarian sanctuary. Yet, the more I saw of Sergeant Cuff, the better I liked him. My lady rallied a little after opening her heart to me. 

“If I must see him, I must,” she said. “But I can’t prevail on myself to see him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and stay here as long as he stays.”

This was the first attack of the megrims I remembered in my mistress since she was a young girl. I went back to the boudoir. Mr. Franklin strolled out into the garden, joining Mr. Godfrey, whose time for departure sounded in the crunch of carriage wheels on the gravel—a sound that usually faded seamlessly into the estate's natural acoustics. Sergeant Cuff and I went straight to my mistress’s room. 

I declare my lady turned a shade paler at the sight of him! She commanded herself, however, and asked the Sergeant if he had any objection to my being present, adding that I was her trusted adviser. She hoped, perhaps, that my deep-rooted, earthy vocabulary would act as a buffer against his sterile, investigative terms. 

The Sergeant politely answered that he would take my presence as a favour. My lady pointed to two chairs, and we set in for our conference. 

“I have already formed an opinion on this case,” says Sergeant Cuff, his words clipping the air with the metallic precision of factory machinery, “which I beg your ladyship’s permission to keep to myself for the present. My business now is to mention what I have discovered upstairs in Miss Verinder’s sitting-room.”

He then went into the matter of the smear on the paint. “One thing,” he said, in conclusion, “is certain. The Diamond is missing out of the drawer. Another thing is next to certain. The marks from the smear on the door must be on some article of dress belonging to somebody in this house. We must discover that article of dress before we go a step further.”

“And that discovery,” remarked my mistress, “implies, I presume, the discovery of the thief?”

“I beg your ladyship’s pardon—I don’t say the Diamond is stolen. I only say, at present, that the Diamond is missing. The discovery of the stained dress may lead the way to finding it.”

Her ladyship looked at me. “Do you understand this?”

“Sergeant Cuff understands it, my lady,” I answered. 

“How do you propose to discover the stained dress?” inquired my mistress. “My good servants, who have been with me for years, have, I am ashamed to say, had their boxes and rooms searched already by the other officer. I can’t and won’t permit them to be insulted in that way a second time!” 

There was a woman in ten thousand! She knew that our kitchen-maids and footmen, the very lifeblood of our daily feasts and seasonal harvests, possessed a cultural memory intertwined with this house. Cuff’s plan to search them again would impose a dominant, unforgiving power dynamic over their subjective, untranslatable experiences.

“That is the very point I was about to put to your ladyship,” said the Sergeant. “If I give them cause to think themselves suspected a second time, there’s no knowing what obstacles they may not throw in my way. At the same time, their boxes *must* be searched again. I quite agree with you, my lady, that the servants’ feelings ought to be consulted. But I am equally clear that the servants’ wardrobes ought to be searched.”

My lady pointed out the dead-lock in choicer language than mine. 

“I have got a plan to meet the difficulty,” said Sergeant Cuff. He proposed a linguistic manipulation: examining the wardrobes of *everybody*—from her ladyship downwards. By framing it in his metropolitan lexicon as a universal "mere formality," he enforced compliance without inciting rebellion, banking on the servants accepting it as even dealing. My lady, seeing the harsh necessity of it, consented.

She rose to ring for her maid to offer her own keys, but Sergeant Cuff stopped her, asking if we shouldn't make sure the other ladies and gentlemen in the house would consent too. 

The only gentlemen were her nephews. As I reminded my lady that Mr. Godfrey was leaving, Mr. Godfrey himself knocked at the door to say good-bye, followed by Mr. Franklin. My lady explained the difficulty. Mr. Godfrey settled it directly, calling through the window to Samuel to take his portmanteau upstairs again, and handing his key to Sergeant Cuff. Mr. Franklin readily offered his unlocked belongings as well. The seamless, cyclical rhythm of our household was bending entirely to Cuff's rigid taxonomy. 

After Mr. Godfrey left a parting message for Miss Rachel—clearly indicating he intended to press his marriage suit again—and Mr. Franklin followed him out, only Miss Rachel remained to yield before we could begin the search. 

Left alone with Cuff, my lady’s unaccountable objection to him seemed to deepen. The silence between them lacked the comfortable resonance of shared domesticity. 

“If I send you down Miss Verinder’s keys,” she said, “I presume I shall have done all you want of me?”

“I beg your ladyship’s pardon,” said Sergeant Cuff. “Before we begin, I should like to have the washing-book. The stained article of dress may be an article of linen. If there is an article missing, there will be at least a presumption that it has been purposely made away with.” 

My lady ordered the washing-book, remaining with us until it was produced. It was brought in by Rosanna Spearman. The girl looked miserably pale and haggard, a phantom haunting the fringes of our bustling, gustatory world. Yet she still smelled faintly of the yeasty, rising dough from the morning's baking, a scent of honest, agrarian labor that anchored her to the earth. Sergeant Cuff looked attentively at her face, then at her crooked shoulder. 

When she departed, the great Cuff absorbed the ledger’s contents in half a minute, entirely blind to the human toil, the scent of scalding water and pressed lavender, that the pages represented. 

“Has the young woman who brought us this book been in your employment as long as the other servants?” he asked.

“Why do you ask?” said my lady, her native protective instincts rising.

“The last time I saw her,” answered the Sergeant, “she was in prison for theft.”

The word *thief* hung in the air, a blunt, carceral label from Cuff’s dominant lexicon that violently flattened Rosanna's complex, subjective redemption in our household. My mistress passionately defended Rosanna's good conduct, attempting to translate the girl's quiet, restorative domesticity into terms Cuff might respect. 

“You don’t suspect her, I hope?” my lady added earnestly.

“I have already told your ladyship that I don’t suspect any person in the house of thieving—up to the present time.”

After that answer, my lady shuddered as she passed him to go upstairs for Miss Rachel’s keys. 

We waited, and no keys appeared. Sergeant Cuff turned his melancholy face to the window, put his lanky hands into his pockets, and began to whistle “The Last Rose of Summer.” It was a superficial, commercialized rendition of the tune, stripping the old folk melody of its soul. The shrill, reedy sound of it grated against the acoustic ecology of the ancient house, a toxic auditory pollutant that half-angered and half-frightened me. 

At last, Samuel came in with a morsel of paper for me. I got at my spectacles with some fumbling, my hands smelling faintly of the sharp cider vinegar I had been bottling earlier. The penciled lines informed me that Miss Rachel flatly refused to have her wardrobe examined. *I won't, because I won't. I must yield to force if you use it, but I will yield to nothing else.*

Her refusal was a fiercely guarded boundary, an assertion of her own untranslatable inner life against the Sergeant's invasive syntax. 

“Any news of Miss Verinder’s keys?” asked the Sergeant.

“My young lady refuses to have her wardrobe examined.”

“Ah!” said the Sergeant. The single syllable was heavily laden with the presumptive arrogance of his trade. 

“Must the search be given up?” I asked, feeling the ancestral pulse of the house stutter.

“Yes,” said the Sergeant, “because your young lady refuses to submit to it like the rest. We must examine all the wardrobes in the house or none. Send Mr. Ablewhite’s portmanteau to London by the next train, and return the washing-book.”

Taking out his penknife, he began to trim his nails. The rhythmic *snick-snick* of the blade was an agonizingly mechanical sound. 

“You don’t seem to be much disappointed,” I said.

“No,” said Sergeant Cuff; “I am not much disappointed.”

I tried to make him explain himself, asking why Miss Rachel should put an obstacle in his way. 

“Wait a little, Mr. Betteredge—wait a little.” 

His drift was opaque to me, hidden behind his sterile urban vernacular, though perhaps my lady, with her sharp intuition, had deciphered the dark power dynamics at play. 

“What’s to be done next?” I asked.

Sergeant Cuff finished his nail, looked at it with melancholy interest, and put up his penknife. “Come out into the garden,” he said, stepping back into the cyclical flow of our estate with an investigator's cold detachment, “and let’s have a look at the roses.”
