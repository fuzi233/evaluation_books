“The spirits of your fathers
Shall start from every wave;
For the deck it was their field of fame,
And ocean was their grave.”

“So you see, my dear Mrs. Hawkins,” said Adrian Gilbert, leaning forward in his chair, “with silver already in hand—as your own eyes confirm—alongside lead, manganese, and copper ores, and above all this gossan—the Cornish call it so—I suspect not merely a matrix of metal, but the very crude essence, the *materia prima* of all metals. Mark me well. If my recipes—those I received from Doctor Dee—succeed even half as I expect, then I shall refine out the luna, the silver, and lay it aside. Then I shall transmute the rest into sol, gold. And thus Peru and Mexico become mere footnotes, while England rises to command the globe. Strange, no doubt. Remote, perhaps. But possible, madam—possible!”

“And what good is it to you, Mr. Gilbert?” she replied softly, her voice heavy with sorrow. “If you could find a philosopher’s stone that turned sinners into saints—well, that would be divine work. But only God’s grace can do that. And His grace often comes too late.”

She sighed.

“But truly, my dear sir,” she continued, “what if you succeed? What then?”

“Then the Comb Martin mine becomes a source of endless gold,” he answered, his eyes alight with vision. “It gives me wealth enough to pursue my North-West patent. Meanwhile, my brother Humphrey holds Newfoundland, and builds new ships each year—forests of pine are limitless—for my voyage to China.”

“Sir Humphrey has higher thoughts than gold in his heart, Mr. Adrian,” she said gently. “He has walked closely with God these seven years. I wish Captain John were the same.”

“And how do you know I have nothing better in mind than gold?” he asked. “Or indeed, what could be better? Is not gold the strength of Spain—the very engine of Antichrist? Only through gold, therefore, can we wrest power from them. You shake your head, but say this: which is better—making gold at home without bloodshed, or seizing it abroad by war and violence?”

“Oh, Mr. Gilbert!” she breathed, her hands clasped. “Is it not written that those who rush to riches pierce themselves through with many sorrows? Oh, Mr. Gilbert! God’s blessing cannot rest on such things.”

“Not on you, madam?” he countered. “I swear, when I cast Captain John Hawkins’s nativity, his star told me otherwise. Born under stormy planets, yes—but also under royal and fortunate ones.”

“Ah, Mr. Adrian,” she murmured, “I am a simple woman, and you a great philosopher. But I believe no star for the seaman shines brighter than the Star of Bethlehem. That one brings peace on earth and goodwill to men—not arms like that.” She pointed upward, to the carved oak beam where the crest was displayed: the *Demi-Moor proper, bound*, granted long ago by Clarencieux Hervey to her husband.

“Ah, Mr. Gilbert,” she whispered, “since he first sailed to Guinea after those poor negroes, my heart has known little joy. And the very day that crest was hung in our new house, the parson read the first lesson—and there it was: ‘Woe to him that buildeth his house by iniquity, and his chambers by wrong. Shalt thou live because thou closest thyself in cedar?’ It burned into my ears like fire, into my heart like lead. Then came, ‘Did not thy father eat and drink, and do judgment and justice? Then it was well with him.’ I thought of good old Captain Will. And now—now I carry those negroes on my soul from dawn till dusk. We are grand now, money flows fast—but the Lord will demand their blood at our hands. He will!”

“My dearest madam,” he said, his voice softening, “who could prosper more than you? If your husband once followed the Spaniards too closely in regard to those people—though I do not deny it—he was punished swiftly. He lost ships, men, everything but life at St. Juan d’Ulloa.”

“Ay, yes,” she said. “That gave me some comfort—especially when the queen, God save her tender heart, rebuked him for pitying the poor wretches. But it did not change him. He grows like the rest now—greedy to gain, stingy to spend. Always plotting, always fretting. Envious of Drake, jealous of others who rise higher. Gold, gold—nothing but gold on every tongue. Ah! I remember when Plymouth was a quiet, God-fearing place. But ever since John, Sir Francis, and poor Mr. Oxenham found the way to the Indies, it has been a sorrowful town. Not a sailor’s wife but cries, ‘Give, give!’ like the daughters of the horse-leech. Every woman drives her husband across the seas to bring her back money to waste on hoods and farthingales, to strut with outstretched necks and wanton eyes. Soon they’ll learn worse, for the sake of profit. But the Lord’s hand will strike at their crisping-pins and mufflers, just as it struck the Jews of old. Oh, dear me!”

They sat in a low-ceilinged room in Plymouth, panelled in oak, richly furnished with carvings, gilding, and coats of arms. On the sideboard rested Spanish silver and gold vessels; strange birds, animal skins, and rough maps of coastlines adorned the walls. Above the fireplace, beneath the portrait of old Captain Will Hawkins—favored by Henry VIII—hung the Spanish ensign captured by Captain John at Rio de la Hacha fifteen years prior. With two hundred men, he had seized the town against a thousand Spanish soldiers, and triumphantly watered his ship at the enemy’s wells.

The man was tall and fair, with a broad forehead furrowed by study, eyes dimmed from long hours over crucibles and furnaces. The woman, once comely, was aged and worn—like so many sailors’ wives, shaped by sorrow. Many sad days had passed for her. Though John Hawkins, port-admiral of Plymouth and patriarch of British shipbuilding, was faithful and forgiving, he was stubborn, ruthless, and far from a consistent follower of God. And darker days still awaited her.

Nine years hence, she would be asked to name her son’s new ship. She would christen it *The Repentance*, offering no explanation—only that “Repentance was the best ship in which we could sail to the harbor of heaven.” Queen Elizabeth, displeased with the name, would rename it *The Dainty*, perhaps with a sneer at her son Richard Hawkins, the perfect seaman and Euphuist afloat. And she would watch that gallant vessel sail westward, around the world, as countless others had done before. Then she would wait—like so many mothers before her—for the sail that never returned. Faint news would come: her son fighting three great Armadas, wounded, imprisoned, broken, languishing in Spanish dungeons for years.

And worse was yet to come: a fleet rounding the Ram Head, not with drum and trumpet, but with solemn minute-guns, flags at half-mast, announcing that her terrible husband had died—his heart shattered by failure and fatigue, his body laid beside Drake beneath distant tropical skies.

If, at the end of her long life, a ray of sunlight broke through—when her son Richard returned from prison, knighted for valor, made privy councillor for wisdom—then soon, oh so soon, the shadow would return. Her son would drop dead, some say at the council table, leaving behind shattered fortunes and unfulfilled dreams. The stormy star of that bold line would set forever. Lady Hawkins would bow her weary head and die, the groans of those stolen negroes echoing in her ears—having lived long enough to see her husband’s youthful sin become a national institution, a curse upon generations unborn.

Why, then, did she open her heart so freely to Adrian Gilbert, with a frankness she would never have shown her own family? Perhaps because Adrian, like his brothers Humphrey and Raleigh, was filled with lofty, tender passions—dreams that stirred the hearts of lonely women. So it was. And Adrian, ashamed of his own ambition, sat silent for a moment, staring at her.

“The Lord be with you, dearest lady,” he said at last. “Strange how you women sit at home, loving and suffering, while we men rush forth to break our hearts—and yours—against rocks of our own making. Ah, well—if it were not for Scripture, I might think Adam, not Eve, plucked the forbidden fruit.”

“We women,” she replied softly, “bear the burden of it all our lives.”

“You always remind me,” he said, “of my dear Mrs. Leigh of Burrough, and her wise counsel.”

“Do you see her often? I hear she is one of the Lord’s most precious vessels.”

“I would have done more than see her,” he said, blushing, “had she allowed me. But she lives only for the memory of her husband and the glory of her noble sons.”

As he spoke, the door opened. A man wrapped in a rough sea-gown stepped inside—none other than one of those noble sons.

Adrian paled.

“Amyas Leigh! What brings you here? How fares my brother? Where is the ship?”

“Your brother is well, Mr. Gilbert,” Amyas replied. “The *Golden Hind* has gone on to Dartmouth with Mr. Hayes. I came ashore to go north to Bideford before London. I stopped at Drake’s, but he was away.”

“The *Golden Hind*? Why has she returned so soon?”

“Yet welcome ever, sir,” said Mrs. Hawkins. “This is a great surprise. Captain John expected you not until next year.”

Amyas remained silent.

“Something is wrong!” Adrian exclaimed. “Speak!”

Amyas tried, but could not.

“Will you drive a man mad?” Adrian cried. “Has the adventure failed? You said my brother was well.”

“He is well.”

“Then what—why do you look at me like that?” Adrian sprang up, snatched the candle, and held it close to Amyas’s face.

Amyas’s lip trembled. He placed a hand on Adrian’s shoulder.

“Your great and glorious brother, sir,” he said, “is better off than in settling Newfoundland.”

“Dead?” Adrian shrieked.

“He is with the God whom he served.”

“He was always with Him, like Enoch. Do not tell me parables if you love me!”

“And like Enoch, he was not taken—God took him.”

Adrian clapped his hands over his forehead and leaned against the table.

“Go on, sir. Go on. God will give me strength to hear all.”

Slowly, Amyas told the tragic tale—already recounted too well by Mr. Hayes for me to repeat it fully. Of mutinous men, ruffians gathered haphazardly; conspiracies to seize the ships; plundering of fishing boats; desertions growing daily; licenses granted by the general to the lazy and fearful to return home.

“From him?” Adrian groaned. “Conspired against him? Deserted from him? Dotards, buzzards! Where would they have found another such leader?”

“Your illustrious brother, sir,” Amyas said, “was a great philosopher, but not a general.”

“General? Where was braver man?”

“Not on God’s earth. But bravery alone does not make a general. Cortez was brave—but if he had been only brave, Mexico would still be Mexico. The truth is, Cortez, like my Captain Drake, knew when to hang a man. Your brother did not.”

Amyas was likely right. Gilbert was capable of anger at cowardice or neglect—but too kind to punish it. He could devise the wisest plans, yet lacked the grueling, everyday discipline that great captains mastered.

Then Amyas continued: how they sailed from St. John’s to explore the southern coast; how Sir Humphrey insisted on going in the tiny *Squirrel*, ten tons, “overcharged with nettings, fights, and small ordnance”—not only because she was better suited for exploring creeks, but because he’d heard whispers among the crew that he feared the sea.

Then came disaster. Seven days after leaving Cape Raz, their largest ship, the *Delight*, after a night of revelry—“like the swan that singeth before her death”—struck unknown shoals. The *Golden Hind* and *Squirrel* veered clear just in time. All but fourteen perished, including Frank’s philosopher friend, poor Budaeus. The survivors, starving and half-naked, washed ashore in Newfoundland.

Worn down, the crews of the two remaining ships persuaded Sir Humphrey to turn for England on August 31st. As they turned about, they saw something extraordinary: “a very lion in shape, hair, and color, not swimming, but sliding on the water, whole body visible, turning his head, yawning wide, showing long teeth and glaring eyes. And as he passed, he roared—a horrible voice, like a lion bellowing.”

“What others thought, I leave unsaid. But the general took it as a good omen—joyful that he would fight such an enemy, even if it were the devil.”

“And the devil it was, doubtless,” said Adrian. “The roaring lion who goes about seeking whom he may devour.”

“He has not got your brother, at least,” Amyas said.

“No,” replied Mrs. Hawkins. “He roared in joy at how many souls would remain in heathen darkness without Sir Humphrey. God be with that good knight, and send all mariners where he is now!”

Then Amyas told the final scene: how, near the Azores, storms grew fiercer, with “terrible seas, breaking short and pyramid-wise.” On September 9th, the *Squirrel* nearly sank—then recovered.

“The general,” Amyas said, “sat abaft with a book in hand, calling out to us in the *Hind*: ‘We are as near heaven by sea as by land.’ He repeated it often—fitting words for a soldier resolute in Christ. I can testify he was.”

On Monday, at noon, the *Squirrel* led ahead. Suddenly, her lights went out. The watch shouted: “The general is lost!” And it was true. In that instant, the frigate was swallowed by the sea.

So ended Amyas Leigh’s story.

“Oh, my brother!” Adrian moaned. “The glory of his house, the pride of Devon!”

“What will the queen say?” Mrs. Hawkins asked, weeping.

“Tell me,” Adrian said, “did he wear the jewel when he died?”

“The queen’s jewel? He always wore it—and his own motto: *Mutare vel timere sperno*. He wore it. And he lived it.”

“Ay,” said Adrian. “To the last.”

“Not quite,” said Amyas. “He was a quieter man lately. As he once said, a better refiner than any aboard had followed him across the seas, purifying him in fire. And gold seven times refined—he was, when God, having finished His work, took him home.”

So the talk ended.

There was no doubt: the expedition had failed utterly. Adrian was ruined. Amyas had lost his investment.

Adrian rose, asking leave to retire. He must gather himself.

“Poor gentleman,” said Mrs. Hawkins. “It is little else he has left to collect.”

“Nor I,” said Amyas. “I was going to ask you to lend me one of your son’s shirts—and five pounds to get myself and my men home.”

“Five? Fifty, Mr. Leigh! God forbid John Hawkins’s wife should refuse a penny to a distressed mariner, especially one born a gentleman. But you must eat and drink.”

“It’s more than I’ve had in many a day worth speaking of.”

Amyas sat down in his tattered clothes and ate a generous supper. Mrs. Hawkins told him all she knew of his mother—whom Adrian had seen in London months before—and then naturally moved on to Bideford news.

“And by the by, Captain Leigh,” she said, “I have sad news from your place. I heard it from one who was there. You must know a Spanish captain, a prisoner—”

“The one I sent home from Smerwick?”

“You sent? Mercy on us! Then perhaps you’ve heard—”

“How can I have heard? What?”

“That he’s escaped—the villain!”

“Without paying his ransom?”

“I can’t say that. But a poor innocent young maid has gone with him—one Salterne’s daughter—the Popish serpent!”

“Rose Salterne, the mayor’s daughter, the Rose of Torridge!”

“That’s her. Bless your dear soul, what ails you?”

Amyas sank back in his seat as if struck. But he regained himself before Mrs. Hawkins could rush to fetch cordials.

“Forgive me, madam,” he said. “I’m weak from the sea. And your ale has turned me a bit dizzy, I think.”

“Ay, yes,” she said. “It’s too strong until you’ve been ashore a while. Try the aqua vitae. My Captain John has it excellent—though a bit too fond of it, poor soul, Heaven forgive him!”

She poured strong brandy and water down his throat, despite his protests, and sent him to bed. But he did not sleep. After a restless night, he set off for Bideford, having secured funds from Mrs. Hawkins.
