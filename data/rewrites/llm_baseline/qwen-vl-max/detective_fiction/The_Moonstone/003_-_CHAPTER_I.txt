In the first volume of *Robinson Crusoe*, at page one hundred and twenty-nine, there is a line that reads: “Now I saw, though too late, the Folly of beginning a Work before we count the Cost, and before we judge rightly of our own Strength to go through with it.” Only yesterday, I opened my well-worn copy of that very book and fell upon those words by chance. And only this morning—May twenty-first, eighteen hundred and fifty—Mr. Franklin Blake, my lady’s nephew, came to call and spoke with me in these terms:

“Betteredge,” he said, “I’ve been to see the lawyer about some family affairs. Among other things, we discussed the disappearance of the Indian Diamond from my aunt’s house in Yorkshire two years ago. Mr. Bruff agrees with me: the truth of what happened should be set down in writing—and without delay.”

Not yet grasping his purpose, and always inclined to side with the lawyer for the sake of peace, I nodded and said I thought so too.

He continued: “In this matter of the Diamond, innocent people have already suffered under suspicion—as you know. In time, their reputations may suffer further if no clear record is left behind for future generations to consult. There can be no doubt that our strange family story must be told. And Mr. Bruff and I believe we’ve found the best way to tell it.”

That sounded satisfactory enough to him, no doubt. But I still couldn’t see where I fit into it.

“We have certain events to recount,” he went on, “and certain individuals who were present and capable of describing them. So the plan is this: each of us will write our part of the story—only as far as our own experience reaches, and no further. We’ll begin with how the Diamond came into the hands of my uncle Herncastle when he served in India fifty years ago. That part I already have in an old family document, written by an eyewitness. Then comes the tale of how the Diamond reached my aunt’s house in Yorkshire two years past—and how it vanished within less than twelve hours. No one knows more about what happened during that time than you do, Betteredge. You must take up the pen and begin the account.”

And so, in those words, I was made aware of my role in the matter.

If you’re curious what I did next, let me tell you plainly: I reacted just as any sensible man might. I modestly declared myself wholly unfit for such a task—and all the while, privately, I felt quite certain I could manage it, provided I gave myself a fair chance.

Mr. Franklin, I suspect, read my true thoughts in my face. He refused to accept my humility and insisted I give my abilities their due.

Two hours have passed since he left. As soon as he turned away, I went straight to my writing desk to begin. But there I have sat ever since—useless, despite my confidence—seeing exactly what Robinson Crusoe saw: the folly of starting a work without measuring its cost or judging whether one has the strength to finish it.

Let me add this: I opened *Robinson Crusoe* at that precise passage only the day before I rashly agreed to write this account. If that isn’t prophecy, then what is?

I am not superstitious. I’ve read many books in my life, and though I’m now seventy, I still possess a sharp memory and sturdy legs. Please don’t mistake me for a fool when I say that no book like *Robinson Crusoe* was ever written before, nor will ever be again. For years, I’ve turned to it—usually with a pipe in hand—and found it my steadfast companion through every trial of mortal life. When my spirits sink—*Robinson Crusoe*. When I need counsel—*Robinson Crusoe*. When my wife once tormented me; when I’ve overindulged lately—*Robinson Crusoe*. I’ve worn out six solid copies of the book serving me faithfully. On my lady’s last birthday, she gifted me a seventh. I celebrated with a little too much wine—and *Robinson Crusoe* brought me back to reason. Four shillings and sixpence, bound in blue, with a picture inside. A bargain, truly.

Still, this doesn’t look much like the start of the Diamond’s story, does it? It seems I’ve wandered off into some distant land, chasing shadows. Let us try again. Fresh paper, clean mind, and my deepest respects to you.
