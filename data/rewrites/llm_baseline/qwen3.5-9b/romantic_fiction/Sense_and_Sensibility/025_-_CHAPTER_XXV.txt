Although Mrs. Jennings spent much of the year visiting her children and friends, she maintained a permanent residence of her own. Following the death of her late husband—a successful merchant who operated in a less fashionable district of the city—she had taken up lodging every winter in a house situated near Portman Square. As January approached, she began to turn her thoughts toward returning to this home, and one day, she abruptly and surprisingly invited the elder Miss Dashwoods to join her.

Elinor, failing to notice the shifting expression on her sister's face or the animated look that betrayed her lack of indifference to the proposal, immediately offered a grateful yet firm refusal. She believed she was voicing their shared sentiment. "We have made a determined resolution not to leave our mother at this time of year," she stated.

Mrs. Jennings received the rejection with surprise and immediately renewed her invitation. "Oh, Lord! I am certain your mother can spare you quite well, and I *beg* you to favor me with your company. My heart is truly set on it. Do not imagine you will be any inconvenience; I shall not put myself out of my way for you. It will simply involve sending Betty by the coach, and I trust I can afford that. The three of us shall travel comfortably in my chaise. Furthermore, once we are in town, if you prefer not to follow where I go, you are welcome to stay with one of my daughters. I am sure your mother will not object; having successfully managed to send my own children away, I believe I am a fit person to take charge of you. If I fail to secure a marriage for either of you before I am done with you, it shall not be my fault. I shall speak highly of you to all the eligible young men; you may depend on it."

"I suspect," interjected Sir John, "that Miss Marianne would not object to such a scheme if her elder sister would join her. It is terribly unfair that she should be denied even a little pleasure simply because Miss Dashwood refuses. I would advise you both to depart for town as soon as you grow weary of Barton, without telling Miss Dashwood anything about it."

"Nay!" cried Mrs. Jennings. "I am absolutely delighted to have Miss Marianne's company, regardless of whether Miss Dashwood accompanies her. The more the merrier, as they say. I thought it would be more comforting for them to be together; if they tire of me, they can converse and laugh at my peculiarities behind my back. However, I must have one or the other, if not both. Good heavens, how can I endure poking around alone, accustomed as I have been until this winter to have Charlotte with me? Come, Miss Marianne, let us seal the bargain with a handshake. Should Miss Dashwood change her mind later, so much the better."

"I thank you, madam, sincerely," said Marianne warmly. "Your invitation secures my gratitude forever, and it would bring me such happiness—almost the greatest happiness I am capable of feeling—to accept it. Yet, my mother, my dearest, kindest mother... I recognize the validity of what Elinor has argued. If our absence were to make her less happy or less comfortable—Oh! No, nothing should tempt me to leave her. It must not, and it cannot be a struggle."

Mrs. Jennings reiterated her assurance that Mrs. Dashwood could spare them perfectly well. Elinor, now understanding her sister and seeing how her eagerness to reunite with Willoughby had rendered her indifferent to nearly everything else, ceased her direct opposition. Instead, she deferred the matter entirely to her mother's decision. From her mother, however, she scarcely expected support in preventing a visit she disapproved of for Marianne and one she personally wished to avoid due to specific reasons. Whatever Marianne desired, her mother was eager to facilitate; Elinor could not expect to sway her toward caution regarding an affair in which she had never instilled distrust. Moreover, she dared not reveal the motives behind her own reluctance to travel to London.

It struck Elinor as particularly striking that Marianne, despite being fastidious and thoroughly aware of Mrs. Jennings' manners, chose to overlook every potential discomfort and disregard anything likely to wound her sensitive feelings in pursuit of a single objective. This demonstrated the profound importance of that goal to Marianne, a fact Elinor found difficult to reconcile with everything that had transpired between them.

Upon learning of the invitation, Mrs. Dashwood, convinced that the trip would provide immense amusement for both her daughters and perceiving through her affectionate attentions just how deeply Marianne's heart was involved, refused to hear of their declining the offer on her account. She insisted they accept it immediately and, with her characteristic cheerfulness, began anticipating various advantages for everyone resulting from this separation.

"I am delighted with the plan!" she exclaimed. "It is precisely what I could wish. Margaret and I shall benefit as much as you. Once you and the Middletons depart, we shall settle down quietly and happily with our books and music! You will find Margaret greatly improved upon your return. I also have a small plan to alter your bedrooms, which can now be executed without inconveniencing anyone. It is very right that you should visit London; I believe every young woman of your station ought to be acquainted with its manners and amusements. You will be under the care of a motherly, kind woman, and I have no doubt of her goodness. In all likelihood, you will see your brother. Regardless of his faults—or those of his wife, considering who his father was—I cannot bear to have you so completely estranged from one another."

"Though, with your usual concern for our happiness," Elinor remarked, "you have removed every obstacle you could conceive, there remains one objection which, in my opinion, cannot be so easily dismissed." Marianne's countenance fell.

"And what," asked Mrs. Dashwood, "does my dear, prudent Elinor intend to suggest? What formidable obstacle is she bringing forth now? Pray, do not mention the expense."

"My objection is this: while I hold Mrs. Jennings' heart in high regard, her society offers us neither pleasure nor the social standing we require."

"That is very true," replied her mother, "but apart from accompanying others, you will have little interaction with her socially anyway, and you will almost invariably appear in public with Lady Middleton."

"If Elinor is deterred by her dislike of Mrs. Jennings," said Marianne, "it need not prevent *my* acceptance. I have no such scruples, and I am confident I could endure every unpleasantness associated with it with minimal effort."

Elinor could not suppress a smile at this display of indifference toward the manners of someone she had often struggled to persuade Marianne to treat with basic politeness. Resolving internally that if her sister persisted, she would go as well—deeming it improper for Marianne to rely solely on her own judgment or for Mrs. Jennings to be left to Marianne's mercy during their domestic hours—she felt more easily reconciled to this decision. She recalled that Edward Ferrars, according to Lucy's report, would not arrive in town until February, meaning their visit could conclude reasonably early.

"I will have you *both* go," declared Mrs. Dashwood. "These objections are nonsense. You will derive great pleasure from being in London, especially from being together. If Elinor would ever condescend to anticipate enjoyment, she would foresee it arising from various sources; perhaps she expects some from improving her acquaintance with her sister-in-law's family."

Elinor had long wished for an opportunity to gently undermine her mother's reliance on the attachments of Edward and herself, hoping the eventual revelation of the whole truth would be less shocking. Seizing this moment, though she knew success was unlikely, she forced herself to begin her design as calmly as possible. "I like Edward Ferrars very much and shall always be glad to see him," she said. "But regarding the rest of the family, it is a matter of perfect indifference to me whether I am ever known to them or not."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled and said nothing. Marianne looked up in astonishment, and Elinor realized she might as well have remained silent.

After very little further discussion, it was finally settled that the invitation would be fully accepted. Mrs. Jennings received the news with boundless joy, offering countless assurances of kindness and care; it was a source of pleasure not only for her. Sir John was equally delighted; for a man whose chief anxiety was the fear of solitude, acquiring two additional companions in London was a significant gain. Even Lady Middleton made the effort to feign delight, stretching herself somewhat; as for the Miss Steeles, particularly Lucy, they had never been happier in their lives than this intelligence made them.

Elinor submitted to the arrangement contrary to her wishes with less reluctance than anticipated. Regarding herself, it became a matter of indifference whether she traveled to town or not. Seeing her mother so thoroughly pleased and her sister so exhilarated—restored to her usual animation and elevated to a gaiety surpassing her normal state—Elinor could not be dissatisfied with the cause and hardly allowed herself to doubt the outcome. Marianne's joy bordered on ecstasy; her spirits were so agitated and her eagerness to depart so intense that her only anchor to calmness was her reluctance to leave her mother. At the moment of parting, her grief over this loss was excessive. Her mother's sorrow was hardly less, and Elinor was the only one of the three who viewed the separation as anything short of eternal.
