My hand fell from the curtain as if the cloth itself had scorched me. Yet it was not—pray understand it was not—the humiliation of my posture, crouched like a thief behind household drapery, that burned highest in my thoughts. It was the sentence I had heard, dropped by Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite with that dreadful steadiness which makes a resolution sound like a verdict:

He would do it—today.

Do what? I never once stopped to wonder why he was absent from the concert, though the violins below must at that moment have been drawing their bright, obedient threads through the air. I thought only of what new fall might be preparing for a man who had so lately stood—ah, how movingly he had stood!—like a tower among charitable causes, to be leaned on by Ladies’ Committees as wheat leans on a good season.

Would he turn renegade? Would he desert the Mothers’-Small-Clothes? Would the Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision find itself suddenly without its favourite orator—without that angelic smile which seemed, like a well-glazed tart, to promise sweetness before one had even tasted? These were my terrors—pious terrors, if you please—when another voice cut in through the folds of my hiding-place.

A voice loud, bold, and lamentably deficient in every feminine modulation: Miss Rachel Verinder’s.

“Why have you come up here, Godfrey?” she said. “Why didn’t you go into the library?”

He answered with a low laugh—soft as the last fizz in a poured glass when the bubbles begin to die. “Miss Clack is in the library.”

“Clack in the library!” She sat down at once on the ottoman in the back drawing-room. “You are quite right, Godfrey. We had much better stop here.”

In the instant before, I had been fever-hot, uncertain what my next duty might be. I grew cold all over now, and certainty settled on me like a damp napkin laid to still an impatient dough. To show myself, after what I had already heard, was impossible. To retreat was equally impossible, unless I proposed to exit by the chimney. A martyrdom—plain, domestic martyrdom—was before me.

In justice to myself (for it is always in justice to myself that I must speak), I adjusted the curtains so that a slit of vision and a channel of sound remained. Then I met what was coming with the composure of a primitive Christian—one who is obliged, in suppressed hysterics, to hold on to something.

“Don’t sit on the ottoman,” Miss Rachel said. “Bring a chair, Godfrey. I like people to be opposite to me when I talk to them.”

He took the nearest seat, a low chair quite unequal to his tall frame. I had never seen his legs to such disadvantage; they looked, poor things, like long loaves forced into a shallow tin.

“Well?” she went on. “What did you say to them?”

“Just what you said, dear Rachel, to me.”

“That mamma was not at all well today? And that I didn’t quite like leaving her to go to the concert?”

“Those were the words. They were grieved to lose you at the concert, but they quite understood. All sent their love; and all expressed a cheering belief that Lady Verinder’s indisposition would soon pass away.”

“You don’t think it’s serious, do you, Godfrey?”

“Far from it! In a few days, I feel quite sure, all will be well again.”

“I think so, too,” she said. “I was a little frightened at first, but I think so too. It was very kind to go and make my excuses for me to people who are almost strangers to you. But why not have gone with them to the concert? It seems very hard that you should miss the music too.”

“Don’t say that, Rachel! If you only knew how much happier I am—here, with you!”

He clasped his hands and looked at her; and, in turning, he turned toward my curtain. My stomach sank in a clean, sick drop, as when a cook lifts a lid and finds the milk has boiled over—because there on his face was the very same pathetic expression with which he had charmed me while pleading at Exeter Hall. It was the expression that seems to say: I am suffering for the world; only, now, it was suffering for Rachel.

“It’s hard to get over one’s bad habits, Godfrey,” she answered. “But do try to get over the habit of paying compliments—do, to please me.”

“I never paid you a compliment, Rachel, in my life. Successful love may sometimes use the language of flattery, I admit. But hopeless love, dearest, always speaks the truth.”

He drew his chair close and took her hand when he said “hopeless love.” There was a silence—so still that I noticed, beyond the walls, the house’s own small music: a faint settling of boards, a far-off murmur of the concert like a sea in another weather, and—worse than either—the modern city’s distant grinding undertone, that eternal iron rasp of wheels and commerce. Even in a fine house, it seeps in; and once it does, it seems to make every tender word a little more desperate, as if affection must raise its voice to compete.

I understood then what his earlier words had meant. “I’ll do it today.” Alas, the most rigid propriety could scarcely fail to observe that he was doing it now.

“Have you forgotten what we agreed on, Godfrey, when you spoke to me in the country? We agreed that we were to be cousins, and nothing more.”

“I break the agreement, Rachel, every time I see you.”

“Then don’t see me.”

“Quite useless! I break the agreement every time I think of you. Oh, Rachel! how kindly you told me, only the other day, that my place in your estimation was a higher place than it had ever been yet! Am I mad to build the hopes I do on those dear words? Am I mad to dream of some future day when your heart may soften to me? Don’t tell me so, if I am! Leave me my delusion, dearest! I must have that to cherish, and to comfort me, if I have nothing else!”

His voice trembled. He put his white handkerchief to his eyes. Exeter Hall again! Nothing wanting but the cheers, the glass of water, and the practiced pause that invites applause. And yet—here there was only one listener; and I, behind my curtain, a second, hidden and unwilling.

Even Miss Rachel’s hard nature softened. She leaned a little nearer.

“Are you really sure, Godfrey, that you are so fond of me as that?”

“Sure! You know what I was, Rachel. Let me tell you what I am. I have lost every interest in life, but my interest in you. A transformation has come over me which I can’t account for, myself. Would you believe it? My charitable business is an unendurable nuisance to me; and when I see a Ladies’ Committee now, I wish myself at the uttermost ends of the earth!”

If apostasy ever had a taste, it was in that sentence: sharp as vinegar where one expects honey. I thought of the Mothers’-Small-Clothes—folded, counted, stored like provisions against moral famine. I thought of the Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision and the other Societies too numerous to mention, all of them kneaded and risen on this man’s influence as bread rises on yeast. I thought of the poor struggling Female Boards who, figuratively speaking, drew the breath of their business-life through the nostrils of Mr. Godfrey—of that same Mr. Godfrey who now called our work a “nuisance,” and wished himself at the ends of the earth when he found himself in our company.

It tried my discipline before I could swallow my indignation dry and keep it down. Yet I did not lose a syllable.

“You have made your confession,” Miss Rachel said. “I wonder whether it would cure you of your unhappy attachment to me, if I made mine?”

He started. I started too. For one instant, both he and I thought we were about to hear the secret of the Moonstone.

“Would you think, to look at me,” she went on, “that I am the wretchedest girl living? It’s true, Godfrey. What greater wretchedness can there be than to live degraded in your own estimation? That is my life now.”

“My dear Rachel! it’s impossible you can have any reason to speak of yourself in that way!”

“How do you know I have no reason?”

“Can you ask me the question! I know it, because I know you. Your silence, dearest, has never lowered you in the estimation of your true friends. The disappearance of your precious birthday gift may seem strange; your unexplained connection with that event may seem stranger still—”

“Are you speaking of the Moonstone, Godfrey?”

“I certainly thought that you referred—”

“I referred to nothing of the sort. I can hear of the loss of the Moonstone, let who will speak of it, without feeling degraded in my own estimation. If the story of the Diamond ever comes to light, it will be known that I accepted a dreadful responsibility; it will be known that I involved myself in the keeping of a miserable secret—but it will be as clear as the sun at noon-day that I did nothing mean! You have misunderstood me, Godfrey. It’s my fault for not speaking more plainly. Cost me what it may, I will be plainer now.

“Suppose you were not in love with me? Suppose you were in love with some other woman?”

“Yes?”

“Suppose you discovered that woman to be utterly unworthy of you? Suppose you were quite convinced that it was a disgrace to you to waste another thought on her? Suppose the bare idea of ever marrying such a person made your face burn, only with thinking of it?”

“Yes?”

“And, suppose, in spite of all that—you couldn’t tear her from your heart? Suppose the feeling she had roused in you (in the time when you believed in her) was not a feeling to be hidden? Suppose the love this wretch had inspired in you—Oh, how can I find words to say it in! How can I make a man understand that a feeling which horrifies me at myself, can be a feeling that fascinates me at the same time? It’s the breath of my life, Godfrey, and it’s the poison that kills me—both in one!

“Go away! I must be out of my mind to talk as I am talking now. No! you mustn’t leave me—you mustn’t carry away a wrong impression. I must say what is to be said in my own defence. Mind this! He doesn’t know—he never will know, what I have told you. I will never see him—I don’t care what happens—I will never, never, never see him again! Don’t ask me his name! Don’t ask me any more!

“Let’s change the subject. Are you doctor enough, Godfrey, to tell me why I feel as if I was stifling for want of breath? Is there a form of hysterics that bursts into words instead of tears? I dare say! What does it matter? You will get over any trouble I have caused you, easily enough now. I have dropped to my right place in your estimation, haven’t I? Don’t notice me! Don’t pity me! For God’s sake, go away!”

She turned suddenly and beat her hands on the back of the ottoman—an ugly, desperate sound, like someone striking a loaf that refuses to rise. Her head dropped on the cushions; she burst into crying.

Before I had time to be properly shocked, Mr. Godfrey committed an act so entirely unforeseen that my very sense of propriety seemed to lose its language for naming it.

He fell on his knees at her feet—on both knees, I solemnly declare. Then he put his arms round her. And then—by two words only—he struck her as if with electricity.

“Noble creature!”

He said it with one of those practiced bursts that have made his fame. She sat still, either thunderstruck or fascinated—I do not know which—without even attempting to put his arms back where his arms ought to have been. As for me, I was so bewildered as to whether I ought first to close my eyes or stop my ears, that I did neither. I held the curtain, because in suppressed hysterics one must hold on to something.

“Yes,” he said, still in that evangelical cadence which turns common syllables into a kind of hymn, “you are a noble creature! A woman who can speak the truth, for the truth’s own sake—a woman who will sacrifice her pride, rather than sacrifice an honest man who loves her—is the most priceless of all treasures. When such a woman marries, if her husband only wins her esteem and regard, he wins enough to ennoble his whole life. You have spoken, dearest, of your place in my estimation. Judge what that place is—when I implore you on my knees, to let the cure of your poor wounded heart be my care. Rachel! will you honour me, will you bless me, by being my wife?”

By this time I should certainly have stopped my ears—if Miss Rachel had not answered him in the first sensible words I had ever heard from her.

“Godfrey,” she said, “you must be mad!”

“I never spoke more reasonably, dearest—in your interests, as well as in mine. Look for a moment to the future. Is your happiness to be sacrificed to a man who has never known how you feel towards him, and whom you are resolved never to see again? Is it not your duty to yourself to forget this ill-fated attachment? and is forgetfulness to be found in the life you are leading now? You have tried that life, and you are wearying of it already.

“Surround yourself with nobler interests than the wretched interests of the world. A heart that loves and honours you; a home whose peaceful claims and happy duties win gently on you day by day—try the consolation, Rachel, which is to be found there! I don’t ask for your love—I will be content with your affection and regard. Let the rest be left, confidently left, to your husband’s devotion, and to Time that heals even wounds as deep as yours.”

She began to yield—she, who had been raised (oh, what a bringing-up!) with so little understanding of the sacred rituals that keep a household’s soul in order: not merely how one speaks, but how one listens; not merely what one names, but what one dares not name; and how easily the dominant words of the world—wife, husband, duty—can be pressed like a seal over feelings that have no fitting language.

“Don’t tempt me, Godfrey,” she said. “I am wretched enough and reckless enough as it is. Don’t tempt me to be more wretched and more wreckless still!”

“One question, Rachel. Have you any personal objection to me?”

“I! I always liked you. After what you have just said to me, I should be insensible indeed if I didn’t respect and admire you as well.”

“Do you know many wives, my dear Rachel, who respect and admire their husbands? And yet they and their husbands get on very well. How many brides go to the altar with hearts that would bear inspection by the men who take them there? And yet it doesn’t end unhappily—somehow or other the nuptial establishment jogs on.

“The truth is, that women try marriage as a Refuge, far more numerously than they are willing to admit; and, what is more, they find that marriage has justified their confidence in it. Look at your own case once again. At your age, and with your attractions, is it possible for you to sentence yourself to a single life? Trust my knowledge of the world—nothing is less possible. It is merely a question of time. You may marry some other man, some years hence. Or you may marry the man, dearest, who is now at your feet, and who prizes your respect and admiration above the love of any other woman on the face of the earth.”

“Gently, Godfrey! you are putting something into my head which I never thought of before. You are tempting me with a new prospect, when all my other prospects are closed before me. I tell you again, I am miserable enough and desperate enough, if you say another word, to marry you on your own terms. Take the warning, and go!”

“I won’t even rise from my knees, till you have said yes!”

“If I say yes you will repent, and I shall repent, when it is too late!”

“We shall both bless the day, darling, when I pressed, and when you yielded.”

“Do you feel as confidently as you speak?”

“You shall judge for yourself. I speak from what I have seen in my own family. Tell me what you think of our household at Frizinghall. Do my father and mother live unhappily together?”

“Far from it—so far as I can see.”

“When my mother was a girl, Rachel (it is no secret in the family), she had loved as you love—she had given her heart to a man who was unworthy of her. She married my father, respecting him, admiring him, but nothing more. Your own eyes have seen the result. Is there no encouragement in it for you and for me?”

“You won’t hurry me, Godfrey?”

“My time shall be yours.”

“You won’t ask me for more than I can give?”

“My angel! I only ask you to give me yourself.”

“Take me!”

In those two words she accepted him.

He broke into a burst—an unholy rapture this time—drew her nearer and nearer until her face touched his; and then—No. I cannot prevail upon myself to write it plainly. Let me say only that I tried to close my eyes before it happened, and that I was one moment too late. I had calculated—fool that I was—on resistance. She submitted. To any right-feeling woman, that is enough said.

By then they understood each other so completely that I expected to see them walk off, arm in arm, to be married before the kettle could reboil. But Mr. Godfrey’s next words showed there remained one trifling formality.

He seated himself—unbidden this time—on the ottoman by her side.

“Shall I speak to your dear mother?” he asked. “Or will you?”

Miss Rachel refused both.

“Let my mother hear nothing from either of us, until she is better. I wish it to be kept a secret for the present, Godfrey. Go now, and come back this evening. We have been here alone together quite long enough.”

She rose, and in rising looked—at last—toward the little room where my martyrdom continued.

“Who has drawn those curtains?” she exclaimed. “The room is close enough, as it is, without keeping the air out of it in that way.”

She came to them. Her hand lifted—an inch from the fabric—and my discovery stood as certain as a spilled stain on a white cloth.

At that instant, the sound of hurried feet and a young man’s voice on the stairs tore through the house like a knife through paper. It was unmistakably the voice of alarm—raw, stripped of all servantly polish.

“Miss Rachel! where are you, Miss Rachel?”

She sprang back from the curtains and ran to the door. The fresh-coloured young footman blundered just inside; his ruddy complexion had drained away to a pale dough.

“Please to come downstairs, Miss! My lady has fainted, and we can’t bring her to again.”

In another moment I was alone—free to go down, and, by providence, still unobserved.

Mr. Godfrey passed me in the hall, hurrying out to fetch the doctor. “Go in, and help them!” he said, pointing.

I found Miss Rachel on her knees by the sofa, with Lady Verinder’s head on her bosom. One look at my aunt’s face—knowing what I knew—warned me of the dreadful truth; it was as if the usual living sounds around her had been stopped, and the room’s air had thickened into silence.

I kept my thoughts to myself until the doctor came. He arrived quickly enough, sent Miss Rachel out at once, and then told the rest of us—plainly—that Lady Verinder was no more.

Serious persons, in search of proofs of hardened scepticism, may be interested to hear that he showed no remorse whatever when he looked at me.

At a later hour I peeped into the breakfast-room and the library. My aunt had died without opening one of the letters I had addressed to her.

The shock of that discovery was so great that it never occurred to me—until some days afterwards—that she had also died without giving me my little legacy.
