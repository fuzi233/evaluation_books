It is our pleasant duty now to reveal the secret that Mr. Lambert whispered to his wife at the close of the previous chapter—a secret that brought such joy to every member of the Oakhurst household. With the hay safely stored and the corn not yet ready for harvest, the farm horses stood idle. “Why,” asked Colonel Lambert, “should we not put them into the coach and make a journey to Tunbridge Wells? We could stop by Westerham on the way and visit dear friend Wolfe.”

Mamma embraced the idea with delight—and so did the Colonel himself. The children danced with excitement. The girls rushed off to gather their finest attire: calamancoes, paduasoys, falbalas, furbelows, capes, cardinals, sacks, negligees, solitaires, caps, ribbons, mantuas, clocked stockings, and high-heeled shoes—indeed, every article of finery they possessed. Mamma unearthed her most elegant gowns from the presses, garments reserved only for rare and solemn occasions, which would soon return to lavender-scented silence. The Colonel donned his laced hat and waistcoat, and fastened his silver-hilted hanger at his side. Charley wore a holiday suit once belonging to his father—the very coat in which the Colonel had been married—though Mrs. Lambert had to cut it down, not without a pang of sorrow.

Ball and Dumpling, the family’s best horses, had their manes and tails tied with ribbons. Chump, the old white cart-horse, took his place as unicorn leader, helping pull the carriage up the first five miles of hilly road from Oakhurst to Westerham. The vehicle itself was ancient, believed to have carried King George I from Greenwich to London upon his arrival as monarch. It had belonged to Mr. Lambert’s father and had long been regarded by the family as one of the grandest coaches in all the realm.

Brian, the family coachman—and, let it be confessed, also their ploughman—sat proudly on the box seat beside young Charley. Their valuables were packed in imperials atop the coach. The Colonel’s pistols were tucked into the carriage pockets, and a blunderbuss hung behind the box within Brian’s reach—he was an old soldier, after all. Yet no highwayman dared molest them, nor did any innkeeper demand tolls from the Colonel, whose purse was slender but whose family was large. He was not to be plundered by those who preyed on the king’s highway.

A modest lodging had been arranged by young Colonel Wolfe, at the house where he usually stayed. He rode ahead with them on horseback, and arrived just in time to welcome them. Their rooms happened to be directly across from Madame Bernstein’s residence. On Saturday evening, as the Oakhurst family settled in, they saw chair after chair arriving at the Baroness’s door, each disgorging powdered gentlemen and brocaded ladies for one of her famed card parties.

The sun had not yet set—for our ancestors began their diversions early, indulging in food, drink, or cards from three o’clock onward until dawn. From their window, the young country ladies and their mother watched the guests arrive. Colonel Wolfe identified many of them for the family. It was almost as enjoyable as attending the party themselves, Hetty and Theo agreed. Not only could they see the arrivals, but through the open casements of the Baroness’s house, they could glimpse the guests inside.

Some of these figures we’ve already met. When the Duchess of Queensberry passed by, and Mr. Wolfe explained who she was, Martin Lambert recited several lines from Mat Prior’s poem about “Kitty, beautiful and young.”

“Think that old lady was once like you, girls!” the Colonel remarked.

“Like us, papa? Well, certainly we never claimed to be beauties!” Miss Hetty declared, tossing her head.

“Yes, like you, you little baggage—like you right now, wanting to go to that drum yonder:
*Inflamed with rage at sad restraint
Which wise mamma ordained,
And sorely vexed to play the saint
Whilst wit and beauty reigned.*”

“We were never invited, papa,” said Theo. “And I’m sure if there’s no beauty worth seeing, the wit can’t be much worth hearing.”

“Oh, but Mat Prior is a rare poet!” insisted the Colonel. “Though mind you, girls, skip over any poems I’ve marked with a cross. A rare poet! And to think you’re seeing one of his heroines:
*Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way* (she always will, Mrs. Lambert!)—
*Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way,
Kitty at heart's desire
Obtained the chariot for a day,
And set the world on fire!*”

“I’m sure it must have been very inflammable,” murmured mamma.

“So it was, my dear, twenty years ago—much more so than today,” replied the Colonel.

“Nonsense, Mr. Lambert,” she said.

“Look! Look!” cried Hetty, rushing forward and pointing toward the square and the covered gallery leading to Madame Bernstein’s apartments, where a crowd of street urchins and idlers gathered.

“It’s Harry Warrington!” exclaimed Theo, waving a handkerchief—but Warrington didn’t see her.

The Virginian walked arm-in-arm with a portly clergyman in a crisp, rustling silk gown, and together they entered the Baroness’s door.

“I heard him preach a most admirable sermon here last Sunday,” said Mr. Wolfe. “A little theatrical, but striking and eloquent.”

“You seem to be here most Sundays, James,” observed Mrs. Lambert.

“And Monday, and Tuesday—right through to Saturday,” added the Colonel.

“See, Harry has already dressed himself up—his hair in buckle, and I have no doubt he’s going to the drum too.”

“I’d rather sit quietly on a Saturday evening,” said sober Mr. Wolfe. “Away from card-playing and scandal. But I confess, dear Mrs. Lambert, I am under orders. Shall I go across and send Mr. Warrington to you?”

“No,” said meek Mrs. Lambert. “Let him enjoy his evening. We’ll see him tomorrow. He won’t want to be disturbed among his fine company by us country folk.”

“I’m glad he’s with a clergyman who preaches so well,” said Theo softly. Her eyes seemed to say: *You see, good people, he is not so bad as you thought him, nor as I ever believed him to be.*

“The clergyman has a kind, handsome face,” someone noted.

“Here comes a greater clergyman!” cried Mr. Wolfe. “It’s my Lord of Salisbury, with his blue ribbon, and a chaplain following behind.”

“And whom in mercy’s name do we have here?” broke in Mrs. Lambert as a gilded sedan-chair, adorned with no fewer than five earl’s coronets and carried by bearers in richly laced clothes, approached the Baroness’s door. Three footmen in matching livery preceded it.

The Bishop, who had been about to enter, stopped and bowed deeply, stepping back with reverence as the sedan-chair drew near. He offered his hand to the woman who stepped out.

“Who on earth is this?” asked Mrs. Lambert.

“Sprechen sie Deutsch? Ja, meinherr. Nichts verstand,” joked the Colonel.

“Pooh, Martin,” said mamma.

“Well, if you can’t understand High Dutch, my love, how can I help it? Your education was neglected at school. Can you understand heraldry? I know you can.”

“I make,” cried Charley, reciting the shield, “three merions on a field or, with an earl’s coronet.”

“A countess’s coronet, my son,” corrected the Colonel. “The Countess of Yarmouth, my son.”

“And pray who is she?” asked the children.

“It has always been the custom of our sovereigns to elevate persons of distinction,” the Colonel continued gravely. “This eminent lady has been so honored by our gracious monarch, raised to the rank of Countess in this kingdom.”

“But why, papa?” the daughters asked together.

“Never mind, girls!” said mamma.

But the incorrigible Colonel persisted.

“Y, my children, is one of the last and most awkward letters of the alphabet. When I tell you stories, you’re always asking Why. Why should my Lord Bishop be bowing to that woman? Look at him rubbing his fat hands together, smiling into her face! It’s not a handsome face anymore—it’s painted red and white like Scaramouch in the pantomime. See—there comes another blue-ribbon! My Lord Bamborough, descendant of the Hotspurs, proudest man in England. He stops, bows, smiles, hat in hand. She taps him with her fan. Get away, you little blackguard boys, don’t tread on the robe of the lady whom the King delights to honor.”

“But why does the King honor her?” the girls asked again.

“There goes that odious last letter but one!” said the Colonel. “Have you ever heard of her Grace, the Duchess of Kendal? No. Of the Duchess of Portsmouth? Non plus. Of the Duchess of La Vallière? Or Fair Rosamond?”

“Hush, papa!” said the mother, placing a finger to his lips. “There’s no need to bring blushes to my dear ones’ cheeks, Martin Lambert!”

“It’s not I,” replied the son of the old republican. “It’s their sacred Majesties who are the cause of the shame. Think of bishops and the proudest nobles cringing before that painted High Dutch Jezebel. Oh, it’s a shame! A shame!”

“Confusion!” cried Colonel Wolfe, snatching up his hat and dashing from the room. He had seen the young lady he admired and her guardian walking across the Pantiles toward the Baroness’s party. They arrived just as the Countess of Yarmouth-Walmoden was speaking with the two lords—spiritual and temporal—and both men made the deepest bows and waited until she had entered the house on the Bishop’s arm.

Theo turned from the window with a sorrowful, awestricken expression. Hetty remained, staring with indignation in her eyes and a faint flush on each cheek.

“A penny for little Hetty’s thoughts,” said mamma, coming to the window to lead her away.

“I’m thinking what I’d do if I saw papa bowing to that woman,” Hetty said.

Tea and a hissing kettle appeared, and the family sat down to their evening meal—though Hetty begged her brother not to close the curtains, keeping command of the window from her seat. Young Charley had gone down among the crowd to inspect the armorial bearings on the sedans, no doubt, and also to spend sixpence on a cheese-cake, as ordered by mamma and desired by himself. He returned with the treat wrapped in paper.

“Look, mother,” he said, “do you see that big man in brown beating the pillars with a stick? That’s the learned Mr. Johnson. He visits our master at the Friars sometimes. He was sitting with friends just now at the tea-table before Mrs. Brown’s tart-shop. They serve tea there for twopence a cup—I heard Mr. Johnson say he’d had seventeen cups—that makes two-and-tenpence. What a sum for tea!”

“What would you have, Charley?” asked Theo.

“I think I’d have cheese-cakes,” said Charley, sighing as he bit into a large slice. “And the gentleman Mr. Johnson was with,” he continued, mouth full, “was Mr. Richardson, who wrote—”

“Clarissa!” cried all the women at once, rushing to the window to catch a glimpse of their favorite author.

By now the sun had set, stars twinkled above, and the footman lit the candles in the Baroness’s room opposite. Theo and her mother stood together, watching. There was a small illumination at Mrs. Brown’s shop, where they saw one lady retrieving Mr. Richardson’s hat and stick, another tying a shawl around his neck, and then he walked home.

“Oh dear me! He doesn’t look like Grandison!” exclaimed Theo.

“I rather think I wish we hadn’t seen him, my dear,” said mamma, known for her sentimental nature and love of novels.

They were interrupted again by Hetty, who called: “Never mind that little fat man—look yonder, mamma!”

They looked. There was Mr. Warrington being presented to the Countess of Yarmouth, still followed by the obsequious peer and prelate with blue ribbons. Then the Countess graciously seated herself at a card table, joined by the Bishop, the Earl, and a fourth partner. Soon, Mr. Warrington appeared in the window embrasure with a lady they recognized—the same one they had briefly seen at Oakhurst.

“How much finer he looks!” remarked mamma.

“What has he done to himself?” asked Theo.

“Look at his grand lace frills and rules! My dear, he’s not wearing our shirts anymore,” said the matron.

“What are you talking about, girls?” asked papa, reclining on his sofa, perhaps dozing like a dutiful house-father.

The girls told him Harry Warrington was in the window, speaking with his cousin Lady Maria Esmond.

“Come away!” shouted papa. “You have no right to spy on the young fellow. Down with the curtains, I say!”

And down they went—so the girls saw nothing more of Madame Bernstein’s gathering that night.

I beg you not to take offense if I note, merely for contrast, that while Madame Bernstein and her guests—bishop, dignitaries, noblemen—were gambling, whispering scandals, drinking champagne, and honoring the king’s favorite, the Countess of Yarmouth-Walmoden, our country friends knelt around their table. Brian, the coachman, came silently, his creaking boots barely audible. Mr. Lambert stood and read aloud a quiet prayer, asking Heaven to lighten their darkness and guard them from danger, and to grant the requests of those gathered.

Our young folks rose early on Sunday morning, dressed in their new finery meant to charm the people of Tunbridge, and, escorted by Charley, strolled through the town, the quaint Pantiles, and the lovely common long before breakfast or church bells rang.

It was Hester who discovered where Harry Warrington lodged, spotting Mr. Gumbo in undress, his beautiful hair in curl-papers, drawing aside red curtains and opening a window sash to breathe in the morning air. Mr. Gumbo did not see the Oakhurst children, though they saw him clearly. He leaned gracefully from the window, waved a large feather brush—used to dust the furniture—engaged in cheerful conversation with a milkmaid below, and kissed his hand to her. His fingers sparkled with rings, and his person gleamed with jewels—gifts, no doubt, from the fair ladies who adored the young African.

Before breakfast, the girls passed the window again. It remained open, but the room was empty. No sign of Harry Warrington. Neither spoke of the subject weighing on both their minds.

Hetty was annoyed with Charley, who clamored for breakfast and accused him of always thinking of food. When she asked sarcastically what he wanted, he honestly admitted he’d like another cheese-cake. Good-natured Theo laughed and said she had a sixpence—if the shop was open, Charley could have one.

It was open. Theo took out her little netted purse, made by her dearest schoolfriend, containing her pocket-piece, her grandmother’s guinea, her few shillings, and even some copper coins. She treated Charley to his beloved snack.

Fine company filled the church. There was the comical old Duchess, old Madame Bernstein with Lady Maria beside her, Mr. Wolfe next to Miss Lowther, singing from the same psalm-book, and Mr. Richardson with a group of ladies. One of them, Papa told them afterward, was Miss Fielding, sister of Harry Fielding.

“Oh, girls, what excellent company he was! And his books are worth a dozen of your milksop Pamelas and Clarissas, Mrs. Lambert. But what woman ever loved true humor? And there was Mr. Johnson sitting among the charity children. Did you see how he turned to the altar at the Belief and knocked over two or three of the frightened little urchins in leather breeches? And what a powerful sermon Harry’s parson gave—about scandal! How he stirred up some of the old harridans nearby! Why wasn’t Mr. Warrington at church? It was a shame he wasn’t.”

“I really didn’t notice whether he was there or not,” said Miss Hetty, tossing her head.

But Theo, truthful as ever, said, “Yes, I thought of him, and was sorry he wasn’t. And you thought of him too, Hetty.”

“I did no such thing, miss,” Hetty insisted.

“Then why did you whisper to me it was Harry’s clergyman who preached?”

“To think of Mr. Warrington’s clergyman is not to think of Mr. Warrington. It was a splendid sermon, and the children sang terribly out of tune. And there’s Lady Maria at the window opposite, smelling roses. That’s Mr. Wolfe’s step—I know his military tramp. Right left—right left! How do you do, Colonel Wolfe?”

“Why do you look so glum, James?” asked Colonel Lambert kindly.

“Has the charmer been scolding you, or is your conscience pricked by the sermon? Mr. Sampson, isn’t that the parson’s name? A famous preacher, I say!”

“A pretty preacher, and a pretty practitioner!” said Mr. Wolfe, shrugging.

“Why, I thought the sermon lasted less than ten minutes, and madam didn’t sleep a wink—did you, Molly?”

“Did you see when the fellow came into church?” asked the indignant Colonel Wolfe.

“He came in at the open door of the common, just in time, as the psalm ended.”

“Well, he may have been reading the service to a sick person. There are many here,” suggested Mrs. Lambert.

“Reading the service! Oh, my good Mrs. Lambert! Do you know where I found him? I went to look for your young scapegrace of a Virginian.”

“His own name is a very pretty name, I’m sure,” cried Hetty.

“It isn’t Scapegrace! It’s Henry Esmond Warrington, Esquire.”

“Miss Hester, I found the parson in his cassock and Henry Esmond Warrington, Esquire, in his bedgown, at quarter past ten in the morning, when all the Sunday bells were ringing, and they were finishing a game of piquet they’d played the night before!”

“Well, many good people play cards on Sundays. The King plays cards on Sundays.”

“Hush, my dear!”

“I know he does,” said Hetty, “with that painted person we saw yesterday—Countess what-d’you-call-her?”

“I think, my dear Miss Hester, a clergyman had better read God’s Word than the Devil’s books on that day—and so I took the liberty of telling your parson.”

Hetty looked as if she thought it was a liberty indeed.

“And I told our young friend he’d better be on his way to church than lounging in his bedgown.”

“You wouldn’t have Harry go to church in a dressing-gown and nightcap, Colonel Wolfe? That would be a sight indeed!” said Hetty fiercely.

“I’d have my little girl’s tongue not wag quite so fast,” remarked papa, patting her flushed cheek.

“Not speak when a friend is attacked, and nobody says a word in his favor? No; nobody!”

Her lips closed tightly. Her whole frame trembled. She cast a defiant glance at Mr. Wolfe and fled the room, just in time to shut the door and burst into tears on the stairs.

Mr. Wolfe looked deeply embarrassed.

“I’m sure, Aunt Lambert, I didn’t mean to hurt Hester’s feelings.”

“No, James,” she said gently—the young officer had called her Aunt Lambert since childhood—and she gave him her hand.

Mr. Lambert whistled his favorite tune, “Over the hills and far away,” tapping a rhythm on the windowpane.

“You mustn’t whistle on Sunday, papa!” cried Charley, artless as ever.

Then he added, “It’s three hours since breakfast, and I’d like to finish Theo’s cheese-cake.”

“Oh, you greedy child!” laughed Theo.

But hearing a sudden noise outside, she ran from the room, closing the door behind her.

We will not follow her. The sound was the sob rising from Hester’s overwhelmed heart. Though we cannot see, I am certain the little maid flung herself into her sister’s arms and wept upon Theo’s kind breast.

Hetty did not join the family’s afternoon walk on the common. She claimed a headache and lay in bed, watched over by her mother. Charley found a companion from Grey Friars. Mr. Wolfe naturally paired with Miss Lowther. Theo and her father took a quiet stroll in the Sabbath sunshine and found Madame Bernstein basking on a bench beneath a tree, her niece and nephew nearby.

Harry ran up to greet them, radiant with pleasure at seeing his dear friends. The elder ladies were gracious to the Colonel and his wife, who had so kindly welcomed their Harry. How noble and handsome he looked! Theo thought. She called him by his Christian name, as if he were truly her brother.

“Why didn’t we see you sooner today, Harry?” she asked.

“I never thought you were here, Theo.”

“But you might have seen us if you’d wished.”

“Where?” asked Harry.

“There, sir,” she said, pointing to the church. She held her hand up as if in gentle reproach, yet kindness shone in her honest face.

Ah, friendly young reader, wandering through life and battling temptation—may you, too, have one or two pure hearts to love and pray for you!
