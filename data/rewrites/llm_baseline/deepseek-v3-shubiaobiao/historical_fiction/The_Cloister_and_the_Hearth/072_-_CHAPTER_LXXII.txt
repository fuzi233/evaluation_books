The Dominicans, once the most formidable order in Christendom, now found their influence waning before the rising power of their rivals, the Franciscans. Even in England—a land both wealthy and devout—where they had once reigned supreme as the Black Friars, their dominance was crumbling.

Thus, the order’s shrewd leaders sought to cultivate zealous and capable men, sending them forth to reclaim lost ground. Among them was Clement, whose gifts—his mastery of languages, his eloquence, and his fervor—marked him as a promising instrument. He was to be dispatched to England, a land ripe for reconversion, to preach and correspond with Rome.

But Jerome, ever cautious, resisted this plan.

*"Clement,"* he argued, *"still carries the world’s milk in his veins—its passions, its frailties. Let not his newfound piety blind us to prudence. Test him first, temper him, lest we lean upon a reed that snaps beneath us."*

The prior assented. *"Then take him in hand yourself."*

And so Jerome did.

He led Clement to a field where young men gathered for sport—a place where Clement’s former companions might still linger. Soon enough, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea passed by, casting indifferent glances at the two friars. They did not recognize their old friend beneath the shaven crown and somber robes.

Clement’s breath caught, but he lowered his eyes and murmured a prayer.

*"Would you not speak to them?"* Jerome prodded.

*"Good,"* said Jerome, and his report to the prior was measured but approving.

Next, Jerome subjected Clement to sterner trials. He took him to the beds of the dying, to noisome dungeons where men lay coiled in filth and despair. Clement’s body recoiled, but his spirit held firm. He offered comfort, prayer, and hope where there had been none. Jerome noted his resolve but also his weakness.

*"His flesh is frail,"* Jerome admitted, *"but his will is strong."*

*"Then let it be strengthened further,"* replied the prior.

The greatest trial came unexpectedly.

One morning, a crowd gathered in a Roman street, necks craning toward a scaffold. A governor had been murdered in his bed—his throat slit, his hand severed in defense. The culprit, his own servant, was to be executed with brutal precision.

The condemned was first fastened to the wall of his victim’s house, his left hand struck off in grim symmetry. A freshly killed fowl was pressed to the stump—some physician’s futile superstition—before he was dragged to the scaffold. There, a young friar—Clement—clutched a crucifix and prayed fervently for his soul.

Then the executioner shoved Clement aside, felled the man with a mallet, and slit his throat.

The crowd roared in horror.

Clement fainted.

A towering friar swept him up like a child and carried him away.

Back at the convent, Clement confessed his failure with tears.

*"Courage,"* said the prior. *"A Dominican is not forged in a day. You shall face another trial—but not on an empty stomach."*

Soon, another criminal stood upon the scaffold—a thief and murderer, a man whose soul seemed beyond redemption. Clement spent the night with him in prison, praying, urging repentance. At dawn, he walked beside him to the gallows, holding the cross before his eyes. The man wept, embraced Clement, and died garroted. Then the executioner quartered his corpse with an axe.

The people, who had watched the living man die unmoved, now cried out at each blow to the lifeless flesh.

Clement shuddered but stood firm.

Then Jerome’s voice hissed in his ear:

Clement hesitated, his breath uneven, but he obeyed.

He climbed onto the cart and delivered his first sermon to a crowd. At first, his voice trembled; his hands shook. But as he spoke—of sin, of redemption, of the thief who had died beside Christ—his fear melted into fire. He held them, swayed them, made them weep.

When he finished, Jerome stared at him, astonished.

*"That was my first attempt,"* Clement said humbly. *"Your command took me by surprise. Did I—was I—?"*

*"You were born to this,"* Jerome admitted grudgingly. *"Give the glory to God."*

Yet Jerome remained wary. When a man named Julio Antonelli faced trial by ordeal—accused of sacrilege, his guilt uncertain—Clement watched as he was bound and lowered into the Tiber. If he floated, he was guilty; if he sank, innocent. The ropes grew heavy, and he sank—proclaimed innocent, but half-drowned.

*"Should he not be compensated?"* Clement asked afterward. *"For the terror, the pain?"*

*"He is rewarded enough in his acquittal,"* Jerome replied sternly. *"In this world, men suffer unjustly. But in the next, justice prevails."*

Clement bowed his head. *"May your wisdom take root in my heart."*

But the final test came in a debate with Fra Colonna, a scholar enamored of pagan learning. When Jerome dismissed his work as obscene, Colonna erupted—defending the ancients’ art, philosophy, and even their piety with a torrent of examples.

*"We invent nothing!"* he cried. *"Our rituals, our relics, even our miracles—they are echoes of older faiths. The heathens had their Palladium, we have our sacred images. They kissed statues, we kiss relics. Their gods bled, ours weep. Where is the difference?"*

Jerome scoffed. *"The difference is truth."*

*"Truth?"* Colonna laughed. *"Then why do we cling to fables even the pagans mocked?"*

Clement listened, torn between admiration and unease.

Jerome turned to him. *"What say you, brother? Does ancient wisdom outshine the light of Christ?"*

Clement hesitated, then spoke softly. *"Truth is one, though paths to it may differ. Let us take what is good—but never mistake the shadow for the substance."*

Jerome’s lips thinned, but he said nothing.

The trial had ended.

Clement had passed.
