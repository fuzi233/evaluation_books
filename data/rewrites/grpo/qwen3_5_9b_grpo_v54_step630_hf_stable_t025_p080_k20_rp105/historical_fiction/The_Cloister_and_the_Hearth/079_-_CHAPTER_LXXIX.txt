Margaret, who had wept so bitterly for the frailty of old Martin, could not endure the finality of his passing with the stoic resignation that often accompanies such losses. Catherine's attempts to soothe her with platitudes—that it was a blessed release, that the tree was ripe, that now there were fewer mouths to feed—fell flat. Her most grievous error came when she suggested that the burden of care had lightened.

"Such cares are the only joys I possess," Margaret replied, her voice trembling. "They fill this desolate heart, leaving it void and wasted. Look at this empty chair; my bosom aches to see it. Poor old man, how could I love him by halves, when I used to sit and gaze upon him, thinking, 'Without me, you would starve'? He, once so wise and learned, has become helpless as my own infant, and I loved him as if he were my child rather than my father. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Well-a-day, well-a-day!"

Her tears flowed freely, unbidden and deep. Catherine fell silent, lowering her head in shame. Later, she confessed, "I speak from my head, not my heart; you are right to ignore me. If I were in your place, I would mourn the old man exactly as you do."

Moved by this rare moment of genuine empathy, Margaret embraced her. It brought her some comfort, unlike the hollow words before. Catherine's heart ached for her, and she spoke with sudden urgency. "My poor girl, you were not born to live alone. I must regard you as my own daughter. Do not waste your youth on my son Gerard. He is either dead or a traitor; it cuts me to say it, but the truth stares us in the face. Your father is gone, and I cannot watch over you forever. Here is an honest lad who has loved you for many days. I would take him and Comfort together. Heaven sends us creatures both to torment and to comfort us; without them, we are nothing."

Seeing Margaret's perplexed expression, Catherine pressed on, "Surely you are not so blind as not to see it?"

"See what?"

"Who else but Luke Peterson?"

"What? Our Luke? The boy who carries my basket?"

"Nay, he is nearly nineteen, a fine, healthy lad. I have made inquiries, and they all say he is a capable worker who never touches a drop. That is rare in a Rotterdam lad, who are mostly half-man, half-sponge."

For the first time in days, Margaret smiled. "Luke loves dried puddings dearly," she admitted. "I make them to his taste; that is why he comes courting here." Suddenly, she turned crimson. "But if I thought he came after your son's wife—or the one who ought to be—I would turn him out at once."

"Nay, nay! For Heaven's sake, do not make mischief. Poor lad! Why, girl, fancy cannot be bridled. Bless you, I wormed it out of him nearly a year ago."

"Oh, Mother, and you allowed it?"

"Well, I thought of you. I told myself, 'If he is foolish enough to be her slave for nothing, all the better for her. A lone woman is lost without a man to fetch and carry her little matters.' But now my mind has changed; I think the best use you can put him to is to marry him."

"So then, his own mother opposes him and wishes to wed me to the first comer?" Margaret cried out. "And Gerard, thou hast but me. I will not believe thee dead until I see thy tomb, nor false until I see thee with another lover in thine hand. Foolish boy, I shall never be civil to him again."

Afflicted by Catherine's meddling protection, Luke Peterson received a cold reception in the household where he had previously found kindness. Soon, finding himself ignored and then sharply rebuked, the gentle giant began to whimper. He approached Margaret directly, asking if he had done something to offend her.

"Nothing," she replied softly. "I am to blame. I am cursed. If you will take my counsel, keep out of my way for a while."

"It is all because of me, Mistress Catherine," Luke said.

"You, Luke? What have I done to set her against me?"

"Nay, I meant all for the best. I told her I saw you looking at her through a wedding ring, but she would not hear of it."

"There was no need to tell her that, wife; she knows I have been courting her for a year."

"Not I," Margaret said firmly. "Or I should never have opened the street door to you."

"Why, I come here every Saturday night. That is how the lads in Rotterdam court. If we sup with a lass on Saturdays, that is the wooing."

"Oh, that is Rotterdam, is it? Then next time you come, let it be Thursday or Friday. For my part, I thought you came after my puddings, boy."

"I like your puddings well enough. You make them better than my mother does. But I like you still better than the puddings," Luke said tenderly.

"Then you have seen the last of them," Margaret declared, her tone gentle yet resolute. "How dare you speak so to another man's wife, especially one whose husband is far away?" She concluded, "You need not trouble yourself to come here any more, Luke. I can carry my basket myself."

"Oh, very well," Luke said. After sitting silently and stupefied for a moment, he rose and addressed Catherine sadly, "Dame, I suppose I have lost my job," before walking out.

The following Saturday, Catherine found him seated on the doorstep, weeping openly. He explained that he had grown accustomed to coming there, and every other place seemed strange. Catherine went inside and told Margaret. Margaret sighed, saying, "Poor Luke; he might have had a chance if he knew his place and treated her like a married wife."

When this was communicated to Luke, he hesitated. "Pshaw!" Catherine scoffed. "Promises are like pie-crusts. Promise her the whole world sooner than sit outside like a fool. If you simply humour her in everything, and then, if poor Gerard does not return to claim her, you will surely have her—in time. A lone woman is always worn out, thou foolish boy."
