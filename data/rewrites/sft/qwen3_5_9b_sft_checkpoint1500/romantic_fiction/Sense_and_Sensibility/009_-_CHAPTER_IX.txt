Life at Barton Cottage had settled into a rhythm of quiet contentment for the Dashwoods. The house, once a mere refuge, now felt like home; the garden and the rolling landscapes surrounding them were no longer strangers but friends. Where Norland had once held charm through the mundane activities of country life, those same simple pursuits now filled their hearts with a joy they had never known before the loss of their father. Sir John Middleton, accustomed to a sedentary existence at Barton Park, visited them daily during the first fortnight and could barely contain his astonishment at discovering them perpetually occupied.

Despite Sir John's earnest pleas for them to socialize more widely and his repeated offers of his carriage for travel, Lady Anne Dashwood's spirited independence proved too strong for the local society's desires. She resolved firmly to visit no families beyond walking distance, a boundary that effectively limited her circle to a handful of neighbors, none of whom were easily accessible. Yet, nature itself offered ample diversion. About a mile and a half from their cottage lay the valley of Allenham, where stood an ancient, dignified mansion. During one of their early strolls, the sisters had stumbled upon this residence, which, echoing faint memories of Norland, sparked a longing for deeper acquaintance. However, inquiry revealed that the owner, a venerable lady of impeccable character, was hopelessly frail and rarely ventured from her grounds.

The countryside around them teemed with picturesque paths. From nearly every window of the cottage, the high downs beckoned, offering a sanctuary of pure air whenever the valleys below grew muddy under persistent rain. On a memorable morning, when the sky broke into patches of brilliant sun following two days of gloom, Marianne and Margaret decided they could endure no longer the stifling dampness indoors. While Elinor and her mother remained captivated by sketching and reading, convinced that the clouds would linger, the two younger sisters ascended the downs with buoyant spirits. They revelled in glimpses of azure sky and welcomed the invigorating gusts of the south-west wind, pitying their mother and sister for missing such bliss.

"Is there any happiness in this world," Marianne declared, "that surpasses this?"
"I agree," Margaret replied, and they pressed forward against the breeze, laughing in triumph for another twenty minutes. Suddenly, the heavens opened above them; the clouds merged overhead, and a torrential downpour lashed them in the face. Disappointed and soaked, they were compelled to retreat, though reluctantly, as no shelter lay closer than their own cottage. Yet, fate offered a silver lining: they could race down the steep slope leading to their garden gate with all the speed they possessed.

Off they went. Marianne gained the initial lead, but a misstep sent her crashing to the earth. Margaret, unable to halt her momentum to help, was swept along involuntarily, reaching the bottom unscathed. Just as Marianne fell, a gentleman carrying a shotgun, flanked by two eager pointers, passed near her. Hearing the commotion, he dropped his gun and rushed to her aid. Marianne had managed to raise herself, but her foot was severely twisted, leaving her trembling on the verge of standing. The gentleman offered his assistance. Sensing her modesty might shy away from physical contact, he nevertheless lifted her gently into his arms without further hesitation and carried her down the hill.

Passing through the garden—which Margaret had inadvertently left ajar—he bore her straight into the house, where she was just arriving. He did not release his hold until he had settled her comfortably in a chair within the parlor.

Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor rose in sheer amazement at his entrance. Both women stared at him with a mixture of wonder and a silent, burgeoning admiration, drawn by his striking presence. He offered a brief apology for his intrusion, recounting the circumstances with a frankness and grace that seemed to enhance his already exceptional handsomeness. Even if he had been aged, plain, and coarse, Mrs. Dashwood's gratitude alone would have ensured her goodwill toward any who cared for her daughter. But the combination of his youth, beauty, and elegant demeanor struck a chord deep within her heart, lending a profound poignancy to his actions.

She thanked him repeatedly, inviting him to sit with a warmth of address characteristic of her nature. He politely declined, citing his mud-soaked state. When Mrs. Dashwood finally inquired after his name, he replied, "Willoughby," and added that his current residence was Allenham, where he hoped to pay a courtesy call the following morning. This invitation was graciously accepted, and he departed, making himself increasingly intriguing amidst the pouring rain.

Within moments, Willoughby's masculine beauty and extraordinary grace became the subject of universal acclaim. The laughter his gallantry inspired regarding Marianne's tumble was amplified by his undeniable charm. Though Marianne, flushed with embarrassment, had not fully taken in his features when he lifted her, the fleeting sight was enough for her to join the chorus of admiration with fervor. In her mind, his person and bearing equaled the heroes of her favorite tales. The lack of formal ceremony in his rescue impressed her greatly; it spoke of a mind that moved swiftly and naturally. Every detail about him seemed captivating: his noble name, his proximity to their beloved village, and the fact that he wore a shooting jacket, which she secretly deemed the most becoming attire for a man. Her imagination flourished, her thoughts were light, and the pain of her sprained ankle faded into insignificance.

The next day, as soon as the weather permitted, Sir John paid a visit. Upon hearing details of Marianne's accident, he was immediately intrigued. "Willoughby!" he exclaimed. "Is *he* in the country? That is excellent news. I shall ride over tomorrow and invite him to dine on Thursday."
"Do you know him, then?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.
"To be sure I do," Sir John assured her. "He visits here annually. He is a decent shot, and perhaps the boldest rider in England."
"And is that all you can say?" Marianne interjected, indignant. "What of his manners? His talents? His genius?"
Sir John appeared slightly baffled. "Upon my soul," he admitted, "I know little of those things. But he is a pleasant, good-humoured fellow, and possesses the finest little black pointer I've ever seen."
Marianne could not satisfy him regarding the dog's color, nor could he elucidate the depths of Willoughby's mind.
"But who is he?" Elinor pressed. "Where does he hail from? Does he possess a home at Allenham?"
Here, Sir John could offer certainty. Mr. Willoughby owned no property locally; he resided only because he was visiting an elderly relative at Allenham Court, whose estates he stood to inherit. "Besides, he holds a small estate of his own in Somersetshire," Sir John noted, winking conspiratorially. "If I were you, I'd not give him up to my younger sister, despite this tumbling business. Miss Marianne must not expect to monopolize all the eligible bachelors. Brandon will be jealous if she doesn't look out."
"I do not believe," Mrs. Dashwood smiled, "that Mr. Willoughby will be inconvenienced by the attentions of my daughters. It is not a pursuit they were raised for. Men are quite safe with us, whatever their wealth."
"He is a good sort of fellow," Sir John reiterated, recalling a ball where Willoughby danced from eight until four without sitting, only to mount his horse again at eight for a hunt.
"That is precisely what I admire," Marianne said, her eyes sparkling. "Whatever his pursuits, his zeal knows no bounds, leaving him no sense of fatigue."
"Aye, aye," Sir John chuckled. "I see how it will go. You'll be setting your cap at him now, and forgetting poor Brandon entirely."
"That is a phrase, Sir John," Marianne protested warmly, "which I detest. I loathe every trite expression used to mock wit. 'Setting one's cap' or 'making a conquest' are the most odious. If their construction were ever clever, time has long destroyed their ingenuity."
Sir John, somewhat perplexed by her reproof, laughed heartily nonetheless. "Ay, you'll make enough conquests, one way or another. Poor Brandon! He's quite smitten already, and he's certainly worth considering, in spite of all this tumbling and spraining."
