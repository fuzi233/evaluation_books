"Ignorance and evil, even in full flight, deal terrible backhanded strokes at their pursuers." — HELPS.

Let it be confessed, to the shame of my nation, that in those days it was perilous indeed to travel from Plymouth northward along the coast of Devon. Unless one wished to detour many miles, the road led straight through the domain of a foreign and hostile potentate—a man who had repeatedly ravaged the lands and defeated the armies of Her Majesty, Queen Elizabeth. Behind his back, at least, he was known as the "King of the Gubbings." As the chronicler Fuller noted, "Secured by distance, which one of more valor durst not do to their face, for fear their fury fall upon him." Yet, despite the ingenuity of scholars like Mr. Joseph Maynard, who suggested the name derived from *gibberosos* (hunchbacks), the truth remains obscure. Some claim the term refers to fish shavings—worthless scraps that carry a stigma of shame—but the reality is far darker.

This land lies near Brent, a true Scythia within England, inhabited by pure heathens. Legend holds that two centuries prior, two unwed mothers fled there to hide, followed by lewd fellows, creating a peculiar society exempt from all civil or ecclesiastical authority. Living in hovels indistinguishable from pigsties, they multiply without marriage, speaking a dialect so coarse that the more educated find it impossible to comprehend. During the Civil Wars, no soldiers were quartered among them, lest they become trapped within. Their wealth consists solely of stolen goods, particularly sheep from the moors; searching their homes is deemed beneath a sheriff and beyond a constable's power, for these creatures possess such speed they outrun horses and such vitality they outlast men. Living in ignorance of luxury—the true extinguisher of life—they cling together like bees, avenging any offense against one member upon the entire community.

Though reports suggest they are beginning to accept baptism and return to civilization, I trust no civilized people will emulate their former barbarism. Thus ended Fuller's tale, leaving us to imagine Amyas riding out of Plymouth on a fine steed, clad in full armor, lance and sword in hand, accompanied by two great daggers or horse-pistols. Following him was Salvation Yeo and five or six North Devon men, veterans of the Irish campaign returning on furlough, armed with pikes, swords, arquebuses, and matches. Two sumpter ponies bore their baggage, forming a formidable procession.

Pressing onward as fast as possible through Tavistock, they aimed to reach Lydford before dark. However, delays in purchasing horses meant they did not depart until noon. Night fell precisely as they crossed the frontier into enemy territory. It was a desolate landscape, illuminated by the wild glare of sunset: a high tableland of heath bordered on the right by the craggy heights of Dartmoor, sloping south and west toward the massive cone of Brent-Tor. This peak rose like an extinct volcano, crowned by a tiny church—a votive offering built by a distressed Plymouth merchant who vowed to construct a chapel to the Blessed Virgin upon the first English soil he sighted. From afar, faint threads of blue smoke drifted from the Gubbings' dens. More than once, the party halted, mistaking distant furze bushes and grazing ponies for the vanguard of an invading army. While amusing to mock such caution in the nineteenth century, it was a grave necessity then; they discovered this truth shortly after traveling two miles further.

In the center of the downs stood a wayside inn: a desolate, villainous lump of lichen-spotted granite, its windows patched with paper and its rotting thatch weighed down by stones and straw banks. Behind lay a rambling courtyard of barns and walls, where pigs and barefoot children grunted in communal filth. Standing at the door, seemingly contemplating the orange-glowing mountain peaks but actually tracking the movement of sheep on the moor, was the innkeeper. He was a brawny, sodden-faced giant of six feet, blear-eyed and brutish, holding up his hose with one hand due to missing buttons and clawing at his elf-locks with the other. These locks were sprinkled with feathers—a sign, according to the great apostle of sluttishness, Fridericus Dedekind, that he had recently risen from a bed of straw after a night of sheep-stealing. Feathers, Dedekind argued, serve as an excellent ornament, proving one has slept on soft down; thus, the innkeeper's plumage testified to his luxurious habits.

When he spotted Amyas's party approaching slowly over the hill, he pricked up his ears, counted them, noted Amyas's armor, shook his head, and grunted. Being a man of few words, he emitted a sleepy howl: *"Mirooi! Fushing pooale!"*

A strapping lass, wearing nothing but a green bodice and red petticoat, emerged carrying a fishing rod and basket. The innkeeper tied his hose with string and examined his footwear. *"Don vlies' gone!"*

"Maybe," replied Mary, "he shouldn't have left Mun out to court. Perhaps the old hen ate him. I saw her choking about a while ago."

The host received this news with an oath, striking Mary violently on the head. She, accustomed to such treatment, dodged and retaliated effectively against his shock-hard skull. The innkeeper, equally used to minor skirmishes, shuffled away howling, shouting, *"Tell Patrico!"*

Mary ran inside, combed her hair, slipped on stockings and her finest gown over her working clothes, and awaited the guests. Amyas and his men made several grimaces at the squalid surroundings but preferred spending the night there rather than camping near potential enemies. The innkeeper's meal was prepared: the "old hen" (a chicken) was killed, plucked, and roasted, joined by "black Dartmoor mutton" placed on the gridiron. Compelled by the heat to reveal its true nature, the meat proclaimed itself red-deer venison to all present noses. Meanwhile, Amyas secured his horse and ponies in a shed lacking locks or keys, returning with a grumble and concern for their safety. Baggage was piled in a corner, and Amyas stretched his legs before a turf fire. Salvation Yeo, suspicious of the location, posted himself at the door, while the others watched the cooking, likely motivated by Mary's presence as chef.

Soon, Yeo returned. *"There's a gentleman just coming up, sir, all alone."*

*"Invite him to join our party, with my compliments,"* Amyas replied.

Yeo departed and returned five minutes later. *"Please, sir, he's gone in the back way, through the courtyard."*

*"Well, he has strange tastes if he feels comfortable here,"* Amyas remarked.

Yeo went out again and returned once more, visibly excited. *"Come out, sir! For goodness' sake, come out! I've got him! Safe as a rat in a trap!"*

*"Truth, sir! When he approached, I didn't like his appearance. I knew instantly he was one of those villains by the way he turned his toes inward and stepped so carefully, as though fearing to offend God at every step. Peering between the wall and the gate post, I saw him approach the back door, knock softly, and call 'Mary!' quietly, exactly like a Jesuit. The girl flew out ready to devour him, saying, 'Go away, there's a dear man,' mentioning some 'queer cuffin''—their thieves' Latin for a justice—and then produced what I swear was one of those Popish Agnuses. She kissed it, crossed herself, asked if that was the proper method, tucked it into her bosom, and he said, 'Bless you, my daughter.' That confirmed it. He slipped silently to the stable, peeked inside, found it empty, entered, and I followed, shutting the door and backing a cart against it. I called one of the men to guard the stable while the girl screamed hysterically."*

*"Fool or not, honest gentlemen do not give maidens Agnuses. I have detained him. If you wish to release him, you must come personally, for my conscience forbids otherwise. Scripture says, 'If any let one of them go, his life shall be for the life of him.'"*

Thus, Amyas rushed out, pulled back the cart grumbling, opened the door, and began apologizing profusely to his cousin Eustace. Indeed, Eustace appeared with a countenance half foolish, half venomous, resembling a fox caught in a trap with the final shovel of earth thrown over him, sitting disconsolately within yards of terriers sniffing his scent.

For a minute or two, neither spoke. Finally, Amyas asked, *"Well, cousin, how long have you added horse-stealing to your other trades?"*

"My dear Amyas," Eustace replied meekly, *"I may surely enter an inn stable without intending to steal what lies within."*

"Of course, old fellow," Amyas said, mollified, *"I was merely joking. But what brings you here? Not prudence, certainly."*
