The air thickened to pitch-black ink as the otherworldly winter coiled tight around me, and the fluttering, fire-winged sprite I had trailed for hours blazed brighter, its erratic flight stilling to hover, waiting. The moment I stepped beneath its radiance, it drifted slowly ahead, pausing every few beats of its wings to linger over patches of jutted, half-buried rock. Each time I glanced up it had swelled in size, until at last it cast a long, wavering shadow at my feet. It was no creature I recognized from Earth: half swallow, half giant butterfly, its wings broad and nearly square, strobing every hue of the rainbow with that sharp, darting double-beat of flight no terrestrial bug or bird could match.

I was so transfixed by the riot of unnameable colors spilling off its plumage that I tripped over a low, moss-crusted rock, sprawling hard and stunned. When I dragged my wits back, it hovered an arm’s length above my face, radiating a full, unbroken chord of light streaked with thousands of subtle gradients, tints no human eye had ever registered back on my home planet. I staggered to my feet and pressed on, but I could not tear my gaze from the glowing little wonder long enough to watch my steps, and soon my foot slammed hard into a half-buried stone. Afraid I’d crack my ankle and fall prey to the endless dark, I sank down onto the cold heather just to watch it, and a sharp, hungry longing bloomed hot in my chest: I had to hold it in my hands.

To my wild, disbelieving delight, it started sinking toward me. Slow at first, then faster, growing larger and brighter, like it was pouring the entire universe’s hoard of light right into my waiting palm. I reached out, fingers closing around it — and the second my skin made contact, its light snuffed out entirely. All went pitch black, and a cold, heavy book, its boards splayed wide open, lay dead and inert in my grip. I yelped and hurled it as hard as I could, only to hear it land with a dull thud somewhere in the thick heather. I buried my face in my hands, hunched there in shivering, utter misery.

But the cold bit so deep it started to burn through my coat, and I knew if I stayed still I’d freeze solid before dawn. The moment I lurched to my feet, a faint prickle of light pricked at the edge of my vision. “Did it come back to life?” I gasped, and a stabbing, bright spike of hope went through me. No. It was the thin, sharp edge of an unfamiliar moon, peeking over the perfectly flat horizon. It gave me light, yes — but no guidance. It would not hover, would not wait for my stumbling steps. It only offered me a blind, unknowing choice of paths. It rose full, pale and strange, and I started to pick my way west toward the line of low hills I could just make out breaking the flat dark horizon.

What a night that was, before I ever got there! The moon stared down at me, odd and unblinking, like it knew a secret I didn’t. Its light was icy, but not uncurious, almost pitying, like it was wondering what a human was doing wandering out in this cursed, unlighted waste. It was no moon I’d ever seen hanging over Earth — its face bore a completely unfamiliar pattern of craters, its light a strange silver-blue glow I could not place, as if it reflected a sun that had burned out a million years before. Every time I looked up, it felt like it was staring right at me, full bore. At first I was annoyed, like it was a rude stranger prying into my business, but soon I thought I saw that soft, wondering pity in its gaze: why are you out here, all alone, in the dark? That was the first moment I truly grasped how horrifying it was to be awake, alive, adrift in an endless, uncaring universe. I was. There was no opting out.

As I walked, the heather under my boots gave way to bare, spongy soil, like dry, powdery peat. To my horror, the whole ground heaved under my feet for a split second, then I saw what looked like the ripple of an earthquake snaking ahead of me, faint and shadowy in the low moonlight. It vanished into the distance, but before I could even process what I’d seen, a single wave of the soil rose up and rolled slow toward me. It burst a yard from my feet, and a tawny, tiger-like creature scrambled out, mouth and matted ears crusted with dark mould, its eyes blazing like embers as it charged at me, fangs bared in a soundless snarl. I stood frozen, not feeling fear or courage, just transfixed. It snapped its head down and plunged straight back into the dirt, vanishing like it had never been there.

“The moon’s addling my brain,” I muttered, getting back on my path. “Nothing here is real, it’s all phantoms, the stuff of dreams. I’m walking through a hallucination, a vain show.” I told myself that to keep my panic at bay, never guessing that the strange moon I was mocking was the only thing keeping me safe from the very real horrors I thought were just my imagination. Its light held the monsters paralyzed, or I would have never survived a second step on that shifting, living ground. “I won’t be scared of something that’s only pretend,” I told myself, but my skin crawled at the thought of walking across a sea of dirt where things like that lurked just below the surface.

Then, not two steps ahead of me, the head of a worm started slowly pushing up through the soil. It was as big as a polar bear’s head, looked almost identical, a thick white mane cascading down over its bright red neck. The wriggling, heaving movements as it pulled its massive length out of the earth were so grotesque I couldn’t look away. The second its tail was free, it collapsed, exhausted, flopping weakly to try and burrow back down into the dirt. “Does it feed on the dead?” I wondered. “Is it too weak to hurt the living? If they can smell prey and come all the way out, why aren’t they tearing me apart?” I know now it was the moon’s light that was holding them, locking their muscles, draining their strength.

All that long night, hideous creatures no two alike rose up out of the dirt to threaten me. Some were even more grotesque for their bright, beautiful coloring: one massive serpent was covered head to flickering tail in feathers that glowed every color of a tropical sunset. I got so used to their harmless, frozen snarls that I started making up my own silly monstrosities in my head to pass the time, never suspecting I was only alive because that staring moon was holding them back. Even though its light was only reflected — second, third, hundredth hand — it was still light, enough to chain the evil things down so I could walk safe. If I had known what would happen the second its light vanished from this cursed stretch of ground? That they’d be on me in a heartbeat, no mercy, writhing all over me, every last one of them as deadly as they’d seemed harmless before? I never would have sauntered so carelessly. I watched the moon sink low, slow, solemn, anxious, the only unease I felt being the stupid fear I’d get lost — as if I even had a path to lose.

I was almost at the hills I’d set as my goal, the moon just a hand’s breadth above their skyline, when all the burrowing and wallowing stopped. The soil lay still, bare, silent. That was when I saw a woman, walking slow across the glowing dirt. A thin white mist curled around her, shifting in and out of the shape of a soft white gown, gathering tight to her form then blowing loose again, pushed and pulled by a wind that followed only her steps. She was beautiful, but her face was twisted with equal parts unbearable pride and crushing misery, so much so I could barely believe my eyes. She paced back and forth, over and over, grabbing at the mist trying to wrap it around herself to cover the dark, festering spot on her left side, pressing her hand to it like she was trying to smother the pain welling up from it. Her hair hung all the way down to her ankles, pale gold in the moonlight, and sometimes the wind tangled it so tight with the mist I couldn’t tell where her hair ended and the fog began.

Suddenly she pressed both hands hard to her heart, and crumpled to the ground. The mist drifted up off her, dissolving into the air. I ran for her, but she started writhing in such searing, silent torture that I froze, horrified. A heartbeat later, her legs tore free of her body and slithered away as serpents. Her arms flew off her shoulders, panicking, also serpents. Something bat-like flapped up from what was left of her, and when I blinked, she was gone.

The ground heaved like a storm-tossed sea, and raw, cold terror seized me. I turned and ran for the hills. I was already halfway up the base slope when the moon sank behind the nearest peak, plunging me into total shadow. Behind me rose a long, wailing, sickening cry, like a thousand frustrated, hungry voices all at once — the only sound I’d heard since the dead butterfly-book had thudded into the heather. It shook my bones so hard my heart thudded like a flag in a gale. I glanced over my shoulder, saw dozens of dark shapes bounding toward me, and ran for the crest of the ridge where the moon still spilled light over the edge. It seemed to linger there, just for me, so I could see what I was doing to defend myself.

I scrambled faster, and soon crested the ridge in full moonlight. I could hear the creatures panting right at my heels. The very first one launched itself at me, fangs bared, snarling with greedy hate — and we both stumbled straight into the moon’s silver glow. It flared bright, angry, and the beast fell off me, melting into a shapeless, bodiless splotch of shadow. A surge of strength flooded my veins, and I turned to face the rest. One by one, as they lunged into the light, they dissolved with thin, wailing howls. I swore I saw a faint, knowing smile on that round, pale face hanging above me.

I climbed to the very top of the ridge. Far below, the moon sank fast toward a new, far horizon. The air up here was pure, sharp, warm in my lungs. I climbed down a little way, found a sheltered spot that held the heat, and sat down to wait for the dawn. The moon vanished over the edge of the world, and the dark closed in around me, thick and total once again.
