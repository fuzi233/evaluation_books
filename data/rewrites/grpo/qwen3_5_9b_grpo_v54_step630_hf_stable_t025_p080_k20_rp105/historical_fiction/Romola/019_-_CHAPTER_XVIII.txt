The Portrait.

Tito departed from the Via de' Bardi that day with a heart swelling in exultant satisfaction, convinced at last that the shadow of threatened peril had been entirely dispelled. Yet, as the immediate presence of Romola and her father faded from his view, his thoughts drifted back to those futile hours of dread. He recalled with a shudder the actions he had taken, realizing now that he would never have parted with his ring had he possessed true foresight. To surrender a gem he had professed to cherish would have appeared sordid to Romola and her circle; he would have needed to feign a noble purpose, a desire to make a special gift with the proceeds. At that moment, however, Tito felt a nauseating weariness of all simulation. He was safely clear of the consequences that might have arisen from that initial deception, and the weight of it had vanished. Kind fortune had granted him immunity, and he felt it was only fair that she should reap the reward. Who truly suffered? The results for Baldassarre remained too murky to calculate, but Tito desired now to be free from any hidden shackles that might chafe beneath his ties to Romola. Unaware that the very delight in this immunity was deadening the sensibilities required to save him from future entanglements, he moved forward with a lightness he did not fully comprehend.

After all, the sale of the ring was a trifling matter. Was it equally slight that little Tessa lived under a delusion, her small head filled with expectations destined for disappointment? Should he seek her out alone to undeceive her immediately, or leave the revelation to time and chance? Happy dreams were pleasant, yet they ended swiftly with the daylight and the stir of life. The image of her—sweet, pouting, innocent, and round—was impossible to banish from his mind. He considered taking her a present to cheer her, perhaps to observe if her stepfather treated her more cruelly now that her mother was gone. Or, he mused, should he undeceive her at once and then inform Romola, so they might secure a happier fate for the poor child? No. That unfortunate incident involving the *cerretano* and the marriage, compounded by his allowing Tessa to depart in delusion, must never be known to Romola. Since no enlightenment could erase the memory from Tessa's mind, there would always be a risk of betrayal; moreover, even a child might harbor gall when her love proved unrequited. Yes, she must be in love with him, and that made it prudent that he see her no more. Yet, it was merely a trifling adventure, the sort a country girl might ponder until some ruddy peasant won her affection, at which point she would break her silence and discover her freedom. *Dunque*—good-bye, Tessa! Kindest wishes! Tito resolved that the silly affair of the *cerretano* would bear no further consequences for himself. People often believed their own resolutions to be ironclad. As for the fifty-five florins, the purchase-money for the ring, he had already decided what to do with a portion of it. He conceived an ingenious plan that would ease his conscience regarding the missing ring and serve as a safeguard against the recurrence of Romola's monkish fancies, which were especially repugnant to him. With this purpose fixed in his mind, he turned toward the Via Gualfonda to seek Piero di Cosimo, the artist whose mastery of fantastic mythological design was essential to Tito's scheme.

Entering the courtyard where Piero's dwelling stood, Tito observed the heavy iron knocker, thickly bound in wool and ingeniously secured with cords. Remembering the painter's habit of stuffing his ears against intrusive noise, Tito was unsurprised by this defense against visitors' thunder. He tapped modestly with his knuckles, then escalated to a more importunate shaking of the door. In vain! Just as Tito prepared to withdraw, blaming himself for wasting time on a visit he could wait for at Nello's, a little girl emerged carrying a basket of eggs. She approached the door, stood on tiptoe, and pushed up a small iron plate sliding in grooves. Peering through the aperture, she called out in a piping voice, "Messer Piero!"

Moments later, the bolts clicked, the door swung open, and Piero appeared in a red night-cap and a loose brown serge tunic, sleeves rolled to the shoulder. He darted a look of surprise at Tito but, without further acknowledgment, reached out to take the basket from the child. Re-entering the house, he soon returned with the empty basket, asking, "How much to pay?"

"Two grossoni, Messer Piero; they are all ready boiled, my mother says."

Piero extracted the coins from the leather pouch at his belt, and the little maiden trotted away, casting upward glances of awed admiration at the surprising young signor.

Piero's gaze, however, was far less complimentary as he addressed Tito. "What do you want at my door, Messer Greco? I saw you this morning at Nello's; if you had asked me then, I could have told you that I see no man in this house without knowing his business and agreeing with him beforehand."

"Pardon, Messer Piero," Tito replied with his characteristic imperturbable good humor. "I acted without sufficient reflection. I remembered nothing but your admirable skill in inventing pretty caprices, when a sudden desire for something of that sort prompted me to come to you."

The painter's manners were notoriously odd to everyone, so this reception was not a special affront. Even if Tito had suspected offensive intent, the impulse to resentment would have been weaker than his desire to conquer goodwill. Piero made a grimace habitual to him when spoken to with flattering suavity. He grinned, stretching the corners of his mouth and pressing down his brows to defy any divination of his feelings beneath that kind of stroking.

"And what may that need be?" he asked after a pause. In his heart, he was tempted by the hinted opportunity to apply his invention.

"I require a very delicate miniature device drawn from certain fables of the poets, which you will know how to combine for me. It must be painted on a wooden case—I will show you the size—in the form of a triptych. The interior may be simple gilding; it is on the exterior that I wish the device. It is a favorite subject with you Florentines—the triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne—but I desire it treated in a new way. A story in Ovid will provide the necessary hints. Young Bacchus must be seated in a ship, his head bound with clusters of grapes, a spear entwined with vine-leaves in his hand: dark-berried ivy must wind about the masts and sails, the oars must be thyrsi, and flowers must wreathe themselves about the poop; leopards and tigers must crouch before him, and dolphins sport around. But I want the fair-haired Ariadne beside him, made immortal with her golden crown—that is not in Ovid's story, but no matter, you will conceive it all—and above them, young Loves, such as you know how to paint, shooting with roses at the points of their arrows—"

"Say no more!" Piero interrupted. "I have Ovid in the vulgar tongue. Find me the passage. I love not to be choked with other men's thoughts. You may come in."

Piero led the way through the first room, where a basket of eggs rested on the open hearth near a heap of broken shells and a bank of ashes. In strange keeping with that sordid litter stood a low bedstead of carved ebony, covered carelessly with a piece of rich oriental carpet that looked as if it had once served to cover the steps to a Madonna's throne. Beside it was a carved *cassone*, or large chest, adorned with painted devices on its sides and lid. There was hardly any other furniture in the large room except casts, wooden steps, easels, and rough boxes, all festooned with cobwebs.

The next room was larger but even more crowded. Apparently, Piero was keeping the Festa, for the double door beneath the window, which admitted the painter's light from above, was thrown open, revealing a garden—or rather a thicket—where fig-trees and vines grew in tangled, trailing wildness among nettles and hemlocks, and a tall cypress lifted its dark head from a stifling mass of yellowish mulberry leaves. It seemed as if that dank luxuriance had begun to penetrate even within the walls of the wide, lofty room. In one corner, amidst a confused heap of carved marble fragments and rusty armor, tufts of long grass and dark feathery fennel had made their way, and a large stone vase, tilted on one side, seemed to be pouring out the ivy that streamed around. Hanging about the walls were pen and oil sketches of fantastic sea-monsters; dances of satyrs and maenads; Saint Margaret's resurrection from the devouring dragon; Madonnas bathed in supernal light; studies of plants and grotesque heads. On irregular, rough shelves, a few books were scattered among great drooping bunches of corn, bullocks' horns, pieces of dried honeycomb, stones with patches of rare-colored lichen, skulls and bones, peacock feathers, and large birds' wings. Rising from the dirty litter of the floor were life-sized figures: one in the frock of a Vallombrosan monk, strangely surmounted by a helmet with a barred visor, another smothered in brocade and skins hastily tossed over it. Among this heterogeneous still life, several speckled and white pigeons were perched or strutting, too tame to fly at the entrance of men; three corpulent toads crawled in an intimate, friendly manner near the door-stone; and a white rabbit, apparently the model for the creature frightening Cupid in the picture of Mars and Venus placed on the central easel, twitched its nose with content on a box full of bran.

"And now, Messer Greco," Piero said, gesturing for Tito to sit on a low stool near the door, then standing over him with folded arms, "do not try to see everything at once, like Messer Domeneddio, but let me know how large you would have this same triptych."

Tito indicated the required dimensions, and Piero marked them on a piece of paper.

"And now for the book," Piero said, reaching down a manuscript volume.

"There's nothing about the Ariadne there," Tito replied, handing over the passage. "But you will remember I want the crowned Ariadne by the side of the young Bacchus: she must have golden hair."

"Ha!" Piero said abruptly, pursing his lips again. "And you want them to be likenesses, eh?" he added, looking down into Tito's face.

Tito laughed and blushed. "I know you are great at portraits, Messer Piero; but I could not ask Ariadne to sit for you, because the painting is a secret."

"There it is! I want her to sit to me. Giovanni Vespucci wants me to paint him a picture of Oedipus and Antigone at Colonos, as he has expounded it to me: I have a fancy for the subject, and I want Bardo and his daughter to sit for it. Now, you ask them; and then I'll put the likeness into Ariadne."

"Agreed, if I can prevail with them. And your price for the Bacchus and Ariadne?"

"Baie! If you get them to let me paint them, that will pay me. I'd rather not have your money: you may pay for the case."

"And when shall I sit for you?" Tito asked. "For if we have one likeness, we must have two."

"I don't want your likeness; I've got it already," Piero replied. "Only I've made you look frightened. I must take the fright out of it for Bacchus."

As he spoke, Piero laid down the book and went to look among some paintings propped against the wall. He returned with an oil-sketch in his hand.

"I call this as good a bit of portrait as I ever did," he said, examining it as he advanced. "Yours is a face that expresses fear well, because it's naturally a bright one. I noticed it the first time I saw you. The rest of the picture is hardly sketched; but I've painted *you* in thoroughly."

Piero turned the sketch and held it toward Tito's eyes. He saw himself with his right hand uplifted, holding a wine-cup, in the attitude of triumphant joy, yet with his face turned away from the cup, displaying an expression of such intense fear in the dilated eyes and pallid lips that a cold stream ran through his veins, as if he were being thrown into sympathy with his imaged self.

"You are beginning to look like it already," Piero said with a short laugh, moving the picture away again. "He's seeing a ghost—that fine young man. I shall finish it some day, when I've settled what sort of ghost is the most terrible—whether it should look solid, like a dead man come to life, or half transparent, like a mist."

Tito, somewhat ashamed of himself for a sudden sensitiveness strangely opposed to his usual easy self-command, said carelessly, "That is a subject after your own heart, Messer Piero—a revel interrupted by a ghost. You seem to love the blending of the terrible with the gay. I suppose that is the reason your shelves are so well furnished with death's-heads, while you paint those roguish Loves running away with the armor of Mars. I begin to think you are a Cynic philosopher in the pleasant disguise of a cunning painter."

"Not I, Messer Greco; a philosopher is the last sort of animal I should choose to resemble. I find it enough to live, without spinning lies to account for life. Fowls cackle, asses bray, women chatter, and philosophers spin false reasons—that's the effect the sight of the world brings out of them. Well, I am an animal that paints instead of cackling, or braying, or spinning lies. And now, I think, our business is done; you'll keep to your side of the bargain about the Oedipus and Antigone?"
