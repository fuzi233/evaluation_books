"I pity the man who travels from Dan to Beersheba and declares, 'It is all barren.' Yet so it is; and so is the entire world to anyone unwilling to cultivate its hidden fruits." —Sterne, *Sentimental Journey*

To label Daniel Deronda merely "romantic" would be a profound misunderstanding. Beneath his composed, slightly restrained exterior burned a fervor that allowed him to discover poetry and drama in the most mundane threads of daily life. Perhaps such magic is as abundant as ever, waiting only to be seen by those whose hearts are not numbed by a phlegmatic indifference. It exists in the same room as the microscope and rattles within railway carriages, vanishing only from the minds of those who have lost the capacity for awe or the sense of fellowship that connects the near to the distant. For a mind devoid of these tremors, how could the vast machinery of heaven and earth—from the furthest star to the tender bosom of a nursing mother—fail to compose a verse?

For Deronda, the moment of finding Mirah was as electrifying as any mythic encounter for Orestes or Rinaldo. He sat awake half the night, reliving the instant he first glimpsed her on the riverbank with a vividness that felt newly minted. When he attempted to read a book to dull this urgent inner vision, the printed words dissolved into a mere lattice through which he still saw and heard everything with crystal clarity. He perceived not only the actual events of the past two hours but also the swirling possibilities of what had been and what might be, fed by the warm blood of passionate hope and fear. A deep resonance within his own soul caused Mirah's desperate search for her mother to grip his imagination with peculiar force. His initial impulse was pure sympathy: to aid her, utilizing the subtle, almost scientific methods available in London to locate missing persons. Yet, the complex emotions born of his own family history quickly transfused themselves into his anxiety for her.

The desire to know his own mother was perpetually haunted by dread. In imagining Mirah's fate, it struck him with sudden clarity that finding the mother and brother from whom she had been torn as a child could prove to be a calamity. In the boat, she had spoken of them as good; yet that goodness might have been a projection of her own innocent ignorance and yearning. Ten or twelve years were ample time for change, for corruption. Despite his natural tendency to side with the oppressed, Deronda had never felt a practical draw toward existing Jews. The facts he knew of them—whether they walked conspicuously in fine clothes or lurked in shadowed by-streets—were largely repugnant. He assumed that learned and accomplished Jews had abandoned their faith to merge with their host nations. While scorn cast at a Jew as a member of a race would have stirred his deepest sympathies, the indiscriminate hatred of a people often strikes the individual who has well-earned it, marking them as a rascal son of Adam. Even the Caribs, with their limited theology, seem to view thieving as uniquely Christian; perhaps they could cite experimental grounds for such a bias. Deronda could not escape knowing the ugly stories of Jewish characteristics and occupations. Though he protested against severing past from present, he shared the common failure of never caring to look deeper than the general virtues and vices of a long-oppressed race. Now, however, Mirah's longing forced a closer survey of details, and very disagreeable images arose: what it might mean to find this middle-aged woman and her son.

Surely, there was the exquisite refinement and charm of Mirah herself to suggest her kin were equally noble, but he must wait. Perhaps through Mrs. Meyrick he could gather guiding hints from Mirah's own lips. Her voice, her accent, her looks—all the sweet purity that clothed her like a consecrating garment—made him shrink even further from associating her, either in thought or deed, with anything hateful or contaminating. Yet these fine words are often used to fumigate and obscure unpleasant realities; they are not the language of true thought. Deronda's mind raced with rapid images of possibility: he saw himself guided by an official scout into a dingy street, entering a dim doorway where a hawk-eyed, rough-headed woman bargained away a hungry girl's last finery. Or, in a quarter hideous precisely because of its pretension, he found himself breathing the air of a young Jew, talkative and familiar, eager to display his acquaintance with gentlemanly tastes and unfastidious in any transaction they might favor. Such scenes played out in the brief chapter of his experience. Excuse him: his mind was not prone to spontaneous insults or the wit that equates Moses with an advertisement sheet. But he was currently governed by dread. If Mirah's parents had been Christian, the only difference would be that his forebodings would have been fed by wider knowledge. It was the habit of his mind to connect dread with unknown parentage, and in this case, as in his own, there was enough reason to make the connection logical.

But what was to be done with Mirah? She needed shelter and protection in the fullest sense. All his chivalrous sentiment rose to insist that the sooner and more fully he could engage the interest of others besides himself, the better he would fulfill her claims upon him. He had no right to provide for her entirely, though he might be able to; the very depth of the impression she had made drove him to desire that she understand herself as entirely independent of him. Vague visions of the future, which he tried to dispel as fantastic, left their influence behind as an anxiety stronger than any rational motive: that those who observed his actions closely should be acquainted from the start with the history of his relation to Mirah. He had learned to hate secrecy regarding the grand ties and obligations of his life, hating it the more because a powerful spell of interwoven sensibilities hindered him from breaking such silence. Deronda had vowed to himself that since the truths which disgrace mortals are not always of their own making, he would never make the truth a disgrace to another through his own act. He was not without terror lest he break this vow and fall into the apologetic philosophy that explains the world away as containing nothing better than one's own conduct.

At one moment, he resolved to tell the whole of his adventure to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger the next morning at breakfast. But the possibility that something entirely new might reveal itself on his next visit to Mrs. Meyrick checked this impulse. Finally, he fell asleep on the conclusion that he would wait until that visit had been made.
