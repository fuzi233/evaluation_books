I stood on the bluff above my cottage while March tightened its cold fingers around the Hudson, and the river below lay long and grey and hushed, like broth gone thin and forgotten on the back of a stove. The night had its own kitchen-sounds: ice ticking along the shore, a far train worrying the darkness with its iron whine, the wind combing dead reeds into a dry susurrus. Between those honest noises—water, wood, breath—there intruded the city’s distant clamor, a dull metallic grinding that made the whole air feel bruised. I had learned in ten years of waiting that such sound is not merely heard; it settles in the bones and seasons the mind, for good or ill. Tonight it only sharpened my hunger.

Not for food—though the body always asks its due—but for Mars.

I lifted my arms toward that ruddy point of light, the war-god’s eye, and prayed as I had prayed a thousand times: not with eloquent words, but with the plain insistence of a man begging the universe to return what it had once set upon his table and then snatched away. Ten years I had stood here, in one language and then another, trying to name what I missed. There are losses the dominant tongue can only flatten into a single convenient word—love, exile, longing—when the truth is a whole pantry of scents and textures that refuse translation: the warm spice of Martian air, the copper tang of its dust, the particular way a voice sounds under two moons.

Tonight the old pull answered.

A qualm swept through me—sudden, sour, rising from the belly as if some unseen hand had jerked the world sideways. My knees failed. I pitched forward, and for an instant the edge of the bluff was a knife beneath my face.

The nausea snapped into a crystal clarity that brought with it a memory I had tried, uselessly, to bury: that ghostly Arizona cave; the dark that had a weight; the thin, horrible rustle of something that did not belong among men. My muscles refused me again. I lay pinned, awake inside my own skin, hearing—here, absurdly, above the placid Hudson—the echo of those old moans.

Then I made the same superhuman effort, not with limbs but with will, as though I could tear myself free by sheer refusal to submit. There came the sharp click, like a taut wire parting, and I stood naked and free beside the staring shell that had been John Carter a heartbeat before.

I did not linger over that empty thing. My eyes went back to Mars. My hands rose. I waited.

The answer came like thought itself—swift, soundless, absolute. Cold and black wrapped me, an unthinkable chill that had no wind and no mercy, and then the darkness broke and sunlight poured through a tiny opening in a living dome.

I opened my eyes on red turf, cropped close as if by careful shears. The grass—if grass it was—held a sharp, mineral-green scent that pricked the nose like crushed stems and clean iron. Above me towered trunks so immense that the mind balked at their scale. Their surfaces shone as if polished by a million patient hands: ebony-black here, china-white there, and beyond, stems that were azure, scarlet, yellow, purple—colors with the intensity of spices ground fresh, not dulled by time. Blossoms clustered thickly, and the air was heavy with their perfume: sweet, resinous, almost edible, like nectar warmed on stone.

Birds—winged things—flickered among the branches, bright as tossed fruit. They were voiceless. Their silence unsettled me more than any cry would have. A forest should speak: the click of beaks, the argumentative chatter, the rhythm of life. Here, the only sound was the soft stir of leaves far overhead and my own breathing. Even the lack of song felt like a kind of theft, as though some ancient dialect of nature had died here and left only gesture behind.

Through the trees I saw something that made my heart stumble: an open sea, blue and shimmering under the brazen sun.

For an instant terror seized me that I had been flung to some alien world, a cruel miscast of fate. What guide had I through that trackless void? What certainty that the god I begged had heard me correctly? The mind scrabbled for familiar signs the way a cook scrabbles for the right herb in a dark pantry.

I rose—and immediately made the same ridiculous blunder I had made the first time I had awakened on Mars. The lesser gravity and thin air offered so little resistance that the simple act of standing launched me several feet upward. I came down hard, face-first, in the brilliant turf, the red blades springing back under me like resilient chaff.

The smell of the crushed vegetation rose quick and sharp, a green bitterness with a faint sweetness, and it steadied me. Mars or not, this was no Earth. I laughed aloud at my own forgetfulness, and when I rose again I did it with care, mastering once more the art of measuring effort to this lighter world.

I walked downslope toward the glint of water. The land had the look of deliberate husbandry. The sward was kept like an old English lawn; the trees were pruned to a uniform height of perhaps fifteen feet above the ground before their great crowns began, so that the forest became a high-ceiled chamber, a cultivated hall. Whoever tended this place understood the discipline of growth—how to cut back a living thing to make it flourish in the shape one wants. It was civilization’s signature, as unmistakable as bread baked daily or a hearth kept clean.

And yet the silence persisted, a careful silence, like a kitchen where knives are sharp and voices restrained.

As I neared the forest’s edge the scent changed. Salt threaded the air, and beneath it came the cool wet smell of a great river. Then the trees opened and I stepped from shadow into light and saw the meadow spread wide before the sea.

To my left the ocean ran to the horizon. Before me its far shore was only a faint line. To my right a mighty river flowed between scarlet banks, broad and majestic, sliding toward the sea with the calm confidence of something that had done this for ages. Upstream, perpendicular bluffs rose, and it seemed as though the river issued from their base like poured metal.

It should have been a scene to feed the eyes and soothe the mind.

Instead, my attention was torn away by movement: a score of figures in the meadow near the riverbank, shambling and feeding. At a distance they were manlike; closer they were a blasphemy stitched together from familiar parts.

The largest stood ten or twelve feet high when erect, with torsos and legs proportioned like a man’s. But their arms were short and moved in sinuous undulations, like trunk or serpent, boneless in appearance. They ran those odd hands over the turf as they went, as though feeling for something hidden beneath the close-cropped growth.

I slipped behind the bole of an enormous tree and watched.

One wandered nearer, and I saw it plainly. Its body was hairless and a ghoulish blue, except for a broad white band around a single protruding eye. That eye was all dead white—no pupil, no iris, no hint of mind behind it, just a blank staring disk. In the center of its face yawned a ragged circular hole, inflamed and wet, like a fresh bullet wound before the blood comes. Below it, the face went smooth and blank to the chin. No mouth.

The head—everything but that empty face—was capped with a tangled mass of jet-black hair eight or ten inches long. Each “hair” was as thick as an angleworm. As the creature’s scalp muscles shifted, the mass writhed, each strand seeming to move with its own intent, crawling and coiling around the fearsome visage. The sight turned the stomach. The imagination insisted on giving each strand an ear, an attention, a hunger.

Its feet were human in shape but monstrous in size, flat, broad, three feet from heel to toe. Behind it dragged a massive tail six feet long, round at the base, tapering to a flat blade that trailed at a right angle to the ground.

Then I understood the motion of its hands. It fed.

With razorlike talons it cropped the tender red vegetation and, in a gesture both intimate and obscene, sucked it into two mouths set in the palms of its hands. The arms were not merely arms but throats, pulsing as the stuff was drawn inward. The act had the methodical steadiness of grazing animals, yet it felt like a parody of human eating—hands made into hunger, the face denied even the dignity of a mouth.

And from each armpit dangled a tiny replica of the adult creature, each perhaps six inches long, suspended by a stem that grew from the crown of its head to the parent’s body. Some adults bore them, some did not. The small ones varied from tight buds no larger than an inch to perfectly formed little horrors ten or twelve inches in length. Others of similar size fed freely among the herd, and the whole group graded upward in size to the great adults.

I had faced green warriors and great white apes; I had seen death deal its brutal, honest work. This was something else—life arranged in a manner that mocked the categories by which men remember the world.

Still, fear is a practical thing. They did not look built for fighting. I was naked and unarmed, but curiosity—rash, familiar—rose in me. I found myself on the verge of stepping out to see how they would react to a man.

The decision died in my throat.

A shrieking wail split the air from the direction of the bluffs. It was not a natural cry. It had the harsh insistence of a signal, a sound made to be obeyed. The herd froze as one, every white eye turning toward the cliffs. At the same instant, every wormlike “hair” upon their heads rose stiffly upright, like a thousand antennae listening.

In that motion lay the explanation: those writhing strands were ears—countless ears—the last remnant of some strange evolution from an original Tree of Life. The thought came with a chill. A creature that hears through a thousand living filaments lives in a different acoustic world than any man. It is tuned to whispers. It feeds on sound the way it feeds on grass.

All eyes turned toward one of their number, larger than the rest—clearly the leader. A purring sound issued from the mouth in the palm of one hand. Then it sprang, and the herd followed, bounding toward the bluffs with leaps of twenty or thirty feet, like grotesque kangaroos.

They were fleeing—or going to kill. The wail had summoned them.

I followed.

It was madness for a naked man to chase monsters across an open meadow, but the urgency of that cry, and the way it had commanded them, hooked me. In this lighter gravity my own leaps were prodigious; Earth muscles, given Martian conditions, turn a man into a projectile. I crossed the meadow in long bounds, the wind of my passing stirring the cropped red blades.

Near the cliffs the ground was littered with great boulders fallen from the crags above. Using these as cover, I came close before the scene broke upon me.

The plant men—there was no other name—had surrounded a small party of green Martians: five or six of them, men and women. The sight of them snapped certainty into place. This was Barsoom. The sea and forest might be strange, but the green warriors were unmistakable: towering males with gleaming tusks thrusting up from massive lower jaws; eyes set laterally so they could look forward and back without turning the head; antennae-like ears rising from the forehead; the extra pair of arms between shoulder and hip. Their glossy green hides and metal ornaments marked different hordes.

Different hordes. That puzzled me even as dread rose, because the green Martians war eternally among themselves. Only once had I seen them gathered across tribal lines—when Tars Tarkas had marshaled the great host to march upon Zodanga for Dejah Thoris. Yet here they stood together, back to back, as if necessity had forged a brief, brittle alliance.

They held longswords and daggers. No firearms. Against these plant men, that absence was a death sentence.

The leader of the plant men charged.

It came within a dozen feet, then sprang up and over them, as though to pass directly above their heads. Its tail rose high to one side, and as it flew over, it swept that blade down in one terrible arc.

The blow crushed a green warrior’s skull like an eggshell.

The herd began to circle the remaining Martians with bewildering speed, their leaps long and erratic, their purring screeches designed to confuse and terrify. Two more plant men leaped simultaneously from either side, tails swinging.

Two more green Martians fell.

Now only one warrior and two females remained, and the plant men tightened their ring. The lone warrior’s stance was magnificent, the old courage of his race burning bright even when all else fails. When the next two charged, he was ready. He swung his great longsword up and met one hurtling bulk with a clean cut that clove it from chin to groin.

But the other’s tail swept down and crushed both females where they stood.

The last green warrior saw the end and did not wait to be penned. When he perceived the entire herd charging as one body, he rushed to meet them, swinging that sword in the ferocious figure-eight I had seen only the greatest warriors achieve. He cut a path through them and broke for the forest, seeking cover where their aerial blows would be harder to deliver.

They overtook him before he was halfway.

He backed against a boulder, panting, sword up, while the herd hissed and screeched and bounded about him, balked only for an instant by the stone at his back.

Admiration swelled in me until it became action. I have never been able to watch courage die unaided. Acting on impulse, with a plan formed in the heartbeat it took to commit, I sprang from my concealment.

In half a dozen leaps I reached the corpses of the fallen green Martians. The smell of blood—real blood this time, metallic and warm—hit the air. I snatched up a mighty longsword from a dead warrior’s hand. The weight of it was familiar, honest. It fit my grip like a remembered tool, as if my palm had been made for it in another life.

Then I bounded toward the plant men.

The old battle-joy rose in me, that red mist that narrows the world to edge and target, and I smiled the smile that has always betrayed me in the midst of danger.

They did not see me at first. Their single eyes were fixed on their prey; their thousand ears were tuned to his breath and the scrape of his feet and the nervous rustle of the meadow grass. My approach was soundless in the light gravity.

I was among them before they understood. Four of them fell in quick succession, my blade biting through blue flesh with a wet, resistant give. Instead of blood, a green sticky syrup spurted and smeared, like the viscous ooze from a crushed caterpillar, and the air took on a faint, bruised-plant odor—sap and bitterness.

For an instant the herd recoiled.

In that instant the green warrior sprang to my side, seizing the chance as only a seasoned fighter can. Together we fought with our backs near the boulder, denying them the space to leap cleanly overhead.

Their tails lashed like scythes. Talons raked. The purring screeching churned the air, and above it, again, rose that shrill wail from high on the cliff—once, twice, again and again—calling more.

We were splashed head to foot with their syrupy lifefluid. It clung to skin and hair, tacky and cold, and when it dried it tugged like spilled sugar. A tail’s blade grazed my arm; the pain was sharp, clean, like a knife drawn across flesh. Another creature sprang, and I felt its great weight slam onto my back.

Talons sank into me.

Then came the most horrifying sensation of all: moist lips—if one could call them that—fastening to the wounds while the claws still held me, sucking at my lifeblood. Not from a face, but from a palm-mouth, drinking with the efficiency of a man siphoning broth through a tube. The intimacy of that feeding, the theft of warmth, made my vision flare white with fury.

I fought to keep my throat safe. In front of me one plant man lunged, reaching for my neck, while two more on either side whipped their tails in murderous rhythm. The green warrior beside me was hard-pressed, ringed and slashing, holding his ground with brute skill and relentless endurance.

I felt the unequal strain—two men against too many—and knew we could not keep it long.

Then the green warrior tore free, saw my plight, and with one great sweep of his blade raked the creature from my back. It fell twitching, syrup jetting from its severed channels. Freed, I dispatched the others nearest me, and we closed together again near the boulder, almost back to back, making a wall of steel.

We were winning ground now, because on the earth their advantage lessened. They could not soar above us for the killing tail-sweep without the clear space to leap, and each time they rushed in we met them with edge and point.

The shrill wail rose again.

This time I glanced up.

High above, on a little natural balcony in the cliff face, stood a man-shaped figure shrieking the signal. He waved one hand toward the river’s mouth as though beckoning, and with the other he pointed and gesticulated down at us, directing doom with the careless authority of a conductor leading a harsh, repetitive anthem.

I followed his gaze.

Across the meadow, from the forest, from the far flat land beyond the river, lines of leaping plant men converged—scores, then more, a hundred separate streams of bounding blue bodies. With them came new shapes, stranger still: swift-running monsters that moved now upright, now on all fours, their gait switching like a machine changing gears.

The meadow that had looked so carefully tended—this domestic, civilized carpet of red growth—was suddenly a battlefield about to be drowned in bodies.

“It will be a great death,” I said to my companion, because there are moments when plain words are all that remain. I nodded toward the oncoming tide. “Look!”

He cast one quick glance, and then—of all things—he smiled.

“We may at least die fighting and as great warriors should, John Carter,” he replied.

At the sound of my name in his guttural Martian tongue, astonishment cut through even the battle-haze. I turned, and through the slime and the sunlight and the stench of crushed sap I recognized the face beside me: the broad, fierce features; the intelligent eyes; the bearing that no rank can counterfeit.

Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark—my friend, the mightiest of the green men of Barsoom—stood at my side.
