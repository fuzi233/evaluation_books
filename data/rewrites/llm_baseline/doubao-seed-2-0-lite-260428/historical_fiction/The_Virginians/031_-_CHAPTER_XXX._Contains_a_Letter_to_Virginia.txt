One fine afternoon, Harry Warrington turned up for his customary dinner at the White Horse ordinary, and was delighted to find the whole common room roaring with laughter at the jolly, rosy countenance of Parson Sampson, who held the table enthralled with a string of racy bons mots. Though he had not ventured up to London for months, the parson carried all the latest town gossip — or what passed for it among the country squires who frequented the tavern: the goings-on at the King’s Kensington palace, the latest scandals out of Pall Mall, how Admiral Byng was passing his days in prison, who called on him, the Newmarket race odds, and the reigning toast of Covent Garden. Sampson’s facts were rarely perfectly accurate: he might misname a nobleman’s mistress, mix up which lady had run off with an actor, or muddle the names of the officers who quarrelled at Marybone Gardens — but none of his audience cared a whit. So long as the stories were lively and charmingly wicked, their correctness was of no moment. A century past, the worldly parson who haunted the tavern, the racecourse, the theatre pit, and the fringes of fashion was no oddity in English society: he could shout loudest in the hunting field, sing the jolliest drinking song after a Covent Garden performance, and handle a hand of cards at the gaming table as deftly as any gentleman present.

Whether from a flush of modesty or the glow of claret, Sampson’s red cheeks burned even brighter when he spotted Harry enter. He whispered a line from Horace to the gouty country squire at his side, stumbled over his own feet in his haste, and seized Harry’s hand with warm enthusiasm. “My dear sir, my very dear sir, my conqueror at spades, clubs, and hearts too — I am overjoyed to see your honour looking so fresh and well!” he cried. Harry returned his greeting gladly, and complimented the parson on his cheerful, ruddy looks. The squire in the drab homespun coat edged with gold lace shifted over to make space for Harry beside him, and called for Sampson to return to his own seat across the table to finish his story about Lord Ogle and the grocer’s wife — only for his sentence to end in a loud oath and yell when the flustered parson trod full force on his aching gouty toe.

Sampson begged his pardon, then turned back to rattle off every bit of Castlewood news to Harry, loud enough for the whole room to hear: Lord Castlewood sent his cousin his warmest affectionate remembrances, and had given him special orders to travel down to Tunbridge Wells to look after Harry’s morals; the Viscountess and Lady Fanny were gone to Harrogate to take the waters; Will had won a handsome sum at Newmarket and was off on a visit to a royal duke; even the Castlewood housemaid Molly had cried her eyes out at the departure of Harry’s valet Gumbo. He assured the gathered guests that all Hampshire was gossiping of Harry’s remarkable string of successes at Tunbridge, casting a knowing, arch little look as he implied his popularity with the ladies, and that the whole Castlewood household only hoped he would not grow too spoiled to enjoy their quiet Hampshire home.

By and by the rest of the diners drifted off to their own pursuits, leaving Harry and the parson alone with their bottles. “I have already had more than my fair share of wine,” Sampson laughed, “but that is no reason I should not have plenty more!” He tossed off toast after toast, bumper after bumper, to Harry’s great amusement, and after a while Harry grew so warm-hearted and generous that he insisted Sampson must not waste his coin on a separate tavern room — there was a perfectly good spare chamber in Harry’s own lodgings, free of charge. He ordered Sampson’s trunk sent up at once, and bade Gumbo make his guest as comfortable as possible, before dragging the parson down to the stables to show off his new horses. There, Sampson spotted the very horse he had lost to Harry at cards months prior; the old beast whinnied happily and nuzzled against his former master’s coat, and Harry swore by Jupiter that the horse was Sampson’s again, a gift no strings attached. The parpson seized Harry’s hand to bless him, then flung his arms around the horse’s neck, weeping tears made half of Bordeaux and half of pure grateful joy.

Arm in arm, the two friends trundled over to Madame Bernstein’s apartments, trailing the mixed scent of stables and claret behind them, their cheeks flushed and eyes bright enough that no one could mistake what had been their afternoon amusement. The old baroness received Harry’s companion pleasantly enough: she found Sampson’s raucous broad jokes and rattling good talk an amusing novelty, like a highly spiced new dish her cook might serve the first two or three times it was prepared. The only amusement she never tired of, she insisted, was cards. “The cards never cheat,” she would say. “A bad hand tells you the truth to your face, and there is no flattery in the world sweeter than a good suite of trumps.” When she was in high spirits about to sit down to a high-stakes game, she would laughingly bid her nephew’s chaplain say grace before the “feast”. Sampson had avoided her table at first, slapping his empty pockets comically and complaining that her play was far too high for a poor clergyman, who had already emptied his purse playing cards against Harry back at Castlewood. But Harry, whose purse was overflowing with guineas and whose heart overflowed with generosity, happily pressed a handful of ready coin on him, and the parson was soon seated at the card table beside her ladyship.

Harry adapted to Tunbridge fashionable life with remarkable speed. His suits were still cut from plain black cloth, but they were of the finest possible make and quality. “Give him a star and ribbon, let his stocking fall down a little, and his hair fall over his shoulder, and he would make a perfect Hamlet,” the gay old Duchess of Queensberry joked. “I have no doubt he has already been the death of a dozen Ophelias, back home among the American Indians.” His lace and linen were as fine as his aunt could have wished. He bought gilded shaving plates from the Pantiles toy vendors, and two magnificent rich brocade morning gowns in which he lolled at ease sipping chocolate each morning. He collected custom swords and carved walking canes, French watches with painted backs and diamond settings, and enamel snuffboxes crafted by the most skilled Parisian artisans. Every day his anteroom was crowded with grooms, jockeys, and tradesmen waiting to see him, all ushered in one by one by Gumbo, who acted as master of his household — and no single negro could possibly have tended all of Harry’s new fine possessions, let alone his stable of horses and the custom postchaise he had just purchased.

Harry also applied himself to mastering all the accomplishments proper for a gentleman of his station. A French fencing master and a French dancing master resided in Tunbridge that season, and Harry attended their lessons diligently, soon growing skilled enough with the foil to hold his own against his instructor, and moving through the steps of a minuet so gracefully that Lady Maria — herself an elegant dancer — sighed and admitted no gentleman at the royal court could move through the dance half so finely. When a riding master offered to teach him to “school” the great war horse, Harry laughed it off. His Virginia riding seat was more than good enough, he said, and he had yet to meet a man in all of the fine company of England who could ride half as well as his friend Colonel George Washington, out at Mount Vernon.

Sampson meanwhile was living better than he had in years. He knew the world, and told far more of it than he knew, and Harry, barely twenty, adored his old college jokes, stale puns, and travel anecdotes sloshed through with the wine of a hundred dinner tables. A hundred years ago — and we may be certain no such people exist today, in our blessed Victorian age — there was a class of grown men in London who loved to attach themselves to fashionable young men new to town: act as their unofficial mentors to Covent Garden, escort them to the gaming tables, drink lemonade while their young charges drank burgundy, and stagger home perfectly sober when the young gentleman reeled off to fight the night watch. Sampson was a perfect specimen of this now-extinct breed of companion, and he thrived on Harry’s generosity as cheerfully as any man could. He soon became Harry’s confidential guide, friend, chaplain, and informal secretary, as the following carefully preserved letter from the Warrington family archives makes plain:

>
> Mrs. Bligh's Lodgings, Pantiles, Tunbridge Wells.
> August 25th, 1756.
>
> HONOURED MADAM -- Your honoured letter of 20 June, per Mr. Trail of Bristol, has been forwarded to me duly, and I have to thank your goodness and kindness for the good advice which you are pleased to give me, as also for the remembrances of dear home, which I shall love never the worse for having been to the home of our ancestors in England.
>
> I writ you a letter by the last monthly packet, informing my honoured mother of the little accident I had on the road hither, and of the kind friends who I found and whom took me in. Since then I have been profiting of the fine weather and the good company here, and have made many friends among our nobility, whose acquaintance I am sure you will not be sorry that I should make. Among their lordships I may mention the famous Earl of Chesterfield, late Ambassador to Holland, and Viceroy of the Kingdom of Ireland; the Earl of March and Ruglen, who will be Duke of Queensberry at the death of his Grace; and her Grace the Duchess, a celebrated beauty of the Queen's time, when she remembers my grandpapa at Court. These and many more persons of the first fashion attend my aunt's assemblies, which are the most crowded at this crowded place. Also on my way hither I stayed at Westerham, at the house of an officer, Lieut.-Gen. Wolfe, who served with my grandfather and General Webb in the famous wars of the Duke of Marlborough. Mr. Wolfe has a son, Lieut.-Col. James Wolfe, engaged to be married to a beautiful lady now in this place, Miss Lowther of the North -- and though but 30 years old he is looked up to as much as any officer in the whole army, and has served with honour under his Royal Highness the Duke wherever our arms have been employed.
>
> I thank my honoured mother for announcing to me that a quarter's allowance of 52l. 10s. will be paid me by Mr. Trail. I am in no present want of cash, and by practising a rigid economy, which will be necessary (as I do not disguise) for the maintenance of horses, Gumbo, and the equipage and apparel requisite for a young gentleman of good family, hope to be able to maintain my credit without unduly trespassing upon yours. The linnen and clothes which I brought with me will with due care last for some years -- as you say. 'Tis not quite so fine as worn here by persons of fashion, and I may have to purchase a few very fine shirts for great days: but those I have are excellent for daily wear.
>
> I am thankful that I have been quite without occasion to use your excellent family pills. Gumbo hath taken them with great benefit, who grows fat and saucy upon English beef, ale, and air. He sends his humble duty to his mistress, and prays Mrs. Mountain to remember him to all his fellow-servants, especially Dinah and Lily, for whom he has bought posey-rings at Tunbridge Fair.
>
> Besides partaking of all the pleasures of the place, I hope my honoured mother will believe that I have not been unmindful of my education. I have had masters in fencing and dancing, and my Lord Castlewood's chaplain, the Reverend Mr. Sampson, having come hither to drink the waters, has been so good as to take a vacant room at my lodging. Mr. S. breakfasts with me, and we read together of a morning -- he saying that I am not quite such a dunce as I used to appear at home. We have read in Mr. Rapin's History, Dr. Barrow's Sermons, and, for amusement, Shakspeare, Mr. Pope's Homer, and (in French) the translation of an Arabian Work of Tales, very diverting. Several men of learning have been staying here besides the persons of fashion; and amongst the former was Mr. Richardson, the author of the famous books which you and Mountain and my dearest brother used to love so. He was pleased when I told him that his works were in your closet in Virginia, and begged me to convey his respectful compliments to my lady-mother. Mr. R. is a short fat man, with little of the fire of genius visible in his eye or person.
>
> My aunt and my cousin, the Lady Maria, desire their affectionate compliments to you, and with best regards for Mountain, to whom I enclose a note, I am, -- Honoured madam, your dutiful son,

Scrawled at the foot of the letter in Madam Esmond's sharp, looping hand was a note:
From my son. Received October 15 at Richmond. Sent 16 jars preserved peaches, 224 lbs. best tobacco, 24 finest hams, per Royal William of Liverpool, 8 jars peaches, 12 hams for my nephew, the Rt. Honourable the Earl of Castlewood. 4 jars, 6 hams for the Baroness Bernstein, ditto ditto for Mrs. Lambert of Oakhurst, Surrey, and 1/2 cwt. tobacco. Packet of Infallible Family Pills for Gumbo. My Papa's large silver-gilt shoe-buckles for H., and red silver-laced saddle-cloth.

Tucked inside the first letter was a second, far messier note, written for Mrs. Mountain:
> For Mrs. Mountain.
> What do you mien, you silly old Mountain, by sending an order for your poor old divadends dew at Xmas? I'd have you to know I don't want your 7l. 10, and have toar your order up into 1000 bitts. I've plenty of money. But I'm obleaged to you all same. A kiss to Fanny from -- Your loving HARRY.

Another note in Madam Esmond's handwriting sat below it:
This note, which I desired M. to show to me, proves that she hath a good heart, and that she wished to show her gratitude to the family, by giving up her half-yearly divd. (on L500 3 per ct.) to my boy. Hence I reprimanded her very slightly for daring to send money to Mr. E. Warrington, unknown to his mother. Note to Mountain not so well spelt as letter to me. -- Mem. to write to Revd. Mr. Sampson desire to know what theolog. books he reads with H. Recommend Law, Baxter, Drelincourt. -- Request H. to say his catechism to Mr. S., which he has never quite been able to master. By next ship peaches (3), tobacco 1/2 cwt. Hams for Mr. S.

The two Warrington sons and their formidable mother have long been dust, now. How then to explain that the formal letter home to Madam Esmond is almost perfectly spelled, save for one tiny grammatical slip, while the playful little note to the old family nurse is dotted with charming misspellings? It takes no great detective to guess: the first letter was rewritten and corrected by Sampson, Harry's informal tutor, for his mother's careful eyes. The little note to Mountain Harry dashed off in a hurry, half distracted by a pretty milliner's girl passing the window, or a dancing bear with a tabor parading across the common, or a jockey trotting under his balcony to show off a new horse. There are days when the best of us misspell a word or fumble a grammar rule, after all.

As for the folk who cry out that Harry was hypocritical to send such a demure, prim letter home to his mother, when he spent his days drinking, gambling, and running about with his friends? No young person alive has never written home with a carefully edited account of their doings, to avoid worrying their parents. No parent alive, if they are honest, ever expected their grown son to be perfectly meek and decorous the moment they step outside their front door. It is the gentle, amiable, virtuous hypocrisy that makes the bond between generations work. The parent is king or queen of their castle, and expects their household to treat them with the deference due their rank; and once the young folk are out of their sight, they can dance, skip, tumble, and eat their fill of cakes and ale, as all young people have always done. No one ever tells their dear grandmamma every last silly, merry prank of their youth — and no grandmamma, for all her loud protests that she was perfectly well-behaved in her own springtime, was ever quite as good as she claims to have been.
