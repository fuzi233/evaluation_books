Xodar listened with a mixture of shock and disbelief as I recounted the chaos that had erupted within the arena during the rites of Issus. Even though he had already voiced his skepticism regarding the goddess's divinity, he struggled to comprehend how one could brandish a sword in her presence without being instantly shattered into a thousand pieces by her supposed divine wrath.

"It is the final proof," he declared finally. "Nothing more is required to obliterate the last vestige of my superstitious belief in Issus's divinity. She is merely a wicked old woman, wielding immense power for evil through schemes that have kept her own people—and all of Barsoom—in religious ignorance for ages."

"She remains all-powerful here, however," I replied. "Therefore, it is imperative that we depart at the first opportunity that presents itself."

"I trust you will find a propitious moment," Xodar said, laughing dryly. "For in all my life, I have never witnessed a scenario wherein a prisoner of the First Born managed to escape."

"Tonight will suffice," I answered.

"It will soon be night," Xodar noted. "How can I assist in this venture?"

"Can you swim?" I asked.

"No slimy *silian* that haunts the depths of Korus is more at ease in water than Xodar," he responded confidently.

"Excellent. The Red Man likely cannot swim," I reasoned. "There is scarcely enough water in their dominions to float the smallest craft. Consequently, one of us must support him through the sea to the vessel we choose. I had hoped to traverse the entire distance submerged, but I fear the Red Youth could not endure such a journey. Even the bravest among them are terrified by the mere thought of deep water, having gone ages without seeing a lake, river, or sea."

"The Red One accompanies us?" Xodar asked.

"Yes."

"That is wise. Three swords are superior to two, especially when the third wielder possesses the prowess of this young man. I have observed him battle in the arena at the rites of Issus numerous times. Until I saw you fight, I had never encountered anyone who appeared unconquerable despite overwhelming odds. One might mistake you two for master and pupil, or father and son. Recall his face; there is a striking resemblance between you. It becomes particularly marked in combat—that same grim smile, that same maddening contempt for the adversary visible in every movement and facial expression."

"As it may be, Xodar, he is a formidable fighter. I believe we shall form a trio nearly impossible to defeat. Should my friend Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, join us, we could carve a path from one end of Barsoom to the other, even if the entire world opposed us."

"They will learn from whence you hail," Xodar countered. "That is but one of the superstitions Issus has instilled in a credulous humanity. She operates through the Holy Therns, who are as ignorant of her true nature as the Barsoomians of the outer world. Her decrees arrive at the Therns' temples inscribed in blood upon strange parchments. These poor, deluded fools believe they receive revelations from a goddess via supernatural agency, finding these messages on their guarded altars, inaccessible to any but the chosen few. I myself have delivered these messages for Issus for many years.

"A long tunnel connects the Temple of Issus to the principal Temple of Matai Shang, excavated eons ago by the slaves of the First Born in such absolute secrecy that no Thern suspected its existence.

"For their part, the Therns maintain temples scattered throughout the civilized world. Here, unseen priests disseminate the doctrines of the Mysterious River Iss, the Valley Dor, and the Lost Sea of Korus, persuading the gullible masses to undertake voluntary pilgrimages. This influx swells the wealth of the Holy Therns and increases their ranks of slaves.

"Thus, the Therns serve as the primary mechanism for extracting the wealth and labor the First Born demand. Occasionally, the First Born launch raids upon the outer world, capturing females from the royal houses of the Red Men, seizing the latest battleships, and appropriating skilled artisans capable of replicating technologies they cannot invent themselves.

"We are a non-productive race, proud of our idleness. It is considered criminal for a First Born to labor or innovate; that is the sole purpose of the lower orders, existing merely to sustain the long, luxurious lives of the First Born. With us, combat is everything. Were it not for war, there would be more First Born than Barsoom could support, for as far as I know, none of us die a natural death. Our females would live forever were it not for our weariness of them, compelling us to replace them with others. Issus alone is shielded from death; she has endured for countless ages."

"Do not the other Barsoomians live forever but for the doctrine of the voluntary pilgrimage, dragging them to the bosom of Iss at or before their thousandth year?" I queried.

"I now feel certain there is no doubt that they are precisely the same species as the First Born," Xodar admitted. "I pray I live long enough to fight for them, atoning for the sins I have committed against them through generations of ignorance bred by false teachings."

As he finished speaking, a strange call echoed across the waters of Omean. I had heard it the previous evening as well; it signaled the end of the day, when the men of Omean spread their silks upon the decks of their battleships and cruisers to sink into the dreamless sleep of Mars.

Our guard entered to conduct his final inspection before dawn. His duty completed quickly, the heavy door of our prison slammed shut behind him, leaving us alone for the night.

I allowed him time to retreat to his quarters, as Xodar predicted, before springing to the grated window to survey the nearby waters. A quarter-mile offshore lay a monstrous battleship, flanked by several smaller cruisers and one-man scouts. Only the battleship maintained a watch. I spotted the sentinel clearly in the upper works; watching him, I saw him spread his sleeping silks upon the tiny platform where he stood. Soon, he collapsed fully onto his couch. The discipline on Omean was remarkably lax. Yet, it was understandable; no enemy suspected the existence of such a fleet on Barsoom, nor of the First Born, nor of the Sea of Omean itself. Why maintain a watch when no threat existed?

Shortly thereafter, I descended to the floor to discuss the vessels with Xodar.

"There is one there," he pointed out. "My personal property, built to carry five men. It is the swiftest of the swift. If we can board her, we can certainly make a memorable dash for liberty." He proceeded to detail the craft's equipment, its engines, and the features that made it a flying marvel.

Listening to his description, I recognized a specific gear trick that Kantos Kan had taught me during our clandestine voyage under false names in the navy of Zodanga beneath Sab Than, the Prince. I realized then that the First Born had stolen this technology from the ships of Helium, for only Helium vessels possessed such gearing. Furthermore, I confirmed Xodar's claim regarding the craft's speed; nothing that cleaved the thin air of Mars could approach the velocity of Helium's ships.

We resolved to wait at least an hour for the stragglers to settle into their silks. During this interval, I fetched the Red Youth to our cell so we could prepare for our reckless bid for freedom.

I scaled the partition wall separating our cells, finding a narrow ledge about a foot wide. Walking carefully along it, I reached the cell where the boy sat on his bench. He had been leaning back, gazing up at the glowing dome of Omean. When he spotted me balancing above him, his eyes widened in astonishment, followed immediately by a broad grin of appreciative understanding.

As I prepared to drop to the floor beside him, he gestured for me to wait. Moving close below, he whispered, "Catch my hand; I can almost leap to the top of that wall myself. I have tried many times, succeeding a little each day. Someday, I should be able to make it."

Lying prone across the wall, I extended my hand downward. With a short run from the center of the cell, he spranged upward until I grasped his outstretched fingers. Pulling gently, I hauled him up to the ledge beside me.

"You are the first jumper I have ever seen among the Red Men of Barsoom," I remarked.

He smiled. "It is not strange. I will explain why when we have more time."

Returning to Xodar's cell, we discussed our plans until the allotted hour passed. There, we bound ourselves by a solemn oath to fight to the death for one another against whatever enemies confronted us, knowing that even if we escaped the First Born, the rest of the world—with its powerful religious superstitions—might still oppose us.

It was agreed that I would navigate the craft once we boarded, and if we reached the outer world safely, we would attempt to proceed directly to Helium without stopping.

"Why Helium?" the Red Youth asked.

"I am a prince of Helium," I replied.

He offered me a peculiar look but said nothing further. I wondered at the time what significance his expression held, but amidst the pressing urgency of our escape, the thought faded, never to trouble me again until later.
