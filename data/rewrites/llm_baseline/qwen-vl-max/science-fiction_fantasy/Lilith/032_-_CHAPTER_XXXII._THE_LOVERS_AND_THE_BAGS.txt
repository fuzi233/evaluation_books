The night turned sharp with cold. Beneath me, the body stiffened into ice. The wolves drew near—their low growls echoing through the silence, their padded feet whispering over stone, their breath hot and ragged in the air. I saw them: a ring of glowing eyes, narrowing, closing in. My end had come.

I rose—only to realize I had no weapon, not even a stick. They surged forward, jaws wide, eyes blazing with hunger. I stood still, helpless, waiting for the end. For a heartbeat, they paused over the horse—then came at me.

Then, like a sudden storm from the sky, a green-eyed shadow fell upon them. A host of cats, led by a great gray one whose presence I felt before I saw him—only his eyes, fierce and luminous, burned through the dark. I knew him instantly. And I knew his size, his strength, his fury.

A battle erupted—silent but violent, its chaos carried only by soundless movements and flashes of light. I wanted to flee, but where? Every step would be a stumble. And both predator and feline could scent me, see me, in the blackness.

Then the howling ceased. The cats’ cries grew louder, wilder. Soon, the soft pad of retreating paws told me the wolves were gone. The cats had won.

But now they turned on me.

Teeth sank into my legs. In seconds, I was swarmed—dozens of bodies leaping, biting, clawing. They clung to me like living vines, tearing at my flesh, scratching with hooked claws that left deep scores. I fought back with desperate rage—ripping, crushing, kicking—but they held fast. When I tried to throw them off, they clung to my hands like barnacles. I stomped, clawed their eyes, locked my fingers around their necks—but not one would let go.

They found new places to bite, new angles to scratch. They spat and hissed in my face—until, driven mad by pain and terror, I collapsed onto the ground. Then they abandoned my body and attacked my face.

I rose—and they fled from it, turning instead to my legs once more.

In agony, I broke free and ran—no direction, no thought, just movement through the thick dark. They followed in a living tide, brushing against me, leaping up, but no longer mauling. When I fell—often—they waited for me to rise. When I slowed, they renewed their assault.

All night, they drove me forward—yet guided me carefully. No gullies, no traps. I passed the Evil Wood unseen, leaving it behind in the shadows.

At last, dawn broke. I emerged beyond the river channels, standing on the edge of the orchard valley. Joy surged through me. I called out to my tormentors—only to find none remained.

I collapsed onto the moss and slept.

I awoke to a kick—then another. Bound hand and foot, I was once again the prisoner of the giants.

“What better fate?” I whispered, laughing bitterly. “Who else should I belong to?”

Another kick silenced my sarcasm. With repeated prods, they helped me to my feet. Six of them surrounded me. They untied my legs, lashed ropes to each ankle, and dragged me away.

I walked as best I could, but when they pulled both ropes at once, I fell. Each time, they kicked me back up.

They led me straight to my old task. Tied to a tree, my arms freed, they placed the hated flint in my left hand. Then they lay down and began pelting me with fruit and stones—mostly missing.

If I’d been free, if I’d had that stick lying nearby—I would have torn them apart. But I couldn’t move. Not yet.

“Still,” I thought, “the Little Ones will come at night.”

That gave me strength.

All day, I labored under the sun. At dusk, they bound my hands and left me tied to the tree.

I slept deeply, but woke often—each time from dreams of being buried beneath a mountain of children.

Morning came. The giants returned, with their kicks and their brutish presence.

Near noon, weak from hunger and exhaustion, I heard rustling in the brush. Then—laughter.

Not any laughter. That bell-like, clear, joyous sound that stirred my soul.

I cried out in delight.

Instantly, the forest exploded. Trumpeting like baby elephants, neighing like foals, bellowing like calves—through the trees came the Little Ones.

On tiny horses, small elephants, miniature bears. But the sounds came from the riders, not the beasts.

Among them walked larger boys and girls—a woman cradling a baby who crowed with laughter.

The giants sprang up—only to be met with a hail of sharp stones. Horses charged their legs. Bears hugged them mid-thigh. Elephants wrapped trunks around their necks, pulled them down, and trampled them—just as they’d done to others, but never before had they received such treatment.

In moments, my ropes were cut. I was lifted into a sea of arms, embraced by countless little ones.

For hours, I saw no giants.

They made me sit. Lona came, wordless, and fed me—sweet red and yellow fruits, the most delicious I’d ever tasted. I ate while the entire colony guarded me.

Then two of the largest elephants were brought forward. Side by side, their trunks were hooked together, their tails tied. The animals could have undone it easily—but they didn’t. They understood. They stood still, parallel, obedient.

I climbed between them, settling into the hollow formed by their backs. They leaned inward, balancing the weight, creating a gentle, rocking litter.

My feet dangled past their tails, but my head rested on an ear of each. Smaller children mounted the elephants’ backs, forming a guard along the ridge. Then, in procession, we moved forward.

I did not ask where we were going. I surrendered myself to their care—almost as happy as they were.

At first, they chattered, laughed, played tricks. But when I closed my eyes, they grew quiet—so silent it was almost loud.

I woke to music.

A sudden burst of birdsong filled the forest. We were passing through the woods where the babies were found—the same stretch that ran from the valley to the warm stream.

A tiny girl sat beside me, her bare feet near my face. She stared at me, then spoke—others listened closely.

“We make a petisson for king,” she said.

“What is it, my darling?” I asked.

“Shut eyes one minute,” she replied.

“Certainly! Here goes!” I said, closing my eyes tightly.

“No, no! Not fore I tell oo!” she cried.

I opened them. We talked, laughed—for another hour.

Then, suddenly: “Close eyes!”

I obeyed. The elephants stopped. I heard soft scurrying, a rustle, then silence—deep, complete silence. In that world, silence is sometimes louder than sound.

“Open eyes!” twenty voices shouted at once.

I opened them.

Only the elephants remained. No children.

I knew they were fast—too fast for giants to catch. But when I raised myself, scanning the open, bushless forest, I saw nothing—not a trace.

I stared in stunned wonder.

The sun had set. Darkness crept in. Yet soon, birds began to sing—more and more, until the air trembled with song.

“I know,” I murmured, “it must be them. But how can children make birds sing?”

Then, glancing up into the tree above, I caught motion among the leaves.

White spots flickered in the dark. The music faded. A wave of childish laughter rolled through the air. Then—white spots everywhere. The trees were full of children!

They tumbled down—some dropping from branch to branch so fast I thought they’d fall. But they landed safely, laughing wildly.

I stepped from my litter. Instantly, I was surrounded—target of every prank, every joke, every joyful attack.

With stately calm, the elephants moved toward their sleeping place.

When the noise finally subsided, I asked, “Why did I never hear you sing like this before? Even when I thought it was you, I couldn’t believe it.”

One of the wildest answered: “Ah, but we weren’t birds then. We were run-creatures, not fly-creatures. We hid in bushes. But when we came to no-bushes—only trees—we had to build nests. When we built nests, we became birds. And when we were birds, we had to do bird-things. We asked the birds to teach us their noises. They taught us. Now we are real birds!”

He pointed upward. “Come see my nest. It’s not big enough for king—but big enough for king to see me in it!”

I smiled. “I cannot climb a tree without the sun to show me the way. When it returns, I’ll try.”

“Kings seldom have wings!” I added.

“King! king!” he shouted. “Oo knows none of us hasn’t no wings—foolis feddery tings! Arms and legs is better.”

“That’s true,” I said. “I can climb without wings—and carry straws in my mouth to build my own nest.”

“Oo knows!” he replied, already sucking his thumb.

Moments later, I heard his voice, high up in a giant walnut tree: “Up adain, king! Dood night! I seepy!”

And then—silence.

Until morning.
