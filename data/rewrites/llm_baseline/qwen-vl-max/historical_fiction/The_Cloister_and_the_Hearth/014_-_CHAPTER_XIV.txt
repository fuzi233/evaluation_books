The back door burst open with a violent crash, as if summoned by some hidden signal. The house was suddenly surrounded—trapped. In that moment of shock, Margaret found a flicker of composure. She whispered urgently, “Say he was here—but is gone.” Then, gripping Gerard’s arm, she nearly dragged him up the rough wooden steps leading to her father’s bedroom. Her own room lay just beyond.

Outside, the pounding on the door echoed again.
“Who knocks at this hour?” Martin called out, his voice steady despite the fear gnawing at his gut.
“Open, and you will see!” came the reply.
“I open not to thieves,” Martin answered. “Honest men are all abed now.”
“Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or you shall rue it!”

“That’s Dirk Brower’s voice,” Martin muttered under his breath. “What brings him so far from Tergou?”

“Open, and you will know!”

Slowly, deliberately, Martin drew the bolt. The door swung inward, and Dierich Brower stepped through, followed by four others. One man lingered behind at the back door, waiting for the signal.

“Now, Martin,” Dierich demanded, “where is Gerard Eliassoen?”

Gerard Eliassoen? Why, he was here but now!” Martin replied, feigning surprise.

“Was here?” Dierich’s face darkened. “And where is he now?”

“They say he has gone to Italy,” Martin said, shrugging. “Why, what is to do?”

“No matter,” Dierich growled. “When did he go? Tell me not that he left in such a storm as this!”

“There’s a coil about Gerard Eliassoen,” Martin remarked, dismissive and calm. He lit the candle and sat himself by the fire, coolly winding fine silk around his bowstring—the spot where the arrow’s nick wore it thin.

“I’ll tell you,” he continued, voice light. “You know his brother Giles? A little misbegotten imp, all head and arms? Well, he came galloping over on Peter Buyskens’s mule, bellowing something—I was too far off to hear the words, only the noise. Anyway, he startled Gerard. As soon as he was gone, there was crying and kissing, and then Gerard vanished. They say he’s gone to Italy—mayhap you know where that is, for I don’t.”

Dierich’s expression sank further still. There was no flaw in the tale. A cleverer man might have woven too many lies and raised suspicion. But Martin had done exactly what he was told—told one falsehood, and nothing more. He had not invented a single word beyond necessity.

“Mates,” Dierich said, turning to his companions, “I doubt he speaks truth. I told the burgomaster how it would be. He met the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens’s mule from Sevenbergen. ‘They’ve sent that imp to Gerard,’ he said. ‘Then Gerard must be at Sevenbergen.’”

“Ah, master!” Dierich continued, addressing himself now to the air, “It’s too late now. We should have thought of Sevenbergen before, instead of wasting our time hunting every corner of Tergou for those cursed parchments we’ll never find until we find the man who took them. If he was at Sevenbergen—and they sent the dwarf to warn him—we’re too late. He’s leagues away by now.”

He spat. “Confound that chalk-faced girl! She’s outwitted us bearded men. I told the burgomaster, but he wouldn’t listen. A wet jerkin apiece—that’s all we’ll get from this job.”

Martin grinned faintly, watching Dierich closely.

“However,” Dierich added, “to satisfy the burgomaster, we’ll search the house.”

At once, Martin’s face turned grave. The change did not escape Dierich. He paused, then said: “Two of you watch outside—one on each side of the house. No one must leap from the upper windows. The rest come with me.” He took the candle and climbed the stairs, followed by three of his men.

Martin was left alone.

He hung his head. Everything had gone so well at first. And now—this fatal turn. But then, like a spark in darkness, an idea flared. Gerard must be either in Peter’s room or Margaret’s—both low enough to jump from. If Gerard tried to escape, he’d leap out. Dierich had left a guard below—but what then? For half a minute, Gerard and he would be two against one. In that brief span, anything could happen.

Martin slipped the back door open slightly and waited, eyes fixed on the dim light spilling into Peter’s room.

“Curse the fool,” he muttered, “is he going to let them take him like a girl?”

The light moved into Margaret’s room. Still—no window opened. Had Gerard planned to flee, he would not have waited until the men were inside. Martin saw that instantly. He left the door and crept to the foot of the stairs, listening.

He began to think Gerard had escaped while the men were searching. The longer the silence lasted, the stronger the belief grew. But then—suddenly, rudely—it shattered.

Faint cries came from the inner chamber—Margaret’s room.

“They’ve taken him,” Martin groaned. “They’ve got him.”

A new terror seized him. If Gerard were taken, his life would be forfeit. And if harm came to Gerard, Margaret’s heart would break. He looked wildly around, like a beast cornered and desperate. In a flash, he made a decision—terribly fitting for the iron age and the soldier driven to the edge.

He stepped to each door in turn, mimicking Dierich’s voice sharply: “Watch the window!”

Then he quietly closed and bolted both doors. He picked up his bow and six arrows—fitted one to the string, stowed the rest in his quiver. His knife he placed on a chair behind him, hilt toward him.

There he stood, at the foot of the stairs, ready. Calm. Resolute. Determined to kill these four men—or die trying.

Two he could dispatch with arrows before they reached him. Then, Gerard and he would fight hand-to-hand with the remaining pair. He had seen men freeze in panic when attacked suddenly. If Dierich and his men hesitated even a second, he could shoot three instead of two—and then the odds would shift.

He did not wait long. Heavy footsteps echoed in Margaret’s room, drawing closer. The candlelight approached. Voices rose.

Martin’s heart pounded—not from fear, but from the weight of what was coming. Men walking into death, perhaps into his own. Four against two, in a space no larger than ten feet square. Gerard might be bound—helpless. Yet this man, who once trembled at the sight of a Giles-o’-lanthorn, did not falter now. He stood braced, body tensed, eyes blazing—ready to give life or take it.

Desperate stakes. To win meant exile—for life. To lose meant death, right there on that floor.

Dierich and his men found Peter asleep in his bed. They ransacked his cupboards, slashed their knives through the stuffed alligator nailed to the wall, searched beneath the bed. The room was large, full of hiding places—yet no sign of Gerard.

Then they moved to Margaret’s room.

Even at first glance, it discouraged them. Small, bare, without a cupboard. Only a large fireplace and chimney. Dierich’s eye fell upon them immediately.

There, on a chest barely a foot high, lay the beauty of Sevenbergen—sleeping, uncovered, wrapped only in snowy white sheets. Her linen was pure as snow. She looked like a lily fallen into a mud puddle.

She stirred, sat up, wide-eyed with shock. Seeing the men, she began to cry softly, pleading for mercy.

Her voice pierced Dierich’s pride.

“This is a to-do,” he muttered, uneasy. “We’re not going to hurt you, my pretty maid. Lie still. Shut your eyes. Think of your wedding night, while I look up this chimney to see if Master Gerard is hiding there.”

“Gerard—in my room?” she gasped.

“Why not?” Dierich pressed. “They say you and he—”

“Cruel!” she cried. “You know they’ve driven him away from me—driven him from his home! This is a lie! You are thieves! Wicked men! You are not of Sevenbergen, or you would know Margaret Brandt better than to search for her lover in this room—of all rooms in the world! Oh, brave! Four great hulking men, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor honest girl! The women in your own homes must be naught, or you would respect them enough to spare a girl of good character!”

“There!” Dierich snapped, turning abruptly. “Come away, before we hear worse.”

He didn’t look up the chimney. “He’s not there. Plaster can mend what a cudgel breaks—but a woman’s tongue is a double-edged dagger. And a girl is a woman with her mother’s milk still in her.”

He backed out quickly. “I told the burgomaster how it would be.”
