The air inside the tiny chapel was heavy with a hushed peace, broken only by the soft, natural murmur of summer drifting through the open door. Golden sunlight streamed through the high windows, casting a warm haze over the dust motes dancing near the ceiling. Ethne sat in the corner of a pew, her posture rigid, while Harry Feversham stood beside her, his hands clasped behind his back, feeling the weight of the moment press down upon his chest.

"It is good that you remember our conversation on the drive," Ethne began, her voice steady despite the tremor that threatened to shake it apart. "It matters deeply to me that you recall what we said. Because, though I have brought you back to me, I am sending you away again. You will become one of those absent friends whom I shall not lose, precisely *because* you are absent."

She spoke slowly, refusing to look at him, her gaze fixed on some point far beyond the pew. Six years of agony, of silent waiting, had narrowed her world to this single, terrible truth. At first, seeing him restored after so many years of fear and suffering, she had believed she could never utter the necessary words. But standing before him now, the necessity had crystallized into steel. She had retreated into the sanctuary to gather her strength, resolving to offer no outward sign of the cost this separation entailed. If she showed emotion, it would break the spell; if she remained stoic, he would understand that not a single moment of his exile had been wasted.

"It would be kinder for both of us if I show no sign of what this parting costs," she continued, her tone even and calm. "You will know enough, and I want you to know it. Not a single instant of your six years has been spent in vain for me. Yet, we need no signs to prove that."

A heavy silence fell over the church. To Feversham, her words were like rain falling on parched earth, washing away the dust of his trials. The years of hiding in shadows, of loneliness and physical pain, shrunk in significance under the weight of her forgiveness. He recalled the bitter words she had once spoken—words that had haunted his nightmares and nearly driven him to despair—but those were silenced now. What he heard instead was a gentle voice affirming his worth, speaking truths he had dared not hope for.

As for her proposal of separation, Feversham was not unprepared. Rumors had reached him of an engagement, and he accepted her wish without argument, sensing there was more to be said.

"I am going to be married," Ethne announced finally, breaking the long silence. "Immediately. I am to marry a friend of yours, Colonel Durrance."

Feversham replied instantly. "He has cared for you a long while. I was unaware of it until I left, but reflecting on everything, I always suspected it. And in very little time, I became certain."

"He is blind," Ethne said simply.

"Blind!" Feversham exclaimed, the word bursting from him. "He, of all men! Blind!"

"Exactly," she affirmed. "His blindness explains everything—the reason I marry him, the reason I send you away. It was after he lost his sight that our engagement began. It was before Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather. Between those two events, I realized that two men's lives should not be ruined because of me."

"My life was not ruined," Feversham interjected. "Please believe that."

"Partly it was," she returned softly. "I know you would never admit it for my sake, but it was. I was determined that a second man should not suffer as you did. When Colonel Durrance went blind—you know what that meant to him, the loss of everything he cherished—"

"Except you."

"Yes," Ethne whispered. "Except me. So I became engaged to him. But he has grown remarkably quick-witted. A hint reveals the whole hidden truth to him. Currently, he knows nothing of the four feathers."

"Are you sure?" Feversham demanded, his heart leaping.

"Yes. Why?" she asked, turning her face to him for the first time since sitting down. Her cheeks flushed with sudden color.

"Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin while I was at Omdurman," Feversham explained. "He knew I was a prisoner there. He sent messages, he tried to organize my escape."

Ethne started. "Oh," she said. "Colonel Durrance certainly knew you were in Omdurman. He saw you in Wadi Halfa, heard you had gone south into the desert. He was distressed, asked a friend for news, and learned you were in Omdurman. He told me so himself—and yes, he said he would try to arrange your escape. No doubt he acted through Lieutenant Sutch. He has been at Wiesbaden with an oculist and only returned a week ago. Otherwise, he would have told me. Very likely he was the reason Sutch was at Suakin, but he knows nothing of the four feathers. He only believes our engagement was broken off and that I have no thought for you at all. But if you return, if we meet—even calmly, indifferently—he would guess. He is so perceptive, he would be sure." She paused, her voice dropping to a whisper. "And he would guess right."

Feversham saw the blood rush to her forehead. He did not move closer, nor did he soften his voice, for he understood that any sign would make leaving impossible.

"Yes, I see," he said. "And he must not guess."

"No, he must not," Ethne agreed. "I am glad you understand that, Harry. The straight and simple path is the only one for us. He must never guess, for, as you said, he has nothing left but me."

"Is Durrance here?" Feversham asked.

"He is staying at the vicarage."

"Very well," Feversham said. "It is only fair that I tell you I never expected you to wait. I had no wish for you to wait, and I had no right to expect it. When you gave me the fourth feather in the little room at Ramelton, with the faint music drifting through the door, I understood your meaning. There was to be a complete, irrevocable end. We were not to remain mere acquaintances. So I said nothing to you of the plan that formed clearly in my mind at that exact moment. You see, I might never have succeeded. I might have died trying, or perhaps I might have shirked the attempt entirely. It would have been time enough to speak if I had returned. Therefore, I never formed any wish that you should wait."

"That was what Colonel Trench told me," Ethne noted.

"I told him that too?"

"On your first night in the House of Stone."

"Well, it is just the truth. All I ever hoped for—and I did hope for it every hour of every day—was that, if I came home, you would take back your feather, and that we might... not renew our friendship here, but see something of one another afterwards."

"Yes," Ethne said. "Then there will be no parting."

She spoke simply, without a sigh, but her gaze held a depth that told Feversham the immense cost of this separation. Understanding it now, he grasped with infinite clarity what it must have felt like six years ago when she sat alone with her pride shattered as severely as her heart.

"How much trouble you must have gone through!" he cried.

She turned to look at him gently. "Not I alone," she said. "I passed no nights in the House of Stone."

"But it was my fault. Do you remember what you said when the morning light came through the blinds? 'It is not right that one should suffer so much pain.' It was not right."

"I had forgotten those words," she admitted, a long time since, "until Colonel Trench reminded me. I should never have spoken them. When I did, I did not think they would live so vividly in your thoughts. I am sorry I spoke them."

"Oh, they were just enough," Feversham laughed, the tension easing slightly. "I used to think they would be the last words I heard when I turned my face to the wall. But you have given me others today wherewith to replace them."

"Thank you," she said quietly.

There was nothing more to say. Feversham wondered why Ethne did not rise from her seat. The occasion was too solemn, too vital for casual chatter. Yet, she kept him at her side. Though she sat calmly, her face grave, her heart ached with longing. Just a little longer, she pleaded to herself. The sunlight was retreating from the walls, shrinking the pool of gold where it glowed. When the stone turned cold and grey, she would send Harry Feversham away.

"I am glad you escaped Omdurman without the help of Lieutenant Sutch or Colonel Durrance," she said. "I wanted so much that everything be done by you alone, without anyone's help or interference."

Afterward, a silence stretched between them. Twice she glanced toward the wall, watching the golden light narrow, knowing her minutes were slipping away. "You suffered horribly at Dongola," she said in a low voice. "Colonel Trench told me."

"What does it matter now?" Feversham answered. "That time seems rather far away to me."
