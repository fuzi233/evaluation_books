The silence within the Hale household was not merely an absence of noise; it was a physical weight, a vacuum left behind by the terrifying commotion of recent days. It felt unnatural, a fragile shell surrounding a core of shattered reality. Mr. Hale sat in his accustomed chair, a figure carved from wood and despair, sinking deeper into one of his habitual waking dreams, unable to bridge the chasm between the living room and the death chamber upstairs. Dixon had delegated the management of the kitchen to Mary Higgins, whose scolding was rendered all the more potent by its delivery in a hushed, reverent whisper; to raise one's voice above a murmur in a house where a corpse lay uncoffined was deemed an act of irreverence.

Margaret held her tongue regarding the final, crowning terror of the ordeal. To speak of it would serve no purpose; the immediate danger had passed, yet a new shadow loomed: the fear that Mr. Leonard might secure funds to pursue Frederick to London, to hunt him down in the labyrinthine streets of the capital. Yet, Margaret reasoned with a desperate clarity, the odds were immense against such a scheme succeeding. She refused to torment herself with scenarios beyond her control. Frederick would be vigilant, a shield against her own fears, and within a day or two, he would be safely beyond the reach of English soil.

"I suppose we shall hear from Mr. Bell tomorrow," Margaret said, her voice steady despite the turmoil beneath.

"Yes," her father replied, his eyes unfocused. "I suppose so."

"If he can come, he will be here by tomorrow evening, I should think."

"If he cannot, I shall ask Mr. Thornton to accompany me to the funeral. I cannot go alone. I should break down utterly."

"Don't ask Mr. Thornton, papa. Let me go with you," Margaret interjected, her tone impetuous, cutting through his hesitation.

"You?" Mr. Hale looked up in surprise. "My dear, women do not generally go."

"No," Margaret corrected gently, her gaze intense. "Because they cannot control themselves. Women of our class do not go because they lack the power to govern their emotions, yet they are too proud to display them. Poor women go, regardless of the spectacle of their grief. But I promise you, papa, if you permit me, I shall be no burden. Do not have a stranger stand beside you while I am excluded. Dear papa, if Mr. Bell fails to come, I shall go. I will not press my wish against your will if he arrives, but if he does not..."

Mr. Bell, alas, could not come. The gout had seized him, rendering travel impossible. His letter was a masterpiece of affection, dripping with genuine regret, explaining that his Milton property demanded his immediate attention and that his agent insisted on his presence. Or, perhaps, he had simply fled the vicinity of Milton as long as possible, finding the only solace in the prospect of seeing his old friend once more.

Margaret fought a losing battle to dissuade her father from inviting Mr. Thornton. An indescribable repulsion rose within her at the suggestion. The night before the funeral, a stately note arrived from Mrs. Thornton to Miss Hale, stating that, at her son's request, their carriage would attend the funeral, provided it did not displease the family. Margaret tossed the paper onto the table.

"Oh, let us avoid these formalities," she said sharply. "Let us go alone—you and me, papa. They do not care for us, or else he would have offered to go himself rather than propose sending an empty carriage."

"I thought you were so extremely averse to his coming, Margaret," Mr. Hale remarked, surprised by her vehemence.

"And so I am," she admitted, her voice trembling slightly. "I do not want him to come at all; I should especially dislike the notion of asking him. But this... this seems such a mockery of mourning that I did not expect it from him."

Startled by her sudden shift in demeanor, Mr. Hale watched as she burst into tears. Throughout her grief, she had been subdued, thoughtful of others, gentle and patient in all things. Tonight, however, she was agitated, restless, her composure fraying at the edges. When her father lavished tender reassurances upon her, her crying only intensified. She endured a sleepless, agonizing night, leaving her ill-prepared for the fresh anxiety brought by a letter from Frederick.

Mr. Lennox was out of town, with his clerk predicting a return no earlier than the following Tuesday, perhaps Monday. Consequently, Frederick decided to remain in London for a day or two longer. He had considered descending to Milton again; the temptation had been overwhelming. Yet, the image of Mr. Bell comfortably settled in his father's house, combined with the alarm he had suffered at the railway station moments before, had hardened his resolve to stay in the city. Margaret could be assured he would take every conceivable precaution against being tracked by Leonard.

Margaret was profoundly grateful to receive this letter while her father was absent in her mother's room. Had he been present, he would have demanded she read it aloud, and the news would have triggered a state of nervous alarm in him that she would have found impossible to soothe. There was not merely the disturbing fact of Frederick's detention in London, but the allusions to the recognition at Milton and the very real possibility of pursuit that sent a chill through her veins. How would such news affect her father?

Many times, Margaret repented of having urged the consultation with Mr. Lennox. At the time, it had seemed a prudent measure, adding little delay to the already slim chances of detection. Yet, every subsequent event had proven it to be a catastrophic error. She battled hard against this regret, against the self-reproach for having spoken wisely at the time only to prove foolish in hindsight. But her father was too depressed physically and mentally to engage in healthy reasoning; he would succumb to the morbid fixation on what could not be changed.

Margaret summoned every ounce of her strength to support him. He seemed oblivious to the expectation of Frederick's letter, consumed by a single, consuming dread: that the last visible token of his wife's presence was to be removed from his sight forever. He trembled pitifully as the undertaker arranged the crape draperies around him. He looked wistfully at Margaret, and when finally released, he tottered toward her, his voice a broken whisper.

"Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left. I cannot pray. I give her up because I must. I try to bear it; indeed I do. I know it is God's will. But I cannot comprehend why she died. Pray for me, Margaret, that I may find the faith to pray. It is a great strait, my child."

In the carriage, Margaret sat beside him, almost supporting his failing frame in her arms. She repeated the noble verses of holy comfort, the texts of faithful resignation she could recall. Her voice never faltered, and strangely, she drew strength from the act of voicing them. Her father's lips moved in rhythm with hers, repeating the familiar phrases as her words suggested them. It was terrible to witness the patient, struggling effort to achieve a resignation he lacked the spiritual fortitude to internalize.

As they approached the churchyard, Dixon, with a subtle gesture of her hand, drew Margaret's attention to Nicholas Higgins and his daughter, standing apart yet deeply attentive to the ceremony. Nicholas wore his usual fustian, save for a strip of black cloth sewn around his hat—a mark of mourning he had never displayed for his daughter Bessy's sake. But Mr. Hale saw nothing. Absorbed in his own devastation, he mechanically recited the funeral service as the clergyman intoned it, sighing twice or thrice when the rites concluded. Then, placing his hand on Margaret's arm, he mutely begged to be led away, acting as if blind, requiring her as his faithful guide.

Dixon sobbed aloud, covering her face with a handkerchief, so consumed by her private grief that she failed to notice the crowd dispersing until someone spoke directly to her. It was Mr. Thornton. He had been present the entire time, standing with bowed head behind a cluster of mourners, unrecognized by anyone.

"I beg your pardon—but, can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale, too? I should like to know how they both are."

"Of course, sir," Dixon replied, her voice thick with emotion. "They are much as is to be expected. Master is terribly broke down. Miss Hale bears up better than likely."

Mr. Thornton preferred to believe she was suffering the natural sorrow. First, there was a selfishness within him sufficient to derive a twisted pleasure from the idea that his great love might come to comfort her—a passionate sting akin to a mother's joy when a drooping infant nestles close, dependent entirely upon her. This delicious vision of what might have been, in which he would have indulged only days prior, was miserably shattered by the memory of the handsome young man with whom Margaret had stood in an attitude of such familiar confidence near the station.

"Miserably disturbed!" was not strong enough to describe it. He was haunted by the image, a phantom that shot through him like an agony, forcing him to clench his hands tightly to subdue the pain. So far from home, at such a late hour! It required a monumental moral effort to rekindle his trust—once so absolute—in Margaret's purity and exquisite maidenliness. As soon as that effort waned, his trust collapsed, dead and powerless. Wild fantasies chased one another through his mind.
