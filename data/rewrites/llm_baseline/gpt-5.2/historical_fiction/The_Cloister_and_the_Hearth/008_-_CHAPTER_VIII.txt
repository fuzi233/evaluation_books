While the burgomaster in far Tergou was busy unmasking Gerard, another kind of calamity gathered in Sevenbergen, and it began, as so many storms in human houses do, with an empty shelf and a hungry heart.

Margaret came to old Martin Wittenhaagen with tears that seemed to have salt in them like the brine of a winter ham. “Martin,” she said, “there is nothing in the house. Nothing. And Gerard is coming; and he is so thoughtless—he forgets to sup at home. When he gives over work he runs to me straight, poor soul, and often he comes quite faint. And to think I have nothing to set before my servant that loves me so dear.”

Her distress was the housewife’s distress, yet deeper than a bare stomach: it was the dread of failing in that ancient covenant between hearth and wanderer—the one language that needs no grammar, only fire, knife, and bread.

Martin scratched his grey head, as if counsel might be found under the scalp. “What can I do?”

“It is Thursday,” said she, and wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, already smelling of soot and yesterday’s onions. “It is your day to shoot. Sooth to say, I counted on you to-day.”

“Nay,” said Martin, with the stubbornness of a man who has learned obedience by pain. “I may not shoot when the Duke or his friends are at the chase. Read else. I am no scholar.” And from his pouch he drew a parchment, stiff with age, fat with the Duke’s seal.

Margaret bent, and though she could not read the words, she read the power in the wax. Martin, with soldierly patience, spelled the sense: Philip of Burgundy granted him four merks yearly, and licence to shoot three arrows once a week—on Thursday, and on no other day—in any of the Duke’s forests in Holland, at any game save a buck of seven years or a doe with fawn; and even then not if the Duke hunted, nor any friend of his. If so, Martin must not disturb the woods, on peril of salary and head, and a fine of one penny—set down with a cruelty almost comic beside the loss of life.

Margaret sighed. The house was empty; the forest was full; and between them lay a strip of law as sharp as a carving-knife.

“Come, cheer up, mistress,” said Martin at last, his old scarred face softening for her. “For your sake I’ll peril my carcass; I have done that for many a one not worth your forefinger. It is no such mighty risk either. I’ll but step into the skirts of the forest here. It is odds but they drive a hare or a fawn within reach of my arrow.”

“If I let you go,” she said quickly, “you must promise me not to go far, and not to be seen. Far better Gerard went supperless than ill should come to you, faithful Martin.”

He promised; and she watched him take his bow and three arrows as a woman watches the last loaf go out of doors. He stole off, cautious as a thief, into the wood scarce a furlong away.

The forest already held the day’s music. Far off, horns sounded thin and ghostly, as if blown through old linen. There was the low stir of frightened life—the tick and hush of leaves, the sudden percussion of hooves, the quick snapping of twigs. All game was afoot. Even the air had that raw green smell that rises when something large has just passed: bruised fern, torn moss, sap bleeding in silence.

“Come,” thought Martin, “I shall soon fill the pot, and no one be the wiser.”

He took his stand behind a thick oak that commanded an open glade. He strung his bow—no toy, but a true engine of war: English yew, six feet and more, thick-limbed, with a string that, when it sang, sang clean as a harp. Martin’s chest was broad, his arms corded like ropes; he could draw a three-foot arrow to the head, and when it flew, the eye could scarce keep pace with it. This bow had once sent its message through mail and leather in the old quarrels of Hoeks and Kabeljauws, when men fought near enough to hear each other breathe, and death came with a visible flight, not hidden in smoke.

A hare came cantering into view, then sat sprightly, her ears making a neat V against the grass. Martin raised the great bow; he was thinking of the pot, of the comfort of broth, of the smell of fat hissing on coals, and he hurried. The string twanged; the arrow went like a needle of light—so near it seemed to strike her—yet it kissed the earth, flashed under her belly, hissed along the short grass, and was gone.

The hare sprang three feet straight up as if the ground had bit her, and then fled at full speed.

“Bungler!” said Martin, and the word was honest: he blamed himself, not the beast.

He had scarce fitted another arrow when a wood-pigeon settled on the very tree beneath which he stood, plump and grey, thinking itself safe in a soldier’s shadow.

“Aha,” thought he, “you are small, but dainty.”

This time he took pains. He drew smoothly, loosed without snatch. The shaft struck; feathers flew up like dust in sun. Yet it had torn, not fairly pierced. The bird fluttered away, breast ragged, and by a great effort rose above the branches, flew fifty yards, and died somewhere in the thick green where a man could not follow without betraying himself.

“Luck is against me,” he muttered, and his mouth tasted bitter as burnt crust.

He nocked the third arrow and watched the glade with a hunger that was half his own, half Margaret’s. Then came a bustle behind him—leaves torn in haste, the ground drummed—he turned just in time to see a noble buck cross the open, too late for a shot. Rage seized him; he dashed his bow down with an imprecation.

At that very moment a long spotted thing slid swift as poured oil across the grass after the deer, belly near the earth, shoulders rolling. Martin knew her: the Duke’s leopard.

“The hunters will not be far,” he thought, cold sweat pricking him. “And I must not be seen. Gerard must go supperless this night.”

He plunged into the wood, for that was his way home—following the buck and the leopard by instinct more than choice. He had not gone far when an unusual sound met him: leaves rustling violently, the harsh trampling of a great struggle.

He hurried, and came upon a sight to turn a man’s stomach and sharpen it too. The leopard was on the buck’s back, tearing with tooth and claw, and the buck ran in a broken circle, bounding as if the earth itself were flinging him. Blood poured down his hide in dark ropes, and the air stank of iron and warm life.

Then in Martin rose a desperate resolve—not the thought of profit, but of Margaret’s bare table, the empty pot, and Gerard coming in with that faint look. Venison was not merely meat; it was a feast, a kind of mercy. He drew his arrow to the head and drove it home into the deer. Even with the brute on his back the buck bounded high, as though trying once more to escape the world, and fell dead.

The leopard went on tearing as if nothing had happened.

Martin waited, hoping she would gorge and leave him the carcass. The minutes crawled; the forest’s sounds seemed suddenly too loud: far horns, near crows, the constant whisper of leaves like talk in a tongue older than men. At last he walked up and laid his hand on the buck’s leg.

The leopard gave a growl that shook the air. She ceased sucking, lifted her head, and fixed on Martin a look like green fire under water—sulky, suspicious, and wholly without law.

What was to be done? Martin had heard it said that wild creatures cannot stand the human eye. He stood erect and stared her down.

She stared back—and then, brutally ignorant of all that men tell each other in taverns, she sprang.

There was barely time. Martin caught her by the throat before her teeth could crush his face. One claw seized his shoulder and rent it; the other, aimed at his cheek, would have ended him, but Martin wore no hat—only a scapulary of tough leather like his jerkin, and he had drawn it over his head like a hood; the claw caught in that loose leather and tore at it, snarled, and slipped.

He kept her jaws from him with great difficulty, his fingers sunk into her throat as if he would strangle the life itself. She kept rending his shoulder. It was like blunt reaping-hooks grinding and tearing through flesh. Pain like hot iron ran along his arm and down his side; yet instead of cowing him it raised his blood. He gnashed his teeth, rage answering rage, and squeezed her neck with a force that had once drawn bows and hauled men from ditches.

Their eyes flared at one another. Now the man’s were near as furious as the brute’s.

She felt the throttling and made a wild effort to free herself. In the struggle she dragged his leather hood across his face and blinded him, and tore her claw out of his shoulder—flesh and all. But still he throttled, hand and arm like iron.

Presently her long tail, that had lashed the air high and proud, sank.

“Aha!” cried Martin, joyfully, and gripped her like death. Her body lost its spring; in his hands she became a choked, powerless thing. He held on till all motion ceased, then dashed her to the earth. Panting, he threw back his hood.

The leopard lay mute at his feet, tongue out, paw bloody.

And then, for the first time, terror fell on Martin—not the fear of teeth, but of men. “I am a dead man,” he whispered. “I have slain the Duke’s leopard.”

He snatched handfuls of leaves and flung them over her in a foolish, desperate attempt to hide what could not be hidden. Then he shouldered the buck—warm, heavy, a whole feast across his back—and staggered away, leaving a trail of blood all the way: his own mixed with the deer’s, both darkening the moss.

He burst into Peter’s house a horrible figure, bleeding and bloodstained, and flung the carcass down so that it thudded on the floor like a delivered sentence.

“There—no questions,” he gasped. “But broil me a steak on’t, for I am faint.”

Margaret did not see he was wounded; she thought the blood was all from the deer. Her hands flew at once to the work as if work were prayer. She set the fire to a fiercer heat, raked embers, laid iron to glow. The smell of fresh venison rose—sweet and wild, with that clean woodland tang. She cut thick steaks across the grain, seasoned simply as poor folk do when the meat itself is rare: a little salt, perhaps a pinch of dried herb saved from summer. Fat hissed as it touched the hot iron; smoke curled, smelling of pine and flesh; the sound of sizzling was like comfort spoken aloud.

Apart, the stout soldier stanching and binding his own wound, bit on a strip of leather, tied tight with practised fingers, and said nothing. Soon Gerard came, and the three of them supped royally—broiled venison hot from the fire, juices running, crust browned, the hearth giving its red light to their faces. Gerard, with a wonderful thoughtfulness, had brought a flask of Schiedam; the sharp spirit cut the richness, warmed Martin’s blood, and made the room for a moment seem like the safest place on earth.

Under that warmth Martin revived enough to tell them how the venison was got; and they made merry over it, laughing as if laughter could salt the meat against tomorrow. Their voices mingled with the household sounds—the crack of firewood, the scrape of knife on trencher, the swallow of drink. Outside, the wind moved in the trees like distant surf.

Their mirth was broken as suddenly as a bone snaps.

Margaret’s eye fixed and would not move. The color left her cheek. She tried to speak and could not; only lifted a trembling finger toward the window.

They followed her pointing, and there, in the twilight, crouched a dark form, low and patient, with eyes like glowworms—green fire in a living face.

It was the leopard.

They stood petrified, held by those eyes as by a spell. Then from the wood came a single deep bay—one note, heavy as doom.

Martin trembled at it. “They have lost her,” he whispered, “and laid muzzled bloodhounds on her scent. They will find her here—and the venison. Good-bye, friends. Martin Wittenhaagen ends here.”

Gerard snatched up the bow and thrust it into the soldier’s hands. “Be a man,” he cried. “Shoot her, and fling her into the wood ere they come up. Who will know?”

More voices of hounds broke out—nearer, many-throated. The air seemed to fill with sound, a hunting music that was not music to the hunted: baying, yelping, men shouting, feet crashing through underbrush. It pressed on the little house like water rising.

“Curse her!” cried Martin. “I spared her once; now she must die, or I—or both more likely.” He reared the bow and drew an arrow to the head.

“Nay! nay!” cried Margaret, and seized the arrow with both hands.

It broke in half. The pieces fell on either side of the bow.

At that same instant the hounds’ tongues burst all about the house: they were hot upon the scent. Men’s voices rang close; the yard seemed suddenly full.

“What have you done, wench?” Martin cried, hoarse with panic and pain. “You have put the halter round my throat.”

“No!” Margaret flung back. “I have saved you. Stand back from the window, both! Your knife—quick!”

And before either could hinder her, she seized Martin’s long-pointed knife—almost tore it from his girdle—and darted out of the room.

Outside, the house was now ringed with baying dogs and shouting men. And still the glowworm eyes at the window did not move.
