The cycles of sustenance in the Dunwich valley had always been dictated by the turning of the seasons, a slow, generational rhythm of planting, rearing, and butchering. Yet Old Whateley’s agrarian routines had begun to twist into a strange, circular pacing. He continued to purchase cattle, yet the heavy scent of curing meats, rendered tallow, and woodsmoke never thickened the air of his cellar, nor did his herd measurably increase in the stony pastures. The livestock were being consumed, swallowed by a domestic routine that defied all natural laws of nourishment. 

Simultaneously, the traditional elder began to harvest timber, repairing the spacious, peaked-roofed farmhouse whose rear foundational stones remained buried in the hillside. He possessed prodigious reserves of strength for a man of his weathering, driven by the need to fortify the abandoned upper story. Though he muttered in fragmented, dying dialects as he worked, his carpentry was the meticulous craft of a man who understood the heavy, physical toll of true provisions. The upper windows were boarded tight, starving the reclaimed section of light and air. 

Downstairs, he fitted a room for his new grandson with tall, sturdy shelving made of fresh-sawn pine. Here, Old Whateley assumed the role of a meticulous archivist of forgotten hungers. He gathered the rotting, ancient books that had been heaped promiscuously in damp corners, treating them as vital culinary and cultural histories of a lineage on the brink of erasure. Standing over the radiant heat of the rusty kitchen stove, the air thick with the domestic aromas of Lavinia’s simmering root vegetables and wild thyme, the old man boiled a binding paste of rye flour, spring water, and rendered bone-glue. 

"I made some use of 'em," he would say, carefully mending a brittle, black-letter page stained with the oils of centuries of handling. "But the boy's fitten to make better use of 'em. He'd orter hev 'em as well sot as he kin, for they're goin' to be all of his larnin'." He understood the profound power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons; the modern English of the valley could never translate the subjective, mythic experience of his bloodline. Wilbur’s identity would be rooted in this dead language.

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was a year and seven months old, his physical consumption and growth were alarming. He possessed the dense, heavy frame of a four-year-old, fueled by an insatiable appetite, and spoke with a terrifying fluency. He roamed the wild hills with his mother, but at home, he sat steeped in the olfactory landscape of old paper and dried ink, poring over the queer charts in his grandfather’s texts. Through long, acoustically dampened afternoons—where the only sounds were the crackle of the hearth and the hushed, guttural phonemes of a native tongue resisting extinction—Old Whateley catechized the boy.

When the house restoration was complete, the valley folk wondered why an upper window in the rear east gable had been replaced with a solid plank door, and why a heavy, cleated wooden runway had been built up to it from the earth. The tool-shed, locked tight since Wilbur’s birth, was suddenly abandoned, its door swinging listlessly on rusted hinges. When Earl Sawyer stepped inside after negotiating a cattle sale, the olfactory shock nearly drove him to his knees. It was not the earthy, utilitarian smell of manure or aging hay. It was a dense, suffocating stench of spoiled blood, oxidized copper, and fermented marrow—an odor he had only ever caught in faint, diluted wafts near the ancient Indian circles on the hills. It was a smell that mocked the wholesome, life-sustaining fragrance of agrarian life.

The acoustic ecology of the farm shifted in the following months, reflecting the untranslatable horrors gestating within. The natural soundscape of wind and wildlife was slowly drowned out by mysterious, deep-earth rumblings. On May Eve of 1915, tremors vibrated through the floorboards of Aylesbury homes, and by Hallowe'en, an underground resonance synchronized with bursts of mythic flame atop Sentinel Hill. 

Wilbur was growing with uncanny ferocity. Entering his fourth year, he looked ten. He read avidly, but his use of human language was dying, replaced by a settled taciturnity. When he did speak, it was in unfamiliar, rhythmic chants—a bizarre, ancient jargon that carried the psychological weight of an unexplainable terror. The local dogs, highly sensitive to disruptions in both their sonic environment and olfactory territory, despised him. Their frantic barking shattered the quiet of the countryside, forcing Wilbur to carry a pistol. The sharp, industrial crack of the weapon whenever he was forced to fire upon a dog was a violent, polluting noise that only deepened the valley's animosity.

The few callers who visited found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, preparing simple meals, while the domestic quiet was ruined by odd cries and heavy footsteps resonating from the boarded-up second story. When a jocose fish-peddler—bringing the sharp, briny scents of salted cod and smoked herring—tried the locked stairway door, Lavinia turned pale with abnormal dread. The peddler later told the store loungers at Dunwich Village that the heavy, rhythmic thudding from the floorboards above sounded like a massive draft horse stamping in its stall. The loungers shuddered, remembering old tales of Whateley's youth, of livestock sacrificed in the dark soil to feed heathen gods, merging mythic horrors with the everyday butchery of farm life.

By 1917, the war draft exposed the deep, regional decadence of Dunwich. The government’s medical experts descended upon the valley, their arrival bringing a cacophony of modern industrial noise that shattered the rural acoustic peace. Reporters from the *Boston Globe* and *Arkham Advertiser* followed, eager to commodify the Whateleys' lives. They turned the family's deep, ancestral nightmare into superficial commercial entertainment, printing flamboyant Sunday features on Wilbur’s precociousness, the sealed farmhouse, and the archaic books. 

Wilbur was four and a half, yet he possessed the stature of a fifteen-year-old, his face dusted with coarse dark down, his voice cracking into deeper, dissonant registers. Earl Sawyer led the noisy, camera-wielding reporters to the house, pointing out the rancid stench of spoiled meat and ancient earth that now constantly trickled down from the sealed upper spaces. The reporters breathed it in, yet entirely lacked the sensory appreciation or cultural context to understand it. They grinned over the obvious mistakes in their own printed stories, willfully blind to the truth. They even marveled at Old Whateley paying for his cattle with extremely ancient gold pieces, seeing only the monetary value of the coins rather than recognizing them as artifacts of a forgotten, untranslatable past—a final, stubborn resistance against the erosion of their terrifying cultural memory.
