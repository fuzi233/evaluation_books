"Eleven days," Messer Naldo confirmed, his voice cutting through the twilight of the twenty-eighth of November. "Not a month's march. Just a short detour past the mountains."

The scene unfolding in the Via de' Bardi served as a grim epilogue to the morning's confrontation, illuminating the desperate calculus that drove Naldo back toward the hill of San Giorgio. It was a man cornered by his own momentum, returning to the source of his obsession before the dust of his departure had settled.

Inside the cottage, the atmosphere was thick with the scent of woodsmoke and unwashed linen, a stark contrast to the sterile grandeur of the city. Tito stood in the doorway, his silhouette framed by the gloom of the street. When he had first secured this haven for Tessa over a year prior, he had convinced himself it was an act of pure charity—a reflexive kindness toward a fragile, trusting creature who mistook him for a husband. Yet, the truth was more tangled. While he possessed a natural inclination to coddle the pretty, prattling girl, his heart belonged elsewhere. For the first time in his life, Tito loved Romola with a ferocity that rendered Tessa's charms irrelevant; her presence was merely a soothing balm, a vibration against his cheek, whereas Romola possessed him entirely.

His intellect was too sharp, his fiber too refined, to indulge in the gross pleasures of a hedonist. Instead, he had woven a gossamer web around himself and Tessa, a trap he felt powerless to dismantle. In the initial moments following their sham marriage, he had calculated the utility of keeping her under an illusion, banking on his future needs. But since that pivotal decision, the web had grown autonomously, spiraling outward regardless of his intent. The distinction between kindness and self-deception blurred in a soft nature like his. The irritation he felt during her relentless pursuit on the day of his betrothal, coupled with his solemn vow to reveal the truth, had paradoxically strengthened his resolve to maintain the deception. He had chosen to provide a home, to let the lie stand, driven by a twisted form of benevolence.

Now, in the throes of his first true devotion to Romola, Tito required a shield. He needed a narrative to justify his secret infidelity, a way to reconcile the guilt of betraying his wedded love with the overwhelming pull of his new passion. Every intense emotion constructs its own private morality, its own sacred piety, just as a son's love for a mother can endure amidst the foulest corruption. Tito could not yet bear the weight of committing a silent crime against his vows.

Consequently, he redoubled his efforts to preserve the secrecy of this offense. Monna Lisa, a woman of limited horizons who rarely ventured beyond specific shops or the confessional, knew nothing of his true identity or whereabouts. She believed only that he paid her handsomely to ensure her comfort, caring little else except for her growing affection for Tessa and the domestic duties she performed. A veil of mystery hung over the arrangement; Tessa was a peasant girl (*contadina*), while Messer Naldo was a gentleman. Yet, thanks to Tito's terrorizing of Tessa—who had been warned that silence was the price of his continued presence—and Monna Lisa's profound deafness, the truth remained buried. Tessa, unable to articulate her suspicions to the mute landlady, kept her mouth shut, unlike her earlier indiscretion with Baldassarre.

For months, Tito's visits had been sporadic, seemingly necessitating long journeys. These trips were motivated primarily by a paternalistic desire to verify that Tessa's world remained intact. Though he found the prospect of visiting less pleasant than the reality, he cherished the naive, ignorant trust she placed in him. He was determined to preserve this simplicity, to keep Tessa a genuine country flower, untainted by the complexities of higher society. He would be horrified to see her dressed in finery inappropriate to her station; the zest of her conversation would evaporate if her world expanded, if her pleasures matured beyond the squirrel-like hoarding of nuts. This strategy preserved both Tessa's innocence and Tito's finances, sparing him the escalating costs of maintaining a household that demanded resources he reserved for his own lavish habits.

Previously, the mere knowledge of Tessa's existence up the hill had occasionally lured Tito away from the weariness of caring for the aging Bardo, offering a respite from sharing Romola's burdens. However, the current urgency was different. It stemmed from a visceral hunger for Tessa's unconditional faith in *him*, a craving ignited only recently. This shift was marked as clearly as a sudden blindness or deafness. It occurred the night he returned from buying the armor, crossing the bridge with the steel clinking in his hands. Facing Romola, who waited with innocent eyes, he felt an insurmountable dread. She trusted him blindly, yet behind that frank gaze lay a judgmental intelligence capable of dissecting his motives. Any misplaced trust she held was born not of ignorance, but of a nobility that threatened to expose him. Exhausted by the day's exertions and the tension of self-control, he sought a sanctuary where he could relax his guard, unburdened by public scrutiny. Within reach lay a creature of sweet adoration, safe and unconstraining, who would accept any fabrication without flinching. Thus, that evening, despite Romola's waiting presence, he turned his steps upward toward the hill.

It was inevitable, therefore, that he retraced these exact steps eleven days later, recoiling from Romola's first explosive rebuke. He could not replace Tessa in his wife's position, nor could he wish for reconciliation with Romola, for she belonged to the realm of ambition and high purpose. What he craved was a refuge from a moral rigor he could not dismiss as mere folly; Tessa's small, pliable soul offered exactly that escape hatch.

It was barely past eight o'clock when Tito ascended the stone steps to Tessa's chamber. Usually, she greeted him eagerly upon his entry, but tonight the house was silent. Pushing open the door, he found the cause. A solitary, dim lamp flickered above the dying embers of the hearth, casting long shadows. Tessa knelt beside the bedside where the infant lay. Her head had slumped onto the pillow, her brown rosary slipping from her fingers to rest loosely in her right hand. She had drifted into sleep mid-prayer.

Tito moved quietly across the small room, settling close beside her. She must have sensed his approach in her dreams, for she opened her eyes without a start, remaining perfectly still as if his smiling presence had sealed off any disturbing impulses. When he reached up to stroke her chin and lean in for a kiss, she whispered, "I dreamed it, and then I said it was dreaming—and then I awoke, and it was true."

"Little sinner!" Tito teased, pinching her chin playfully. "You haven't finished your prayers. I'll punish you by refusing to look at your baby; he's ugly."

Tessa's face tightened with pouting distress, though she tried to hide it as she bent anxiously over the sleeping child. "Ah, that's not true! He's prettier than anything. You don't think he's ugly. You'll look at him. He's even prettier than when you saw him before—only he's asleep, so you can't see his eyes or his tongue, and I can't show you his hair. But it grows! Isn't that wonderful? Look at him! His face stays the same when he sleeps; there's less to see than when he's awake. If you kiss him very gently, he won't wake. Do you want to kiss him?"

Satisfied, Tito bestowed a butterfly kiss upon the small bundle, then turned Tessa's face toward him, placing a hand on her shoulder. "You prefer looking at the baby to looking at your husband, you false one!"

Still kneeling, Tessa rested her hands on his knee, gazing up at him with the round-cheeked, adoring expression of an angel from Fra Lippo Lippi's frescoes. "No," she shook her head firmly. "I love you best always. Only I want you to look at the bambino and love him. Before, I only wanted you to love me."

"And did you expect me to return so soon?" Tito asked, indulging her chatter. He still felt the aftershocks of the violent agitation he had endured, shaken like a vessel tossed in a storm. This quiet intimacy was the only relief available to him.

"No," Tessa replied, counting on her fingers. "I've counted the days—I started at my right thumb today—since you wore the beautiful chain-coat that Messer San Michele gave you for your journey. And you're wearing it now," she added, peering through the slit in his tunic. "Perhaps that's why you came back sooner."

"Perhaps it did, Tessa," he conceded. "Forget the coat for now. Tell me what has changed since I was last here. Did you see the tents in the Prato? The soldiers and horsemen crossing the bridges? Did you hear the drums and trumpets?"

"Yes," she admitted, her voice dropping. "I was frightened, thinking the soldiers might come up here. Even Monna Lisa was scared; she said they might take our children away, that it was their business to cause mischief. But the Holy Madonna protected us—we never saw one up here. Something has happened, though. I hardly dare tell you, which is why I've been saying extra *Aves*."
