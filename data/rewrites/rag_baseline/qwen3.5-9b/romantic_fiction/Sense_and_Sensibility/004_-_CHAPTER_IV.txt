"What a pity it is, Elinor," Marianne observed, her tone laced with the sharp edge of disappointment. "That Edward should possess no taste for drawing."

"No taste for drawing!" Elinor corrected, her composure holding firm against the tide of her sister's enthusiasm. "Why should you suppose that? He does not draw himself, it is true, but he derives genuine satisfaction from observing the works of others. I assure you, he is by no means deficient in natural taste; he simply lacks the opportunity to refine it. Had he been afforded the chance to learn, I am convinced he would have excelled. His hesitation stems from a profound distrust of his own judgment in artistic matters, rendering him reluctant to offer an opinion on any canvas. Yet, he possesses an innate propriety and a simplicity of discernment that invariably guide him correctly."

Marianne, fearing to offend, fell silent on the subject. However, the specific approval Elinor attributed to Edward regarding the sketches of others was worlds away from the rapturous ecstasy Marianne believed constituted true taste. Still, suppressing a private smile at her sister's error, she honored Marianne's blind devotion to Edward.

"I trust, Marianne," Elinor continued, "that you do not view him as lacking in general taste. Indeed, I daresay you cannot, for your demeanor toward him remains perfectly cordial. Were that your assessment, I am certain you could never treat him with civility."

Marianne hesitated, torn between wounding her sister's feelings and voicing a truth she did not hold. Finally, she replied, "Do not be offended, Elinor, if my praise falls short of your estimation of his merits. I have lacked the numerous occasions you have enjoyed to gauge the minutiae of his mind, his inclinations, and his tastes. Nevertheless, I hold the highest opinion in the world of his goodness and sense. To me, he embodies everything worthy and amiable."

"Indeed," Elinor smiled, "his dearest friends could not be dissatisfied with such commendation. I struggle to conceive how you could express yourself more warmly."

Relieved to find her sister so easily contented, Marianne pressed on. "Regarding his sense and goodness," Elinor elaborated, "there can be no doubt among those who have engaged him in unreserved discourse. The brilliance of his understanding and the solidity of his principles remain obscured only by that shyness which too often silences him. You know him sufficiently to render justice to his substantial worth. As for these 'minuter propensities,' you have been kept more ignorant than I due to peculiar circumstances. While you were wholly engrossed by maternal affection, I and Edward have spent considerable time together. I have scrutinized his sentiments and listened to his opinions on literature and art. Upon reflection, I venture to declare his mind well-informed, his appreciation for books profound, his imagination vivid, his observations just and correct, and his taste delicate and pure. His abilities improve with familiarity as surely as his manners and appearance. Initially, his address lacks striking impact, and his features are hardly handsome until one perceives the exceptional quality of his eyes and the inherent sweetness of his countenance. Knowing him as I do now, I consider him truly handsome—or nearly so. What say you, Marianne?"

"I shall very soon deem him handsome, Elinor, if I do not already," Marianne declared. "When you instruct me to love him as a brother, I shall cease to perceive imperfection in his face as readily as I now ignore flaws in his heart."

Elinor recoiled internally at the declaration, regretting the warmth she had inadvertently displayed. She acknowledged that Edward held a lofty position in her own regard. She suspected the sentiment was mutual, yet she demanded greater certainty before accepting Marianne's conviction of their attachment as fact. She knew that for Marianne and her mother, wish equaled hope, and hope equaled expectation.

She endeavored to clarify the reality of their situation. "I do not deny," Elinor stated calmly, "that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem, and indeed, like him."

At this, Marianne erupted in indignation. "Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh, worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed of feeling otherwise! Repeat those words, and I shall vacate this room immediately."

Elinor could not suppress a laugh. "Forgive me," she said gently. "Assure yourself that I intended no offense by stating my feelings so quietly. Believe them to be stronger than I have articulated; believe them, in short, to match his merit and the hope of his affection for me, provided such hope is not imprudent. But go no further. I am by no means assured of his regard. There are moments when the extent of it appears doubtful. Until his sentiments are fully revealed, you cannot blame me for avoiding any encouragement of my own partiality by assuming more than exists. In my heart, I feel little—scarcely any doubt of his preference. Yet, other factors must be weighed. He is far from independent. We cannot know the full extent of his mother's character, but based on Fanny's occasional remarks, we have never deemed her amiable. I am mistaken if Edward is unaware that marrying a woman without significant fortune or high rank presents formidable obstacles."

Marianne was astonished to discover how far their imaginations had outrun the truth. "And you are not engaged to him!" she exclaimed. "Yet, it will undoubtedly happen soon. This delay offers two distinct advantages: I shall not lose you so quickly, and Edward will gain greater opportunity to cultivate that natural taste for your favored pursuit, which is indispensable to your future happiness. Oh, if he were stimulated by your genius to learn to draw himself, how delightful it would be!"

Elinor had voiced her true opinion. She could not view Marianne's infatuation with Edward as secure as her sister believed. At times, a lack of spirit clouded his demeanor; if this did not signal indifference, it suggested something far more discouraging. Doubt regarding her regard, should he feel it, would cause mere unease, not the profound dejection that frequently plagued him. A more logical explanation lay in his dependent status, which forbade the free expression of his affection. He knew his mother neither maintained a comfortable household for him nor offered assurances that he could establish one without adhering strictly to her ambitions for his elevation. Armed with this knowledge, Elinor could not feel at ease. She relied heavily on the outcome of his preference, a certainty her mother and sister still clung to. Conversely, the longer they remained together, the more ambiguous his regard appeared; occasionally, for agonizing intervals, she feared it was nothing more than friendship.

Regardless of its true boundaries, the fact that Marianne perceived it was enough to unsettle her, and simultaneously, more commonly, to render her uncivil. Seizing the first opportunity, she confronted her mother-in-law, speaking expressively of her son's grand expectations, Mrs. Ferrars' resolve that both sons marry well, and the peril facing any young woman who dared to "draw him in." Mrs. Dashwood could neither feign ignorance nor maintain composure. She received an answer dripping with contempt and departed instantly, vowing that regardless of the inconvenience or cost of such a sudden departure, her beloved Elinor should not endure another week of such insinuations.

In this state of agitation, a letter arrived from the post, offering a solution perfectly timed. It proposed a small house on exceptionally favorable terms, owned by a relative—a gentleman of substance and property in Devonshire. Written in the true spirit of friendly accommodation, the correspondent understood her need for housing. Though the property was merely a cottage, he promised renovations to suit her needs if the location appealed. He earnestly invited her and her daughters to Barton Park, his residence, where she could personally assess whether Barton Cottage, situated in the same parish, could be rendered comfortable. His anxiety to accommodate them was palpable, and the entire letter exuded a friendliness that would surely please his cousin, especially amidst her recent suffering at the hands of her closer kin.

She required no time for deliberation. Her resolution crystallized as she read. The remoteness of Barton, once a sufficient objection to outweigh all advantages, became its primary asset. Abandoning the neighborhood of Norland ceased to be an evil; it became a desired objective, a blessing compared to the misery of remaining a guest in her daughter-in-law's home. To depart forever from that beloved place would prove less painful than inhabiting or visiting it while such a woman ruled it.

Instantly, she penned her acknowledgment of Sir John Middleton's kindness and accepted his proposal. Hastening to show both letters to her daughters, she sought their approval before dispatching her reply. Elinor had long considered it prudent to settle at a distance from Norland rather than remain immediately among their current acquaintances. Therefore, she opposed her mother's intention to move to Devonshire on no grounds. Furthermore, the house, as described by Sir John, was modest in scale and rented at an unusually moderate price, leaving her no valid objection. Although the plan lacked romantic charm and represented a removal beyond her initial wishes, she made no attempt to dissuade her mother from sending a letter of acquiescence.
