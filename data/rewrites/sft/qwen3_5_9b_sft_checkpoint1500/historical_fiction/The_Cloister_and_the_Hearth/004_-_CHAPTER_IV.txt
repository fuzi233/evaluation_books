It was nearly four in the afternoon when Elias returned to his shop, finding both his eldest and youngest sons away on errands. Catherine, burdened by worry for Gerard, had ventured down the street with her small, crippled daughter, hoping to catch a glimpse of the boy returning. Leaving the children to search, Elias remained alone in the sitting room—a space defined as much by its peculiar inhabitants as by its furnishings.

The citizens of Tergou, descendants of the Dutch, prided themselves on inventing everything from printing and banking to cleanliness itself. While the local English gentry still trod upon floors matted with stale rushes and scattered with bones and debris, Elias’s parlor boasted tiles so brilliantly glazed and scrubbed that they might have served as a plate. Dominating the wall was a single large window, its central stone tracery forming a massive cross that seemed to stand in solemn relief, watched over with devotion by the family. The glass panes were tiny, diamond-shaped lozenges held together by lead strips, a rustic charm reminiscent of country cottages. Within the room stood rough, primitive chairs, save for one towering oak armchair whose back rose at right angles to the seat, forcing any sitter to stop two feet short of the top. Beside it lay a copper basin filled with holy water, attended by a besom brush, and a heavy oak table. Clinging to its edge was Giles, a dwarf whose proportions defied nature's usual balance. Where a normal child might expect symmetry, Giles was an inverted cone, his upper body disproportionately heavy compared to his legs. Yet, this very imbalance granted him supernatural agility; his light limbs allowed him to scale walls like squirrels and hang suspended by a single hand. His favorite pastime involved seizing the table with both hands, swinging wildly, and finally locking onto it with his teeth, freeing his hands to grip the wood with his jaws like a vice.

Peace was shattered when little Kate interrupted this display. Seeing her brother Sampsonet clinging to the furniture, she stood frozen, horrified not by the gymnastics, but by the damage to the wood. "Oh, Giles!" she cried, "Mother is coming! You are denting the table!"

Giles snarled, snapping his teeth shut on the oak. "Go tell your mother, little gossip. You are born to make trouble."

"Am I?" Kate asked, her voice calm and dry. "That is news to me."

"The biggest mischief-maker in all of Tergou," Giles grunted, tightening his grip.

"Oh, truly," Kate replied with a hint of sarcasm.

Having unleashed this unexpected wit without consequence, Giles settled back into his monstrous hug of the table. Kate, undeterred, sat down quietly and began to weep. Just then, Catherine entered, and Giles, startled, dove beneath the table, peering out through a gap with wide, glowing eyes.

"What is happening now?" Catherine demanded sharply. Her gaze shifted between Kate and the huddled figure below, noting the boy's sheepish expression. She raised a finger, threatening a scolding, but softened immediately.

"Nay, Mother," Kate said gently, wiping her tears. "It was merely a foolish word Giles spoke. I was tired and worried sick about Gerard, you know."

Before the mother could respond, a faint, tottering voice echoed from the doorway. "Let no one bear care for me." Gerard stepped into the room, pale, dusty, and utterly spent. Amidst a chorus of delighted cries and anxious embraces, he collapsed into the nearest chair, completely drained by his journey.

Their travels from Rotterdam, searching for Margaret, had broken him down, yet youth proved resilient. Instantly, Gerard became the center of an eager circle. Before all else, they clamored to hear about the prizes. Gerard recounted being admitted to view the competitors' works, laid out in a vast hall awaiting judgment. "Oh, Mother! Oh, Kate!" he exclaimed, his voice trembling with awe. "When I saw the goldsmiths' creations, I felt as though I should fall to the floor. I thought no smith alive possessed so much gold, silver, and jewel, nor crafted designs of such divine beauty. Truly, all art approaches the divine."

To satisfy the women, he detailed the religious treasures—reliquaries, chalices, crosses, and monstrances—and the secular wonders like goblets, clocks, chains, and brooches, causing their hearts to swell with longing. Then, his tone grew somber. "But, Kate, when I reached the illuminated manuscripts from Ghent and Bruges, my soul sank within me. Compared to them, mine was mere dust. For the first minute, I could hardly speak, but I prayed for a purer spirit. Soon, I was able to admire them, thanking God for these beautiful works and the skilled, patient artisans who created them. I realized how much I owed to them. The colored pages were so stunning I forgot the stark black-and-white sketches entirely."

"But what of the writing contest?" Elias pressed eagerly. "Whose name did the herald shout first?"

"Yours," Kate answered quickly.

The brothers scoffed, laughing her down.

"You may laugh," Gerard said firmly, "but the truth remains. Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou was the first name proclaimed. I stood stunned, dazed, until they pushed me forward. Everything swam before my eyes. I found myself kneeling on a cushion at the feet of the Duke. He spoke to me, but I was so fluttered I could not reply. Then, instead of drawing his sword to sever my dull head, he placed his hand to his side and bestowed upon me a gold medal. And here it is."

A wild cheer erupted, followed by a scramble for the prize. Gerard produced the medal and, to further delight, revealed fifteen golden angels. "I had seen one before, but I never held one until now. Here they are."

"Oh, Gerard! Oh, Gerard!" the family gasped.

He distributed the wealth among them: coins for his brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt, and for Kate and his sister Lily—"because God hath afflicted thee," he noted tenderly regarding Lily. He kept one for himself to buy colors and vellum, and set aside nine for the nurse who had risked two crowns for Gerard's sake.

The gold instantly revealed the true natures of the siblings. Cornelis and Sybrandt clutched their coins with greedy glares, their eyes darting enviously toward Kate, who held two pieces. Elias, meanwhile, seized his coin and rolled it across the floor, chasing it with gamboiling steps. Kate lowered her crutches, sat, and extended her small arms to Gerard in a gesture of pure, heavenly love. Catherine, initially stunned by the sudden shower of riches falling on her lap, eventually broke into tears. "Stop kissing him, Kate!" she cried. "He is my son, not yours. Ah, Gerard, my boy! I have not loved you as you deserved."

Gerard threw himself on his knees beside her, and she embraced him, weeping with overwhelming joy and pride. "Good lad! Good lad!" Elias cried, overcome with emotion. "I must go tell the neighbors! Lend me the medal, Gerard; I shall show it to my good friend Peter Buyskens. He is always boasting that his son Jorian won a tin mug at the shooting butts."

"Ay, do, Father," Gerard agreed, handing over the angel. "Show Peter Buyskens one of these angels. Tell him there are fourteen more where that came from. But mind you bring it back!"

"Eli," Catherine interjected softly, "stay a moment. There is better news behind."

Gerard flushed with pleasure at the anticipation. "Better? Better than this?"

Catherine nodded. Gerard then recounted his audience with the Countess, and the house rang with jubilation. "Now, God bless the kind lady, and bless Dame Van Eyck! We have secured a benefice. Our son! My cares are finally at an end. Elias, my friend and master, we two may now die happy whenever our time comes. This dear boy will take our place, and none of our loved ones will lack a home or a friend."

From that hour, Gerard became the pillar of the household, treated as a son apart, always in the right, and entitled to every kindness. Jealousy festered within Cornelis and Sybrandt; they longed for the day Gerard departed for his clerical post, dreaming that his departure would open the Reverend's purse to them. They actively cooperated with Gerard's ambition, their resentment fueled by the wound love had inflicted upon them. As Gerard's success grew, his self-regard inflated, and his bitterness toward Margaret deepened. Yet, she retained the power to chill his affections toward women generally, and now, driven by every motive, he resolved to become a priest as soon as he could advance through the orders. Already knowing the Vulgate better than most clergy, he studied the rubrics and dogmas alongside the monks. When the Bishop visited, Gerard applied to be admitted as an exorcist, the third step in holy orders. The Bishop examined him and ordained him immediately. After a brief prayer, the Bishop handed Gerard a small manuscript filled with exorcisms, commanding, "Take this, Gerard, and have power to lay hands on the possessed, whether baptized or catechumens!" Reverently receiving the book, Gerard returned home, now invested by the Church with the authority to cast out demons.
