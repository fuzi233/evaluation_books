*"Gorgibus"—* "I tell you that marriage is a holy and sacred thing; and it is proper for honest folk to begin with it."

*"Madame"*—"Good heavens! If everyone were like you, a novel would soon be over! What a fine thing it would be if Cyrus married Mandane right off, and Aronce wedded Clélie straightaway! Let us take our time weaving the fabric of our romance, and do not rush its conclusion so." —Molière, *The Pretentious Ladies*.

It would be somewhat harsh to fault the Rector of Pennicote for viewing matters from every angle, including seeing Gwendolen as a candidate destined for a dazzling marriage. Was he to be expected to diverge from his peers in wishing his niece a fate inferior to what society deemed optimal for her charming youth? Rather, it speaks to his kindness that his sentiments were purely benevolent. Indeed, to advise her to dress in rags like Griselda's merely to attract a Marquis, or to insist that a fair maiden seeking a husband must hide away, would have been sheer folly. Mr. Gascoigne's plans were rational; he did not scheme to throw her upon a dangerous horse so a wealthy suitor might rescue her. He genuinely wished her well and intended to present her to the finest society in the neighborhood. His intentions aligned perfectly with Gwendolen's own desires.

Yet, let no one mistake this alignment. Gwendolen did not envision marriage as the ultimate goal of her existence, nor did she imagine herself conquering the world solely through equestrian grace or other accomplishments. While she acknowledged that she would eventually marry—and that it would certainly not be a mediocre union like those accepted by most women—she felt this quietly, without argument. Marriage was never the climax of the dramas where she pictured herself as a heroine. Being courted fervently or sighing over hopelessly was indeed a delightful assurance of female power; but becoming a wife, bound by the domestic chains of that state, seemed largely a tedious necessity. Her observations led her to view matrimony as a dreary confinement where a woman could do nothing as she pleased, bore more children than desired, grew dull, and sank irretrievably into the mundane.

Of course, marriage represented social elevation; she could not foresee a single life. Yet, promotions sometimes require swallowing bitter herbs—a title does not replace the need for actual leadership for a man intent on leading. This delicate, ethereal figure of twenty years old meant to lead. For such passions reside within feminine hearts as well. In Gwendolen's case, however, they dwelled exclusively among strictly feminine pursuits, holding no disruptive connection to the progress of learning or the stability of the state; her knowledge was such that no leverage, short or long, could ever move the world. She intended to act pleasantly for herself in a striking manner—or rather, anything she could do to strike others with admiration, thereby gaining a more intense sense of living through their reflection, seemed delightful to her imagination.

"Gwendolen will not rest until she has the world at her feet," declared Miss Merry, the gentle governess. These hyperbolic words had long since acquired their most moderate meanings; after all, who has not heard of private individuals having the world at their feet in the form of a dozen items of flattering attention, commonly known in a genteel suburb? Words could scarcely be broad or vague enough to describe the hazy grandeur that surrounded poor Gwendolen atop the heights of her youthful self-exaltation. Others allowed themselves to be enslaved, their lives tossed like empty vessels with no will steering them. Not so with her; she would no longer be sacrificed to beings lesser than herself. Instead, she would seize the best opportunities life offered and conquer circumstances through her exceptional cleverness.

Certainly, settling at Offendene, with the patronage of Lady Brackenshaw, the Archery Club, and invitations to dine with the Arrowpoints, did not seem to promise extraordinary prospects. Yet, Gwendolen's confidence rested chiefly in herself. She felt fully equipped to master life. Regarding much of her past, she believed she had been treated unfairly; yet concerning her "education," she admitted it had left her with no disadvantages. In the classroom, her quick mind had eagerly absorbed the strong starch of unexplained rules and disconnected facts, shielding her from any painful sense of inadequacy. Whatever remained of knowable things, she felt sufficiently acquainted with through novels, plays, and poetry. Concerning French and music—the two essential accomplishments of a young lady—she felt no anxiety. Adding to these qualifications, both negative and positive, was the spontaneous sense of capability some fortunate souls are born with: the belief that any subject they touch impresses them with their own power to judge it correctly. Who could wonder then that Gwendolen felt ready to steer her own destiny?

There were many subjects in the world—perhaps the majority—in which she felt no interest, simply because they were stupid. Subjects often appear dull to the young as light appears dim to the elderly; yet she would not have felt helpless regarding them if they arose in conversation. It must be remembered that no one had ever disputed her power or her general superiority. Just as upon her arrival at Offendene, so always, the first thought of those around her was, *What will Gwendolen think?* Whether the footman tread heavily in creaking boots, or the laundrywoman's work was unsatisfactory, or the maid remarked, "This will never do for Miss Harleth"; if the wood smoked in the bedroom fireplace, Mrs. Davilow, whose own weak eyes suffered greatly from the irritation, apologized to Gwendolen. If, during travel, she did not appear at the breakfast table until everyone else had finished, the sole concern was ensuring her coffee and toast remained the hottest and crispest. When she finally arrived, with her freshly brushed light-brown hair streaming back, awaiting her mother's hand to coil it, her large brown eyes gleaming brightly like a wave-washed onyx beneath long lashes, it was always she who had to exercise tolerance—begging Alice not to stiffen her shoulders in that dreadful manner, and urging Isabel to leave her alone rather than pressing questions.

Always she was the princess in exile, entitled to have her breakfast roll made of the finest flour from the seven thin ears of wheat in times of famine, and to have her silver fork preserved from the baggage during a general retreat. How was this to be explained? The answer may seem obvious: in her beauty, a certain unusual quality, and a decisive will that manifested in her graceful movements and clear, unhesitating tone. So, if she entered a room on a rainy day when everyone else was listless and the purpose of things seemed unclear, there seemed a sudden, sufficient reason to maintain the forms of life. Even hotel waiters displayed greater alacrity in removing crumbs, creases, and dregs mixed with struggling flies.

This potent charm, combined with the fact that she was the eldest daughter, toward whom her mother had always maintained an apologetic attitude regarding the hardships inflicted by a stepfather, may seem a complete explanation for Gwendolen's domestic empire. To seek another cause would be to ask why there is daylight when the sun shines. But beware of drawing conclusions without comparison. I recall witnessing similar diligent, apologetic attention bestowed upon persons who were neither beautiful nor unusual, whose firmness lacked grace or euphony, and who were not eldest daughters with tender, timid mothers filled with remorse. Some were merely common men. The only similarity among them was a strong determination to obtain what was pleasant, coupled with a total fearlessness in becoming disagreeable or dangerous when denied. Who is cajoled and served with trembling by the weak females of a household more than the unscrupulous male—capable, if he lacks freedom at home, of causing far worse trouble elsewhere? Thus, I am compelled to doubt whether, even without her potent charm and unique filial position, Gwendolen might not still have played the queen in exile, provided she retained her innate energy of egoistic desire and her power to inspire fear regarding what she might say or do.

However, she possessed the charm, and those who feared her were also fond of her; the fear and affection perhaps heightened by what may be termed the iridescence of her character—the interplay of various, nay, contradictory tendencies. For Macbeth's rhetoric regarding the impossibility of being many opposite things simultaneously referred to the clumsy necessities of action, not the subtler possibilities of feeling. We cannot speak a loyal word and remain meanly silent; we cannot kill and refrain from killing in the same instant. But a moment is wide enough to hold both loyal and base desires, the outburst of a murderous thought, and the sharp, backward stroke of repentance.
