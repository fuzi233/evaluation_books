For three days, Feversham drifted in a fever-dream, wandering the edges of consciousness while Trench tended to his physical needs—fetching water from the Nile, sharing his meager food, and standing vigil alongside Ibrahim in the stone courtyard. On the third night, Trench guarded Feversham's flank within the House of Stone. But on the fourth morning, the delirium broke.

Feversham woke with a start, his vision clearing to reveal Trench leaning over him. At first, the face appeared as a specter from a childhood nightmare, looming too large, too close. Instinctively, Feversham flung a weak arm outward, pushing the figure away. Yet, as he forced his gaze around the grim reality of his surroundings—the harsh blue sky, the trampled earth, the ragged forms of chained prisoners—the illusion shattered. He grabbed Trench's arm, clutching him as if fearing sudden abandonment, and then a smile broke through his exhaustion.

"In the prison at Omdurman," Feversham gasped, the words sounding foreign to his tongue. "Actually in the prison! This is Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true."

Leaning back against the rough wall, he exhaled as if a heavy burden had vanished. To Trench, the tone of such relief sounded almost ironic—a performance of indifference that masked deep emotion. But Trench knew better. The man who had feigned stoicism was gone, replaced by someone genuinely overwhelmed. The danger of Dongola was behind them; the journey to Omdurman, the ultimate destination Feversham had dreamed of, was complete. Instead of hanging on a gibbet far upstream, vultures circling his shoulders, he lay here, safe within the walls.

"You've been here a long time," Feversham observed.

"Three years."

Feversham scanned the *zareeba*. "Three years of it," he murmured. "I was terrified I wouldn't find you alive."

Trench nodded silently.

"The nights are the worst," Feversham continued, his voice trembling slightly. "A miracle anyone survives a week, yet I have endured a thousand." Even to Trench, whose own endurance was legendary, the sheer volume of suffering Feversham described seemed impossible. "A thousand nights!"

"But we can reach the Nile by daylight," Feversham urged, rising abruptly and staring at the thorny fence surrounding them. "We must have some freedom. An Arab at Wadi Halfa promised me—"

"It is true," Trench interjected gently. "Look." He gestured to the clay bowl beside him. "I filled that from the Nile this morning."

"I must go," Feversham insisted, lifting himself unsteadily. "Right now." His voice rose with urgency, prompting Trench to whisper sharply, "Hush! Many prisoners listen, and among them are many traitors."

Feversham slumped back down, obedience overriding his desperation. "But they won't understand what we're saying," he protested, his excitement draining away.

"They will see we speak earnestly. Idris will know within the hour, and the Khalifa before sunset. We'd lose everything to heavier chains and the *courbach* if we spoke. Lie still. You're weak, and I'm weary. We'll sleep, and later we'll walk to the Nile together."

Trench lay down beside him, and moments later, sleep claimed him. Feversham watched, noting how deeply the three years of hardship etched themselves into Trench's features. It was not until noon that Feversham stirred.

"Is no one bringing you food?" he asked quietly.

"Yes," Feversham replied. "A boy should arrive soon. He brings news, too."

They waited until the gate creaked open, allowing relatives and wives into the enclosure. Instantly, the yard transformed into a chaotic cage of wild animals. Gaolers distributed their daily rations immediately. Only enough *aseeda*—the damp, pounded millet cake—to sustain bare life was permitted, and even that sparked fierce competition. The strong devoured from the weak; groups fought for scraps. Men, gaunt as skeletons, hopped and leaped toward the entrance, limited only by the weight of their chains. One man stumbled, fell, and lay motionless in despair, knowing another day without food awaited him. Others lunged at the messengers, tearing bread from their hands despite the lashings that followed. Thirty gaolers, wielding rhinoceros-hide whips, patrolled the perimeter, yet for this brief window, their authority seemed suspended.

Amidst the scramble, a young boy hid behind the crowd, glancing around hesitantly. Soon, he was spotted. Knocked down, his food snatched away, the boy began to scream. His cries drew Idris-es-Saier, the jailer, to the scene.

"For whom do you come?" Idris demanded, shoving the attackers aside.

"For Joseppi, the Greek," the boy stammered. Idris pointed toward the corner where Feversham lay. The boy stepped forward, holding out his empty hands to explain his failure, then squatted beside Feversham. As he spoke, he loosened a gazelle-skin pouch from his waist and let it drop. Inside lay a live chicken. With this unexpected feast, Feversham and Trench ate breakfast, lunch, and dinner.

An hour later, the gates opened, and the pair walked slowly toward the Nile, pausing frequently. Amidst the crowds unloading boats and shouting along the muddy banks, the gaolers remained visible but out of earshot.

"We can talk here," Trench suggested.

"Why have you come?" Feversham asked.

"I was captured in the desert on the Arbain road," Feversham admitted slowly. "Masquerading as a mad musician who wandered from Wadi Halfa with a zither."

"I know," Trench said. "But you were captured by your own deliberate choice. You sought me out in Omdurman."

"How do you know?"

"You told me," Trench replied, drawing out the words. "During these last three days, you've told me much. Very much." He sat up, shielding his face with his hands so Feversham couldn't see him, speaking quietly. "You came to join me because five years ago, I sent you a white feather."

"And was that all?" Feversham asked, anxious.

"No," Trench said, his voice thick with emotion. "That wasn't all. You spoke of a girl—the same girl you mentioned when Willoughby, Durrance, and I dined with you in London. I know her name now. Her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers arrived. I hadn't considered that possibility. She handed you the fourth feather, adding to our three. I am sorry."

A long silence followed. Finally, Feversham spoke softly. "As for me, I am not sorry. Nor am I sorry she was present. In fact, I am glad. She gave me the fourth feather, yes, but I am glad because of it. Without her presence, without seeing that fourth feather snap from her fan, I might have surrendered there and then. Who knows? I doubt I could have survived three long years in Suakin alone. I used to see you, Durrance, Willoughby, and others who were once my friends, carrying out duties I was accustomed to. You can't imagine how the mere routine of a regiment—one I cursed when I had to endure it—appealed as something so desirable. I could have slipped onto a boat and returned to Suez so easily. The chance I waited for never materialized—for three years."

"You saw us?" Trench asked. "And gave no sign?"

"How would you have reacted if I had?" Feversham countered, silencing Trench. "I saw you, but I was careful not to be seen. I doubt I could have endured it without the memory of Ramelton, without the physical sensation of the fourth feather keeping that night real and immediate in my mind. I never would have left Obak for Berber. I certainly never would have joined you in Omdurman."

Trench turned quickly. "She would be glad to hear you say that. I have no doubt she regrets sending the fourth feather, just as I regret the other three."

"There is no need for regret," Feversham said firmly. "I don't blame you, nor her." Both men fell silent, gazing at the river. The air rang with the clamor of the shore, crowded with Arabs and Negroes in robes of blue, yellow, and brown, working tirelessly to unload dhows. Beyond the river's bend, the palm trees of Khartum stood against the cloudless sky, bathed in the setting sun. Soon, the terrors of the House of Stone would resume. Yet, in their minds, both men envisioned the elm trees by the Lennon River, a hall with an open door echoing to the soft music of a waltz, where a girl and a man stood amidst three fallen white feathers. One recalled the image; the other imagined it. To both, it was vividly real. Feversham smiled.

"Perhaps she has now seen Willoughby; perhaps she has taken his feather."

Trench extended his hand. "I will take mine back now."

Feversham shook his head. "Not yet." Suddenly, hope ignited in Trench's eyes—a hope he had suppressed for three days and nights
