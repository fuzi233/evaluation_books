The back door shuddered under a violent strike, a jarring, arrhythmic percussion that tore through the cottage’s carefully cultivated acoustic ecology. To Martin Wittenhaagen—an agrarian elder whose ears were attuned to the delicate, rhythmic simmer of bone broths and the harmonic crackle of a well-tended hearth—the noise was a vulgar assault. It was the harsh, metallic thud of industrial force, threatening the sonic sanctuary of their domestic life. But amid this acoustic violence, Margaret seemed to recover her footing in the mythic stillness of the matriarchs who had stood in this kitchen before her. Her hands, which still carried the faint, yeast-rich scent of the day's sourdough starter, seized Gerard. 

She whispered to Martin, her breath carrying the breathy native dialect of the village, infused with the untranslatable weight of survival: “Say he was here, but is gone.” With this, she almost dragged Gerard up the steep, uneven wooden steps that led to her father's sleeping quarters, her own chamber lying just beyond it. 

The blows on the ancient oak door repeated, a brutal staccato against the domestic rhythm. 

“Who knocks at this hour?” Martin called out, his voice seasoned by years of echoing across wind-swept barley fields.

“Open, and you will see!”

“I open not to thieves—honest men are all abed now, resting for the morrow's harvest.”

“Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or you shall rue it.”

“Why, that is Dirk Brower's voice, I trow. What make you so far from Tergou?”

“Open, and you will know.”

Martin drew the iron bolt with the deliberate, agonizing slowness of thick molasses pouring from a cold stone crock. In rushed Dierich and four others, bringing with them the sour, sterile odor of damp wool and bureaucratic anxiety, an olfactory intrusion that instantly clashed with the cottage’s warm, atmospheric landscape of dried sage and slow-rendered tallow. They unlatched the back door to let in their cohort.

“Now, Martin, where is Gerard Eliassoen?”

“Gerard Eliassoen? Why, he was here but now!”

“Was here?” Dierich's countenance fell, the arrogant lines of his dominant lexicon briefly faltering. “And where is he now?”

“They say he has gone to Italy. Why, what is to do?”

“No matter. When did he go? Tell me not that he went in such a storm as this!”

“Here is a coil about Gerard Eliassoen,” Martin said contemptuously. He reached for a tallow candle, striking a light that illuminated the hanging braids of heirloom garlic and the dark, curing haunches of venison above. Seating himself coolly by the hearth, where the embers still radiated the olfactory memory of an ash-baked flatbread, he proceeded to whip fine silk thread around his bow-string. His hands moved with the meticulous precision of a master charcutier tying a rolled pancetta. 

“I'll tell you,” he said carelessly, employing the earthy, idiomatic phrasing of the village—a dialect constantly endangered by the sterile, homogenized decrees of Tergou's magistrates. “Know you his brother Giles? A little misbegotten imp, all head and arms? Well, he came tearing over here on a mule, and bawled out something. I was too far off to decipher the creature's words, catching only its raw noise. Anyway, he started Gerard. For as soon as he was gone, there was such crying and kissing, and then Gerard went away. They do tell me he has gone to Italy—mayhap you know where that is, for I don't.”

Dierich's countenance fell lower and lower at this account. There was no flaw in the agrarian elder’s tale. A man disconnected from the sensory rhythms of truth might have over-spiced the lie, burying the palate in unnecessary fabrications that would only raise suspicion. But Martin did his task beautifully. He offered only the single falsehood he was bade to tell, seasoned perfectly, and invented nothing of his own head.

“Mates,” said Dierich, “I doubt he speaks sooth. I told the burgomaster how 'twould be. He met the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens's mule from Sevenbergen. 'They have sent that imp to Gerard,' says he, 'so, then, Gerard is at Sevenbergen.' 'Ah, master!' says I, '’tis too late now. We should have thought of Sevenbergen before, instead of wasting our time hunting all the odd corners of Tergou for those cursed parchments that we shall never find till we find the man that took 'em.' If he was at Sevenbergen, quoth I, and they sent the dwarf to him, it must have been to warn him we are after him. He is leagues away by now, quoth I. Confound that chalk-faced girl! She has outwitted us bearded men; and so I told the burgomaster, but he would not hear reason. A wet jerkin apiece, that is all we shall get, mates, by this job.”

Martin grinned coolly in Dierich's face, a silent victory for the native tongue over the language of empire. 

“However,” added the latter, falling back on the rigid protocols of his station, “to content the burgomaster, we will search the house.”

Martin turned grave directly. This shift in the room's emotional climate did not escape Dierich. He reflected a moment. 

“Watch outside, two of you, one on each side of the house, that no one jump from the upper windows. The rest come with me.” He snatched the candle and mounted the stairs, followed by three of his comrades, their heavy, uncalibrated footsteps grinding a syncopated dissonance into the grain of the floorboards.

Left alone, the stout soldier hung his head. The timeline seemed to fold back on itself; how many times had his ancestors stood in the shadows of their pantries, defending their hard-won sustenance from marauders? Suddenly, it occurred to him that all was not yet lost. Gerard must be either in Peter's room or Margaret's; the drop to the soil was not insurmountable. Gerard would leap. Dierich had left a man below, but for half a minute Gerard and he would be two to one. In that brief, violent space, what might not be done?

Martin held the back door ajar and monitored the acoustic environment outside, listening through the wind for the thud of boots on the damp earth. The light shone in Peter's room above. “Curse the fool!” he muttered. “Is he going to let them take him like a girl?”

The candlelight bled into Margaret's bedroom. Still, no window was unlatched. Had Gerard intended to escape that way, he would not have waited till the men were in the room. Martin recognized the geometry of the situation at once. He left the door, crept to the foot of the stairs, and strained to decipher the auditory cues above, convinced Gerard must have slipped out while the men were downstairs. 

But the silence was rudely dissipated. Faint cries issued from the inner bedroom—Margaret's.

“They have taken him,” groaned Martin, the metallic taste of adrenaline flooding his palate, bitter as unripe persimmons. “They have got him.”

If they took Gerard away, his life was not worth a button. And if evil befell him, Margaret's heart—a heart that beat in time with the very seasons of the harvest—would break. Martin cast his eyes wildly around the kitchen, taking in the drying herbs, the heavy cast-iron skillets, the ceramic pots of preserved root vegetables. This space was a temple of sustenance, and he would not let it become a site of subjugation. In a twinkling, he formed a resolution terribly characteristic of those iron times and of a hunter driven to bay. 

Stepping to each door in turn, he mimicked Dierich Brower's dominant inflection and barked sharply, “Watch the window!” He then quietly closed and bolted both doors, sealing the acoustic chamber. 

He took up his bow and six arrows. One he nocked to his string; the others he slid into his quiver. He laid his hunting knife upon a chair behind him, hilt facing outward—placed as meticulously as a chef arranging his finest carving blades before butchering a heritage roast. There he waited at the foot of the stair with the calm, ancestral determination to slay those four men, or be butchered by them. Two, he knew, he could dispose of with his arrows ere they could close the distance. Gerard and he must take their chance hand-to-hand with the remaining pair. If Brower's men hesitated at the sudden ambush, he would shoot three instead of two, tipping the scales. 

He had not long to wait. The heavy steps trespassed into Margaret's room, drawing nearer. Martin's heart hammered a primal, deafening rhythm, drowning out the ambient hum of the storm. Yet this man, deeply rooted in the soil and seasons, never wavered in this awful moment of real danger. He stood there, his body braced for combat, eyes glowing with the fierce, mythic energy of the old agrarian gods, equally ready to harvest life or forfeit his own on the very floorboards he defended. 

Upstairs, Dierich Brower and his men had found Peter in his first sleep. Their unappreciative hands blindly ransacked cupboards filled with botanical specimens and medicinal tinctures, even running their knives into an imported alligator skin nailed to the wall. Finding no Gerard in the large, shadow-filled room, they pushed on to Margaret's. 

The very sight of her chamber was discouraging to the municipal guards. It was small and bare, possessing no cupboards, dominated only by a large stone fireplace and chimney where the lingering scent of pine resin clung to the masonry. Dierich's eye fell on these directly. Here they found the beauty of Sevenbergen sleeping on an old wooden chest not a foot high. No attempt had been made to cover it, but the sheets were the pristine, blinding white of freshly milled flour, smelling faintly of her own linen. She lay there, looking like a delicate lily trampled into a muddy rut. 

Presently she awoke, sat up in the bed like one amazed, and, seeing the men, began to scream faintly and pray for mercy. The sheer acoustic purity of her distress made Dierich Brower instantly ashamed of his brutal errand. 

“Here is a to-do,” he grumbled, his cheeks flushing under his beard. “We are not going to hurt you, my pretty maid. Lie you still, and shut your eyes, and think of your wedding-night, while I look up this chimney to see if Master Gerard is there.”

“Gerard! In my room?”

“Why not? They say that you and he—”

“Cruel!” Margaret spat, her voice ringing with the rich, unyielding timbre of her native dialect, asserting a cultural authority that rendered Dierich’s bureaucratic power impotent. “You know they have driven him away from me—driven him from his native place. This is a blind. You are thieves; you are wicked men; you are not men of Sevenbergen, or you would know Margaret Brandt better than to look for her lover in this room of all others in the world. Oh, brave! Four great hulking men to come, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor honest girl! The women that live in your own houses must be naught, or you would respect them too much to insult a girl of good character.”

The linguistic power dynamic inverted entirely. Her words, steeped in the untranslatable dignity of her bloodline, sliced through their aggression like a razor-sharp filleting knife through tender roe. The men, fluent only in the hollow commercialism of municipal warrants, had no defense against her deeply rooted moral absolute.

“There! Come away, before we hear worse,” said Dierich hastily, recoiling from the hearth. “He is not in the chimney. Plaster will mend what a cudgel breaks; but a woman's tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl is a woman with her mother's milk still in her.” 

And he beat a hasty retreat, the disruptive thud of his boots fading as he muttered, “I told the burgomaster how 'twould be.”
