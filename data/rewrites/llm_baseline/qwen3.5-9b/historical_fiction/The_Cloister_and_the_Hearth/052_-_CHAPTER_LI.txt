Upon returning downstairs, Margaret was a transformed figure. Though her eyes remained glistening with unshed tears, the storm within her had settled into a profound, quiet calm. The sharp edges of her bitterness and the feverish intensity of her recent emotions had melted away entirely.

"Denys," she whispered, "I have received my instructions. I am to read my lover's letter aloud to his family."

"You will never do that?" Denys asked, disbelief coloring his voice.

"I shall," she replied firmly.

He stared at her, searching for some sign of hesitation. "I perceive that something in the letter has softened your heart toward them."

"Not a single jot, Denys, not a single jot," she insisted gently. "Yet, even if I hated them as one hates poison, I would not disobey my love. Denys, how sweet it is to obey! But sweeter still is to obey a soul who stands afar, unable to enforce my duty through force, but who must rely solely upon the fidelity of my love. Ah, Gerard, my darling... had you stood before me, close at hand, I might have slighted your commands, repelled by your kin as I have every reason to despise them. But now, if you were to bid me wade into the raging sea to read your sweet letter to the very sharks, I would go without a second thought. Therefore, Denys, tell his mother I possess the letter. If she and her kin are willing to listen, I am their humble servant. Let them set the hour, and I will seat them as comfortably as possible and welcome them with all the warmth I can muster."

Denys departed immediately to deliver this news to Catherine. He found the family gathered around the dinner table. Announcing that a lengthy letter from Gerard had arrived, he watched the joy ripple through the room. Then, amidst the celebration, he added, "Her heart has also been softened; she intends to read it to you personally. You may choose whatever time suits you best."

"What makes her believe no one else can read it but her?" Catherine queried.

"Let her send the letter, and we shall read it ourselves," someone suggested.

"Nay, but, Mother," interjected little Kate, "perhaps she cannot bear to part with it from her own hands; she loves him so dearly."

"Doth she think we intend to steal it?" Cornelis offered a cynical suggestion, implying that Margaret sought merely to wedge herself into the household through this token of affection.

Denys shot a look of pure scorn at the speaker. "That speaks a base heart," he declared. "Comrade La hates you all like venom. Do not mistake me, Dame; I offer no defense for her nature—so it is indeed—but despite her spleen at a mere word from Gerard, she offers to read you his letter with her own delicate lips. She possesses a voice like honey—it is certainly a fair proposal."

"'Tis true, my honest soldier," the father, Eli, agreed warmly. "It merits a courteous response. Hold your tongues, you women, and allow me to answer. Tell her that I, her son's father, setting aside all ancient grudges, accept this grace with gratitude. Should she desire double thanks, let her dispatch my son's letter through your faithful hand. I shall read it to his flesh and blood, and thereafter, I will return to her with such surety and faithfulness as I am Eli, a Dirick, a William, a Luke—a free burgher of Tergou, following in the footsteps of my ancestors, and like them, a man of my word."

"Ay, and a man who keeps his word better than most," cried Catherine, adding, "The only one I have ever encountered worthy of such praise."

"Hold thy peace, wife!" Eli commanded sharply.

"Are you a man of sense, Eli, a Dirk, a Chose?" Denys shouted, using local dialect terms for a person of substance. "The female comrade will be delighted to obey Gerard while avoiding facing you all, whom she detests as wormwood, save for your presence. Blessed be, the world has changed; today she is pure submission. 'Obedience is honey,' she proclaimed. In truth, it is a sweetmeat she cannot resist, though she eats sparingly of it. With her fair face and mellifluous tongue, coupled with her tendency to fly into fits that terrify us soldiers to death when thwarted, and her alternating between scolding us like children and petting us like lambs, she has turned two of the most crawling slaves imaginable out of two honest swashbucklers. I am the ironing rogue, and the other washes."

"What comes next?" Eli asked.

"Why, whenever the brat enters the world, I shall rock the cradle, and the other knave shall wash the tucker and bibs. So, I shall fetch the letter instantly. Will you permit me to stay and hear it read?"

"Unless our hearts are blacker than coal," Catherine assured him.

Denys rushed forth to retrieve the letter but returned crestfallen. "She will not yield it to me, nor to you, nor to any living soul."

"I knew she would not," Cornelis muttered.

"Hush! Hush!" Eli commanded. "Let Denys recount his tale."

"Nay," Denys reported, "'Be ruled by me.' 'Not I,' she retorted. 'Well then,' I pressed, 'consider that honeyed Obedience you spoke of.' 'You are a fool,' she said. 'Obedience to Gerard is sweet, but obedience to any other mortal, whoever they may be, holds no sweetness for me.'"

"At last, she seemed to soften slightly and handed me a written note for you, Mademoiselle. Here it is."

"For me?" Little Kate blushed, reaching out.

"Give it here!" Eli took the paper and scanned the handwriting, speaking almost in a whisper. "These are words from the letter. Hearken!"

"And, sweetheart, if these lines should journey safely to thee, make trial of my people's hearts as well. Perhaps they are somewhat turned toward me, being far away. If so, they will demonstrate it to thee, since they may not do so openly to me. Read, then, this letter! Yet, I strictly forbid thee to let it from thy hand. And if they still hold aloof from thee, then say nothing, but let them think me dead. Obey me in this; for, if thou dost disrespect my judgment and my will herein, thou lovest me not."

A heavy silence fell upon the room as Gerard's words, transcribed by Margaret, were passed around and examined.

"Well," Catherine finally said, "that is another matter entirely. But methinks it falls to her to come to us, rather than us going to her."

"Alas, Mother! What difference does that make?" little Kate exclaimed.

"Much," Eli replied gravely. "Tell her we are too numerous to visit her, and bid her come hither, the sooner the better."

Once Denys had withdrawn, Eli confessed privately that it was a bitter pill to swallow. "When that girl crosses my threshold, all the mischief and misery she has wrought here will seem to tumble into one heap at my doors. But what choice did I have, wife? We must hear the news of Gerard. I saw it in thine eyes, and felt it in my own heart. She is backed by our undutiful yet still beloved son, making her stronger than we, bringing our noses down to the grindstone, that sly, cruel jade. But never mind. We shall hear the letter; and then, let her depart unblessed, just as she arrived unwelcome."

"Make your mind easy," Catherine soothed him. "She will not come at all." A tone of deep regret lingered in her voice.

Shortly thereafter, Richart arrived from Amsterdam, as had been hourly anticipated. Grave and dignified in his burgher's robes, adorned with a gold chain, ruff, and fur-lined cap, he was received not merely with affection but with profound respect. For he had risen a step higher than his parents, and in medieval society, such ascents were marked as visibly as new flights of stairs.

Admitted to the family council in due course, Richart displayed plainly, though without overt discourtesy, that his pride was deeply wounded by their decision to deign treating with Margaret Brandt.

"I see the temptation," he remarked dryly. "But which of us has not, at times, wished one thing and done another?"

This remark cast a considerable chill over the older members of the family. Little Kate quickly stepped forward to diffuse the tension. "Vex not thyself, dear Richart. Mother says she will not come."

"All the better, sweetheart," Richart replied coolly. "I fear me, if she does arrive, I shall hasten back to Amsterdam."

Just then, Denys poked his head through the doorway. "She will be here at three o'clock on the great dial."

They all exchanged looks, falling into a silent, tense contemplation.
