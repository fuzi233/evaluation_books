If Harry Warrington harboured a passion for martial pursuits, the atmosphere of Europe in 1757 was thick enough with the smoke of battle to fan it into a blaze. Though the Hanoverian cause had stumbled, the King of Prussia stood triumphant, his victories echoing through every salon and society where Harry moved. Yet, fortune seemed determined to spare him the horrors of the battlefield. Had Harry ridden to Rossbach on that infamous "Gunpowder Fête" or to the slaughter at Leuthen, the pages of history would have been filled with accounts written by another pen. It was a mercy, I say, that Harry did not follow the Borussian King. Imagine, if you will, the clash of my jaunty yellow phaeton against the grim chariot of thundering war; could my meek little Pegasus withstand the shock of a foaming, flame-breathed steed? I am content, dear reader, to let the great battles wait for later volumes, lest I be forced to compete with masters who paint the grim realities of conflict far more vividly than I could.

Yet, Harry did not merely avoid war; he pined for it. His existence devolved into a sulky, useless languor. He haunted military coffee-houses, his sole reading material limited to newspapers, dismissing literature and novels alike as foolish trifles. He possessed a hearty laugh, but it often came a beat too late, long after the joke had evaporated. Was it necessary for every gentleman to be a scholar? Why should one friend be deemed inferior for never having penned a couplet? Ruined, idle, and dependent on his brother's charity, Harry slept over books he could not comprehend, his great, strong hands finding no work to grip. Consider Achilles, exiled to the court of King Peleus to escape harm, yet idled away among women, eventually fathering Pyrrhus. A mettlesome youth like Harry, trailing behind distaffs or serving coffee, risked falling into mischief far worse than any battlefield.

In the circle of the Lambert family, the reaction to Harry's state varied sharply. The two elder sisters, particularly Theo, offered an untiring, maternal pity, the sort reserved for a few kind souls alone. They sought to cheer his grief, soothe his wrath, and mend the wounds inflicted by Fortune. But Hetty, the youngest, possessed a heart that seemed hardened and savage toward him. While the world was in arms, she chafed that Harry carried no musket. To her, it was harvest time; surely he could wield a sickle and reap a sheaf of glory instead of loafing around.

"Papa," Mrs. Lambert observed after a scene where Miss Hetty had been firing little verbal shots at Harry's quivering self-esteem, "how savage the little thing is with him!"
"Mamma," Theo interjected, "her conduct is abominable. She deserves to be whipped. Perhaps it is because she likes him better than any of us?"
Mrs. Lambert smiled gently. "Your father is so superior to the world, he has spoiled us."
"And so brave?" Mrs. Lambert pressed.
"I think Mr. Wolfe, Harry, or George are very brave," Theo admitted. "As for learning, Mr. George is certainly the most learned and witty. He chooses the finest things in books, loving them despite his satire. He treats the children to apples and gives coins to beggars with a charm I have rarely seen. Even regarding *Tartuffe*, he admits it is clever, though a mere villain is too mean a subject for a tragedy's lead."
"But you know the verses by heart?" Mrs. Lambert asked.
"Oh yes, Mamma! ... Nonsense!" Theo replied, blushing like a rose and fleeing the room. Her cheeks burned not merely from modesty, but from a fluttering heart, perhaps culling fresh flowers in honor of George's arrival. Where George came, Theo descended; where Harry knocked, she remained upstairs, sending a brother or sister to greet him. Theo, with her keen eyes, noted instantly the distinction between Harry's golden hair and George's dark locks, ensuring she never mistook them, whereas Hetty showed no excitement whatsoever when the black-haired gentleman entered.

One afternoon, Harry sat on a couch, draped in despondency, when Miss Hetty descended. After the usual pleasantries, she inquired, "Pray, sir, what makes your honour look so glum?"
"Ah, Hetty!" Harry sighed. "I have nothing else to do but to look glum. Remember when we were boys? I was always asking for holidays to swing on gates or make ducks and drakes. Those do-nothing days were the most melancholy. What have I to do now from morn till night?"
"Breakfast, walk—dinner, walk—tea, supper, and a pipe of your Virginia," Hetty tossed back.
Harry confessed that returning to the Chartreux, he wished to plead with the master to teach him Latin and Greek again, to repair his childhood idleness. "I might as well go to my books," he murmured. "Why do you laugh?"
"I laugh to fancy you at the head of a class!" Hetty cried.
"I shouldn't be," Harry humbled himself. "George might lead any class, but I am no bookman. I neglected myself. If our tutors had caned us, it might have done me good."
"What does Tom say about the caning?" Hetty asked, tapping her foot.
"It makes me see that I was idle when I should have worked," Harry replied simply. "I have no genius for books. Am I good for anything? Only to spend my patrimony, lounge at coffee-houses, or gallop behind hounds."
"Such a great, brave, strong fellow good for nothing?" Hetty exclaimed. "I would not confess as much to any woman, if I were twice as useless!"
"What am I to do?" Harry asked, his voice dropping. "I seek leave to join the army, but Madam Esmond ignores me. I have spent all I have and borrowed heavily from my brother. I cannot ask for more. If Mother would send me, I would jump to go."
"Eh!" Hetty scoffed. "A gentleman of spirit does not want a woman to buckle his sword on! What of Sir John Armytage? Engaged to a famous beauty, Miss Howe, yet he volunteered immediately when the King asked."
"He used to frequent White's," Harry noted.
"So?" Hetty marched about the room, adopting a courageous military stance. "An obstacle to glory? I think no woman of spirit would say 'Stay!' when her country says 'Go!' My father longs to serve, and both my brothers are destined for the Church. As for me, I know I should have made a famous little soldier!" Striding boldly, she mimicked a warrior.
Harry watched her with tender admiration. "I hardly think I could see a musket on those shoulders, Hetty, nor a wound on that pretty face."
"Wounds! Who fears wounds?" Hetty cried. "Muskets? Men think us good only for puddings. Look, George read Tasso to us yesterday. Hear these verses: 'Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail / With lively force and vigour strengthen, so / That I this silken gown... Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo; / Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail, / Nor storms withhold me; but I would, both day and night, / In pitched field or private combat, fight.'"
"Silken gown?" Harry inquired, puzzled.
"Well, sir, it's Calimanco," Hetty corrected. "'Then should not heat... Withhold me; but I would, both day and night, / In pitched field or private combat, fight.'"
"Why are both my brothers to be parsons?" Hetty demanded. "One of Papa's children ought to be a soldier!"
Harry laughed, a gentle, kind sound. He felt no desire to strike such a tender little warrior. "I think," he said, extending a finger, "here is a digit nearly as large as your arm. How would you stand before a great, strong man?"
"Do you suppose any scoundrel would dare injure you?" Harry paced the floor, agitated by the thought of a French rogue offending Miss Hester Lambert. This silent courage subjugated Hetty; she believed in his strength, causing her specially to admire him. Yet, Miss Hetty was no virago. Had she truly been bold, she might have preferred a soft, literary suitor, but nature drew the burly hero to the frail, tender woman. If Harry fell in love with her, the end would likely be a union of opposites.

For a while, Harry barely felt the sting of Hetty's sarcasm, letting her shoot her arrows without parrying. Did she intend to shame him by suggesting a weak woman would don armor? The simple fellow either melted at the prospect of her danger or laughed at the absurdity.
"What is the use of a strong hand if it only holds silk for my mother?" Hetty cried. "James Wolfe seems weakly and ill, coughing like a ghost-seer."
"I never understood why a man should fear a ghost," Harry said.
"Haven't you seen one?"
"No. Nathan in his night-shirt at home. Our nurses lie."
"George was frightened."
"Yes, but he is different from me."
