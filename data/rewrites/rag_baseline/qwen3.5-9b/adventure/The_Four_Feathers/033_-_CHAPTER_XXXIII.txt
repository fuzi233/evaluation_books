The words that shattered the evening were ones no sane person should ever speak aloud. That night, Ethne retreated to her farmhouse, sealing herself within the parlour. The summer air held a deceptive chill, prompting her to ignite the fire. She sat before the hearth, staring into the dancing embers with a stillness that betrayed the violent tremors of her spirit; this rigid composure was her signature when the storm raged beneath the surface. The moment she had hungered for, the hour she had measured her life by, had arrived and vanished. Now, she was utterly solitary in her remote hilltop village, cut off from the world below, more isolated than she had been since Willoughby sailed away that August morning down the Salcombe estuary. Since his arrival, her existence had revolved around the single half-hour when Harry Feversham would be present. That interval had come and gone. She knew now the precise calculus of her longing, how she had constructed her days around its anticipation. Yet, here she sat, shivering by the flames, consumed by a profound emptiness.

Yet, this desolation was not a surprise. It was a prophecy she had written in her own blood. She had always known that sending Harry away would trigger a catastrophic hour, a time when her entire being would scream for his return. And so, she forced herself to endure the cold, reminding herself that she had always seen this shadow looming ahead and that she must look past it. Tomorrow, she would realize they had not parted forever; tomorrow, she would weigh today's separation against the finality of the night at Lennon House and recognize how trivial this pain truly was. Her mind drifted to Harry, speculating on his next move, constructing for him a future of immense distinction. In that reverie, Dermod's ancient collie nudged her hand, his instinct sharp despite his failing eyesight, sensing the depth of her distress. Ethne rose, cupping the dog's head between her palms and pressing a kiss to his fur. He was old, she thought, nearing the end of his tether. Soon he would leave her, too, leaving behind decades of solitude before she, in turn, faced her own final resting place.

Before the silence could deepen, a knock echoed at the door. A servant announced that Colonel Durrance awaited her. "Yes," she replied, rising immediately. As he entered, she moved forward to greet him, refusing to retreat into the sanctuary of her grief. She stepped out from her private chamber of sorrow the instant she was summoned. She conversed with her visitor as though nothing extraordinary had transpired an hour prior, discussing their impending marriage and the reconstruction of Lennon House. It was arduous, but she had grown accustomed to the weight of her burdens. Only tonight did Durrance make the path steeper. After the maid served tea, he requested that she play the *Musolli* Overture on her violin.

"Not tonight," Ethne said softly. "I am rather tired."

She had scarcely finished speaking before her resolve wavered. Ethne understood that in the minutiae of daily life, as much as in grand gestures, she could not afford to falter. The small things demanded vigilance above all else. "Still, I believe I can manage the overture," she said, forcing a smile, and lowered her case. She played the piece from start to finish. Durrance stood at the window, his back to her, silent until the final note faded. Then, he approached her side.

"I was rather a brute," he murmured quietly, "to ask you to play that overture tonight."

"I wasn't anxious to play," she admitted, setting the violin aside.

"I know. But I was anxious to discover something, and I knew no other method of finding it out."

Ethne turned her face upward, startled, her expression tight with suspense. "What do you mean?" she asked, her voice trembling slightly.

"You are rarely off your guard," Durrance continued, his tone steady. "Only when you play. Once before, when you performed that overture, you were vulnerable. I recalled that the overture was once strummed in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa. I hoped that by asking you to play it again tonight, I might catch you off guard once more."

His words stole her breath, draining the color from her cheeks. She rose slowly, staring at him with wide, bewildered eyes. He could not possibly know. It was impossible. He did not know. Yet, Durrance pressed on calmly. "Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?"

These were the incredible words. Durrance spoke them with a faint smile. It took Ethne a long moment to process the reality of his query. She was unsure at first if her overtaxed senses were deceiving her, but he repeated the question, and disbelief dissolved into certainty.

"Who told you of any fourth feather?" she asked, her voice thin.

"Trench," Durrance answered simply. "I met him at Dover. But he only informed me of the fourth feather," he clarified. "I already knew of the three preceding ones. Trench would never have mentioned the fourth unless I had acknowledged knowledge of the first three. Otherwise, I would not have met him as he disembarked from the steamer at Dover. I would not have asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' Knowing of the three was sufficient."

"How do you know?" Ethne cried out, a cry of despair, and moving closer, he gently grasped her arm.

"But since I know," he protested gently, "what matters the source? I have known for a long while, ever since Captain Willoughby arrived at The Pool with the first feather. I waited to reveal my knowledge until Harry Feversham returned, and he returned today."

Ethne sank back into her chair, stunned by this unexpected revelation. She had guarded her secret so meticulously that realizing it had been exposed for a year came as a jolt. Even amidst her confusion, she understood she needed time to regain her composure. She spoke of trivialities to buy seconds. "You were in the church, then? Or did you hear us on the steps? Or did you meet him as he rode away?"

"Not one of your conjectures is correct," Durrance smiled.

Ethne had chosen the perfect topic to delay the inevitable declaration she sensed approaching. Like others, Durrance possessed his own vanities; specifically, one that had bloomed since his blindness. He took pride in the acuity of his perception. It delighted him to make discoveries that no one expected a blind man to achieve, and to announce them unexpectedly. It was further pleasure to explain to his puzzled listeners the intricate steps leading to his conclusion.

"Not one of your guesses is right, Ethne," he said, practically inviting her to interrogate him.

"Then how did you find out?" she asked.

"I knew from Trench that Harry Feversham would arrive someday, soon. This afternoon, I passed the church. Your collie dog barked at me. Thus, I knew you were inside. However, a saddled horse was tied beside the gate. Therefore, someone else was with you, and not a villager. Then I persuaded you to play, and that revealed who rode the horse."

"Yes," Ethne said vaguely, having barely registered his logic. "Yes, I see."

Then, in a firm voice signaling the return of her self-command, she declared: "You went away to Wiesbaden for a year. You departed just after Captain Willoughby arrived. Was that the reason for your departure?"

"I left because neither you nor I could sustain the game of pretense we were playing," Durrance explained. "You pretended to harbor no thoughts for Harry Feversham, caring little if he lived or died. I pretended not to have discovered that, above all else, you loved him. Eventually, one of us would have failed, each in turn. I dared not fail, nor did you dare. I could not allow you, who had said, 'Two lives must not be spoiled because of me,' to spend a year believing two lives had been destroyed. On your side, you dared not let me, who had stated, 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship on both sides,' know that on one side there existed only friendship, and we were so close to collapse. So I left."

"You did not fail," Ethne said quietly. "It was only I who failed."

She condemned herself most bitterly. She had dedicated herself to shielding this man from knowledge that would crown his misfortunes, and she had failed. He had endeavored to protect her from understanding that she had failed, and he had succeeded. It was not merely the humiliation of being outwitted by the man she believed she had deceived that troubled her. Rather, she felt she ought to have succeeded, for in her failure, she had deprived him of his last chance at happiness. That was the sting.

"But it was not your fault," he said. "As I mentioned, you were off your guard occasionally, but the conclusive evidence was not revealed solely through that. When you played the *Musolli* Overture previously, on the night Willoughby brought you such joy, I attributed your happiness to the performance itself. You must not blame yourself. On the contrary, you should be glad I have found out."

"Glad!" she exclaimed.
