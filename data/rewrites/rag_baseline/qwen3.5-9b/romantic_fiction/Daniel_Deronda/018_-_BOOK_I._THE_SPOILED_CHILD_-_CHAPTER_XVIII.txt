Life is a various mother: sometimes she adorns herself in plumes and brilliants, ascending marble staircases with head held high, refusing to cast a glance upon the lackeys who attend her; at other times, she dwells grim-clad within dark alleys, breathing the fumes of hot gin amidst the screaming chaos of pauper riots. Yet tonight, she appeared as a frugal matron, neat and deft, possessing cheerful morning thoughts and a quick ingenuity to find abundance in the smallest measure.

Mrs. Meyrick's house was not noisy. The front parlor overlooked the river, while the rear faced the gardens, allowing the window to remain open even as she read aloud to her daughters, thereby freshening the air of the small double room where a single lamp and two candles burned. These candles rested on a separate table for Kate, who was sketching illustrations for a publisher; the lamp illuminated not only the reader but also Amy and Mab, who were embroidering satin cushions destined for "the great world."

Outside, the house appeared narrow and shabby, the bright light filtering through the holland blind revealing the heavy, old-fashioned window frame. Yet there is a peculiar comfort in knowing that countless such grim-walled slices of space in our foggy London have been—and still are—the sanctuaries of a culture far freer from vulgarity precisely because poverty renders every display an impersonal necessity, transforming the grand spectacles of the world into mere phenomena that stir only petty rivalry or vain efforts at possession. The Meyricks' home belonged to this category; indeed, they clung to this specific row-house because its interior was filled with objects permanently stationed in their accustomed places. For the mother, these items held memories of her marriage; for the young ones, they seemed as essential and unchallenged a component of their universe as the stars of the Great Bear viewed from the back windows.

Mrs. Meyrick had endured significant privation in other areas solely to preserve certain engravings specially cherished by her husband. Consequently, the narrow stretches of wall displayed a condensed world history in scenes and portraits that the children had memorized since infancy. The chairs and tables were likewise old acquaintances preferred to any new acquisitions. In these two modest parlors, devoid of furniture a broker would deign to cheapen save for the prints and the piano, resided the apparatus for a wide-glancing, carefully selected life, opened to the loftiest realms of music, painting, and poetry.

One cannot be certain that during the times of greatest scarcity, prior to Kate securing paid employment, these ladies always possessed a servant to tend their fires and sweep their floors. Nevertheless, they remained fastidious in certain particulars and refused to accept the notion that the manners of ladies in the fashionable world were saturated with the coarse selfishness, petty quarreling, and slang depicted in so-called literary photographs. The Meyricks possessed their own peculiarities, streaks of eccentricity flowing from both maternal and paternal blood, their minds resembling medieval houses with unexpected recesses and openings connecting one chamber to another, complete with flights of steps and sudden vistas.

Yet mother and daughters were bound together by a triple cord: family love, an admiration for the finest works and noblest actions, and a habit of industry. Hans's desire to expend some of his funds in rendering their lives more luxurious had been firmly resisted by all. Thus, both parties were spared the regret of threatened triumphs over his yearning for art in favor of the allurements of secure income—a victory that would eventually compel him to relinquish his fellowship. They could all afford to laugh at his Gavarni caricatures and regard him as blameless for following a natural inclination, an impulse their unselfishness and independence had left unhindered. It sufficed for them to continue in their established manner, punctuated only by a grand treat of opera-going (from the gallery) whenever Hans returned home for a visit.

Observing the group they formed this evening, one could scarcely wish for any alteration to their mode of existence. They were all similarly diminutive, perfectly scaled to their miniature rooms. Mrs. Meyrick was reading aloud from a French volume; she was a lively little woman, half-French, half-Scotch, possessing a pretty articulateness of speech that seemed to conjure daylight within her listener's understanding. Though not yet fifty, her rippling hair, concealed beneath a Quakerish net cap, was chiefly gray, while her eyebrows remained brown, matching the bright eyes below them. Her black dress, resembling a priest's cassock with its rows of buttons, suited a neat figure barely five feet tall.

The daughters mirrored their mother, save that Mab inherited Hans's light hair and complexion, accompanied by a bossy, irregular brow and other quaint features reminiscent of him. Everything about them was compact: from the firm coils of their hair, fastened back *à la Chinoise*, to their gray skirts standing in Puritan nonconformity to the prevailing fashion, which at that time would have demanded that four feminine circumferences occupy every inch of free space in the front parlor. If they had been waxworks, all four might have been packed effortlessly into a fashionable lady's traveling trunk. Their faces seemed brimming with speech, as if their minds had been shelled, akin to horse-chestnuts, becoming brightly visible.

The only large entity of its kind in the room was Hafiz, the Persian cat, comfortably perched on the brown leather back of a chair, occasionally opening his large eyes to ensure the lower animals were not causing mischief. The book Mrs. Meyrick held was Erckmann-Chatrian's *Histoire d'un Conscrit*. Having just concluded reading it aloud, Mab—who had dropped her work to the floor while stretching her head forward to fix her gaze upon the reader—exclaimed, "I think that is the finest story in the world."

"Of course, Mab!" replied Amy. "It is the last you have heard. Whatever pleases you is the best in its turn."

"It is hardly to be called a story," corrected Kate. "It is a fragment of history brought near us with a powerful telescope. We can discern the soldiers' faces; no, it is more than that—we can hear everything—we can almost hear their hearts beating."

"I don't care what you call it," said Mab, flicking away her thimble. "Call it a chapter in Revelations. It compels me to do something good, something grand. It fills me with sorrow for everyone. It makes me like Schiller—I want to take the world in my arms and kiss it. I must kiss you instead, little mother!" She flung her arms around her mother's neck.

"Whenever you are in that mood, Mab, down goes your work," Amy remarked. "It would be doing something good to finish your cushion without soiling it."

"Oh—oh—oh!" groaned Mab as she stooped to retrieve her work and thimble. "I wish I had three wounded conscripts to care for."

"You would spill their beef tea while you were talking," Amy retorted.

"Poor Mab! Don't be hard on her," interjected the mother. "Give me the embroidery now, child. You proceed with your enthusiasm, and I will continue with the pink and white poppy."

"Well, ma, I think you are more caustic than Amy," Kate observed, pulling her head back to examine her drawing.

"Oh—oh—oh!" cried Mab once more, rising and stretching her arms. "I wish something wonderful would happen. I feel like the deluge. The waters of the great deep are broken up, and the windows of heaven are opened. I must sit down and play the scales."

Mab began opening the piano while the others laughed at this dramatic climax, when suddenly a cab halted before the house, followed immediately by a rapid rap of the knocker.

"Dear me!" exclaimed Mrs. Meyrick, springing to her feet. "It is after ten, and Phoebe is already asleep." She hastened outward, leaving the parlor door ajar.

"Mr. Deronda!" The girls heard this exclamation from their mother. Mab clasped her hands, whispering loudly, "There now! Something _is_ going to happen." Kate and Amy abandoned their work in astonishment. However, Deronda's tone in reply was so low they could not catch his words, and Mrs. Meyrick promptly closed the parlor door.

"I know I am relying on your goodness in a most extraordinary manner," Deronda continued after delivering his brief narrative. "But you can imagine how helpless I feel with a young creature like this on my hands. I could not accompany her among strangers, and in her nervous state, I should dread bringing her into a house full of servants. I have placed my trust in your mercy. I hope you will not deem my act unwarrantable."

"On the contrary," Mrs. Meyrick responded. "You have honored me by trusting me. I perceive your difficulty. Please bring her in. I shall go and prepare the girls."

While Deronda retreated to the cab, Mrs. Meyrick re-entered the parlor and declared, "Here is someone to care for instead of your wounded conscripts, Mab: a poor girl who intended to drown herself in despair. Mr. Deronda discovered her only in time to save her. He brought her along in his boat and knew nothing else safe to do with her, so he has entrusted us and brought her here. It appears she is a Jewess, but quite refined, he says—she knows Italian and music."
