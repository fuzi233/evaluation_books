As Archer stepped off the Fall River train, the sweltering midsummer air enveloped him like a damp shroud. Boston's streets near the station pulsed with the sweet scent of ripening fruit, the acrid tang of stale beer, and the rich aroma of freshly brewed coffee. People in short sleeves and worn trousers strolled through the crowds, their faces relaxed and unhurried, as if they'd just risen from a leisurely breakfast.

Archer hailed a cab and headed to the Somerset Club for a late breakfast. Even the city's fashionable districts seemed to exude a carefree, slightly disheveled charm, a quality that neither heat nor summer's languor could quite dispel. Caretakers in calico lounged on doorsteps, and the Common appeared as a serene, post-picnic haven. Ellen Olenska, in this setting, was an incongruous presence – a vision he couldn't quite conjure, even in his most fanciful reveries.

Over a simple but satisfying meal, Archer devoured a slice of melon and scanned the morning paper, his mind whirling with the thrill of his sudden decision to leave for New York the following evening. He'd told May the night before that he had business in Boston, and now, with the letter from the office serving as a convenient excuse, he felt a surge of energy and purpose. The ease with which he'd conjured up this plan reminded him of Lawrence Lefferts's clever stratagems for securing his freedom, but Archer didn't dwell on this thought for long.

After finishing his meal, he lit a cigarette and perused the Commercial Advertiser, taking in the day's news and advertisements. As he sat there, a few acquaintances dropped by, exchanging pleasantries with the familiar ease of old friends. Though the world outside seemed unchanged, Archer felt a disorienting sense of displacement, as if he'd somehow slipped through the fabric of time and space.

Checking his watch, he discovered it was already past nine o'clock, and he rose to head to the writing room. There, he scribbled a few lines and dispatched a messenger to the Parker House with a note, instructing him to wait for a response. As he sat down behind another newspaper, he calculated the time it would take for the cab to reach its destination.
