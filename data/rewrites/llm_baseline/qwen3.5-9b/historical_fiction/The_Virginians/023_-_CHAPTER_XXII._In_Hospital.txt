Sinking into a sweet slumber, lulled by harmonious sounds, our young patient passed a night of pleasant unconsciousness. He awoke in the morning to find a summer sun streaming through the window, illuminating his bed where his kind host and hostess smiled warmly at him. Ravenously hungry, he was immediately permitted by his doctor to partake of a hearty mess of chicken, which the doctor's wife proudly announced had been prepared by one of her own daughters.

A fleeting image of a young person—or rather, two young persons—with flushed cheeks and dark, waving locks, smiling around his couch before suddenly vanishing, arose in the young man's mind. Immediately following this, the memory of a different woman returned: lovely, yet undeniably elderly, possessing... well, let us simply say she was considerably older. Oh, horror and remorse! He writhed with anguish as a specific recollection crossed his mind. An immense gulf of time yawned between him and the past. How long had it been since he realized those pearls were artificial and those golden locks merely brass? A long, long time ago, when he was an innocent boy. Now he was a man—quite an old man. He had been bled copiously, suffered a slight fever, gone without food for many hours, and finally succumbed to a sleeping draught followed by a deep slumber.

"What is it, my dear child?" cried kind Mrs. Lambert as he startled awake.

"Nothing, madam; just a twinge in my shoulder," replied the lad. "Am I speaking to my host and hostess? You have been very kind to me."

"We are old friends, Mr. Warrington," said Mrs. Lambert. "My husband, Colonel Lambert, knew your father, and I and your mother were schoolgirls together at Kensington. You were no stranger to us when your aunt and cousin informed us of your identity."

"Are they here?" asked Harry, looking somewhat blankly.

"They must have stayed at Tunbridge Wells last night. They sent a horseman from Reigate yesterday seeking news of you."

"Oh!" said Harry, glancing at his bandaged arm. "I remember."

"I have effected a good cure of you, Mr. Warrington. From now on, Mrs. Lambert and the cook will attend to you."

"Nay; Theo prepared the chicken and rice, Mr. Lambert," interjected the lady. "Shall Mr. Warrington rise after breakfast? We will send your valet to assist you."

"If howling proves fidelity, your man must be a most devoted creature," remarked Mr. Lambert.

"He allowed your baggage to depart in your aunt's carriage after all," noted Mrs. Lambert. "You must wear my husband's linen, which, I assure you, is not inferior to your own."

"Pish, my dear! My shirts are fit for any Christian," cried the Colonel.

"They are Theo's and Hester's work," said the mother, prompting her husband to arch his eyebrows and glance at her. "Moreover, Theo has ripped and resewn your sleeve to ensure comfort for your injured shoulder," she added.

"How beautiful those roses are!" exclaimed Harry, admiring a fine Chinese vase filled with them on the dressing table beneath a Japanese-framed glass cover.

"My daughter Theo cut them this morning. Well, Mr. Lambert? Did she indeed cut them?"

Presumably, the Colonel was reflecting that his wife had introduced Theo too prominently into the conversation, stepping on Mrs. Lambert's slipper, pulling her robe, or otherwise nudging her toward propriety.

"And I fancied I heard someone singing the Evening Hymn very sweetly last night—or was it merely a dream?" asked the young patient.

"Theo again, Mr. Warrington!" laughed the Colonel. "Our servants claimed your negro servant began humming it in the kitchen as if he were a church organ."

"Our people sing it at home, sir. My grandfather loved it dearly. His wife's father was a close friend of the late Bishop Ken, who composed it; and—and my dear brother also cherished it," said the boy, his voice softening.

It was then, presumably, that Mrs. Lambert felt inclined to kiss the boy. His recent accident, illness, and recovery, combined with the kindness of those around him, had softened Harry Warrington's heart, opening it to nobler influences than those imposed upon him during the previous six weeks. He breathed a purer air than the tainted atmosphere of selfishness, worldliness, and corruption into which he had been thrust upon arriving in England. Often, a young man's fate, choices, or weaknesses lead him into the company of the frivolous and vain; fortunate is he whose lot introduces him to wiser circles, where lamps are trimmed and pure hearts maintain a modest vigil.

The pleased matron left her young patient devouring Miss Theo's portion of rice and chicken, while the Colonel sat by the lad's bedside. Gratitude toward his hospitable hosts and satisfaction from a comfortable meal induced a very pleasant state of mind and body in Mr. Warrington. He was now ready to speak more freely than usual; for, unless stirred by strong interest or emotion, the young man was typically taciturn and cautious in conversation, lacking an imaginative turn of mind. Regarding books, his youth had been a negligent student, and his comments on even simple works lacked profundity or value. However, concerning dogs, horses, and the ordinary business of life, he was a far superior critic, conversing freely with anyone interested in such matters.

Harry's host, possessing considerable shrewdness and experience with books, livestock, and men, quickly gauged his young guest through their ensuing discussion. It was now, for the first time, that the Virginian learned Mrs. Lambert had been an early friend of his mother, and that the Colonel's own father had served alongside Harry's grandfather, Colonel Esmond, in the renowned wars of Queen Anne. He found himself in a friend's country, soon at ease with his honest host, whose manners were simple and cordial, appearing perfectly gentlemanly despite wearing a plain fustian coat and a lace-less waistcoat.

"My sons are both away," said Harry's host. "Otherwise, they would have shown you the countryside upon your rising, Mr. Warrington. Currently, you have only the company of my wife and her daughters. Mrs. Lambert has already spoken of one of them, Theo, our eldest, who made your broth, cut your roses, and mended your coat. She is not such a marvel as her mother imagines; however, little Theo is a smart little housekeeper and a very good, cheerful lass, though her father admits it."

"It is very kind of Miss Lambert to take such care of me," said the young patient.

"She is no kinder to you than to any other mortal; she merely performs her duty." The Colonel smiled. "I laugh at their mother for praising our children," he said, "and I confess I am equally foolish about them. The truth is, God has gifted us with excellent and dutiful children, and I see no reason to disguise my gratitude for such a blessing. Do you have no sisters, I trust?"

"No, sir, I am alone now," Mr. Warrington replied.

"Ay, truly; I beg your pardon for my thoughtlessness. Your man has told our household what happened last year. I served with Braddock in Scotland and hope he recovered before passing. He was a wild fellow, sir, yet possessed a fund of truth beneath his rough, swaggering manner. Your black fellow speaks very freely about his master and his affairs. I suppose you allow him these liberties since he rescued you—"

"Rescued me?" cried Mr. Warrington.

"From numerous Indians on that very expedition. My Molly and I did not anticipate entertaining such a prodigiously wealthy gentleman. He claims half of Virginia belongs to you; but if the entirety of North America were yours, we could offer only our best."

"Those negro boys, sir, lie like the father of all lies. They deem it honorable to represent us as ten times richer than we are. My mother possesses an estate in England that would be considered vast, and she is very well-off at home. We are as prosperous as most of our neighbors, sir, but no better; all our splendor exists only in Mr. Gumbo's foolish imagination. He never rescued me from an Indian in his life and would flee at the sight of one, just as my poor brother's boy did on that fatal day when he fell."

"The bravest man acts thus at unfortunate times," said the Colonel. "I witnessed the finest troops in the world running at Preston before a ragged mob of Highland savages."

"That was because the Highlanders fought for a righteous cause, sir."

"Do you think," asked Harry's host, "that the French Indians held the righteous cause in the battle of last year?"

"The scoundrels! I would have the scalp of every murderous redskin among them!" cried Harry, clutching his fist. "They were robbing and invading British territories as well. Yet the Highlanders fought for their king."

"We, on our side, fought for our king; and we ultimately won the battle," said the Colonel, laughing.

"Ah!" cried Harry. "If His Royal Highness the Prince had not retreated at Derby, your king and mine would now be His Majesty King James the Third!"

"Who made such a Tory of you, Mr. Warrington?" asked Lambert.
