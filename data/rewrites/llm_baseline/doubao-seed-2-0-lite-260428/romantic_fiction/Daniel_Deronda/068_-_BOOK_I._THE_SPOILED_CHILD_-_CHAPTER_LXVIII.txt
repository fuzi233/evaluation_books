*“All thoughts, all passions, all delights, Whatever stirs this mortal frame, All are but ministers of Love, And feed his sacred flame.” —COLERIDGE*

Hans’s easy assurance that Mirah was quietly pining, unknowingly hurt by the unspoken space between her and Deronda, had lit a fire under the quiet, long-buried longing Deronda had carried for months. He set out for Ezra’s rooms that day resolved to be bold: no more hiding, no more lingering in limbo, he would ask for a moment alone with her, and lay his whole heart bare. If she returned his love, he knew he could face every obstacle ahead—least of all, the growing, unspoken threat of her father Lapidoth’s restlessness. As her betrothed, he would have the right, the gentle protective authority, to shield both Ezra and Mirah from whatever petty schemes or cruel humiliations the old man might dream up next.

Deronda would have been far more worried if he could see inside Lapidoth’s head that week. The quiet routine of the small flat, the soft restraints Ezra and Mirah had put on his old reckless habits, felt like a prison to him, only bearable because he was certain he’d soon find a way to weasel a large lump sum out of Deronda. His gambling itch burned under his skin every waking moment, weaving all his thoughts together into a web of schemes, and he’d been hunting for days to find where Mirah stashed her savings and her keys. But Mirah, sharp and practical, had kept her promise to Ezra perfectly: every penny beyond her immediate spending money was locked safe with Mrs. Meyrick, and Lapidoth was seething, trapped in the flat like a man in a carefully guarded asylum, given every comfort but no access to a single spare coin. He’d convinced himself taking any money from his own daughter, or from Deronda—who was practically family, after all—was no theft, no disgrace. The fact that no loose cash was within reach only made him hungrier, and he’d half-plotted to beg Deronda for a large sum to fund a “lucrative opportunity abroad,” claiming the whole family would be far happier without him hanging around. He was too intimidated by Deronda’s quiet, steady honor to ask outright, though, and kept putting the plan off day after day.

When Deronda walked in that afternoon, glowing bright with his secret, barely concealed joy, Lapidoth was at his wits’ end, and Deronda’s warm, easy friendliness only stoked his greedy schemes higher. He could barely pretend to pay attention to the men’s reading, so after a few minutes he slipped out to smoke and pace the square, leaving Deronda and Ezra to relax in their quiet work. Mirah was out, but Deronda knew she’d be back before he left, and every nerve in his body hummed with anticipation: he was sure when he saw her this time, he’d catch the same soft, hidden love in her face he’d only ever dared to dream of.

“This little room is far too hot for you, Ezra,” he said, pausing mid-read, fanning himself with his handkerchief. “The summer heat here is worse than Genoa’s, where you can sit in the shade of huge, cool rooms. I’m going to arrange for you to have a far nicer home now—you can’t argue, I’m the stronger half here.”

Ezra smiled back, quiet and soft. “I lack nothing but breath in here. You, who could be in a sprawling country estate with miles of green fields around you, must feel this like a tiny prison. But I will never ask you to leave.”

“Country would be banishment while you’re here,” Deronda laughed, strolling the short length of their double room, before he paused to tug off his tight cravat and the heavy, meaningful old ring he always wore, setting both down on the small side table behind Ezra, piled high with books and papers. “This is the happiest room in the whole world to me. Besides, I’m practicing for our eventual trip to the East—no stuffy cravats, no heavy rings allowed out there.”

They were soon absorbed again, Deronda reading a passage of rabbinical Hebrew while Ezra corrected him, their voices low, when Lapidoth slipped back inside and sat in the shadows at the back. His roving eyes landed immediately on the ring, glittering bright on the dark mahogany of the little table. All the half-formed schemes of asking Deronda for hundreds of pounds vanished for a second—here was a solid, easy sum, no begging required. He told himself it wasn’t theft, not really: Deronda was practically family, he’d have given Lapidoth far more than the ring’s worth if he’d asked. He told himself he’d just grab it, slip away, sell it, and be on a ship before anyone noticed, no arguments, no awkward pleas. Before he even had time to second-guess it, his fingers brushed the ring noiselessly, he slipped it into his pocket, grabbed his hat, and was out the front door, already hurrying toward the nearest pawnshop. Deronda and Ezra barely noticed he’d left.

Not long after, Mirah stepped through the door, her hat still pinned to her hair, her cheeks pink for no reason she could name, flustered by the bright, warm energy rolling off Deronda. “I only popped in to drop off Ezra’s new medicine,” she said, half flustered. “I need to head straight back to Mrs. Meyrick’s to fetch something.”

“Let me walk with you,” Deronda said urgently, standing to greet her. “The heat’s turned my brain to mush, I’m happy to get out a minute.”

Mirah nodded, blushing harder, that strange new softness in his eyes making her heart flutter as she poured out Ezra’s dose. Deronda stood for a second, caught up in thoughts of their walk, before he suddenly remembered he’d taken his cravat off. “Forgive my messy state,” he laughed, turning toward the side table. Then his voice broke, sharp with shock. “Good heavens—where has my ring gone?”

He dropped to his knees, patting the floor, shifting small pieces of furniture, searching every nook of the variegated carpet, certain it must have slipped off the table and rolled out of sight. But the color drained straight from Mirah’s face. She leaned in fast to whisper in Ezra’s ear. “Was my father in here just now?” Ezra nodded, his face falling, and they shared a single, terrible understanding.

Mirah darted out of the main room, checking his empty bedroom first, then the hook where his hat usually hung—bare. She stumbled back to the main doorway, hands clasped tight, knuckles white, staring out the window at the square, the hot burn of shame searing through her. Her own father had stolen from the man she adored, the man she’d secretly loved for years, and run off, leaving her to face this humiliation right in front of him.

But Deronda didn’t see shame. He stepped forward, gently wrapping both of his hands around her cold, clasped ones, his voice soft, reverent, like he was speaking a sacred truth. “Mirah, let us count him as our father, not just yours. There is no sorrow, no disgrace, no joy that I want to keep separate from you. I would rather take this grief, this burden of yours, for my own, than take the brightest, most perfect joy any other woman on earth could offer me. Don’t reject me—please. Let me share every part of your life. Marry me. I’ve hidden this love for so long, been too scared to speak. Let me prove to you, for the rest of our lives, that my love for you is whole and unshakable.”

Mirah didn’t leap straight to joy. At first she thought this was only his kindness, extended out of pity for her and Ezra. But then she looked up at his face, and saw the raw, glowing love there, unhidden at last, wrapping around her like sunlight. Warmth flooded her cheeks, and she couldn’t find the words to answer, no speech worthy of the gift he was giving her. She only leaned in, soft and trembling, and pressed a light, sweet kiss to his lips—her simple, perfect yes.

They stood there for long minutes, just holding hands, looking at each other, too full of quiet joy to move, until Mirah whispered, soft against the air between them: “Come. Let’s go in and tell Ezra. Let us comfort him, now that we have this good thing to share.”
