When Calder, balked of news and heavy with the disappointment of it, stepped into his carriage at Assouan, Lieutenant Sutch was far away on a Hampshire height-road as white as sifted flour. The afternoon lay clean and bright on the common: gorse flared like banked embers; heather spread a dull purple as though the earth wore mourning in its own homely fashion. A pony’s hooves made a measured, domestic music on the chalk—tap, scuff, tap—steady as a kitchen clock; but Sutch could find no comfort in that rhythm. Like many men who live much alone, he had taken to speaking to himself, as though sound itself might hold thought together.

“From the beginning I foresaw trouble,” he said, and again, because repeating was a kind of oath, “from the beginning.”

The ridge dipped abruptly. Below, a small railway station sat snug as a loaf under its red tiles. The rails shone in the sun with a cold, metallic insistence; telegraph poles marched away, a rigid line of black strokes against the sky, and the whole straightness of it—iron, wire, and regulation—seemed to press on Sutch’s nerves. He pulled up, staring down, frowning.

“I promised Harry to say nothing,” he muttered; and as if the words could be kneaded into something firmer than scruple, he worked them again: “Faithfully—in the Criterion grill-room.”

A whistle cut the air far off: shrill, thin, and modern, a noise like a blade. It jarred the afternoon’s softer acoustics—the wind’s hush through pine needles, the pony’s patient breathing. Sutch saw the white smoke of a train unspool in the distance like a ribbon torn from a parcel.

“I wonder what brings him,” he said, doubtful; and then, with the effort of a man forcing himself down a hard road, “No use shirking.”

He flicked the pony, drove down between dark pines. Their resinous scent—sharp as a cut branch—filled the hollow. The train drew in; steam sighed and settled. Only two passengers stepped down: Colonel Durrance and his servant. They came straight out to the road as though they had been expected there by fate itself.

Sutch hailed him. Durrance turned his blind face towards the voice, the lifted foot feeling for the step.

“You received my telegram in time?” Durrance asked.

“Luckily it found me at home.”

“I have brought a bag. May I trespass upon you for a night’s lodging?”

“By all means,” Sutch said; but the words, meant to be open-handed, came out with a reluctance Durrance could hear. Durrance had anticipated that; he had sent the telegram at the last moment for that very reason. The message had carried no explanation, but it was enough to make Sutch’s mind bristle. Something grave must have happened—something untoward between Ethne Eustace and Durrance. And with the suspicion came the old dread: Durrance would renew those questions about Harry Feversham which Sutch was sworn not to answer.

Then Durrance’s raised foot groped again, and blindness—no longer an abstract misfortune but a fact of flesh and bone—was thrust upon Sutch’s notice. He reached out, caught Durrance’s arm, and helped him up.

After all, he thought with a mean little wash of relief, it should not be hard to keep a blind man from the truth. Ethne herself, somewhere in Devonshire, had once found the same cruel comfort.

But pity rose in him too, unasked.

“I was very sorry, Durrance, to hear of your bad luck,” he said, as the trap climbed again out of the pine hollow. “I know something of being stopped and put aside just when the road seems to level—though my leg’s nothing beside your eyes. I won’t talk compensations. That’s the gabble of people who have their health and call it philosophy. We know better. For a man who’s young and active, and doing well in a career that needs activity—there are no compensations if it’s cut short through no fault of his.”

“Through no fault of his,” Durrance repeated, slowly. “I agree. It is only the man whose career is cut short through his own fault who gets compensations.”

Sutch glanced at him sharply, as if a blind man could not feel a look. The sentence was too apt. Was it chance—or knowledge?

“Compensations of what kind?” Sutch asked, uneasily.

“The chance of knowing himself, for one,” Durrance said. “The chief thing. He is brought up short, perhaps disgraced.” The word fell like a dropped utensil in a silent room; Sutch started. “Yes—disgraced. But the shock is his opportunity. Up to then his life has been sham and illusion. The man he believed himself—he never was. Now he knows. And once he knows, he can set himself to retrieve. There are compensations for such a man. You and I know a case in point.”

Sutch felt the trap’s wood tremble faintly under the pony’s pull. So it was Harry. Durrance had some knowledge—how gained, Sutch could not guess—and, worse, the shape of it seemed to do Harry an injustice. Sutch’s regard for Feversham had hardened with years into something fierce and maternal; he could not bear to hear the memory bruised.

“The case isn’t quite in point,” he argued. “You mean Harry Feversham.”

“Who believed himself a coward—and was not,” Durrance replied. “He commits the fault that stops his career, he finds his mistake, he sets himself to retrieve his disgrace. It’s in point enough.”

“I see,” Sutch said slowly. “There’s another view—a wrong one, as I know—but for a moment I thought you held it: that Harry fancied himself brave and found out he was a coward. How did you find out? No one knew the whole truth except myself.”

“I am engaged to Miss Eustace,” Durrance said.

“She didn’t know everything. She knew of the disgrace—but not the determination to retrieve it.”

“She knows now,” Durrance returned; and his voice sharpened, as though he heard a change of breath. “You are glad of that—very glad.”

Sutch, startled, wondered how he had betrayed anything at all. Yet he did not deny it.

“It’s true,” he said stoutly. “I am glad. From your point of view it might be better if she didn’t know. But I can’t help it. I am glad.”

Durrance laughed—briefly, not unkindly. “I like you the better for being glad.”

“But how does Miss Eustace know?” Sutch pressed. “Who told her? I didn’t—and there’s no one else could.”

“You are wrong. There is Captain Willoughby. He came to Devonshire six weeks ago. He brought with him a white feather, which he gave Miss Eustace, to prove he withdrew his charge.”

Sutch drew up in the road as if the pony had hit a sudden bank of wind. He sat quite still, and for a moment Durrance might have thought him stunned. The truth was simpler and more astonishing: joy—real joy, the kind that makes an old man feel his blood move like it did when he was twenty—rose in him and held him speechless.

Five years ago, on Dover pier, he had watched a mail packet steam away into rain and darkness, and had prayed—prayed like a man bargaining with God—that he might live to see Harry’s name cleared. Since the night outside the War Office in Pall Mall, when Feversham had touched his arm and spoken out his despair, Sutch had carried a weight that was not wholly Feversham’s disgrace but his own failure. Harry was Muriel Feversham’s boy. Muriel was dead. It had seemed to Sutch that her eyes watched him from beyond, not accusing loudly, but reproaching gently: Since I was taken away where I could only see and not help, surely you might have helped—just for my sake—your work being ended. And the long account of his quiet, inactive years had risen nightly to condemn him.

Now the guilt lifted, not by Sutch’s word, but by Harry’s act. The air itself seemed to brighten. The world, which had been grey with worry, took colour again as though sunlight had been poured over it like wine.

“I knew it,” he burst out. “I knew he wouldn’t fail. Oh, I’m glad you came to-day, Colonel Durrance. It was partly my fault, you see, that Harry ever incurred that charge. I could have spoken—there was a chance at Broad Place, one of those Crimean nights—a word might have helped—and I held my tongue. I’ve never ceased blaming myself. You have the particulars? Willoughby was in peril—and Harry came to his aid?”

“No,” Durrance said. “Not that exactly.”

“Tell me. Tell me.”

Durrance told him of the Gordon letters, and how Feversham had recovered them. To Sutch it was too short, too bare, like a story of a banquet related by a man who had eaten nothing but remembered the plates.

“Oh, but I’m glad you came,” he cried again, and meant it.

“You understand, at all events,” Durrance said, “that I have not come to repeat to you the questions I asked in the courtyard of my club. On the contrary, I can give you information.”

Sutch drove on, up into the clearer air. Durrance’s certainty about Sutch’s fears made him uneasy—yet the new gladness would not let small perplexities lodge.

“So Miss Eustace told you, then,” Sutch said, “and showed you the feather?”

“No, indeed,” Durrance answered. “She said not a word. She never showed me. She even forbade Willoughby to hint of it. She sent him away from Devonshire before I knew he had come.” Then, quick as a knife finding its mark: “You are disappointed at that.”

Sutch stiffened. It was true. He was jealous on Harry’s behalf; he wanted Harry first in her thoughts. Harry had taken up his dreadful work for her sake. Sutch wanted her to remember as fiercely as he did. He had hoped she would go to Durrance at once, break the engagement, set Harry’s image back in its rightful place. It would be hard on Durrance—but love is capable of a perfect cruelty.

“Then how did you learn it?” he asked.

“Some one else told me that Willoughby had come and brought a white feather, and that Ethne had taken it. Never mind who. That gave me a clue. I lay in wait for Willoughby in London. He is not clever; he tried to obey Ethne’s command of silence, but I managed to extract what I wanted. The rest I put together. Ethne now and then was off her guard.” He laughed. “You are surprised that I was clever enough to find out truth with my own wits?”

Sutch jumped in his seat; again that uncanny accuracy. “I have said nothing,” he returned testily, “to suggest I was surprised.”

“Quite true,” Durrance said, “and yet you are surprised. You could not know that it is only since I have been blind that I have begun to see. Shall I give you an instance? This is the first time I have come into this neighbourhood or got out at your station. Yet I can tell you you drove me up a hill between forests of pines, and now across open country of heather.”

Sutch turned quickly. “The hill—yes. But the pines?”

“The air was close. Trees. I guessed pines.”

“And the open country?”

“The wind blows clear across it. There is a dry stiff rustle besides. I have never heard that sound except when wind goes over heather.”

For a moment Sutch listened—not merely to Durrance’s words, but to the sound-world those words described: resin-thick hush, then the heather’s crackling whisper. It made him think—strangely—how a man’s health of mind might depend on the honest voices of a place, and how the shriek of engines and the hard straight lines of telegraph poles were an invasion, a new language laid over an old one.

Durrance turned back to Feversham. He spoke of Harry’s disappearance and its cause. He made no mention—Sutch noted it—of the fourth white feather Ethne herself had snapped from her fan. But he knew the history of the three feathers that had come by post to Ramelton down to the last detail.

“I was acquainted with the men who sent them,” he said. “Trench, Castleton, Willoughby. I met them daily in Suakin—ordinary officers. One rather shrewd, one commonplace, one distinctly stupid. I watched them go quietly about their routine. It seems strange now. There should have been a mark on them, setting them apart as messengers of fate. But there was nothing. They were prosaic, like everybody. And yet—by a thoughtless act, without a spoken word—they dealt out misery, estrangement, years of suffering, and went about their business as though they’d only ordered dinner. Then much later a consequence of what they did—perhaps forgotten—rises up and strikes you down.”

“Yes,” Sutch said, and meant it. He wondered again what object had brought Durrance into Hampshire if not those questions; but Durrance did not yet say.

They came at last to Sutch’s house, standing alone by the roadside and looking over a wide sweep of downs. Within its walls life had the careful quiet of a man long solitary: doors that shut without clamour, clocks that ticked, a fire laid ready though unlit, and windows that admitted the countryside’s steady breathing. Sutch took Durrance over the stable, described his horses; he explained the garden with the pride of one who has learned to husband small things—where the beds lay, how the paths turned, what flowers came earliest, and which scents were strongest at dusk. Durrance listened with real interest. In that small domestic inventory Sutch heard the long practice of surviving—how to keep the days from turning into an unbroken grey.

“You don’t live within sight of the sea,” Durrance said at last, as they stood at the door.

“No,” Sutch answered quickly. “I dare not.”

Durrance nodded, understanding without sight. “You care for it too much. You would have the full knowledge of your loss presented to you each moment.”

They went in. They dined together and sat over their wine. The room held the soft, civil sounds of a private house: the clink of glass, the servant’s discreet tread, the sigh of air at the windows. Outside, the day’s louder noises had thinned; the countryside spoke more plainly. Still Durrance did not name the purpose of his visit, and at last Sutch, with an awkwardness born of thinking of Ethne and of Harry together, forced his thought into speech.

“Harry Feversham must come back to England,” he said. “He has done enough to redeem his honour.”

Durrance answered at once. “I was waiting for you to say it. I wanted you to realise, without any suggestion of mine, that he must return. That is why I came.”

Sutch’s relief was so great he blurted out another sentence with clumsy haste.

“His return will not really trouble you or your wife—since Miss Eustace has forgotten him.”

Durrance shook his head. “She has not forgotten him.”

“But she kept silence—after Willoughby brought the feather back. You told me so. She said nothing to you. She forbade Willoughby to tell you.”

“She is true,” Durrance said. “Loyal. She has pledged herself to me, and nothing—no promise of happiness, no thought of Harry—would induce her to break her pledge. I know her. But I know, too, she only plighted herself to me out of pity, because I was blind. And I know she has not forgotten Harry.”

Sutch leaned back and smiled; he could have laughed outright. He asked for no proofs. Pride flooded him—pride in Harry, his favourite, his charge, still held in that girl’s heart after disgrace and absence. In the same instant he endowed Ethne with every virtue imaginable, because her constancy magnified Harry’s worth. In his mind, Durrance would have to step aside—of course—but then Durrance should never have come between Harry and Ethne in the first place. Love made him merciless.

“You are very glad of that,” Durrance said quietly. “Very glad Ethne has not forgotten him. It is a little hard on me, perhaps—who have not much left. It would have been less hard if, two years ago, you had told me the truth when I asked it of you that summer evening in the courtyard of the club.”

Compunction caught Sutch by the throat. The gentleness of Durrance’s voice, and a tiredness in it, made Sutch ashamed of the sharp, bright joy he had just been nursing.

“It was impossible,” he exclaimed. “I promised Harry—solemnly—that I would tell no one until he came back himself. I was sorely tempted. But I had given my word. Even if he never came back—even if I knew for certain he was dead—I was only to tell his father, and even his father not all that could be told on his behalf.”

He pushed back his chair and went to the window. “It is hot in here,” he said, and raised the sash without waiting. Cooler air moved in, carrying the faint night-smell of earth and leaf. For a little he stood there, silent, balancing pity against loyalty.

Durrance did not know of Ethne’s fourth feather. He had not heard Harry’s words in the Criterion. There were certain sentences, spoken then, that it seemed only fair Durrance should hear now. Silence had already done harm. Sutch turned from the window, mastered his love, and went back to the table.

“There is something it is fair you should know,” he said. “When Harry went away to redeem his honour, he had no hope—indeed no wish—that Miss Eustace should wait for him. She was the spur to urge him, but she did not even know that. He did not wish her to know. He had no claim upon her. There was not in his mind a hope that she might one day be his friend—in this life, at all events. When he went away from Ramelton he parted from her, as he thought, for all his mortal life. It is fair you should know.” Sutch’s voice tightened, but he went on. “You tell me Miss Eustace is not the woman to withdraw from a pledged word. Well—what I said to you that evening at the club I say again: there will be no disloyalty to friendship if you marry her.”

It cost him; he was glad when it was done. Still he waited, uncertain what answer would come.

“It is kind of you to tell me,” Durrance said, and smiled with genuine friendliness. “I can guess what it cost you. But you have done Harry Feversham no harm by speaking. For Ethne has not forgotten him; and I have my point of view. Marriage between a man blind like myself and any woman—let alone Ethne—could not be fair or right unless on both sides there was more than friendship.” He paused, and his next words were as practical as orders given on a march. “Harry must return to England. He must return to Ethne, too. You must go to Egypt and do what you can to bring him back.”

Sutch’s suspense broke cleanly. Conscience was satisfied; Harry was not betrayed.

“I will start to-morrow,” he said. “Harry is still in the Soudan?”

“Of course.”

“Why of course?” Sutch demanded. “Willoughby withdrew his accusation; Castleton is dead—killed at Tamai; and Trench—I know, for I have followed their careers—Trench is a prisoner in Omdurman.”

“So is Harry Feversham.”

For a moment Sutch did not understand. The statement was too abrupt, too large; it did not fit the bright relief he had been holding. Then comprehension came, and with it a refusal—no, it cannot be—and then the knowledge that refusal was folly. He sat down opposite to Durrance as though the chair had been pulled from under him and placed again.

The room fell silent for a long time. Through the open sash came the countryside’s night-voices: a wind’s low passage over grass, some small creature’s stir, the far-off, softened mutter of the world. Compared with those living, ancient sounds, the railway’s shriek and the telegraph’s rigid line seemed like a foreign tongue trying to overwrite the old dialect of the land.

“What am I to do?” Sutch asked at last.

“I have thought it out,” Durrance replied. “You must go to Suakin. I will give you a letter to Willoughby—he is Deputy-Governor—and another to a Greek merchant there whom I know, and on whom you can draw for as much money as you require.”

“That’s good of you, Durrance—upon my word,” Sutch broke in; and forgetting, as men forget in strong feeling, that Durrance could not see, he held out his hand across the table. “I wouldn’t take a penny if I could help it—but I am a poor man. Upon my soul, it’s good of you.”
