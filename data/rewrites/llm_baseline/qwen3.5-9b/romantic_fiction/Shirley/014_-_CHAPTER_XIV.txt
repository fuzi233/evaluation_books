As soon as Shirley woke, her mind was already racing toward a grim conclusion. "Of course, I know he will marry Shirley," she whispered to the empty air. "And he ought to. She can help him." She spoke the words with conviction, but immediately, a cruel shadow fell across her resolve. "But I shall be forgotten the moment they are wed," she thought, the realization stinging like a physical blow. "Wholly forgotten! And when Robert is completely gone from my life... where will I turn? *My* Robert! I wish I could claim him as my own. But I am poverty and incapacity; Shirley is wealth and power. She possesses beauty and love—things I lack. I cannot deny it. This isn't a sordid affair; she loves him—or will love him—with a depth that makes him proud. There is no valid reason to stop it. Let them marry. But afterward? I will be nothing to him. To pretend to be his sister, to play the role of the calm friend while my soul is torn apart by unrequited passion—that is degradation I cannot accept. I will offer myself to a man like Robert as all or nothing. No feigning, no false pretenses. Once that union is sealed, I will leave them. I cannot stand between them as a friend, nor trample them as an enemy. Robert is a fine man in my eyes. I have loved, I do love, and I must love him. Since I cannot be his wife, I must go where I shall never see him again. There is only one path: cling to him until I am consumed, or sever the bond entirely. Sunder me, Providence. Separate us swiftly."

These dark thoughts haunted her throughout the afternoon. Late in the day, as she sat lost in reverie, the figure of Miss Keeldar drifted past the parlor window. The schoolmistress moved with a slow, languid grace, her face wearing that familiar blend of wistfulness and careless indifference that defined her usual demeanor. Yet, when stirred, that carelessness vanished, replaced by a warm, genial gaiety that seasoned her laughter and glances with a unique tenderness. Her mirth never sounded harsh; it lacked the sharp crackle of dry thorns under a pot.

When she entered the room, she greeted Caroline sharply. "What do you mean by not keeping your promise to visit this afternoon?"

"I simply wasn't in the mood," Miss Helstone replied honestly.

Shirley, who had been watching her intently, narrowed her eyes. "No," she said, her tone cutting through the air. "I see you aren't in the mood for loving me. You're in one of your sunless, gloomy spells where a companion's presence feels unwelcome. You have these moods. Are you aware of them?"

"Do you intend to stay long, Shirley?"

"Yes. I've come for tea, and I must finish it before I go. Therefore, I shall remove my bonnet without asking permission."

She did exactly that, standing on the rug with her hands clasped behind her back.

"You have a striking expression today," Shirley continued, studying Caroline with a gaze that was keen but not hostile—perhaps even tinged with pity. "You look wonderfully self-contained, you solitary, wounded deer. Are you afraid I'll worry if I discover you are hurt and bleeding?"

"I never fear Shirley."

"But sometimes you dislike her; often you avoid her. Shirley knows when she is slighted. If you hadn't walked home with that company last night, you'd be a different woman today. What time did you arrive at the rectory?"

"By ten."

"Hmph! Three-quarters of an hour to walk a mile. Was it you, or Moore, who dawdled?"

"Shirley, you're talking nonsense."

"*He* talked nonsense—I doubt that; or he looked it, which is infinitely worse. I see the reflection of his eyes on your forehead right now. I'm tempted to call him out, if only I had a trustworthy ally. I feel desperately irritated. I felt it last night, and I've carried it all day."

"Why don't you ask me why?" Shirley pressed, after a pause, addressing Caroline as a "little silent, over-modest creature." "You don't deserve for me to spill my secrets into your lap without an invitation. Honestly, I could have found it in my heart to follow Moore yesterday evening with deadly intent. I have pistols, and I know how to use them."

"Nonsense, Shirley! Who would you have shot—me or Robert?"

"Perhaps neither. Maybe myself. More likely a bat or a tree branch. He is a puppy, your cousin—a quiet, serious, sensible, ambitious puppy. I see him standing before me, delivering his half-stern, half-gentle speeches, bearing me down with his sheer determination. And then... I have no patience with him!"

Miss Keeldar paced rapidly across the room, muttering energetically about her lack of patience with men in general, and her tenant in particular.

"You are mistaken," Caroline urged, showing signs of anxiety. "Robert is no puppy or male flirt; I can vouch for that."

"*You* vouch for it! Do you think I'll accept your word on this? There is no testimony I would trust more readily than yours. You would cut off your right hand to advance Moore's fortune."

"But I wouldn't lie. And if I speak the truth, I must assure you he was merely civil to me last night—that was all."

"I never asked what he *was*. I can guess. I saw him from the window take your hand in his long fingers just as he exited my gate."

"That matters nothing. I am not a stranger; I am an old acquaintance and his cousin."

"I feel indignant, and that sums it up," Shirley declared. "All my comfort has been shattered by his maneuvers. He keeps inserting himself between you and me. Without him, we would be close friends; but that six feet of puppyhood causes a perpetual eclipse of our friendship. He constantly crosses and obscures the view I desire to remain clear, turning me into a mere nuisance in your eyes."

"No, Shirley, no."

"He does. You didn't want my company this afternoon, and I find it hard to accept. You are naturally reserved, but I am a social creature who cannot exist in isolation. If we were left undisturbed, I hold such regard for you that I could bear your presence forever. Not for a single second do I wish to be rid of you. You cannot say the same of me."

"Shirley, I can say anything you wish. Shirley, I like you."

"You will wish me at Jericho tomorrow, Lina."

"I shall not. Every day I grow more accustomed to you, fonder of you. You know I am too English to forge a vehement friendship instantly; but you are so superior to ordinary young ladies, so distinct from everyday women, that I esteem and value you. You are never a burden to me—not even for a moment. Do you believe me?"

"Partly," Shirley replied, smiling with a touch of skepticism. "But you are a peculiar personage. Though you look quiet, there is a force and depth within you that is hard to reach or appreciate. Besides, you certainly are not happy."

"And unhappy people are rarely good. Is that what you mean?"

"Not at all. I mean that unhappy people are often preoccupied, lacking the mood to converse with companions of my nature. Moreover, there is a specific type of unhappiness that doesn't just depress—it corrodes. And I fear that is your lot. Will pity help you, Lina? If it will, take some from Shirley; she offers it generously, and guarantees its authenticity."

"Shirley, I never had a sister—you never had one either—but it strikes me now how sisters feel toward each other. Affection woven into their lives, unshakable by emotional shocks, trampled only briefly by petty quarrels so it may spring fresher when the pressure lifts. An affection that no passion can ultimately surpass, competing with love itself in force and truth. Love hurts us so, Shirley. It is tormenting, racking, burning away our strength with its flame. In affection, there is no pain, no fire—only sustenance and balm. I am supported and soothed when you are near. That is, *you only*, Shirley. Do you believe me now?"

"I am always easy to convince when the creed pleases me. So, we are friends, then, Lina, despite the black eclipse?"

"We really are," the other replied, pulling Shirley down beside her, "whatever chance may bring."

"Then, let us talk of something else than the Troubler." Just then, the rector entered, and the topic Shirley intended to discuss was postponed until her departure.
