George Warrington did not suffer his law to starve his body, nor his body to dull his mind. The gown of a student sat upon his shoulders; but his mother’s letters sat nearer his conscience, and they were stitched with that old Esmond pride which holds a man upright as a stays-bone holds a coat.

“You have a name,” Madam Esmond wrote, “and a very ancient one. You are not bound to grind like a clerk with ink in his pores. Study as a gentleman.”

So he obeyed, and contrived to give Themis her due without giving her his marrow. He went to hall with the crowd of unknown young men and ate the same commons—mutton that had marched too long in the pot, bread whose crust remembered yesterday’s oven, ale that smelt of the cask’s damp staves—yet he took care, when he could, to refresh himself elsewhere, as a man will turn from thin broth to a stew that has been tended. The law even then was a jealous mistress; she liked her votaries pale and sleepless. But a century ago she had not yet learned the modern tyranny of denying a man his dinner. She allowed more coffee-house air, more bowls of punch, more nights wherein talk and tobacco could make their own kind of study.

George, who loved letters as other men love hunting, found the black-letter of Chaucer more savoury than the crabbed Gothic of Hale and Coke. In those days, too, books were not mere ornaments for a shelf: the ancients were still used like spices in conversation. A gentleman might season an argument with Virgil as readily as he seasoned his soup with mace. In Parliament and in courts they called Statius, Juvenal, and the rest to witness, as if the Muses were still living neighbours. Their reign has waned in our time; yet in George’s London they were not yet wholly turned out of doors. A man might still ask, over a dish of oysters and a bottle, whether Melpomene’s name ran in three syllables or four, and feel himself the wiser for it.

It had been told already how, in the long leisure of his youth—at home, and in that harsher leisure of captivity in a French fort by Monongahela—George had courted the tragic Muse. He had done it as most young men do: with immodest hopes. No youth aims first at an epigram. He must storm Olympus. It is only later, when the blood has learned its limits, that he will content himself with translating Horace into decent measures, like a man who, after once dreaming of feeding an army, is proud to keep a household well.

So, when he came to London, the theatre drew him as kitchens draw a hungry traveller: not merely for the eating, but for the heat, the talk, the bustle, the noises of utensils and voices, the very smell of it all. He attended performances at both houses; he haunted the theatrical coffee-houses; he listened to the critics as a young cook listens to old hands arguing over a sauce.

At the Bedford between plays, at the Cecil after them, he might be seen among the wits and actors. The tough old Macklin was there, biting like a peppercorn. Foote flitted and snapped, frolicsome as a fish in a pan. Hippisley laughed. Garrick—small of stature, large of spirit—came and went with that quick, charged presence which made even his silences audible. Around them clinked glasses, scraped chairs, rattled dice-boxes; punch was compounded with solemn care—lemons cut, sugar crushed, rum measured, water and spice married till the steam rose scented above the table; and the whole room had its own acoustic weather: the thud of tankards, the crackle of wit, the low drone of argument, now and then split by a burst of laughter like a sudden gull-cry over a river.

George’s modesty, his ready talk, and—no doubt—his reputation for money, made him welcome. Actors would drink a bowl with him; critics would sup at his expense with a hearty civility. He delighted, too, in the sense of tasting something half forbidden; for his mother approved plays but by halves, and he had the relish which comes when a dish is spiced with disobedience. He gave more than one handsome entertainment to players, and it was whispered that one or two celebrated geniuses had condescended to borrow from him—genius, like fire, consuming whatever fuel lies nearest.

All this while he was polishing his tragedy—his masterpiece, as he called it in the honest vanity of youth. He sought advice, and received the sort of applause that is easily given when negus is warm and the company benevolent. Mr. Spencer of the Temple—new acquaintance and already friendly—gave a breakfast at his chambers in Fig Tree Court, and invited a handful of gentlemen to hear Mr. Warrington read.

Breakfast in those chambers had its own proper ritual. The air carried the smell of toasted bread, and the slight bitterness of tea newly scalded; there was butter that had not been too long out of the cool, and marmalade whose orange peel still held the sharpness of the fruit. The cups chimed upon saucers; a spoon stirred and rang; a fire hissed faintly; outside, the Temple had its customary murmur—footsteps on stone, distant coach-wheels, the river’s presence felt more than heard. Against that domestic soundscape George set his louder world of convent-bells, war-horns, and slaughter.

Even Dr. Johnson was there—big, brown-coated, near-sighted, formidable; a man who could swallow tea and opinions with equal heat. He was good enough to say the piece showed talent. It offended the unities—certainly; but others had offended them, and Mr. Warrington might sin in company. There was something, Johnson said, that reminded him of Coriolanus and Othello.

“And two very good things too, sir!” George pleaded, eager as any cook whose dish is accused of tasting of a great predecessor’s.

“Well, well,” Johnson returned, not unkindly; “your catastrophe is terrible, just, and being in part true, is not the less awful.”

Then George began in earnest, and the first act opened before the breakfast-table.

His hero, Gaspar Carpezan, appears before a convent on the Rhine, which the Lutherans besiege. There is an armistice. The convent’s bell strikes noon—ding, ding, dong—its sound written so plainly in the author’s voice that the gentlemen at table might have sworn they heard iron on iron. No succours arrive. The gates open. In comes Carpezan, clad in full steel, with the great hammer of battle over his shoulder, trumpets blowing, flags displayed.

“No rudeness, my men!” cries Carpezan. “The wine is yours, and the larder and cellar are good. The church plate shall be melted. Any of the garrison who will take service with me shall have good pay. No insult to the religious ladies! I have promised safe-conduct; and he who lays a finger on them—hangs! Mind that, Provost Marshal!”

The Provost Marshal, a huge fellow in a red doublet, nods like an ox.

“We shall see more of that executioner,” Mr. Spencer murmured, leaning over his cup.

“A very agreeable acquaintance, I am sure,” said Johnson, wagging his head; “I shall be delighted to meet him again. This scene of mercenaries and camp-followers is novel and stirring, Mr. Warrington. The Colonel has gone into the convent? Let us hear what he does there.”

The Abbess appears—high-born, violent, sharp-tongued, with old ladies at her side. She has heard of Carpezan’s former violences: that hammer has smashed many images; pounds of plate have been melted down by the sacrilegious plunderer. She detests him, and tells him so with a fury of words that, for a moment, shakes even the conqueror. George read this scene with relish, letting the Abbess’s contempt crack like a whip.

But Carpezan recovers his ground. “Pray, madam,” he says, “how many ladies are there in your convent, for whom my people shall provide conveyance?”

The Abbess, troubled and angry, answers, “Besides myself, the noble sisters of Saint Mary’s House are twenty—twenty-three.”

She had been going to say twenty-four. That hesitation was a small sound in the room, but it rang like a dropped knife.

“Ha! why this stop?” asks Captain Ulric, one of Carpezan’s gayest officers, a man whose levity seems made to mock the solemn walls.

Carpezan draws a letter. “I require from you,” he says sternly, “the body of the noble lady Sybilla of Hoya. Her brother was my favourite captain, slain by my side in the Milanese. By his death she becomes heiress of his lands. A greedy uncle, they say, brought her hither and immured her. The damsel shall herself pronounce her fate—whether to remain a cloistered sister, or to return to home and liberty as Baroness of—”

The Abbess denies it. “There is no Lady Sybil in this house. The Sister Agnes was a nun professed. What was her wealth reverts to the Order.”

“Give me straightway the body of the Lady Sybil of Hoya!” roars Carpezan. “If not, I make a signal to my Reiters and give you and your convent up to war.”

Captain Ulric, peering under veils with ribald curiosity, begins some jest about choosing a plump smiling maid—like—like—when the Abbess cuts him down with a cry of “Silence!” and declares, with haughty composure, that Sister Agnes died three days ago and was buried.

Carpezan is struck as if the Abbess had hit him. The chaplain—ghastly, trembling—confirms it.

But Carpezan has in his armour a letter from Sister Agnes herself, saying she was to be buried alive in an oubliette—kept on water and bread, or left to starve. Rage takes him. He seizes the Abbess. Ulric throttles the chaplain. Carpezan blows his horn; the sound, as George read it, seemed to widen the chambers. In rush the Lanzknechts. Crash, bang! They batter doors and walls. Flames, screams, slaughter. And then Carpezan himself staggers in, bearing a fainting woman on his shoulder—Sybilla.

A little sister with red lips—one of the veiled attendants—has shown them the way to the dungeon. Betrayal comes, in this first act, from within the convent’s own kitchen of secrets.

“The convent is suppressed with a vengeance,” George said, lifting his eyes from the page. “We end our first act with the burning of the place, the roars of the soldiery, and the outcries of the nuns. They had best go change their dresses, for they will be court ladies in the next act.”

This pleased the company; they began at once to cast the play as if it already stood behind a curtain. Mrs. Pritchard would not like to be Abbess and vanish after the first act. Miss Pritchard might make a pretty Sybilla; Miss Gates the attendant nun. Garrick scarce tall enough for Carpezan—though when excited he always seemed a grenadier. Woodward would make a good Ulric. The breakfast-table became a little stage, with names placed as neatly as dishes.

Act the second: Carpezan has married Sybilla. He has enriched himself in war, is ennobled, and lives on the Danube in splendour. George’s voice slowed here; the noise of battle withdrew, and the household sounds of a great house replaced it—doors, servants, the clink of plate, the rustle of silks. Yet for all the state, the Baron is not happy. His rough soldier-manners chafe against his high-born bride. She taunts him with his low birth, his vulgar companions, whom he still loves to see about him, old comrades with hands scarred by pike and rope. There are moments, George read, when the man wishes he had never fished this lovely quarrelsome vixen from her dungeon. The act is chiefly domestic war—words like blows—until horns blow and news comes that the young King of Bohemia and Hungary is hunting that way.

Act the third shifts to Prague. The King invites Carpezan and his wife, promising preferment: from Baron to Count, from Colonel to General-in-Chief. Sybilla shines at court, the most brilliant of ladies. The King’s admiration is no longer hidden. Even in George’s reading, one could hear, beneath the words, the altered soundscape of Prague—music, laughter, the careful hush around power, the thin applause which is a kind of currency.

Johnson stirred. “Oh, stay—I have it—I know your story now,” he said. “’Tis in Meteranus, in the Theatrum Universum. I read it in Oxford as a boy—Carpezanus—Carpzoff—”

“That is the fourth act,” George answered, and went on.

In the fourth act the King’s attentions to Sybilla grow marked. Carpezan struggles against jealousy, refusing long to yield to it, until his wife’s guilt is put beyond doubt. Then comes the true, dreadful matter: the executioner who follows Carpezan’s regiment is commanded to slay Sybilla in her own palace. George read that closing scene with a cold steadiness that made the air of the chambers feel thinner: a side-room, moonlight through an oriel window, the hush after violence. And under the window the King comes with his lute and plays the song that had been their signal—an air heard earlier in festivity, now returned like a taste remembered at the worst moment, sweet and sickening together.

The song—written in an ancient manner, and repeated in the piece—Johnson, still awake then, pronounced a happy imitation of Waller; and he allowed that its gay repetition at the moment of guilt and murder deepened the gloom.

“But what comes afterwards?” Johnson asked. “In the Theatrum, Carpezan is taken into favour again by Count Mansfield, and doubtless murders other folks on the reformed side.”

George had departed from the printed history. His fifth act returned to the grand sweep of armies and fate. King Louis—terror-stricken by the bloody end of his intrigue—receives word that Solyman invades Hungary. Two noblemen tell how, in council, the injured Carpezan burst in, broke his sword, flung it at the King’s feet, and threw down a glove—daring him to wear it, swearing he would one day claim it. Then Carpezan fled, and rumor says he has joined the Turk, assumed the turban, and now stands in the Sultan’s camp, whose white tents gleam across the river.

The King prepares for battle at Mohacz. He keeps with him an aged, faithful knight, Master of the Horse, and confesses repentance, esteem for his injured Queen, and resolve to meet the day as a man.

“What is this field called?” the King asks.

“Mohacz, my liege,” says the old warrior, with a brave prophecy that before sunset Mohacz shall see a battle nobly won.

Trumpets and alarms sound—cymbals, barbaric music, the cry of Janissaries. The stage shifts to the Turkish camp. There walks the Grand Vizier, conqueror of Rhodes, dark and stern. And there too is Carpezan, in Eastern habit, with the glove in his cap like a poison flower worn for remembrance. He advises the Turks where Louis is weakest; how to meet the Hungarian cavalry’s tremendous shock and draw them toward a morass. He begs command in front, near the place where the King will engage.

“’Tis well,” says the Vizier. “Our invincible Emperor surveys the battle from yonder tower. At day’s end he will know how to reward your valour.”

Signal-guns. Trumpets. Captains retire, vowing death to the infidel and fidelity to the Sultan. The fight begins: Christians and Turks clash and skirmish; troops advance and retreat; alarms continue. George’s voice quickened; he made a sort of percussion with his consonants, as if beating a drum. Carpezan rages over the field, hammer smashing, calling for King Louis. He is about to kill a warrior who faces him when he recognises Ulric, his former captain. For a moment the renegade softens; memory breaks through like the smell of old bread in a new kitchen.

“Fly,” Carpezan bids him. “Think of Carpezan.”

Ulric begs him to return; it is impossible. They fight. Ulric will have it, and goes down under the hammer. Carpezan bends, melted at seeing his young friend wounded—when King Louis appears, plumes torn, sword hacked, shield battered with a thousand blows received and given. The guilty monarch would turn away. Carpezan is on him.

“An equal fight!” he roars. “A traitor against a traitor! Stand, King Louis! False King, false knight, false friend—by this glove in my helmet, I challenge you!”

He tears the glove out and flings it at the King. They set to. The monarch falls under the terrible arm of the man he injured, dying with a few broken words of repentance. Carpezan leans on his murderous mace and speaks a heartbroken soliloquy over the corpse.

Turkish warriors gather. The day is theirs. The Grand Vizier stands with Janissaries, whose bows and swords are tired of drinking death. He surveys the renegade.

“Christian renegade,” says the Vizier, “Allah has given us a great victory. The Christian King is slain by you.”

“Peace to his soul!” gasps Ulric, dying on the field. “He died like a good knight.”

“In this day’s battle,” the Vizier continues, “no man hath comported himself more bravely than you. You are made Bassa of Transylvania. Advance bowmen—Fire!”

At that word Mr. Fountain, even while George read, pricked up his head as if he had heard a cook say “roast” when he meant “boil.” But the scene rolled on. An arrow quivers in Carpezan’s breast.

“Bassa of Transylvania,” says the Vizier, “you were a traitor to your King, who lies murdered by your hand. You contributed more than any soldier to this day’s victory. ’Tis thus my sublime Emperor meetly rewards you. Sound trumpets! We march for Vienna to-night!”

The curtain falls in George’s manuscript as Carpezan crawls to Ulric, kisses his hands, and gasps, “Forgive me, Ulric!”

When George ended, he turned to Johnson, his face more pale than when he began. “What say you, sir?” he asked modestly. “Is there any chance for me?”

But the greatest critic in the room was scarce to be had; Johnson had been asleep for some time, and now frankly owned he had lost the latter part of the play.

The little auditory hummed and stirred, relieved as men are when a storm has passed. Yet they allowed—truly enough—that George had read the last acts uncommonly well, and they paid him compliments on matter and manner; perhaps, too, they were in good humour because the business had come to an end. Mr. Spencer’s servant handed about negus—wine warmed, lemon and spice rising in a fragrant steam. The Templars sipped, and began their grave talk, as if they were judges at a tasting, pronouncing on what should be altered for a finer flavour.

Mr. Fountain objected that the Vizier should not say “Fire!” when he bids archers kill Carpezan, since one does not fire a bow. A note was taken, as carefully as a receipt is written down.

Mr. Figtree, sentimental, regretted that Ulric could not be saved and married to a comic heroine.

“Nay, sir,” said Johnson, waking to stern historical appetite, “there was utter annihilation of the Hungarian army at Mohacz, and Ulric must take his knock with the rest. He could only be saved by flight, and you would not have a hero run away. Pronounce sentence of death against Captain Ulric, but kill him with the honours of war.”

Messrs. Essex and Tanfield, meanwhile, wondered to each other who this queer-looking pert was—this man who contradicted everybody between sips—and proposed, after the fatigues of tragedy, a boat up the river and a little fresh air, as if to wash their ears clean of battle-cries and bring them back to the kinder sounds of oars in water and wind among trees.
