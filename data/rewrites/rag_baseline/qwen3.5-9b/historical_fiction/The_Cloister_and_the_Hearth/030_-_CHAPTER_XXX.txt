Gerard's path led him into the quiet orbit of a monk whose hands spoke a language older than words. This brother, a craftsman of the divine mechanics, had conjured the great sundial that governed the Prior's garden, engineered a wheel to lift water from the earth's thirst, and devised a winnowing machine to separate grain from chaff. His workshop was a sanctuary of ingenuity, perpetually humming with the potential of creation. He had already birthed several psalteries and two dulcimers, and now, his fingers danced over the reeds, attempting to coax life from a set of regalles—a small organ destined for the choir's praise.

Gerard, humbled by the disparity between his own clumsy attempts and the monk's mastery, plucked a few notes on the humble psaltery. Yet, the monk's touch transformed the instrument; he played it with a divinity that laid bare Gerard's profound ignorance of music, turning the novice's failure into a lesson in grace. In illumination, the monk was skilled, though he lacked the delicate hand required to match Gerard's beauty. As they compared their disparate talents—the earnestness of the young traveler against the seasoned simplicity of the age—it became clear that Youth and Age had found kinship. They were like brothers, bound by a shared reverence for craft, and the pressure to remain was heavy upon Gerard's heart.

He sought counsel from Denys, who assented with a rueful shrug, acknowledging the pull of the moment. Gerard recounted his journey to the old man, softening the edges of his recent trials. "Alack!" sighed the good old man, his eyes clouding with memory. "I have wandered far in my day, yet no shadow ever molested me." He urged Gerard to shun the inns, places teeming with rogues and rioters where the soul could be poisoned even if the body escaped. Instead, he advised finding refuge in the peace of a monastery for every night's rest. Suddenly, his demeanor shifted; he looked at Gerard with the sharp intensity of a needle, asking, "How long hast thou been shriven?"

Gerard flushed, his reply weak and halting. "Better than a fortnight."

"And thou an exorcist!" the monk exclaimed, disbelief coloring his tone. "No wonder peril has hunted thee. Come, thou must be absolved immediately."

"Yes, father," Gerard agreed fervently, sinking to his knees, palms joined in supplication. But the monk halted him, half-fretful. "Not to me! Not to me! Not to me! My soul is as cluttered with the world as any living man's. My entire devotion lies in these wooden pipes and sorry leather stops, instruments that shall perish with those whose minds are fixated on such vanities."

"Dear father," Gerard pleaded gently, "these are for the service of the Church. Surely that sanctifies the pain and labor poured into them?"

"That is precisely what the Devil has been whispering in my ear lately," the monk retorted, placing one hand behind his back and shaking his finger at Gerard with a gesture that was both threatening and playful. "He was so kind, so thoughtful, to remind me that Solomon built the Lord a house adorned with rare hangings, and that this act was deemed gracious and sinless. Oh! He quotes Scripture with remarkable flair. But I am not so simple a monk as you suppose, my lad," he cried, his voice rising in sudden defiance, addressing not Gerard but the empty space beside him. "This one toy finished, vigils, fasts, and prayers await me. Prayers standing, prayers lying on the chapel floor, and prayers submerged in a right good tub of cold water."

He nudged Gerard, winking with conspiratorial knowingness. "Nothing he hates and dreads more than seeing us monks at our orisons, up to our chins in freezing water. *Corpus domat aqua*—the body dwells in water. So now, go confess thy trifling sins, those pardonable errors of youth and secular wandering, and leave me to mine. They are sweet to me as honey, to be expiated in due measure."

Gerard bowed his head, unable to suppress the question, "Where shall I find a confessor more holy and merciful?"

"In each of these cells," the monk replied simply, gesturing down the corridor. "There, seek Brother Anselm, yonder."

Gerard moved toward the indicated cell, but the doors stood in tight succession, and fate seemed to conspire against him. Just as he raised his hand to knock, he heard his old friend cry out in an agitated whisper, "Nay! Nay! Nay!" He spun around to find the monk pacing frantically before his own cell door, beating the air with doubled hands like a man sawing at a phantom log. Gerard feared the room housed some ferocious beast, or perhaps the very personage whose presence in the convent had been so loudly proclaimed. He glanced back, bewildered, and stepped toward the next door.

Then, his old friend nodded vigorously, his face bursting into a blissful expression before he retreated swiftly into his sanctuary. Inside, he seized his hourglass, flipped it, and returned to his regalles. Often, he would pause, gazing upward, murmuring to himself, "Well-a-day, the sands flow so swiftly when the man bends over earthly toys."

Father Anselm awaited him. He was a venerable figure, his ample head crowned with dignity and love. As Gerard confessed, responding to the priest's gentle yet piercing inquiries, he felt a strange mixture of awe and absurdity. "Here is a head!" he thought, panic rising. "Oh dear, oh dear! I wonder if you will allow me to sketch it once the confession is ended." His own mind grew foggy, crimes forgotten in the haze of admiration. Yet, he did not soften his account of the pagan nature of his travels, nor did he diminish the truth of his experiences.

The penance assigned was brief but solemn: Gerard was to enter the convent church, prostrate himself, and kiss the lowest step of the altar three times. Then, kneeling on the floor, he was to recite three Our Fathers and a Creed. Upon completion, he was to return instantly.

True to his word, Gerard performed his short mortification and returned to find Father Anselm mixing plaster. "After the soul comes the body," the monk declared. "Know that I am the surgeon here, lacking a better title. This is for thy leg; to cool it, not to burn it. The saints forbid otherwise."

During the treatment, the monastic leech, having taken a keen interest in the Düsseldorf episode of Gerard's confession, leaned toward Denys's view on "bleeding." "We Dominicans seldom let blood these days," he explained, his voice steady. "The lay leeches claim it stems from timidity and lack of skill, but in truth, we have long discovered that simples cure most ailments that can be cured at all. Furthermore, they never kill in capable hands, whereas other remedies strike like thunderbolts. As for blood, the Vulgate states expressly that it is the life of a man. And in medicine, as in law or divinity, to be wiser than the All-Wise is to be a fool. Moreover, simples are mighty. Consider the little four-legged creature that kills the poisonous snake; if bitten itself, it finds an herb powerful enough to quell that venom, stronger and swifter than any mortal remedy. Taught by her wisdom and our own traditions, we continue to search and test the virtues of the plants God has strewn across this earth—some to feed the body, others to heal it. Only in desperate cases do we mix heavenly with earthly virtue, steeping the hair or bones of departed saints in the medicine to work miracles."

"Do you truly believe it is the relics, father?" Gerard asked. "For Peter a Floris, a learned physician and no pagan, denies it stoutly."

"What does Peter a Floris know? And what do I know?" the monk countered calmly. "I do not presume to claim we can command the saints, nor that they will, regardless of our will, draw corporal virtue from their blessed remains. Yet, I observe that the patient who drinks thus in faith is often improved as by a charm. Doubtless, faith in the recipient plays a vast role in all such cures. Thus it has always been. A sick woman whom all Jewish physicians failed to heal merely touched Christ's garment and was cured in an instant. Had she not touched that sacred cloth, she would never have recovered. If she had worn it to her grave without faith, I suspect she would have been no better. But we do ill to probe these matters too curiously. Everything around us demands faith. Have then a little patience. We shall soon know all. Meanwhile, I, thy confessor for this nonce, strictly forbid thee, under penalty to thy soul's health, to listen to learned laymen on religious matters. Arrogance is their bane; with it, they shut heaven's open door against their own faces. Mark my words: learned laics. The unlearned have often been my masters in humility, and may become thine."

"The wound is tended; in three days it shall be but a scar," the monk concluded warmly. "Now, God speed thee, and may the saints make thee as good and as happy as thou art thoughtful and gracious."

Gerard lingered, hoping to delay departure for the refectory dinner, but Father Anselm, with a shade of regret barely perceptible, informed him he would not leave his cell that week, being himself in penitence.
