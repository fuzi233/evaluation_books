Princess Claelia had set her heart on a full-length likeness—herself, entire, to outface time.

Gerard, who knew the temper of patronage and the price of it, ventured gently, “Signora, there is in Rome a master—Pietro Vanucci—my friend—who—”

She cut him off with a small, queenly lift of the chin. “’Twill be time enough to slight Gerardo when his work discontents me.”

The refusal did not wound him; it warmed him. He had, besides, a notion that belonged to his craft like a secret spice in a sauce: he could make paper look like marble.

“Then stand to me,” he pleaded, “as a Roman statue. I will draw you so—so cold and noble—that a man would think you carved, not born.”

At the first, she was half amused. But when he explained, with the innocent zeal of an artist who forgets everything but form, that she must wear tunic, toga, and sandals like the ancients—

“Why,” she said, with that mediaeval frankness that could be more startling than rudeness, “I had as lieve be presented in my smock.”

He fell to praising the antique habit—free, ample, simple, and noble—and, with the heedless sincerity that is the truest flattery, told her Heaven had given her Roman features, and an arm and hand that modern sleeves did but wrong.

She narrowed her eyes, smiling as if to weigh whether he mocked her. “What, can you flatter, like the rest, Gerardo? Well—give me time to think on’t. Come o’ Saturday, and then I will say ay or nay.”

Saturday came; but all the week between had been spent in her private rooms with needle and pins and trying-on—toga folded, tunic set, sandals chosen—antiquity measured against the face in the mirror, a thousand years of fashion argued with a single woman’s vanity.

Gerard, hurrying to the appointed sitting, was stopped dead in the street by a shop-window, as if a hand had gripped his collar.

There lay a Lactantius open; the page was so even, so fair, that his calligrapher’s eye drank it like clear wine. He leaned closer. No pen had done that. No hand, however patient, however trained to thin strokes and swelling hairlines, could have made letters so uniform, so inexorably alike.

It was printed.

A groan came from him, low and bitter, like a man hearing his own trade pronounced dying.

“I am sped,” he whispered. “Mine enemy is at the door. The press is in Rome.”

He went in, schooling his face to indifference. “How long hath the printing-press been in Rome?” he asked, as carelessly as if inquiring the price of figs.

The trader laughed. “In Rome? There is no such thing, messer. Oh, you mean the Lactantius. That was printed on the top o’ the Apennines.”

“What,” said Gerard, staring, “did the printing-press fall down there out o’ the moon?”

“Nay; it shot up there out of Germany. See the title-page.”

Gerard took the book with the eagerness of a man handling his rival’s blade. The black, mechanical letters sat on the page with a cold perfection:

Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz Alumnorum Joannis Fust. Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.

“Will ye buy?” the man coaxed. “See how fair and even be the letters. Few are left can write like that; and scarce a quarter of the price.”

“I would fain have it,” Gerard answered, and the sadness in him was like hunger turned to ash, “but my heart will not let me. Know I am a caligraph, and these disciples of Fust run after me round the world, taking the bread out of my mouth. Yet I wish them no ill. Heaven forbid!”

He fled the shop, and the street seemed altered: every sound sharper, every shout and iron-shod hoof a prophecy of pounding type. “Dear Margaret,” he said within himself, as if she walked beside him, “we must lose no time. We must make our hay while shines the sun. One month more, and an avalanche of printer’s letters shall roll down on Rome from those Apennines and lay us waste that writers be.”

He quickened his pace toward Claelia, as though he could outrun the press by speed alone.

He was ushered into an apartment he had not seen before—small, rich, cunningly contrived for softness. A fountain played in the center, its thin jets making a continual whisper; the floor was strewn with panthers’ skins, hair upward, so that no footfall spoke. Even the air seemed trained to hush itself. Beyond, instead of a door, a great curtain of tapestry hung, heavy as a wall.

Gerard was left there long enough to doubt whether he had been misplaced. The fountain’s voice became a kind of counting—time, time—each drop like a tiny admonition. Then, behind the curtain, came a step: light, swift, almost noiseless on the furred floor. The tapestry parted.

She stood there in tunic and toga and sandals, not modern in a single line. The figure might have been worshipped by pagans: not wholly Venus, not wholly Minerva, but something between—nobler than the one, more womanly than the other. Beauty, which laughs at centuries, had taken the antique dress and made it present.

Gerard started, the artist in him flushing as if someone had set a lamp behind his eyes. “Oh!” he cried—nothing more, because nothing else would come—and he stared in rapture.

That cry was the sweetest praise she had ever heard, because it was not dressed in courtly phrases. A blush tinted her cheek; her eyes brightened; her mouth curved into complacency that tasted like honey to her pride.

When his eloquence proved to be only gaze and breath, she spoke first. “Well, Gerardo, thou seest I have made myself an antique monster for thee.”

“A monster?” he said, still drinking in line and proportion. “Fra Colonna would fall down and adore your highness, seeing you so habited.”

“Nay,” she returned quickly, “I care not to be adored by an old man. I would liever be loved by a young one—of my own choosing.”

He set to work at once—pencils out, paper stretched on his canvas—so absorbed that he forgot her pulse, her lungs, the weight of her arms held aloft. He had the mercilessness of concentration. Near an hour passed before she spoke again, and then faintly: “Gerardo, I can stand so no more—even for thee.”

“Sit down and rest awhile, signora,” he said, startled at his own cruelty when it was named aloud.

“I thank thee,” she breathed; and sinking into a chair, she turned pale and sighed as if the toga had suddenly become lead.

He was alarmed. He dipped his hand into the fountain and, thinking like a simple man with a simple remedy, would have flung water on her face; but she lifted her hand, deprecating.

“Nay. Hold it to my brow with thine hand—prithee, do not fling it at me.”

Timidly, hesitating as if the touch were a sacrilege, he laid his wet hand on her forehead.

“Ah,” she sighed, “that is reviving. Again.”

He did it again, and her lashes fluttered; she thanked him and bade him ring the little bell on the table. A maid came. Claelia sent her to Floretta for a large fan.

Floretta arrived quickly, fan in hand—and before she could even kneel, the princess’s nostrils dilated like a bloodhorse scenting something foul.

“Wretch!” Claelia cried.

In an instant she was on her feet, vigor returning like flame to dry straw. With her left hand she seized Floretta by the hair; with her right she struck—once, twice, thrice—sharp, resounding blows. The bare arm the toga displayed was beautiful, and in that moment seemed terrible in its strength: it rose and fell with a brutal regularity, as if power were a machine. The slaps cracked through the hush-room and even drowned the fountain’s whisper. Floretta screamed and sobbed, and was driven through the tapestry as if through a storm.

From beyond the curtain her crying went on, bitter as smoke.

“Saints of heaven!” Gerard cried. “What is amiss? What hath she done?”

“She knows right well,” Claelia snapped, still breathing hard. “’Tis not the first time. The nasty toad! I’ll learn her to come to me stinking of the musk-cat.”

Gerard, whose senses were not dulled to smell—who knew how a rank perfume could spoil clean air the way a wrong spice ruins broth—still winced at the violence. “Alas, signora,” he ventured, “’twas a small fault, methinks.”

“A small fault? Nay—’twas a foul fault.”

Then, as if a cord had been cut within her, she dropped from fury into sweetness with amazing speed. “Are you wroth with me for beating her, Gerar-do?”

“Signora,” he said, uneasy, “it ill becomes me to school you; but methinks such as Heaven appoints to govern others should govern themselves.”

“That is true,” she answered softly. “How wise you are, to be so young.” She called the other maid, gave her a little purse. “Take that to Floretta, and tell her ‘the Gerardo’ hath interceded for her; and so I must needs forgive her. There, Gerardo.”

He coloured, caught between shame and the awkward pleasure of being made powerful by another’s words. To hide it, he asked if he should resume her picture.

“Not yet. Beating that hussy hath somewhat breathed me. I’ll sit awhile, and you shall talk to me. I know you can talk, an it pleases you, as rarely as you draw.”

“That were easily done,” he said, lightly.

“Do it then, Gerardo.”

The command struck him dumb. He searched for a safe subject, like a man groping for the right herb to temper a too-hot dish. “But, signora, I know not what to say. This is sudden.”

“Say your real mind,” she said. “Say you wish you were anywhere but here.”

“Nay,” he replied, honestly, “that would not be sooth. I wish one thing though.”

“Ay? and what is that?” The gentleness was almost dangerous.

“I wish,” he blurted, “I could have drawn you as you were beating that poor lass. You were awful—yet lovely. Oh, what a subject for a Pythoness!”

She sighed as if disappointed. “Alas, he thinks but of his art. And why keep such a coil about my beauty, Gerardo? You are far fairer than I am. You are more like Apollo than I to Venus. Also, you have lovely hair and lovely eyes—but you know not what to do with them.”

“Ay, do I,” he said, clinging to his refuge. “To draw you, signora.”

“Ah, yes—see my features. But you cannot see what any Roman gallant had seen long ago in your place.” Her voice sank, and the room’s hush seemed to lean closer to listen. “Yet sure you must have noted how welcome you are to me, Gerardo?”

“I can see your highness is ever passing kind to me; a poor stranger like me.”

“No,” she said, and the word fell like a stone into still water. “I am not. I have often been cold to you—rude sometimes—and you are so simple you see not the cause. Alas! I feared for my own heart. I feared to be your slave—I, who have hitherto made slaves.” Her breath trembled. “Gerardo, I am unhappy. Ever since you came here I have lived upon your visits. The day you are to come I am bright; the other days I am listless. You are not like the Roman gallants. You make me hate them. You are ten times braver to my eye; and wise, and scholarly, and you never flatter and lie. I scorn a man that lies. Gerar-do—teach me thy magic; teach me to make thee as happy by my side as I am still by thine.”

As she spoke, her mellow voice slid toward a whisper; her hand—white, careful, almost timid—stole down his arm and came to rest upon his wrist, as lightly as a bird that might fly at the least alarm.

Gerard, wrapped in Margaret as in a garment under all his garments, had not foreseen this. Admiration of Claelia’s beauty had never displaced the absent image that governed him. Yet here was regal loveliness wooing with a grace he had never imagined, and he stood as helpless as a boy asked to translate a language he does not know.

He blushed. He trembled.

The siren saw it and mistook it for yielding. “Poor Gerardo,” she murmured. “Fear not. None shall ever harm thee under my wing. Wilt not speak to me, Gerar-do mio?”

“Signora!” he muttered, pleading without words.

His lowered eye fell upon that same shapely arm and delicate hand curled round his elbow—and in a flash he saw it as he had just seen it: rising and falling on Floretta. Beauty and violence braided together. His skin went cold beneath the blush.

“Alas!” Claelia said, misreading his recoil. “I scare him. Am I then so very terrible? Is it my Roman robe? I’ll doff it, and habit me as when thou first camest to me. Mindest thou? ’Twas to write a letter to yon barren knight Ercole d’Orsini. Shall I tell thee? ’twas the sight of thee—thy pretty ways—thy wise words—made me hate him on the instant.” Her voice quickened with its own memory. “Tell me now how many times hast thou been here since then. Ah! thou knowest not—lovest me not, I doubt, as I love thee. Eighteen times, Gerardo. And each time dearer to me. The day thou comest not, ’tis night, not day, to Claelia. Cruel boy—am I not worth a word? Hast every day a princess at thy feet? Prithee, prithee—speak to me, Gerar-do.”

“Signora,” he faltered, “what can I say that were not better left unsaid? Oh, evil day that ever I came here.”

“Ah! say not so. ’Twas the brightest day ever shone on me—or indeed on thee. I’ll make thee confess so much ere long, ungrateful one.”

“Your highness,” he began, low and earnest.

“Call me Claelia,” she breathed.

He could not. The word stuck. “Signora, I am too young and too little wise to know how I ought to speak to you, so as not to seem blind nor yet ungrateful. But this I know: I were both naught and ungrateful—the worst foe e’er you had—did I take advantage of this mad fancy. Sure some ill spirit hath had leave to afflict you withal; for ’tis all unnatural that a princess adorned with every grace should abase her affections on a churl.”

Her hand withdrew from his wrist, slowly—yet, as it passed over his arm, it lingered a heartbeat, like a leaf that clings before falling.

“You fear the daggers of my kinsmen,” she said, half sad, half contemptuous.

“No more than I fear the bodkins of your women,” Gerard answered, with a sudden honest haughtiness. “But I fear God and the saints—and my own conscience.”

“The truth, Gerardo, the truth!” she said sharply. “Hypocrisy sits awkwardly on thee. Princesses, while they are young, are not despised for love of God, but of some other woman. Tell me whom thou lovest; and if she is worthy thee, I will forgive thee.”

“No she in Italy,” he said, “upon my soul.”

“Ah!” Her eyes flashed. “There is one somewhere then. Where? where?”

“In Holland—my native country.”

“Ah! Marie de Bourgoyne is fair, they say—yet she is but a child.”

“Princess,” he said, and his voice steadied on what was sacred to him, “she I love is not noble. She is as I am. Nor is she so fair as thou—yet is she fair—and linked to my heart for ever by her virtues, and by all the dangers and griefs we have borne together, and for one another.” He swallowed, but did not retreat. “Forgive me; but I would not wrong my Margaret for all the highest dames in Italy.”

The change in Claelia was like winter coming in a breath. She sprang to her feet and stood opposite him—beautiful still, but far more terrible now than when she struck Floretta; then her cheeks had been red, but now they were pale, and her fury was gathered and quiet, like a blade held steady.

“This to my face, unmannered wretch!” she cried. “Was I born to be insulted as well as scorned by such as thou? Beware! We nobles brook no rivals. Bethink thee whether is better—the love of a Cesarini, or her hate. After all I have said and done to thee, it must be love or hate between us—and to the death. Choose now!”

Gerard looked up at her with wonder and awe. In her Roman drapery, towering, imperious, she seemed some antique goddess affronted by a mortal. He sighed—deep, patient—and could not bring himself to answer.

The sigh struck some hidden string. Or perhaps passion, spent in its own blaze, made room for another. Claelia sank down, suddenly languid, and tears began to fall rapidly, bright in the soft room, each drop audible in that muffled place as if the very skins and tapestry listened.

“Alas! alas!” Gerard cried, miserable. “Weep not, sweet lady; your tears do accuse me, and I am like to weep for company. My kind patron—be yourself. You will live to see how much better a friend I was to you than I seemed.”

“I see it now, Gerardo,” she whispered. “Friend is the word—the only word can ever pass between us twain. I was mad. Any other man had ta’en advantage of my folly. You must teach me to be your friend—and nothing more.”

He seized upon the escape as a man seizes shore after a long current. He spoke to her of friendship out of Cicero—how godlike, how rare, how lasting beyond love’s fever—and, to prove himself capable of it, he even told her of Denys and himself. She listened with half-closed eyes, not merely hearing but studying, as though each phrase were an ingredient to be tasted for what it revealed.

At last she said calmly, “Leave me now, Gerardo, and come as usual to-morrow. You will find your lesson well bestowed.”

She held out her hand. He kissed it and went away, pondering, wondering whether he had walked wisely or only narrowly.

The next day he was received with marked distance. She stood before him literally like a statue—still, cold, offering him her outlines and nothing else—and after a very short sitting she excused herself and dismissed him. He felt the change like a draft in a once-warm room; yet he told himself, “She is wise.” In her way, she was.

The next day he found her surrounded by young nobles, their voices buzzing in the muffled chamber like insects that did not know silence had rights. They flattered her to the skies, and she and they treated Gerard as one treats a dog that can do a trick—useful, contemptible, amusing. The cavaliers leaned over his work and criticised it with such ignorance married to insolence that his cheeks burned.

Claelia watched his face, demure, half-lidded, at each sting; and when at last the insects had flown, she had her doors closed against every one of them—for their pains.

The next day she was alone again: cold, silent. After standing so long she said abruptly, “You treated my company with less respect than became you.”

“Did I, signora?”

“Did you? You fired up at the comments they did you the honour to make on your work.”

“Nay, I said nought.”

“Oh—high looks speak as plain as high words. Your cheeks were red as blood.”

“I was nettled a moment,” he confessed, “at seeing so much ignorance and ill-nature together.”

“Now it is me—their hostess—you affront.”

“Forgive me, signora, and acquit me of design. It would ill become me to affront the kindest patron and friend I have in Rome but one.”

“How humble we are all of a sudden,” she said, and her voice had the edge of a thin knife. “In sooth, Ser Gerardo, you are a capital feigner. You can insult—or truckle—at will.”

“Truckle? to whom?”

“To me, for one,” she said. “To one whom you affronted for a base-born girl like yourself; but whose patronage you claim all the same.”

He rose and put a hand to his heart, as if to keep the words from cutting too deep. “These are biting words, signora. Have I really deserved them?”

“Oh, what are words to an adventurer like you?” she returned. “Cold steel is all you fear?”

“I am no swashbuckler,” he said, stung into courage, “yet I have met steel with steel; and methinks I had rather face your kinsmen’s swords than your cruel tongue, lady. Why do you use me so?”

“Gerar-do,” she said, and for once it was almost honest, “for no good reason but because I am wayward, and shrewish, and curst—and because everybody admires me but you.”

“I admire you too, signora,” he answered. “Your friends may flatter you more; but believe me, they have not the eye to see half your charms. Their babble showed me that. None admire you more truly, or wish you better, than the poor artist, who might not be your lover, but hoped to be your friend; but no—I see that may not be between one so high as you and one so low as I.”

“Ay—but it shall,” she said quickly, seizing on the word “friend” as though it were a rope. “I will not be so curst. Tell me now where abides thy Margaret; and I will give thee a present for her; and on that you and I will be friends.”

“She is a daughter of a physician called Peter,” he said, and his voice softened into homesickness like broth cooling, “and they bide at Sevenbergen. Ah me—shall I e’er see it again?”

“’Tis well,” Claelia replied, and something in her tone closed like a clasp. “Now go.”

He went, more uneasy than before. He had stepped into deep water the day he met this Italian princess—callida et calida, the sun’s own child in craft and heat. He resolved to go no more when once her likeness was finished; already he repented having taken so long and laborious a task.

That resolution wavered at his next reception, for she met him all gentleness. After standing to him awhile she said she was fatigued and would have his aid in another way: would he teach her to draw a little?

He sat beside her and guided her hand through easy lines. She was wonderfully apt—quick as fire catching. He told her so.

“I had a teacher before thee, Gerar-do,” she said softly. “Ay—and one as handsome as thyself.”

She went to a drawer and brought out several heads—patiently done, ignorant of rules, yet full of spirit. All were Gerard’s face, again and again, as though she had been trying to learn him the way a cook repeats a dish until it matches the taste in the mind. One was his very image.

“There,” she said. “Now thou seest who was my teacher.”

“Not I, signora,” he murmured, astonished.

“What—know you not who teaches us women to do all things?” She laughed, and the laugh held hunger. “’Tis love, Gerar-do. Love made me draw because thou drawest. Love prints thine image in my bosom. My fingers touch the pen, and love supplies the want of art, and lo—thy beloved features lie upon the paper.”

His astonishment turned to dismay. “Oh, signora—you promised me to be friends and nothing more.”

She laughed outright. “How simple you are! Who believes a woman promising nonsense—impossibilities? Friendship, foolish boy—who ever built that temple on red ashes?” Then the gaiety fell away. “Between thee and me it must be love or hate.”

“Which you will, signora,” Gerard said, firm now as a man who has found the one language he will not betray. “But for me I will neither love nor hate you; but with your permission I will leave you.” He rose abruptly.

She rose too, pale as death. “Ere thou leavest me so, know thy fate: outside that door are armed men who wait to slay thee at a word from me.”

“But you will not speak that word,” he said, clinging to the last hope of her better self.

“That word I will speak,” she answered, quiet and dreadful. “Nay, more: I shall noise it abroad ’twas for proffering brutal love to me thou wert slain. And I will send a special messenger to Sevenbergen—a cunning messenger, well taught his lesson. Thy Margaret shall know thee dead and think thee faithless. Now go to thy grave—a dog’s. For a man thou art not.”

He went ashen, struck dumb. The room seemed suddenly louder, though no one spoke: the fountain’s whisper mocked him, the tapestry held its breath, the skins swallowed even the scuttle of panic. “God have mercy on us both,” he said at last.

“Nay—have thou mercy on her and on thyself,” she said, leaning toward him with cruel persuasion. “She will never know in Holland what thou dost in Rome—unless I be driven to tell her my tale. Come, yield thee, Gerar-do mio. What will it cost thee to say thou lovest me? I ask thee but to feign it handsomely. Thou art young—die not for the poor pleasure of denying a lady what—the shadow of a heart. Who will shed a tear for thee? Men will laugh, not weep, over thy tombstone—ah!”

She broke off with a little scream, for he flung himself at her feet and poured out, in one torrent, the whole story—his and Margaret’s—raw as newly bled truth. Imprisoned; hunted with bloodhounds for her; driven to exile; how she had shed her blood for him and now pined at home; how he had walked across Europe ringed with perils—torn by savage brutes, attacked by furious men with sword and axe and trap, robbed, shipwrecked—for her.

She tried to pull away, but he clung to her robe, held her there with the desperate strength of one who has nothing left but speech. He caught her hand and clasped it between both of his, and his tears fell fast upon it as he begged her to think on all the woes of true lovers she would part; and what could come of so deep a love betrayed, and so false a love feigned, with hatred lurking at the bottom, but remorse—swift and lasting.

In such moments none ever resisted Gerard. His sincerity was like a plain, strong bread: no ornament, but it fed.

Claelia wavered. Her breath came in broken sighs; her bosom rose and fell; her fiery eyes filled. At last she sobbed, “You conquer me—you, or my better angel. Leave Rome!”

“I will,” he cried. “I will.”

“If you breathe a word of my folly,” she whispered, “it will be your last.”

“Think not so poorly of me,” he answered. “You are my benefactress once more. Is it for me to slander you?”

“Go,” she said, and the command was half a plea. “I will send you the means. I know myself; if you cross my path again, I shall kill you. Addio—my heart is broken.”

She touched the bell. “Floretta,” she called, voice choked, “take him safe out of the house—through my chamber—and by the side postern.”

At the door he turned. She leaned with one hand on a chair, crying, her head averted. In that moment he remembered only her mercy, not her threat. He ran back and kissed her robe. She never moved.

Once clear of the house he darted home, thanking Heaven for escape—soul and body.

“Landlady,” he said, breath still unsteady, “there is one would pick a quarrel with me. What is to be done?”

She flashed her eyes. “Strike him first, and at vantage! Get behind him; and then draw.”

“Alas, I lack your Italian courage,” he said. “To be serious—’tis a noble.”

She crossed herself. “Oh, holy saints, that is another matter. Change thy lodging awhile, and keep snug; and alter the fashion of thy habits.”

She took him to her niece, who let rooms some little distance off, and installed him there.

He had little to do now—no princess to draw, no soft-footed visits to live upon—and the sudden leisure felt like a silence after music. So he set himself resolutely to read the deed of Floris Brandt, from which he had hitherto been driven by its abominably bad writing. He bent to it as one bends to a pot whose crust must be scraped, not because it is pleasant, but because the meal depends on it. Slowly the crabbed lines yielded; slowly the sense rose.

When he mastered it, he saw at once what had been hidden plain in the words: the loan on the land must have been paid over and over by the rents, and Ghysbrecht was keeping Peter Brandt out of his own.

“Fool!” Gerard cried, striking his palm with the paper’s edge. “Not to have read this before.”

He hired a horse and rode to the nearest port. A vessel was to sail for Amsterdam in four days. He took a passage and paid a small sum to secure it.

“The land is too full of cut-throats for me,” he told himself, tasting again the muffled menace of Claelia’s house, the invisible men beyond a door. “And ’tis lovely fair weather for the sea. Our Dutch skippers are not shipwrecked like these bungling Italians.”

When he returned, there sat his old landlady, her eyes sparkling, as if she had news that crackled like a hearth newly fed.
