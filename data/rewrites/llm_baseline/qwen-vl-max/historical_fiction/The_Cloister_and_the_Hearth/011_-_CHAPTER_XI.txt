The cold grip of fear had settled deep within Gerard’s chest since the burgomaster’s venomous glance and his unjust imprisonment. He believed now, without doubt, that Ghysbrecht was his sworn enemy—deadly, relentless, and cruel. As he crept around the shadowed corner of the ancient tower, he expected not a ghost, but a trap: some dark scheme laid by a man bent on his destruction. Escape from the prison had been impossible to conceal, and he knew full well that if anyone had seen him slip away, they would be watching.

But as he stepped forward into the open, a soft hand slipped into his—warm, steady, and unafraid. It was Margaret, standing beside him, her presence calm in the face of danger.

No sooner had they glimpsed the tower than their breath caught in their throats. Halfway up its stone face, a creature with a blazing head—like a monstrous glowworm—was slowly climbing the wall. Its body was black, barely visible except for the fiery outline it cast, and it stretched nearly four feet long. Below, at the base of the tower, stood a pale figure wrapped in white, perfectly still, like a statue carved from moonlight.

Gerard and Margaret froze, hearts pounding. “The rope! The rope!” Gerard whispered, voice trembling. “It’s climbing the rope!”

As they watched, the glowing creature vanished into the window of the cell where Gerard had been held. But its light spilled through the bars, painting the interior in crimson and casting a hellish glow upon the stones.

The white figure remained motionless beneath.

There is a strange twist to terror: once the first shock passes, a wild impulse often takes hold—a desire to rush toward the horror, to confront it, even to throw oneself at it. Like a bird drawn to a serpent’s gaze, the mind becomes entranced. This was what seized Margaret. She gently pulled her hand from Gerard’s, stood stunned for a moment—and then, with a cry torn from her soul, darted forward toward the spectre.

Gerard, unaware of this instinct, thought only that the devil was dragging her into damnation. He dropped to his knees, hands clasped, and began to shout: “Exorcizo vos! In nomine beatae Mariae, exorcizo vos!” His voice cracked with terror, yet relief surged through him when he heard a faint, terrified whimper from the apparition.

Hell, it seemed, had weaknesses too.

He redoubled his prayers. And soon, the ghastly shape knelt before Margaret, pleading for mercy, its voice broken and small.

Not long after, Kate and Giles arrived at the tower, breathless from their haste. They stared in astonishment at the scene: a new rope hanging down from the prisoner’s window, swaying gently in the wind.

“Ah,” said Giles, practical as ever, “I see it now. Gerard has escaped. Up I go to check.”

“No, Giles, no!” cried Kate, eyes wide with alarm. “This is glamour! A trick of the devil! That rope is a snare—cast to lure you to your death. He knows our hearts, our desires. You love climbing—so he offers you a rope. But how could Gerard get such a thing? How could he fasten it so high? It defies nature! Holy saints protect us—evil walks abroad tonight!”

“Nonsense,” said Giles, grinning. “The way to hell is down. This rope goes up. And I’ve never had the luck to climb one so long. Might be years before I find another ready-made. Better to die happy than never know joy.” With a joyful leap, he sprang onto the rope like a cat pouncing on a table with fish. His arms swung strong, his legs light as feathers, and he climbed faster than Gerard had descended.

Kate tied a lantern around his neck. “Light frightens evil spirits,” she murmured.

And so, with the glow of the lantern illuminating his path, Giles became a living torch, ascending the night air like a firefly made flesh.

Kate watched, trembling. Then, suddenly, a woman burst forth from the very wall itself—flying toward her with speed beyond mortal limits.

Kate gasped, unable to speak. Her tongue felt glued to her palate. She dropped her crutches and sank to her knees, covering her face. “Take my body,” she moaned, “but spare my soul!”

Then came a voice—panting, astonished—“Why, it’s a woman!”

Kate looked up, shaking. “It’s a woman!”

Margaret laughed weakly. “You scared me half to death!”

“I’m scared enough myself,” Kate sobbed. “Oh, oh, oh!”

“This is strange,” Margaret said. “But the fiery-headed thing—where is it?”

“It was with you,” Kate replied, “and yet you’re harmless. Why are you here?”

“And why are you?” asked Margaret.

“Perhaps we’re both seeking the same thing?” she smiled. “Ah—your brother’s sister, Kate!”

“And you’re Margaret Brandt.”

“Yes.”

“Then all the better. You love him. You’re here. So Giles was right—Gerard is free.”

Gerard stepped forward, confirming it.

But before any words could follow, a terrible sound tore through the silence—a voice from the grave, echoing like a tomb come alive: “PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!”

With each repetition, the cry grew louder, more unearthly.

They looked up—and there was Giles, hands full of scrolls, face lit with a diabolical glee. The lantern at his neck turned him into a glowing demon, his features twisted in infernal triumph.

At the word, he hurled the parchments downward. They fell like wounded birds—some tumbling, others fluttering, spinning slowly through the air, drifting to earth in silent circles.

Before they landed, the sepulchral roar echoed again: “Parchment! Parchment!”

Another shower rained down. Then another. Soon the grass was blanketed in white.

Finally, the fire-headed figure slid down the rope like a falling star, landing with a thud. Without a word, it turned to Gerard and said, business-like: “We should settle payment for these documents. Right away.”

“Hush,” Gerard said sharply. “You speak too loudly. Gather them up. We must go somewhere safer.”

“Will you come home with me, Gerard?” little Kate asked softly.

“I have no home,” he replied.

“You shall not say that,” she insisted. “After all this wrong, you’ll be welcome at my father’s house.”

“Father?” Gerard said, voice hardening. “I have no father. He who was my father is now my jailer. I escaped his grasp. I will never return under his roof.”

“It wasn’t your father,” Kate said gently. “It was an enemy. I overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt plotting—”

But Gerard would not be comforted. “Cornelis and Sybrandt have shown me their teeth long enough. But they could do nothing without authority. My father gave it. He gave the burgomaster power to touch me—though I am a free citizen of Tergou. So let it be. I was his son. Now I am his prisoner. He has played his part. I shall play mine. Farewell, Tergou. I was born here. I lived honestly. I was imprisoned. While there is another town in the world, I will never set foot here again.”

“Oh, Gerard!” Kate whispered.

Margaret leaned close. “Do not oppose him now. Let his anger cool.”

Kate turned quickly. “May I look at your face?”

She studied Margaret closely—and nodded. “It is a fair face. Not one of ill intent.”

“Fear me not,” Margaret said softly. “I cannot be happy without your love—as well as Gerard’s.”

Kate wept. “These words bring comfort. I never dreamed I’d come to love you so. My heart is willing—but my body cannot embrace.”

Without hesitation, Margaret moved gently around her and kissed her tenderly.

“Often he spoke of you,” she said. “Often I longed for this.”

“You too, Gerard,” Kate whispered. “Kiss me before you leave. My heart weighs heavy at parting.”

Gerard kissed her, and she limped away on her crutches, the last sound they heard a quiet, patient sigh.

Tears filled Margaret’s eyes, but Gerard saw nothing. He was already turning toward Sevenbergen.

As they walked, Giles nudged Gerard with his bundle of parchments and held out a crooked hand.

Margaret shook her head. “Why take what isn’t ours?”

“Spoil an enemy however you can,” Gerard said.

“But they might use this as an excuse for more violence.”

“How?” Gerard replied. “Think you I’ll stay in Tergou after this? The burgomaster stole my freedom. If I could, I’d take his life for it. But I can’t—so I take what comes to hand.”

He handed Giles a few coins. The boy cheered and shuffled after Kate.

Soon Martin joined them, and together they walked into the night, leaving behind the haunted tower, the ghosts, and the city that had betrayed them.
