Mrs. John Dashwood settled into the role of mistress at Norland with the absolute authority of a conqueror entering a conquered province. Her mother and sisters-in-law were instantly demoted to the status of guests, a title that implied both hospitality and a distinct lack of permanence. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood maintained a veneer of quiet civility toward them, while her husband extended a kindness that was as genuine as it was limited, reserved exclusively for himself, his wife, and their infant son. He pressed them, with a sincerity that bordered on earnestness, to regard Norland as their permanent residence. Since no alternative plan appealed to Mrs. Dashwood more than staying until she could secure a separate household nearby, the invitation was accepted. Remaining in a place saturated with memories of past joy was precisely the psychological anchor she required. In moments of cheer, her temperament was radiant, possessing a sanguine optimism that equated hope with actual happiness. Conversely, in sorrow, she was equally susceptible to her imagination, retreating into fantasies that placed her far beyond the reach of consolation, just as her pleasure often transcended mere alloy.

However, Mrs. John Dashwood harbored a profound objection to her husband's intention regarding his sisters. To strip three thousand pounds from the inheritance of their beloved young son would be to reduce him to destitution. She implored him to reconsider, asking how he could justify robbing his only child of such a substantial sum. Furthermore, what moral claim did the Miss Dashwoods hold? They were connected to him solely by half-blood, a distinction she regarded as non-existent. Society universally acknowledged that no affection existed between children born of different marriages; thus, why should he ruin himself and their poor little Harry by squandering his wealth on these half-sisters?

"It was my father's last request," her husband replied firmly. "That I should assist his widow and daughters."

"He was likely light-headed at the time," she countered sharply. "Ten to one he lacked his faculties. Had he been in his right senses, he could not have conceived of begging you to give away half your fortune from your own child."

"He made no specific stipulation for a sum, my dear Fanny," he corrected gently. "Only a general request to assist them and improve their circumstances. Perhaps it would have been wiser if he had left the matter entirely to my discretion. He could hardly have imagined I would neglect them. But since he demanded a promise, I felt bound to give it. At least, that was my reasoning at the time. The promise stands and must be fulfilled. Something must be arranged for them once they depart Norland."

"Very well, then," she conceded reluctantly. "_Let_ something be done. But that 'something' need not amount to three thousand pounds. Consider," she added, her voice tightening, "once money leaves your possession, it never returns. Your sisters will marry, and it will be gone forever. If, indeed, it could be restored to our poor little boy—"

"Why, to be sure," her husband said gravely, "that would alter the equation significantly. The day may come when Harry regrets parting with such a large sum. Should he establish a numerous family, for instance, it would serve as a remarkably convenient addition to his resources."

"To be sure, it would."

"Perhaps, then, reducing the sum by half would benefit all parties. Five hundred pounds would constitute a prodigious increase to their fortunes!"

"Oh! Beyond anything great! What brother on earth would offer half so much to his sisters, even if they were truly his sisters? And as it is—they are only half-blood!—But you possess such a generous spirit!"

"I would not wish to act meanly," he replied. "On such occasions, one errs more by doing too little than too much. No one, at least, can accuse me of insufficient effort; indeed, they could hardly expect more."

"There is no knowing what _they_ may expect," the lady interjected, "but we must disregard their expectations. The question remains: what can you afford?"

"Certainly. I believe I can afford to give them five hundred pounds apiece. Without my contribution, they will each inherit approximately three thousand pounds upon their mother's death—a very comfortable fortune for any young woman."

"To be sure it is. Indeed, it strikes me they require no further augmentation. With ten thousand pounds divided among them, they are assured of success if they marry, or a life of comfort if they remain single, living off the interest."

"That is accurate. Therefore, I fail to see why it would not be more prudent to provide for their mother during her lifetime rather than for the daughters. I refer to an annuity. My sisters would feel the benefits as acutely as she would. A hundred pounds annually would render them all perfectly content."

Mrs. Dashwood hesitated slightly before consenting to this proposal. "Admittedly," she said, "it is preferable to parting with fifteen hundred pounds outright. However, if Mrs. Dashwood survives fifteen years, we shall be thoroughly deceived."

"Fifteen years! My dear Fanny, her life cannot be valued at half that price."

"Certainly not; yet, observe, people invariably live indefinitely when an annuity is involved. She is robust, healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a binding commitment; it recurs annually and offers no escape. You are unaware of the entanglement you invite. I have witnessed the turmoil surrounding annuities firsthand. My mother was burdened by payments to three elderly servants mandated by my father's will. It was astonishing how disagreeable it became. Twice yearly, funds had to be disbursed, accompanied by the logistical nightmare of delivery. Then came the false reports of death, later proven untrue. My mother grew utterly sick of it. She claimed her income was no longer her own, shackled by perpetual demands. It was particularly cruel, considering the money would have been entirely at her disposal without restriction. I harbor such an abhorrence of annuities that I would not agree to one under any circumstance."

"It is certainly an unpleasant mechanism," Mr. Dashwood admitted. "Those yearly drains on one's income are vexatious. As your mother rightly noted, a fortune is not truly one's own. Being tethered to the rigid obligation of such payments on every rent day strips away one's independence."

"Undoubtedly. Moreover, you receive no gratitude. They assume security; you perform merely the duty expected of you, eliciting no appreciation. If I were you, I would exercise complete discretion. I would not bind myself to annual allowances. It may prove inconvenient in lean years to spare a hundred, or even fifty pounds from our own expenses."

"I believe you are right, my love. It is superior to establish no annuity. Any occasional gift will offer far greater assistance than a fixed allowance, as they might simply elevate their lifestyle if assured of increased income, gaining no net wealth by year's end. It is undoubtedly the optimal path. Occasional gifts of fifty pounds will prevent distress and, I trust, adequately discharge my promise to my father."

"To be sure it will. Frankly, I am convinced your father never envisioned you providing monetary aid. The assistance he contemplated was likely reasonable social support: securing a modest dwelling, aiding their relocation, and sending seasonal provisions like fish and game. I wager my life he meant nothing more. It would be bizarre and unreasonable to expect financial ruin. Consider, my dear Mr. Dashwood, how comfortably your mother-in-law and her daughters can subsist on the interest of seven thousand pounds, plus the five hundred pounds generated by the individual legacies of the girls. They will effectively pay their mother for board from this sum. Altogether, they command five hundred pounds annually. What on earth can four women desire beyond that? They will live frugally. Housekeeping costs will be negligible. They will own no carriages, no horses, and employ minimal servants. They will avoid society and incur no expenses whatsoever. Conceive the comfort of five hundred pounds a year! I cannot imagine how they would spend half of it. Regarding any further provision from you, it is absurd. They will be far more capable of offering _you_ something."

"Upon my word," Mr. Dashwood conceded, "I believe you are entirely correct. My father surely meant nothing more than you suggest. I now clearly comprehend his intent, and I shall strictly fulfill my engagement through the acts of assistance and kindness you have outlined. When your mother relocates, my services will be freely offered to facilitate her transition. A modest donation of furniture may also prove acceptable."

"Certainly," Mrs. John Dashwood agreed. "However, one critical factor must be weighed. When your parents moved to Norland, although the Stanhill furniture was sold, all the china, plate, and linen were preserved and now belong to your mother. Consequently, her new home will be nearly fully furnished upon her arrival."

"This is a material consideration, undoubtedly. A valuable legacy indeed! And yet, some of the silverware would have been a delightful addition to our own stock here."

"Yes; and the breakfast service is twice as exquisite as ours. Far too handsome, in my opinion, for any establishment they can realistically afford. Nevertheless, so it is. Your father thought only of _them_. I must add this: you owe him neither particular gratitude nor strict adherence to his wishes. We know full well that if he possessed the ability, he would have bequeathed almost everything in the world to _them_."

This argument proved irresistible. It supplied the necessary resolve to his wavering intentions. He finally concluded that it would be not only unnecessary but highly indecorous to provide the widow and children of his father with more than the neighborly gestures his wife had suggested.
