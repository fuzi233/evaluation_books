Tito possessed ample justification for his claim of safety. Over the preceding three months, anticipating the inevitable exposure of the Medicean conspirators, he had meticulously fortified his position. He had expanded his influence in Rome and Milan, acting as a conduit for clandestine intelligence and executing subtle maneuvers against the Frate and the popular faction. Simultaneously, he had cultivated the favor of that very popular party with deceptive fervor, projecting an image of unwavering political alignment. Yet, paradoxically, he had deepened his reliance on the Mediceans, seeking their exclusive trust and active employment. This triple game was effortlessly maintained. The Mediceans, accustomed to duplicity in their own dealings, lacked the moral framework to judge a colleague devoid of hereditary ties and traditional prejudices. To them, deceiving the enemy was a legitimate strategy; deceiving their own was a baseness they did not entertain. In employing Tito's facile talents, they remained blind to the fact that his lack of ancestral attachment—their primary safeguard of honor—was precisely what rendered him a perfect instrument of betrayal. Similarly, the *Arrabbiati*, the aristocratic enemies of Savonarola, operated a network of espionage rooted in profound hypocrisy, demanding exorbitant fees for their services. Suspecting an agent merely because he played a role convincingly was, in their eyes, a logical fallacy. Conversely, the *Piagnoni* of the popular party, driven by the directness of energetic conviction, were inclined to believe Tito's professed loyalty, largely because he feigned no religious sympathy.

Under these conditions, the last quarter had been a period of flattering success for Tito. His primary ambition was to secure a permanent station in Rome or Milan. He harbored a growing resolve to abandon Florence for a great capital where life was less arduous and the rewards for talent more splendid. Currently, the scales tipped toward Milan; if he could render decisive services to Duke Ludovico Sforza within the year, the prospect of a court appointment outweighed the allure of Rome.

The revelation of the Medicean conspiracy was, therefore, a calculated risk Tito had anticipated, though not the specific manner of its occurrence. The arrest of Lamberto dell'Antella, found in possession of incriminating correspondence and fueled by bitter hatred for the Medici, was an unforeseen variable. Despite the careful pretexts guarding his agency, Tito could not escape implication in a broad discovery. However, his sharp mind quickly charted a course to ensure his safety with minimal collateral damage. While it is pleasant to remain unscathed, one must acknowledge that the skin remains sensitive to the atmosphere.

His calculations proved accurate. That night, before returning home, he secured the three outcomes he desired: immunity from prosecution regarding his Medicean complicity, the retention of his secretaryship for another year, and the strict confidentiality of the price exacted for these guarantees. A sum considered heavy by most, and one Tito himself would have preferred to avoid paying, it was nonetheless the currency of his survival.

His first move was to win over Francesco Valori, head of the *Piagnoni* and one of the Ten under whom Tito served. Valori, a man of fine qualities tempered by violent partisanship, an arrogant temper, and deep-seated animosity toward Bernardo del Nero, was also a member of the special council investigating the plot. In a brief, private interview sealed by a pledge of secrecy, Tito confessed his agency for the Mediceans, citing motives he presented with frankness. He declared his belief in the futility of their efforts and his sincere preference for the popular government. He feigned confidence in Valori, revealing his personal dislike for Bernardo del Nero, before delivering his crucial proposition: there existed another Medicean plot, and if granted certain conditions, he could travel to Siena and Romagna, where Piero de' Medici was gathering forces, to obtain documentary evidence for the council. This mission required that his reputation as a Medicean agent remain unblemished in the eyes of his former masters; thus, his role as a source of information to the authorities had to be buried in profound secrecy. Yet, fearing that even precautions might leak, he insisted on immediate assurances of immunity from prosecution and job security for the coming year.

These propositions did not resonate with Valori as they do with modern sensibilities. Valori's mind was not focused on weighing Tito's moral conduct; it was singularly obsessed with securing an extreme sentence against the five prisoners. He anticipated immense efforts to save them and believed that the strongest possible evidence was necessary to alarm the populace to the dangers of clemency. In the legal culture of the time, evidence was a commodity attainable only through foul means. Torturing suspects into confession was viewed as a step toward justice, and the appearance of a traitor was often welcomed to validate the guilt of others. Lamberto dell'Antella had been tortured to extract knowledge he barely possessed, yet stronger facts were deemed essential, particularly against Bernardo del Nero, who had thus far refrained from betraying the plot despite previous attempts to discourage it. For Valori, the welfare of Florence demanded that Bernardo's guilt be illuminated in the starkest light. Driven by zeal, perhaps unconsciously fueled by his own hatred, Valori accepted Tito's proposal. He presented it to his colleagues anonymously and secured their agreement. Late that afternoon, Tito was summoned before the Special Council. By revealing a second plot designed to install Piero de' Medici as master of Florence, scheduled for execution in mid-August, he caused a sensation. He argued that documentary evidence would clarify the right course of action. He was commissioned to depart for Siena at dawn and, departing from the council chamber, carried with him a written guarantee of his immunity and the retention of his office.

Among the twenty Florentines who regarded Tito with grave eyes as he stood before them, speaking of startling matters with easy circumlocution and an apparent lack of higher motive—a mask often worn by the most intense affectation—some began to reassess him. They silently concluded that this ingenious and serviceable Greek was now to be utilized for public necessity rather than private intimacy. Unprincipled men were useful tools, allowing those with scruples to keep their hands relatively clean in a world requiring dirty work. Indeed, it was unclear to the respectable Florentine mind, unless one adhered to the Frate's extravagant belief in earthly purity, how any department of life could function without instruments whom it was proper to kick or spit upon while paying their wages. Some of these very men, soon to be engaged in a memorable transaction requiring unscrupulousness as resolute as Tito's, reflected on the dichotomy of their nature. As their own poet Pulci had noted: "Betrayal pleases many, but no one likes the traitor." Society had erected gibbets for both murderers and martyrs, offering execrations for both dastardly acts and words of generous truth. This mixed condition signaled not hopelessness, but a struggling order.

For Tito himself, he was acutely aware that he had slightly diminished in the esteem of those who had accepted his services. Possessing the self-contemplation inherent in acting on well-considered reasons, he would have preferred to avoid their disapproval. He had never intended to be judged poorly; he aimed to conduct himself so ably that opposition to societal standards went unnoticed. The barrier between himself and Romola had been raised by the impossibility of such concealment with her. He shrank from condemnatory judgments as one shrinks from a climate to which he cannot adapt. Yet, reality was not as malleable as cleverness wished, and events had turned inconveniently. He bore no rancor against Messer Bernardo del Nero; he held personal likings for Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci. He had served them ably, meriting high reward had their party prevailed. Was he to relinquish the agreeable fruits of life solely because their party had failed? His offer of additional proof against them would likely alter their fate little; he was convinced they would escape extreme consequences. Had he withheld it, however, his own promising fortunes would have been utterly ruined. What motive could any man possess but self-interest? Florentines engaged in petty political schemes might derive self-interest from family pride and ancient hatreds; a modern simpleton swallowing an old philosophy might mistake indigestion for divine influx; fanatics believing in the coming Scourge might find their interest in a future palm-branch. But no man of clear intellect allowed his course to be determined by such puerile impulses or questionable inward fumes. Did not Pontanus, poet and philosopher of unrivalled Latinity, deliver the finest oration at Naples welcoming the French king, who came to dethrone his royal friend and patron? And yet, Pontanus held his head high and prospered. Men did not truly care about such things unless their personal spleen was touched. Weakness alone was despised; power carried immunity. No man could mount high without incurring unpleasant necessities that laid him open to enmity, and perhaps to hissing, when enmity sought a pretext.

It was a faint prognostic of that hissing, gathered by Tito from indications during his audience, that gave his present conduct the character of an epoch. It was not that he was taking a deeper step in wrongdoing, for he felt no tie to the Mediceans stronger than that to his father. His conduct toward his father had been hidden by successful lying; his present act, however, admitted of no total concealment—it was, in its very nature, a revelation. Tito winced under this new liability to disesteem.

Very well. A little patience, and in another year, or perhaps half a year, he might turn his back on these hard, eager Florentines with their futile quarrels and sinking fortunes. His brilliant success in Florence had indeed possessed ugly flaws: he had fallen in love with the wrong woman, and Baldassarre had returned under incalculable circumstances. But as Tito galloped with a loose rein toward Siena, he saw a future ahead where he would no longer be haunted by those mistakes. He had much money safely out of Florence; he was in the fresh ripeness of twenty-eight years; he was conscious of well-tried skill. Could he not strip himself of the past, as of rehearsal clothing, and discard the old bundle to robe himself for the real scene?

It did not enter Tito's meditations on the future that, as he issued from the council-chamber and descended the stairs, he had brushed against a man whose face he had not paused to recognize in the lamplight. The man was Ser Ceccone—also willing to serve the State by providing information against unsuccessful employers.
