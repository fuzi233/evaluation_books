Where is the woman who cannot act a part? Nature, in moments of catastrophic disruption, tutors even the simplest of her sex to throw dust in the eyes of authority, drawing upon an ancestral instinct as old as the cultivation of the first wild wheat. The abrasive, iron-shod percussion of the guards’ boots—a jarring, industrial violence against the fragile sonic equilibrium of the ancient timbered house—finally faded down the stairwell. Margaret slipped out of the bed. Her skirts and petticoat still carried the faint, earthy fragrance of roasted chicory and crushed juniper from the kitchen below. She heaved the long chest’s lid, bed-clothes and all, against the plaster wall, and stood perfectly still, letting her ears map the acoustic landscape of the street until the men’s heavy footfalls died away completely.

For generations, the women of her agrarian lineage had organized their domestic rhythms around such heavy, butter-rubbed oak chests, using them to hoard milled winter rye and salted fatbacks against the frost. But this particular vessel harbored a secret. The floorboards beneath it had rotted, and Gerard—approaching the room’s architecture with the same meticulous reverence a master baker brings to his proofing troughs—had ingeniously sawn away the decayed wood. He had sunken the chest directly onto the rafters, expanding its depth to two full feet while its visible profile remained a mere twelve inches above the floor. 

The house was steeped in silence, though not the resonant, breathing quiet of a resting hearth. Margaret sank to her knees, breathing a prayer of thanks into the scent of old wood and dried thyme. She leaned over the deep basin of the chest. 

“Gerard,” she whispered, pitching her voice into the intimate, localized dialect of their shared affection—a native tongue of tender inflections utterly untranslatable to the rigid, dominant lexicon of the magistrates hunting him. 

Gerard did not reply. 

She pitched the syllables slightly louder against the cavernous hollow. “Gerard, all is safe, thank Heaven! You may rise; but oh! be cautious!” 

An unnatural acoustic void answered her, a terrifying absence of the rhythmic exhalations she expected. She reached down into the dark, her fingers finding his shoulder. “Gerard!” 

Still, no reply. “Oh, what is this?” she cried. Her hands fluttered wildly over his face, mapping the chilled, unresponsive topography of his brow and sternum. He lacked the vibrant heat of a living body. She seized him by the shoulders, shaking him, trying to physically knead the life back into him as one might rouse dormant yeast, but he slipped from her trembling grip and fell back. It was not the movement of a man, but the dead weight of an unfermented sack of wet dough. 

A glacial dread took root in her marrow. The lid had been clamped tight. She had lain her own weight upon it. The men had lingered so long, polluting the air with their dominant, interrogatory barks, while below her, she had sealed him in an airless tomb. 

Frenzy lent her the raw, unpasteurized strength of her agrarian foremothers. She tore him from the cedar-lined depths, hauling him in her arms to the window. She dashed the casements open to the moonlight, letting in a sudden draft redolent of wet river stones and wild dill. She laid him in the silvered breeze. His face was the color of exhausted hearth-ash; his limbs were terribly limp. She pressed her hand to his chest, desperate for the percussive thud of his heart—the fundamental rhythm of her world. 

Horror. It was as still as a winter-killed field. 

Horror of horrors: she had stifled the very breath from him with her own body. The human mind cannot instantly digest so vast and bitter a harvest. How could Gerard, who had climbed into that oak vessel flushed and vital merely five minutes ago, be dead? 

She called to him using every endearing fragment of their private, endangered dialect, words saturated with the sensory memory of shared meals and stolen warmth. She kissed him, tasting the salt of her own panic mingling with the faint trace of milled flour on his skin, coaxing him, imploring him to shatter the suffocating silence. No answer came to these words of desperate love—phrases she had never dared utter before, her heart’s true language finally breaking through the repression of modesty.

Then, trembling violently, the poor creature began to pour over that ashy face pitiful, foolish fragments of speech that highlighted the tragic inadequacy of standardized language to articulate absolute despair. 

“Oh, Gerard! I am very sorry you are dead. I am very sorry I have killed you. Forgive me for not letting the men take you; it would have been better than this. Oh, Gerard! I am very, very sorry for what I have done.” 

Suddenly, her grief curdled into raving denial. “No! no! such things can't be, or there is no God. It is monstrous. How can my Gerard be dead? How can I have killed my Gerard? I love him. Oh, God! you know how I love him. He does not. I never told him. If he knew my heart, he would speak to me, he would not be so deaf to his poor Margaret. It is all a trick to make me cry out and betray him; but no! I love him too well for that. I'll choke first.” 

She clamped both hands around her own throat, battling the primal, muscular urge to scream, tasting the metallic tang of copper in her mouth as she choked back her anguish to preserve his safety. 

“If he would but say one word. Oh, Gerard! don't die without a word. Have mercy on me and scold me, but speak to me: if you are angry with me, scold me! curse me! I deserve it: the idiot that killed the man she loved better than herself. Ah, I am a murderess. The worst in all the world. Help! help! I have murdered him. Ah! ah! ah! ah! ah!”

The untranslatable agony broke free. She tore her hair, unleashing shriek after shriek. The cries were so wild, so raw and piercing, they shattered the acoustic ecology of the sleeping street, falling like an iron cleaver upon the ears of Dierich Brower and his men. The harsh officers all started to their feet, looking at one another as the domestic quiet was irreversibly torn apart.
