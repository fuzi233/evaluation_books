A couple of my close pals snuck a peek at these scribbled pages when they were still messy handwritten drafts, and told me, to my great surprise, that they weren’t half bad. Even better, a handful of my relatives made a very solemn, very specific promise: if I ever actually got the thing published, they would buy a copy. (Full transparency: most of them already admitted they planned to use it to prop open a wobbly kitchen window or set under a teacup as a coaster.)

Given this overwhelming wave of public demand, I figured I had no right to drag my feet on releasing it any longer. If it weren’t for this tiny, enthusiastic group of cheerleaders, I doubt I’d ever have dared to serve up my silly little “idle thoughts” as proper mental nourishment for every English-speaking reader across the globe.

You see, these days everyone agrees that a good book’s job is to improve you, instruct you, and elevate your whole soul. This book wouldn’t elevate a dozy cow sunning itself in a clover field. I can’t in good conscience recommend it for any useful, self-improving purpose at all.

The only suggestion I will make? The second you get sick to death of slogging your way through that stuffy list of *The 100 Best Books You Absolutely Must Read*, pick this little volume up for half an hour. It’ll be a nice, brainless change.

Idle Thoughts of an Idle Fellow

Now, this is a topic I can confidently say I am a total world expert on. Back when I was a kid, the tutor who charged my dad nine guineas a term (no extra fees, mark you) used to say he’d never met a boy who could burn through twice the time doing half the work of anyone else. And I still remember my poor old grandma, mid-lesson on how to use the prayer book, offhandedly noting that she didn’t think I’d ever do much of the bad stuff I wasn’t supposed to do—but she was dead certain I’d skip every single chore and obligation I was supposed to get done.

Turns out I let her down on half that, oops. Heaven help me, I’ve done no shortage of dumb, irresponsible nonsense I had no business doing, even with my legendary laziness. But I 100% delivered on the other half of her prediction: I’ve neglected almost every task I ever should have finished, no exceptions. Being idle has always been my superpower, and I don’t take any credit for it—it’s a natural-born gift, rare as hen’s teeth. There are loads of lazy people plodding through life slow as molasses, but a genuine professional idler is a unicorn. He’s not the guy slouching around the street with his hands in his pockets looking like he’d rather die than move. No, his most unexpected quirk is that he always looks intensely busy, even when he’s doing absolutely nothing at all.

And here’s the big secret no one tells you: you can’t actually enjoy idling properly unless you’ve got a mountain of urgent work you’re supposed to be doing instead. Doing nothing when you have literally nothing to do? That’s not fun. It’s not a treat. It’s just a tedious, exhausting chore all on its own. Idleness, like a stolen kiss, only tastes sweet when you’re sneaking it when you shouldn’t.

Years ago when I was a young man, I got very sick—though personally I thought all I had was a beastly annoying cold. But the doctor acted like I was one foot in the grave, said I should have come to see him a whole month earlier, and if whatever it was had dragged on another week he wouldn’t have bet a penny on my survival. Funny how that works, right? You’ll never meet a doctor, no matter what’s wrong with you, who doesn’t say you were literally one day away from an incurable, hopeless case. Our medical savior is exactly like the hero in a cheap melodrama—he always bursts through the door in the very nick of time, no exceptions. Pure providence, that’s what that is.

Anyway, he packed me off to Buxton for a full month, with strict orders to do absolutely nothing the entire time. “Rest is all you need,” he said. “Perfect, total rest.” Sounded like the best news of my life. “This man gets it,” I thought, and pictured four whole glorious weeks of do-nothing bliss, with just a little dash of mild illness to make it feel poetic, not miserable. I’d get up at noon, sip hot chocolate, eat breakfast in my slippers and fluffy dressing gown. I’d hang in a hammock in the garden reading sad novels that end in heartbreak, until the book slips out of my limp hand and I just lie there staring up at the deep blue sky, watching puffy white clouds drift past like little sail ships, listening to birds sing and the leaves rustle. Or if I felt too wimpy to go outside, I’d prop myself up on pillows by the ground floor window, looking all pale and tragic so every pretty girl walking past would sigh at what a delicate interesting poet I was.

And twice a day I’d get wheeled down in a Bath chair to the Colonnade to drink the famous spa waters. Oh, those waters! Back then I thought “drinking the waters” sounded so fancy, all posh old Queen Anne vibes, and I was low-key excited to try it. Ugh. After three or four mornings, Sam Weller’s famous line about them tasting like warm flatirons barely even scratches the surface of how repulsive and sickly they were. If there was anything on earth that could make a sick man recover overnight, it’s knowing he has to chug a full glass of that gunk every single morning until he’s better. I drank them straight for six days, and they nearly finished the job my cold started. Then I figured out the hack: right after downing the nasty water, I’d knock back a full stiff glass of brandy and water. All the fancy doctors later told me the booze completely canceled out all the supposed mineral health benefits of the water. Great! That was the whole point.

But drinking those waters was nothing compared to how miserable that whole month of forced rest was—without a doubt the worst four weeks of my entire life. I followed the doctor’s orders to the letter for most of it, doing nothing but wander the house and garden, and get wheeled around for two hours a day in that Bath chair. That was a tiny bit fun, at first. You’d think riding around in a wheeled chair pushed by an old guy would be boring, but there’s a weird little edge of danger to it no one tells you about. Every single minute you’re fully convinced the whole rickety contraption is going to tip over, especially when you pass a ditch or a stretch of bumpy new gravel road. Every cart that drives past you, you’re sure it’s going to swerve and run you over. And any time you’re going up or down a hill, all you can think is, “What if this old man pushing me lets go of the handles?”

Even that novelty wore off eventually, and the boredom got so bad I swear my brain was starting to melt. It’s not a very tough brain to begin with, so around the 20th morning I got up early, ate a huge breakfast, and hiked straight off to Hayfield, the little town at the foot of Kinder Scout. It’s a cute busy little spot down a lovely valley, and back then there were two very pretty girls there—one smiled at me as we crossed paths on the bridge, the other was standing in a doorway planting kisses on a chubby red-faced baby. It’s been ages, though, so I expect they’re both stout and cranky by now. On the walk back I saw an old man by the side of the road breaking stones for the highway, and the urge to use my muscles was so strong I bought him a drink to let me take the hammer. He was nice enough to indulge me, and I wailed on those stones with three whole weeks of pent-up energy, doing more work in half an hour than he’d gotten done all day. He didn’t even get mad.

After that I went full rebel against the “do nothing” order, going on long walks every morning and hanging out to listen to the band play in the pavilion every night. But the days still dragged by, and I was over the moon when the month ended, and I was on the train leaving stuffy, sickly Buxton behind to head back to London, all noise and work and life. I stared out the carriage window as we rolled through Hendon at dusk, and the orange smog glow hanging over the huge city felt warm and welcoming. When my cab rumbled out of St. Pancras station, that familiar loud roar of the city swelled up around me, and it sounded like the best music I’d heard in months.

I didn’t enjoy that month of idling at all, and that’s the thing: I only like idling when I’m not supposed to be idling. Not when it’s the only thing I have to do. That’s just my stubborn nature. The absolute best time to lean against the mantelpiece calculating how much money I owe everyone is when my desk is piled high with letters I have to answer before the post goes out that night. The time I drag out my dinner the longest, dawdling over every bite, is when I’ve got a huge pile of work waiting for me once I finish. And if for some urgent reason I have to wake up extra early the next day? That’s exactly when I love to roll over and squeeze in that extra half hour in bed more than any other time.

Oh, that five more minutes nap when you’re supposed to get up! Is there any living person, besides the impossibly perfect hero of some pious Sunday school story for boys, who actually gets out of bed willingly? There are loads of us who just physically cannot wake up on time no matter how hard we try. If we’re supposed to be up at 8, we’ll sleep till 8:30. If suddenly 8:30 is early enough, we’ll sleep till 9. We’re exactly like that famous politician who was reliably half an hour late to every single event. We try every trick. We buy fancy alarm clocks—those evil little gadgets that always go off at the wrong time, wake the cat, and leave us snoring right through them. We tell the maid Sarah Jane to bang on our door and yell at us to get up, and she does bang, and we mumble “mmhm coming” and then snuggle right back under the covers. I knew one guy who actually dragged himself out of bed to take an ice cold shower, and even that didn’t work—he immediately jumped back into bed to warm up afterward.

I think if I could just get my whole body out of bed I could stay up, no problem. But the worst part is prying my head off the pillow. No amount of determined swearing the night before makes that any easier. I’ll waste the whole evening messing around, then say to myself “fine, I’ll quit work now and wake up super early tomorrow to finish everything.” And at 11pm I’m 100% serious about it. Then the morning comes, and I think “well, that was a dumb plan. I definitely should’ve just stayed up late last night instead.” And then you start thinking about how much of a hassle getting dressed is, and the longer you think about it the more you want to put it off.

Bed is such a weird magical thing, right? That little fake grave where we stretch our tired limbs and sink down into quiet rest. As poor Thomas Hood wrote: “O bed, O bed, delicious bed, that heaven on earth to the weary head.” It’s the kindest old nurse to all of us, all the grumpy tired people. Smart or dumb, good or bad, it tucks us into its soft lap and hush us when we’re cranky and sad. The stressed out worker, the aching sick person, the little girl sobbing because her boyfriend dumped her—we all lay our tired heads on its white soft pillows, and it soothes us right to sleep. The only time we know real misery is when it won’t comfort us, when we can’t fall asleep. Those feverish nights tossing and turning, staring into the dark, waiting for the sun to come up? They last forever. Those even worse nights when you’re sitting up with a sick loved one, every little spark of the fire makes you jump, and the tick of the clock sounds like a hammer thumping slow as the seconds drag by? Pure torture.

But enough gushing about beds. Even for a professional idler like me, I’ve rambled on about them too long. Let’s get up, go outside, and have a smoke. Wasting time with a pipe is just as effective, and it looks way less lazy than hanging around in bed all day. Tobacco is the single greatest blessing ever given to us idlers. I can’t even imagine what poor civil servants did with all their free time back before Sir Walter Raleigh brought smoking over from the Americas. I’m fully convinced all the medieval knights were so quarrelsome and always picking fights purely because they didn’t have cigarettes. They had no work to do, no way to chill out, so they were constantly brawling. If there wasn’t a proper war going on, they’d start a deadly generations-long feud with the neighbors, and if they still had extra time left over, they’d argue over whose girlfriend was prettiest, and settle the debate by bashing each other’s heads in with battle axes. They settled art criticism real fast back in the 12th century. A boy wouldn’t stare into a girl’s eyes and tell her she was too beautiful for this world. He’d say “hold my ale, I’m gonna go prove it.” If he met another guy outside and bashed his head in, that meant his girlfriend was the prettier one. If the other guy bashed his head in? Well, his girlfriend was ugly, the other guy’s girlfriend was way better. These days we just light a pipe, sit back, and let the girls sort that argument out all by themselves. They’re very good at it.

Women are doing all our work for us these days, anyway. They’re doctors, lawyers, artists. They run theaters, pull off elaborate investment scams, edit newspapers. I’m looking forward to the future soon, where us men don’t have a single job to do. We’ll sleep till noon, read two trashy novels a day, have fancy little 5 o’clock teas all to ourselves, and the hardest thought we’ll have to think about all day is debating the latest men’s trouser trends, and arguing over what exactly Mr. Jones’ new coat was made of, and whether it fits him even a little bit. It’s a glorious future to look forward to—for every idle fellow who’s ever lived.

You’ve been in love, of course! C’mon, don’t act so shy. If you haven’t? Don’t fret—it’s coming for you, no dodging it. Love’s exactly like the measles, isn’t it? We all have to get through the fever, the goopy eyes, the ridiculous urge to do stupid, giddy things. And just like the measles? You only catch it once. No one ever needs to panic about a second bout. The bloke who’s already had love can waltz straight into the most supposedly dangerous situations and pull the dumbest, foolhardiest stunts perfectly safely, no more worried than you’d be walking past a plate of leftover pork pies at your best mate’s house.

He can picnic in a shady wood, ramble down leafy country lanes, linger on a mossy stone gawping at the sunset without turning all moony. A quiet country house full of unmarried cousins and their chaperones? He fears that no more than his own messy, tobacco-smelling club armchair. He can tag along on a family Rhine cruise without catching feelings for the eldest daughter. He can even follow a mate all the way into the jaws of a wedding ceremony to wave him off, and leave completely unsmitten. He can whirl through a ravishing waltz with the prettiest girl at the ball, rest afterwards in a drafty dark conservatory, and the only thing he catches is a head cold, no lasting heartache attached. He can brave a moonlight stroll down a jasmine-scented lane, or a twilight row through the rushy river banks. He can clamber over a stile without tripping, scramble through a thorny hedge without getting snagged, slither down a muddy path without faceplanting in the muck. He can look straight into someone’s bright sunny eyes and not get dazzled, listen to siren voices and sail on with his helm nailed straight, clasp soft white hands in his and not get zapped by some weird electric magic that pins him there forever.

No, we never sicken with love twice. Cupid doesn’t waste a second arrow on the same heart. Love’s handmaidens—respect, admiration, quiet affection—those are our lifelong mates, we can keep the front door propped open for them forever. But their big fancy celestial boss, that royal Cupid fellow? He only pays one grand visit, tips his hat, and vanishes. We like people, we cherish them, we’re very, very fond of them—but we never fall that mad, head-over-heels all-consuming love again. A man’s heart is a firework that only shoots heavenward once. For a split second it blazes like a meteor, lighting up the whole entire world with its glow. Then the dark old boring routine of everyday life slams back down around it, and the burnt out husk falls back to earth, lying unused, slowly smoldering to ash. Once, we break free of all our boring little prison chains, just like mighty Prometheus, climb Olympus and steal a spark straight from Phoebus’s sun chariot. Happy are the folks who scramble back down fast before that divine flame dies out, and light their own little home hearth with it.

Love is far too pure and sharp a light to burn long in our stuffy, smoky everyday air, but before it chokes out, we can use it as a torch to ignite the warm, cozy little fire of affection. And honestly? That steady, warming glow is way better suited to our tiny, drafty little back-parlor of a world than that wild, blazing love fire. Love should be the sacred vestal flame in some huge, grand old temple, where the organ music is the whole universe rolling and rumbling. But affection? That burns bright and cheery long after love’s sharp white flame has flickered out. It’s a fire you can stoke every single day, pile higher and higher as the cold winter years creep up. Old couples can sit by it holding their gnarled thin hands, the kids can curl up right in front of the hearth, your friend and neighbor always have a welcome spot by the fire, even shaggy old Fido the dog and sleek purring Titty the cat can snuggle up to toast their cold little noses on the bars. Heap the coals of kindness on that fire, throw on your pleasant little words, your gentle hand squeezes, your thoughtful unselfish little deeds. Fan it with good humor, patience, and letting the small stupid stuff go. Then the wind can howl outside, the rain can lash the windows, and you won’t care a bit. Your hearth will be warm and bright, and the faces sitting round it will make their own sunshine no matter how cloudy it gets outside.

I hate to burst your bubble, dear Edwin and Angelina, but you two lovebirds expect way too much from that new little wobbly flicker you call passion. You think your tiny little hearts have enough fuel to feed that ravenous, fire-breathing love for your whole entire lives? Ah, you silly young things! Don’t bet on that unsteady little flame. It’ll dwindle down month by month, and there’s no topping up the fuel tank. You’ll watch it sputter out all cross and disappointed, and each of you will be convinced the other one’s gone cold. Edwin will get all bitter and sore when Angelina no longer runs to the gate all grinning and blushing to meet him. When he comes down with a cough these days, she doesn’t burst into tears, wrap her arms round his neck and wail that she can’t live a second without him. No, the best she offers is a peppermint lozenge, and even that in a huffy tone that makes it obvious she’s just sick of listening to him hack. Poor Angelina, too, will shed quiet little tears into her tea, because Edwin has stopped tucking her old crumpled handkerchief into the inner pocket of his waistcoat like it’s the most precious thing he owns. Both of you will gawp at how terrible the other one’s gotten, and neither of you will notice you’ve changed too. If you did, you wouldn’t be so miserable. You’d blame the right thing—just how small and silly regular old human nature is, hold hands over your shared dumbness, and build your little home on a sturdier, earthier foundation. But we’re all like that, aren’t we? Blind as bats to our own faults, but got eagle eyes for everyone else’s. Everything that goes wrong is always the other guy’s fault. Angelina swears she’d have loved Edwin forever and a day if only Edwin hadn’t gotten all weird and different. Edwin would have worshiped Angelina for all eternity if only she’d stayed exactly the same as the first day he saw her. It’s a rotten, cold hour for you both when the lamp of love has gone out, and you haven’t lit the fire of affection yet, and you’re groping around in the cold raw dark trying to get a spark going. God grant you get that fire lit before the sun goes down. Too many couples just sit there shivering in front of a pile of dead cold coals until it’s too late.

But what’s the use of preaching? Who that feels the rush of young love thundering through their veins could ever believe it’ll one day flow slow and feeble? To a lad of twenty, it’s impossible to imagine he won’t love as wildly at sixty as he does right now. He can’t name a single middle-aged or elderly gentleman he knows who’s still got that frantic puppy attachment, but that doesn’t shake his total belief that he’s the exception. His love will never fade, whoever else’s does. Nobody ever loved as he loves, so of course all the rest of the world’s boring old experience doesn’t apply to him. Alas, alas! Before he hits thirty, he’s joined the ranks of the sneerers. It’s not his fault. All our passions, good and bad, fade out the second our blushes do. We don’t hate, grieve, rejoice, or despair in our thirties half as hard as we did in our teens. Disappointment no longer makes us want to fling ourselves off a bridge, and we drink in success without getting stupidly drunk on it. We take all things in a minor key as we get older. There are very few grand, majestic moments in the later acts of life’s little opera. Ambitionaims for something far less grand. Honor gets a lot more reasonable, and conveniently bends itself to fit the circumstances. And love? Love dies. That irreverence for youth’s silly dreams creeps like a killing frost across our hearts. The tender little shoots and opening flowers get nipped and withered, and of that vine that once yearned to wrap its tendrils round the whole world, all that’s left is a dry, sapless stump.

I know my fair lady readers will call all this rank heresy. To hear them talk, it’s not until a man’s got a whole head of gray hair that his sweet nothings are even worth listening to. Young ladies get their ideas of men from the novels written by other women, and compared to the absurd “heroes” that masquerade as men in those nightmare books, Pythagoras’s plucked “man is the only animal that laughs” bird and Frankenstein’s monster were perfectly average, decent specimens of humanity. In those so-called stories, the leading lover, that “Greek god” everyone gushes over—by the way, no one ever bothers to say which Greek god he looks like! It could be hump-backed Vulcan, or two-faced Janus, or even dribbling old Silenus, the god of weird, unknowable mysteries. He’s just as big a scoundrel as the whole lot of them, so I suppose that’s what they mean. He doesn’t even have the little bit of manliness his classical prototypes had, though—he’s a listless, floppy effeminate noodle, pushing forty, already bored of everything. But oh! The depth and strength of this old bore’s “fervent emotion” for some sixteen-year-old schoolgirl still wet behind the ears! Hide your heads, you silly young Romeos and Leanders! This world-weary old beau loves with such hysterical, over-the-top passion that he needs four adjectives for every single noun to properly describe it.

It’s a lucky thing for us old sinners that you ladies only get your ideas from books. If you read real men, you’d know that a shy lad stammering over his words tells a far truer tale than all our smooth, bold grown-up eloquence. A boy’s love spills out of a full, overflowing heart. A grown man’s “passion” is far more often the result of a full stomach, and too much boredom. Compared to the rushing, gushing fountain that bursts up when a boy’s heart gets struck with that heavenly spark, a grown man’s slow sluggish trickle doesn’t even deserve to be called love. If you want to taste real love, drink from that pure clear stream youth pours right at your feet. Don’t wait until it’s turned into a muddy, mucky river before you stoop to dip your cup. Are you supposed to like that bitter mucky flavor? Does the clear limpid water taste too plain for your fancy, so you prefer the gunk it picks up later? Must we really believe all the silly folks who say that a hand covered in the grime of a wasted, shameful life is the only one a young girl wants to be caressed by? That’s the garbage those yellow-covered romance novels yell out day after day. Do those devil’s little helpers ever stop to think what mischief they’re doing, crawling around God’s garden and telling silly Eves and Adams that sin is fun, and decency is ridiculous and boring? How many innocent girls do they warp into mean, evil-minded women? How many weak lads do they point down the dirty little shortcut and tell them that’s the fastest way to win a girl’s heart? They don’t even write life as it really is. If you just told the truth, right would take care of itself. But their stories are just coarse, dauby paintings made up from their own sick, overactive imaginations.

We ought to think of women not as the sirens luring men to ruin, but as good angels pulling us upwards. They have way more power for good or evil than they ever dream. Right at the exact age when a man’s character is hardening into shape, he tumbles head over heels in love, and the lass he adores can make him or break him. Without even trying, he molds himself into whatever he thinks she wants him to be, for better or worse. I’m sorry to be ungentlemanly enough to say it, but I don’t think women always use that power for the best. Too often their whole world is bounded tight by boring, tiny little commonplace rules. Their ideal hero is a little man of no ambition, and loads of brilliant, powerful minds get wasted forever because they’re enchanted by love and shrunk down to fit that tiny mold. And yet, ladies, you could make us all so much better if you only wanted to. It’s up to you far more than all the preachers put together, to roll this whole world a little closer to heaven. Chivalry isn’t dead! It’s just asleep, because no one’s given it any work to do. You’re the ones who have to wake it up to noble deeds. You have to be worthy of that knightly worship. You have to be higher than us. It was for Una that the Red Cross Knight went off to war. No painted, simpering court lady could ever have gotten him to slay that dragon. Oh, fair ladies, be fair in mind and soul as well as face, so brave knights can win glory fighting for you! Oh women, throw off those silly disguises of selfishness, boldness, and fake affectation! Step forward once more as a queen, in your royal robe of simple, uncomplicated purity. A thousand swords that are now rusting away in lazy disgrace will leap out of their scabbards to fight for your honor against all that’s wrong. A thousand Sir Rolands will lower their lances, and Fear, Avarice, Pleasure, and Ambition will all go crashing down into the dust under your banner.

What noble deeds weren’t we ready for back when we were in love? What grand, good lives couldn’t we have lived, just for her sake? Our love was a religion we would have happily died for. It wasn’t just any ordinary human we worshiped. It was a queen we bowed to, a goddess we adored. And how madly we worshiped! And how sweet it was to worship! Ah lad, hold tight to that young love dream while it lasts! You’ll soon find out how right little Tom Moore was when he said there’s nothing half so sweet in life. Even when it brings you misery, that misery is wild and romantic, nothing like the dull, boring worldly pain of later heartaches. When you’ve lost her—when the light’s gone out of your life and the whole world stretches out before you like a long dark horror—even then, a little bit of that magic mixes in with your despair. And who wouldn’t risk all that terror just to get a taste of those raptures? Oh, what raptures they were! Even the memory of it makes you tingle. How delicious it was to tell her you loved her, that you lived for her, that you’d die for her! How you ranted, you fool! What floods of ridiculous, over-the-top nonsense you poured out! And oh, how cruel it was of her to pretend she didn’t believe a word of it! How awestruck you were of her! How miserable you got when you accidentally offended her! And yet, how lovely it was to get scolded by her, and beg for forgiveness without even having the faintest clue what you did wrong! How dark the whole world got when she snubbed you (the little rascal, she did it on purpose just to watch you look miserable)! And how bright and sunny everything got the second she smiled! How jealous you were of every single person near her! You hated every man she shook hands with, every woman she kissed, the maid who did her hair, the boy who polished her shoes, even the little dog she fussed over—though you had to be extra polite to the last one, obviously. How you’d count the minutes till you could see her, then when you did see her you turned stupid and just stared at her without saying a single word! It was impossible for you to leave the house at any time of day or night without somehow ending up loitering opposite her front windows. You didn’t have the guts to go knock, but you’d hang around the corner staring at the brickwork, daydreaming the house would catch fire (it was insured, so no harm done) and you could rush in and rescue her, get all horribly burned and injured, and be her hero forever. Anything, to do something for her. Even the tiny little things were so sweet. How you’d trail after her like a spaniel, trying to guess her tiniest wish before she even said it! How proud you were to do exactly what she told you! How lovely it was to be bossed around by her! Devoting your whole life to her and never thinking of yourself seemed like the easiest thing in the world. You’d skip your holiday just to bring her a silly little cheap gift, and you felt more than repaid if she just deigned to say thank you. Everything she touched was precious to you—her little kid glove, the ribbon she wore in her hair, the rose that once nestled behind her ear, whose crumpled dried leaves are still marking the pages of that poetry book you never, ever open now.

And oh, how beautiful she was! Wondrously beautiful! It felt like an angel had just walked into the room, and everyone else around suddenly looked plain and boringly human. She was too sacred to even touch. It almost felt like a crime to stare too hard at her. You’d sooner have thought to kiss her as you would to belt out a rowdy comic song in the middle of a cathedral. It was almost desecration just to kneel and timidly lift that soft little hand of hers to your lips. Ah, those foolish days—those foolish days when we were unselfish and pure hearted; those foolish days when our simple little hearts were full of truth, faith, and reverence! Those foolish days of grand, noble longings and strivings! And oh, these so-called wise, clever days we live in now, when we all know money’s the only thing worth chasing, when we don’t believe in anything but meanness and lies, and we don’t care about any living soul but ourselves!

I’ll be the first to admit I love a good intentional melancholy, and there’s a weird cozy satisfaction in wallowing on purpose through properly miserable moods you choose for yourself. But that uninvited, random thing we call the blues? Nobody likes that.

And the worst part? Everyone gets them, and no one can explain why they show up. There’s zero accounting for them. You’re just as likely to get smacked full in the face with a blues spell the day after you win a giant lottery payout as you are the day you left your brand-new fancy silk umbrella on the train. The feeling hits like a triple threat of a throbbing toothache, brutal post-kebab indigestion, and a fully blocked head cold all rolling through you at once. You turn into a total mess: slow, jittery, snappy. Rude to random strangers, actively dangerous to your closest mates. Clumsy, overemotional for no reason, and itching to pick stupid tiny fights over nothing. A full nuisance to yourself and every poor sod within 10 feet.

While the spell’s on you, you can’t do a single useful thing or think a single coherent thought, but you’re absolutely convinced you have to do something, right now. You can’t sit still, so you yank your hat on and stomp out for a walk, but before you even get to the end of your front path you regret every decision that led you out the door, and trudge right back inside. You grab a book to distract yourself, and suddenly Shakespeare is boring and generic, Dickens rambles forever without getting to the point, Thackeray is a total drag, even Carlyle is far too sappy for you. You hurl the book across the room and yell very rude names at the dead author. Then you shoo the cat right off the couch and slam the door after it like it personally ruined your whole week.

You think, fine, I’ll catch up on my letters! You sit there for 15 whole minutes staring at the page, having gotten no further than “Dearest Auntie, I have five free minutes so I thought I’d dash you a note” and can’t think of a single other sentence. So you crumple the paper up, fling the still dripping pen right onto the tablecloth, and decide you’ll pop round to see the Thompsons instead. Halfway through pulling on your gloves, though, you go wait a second: the Thompsons are total idiots. They never put out a proper spread for guests, and they’re definitely gonna make you bounce their teething baby around for an hour. You curse the Thompsons to hell and back and flop back down on the couch.

At that point you’re fully defeated. You bury your face in your hands and decide you’d honestly rather just die and go to heaven right now. You daydream your own deathbed, all your friends and family crowded around you sobbing their eyes out. You give them all your last blessings, especially the pretty young ones, obviously. You tell yourself they’ll realize how awesome you were the second you’re gone, kicking themselves for taking you for granted when you were alive, and you do a little bitter mental compare and contrast between that hypothetical posthumous worship and the fact that right now none of them even text you back within 24 hours.

That little daydream cheers you up for, like, 90 seconds max. Then your brain snaps to: wait, I’m an idiot, no one would be sad if I dropped dead tomorrow. Who actually cares (and for that matter, how much actual care is “two straws” worth, anyway?) if you blow up in a freak accident, get hanged, get married, or fall off a ferry and drown? Nobody. Nobody’s ever properly appreciated you, you’ve never gotten the credit you deserve for a single thing your whole life. You replay every second of your past, and it’s crystal clear you’ve been getting raw deals since the day you popped out of the cradle.

Half an hour of that nonsense has you frothing mad at every living thing on the planet, especially yourself, who you’d absolutely kick if your legs could bend that far. Finally bedtime rolls around, just in time to stop you from doing something properly daft. You sprint up the stairs, fling your clothes every which way across the floor, blow out the candle, and launch yourself into bed like you bet a guy 20 quid you could do the whole getting undressed routine in 12 seconds flat. Then you toss and turn for two whole hours, alternating between yanking all the covers off and then getting too cold and dragging them all back on again. Eventually you pass out into a jittery bad-dream filled sleep, and wake up 3 hours late the next morning.

That’s the single guy’s blues routine, anyway. Married blokes? Oh, they’ve got an upgrade. They bully their wives, complain the roast is overcooked, and demand the kids go straight to bed at 7pm for no reason. Kicking up a whole ruckus around the house must be such a good release, right? Because the only kind of fun a bloke in the blues can stand is picking stupid little fights with everyone nearby.

Everyone calls this dumb mood something different, y’know? Poets call it “a soft sadness that comes over me.” Your average cockney bloke named ’Arry will lean in to his mate Jimee and say he’s got the blooming hump. Your little sister will say she doesn’t know what’s wrong with her tonight, she just feels all out of sorts and hopes nothing terrible is about to happen. That posh young fella you run into at the pub will greet you all “so glad to see you old pal, I’ve been feeling jolly miserable all evening.” Me? I just say I’ve got a weird unsettled feeling, and I think I’ll pop out for a bit.

Funny thing, the blues never show up when the sun’s out. When the whole world’s zooming around busy and full of life, you don’t have time to mope and sigh. The racket of the workday drowns out those little gremlins in your head that keep humming sad little funeral dirges under their breath. During the day you get mad, or disappointed, or furious, but you never get this specific blues vibe. If something goes wrong at 10am, you swear and kick the leg of the table. If the exact same bad thing happens at 10pm? You sit in the dark, read poetry, and go on a whole 45 minute tangent about how the whole world is hollow and meaningless.

And it’s almost never actual real trouble that makes you feel this way. Real misery is too harsh for that fancy sentimental wallowing. We love to linger and cry over a sad painting, but if we saw that same sad scene play out in real life we’d look away immediately. Real grief doesn’t feel nice, no one chooses to snuggle up to a sharp stab wound or a gnawing pain on purpose. When someone keeps going back to dwell on an old sad memory, making sure it stays sharp and clear? You can bet it doesn’t hurt them anymore. Whatever pain they went through at the start, the memory’s turned into something soft and pleasant now.

All those dear old ladies who keep tiny baby shoes in lavender-scented drawers, crying when they think of their kids' tiny feet that don’t toddle anywhere anymore? And those sweet young girls who keep a lock of hair under their pillow, from the boy who drowned at sea? They’ll call me a horrible cynical brute and say I’m talking rubbish, but I bet if they’re honest with themselves, they don’t hate going back to those memories. Tears can be just as sweet as laughter, for some folks. That old chronicler Froissart said the Englishman takes his pleasures sadly, and the Englishwoman goes one step further: she finds her pleasure in sadness itself.

I’m not making fun of that, not at all. I’d never mock anything that keeps people’s hearts soft in this rough old world. Us men are already dry and practical enough for everyone, we don’t need women to turn out the same. No no, ladies, stay soft and sentimental like you are - you’re the creamy butter on our boring dry bread. Sentimentality is to you what silly jokes and pints at the pub are to us. You don’t care about our dumb humor, it’d be dead unfair to take your little sad happy moments away from you. And who’s to say that hunched over cackling, red-faced, screaming with laughter is any more intelligent a kind of happiness than leaning your soft cheek on your hand, eyes misty with gentle tears, looking back through the hazy years at the old days that are long gone?

I love seeing people treat Regret like an old friend, because that means all the salt’s gone out of those old tears, the sharp sting is gone from that Sorrow before anyone dares hold it close. When you can look back at a pain that once made you faint, and not feel any bitterness or despair pop up in your chest? That’s Time’s healing hand at work. The old burden isn’t heavy anymore, you just feel that soft sweet mix of fondness and pity that you get when you read about good old Colonel Newcome saying “adsum” at the great final roll call, or Tom and Maggie Tulliver holding hands through all the years that kept them apart, going down locked together under the flooding Floss river.

Speaking of Tom and Maggie Tulliver, that makes me think of that perfect line George Eliot wrote, the one about the sadness of a summer evening. It’s so true, exactly the kind of quiet genius she always had. Who hasn’t felt that weird magic soft sad vibe when those summer sunsets drag on forever? The whole world belongs to Melancholy then, that thoughtful soft-eyed girl who hates the bright harsh glare of midday. She doesn’t come out till the light fades, the crows are flying home to their nests in the rocky woods, and twilight wraps everything up. Her home is in that dim soft in-between place, that’s where she finds you. She takes your hand, and walks you through that quiet magic world. You can’t see her face, but you swear you can hear the soft rustle of her wings.

Even in the loud boring city, you can feel her. There’s that quiet soft hush over the long dull streets, the dark river slips ghostlike under the black bridges like it’s hiding some huge secret under its muddy waves. Out in the country, when the hedges and trees turn all dim and blurry as night creeps in, bats flutter right past your face, and the land rail’s call drifts far across the fields all mournful? That feeling sinks even deeper. It feels like you’re standing next to some invisible deathbed, and the swaying elm trees are sighing along with the dying day.

A soft solemn sadness hangs over everything, and there’s this huge quiet peace all around you. All the stupid little workday worries shrink down to nothing, and suddenly bread and cheese - heck, even kisses - don’t feel like the only things worth chasing. You get all these thoughts you can’t put into words, that just drift into your head, and you stand there in the stillness under the darkening sky, and you realize you’re bigger than all your silly tiny little daily problems. Wrapped in that dim dusky veil, the world isn’t just a shabby little workshop where you go to grind out work all day. It’s a grand old temple, where you can stop and worship, and sometimes in that dim quiet, your groping hands brush against God's.

Funny how the best laid idle plans go up in smoke, isn't it? I plopped down at my desk fully intending to churn out something sharp, original, the sort of quip people quote over dinner and go "that fellow's got a real brain on him!" But try as I might, not a single clever, original thought will bubble up. Not right now, anyway. The only thing my whole brain can fixate on is being skint.

I reckon it's all because I've got both hands stuffed down my trouser pockets this very second. It's my default pose, really. The only time I yank them out is when I'm in the company of sisters, cousins, or aunts, who kick up such a ferocious fuss about it—they frame it as polite, eloquent remonstration, of course—that I have to give in and extract my poor cold hands. Their whole big gripe? It's not gentlemanly.

Now hang on, all you sticklers for proper behavior, let's talk. I get why shoving your hands in *other people's* pockets would be deemed ungentlemanly—especially by the person whose pocket it is, fair play. But how, pray tell, does putting your hands in your own pockets make you any less of a gentleman? Maybe they're right, though, now I think on it. I have seen some old gents grumble like they're about to combust when they root around in there for loose change. But us young folks? We're never comfortable unless our hands are in our pockets. We get all twitchy, shifty, like a music hall Lion Comique who's been stripped of his opera hat, the poor sod. But stuff our hands in our pockets? Tuck a little loose change in the right one, a jangling bunch of keys in the left? Suddenly we're brave enough to stare down a no-nonsense female post office clerk without breaking a sweat.

Trouble is, when your pockets are totally empty, even having your hands in them feels daft. Years back, there were days my entire worldly capital added up to what Londoners call a bob: a whole single shilling. And I'd recklessly blow a penny of it just so I had a pocket full of jangling coppers left. Eleven pennies jangling in your pocket feels a million times richer than one lonesome whole shilling sitting silent. If I was one of those poser la-di-da broke lads we all love to sneer at? I'd even change that single penny into two ha'pennies just to make more noise.

Oh, I'm fully qualified to talk about being hard up, no two ways about it. I've been a provincial stage actor, for starters. If you need more proof than that? Fine, I'll add that I once worked as a "gentleman connected with the press"—you know the type. I've lived on 15 shillings a week. I've survived a whole seven days on ten shillings, owing the other five to my landlady. Hell, I once went a full fortnight living off nothing but a greatcoat.

Being properly skint gives you a PhD in domestic economy, no exams required. If you ever want to learn the actual, real value of a penny? Go live on 15 shillings a week, and see how much you can scrape aside for new collars and a pint out. You'll learn real quick it's worth loitering three minutes at the counter waiting for your farthing change, it's worth walking a full mile to save a single penny, that a glass of beer is a luxury you only get once a fortnight if you're lucky, and that a single cotton collar can be stretched to four whole days of wear if you're gentle with it.

Try that before you get married. Brilliant practice. Make your son do that before you send him off to college, and he'll never grumble about a hundred a year pocket money ever again. There's a whole crowd of people this would do the world of good for. Take that delicate hothouse flower who turns his nose up at any claret that's not vintage 94, who'd sooner eat cat meat than plain roast mutton. You run into those poor wretches every so often, though to humanity's credit, most of them only exist in the fancy drawing rooms of lady novelists. Every time I hear one of those blokes whinging over a restaurant menu, I get this overwhelming urge to drag him to some grubby East End pub, shove a sixpenny dinner down his throat: fourpenny beefsteak pudding, penny potatoes, penny half pint of porter. The memory of that (the mix of beer smoke, tobacco, and roast pork sticks with you, trust me) might make him stop turning his nose up at every plate put in front of him for a good long while. Then there's that other generous sod, every cadger's favorite, who's all free with tipping waiters shillings but somehow never remembers to pay back the five quid he borrowed off you three months ago. Even he'd learn a bit of common sense living on 15 bob a week.

"I always tip the waiter a shilling," some young government clerk told me over lunch in Regent Street the other day. "Can't give the poor bloke less, you know." I nodded along, agreed it would be practically impossible to hand him elevenpence ha'penny with a straight face—but I also made a mental note to lure him one day to that grimy little eating house near Covent Garden I know, where the waiters work in their shirt sleeves, and those sleeves are caked in grease by the end of the month. I know that waiter. If my fancy clerk friend tips him more than a penny? That bloke will grab his hand and shake it like they're long lost brothers, I guarantee it.

Lots of jokes get told about being skint, but the real thing isn't funny at all. It's not funny haggling over pennies with the baker. It's not funny when everyone thinks you're just a mean, stingy sod. It's not funny wearing a coat with a frayed cuff and lying about your address because you're too ashamed to say where you live. No, poverty's not a single bit funny—to the people living it. For a sensitive soul, it's hell on earth. Many a brave man who'd cheerfully take on all twelve of Hercules' labors has had his heart broken by these tiny, constant miseries.

It's not the actual physical discomfort that's the hard part, you see. Who'd mind roughing it a bit if that was all? Robinson Crusoe never cared if there was a patch on his trousers—did he even wear trousers? I can't remember, in the pantomimes he just wanders around half naked, right? He didn't care if his toes poked through his boots, or if his umbrella was cheap cotton as long as it kept the rain off. His shabbiness never bothered him, because there were no mates around to sneer at him.

Being poor is nothing. The sting is everyone else knowing you're poor. It's not the cold that makes the man without a greatcoat hurry down the street at double speed. It's not even all shame at lying badly (and he knows no one believes him) when he tells you greatcoats are unhealthy and he never carries an umbrella on principle. People say poverty's no crime, and that's true—if it was a crime, no one would be embarrassed by it. But it's a stupid blunder, and you get punished for it every single time. A poor man gets despised all over the world, equally by pious Christians and snobby lords, by radical demagogues and stuck-up footmen. No amount of copy-book maxims written for ink-stained schoolkids will make anyone treat him with respect.

Human opinion runs entirely on appearances. That man who'll happily walk arm in arm down Piccadilly with London's most notorious scamp, as long as the scamp is wearing a nice tailored suit? He'll duck down a back alley just to avoid saying two words to a shabby-looking gent. And the shabby gent knows that better than anyone. He'll walk a mile out of his way to avoid running into someone he used to know when he had money. His old friends don't even need to awkwardly look the other way—he's a thousand times more desperate not to be seen by them than they are to avoid him. And the last thing on earth he wants is their pitying offers of help. All he asks is to be forgotten, and more often than not, he gets exactly what he wants.

Time, that old homeopathic quack doctor, gets you used to being hard up just like it gets you used to everything else. You can spot at a glance the difference between the old hand who's been juggling empty pockets for years, and the new poor bloke, the novice, who's constantly panicking that everyone can see how broke he is. Nothing shows that difference clearer than how they pawn their watch. As the poet almost said: "True ease in pawning comes from art, not chance."

The old pro walks right into his local "uncle's" (that's pawn shop slang, for the uninitiated) as calmly as he'd walk into his tailor's, probably calmer. The assistant greets him like an old mate, serves him right away, much to the irritation of the lady in the next booth who huffs that she doesn't mind waiting "if it's a regular customer, obviously." The whole transaction is so friendly and businesslike, you'd think he was buying a big batch of government stock, not hocking his watch for half a crown.

Compare that to the bloke doing his first ever "pop." A schoolboy popping his first awkward question to a pretty girl is a paragon of confidence next to him. He loiters outside the pawn shop for twenty minutes, drawing the eye of every local loafer, and making the beat copper suspicious that he's about to burgle the place. Finally he spends ages staring at the window display, pretending to browse like he's there to buy a diamond bracelet or some other trivial luxury, then swaggers in like he's a member of the criminal gentry. He mumbles so quiet no one can hear a word he says, has to repeat everything three times. When he finally gets around to mentioning he wants to "borrow some money on a friend's watch," the shopkeeper just points him up the court to the right, first door round the corner. He stumbles back out red as a tomato, so flustered you could light a cigarette off his face, 100% convinced every single person on the street is staring at him. By the time he gets to the actual pawn booth, he's forgotten his own name and address, he's a total bumbling mess. When the attendant barks at him "where'd you get this?" he stammers, contradicts himself three times, and it's a miracle he doesn't blurt out that he stole it that very morning. Half the time he gets kicked out before he even gets the money, and he comes to his senses three miles away, no idea how he got there.

Oh, and another awkward thing: when you've pawned your watch, you have to beg the time off public house clocks and church clocks, and public house clocks are always five minutes fast, church clocks ten minutes slow. Trying to peek at the pub clock from outside is a whole new level of hell. If you push the swing door open an inch to peer in, the barmaid gives you a look of pure contempt, immediately lumping you in with gutter sneaks and cadgers. All the married regulars get flustered, the clock is hidden right behind the door so you can't see it anyway, and when you try to back out quietly you jam your head in the door. The only other option is jumping up and down outside the window to see over the blinds. If you don't pull out a banjo and start singing after that, all the local kids who gathered round to watch get deeply disappointed.

And what cruel, weird law of nature makes sure that the second you've left your watch "to be repaired" half an hour, some random bloke stops you on the street and yells loud enough for everyone to hear "oi, mate, what's the time?" No one ever asks you the time when you actually have the watch on you.

Sweet little old dears who have never known a day of being hard up (bless their gray heads, may they never have to) think the pawn shop is the absolute final stop on the road to degradation. But anyone who's been inside a few will tell you, it's like that little boy who dreamed he went to heaven: you keep running into people there you never in a million years expected to see. For my money, pawning your stuff is a hundred times more dignified than borrowing cash off your friends, and I always tell my mates who're about to beg for "a couple of quid till next Tuesday" to go do that instead. But not all of them see it. One of them once said he objected to the whole principle of the thing. I reckon what he really objected to was the 25% interest, that stings a bit, fair play.

There are all sorts of levels to being hard up, of course. We're all skint, more or less—most of us more. Some people are hard up for a thousand pounds, some for a single shilling. Right this second? I'm hard up for a fiver. I only need it for a day or two, I 100% swear I'll pay it back in a week max, and if any kind lady or gentleman reading this would be so sweet as to lend it to me? I'd be endlessly grateful. You can send it c/o Messrs Field & Tuer, just please seal the envelope real careful, and I'll send you my personal IOU as total security.

All is vanity, and every last one of us is vain. Women are absurdly vain. Men? Worse, if you can believe it. Kids? Even more so, bless their scuffed little boots. Right this very second, a tiny grubby cherub is hammering my shins demanding I deliver a full, glowing review of her brand new shoes.

Candidly, I don’t think much of them. They’re lopsided, lumpy, and have that indescribable wonky look that tells me for certain they’re on the wrong feet. But I don’t say that. She doesn’t want constructive criticism, she wants unhinged flattery, and I gush over those ugly little shoes with a level of over-enthusiasm I find deeply embarrassing to admit to. Nothing less would satisfy this tiny self-appointed queen.

I tried the “honest conscientious uncle” bit once, and it was a total disaster. She toddled up to me, big eyes wide, and asked “Wot oo tink of me? Oo peased wi’ me?” and I thought it was a perfect chance to nudge her toward better behavior. So I told her no, I wasn’t pleased, and reeled off the list of her crimes that very morning: waking the whole house up at 5 a.m., dumping a full water jug and tumbling down the stairs after it at 7 a.m., trying to stuff the cat into the bathtub at 8 a.m., and sitting square on her dad’s best Sunday hat at 9:35 a.m.

Did she thank me for my plain speaking? Did she vow to turn over a new, better leaf right then and there? Of course not. She howled. Then she got abusive, yelling “Oo naughty—oo naughty, bad unkie—oo bad man! Me tell MAR!” And she did.

Since then, when my opinion’s requested, I keep my real thoughts locked tight, and gush unreservedly over whatever this small tyrant does, no matter how dumb. She nods all smug, then trots off to spread my testimonial around the whole house, using it as currency for snacks. I’ll hear distant echoes down the hall later: “Unkie says me dood dirl—me dot to have two bikkies!” Right now she’s standing there cooing rapturously at her own toes, murmuring “pittie” to herself. Two and a half feet of pure, unadulterated conceit, no notes.

They’re all alike. I remember sitting in a sunny London suburb garden once, when I heard a shrill little voice yelling from a top-story window to some unseen kid in the next yard: “Gamma, me dood boy! Me wery good boy, gamma! Me dot on Bob’s knickiebockies!”

Even animals are vain. I saw a massive Newfoundland dog the other day parked right in front of a shop mirror at Regent’s Circus, preening at his own reflection with a level of smug self-satisfaction I’ve only ever seen matched at a stuffy local parish vestry meeting.

I was at a farmhouse once for some sort of big holiday—can’t remember if it was May Day or Quarter Day, no matter—they wreathed one of the cows’ heads in flowers. That silly cow pranced around all day as perky as a schoolgirl in her first ever new party frock. When they took the garland off at the end of the day, she threw a full sulk, refused to stand still to be milked until they put the flower wreath right back on her. That’s no silly tall tale, that’s plain, sober truth.

As for cats? They’re almost as vain as humans. I’ve known a cat get up and storm straight out of the room just because a visitor made a rude little joke about cats being stuck-up. Give them one neatly worded compliment, though, and they’ll purr happily for an hour straight.

I adore cats. They’re so unconsciously funny, all that regal “How dare you! Go away, don’t touch me” energy. Dogs have no dignity at all, of course—they’ll slobber all over every Tom, Dick, or Harry they meet. I’ll slap a dog I know on the head, call him all sorts of silly rude nicknames, roll him over on his back, and he’ll just lay there gaping at me, not caring a bit.

Try that with a cat? She’d never speak to you again for the rest of your life. If you want to win a cat over, you have to tread very carefully. If you don’t know her, you start soft, saying “Poor pussy,” then add a soothing little “did ‘ums” in the most sympathetic tone you can manage. Neither of you know what that means, but it strokes her ego just right. If you pass the vibe check, she’ll arch her back and rub her nose against your hand. Then, of course, you can venture to tickle her under the chin, and she’ll sink her claws straight into your calf. All the sweet friendship the old nursery rhyme promises!

That silly little poem’s last two lines tell you everything about a cat’s definition of human goodness: as far as they’re concerned, being a good person means stroking them, patting them, and feeding them snacks. And that narrow view of virtue isn’t just for cats! We all do it. A “good man” to us is a man who’s good to us. A “bad man” is one who won’t do what we want him to.

Deep down, every single one of us is convinced the whole universe was created as a little side accessory just for ourselves. Every other person on the planet exists to admire us, and fetch us things we need. You, dear reader? I’m pretty sure a considerate Providence put you on this earth so you could read my little essays and pay me for the work. Meanwhile, you’re fully convinced I was sent into the world to write something entertaining for you to pass the time with. The stars? Placed in the sky to look pretty for us at night. The moon? A custom setup so we have dim, romantic lighting to flirt under. We’re all exactly that silly bantam cock from Mrs. Poyser’s story, fully convinced the sun rises every single morning just to hear him crow.

They say vanity is what makes the whole world go round, and I believe it. There never was a person alive with no vanity at all, and if there was, he’d be the most tedious company on the planet. He’d be very good, sure—we’d stick him under a glass case, put him on a pedestal, respect him deeply. But we’d never want to grab a pint with him, never want to shake his hand like a friend. Angels are probably very upstanding sorts, but us poor messy mortals would find them unbearably boring to hang out with.

It’s not our virtues that bond us to each other, it’s our flaws. We differ wildly in how pious, generous, honest, or truthful we are. But in vanity, we’re all exactly the same. It’s that small touch of nature that makes the whole world kin. From the Indian hunter proud of his scalp belt, to the European general strutting under a chest full of medals, from the Chinese man showing off his impossibly long pigtail, to the fancy society woman torturing herself to get a waist smaller than a peg-top, from the ragged little urchin Polly Stiggins parading through Seven Dials with a tattered old parasol over her head, to the princess sweeping through a ballroom with a four-yard train, from the working class lad cracking crude jokes to make his mates hoot with laughter, to the politician preening over the crowd’s cheers for his big fancy speeches, from the dark-skinned African trader bartering rare ivory for cheap glass beads to hang around his neck, to the rich Christian girl marrying a stuffy old aristocrat for a fancy title and a handful of tiny shiny diamonds—every last one of us marches, fights, bleeds, and dies under vanity’s gaudy little flag.

Vanity is the engine that drives all human behavior, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels turning smooth. If you want to get anywhere in this world, win friends and influence people? Flatter everyone, high and low, rich and poor, silly and wise. Tell the ugly guy he’s strikingly handsome, the bore he’s hilarious, the rude boor he’s the most polite person you’ve ever met. They’ll all sing your praises to the skies, gushing about what incredible discernment you have.

Everyone’s susceptible to flattery. The belted earl—whatever that even means, unless it’s an earl who wears a belt instead of braces, which sounds deeply uncomfortable—you can win him over. The duchess, the street cat-meat seller, the plowboy, the poet? All of them. Poets are far easier to flatter than plowboys, too: butter sinks way better into soft white bread than hard oaten cake.

For love? Flattery is its very lifeblood. As that witty French writer once put it (damn, I can never remember names when I need them), fill someone up with love for themselves, and the overflow will spill right over onto you. Tell a girl she’s more angelic than any actual angel, more beautiful than Venus, more perfect than any woman who ever lived or ever will live, and she’ll eat it right up. They all tell you they hate flattery, but if you murmur “Darling, this isn’t flattery, this is just plain, sober truth—you really are the most perfect human to ever walk the earth,” they’ll smile that little soft smile, lean against your shoulder, and call you a dear good fellow after all.

Imagine the poor fool who tried to court people strictly on truthful principles, no compliments allowed! He’d gaze into his beloved’s eyes and whisper, “You’re not bad-looking, as girls go.” He’d hold up her hand and tell her it’s “a sort of pale pink drab shot with red, not the worst I’ve seen.” He’d hug her and say “Your nose, for a turned-up one, is rather pretty, and your eyes are well above average.” He’d stand zero chance next to the rival who tells her her face is a fresh blush rose, her hair is a sunbeam caught in a net, and her eyes are two glowing evening stars.

Of course, you have to adjust your flattery style to fit the person. Some people like it slathered on with a trowel, zero subtlety needed. Sensible folk prefer it slipped in quietly, by suggestion rather than loud declaration. A good few people like their flattery wrapped up as an insult: “Oh you’re such an idiot, you’d give your last sixpence to the first hungry beggar you saw.” Others will only believe flattery if they hear it secondhand, from a friend who swears he was told not to repeat it to you. And those gruff old John Bulls who brag “I don’t fall for flattery, sir! No one can fool me with that nonsense”? Just flatter them on how little vanity they have, how tough and no-nonsense they are, and you can get them to do literally anything you want.

At the end of the day, vanity is as much a virtue as it is a vice. It’s easy to recite old copybook maxims about what a sin it is, but vanity pushes us to do good just as often as it pushes us to do bad. Ambition is just vanity, all dressed up in a noble name. We crave praise and admiration—what we call “fame”—so we write great books, paint grand pictures, invent new technology, toil in labs and mills to make the world better. We chase wealth not for simple comfort, which you can buy for 200 pounds a year, but so our houses are nicer than our neighbors’, our dinner parties more lavish, our wives’ dresses more beautifully absurd and expensive. That silly little vanity drives us to spread trade across the globe, carry civilization to its farthest corners.

So don’t abuse vanity. Use it. Honor itself is just the highest form of vanity. The instinct isn’t only for foppish dandies and silly socialites. Peacocks are vain, but eagles are vain too. Snobs are vain, but heroes are vain too. Come on, all you young lads, let’s be vain together. Let’s be vain not of our fancy trousers or our nice hair, but of brave hearts and working hands, of truth, of purity, of nobility. Let’s be too vain to stoop to anything mean or base, too vain for petty selfishness and small-minded envy, too vain to say an unkind word or do an unkind deed. Let’s be proud of being honest, upright gentlemen in a world full of knaves. Let’s be vain of thinking high thoughts, doing great deeds, and living lives worth being proud of.

Let’s be real, that’s exactly the sort of grind-focused topic a professional idle layabout like me has no business wasting brain cells on, right? But it’s always the outsiders hanging back who spot the silliest bits of the game, isn’t it? So there I sit, camped out in my overgrown little arbor off the main roadside, puffing my fancy pipe of fake zen and munching the lazy lotus leaves of indolence, staring at the absolute stampeding circus tumbling past on the great highway of life.

That procession never lets up. Day or night, you can hear the thud of ten thousand feet: some sprinting, some plodding, some limping along with a cane, but all of them hurrying, all hopped up on that success fever, yanking every muscle and fraying every last nerve to chase that horizon that keeps darting further away the second you get within a mile of it.
Take a good look at the crowd surging past: men, women, kids, grandparents, lords and street sweepers, pretty and plain, rich and so poor they can barely afford a crumb, cackling happy and sniffling sad, all jostling and bustling and shoving to get ahead. The strong knock the weak flat on their backs, the sneaky creeps slip around the side past the dumb folks, the people behind elbow the ones in front, and the folks at the front kick back over their shoulders as they run.
Gloss in close and spot the chaos: there’s an old man huffing so hard he’s about to hack up a lung, there’s a shy teenage girl being herded along by a scowling sharp-faced chaperone, there’s a bookish youth with his nose stuck in *How To Get Rich In 10 Easy Steps* so hard he trips over every curb and lets half the crowd pass him by. There’s a bored bloke whose very fashionable wife is jabbing him in the ribs to make him walk faster, there’s a little boy staring wide-eyed back at his sunny village home he swears he’ll come back to rich and famous, and we all know how that usually goes. There’s a broad-shouldered fella striding along like he owns the whole road, and a stoopy weaselly guy skulking and dodging past everyone like he’s pulling a heist. There’s a shifty rogue staring so hard at the ground at his feet he zig-zags from one side of the road to the other for an hour without moving 10 feet forward, and there’s a noble-looking kid frozen halfway between the shiny distant finish line and the ankle-deep mud under his boots.
Then past trundles a pretty girl whose face gets a new little wrinkle every step she takes, a worn out father who hasn’t had a full night’s sleep in 7 years, and a goofy teen with his whole life ahead of him grinning like he’s already won. What a mixed bag! Kings and beggars, saints and con artists, butchers and bakers and candlestick makers, tinkers, tailors, farm boys, sailors, all cheek by jowl shoving each other. There’s the fancy judge in his wig and silk robes, the old Jewish clothes peddler with his crumpled hat, the soldier in his bright red uniform, the undertaker’s mute with his dripping black hat band and holey cotton gloves, the musty old scholar flipping through his yellowed books, the fancy actor swinging his gaudy pocket watch chains around like they’re worth a fortune.
There’s the slippery politician yelling about his amazing new law that’s definitely gonna fix everything, the street hawker holding up a bottle of fake tonic that cures everything from gout to heartbreak, the fat sleek capitalist rolling past in his carriage, the sinewy day laborer carrying a 50 pound sack on his back, the scientist muttering to himself, the guy who shines your shoes on the corner, the poet scribbling verses on a scrap of paper, the water rates collector who everyone hides from when he knocks, the cabinet minister, and the ballet dancer in her sparkly tutu. There’s the red nosed pub landlord yelling at the top of his lungs about how his latest ale is so good it’ll make your dead grandma dance on the bar, and the temperance lecturer getting paid 50 whole pounds a night to yell back at him about how all booze is evil. There’s a judge and a swindler walking side by side, a priest and a professional gambler bumping shoulders. There’s a bejeweled duchess smiling like butter wouldn’t melt, a harried lodging house keeper who’s 2 seconds away from blowing a fuse because the burned the stew, and a wobbly over made up woman tottering past in a too-tight fancy dress that cost more than most people make in a month.
They all scream, curse, pray, laugh, sing, and sob as they rush past each other, and the pace never slows. No little rest stop by a cool fountain, no break under a shady tree for these folks. On, on, on – through the heat, the dust, the crowd – or you get trampled underfoot and forgotten. On with your brain throbbing and your legs shaking, on until your heart gives out, your eyes go blurry, and a gurgling groan tells the people behind you they can shuffle up a step and take your spot.

And yet for all that, who but a total lazy oaf or a complete idiot would just stand on the sidelines and not join in? Who’s that much like the hiker that stumbles on a fairy rave, stares for 10 minutes, then grabs that goblin cocktail and chugs it before leaping straight into the spinning dance circle? I know I couldn’t stay out of it.
Full disclosure, that whole poetic bit about my serene arbor, my pipe of contentment, and the lotus leaves? Total rubbish. Sounded very wise and philosophical when I thought it up, but let’s be honest – I’m not the sort of guy to sit around calmly smoking if there’s any fun chaos going on down the road. I’m exactly that Irish fellow who sees a crowd gathering, sends his little girl over to ask what’s up, and tells her “if there’s gonna be a row, your da wants a front row seat.”
I love that messy fierce fight for life. I love watching it. I love hearing about people clawing their way ahead bravely and fairly – no cheap lucky breaks, no underhanded tricks. It gets that old Saxon fighting blood in your veins pumping, same as the stories we read at school about knights beating impossible odds. And fighting the battle of real life is all about beating impossible odds, too. There are giants and dragons running around in the 19th century, and the shiny treasure chest they guard is way harder to pry open than the storybooks make it sound. In those novels, the hero Algernon takes one last sad look at his family’s falling down ancestral home, wipes a tear off his cheek, and leaves – then comes back three years later rolling in cash. The authors never tell us how he did it, which is such a shame, I bet that part would be way more exciting than all the boring tea party scenes they drag on for 12 pages.
Hardly any novelist actually writes the real story of their hero, you know. They’ll ramble for pages about a dinner party, then sum up 20 years of a guy’s life in one line: “and then he became one of our greatest merchant princes, with the whole world at his feet.” Half those biographical novels have less real life in them than one of Gilbert’s silly patter songs! He walks you through every single little step the office boy takes to end up ruler of the Queen’s navy, and explains exactly how the barrister who had no clients for 10 years ends up a great judge happily presiding over breach of promise cases. All the interesting bits of life are in the tiny messy details, not the big fancy end results.
What we really need is a novel that shows every hidden messy bit of an ambitious guy’s career: his struggles, his failures, his dumb little hopes, his disappointments, the tiny wins that felt like huge triumphs. That book would sell a million copies, no question. Chasing after Fortune is every bit as fun and dramatic as chasing after a pretty girl – and honestly, the two things are basically identical. The ancients were spot on when they drew Fortune looking exactly like a woman: she’s almost as unreasonable, almost as inconsistent, and the chase works exactly the same way. Ben Jonson’s old couplet says it all:
> Court a mistress, she denies you;
> Let her alone, she will court you.

It fits Fortune to a T. A woman won’t properly fall for you until you stop caring that much about her, and Fortune won’t start smiling at you until you snap your fingers at her, turn on your heel, and walk away. And by that point? You don’t give a damn if she’s frowning or smiling. Why couldn’t she have smiled when you would have been over the moon about it? Everything good in this world shows up three days too late.
All the boring goody-two-shoes people say that’s proof ambition is wicked, and that’s how it should be. Total garbage! Those people are always wrong, I’ve never agreed with them on a single thing in my life. What would this world even be without ambitious people? It’d be as flabby and tasteless as a plain Norfolk dumpling. Ambitious folks are the yeast that makes the whole world rise into something halfway edible. They’re the noisy busybodies who wake up at 5 a.m. banging pans, yelling, and rattling the fire pokers so loud the rest of us can’t sleep in any longer.
Ambition is wrong? Those guys who break their backs hacking out smooth new roads so all of humanity can march forward for hundreds of years after them – they’re wrong? Using the talents you were given to build something while everyone else is playing cards and napping – that’s wrong? Sure they’re chasing their own reward, no human is perfectly unselfish enough to only care about other people. But when they work for themselves, they’re working for all of us. We’re all tangled up together, no man can do something only for himself. Every swing of the hammer he takes to build his own fortune helps shape the whole world. The river churning forward spins the mill wheel, the tiny coral bug building its little cell joins whole islands together, and the ambitious guy building his own little pedestal leaves a monument that the rest of us get to stand on later. Alexander and Caesar conquered the world for their own glory, but they dragged half the planet out of the dark ages along the way. Stephenson invented the steam engine to get rich, Shakespeare wrote all his plays so he could keep Mrs Shakespeare and the kids well fed and in a nice comfortable house.
Unambitious, contented people are fine enough, in their place. They make a nice plain background to paint all the great portraits on, and they’re a perfectly respectable audience for all the lively active people of the world to perform for. I don’t have a single bad word to say about them as long as they sit quiet and leave everyone else alone. But for heaven’s sake, don’t let them go strutting around yelling that they’re the perfect role model for everyone else on the planet! They’re the dead weight, the lazy drones in the beehive, the crowd loitering on the street corner gawking at everyone else who’s actually working.
And don’t let them pretend they’re super wise and philosophical for being content, either. Sure, “a contented mind is happy anywhere”, but so is a cheap little Jerusalem pony, and the result is everyone shoves you anywhere and treats you any old way. People will pat you on the back and say “oh don’t bother trying to promote him, he’s perfectly happy where he is, no need to rock the boat.” So your perfectly content guy gets passed over, and the grumpy discontented guy gets the job.
If you’re dumb enough to be perfectly happy with what you’ve got? Don’t tell anyone. Grumble along with the rest of us. If you’d be perfectly happy with 100 pounds, ask for 1000. Because if you ask for 100, you’ll end up with 10. The whole world runs on the same rule as a lawsuit for damages: you demand ten times more than you’d actually be happy to accept.
Poor old Jean Jacques Rousseau found that out the hard way. He decided his absolute perfect dream life was just an orchard, a nice amiable wife, and one cow. And he never even got that! He got the orchard, sure, but the wife was a total misery who brought her nagging mother to live with them, and there was no cow. If he’d aimed for a giant country estate, a whole house full of sweet angels, and a grand county cattle show? He’d have ended up with a tiny kitchen garden, one cow, and maybe even that super rare thing – a genuinely nice wife who doesn’t yell at you all day.
Life must be so boring for those contented folks, too. How do they even fill the hours? Read the newspaper and smoke a pipe, mostly, and the extra energetic ones learn to play the flute and gossip about what the neighbors are up to. They never feel that thrill of waiting for something exciting, that sharp good feeling of nailing a big goal after months of work. For the ambitious guy, life is the best game at the fair – a game that makes you use every scrap of your wits, your energy, your nerve, a game you win with a sharp eye and a steady hand, but with just enough random luck to make it feel properly thrilling. He loves it the way a strong swimmer loves choppy waves, the way a wrestler loves a good tough match, the way a soldier loves a good fight. And if he loses? He still got that grim good fun of fighting hard. If he doesn’t win the race, at least he got to run his fastest. It’s way better to work hard and fail than to sleep your whole life away and never do a single interesting thing.

So come on up, everyone! Ladies, gents, kids, grandpas! Show off your skills, test your strength, take your chances, prove you’ve got pluck. Step right up! The game never closes, it’s always running. It’s the only real fun the whole fair has to offer, folks – totally respectable, strictly moral, endorsed by lords, priests, and fancy gentry. It opened for business in year 1, and it’s been going strong ever since. Step right up! Everyone can play, there’s a prize for every single person. There’s gold for the working man, fame for the kid, status for the young lady, and a good fun time for the fool. Step right up, everyone – the prizes are real, there’s no totally useless blanks. The few of us who cross the finish line win big, and for the rest?
> The rapture of pursuing
> Is the prize the vanquished gain.

Things really do go deliberately out of their way to annoy me, don’t they? I set out the other week with a grand plan: I’d write an article on a subject so wildly original, so far off the beaten track, that no other living soul had ever bothered to pen a single word about it. I’d have the whole conversation to myself, no tiresome takes from other people clogging up the space. I wandered around for three whole days wracking my brains for that perfect unique topic, and drew a total blank.

Then in trotted Mrs. Cutting, our charwoman, yesterday. I don’t mind naming her, she’ll never lay eyes on this book—she thinks any publication that isn’t the Bible or *Lloyd’s Weekly News* is frivolous at best, actively sinful at worst. “Lor’, sir, you look proper worried,” she said, wiping her hands on her apron.

“Mrs. Cutting,” I told her, deadly serious, “I am trying to land on a subject that will hit the whole world like a firework. Something no human being has ever had the gall to discuss before. Something fresh, something surprising, something nobody saw coming.”

She snort-laughed and called me a funny old gentleman. That’s my whole life in a nutshell, isn’t it? When I say something dead sincere, everyone cackles. When I crack a perfectly good joke, no one even notices. I had a ripper of a one last week, worked it up so artfully at a dinner party, slipped it right after we’d been arguing about Shakespeare’s take on the Reformation. “Oh, that reminds me!” I said, all casual. “The funniest thing happened down Whitechapel the other day.” They all leaned in. I told the whole long, clever joke, waited for the roars. Dead silence. Then one guy cleared his throat and went, “…And that was the joke?” I swore it was, they were all polite enough to take my word for it, except one old codger at the far end of the table who spent ten minutes quizzing me on whether the funny part was what the man said or what the woman said.

Some people swing too far the other way, of course. I knew a fellow once who was so hardwired to laugh at everything, you had to warn him three times over if you were about to say something serious or he’d roll around cackling at every other word. I once saw someone ask him the time, and he stopped dead in the middle of the road, slapped his thigh, and howled for five solid minutes. You’d never dare tell that man a really good joke—it’d kill him on the spot.

Back to Mrs. Cutting, though. I insisted I was a perfectly serious writer, not a silly jester, and begged her to throw out ideas. She thought for a minute, and suggested samplers—said no one talked about those much these days, even if they were all the rage when she was a girl. I told her samplers were already done to death, make her think harder. She stood there holding a tea tray for a full two minutes, then brightened up. “The weather!” she said. “I’m sure no one’s talking about how rotten it’s been lately.”

And I haven’t been able to shake the weather out of my head since. It’s all I think about. And it *is* wretched weather, isn’t it? Even if it’s nice when you read this, don’t worry—it’ll turn foul soon enough. The weather is exactly like the government: it’s always in the wrong. Summer’s too stifling, winter’s a death trap, spring and autumn are both wishy-washy messes that refuse to pick a temperature, so we complain they won’t make their minds up. If it’s too sunny, we whine the crops will die of drought. If it rains, we beg for sun. If there’s no snow in December, we huff and act like we’ve been robbed of a good old-fashioned winter we paid good money for. Then when it does snow, we curse so loud it’d make a vicar blush. We’ll never be happy until every man gets his own private custom weather, no one else allowed to touch it. If we can’t have that, we’d just as soon have no weather at all.

Though, to be fair, it’s only in cities that weather is such an unwelcome nuisance. Out in the countryside, Nature looks lovely no matter what mood she’s in. What’s prettier than soft big snowflakes drifting down in silence, draping the fields and trees all white like they’re getting ready for a fairy wedding? A walk on crisp frozen ground, where every step rings out sharp, your blood fizzing in that thin cold air, and the distant bark of a sheepdog or the peal of kids’ laughter carrying across the hills like little Alpine bells? And skating! Zooming across the ice on steel blades, making that lovely whirring hum as you fly, wind in your ears. Then spring, all dainty and 18 years old, the tiny bright new leaves peeking out shyly like young people tiptoeing into a party, the pink and white blossom on fruit trees wrapping every little white cottage in a fluffy pink cloud, the cuckoo’s call drifting soft through the woods. And summer, with its dark deep green leaves and drowsy bee hum, raindrops whispering secrets to the foliage, twilight lingering slow in the lanes. And autumn, oh autumn, all sad and beautiful, glowing gold, the woods draped in fiery red and orange, sunsets bleeding crimson, ghostly mist rolling over the fields at dusk, reapers chattering as they work, orchards heavy with fruit, gleaners calling to each other across the stubble. Even the rain, sleet and hail out there just feel like little hardworking nature servants, doing their jobs. Even the east wind is just a loud, boisterous pal you’d meet between two hedgerows, no harm in him.

But in the city? Where stucco walls blister under smoky sun, sooty rain turns the pavement into slush and mud, snow piles up in dirty grey heaps in the gutters, cold wind whistles down grimy streets and shrieks around flickering gas lamps? Not a single bit of Nature charms us there. Weather in a town is like a skylark locked in an accountant’s counting house: totally out of place, nothing but a nuisance. Cities ought to be fully domed, heated by hot water pipes, lit all year round by electric light, no outdoor nonsense allowed. Weather is a country lass, and she doesn’t look half as fun in a London street. We loved flirting with her out in the hayfields, but when you run into her on Pall Mall? She’s way too much. That hearty, unselfconscious laugh that sounded so nice in a dairy just jars against all the stuffy, polished pretence of town life, and suddenly all her little quirks are incredibly annoying.

Just lately, she’s favoured us with three whole weeks of nonstop rain. I’m a damp, moist, unpleasant body, as Mr. Mantalini once put it. Our next door neighbour keeps trotting out into his back garden every hour or so, yelling that this rain is doing the country a world of good. Not him going out in the garden, obviously—the weather. He hasn’t got a clue about farming, but ever since he built a cucumber frame last summer he’s decided he’s a retired horticulturist, and runs his mouth off like that to make everyone else on the terrace think he’s some sort of landed gentleman. I hope for his sake he’s right, and the rain is doing some patch of field somewhere some good, because it’s ruining me. It’s wrecking my temper first—though that’s no big loss, I’ve got a whole spare store of temper knocking about somewhere—but it breaks my heart to see my favourite old hats and trousers getting ragged and stained way before their time, worn down by the rain and mud.

And my new spring suit! It was a beautiful thing, once. Now it’s hanging in the closet caked in mud, I can’t even bear to look at it. That was all Jim’s fault. I never would’ve stepped foot outside in it that night if he hadn’t turned up unannounced. I was just trying it on for size when he burst through the door, threw his arms up, and yelled so loud the neighbour banged on the wall: “SPiffing, old man!” He wanted to know if I was coming out with him. I said no, at first, but he talked me round. “A man with a suit that good has a public duty to show it off,” he said. “Every citizen owes the public a little joy. C’mon, you’ll give every girl that walks past a nice treat.” Jim talks in the strangest slang, I’ve no idea where he picks it up—definitely not from me. I asked him if he really thought people would like it, he said it’d be like an unexpected day trip to the countryside for them. That settled it. Off I went.

When I got home, I was soaked to the bone. I rubbed myself down with whisky, stuck my feet in hot water, slapped a mustard plaster on my chest, ate a basin of gruel, knocked back a glass of hot brandy and water, slathered tallow all over my nose, and hopped straight into bed. All that prompt, over-the-top medical action, plus my naturally tough constitution, saved my life. But the suit? It’s not a suit any more. It’s a decorative mud splash board.

I was very fond of that suit, too. That’s always the way, isn’t it? The second I get attached to any little thing in this world, something terrible happens to it. When I was a boy I had a pet water rat, and I loved that little creature more than any toy. One day he fell headfirst into a big dish of gooseberry fool that was sitting out cooling in the kitchen. No one noticed he was missing until someone was halfway through their second helping of dessert.

I hate wet city weather. Not even the rain itself, really—the mud. I swear I have some weird magnetic power over mud. On a wet day, the second I step foot on the pavement, every single speck of mud within half a mile zeroes in on me. Everyone else can wander around for hours on a rainy day without getting a single spot on their coat. I just cross the road, and I come back looking so filthy my mum used to yell at me for disgracing the family. If there was only one single dab of mud left in the whole of London, I guarantee you I’d fight every other person on the street to get to it first.

I don’t return the mud’s affection, I swear. I hate that thick, murky “London particular” grey rain. I feel all muggy and miserable the whole day, and the only relief is stripping all your wet clothes off and crawling into bed, far away from all the muck. Everything goes wrong in wet weather. There are suddenly twice as many people, dogs, prams, cabs, and carts on the streets as normal, all of them deliberately getting in your way, everyone’s grumpy as hell (except me, of course, I’m a perfect gentleman), and it makes me see red. And somehow, on wet days I always end up carrying three parcels, a canvas bag, and a crumpled newspaper, right? The second it starts pouring, you realise you can’t get your umbrella open.

Which brings me to April weather—the thing they call April, by the way, because it almost always turns up in May. Poets are obsessed with it. They think it’s charming, because it can’t make up its mind for five whole minutes, so they compare it to a flighty, changeable woman, and gush about how delightful that is. I don’t see the charm. Sure, it’s fun enough when a girl grins for no reason one minute, snivels for no reason the next, then giggles, then sulks, then is all over you then acts like she doesn’t know you—all in the space of 60 seconds. (Don’t get mad at me, the poets are the ones saying this, they’re the self-appointed connoisseurs of flighty ladies.) But weather’s different. A woman’s mood swing won’t leave you soaked to the skin. A woman’s cold shoulder won’t give you lifelong asthma or rheumatism the way a sharp east wind will. I can prepare for a properly bad, consistently rainy day. But these half-sun half-shower days? They drive me insane. It makes you so mad to look up and see a bright, perfect blue sky when you’re already soaked through to your socks. The sun comes out after a torrential downpour, grinning like an idiot, and goes, “Aw, c’mon, you’re not actually wet? That was just a little fun prank!”

You can’t even open or shut your umbrella fast enough in English April, especially if it’s one of those “automaton” umbrellas—you know, the ones the shop swears open and shut themselves, no effort required. I bought one once, in April. I walked into the Strand shop, told them I wanted an umbrella that kept rain off, and that I wouldn’t accidentally leave on a train. “Try the automaton!” the shopman said, all excited. “It’s brilliant, opens and shuts all by itself.”

I bought it. He was not lying. That thing did whatever it wanted, I had zero control over it. It would be pouring buckets, I’d be fighting it, shaking it, swearing at it, trying to get the darn thing to pop open—and it wouldn’t budge an inch. Then the second the last raindrop fell, that stupid umbrella would fly open with a jerk, and refuse to close no matter what I did. I’d have to wander around under a perfectly clear blue sky, holding a fully open umbrella over my head, hoping it would rain again so I wouldn’t look like I’d escaped the lunatic asylum. Then the one time it *did* decide to shut, it did it out of nowhere and smacked my hat clean off my head.

Nothing makes a man look more ridiculous than losing his hat. That cold, helpless little bolt of misery that shoots down your spine when you suddenly realise your head is bare? That’s one of the worst miseries a human can suffer. Then you have to chase it down the street, the local excitable terrier thinks it’s the best game ever, you trip over three toddlers, knock their flustered mothers over, butt a fat old gentleman straight on top of a pram, bounce off the gates of a girls’ boarding school, and land straight in the arms of a sooty crossing sweep. By the time you catch your hat, it’s covered in mud, all the spectators are cackling so hard they can barely stand, and you don’t even care any more.

Altogether, between the bitter March winds, the endless April showers, and the total lack of any May flowers? Spring is a total flop in cities that have more than 10,000 people. It ought to be banned. Spring, like small children, has no place in a grim, smoky city workshop. It’s sad to see little dirt-streaked kids trying to play in muddy alleyways and noisy courtyards. Those aren’t real children, bright-eyed, chubby, and shy. They’re little grubby, screeching elves, their tiny faces already worn thin, their baby laughs hoarse and cracked.

The spring of the year, and the spring of life, were both meant to be cradled in the green lap of the countryside. In the city all we get from spring is cold wind and drizzle. If you want to feel that real bright spring freshness, you have to go out to the bare woods, the bramble-choked lanes, the heathery moors, the big quiet hills. The scurrying clouds, the sharp open air, the rushing wind, they fill you up with this vague, happy energy, this quiet hope you can’t name. Life suddenly feels bigger, wider, freer, like a rainbow road stretching off to some wonderful unknown place. Through the thin silver gaps in the clouds, you swear you can catch a glimpse of all the grand, glorious hope that wraps around our tiny little spinning world, and the wild March wind carries a little whiff of that magic straight to you.

Strange, soft, nameless thoughts stir in your chest, voices calling you to do something big, something meaningful. But we don’t know what they mean yet. The echoes inside us that ought to answer are still half-asleep, dumb, fumbling. We stretch our hands out like little children reaching for the light, trying to grab something we can’t even name. Our thoughts are long, long, vague thoughts, we can’t see where they end. All thoughts that reach beyond our tiny little everyday world are meant to be soft and shapeless, right? The only thoughts we can grasp perfectly are the tiny boring ones: two plus two is four, eating food is nice when you’re hungry, honesty is usually the easiest policy. All the bigger thoughts are too vast for our little childish brains to wrap around. We only ever see them dimly, through the mists that wrap around our little island of life, only ever hear the distant roar of the great big endless sea waiting just over the horizon.

I couldn’t even begin to put into words the absolute chaos cats and dogs have put me through this morning. It all started with Gustavus Adolphus—Gusty, as the downstairs folks shorten it to, to save their breath. Out in a big open field or a sprawling common? He’s the best-behaved, sweetest dog you could ask for. Indoors, though? He’s a 70-pound walking natural disaster who has never once grasped that a standard terraced house was not built to accommodate his personal brand of nonsense. Stretch one paw lazily, and two chairs plus your favorite knickknack shelf full of tiny porcelain trinkets go toppling. Wag his tail once, and the whole room looks like a rampaging toddler with a wrecking ball just passed through. Breathe too hard at the parlor fire, and it blows out like a birthday candle.

At dinnertime he creeps under the table, lies quiet for ten whole minutes, then leaps up like he just remembered he left the gas on. The first clue we get is the table doing a very dramatic impression of someone trying to pull a somersault. We all cling to its edges for dear life like we’re bobbing in a storm-tossed life raft, trying desperately to keep the roast beef on the horizontal. But Gusty, fully convinced we’re hatching some evil plot to force-feed him Brussels sprouts, panics worse, and the whole scene ends with the table overturned, the entire dinner smashed, and three sprawling, infuriated grown-ups covered in gravy and mashed potatoes.

This morning he barreled in like he was an American cyclonewith a waggy tail, and his very first move was to swipes my full coffee cup straight off the table, dumping the entire contents dead-center of my brand new waistcoat. I shot up so fast my chair tipped over, muttered a few very choice words, and made a beeline for him. He bolted for the door, and skidded straight into Eliza coming in with a full tray of eggs. Eliza yelped “UGH” and plopped straight down on the carpet, every single egg abandoned its tray and splattered across the rug in a messy yellow splay, and Gusty vanished out of the room. I yelled after him very firmly that he better hot-foot it downstairs and not let me see his face for the next hour at least. He seemed to agree, dodged the coal scoop I accidentally hurled in his direction, and vanished. I dried myself off, finished my cold toast, and assumed he was moping in the back yard.

Ten minutes later, though, I peeked into the passage and he was perched right at the top of the stairs, staring daggers at something on the step just below him. It was Tittums, our kitten. She’s roughly the size of a buttered penny roll, her back arched so high she looks like a tiny fuzzy rainbow, and she’s swearing so foully I’d turn bright red if the local parson overheard it. She swears worse than any medical student on a pub crawl. I dabble in the occasional curse myself, but next to her I’m a rank amateur.

Between you and me—keep this strictly confidential, please, I’d hate for your wife to hear I said this, the ladies never quite get the point—swearing is good for a man. It’s the safety valve that lets all your pent-up bad temper escape as harmless huffing, instead of festering inside and wrecking your whole mental state. When you’ve stepped on a lego barefoot, and you’ve said, “Oh bless you, my sweet dear fellow, what in the name of all the planets made you decide to leave that scattered right where my bare foot would land? Are you physically incapable of looking down? You wonderful clever thing you!” you feel ten times better. It does the same soothing work as slamming doors or smashing furniture, but it’s way cheaper. Swearing clears your head like a penny’s worth of gunpowder blowing out a soot-clogged washhouse chimney. A little explosion every now and then is good for everyone. I don’t trust a man who never swears, never kicks a footstool across the room in frustration, never stabs the poker into the fire like he’s got a personal grudge against the coals. If you’ve got no outlet for all the tiny annoyances of daily life, they don’t disappear—they sit next to you, slowly turning from tiny petty nuisances into big bitter grudges that breed hatred and spite.

I tried explaining that to my aunt once, and she was furious. Said I had no business having bad temper to vent in the first place. Sure, Aunty.

That’s exactly what I told Tittums, too. I told her she ought to be ashamed of herself, raised in a perfectly respectable Christian household and all. I don’t mind hearing a fully grown cat curse up a storm, but a tiny kitten? It feels like seeing a toddler puff on a cigar, downright tragic. I tucked her into my pocket and headed back to my desk to write, promptly forgot she was there, and by the time I looked over she’d squirmed out onto the table, was trying to swallow my pen, stuck her whole leg in the open ink pot and tipped the whole thing over, licked the ink off her leg, and then swore at me like I was the idiot who left an open ink pot unattended.

I plopped her down on the floor, and Tim—our annoying two-year-old fox terrier who goes around acting like a wise 80-year-old Scotch collie—started yapping at her like he’s the neighborhood moral police. I wish Tim would mind his own business, honestly. It had nothing to do with him. Tittums’ mom heard the racket, trotted in, and scratched Tim right on the nose, which I was extremely pleased about. I herded all three of them out into the passage, where they’re still rolling around brawling as I write this. I’m covered in ink, my waistcoat is still stained with coffee, and if any other cat or dog dares come bounding over to bother me this morning, they better pre-book their own funeral contractor and pay for the daffodils themselves.

For all that chaos, though? I absolutely adore cats and dogs, general. They’re far better companions than almost any human being I’ve ever met. They don’t argue with you, they don’t corner you at dinner and loudly mention to Miss Brown that they always heard she was madly in love with Mr. Jones, who just married Miss Robinson last week. They never mix up your wife’s cousin for her husband and accidentally call you the father-in-law. They never look at the poor young writer with fourteen tragedies, sixteen comedies, seven farces, and two burlesques stuffed in his desk and ask him why he hasn’t written a hit play yet.

They never pull you aside and say “I’m only saying this for your own good, but you’ve really let yourself go lately, and remember that time you messed up the office holiday party two years ago?” They don’t say, sarcastic as anything, “Oh yeah, you’re real helpful whenever I actually need a hand, aren’t you?” They never pout and tell you you’re not half as nice as you were when you first started dating. To them, you’re always the same, no matter what mood you’re in. They’re merry when you’re giddy, quiet when you want to think, and sad when you’re moping.

“Wanna run wild and tear around the drawing-room? Don’t even worry about the furniture, I’m right here,” your dog will say, frisking around your ankles, “we’ll scamper across the fields, chase the geese out of the old farmer’s yard, and have the best day ever. C’mon!” Or if you want to sit quiet and think? The cat will curl up on the arm of your chair, purring so loud it rumbles, and the dog will stretch out on the rug, half-asleep but keeping one eye cracked open just in case you suddenly decide you want to chase rats.

And when you bury your face in your hands and wish you’d never been born? They don’t sit up very straight and go “well you brought all this on yourself, I hope it’s a lesson to you.” They just nudge at you softly. The cat will climb up on your shoulder, knead your hair with her tiny paws, and purr so hard it feels like she’s saying “Oh buddy, that sucks. I’m here.” The dog will look up at you with those big, true, dopey eyes, like he’s telling you “you’ve always got me, yeah? We’re in this mess together, no matter what.”

Dogs don’t ever stop to ask if you’re right or wrong, rich or poor, a sinner or a saint. You’re their pal. That’s all that matters. If luck turns to garbage, everyone else abandons you, your reputation’s in the gutter? That stupid, loyal, big soft dog will stick right by you, guard you, comfort you, and even die for you if he has to. People love to go on and on about how humans are the smartest, noblest, best creatures in the whole universe. But that gin-soaked lout leaning against the pub wall? He’s supposed to be intellectually superior to that loyal dog? That two-bit lying scammer who cheats everyone out of their money, never does a kind deed in his whole life? He’s nobler than that brave, selfless dog? Sure, mate, whatever you say. We all know who the better creature is. We’re all just stupid enough to love the dogs and cats more, anyway. We love stupid things, don’t we? Men hate super clever women, every woman’s ideal man is some guy she can call a “dear old stupid.” It’s so nice to hang out with something that’s dumber than you are, no hidden agenda, no snarky comments.

Everyone says cats are selfish, worldly wise, only looking out for their own comfort. It’s total slander. A cat who’s your friend will stick with you through thick and thin, no questions asked. I had one cat once who followed me absolutely everywhere, even all the way down the high street, until people started staring so hard I had to beg her as a personal favor to stop escorting me past the bakery. She’d sit up for hours waiting for me to come home late, and meet me at the door, which made me feel almost like a married man—except she never asked where I’d been and then refused to believe me when I told the truth. Another cat I owned snuck into the cellar every single day to lap up beer drippings from the barrel, and got so drunk she’d stagger around the living room. Whenever she caught a big rat, she’d drop it dead in the middle of our group, stand there waiting for everyone to clap, making all the girls shriek so loud the neighbors would knock on the door to check if we were being murdered.

I love all those spooky old rat tales, too. The story of Bishop Hatto, who burned all the starving villagers to death in his barn, then thousands of rats swam the Rhine to his tower and ate him alive? The Pied Piper of Hamelin, who piped all the rats away, then took all the children when the mayor cheated him out of his pay? I swear the Pied Piper still wanders around our streets today, piping so soft only the kids can hear it. That’s why little kids stop mid-game, go all quiet and solemn, staring off into space—they’re listening to that magic music we grown ups can’t quite hear over all the loud blaring noise of the world. One day soon the music will get loud enough for all of us to hear, and we’ll drop all our stupid little toys and follow, too.

I only wish more people could love their pets without being completely ridiculous and sappy about it. The gushing young ladies who kiss their long-haired stray dog’s nose right in front of every eligible young man, the old ladies who feed their poodle a mutton cutlet for breakfast every day and skip church on Sundays to keep the dog company? There are whole families where the entire conversation revolves around their stupid dog from morning till night. If you want to borrow their garden roller, or ask their daughter out, or get them to donate to the very necessary Society for Banning Solo Cornet Players in Theater Orchestras, you have to win the dog’s approval first. If the dog snaps at you? You’re banned forever. “If Fido doesn’t like you,” the dad will say thoughtfully, “then you can’t be a good person. Bless his little heart.” Ugh.

It’s funny, isn’t it? Even that stuffy dignified old pussy cat with her slow walk and sleepy eyes was once a feral little kitten bouncing off the walls, so full of life she didn’t know what to do with all of it. Doesn’t that remind you of when we were young? Those moonlit walks home where we were so bursting with life we couldn’t just walk, we had to skip and jump and wave our arms and yell, scaring the local farmers’ wives so bad they’d scuttle into the hedges, and we’d laugh so hard tears came out of our eyes. Our pulses are slow and steady now, our joints ache, we sit in our easy chairs with our pipes and sneer at “silly teenage enthusiasm.” But man, wouldn’t we all give anything to feel that bright, wild, full life in our veins again, even for just ten seconds?

All great literary men are shy. I am myself, though I’ve been told the quirk is barely noticeable, for which I’m deeply grateful. Back in my younger days, though, shyness ruled every waking minute, and it made life a waking misery for me, and a general nuisance for every poor soul stuck within 10 feet of me — my lady friends, in particular, complained about it at such length I thought they might write a public petition.

A shy man’s lot is no picnic. Other men side-eye him as a weirdo, women write him off as a boring dud, and he himself dislikes and despises his own awkward skin more than anyone else alive. No amount of practice eases the discomfort, and the only real cure is the slow grind of time. Though once, long ago, I stumbled on the most deranged piece of shyness advice ever printed, tucked in the “Answers to Correspondents” section of a cheap weekly rag, and I’ve never forgotten it: “Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies.”

Poor fool who wrote in asking for help! I can picture the pained little grin he must have read that line with. “Just be charming, especially around women,” forsooth! If he knew how to do that, he wouldn’t be writing to a tuppenny magazine agony aunt, would he? Let me save every shy young reader the trouble: never try to fake that easy, breezy persona. You will absolutely overdo it, turn ridiculously over-familiar and cloyingly gushy, and everyone will think you’re coming on to their pet goldfish. Stick to being your natural, awkward self, and the worst anyone will say is that you’re a bit surly and stupid — a far lighter sentence than being labeled a creepy weirdo.

The shy man does get one tiny, petty revenge on the society that tortures him: he passes his misery right along. He makes everyone else around him just as nervous as he is. He sucks the joy right out of a room like a damp blanket, turning even the loudest, jolliest partygoers quiet, twitchy, and ill at ease. Most of this chaos comes from plain old misunderstanding. Everyone misreads his timidity as stuck-up arrogance, and walks away both awed and offended. His fumbling awkwardness gets written off as insolent carelessness, and when a random stranger says hello to him out of the blue, he blushes bright red, his brain short-circuits, and he can’t get a single word out — at which point everyone decides he’s a prime example of what happens when you let your temper run away with you.

Misunderstanding is the shy man’s permanent fate. Whatever impression he tries to make, he accidentally delivers the exact opposite. Crack a joke? People think you’re telling a blatant lie and lecture you for being dishonest. Try some dry sarcasm? They take it 100% literal and decide you’re a total idiot. Work up the nerve to give a little genuine compliment? They assume you’re roasting them, and hate you for the rest of your life.

All these silly little trials of the shy man have been go-to comic fodder for centuries, and other people never tire of laughing at them. But dig a little deeper, and there’s a soft, almost tragic side to the whole mess. A shy man is a lonely man, cut off from all casual friendship and fun. He walks through the same world as everyone else, but never really mixes with it. An invisible, unbreakable wall stands between him and every other human, and every time he tries to climb it, he just bruises his hands raw. He sees all the smiling faces, hears all the loud, happy laughter on the other side, but he can never reach across to grab a friend’s hand. He hangs around the edges of merry groups, dying to join in, but they all walk past chattering, and he can’t bring himself to call them back. That invisible wall follows him everywhere: busy high streets, crowded parties, the work grind, weekends out dancing. Wherever people gather, wherever voices hum and eyes crinkle with jokes, the shy man stands apart like a leper, shunned and solitary. His soul is full of warmth and longing, but the world never sees it. The iron mask of shyness is riveted to his face, and the soft, funny guy under it never gets to come out. Genial greetings and kind little remarks rise to his lips a hundred times a day, but they die into unheard whispers behind that steel mask. His heart aches for a friend who’s upset, but he can’t get his sympathy out loud. Rage at unfairness chokes him up, and with no way to say it out loud, it just turns inward and eats at him, turning him into a grumpy, snarky cynic no one realizes is just a big softie underneath.

Shy men, much like very ugly women, get a raw deal in this world, which requires a rhinoceros-thick hide to get through comfortably without crying. Thick skin is our moral clothing — without it, we’re basically running around civilized society buck naked, gasping and blushing, trembling at every hello, which is a painful sight for everyone involved.

But the disease is curable, I promise you, from personal experience. I don’t usually like rambling on about myself, but for the good of my fellow shy folks, I’ll spill: I was once, as the silly Bab Ballad line goes, “the shyest of the shy,” and “whenever I was introduced to any pretty maid, my knees they knocked together just as if I was afraid.” Now? Two days ago, I pulled off a feat I could never have managed 10 years back. All on my own, no one to hide behind, I marched straight up to that notoriously grumpy railway refreshment room attendant, in her own little lair, and rebuked her, equal parts gently disappointed and sharply firm, for her total refusal to acknowledge my existence for 12 whole minutes. I insisted, politely but unshakably, that she give me the level of deference every traveling British citizen deserves, and at the end? I stared her dead in the face. Fine, I immediately left the room at a very brisk, almost running pace without waiting to buy my scone. But that was because I changed my mind about wanting a snack, obviously. Not because I panicked. Don’t be absurd.

One big comfort for shy folks: shyness is in no way a sign you’re stupid. All the loud, brassy guys with zero moral shame can sneer at “nerves” all they want, but the most sensitive, thoughtful people aren’t the ones with the thickest skulls and the loudest voices. A horse isn’t inferior to a squawking, overconfident sparrow, and a forest deer isn’t dumber than a grunting pig. Shyness is just extreme sensibility, nothing to do with conceit, no matter what those parrot-minded self-help gurus will tell you.

In fact, conceit is the fastest, most effective cure for shyness there is. Once it dawns on you that you’re quite a lot cleverer than almost everyone else in the room, bashfulness gets so offended it packs its bags and leaves. Once you can look around a whole crowd of people and think every single one of them is a total toddler next to your brain, you won’t feel shy around them any more than you’d feel nervous around a room full of chatty magpies or orangutans. Genuine conceit is the best armor a man can wear. All the petty little jabs of spite and envy glance right off its smooth, unbreakable surface. Without that breastplate, even the most talented guy can’t fight his way through the mess of life, because you have to take as many blows as you dish out.

I’m not talking about the silly, performative conceit of the guy who walks around with his nose in the air and talks in a weird falsetto. That’s not real conceit, that’s just kids playing dress-up at being royalty, strutting around with feather boas and fake crowns. Real, deep-seated conceit doesn’t make you unpleasant. It makes you relaxed, kind, and unpretentious. You don’t need to fake any persona, because you’re perfectly happy with who you are. Your pride runs so deep it never shows on the surface. You don’t care if people praise you or insult you, you can afford to be honest. You’re far too busy living on your own little cloud of self-satisfaction to care about other people’s silly little social ranks, so you’re just as comfortable chatting to a duke as you are to a street fruit seller. And since you only care about your own opinion of yourself, you never waste time doing those silly little lies people do to impress their neighbors every five minutes.

The shy man, by contrast, is humble — he doubts his own judgment, and obsesses way too much over what everyone else thinks of him. For a young guy, that’s not even a bad thing! His personality is still forming, working its way out of a mess of doubt and confusion, and as he gains experience, that shyness fades. Most men shake off their shyness by their mid-20s at the latest. Even if their own inner strength doesn’t kick it out, the constant bumping and rubbing against the rest of the world sands it smooth eventually. You almost never meet a truly, deeply shy grown man in real life — except in novels and on the stage, where the shy man is a huge fan favorite, especially with women.

On that magical fictional stage, the shy man is always a saintly, fair-haired soft boy. No respectable audience would buy a good hero with dark hair, of course. I once knew an actor who lost his blond wig right before he had to play the noble hero, and ran out on stage in his natural jet-black hair, and the whole gallery booed every one of his noble lines, fully convinced he was the villain. That stage shy boy loves the heroine so devotedly — but only in quiet asides, he never dares tell her out loud. He’s so unselfish, speaks in a soft sweet voice, is so good to his mum. All the bad guys in the play mock him, and he takes it so gently, until the big ending where everyone finds out he’s secretly a genius, and the heroine confesses she loves him, and he looks so shocked and so happy, and everyone apologizes, and he forgives them all in a few elegant, soft lines. He has such a wonderful time of it that all the non-shy young lads watching wish they could be shy too.

The real shy man knows better. He knows real life is nothing like that. He’s not that charming, saintly tragic hero. He’s clumsier, stupider, far less devoted and soft-spoken, and half the time he has dark hair, which completely ruins the whole vibe. The only thing he does share with that fictional ideal is loyalty. The shy young man is absolutely constant in love, and there’s no big mystery why: it takes every single scrap of courage he has to even make eye contact with one woman. There is zero chance he has the energy to go through that whole terrifying ordeal twice. He’s far too scared of the entire female gender to gallivant around chasing multiple women. One is more than more than enough for him.

Things are so different for the non-shy young man! He’s surrounded by temptations his bashful brother will never face. He looks around and sees nothing but roguish grins and laughing lips everywhere. Is it any wonder he gets confused, forgets which pretty girl he’s supposed to be courting, and ends up accidentally flirting with the wrong one? The shy man never even lifts his gaze from his own boots long enough to see any of that, so he never gets tempted. Lucky him!

Though the shy man himself would trade that “blessed” lack of temptation in a heartbeat. He longs to be a fun, roguish guy running around having adventures, and curses himself every single day for not being able to pull it off. Every once in a while he screws up every ounce of courage he has, and plunges headfirst into trying to be cheeky and fun. It always ends in total, humiliating disaster. After one or two sad, flailing attempts, he crawls back out, limp and pathetic.

And no one ever feels sorry for him! There’s a whole category of misfortunes that cause untold suffering for their victims, and gain zero sympathy from anyone else: losing an umbrella, falling in unrequited love, toothache, getting a black eye, having someone sit on your new hat. But the king of all no-sympathy misfortunes is shyness. The shy man is treated like a walking, talking joke. His tortures are the main entertainment at every house party, pointed out and gossiped about with glee. “Look!” everyone whispers snickering to each other, “he’s blushing!” “Watch his legs shake,” says one. “Did you see he’s sitting right on the very edge of the chair?” adds another. “Plenty of color in his cheeks, that’s for sure,” sneers a bluff military guy. “Pity he has so many hands,” murmurs an elderly lady, calmly folding her own in her lap, “he has no idea what to do with them.” “Couple feet shorter wouldn’t hurt him,” cackles the office funny guy, “he’s clearly dying to hide those things under the rug.” Then someone else jokes that with his tiny, trembling voice he should have been a sea captain. They point out the death grip he has on his hat, make fun of his 3-word vocabulary, complain about his weird nervous cough. They don’t stop until they’ve dissected every single one of his quirks, and the whole party is bored out of its mind.

His own family and friends make it even worse, of course — friends and family are allowed to be far more annoying than random strangers. They don’t just make fun of him behind his back, they make sure he sees the joke. They reenact his awkward walk into a room for him, yell “this is how you shake hands!” and grab people’s hands like they’re scalding hot plates, then drop them limply. Then they ask him why he’s blushing, why he stammers, why he speaks so quiet you can barely hear him, like they genuinely think he’s doing it on purpose for a laugh. Then his dad sticks out his chest, struts around like a puffed-up pigeon, and tells him very seriously that this is how he should act. His mum beams and says “Algernon, as long as you never do anything to be ashamed of, you’ll never be ashamed of anything you do!” and looks so proud of that genius line she came up with. His brothers tease him that he’s “worse than a girl,” and his sisters get huffy and say that’s an insult to girls, because no girl could ever be that bad.

And they’re completely right. There is no such thing as a shy woman, or at least I’ve never met one, and until I do I refuse to believe they exist. The popular myth that all women are timid little fawns, blushing and casting their eyes down when you talk to them, running away if you say hello? Total nonsense. A 12-year-old girl is as cool as a cucumber, completely self-possessed, while her 20-year-old brother stammers and stutters next to her. A woman will walk 10 minutes late into a packed concert, clatter down the aisle, interrupt the whole performance, and not even blink, while her husband trails behind her, a crumpled heap of nonstop apologies to every single person he passes. Women have nerves of absolute steel, especially when it comes to all matters of love, from the first flirty glance all the way to the end of the honeymoon. That makes perfect sense, of course — love is women’s “business,” and everyone drops all their natural weaknesses when they’re on the job. The shyest man I ever met, who couldn’t order a pint of beer without stammering for 5 minutes? He was the most aggressive, pushy photography sales rep in the whole city.

Oh, don’t look at me like that – I know babies. Loads about ’em, actually. I was one myself once, though not for nearly as long as my baby clothes stuck around. I remember those things like they were yesterday: three full yards of unnecessary swaddle fabric, always tangling around my ankles right when I was mid-kick. Why do babies get dressed in what amounts to a full bed curtain? This isn’t a riddle, I’m genuinely asking. I never cracked it. Are parents embarrassed their new human is barely bigger than a loaf of bread, and trying to trick everyone into thinking it’s six inches longer than it actually is?

I asked a nurse about it once. She blinked at me and said, “Lor’, sir, they all wear long clothes, bless their little hearts.” When I pointed out that this was very sweet but didn’t answer my actual question, she gasped like I’d suggested we feed the baby pickles for breakfast, and went, “Lor’ sir, you wouldn’t put the poor little dears in short clothes, would you?” The tone she used made me feel like I’d just proposed a thoroughly ungentlemanly crime. I’ve never dared bring it up to anyone else since, so the whole long baby coat conspiracy remains unsolved for me.

Honestly, though, strapping any clothes at all on a baby seems absurd to me. We all spend half our lives getting dressed and undressed anyway – why start before you even have to? You’d think people who spend 23 hours a day in bed could skip the whole changing routine. Why yank the poor little thing awake at dawn, peel one set of clothes off, wrap another one on, tuck it back in bed, then drag it out again at bedtime just to swap it all back? And for what? A baby’s day shirt and its nightgown look exactly the same to anyone who isn’t a qualified nursery lunatic.

I’ll stop nitpicking the clothes now, I know I look ridiculous – people tell me that a lot – except to say it would be unbelievably convenient if someone invented a fashion system that lets you tell a boy baby apart from a girl baby. Right now it’s a nightmare. No hair difference, no outfit clues, no chattering little personality to tip you off. You just have to guess. And by some universal cruel magic, you will always guess wrong. Then every relative in the room will look at you like you’re half idiot, half villain, because calling a little boy “she” is a sin on par with calling the grandma a thief, and calling a little girl “he” is basically an insult to the entire family bloodline.

And for the love of all that is holy, never call the baby “it.” You can murder an entire respectable family in cold blood and dump their bodies in the town water reservoir, and you’ll still be less hated than you will be the second a new mom hears you refer to her little darling as “it.”

Here’s my free hack, take it to the bank: call the baby a little angel. “Angel” is perfectly gender-neutral, no way to mess that up. Throw in a silly giggle when you say it, grin as big as you can, and you can call them “pet” or “little beauty” if you want some variety. But the big one, the hack that makes you the favorite guest, is to insist at the top of your lungs that the baby has its father’s exact nose. The parents will pretend to scoff and say “oh nonsense!” – you double down, get all excited, swear it’s the spitting image. You don’t even have to lie. That baby’s nose is just a little smudge of skin, it looks as much like its dad’s nose as it looks like a potato. No one can prove you wrong.

Don’t ignore these tips, trust me. There will come a day when you’re trapped between mom on one side, grandma on the other, a whole crowd of gossiping ladies behind you, and a bald little squish of a human in front, and you’ll thank me for having something to say. There is no more embarrassing situation on earth for a single unmarried man than being roped into the “come see the baby” ritual. You get that cold shiver down your spine the second they suggest it, you paste on that sickly fake smile and say “oh I’d love to!” and you’re 90% sure the whole thing is just a trick wives use to chase off their husbands’ boring bachelor friends.

They ring the bell, send a servant to get the nurse and bring baby down. All the women in the room immediately start chattering exclusively about baby business, leaving you there panicking, trying to think of a plausible excuse to leave right now – maybe I suddenly remembered I left my goldfish in the oven? Maybe my horses escaped? – and just when you’ve settled on the most unbelievable lie ever told, the door creaks open. A tall, severe-looking nurse walks in, holding what you at first glance think is a very skinny bolster pillow with all the feathers squashed to one end. Then your brain catches up: that’s the baby. You jump up, try to look excited, and immediately fail.

Once all the ladies are done cooing loud enough to rattle the windows, their little circle of frilly dresses parts, and gestures for you to come forward. You walk over like you’re heading to the magistrate’s dock to get sentenced, stand there staring at the baby in total silence, and you know every single person is waiting for you to say something. Your entire brain empties out. No thoughts. Nothing. Your evil inner idiot seizes the chance, and you snigger out, “Wow… he doesn’t have much hair, huh?”

Dead silence for three whole seconds. Then the nurse looks at you with the gravity of a judge handing down a death sentence, and goes, “Sir, it is not normal for five week old children to have long hair.”

Another silence. You scramble for a second chance, and ask if the baby can walk yet, or what they feed it – like they give it steak and ale, I guess. Now everyone’s looking at you like you’re a little touched in the head, and they feel bad for you. But the nurse is not letting you escape. She holds the squishy bundle out to you, tone of a high priestess leading a pagan ritual, and goes, “Take her in your arms, sir.”

You’re too scared to argue. You take the baby. “Put your arm further down her middle, sir,” she commands. Everyone steps back and stares at you, like you’re about to pull a magic rabbit out of its bonnet. You have no clue what to do. The only thing you can think of is to bounce the poor little thing up and down, chanting “oopsee-daisy” like a complete fool. “I wouldn’t jiggle her too much, sir,” the nurse says sharply. “A very little jiggling upsets her.” You freeze, stop bouncing immediately, and pray you didn’t break anything.

Up until this point, the baby has been staring at you with a face that says equal parts “this man is a stranger” and “this man is deeply unpleasant.” Now it decides to end the whole farce, and screams at the top of its tiny lungs like you just stuck a pin in its bottom. The nurse snatches it out of your arms immediately, cooing “there there there, what did the nasty man do to our little love?” “How weird,” you say, trying to sound casual. “What made it do that?” “Oh you must have done something to her!” the mom snaps, clearly convinced you’ve been torturing her child. They calm it down eventually, and everything would be fine, except some nosy old lady points at you and goes “who’s that, baby?” The baby sees you, remembers, and howls so loud the windows rattle.

Then all the old ladies start muttering. “Strange how babies take a dislike to some people,” one says mysteriously. “Oh they know,” another nods. “It’s a wonderful instinct.” Every single one of them looks at you out of the corner of their eye, fully convinced you’re a secret scoundrel, and the pure innocent baby has sussed out your evil true character before anyone else did.

But for all that, babies aren’t all trouble and awkward social torture. They fill empty houses with soft love, make tired parents forget their work worries in two seconds flat, their tiny little fingers smooth out all the grumpy wrinkles off grown people’s faces. They’re the accidental comedians of the whole world’s big boring play. They’re tiny, stubborn rebels against every boring adult plan, forever doing the exact wrong thing at the exact wrong time in the exact wrong place. The nurse who sent little Jenny to check what Tommy and Totty were doing and “tell them they mustn’t” knew exactly how baby brains work. If a normal, healthy baby isn’t doing something they absolutely should not be doing at any given moment, you need to call a doctor immediately.

They do the silliest things in the most serious, stoical way possible, and you can’t help but laugh. Two tiny tots will grab hands and toddle as fast as their wobbly legs can carry them due east, while their big sister screams at the top of her lungs for them to come west, and they don’t even notice. They circle a uniformed soldier on the sidewalk, poke his calf very carefully to make sure he’s not a statue. They will point at the shyest, most embarrassed stranger on the crowded bus and yell “DADDA” loud enough for the whole vehicle to hear. They decide a busy street corner is the perfect spot to yell all their family’s private business at the top of their high little voices. Halfway across a zebra crossing, they get a sudden overwhelming urge to stop and dance. And the second you step outside a shop, they plop down right on the doorstep and start trying to take their shoes off.

At home, they grab the biggest heaviest walking stick or an open umbrella to haul themselves up the stairs. The exact second the maid is covered head to toe in stove blacklead, they decide they love her more than anything in the world and insist on giving her a huge hug. Their favorite snacks are tiny bits of coal and cat food. They hold the family cat upside down to “nurse it,” and show their love for the dog by yanking its tail as hard as they can. They’re a mess, they make the whole house untidy, they cost a fortune to feed and clothe, but no one wants to live in a house without them. It wouldn’t feel like home without their tiny pattering feet, their chattering nonstop voices, their little hands getting into every single drawer. Wouldn’t the whole place feel dead quiet without them? Wouldn’t the grown-ups drift apart if there wasn’t a little prattling voice to call them back to each other?

Though I will say, sometimes that tiny little hand feels like a wedge, driving people apart. It’s rude to make fun of mother’s love, that soft pure thing that makes women so gentle and brave – us rough old men can barely even understand it. But it doesn’t have to swallow up every other bit of love in the house. The baby doesn’t have to take over your whole heart, like that greedy rich man who bricked up the desert well so no one else could drink from it. Your husband is standing right there, thirsty for a little of your attention too. When poor old Edwin asks you to go for a walk with him, don’t snap “what, and leave the baby?” Don’t spend every single evening upstairs in the nursery, don’t only ever talk about whooping cough and measles. Your baby is not going to die every time it sneezes. The house isn’t going to burn down, the nurse isn’t going to run off with a soldier, the cat isn’t going to climb into the crib and sit on the baby’s chest the second you step out the door for 20 minutes. Stop stressing over that one little chick so much, you’re driving yourself and everyone else crazy. Think a little of your other big baby – the husband. Dance him around a little, call him silly pet names, laugh at his bad jokes. You’d be surprised: five or six kids never take up as much attention as that first precious one did. But by the time you have five or six, the damage is already done. The house feels too small for him, his wife is too busy to remember he exists, and he’s already off looking for fun and conversation somewhere else.

Alright, alright! I’ll stop, before everyone decides I’m a confirmed baby hater. I’m not. How could I be? Look at all those tiny soft faces, peering out at the big world for the first time, all shy and wobbly. That huge round world must look so massive and mysterious through baby eyes! The back garden seems like an entire uncharted continent. The dark little cellar under the stairs is full of treasures no grown-up has ever seen. They stare down the long street, wide-eyed, the same way us old big babies stare up at the stars at night, wondering where it all ends. And that longest street of all, the long dim road of life stretching out in front of them – they look down it sometimes, with such grave old little faces. I saw a tiny tot sitting on a Soho slum doorstep once at night, the gas lamp lighting up his little thin face, and I’ll never forget that look on it. Dull, quiet despair, like he could see his whole whole hard squalid life stretching out in front of him, ghost-like, scaring him half to death.

Poor little feet, just starting down this stony road. We old travelers, far further down the path, can only stop for a second and wave. They come out of the mist at the top of the hill, tiny little things, arms stretched out towards us. God speed you, little ones. We wish we could stay, take your tiny soft hands in ours, walk a little way with you. But we can hear the murmur of the great sea at the bottom of the hill, and we can’t linger. Those dark shadowy ships are waiting for us, sails unfurled, and we have to go.

I’ve been a shameless sucker for a good meal and a decent drink my whole life, even back when I was small — and in those early days, it was 90% eating, let’s be real. I had an appetite that could outrun a farmhand, and a digestion so ironclad I could chomp through a bag of unripe green gooseberries before breakfast and not even burp once. I still remember one pasty, dead-eyed gent coming over to dine at our house once. He sat watching me shovel food into my mouth for a full five minutes, looking like he’d just stumbled on a unicorn in the dining room, then turned to my dad and went, “Does your boy ever get dyspepsia?”
“Never heard him complain of anything of the sort,” Dad said, then nudged me. “Colly Wobbles, do you ever get dyspepsia?” (That stupid nickname stuck to me for years, for no reason I can fathom. It’s not even close to my real name.)
“Nope, Pa,” I mumbled around a mouthful of mashed potato, then added, “What even is dyspepsia, anyway?”
That pale dyspeptic looked at me like I was some kind of miracle he’d never thought possible, mixed with the bitter envy of a man who hasn’t tasted a good pork chop without paying for it with three hours of stomach ache in ten years. He sighed slow as molasses and said, quiet as a funeral parlor attendant, “You’ll find out someday, kiddo.”
My sweet old mum used to say seeing me eat made her happy, and that’s one of my favorite old memories now — I must have given her a whole lot of joy on that front, because I ate enough to feed three scrawny toddlers back then. A healthy growing lad who runs around all day and makes very, very sure he never overdoes the studying? He’ll easily out-eat every expectation you could possibly have for his feeding capacity.
It’s uproariously funny watching teen boys chow down, as long as you’re not the one footing the bill. Their idea of a normal “light” meal is a pound and a half of roast beef, six mealy big potatoes (the soggy ones, obviously, they fill you up more), heaps of greens, four thick slabs of Yorkshire pudding, two currant dumplings, a handful of sour green apples, a penny’s worth of walnuts, six jam biscuits, and a whole bottle of ginger beer. And after that? They run off to play at horses like they didn’t just pack away half a larder. They must look at us grown men like we’re absolute weaklings, who need to slump on the couch for two hours after a meal that’s just a spoonful of clear soup and the wing of a single chicken.
But boys don’t have all the wins, not really. A boy never gets the pure luxury of being properly, blissfully full. He can never flop back, stretch his legs, tuck his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and sink into that hazy, ethereal bliss that wraps around a man who’s just finished a perfect dinner. A meal doesn’t change a boy’s whole world. For a grown man? A good dinner is magic potion. Suddenly the whole world looks brighter, kinder, softer. A man who’s eaten to his heart’s content feels warm, fuzzy love for every single person he passes. He pets the cat so gently he calls it “poor little puss” in this wobbly, tender voice. He pauses when he hears the German buskers outside, and worries if their fingers are cold in the wind. For ten whole minutes, he doesn’t even hate his wife’s side of the family.
A good meal pulls out every soft part of you. Even the grumpy, sour-faced old curmudgeon who spends all day looking like he survives on vinegar and laxatives will crack a smile after dinner, start patting small kids on the head, and mumble vaguely about giving them a sixpence sometime. Serious stiff types thaw out and get all mild and jolly, even those snobby young guys with the big fuzzy mustaches who usually spend all day being insufferable, they forget to be rude for an hour.
I get all sentimental myself after dinner, it’s the only time I can stand sappy love stories. When the hero clasps the heroine to his chest for that last dramatic embrace and stifles a sob, I get so sad I feel like I got dealt nothing but low cards at whist all night. When the heroine dies at the end? I full-on weep. If I read that exact same story first thing in the morning, I’d snort and throw it across the room. Digestion — or more accurately, bad digestion — does wild things to your heart. If I ever want to write something super tragic, I eat a giant plate of hot buttered muffins an hour before I sit down at the desk. By the time I pick up my pen, I’m drowning in unutterable melancholy. I can picture heartbroken lovers parting forever at a country stile, twilight sinking all around them, the only sound a distant sheep bell tinkling through the sad silence. Old men sit staring at dried, wilted flowers till their eyes blur over with tears. Dainty young maidens wait at their open windows for their sweethearts, but “he never comes,” their sunny blonde hair fades thin and white, the years roll past. The babies they once bounced on their knees are now grown up with their own headaches, the friends they laughed with are buried under the waving grass. But they still wait, till the dark night creeps up and the whole sad old world fades away from their sore eyes. I see pale corpses bobbing on white foam, deathbeds streaked with tears, graves out in the middle of empty deserts. I hear women wailing, little kids whimpering, strong men sobbing dryly. It’s all the muffins. You could never get that much tragic fancy going on a mutton chop and a glass of champagne.
A full stomach is poetry fuel, plain and simple. No sentiment in the world can survive on an empty belly. We don’t have the time or energy to mope over made-up woes till we’ve fixed our real problems first. You’re not going to sigh over your dead pet canary if the bailiff is standing in your front parlor. When you have no clue where your next shilling is coming from, you’re not going to lie awake worrying if your girlfriend’s smiles are cold, hot, or lukewarm, or anything else about her.
Folks will tell you mental hurt is way worse than physical pain — the kind of folks I call fools, who happen to have opinions different from mine. If there’s one type of person I can’t stand, it’s the guy who doesn’t agree with me on every single topic. That romantic tripe about heartbreak being worse than a sore body? It’s so comforting to the lovesick young idiot who looks down at some poor starving guy and thinks “wow, you’re so lucky compared to me,” and so soothing to fat rich old gents who cackle about how poverty is so much better than being wealthy. But it’s all nonsense, all hot air. A splitting headache will make you forget a broken heart in ten seconds. A cut finger will wipe every memory of the person who left you out of your brain. When you’re actually, properly hungry? You don’t feel a single other thing.
Us well-fed soft city folks have no idea what real hunger is. We know what it’s like to have no appetite, turn down fancy food laid out in front of us, but we don’t know what it’s like to crave food so bad it hurts — to be dying for a crust of bread while other people throw loaves away, to stare through a grimy window at a plate of steaming cheap pea pudding, your mouth watering, but you don’t even have a penny to buy it. Hunger is just a fancy fun sauce for us, the thing that makes a meal taste better. It’s worth getting ravenously hungry just to see how much pure joy you can get out of a simple meal. If you want to enjoy your dinner more than you ever have, go on a thirty mile walk after breakfast and don’t eat a single thing till you get home. Your eyes will light up the second you see that white tablecloth and the steaming dishes! You’ll put down your empty mug of beer with a happy sigh, grab your knife and fork, and then lean back after, light a cigar, and beam at everyone in the room like you’re the luckiest man alive.
Just make very sure that amazing dinner is actually there waiting for you, though, or the disappointment will break your heart a little. I remember once my old pal Joe and I — dear old Joe, man. It’s been eight years since I saw Joseph Taboys last, how weird we all drift apart in life. I’d love to see his big jovial face again, shake his strong hand, hear that loud cheery laugh of his. Also, he still owes me 14 shillings. Anyway, we were on holiday, got up super early for breakfast, and headed out for a massive long walk. We’d ordered a duck for dinner the night before, told the landlady “get a big one, we’re gonna be so hungry when we get back.” Right as we left, she ran up to us grinning, holding up a bird the size of a doormat, and said “If you two lads finish that, you’re legends.” We snickered, puffed out our chests like we were world champion eaters, and promised we’d give it our best shot.
Then we immediately got lost. That’s what I do in the country, every single time. It’s infuriating, because asking a local for directions is useless, you might as well ask a chambermaid how to run a bank. You have to yell your question three times before it even penetrates their thick skull. The third time, they slowly lift their head, stare at you blankly. You yell it a fourth time, they parrot it back to you, then stand there pondering for two whole minutes, speaking three words a minute, going “Well, you couldn’t do better than —” then they spot another dumb yokel walking down the road, yell the question at him, and the two of them argue for 15 minutes before they tell you to go down the lane, turn right, cross the third stile, keep left past Jimmy Milcher’s cowshed, cut across the seven-acre field, go through the gate by Squire Grubbin’s haystack, follow the bridle path till you’re opposite the old windmill — which isn’t there anymore, obviously — then turn right, leave Stiggin’s plantation behind you. You thank them, walk away with a throbbing headache, and have no clue where you’re going, the only thing you’re sure of is that somewhere there’s a stile you need to climb. Then you turn the next corner, and find four stiles, all leading in completely different directions.
We went through that whole mess three separate times. We tramped across fields, waded through brooks, scrambled over hedges and stone walls. We had a full blown fight about who was the idiot who’d gotten us lost in the first place. We were grumpy, footsore, exhausted. But the thought of that duck kept us going. It was like a fairy tale vision floating in front of our eyes, pulling us forward. We talked about it the whole time, cheered each other up going “come on, the duck’s gonna get cold.” We passed a village inn halfway, got so tempted to go in and get a loaf of bread and a block of cheese, but we were heroes. We decided to hold out, because the duck would taste even better if we were starving. We thought we smelled it the second we walked back into town, and did the last quarter mile in three full minutes flat.
We ran upstairs, washed up, changed our clothes, zoomed back down, pulled our chairs up to the table, rubbed our hands together while the landlady took the lids off the dishes. I grabbed the knife and fork and went to carve the duck.
It did not want to be carved. I hacked at it for five whole minutes, didn’t make a single dent. Joe, who was already shoveling potatoes into his mouth, went “Uh, maybe someone who actually knows how to carve should do that?” I ignored his stupid comment, attacked the duck again so hard the whole bird slid off the plate and landed in the fender. We fished it out, of course, and I was about to try again, but Joe was getting mad. He said if he’d known we were gonna play blind hockey with our dinner he would’ve stopped for bread and cheese back in the village. I was too tired to argue. I put the knife and fork down all dignified, sat to the side, and Joe stepped up to fight the wretched thing. He worked in silence for a while, then muttered “Damn the duck” and took his coat off.
We did finally break it apart, with a chisel we found in the kitchen, but it was completely inedible. It was like trying to chew a piece of rubber. We had to eat our dinner off the vegetables and an apple tart. That duck was so tough, no one should’ve been allowed to kill it, it was older than the Queen. But there you go, no one respects old age in this country anymore.
I started this whole essay meaning to talk about eating and drinking, and I’ve only gone on and on about eating so far. Well, drinking is one of those subjects you don’t want to look like you know too much about, right? The days where going to bed blackout drunk every night was considered manly are long gone. Now if you smell like stale beer, have a blotchy red face, can barely walk straight, and sound like you gargled gravel? Everyone assumes you’re a total cad, not a gentleman.
Even so, humans have a thirst that’s straight up supernatural. We drink for every excuse under the sun. No man feels comfortable unless there’s a glass sitting in front of him. We drink before meals, with meals, after meals. We drink when we meet a friend, drink again when we say goodbye. We drink when we’re talking, when we’re reading, when we’re thinking. We drink to everyone else’s health and ruin our own. We toast the Queen, the army, the ladies, every person we can think of that we can raise a glass to. I swear if we ran out of other people to toast, we’d raise a glass to our mothers-in-law. And hey, we never eat someone’s health, always drink it. Why don’t we ever stand up and eat a whole tart to someone’s success?
I still don’t get why everyone has to drink all the time, honestly. I get drinking to drown your sorrows, that makes sense. I get why the poor folks living in damp cellars drink to get drunk — of course we all act all shocked about it, us cozy people sitting in our warm nice houses with all our little luxuries, going “how terrible that the poor people go to the pub and drink gin to forget their lives for a little while.” But before you clutch your pearls, stop and think what their lives are actually like. They drag out every year in a tiny, stinking room that’s no better than a sewer, huddled with a dozen other people. Dirty kids scream and fight, loud drunk women yell and cuff each other, the street outside is full of filth and noise, the whole building is bedlam. Their life has no joy, no softness, no light. A horse in a stall gets to eat sweet hay and munch corn in peace. A dog in a kennel gets to nap in the sun and dream about chasing rabbits. These people never get a single moment of that. No fun, no friends, no nice things. They never feel a little bit of kindness, never have a single happy hope. In the name of all that’s merciful, let them drink that gin, if it makes them feel like they’re alive for ten whole minutes.
We can spout all the sappy sentiment we want, but the stomach is the real seat of all happiness on this planet. The kitchen is our main temple, the roaring fire is our sacred flame, and the cook is our high priest. He’s a kind, powerful magician. He soothes away all your sorrows, chases all the fights out of you, makes every happy feeling twice as bright. Our God is great, and the cook is his prophet. Let’s all eat, drink, and be merry.

“Oh right, you’ve got rooms to let, then?”
“What is it now?”
“Gentleman at the door asking after the spare rooms!”
“Invite him in, I’ll be two ticks, just got these potatoes not to burn.”
“Step right in, sir, won’t you? Mum’ll be up in a jiffy.”

You shuffle inside, sure enough, after a minute the landlady trundles up the kitchen stairs, undoing her apron strings one by one while yelling instructions down the stairwell about not over-salting the spuds. She gives you a smile that looks like it’s been through three washes and hung out in the rain, and says “Good morning, sir, if you’ll just follow me up?”
“Honestly, no need for all that,” you protest, already mentally counting the change in your pocket to make sure you can afford the weekly rent before you even haul yourself up a flight of stairs. “What’s the place like, how much a week?”
“Ah, you’ll get a far better idea once you see it, sir, c’mon up.”
You huff a bit, grumbling under your breath that if this turns out to be a waste of your half hour you’re definitely billing her for it later, and trail after her.

Halfway up the first landing you nearly trip over a bucket and broom left dead center of the step, which gives her the perfect opening to rant for five whole minutes about how useless the young servant girls are these days, before bawling over the banister at Sarah to drag that lot out of the passage RIGHT NOW. When you finally get to the door of the flat, she plants a hand on the doorknob and pauses to pre-emptively make excuses: it’s a bit messy, see? Last lodger only cleared out yesterday. And, wouldn’t you know it, it’s their cleaning day today. It’s always their cleaning day, every single time anyone comes to view. You nod, unsurprised, and step inside, and the two of you stand there staring at the space like you’ve both just walked into a funeral no one told you about.

The place does not exactly scream “come move in and have a lovely time”. Even the landlady’s face isn’t putting on a show of pretending it’s nice. Empty furnished apartments, hit by harsh mid-morning sunlight, are about as cheerful as a wet Monday commute. They’ve got no life in them at all, nothing that makes you feel welcome. It’s a whole different story once you’ve moved in, of course. Once you’ve dragged all your silly little junk out of your suitcase to scatter everywhere: the photos of every girl you ever had a crush on lined up along the mantelpiece, six scruffy old pipes sitting out where everyone can see them, one slipper peeking out under the coal bin and the other balanced precariously on top of the piano. You’ve got your own tattered posters taped up to hide the dingy patches on the wall, your dog-eared books piled all over every flat surface, that chipped blue teacup your mum gave you that you refuse to throw out, and that old embroidery screen she made when she was a girl, back before her hair turned white and she had to wear those stuffy old bonnets with the little brims.

Oh, that old screen. Back when it was new, all those roses and lilies stitched on the same stem were bright and shiny as anything. Decades have rolled past since then, and it’s sat in front of the fire so many times its colours have faded, the moths have chewed little holes in the silk, and it’s all gone soft and grey at the edges, just like the hands that stitched it. Sometimes it looks so serious, sitting there by the embers, I swear it remembers those old days, when my mum was a teenager laying it across her lap working on the stitches, and some daft lad (my dad, turns out!) kept grabbing her hand to kiss it and messing up her stitching. He’d even toss the screen aside just so he could hold both her hands and stare at her face, the selfish git. Makes you wonder, doesn’t it? Did people make eyes at each other 150 years ago exactly the same way they do now? Do girls’ hearts beat just as fast under those old frilly pearl bodices as they do under a baggy hoodie these days? Has anyone’s brain changed at all since we swapped pointy helmets for bowler hats? Oh, old Father Time, you’ve dried up seas and flattened mountains, but you never messed with the human heart, did you? You can chop down the flowers, but the roots run way too deep for your scythe. The whole world spins round following your rules, but people’s silly soft hearts don’t answer to you at all.

Oops, got a bit sidetracked there, back to the rented flats. Old furniture makes you do that, right? Fancies cling to them like moss on an old stone wall. Your own chairs and tables turn into quiet little mates, part of your life. Think of all the secret dramas they’ve seen! All the people crying their eyes out into the sofa cushions, all the secret whispered proposals no one else ever heard. New furniture’s got nothing on that. You can build a palace out of brand new stuff, but you can’t build a home. A home needs junk that’s got stories attached to it, not just random old junk that came with the furnished apartment.

That lodging house furniture, no matter how beat up it is, never feels like yours. It’s a new stranger, and all strangers show you their worst side first. The “comfy easy chair” has got lumpy wood under the scratchy horsehair that digs right into your back. The mirror’s so smoky you can barely tell if you’ve shaved one side of your face properly. The curtains look like they haven’t seen a washing machine since the 1890s, the carpet’s frayed right through to the floorboards by the door, and the dining table looks like it’d collapse the second you set a full plate of stew down on it. The grate’s sad and empty, the wallpaper’s that horrible pea-green colour that gives you a headache, and the ceiling looks like someone spilled a whole pot of coffee on it by accident.

And the ornaments! You’d swear there’s a secret factory somewhere only making lodging house tat, you see the exact same stuff in every single rented flat across the whole country, and nowhere else. There’s those two glass things on either end of the mantelpiece, with long dangling triangle bits of glass that clink together every time a lorry drives past, and they make you so jittery you’re convinced they’re gonna fall on your head any second. In the cheaper flats, you get a couple of china bits that could be a cow sitting down, or the ruins of a temple, or a dog, who can tell. Then there’s that weird pale yellow lump thing that looks like half-baked dough someone forgot to throw out, which the landlady proudly calls a “statue of Cupid”. On the wall you’ve got a terrible sampler some distant cousin stitched when they were 10, a print of that painting of the Huguenots getting stabbed, two random bible verses, and a framed certificate proving someone in the family had their smallpox jab, or was a member of the Odd Fellows, or something equally uninteresting.

You stare at all this mess for a bit, then turn to the landlady and go, “So how much a week, then?”
When she tells you the number, you go, “Blimey, that’s a bit steep, innit?”
And she’ll immediately burst into fake honesty, go “Well, to tell you the truth, I used to charge *three shillings more* than that, and before the last lodger I was getting even more than that!”
Makes you wonder what rent was like 20 years ago, those old lodgers must have been made of money, or they all ended up bankrupt. If I was around back then I’d have had to live in the attic, no question.

Funny thing about lodging houses, the whole world’s upside down. The richer you get, the lower down the stairs you live. The poor bloke’s at the very top in the attic, the rich gentleman’s right down on the ground floor. You start out in the garret, and if you do well enough you work your way down, not up. Loads of great famous people lived in attics! The dictionary says an attic’s “a place to store junk”, and the world’s spent centuries shoving all its clever “junk” up there: preachers, painters, poets, all the blokes who won’t stop saying the truth no one wants to hear. Haydn grew up in an attic, Chatterton starved in one. Addison and Goldsmith wrote their best stuff in garrets, Dr Johnson slept on the rickety little trundle beds up there no problem. Dickens spent his teen years in attics, Hans Andersen thought up all his fairy tales under the slanted roofs. The list goes on forever, right? As long as houses have had two floors, the attic’s been where all the genius gets born. If you stacked up all the greatest art and thoughts and inventions humanity ever made, the biggest pile by far would be the stuff made in those cold, damp garrets, by blokes living on bread and tea. The world can lock those clever blokes up in the attics, leave them to starve, laugh at them, forget they exist… but those blokes’ thoughts fly out the window, and everyone hears them anyway. They shoved Rousseau in a tiny garret in Paris and laughed at him, and a hundred years later his words started the French Revolution, and we’re still feeling the effects of that today.

Don’t get me wrong, I don’t want to *live* in an attic. For starters, you’re running up and down the stairs all the time so much it feels like you’re on a treadmill. The slanted ceiling means you’re constantly bumping your head on the rafters when you try to shave, and the tomcats yowling on the tiles right outside your window at 2am are so loud they’ll shake you awake out of a dead sleep. No, give me a fancy first floor flat in Piccadilly to live in, please (someone offer, I’ll move in tomorrow). But if I want to sit and think? Give me an attic ten flights up, in the middle of the busiest, noisiest part of the city. You can sit up there, look down on all the people rushing around like ants on an anthill, all yelling and shoving and running after silly tiny little things, and none of their noise ever reaches you up there. You’re all alone up there with the stars.

The silliest attic I ever shared with a mate years ago was absolutely insane. I’ve seen less weird shapes in a maze at Hampton Court. The bloke who designed it must have been a genius, but he should’ve made puzzle boxes instead of houses. It had seven corners total, two of the walls sloped right inwards to a point, and the window was directly above the fireplace. The only spot big enough for the bed was right between the door and the cupboard, so to get anything out of the cupboard you had to clamber over the bed, and half the stuff you fetched out got dropped straight into the duvet. By the end of every evening the bed was like a little general store, full of pens, mugs, bits of bread, and mostly coal. We kept the coal under the cupboard, right? So every time we needed a shovel of coal for the fire, we’d climb over the bed, fill the shovel, then try and tiptoe back without falling over. 9 times out of 10, we, the shovel, the coals and the whole bed would end up in a tangled heap on the floor. I’ve heard people gush about “beds of coal” before – we slept in one every night, and we never got snobby about it.

And that wasn’t even the funniest bit of the house. Every single door opened outwards, so if you were walking down the stairs and someone inside a room decided to yank the door open at the same time, you’d get knocked flying straight down the steps. There wasn’t even a proper ground floor – the bit that should’ve been the ground floor was part of the house next door, so the front door opened directly onto a flight of stairs leading straight down to the cellar. Every new visitor who walked in would shoot past the person who answered the door and tumble straight down into the dark, and the nervous ones would start screaming “murder!” at the top of their lungs laying on the cellar floor until someone came and hauled them up.

I haven’t lived in an attic in donkey’s years now. I’ve lived on all the different floors since, but none of them change much, do they? Life tastes the same whether you’re drinking it out of a cheap tin mug or a fancy golden goblet. You laugh just as hard sitting on a cheap wooden chair as you do on a velvet cushion. The sad bits hurt just as bad no matter what kind of walls you’ve got around you. The world’s got a perfect little balancing trick going on: every bit of extra happiness you get from more money, you lose somewhere else, ‘cause your wants get bigger just as fast as your wallet does. When you live in an attic, a supper of fried fish and a pint of stout feels like the best meal you’ve ever had. When you move down to the fancy first floor, you need a three course dinner at a fancy West End hotel just to get the exact same little buzz out of it.

There’s a crowd of people who ought to be downright ashamed of themselves that love to spout the line: wearing an outfit that makes you feel like a million bucks gives you a bliss high no church sermon or well-meaning homily could ever match. And hate to break it to the prudes, those cynical sods are mostly right.

I still remember back when I was a spry, stupid young lad (the kind of “many, many years ago” storybooks reference to skip all the boring backstory bits), whenever life kicked my self-esteem right in the teeth, I’d drag out my absolute best duds and prance right out the front door. If my washerwoman dumped me for owing three months’ bills, or my 10th blank verse poem got returned with the editor’s dorky little note saying “regret no space to use your very kind offer”, or the lass I’d been head over heels for snubbed me in front of all her friends? I’d yank on that glossy new hat, slip into the pair of trousers with the perfect razor-sharp crease I’d spent three nights tucking under my mattress (not on the floor, you absolute heathen), and suddenly I was a new man. I remembered there were a dozen other washerwomen in the city, and a dozen more girls who’d absolutely fawn over how clever and handsome I was. I was ready to go flirt with every pretty maid I passed, and I swear that suit gave me the guts to do it.

Clothes do half the work when you’re courting, no question. Any young bloke will confirm that – he’ll spend two whole hours dithering over what to wear before he even leaves the house. The first half hour is just debating: light summer suit, cane, and drab little flat cap? Or black tails, top hat, and brand new never-used brolly? The Fates make sure he loses no matter what he picks. If he goes for the light suit and cane? It’ll bucket down out of nowhere ten minutes later, he turns up at her doorstep soaked, boots caked in mud, and spends the whole evening trying to tuck his feet so far under the sofa no one can see them. If he goes for the top hat and brolly? No self-respecting man dares go out in a top hat without an umbrella – it’s like letting a wobbly toddler toddle down the street alone without a nurse, for heaven’s sake. I hate top hats, by the way. Mine’s five years old now, everyone said it was hopelessly out of style last summer, turns out the old silly shapes are back, so I’m practically a trendsetter these days. But back to that poor courting sod: if he’s hauling the top hat and umbrella, the afternoon turns so sweltering the soap he used to slick his mustache melts, and that perfect little 20-minute curl he arranged on his forehead droops into a sad limp strand that looks exactly like a piece of soggy seaweed. And if by some absolute miracle he makes it to her door all crisp and put-together? She’s out with her cousin, won’t be back till 9 at the earliest.

No wonder poor young lovers stare at those old Christmas card gallants from 70 years ago with pure envy. Curly hair, neat little hats, tight breeches that show off their nice legs, dainty Hessian boots, ruffled frills, dangling watch seals. No wonder the lass in the giant poke bonnet and pale blue sash drops her eyes and melts instantly – you could win a dozen hearts in that getup. What chance do we have in baggy trousers and sad, shapeless monkey jackets?

Clothes shape your entire personality, you know. Stick a bloke in threadbare rags that haven’t seen a wash in months, he’ll skulk down the street staring at his feet like he’s sneaking out to buy a pint of beer behind his wife’s back. Deck that exact same man out in fancy threads and crisp starched linen? He’ll strut down the high street swinging his cane, winking at every lass that passes, as perky as a bantam rooster. Give a man a plumed bonnet, a dagger stuck in his belt, and all those fluffy ruffled bits down his sleeves? He’ll instantly be ready to pick a duel with a stranger over a bad look. Stick him in a heavy shapeless overcoat? He’ll duck behind the nearest lamppost and yell for a copper at the slightest weird noise. I’ll admit all the good, loyal, honest affection you can find under boring tweed and broadcloth is just as solid as anything they had under silk and velvet back in the knight days – but that chivalric spirit of riding a whole tournament just to make a lady smile? You don’t summon that out of a bloke wearing a stuffy black three-piece suit. You need clanking armor, swishing plumes, the rustle of bright satin.

This whole world’s gotten so middle-aged and boring, isn’t it? We went through the baby human phase, running around in loose robes barefoot, then the barbarian teen phase where we tattooed our whole bodies and never combed our hair, then the foppish young adult phase where we pranced around in scarlet doublets and curly wigs flirting and showing off. Now we’re all so sober, so serious, and some would say so stupid. The 19th century world is a stuffy middle-aged gentleman who’d clutch his pearls if he caught himself wearing anything fun. We all dress in black coats, black hats, black boots, acting like it’s deeply embarrassing that we ever pranced around as troubadours or knight-errants in bright colorful clothes. We’re so “sensible” these days, turns out sensible and dull are practically synonyms. And if you’re extra pious? You wear all black, even gloves and neckties, and soon they’ll all be wearing black shirts, mark my words. The halfway decent, moderately sinful folk wear light trousers on weekdays, some even rock a fun patterned waistcoat. And the absolute heathens? The worst sinners alive? They walk around in light summer suits, some even wear white hats. You can’t mention those people in polite company, I probably shouldn’t have named them here.

Talking of light suits, have you ever noticed how every stranger on the street gawks at you the very first time you wear a new light suit? By the third time you’ve worn it, the entire population of London is used to it. Not that I’d know, of course. I don’t wear those, those are only for bad people. But sometimes I look in the mirror at my two sad long baggy trousers, all rugged and worn at the knees, my stiff stand-up collar and scuffed flat cap, and wonder why I’m out here making God’s green earth look ugly. And then I get these wicked, wonderful thoughts. Why can’t I wear lavender tights, red velvet breeches, a bright green doublet slashed with yellow? A pale blue silk cape over my shoulder, a black eagle feather sticking out of my hat, a big sword at my hip, a falcon on my wrist, a prancing white horse under me? I could make every stranger’s day brighter just walking down the road. Why do we all have to look like a bunch of identical ants crawling over a dust heap? Why not dress a little fun? We’d be happier for it, wouldn’t we? Let the philosophers dress like old crows all in black, I want to be a butterfly.

Women definitely get to dress as pretty as they want, it’s their superpower. They’re the flowers of the earth, after all – the whole world would be unbearably dreary without their bright dresses and smiling faces. Those “relations” that pop round your dreary bachelor flat? They leave ribbons and laces and gloves and parasols scattered all over the place, it’s like a rainbow wandered in for tea. Summer’s best bit is seeing all the girls in bright pink, blue, white, bobbing between the trees, dotting the green fields, glinting in the sun. You can spot those bright dresses for miles. Right now I can see four white dresses climbing a hill three miles from my window, I thought at first they were mile markers that snuck out to have a laugh. Though that’s extra nice if it turns out those four dresses are your wife and your mother-in-law – you get a whole three miles to hide.

Speaking of long walks and stile posts, can we talk for a second about how every English woman’s boots are at least two sizes too big? I swear no woman in this island can find a boot that fits properly. I’ve lost count of how many times I’ve seen a lass plop down on the top rail of a stile and say she can’t walk another step, her boots are killing her. Every single one says they’re too big. We need a boot revolution, for the love of all that’s holy. In the name of every husband, father, and cousin in England, we demand more narrow size 2 boots in stock. Stop torturing our ladies. And don’t even get me started on waistbands! Dressmakers make them so loose the hooks and eyes pop off every five minutes with a bang that sounds like a thunderclap. Why do they put up with that? They’re obsessed with clothes, for goodness sake, they think about it 24/7. If you ever see two women huddled by a window chatting, don’t stand there thinking they’re talking about poetry or charity work. Move a little closer, you’ll hear one say “I took in the waist and let out one seam, it fits perfectly now” and the other go “Oh I’m wearing my plum colored bodice to the Jones’s house, with a yellow plastron, and Puttick’s has the nicest gloves for one and eleven pence!” Once I took two ladies for a drive through beautiful Derbyshire, all rolling hills and postcard-perfect views. I’d wave my umbrella and go “Look at that! The blue distant hills, that little white house in the woods is Chatsworth!” and one of them would go “Lovely. Hey, how about a yard of sarsenet for the skirt?” I’d point out a waterfall, they’d go “Charming. Have you seen my new handkerchief trim?” I swear if you marooned two women on a desert island, they’d spend all day arguing over whether sea shells or bird eggs make better dress trimmings, and come up with a brand new fig leaf fashion every single month.

Young lads care a whole lot about clothes too, but they never talk about it to each other. Fops get a totally unfair rap from other men. It’s a harmless little phase, it wears off fast. A 20 year old with a little bit of dandy in him is gonna turn into a 40 year old who wears clean collars and brushes his coat. You don’t want a bloke who’s too meek and humble. All that prattle about “modest worth” is rubbish, no one actually likes it. Uriah Heep’s dad was an idiot for telling his son everyone loves humble people. Nothing annoys people more. Half the fun of life is a good row, and you can’t have a proper satisfying fight with someone who just meekly apologizes. You get all riled up ready for a good yell, and they just sit there all soft, you can’t even let your anger out. Imagine being Xantippe, married to Socrates! The man was so infuriatingly calm she poured a full bucket of dirty water all over him, and he just sat there, smiled, and said something wise about rain. She probably spent ages trekking to get that extra muddy water, waited all afternoon for him to come home, thinking finally, finally we’ll have a proper argument. And nothing. She probably cried for an hour after. What good is a great philosopher husband if he won’t even have a silly little fight with you? Women’s lives are so boring, anyway! They can’t go to political meetings, they can’t join the local mock parliament, they’re banned from smoking carriages on the tube, no one lets them read comic papers. The least a good husband can do is give them a nice little row every once in a while for fun. They love that, it makes them tell all their friends what a wonderful, thoughtful man he was after he dies.

Everyone’s looking for the wrong thing in a husband, anyway. You don’t want some super clever literary or scientific genius who messes up the whole house trying to do experiments. What you want is a bloke who can run an errand perfectly without inserting his own stupid opinions, who can hold a baby the right way up, and who doesn’t complain when dinner is lukewarm mutton. That’s the ideal husband. Brains don’t get you any points at all in married life. Perfect example: there’s a little boy who wanted to be a sailor, he told the captain he could recite the multiplication table backwards, he knew how many times “begat” was in the whole Old Testament, he could recite all the classic poems. The captain said “Great, but can you carry lumps of coal?” That’s married life right there. Doesn’t matter how much fancy stuff you know, can you carry the coal?

“I remember, I remember, In the days of chill November, How the blackbird on the—”

Dash it all, I’ve blanked on the rest. That’s the opening of the first proper poem I ever learned, mind you—none of that frivolous “Hey, diddle diddle, the cat and the fiddle” nonsense counts as real poetry, obviously. I raked in four whole pennies reciting that little verse for doting relatives, too. I knew it was exactly fourpence because they kept badgering me that if I held onto it long enough to earn two more, I’d have sixpence, a mathematical miracle even a small kid couldn’t argue with. Did that stop me? Not a bit. I blew every last penny the very next morning, to the best of my recollection. What on? Don’t ask. Memory’s gone completely blank on that score.

That’s Memory for you, isn’t it? A spoiled, stubborn little gremlin, and every single toy she drags out to show you is broken. I can vividly remember tripping and tumbling headfirst into a massive dust hole when I was tiny as anything, but I have zero recollection of ever climbing back out. If all I had to go on was my memory, I’d be fully convinced I’m still there, buried under three feet of ancient household sweepings.

A few years later, I was right in the middle of a very dramatic, very promising love scene—you know the one, the pauses, the soft hand touching, all the flowery lines I’d rehearsed for three whole days. The only thing I can still picture clearly about the whole moment? Right when we were about to kiss, some fool wrenches the door open and goes “Emily, you’re wanted” in a tone so grim you’d think the police had turned up to arrest her for grand theft. Every tender thing she whispered to me, every dazzling romantic line I spent hours perfecting? Poof. Vanished. Not a trace.

Looking back at your own life is like wandering around a half-ruined castle, isn’t it? There’s a shattered column where the grand front entrance used to be, a jagged bit of window frame marking where the pretty lady’s bedchamber stood, and a moldery heap of black rubble where the great fireplace once roared. And everything looks nicer when you see it through the soft, blurry haze of old time. Even the sad stuff that’s over tastes sweet, somehow.

Our boyhood days look like nothing but nutting trips, hoop races, and unlimited gingerbread now, don’t they? We’ve completely forgotten the snubs, the toothaches, and the Latin verb conjugations—especially the Latin verb conjugations. We look back at our awkward teen years when we were first falling in love and think we were the happiest creatures alive, and we’d give anything to feel that giddy rush again. We never spare a thought for the way our chest ached for weeks, the nights we laid awake staring at the ceiling, the dry, tight burn in our throat when she said she could only ever be a sister to us—like any man on earth needs more sisters!

Yep, when we glance backwards, we only see the sunshine, no shadows. The whole road we’ve already walked stretches out all nice and smooth behind us. We don’t spot the sharp stones that cut our socks and our feet, we only notice the roses growing along the edge, and the big thorny brambles that used to leave our ankles scratched raw? From far away they just look like soft green tendrils swaying gently in the breeze. Thank the good Lord for that, right? Every new link we add to that long, long chain of our memory is a nice one, and all the bitterness and grief that feels unbearable today will be something we smile at, no big deal, tomorrow.

All the bright, happy bits of our lives are the last to sink below the dark waves of forgetfulness, too. They stick up above the water, still in plain sight, long after all the angry thoughts and stinging pains are buried deep where they can’t bother us ever again.

That’s exactly why old people go on such absolute nonsense about the “good old days” when they were young, I reckon. Back then, according to them, the whole world was run properly. Boys were *real* boys, girls were way more proper than the ones nowadays, winters were actually cold and snowy like winters are supposed to be, and summers weren’t the sad, lukewarm, drizzly mess we get stuck with now. And the feats of daring they pulled off back then? You’d need three strapping burly men just to even *pretend* to believe half of it.

I love sitting and listening to one of these old blokes spin his tales to a crowd of kids who are way too polite to contradict him. Give him ten minutes, and he’ll be swearing that the moon shone every single night of his childhood, and tossing mad bulls up and down in a blanket was the standard after-school sport at his school. This isn’t new, either. The old folks back when our grandpas were teens sang the exact same tune. And today’s young lot will be spouting the exact same nonsense in fifty years, to annoy their own grandkids. “Oh, give me back the wonderful good old days!” people have been wailing ever since Adam turned 51 and looked back on his youth.

Dig up a book of poetry or a novel from 1835, and you’ll see the writers begging for that same impossible trip back, same as the old German minstrels before them, same as the Norse saga scribes, same as the ancient Greek philosophers and the earliest prophets. From everything anyone’s ever written, the world has been getting steadily worse ever since the day it opened for business. All I can say is, it must have been a right rollicking delight when it was brand new, because even now it’s pretty great if you stay mostly in the sunshine and don’t throw a fit when it rains.

Still, no use lying about it: the world must have been extra sweet back in that dewy, fresh morning of creation, before millions of tramping feet had ground all the soft grass to dust, before the constant roar of cities had chased all the quiet away. Those first loose-robed, barefoot ancestors of ours must have had such a noble, calm life, wandering under the big sky, living in sun-warmed tents next to their lowing herds, taking what they needed straight from kind Nature—no bills, no rush-hour trains, no French grammar exercises. That quiet, innocent childhood of the whole human race is gone forever now, though. We’re all grown up now, stuck in a noisy, chaotic world full of doubts and big hopes, and we’ve got work to do, so we’d better hurry along.

We don’t know what that big work even is, of course, what tiny little part we play in the whole grand design. We’re just like those little coral bugs working deep under the dark sea, scurrying around after our own small silly goals, with no clue we’re helping build a whole giant island for God.

So let’s stop moping and pining for days that are never ever coming back. Our work’s out in front of us, not behind. “Forward!” ought to be our motto. We shouldn’t sit around staring at the past like it’s the whole finished house—all it is, is the foundation. We waste so much time being sad about “what might have been” that we miss all the great stuff that still could be, right in front of our noses. Opportunities zoom past us while we’re still crying over the chances we already missed, and we don’t even notice the happiness that’s knocking at our door right now, because we’re too busy grieving the happiness that’s already gone.

I read a story once, ages ago, back when I used to sit by the fire and get lost in fairy tales, about a very brave, very true knight. He’d fought all sorts of monsters, traveled all over the world, everyone knew him as fearless—well, almost fearless, the kind of brave that makes sense, not the stupid kind where you fight a dragon with a teaspoon just to prove you’re tough. One day he’s riding down a terrible, hard road, and he’s worn out, and his heart’s heavy. Giant dark rocks hang over his head, ready to fall on him any second, chasms yawn on both sides full of robbers and fire-breathing dragons, and the whole path is pitch black like midnight. He decides he’s had enough, he’s turning back to find an easier road for his poor old horse. But when he twists around to look behind him? The entire road he just rode is gone. There’s nothing but a massive, bottomless chasm right behind his horse’s hooves, no way to go back at all. So he says a quick prayer to Saint Cuthbert, spurs his horse forward, and rides on as brave as can be. And nothing hurts him at all.

That’s the road of life, isn’t it? No U-turns allowed. Every single step you take, the little bit of time you just stood on sinks back into eternity forever. The past is gone, it’s locked away, it’s not yours to mess with anymore. You can never un-say a single dumb word, you can never step backwards even one tiny step. So we might as well be that brave knight, right? Ride forward cheerfully, don’t waste time sniveling because we can’t turn back. Every second gives you a whole new fresh life, why not go meet it happy? You’ve got to keep moving no matter what, anyway, and you’ll walk a whole lot steadier looking forward than staring over your shoulder the whole time.

The other day a friend came at me, all fired up, trying to make me buy some fancy memory system that lets you never forget a single thing ever. I don’t know why he was so insistent, unless it’s because I’m always “accidentally” borrowing his umbrellas, and once in the middle of a whist game I’ll blink and go “Wait a second, lads—did we decide clubs were trumps? I’ve been playing hearts for the last three rounds, sorry.” I turned him down flat, even though he listed all the perks. I don’t *want* to remember everything. Most people’s lives are full of stuff that really, truly deserves to be forgotten. That time ages ago you acted like a total fool, that little incident where you were downright dishonest and stupid and *everyone* found out, that one mean thing you said to someone you loved, that made you burn with shame for weeks? You already paid the price, didn’t you? You laid awake at night replaying it, you cringed so hard your face hurt, you got the cold shoulder from the people you cared about. Let’s forget it. Come on, Father Time, do us a favor, lift those awful heavy memories off our hearts. We’ve got more than enough new griefs coming our way every hour, and our strength is only enough for one day’s troubles at a time.

That doesn’t mean we should bury the whole past, of course. Life’s music would be completely silent if we snapped all the memory strings. We just need to yank out the nasty weeds, not dig up all the pretty flowers in Mnemosyne’s garden. Remember that guy in Dickens’ *The Haunted Man*? He begs to forget everything, right? And the second his wish comes true, he’s begging to get all his memories back again. We don’t need to chase away all the ghosts. We can keep the nice, gentle ones. Only the cruel, haggard, mean-eyed specters need to go.

Oh, the world gets so full of ghosts as you get older, you don’t even need to go hang around a spooky old graveyard or a moated manor house to see them. Every house, every old creaky chair, every little corner has its own ghost. They crowd around us like dead leaves swirling in the autumn wind, some of them still alive somewhere, some long dead. We held their hands, loved them, fought with them, laughed till our sides hurt, told them all our silliest hopes and dreams, back when we thought our friendship was so strong even Death couldn’t break it. Now they’re gone, their eyes will never look into ours again, we’ll never hear their voices. Only their ghosts come visit us, faint and wispy, through a haze of tears. You reach out to grab their hand, and they’re just air.

Ghosts are with us all the time. They walk next to you down the busy high street in the middle of the sunny day, they sit next to you by the fireside at twilight. You see their little faces peeking out the windows of your old elementary school, you run into them in the woods where you used to run around screaming and playing tag as a kid. Hark! Don’t you hear their laughter behind the blackberry bushes, and their shouts down the grassy lane? That little path through the fields where you used to wait for your first sweetheart at sunset? There she is right now, in that little white dress you thought was the prettiest thing you ever saw, bonnet dangling out of her hand, her brown hair all tangled. She’s five thousand miles away! Maybe even dead! Who cares? She’s right here next to you now, you can hear her laugh, you can see her eyes. She’ll vanish when you get to that stile by the woods, though, and you’ll be alone again, the shadows will creep over the fields, and the night wind will go moaning past.

We’d be far lonelier without all these ghosts, though. Come visit us, ghosts of our old playmates, our first sweethearts, our long-gone friends! The new people you meet later in life are never quite the same, you can never laugh or love with them the same way you did with those old pals when you were all young. The world was so bright and fun back then, and now it’s grown old and we’re all tired, and only those old ghosts can bring that old brightness back.

Memory is the best ghost-maker there is. It’s like living in a haunted house, your ears always picking up the sound of unseen feet padding around. Through the cracked old windows you watch all the flitting shadows of people who are gone, and the saddest shadows of all are the shadows of all your own old dead selves. Those bright, earnest young faces, full of big noble plans and honest hearts, staring back at you with their clear eyes, judging you a little? Oh, they’ve got every right to be disappointed. Since we were that 14-year-old kid, we’ve picked up lies, and cunning, and cynicism, when back then we swore we were going to grow up to be so good and so great. Thank God we can’t see the future, really. Hardly any 14-year-old would look at their 40-year-old self and not wince in shame.

I love sitting by the fire with my pipe at night, just chatting with that weird little kid that was me decades ago. I think he likes it too. I see his solemn little face peeking through the pipe smoke floating up, I smile at him, he smiles back, all grave and serious. We talk about the old days. Sometimes he grabs my hand, and we slip through the bars of the fireplace, right into that glowing little world behind the flames, back to all the old days we forgot. He tells me all his little thoughts. I laugh at him sometimes, but then I feel terrible, because he looks so serious I get all guilty. Also, that little kid is technically older than me, so it’s rude to make fun of him.

He tells me he can recite 6 whole fairy tales all the way through, he’s got a pet guinea pig, and his dad says fairy tales aren’t real, which he thinks is the biggest tragedy in the world, because he wants to be a knight and slay a dragon and marry a princess. When he turns seven, he decides that’s all silly, he wants to grow up to be a bargee, make loads of money, and marry the six-year-old girl who works behind the milk shop. He loves her so much he gives her his most prized possession: a giant pocket knife with four rusty blades and a corkscrew that always pops out by surprise and stabs him in the leg. She’s so happy she throws her arms around his neck and kisses him right there outside the shop, but the little boy who works next door at the cigar shop teases him, so he tries to punch that boy out… and gets punched instead.

Then there’s school, full of silly little tears and wild shouts, pranks, and hot tears dripping on those awful Latin grammars. He ruins his throat forever trying to pronounce German words, and learns those ridiculous French textbook conversations where every Frenchman you meet immediately asks you “Have you got pens, ink, and paper?” and you go “No, but my brother’s uncle has,” and they go “What about your mother’s neighbor?” and by the end no one has any pens ink or paper at all, but the guy just goes “Well, my aunt has mustard,” like that’s a perfectly normal follow-up.

Then the little short jacket gets grown-up tails, the beat-up cap you used as a handkerchief and a weapon turns into a fancy stiff top hat, you start smoking cigarettes that make the smoke go up your nose and choke, then try a big black cigar and end up leaning over a bucket in the back kitchen swearing you’ll never touch tobacco again. Then you get a wisp of a mustache, start drinking brandy sodas, lie about losing “thousands” at cards when you’ve actually lost one and twopence, stick an eyeglass in your eye and trip over every curb. Your poor aunts see visions of you getting dragged off to the Old Bailey and hanged, they’re so convinced you’re going to the bad. Then you fall in love, wear boots two sizes too small, comb your hair till it looks like a bird’s nest, and write terrible poetry about cruel hearts and pretty eyes, then your heart gets broken and you mope around walking for hours and losing sleep.

And then that boy’s gone. I’m standing alone on a dark road, tripping along, no light to guide me. Then the sun comes up, and I look down, and I’m just… me.
