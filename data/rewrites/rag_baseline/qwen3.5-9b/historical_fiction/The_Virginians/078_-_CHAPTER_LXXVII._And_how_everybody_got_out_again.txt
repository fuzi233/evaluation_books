The morning light filtered through the window, illuminating the dust motes dancing above the mahogany table where I sat. Across from me, Captain Miles Warrington adjusted his cravat, his reflection catching the edge of the mirror. He was handsome, yes, but his posture suggested a man already weary of the performance required of his station.

"You possess the rare fortune of winning the affections of a lady," I began, my voice steady despite the weight of the subject. "Of several, in fact. The Duchess of Devonshire, Mrs. Crew, the Queen of Prussia... the list is endless. Yet, when such a triumph occurs, do you retreat to the mess hall to recount the particulars?"

"Not such a fool, Squire," Captain Warrington replied, his tone flat, devoid of the expected bravado. He surveyed his sideburns in the glass, a gesture of mundane vanity that belied the gravity of the situation.

"Have you, Miss Theo, confessed every syllable spoken to Mr. Joe Blake, junior, in the shrubbery this morning?"

"Joe Blake, indeed!" Theo Junior cried out, his voice sharp with indignation.

"And you, Mademoiselle? That scented billet-doux delivered under Sir Thomas's seal—have you disclosed its contents? Observe her flush. It rivals the crimson of the theater curtains. No, Mademoiselle, we all harbor our secrets." I executed a bow, mimicking the French affectations of the era, though the sentiment felt increasingly archaic. "No, Theo, the shrubbery yielded nothing but acorns. No, Miles, my son, we do not disclose everything, not even to the most indulgent of fathers. Should I recount the incident involving the landau on the Hampstead Road on the twenty-fifth of May, 1760, may the Chevalier Ruspini extract every tooth from my skull!"

"Please, Father," my mother interjected, her voice rising with genuine urgency. "Or, since Jobson now serves you, perhaps summon him from the stables! I demand you speak!"

"What is this mystery?" Mademoiselle asked, her French accent lending a melodic quality to her confusion.

"Eh, ma fille," my wife whispered, leaning close. "If you inquire what I said, I replied 'Yes.' Behold, that is the totality of my discourse." Thus, it was my wife who had betrayed us, not I. This was the summary of our exchange as the carriage, moving faster than I deemed prudent, thundered toward Hampstead and retreated. Theo had not chosen to defy her honored parents; no such rebellion existed in her nature. Yet, we could not wed anyone else—not if we lived to the age of Methuselah, not even the Prince of Wales. Her heart had been surrendered with her father's consent, nay, his command. It was not hers to reclaim. Perhaps, eventually, such a benevolent father would soften; and if George kept his promise—whether today, in twenty years, or in the next life—she knew she would never breach hers.

Hetty's face lit with joy when, following my brief interview, she observed Theo's serene expression. "All the physician's remedies have achieved less for her," the devoted sister noted. After their act of defiance, the girls departed. I relinquished the chair I had occupied beside my invalid sister during a fleeting period of contentment. Hetty skipped back to her seat, and Charley ascended to the box. Later, he informed me it had been a tedious, obtuse sermon, noting that the boy was too orthodox to appreciate the rhetoric of dissenting preachers.

Hetty was not the sole family member to notice her sister's transformed visage and elevated spirits. Upon their return, I learned that their mother embraced both, particularly the invalid, with an intensity surpassing the norm. "Nothing compares to a country ride," Aunt Lambert declared. "She traveled to Hampstead, did she? She requires another tomorrow. Blessed be Heaven, Lord Wrotham's horses await instruction three or four times weekly, and the sweet child might avail herself of them!" Regarding the notion that Mr. Warrington might have intercepted the children on their drive, Aunt Lambert never entertained it—or at least, never voiced it. I leave it to the curious observer to deduce whether Mrs. Lambert could possibly have imagined her daughter and her sweetheart reuniting. Do women assist one another in matters of romantic entanglement? Do they scheme, plot, weave small deceptions, arrange clandestine encounters, lower the rope ladder, distract the guardians, and create opportunities while Strephon and Chloe whisper in the twilight or flee to Gretna Green? Some do. Some, having loved tenderly in their own time, retain a disposition of kindness toward their young successors as they commence similar games.

Miss Prim does not. If she learns of two youths attached to one another, she scoffs at them as fools or imagines every conceivable malice. Possessing a hunchback herself, she bites at everyone else's heels. I suspect if she witnessed turtles cooing in a forest, she would cast a stone to startle them. However, I address you, young ladies, regarding your grandmother, Aunt Lambert, who was a vessel of pure human kindness. Furthermore, concerning the current affair, how am I to determine if she possessed knowledge of it?

Thus, her greeting to Theo upon arrival was, "My child, the country air has benefited you immensely. I trust you will ride again tomorrow, and the day after, and so forth."

"Do you not think, Papa, that the ride has wrought wonders upon the child, and ought she not be compelled to breathe the fresh air?" Aunt Lambert queried the General as he entered for supper.

"Yes, certainly. If a coach-and-six benefits little Theo, she shall have it," Lambert affirmed. "Or he shall drag the landau up Hampstead Hill himself if horses are unavailable." The good man would willingly spend his guineas, his breath, or his blood to procure his child's pleasure. He was enchanted by her changed appearance; she ate a piece of chicken with appetite, drinking a negus he prepared for her—a beverage seemingly superior to the physician's best concoctions, which, God knows, had offered little relief thus far. Mamma was gracious and happy. Hetty radiated joy. It resembled an evening at home at Oakhurst. For months past, never since that fatal, cruel day—the one no one mentioned—had they shared such a delightful night.

Yet, if the other women chose to coax and cajole the simple, good-hearted father, Theo herself was too honest to sustain that sweet delusion for long. When, for the third or fourth time, he returned to the topic of his daughter's restored health, asking, "What has effected this change? Is it the country air? The Jesuit's bark? The new medicine?"

"Cannot you surmise, dear, what it is?" she asked, placing a hand upon her father's, her voice trembling, though her eyes remained clear and bright.

"And what is it, my child?" the General inquired.

"It is because I have seen him again, Papa!" she declared.

The other two women turned pale. Theo's heart began to pound, her cheeks flushing white as she continued to gaze into her father's terrified face.

"It was not wrong to see him," she continued, more urgently. "It would have been wrong not to inform you."

"Great God!" groaned the father, recoiling, his countenance etched with such profound grief that Hetty ran to her nearly fainting sister, clutching her to her bosom and shouting, "Theo knew nothing of it, sir! It was my doing—it was entirely my doing!"

Theo lay upon her sister's neck, kissing it dozens of times.

"Women, women! Are you trifling with my honor?" the father cried, erupting in a fierce exclamation.

Aunt Lambert sobbed wildly, "Martin! Martin! Speak not a word to her!" Hetty called out again, collapsing backward, staggering toward the wall, for Theo had fainted onto her shoulder.

I took my breakfast the following morning with whatever appetite I retained. My door opened, and my faithful black servant announced, "General Lambert." Instantly, by the General's expression, I discerned that the previous day's transaction was known to him. "Your accomplices did not confess," the General stated as soon as my servant departed, "but sided with you against their father—a testament to the desirability of clandestine meetings. It was from Theo herself I learned she had seen you."

"Accomplices, sir!" I replied, perhaps unwillingly shifting the focus from the core issue. "You know how fondly and dutifully your young people regard their father. If they oppose you in this instance, it must be because justice lies against you. A man of your stature cannot establish *sic volo, sic jubeo* as the sole law within his household!"

"Pshaw, George!" cried the General. "Though we are separated, heaven forbid I wish to cease loving one another. I held your promise that you would not seek her out."

"Nor did I go to her, sir," I said, turning red; for while this was truth, I admitted it was also a falsehood.

"You imply she was brought to you?" Mr. Lambert asked, agitated. "Will you hide behind Hester's petticoat? What a splendid defense for a gentleman!"
