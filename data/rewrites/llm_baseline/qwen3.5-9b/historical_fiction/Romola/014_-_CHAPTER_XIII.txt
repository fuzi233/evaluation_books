It was a languid afternoon on the seventh of September, more than two months since Romola and Tito had dared to confess their love to one another. Tito had just stepped into Nello's shop, finding the barber sprawled on the bench, his cap pulled low over his eyes. One leg was drawn up, while the other had slipped toward the floor, dragging behind it a manuscript volume of verse whose pages were now crushed beneath the weight. In a corner, Sandro played *mora* alone, watching his left fingers respond to the arithmetic demands of his right with solemn fascination.

Tito moved with the lightest tread, snatching up the lute. He bent over the sleeping barber and plucked the strings softly, singing:

(Beauteous is life in blossom! And it fleeteth—fleeteth ever; Whoso would be joyful—let him! There's no surety for the morrow.)

Nello woke as easily as a startled bird. The cap flew from his eyes instantly, and he sprang upright. "Ah, my Apollino! I seem to be somewhat late with my siesta on this scorching day. Blame lies in not sleeping naturally, but drinking a potion of potent poetry. Listen, how I begin to match my words by their initial letters, like a *Trovatore*? That is one of my bad symptoms: I fear the fine wine of my understanding is running off at the spigot of authorship, leaving me an empty cask smelling of dregs, like many another genius I know. Well, what is it, my Orpheus?"

He stretched his arms fully, then curled them around to grasp Tito's curls, pulling them out playfully. "What do you want of your well-tamed Nello? I hear a coaxing sound in that soft strain of yours. Let me see the very needle's eye of your desire, as the sublime poet says, so I may thread it."

"That is merely a tailor's metaphor for your sublime poet," Tito replied, letting his fingers drift lightly across the strings. "But you have guessed the cause of my affectionate impatience. I need an extra touch of your art—not on my chin, certainly, but on the *zazzera*, which is as tangled as Florentine politics itself. You have an adroit way of inserting your comb that flatters the skin and stirs the animal spirits agreeably in that region. Also, a hint of your delicate orange scent would not go amiss, for I am bound for the Scala Palace. The young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici will be there, bringing with him a certain young Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena, whose wit is so rapid that I see no way to outshine it save with the fragrance of orange blossoms."

Nello had already seized his comb and flourished it, pushing Tito gently backward into the chair and draping a cloth around him. "Never speak of rivalry, *bel giovane mio*. Bernardo Dovizi is a keen youngster who will never cast a net to catch the wind; yet he bears something of the same sharp-muzzled look as his brother, Ser Piero—the weasel that Piero de' Medici keeps at his beck to slip through small holes for him. No! You distance all rivals, and may soon touch the sky with your forefinger. They say you have even carried enough honey to sweeten the sour Messer Angelo, for he has pronounced you less of an ass than might be expected, considering the good understanding between you and the Secretary."

"And between ourselves, Nello mio, Messer Angelo possesses more genius and erudition than I can find in all the other Florentine scholars combined. It suits them well to praise me now that Poliziano is bowed down by grief or illness. I might try a flight with a sparrow-hawk like Pietro Crinito, but Poliziano is a large-beaked eagle who would swallow me, feathers and all, without noticing the difference."

"I will not contradict your modesty if you prefer it; but do not expect us clever Florentines to repeat the same phrases daily, as we must if we always told the truth. We cry down Dante and cry up Francesco Cei simply for variety. If we hail you as a new Poliziano, Heaven has ensured it is not quite such a great lie as it might have been. Besides, are you not a pattern of virtue in this wicked city? With your ears double-waxed against all siren invitations that would lure you from the Via de' Bardi and the great work destined to astonish posterity?"

"Posterity, in good truth, whom it will probably astonish as the universe does, by the impossibility of seeing what the plan actually was."

"Yes, something akin to that was prophesied here the other day. Cristoforo Landino remarked that the excellent Bardo was one of those scholars who lie overthrown in their learning, like cavaliers in heavy armor, and then become angry because they are over-ruled—a pithy remark that seems to me not to be a herb from his own garden. For of all men, for feeding one with an empty spoon and gagging one with vain expectation through long discourse, Messer Cristoforo is the pearl. *Ecco!* You are perfect now."

Here Nello withdrew the cloth. "Impossible to add further grace! But love is not always fed on learning, is it? I shall have to dress the *zazzera* for the betrothal soon, shall I not?"

"Perhaps," Tito smiled, "unless Messer Bernardo recommends that Bardo require me to yoke a lion and a wild boar to the car of the Zecca before I can win my Alcestis. But I confess he is right in holding me unworthy of Romola. She is a Pleiad that may grow dim by marrying any mortal."

"*Gnaffè*," Nello laughed. "Your modesty is in the right place there. Yet fate seems to have measured and chiselled you for the niche left empty by the old man's son. By the way, *Cronaca* told me he is now at San Marco. Did you know?"

A slight electric shock passed through Tito as he rose from the chair, though it was not outwardly visible. He immediately stooped to pick up the fallen book, busy flattening the leaves while he asked, "No; I thought he was at Fiesole. Are you sure he has returned to San Marco?"

"Cronaca is my authority," Nello shrugged. "I do not frequent that sanctuary, but he does. Ah," he added, taking the book from Tito's hands, "my poor Nencia da Barberino! It jars your scholarly feelings to see the pages dog-eared. I was lulled to sleep by the well-rhymed charms of that rustic maiden—'prettier than the turnip-flower,' 'with a cheek more savoury than cheese.' But to gain such a well-scented notion of the *contadina*, one must lie on velvet cushions in the Via Larga—not go to watch the *Fierucoloni* stomping into the Piazza della Nunziata this evening after sundown."

"And pray, who are the *Fierucoloni*?" Tito asked indifferently, settling his cap.

"The country women who come from the mountains of Pistoia and the Casentino, and heaven knows where else, to keep vigil in the church of the Nunziata and sell their yarn and dried mushrooms at the *Fierucola*, as we call it. They make a queer show with their paper lanterns, howling their hymns to the Virgin on this eve of her nativity. If you had the leisure to see them... No? Well, I have had enough of it myself, for there is wild work in the Piazza. One may get stones thrown at one's ears or shins without asking for it, and I was never fond of such pressing attention. *Addio*."

Tito departed carrying a little uneasiness, his visit ending sooner than anticipated. The boy-cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, the youngest of the red-hatted fathers—who would later present his broad, dark cheek to posterity as Pope Leo X—had been detained at his favorite pastime, the hunt, and failed to appear.

It was nearly half an hour before sunset when Tito left the Scala Palace, intending to proceed immediately to the Via de' Bardi. However, he had not gone far when, to his astonishment, he saw Romola advancing toward him along the Borgo Pinti. She wore a thick black veil and a black mantle, yet it was impossible to mistake her figure or her walk. Beside her was a short, stout form, which he recognized as Monna Brigida, despite the unusual plainness of her attire.

Romola had not been bred for devotional observances, and the occasions on which she took the air outside the loggia on the roof of her house were so rare—and so heavily discussed beforehand due to Bardo's dislike of being left without her—that Tito felt certain there must be some sudden, urgent reason for her absence, of which he had heard nothing the previous day.

She saw him through her veil and hastened her steps. "Romola, has anything happened?" Tito asked, turning to walk beside her.
