The unripe grape, the ripe, and the dried. All things are changes, not into nothing, but into that which is not at present. Marcus Aurelius had written these words, yet they hung in the air of the room like a benediction that could not quite reach the broken woman seated by the window.

In the evening, she summoned him again. It was nearly the hour when she had been brought in from the sea the previous day, a time marked by the intrusion of the ocean's violence into her domestic sphere. Now, the light was subdued, filtered through drawn blinds and open windows that breathed the cool, stagnant air of the room. She sat gazing fixedly at the darkened pane, her cheek pressed against her hand, appearing less shattered than when he had last departed, yet wearing a deep melancholy that transformed into anxious timidity as Deronda approached.

She did not offer her hand. Instead, her voice trembled with the weight of elapsed time. "How long ago it is!" she whispered. Then, with a fragile plea, "Will you sit near me again a little while?"

Deronda moved to place himself beside her, mirroring the position he had held before. He observed her turn toward him, her face etched with that indefinable expression implying a desperate need to speak, yet finding the words locked behind a barrier of fear. He waited, patient and still. Again she looked toward the window, silent, the same expression returning, refusing to break into speech. Some terror hindered her utterance. Seeking to relieve her timidity, Deronda averted his face, giving her space to gather her courage.

Presently, her voice cut through the silence, imploring and raw. "You will not say that any one else should know?"

"Most decidedly not," Deronda replied immediately. "There is no action that ought to be taken in consequence. There is no injury that could be righted in that way. There is no retribution that any mortal could apportion justly."

She remained so still during the ensuing pause that she seemed to be holding her breath, suspending her existence until she finally spoke. "But if I had not had that murderous will—that moment—if I had thrown the rope on the instant—perhaps it would have hindered death?"

"No—I think not," Deronda answered slowly, his tone measured against the chaos of her guilt. "If it were true that he could swim, he must have been seized with cramp. With your quickest, utmost effort, it seems impossible that you could have done anything to save him. That momentary murderous will cannot, I think, have altered the course of events. Its effect is confined to the motives in your own breast. Within ourselves our evil will is momentous, and sooner or later it works its way outside us—it may be in the vitiation that breeds evil acts, but also it may be in the self-abhorrence that stings us into better striving."

"I am saved from robbing others—there are others—they will have everything—they will have what they ought to have. I knew that some time before I left town. You do not suspect me of wrong desires about those things?" She spoke hesitatingly, her voice fraying at the edges.

"I had not thought of them," Deronda admitted, "I was thinking too much of the other things."

"Perhaps you don't quite know the beginning of it all," Gwendolen said slowly, as if wrestling with the reluctance to expose the rot at the core of her narrative. "There was some one else he ought to have married. And I knew it, and I told her I would not hinder it. And I went away—that was when you first saw me. But then we became poor all at once, and I was very miserable, and I was tempted. I thought, 'I shall do as I like and make everything right.' I persuaded myself. And it was all different. It was all dreadful. Then came hatred and wicked thoughts. That was how it all came. I told you I was afraid of myself. And I did what you told me—I did try to make my fear a safeguard. I thought of what would be if I—I felt what would come—how I should dread the morning—wishing it would be always night—and yet in the darkness always seeing something—seeing death. If you did not know how miserable I was, you might—but now it has all been no use. I can care for nothing but saving the rest from knowing—poor mamma, who has never been happy."

Silence settled over them again, heavy and suffocating, before she spoke with a repressed sob. "You cannot bear to look at me any more. You think I am too wicked. You do not believe that I can become any better—worth anything—worthy enough—I shall always be too wicked to—" Her voice broke off, helpless and spent.

Deronda's heart was pierced by the sound. He turned his eyes onto her poor, beseeching face. "I believe that you may become worthier than you have ever yet been—worthy to lead a life that may be a blessing. No evil dooms us hopelessly except the evil we love, and desire to continue in, and make no effort to escape from. You *have* made efforts—you will go on making them."

"But you were the beginning of them. You must not forsake me," Gwendolen said, leaning forward with her clasped hands gripping the arm of her chair. She looked at him, her face bearing piteous traces of the life-experience concentrated in the preceding twenty-four hours—that new, terrible life lying on the other side of the deed which fulfilled a criminal desire. "I will bear any penance. I will lead any life you tell me. But you must not forsake me. You must be near. If you had been near me—if I could have said everything to you, I should have been different. You will not forsake me?"

"It could never be my impulse to forsake you," Deronda said promptly, his voice carrying that unintentional resonance that made his ready sympathy feel more personal and special than it truly was. In that moment, however, he was not entirely free from a foreboding of some such self-committing effect. His strong feeling for this stricken creature could not hinder the rushing images of future difficulty. He continued to meet her appealing eyes, but with the painful consciousness that to her ear his words might carry a promise which one day would seem unfulfilled: he was making an indefinite promise to an indefinite hope.

Anxieties, both immediate and distant, crowded on his thought. It was under their influence that, after a moment's silence, he said, "I expect Sir Hugo Mallinger to arrive by tomorrow night at least; and I am not without hope that Mrs. Davilow may shortly follow him. Her presence will be the greatest comfort to you—it will give you a motive to save her from unnecessary pain?"

"Yes, yes—I will try. And you will not go away?"

"Not till after Sir Hugo has come."

"But we shall all go to England?"

"As soon as possible," Deronda said, unwilling to enter into particulars.

Gwendolen looked toward the window again with an expression which seemed like a gradual awakening to new thoughts. The twilight was perceptibly deepening, but Deronda could see a movement in her eyes and hands such as accompanies a return of perception in one who has been stunned.

"You will always be with Sir Hugo now!" she said presently, looking at him. "You will always live at the Abbey—or else at Diplow?"

"I am quite uncertain where I shall live," Deronda said, coloring slightly.

She was warned by his changed countenance that she had spoken too rashly, and she fell silent. After a little while, she began again, looking away, "It is impossible to think how my life will go on. I think now it would be better for me to be poor and obliged to work."

"New promptings will come as the days pass. When you are among your friends again, you will discern new duties," Deronda said gently. "Make it a task now to get as well and calm—as much like yourself as you can, before—" He hesitated.

"Before my mother comes," Gwendolen supplied softly.

"Ah! I must be changed. I have not looked at myself. Should you have known me," she added, turning toward him, "if you had met me now?—should you have known me for the one you saw at Leubronn?"

"Yes, I should have known you," Deronda said mournfully. "The outside change is not great. I should have seen at once that it was you, and that you had gone through some great sorrow."

"Don't wish now that you had never seen me; don't wish that," Gwendolen implored, tears gathering in her eyes.

"I should despise myself for wishing it," Deronda replied. "How could I know what I was wishing? We must find our duties in what comes to us, not in what we imagine might have been. If I took to foolish wishing of that sort, I should wish—not that I had never seen you, but that I had been able to save you from this."

"You have saved me from worse," Gwendolen said in a sobbing voice. "I should have been worse if it had not been for you. If you had not been good, I should have been more wicked than I am."

"It will be better for me to go now," Deronda said, worn in spirit by the perpetual strain of this scene. "Remember what we said of your task—to get well and calm before other friends come."
