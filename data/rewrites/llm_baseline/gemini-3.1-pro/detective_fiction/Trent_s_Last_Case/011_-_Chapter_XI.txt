My Dear Molloy:—

This is in case I do not find you at your office. I have found out who killed Manderson, as this dispatch will show. The raw ingredients of this tragedy are now gathered; yours is the task of deciding how to plate and serve them to the public. It definitely charges an unsuspected person with the crime, so I do not suppose you will publish it before his arrest. Meanwhile, let Scotland Yard taste the bitter broth I have brewed here. I have done with the Manderson mystery, and I wish to God I had never let its rancid flavor cross my palate. 

Here follows my dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, *June* 16*th*.

I begin this, my third and likely final dispatch to the *Record* upon the Manderson murder, with the heavy, metallic taste of conflicting duties on my tongue. I feel a profound relief, having previously been forced to withhold certain aromatics of the truth lest I alert a man of uncommon, predatory resourcefulness. But I possess no appetite for the story of perverted cleverness I must now lay out. It leaves an alkaline, curdled finish in the mouth, a rotting under-layer beneath the everyday domestic routines of the Marlstone estate.

You will recall the state in which the body was found—a disruption of the sacred agrarian morning. I detailed the man’s final movements and noted the puzzling evaporation of a significant quantity of aged, peat-smoked whisky from his private decanter, an anomaly for a man who treated all earthly sustenance, food and drink alike, as mere utilitarian fuel for his industrial conquests. Today, I have completed a slow reduction of the facts, boiling away the impurities until only the killer remains.

Two profound disruptions to the environment’s natural harmony have been apparent from the outset. First was the violation of the estate’s acoustic ecology. The body lay a mere thirty yards from the house, yet the domestic elders—most notably Martin the butler, a man whose ears are as precisely attuned to the rhythmic simmering of a master stock as to the creak of an intruder’s footfall—heard no cry, no struggle, no gunshot. The industrial violence of the firearm should have shattered the quietude of the sleeping household; that this sonic trauma was completely erased from the night’s auditory landscape struck me as a deliberate manipulation of the house’s acoustic space.

The second anomaly was Manderson’s dental plate, left abandoned by the bedside. This man had purportedly risen, dressed meticulously down to his watch-chain, and walked out into the night without the very tools required to engage in the sensory act of mastication or the cultural act of clear speech. Anyone who relies on such a plate knows that inserting it is a ritual as instinctual as the morning’s first draught of water. 

The path to the truth opened to me through the olfactory and tactile landscape of the dead man’s dressing room. Upon the upper shelf sat the patent leather shoes Manderson wore the evening prior. I am a meticulous historian of such crafted goods, and I immediately caught the rich scent of fine beeswax polish and oak-tanned calfskin. But my fingers, tracing the vamp where the laced parts rise, discovered a brutal structural failure. The intricate stitching had parted, the leather stretched and torn like the delicate casing of an over-stuffed sausage. The tearing was fresh. 

These indications sang a singular, undeniable truth: the shoes had been worn by someone whose physical root in the earth was broader and heavier than Manderson’s narrow, vain little feet. 

Suddenly, the timeline of that dark evening collapsed upon itself in my mind, the mythic shadow of a masquerade bleeding into the mundane domestic reality of the house. *It was not Manderson who was in the house that night.* A flock of disparate ingredients instantly emulsified into a single, cohesive realization. It was unheard-of for the utilitarian Manderson to savor a deep pour of highland malt at night. It was wildly uncharacteristic for him to leave his garments dragged and untidy, or to neglect the cleansing rituals of the washbasin. Most of all, it was extraordinary that he would suddenly adopt a communicative, almost narrative tone with his estranged wife. 

The impersonator, having freshly engaged in the bloody, metallic-smelling ritual of murder, had forced his wider feet into the narrow leather confines to leave Manderson’s footprint upon the house’s memory. Returning the shoes to the maid for her morning polishing routine seamlessly integrated this deception into the estate's domestic cycle. He left the dental plate to establish that Manderson had prepared for slumber. This meant, invariably, that the true Manderson was dead before the false Manderson ever crossed the threshold.

The killer possessed Manderson’s unmistakable shooting jacket, donning it to manipulate the visual and acoustic perception of Martin the butler. Martin, summoned to the library, saw only the broad-brimmed hat and the familiar drape of the jacket as the imposter crouched over the telephone, projecting his voice into the mouthpiece. 

Then came the supreme test of this acoustic masquerade: the bedroom. The imposter stood in the dark, his heart hammering a frantic, primal rhythm against his ribs, smelling the lavender and starched linen of the adjoining room. The drowsy voice of Mrs. Manderson drifted through the half-open door. 

Here we witness a profound weaponization of native dialects and dominant lexicons. The imposter did not merely freeze; he answered her. He hijacked the distinct, rhythmic cadence of her dead husband. He perfectly replicated Manderson’s acoustic signature, exploiting the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience—she heard the familiar dominant timbre and believed it to be her husband, her cultural memory of the man momentarily overwritten by a flawless auditory forgery. Through this stolen voice, he volunteered the alibi: he had sent John Marlowe in the car to Southampton.

Let me lay out the recipe of the night’s events: Between ten and eleven, Manderson was shot far enough from the estate to preserve its sonic tranquility. The imposter brought the body to the shed, stripped it of its outer garments, and slipped into the library through the garden window, carrying the dead man’s clothing, teeth, and the murder weapon. He drank the whisky—a desperate, fiery necessity to ground his frayed nerves. He used the telephone while Martin was present, utilizing the hat and jacket to feign identity. He then crept upstairs, returned the revolver to Marlowe’s room, and arranged the bedroom artifacts to simulate Manderson’s retiring, before shedding the costume and selecting a new suit of clothes from the wardrobe.

Who, then, was this false Manderson? I reduced the profile to five essential traits:
1. He shared an intimate, familial relationship with the deceased, flawlessly navigating the household's complex interpersonal dynamics.
2. He matched Manderson’s height and heavy-shouldered build, though possessing a slightly larger, broader foot.
3. He was a master of vocal imitation, capable of wearing another man’s dialect like a tailored suit.
4. He possessed an intricate, almost agrarian understanding of the household’s micro-routines.
5. He harbored a vital, life-or-death need to establish the myth that Manderson survived past midnight.

The harvest of my inquiries yielded one man: John Marlowe. 

Marlowe, the private secretary, lived for four years under that roof, sharing the daily bread and absorbing the precise rhythms of the house. He and Manderson shared a frame, though Marlowe’s shoes, which I examined, are precisely a size broader. 

To confirm his acoustic mastery, I telegraphed an Oxford food and cultural historian of my acquaintance regarding Marlowe’s university days. The reply confirmed that Marlowe was a celebrated member of the O.U.D.S., excelling in character acting, profound vocal imitations, and historic hoaxes. He knew how to erase his own identity and command the lexicon of another.

Finally, we arrive at the alibi. Marlowe arrived at a Southampton hotel at 6:30 on Monday morning, faithfully executing the very errand that the false Manderson had so helpfully described to the drowsing wife in the dark. The acoustic deception planted the seed; the morning's journey provided the bloom.
