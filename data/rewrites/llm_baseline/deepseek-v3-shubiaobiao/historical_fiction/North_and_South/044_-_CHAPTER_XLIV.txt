Margaret found the quiet of Harley Street a welcome reprieve after the turbulence of Milton. The house moved with effortless grace, its rhythms smooth as silk, untouched by the worries that had once filled her days. Yet, in this very comfort, she felt an odd disquiet. The absence of struggle left her restless, as though she were drifting in a world too polished to feel real.

Edith, now recovered, showered her with affection, eager to drape her in the same luxuries she herself enjoyed. Mrs. Shaw fussed over Margaret’s wardrobe, as if fine fabrics could mend the quiet ache in her heart. Captain Lennox was kind, though his attentions to Edith’s appearance—as though her beauty were a duty—sometimes grated on Margaret’s patience.

The days passed in a soft monotony: late breakfasts, endless notes penned on Edith’s behalf, the fleeting company of little Sholto, and the hollow cheer of social calls. The evenings, when the house emptied into the whirl of London’s season, left Margaret alone with her thoughts—thoughts that wandered inevitably back to Milton. She missed the rawness of it, the purpose, even the hardships. Here, life was a performance, all surface and no depth.

Dixon’s letters were her only tether to that past. Brief, practical, they carried faint echoes of Milton—a mention of Mr. Thornton’s advice on furniture, a passing reference to the house in Crampton Terrace. But they were sparse, and Margaret often found herself staring at them, wondering if the world she had left behind moved on without her, as though she had never been there at all.

Then, unexpectedly, Mr. Bell arrived.

His presence was a jolt of warmth in the stifling stillness. He teased her, coaxed her into conversation, and with him, she felt she could breathe again. Yet even he brought reminders of Milton—of the life she had lost.

“You never told me Frederick was in England,” he chided gently when the subject arose.

Margaret flushed. “I thought you knew.”

Mr. Lennox, ever the lawyer, was cautious, measuring his words. But Mr. Bell, blunt as ever, dismissed secrecy. “I’m no gossip,” he said, though his eyes twinkled with curiosity.

The conversation turned, inevitably, to Mr. Hale’s choices—his resignation, his move to Milton. Mr. Lennox, with detached politeness, called it quixotic. Mr. Bell bristled. Margaret listened, torn between loyalty and the quiet sting of truth.

When Mr. Lennox departed, Mr. Bell lingered, watching her. “You look pale,” he remarked.

“I am well enough,” she said, too quickly.

He studied her, then sighed. “Milton changes a person. Even those who leave it.”

She had no answer.

That night, as the house settled into silence, Margaret sat by the window, the letters still in her lap. Outside, London glittered, indifferent. Somewhere beyond it, the smoke of Milton still rose, the machines still turned, the people still labored. And she—she was here, caught between two worlds, belonging to neither.

Sleep did not come easily. When it did, her dreams were of the north.
