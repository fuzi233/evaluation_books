Margaret shut herself up in her own room after leaving Mrs. Thornton's. She began to pace back and forth, her usual physical manifestation of inner turmoil, but soon recalled that in the small, thin-walled house, every footstep echoed from chamber to chamber. She sat down, waiting until she was certain Mrs. Thornton had safely departed. Only then did she force herself to replay the entire conversation, speech by speech, compelling her memory to traverse the painful dialogue once more.

Finally, rising, she spoke to herself in a mournful tone: "At least, her words do not strike home; they slide off me. For I am innocent of every motive she imputes to me. Yet, it is bitter to realize that anyone—any woman—could so readily believe such things of another. It is hard and sad. Where I have erred, she does not accuse me; she simply does not know. He never told her. I should have guessed he wouldn't!"

She lifted her chin, feigning pride in the delicacy of feeling Mr. Thornton had displayed. Then, a fresh thought struck her, causing her to press her hands tightly together: "He, too, must suspect poor Frederick of being some lover." A flush crept up her neck as the word passed her lips. "I see it now. It is not merely that he knows of my deceit, but that he believes someone else cares for me; and that I... Oh, dear! Oh, dear! What am I to do? What do I mean? Why do I care what he thinks, beyond the mere loss of his regard for my honesty? I cannot say. But I am wretched! Oh, how unhappy this past year has been! I have skipped from childhood straight into old age. I have had no youth, no womanhood; the hopes of womanhood have closed forever for me, for I shall never marry. I anticipate cares and sorrows as if I were already an elder woman, with the same tearful spirit. I am weary of this constant demand for strength. I could endure it for Papa, for that is a natural, pious duty. I think I could also endure—or possess the energy to resent—Mrs. Thornton's unjust, impertinent suspicions. But it is crushing to feel how completely he must misunderstand me. What has happened to make me so morbid today? I do not know. I only know I cannot help it. I must yield sometimes. No, I will not," she declared, springing to her feet. "I will not—I *will* not dwell on myself or my position. I won't dissect my own feelings. It serves no purpose now. Someday, if I live to be an old woman, I may sit by the fire, gazing into the embers, seeing the life that might have been."

Throughout this internal storm, she hastily dressed to leave the house, pausing only to wipe her eyes with impatient gestures, knowing the tears would return despite her bravado. "There are surely many women who make as sad a mistake as I have, discovering it only too late," she muttered. "And how proudly and impertinently I spoke to him that day! But I did not know then. It crept upon me gradually, and I know not where it began. Now, I shall not yield. It will be difficult to treat him as before, burdened by this misery, but I will remain calm, quiet, and say very little. Though, to be sure, I may not see him; he seems determined to avoid us. That would be worse than anything. And yet, no wonder he shuns me, believing what he must about me."

Stepping out, she moved rapidly toward the countryside, attempting to drown her reflections in the speed of her motion. As she paused on the doorstep upon her return, her father approached.

"Good girl!" he said. "You've been to Mrs. Boucher's. I was just intending to go there, if I had time, before dinner."

"No, Papa; I have not," Margaret replied, flushing. "I never thought of her. But I shall go directly after dinner; I'll go while you are taking your nap."

True to her word, Margaret proceeded to the Bouchers'. Mrs. Boucher was truly ill, dangerously so—not merely ailing. The kind, sensible neighbor who had visited earlier seemed to have assumed total charge of the household. Some children had been sent to neighbors; Mary Higgins had fetched the three youngest at dinner time, and since then, Nicholas had gone for the doctor. He had not yet returned. Mrs. Boucher was dying, and there was nothing left to do but wait.

Margaret felt she needed the doctor's opinion and decided to visit the Higginses in the interim. Perhaps she could learn if Nicholas had managed to approach Mr. Thornton. She found Nicholas busily crafting a penny top on the dresser, entertaining three tiny children who clung to him fearlessly. He, along with them, smiled at a particularly long spin, and Margaret noted that this look of absorbed happiness was a promising sign.

When the penny finally halted, little Johnny began to cry.

"Come here," Margaret said, lifting the child from the dresser and cradling him. Holding her watch to his ear, she asked Nicholas if he had seen Mr. Thornton.

Nicholas's expression shifted instantly. "Ay!" he said. "I've seen him and heard far too much about him."

"He refused you, then?" Margaret asked, sorrowfully.

"To be sure. I knew he'd do it all along. There's no mercy to be expected from masters like him. You're a stranger and a foreigner, unlikely to understand their ways; but I knew it."

"I am sorry I asked you. Was he angry? Did he not speak to you as Hamper did?"

"He wasn't overly civil!" Nicholas spun the penny again, seemingly for his own amusement as much as the children's. "Never mind your fretting. I'm exactly where I was. I'll go tramping tomorrow. I gave him as good as I got. I told him I didn't hold the high opinion of him that would lead me to seek him out a second time on my own; but you advised me to come, and I was beholden to you."

"You told him you were sent by me?"

"I dunno if I called you by your name. I don't think I did. I said a woman who knew no better had advised me to come and see if there was a soft spot in his heart."

"And he—?" Margaret prompted.

"Said I should tell you to mind your own business. That's the longest spin yet, my lads. And those are civil words compared to what he used to me. But never mind. We're where we were, and I'll break stones on the road before I let these little ones starve."

Margaret placed the struggling Johnny back onto the dresser. "I am sorry I asked you to go to Mr. Thornton's. I am disappointed in him."

A faint noise sounded behind her. Both she and Nicholas turned simultaneously to find Mr. Thornton standing there, his face etched with displeased surprise. Acting on an instant impulse, Margaret stepped aside before him, offering not a word, only a deep bow to conceal the sudden pallor that washed over her face. He bowed equally low in return before closing the door after her.

As she hurried toward Mrs. Boucher's, she heard the distinct sound of the latch clicking shut, a noise that seemed to complete the measure of her humiliation. He, too, was annoyed to find her there. Within his heart lay tenderness—a "soft place," as Nicholas had termed it—but he possessed a fierce pride in guarding it. He kept it sacred and safe, jealous of any circumstance that might breach its defenses. While he dreaded exposing this tenderness, he was equally eager that all men recognize his justice. He felt unjustified in having given such a scornful hearing to anyone who had waited with humble patience for five hours to speak to him. Whether the man had spoken saucily when given the chance mattered little to Mr. Thornton; he actually admired that audacity and felt conscious of his own irritability at the time, believing they were even-handed.

It was the five hours of waiting that struck him deeply. He had no five hours to spare himself, yet he had surrendered one or two hours of his rigorous intellectual and physical labor to investigate Higgins's story, examining the nature of his character and the tenor of his life. He tried not to admit it, but he was convinced Higgins spoke the truth. This conviction entered his mind like a spell, touching the latent tenderness of his heart. The patience of the man, the simple generosity of his motive (having learned of the quarrel between Boucher and Higgins), caused him to forget entirely the cold calculations of justice, leaping over them via a higher instinct.

He had come to offer Higgins employment, but he was more irritated to discover Margaret present than by hearing her final words. Upon realizing she was the woman who had urged Higgins to approach him, he dreaded admitting that thoughts of her—even as a motive for doing what was right—had influenced his actions.

"So that was the lady you spoke of as 'a woman'?" he said indignantly to Higgins. "You might have told me who she was."
