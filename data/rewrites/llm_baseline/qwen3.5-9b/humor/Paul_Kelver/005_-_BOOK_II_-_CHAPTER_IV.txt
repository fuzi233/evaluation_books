The East India Dock Road today is a chaotic artery of London, choked with traffic where the jingle of tram bells and the rattling of omnibuses mix constantly with the relentless drumming of thousands of footsteps on wet pavement. But in the era of my story, it was a silent, hollow corridor. Bounded on one side by the long, echoing walls of the docks and on the other by scattered cottages rising amidst market gardens, drying yards, and piles of refuse, the street felt vast and empty. The only relic linking this modern thoroughfare to its past was a single-story brick cottage at the start of the bridge—the former toll-house. I remember that building vividly because it was there that my childhood slipped away from me, leaving me small, frightened, and staring out at a terrifying world beyond.

I cannot articulate it better than this: that specific afternoon, I had visited Plaistow to see the family dentist. It was a remote location, yet it held compensating virtues. My mother would instruct me, ensuring the half-crown sat safely in the bottom of my knickerbocker pocket: "Keep the half-crown in your hand, but if he refuses it, you must bring it home."

Whether he was a distant relative or simply a kind acquaintance, I am unsure. All I know is that he was a benevolent figure. Seated in his velvet throne, he would unroll his case of gleaming instruments and ask me to pick one, praising the most gruesome-looking tools with affectionate hyperbole. But the moment I opened my mouth to discuss these fearsome objects, *lo!*—a pair of forceps would spring from under his sleeve. Before I could process the event, the ordeal was over. Following this, we shared tea. He was an elderly bachelor whose house sat within a magnificent garden—for Plaistow was then a charming village—and his housekeeper produced jams and jellies of such exquisite quality that the memories linger. Our conversations usually revolved around my mother, whom he described as a mischievous, willful child—a stark contrast to the angelic figure I imagined. When I recounted his stories to her, she would laugh, claiming no memory of such antics, and sternly suggest we find better topics. Yet, her next question would always be, "And what else did he say?" specifically asking about *me*.

After clearing the tea things, he would produce his great microscope. To me, it was a portal to a fairy realm inhabited by strange dragons and mighty monsters, cementing his status as a harmless magician. Looking back, I associate his microscopic obsession with the fact that he was a tiny man himself, yet possessed a heart larger than most. Upon leaving, I would formally return my half-crown "with mamma's compliments," and he would formally accept it. However, stepping outside the gate and reaching for my pocket, I would invariably find the coin missing. On returning it to him, he would deny knowing anything about it.

"Must be another half-crown," he would suggest breezily. "Such things happen. People put change in pockets and overlook it. Half-crowns are slippery things."

That day, returning home, I paused on the bridge watching lazy barges navigate between the piers. It was one of those hushed summer evenings when even a grim city holds its breath. Turning away from the river and passing through the white toll-gate, I felt a profound sensation of leaving myself behind on the bridge. The impression was so vivid that I turned back, half-expecting to see my younger self still leaning over the iron railing, gazing down at the sunlit water.

It sounds foolish, but I leave the spot standing, wondering if anyone else has ever experienced such a detachment. The little boy never returned to me. He drifted away as a physical body might depart, leaving only memory and regret. For a time, I tried to play his games and dream his dreams, but the substance was gone. I was merely a thin ghost pretending to be alive.

This realization troubled me deeply, bordering on tears. The feeling that my childhood lay dead behind me, and that I was rapidly traveling down the strange path called "growing up," terrified me. I didn't want to mature; couldn't I stay forever playing and dreaming? The dark road ahead frightened me. Was I forced to move forward?

Gradually, over long months and years, a new consciousness emerged. New pulses, sensations, and throbbings rooted themselves in me, distinct from the old ones. Little Paul, the protagonist of my previous chapters, began to fade from my life.

Thus, I must let him fade with sorrow from this book. But before I sever ties completely, let me recall what else I remember of him. In doing so, we shall finally be rid of him, and he will no longer interfere with our narrative.

The clearest image I retain is of my Aunt Fan, crouched over the kitchen fire. Her skirt and crinoline were rolled up to her waist, sacrificing modesty to custom, leaving only her petticoat exposed. Rhythmic waves moved up and down her body as her hands stroked her own knees affectionately. Opposite her, armed with a paper knife as a sword or a broomstick as a steed, I flourished and declaimed. Sometimes I was a knight and she a wicked ogre. She would roar and swear, only to instantly transform into the beautiful princess I secured and carried away on my prancing broomstick. While the princess conversed sweetly from her high window, the scene was realistically staged; the abduction, however, remained pure make-believe, for Aunt Fan refused to leave her chair before the fire, obsessed with rubbing her knees.

Other times, aided by the meat chopper, I was an Indian brave and she was Laughing Water or Singing Sunshine, venturing out to scalp together. In quieter moods, I was the Fairy Prince and she the Sleeping Beauty, though she wasn't at her best in those roles. Much better was the tableau where I sat atop an overturned table as Captain Cook and she was the Cannibal Chief.

"I shall skin him and hang him in the larder till Sunday week," Aunt Fan would declare, salivating, "then he'll be perfectly tender—not too tough, not too lean." My aunt was always precise in her details.

I owe her no ill will, but the more I scrutinize my memory, the more convinced I am that her participation in these games lacked genuine self-sacrifice. Often, she suggested the plot and cast herself as the villainous lead—the dragon, the wicked uncle, the fussy necromancer, or the unwelcome fairy. As an author of a new cookbook for Giant-Land, she surely would have succeeded. Most recipes are monotonously sparse: "Boil him," "Roast her for supper," "Bake them in a pie with gravy." But Aunt Fan injected variety and refinement into the domestic economy of Ogredom.

"My dear," she would direct, "let's stuff him with chestnuts and serve him on toast. And don't forget the giblets; they make excellent sauce."

Regarding the diet of imprisoned maidens, her advice was pragmatic: "Not too much fish—it spoils the flesh for roasting."

She delighted in transforming kings' sons and rightful princesses into beings who seemed to forget their origins. Letting her have her way taught me lessons in both present and prehistoric natural history. The most beautiful damsel imaginable would, without hesitation, become a Glyptodon or a Hippocrepis. Once I learned to spell, I would look these creatures up in an illustrated dictionary and realize I could never love the lady again. Warriors and kings were transformed into plaice or prawns; haughty queens became Brussels sprouts.

With gusto, she planned complex slaughters, attending to every detail: sharpening knives, preparing mops and buckets for cleanup. As a writer, she would have belonged to the Realistic School.

Our performances invariably concluded with her death, a conscientious effort indeed. Her groans and writhings sometimes frightened me; I always welcomed the final gurgle. Immediately after, but not a moment sooner, Aunt Fan would lower her skirt—signaling the fall of the curtain—and begin preparing tea.

Another recurring vision is of myself, wearing a glazed-peaked cap, explaining various things while walking through dingy streets alongside a smaller, curly-haired, wide-eyed figure. Every now and then, she would dash ahead to turn and gaze admiringly into my face, captivated by the praise and fame I bestowed upon her.

I cherished her company for reasons she likely didn't understand. First, she protected me from my baser instincts. With her beside me, I wouldn't dare flee from sudden foes. Together, we courted adventure; once accustomed to it, the constant risk of attack added a unique charm to outdoor exercise that older, safer citizens miss. Perhaps my dog feels this way too. To me, a walk was often tedious, dictated by the need to check my waistband rather than a desire for movement. To him, it was an expedition fraught with peril: *"The gentleman sleeping with one eye open on The Chequer's doorstep! Will he greet me with a sniff or try to bite my head off? This cross-eyed, lop-eared loafer lurching against the lamp-post! Shall we pass with a casual wag or engage in a life-and-death struggle? Impossible to say. This coming corner... beware! Is someone waiting to kill me?"*
