Major Pendennis, ever the man of principle and strategy—especially when it came to keeping his nephew under wraps—decided that Fairoaks would remain under siege until the enemy had fully retreated. Which, in this case, meant simply that no one was allowed to leave the estate without a proper escort, or at least a suspiciously timed glance from an agent who looked like he’d been sent by the government to spy on your toothbrush.

Arthur Pendennis, the poor lad, wasn’t exactly being watched *like* a prisoner—no chains, no guard dogs, no midnight checks with a flashlight—but he might as well have been. His every movement was recorded, his footsteps tracked, and his thoughts likely filed under “Potential Risk: Romantic Inclinations.” If Arthur wanted to go fishing, someone was already packing a thermos of tea and a copy of *The Times* just in case.

Ah, love! What a fool’s errand. Who among us hasn’t been thwarted by the cruel hand of fate, the treachery of a woman (or worse, a man), or the sheer stupidity of our own choices? Let the reader pause, reflect, and remember the last time their heart was crushed by rejection—perhaps while trying to order coffee in a foreign country, or during a particularly awkward Tinder date. It’s not pretty. It’s not dignified. It’s usually accompanied by excessive sweating and a sudden interest in existential philosophy.

Now imagine Arthur Pendennis going through all that, but with a horse named Rebecca and a tendency to gallop into Chatteris screaming the name of the girl who rejected him—while also wearing a hat that probably cost more than most villagers’ annual incomes.

His mother, Mrs. Pendennis, was beside herself. Not because she understood the emotional turmoil her son was enduring—she didn’t. She was too busy worrying about whether he was eating enough bread and whether he’d grown taller since yesterday. But she loved him fiercely, which meant she suffered twice as much, even though she couldn’t quite explain why. Her idea of comfort was a hot cup of tea and a lecture on how boys should never wear hats indoors. Her idea of grief? Probably a long walk with a parasol and a muttered prayer.

Meanwhile, Major Pendennis, the man who could’ve been a general if he hadn’t chosen to live in a country house and pretend he was still relevant, was doing his best to pass the time. He read newspapers with the intensity of a man decoding ancient scrolls. He played backgammon with the widow, who tried her hardest to make it fun, but really only succeeded in making him feel guilty for winning. He dressed every day like he was attending a royal ball—even though the only guest he ever saw was a very polite badger.

And then there was the American war. Oh yes, the Major had recently discovered a deep passion for the American war. Or rather, two volumes of the *Annual Register* that happened to be in his bedroom. He studied them religiously, muttering things like “Saint Lucie was a hero!” and “Wyoming was tragic!”—as if he’d personally fought in both battles. It was impressive, really. A man so bored he could invent a new hobby based solely on secondhand history.

Arthur, meanwhile, found solace in the carp pond at Clavering Park. A murky, whispering pool surrounded by reeds, green alders, and the ghost of a milkmaid who drowned herself centuries ago. (She was said to haunt the place, but mostly just stared sadly at people fishing and sighed.) Arthur didn’t drown himself—he merely fished. And wrote poetry. Terrible poetry. Poems so bad they made you wonder if he’d been using a different kind of bait—perhaps despair?

He once composed a sonnet titled *Ode to My Unrequited Love*, which began:

> "Thou art the moon, my soul’s lone star,
> Whose light I seek, yet cannot bear…"

And ended with something about "the wind" and "a broken lute." Honestly, the fish were probably laughing.

But it wasn’t all sorrow and anguished verses. Arthur had moments of wild energy. When he felt particularly low, he’d saddle Rebecca and ride off into the countryside, screaming the name of the actress who broke his heart. Carters would jump out of their way, turnpike men would drop their tollbooks, and sheep would scatter like they’d seen a dragon. One time, he rode past the Clavering Arms and shouted, “Rebecca! You’ve left me for a man with *baldness*!”—which is probably not what the actress was expecting.

Then there was Mr. Harry Foker, the young lord of the land who arrived on a tandem—yes, a *tandem*—and caused a sensation in Clavering market. He upset a stall selling pickled onions, accidentally cut Mrs. Pybus’s poodle across the rear end (which, according to rumor, was the dog’s favorite spot), and drank raspberry bitters like it was water. The villagers were baffled. How could such a young man have such a title? Why did he wear a waistcoat with buttons shaped like tiny horses? And why did he bring a *dog* named Stoopid?

Foker became a regular visitor, and everyone loved him—except the widow and little Laura, who thought he was slightly unhinged. But then again, who wasn’t slightly unhinged in Clavering?

Clavering itself was a charming little town—when viewed from afar. From the London road, it looked like a painting: a winding river, old trees, a church tower peeking above rooftops, and hills rolling gently toward the sea. Tom Smith, the coachman, used to point to a tree near the river and say, “Artises come and take hoff the Church from that there tree—it was a Habby once, sir.” (Which, naturally, confused everyone.)

But up close? Not so much. On market days, it was lively. On other days, it was quiet enough to hear the creak of the ensign at the Clavering Arms. There hadn’t been a ball in the Assembly Rooms since the old Sir Francis Clavering died, and the stables—once home to a regiment of cavalry—were now occupied by farmers’ carts and the occasional goat.

The church, however, was grand. Towering grey stones, carved windows, and effigies of dead baronets kneeling in eternal prayer. One statue showed Dame Rebecca Clavering being carried to heaven by two blue-veined angels who looked like they were barely holding on. Pen used to stare at them during sermons, wondering if they were tired of the job.

The congregation was decent—mostly servants, schoolchildren, and the occasional apothecary. Wapshot’s boys made noise. Mrs. Pybus alternated between churches. And the Misses Finucane’s girls wore bonnets that seemed to say, “We are here to learn, but also to look presentable.”

But the real drama was happening in the *other* church—the New Church, led by Mr. Simcoe and his wife, the Honourable Mrs. Simcoe. They were modern, evangelical, and absolutely *not* impressed by Doctor Portman’s taste for whist and the theater. Their tracts fluttered into every door like angry birds, and soon, half the town had abandoned the old church for the new one.

Mrs. Portman, bless her, was the chief gossip of Clavering. She knew everything—what the butcher bought, what the milliner sold, and especially what Arthur Pendennis had done in Chatteris. Once she heard the news—about the actress, the proposal, the whole romantic disaster—she couldn’t keep quiet. By the next morning, it was all over town. At the bookshop, the milliner’s, the shoe store, the factory—everyone was talking.

“Gracious goodness,” the ladies whispered, “how infatuated the mother is! That boy thinks he’s a lord just because he rides a horse!”

And indeed, Arthur *did* give himself airs. He didn’t wear corduroys. He didn’t play cricket. He didn’t care for village life. He preferred riding Rebecca, reading novels, and composing poetry that made the cows groan.

So when the scandal broke—when the story of the actress reached Clavering—it was like dropping a lit match into a pile of dry hay. The village erupted.

Wapshot’s boys mocked him. The factory workers jeered. Someone chalked “Fotheringay for ever!” on the gate of Fairoaks. Another brought a playbill from Chatteris and taped it to the wall like a declaration of war.

Then came the incident in the churchyard.

Arthur, riding through Lower Town, heard laughter. He turned into the churchyard—where Wapshot’s boys were lounging. One of them, Hobnell Major (a name that sounds like a villain in a Dickens novel), struck a theatrical pose near a fresh grave and began reciting Hamlet’s speech over Ophelia—with a smirk aimed directly at Arthur.

Arthur, who had spent years suppressing his temper, finally snapped. He charged forward, whipped out his riding crop, and slashed Hobnell across the face. Then he threw the whip aside, roared something unprintable, and punched Hobnell straight into the grave—just as it was waiting for its rightful occupant.

The boys scattered. Doctor Portman arrived, gasping. Arthur stood there, fists clenched, eyes blazing, daring anyone else to step forward. No one did.

Later, Arthur went to Baymouth to consult Harry Foker. Foker, ever the gentleman, sent Stoopid (his dog) with a note to Hobnell. The note read: “Dear Mr. Hobnell, I hope you’re feeling better. I’d like to discuss the matter. Please let me know when you’re available.”

Stoopid returned with word that the note had been read aloud to six boys, who laughed and decided Hobnell would reply “arter arternoon school.” And that Mr. Wapshot would come out in his Master’s gown—which, as Stoopid knew, meant he was ready for a fight.

Thus ended Chapter XV, where love, honor, and a single poorly timed insult led to a brawl in a graveyard, a dog named Stoopid, and the birth of a legend that would be told in Clavering for generations.

And if you ask me, it’s the kind of story that deserves a sequel. Maybe with more ponies.
