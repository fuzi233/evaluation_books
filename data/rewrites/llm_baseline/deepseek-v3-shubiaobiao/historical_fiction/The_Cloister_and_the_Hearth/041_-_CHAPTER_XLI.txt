Denys, flanked by his captors to prevent any desperate bid for freedom, was led away in a storm of grief and bitter remorse. Gerard had abandoned the safer German road to Rome for his sake—and what had it earned him? Abandoned, alone, in the heart of Burgundy. The thought gnawed at Denys like a fanged beast, driving him now to wild curses at the heavens, now to a brooding silence so dark and dangerous that his guards, eyeing him warily, stripped him of his weapons.

They rejoined their leader just beyond the town, and the company halted at the *Tête d'Or*. The young landlady, though preoccupied with the count and even more so with the bastard, caught sight of Denys and asked, with a flicker of concern, what had become of his young companion. The question tore open the wound. Denys, his voice breaking, told her everything—and begged her to send someone after Gerard.

"Now that he’s parted from me, perhaps he’ll listen to sense," Denys pleaded. "The lad was never one for solitude."

The landlady tossed her head, cheeks flushing. "I think I’ve been over-kind already," she snapped.

"You refuse?"

"Most certainly."

Then—he unleashed a torrent of curses so vile that she recoiled, stammering that she was not accustomed to such language, and hurried away, wiping at her eyes. Yet within the hour, despite her wounded pride, she had two mules saddled.

Denys, unaware of this, rode north with his captors, slumped in his saddle, his spirit as heavy as his chains. Behind him, the landlady—swift for once in her life—spurred southward with a servant at her heels.

At dusk, after a hard march, the bastard’s band reached *The White Hart*. Their arrival sent the inn into an uproar, and it was some time before Manon, now a woman of standing, recognized her old acquaintance amidst the throng. She said nothing, but her cheeks burned. The past was a thing to be buried, not revisited.

Denys scarcely noticed. The inn itself was a ghost of Gerard, and the weight of his loss pressed harder with each passing league.

Then, as night deepened, the wind shifted. Manon beckoned him from the doorway. He rose, sullen, his guards shadowing him.

"Nay," she whispered, "a word in private."

She drew him aside, and in a hushed voice asked if he would do her a kindness.

His answer was a bark of laughter. "No. I’ve no kindness left in me for man, woman, or beast. Not even for a dog—if I could help it."

She winced but pressed on. "Good archer, I showed you and your comrade mercy once. For his sake, then—grant me this."

Denys exhaled sharply. "For his sake, then. What is it?"

Her voice dropped lower. "You know my history. I was... unfortunate. Now I am respectable. But today, a woman threw *him* in my face. It will always be so—so long as he hangs there. I would have him taken down."

Denys snorted. "Not my trade."

Manon wrung her hands. "There is no one else I dare ask!"

He shrugged. "Then pay someone. Two angels would buy you a pair of soldiers willing to do the deed. They’re not particular."

The suggestion stung her pride, but necessity won. "Send them to me," she murmured. "But tell them it’s for a friend—not for me. I would die of shame if they thought otherwise. Times have changed."

Denys found two men eager for easy coin and sent them to her. Then he returned to his brooding, drowning in thoughts of Gerard.

Manon and the soldiers struck their bargain swiftly—save for the matter of payment. The men wanted it upfront. She balked. Did they doubt her word? Let them do the deed first—or let her watch from afar.

"*Me?*" Her horror was genuine. "I’d sooner die than see it done!"

"Yet you’d have it done."

"Aye," she admitted, "for my need is great. Times *have* changed."

An hour later, the grim relic of her past was gone—no longer a specter to haunt her newfound respectability. The soldiers returned, expecting whispers of divine wrath for their meddling with the dead. Instead, they found the marketplace seething with murmurs.

Conscience prickling, the bolder of the two edged forward and melted into the crowd. When he returned, his face was pale.

"Fool," he hissed to his companion, "it’s not vengeance they’re talking of—it’s a *miracle*."
