The sight of him brought only a fleeting surprise to Mrs. Dashwood; indeed, his arrival at Barton seemed to her the most natural thing in the world. Yet, her initial wonder quickly dissolved into a joy that deepened with every passing moment. She welcomed him with such warmth and genuine affection that any lingering shyness, coldness, or reserve melted away before he even crossed the threshold. These defenses had already begun to crumble before he entered the room, completely overwhelmed by the captivating kindness of Mrs. Dashwood. It was impossible for a man to harbor love for either of her daughters without extending that tenderness to their mother as well, and Elinor found great satisfaction in seeing him soon return to his true self. His affections seemed to reawaken toward them all, and his concern for their welfare became evident once more.

However, a shadow remained. He was not in spirits. Though he praised their home, admired the view, and attended to them with kindness, a certain heaviness lingered in his demeanor. The entire family noticed it, and Mrs. Dashwood, blaming it on some lack of generosity in his mother, sat down to dinner with an indignation directed at all selfish parents.

"Pray, what are Mrs. Ferrars's views for you at present, Edward?" she asked after the meal, as they gathered around the fire. "Are you still destined to be a great orator, despite your own inclinations?"

"No," he replied. "I hope my mother is finally convinced that I possess no talent for a public life beyond my inclination!"

"But how is your fame to be established?" Mrs. Dashwood pressed gently. "For you must be famous to satisfy your family. With no desire for expense, no affection for strangers, no profession, and no assurance, you might find it a difficult path."

"I shall not attempt it," Edward said firmly. "I have no wish to be distinguished, and I have every reason to hope I never shall. Thank Heaven, I cannot be forced into genius and eloquence."

"You have no ambition, I well know," Mrs. Dashwood observed. "Your wishes are all moderate."

"As moderate as those of the rest of the world, I believe," Edward answered. "I wish, as everyone else does, to be perfectly happy; but, like everyone else, it must be in my own way. Greatness will not make me so."

"Strange that it would!" cried Marianne, her voice brightening. "What have wealth or grandeur to do with happiness?"

"Grandeur has but little to do with it," Elinor corrected softly, "but wealth has much."

"Elinor, for shame!" Marianne exclaimed. "Money can only give happiness where there is nothing else to give it. Beyond a comfortable competence, it affords no real satisfaction regarding the self alone."

"Perhaps," Elinor smiled, "we may come to the same conclusion. Your 'competence' and my 'wealth' are very much alike, I dare say. Without them, as the world goes now, we shall both agree that every external comfort must be lacking. Your ideas are simply more noble than mine. Come, what exactly is your competence?"

"About eighteen hundred, perhaps two thousand a year; not more than that."

Elinor laughed. "Two thousand a year! One is my wealth! I guessed how it would end."

"And yet," Marianne insisted, "two thousand a year is a very moderate income. A family cannot well be maintained on less. I am sure I am not extravagant in my demands. A proper establishment of servants, a carriage, perhaps two, and hunters, cannot be supported on less."

Elinor smiled again, hearing her sister describe their future expenses at Combe Magna with such accuracy.

"Hunters!" Edward repeated, shaking his head. "But why must you have hunters? Not everyone hunts."

"But most people do," Marianne blushed as she replied.

"I wish," said Margaret, striking out a novel thought, "that somebody would give us all a large fortune apiece!"

"Oh, that they would!" cried Marianne, her eyes sparkling with animation, her cheeks glowing with the delight of such imaginary happiness.

"We are all unanimous in that wish, I suppose," Elinor said, "in spite of the insufficiency of wealth."

"Oh dear!" Margaret sighed dreamily. "How happy I should be! I wonder what I should do with it!"

Marianne looked as though she had no doubt on that point.

"I should be puzzled to spend so large a fortune myself," Mrs. Dashwood admitted, "if my children were all to be rich without my help."

"You must begin your improvements on this house," Elinor observed cheerfully. "And your difficulties will soon vanish."

"What magnificent orders would travel from this family to London in such an event!" Edward mused. "What a happy day for booksellers, music-sellers, and print-shops! You, Miss Dashwood, would give a general commission for every new print of merit to be sent you. And as for Marianne, I know her greatness of soul; there would not be enough music in London to content her. And books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would buy them all over and over again. She would buy up every copy, I believe, to prevent them falling into unworthy hands. And she would have every book that tells her how to admire an old twisted tree. Should not you, Marianne? Forgive me if I am very saucy. But I was willing to show you that I have not forgotten our old disputes."

"I love to be reminded of the past, Edward," Marianne said warmly. "Whether it be melancholy or gay, I love to recall it. You will never offend me by talking of former times. You are very right in supposing how my money would be spent—some of it, at least. My loose cash would certainly be employed in improving my collection of music and books."

"And the bulk of your fortune would be laid out in annuities on the authors or their heirs," Edward added playfully.

"No, Edward, I should have something else to do with it."

"Perhaps, then, you would bestow it as a reward on that person who wrote the ablest defense of your favorite maxim: that no one can ever be in love more than once in their life? Your opinion on that point is unchanged, I presume?"

"Undoubtedly," Marianne declared. "At my time of life, opinions are tolerably fixed. It is not likely that I should now see or hear anything to change them."

"Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see," Elinor noted. "She is not at all altered."

"She is only grown a little more grave than she was," Edward replied.

"Nay, Edward," Marianne said, turning to him. "You need not reproach me. You are not very gay yourself."

"Why should you think so!" he replied with a sigh. "But gaiety has never been a part of my character."

"Nor do I think it a part of Marianne's," Elinor said gently. "I should hardly call her a lively girl. She is very earnest, very eager in all she does—sometimes talks a great deal and always with animation—but she is not often really merry."

"I believe you are right," he agreed, "and yet I have always set her down as a lively girl."

"I have frequently detected myself in such kind of mistakes," Elinor confessed. "In a total misapprehension of character in some point or other: fancying people so much more gay or grave, or ingenious or stupid than they really are. I can hardly tell why or in what the deception originated. Sometimes one is guided by what they say of themselves, and very frequently by what other people say of them, without giving oneself time to deliberate and judge."

"But I thought it was right, Elinor," Marianne interjected. "To be guided wholly by the opinion of other people. I thought our judgments were given us merely to be subservient to those of our neighbors. This has always been your doctrine, I am sure."

"No, Marianne, never," Elinor corrected. "My doctrine has never aimed at the subjection of the understanding. All I have ever attempted to influence has been the behavior. You must not confound my meaning. I am guilty, I confess, of having often wished you to treat our acquaintance in general with greater attention; but when have I advised you to adopt their sentiments or to conform to their judgment in serious matters?"

"You have not been able to bring your sister over to your plan of general civility," Edward turned to Elinor. "Do you gain no ground?"

"Quite the contrary," Elinor replied, looking expressively at Marianne.

"My judgment," Edward returned, "is all on your side of the question; but I am afraid my practice is much more on your sister's. I never wish to offend, but I am so foolishly shy that I often seem negligent when I am only kept back by my natural awkwardness. I have frequently thought that I must have been intended by nature to be fond of low company, for I am so little at my ease among strangers of gentility!"

"Marianne has not shyness to excuse any inattention of hers," Elinor said.

"She knows her own worth too well for false shame," Edward replied. "Shyness is only the effect of a sense of inferiority in some way or other. If I could persuade myself that my manners were perfectly easy and graceful, I should not be shy."

"But you would still be reserved," Marianne countered. "And that is worse."

Edward started. "Reserved! Am I reserved, Marianne?"

"Yes, very."

"I do not understand you," he replied, coloring slightly. "Reserved! How, in what manner? What am I to tell you? What can you suppose?"

Elinor looked surprised at his emotion. Trying to laugh off the subject, she said to him, "Do you not know my sister well enough to understand what she means? Do you not know she calls everyone reserved who does not talk as fast, and admire what she admires as rapturously as herself?"

Edward made no answer. His gravity and thoughtfulness returned upon him in their fullest extent, and he sat for some time silent and dull.
