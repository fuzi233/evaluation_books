Detective Mr. Gryce was in a state of profound melancholy. He had reached that particular season of life when the spirit flags and enthusiasm requires a constant, artificial spur. Lately, however, there had been a distinct lack of special excitement, leaving him feeling dull and hopelessly superannuated. In fact, he was seriously contemplating resigning his post on the force to retire to the small farm he had purchased in Westchester. Such a prospect did little to lift his mood; for a man of action, the mere exercise of his mental faculties was far more inspiring than any material comfort retirement might offer. Yet, fate had other plans.

Just as his secret dissatisfaction peaked, a telephone message crackled through Headquarters, instantly rousing the old man to a vigor he hadn't felt in years. It cast a new, unexpected interest over the gray afternoon. The call originated from Carter's famous drugstore. A young boy had been dispatched by a woman to report a bizarre crime committed in the mansion located on ---- Street, just around the corner. The boy claimed he didn't know the woman, though it was obvious she had paid him handsomely. He was terrified, lending credibility to his tale. If the police needed him, he could be found waiting at the drugstore until released.

A *strange* crime. The single word struck Gryce with the force of a gavel, silencing his thoughts on age and retirement. While the officers nearby murmured about the sudden spotlight falling on the property—a historic landmark hemmed in by modern shops and clubs—Gryce seized the opportunity. With a glance at the inspector in charge, he received his orders.

He moved immediately to the drugstore, retrieving the trembling boy. En route to the scene, Gryce tried to draw out details, but the poor waif added nothing to the initial report. His story was simple: a stunningly beautiful woman in flashy clothes and heavy jewelry had approached him while he admired toys in a window. She handed him a coin, dragged him down the block, and then produced another, promising more if he would deliver a specific message to the phone operator. *"Tell him a strange crime has been committed in the old house on ---- Street,"* she had commanded. Fear had gripped the child, causing him to slide away, but the woman had shaken him back to his senses before he could flee.

When questioned about her appearance, the boy's observations were limited to clichés: she was a "stunner," wearing shiny things and lots of bling. Recognizing the limits of the witness, Gryce stopped probing and focused entirely on the looming brick edifice.

Externally, the house offered no clues. A closed door, a pristine stoop, and windows draped in heavy curtains suggested a home of quiet domestic respectability. Its dignified brownstone facade stood in stark, peaceful contrast to the chaotic commercial noise of the neighboring buildings.

"Some mistake," Gryce muttered, struck by the eerie perfection of the silence.

Before he could conclude he was being hoaxed, a movement broke the stillness beneath the stoop. An officer emerged, his face etched with confusion.

"Nothing wrong? No bloodshed? Everything looks perfectly quiet here," Gryce asked urgently.

The officer tipped his hat. "We can't get in. We've rung every bell. I thought the place empty until I spotted some activity in an upper window. Should I try forcing entry through the rear yard via Knapp & Co.'s store next door?"

"Yes," Gryce ordered, handing the boy over. "Lock the kid in one of their offices. Then find a way in from the backyard. It should be straightforward."

As the officer vanished into the adjacent store with the child, Gryce was immediately surrounded by a throng of curious onlookers, eager to share in the drama. However, the swift opening of the front door silenced the crowd. Gryce slipped inside, finding himself in a basement hall leading to a kitchen that appeared immaculate, devoid of the chaos often associated with tragedy. Finding no evidence of violence or disruption, he followed the officer upward.

Upon reaching the parlor floor, the officer shared local gossip: "Mr. Raffner from the shop next door says the resident is an odd fellow, known only as a 'bookworm.' He's lived here six months, yet no one has ever seen anyone else but himself and a peculiar, silent old servant."

"I know," Gryce replied quietly. Everyone knew the truth: the house, once the domain of New York's aristocracy, was currently occupied by Mr. Adams. Adams was a man of immense wealth and striking personal charm, but his temperament was so uncongenial that he avoided all human contact. Entering the home of such a recluse was rare; entering it on a matter of life and death was a thrill that reawakened the dormant passions of an aging detective.

They entered a wide, old-fashioned hall, sparsely furnished in antique style. Two doors stood open. They passed through the nearest, stepping into a room as bare as a doctor's waiting room. There was nothing of note here, and they would have departed without ceremony had they not caught a glimpse of opulence behind a partially drawn portière at the far end.

Pushing through, they froze.

Nothing in their vast experience had prepared them for the solemn, terrifying spectacle before them. Though daylight still filled the room, it was dominated by a jet of rose-colored light. This illumination did not fall upon the endless rows of books lining the lower walls or the grand painting that usually commanded attention. Instead, the light converged solely on the upturned face of a man lying on a bearskin rug.

In his heart protruded a dagger. Across his chest lay a cross, its golden lines sharply defined against the long, dark garment that swathed his body. He resembled a saint prepared for burial in some holy sanctuary, except for the dagger speaking of brutal murder and the expression on his face.

It was an anguish for which Mr. Gryce, despite a lifetime of witnessing death, could find no name. It bore no resemblance to fear, pain, or surprise—the standard reactions of a victim struck down by an assassin. It was something far deeper, far stranger, hanging in the red-lit air of the circular study.

A profound hesitation, bordering on sheer awe, seized Mr. Gryce before he could regain his composure. The dim illumination, the oppressive silence, and the setting—evocative of a romanticized past—combined to create an atmosphere that unsettled even the hardened veteran of the law. His mind, seasoned by decades of solving complex cases, struggled to reconcile the motionless figure lying prone before him with the reality of the situation. The man, recently the master of the house, lay stretched out as if prepared for burial, yet the sacred symbol pinned to his chest violently contradicted the earthly rage that had driven the dagger home.

For merely a heartbeat, Mr. Gryce faltered. However, before his subordinate could voice his own confusion, the detective stepped forward, positioning himself beside the fallen form. There could be no doubt: the man was dead. Yet, his immediate instinct was to verify the cessation of life, placing a hand on the pulse and inspecting the eyes. Their gaze held an expression of such bitter reproach that Gryce had to summon every ounce of professional coolness to withstand the weight of them. The body was still warm, confirming death beyond any shadow of a doubt. Convinced, he refrained from removing the dagger, instead studying the wound with meticulous care before shifting his focus to the rest of the room.

The weapon was extraordinary—a curiosity sourced from an Oriental shop. While such an item might suggest suicide, the angle of entry and the specific placement of the wound ruled out self-infliction. Clues were scarce. Despite the evident violence of the assault, the impeccably ordered apartment bore no marks of a struggle. Aside from a scattering of rose petals on the floor, the room exuded a serene, luxurious tranquility. Even the massive central table, positioned where the victim had stood moments before, offered no initial sign of chaos. Its surface, cluttered with useful and ornamental items, appeared undisturbed, as if the shock of the tragedy had somehow bypassed the objects themselves.

Gryce examined the table's contents: pipes, pens, portraits, weights, measures, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelains, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a musical score, potted flowers, and, most strangely, a row of electric buttons. As soon as Gryce pressed one, the red glow emanating from the cage-like iron fixture above instantly shifted to a sickly green glare, bathing the room in ghastly hues. Seizing another button, he restored a soft, white radiance, banishing the unnatural tint.

"Childish tricks for a man of his station and age," Gryce muttered internally. Yet, glancing once more at the upturned face beneath him, a doubt crept in. Could the owner of those sharply defined, purposeful features possess anything resembling childish whimsy? The face itself was arresting, exerting a magnetic pull that disrupted the flow of the detective's reasoning whenever it captured his attention. To attribute foolishness to such a visage was to admit a failure of judgment regarding human nature. Consequently, the overhead lamp, with its scientific mechanism, took on a deeper significance, hinting at the advanced studies evidenced by the books and apparatus surrounding the corpse.

Setting the light to remain steady, Gryce turned his attention to the walls, draped in tapestries and curios. Nothing immediately aided his investigation, except for one detail: a bent nail, ripped from its socket. It was the nail that had held the cross now resting against the victim's heart. The cord suspending the cross still clung to it, tangling with the crimson thread of the fatal wound. Who had torn down that symbol of peace? Not the victim; with such a grievous injury, such movement would have been impossible. Furthermore, the empty hook on the wall was located at the farthest possible distance from the body within the confined circular room.

Another hand had undoubtedly removed the cross, placing it directly in the path of the dying man's final breaths—a bizarre display of either desperate religious hope or frantic remorse. Gryce spared this only a fleeting thought, too precious were the minutes he spent alone amidst the carnage.

Behind the table, halfway up the wall, hung a single large painting: the portrait of a young woman of striking, poignant beauty. Her attire and hairstyle dated the piece to the era of the Civil War. She shared a haunting intellectual charm with the man slumped on the floor. Though she was fair-skinned and he dark-haired, the resemblance argued a familial bond. Beneath the portrait rested a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a medal awarded for valor during the conflict.

"Mementoes that may prove vital," Gryce mused.

Moving onward, he encountered a narrow curtain, dark in hue and matching the adjacent drapery, which had previously escaped notice. It was not a window, for the apartment's windows sat high near the ceiling. Instead, it concealed an entrance to another chamber. Pushing it aside, Gryce entered a narrow closet furnished with a bed, a dresser, and a small table. The bed was a narrow cot suitable for a bachelor, while the dresser reflected the luxurious habits of its occupant. Everything was in perfect order, save for a silver-backed comb missing from the dresser, now found lying on the floor at the far end of the room. Nearby, on a small stand, stood a pearl-handled parasol. These items confirmed the recent presence of a woman.

Her identity became clear almost immediately. On the threshold separating this room from the study lay a tiny black sequin. As Gryce, drawn by its sparkle, stooped to examine it, his eye caught a second sequin further along the floor, and a third closer to the large center table. This dainty trail affected him strangely. As a keen observer, he knew that sequins were often strung on a thread; if broken, they fell one by one, allowing a detective to track a woman's path by the glittering debris left behind. Whether it was the delicacy of the clue or the irony of fate using vanity as a trap that pleased him, a knowing smile played on Gryce's lips as he regarded the table.

Before inspecting the furniture, however, he sought the origin of the loose thread. Had it snagged on a projection? He checked the doorways and chairs—all cushioned and smooth. Then, his gaze landed on the cross. Its base featured intricate gold filigree. Could this delicate wire have caught in the woman's dress as she tore the cross from the wall, initiating the chain of falling sequins? Rushing to the spot where the cross had hung, he searched the floor but found nothing until he reached the rug beneath the prostrate body. Amidst the long hairs of the bearskin lay another sequin. The woman in shimmering garments had definitely stooped there before him.

Satisfied, Gryce returned to the table for a thorough inspection. The results were promising. He smiled as he rubbed his finger across a specific spot near the inkstand, then carefully lifted the stand and replaced it exactly where he had found it. Had he suspected something hidden beneath? A detective rarely reveals his hypotheses.

Lost in contemplation before the table, Gryce was startled by a quick step behind him. Styles, the officer, had completed his sweep of the house and stood ready to report.

"What is it?" Gryce asked, turning sharply.

Styles gestured toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door. "Go up!" he indicated.

Gryce hesitated, unwilling to leave the room that currently captivated him. Styles grew agitated, breaking his silence. "Come! I've found the culprit. He's in a room upstairs."

"He?" Gryce expressed surprise at the gender assumption.

"Yes, no doubt about it. When you see him—but what is that? Is he coming down? I'm certain no one else is in the house. Can't you hear footsteps, sir?"

Gryce nodded; someone was indeed descending.

"Let us withdraw," Styles suggested. "Not because he is dangerous, but because it is crucial you observe him before he observes you. He acts strangely, sir. If he enters here, he will surely incriminate himself. Where shall we hide?"

Recalling the small room he had just vacated, Gryce guided the officer inside and drew the curtain shut, leaving only a small aperture. The approaching footsteps grew louder, accompanied by the rapid, labored breathing of the intruder.

"Does he know we are here? Did he see you upstairs?" Gryce whispered.

Styles shook his head, pointing urgently toward the opposite door. Just then, the intruder lifted the portière and stood revealed in the doorway.

Both men stared. Was this the murderer? A pale, gaunt servant holding a tray with a single glass of water? So stunned was Gryce that he looked to Styles for clarification. But the officer, concealing his own surprise at this peaceful apparition, urged patience. Together, they watched as the man advanced, stopped, glanced briefly at the body on the floor, and then dropped the glass with a low cry that quickly escalated into a howl.

"Look at him! Watch what he does next!" Styles urged in a hushed tone. "You'll see a murderer at work."

The appeal finally registered. The glass slipped from the man's grasp once more, striking the floor with a sharp *clink* before shattering. Simultaneously, the man emitted a peculiar sound—neither quite a cry nor a scream, but a jagged noise halfway between a moan and a shriek. Having caught sight of the figures attempting to restrain him, his haggard features and cowering posture revealed a sudden, terrifying realization of his precarious situation.

He tried to bolt, but Styles gripped him tighter, dragging him back to where Mr. Gryce stood beside the bearskin rug upon which his dead master lay. Instantly, another metamorphosis seized the trapped butler. A joyous, hellish passion—the kind often seen in the presence of violence and death—illuminated his wandering gaze and twisted his mouth. Seeking no disguise for his satisfaction, he let out a low, thrilling laugh that echoed unholy through the silent room.

Mr. Gryce, overcome despite himself by an abhorrence that the man's irresponsible state seemed only to amplify, waited until the final echoes of this diabolical mirth faded into the high corners of the space above. Then, locking his rare, glittering eyes with those of the strange creature, he spoke sternly: "There now! Speak! Who killed this man? You were in the house with him; you should know."

The butler's lips parted, and a string of strange guttural noises poured forth. With his one free hand—for the other was pinned to his side by Styles—he touched his ears and his lips, shaking his head violently. There was only one conclusion to draw: the man was both deaf and dumb.

The revelation proved too much for Styles. His grip loosened, and the man, suddenly free, retreated to his original spot in the room. There, with renewed vigor, he began to act out the murder and the subsequent mourning, a performance that had earlier left such a profound impression on the observers. When he finished, he stood waiting, but now his quick, restless eyes held that same infernal gleam, making his mere presence in the chamber of death feel like a sacrilege.

Styles could no longer endure it. "Can't you speak?" he shouted. "Can't you hear?"

The man merely smiled—a cruel, gloating grin—which Mr. Gryce deemed necessary to cut short. "Take him away!" the detective ordered. "Examine him thoroughly for bloodstains. I am going upstairs to the room where you initially saw him. Given how closely he is linked to this crime, he cannot possibly leave without carrying traces of it."

For once, however, the seasoned detective found himself at a loss. No marks were discovered on the elderly servant, nor could they find any evidence in the upper rooms linking this sole remaining occupant to the tragedy within. Disappointed, Mr. Gryce turned his attention back to the two rooms below, searching for clues among the overturned furniture and scattered debris. The results were meager. Just as he began to lose himself in contemplation of the deceased's upturned face—a visage frozen in suffering and resolve—a cry drifted down from the dim recesses above.

Forming clearly into two words, it rang out with startling clarity against the silence: *"Remember Evelyn!"*

"Remember Evelyn!" Who was Evelyn? And whose voice was this, emanating from a house already ransacked for other inhabitants? It seemed to originate from the roof. Sure enough, as Mr. Gryce looked upward, he spotted an English starling suspended in a cage near the top of one of the windows. In apparent recognition of the attention it had garnered, the bird craned its neck and shrieked again, with even greater insistence: *"Remember Evelyn!"*

It was the final uncanny element in a series of bizarre occurrences. With a heavy sense of nightmare weighing upon him, Mr. Gryce leaned forward over the study table to get a better look at the bird. Suddenly, without warning, the white light flooding the room shifted to a vivid green. Realizing he had inadvertently pressed a button on the complex electrical apparatus, activating another slide in the curious lamp overhead, he felt a surge of annoyance. These shifting colors presented a puzzle he was currently too consumed by the case to solve.

Disdaining further investigation of the lighting, he moved away from the table, intending to leave the room. However, he heard Styles's voice rising from the adjoining antechamber, where he was guarding the old butler.

"Shall I let him go, Mr. Gryce?" Styles asked. "He seems very uneasy—not dangerous, you know, but anxious. As if he'd forgotten something or recalled some unfulfilled duty."

"Yes, let him go," the detective replied quickly. "But keep watch and follow him. Every movement he makes is of interest. Unconsciously, he may provide us with invaluable leads."

Gryce approached the door to observe the man's actions personally. Was this appeal directed at him, or at the man on the floor whose ears were permanently sealed? It might be trivial, or it might hold the key to the entire tragedy. Yet, at this critical juncture, he could afford no distraction.

Just then, the old butler emerged from the front hall, having hurried there after being released by Styles. Approaching Gryce, he carried his master's hat in one hand and the umbrella in the other. Uncertain of the significance of this new gesture, the detective paused, waiting.

Seeing this, the mute servant, whose face and bearing had reverted to the respectful stillness of a trained employee, handed over the items. Then, with the quiet efficiency of one performing an expected duty, he retreated silently to his usual spot in the antechamber, sitting down and almost immediately lapsing back into his former daze.

"Hmph! Mind quite lost, memory uncertain, testimony valueless," the disappointed detective muttered to himself as he placed the hat and umbrella back on the hall rack. "Was this state induced by the tragedy that just occurred, or does his current insanity precede and cause the event?"

Gryce might have resolved this question internally had the intermittent ringing of the front door bell—which had previously signaled the impatience of the curious crowd outside—not been interrupted by an authoritative knock that instantly silenced all speculation. The coroner, or someone of equal importance, had arrived. The detective's golden hour of solitude was over.

Detective Mr. Gryce found himself at a profound disadvantage in his quest to unravel the mystery of this particular affair, a handicap he had not encountered in years. His obstacles were twofold: first, the victim was a solitary man whose entire household consisted merely of his mute man-of-all-work; second, he resided in a sector of the city where neighbors were virtually nonexistent. Furthermore, it now appeared the deceased possessed no active circle of friends. Despite several hours having passed since news of his death spread, not a single soul had knocked upon the door offering inquiries or information. This silence was peculiar, especially given that the man had been a notable figure in this quarter of the town for months. Yet, everything about him was inherently odd; it would have been incongruous with his eccentric lifestyle and surroundings for him to maintain the ordinary social associations typical of his class.

This lack of conventional avenues for gathering intelligence, rather than diminishing the intrigue, only heightened Mr. Gryce's fascination with the case. Consequently, he felt a wave of relief when, shortly before midnight, he watched the procession of reporters, medical professionals, officials, and others trailing the coroner's van depart from the front door, leaving him once again, albeit temporarily, in sole command of the situation. Before retiring for his much-needed rest, however, there were two critical points he needed to resolve.

"You need not answer," the inspector interjected quickly. "No man is compelled to incriminate himself in this free and independent country."

A smile, the saddest imaginable, traced a path across the prisoner's pallid lips. Then, lifting his head with an air of utter desperation, Adams replied, "Incrimination is not what I fear now. By the looks upon your faces, I perceive I am already lost, for I possess no means of proving my story."

This admission, sounding like the cry of an innocent man crushed under weight, caused the interrogator to pause. "You forget," Gryce suggested gently, "that you had your wife with you. She can corroborate your words and serve as an invaluable witness in your favor."

"My wife!" Adams choked out, the word barely intelligible. "Must she be dragged into this? She is so sick, so weak. It would kill her, sir. She loves me—she..."

"Was she with you in Mr. Adams's study? Did she see him lift the dagger against his own breast?"

"No." With this denial, a flicker of new courage returned to the young man. "She had fainted several moments prior, while the altercation between my brother and myself reached its height. She witnessed neither the final act, nor—" He paused, gathering strength. "Gentlemen, I might as well speak the truth; I have nothing to gain by silence. She finds it just as difficult as you do to believe that Mr. Adams struck himself. I—I have tried with all my arts to impress the reality upon her, but oh, what hope remains when the wife of my bosom, an angel who loves me... oh, sirs, she can never testify for me. She is too conscientious, too true to her own convictions. To force her would mean losing her; she would die."

Mr. Gryce attempted to interrupt, but Adams would not be silenced. "Spare me, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty, accuse me of whatever you please, rather than drag that fragile creature into the light!"

The inspector cut off these rending appeals abruptly. "Have you read the newspapers for the past few days?"

"I? Yes, sir. How could I avoid it? Blood is blood; the man was my brother. I had left him dying. Naturally, I felt anxiety, naturally I saw my own peril, and naturally I read the reports."

"Then you know he was found with a large cross upon his breast—a cross that hung on the wall. How did it come to be torn down? Who placed it upon his bosom?"

"I, sir. I am not a Catholic, though Felix was. Seeing him dying without absolution or extreme unction, I thought of the holy cross and tore down the only one visible, placing it in his arms."

"A pious act. Did he recognize it?"

"I cannot say. I had my fainting wife to attend to. She consumed all my thoughts."

"I see. You carried her out, so absorbed in your care that you did not notice Mr. Adams's valet..."

"He is innocent, sir. Whatever others may whisper, he had nothing to do with this crime."

"You did not observe him, I say, standing in the doorway, watching you?"

At this, the inspector knew the truth: Bartow had not stood in the doorway but hovered at the loophole above. Yet, the opportunity to entrap the witness was too precious to forfeit. Adams fell into the trap, evidenced by the sudden beads of perspiration breaking out on his pale forehead. Still, he struggled to maintain his stance, shouting hoarsely, "That man is mad, and deaf-and-dumb besides! Or so the papers claim. Surely his testimony is worthless. You would not confront me with him?"

"We confront you with no one. We merely ask questions. You did not observe the valet, then?"

"No, sir."

"And you do not understand the mystery of the colored lights?"

"No, sir."

"Nor the plate of steel, nor the other contrivances with which your brother enlivened his solitude?"

"I do not follow you, sir."

Yet, a distinct shift had occurred in his tone.

"I see," the inspector observed, "that the complications which have delayed our collection of testimony have not entered into the crime as you describe it. That is possible. However, there remains a circumstance demanding explanation—a minor detail, yet one of profound importance, since your wife mentioned it to you the moment she regained consciousness. I refer to the six or more words written by your brother immediately preceding his death. The paper containing them has been recovered, and it is evident it was a factor in your quarrel, given her regret that it was left behind. Do you know where we found this paper?"

The eyes Thomas Adams raised at this query held no intelligence. The mere sight of the scrap of paper seemed to drain him of all faculties. He shook his head, reaching futilely toward the document the inspector held out. Then, he erupted in a loud cry.

"Enough! I cannot endure this longer, supported only by a wicked lie. I killed my brother for reasons as valid as any man has ever had for taking a life. But I shall not reveal them. I would sooner be tried for murder and hanged."

It was a total collapse, pitiful in its contrast to the man's herculean physique and noble, albeit contracted, features. If it was the end, it was a tragic one. Mr. Gryce, a deep furrow etched between his brows, slowly rose and stepped beside the young man, who appeared ready to fall. The inspector remained motionless, tapping a rhythmic tattoo with his fingers on the table, seemingly intent on continuing the beat.

Suddenly, another cry shattered the tension. "What is that?" Adams demanded, his eyes fixed on the door, his entire frame trembling violently.

"Nothing," the inspector began, but the door swung open.

From the grasp of a man attempting to restrain her, a figure darted forward—a woman white as a wraith, radiant with a sort of holy passion. It was Adams's young, invalid wife, whom he had left three hours prior at Belleville. So frail in form and exquisite in feature, she might have seemed an unearthly visitor were it not for the human anguish radiating from her gaze. Soon, this anguish found vent in a touching cry.

"What is he saying? Oh, I know exactly what he says. He claims he killed his brother, that he held the dagger that rid the world of a monster whose wickedness went unnoticed. But you must not heed him. Indeed, you must not. He is innocent. I, his wife, have traveled twenty miles from a bed of weakness and suffering to tell you so. He—"

A hand was laid gently, yet firmly, over her mouth. She looked up, meeting her husband's eyes filled with frantic appeal. In return, she gave him a glance that pierced the hearts of every man present. Laying her own hand over his, she drew it softly away.

"It is too late, Tom. I must speak. My father, my own frailty, or your peremptory commands could not keep me at Belleville when I learned you had been brought here. Shall I stop now, in the presence of these men who have heard your words and may believe them? No, that would be a cowardice unworthy of our love and the true lives we hope to lead together. Sirs!"

Every man in the room held his breath, straining to catch the faint, fading intonation of her next words.

"I know my husband to be innocent, because the hand that held the dagger was mine. I killed Felix Cadwalader!"

The horror of such a moment is never fully grasped until long afterward. Not a single person moved—not even her husband. Yet, a slow pallor spread across every cheek, testifying to the impact of such words from lips accustomed to smiles and reflecting the highest instincts. Only when someone shouted from the doorway, "Catch her! She is falling!" did anyone stir, releasing the pent-up breath that awe and astonishment had held captive.

The man in whose evident despair lay the true cause of the terror that had driven him to a false confession sprang forward. Renewed life flashed in every feature as he caught her in his arms. Staggering with her to a sofa and laying her down gently, he seemed transformed; his look and bearing were utterly changed. Mr. Gryce

"It mirrors, yet diverges from, what I have just recounted to you," began young Adams. "In my prior admission, I conflated truth with fabrication. To clarify my position entirely and to illuminate the nature of my wife's actions, I must begin anew, speaking as though I have told you nothing."

"Wait!" Mr. Gryce interrupted, his voice cutting through the room with authoritative force. "We shall hear your full statement shortly." Leaning forward over the inspector, he murmured a brief instruction into the detective's ear. Moments later, Mr. Gryce produced a pencil and scrawled several lines onto a scrap of paper, handing it across to the inspector. These notes appeared to be a set of directives. As the inspector glanced at them repeatedly while Mr. Adams—now properly known as Cadwalader—unfolded his narrative, I transcribe them here for your consideration. They serve as the critical keys to unlocking the mystery:

*   **The Sudden Shift:** How could a woman, composed enough to pause and fix her hair at the outset of their meeting, suddenly descend into such a frenzy of rage that she murdered a man against whom she harbored no prior grievance? (Recall the comb discovered on the floor of Mr. Adams's bedroom.)
*   **The Final Words:** What significance lies in the inscription written by the deceased moments before this confrontation: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"*
*   **The Pronoun "I":** Why was the first-person singular employed in this communication? What standing did Mr. Felix Adams possess vis-à-vis this young girl, granting him the authority to use such language after her marriage to his brother?
*   **The Swallowed Paper:** Having penned those words, why did he, upon being assaulted, attempt to swallow the very sheet containing them, ultimately dying with the paper clenched firmly between his teeth?
*   **Sorrow vs. Hate:** If he was slain in a fit of passion and met his end as monsters often do, why did his countenance reflect sorrow rather than hatred? Furthermore, where was the trace of the overwhelming emotional turmoil one might expect when struck down by an unexpected adversary?
*   **The Signal Light:** Possessing the strength to grasp the paper and bring it to his lips, why did he not utilize that same vigor to activate a light capable of summoning aid? Instead, he left burning the crimson glow—a signal universally understood among us to mean: *No further action required at this time. Withdraw.*
*   **The Steel Plate:** What was the purpose of the massive steel plate discovered wedged between the door casings, and why did it remain stationary within its socket at the precise climax of his life?
*   **Poindexter's Arrival:** Explain the sudden appearance of old Poindexter on the scene so immediately after the incident. His overheard remark was, *"It is Amos's son, not Amos!"* Did he not anticipate encountering someone else in this house? Was the sight of a man lying dead with a cross upon his chest and a dagger in his heart less startling to him than the identity of the victim?
*   **Bartow's Pantomime:** Recall our deductions regarding Bartow's silent acting. Mr. Adams was killed by a thrust delivered from the left. Observe carefully whether the young woman acknowledges being left-handed, and demand an explanation for why she kept her right arm extended rigidly behind her for so long.
*   **Intimacy Required:** For the tragedy to be authentic, the narrative must demonstrate that Mr. Adams knew his brother's bride intimately, both in duration and depth.
*   **Bartow's Joy:** If Bartow is, as we suspect, innocent of any direct involvement in this crime aside from witnessing it, why does he exhibit such palpable joy at its conclusion? While this may exceed the bounds of Thomas Adams's confession, it cannot be overlooked. This deaf-and-dumb servant was driven mad by a specific fact that elicited joy. What was it?
*   **The Lamp Code:** Consider the following schedule, derived from repeated trials with Bartow and the peculiar slides of the strange lamp that cast varying hues in Mr. Adams's study:
*   *White:* Water desired.
*   *Green:* Overcoat and hat to be fetched.
*   *Blue:* Return books to shelves.
*   *Violet:* Prepare the study for the night.
*   *Yellow:* Await the next signal.
*   *Red:* Nothing needed; depart.
The red light was illuminated at the final scene. Can Mr. Adams's account reconcile this detail?

Armed with these questions, let us examine the history of this crime and the distant, perhaps convoluted, causes that precipitated it.

Thomas Cadwalader—then known simply as Adams—did not merely recount his history; he suggested it, weaving a narrative that demanded our full attention. To mimic his reticence would be unjust to the truth, and to strip this tragic tale of the necessary details would rob us of understanding the human passions that forged the chains leading to Felix Adams's violent death. Before the woman who eventually struck Felix was even born, the first link in this fatal chain had been hammered into shape. Thus, we must journey back to nearly forgotten days, telling the story from the vantage points of Felix and Thomas Cadwalader.

Thomas never knew his mother, who died in his infancy. His father remained a ghost until Thomas reached his fifteenth year. Until then, he was raised in France by an elderly Scottish lawyer, a maternal relative who spoke of the mother but never the father. One day, the guardian placed several books in Thomas's hands with a solemn injunction: *"Read these romances, Thomas, but remember that none of them will ever equal the story of your father's bitterly wronged and suffering life."*

When Thomas begged to hear about him, the guardian offered no further specifics, content to let the seed grow in the boy's susceptible mind. Thomas turned to the books, but his thoughts drifted constantly to his father. He hunted through the deepest recesses of his memory for a single feature—a nose, an eye—that might restore the man's image. Often, he thought he saw it, only to be overwhelmed by a strange, awe-compelling sensation: a stretch of darkness pierced by two bars of unchanging orange light, black lines resembling distant gables, an inner thrill, a vague affright, and the rush of a swooping wind. These impressions faded as quickly as they arrived, leaving Thomas troubled and perplexed. Unable to explain them, he asked his guardian, who admitted ignorance but hinted at a profound duty awaiting Thomas across the ocean—a duty requiring the acquisition of power and accomplishment.

This mystery fueled Thomas's ambition. At eighteen, he was a Sorbonne graduate, a musical virtuoso, and a prodigy in fencing, riding, and athletic contests. Yet, he lacked social charm. One morning, a letter from his father arrived, praising his talents but criticizing his manners as too abrupt with men and too timid with women. Confused, Thomas discovered he was not the only son; he had an elder brother, roughly twenty years his senior, who had recently visited Paris. This revelation deepened the enigma surrounding his life. Determined not to disappoint the two souls watching his ascent, Thomas softened his demeanor, absorbing the polish of high society until he became a finished man of the world.

Then came the final summons. His guardian warned him to form no lasting ties, for once recalled to America, he would likely never return. Thomas sailed with feverish anticipation, arriving in New York only to find his father, now named Cadwalader, critically ill. He was dispatched immediately to a small village in northern Pennsylvania.

The journey was a nightmare. Rain lashed the carriage, the air thick with the smell of wet earth and decay. He stumbled up a narrow door, greeted by a woman who resembled a Meg Merrilies, holding a flickering candle. The candle sputtered out, leaving only her voice to guide him: *"Is that you, laddie? Come awa in... The maister's been crying on you a' day."*

Inside, the reality shattered his illusions. There were bare floors, stained walls, and a common pine staircase lit by a miserable candle. Where was the palatial home promised to him? Where was the father who had demanded such perfection? An old woman, dipping and curtseying with senile delight, declared, *"No your faither's bairn ava! All mither, laddie, all mither!"*

*"Where is my father?"* Thomas asked, his authority cracking under the weight of his revulsion.

Through the roar of wind and rain, Thomas strained to catch a distant, wavering cry: *"Thomas! Thomas!"* Thrilled by a superstition of sorts, he ascended the stairs, leaving the old woman shaking in the draft below.

At the top, he found a window dripping with torrential rain. Beside it, in the semi-darkness, lay a massive bed. Two eyes, blazing with fever and a desperate fire, locked onto him. *"Thomas! Thomas!"* The moan turned to a shout. Thomas fell to his knees, grasping the wasted hands, forgetting his fatigue and bitterness in a surge of filial instinct.

But the old man did not crave an embrace. He pushed Thomas back into the lamplight, surveying him with a mixture of pain and supreme satisfaction. *"Handsome, strong, noble in look and character..."* he gasped. *"My revenge will succeed! John Poindexter will find that he has a heart, and that that heart can be wrung."*

*"Who is John Poindexter?"* Thomas stammered.

The father's gaze hardened. *"You do not know who he is? They have kept their promise well. Your name is Cadwalader, and it will not remain so forever."*

*"I know I have a brother,"* Thomas whispered. *"His name is Felix."*

*"Not him! Not him!"* the father cried impatiently. *"A sister! A sister who died before you were born—beautiful, good, with a voice like an angel's. She should be standing by my side today, but she is gone because of him."*

Before the father could elaborate on the betrayal that had destroyed his family, a voice cut through the room—smooth, fresh, and strangely commanding.

*"Felix!"* the father breathed.

*"Felix!"* Thomas echoed, shaken. But before he could rise, a hand pressed firmly on his shoulder.

*"Wait,"* the voice commanded softly, yet peremptorily. *"Think not of me, think only of her. It is she you are called upon to avenge; your sister, Evelyn."*

Yielding to the insistent pressure, Thomas sank down as his brother took up the tale.

*"Evelyn had a voice like a bird,"* Felix began. *"She filled old John Poindexter's house with melody. I thought she sang too much for a girl whose father was rotting in a Southern prison. But when I tried to rebuke her, I remembered Edward Kissam, and was silent. For it was his love that made her glad. She was eighteen, beautiful, and pure. Yet, one day, her blue eyes faltered and shrank in terror before me. Something terrible had happened."*

*"What was it?"* Thomas asked.

*"She screamed, 'Our father! Pray God we may never see him or hear from him again. If you love him, if you love me, pray he may die in prison rather than return here to see me as I am now.'"*

Felix described how Evelyn, terrified and pale, refused to speak of her illness or allow anyone—not even her guardian—to enter her room. She declared herself dead to the world except for him. When the disease progressed, Felix sat vigil at her door, listening for the step she dreaded most. Finally, as her life faded like a dying flame, he confronted Poindexter in the hall.

*"Tell him my father is coming,"* Felix said, his voice hardening. *"Tell him I had a dream where I saw my father turning toward this place. Is he prepared to meet him? Does he have an answer ready when Amos Cadwalader asks what has become of his child?"*

A ragged sigh escaped the dying chest of Amos Cadwalader, a sound louder than any spoken word. It spoke of a grief raw and bleeding, an agony that refused to heal despite thirty years having passed since the terrible hour Felix described.

Felix echoed the sentiment, his voice urgent. "It was dusk when I told my tale. From deepening shadow until dawn, we sat motionless, eyes locked upon one another's faces, sleepless and unrested. Then we sought out John Poindexter."

"And how did he receive us?" Thomas asked, leaning forward.

"Had he fled, we might have found mercy in his absence," Felix replied. "But he met us openly, quietly, with the detached indifference of a man who cannot measure emotion because he is incapable of feeling it himself. His opening words revealed this truth immediately: *'Spare yourselves, spare me all useless recriminations. The girl is dead; I cannot call her back. Live your lives—eat, drink, spend, acquire. There are but a few years left anyway. Why dwell on ancient sorrows?'* His final remark was a victory cry: *'When a man cares for nothing and no one, it is futile to curse him.'*"

"That was it!" Amos gasped, clutching his sheets. "That was the secret of his power. He cared for nothing, loved no one—not even himself. We felt the blow strike us down, bending us low. Yet, before we left his house and the town, you and I, Thomas, swore an oath. We vowed that someday, somehow, we would make that cold heart bleed. We promised to cause that impassive nature to suffer exactly as he had made us suffer, regardless of his apparent happiness or the security of his fortune. That was thirty years ago, and the oath remains unfulfilled."

Felix paused. Thomas lifted his head, eager to speak, but the old man silenced him with a sharp gesture.

"Some men forget in a month; others in a year," Amos continued, his voice trembling with feverish intensity. "Neither I nor Felix have ever forgotten. When you were born—I had remarried, seeking renewed joy—I felt, though I knew not why, that Evelyn's avenger had arrived. And when, a year later, we learned that God had seemingly forgiven John Poindexter's sins—or perhaps merely delayed judgment—and granted him a child after eighteen barren years of marriage, I looked upon your beautiful face. In those features, I saw, or believed I saw, the instrument destined to execute the vengeance we had dedicated our lives to achieving."

"You grew," Amos pressed, ignoring Thomas's growing unease. "Your ardent nature, your generous temper, and your quick mind promised a brilliant adulthood. When your mother died, leaving me a widower for the second time, I hesitated no longer. I devoted you entirely to the purpose for which you seemed born. Thomas, do you remember the start of that journey that took you far from me? Do you recall how I carried you on my shoulders along a dusty road? Until we reached the outskirts of his home, I lifted you up, pointed to the distant gables looming black against the twilight gold, and dedicated you to the destruction of whatever happiness might ever bloom beneath that infant's smile?"

Thomas recoiled in horror at the memory. "Father..."

"I did not think you could forget!" Amos interrupted, seizing Thomas's arm with desperate strength. "And now, the moment has come to fulfill that promise. I call upon you again, Thomas. Avenge our griefs. Avenge your sister. *Poindexter's girl has grown to womanhood.*"

At the implication hidden in those words, Thomas flinched as if struck. But the old man misread his terror. Clinging to his arm, he spoke with passionate fervor. "Court her! Win her! They do not know you. To them, you will simply be Thomas Adams, not Thomas Cadwalader. Gather this budding flower to your bosom, and then—oh, a man must love his child! Through her, we hold the key to his heart. Make her suffer. She is but a simple country girl, while you have lived in Paris. Make her suffer, and if, in doing so, you cause him to tremble, then believe me when I tell you I watch over you from the grave I am about to enter. Be happy, for you will not have lived in vain, nor will I have died in vain."

Amos paused, fighting for breath, but his iron will triumphed over mortality, casting a spell that confused Thomas's instincts and turned him into a puppet of accumulated hatred—a rage that had taken his father and brother twenty-five years to forge, yet filled Thomas completely in a single hour.

"I am dying," Amos panted. "I shall never live to see your triumph. I shall never witness John Poindexter's eyes cloud with the agonies that rend the entrails and make a man question the existence of God. But I shall know it in the realm where I am going. No mound of earth can hold my spirit down when John Poindexter feels his doom approaching. I shall be conscious of his anguish and rejoice. And when, in the depths of darkness to which I descend, he comes faltering along my path—"

"Boy, boy, you were reared for this," Amos urged, his voice rising. "God made you handsome; fate has made you strong; you have forged yourself into intelligence and accomplishment. All you must do is reveal yourself to this country girl, and you will become the master of her will and affection. Once hers, remember *me*. Remember *Evelyn!* "

Never before had Thomas witnessed such consuming passion. It swept him along in a torrent of fire against which he struggled uselessly. Raising his hand in what he intended as a response, he tried to speak but choked on his own hesitation. Misinterpreting his silence, the old man cried bitterly, "You are mute! You dislike the task; perhaps you are virtuous—you who have spent years alone and free in Paris. Or perhaps you possess instincts of honor, habits of generosity that blind you to wrongs that have screamed to heaven for justice longer than your entire lifetime. Thomas, Thomas, if you fail me now—"

"He will not fail you," a calm, suave, and insinuating voice cut in.

It was Felix. Thomas shuddered; he had momentarily forgotten his brother, but hearing those words forced him to turn toward the man who had pledged to guarantee the completion of this unholy deed. Standing there was a figure who commanded attention, admiration, and yes, fear. He was not larger than Thomas, but the frenzied will evident on his father's lips manifested instead as quiet, inflexible resolve in the gray eyes fixed upon him. A steel band seemed to constrict Thomas's heart, yet he simultaneously recognized the elegance and perfect training of the man before him. Even the aura of poverty that clung to his father vanished in Felix's presence. Dressed impeccably, Felix stood as the superior elder brother in every regard demanded by a Parisian gentleman. Behind him, a heavy curtain of dark green serge stretched from floor to ceiling, throwing his pale features into stark relief. For an instant, Thomas wondered if the drapery was staged specifically to create this dramatic effect.

But the demand in his brother's face stole his focus. Bowing his head, Thomas stammered, "I am at your command, Felix. I am at your command, Father. I cannot say more. Only remember that I never saw Evelyn; she died before I was born, and that I—"

Felix's voice broke in, kind yet measured. "Perhaps there is an obstacle we have not considered. Perhaps you already love another woman and intend to marry her. If so, it need be no impediment—"

"No!" Thomas erupted, indignation surging through him. "My heart is whole, save for a few fleeting fantasies that are rapidly fading into dreams." He seemed to have aged decades since stepping into the room.

"Your heart will remain undisturbed," Felix noted smoothly. "I visited the girl myself a year ago. She is as pale as a snowdrop and as listless. You will not be required to recall the radiant smiles of Parisian beauties to resist her charms."

"She must be made to taste the full intoxication of hope," Felix continued, his voice clear and cutting. "To realize despair, she must first experience every delight of satisfied love. Do you possess the skill and the heart to play a role requiring patience, dissimulation, courage, subtlety, and that fusion of will and implacability that feeds on tears and grows stronger through the suffering of its victim?"

"I have the skill," Thomas murmured, "but—"

"You lack the incentive," Felix finished.

"Well, well, we must allow patience for your doubts and hesitations," Felix conceded. "Our hate has been nurtured by memories of a girl you claim never to have seen. Look, then, Thomas. Look at your sister as she was, as she is to us. Look at her, and imagine her despoiled, murdered, and forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever gazed upon a more moving countenance, or one where beauty fights a sharper prophecy of woe?"

Uncertain of what awaited him, fearing he might meet her ghost, Thomas followed the direction of his brother's raised hand. Where moments prior the dismal curtain had blocked the view, there now burned a blaze of light. Within it stood a charming yet tragic figure, such as no gallery in all Europe had ever displayed

Felix had not inherited his father's inability to generate wealth. Over the span of twenty years, since Thomas departed for Europe, he had amassed a fortune. While he refused to share this accumulation with his aging father, he was entirely content to see it expended on their shared vendetta. This financial backing explained the stern instruction Felix delivered to his brother before departing for Montgomery:

"Spend the money; spend whatever you choose. When your mission concludes, there will still be funds remaining for your leisure."

Thomas nodded solemnly. *The laborer is worthy of his hire,* he thought internally. Turning his gaze around the sparse, empty walls—which now seemed to lack any justification for existing since his father's passing—he asked, "And you? Will you stay here?"

Felix's reply was curt yet absolute. "No. I depart for New York tomorrow. I have secured a residence there, which you may eventually wish to inhabit. Under the alias 'Adams, Felix Adams,' I have leased it. Address me thus in Montgomery. The name 'Cadwalader' must never leave your lips in this town, nor must you forget that my identity is widely known there; otherwise, our reliance on your efforts for the success of our revenge would be compromised." A smile played on his lips, revealing his full awareness that he possessed the superior handsomeness of the two brothers. "Now, regarding the introductions from Paris that we instructed you to secure..."

The narrative of the ensuing weeks is best illuminated by examining the correspondence Thomas sent to Felix alongside a private journal maintained by Thomas for his own introspection. The letters, presented in the left column, offer a factual summary of events. The entries, in the right column, chronicle Thomas's internal emotional landscape. Both perspectives are essential to understanding the shifting dynamics of the situation.

*I am here; I have seen her. As you predicted, she is a pale blonde. Tomorrow, I shall present my credentials to John Poindexter. Based on my initial interactions, I anticipate a favorable reception.*

*I could not write the true account of today's encounter to Felix. Why? And why must I record it here? Perhaps to distract my mind from dwelling on it? Maybe. I struggle to comprehend my own feelings, torn between a growing dread of my task and an ardent drive to complete it.*

Surely, Felix would have been stronger. Yet...*

*I traveled from the train station directly to the cemetery. Before attending to my lodging or settling in, I sought Evelyn's grave. It was the place where, nineteen years prior, my father had raised me—a four-year-old child—holding me aloft as a threat toward John Poindexter's home. I managed to locate the old, neglected stone marking her resting place. Bending in the golden sunset light to inspect it, I was startled by the rustle of a woman's skirt. A young girl approached, bathed in a nimbus of rosy light that seemed to elevate her from the earth, lending an ethereal quality to her delicate frame and strangely illuminated hair, a radiance her pure features and tender demeanor confirmed. Clutched in her arms was a massive bouquet of snow-white lilies. Noticing her eyes were fixed not on me, but on the grave beside which I stood, I stepped into the shadow of nearby bushes to observe her as she scattered these flowers—symbols of innocence—over the mound I had just visited.*

*What did this signify? Who was this young girl honoring the grave of my long-buried sister with such graceful tribute? As she rose from her task, I could no longer suppress my emotion or the intense curiosity her actions had sparked. Stepping forward, I greeted her with the utmost respect appropriate to her appearance. Observing that her countenance was even more radiant when lifted in conversation than when bowed in solemnity over her blooms, I asked her, in the detached tone of a stranger, who lay buried here and why a girl of her youth would drop flowers upon a grave whose moss-covered stone indicated it had been opened decades before her birth.*

*Her response struck me with the force of a thunderclap, amidst a life recently filled with startling revelations. "I strew flowers here," she replied, "because the girl interred beneath this stone shares my birthday. I never saw her, it is true, but she perished in my father's house when she was no older than I am today. Since becoming a woman and realizing the tragedy of dying young, I have made it a custom to share my birthday blossoms with her. They say she was a lily in both appearance and character, so I bring her lilies."*

The remainder of this harrowing sequence must be recounted in Thomas's own voice, for it constitutes the core of his confession to the detectives.

"As I promised, I took my young wife to Felix's residence on the agreed day and hour. We walked, as the location was not distant from our hotel. Eva, to whom I had revealed little of this brother, certainly nothing suggesting she would encounter a man of such striking handsomeness or such profound mental poise, looked terrified and awestruck. Did I truly hope to sway him? Perhaps a childish notion had clouded my judgment up to this point. Nothing visible suggested treachery, yet a primal distrust (the first I had genuinely felt) rose within me, triggered by his gaze sweeping the mosque-like interior of his study. The startled young man hesitated, dreading the question he feared to voice. Like everyone else present, he knew Bartow had performed specific gestures attempting to describe the crime, yet he remained unaware of their nature, as this crucial detail had been deliberately withheld by the police. Consequently, he did not react immediately to the suggestion. Instead, he pondered intensely for a moment before extending his left hand, snatching an object from the inspector's table, and lunging across his body toward an imaginary adversary on his right.

He then scanned the faces around him with profound anxiety, finding little reassurance in their expressions.

"Now I have watched her since she entered this place," Mr. Adams replied earnestly. "I have seen no evidence of this."

"She is not left-handed," Mr. Adams continued, his voice trembling slightly. "But she thrust with her left hand because her right was held fast in mine. I had seized her instinctively as she leaped forward for the weapon, and the convulsive grip of our two hands was not released until the horror of her deed caused her to faint. She fell away from me to the floor, crying: 'Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother's breast. I would at least see him die the death of a Christian.'"

Mr. Gryce glanced at the inspector with an air of immense relief. The mystery surrounding the constrained position of the right hand—which had rendered Bartow's pantomime so baffling—was now naturally resolved. Picking up the blue pencil the inspector had set down, Mr. Gryce smiled and wrote a decisive "answered" across paragraph number nine.

A short while later, Mr. Gryce could be observed standing in the outer room, his gaze drifting curiously over the assembled crowd. His mind was still troubled by an unanswered question, and he hoped the gathering might provide the solution. He need not have waited long. In a quiet corner stood Miss Butterworth, her demeanor calm and composed, clearly anticipating the outcome of the intervention she had undoubtedly orchestrated.

Gryce approached her with a smile, gently accusing her of engineering the situation. She immediately denied the charge, though with some warmth.

"I was merely present," she explained. "When the crisis arrived—when this young lady discovered her husband had abruptly departed for New York accompanied by two men—it became evident to everyone that she required a confidante who understood both her plight and the desperation of her position. That role fell to me."

"You are always the woman in the right place at the right time," Gryce replied gallantly. "So, you are already familiar with Mrs. Adams' account?"

"Yes," she confirmed. "Her initial ramblings made it clear she was the one who wielded the dagger that killed Mr. Adams. "Madam," Gryce assured her, "there are not twelve men in this city so lacking in common sense as to label an act of obvious self-defense as 'crime.' No grand jury will return a true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You may rest easy."

The gloom vanished instantly from Miss Butterworth's eyes. "Then I may return home in peace!" she exclaimed. "These past five hours have been a trial by fire, and I feel thoroughly rattled. Shall you see me to my carriage?"

In Gryce's estimation, Miss Butterworth looked far from rattled; indeed, she appeared more serene and comfortable than ever before. Yet, she was the ultimate arbiter of her own state. Satisfied that the person she cared for was recovering under her husband's attentive care, she accepted the arm Gryce extended and moved toward the carriage.

As he helped her inside, he posed a few inquiries regarding Mr. Poindexter. "Where is Mrs. Adams' father? Did he permit his daughter to embark on such a perilous mission without offering to accompany her?"

The response was curt. "Mr. Poindexter is a man without a heart. He traveled with us to New York but refused to come to Police Headquarters. Sir, consider that the combined fury of three passionate souls—and a vengeance deeper than any I have ever known or heard of—has been squandered on a man utterly incapable of suffering. Do not speak of old John Poindexter to me. Now, if you will kindly instruct the driver to take me home to Gramercy Park, I shall inter my finger in the affairs of the police force once and for all, Mr. Gryce—positively, for the final time."

She sank back onto the carriage cushions, wearing an expression of inexorable resolve. Nevertheless, beneath this stern facade lay a quiet satisfaction, suggesting she was neither entirely displeased with herself nor unhappy with the results of her meddling in matters that typically clashed with the instincts of a refined woman.

Mr. Gryce suppressed a smile, bowing lower than usual. Under the shadow of this deep bow, the carriage rolled away. Walking slowly back, he let out a sigh. Perhaps he wondered if a case of such compelling interest would ever bring them together again in consultation?
