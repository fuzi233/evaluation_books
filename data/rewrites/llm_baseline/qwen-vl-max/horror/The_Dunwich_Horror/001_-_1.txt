When a traveler in north central Massachusetts takes the wrong turn at the fork of the Aylesbury pike just past Dean’s Corners, they enter a land that feels untouched by time—and perhaps by reason. The road climbs slowly, flanked by stone walls overgrown with brambles, their edges pressing inward like fingers closing around a secret. Dust clings to the ruts, and the trees lining the forested stretches loom too tall, their branches forming canopies that dim the daylight even in mid-afternoon. The wild grasses grow thick and unruly, as if nature itself refuses to be tamed here. Fields are few and thin, the soil stubbornly barren, while the scattered houses stand hunched and broken, their wooden frames sagging under decades of neglect. They all look alike—crumbling, gray, weathered into a kind of uniform decay.

There are people, but not many. When you see them, they’re still—gnarled figures perched on cracked porches or bent over fields strewn with stones, their eyes avoiding yours. They don’t speak. Not to strangers. And something about their silence feels deliberate, almost *warning*. You don’t ask for directions. You don’t stop. You just keep moving.

Then the hills rise up—round, smooth, unnaturally symmetrical. They seem too perfect, too quiet. At dusk, when the sky is clear, you might notice the rings of standing stones crowning their summits, ancient circles of rough-hewn granite that shouldn’t belong to any known culture. The gorges cut deep into the earth, black mouths swallowing light, and the bridges across them are crude, splintered things that groan underfoot. You cross them only because there’s no other way.

Beyond, marshlands stretch out, flat and wet, where fireflies blink in unnatural numbers, dancing above the reeds like tiny, restless spirits. At night, bullfrogs chant in a rhythm that doesn’t sound natural—too steady, too insistent. Whippoorwills cry from unseen places, their calls echoing long after they should have stopped. And the Miskatonic River winds through it all, narrow and silver, snaking between the hills like a living thing.

Eventually, the road leads to a covered bridge. On the other side lies Dunwich—a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs huddled between the river and the sheer face of Round Mountain. The church steeple is broken, its bell tower missing; now it houses the village store, one grimy window lit late into the evening. Most of the homes are abandoned, their windows boarded, their doors hanging loose. There’s a smell here—something faint but persistent, like damp earth and old mold, or worse: the scent of things left buried too long.

You pass through quickly. The air grows heavier. The shadows deepen. Even the silence feels watchful.

Afterward, you learn—maybe from a passing farmer, maybe from a dusty map—you’ve been through Dunwich. But no one talks about it. No signs point toward it anymore. It’s not on the maps. People avoid it, not because they know what’s there, but because they *feel* it. Something wrong. Something older than memory.

The land is beautiful, yes—by any ordinary measure. But beauty here doesn’t comfort. It unsettles. Two centuries ago, folks said it was cursed. That witches lived in the woods, that the Indians held rites atop those stone circles, calling up things from beneath the hills. They spoke of voices heard underground—names like Azazel, Beelzebub—of groans and hissing that couldn’t come from this world. In 1747, a preacher named Abijah Hoadley stood in Dunwich’s church and declared he’d heard such sounds himself—“a Rattling and Rolling, Groaning, Screeching, and Hissing,” he wrote, “such as no Things of this Earth cou’d raise up.” He vanished shortly after. His sermon survives, printed in Springfield. But no one reads it aloud.

The noises continue. Geologists shrug them off. But locals know better. They say the whippoorwills aren’t birds—they’re psychopomps, soul-hunters who sing in time with dying breaths. If they catch the spirit as it flees, they fly away chittering with laughter. If not, they fall silent—disappointed.

And the hills… the hills are always watching.

Dunwich is old. Older than the rest of New England. South of the village, you can still see the cellar walls of the Bishop house, built before 1700. The mill at the falls, constructed in 1806, is the newest structure in sight. Industry never took root. The town didn’t progress. It regressed.

The people? They’ve changed. Not just in appearance—though their faces are sharp, their eyes hollow—but in spirit. They’ve become something apart. Inbred, dull, twisted by generations of isolation and secrets. Some families—Whateleys, Bishops—still send sons to Harvard or Miskatonic University. But they rarely return. Those who do… well, they don’t stay long.

No one knows exactly what happened in 1928. But it was bad enough that the truth was buried. The town’s name was silenced. The roads were erased.

Yet legends persist. Of forbidden rites beneath the stone circles. Of rituals performed at midnight, when the stars align just so. Of shapes that crawl from the hills when the moon is full and the wind carries the scent of decay.

And sometimes, late at night, if you listen closely near the ravines, you hear it—not a sound, but a *presence*: rushing air, whispering voices, the faintest echo of a drumbeat from deep below the earth.

It’s not just a place.

It’s a wound. And it’s still bleeding.
