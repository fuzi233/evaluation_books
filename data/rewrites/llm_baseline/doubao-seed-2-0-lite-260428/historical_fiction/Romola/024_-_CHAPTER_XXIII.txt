“You are far too quick to take fright, you know,” Piero jeered, barking another scornful laugh as he tapped the edge of his half-finished portrait sketch. “My little drawing of that old wild fellow doesn’t do his glare justice, you see. He had the very look of a caged tiger. I must slip into the Duomo later today to stare at him again, just to fix the face properly in my memory.”
“It’s no pleasant thing to be seized by a raving madman, if madman he truly is,” Lorenzo Tornabuoni put in gently, offering Tito a gracious out for his momentary fluster. “Though more like a common ruffian, for all we know. We’ll hear more of the story soon enough. I say, we ought to step over and see if our authority carries enough weight to quiet this brawl between our townsfolk and your countrymen,” he added, turning to the French captain standing nearby.
They advanced toward the crowd with drawn swords, all the quiet onlookers falling in behind them as an informal escort, and Tito trailed along too. He knew he could not afford to miss a single scrap of information the rest of Florence would soon learn about Baldassarre, and the first icy paralysis of terror had already melted away, replaced by the slippery, quick-witted ingenuity mortal danger stirs even in the most timid of men.
The rabble of rough artisans and jeering boys, far keener to hoot at the French soldier and tease him than to land or suffer any serious blow, scattered at the sight of well-known signori marching toward them with naked steel. The Frenchman was soon questioned: he and his men had simply marched their prisoners back into Florence to beg alms for their ransoms. Two of the captives were Tuscan conscripts taken in the Lunigiana hills; the third, the raving old man, had been travelling with a party of Genoese traders the French foragers had clashed with outside Fivizzano. He might be deranged, the soldier shrugged, but he’d done no one any harm—he could not even make out a single word the old man shrieked, let alone follow his speech.
Tito heard every word of this, but tuned out all the rest of the chatter around him until Tornabuoni addressed him directly.
“Will you walk back with us, Melema?” Lorenzo asked. “Or, since our party is riding out to Signa shortly, would you not do the sensible thing, as is all the fashion these days, and head to hear the Frate preach? They say he’s like a swollen torrent come down off the hills this morning, his sermon thundering loud enough to shake the rafters. We all have to make an appearance there sooner or later, you know, if we want to keep our Medicean skins intact. I’d go myself if I had the leisure for it.”
The colour had flooded back into Tito’s cheeks by then, and he forced himself to laugh easily. “I am of course one of the great orator’s many admirers,” he said, smiling bright as ever. “But unfortunately, I am bound to meet the Segretario Scala long before the procession is due to start.”
“I’m heading straight to the Duomo to gawk at that savage old man again,” Piero cut in.
“Then do us all a charity, Piero mio,” Tornabuoni said dryly. “Take him to the travellers’ hospital on your way. The monks there can work out if the poor creature needs to be locked away somewhere safe.”
The party split ways soon after, and Tito turned his steps toward the Palazzo Vecchio, where he was due to meet Bartolommeo Scala. It was no long walk down the sun-warmed stone streets of Florence, but to Tito it stretched endless, each minute dragging slow as the half-asleep stretches of a morning dream. The short spans of cobblestone lane and open piazza were crammed full of half-forgotten memories, dark premonitions, and torturing fears heavy enough to fill the story of months.
He felt as if a cold serpent had begun coiling itself tight around his limbs. Baldassarre alive, here in Florence, was a living, breathing vengeance that would never rest, no more than a winding serpent would pause until it had crushed its prey in its coils. That man was never the sort to overlook a betrayal, to let a great wrong pass unpunished. His love and his hatred burned with the same wild, unquenchable fervour that consumed every other part of his being, that would drive a man to sacrifice his very self to his passion as if it were a god to be worshipped through self-ruin.
Baldassarre had loosened his grip and vanished. Tito knew exactly what that meant: the vengeance was being planned, plotted carefully, to make certain it struck true.
If only he had not blurted out those fateful words—“He is a madman”—if only he had summoned the courage, the clear-headedness, to admit he knew who Baldassarre was, would the risk have been far smaller? He could have sworn he had had every reason to believe Baldassarre had died at sea, back when the pirates attacked their galley. The only people who could have proved that a lie were Fra Luca, who was long dead, and the crew of the companion galley that had brought him the news of the pirate attack. The odds were infinitesimal that Baldassarre had ever run into a single one of those sailors. Tito’s chest ached with bitter regret: a timely, well-spun lie right then could have spared him every fatal consequence. But to tell that lie he would have needed perfect calm at the very moment he was convulsed with shock. The words had leaped out of his mouth unbidden, like a thing born sudden out of the dark, unplanned.
Tito was learning the unforgiving law of the human soul: that every small, repeated choice for good or ill builds the person you become, and you do not get to choose your reactions in a crisis out of nowhere.
There was only one sliver of hope left for him now: that Baldassarre would fail in his quest for vengeance. And then a thought, crueller than any he had ever dared entertain before, flashed bright in his mind. Could the unplanned words he had blurted out earlier hold a grain of truth? Enough truth, at least, to back him up when he denied every wild claim Baldassarre might make against him? The old man looked wild, his eyes blazing, his clothes ragged. With that fierce heart and overworked brain, it was more than possible that years of suffering had unhinged his wits. If that were the case, the vengeance that sought to drag Tito’s name through the mud would fail entirely.
But there was another kind of vengeance that no clever lie could fend off. Baldassarre was of a temperament where a knife-thrust came as naturally as a tiger lashing out with its claws. Tito shuddered, sick with dread of public disgrace—but he also carried that soft, physical fear inherent to a man who loves ease and pleasure, that makes him recoil from wounds and death far more than he could ever welcome them as an escape from shame. His mind darted at once to the idea of hidden defensive armour, something he could wear under his tunic to shield him from a blow his wits could never talk their way out of. He was stunned by the raw, consuming power of the fear that held him. It was as if he had been struck by some blighting illness that had turned all the bright, joyful ease of his young life to ash and pain.
There was still one way out, of course. He could turn back, hunt Baldassarre down, confess everything to him—to Romola—to all the city. But the thought never even crossed his mind. That kind of repentance, that cuts every last tie to old lies and old cowardice, demands far more than selfish fear. Tito had never learned to see truth as a source of strength and safety. The only strength he had ever trusted was his own quick wit, his own talent for dissembling. Now that the first shock that had made his fear so plain for everyone to see had passed, he was certain he could outmaneuver every threat with cool, calculated deception—and a hidden layer of steel to protect his body.
It was entirely typical of Tito’s state of mind in this crisis that no scheme to actively kill or get rid of Baldassarre ever occurred to him. He ran through every other possibility, even the idea of fleeing Florence entirely, but he never once thought of arranging his enemy’s death. His fear did not curdle into active, cruel malice; he would still have been glad not to cause any unnecessary pain to any living soul. He had simply always chosen to make his own life as easy as possible, to carry the weight of his human lot in such a way that no part of it ever pinched or discomforted him. That repeated small choice had landed him in all kinds of unforeseen messes before now. The question he faced now was not whether he could share the common weight of fate with all his suffering fellow men. It was whether all the cleverness and all the lies he had spent years perfecting could save him from being crushed by the consequences of that lifelong habit of choosing the easy way out.
