The public reception was done at last.

The sentence had been spoken out, the Empress Phorenice named with all the regulated ardor that ancient custom required, and the new Viceroy set in place beneath a roof heavy with age and gilding. I had, in form, delivered up the reins. Tatho had, in form, seated himself upon the snake-throne, and bent his neck to receive the chain of gems that meant supreme office. Then the hall—bright as a brazier’s mouth with gold tile and polished metal—filled with its appointed violence of sound: drums that struck like fists on a door, trumpets whose blare made the roof-beams shiver, and the roar of voices crying, as in duty bound, “Long Live Tatho!” “Flourish the Empress!”

He rose for his first progress around the council-chamber, and I followed behind him with folded arms and lowered head, as the ritual demanded. Twenty years ago I had walked this same circuit to the same clamoring music; now fortune had turned its face. It is a hard schooling, learning to carry a passionless mask when one’s place is taken before a crowd. Yet for all that etiquette forbade any cry for me, it could not forbid the eyes. Along the glittering lines of soldiers, courtiers, chiefs, and statesmen, kindness flickered—quick glances that warmed and stung in the same breath. It is not often the fallen are met with tenderness.

On these ceremonial days the people present may bring petitions; may accuse the retiring head without fear of vengeance; may even offer their own plans for future governance. Vanity may be forgiven me this once: not a voice was raised against my twenty years of rule, nor did any call for change. We made the circuit three times as the rite prescribes, and their silence was approval.

So, one behind the other—the new Viceroy and the old—we marched over the golden tiles toward the farther wall, and the great officers of state left their stations to join our train. We came to the door of the private chambers which an hour ago had been mine.

Ah, well. I had no home now in any of Yucatan’s wondrous cities. A man should be thankful enough to be permitted to sail back to Atlantis with his head still on his shoulders, and yet bitterness rose in me like smoke.

Tatho gave the formal summons, “Open ye to the Viceroy,” and slaves within swung the massive stone valves wide. He entered; I followed at his heels. The others halted at the threshold, sending valedictions like incense, and then the stone clanged shut behind us.

We passed into the inner chamber, and for the first time the court’s forced breathing was behind us. Tatho turned at once, folded his arms meekly, and bowed low before me.

“Deucalion,” he said, “believe me—I have not sought this office. It was thrust upon me. Had I not accepted, my head would have paid forfeit, and another man—your enemy—would have been sent in your place. The Empress does not permit her will to be questioned.”

“My friend,” I answered, “my brother in all but blood, there is no man living in Atlantis or her territories to whom I would rather hand over my government. For twenty years I have ruled Yucatan, and Mexico beyond—first for the old King, then for this new Empress. I know the colony as a man knows the lines of his own palm: her cities, her palaces, her pyramids, her people. I have hunted beasts and savages in the forests. I have built roads and tamed rivers to bear shipping. I have fostered arts and crafts like a merchant, and three times each day have spoken the cult of the Gods with my own lips. Through lean years and fat years I have labored for the land’s prosperity and Atlantis’ strengthening, and I have come to love these peoples like a father. To you I bequeath them, Tatho, with tender supplication for their interests.”

“It is not I who can carry on Deucalion’s work with Deucalion’s power,” he said, “but rest content that I shall do my humble best to follow in your footsteps. Believe me, I came out with a thousand regrets. I would have died sooner than take your place had I known how sharply the supplanting would trouble you.”

“We are alone here,” I said. “A man may show his natural self without tarnishing ceremony. Your coming was sudden. Till an hour ago—till you demanded audience—I thought to rule longer. Even now I do not know why I am deposed.”

“The proclamation said,” Tatho replied, “‘We relieve our well-beloved Deucalion of his present service, because we have great need of his powers at home in our kingdom of Atlantis.’”

“A mere formality,” I said.

He looked uneasily toward the hangings, as though he expected the cloth to grow ears, and drew me toward the center of the chamber. His voice sank.

“I do not think so,” he whispered. “I believe she has need of you. Troublous times are coming, and Phorenice wants the ablest men in the kingdom ready to her call.”

“You may speak openly,” I told him. “We are in the heart of the pyramid, built by a man’s length of solid stone. I oversaw the laying of every course. Besides, in Yucatan we do not make a fine art of listening at doors. We count it shame.”

He shrugged. “I act according to my education. At home, a loose tongue makes a loose head, and there are those who trade in tales. Still—what I say is this: the throne shakes, and Phorenice sees the need of sturdy props. So she has sent this proclamation.”

“But why come to me?” I demanded. “It is twenty years since I sailed to this colony, and from that day I have not returned to Atlantis once. I know little of the old country’s politics. The scraps of news that drift to us over the ocean read thinly here. Yucatan is another world, Tatho—new interests, new people, new everything. To us Atlantis is a shadow far across water. It is for this new world I have striven all these years.”

“If you have had small leisure to brood over your fatherland,” he said, “Atlantis has had leisure to admire her brilliant son. Over yonder your name carries magic. When you and I were lads, colleges taught that the men of the past were greatest; to-day that teaching is changed. It is Deucalion they hold up as model. Mothers name their sons Deucalion as the best birth-gift they can give. Your name is a household word. Indeed there is only one name that approaches it in familiarity.”

“You trouble me,” I said, frowning. “I have tried to do duty for its own sake, not for the pattings of the vulgar. And if names must be on men’s mouths, let them be the Gods’.”

“The Gods?” Tatho said, and his shrug was half weary, half mocking. “They occupy us very little these latter years. With our modern science we have gone past the tether of the older Gods, and no new one has appeared. No, my Lord Deucalion—if it were merely Gods you competed with, your name would be a thousand times the better known.”

“Of human names,” I said, “the Empress’ should come first, now the old King is dead.”

“She would have it so,” Tatho replied—and something in his tone showed there was more behind the words than politeness could carry.

I drew him to one of the marble seats and leaned toward him, familiarly, as we had leaned together over a hundred hard cases in our early postings, when a hay-byre’s ownership might take as much law and sweat as the fate of a rebellious town does now.

“I speak,” I said, “not to the Viceroy of Yucatan, but to my old friend Tatho—of the Priests’ Clan as I am—who worked beside me in hamlets and villages, in lesser towns and greater, as we learned war and the tedious art of ruling men. I speak in your private abode that was mine two hours ago. I would have an answer with the plainness we used then.”

He sighed, almost whimsically. “I almost forget how to speak plain words. We have grown so polished that bald truth would be hissed as indelicate. But for those early years’ sake, I will try. Tell me, old friend—what is it?”

“What of this new Empress?” I asked. “Tell me of the changes. What has Phorenice done to make the throne unstable?”

His frown deepened. “If I did not know you to be honest as our Lord the Sun, your questions would carry mischief. Phorenice has a short way with those who discuss her policies for any purpose but praise.”

“You can leave me ignorant,” I said, letting chill touch my voice.

He saw it at once. “You force me back to my old self,” he said with a half smile. “Though it is hard to cast off caution learned in twenty years. Still—whatever has happened to the rest of us, you at least have not changed. And I am ready to trust you with my life if you ask it. Indeed, you do ask me that when you bid me speak all I know of Phorenice.”

I nodded. The old confidence returned like a familiar cadence.

“The Gods will it that I return to Atlantis,” I said, “and what follows the Gods alone know. But it would serve me to land with some knowledge of Phorenice. At present I am as ignorant of her as some savage from Europe or mid-Africa.”

“What would you have me tell?”

“Tell all. I know only this—that a woman reigns where ancient law said a man should rule; that she is not of the Priests’ Clan from which rulers must be drawn; and that, from what you hint, she has made the throne totter. In the old King’s day it stood firm as the everlasting hills.”

“History has moved with pace since then,” said Tatho, “and Phorenice has spurred it. You know her origin?”

“I know only what I have said.”

“She was a swineherd’s daughter from the mountains,” he said. “This is never whispered now. She has declared herself a daughter of the Gods with miraculous birth and upbringing. She has decreed it sacrilege to question it, and has ordered burnt all those who seem to recollect her earthly origin. The fable passes current for truth. You see what faith I put in you, Deucalion, by telling you this.”

“There has always been trust between us.”

“I know. Yet suspicion becomes a habit, and habits cling. Listen further. Zaemon—you remember—was governor of the swineherd’s province. Zaemon’s wife saw the girl and took her away to adopt and bring up as her own. It is said the swineherd and his woman objected. Perhaps they did. In any case I know they died. Phorenice was taught arts and graces, and raised as a daughter of the Priests’ Clan.”

“But adopted only,” I protested.

“The omission of the word ‘adopted’ was her will early,” Tatho said dryly, “and she learned early to have her will made fact. Before she was fifteen she ruled not only the women of the household, but Zaemon also—and the province beyond Zaemon.”

“Zaemon was learned and devout,” I said. “A searcher into higher mysteries. But as ruler he was always flabby.”

“I do not deny opportunities served her,” said Tatho, “but genius served her too. For a woman to raise herself at all from what she was is remarkable. Not one in a thousand, placed as she was, would have become more than some sturdy countryman’s wife, with no one caring of pedigree. But Phorenice—she took it into her head to train like a man-at-arms, to practice with the utensils of war; and then, before any quite knows how, a rebellion broke out, and there she was—a slip of a girl—leading Zaemon’s troops.”

“Zaemon in the field was a derision.”

“Hear me. She put down the rebellion masterfully, and gave the conquered their choice between sword and service. They fell into her ranks at once and were faithful from that moment. There is a marvellous fascination about the woman.”

“Her historian seems to feel it,” I said, letting the barb show.

“Of course I have,” he replied without shame. “Every one who sees her comes under her spell. Frankly, I am in love with her too, and look upon my coming here as detestable exile. All near her—high and low—love her, even though they know it may be her whim to send them to execution the next minute.”

I suppose contempt showed in my face.

“You despise our weakness?” he said. “You were always strong, Deucalion.”

“At least,” I said, “you see me unmarried. I have found no time to palter with women’s fripperies.”

“Ah,” he laughed softly, “but these colonists here are crude. Wait till you see the ladies of the court, my ascetic.”

“It occurs to me,” I said, “that I lived in Atlantis before I came out here, and saw as much of court life as most men. Yet then also I felt no inducement to marry.”

“Atlantis has changed so you would hardly know her,” Tatho said. “A new era has come over everything—especially the other sex. I remember the women of the old King’s time—how monstrous uncomely, how they knew nothing of walking or carrying themselves, how barbaric their notion of dress. I dare swear your ladies here in Yucatan are not so provincial now as ours were then. But you should see them today at home. They are delicious. And above all in charm is the Empress. Oh, Deucalion—you shall see Phorenice in her beauty and magnificence soon, and you will go down on your knees and repent.”

“I may see,” I said, “and, because you say so, I may alter. The Gods make all things possible. But for the present I remain celibate and not wishful otherwise. Continue your history.”

“It is a long story of success,” he said. “She deposed Zaemon from his government in name as well as in fact. The news spread, and the Priests’ Clan rose in wrath. Two neighboring governors were ordered to join forces, take her, and bring her for execution. Poor men—they tried. They attacked her surely enough, but in battle she laughed at them. She killed both and made slaughter among their troops. To those who remained alive and became her prisoners she made her usual offer—the sword or service. Naturally they chose quickly. To common people one ruler is much like another. So her army was reinforced again.

“Three times bodies of soldiery were sent against her, and three times she was victorious. The last was a final effort. Before, it had been the custom to despise this adventuress. But then the priests began to realize their peril—to see the throne itself in danger—and to know that if she were to be crushed they must put forth their utmost. Every man who could carry arms was pressed. Every known art of war was ordered into employment. It was the largest, best-equipped army Atlantis had then ever raised, and the Priests’ Clan put in supreme command their general—Tatho.”

“You!” I cried.

“Even myself,” he said. “And mark you, I fought my utmost. I was not her creature then. When I set out—because they wished to spur me—the High Council pointed out my prospects. The King we had known so long was ailing and old, wrapped in mysteries till earthly matters nauseated him. At any time he might decide to die. The Priests’ Clan chooses the new ruler, but it notes popular sentiment; and a general who came home victorious from a great campaign, freeing a harassed people from constant war, would be the idol of the moment. These things were told me solemnly in full council.”

“They promised you the throne?”

“Even that. So I went with a high stake. Phorenice I had never seen, and I swore to take her alive and give her to be sport for my soldiery. I had great confidence in my strategy then. But the old Gods I trusted remained old and taught me nothing new. I drilled my army by the forms you and I learned together; I armed them with the choicest weapons we knew—sling and mace, bow and spear, axe and knife, sword and throwing fire. I covered their bodies with metal plates. Even their bellies I cared for, driving droves of cattle behind the fighting troops.

“But when the encounter came, they might have been men of straw for all the harm they did. Out of her own brain Phorenice had made fire-tubes that cast a dart which would kill beyond two bowshots. The way she handled her troops dazzled me. They threatened one flank, harried the other. It was not war as we had been used to. It was a newer and deadlier game. I watched my splendid army eaten away as waves eat a sandhill. Never once did I get the chance to force close action. These tactics—born of her invention—were beyond my art. We were eight to her one, and our close-packed numbers only made us easier slaughter. Panic came, and those who could fled. I had no wish to go back and earn the axe that waits for the unsuccessful general. I tried to die where I stood. But death would not come. It was a fine melee, Deucalion, that last one.”

“And so she took you?”

“I stood with three others, back to back, with a ring of dead around us and a ring of the enemy hemming us in. We taunted them to come on. But in hand-to-hand they had learned we could hold our own, and they called for fire-tubes to strike us down from a distance. Then up came Phorenice.

“‘What is this to-do?’ she says.

“‘We seek to kill Lord Tatho, who led against you,’ say they.

“‘So that is Tatho?’ says she. ‘A fine figure of a man, and a pretty fighter after the old manner. Doubtless he is one who would acquire the newer method. See now, Tatho—my custom is to offer those I vanquish either the sword (which, believe me, was never nearer your neck than now) or service under my banner. Will you choose?’

“‘Woman,’ I said, ‘fairest I have ever seen, finest general the world has ever borne—you tempt me sorely. But there is tradition in our Clan that we should be true to the salt we eat. I am the King’s man still, and I can take no service from you.’

“‘The King is dead,’ says she. ‘A runner has just brought the tidings, meaning them for your hands. And I am the Empress.’

“‘Who made you Empress?’ I asked.

“‘The same most capable hand that has given me this battle,’ says she. ‘It can be a kind hand also, as you may learn if you choose. With the King dead, Tatho is masterless. Is Tatho in want of a mistress?’

“‘Such a mistress as you,’ I said, ‘yes.’

“And from that moment, Deucalion, I have been her slave. You may frown. You may rise and walk away. But keep back your harshest judgment till after you have seen Phorenice yourself—in the warm and lovely flesh. Then your own senses will plead for me, and win back, perhaps, your old esteem.”
