Madame Beck was a figure of remarkable consistency: forbearing toward everyone, yet tender toward no one. Even her own children failed to draw her from her stoic composure. While she was deeply solicitous regarding their futures and vigilant over their physical welfare, she possessed no desire to hold them close, to press their rosy lips against her own, or to envelop them in a warm embrace. I have watched her sit in the garden, observing the tiny bees wandering a distant path alongside Trinette, their *bonne*. In her demeanor, one read only care and prudence. I knew she often fretted anxiously about "their future"; yet, if the youngest, a frail but charming child, happened to catch sight of her, broke free from its nurse, and toddled eagerly and pantingly to clasp her knee, Madame would simply extend one hand to gently halt the child's rush. "Take care, my dear!" she would say, unmoved. She would allow the child to stand near her for a moment, offering no smile, no kiss, no affectionate word, before rising calmly to lead it back to Trinette.

Her treatment of the eldest girl was equally characteristic, though different in kind. This was a vicious child. "What a pest this Désirée is! What poison this child is!" were the exclamations reserved for her, echoing from the kitchen to the classroom. Among her many talents, she boasted an exquisite skill in the art of provocation, occasionally driving her *bonne* and the servants nearly mad. She would sneak into their attics, rummage through their drawers and boxes, wantonly tear their finest caps and soil their best shawls. Seizing opportunities, she would raid the buffet in the dining room, smashing porcelain and glass, or infiltrate the storeroom cupboard to plunder preserves, drink sweet wines, and shatter jars, all while cleverly shifting the blame onto the cook and the maid. Whenever Madame witnessed these acts, or received reports of them, her sole response, delivered with matchless serenity, was: "Désirée needs particularly special supervision." Consequently, she kept this promising olive branch constantly at her side. To my knowledge, she never once explained the nature of Désirée's faults to the child, detailing the evils of such habits or the consequences they would inevitably bring. Surveillance, she believed, was the complete cure. Of course, it failed. Désirée was kept somewhat away from the servants, but she proceeded to tease and pilfer from her mother instead. Whatever belonged to Madame's worktable or toilet that she could reach, she stole and concealed. Madame saw all this but pretended not to; she lacked the rectitude of soul to confront the child directly with her vices. When an item vanished, valuable enough to require restitution, Madame would feign ignorance, suggesting Désirée had taken it merely in play, and beg her to return it. Désirée was not so easily duped; she had learned to use falsehood to support theft and would deny touching the brooch, ring, or scissors. Maintaining this hollow charade, the mother would calmly pretend to believe the denial, then relentlessly track the child until she found her hiding spots—some hole in the garden wall, some crack in the garret or outbuilding. Once located, Madame would send Désirée out for a walk with her *bonne*, using the child's absence to retrieve the stolen goods herself. Désirée proved herself the true daughter of her astute parent by showing not the slightest sign of mortification when discovered.

The second child, Fifine, was said to resemble her deceased father. Certainly, though the mother had bestowed upon her a healthy frame, blue eyes, and ruddy cheeks, her moral character was not derived from her. Fifine was an honest, joyful little soul: a passionate, warm-tempered, bustling creature prone to stumbling into perils and difficulties. One day, she decided to tumble down a steep flight of stone steps. When Madame, who heard every sound, emerged from the dining room and picked her up, she quietly remarked, "This child has broken a bone." Initially, we hoped this was not the case. However, it was unfortunately true; one plump arm hung powerless. "Let 'Meess' [me] take her," said Madame, "and let us immediately go to fetch a cab." With prompt but admirable coolness, she departed to summon a surgeon. It appeared the family doctor was not at home, but that mattered little; she searched until she found a suitable substitute and returned with him. Meanwhile, I had cut the sleeve from the child's arm, undressed her, and laid her in bed. None of us—the *bonne*, the cook, the portress, and myself, all gathered in the small, overheated chamber—scrutinized the new doctor closely when he entered. I, at least, was occupied trying to soothe Fifine, whose cries, given her excellent lungs, were terrifying. These screams intensified as the stranger approached; when he lifted her, she cried passionately in her broken English, "Leave me alone! I won't have you! I want Dr. Pillule!" "Dr. Pillule is my very good friend," he replied in perfect English, "but he is busy three leagues away, and I have come in his stead. So now, when we settle down, we shall begin our work, and soon that unfortunate little arm will be bandaged and restored." He requested a glass of sugared water, fed her spoonfuls of the sweet liquid (Fifine was a frank gourmet; anyone could win her heart through her palate), promised more after the procedure, and promptly began working. Requiring assistance, he asked the cook, a robust and strong-armed woman, but she, along with the portress and nurse, instantly fled. I hesitated to touch the small, tortured limb, but seeing no alternative, my hand reached out to perform the necessary task. I was anticipated; Madame Beck had already extended her own hand. Hers was steady while mine trembled. "It will be better," said the doctor, turning from me to her. He displayed wisdom in his choice. My attempt would have been feigned stoicism, forced fortitude. Hers was neither forced nor feigned. "Thank you, Madam; very well, extremely well!" exclaimed the operator upon finishing. "Here is a composure of blood quite timely, worth a thousand misplaced displays of sensitivity." He was pleased with her firmness; she delighted in his compliment.

Likewise, his overall appearance—his voice, demeanor, and manner—worked in his favor. Indeed, when examined closely, especially as evening fell and a lamp was lit, it became evident that unless Madame Beck was less than human, it could not be otherwise. This young doctor—he was certainly young—possessed no ordinary aspect. His stature looked imposingly tall within that cramped chamber among the group of Dutch-born women; his profile was clear, fine, and expressive. Perhaps his eyes glanced from face to face with a vivacity that was slightly too quick and frequent, yet they held a most pleasant character, as did his mouth. His chin was full, cleft, and perfectly Grecian. As for his smile, one could not quickly decide on a descriptor; it contained elements that pleased, yet simultaneously stirred up all one's foibles and weaknesses, exposing everything to laughter. Nevertheless, Fifine liked this ambiguous smile and considered the owner genial; despite his rough handling, she held out her hand to bid him a friendly goodnight. He patted the little hand kindly, and then he and Madame descended the stairs together. She talked in her highest vein of spirits and volubility, while he listened with an air of good-natured amenity, laced with that unconscious, roguish archness that is difficult to describe. I noted that although he spoke French well, his English was superior; moreover, he possessed an English complexion, eyes, and build. I observed more. As he passed me leaving the room, turning his face briefly in my direction—not to address me, but to speak to Madame, yet positioned so that I naturally looked up at him—a recollection that had been struggling to form in my memory since the first moment I heard his voice suddenly crystallized. This was the very gentleman to whom I had spoken at the bureau, who had assisted me with my trunk, and who had guided me through the dark, wet park. Listening as he passed down the long vestibule into the street, I recognized his exact tread: the same firm, equal stride I had followed beneath the dripping trees. It seemed concluded that this young surgeon's first visit to Rue Fossette would be his last. The respectable Dr. Pillule was expected home the following day, and there appeared no reason for his temporary substitute to remain; yet the Fates had decreed otherwise. Dr. Pillule had been summoned to treat a wealthy, elderly hypochondriac in the ancient university town of Bouquin-Moisi. Upon prescribing a change of air and travel as remedies, he was retained to accompany the timid patient on a tour lasting several weeks. Thus, it remained for the new doctor to continue his attendance at Rue Fossette.

I frequently saw him when he arrived, for Madame refused to trust the invalid to Trinette, requiring me to spend much of my time in the nursery. I believe he was skilled. Fifine recovered rapidly under his care, yet even her convalescence did not hasten his departure. Destiny and Madame Beck seemed allied, ruling that he should make deliberate acquaintance with the vestibule, the private staircases, and the upper chambers of Rue Fossette.
