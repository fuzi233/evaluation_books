Across the street—if you will cross with me, and hush your step upon the flags—you may look in at Lord Wrotham’s town-house, lent with a noble carelessness to his friend the General. There, in a snug front parlour (not the state dining-room, where every chair seems to sit at attention), the little household from Oakhurst and Tunbridge has gathered again, drawn close as a family draws close about a hearth when the wind frets the shutters.

James Wolfe has promised to dine; but James is elsewhere, keeping watch and ward in a drawing-room for the least soft encouragement from Miss Lowther’s eyes, preferring that to all Lord Wrotham’s kitchen can contrive—be it ragout as delicate as a whisper, or a pyramid of sugared fruits that would make a bishop forget a sermon. He will not come. You may take his place. As for me, I will not intrude upon your chair, but stand where I can see and hear, near the sideboard, where the smells of the dinner still linger like the last notes of a tune after the fiddles have stopped.

The room has that comfortable after-feast warmth: wax and wood and woollen cloth, and, under it all, the kitchen’s ghost—roast meat drawn from the fire at the proper moment, onions melted down to sweetness, a faint sting of mustard from the cold meats, and the steady perfume of oranges bruised in the peeling. Even the sounds have changed now the servants are gone: no clink of plate, no hiss of a ladle in sauce, no brisk footfall; only the soft settling of chairs, the cough held back, the breath before speech. Silence here is not emptiness; it is a kind of ecology, a small domestic weather. One may live by it as surely as by bread.

Mark first how happy the women look; how the peace in their faces seems newly baked, still rising.

When Harry Warrington was carried off by those bailiffs, I had meant earlier to tell you how good Mrs. Lambert—who can never hear of a young creature in trouble without her heart flying before her—ran to her husband, and begged him with a thousand reasons, each reason kissed into an entreaty.

“Never mind his rebeldom the other day. Never mind his being angry that his presents were returned—why, who would not be angry, much more such a high-spirited lad as Harry? Never mind that we are poor, and every spare guinea is as carefully portioned as sugar in a hard winter for the boys at college. There must be some way of getting him out. Did you not get Charles Watkins out of his scrape two years ago, and did he not pay you every halfpenny? Yes; and you made a whole family happy, blessed be God! and Mrs. Watkins prays for you and blesses you to this very day. I think everything has prospered with us since. And I have no doubt it has made you a major-general—no earthly doubt,” cries the fond wife, who will always find a providence in her husband’s goodness.

Now Martin Lambert, who needs little coaxing to do a kind act (and is never so tall as when he stoops to help), lets himself be persuaded readily. Having made up his mind to seek friend James Wolfe and give bail for Harry, he takes his leave and his hat; squeezes Theo’s hand, who seems to divine his errand (or perhaps that foolish mamma has already spilled the whole story, as she spills rose-water into every dish); kisses little Hetty’s hot cheek; and goes out. Mrs. Lambert follows him to the door.

When she is alone with him in the passage, her feelings break through all the stays and seams of propriety. She flings her arms round his neck, kisses him—no, not once or twice, but with the prodigality of one who has long been hoarding tenderness like a housewife hoards spice—and calls Heaven to bless him, and cries on his shoulder in such a storm of gratitude that the very house seems softer for it.

In this posture the pair are discovered by old Mrs. Quiggett, the housekeeper—bustling keys and all, sharp as vinegar, and long-lived as a parrot; a white, hook-nosed, vigilant old creature who seems made of starch and curiosity.

“We have had a tiff, and we are making it up! Don’t tell tales out of school, Mrs. Quiggett!” says the General, marching off with as much dignity as a man may carry who has just been embraced like a schoolboy.

“Well, I never!” cries Mrs. Quiggett, with a shrill laugh that startles the air like a cracked trumpet; and she shakes till her keys jingle and clatter, as if the very metal upon her girdle were gossiping too.

“Oh, Quiggett!” sobs out Mrs. Lambert, “what a man that is!”

“You’ve been a-quarrelling, have you, mum, and making it up? That’s right.”

“Quarrel with him? He never told a greater story. My General is an angel, Quiggett. I should like to worship him. I should like to fall down at his boots and kiss ’em, I should! There never was a man so good as my General. What have I done to have such a man? How dare I have such a good husband?”

“My dear, I think there’s a pair of you,” says the old cockatoo. “And what would you like for your supper?”

When Lambert comes back very late to that meal and tells what has happened—how Harry is free, and how his brother has, as it were, risen out of the earth to rescue him—you may fancy what a commotion follows. If Mrs. Lambert’s General was an angel in the morning, what is he now by candlelight? If she longed to embrace his boots at breakfast, pray what further abasement of worship will satisfy her at night?

Little Hetty steals close to her father, silent as if words were too coarse for the moment, and sips a tiny drop from his glass, as children will, tasting not the wine so much as the triumph and safety in it. Theo and mamma beam like two moons set side by side. After supper, at a certain signal known to their small household, the four kneel down together—glad homage in a mirth that is almost solemn, the sort of joy we read angels have when a sinner repents.

Then, in the midst of their kneeling, comes a great knocking at the door. The sound seems enormous in so hushed a house; it shakes the quiet as a sudden drum shakes a pastoral tune. Past midnight—my lord in the country—who can it be?

Mrs. Lambert, I think, guesses before anyone speaks.

“This is George,” says a young gentleman, leading in another. “We have been to Aunt Bernstein. We couldn’t go to bed, Aunt Lambert, without coming to thank you too. You dear, dear, good—”

There is no more speech; it is swallowed in kisses and hands and sobs. Aunt Lambert clasps Harry as if she would keep him safe inside her very ribs. Theo snatches up Hetty—who has turned pale as if the knock had been a cannon-shot—and hugs her back into life. George Warrington stands hat in hand; when Harry’s first storm of gratitude has passed, he steps forward and kisses Mrs. Lambert’s hand. The General wipes his eyes with the back of his fingers and looks away, as men do who would rather face a battery than be seen in tears.

Generous hearts know that moment: when wrong is forgiven, when peace returns, when love—thought lost—comes back and sits down quietly again like a familiar cat by the fire.

“We came from Aunt Bernstein’s,” says Harry at last; “we saw lights here, you see; we couldn’t go to sleep without saying good-night to you all. Could we, George?”

“’Tis certainly a famous nightcap you have brought us, boys,” says the General. “When are you to come and dine with us? To-morrow?”

No: to-morrow they must go to Madame Bernstein’s.

The next day, then. Yes—the next day. And that is the very day we are now upon: this is the dinner. Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe is absent on private affairs, and you sit in his stead.

And why, if you please, are we assembled? Not to a mere Barmecide meal; no, no—though the table still bears, like a memory upon the tongue, the savour of what has been eaten. The cloth is withdrawn; the General has given the King’s health; the servants have gone. A hush lies over the room, as if every ear were a doorway. Even the house seems to listen—those old timbers and plaster accustomed to holding voices the way casks hold wine.

It is Mr. George Esmond Warrington’s statement they wait for.

Here they sit, conticuere omnes—women with their hands folded, men with their mouths set as if bracing against bad news. After a little hemming and a blush that rises and falls like a sudden flame in an oven, Mr. George begins.

“I remember,” says he, “at our General’s table, how the little Philadelphia agent—whose wit and shrewdness we had remarked at home—made the very objections to the campaign which its disastrous issue proved just. ‘Of course,’ says he, ‘your Excellency’s troops once before Fort Duquesne, such a weak little place will never be able to resist such a general, such an army, such artillery as will be found attacking it. But do you calculate, sir, on the difficulty of reaching the place? Your Excellency’s march will be through woods almost untrodden, over roads you must make yourselves, and your line will be some four miles long. This slender line, threading the forest, will be subject to endless attacks in front, in rear, in flank, by enemies you will never see, and whose constant practice in war is the dexterous laying of ambuscades.’—‘Psha, sir!’ says the General; ‘the savages may frighten your raw American militia’ (thank your Excellency for the compliment, Mr. Washington seemed to say, who sat at the table), ‘but the Indians will never make any impression on his Majesty’s regular troops.’—‘I heartily hope not, sir,’ says Mr. Franklin, with a sigh; and of course the gentlemen of the General’s family sneered at the postmaster, as at a pert civilian meddling beyond his comprehension.

“We despised the Indians on our own side; our commander made light of them and their service. Our officers disgusted the chiefs with us by outrageous behaviour to their women. There were not above seven or eight who remained with our force. Had we had a couple of hundred in our front on that fatal ninth of July, the event must have been different. They would have flung off the attack of the French Indians; they would have prevented the surprise and panic. ’Tis known now the French had even made ready to give up their fort, never dreaming of the possibility of a defence; and that the French Indians themselves remonstrated against the audacity of attacking such an overwhelming force as ours.

“I was with our General in the main body when the firing began in front, and one aide-de-camp after another was sent forwards. At first the enemy’s attack was answered briskly by our advanced people, and our men huzzaed and cheered with good heart. But very soon our fire grew slacker, whilst from behind every tree and bush round about us came single shots that laid man after man low. We were marching in orderly line—skirmishers in front, colours and two small guns in the centre, baggage well guarded in the rear—moving over ground open and clear for a mile or two, and for some half mile in breadth, with thick tangled covert of brushwood and trees on either side. After the firing had continued a brief time in front, it opened from both sides of the wood upon our advancing column.

“The men dropped rapidly; the officers in greater number than the men. At first, as I said, our fellows cheered and answered; our guns even opened on the wood, and seemed to silence the French in ambuscade. But the hidden rifle-firing began again. Our men halted, huddled together, in spite of shouts and orders to advance, and fired wildly into brushwood—of course making no impression. Those in advance came running back frightened, many wounded. They reported five thousand Frenchmen and a legion of yelling Indian devils in front, scalping our people as they fell. We could hear cries from the wood around as our men dropped under their rifles.

“There was no inducing the people to go forward now. One aide-de-camp after another was sent, and never returned. At last it came to be my turn, and I was sent with a message to Captain Fraser of Halkett’s in front—a message he was never to receive nor I to deliver.

“I had not gone thirty yards when a rifle-ball struck my leg and I fell straightway. I recollect a rush forward of Indians and Frenchmen after that—the former crying their war-cries, the latter as fierce as their savage allies. I was amazed and mortified to see how few whitecoats there were. Not above a score passed me; indeed there were not fifty in the accursed action in which two of the bravest regiments of the British army were put to rout.

“One of them, half Indian half Frenchman, with mocassins and a white uniform coat and cockade, saw me prostrate and turned back, musket clubbed over his head to dash my brains out and plunder me. I had my little fusil which my Harry gave me when I went on the campaign; it had fallen by me and within reach. I seized it; down fell the Frenchman dead at six yards. I was saved for that time; but I bled and grew faint. I swooned almost in trying to load; the piece dropped from my hand; my hand itself sank lifeless to the ground.

“I was scarcely in my senses, yells and shots ringing dimly, when I saw an Indian before me, busy over the body of the Frenchman I had just shot, but glancing towards me as I lay bleeding. He rifled the Frenchman, tore open coat and pockets; then scalped him; and with his bleeding knife in his mouth advanced towards me. I saw him coming as through a film, as in a dream; I had no power to move or resist.

“He put his knee upon my chest; with one bloody hand he seized my long hair and lifted my head. And as he lifted it, I saw a French officer rapidly advancing behind him.

“Good God—it was young Florac, who was my second in the duel at Quebec. ‘A moi, Florac!’ I cried. ‘C’est Georges! aide moi!’

“He started, ran up at the cry, laid his hand on the Indian’s shoulder and called him to hold. But the savage did not understand French—or chose not to. He clutched my hair the tighter, and waved his dripping knife, motioning Florac away. I could only cry again, piteously, ‘A moi!’

“‘Ah, canaille, tu veux du sang? Prends!’ said Florac, with a curse; and the next moment, with an ugh, the Indian fell over my chest dead, Florac’s sword through him.

“My friend looked round. ‘Eh!’ says he, ‘la belle affaire! Where art thou wounded?—in the leg?’ He bound it tight with his sash. ‘The others will kill thee if they find thee here. Ah, tiens! Put on this coat, and this hat with the white cockade. Call out in French if any of our people pass. They will take thee for one of us. Thou art Brunet of the Quebec Volunteers. God guard thee, Brunet! I must go forward. ’Tis a general débâcle, and the whole of your redcoats are on the run, my poor boy.’ Ah, what a rout it was! What a day of disgrace for England.

“Florac’s rough binding stopped the bleeding. He helped me to rest against a tree, and to load my fusil, which he placed within reach to protect me if any other marauder should fancy my scalp. He gave me the gourd of that unlucky French soldier I had killed; the drink within refreshed and invigorated me. Taking a mark of the tree, noting bearings so as to find me again, he hastened on. ‘Thou seest how much I love thee, George,’ he said, ‘that I stay behind in a moment like this.’

“I forget whether I told thee, Harry, that Florac was under obligation to me. I had won money of him at cards at Quebec—only playing at his repeated entreaty—and there was difficulty about paying; I remitted his debt and lighted my pipe with his note-of-hand. You see, sir,” and here George glances with a half-smile at his younger brother, “that you are not the only gambler in the family.

“At evening, when the dismal pursuit was over, the faithful fellow came back with a couple of Indians—each with reeking scalps at his belt—whom he informed that I was a Frenchman, his brother, wounded early and to be carried back to the fort. They laid me in one of their blankets and carried me groaning, Florac by my side. Had he left me, they would assuredly have set me down, plundered me, and added my hair to those bleeding spoils at their girdles. He promised them brandy at the fort if they brought me safely there. I have but a dim recollection of the journey; the anguish of my wound was extreme; I fainted more than once. We came at last. I was taken into the fort, carried to the officers’ log-house, and laid upon Florac’s own bed.

“Happy for me was my insensibility. I had been brought in as a wounded French soldier. I heard afterwards that during my delirium the few prisoners made in our disaster were brought under the walls of Duquesne by their savage captors, and there horribly burned, tortured, and butchered by the Indians, under the eyes of the garrison.”

A shiver goes round the little room, as if a draught had found a crack in the window. Theo takes Hetty’s hand and looks at George with alarm. Harry’s fist comes down upon the table so that the glasses jump and ring—a sharp little music in the midst of horror.

“The bloody, murderous, red-skinned villains!” cries he. “There will never be peace for us until they are all hunted down!”

“They were offering a hundred and thirty dollars apiece for Indian scalps in Pennsylvania when I left home,” says George, very quietly, “and fifty for women.”

“Fifty for women, my love! Do you hear that, Mrs. Lambert?” cries the Colonel, lifting up his wife’s hair, as if even that domestic gesture must now be pressed into service of indignation.

“The murderous villains!” Harry breaks out again. “Hunt ’em down, sir! Hunt ’em down!”

“I know not how long I lay in my fever,” George resumes. “When I awoke to my senses, my dear Florac was gone. He and his company had been despatched on an enterprise against an English fort in Pennsylvanian territory, which the French claimed too. In Duquesne, when I came to be able to ask and understand, there were not above thirty Europeans left. The place might have been taken over and over again had any of our people had courage to return after the disaster.

“My old enemy the ague-fever set in again as I lay by the river-side. ’Tis a wonder I survived. But for the goodness of a half-breed woman in the fort, who took pity on me and tended me, I never should have recovered; and my poor Harry would be what he fancied himself yesterday—our grandfather’s heir, our mother’s only son.

“I remembered how, when Florac laid me in his bed, he put under my pillow my money, my watch, and a trinket or two. When I woke, these were gone; and a surly old sergeant—the only authority left in that quarter—told me with a curse I was lucky enough to be left with life at all; that only the white cockade and coat saved me from the fate the other canaille of Rosbifs had deservedly met.

“At the time of my recovery the fort was almost emptied. The Indians had retired enriched with British plunder; the chief part of the French regulars were gone upon expeditions northward. My good Florac had left me upon his service, consigning me to the care of an invalided sergeant. Monsieur de Contrecoeur had accompanied one of these expeditions, leaving an old lieutenant—Museau by name—in command at Duquesne.

“This man had long been out of France and serving in the colonies. His character, doubtless, had been indifferent at home; and he knew that, by the system pursued in France where almost all promotion is given to the noblesse, he never would advance. And he had made free with my guineas, I suppose, as with my watch; for I saw it one day on his chest when I was sitting with him in his quarter.

“Monsieur Museau and I managed to be pretty good friends. If I could be exchanged or sent home, I told him, my mother would pay liberally for my ransom. I suppose the idea excited his cupidity; for a trapper coming in winter, whilst I still lay very ill, Museau consented that I should write home—but the letter must be in French, he must see it, and I must say I was in the hands of the Indians and should not be ransomed under ten thousand livres.

“In vain I said I was prisoner to the troops of his Most Christian Majesty; that I expected the treatment of a gentleman and officer. Museau swore that letter should go and no other; that if I hesitated he would fling me out of the fort, or hand me to the tender mercies of his ruffian Indian allies. He would not let the trapper speak with me except in his presence. Life and liberty are sweet. I resisted awhile, but I was pulled down with weakness and shuddering fever; I wrote such a letter as the rascal would let pass, and the trapper went away with my missive, promising in three weeks to deliver it to my mother in Virginia.

“Three weeks, six, twelve, passed. The messenger never returned. Winter came and went; the little plantations round the fort—where French soldiers had cleared corn-ground, planted gardens, peach- and apple-trees down to the Monongahela—were in full blossom. Heaven knows how I crept through the weary time. When I was pretty well, I made drawings of the soldiers of the garrison, and of the half-breed and her child—Museau’s child—and of Museau himself, whom I am ashamed to say I flattered outrageously. There was an old guitar left in the fort; I sang to it and played French airs I knew; and I ingratiated myself as best I could with my gaolers. So the months passed—but the messenger never returned.

“At last news arrived he had been shot by some British Indians in Maryland: so there was an end of my hope for some months more. This made Museau savage and surly toward me; the more so as his sergeant inflamed him by telling him the Indian woman was partial to me—as I believe, poor thing, she was. I was always gentle with her and grateful. My small accomplishments seemed wonders in her eyes; I was ill and unhappy too, and these are always claims upon a woman’s affection.

“A captive pulled down by malady, a ferocious gaoler, and a young woman touched by the prisoner’s misfortunes—sure you expect, with these three prime characters, some pathetic tragedy is to be enacted? You, Miss Hetty, are about to guess the woman saved me?”

“Why, of course she did!” cries mamma.

“What else is she good for?” says Hetty, with that cruel sprightliness of youth which has never yet been made to swallow its own tears.

“You, Miss Theo, have painted her already as a dark beauty—is it not so? A swift huntress—”

“Diana with a baby,” says the Colonel.

“—who scours the plain with her nymphs, who brings down the game with her unerring bow, who is queen of the forest—and I see by your looks you think I am madly in love with her?”

“Well, I suppose she is an interesting creature, Mr. George?” says Theo, blushing; her voice low, as if she feared to disturb the story’s spell.

“What think you,” says George, “of a dark beauty the colour of new mahogany, with long straight black hair usually dressed with a hair-oil or pomade by no means pleasant to approach; with little eyes; with high cheek-bones; with a flat nose sometimes ornamented with a ring; with rows of glass beads round her tawny throat; her cheeks and forehead tattooed; a great love of finery; and an inordinate passion for—oh! must I own it?”

“For coquetry. I know you are going to say that!” cries Miss Hetty.

“For whisky, my dear Miss Hester—in which appetite my gaoler partook; so that I have often sat by on nights when I was in favour with Monsieur Museau, and seen him and his poor companion hob-and-nobbing until they could scarce hold the noggin out of which they drank. In these evening entertainments they would sing, they would dance, they would fondle, they would quarrel, and knock the cans and furniture about; and when I was in favour I was admitted to share their society—for Museau, jealous of his dignity, or not willing that his men should witness his behaviour, would allow none of them to be familiar with him.

“Whilst the result of the trapper’s mission to my home was yet uncertain—and Museau and I expected the payment of my ransom—I was treated kindly enough: allowed to crawl about the fort, even to go into adjoining fields and gardens, always keeping parole and returning before gun-fire. And I exercised a piece of hypocrisy, for which I hope you will hold me excused. When my leg was sound—the ball came out in winter after some pain and inflammation, and the wound healed presently—I yet chose to walk as if disabled; I hobbled on two sticks and cried Ah! and Oh! at every minute, hoping that a day might come when I might treat my limbs to a run.”
