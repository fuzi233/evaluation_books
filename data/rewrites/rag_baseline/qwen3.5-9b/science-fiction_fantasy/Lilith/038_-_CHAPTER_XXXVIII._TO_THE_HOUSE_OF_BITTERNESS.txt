In the morning, we pushed off toward the forest with urgent speed. I rode Lona's horse, carrying her corpse wrapped in her cloak. My destination was her father's domain; I intended to deliver her body to the chamber of the dead, hoping he would grant her a proper couch. If he refused—given that she had not sought redemption willingly—I would be forced to watch her rot in the desert sands until she became dust. Yet, I held onto a grim certainty: he would accept her. She had died long ago, spiritually severed from life. The humiliation of presenting her broken form to him weighed heavily on me. Furthermore, I bore Lilith as well. I possessed no power to force her repentance, no moral standing to execute her, and certainly no right to release her back into the world. Was I not destined forever to be her jailer?

Throughout the march, I repeatedly offered her sustenance, but she met each offering with a gaze of hungry hatred. Her fiery eyes rolled restlessly, refusing closure until we crossed the scorching stream. Beyond that point, they remained sealed until we arrived at the House of Bitterness.

One evening, as we camped, a small child approached Lilith. Instinctively, I lunged to intervene, fearing some malicious trickery. Too late, the child had placed something in the princess's mouth and recoiled with a sharp cry. "Please, King," the child whimpered, holding up a digit. "Suck the finger. The bad giantess made a hole in it!" I obeyed, sucking the tiny wound. "Well now!" the child exclaimed triumphantly, immediately offering a second piece of fruit to a mouth desperate for more. This time, however, Lilith snatched her hand away violently, dropping the fruit to the earth. The child's name was Luva.

The following day, we crossed the hot stream. Back on their native soil, the Little Ones erupted in jubilation. Their nests remained distant, so our progress halted at the Ivy-Hall, chosen specifically for its grapevines. Upon spotting the great clusters, the children recognized them instantly as goodness incarnate. They surged forward, devouring eagerly, and within moments, they were all fast asleep on the green floor and scattered throughout the surrounding woods.

Hoping to witness the spectral dance uninterrupted, I instructed them to leave a wide clearing in the center. I lay down among them, with Lona's body beside me, though sleep eluded me. Night descended, and suddenly, the company appeared—the dancers. I pondered whether they would perform eternally, and if my own stubbornness would eventually compel me to join their endless loop. Then, the children's eyes snapped open. They leaped to their feet, wide-awake and alert. Instantly, every child seized a dancer, and they bounded away, skipping and leaping. The specters seemed to acknowledge them warmly; perhaps these entities knew the truth of the Little Ones, having themselves long since retreated into the innocence of childhood. Regardless, their innocent gambols brought a strange refreshment to weary souls stripped of their present and future, leaving only the shadow of a vanished past.

Many merry pranks ensued, yet none were rude. Occasionally, a child caused a momentary disruption in the rhythm, but the spirits, possessing nothing to smile with, showed no annoyance. Just as dawn began to bleed into the sky, I spotted the Skeleton Princess in the doorway. Her eyes were open and glowing, a fearful black spot marred her side. She paused, then glided inward as if intending to join the dance. I sprang upright. A cry of repugnant terror erupted from the children, and the lights vanished. The low moon peered in, revealing them clinging to one another in the darkness. The ghosts were gone—or at least, invisible. The princess had vanished too. I darted to where I had left her; she lay motionless, eyes closed, appearing undisturbed. Returning to the hall, the Little Ones were already settling on the floor for sleep.

The next morning, as we departed, we spotted two skeletons moving through a nearby thicket. The Little Ones broke formation and raced toward them. I followed, walking freely now, free of splints and ligatures. I recognized the pair immediately. The children befriended them instantly, grasping their arms and stroking the long bones of their fingers; the skeletal figures accepted the attention kindly. The two seemed perfectly aligned; their shared deprivation had forged a bond, marking the start of a new existence. Seeing them gather handfuls of herbs, presumably to rub against their bones for rudimentary nourishment, the Little Ones carefully selected matching specimens and filled the outstretched hands of the skeletons. They bid farewell, promising to return, and resumed their journey, remarking on the kindness of the inhabitants of the forest.

Upon reaching the nest-village, I stayed one night to oversee their resettlement. Lona still appeared freshly deceased, and there was no rush. The princess had eaten nothing, her eyes remaining shut. Fearing death before the journey's end, I woke in the night and laid my bare arm against her lips. She bit into it with ferocious intensity, forcing a cry from me. I escaped her grasp, collapsing beyond her reach. Morning broke, and I prepared for departure. Selecting twelve of the Little Ones—not the largest or strongest, but the sweetest and merriest—I mounted them on six elephants. Two additional CLUMSIES, as the children termed them, carried the princess. I continued riding Lona's horse, bearing her body before me.

I chose the most direct route, crossing the left branch of the riverbed toward the House of Bitterness. My hope was to learn how to traverse the broader, rougher branch ahead and avoid the monster-filled basin. I dreaded the terrain for the elephants and the potential danger for the children.

We endured one terrible night—the third spent in the desert between the two branches of the dead river. We halted the elephants in a sheltered spot and allowed the princess to slip down between them, lying on the sand until dawn. She seemed utterly dead, yet I suspected otherwise. I positioned myself a short distance away, with Lona's body at my other flank, keeping watch over both the dead and the potentially dangerous. The moon hung halfway down the western sky, pale and thoughtful, casting mottled shadows across the desert. Suddenly, it was eclipsed. Visible still, it emitted no light, veiled by a thick, diaphanous film that obscured its patient beauty, giving it a troubled appearance. The film swept aside slightly, revealing the jagged outline of a bat-like wing, torn and hooked, against the lunar surface.

A cold wind, stinging with heat, swept over us—and Lilith was upon me. Her hands remained bound, but with her teeth, she tore the cloak I wore from my shoulder, driving her nails into my flesh. I lay paralyzed as life seemed to drain from me into her. Remembering my resolve, I struck her hand. She raised her head with a gurgling shriek, shivering violently. I flung her off and sprang to my feet. She knelt, rocking back and forth. A second blast of hot-stinging cold enveloped us; the moon cleared, illuminating her face—gaunt, ghastly, and smeared with blood.

"Down, devil!" I cried.
"To your first husband," she asked, her voice a dull echo from a sepulcher.
"He will kill me!" she moaned.
"At least he will take you off my hands!"
"Give me my daughter!" she screamed suddenly, grinding her teeth.
"Never! Your doom is upon you at last!"
"Loose my hands for pity's sake!" she groaned. "I am in torture. The cords are sunk in my flesh."
"I dare not. Lie down!" I commanded.
She threw herself on the ground like a log. The remainder of the night passed in peace, and by morning, she appeared dead once more.

Before evening, we came into view of the House of Bitterness. Immediately, an elephant trotted alongside my horse.
"Please, King, you are not going to that place?" whispered the Little One riding on its neck.
"Indeed I am! We are staying the night there," I answered.
"Oh, please, don't! That must be where the cat-woman lives!"
"If you had ever seen her, you would not call her by that name!"
"Nobody ever sees her: she has lost her face! Her head is back and side all round."
"She hides her face from dull, discontented people! Who taught you to call her the cat-woman?"
"I heard the bad giants call her so."
"What did they say about her?"
"That she had claws to her toes."
"It is not true. I know the lady. I spent a night at her house."
"But she *may* have claws to her toes! You might see her feet, and her claws be folded up inside their cushions!"
"Then perhaps you think that I have claws to my toes?"
"Oh, no; that can't be! You are good!"
"The giants might have told you so!"
"We shouldn't believe them about you!"
"Are the giants good?"
"No; they love lying."
"Then why do you believe them about her? I know the lady is good; she cannot have claws."
"Please, how do you know she is good?"
"How do you know I am good?"
