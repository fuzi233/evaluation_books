Few causes exist to warrant admiration quite so strongly as the combination of courage, youth, beauty, and adversity. To Madame Esmond, the young soldier possessed a claim upon her affection that outweighed all others, even if she had borne twenty sons. Her praise for Mr. Washington grew so fervid that it echoed through every gathering she attended. Before her children, she contrasted his sterling virtues sharply with Harry's proclivity for pleasure—labeling him "the wild boy"—and George's melancholic immersion in books. Yet George remained unmoved by her extravagant eulogies. Instead, the jealousy festering within him curdled into a venomous malice, making him a torment to himself and his circle. He delivered barbs so biting and obscure that his simple mother sat bewildered, unable to decipher the depth of his sarcasm or comprehend his saturnine moods.

While domestic tensions simmered, the wider world shifted gears with terrifying momentum. The skirmishes between the French and English in North America, once merely provincial disputes, had swollen into a national conflict. Reinforcements poured from France into Canada, and English troops began to assemble in Virginia. From Quebec, Madame la Presidente de Mouchy wrote to George Warrington with a mixture of sorrow and pride. She lamented that fate seemed to conspire against them, seeing him abandon his beloved mother to throw himself into the arms of Bellona. She pictured him returning wounded from combat, hesitating to wish victory for the French "lilies" while he stood beneath the banners of the Leopard. Though enemies by nation, hearts remained at peace; everyone in Quebec saluted him, including the Chevalier de la Jabotière, who burned with rivalry, and Monsieur the Bear-hunter, George's stoic brother who preferred the hunt to feminine conversation. Even M. Du Quesne, her husband, sent his regards to the young friend.

Indeed, the Leopard flag snapped violently in the wind. A resolve hardened in the camps to reclaim every conquest the French had made upon British soil. Regiments were raised and paid locally, and a formidable fleet sailed from home under an experienced commander. By February 1755, Commodore Keppel, commanding the famous *Centurion*—the same vessel in which Anson had circumnavigated the globe—anchored in Hampton Roads. Beside him lay General Braddock, his staff, and a contingent of troops, appointed by the Duke of Cumberland. A flotilla of transports swiftly followed, laden with provisions, men, and funds.

General Braddock disembarked at Alexandria on the Potomac and journeyed to Annapolis, Maryland, where he convened the colonial governors. He urged them to mobilize their provinces for the common defense, a summons that rippled through the territories with immense excitement. Nowhere was the stir greater than at Castlewood. Harry immediately sought out the encamped troops at Alexandria. Their orderly lines and the stirring beat of fife and drum thrilled him. He quickly befriended the officers of both regiments, longing to join the expedition, and became a welcomed guest at their mess.

Madame Esmond viewed this exposure to fashionable English gentlemen with delight. She believed their company would refine her sons, marking a stark improvement over the local Virginian squires, whose pastimes involved horse racing and cockfighting, or the petty lawyers and schemers at the lieutenant-governor's table. She possessed a keen eye for flattery and despised the officious etiquette and squabbles for precedence at Williamsburg. However, her perception of the King's officers proved woefully naive. While Harry and George politely smiled at her compliments regarding their elegance, the reality behind the scenes was far grimmer. If Madame Esmond had heard the raucous jokes and bawdy songs sung over wine and punch, or witnessed the disheveled state of many soldiers dragged to their lodgings, she would have recoiled. These men swaggered through the countryside, frightening peaceful villagers with their riot. The General raged against their disorder and the "traitorous niggardliness" of the provincials. The soldiers treated the land as conquered territory, scorning the locals and insulting the wives of their Indian allies who had traveled to fight alongside them. Driven to madness by this conduct, the General forbade the presence of Indian women in his camp. Humiliated and outraged, their husbands withdrew, costing the General their invaluable support mere months later.

Yet, certain narratives were forbidden to Madame Esmond. Those officers who visited Castlewood upon her invitation were, in truth, models of courtesy. Soon, the General himself dispatched polite missives to Mistress of Castlewood. Citing his service under the Duke of Marlborough alongside her late father, Colonel Esmond, he requested the honor of calling upon her. If she had understood the motive behind this sudden politeness, her charm might have faded. At Alexandria, the Commander-in-Chief held levees, and among the visiting gentry were the twin sons of Castlewood, arriving on fine steeds in their finest London attire, accompanied by their black servants in smart liveries. The General, however, was in a foul temper with the provincial gentry and barely acknowledged the youths, casually querying his aide-de-camp over dinner who these "young Squire Gawkeys" in blue and gold waistcoats were.

Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor, and a gentleman from Pennsylvania happened to dine with his Excellency. Upon learning the identity of the visitors—"the sons of the Princess Pocahontas," Dinwiddie noted—the General erupted in a torrent of oaths, demanding to know who such a "deuce" a princess was. Dinwiddie, harboring a lifelong grudge against the imperious Madame Esmond, seized the opportunity to ridicule her pomposity and vast pretensions. He spun absurd anecdotes until the General succumbed to sleep. When the General awoke, Dinwiddie had departed, but the Philadelphia gentleman remained, deep in conversation with the officers. Resuming the topic, the General spoke of Madame Esmond in curt, disrespectful terms typical of soldiers of that era, again questioning the name of the "old fool" Dinwiddie had mentioned. He vented his contempt for the gentry and the country at large.

Then, Benjamin Franklin of Philadelphia interjected. He offered a diametrically opposed view of Madame Esmond, demonstrating a profound knowledge of her character, her father, and her extensive estates. He explained to the General that she possessed ample beef, horses, and stores, vital assets for the current juncture, and strongly advised conciliating her. The General, already impressed by Franklin's acumen, graciously invited the two young men to dinner the following day. True to their station, Harry and George conducted themselves with modesty and breeding, returning home delighted. Their mother, equally pleased by the civilities extended to her boys, promptly penned a gracious reply, inviting the General to visit Castlewood whenever it suited him.

News of the approaching campaign became the dominant topic at Castlewood. For Harry, the prospect of war was a dream realized; he dreamed only of battle, spent his days polishing the household firearms and swords, and drilled the negroes. Madame Esmond, possessing a gallant spirit herself, sensed the time had come for one of her sons to serve the Crown. She trembled as she awaited the decision, admiring the elder but loving the younger with a passion that threatened to overwhelm her reason.

Though eager, Harry dared not broach the subject directly, fearing the gravity of the moment. Twice he tried to mention it to George, whose countenance darkened ominously at the suggestion. Harry, bound by a feudal devotion to his brother, yielded to him in all matters, viewing George as the chief. Yet, to his terror, he observed George preoccupied with military strategy, surrounded by books on the campaigns of Eugene and Marlborough, and practicing fencing with Dempster, the old Scotsman who was a master of martial arts.

When Madame Esmond entrusted her boys with the General's letter, she attached substantial presents for the staff and officers, gifts so generous that the General repeatedly thanked Franklin for securing this alliance. "Would not one of the young gentlemen like to see the campaign?" the General asked. He noted that a friend of theirs, Mr. Washington—who had been unfortunate in the previous year's affair—had already promised to serve as aide-de-camp, and expressed a willingness to accept another Virginian gentleman. Harry's eyes lit up, his face flushing with joy. "With all my heart!" he cried. George, fixing a stern gaze upon his brother, countered that one would accompany the General, while the other's duty lay in caring for their mother. Harry, obedient to his senior's will despite his desperate desire to go, fell silent. On the ride home, they traversed miles in silence, each reading the other's mind, terrified to force the issue.

Upon reaching home, they revealed the General's offer to their mother. "I knew it must happen," she murmured. "At such a crisis, our family must stand forward. Have you decided?" She looked anxiously from one to the other, dreading the answer. "The youngest ought to go, Mother; of course I ought to go!" blurted Harry, turning crimson. "Of course he ought," agreed Mrs. Mountain, who had been listening. "There! Mountain says so! I told you so!" Harry exclaimed, casting a sidelong glance at George. "The head of the family ought to go, Mother," George said softly. "No! No! You are ill, and have never fully recovered from your fever. Should he go, Mountain?" "You would make the best soldier, I know, dearest Hal," Mrs. Mountain replied gently.
