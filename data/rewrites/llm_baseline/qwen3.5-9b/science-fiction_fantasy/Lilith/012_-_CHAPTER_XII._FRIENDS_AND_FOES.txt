I knelt beside a channel, examining what appeared to be a modest shrub—the likely vanguard of an army hidden behind it. It bore a small fruit; unfamiliar and unrecognizable, I hesitated to touch it. Little did I know that hundreds of eyes, burning with curiosity, watched from the shadows of the rocks, waiting to see if I would succumb to temptation. Moving onward, I encountered plants of increasing size, eventually realizing they were not shrubs but dwarf-trees. By the time I reached the second branch of the riverbed, the banks were choked with them, forcing me to leap across gaps I otherwise couldn't cross. In one channel, a sound erupted—a rushing roar like a flock of birds fleeing an ivy-choked wall—yet I saw nothing.

Further along stood massive fruit-bearing trees, though their produce looked coarse. They lined the edge of a vast hollow, clearly the dried bed of an ancient lake. While the surrounding forest teemed with diverse species, the trees within the depression were almost exclusively fruit-laden. Exhausted, I descended a short slope of grass and moss to lie down. Just beyond rested a tiny tree heavy with rosy apples, no larger than cherries, hanging within reach. I plucked one and ate it. The flavor was exquisite. As I reached for a second, a sudden burst of childish shouting, mixed with laughter as clear and sweet as a mountain brook, shattered the silence.

"He likes our apples! He likes our apples!" cried a dozen tiny voices. "He's a good giant! A fine giant!"
"He *is* a giant," one child objected.
"He is rather big," another agreed wisely, "but size doesn't mean wisdom! You can grow tall and foolish unless you watch yourself!"

I propped myself up on my elbow to stare. Surrounding me was a motley assembly of children of every age, from toddlers learning to walk to youths nearing adolescence. A smaller group of three or four elders stood slightly apart, watching with less excitement than the frenzied majority. They chattered in clusters, debating and contradicting one another with the fervor of adults in a marketplace, yet possessing superior manners, sharper wit, and boundless joy. I realized immediately that my reaching for the second apple confirmed my enjoyment of the first. How they deduced my character from a single bite remained a mystery to me, though I wasn't surprised that at least one voice urged caution.

Silence fell as a movement rippled through the crowd. A sweet-faced boy, looking both innocent and mischievously rogue, stepped forward holding a huge green apple. All eyes fixed on me expectantly.
"Eat, good giant," he commanded softly.
I sat up, accepted the offering with a smile, and prepared to bite. But the moment my teeth touched the skin, I spat it out and hurled it far away.

A fresh shriek of delight erupted. The children swarmed me, nearly suffocating me in their embrace. They kissed my cheeks and hands, grabbed my legs, and climbed over my arms and shoulders, burying their faces in my neck. Overwhelmed by the affectionate little goblins, I collapsed onto the ground.
"Good, good giant!" they chorused. "We knew you'd come! Oh, you dear, strong giant!"

Their joyful babbling resumed, rising in waves of jubilation. Suddenly, the noise cut off. Those nearby recoiled; those climbing me scrambled down, helping me upright. Concern replaced their earlier mirth on their bright faces.
"Get up, good giant!" a little girl urged urgently. "Hurry! Hurry! He saw you toss his apple away!"

Before she finished, I was standing. She pointed upward toward the slope's crest. Perched there was a grotesque figure, a few inches taller than me, radiating hostility. Yet, seeing no weapon in his grasp, I felt no immediate fear. My little friends had vanished instantly. He began his descent, and I moved upward to gain a better vantage point. As he approached, he growled like a cornered beast. Once we were on level ground, he stopped and extended a hand. I offered mine in greeting, but he snatched it back, brandished a threat, and thrust it forward again. His intent became clear: he demanded the apple I had discarded. I made a face of disgust and waved my hand in refusal.

"Do you dare tell me your apple was unworthy?" he howled in rage.
"One rotten apple can spoil the finest tree," I replied.
Whether he grasped my metaphor, I could not say. He lunged forward, and I braced myself. However, he paused, allowing a second giant—identical in appearance—to creep up behind me before striking. I parried his initial blow to the face, but the newcomer struck me squarely on the back of the head. Together, they overpowered me easily.

They dragged me into the woods above the valley, home to their tribe. Here dwelled wretched huts constructed of fallen branches and scattered stones. They shoved me inside one, throwing me to the dirt and kicking me. A woman watched with cold indifference. During my captivity, I struggled to distinguish gender; the creatures differed so little in form and manner that I often wondered if I had stumbled upon a fungoid race, possessing just enough intelligence to mimic human emotion—anger, greed, and motion. Their diet of tubers, bulbs, and strange fruits repulsed me, yet nothing angered them more than witnessing my distaste. Women cuffed me and men kicked me whenever I refused to swallow their fare.

That night, I lay on the floor, barely able to move, though sleep brought some refreshment. At dawn, they dragged me back to the valley. Tying my feet to a tree with a rough rope, they placed a flat stone with a jagged, saw-like edge into my left hand. When I shifted it to my right, they kicked me and forced it back into my left. Their command was simple: strip the bark from every branch bearing no fruit. After one final kick, they abandoned me to the task.

I began the dreary labor, hoping that compliance might buy me solitude to observe and plan my escape. Fortunately, a dwarf-tree grew dangerously close. Every few minutes, I plucked a small fruit from it, eating it to sustain my strength against the relentless torment.
