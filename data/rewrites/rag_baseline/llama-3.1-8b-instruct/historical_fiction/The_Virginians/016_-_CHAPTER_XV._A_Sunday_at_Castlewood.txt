Sunday morning sunlight filtered through the chapel windows at Castlewood, casting intricate patterns on the stone floor. The air inside was heavy with the scent of old wood and damp earth. In the family pew, Madame de Bernstein sat upright, her hands clasped together in a gesture of reverence. The rest of the family followed suit, their faces solemn, their eyes fixed on the clergyman as he began to read the service.

As the organist's fingers danced across the keys, a discordant note pierced the air. Gumbo, the lacquey, stood up in the servants' pew, his voice booming out in a rendition of the psalm. The congregation turned, startled, to watch him. The parson's eyes widened behind his spectacles, and he hastily covered his mouth with his handkerchief. The liveried gentlemen from London looked on, their faces a picture of dismay.

Gumbo, undeterred, continued to sing, his voice weaving in and out of the organ's melody. The congregation watched, transfixed, as he performed a sort of improvised solo. The clerk, seated nearby, looked flustered, his mouth opening and closing silently as he tried to keep up with Gumbo's impromptu performance.

When the music finally died away, the clergyman cleared his throat and began his sermon. His words tumbled forth in a rush, a torrent of sound that swept the congregation up in its wake. He spoke of a man, a man who had lived a life of quiet desperation, driven by the whims of fate and the allure of vice. He described the man's downfall, his gradual slide into a world of crime and poverty, and the eventual consequences of his actions.

The clergyman's words painted vivid pictures in the minds of his listeners. They saw the man's face, etched with worry and fear, as he stood before the judge. They heard the rustle of papers, the creak of the hangman's cart, and the final, fatal drop. The sermon ended, leaving the congregation in a state of uneasy silence, their minds still reeling from the vivid images that had been conjured up.
