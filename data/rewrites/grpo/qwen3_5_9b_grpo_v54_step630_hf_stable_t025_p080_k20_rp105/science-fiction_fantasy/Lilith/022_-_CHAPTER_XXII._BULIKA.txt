The disorientation was absolute, a hollow ache in my chest that drove me forward in a blind, helpless sprint. Then, the scent hit me—not the metallic tang of iron, but the coppery richness of fresh life. I stumbled into the path of the leopardess, her paws wading in a torrent of crimson that rushed against my ankles with the force of a mountain stream. My instinct screamed that this was her blood, the life-force of the beast I had just witnessed, but a colder, sharper suspicion seized me. No creature, not even the leviathans of our ancient world, could bleed such a volume unless their entire circulatory system were shattered, draining fields and forests into a single, catastrophic gush.

I dipped a finger into the rushing red river. It was cool, clear, and utterly devoid of the viscosity of blood. It was water, a murmuring, channel-less rivulet flowing over the grass, singing a sweet, deceptive song. Yet, I did not drink. Thirst was a secondary concern to the mystery of its origin. I pressed on, guided by the sound, chasing the faint echo of the hot stream I had known before, hoping the light would return. The wetness of my feet had already stilled the tremors in my legs, and as the darkness began to thin, revealing the pale edge of dawn, I saw them: the wall-towers of a city, looming against the aurora, ancient as time itself.

I looked down to find the brook. It was gone. For some time, I had heard its rhythm fading, but now it was silence. Looking back, the grass lay bent in the direction of the flow, dotted with small, glimmering pools, but toward the city, there was no trace. The fountain had paused, or perhaps vanished entirely.

Beyond the walls lay gardens, teeming with vegetables I could not name, yet utterly barren of water, flowers, or animal life. They pressed right up against the fortifications, separated only by massive heaps of gravel and refuse dumped from the battlements. I approached the nearest gate; it hung half-open, rusted shut yet somehow unbarred, guarded by no sentinel. Beyond lay a long, ancient street, silent and empty. Was this a dead city? Doubt gnawed at me, so I turned and fled back out, toiling over dust-heaps and crossing several roads, refusing to re-enter until I knew the inhabitants were awake.

*What was I here for?* The question echoed in the quiet air. *What did I hope to find?*

I needed to see the woman I had brought to life. I did not crave her company; she had awakened frightful suspicions within me, making friendship, let alone love, a wild impossibility. Yet, her presence had altered me, forcing me to analyze a strange, magnetic influence she exerted. I wanted to penetrate the inscrutable nature of her being, to understand the marvels she represented. But I was too daring; a man should not seek knowledge by willingly courting temptation. Still, I had reinstated an evil force on the brink of extinction. I was accountable for whatever mischief might follow. I knew she was the enemy of children; the Little Ones were in danger. I had left them seeking light, and I had found a sliver of it. I needed more. I needed to know how to protect them.

At last, a faint stir broke the stillness. I moved through the next gate and down a narrow street of towering houses to a small square. I sat on the base of a pillar, where a hideous, bat-like creature perched atop the stone. Soon, citizens began to saunter past. I spoke to one, but he offered only a rude stare and a harsh word before moving on.

I rose and wandered through narrower streets, which gradually filled with idlers. I was not surprised to see no children. Near a gate, a group of young men cornered me. They reminded me of the bad giants I had faced before. They stared, pushed, hustled, and threw objects at me. I endured it, unwilling to provoke enmity where I sought refuge. I tried appealing to those who looked benevolent, but no one stopped to listen. I looked poor, and in Bulika, poverty was an offense punishable by scorn. Deformity and sickness were taxed, and the Princess's laws seemed designed to make poverty serve wealth.

Desperate, I took to my heels. No one pursued me beyond the gate, save for a lumbering fellow sitting by the entrance, eating a hunch of bread. In his stupid eagerness to throw a stone after me, he flung the bread instead. I snatched it; he dared not follow to reclaim it, for beyond the walls, everyone was a coward. I retreated a few hundred yards, collapsed in the grass, and ate. The bread fueled me, and the hot sunlight renewed my strength until sleep claimed me.

Night fell, and I woke to the moon shining down with a friendly, familiar brightness—the same moon that had watched me through the terrors of my first night in this strange world. A cold wind blew from the gate, carrying an evil odor, yet it did not chill me; the sun's warmth remained stored in my bones. I crept back into the city, joining the few souls crouched in corners to escape the shivering blast.

Walking slowly down a long, narrow street, a huge white shape bounded across my path, flashing in the moonlight before vanishing. Eager to catch a glimpse again, I turned down the next opening, which led into a wider thoroughfare. Immediately, I saw it on the opposite side, lurking in the shadow: the creature I had followed, trailing what I assumed was a man.

He glanced at the animal behind him every few moments but never spoke to it, never tried to drive it away. As he crossed a patch of moonlight, I saw the impossible truth: he cast no shadow. He was not a man at all, but a flat, superficial shadow of two dimensions. Yet, he was an opaque shadow, blocking light and rendering anything behind him invisible. In the shade, he was blacker than darkness; in the moonlight, he appeared as if he had drawn his shadow up around him, leaving no trace of depth beside or beneath him.

Following close at his heels was the leopardess, the white shadow of his blackness. She glided silently, dragging her own shadow—a stark black line—across the ground. When they passed from shadow into moonlight, the Shadow deepened into absolute void, while the animal flashed into blinding radiance. I walked abreast of them on the opposite side, my bare feet sounding on the flat stones. The leopardess never turned her head or twitched an ear. The Shadow seemed to look at me; for a split second, I lost his profile, seeing only a sharp, upright line.

That instant, the wind found me, blowing through me with icy force. I shuddered from head to toe, and my heart hammered against my ribs like a pebble rattling in a child's toy.
