*"I see my way as birds their trackless way—
I shall arrive! what time, what circuit first,
I ask not: but unless God sends his hail
Or blinding fire-balls, sleet, or stifling snow,
In some time—his good time—I shall arrive;
He guides me and the bird. In His good time!"*
—Browning’s *Paracelsus.*

Winter lingered, the days stretching longer but bringing little solace. Mrs. Thornton’s visits had ceased entirely, and though Mr. Thornton still called, his conversations were confined to Mr. Hale’s study. Margaret gathered from her father’s remarks that there was no resentment in these dwindling visits—only the press of business, the aftermath of the strike demanding more of his time than the previous year had required. And when Mr. Thornton did speak of her, it was with the same steady, unaltered tone, neither seeking nor avoiding her name.

She had no heart to lift her father’s spirits. The quiet that now enveloped them felt less like peace than exhaustion after the storms they had weathered. Her efforts to occupy herself—teaching the Boucher children, tending to the house—were mechanical, her joy in them hollow. Only in comforting her father did she find purpose, attuned to his every mood, anticipating his unspoken wishes. Her devotion was quiet, unassuming, a balm to his weariness.

March brought news of Frederick’s marriage. His letter brimmed with happiness, his new life in Spain as a partner in Barbour & Co. erasing past bitterness. Dolores, his bride, had written too, her timid, earnest English endearing her at once. A lavish black lace mantilla accompanied the letter—a gift chosen with care for the sister-in-law Frederick had painted as a paragon. Margaret smiled at the irony: her brother, once the dashing naval officer, now a merchant. Yet trade had brought him joy, and that was enough.

Her father’s health weighed on her. His breathing grew labored that spring, though the spells passed as suddenly as they came. Still, she urged him to accept Mr. Bell’s invitation to Oxford, hoping the change would restore him. When he left, Margaret felt the weight of her solitude—not as a burden, but as a respite. For the first time in years, she could be still, could let her own grief surface.

She spent hours in the drawing room, sifting through memories like old letters, confronting each sting of regret. The falsehood she had told for Frederick’s sake haunted her most—its futility laid bare by events. Opening her father’s book at random, her eyes fell on a passage that seemed meant for her:

*"I would not chastise my heart so harshly: 'Die of shame, blind, impudent traitor to your God!' No—I would correct it with compassion. 'Come, my poor heart, we have fallen into the pit we sought to avoid. Let us rise, call upon God’s mercy, and hope in His aid. Take courage, and return to the path of humility.'"*

Humility. The word struck her. Had she not lacked it? Had she not clung to pride even in repentance? She closed the book, resolved to seek that lost path.

Martha, dusting the hearth, proved an unexpected well of information. At the mention of Mrs. Thornton, the maid’s reserve melted. She spoke of her family’s old ties to the Thorntons, of kindnesses shown when her father fell on hard times. "Miss Fanny’s to be married," she added, "to a Mr. Watson—gray-haired, but rich. His mills are near Hayleigh." Margaret caught herself wondering how Mr. Thornton would receive the news, then chided herself for the habit.

The next day, she visited Mary Higgins and found Nicholas home early, uncharacterively subdued. He spoke of Thornton with grudging respect—"good enough to fight with, too good to cheat"—and coaxed his youngest to recite hymns with surprising tenderness. Margaret left with a pang of hope: even Nicholas, once so defiant, had found a measure of peace.

That evening, sorting letters, she reread Henry Lennox’s updates on Frederick’s case. Their stiff propriety reminded her of the gulf between them, though she set them aside as proof of his efforts. Her thoughts drifted to her father, his absence suddenly sharp. She had welcomed solitude, but now she missed him—his quiet presence, his gentle voice.

***

At Oxford, Mr. Hale wandered the familiar streets, weary but content. His old friends welcomed him without the censure he had feared; their kindness undimmed by the years. "You’ve worn yourself out in that Milton air," Mr. Bell chided as Mr. Hale rested in his study.

"I’m fifty-five," Mr. Hale murmured. "That accounts for it."

"Nonsense! I’m past sixty and feel no decay."

Mr. Hale shook his head. "These last years..." He sat forward suddenly, voice trembling. "Bell, if I could have foreseen the cost—even Margaret’s suffering—I would not undo my choice. I could not deny my conscience. But God did not grant me wisdom to spare them."

Mr. Bell blew his nose loudly. "He gave you strength to act on conviction. That’s wisdom enough."

A silence fell. Then, softly: "Bell... if I die—"

"Don’t be absurd."

"What will become of Margaret?"

"The Lennoxes may take her in. Edith is dear to her, though flighty. But Margaret—" Mr. Bell’s voice roughened. "That girl has my heart. I’ve told you before. Whatever I have is hers. I’ll see her provided for, as if she were my own."

Mr. Hale smiled faintly. "You’ll outlive me, Bell. Spare men like you cheat death."

But that night, death came quietly. Mr. Hale lay down and did not rise.

***

Mr. Bell, stunned, booked the first train to Milton. In the carriage, he wept silently behind the *Times*—until he lowered it and saw John Thornton across from him.

"Thornton!" He gripped his hand fiercely. "I’m bound for Milton—to tell Margaret her father is dead."

"Dead?" Thornton’s face paled.

"At Oxford. He went to bed well—was cold by morning."

A long silence. Then Thornton, hoarse: "And she?"

"I’ll care for her," Bell said fiercely. "But there are the Lennoxes—her aunt, that barrister cousin who’s had his eye on her for years. Now, with Hale’s death, he may press his suit."

Thornton’s jaw tightened. "What—what will she do?"

Bell sighed. "That, Thornton, is what we must help her decide."

The train rushed on, carrying them toward the woman who bound them both in grief and hope.
