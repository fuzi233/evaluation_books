The greatest danger we had faced so far was now behind us, but I still carried a heavy weight of worry in my heart. What if another serpent—unseen, unknown—had slipped into the swamp? Or worse, what if it had come from beyond Falconhurst, slipping through the Gap like a shadow? The thought haunted me.

To ease my mind, I decided on two missions: first, a thorough search of the marsh and thicket; second, a bold journey to the Gap itself—the only path by which such a creature could have entered our land.

I called my sons to join me in the marsh, but Ernest and Jack were less than eager. Jack shivered at the memory of the snake’s tail, imagining how easily it might have crushed his ribs. But I would not be swayed by fear. We must face the truth, no matter how grim. Together, we began crossing the bog, laying planks and wicker hurdles as we went, shifting them forward with each step.

We found nothing but footprints in the reeds and a hollowed-out space where the grass and rushes had been flattened—clearly the serpent’s lair. Beyond that, the ground grew firm again, and we emerged near a steep rock wall. There, glistening in the sunlight, flowed a clear, sparkling brook emerging from a dark opening in the cliffside—a cave, or grotto, large and mysterious.

Inside, the vaulted ceiling dripped with stalactites, and thick columns of stone rose like pillars, holding up the roof. The floor was covered in soft, white earth that felt smooth and slippery underfoot—something I immediately recognized as fuller’s earth, a natural clay used for washing clothes.

“Well,” I said, “this is a fine find! It’s as good as soap—and saves me the trouble of making any.”

Fritz stepped forward, peering into the deeper part of the cave. He soon shouted back, “Father, this passage widens! Come see!”

I followed, leaving the others outside. As I entered, I fired a pistol shot. The echoes rolled back from every side, booming and swelling—proof that the cavern stretched far beyond sight. Lighting the small candle I always carried, we ventured deeper. Though the flame burned weakly in such vastness, it revealed walls that shimmered faintly, reflecting light like glass.

“Look!” Fritz exclaimed. “This must be another salt cave! See how the walls gleam?”

“No,” I replied. “Salt crystals leave marks when water runs over them, and this stream tastes sweet. I believe we’ve found a cave of rock crystal!”

“Oh, how magnificent!” he breathed. “A treasure!”

“A treasure indeed—if we can use it,” I said. “Otherwise, it’s no more valuable than the gold Robinson Crusoe found.”

Still, Fritz insisted on breaking off a piece. “Here’s a good one,” he said, holding up a dull lump. “Only… not transparent. A shame!”

“You’re damaging its shape,” I warned. “Crystal forms naturally into pyramids with six sides. You’ve broken that form.”

We explored further, marveling at the glowing walls and the silence that swallowed sound. When the candle flickered low, we turned back, Fritz firing one last shot just to hear the grand echo fade away.

Outside, we found Jack sobbing uncontrollably. At the sight of us, he rushed forward, throwing himself into my arms.

“Oh, father!” he cried. “I thought you were dead! I heard two terrible crashes—like rocks falling—and I believed you and Fritz were buried beneath them! It was awful!”

I comforted him, explaining the echoes were just our shots bouncing through the cave. Then I asked why he was alone.

“Ernest is over there in the reeds,” he said. “He didn’t hear the guns.”

I found Ernest busy weaving a clever fish trap—shaped like a funnel, wide at one end and narrow at the other, so fish could swim in but not out.

“I shot a young serpent while you were gone, father,” he said proudly. “It’s lying under the rushes. About four feet long, as thick as my arm.”

“A serpent?” I gasped, rushing over—fear rising again. “No, son. That’s an eel. And a fine catch, too. It’ll make a splendid supper. I’m glad you stayed and killed it instead of running.”

“It was hard,” Ernest admitted. “I shot it in the head, but it kept writhing even after. I had to beat it until it stopped.”

“The tenacity of life in eels is remarkable,” I said. “The best way to kill them is to hold them by the neck and strike their tails sharply against a stone.”

We returned more easily, following the cliffs where the ground was firmer. My wife waited at the fountain, washing clothes. She greeted us joyfully and praised the fuller’s earth—soap being nearly gone.

That evening, we feasted on the eel and shared stories of our adventure—the swamp, the cave, the crystal grotto. But I could not rest. The question remained: was there still a serpent lurking in our territory?

So we prepared for the next mission—our greatest yet. We would search the land beyond the river, all the way to the Gap. I wanted the whole family to go. Everyone agreed.

For weeks we’d be away, so we packed the small tent, food, tools, firearms, cooking gear, torches—everything we needed. The cart, drawn by Storm and Grumble, carried most of it. Jack and Franz rode the horses, acting as drivers and riders. My wife sat comfortably in the cart. Fritz led the way on horseback. Ernest and I walked beside. The dogs—Bruno, Fangs, and Juno—guarded our flanks.

We headed toward Woodlands, passing through signs of the serpent’s trail—deep furrows in soft soil, like broad ruts left by a giant beast.

At Falconhurst, we paused. The poultry clucked and fluttered around us, the sheep and goats came to greet us—peaceful as ever.

Then we pressed on to Woodlands, arriving just as night fell. Everything was calm. No sign of the boa. No trace of apes. The little farm thrived.

The next day, we explored nearby, gathering cotton for new pillows and cushions. In the afternoon, Franz joined me, carrying a small gun for the first time.

We took Fangs and Bruno with us, walking slowly along the lake’s left bank. Reeds and thickets often blocked our path, forcing us to detour. The dogs ran ahead, flushing ducks, snipes, herons—all flying across the lake before Franz could get a shot.

He grew impatient. “Can I shoot those black swans, father? They look so graceful.”

Just then, a deep, booming sound echoed through the air. Franz jumped. “Father! Is that Swift, our young onager?”

“No,” I said. “That’s not Swift. Listen closely. I think it’s a bittern—a large bird, like a heron.”

“But how can a bird make such a roar?” Franz asked. “It sounds like an ox!”

“It’s not size that determines sound,” I explained. “It’s the shape of the windpipe, the lungs, and the muscles. A tiny nightingale sings louder than many larger animals. Some say the bittern thrusts its bill into the mud to amplify its cry—like a horn.”

Franz was eager to shoot the bird. We sent the dogs into the woods and waited, hidden.

Suddenly—rustling! Franz fired.

“I hit it! I hit it!” he shouted.

“What did you hit?” I called.

“A wild pig,” he said. “Bigger than Fritz’s.”

“Aha!” I laughed. “You remember the agouti. But this may not be a hog at all. Could it be one of our farm pigs? What will the old sow say?”

I hurried to him. The animal looked like a pig—but its snout was blunt, its body covered in bristles, no tail, yellowish-gray skin. Its feet were webbed. I examined its teeth—odd, flat, strong.

“This,” I said, “is a capybara. A South American animal that lives near water.”

Franz was thrilled. “I shot a new creature!”

Carrying it home was hard. He suggested cutting it open and cleaning it—lighter that way. Then, he asked if Bruno could carry it on his back. The dog agreed, and we reached Woodlands safely.

There, we found Ernest surrounded by dead rats.

“Where did these come from?” I asked.

“We stumbled upon them,” he said. “Knips chased one into a mound among the reeds. It tried to escape into a hole, but Knips held it by the tail. I pulled it out and killed it.”

He broke open the mound—thick with mud and plant stems—and dozens of rats tumbled out. Some escaped into the water. Others swarmed around him, hissing and squealing.

“I thought of Bishop Hatto and the Mouse Tower!” he said. “Knips fought them, but I was scared. Just as I shouted for help, Juno burst through the reeds and drove them all away.”

His mother struggled through the marsh to reach him. Together, they collected the dead rats.

I went to inspect the site. To my surprise, I saw a small dam-like structure—made of clay and lined with reeds and lily pads.

“These are muskrats,” I said. “Also called beaver rats. They build homes like beavers. Webbed feet, flat tails—just like beavers. They also have scent glands—musk. We should skin them quickly. Their pelts will make good caps.”

Back at the house, Fritz and Jack returned—no serpents seen. Jack carried a dozen eggs in his hat. Fritz had shot a cock and hen heath fowl.

Supper was ready. Franz was eager to taste his capybara. Even he wrinkled his nose at the flavor.

“That’s the musk,” I said. “Many animals produce it—musk deer, musk ox, crocodiles, Indian muskrats, even the civet-cat.”

I told them about the civet—a black-and-white animal whose scent was once used as medicine, now mainly perfume. The scent comes from glands near the tail. Dutch traders keep them caged, using a special spoon to scrape the oily substance without harming the animal.

Later, Jack returned with a game-bag full of fruit—pale green apples, unfamiliar to us.

“What are these?” I asked.

“Fritz and I didn’t dare eat them,” he said. “They might be poison, like manchineel. So we brought them to Master Knips.”

I cut one open. Instead of a single seed, it had a ring of pips—different from manchineel.

At that moment, Knips crept up behind me, grabbed half, and began munching happily.

The boys couldn’t resist. A frantic scramble broke out. I managed to save two apples for myself and my wife.

I suspected it was the cinnamon apple of the Antilles.

Everyone was tired. We arranged our beds and settled down.

At dawn, we continued our journey—toward a spot between the sugar brake and the Gap. There, we found an old tree arbor we’d built before. We covered it with a sailcloth and made it comfortable.

Our goal: search for signs of the boa. Fritz, Jack, and Franz went with me to the sugar cane field. We found no trace. We drank fresh cane juice—refreshing and sweet.

Suddenly—barking! Rustling through the canes!

I rushed to the edge, guns ready. From the thicket came a herd of gray creatures—small pigs with sharp tusks—running in single file.

I fired. Two fell. The others kept going, ignoring the bodies. Fritz and Jack shot more.

“They’re peccaries,” I said. “Wild pigs. But we must remove the scent gland from their backs right away—or the meat will spoil.”

We worked fast. While Jack fetched the cart, I cleaned the animals. Ernest arrived with his mother and three more peccaries—caught with Juno’s help.

We loaded the cart. The boys decorated it with flowers and sang triumphantly as they carried the haul to the hut.

After dinner, we singed and scalded the bristles, cut the hams, and salted the meat. The boys built a smokehouse—using damp wood to create steady smoke. The meat hung inside for days, curing slowly.

On the second day, the boys wanted to cook the smallest pig Otaheitean style—baked underground.

They dug a hole, burned dry grass and sticks, heated stones, and placed them around the pit. Fritz cleaned and stuffed the pig with potatoes, onions, herbs, salt, and pepper. He sewed it shut and wrapped it in large leaves.

When the fire died, he placed the pig in the hole, covered it with hot ashes, then earth—making it look like a molehill.

Dinner was anticipated with excitement. My wife, skeptical, prepared plain food “just in case.”

But when Fritz opened the oven, a rich, savory aroma filled the air—roast pork, yes, but with a hint of spice.

“Those leaves,” I said. “They’re ravensara—native to Madagascar. Smells like nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon. The fruit makes oil prized in native cooking.”

The peccary looked perfectly cooked. Fritz received praise—even from his mother.

While curing the meat, we roamed the area—finding no snakes, but discovering towering bamboo—fifty to sixty feet tall. Cut at the joints, they made excellent casks and pots. Their sharp thorns served as nails.

One day, we visited Prospect Hill. The apes had been there again—ruining everything. Animals scattered, cottage torn apart. We left it as it was, promising to fix it later.

Finally, we closed the smokehouse, barricaded it, and set off for the Gap.

We’d cleared a path through the cane thicket during our stay—now it helped us move the loaded cart easily.

Beyond, the land opened. In a few hours, we reached the edge of our coastal domain.

We halted at a small wood. To the right, the cliffs rose steep and forbidding. To the left, the torrent rushed past, carving the narrow pass—the Gap—between the rocks and the sea.

High and safe within gunshot of the pass, I chose this wood as our campsite. We unpacked, set up the tent, and checked the trees for danger. Only wild cats—harmless enough—were found.

By dinner, we were exhausted. The heat was oppressive. We rested until evening, when coolness returned.

All was ready for tomorrow’s journey—into the country beyond the Gap. I planned a longer trek across the savannah than we’d ever attempted.

Early the next morning, we set out. My wife stayed with Franz. The three older boys, all dogs except Juno, went with me.

And so we began our final expedition—into the unknown.
