"I desire to speak with you," Baldassarre said, his voice cutting through the silence as Romola regarded him with a look of quiet, suspended expectation. It was evident he had trailed her, waiting patiently for this moment. She was finally prepared to confront the secret that hung over him.

"Yes," she replied, offering a submission that mirrored the tone of one accepting a penance imposed from above. "Yet you wish to withdraw where no ear can catch our words?"

"Where *he* will not stumble upon us," Baldassarre corrected, glancing nervously over his shoulder. "Out—into the open air—far from the noise of the streets."

"I occasionally visit San Miniato at this hour," Romola said. "If you will, I shall go forthwith, and you may follow. The distance is considerable, yet it guarantees solitude."

He nodded, and Romola set out. To lesser minds, venturing to such a secluded spot with a man bearing the outward marks of the madness Tito had attributed to him might have seemed a perilous gamble. But Romola possessed no such personal terrors; rather, she welcomed the separation, viewing it as a necessary buffer before the next blow could fall upon her spirit. The afternoon had deepened, the sun dipping low in the western sky, when she halted on rough terrain beneath the shadow of towering cypress trunks, scanning for Baldassarre. He was not distant, yet when he joined her, he collapsed onto the edge of a stony bank. His thickset frame, once robust and formidable when he first appeared in the Duomo with the rope around his neck, now lacked its former vigor. The exertion of climbing the hill had left his eyes with a helpless, vacant vagueness.

"The ascent is steep," Romola said, her voice soft with compassion as she sat beside him. "And I fear you have been sapped by hunger?"

He turned his head, fixing his gaze upon her in silence. When the moment demanded speech, he seemed unable to seize the words required to convey his thought. She remained as still as possible, lest he interpret her stillness as impatience. In this light, he appeared no higher than a common, neglected old man. Yet Romola was accustomed to the proximity of such suffering souls and pondered their troubles deeply. Slowly, a definite expression returned to his eyes, and with abrupt emphasis, he spoke:

"Ah! You would have been my daughter!"

A swift flush colored Romola's cheeks and faded just as quickly, leaving her lips parted in a pale, marble-like horror. The revelation struck her mind with the force of a physical blow. She divined the truths lying behind that single word, and in that first instant, the impulsive belief born of her keen experience with Tito's nature could not be checked. Her sensitive reaction served as a stimulus to Baldassarre; for the first time, his words achieved their intended effect. He proceeded with growing eagerness and firmness, laying his hand upon her arm.

"You are of proud blood—is it not so? You attend the preacher; you despise baseness—the baseness that smiles and triumphs. You hate your husband?"

"Oh God! Were you truly his father?" Romola whispered, her voice low, entirely consumed by the ghosts of the past and unable to register Baldassarre's question. "Or was it as he claimed? Did you take him when he was small?"

"Ah, you believe me—you know the truth of what he is!" Baldassarre exclaimed, tightening his grip on her arm as if the contact alone restored him power. "Will you aid me?"

"Yes," Romola answered, failing to interpret his words as he intended. She placed her palm gently over the rough hand clutching her arm, and tears welled in her eyes as she looked at him. "Oh, it is pitiful! Tell me—you were a great scholar; you taught him. How is this?"

She broke off. Tito's accusation of this man's insanity flashed through her mind, and where were the signs of past refinement? Yet she maintained her self-command, refusing to withdraw her hand. She sat perfectly still, waiting to listen with renewed caution.

"It is gone!—it is all gone!" Baldassarre cried. "They would not believe me because he lied, claiming I was mad, and they had me dragged to prison. I am old; my mind will not return. And the world stands against me."

He paused, his eyes sinking as if submerged under a wave of despondency. Then he looked up again, his voice renewing its eagerness. "But *you* are not against me. He made you love him, and he has proven false to you; you hate him. Yes, he made *me* love him: he was beautiful and gentle, while I was a lonely man. I took him when they were beating him. He slept in my bosom when he was little, and I watched him grow, giving him all my knowledge, everything I meant to be his. I possessed much—money, books, gems. He had my gems—he sold them; and he left me in slavery. He never sought me, and when I returned poor and in misery, he denied me. He called me a madman."

"He told us his father was dead—drowned," Romola murmured faintly. "Surely he must have believed it then. Oh! He could not have been so base *then*!"

A vision rose in her mind of what Tito represented to her in those early days when she thought no more of wrong in him than a child thinks of poison in flowers. The yearning regret inherent in that memory brought a fleeting relief from the tension of horror. With one great sob, tears rushed forth.

"Ah, you are young, and tears flow easily," Baldassarre said, with a hint of impatience. "But tears serve no purpose; they extinguish the fire within, and it is the fire that acts. Tears will hinder us. Listen to me."

Romola turned toward him with a slight start. Once more, the possibility of his madness darted through her mind, checking the rush of belief. If, after all, this man were merely a mad assassin? Yet her deep conviction in his story remained, and it was more out of sympathy than fear that she avoided risking pain to him by showing doubt.

"Tell me," she said as gently as possible, "how did you lose your memory—your scholarship?"

"I fell ill. I cannot say how long—it was a blank. I remember nothing, only that eventually I was sitting in the sun among the stones, and everything else was darkness. And slowly, by degrees, I felt something besides that: a longing for something—I knew not what—that never came. And when I was on the ship upon the waters, I began to know what I longed for; it was for the Boy to return—to find all my thoughts again, for I was locked away outside them all. And I am outside now. I feel nothing but a wall and darkness."

Baldassarre had become dreamy again, sinking into silence with his head resting between his hands. Once more, Romola's belief in him submerged all cautioning doubts. The pity with which she dwelt on his words seemed like the revival of an old pang. Had she not daily witnessed how her own father missed Dino and the future he had dreamed of in that son?

"It all returned once," Baldassarre continued presently. "I was master of everything. I saw the whole world again, my gems, my books; and I thought I held him in my power, so I went to expose him where—the lights shone and the trees stood. He lied again, saying I was mad, and they dragged me away to prison... Wickedness is strong; and he wears armor."

Fierceness flamed up within him again. He spoke with his former intensity, grasping Romola's arm once more.

"But you will help me? He has been false to you too. He has another wife, and she bears children. He makes her believe he is her husband, and she is a foolish, helpless creature. I will show you where she lives."

The first shock that passed through Romola was visibly one of anger. The woman's sense of indignity was inevitably paramount. Baldassarre instinctively felt her standing in sympathy with him.

"You hate him," he pressed on. "Is it not true? There is no love between you; I know that. I know women can hate; and you have proud blood. You hate falseness, and you can love revenge."

Romola sat paralyzed by the shock of conflicting emotions. She was unaware of the grasp that was bruising her tender arm.

"You shall contrive it," Baldassarre said presently, in an eager whisper. "I have committed to memory that you are his rightful wife. You are a noble woman. You go to hear the preacher of vengeance; you will aid justice. But you must think for me. My mind fails—everything goes sometimes—all but the fire. The fire is God: it is justice: it will not die. You believe that—is it not so? If they will not hang him for robbing me, you will take away his armor—you will make him go without it, and I will stab him. I possess a knife, and my arm is still strong enough."

He slid his hand beneath his tunic and drew out the hidden blade, feeling the edge abstractedly, as if he needed the sensation to keep his ideas alive.

To Romola, it seemed as if every fresh hour of her life was destined to become more difficult than the last. Her judgment was too vigorous and rapid for her to fall into the error of using futile deprecating words to a man in Baldassarre's state of mind. She chose not to answer his final speech. Instead, she would win time for his excitement to subside by asking something else she cared to know. She spoke rather tremulously—

"You say she is foolish and helpless—that other wife—and believes him to be her true husband. Perhaps he is: perhaps he married her before he married me."

"I cannot tell," Baldassarre replied, pausing in his action of feeling the knife, looking bewildered. "I remember no more. I only know where she lives. You shall see her. I will take you; but not now," he added hurriedly, "_he_ may be there. The night is approaching."

"It is true," Romola said, starting up with a sudden realization that the sun had set and the hills were darkening; "but you will come and take me—when?"

"In the morning," Baldassarre said, dreaming that she, too, wished to hasten to her vengeance.

"Come to me, then, where you came to me today, in the church. I will be there at ten; and if you are not there, I will go again towards mid-day. Can you remember?"

"Mid-day," Baldassarre repeated—"only mid-day. The same place, and mid-day. And, after that," he added, rising and grasping her arm again with his left hand while holding the knife in his right, "we will have our revenge. He shall feel the sharp edge of justice. The world is against me, but you will help me."

"I would help you in other ways," Romola said, making a first, timid effort to dispel his illusion regarding her. "I fear you are in want; you must labor and earn little. I should like to bring you comforts, and make you feel again that there is someone who cares for you."

"Speak no more of that," Baldassarre said fiercely. "I will accept nothing else. Help me to wring one drop of vengeance on this side of the grave. I have nothing but my knife. It is sharp; but there is a moment after the thrust when men see the face of death—and it shall be my face that he will see."

He released his hold and sank down again into a sitting posture. Romola felt helpless; she must defer all intentions until the morrow.

"Mid-day, then," she said in a distinct voice.

"Yes," he answered, with an air of exhaustion. "Go; I will rest here."

She hastened away. Turning at the last spot from whence he was likely to be visible, she saw him seated still.
