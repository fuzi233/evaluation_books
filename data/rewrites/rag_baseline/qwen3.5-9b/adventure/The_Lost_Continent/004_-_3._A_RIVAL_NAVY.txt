The horizon fractured, revealing the coast of Atlantis. Tob had navigated with a precision that bordered on the miraculous, yet the final stretch demanded ten more days of laborious retracing until we reached the arm of the sea where the capital waited. The land itself, exhaling a scent of earth and grass carried on the breeze, acted as a divine intervention. Amongst the long-voyaging crews, death and scurvy had carved a hollow silence; men were little more than logs, paralyzed by the unnatural salt and tossing of the deep. But the smell of soil broke the spell. Those who had been helpless suddenly found movement returning to their limbs, transforming from passive victims into active defenders, eager to salvage their lives and vessels.

Since my deposition in Yucatan, despite Tatho's reassurances, a shadow of uncertainty had lingered regarding my reception in Atlantis. Yet, I had surrendered this anxiety to the gods. Whether Empress Phorenice ruled supreme or severed my head the moment I stepped onto the shore mattered little; the Sun watched above her. If my head fell, it was His will. Thus, I had kept my mind empty of fear, turning instead to the higher mysteries.

Then, the navy completed its circuit. Before the two vast headlands marking the entrance to the inland waters—a distance of barely two days from the capital—we encountered another fleet. They were anchored in a sheltered bay, but scouts atop the cliffs signaled our approach. As we rounded the headland, they surged forth to block our path.

Five ships remained of ours, the rest lost to storms or consumed by scurvy. Against us lay three fine ships and three galleys, each bristling with oars. Their hulls were clean, black, and gleaming; ours were ragged, weather-beaten, fouled with seaweed. We displayed the colors of Tatho and Deucalion openly, a declaration of origin and purpose. The opposing navy advanced without banners or standards, moving with the shame of creatures hiding their lineage.

Conflict seemed inevitable. No maritime law binds brothers in separate vessels, and a captain feels free to prey upon another, especially one returning laden with plunder. Tob organized our vessel into fighting trim; the other captains mirrored the action, forming a compact squadron. They refused to accept the proximity of slavery.

The sun blazed, driving the machinery forward with divine impetus. The fleets closed rapidly, the galleys holding back to maintain formation. At roughly a hundred ship-lengths apart, the enemy halted. One galley pulled ahead, unfurling green branches from her masts to signal a parley.

Unusual, perhaps, but we, battered and weary, sought no unnecessary combat. Hoarse shouts passed between the decks. We halted. Tob raised a withered stick, substituting for foliage, signaling readiness for talk and respect for an ambassador.

The galley drew closer, swinging wide before backing until its stern scraped our shield rail. A figure climbed down onto our deck. He was young, robust, dressed in dandyish rigging, smelling of land and fresh provisions. He surveyed our tattered state with a sneer, then spotted Tob and nodded with familiar recognition.

"Old pot-mate," he called out. "Your woman awaits you on the quay-side in Atlantis, with four youngsters at her heels. I saw her not half a month ago."

"You didn't come out here for home news," Tob replied, his voice steady. "I've shared enough pots with you, Dason, to know your pleasantries."

"I wished to remind you that your home remains there, with your wife and children ready to welcome you."

"I am not a man who forgets," Tob said grimly. "Because they occupy my thoughts constantly, I have sailed this ship over more pirates than a single man could endure, choosing freedom over the hap of slavery."

"Oh, I know you're a desperate fellow," Dason admitted freely. "If this turns to blows, we'll lose a few men before we sink your cripples and your crazy ships. Our navy has orders. My embassy seeks to see if you will act sensibly and surrender what we demand, allowing you to depart with your skin intact."

"You seek to pluck the wrong bird," Tob laughed heavily. "We took no treasure or merchandise in Yucatan. We stayed only long enough to cure our stores and refill with water. We sail back as we left: barren ships. You won't believe me, of course; I wouldn't have believed you had our positions reversed. Search the holds if you wish. You'll find nothing but broken sailors suffering from scurvy. You cannot steal anything here, Dason, except blows—and we offer those freely."

"I am glad you value your cargo so lightly," the envoy said. "That is precisely the cargo I must transport back on the galley if you wish safe conduct home."

Tob furrowed his brow. "Speak plainly," he commanded. "I am a common sailor, unfamiliar with fancy talk."

"It is evident," Dason continued, "that you are being used to bring Deucalion back to Atlantis as a prop for Phorenice. We find her difficult enough to oppose without further reinforcement. We require Deucalion in our custody to dispose of according to our own designs."

"And if I refuse?"

The spruce envoy glanced at the splintered ship and the battered fleet beside it. "Then, Tob, we shall send you all to the fishes shortly. Instead of Deucalion standing before the Gods alone, he will descend with a ragged company limping at his heels."

"I doubt it," Tob countered. "As for yielding my Lord Deucalion, that is impossible. First, if I return to Atlantis without him, Tatho will eventually return, and in his hands I shall die slowly. Second, I have witnessed Deucalion slay a great sea-lizard; he proved such a proper man that day that I would not surrender him against his will, not even to Tatho. Third, you owe me for your share in our last wine-bout ashore. I'll see you with the nether Gods before I yield anything until that debt is settled."

"Well, Tob, I hope you drown easily," the envoy retorted. "As for that wife of yours, I've always fancied her, and I shall know how to utilize the woman."

"I'll draw your neck for that, you son of a European," Tob snapped. "If you do not clear this deck, I'll draw it here yourself. Go, you father of monkey children! Depart, and let me fight you fairly, or by my honor I'll stamp your insides out and make your foolish crew wear them as necklaces."

With that, Dason retreated to his galley.

Immediately, Tob engaged the machine on our "Bear," shouting orders to the surrounding ships. Though the crews were weakened by scurvy, they responded with alacrity. Instantly, the five vessels surged forward, catching the full force of the sunlit wind. Our small navy converged, isolating one enemy ship. Unprepared for such rapid aggression, she attempted to turn and flee rather than meet us bow-on. Consequently, all five of our ships struck her simultaneously on the broadside, tearing her planking with their underwater beaks and sinking her before we withdrew.

Reducing the enemy count to five, matching our own numbers, the advantage shifted briefly. The three nimble galleys formed a line; their boatswains' whips cracked as slaves bent to the oars. Soon, one of our ships was gored and sunk, her crew perishing in the water without hope of rescue.

What followed was a melee that would have warmed the heart of any warrior. Ships and galleys were forced together, grinding savagely upon the swells as if sentient beasts. Men exchanged arrows, slashed with axes, thrust with swords, and hurled throwing fire. Yet, every assault converged upon the "Bear." It was her that bore the brunt of their spite; it was to her that the other crews of Tatho's navy rallied as their own vessels caught fire, sank, or were captured.

Battle is an old companion to us of the Priestly Clan, frequent enough to cloy even the most chivalrous appetite. Having heard "Deucalion" shouted as a battlecry for half a lifetime, I had witnessed many lusty encounters. This sea-fight, however, surpassed them in savage ferocity. The bleak, unstable element surrounding us; the swaying decks; the throwing fire that consumed flesh and wood alike; the great, gluttonous man-eating birds hovering overhead; the man-eating fish swarming from the depths to devour the fallen—all combined to create circumstances fit to daunt the bravest armies.

Yet, these seasoned shipmen faced it all with indomitable courage, never uttering a cry of fear. Life on the seas is so harsh, fraught with savage dangers from the beasts of the deep, that Death has lost half his terror through sheer familiarity. These were men who, from pure lust for a fray, would fight to the finish amongst themselves in taverns ashore. Here, in this desperate conflict, the passion for killing burned within them like a fire-stone raging in a forest; they took even their death-wounds laughing.

On our side, the battle-cry was "Tob!" The name of this obscure ship-captain carried a confidence that many renowned commanders might envy. The enemy possessed a dozen rallying cries, confusing their ranks. But as their other commanders fell one by one, and only Dason remained active in mischief, "Dason!" became the shout thrown back at us in response to our "Tob!"
