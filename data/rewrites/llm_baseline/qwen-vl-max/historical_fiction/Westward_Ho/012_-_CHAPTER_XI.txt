“Misguided, rash, intruding fool, farewell! Thou see'st to be too busy is some danger.”

—Hamlet.

It was the spring of 1582–83. The March sky hung low and heavy, a slate-gray vault pressing down upon jagged mountain peaks still capped with winter’s last breath. A biting wind swept across a desolate expanse of bog, where patches of frost lingered in shades of rust and gold. Only one rise broke the flatness—a bare knoll crowned by gnarled oaks, their branches twisted like the limbs of ancient beasts, reaching over a frozen pool that cracked and shivered under the cold. From its edge came a mournful cry, carried on the wind, fading into silence as if swallowed by the bleak air.

Along the bog’s edge, a troop of English soldiers moved swiftly, navigating through wet rocks and spongy moss. Clad head to toe in armor—helmets, quilted jerkins, arquebuses slung over shoulders, pikes dragging behind—they were men hardened by war. Two years earlier, they had manned the guns at Smerwick Fort; since then, they had faced bloodshed again and again, and would face it many more times before their lives ended. At their head rode two captains: one tall and grim, his armor scarred from countless battles; the other resplendent in polished cuirass, plumed helmet, golden hilt, and gaudy sash—an oddly bright figure atop a shaggy pony that seemed ill-suited to such finery.

Beside them walked an Irish kerne, bare-legged, wrapped only in a tattered yellow cloak, his unkempt hair framing wild eyes that darted about with fear and defiance. His wrists were bound to a pikeman’s by a cord—a prisoner, but also guide. He led them in pursuit of Baltinglas, the rebel lord, and if he failed, there would be no mercy.

“A pleasant land indeed, Captain Raleigh,” said the rougher captain, Amyas, glancing at his companion’s gleaming armor. “I wonder how you found the courage to return after escaping to Whitehall, only to ruin your fine clothes in this mud and mire.”

“A very pleasant land,” Raleigh replied, his voice steady. “What you say jestingly, I say earnestly.”

Amyas laughed. “Ah, our tastes have swapped places. I’m already weary of it, just as you predicted. I’d give anything to hear of some adventure Westward-ho!—to feel the sea breeze again, and see those great bones swinging in a hammock. But tell me—what has turned you so suddenly fond of bogs and stone? I thought you’d long since seen through the nakedness of this land.”

“Bogs and rocks?” Raleigh smiled. “Nakedness? What this soil lacks is not nature, but people to work it. Look—this earth could feed thousands if tilled. These stones? Who knows what treasures lie beneath? Gold and jewels have already been found here. My brother Humphrey’s German assayer says these very rocks resemble those in Peru that yield silver. By God’s grace, if Her Majesty would grant me a few square miles of this wilderness, within seven years I’d make it bloom like a rose.”

“Well,” Amyas muttered, “then I might stay.”

“You shall, and serve as my agent—collecting mine rents, corn rents, fishery rents. Could you keep accounts, old knight of the bear’s paw?”

“Good enough for your profit ledger, at least. But no—no more than twelve months in Ireland again. Not among the children of wrath. There’s a curse upon the land, I swear.”

“There is no curse save man’s own sin,” Raleigh answered firmly. “‘Thorns and thistles it shall bring forth to thee.’ But if you root up the thorns, Amyas, who can stop you from planting wheat? If you till the ground like a man, you overturn nature’s curse—and all the foolish fables of the schoolmen besides.” He spoke with a boldness that later earned him accusations of atheism—though none could claim he deserved such a name.

“Sword and bullet are needed here,” Amyas insisted, “before plough and harrow. Until more of these Irish lords follow Desmond into the grave, there’ll be no peace.”

“Perhaps,” Raleigh said slowly, “you’re not far wrong. Yet consider—these rebels may carry purer English blood than we who hunt them. When Yeo slew Desmond recently, he shed no more Irish blood than if he’d killed the Lord Deputy himself.”

“His blood be on his own head,” Yeo interjected. “He looked like a savage, worse than any of them. Shame on him. And the old man nearly cut off his arm before he confessed who he was. Then, sir, with a price on his head and bleeding badly—”

“Enough,” Raleigh said gently. “You’ve done your duty. Strange, isn’t it? Noble Normans reduced to savages—*Hibernis ipsis hiberniores!* Is there some poison in the air?”

“Some venom, at least, that turns Englishmen into traitors,” Amyas replied. “But the Irish themselves are decent enough, if their tyrants let them live. Take Inchiquin—Prince of Themond, they say, rightful king of all Ireland if every man had his due.”

“Don’t speak of rights in a land ruled by wrongs,” Raleigh said. “Yet Inchiquin knows well that the true Irish Esau has no fiercer enemy than the Norman Jacob. Still—would we be better than these men, had we grown up masters over others’ bodies and souls in some distant land where law never reached? Look at Desmond—raised among savages, a Papist among Papists, a despot among slaves. Thousands of maidens saw honor in serving him; thousands of ruffians called piety the fulfillment of his vengeance. Let him who is without sin cast the first stone.”

Raleigh fell into silence. “What would I have become,” he murmured, “had I been born Desmond, whose lands I now desire? What will I become when I possess them? Will my children descend as these noble barons did? Will tyranny and falsehood take root in my heart? What guarantee do I have that I’ll fare better than those who came before? And yet—can I not do justice and love mercy? Can I not build plantations, sow fields, and make desert valleys laugh with grain? Shall I not have Spenser beside me, filling my soul with noble thoughts, raising me to heroic heights? Is this not true knight-errantry—to redeem a generous land and a nobler people to peace, for the glory of our glorious queen? They are trusting, tender-hearted—none more so. Though passionate and fickle, that very softness that makes them easy to lead astray may also make them easy to guide toward good. Yes—here, away from court, among people who might bless me as their benefactor—what golden days might be mine! And yet—is this another mask worn by ambition’s cunning fiend? Will my house truly be God’s house, built on loyalty and righteousness—or merely the house of fame, with walls of soap-bubble and floors of fire-mixed glass? I want to be good and great—but when will I be content simply to be good, like this simple Leigh, walking beside me, doing his duty with no thought beyond tomorrow, like the birds of God? Greatness? I’ve tasted that cup in the past year. I know it’s sweet in the mouth, bitter in the belly. Greatness? Essex was great. John of Austria was great. Desmond was great—his family once stood higher than I could ever dream. Castles, lands, thousands of slaves, five hundred gentlemen sworn to die for him—sworn even to forsake God before they abandoned him. And now—dead in a turf hut, like a rabbit in a burrow!”

“Captain,” Leigh said, “did you hear that sound?”

“I thought it an Irish wail,” Leigh replied. “But it came from the bog. Might be a plover.”

“Something feels wrong,” the ancient soldier said. “They speak of pucks and banshees here. That cry—it sounded like a woman. They say the banshee cried all night before Desmond died.”

“Then perhaps this one mourns Baltinglas,” Raleigh said. “His turn may come next. Though I don’t believe in such tales.”

“Shamus,” Amyas called to the guide, “do you hear it?”

The guide fixed a blank stare. “Shamus hear naught. Perhaps—what you call him?—fishing in ta pool.”

“An otter,” Raleigh said. “And likely right. Wait—did you not hear it, Shamus? It was a woman’s voice.”

“Shamus is shick in his ears since Christmas.”

“Shamus will go after Desmond if he lies,” Amyas said. “Ancient, send a few men to investigate. There may be a poor soul taken by robbers—or starving, as I’ve seen many.”

“I’ve seen them too,” the ancient said. “Not their fault, nor ours. But when lords quarrel, drive each other’s cattle, waste each other’s lands—well, you know—”

“I know,” Amyas snapped. “Why don’t you go?”

“Cry you mercy, noble captain,” the old man said. “I fear nothing born of woman.”

“And what of that?” Amyas asked with a smile.

“But these pucks, sir. The wild Irish say they haunt the pools. They do no harm when you approach—but when you pass, sir, they leap on your back like apes. Who can fight such a fiend?”

“Then by your own logic,” Raleigh said, “you may go safely. And if the puck jumps on you on the way back—just run in with him here. I’ll buy him for a noble. Or keep him caged and show him in London as a monster.”

“Good heavens forefend!” the ancient gasped. “But if I must, Captain Leigh—”

“Lads, who’ll follow me?”

“You ask for volunteers as if leading a forlorn hope,” Raleigh teased. “Pull out the usquebaugh, man—swallow Dutch courage. Your English is leaking out. Stay—I’ll go.”

“And I with you,” Raleigh declared. “As the queen’s true knight-errant, I cannot lag behind in any adventure. Who knows—we may find a wicked magician about to sever the head of some saffron-robed princess?” And he dismounted.

“Oh, sirs, to endanger your precious—”

“Pooh,” Raleigh said. “I wear an amulet. I have a spell at my tongue’s end—no ghost can see me, nor I them. Come, Yeo, the Desmond-slayer. Let’s shame the devil—or be shamed by him.”

“He may shame me, sir,” Yeo said, “but never frighten me. But the bog, captains?”

“Tut!” Raleigh scoffed. “Devonshire men, born on the heath—don’t you know your way over a peat moor?”

And the three set off.

They splashed and scrambled for half a mile, the cry growing louder. “That’s no ghost or otter,” Yeo said. “A true Irish wail, as Captain Leigh said. And I’ll wager Master Shamus knew it all along.”

Soon they heard clearly the cry—“Ochone, Ochonorie”—of a wild woman. Over the boulders of the knoll, they stumbled upon her.

She was young, unkempt, dressed only in a yellow mantle. She sat on a stone, tearing at her black, tangled hair, lifting her head every now and then to wail—a sound like a beast, not a human. On her lap lay the head of a middle-aged man, wearing the long robe of a Roman priest. One glance told them he was dead.

They paused, struck by awe. Raleigh, always sensitive to poetic imagery, felt the weight of the scene—the harsh sky, the shapeless bog, the stunted trees, the solitary girl mourning over the corpse in utter isolation. As she bent over the lifeless face, calling to one who could not hear, then burst into another mournful cry, a sacred horror gripped them—so deep they almost turned away. But Yeo, tougher in spirit, asked quietly:

“Shall I search him, captain?”

“Better,” Amyas agreed.

Raleigh approached the girl gently, speaking in English. She looked up—wide-eyed, startled by his armor and plume—then shook her head and returned to her lament.

Raleigh placed a hand on her arm and lifted her. Meanwhile, Yeo and Amyas examined the body.

It was a large man, coarse-featured, but wasted—shrunken to a skeleton by hunger. His limbs were curled, his torso bowed—clear signs of starvation or exposure. Yeo pulled back the clothing from his chest while the girl screamed, but made no move to stop him.

“Ask her who he is,” Amyas said. “Yeo, you know some Irish.”

Yeo asked. The girl said nothing. “Stubborn jade won’t speak, of course,” he muttered. “If she were a man, I’d make her talk.”

“Ask who killed him.”

“No one,” she said. And Yeo believed her—for he found no wound. “The man starved,” he said. “God help him, though he’s a priest. Yet he seems full down below. What’s this? A pouch—stuffed with something.”

“Hand it here.”

They opened it—papers, papers, no food. Then a parchment. They unrolled it.

“Latin,” Amyas said. “You must translate, Don Scholar.”

Raleigh read. “Is it possible?” he whispered. “This is Saunders himself!”

Yeo jumped back as if bitten. “Nick Saunders, the Legacy?”

“Nicholas Saunders, the legate.”

“The villain!” Yeo spat. “Why didn’t he wait for me to kill him? Dog!” And he kicked the corpse.

“Quiet!” Amyas warned. “Remember the girl.” She shrieked at the insult.

Raleigh continued, half to himself:

“Yes—Saunders. Misguided fool. This is your end. You brought your plotting, your lies, your banners, your bulls, your holy waters, your saints and angels, your Lady of the Immaculate Conception. You called on heaven to judge between us—and here is their verdict. What’s in his hand, Amyas? Give it to me. A pastoral letter to the Earl of Ormond and all nobles of Ireland—‘To all who groan beneath the loathsome tyranny of an illegitimate adulteress… Nicholas Saunders, by the grace of God, Legate…’ Bah! This was your final thought? Incorrigible pedant! *Victrix causa Diis placuit, sed victa Catoni!*”

He scanned the rest—letters filled with grand promises from the Pope and King Philip, vile slanders against Elizabeth, Burghley, Leicester, Essex, Sidney—any man who upheld the realm. Threats of eternal fire for dissenters. Martyrdom proclaimed for every rebel hanged in the past twenty years. Arguments over the bull *In Caena Domini*, Elizabeth’s excommunication, the nullity of English law, the duty to rebel, the right to kill a heretical prince—madness and treachery alike, recorded in Camden, Fox’s *Martyrs*, or the writings of the conspirators themselves.

With a gesture of disgust, Raleigh shoved the documents back into the pouch. They took it and returned to their company, remounted, and marched on—leaving the girl alone with the dead.

An hour passed. Another Englishman stood beside her, surrounded by a dozen kernes—tawny-haired, javelins in hand, their ragged cloaks fluttering as they wailed alongside her.

It was Eustace Leigh. Still a layman, yet still clinging to his old purpose. For two years, he had labored across Ireland, seeking to satisfy his conscience for rejecting the celibate life—though the fire of hope still burned in his heart. His brow was furrowed, his face harder, the scar on his cheek hidden beneath a thick beard. He never forgot it, nor who gave it to him.

He had been with Desmond, wandering through moor and marsh for months, risking death daily. Now he was heading to James Fitz-Eustace, Lord Baltinglas, to deliver news of Desmond’s death—and with him, a remnant of the clan too defiant or too guilty to surrender.

There stood Eustace, looking down at the remains of the most sacred figure in Ireland—the man who, once, he had hoped, would regenerate his homeland, unite Christendom, and restore the island to the gentle yoke of the Church. Here he was—starved to death in a bog, amid faithful Catholics, valiant soldiers, nobles who had vowed to die for the cause, serfs who worshipped him as a god—yet perished in silence.

It was a cruel verdict on his dreams. But not to Eustace.

It was a failure—yes—but an accident. In a world ruled by the devil, such things were expected. And proof, even, of the cause’s righteousness. For had not the true faith always suffered?

“If one sees with eyes purified from carnal impatience,” he murmured, “this is not defeat—but martyrdom.”

“Blest Saunders,” he whispered. “Let me die as the righteous die. May my end be like yours. *Ora pro me*, noble martyr, while I dig your grave on this lonely moor, waiting for your translation to one of those stately shrines—built with blood like yours—that will rise again, restored to heaven, making this land once more ‘The Isle of Saints.’”

The body was buried. A few prayers were said. Then Eustace left—not for Baltinglas. He knew the English soldiers had passed. They would reach the earl before him. The game was over. So he retraced his steps, hiding, disguising himself, until after a month he found himself back in Devon—while Baltinglas fled to Spain, having gained little from his argument with Ormond: “Had not thy ancestor, blessed Thomas of Canterbury, died for the Church of Rome, thou hadst never been Earl of Ormond.” Sound premises, perhaps—but the conclusion was as weak as the rest.

Now, let us return to Raleigh and Amyas, riding along their weary road. They had much to discuss—only three days since they met.

Amyas was beginning to see Ireland through Raleigh’s eyes. Raleigh, inspired by the possibility of a grant of Desmond’s lands, saw bogs and rocks transformed by hope—like a rainbow transfiguring the landscape. Even thirty years later, when old and broken, he saw the world this way. How fortunate for him if his heart had aged with his mind.

Amyas, unaware of the land grant, puzzled at the change.

“What is this, Raleigh?” he asked. “You’re like children in a market—nothing pleases you. You wanted Court, and got it. You’re lord and master, they say. And now, as soon as fortune fills your mouth with sweets, you turn informant on her?”

Raleigh laughed, but said nothing.

“And how is Mr. Secretary Spenser, who was with us at Smerwick?”

“Spenser? He’s thrived—as I have. He’s learned, as I have, that making one friend at Court earns ten enemies. But *Oderint dum metuant* is no longer my motto—or his. I want to be great—great I already am, they say, if princes’ favor can swell a frog into an ox. But I want to be liked. Loved. I want people to smile when I enter.”

“They do,” Amyas said.

“So do hyenas,” Raleigh replied. “They grin because they’re hungry. I may throw them a bone. I’ll throw you one now—old lad, or rather a good sirloin of beef—for the sake of your smile. That’s honest, at least.”

“Have you heard of my brother Humphrey’s new project?”

“How should I, in this howling waste?”

“Kiss hands to the wilderness,” Raleigh said, “and come with me to Newfoundland!”

“You to Newfoundland?”

“Yes. I to Newfoundland—unless this matter here is settled soon. Gloriana doesn’t know, and won’t until I’m gone. She’d send me to the Tower if she caught me playing truant. I barely got leave to come here. But I must go—try my fortune. I’m neck-deep in debt, sick of courts and courtiers. Humphrey must take possession next spring—or his patent expires. With him I go. And you too, my circumnavigating giant.”

And so Raleigh explained to Amyas the details of the Newfoundland venture—details any reader may find in Hakluyt’s writings.
