It was the thirtieth of October, 1496. The morning sky was clear, and a gentle autumnal breeze swept across the city. Yet, the Florentines paid little heed to the land breeze; their minds were consumed by the violent gales raging at sea. These storms seemed to conspire with every other force to refute the Friar's assertion that Heaven watched over Florence with special care. Terrible squalls had driven away from the coast of Leghorn several vessels from Marseilles laden with soldiers and grain. Florence stood in desperate need, first of food, and secondly of fighting men. Pale Famine stalked her streets, and her borders were threatened on all sides.

The French King, hailed as a new Charlemagne, had entered Italy in triumph, conquering Naples with ease, only to depart fifteen months prior. Rumors suggested he was now grieving the loss of a newborn son, causing him to lose even the languid intention of returning to settle grievances and reform the Church. In response, a "Holy League" had been formed under the leadership of Pope Borgia, ostensibly to "drive out the barbarians"—the French garrisoning Naples. While this sounded patriotic, it resembled an agreement among wolves to expel rival wolves merely to determine who could seize the largest share of the prey. Consequently, Florence was viewed less as a fellow wolf and more as a desirable carcass. Of all the major Italian states, Florence alone had refused to join the League, maintaining her allegiance to France. She had declined at her peril.

At this critical juncture, Pisa, fighting savagely for its liberty, was bolstered not only by forces from Venice and Milan but also by the German Emperor Maximilian, invited by the League. Maximilian arrived with troops to aid the Pisans in seizing Leghorn, while Venetian and Genoese ships blockaded the coast. If Leghorn fell, disaster would surely follow for Florence. Cut off from the sea and hemmed in by the Pope's hostility and the jealousy of smaller states, how could supplies reach her? The Florentine government had responded to this crisis with remarkable resolve, meeting losses and defeats with vigor, raising funds and recruiting soldiers, while not abandoning the traditional Italian method of conciliatory diplomacy. Despite the growing scarcity of food, they had decided, breaking with old precedent, not to exclude the starving peasants and mendicants fleeing other cities like birds from a blizzard.

These benevolent acts, characteristic of a regime dominated by Savonarola's disciples, drew sharp criticism. Disaffected citizens argued that the government pursued the wrong path for public welfare. They insisted Florence should join the League to align with powerful neighbors rather than inviting hostility through a futile attachment to a foreign ally. Furthermore, they believed the state should prioritize its own citizens instead of opening its gates to famine and pestilence in the form of destitute foreigners. Distress grew sharper daily, and murmurs grew louder. Compounding these difficulties, Fra Girolamo had been forbidden to preach for over a month by a mandate from Rome. However, upon receiving the terrifying news that the Marseilles ships had been repelled and no grain was arriving, the need for a voice capable of instilling faith and patience became irresistible. Defying the Papal command, the Signoria summoned Savonarola. Two days prior, he had returned to the pulpit of the Duomo, urging the people to wait steadfastly, promising that divine help would inevitably arrive. He boldly offered to strip off his friar's robe if God failed to save Florence despite their piety. Yet, on this bleak morning of the thirtieth, no signs of rescue were visible.

Perhaps, the citizens wondered, bringing the sacred Tabernacle of the Madonna dell'Impruneta into Florence and carrying it in a devout procession to the Duomo would invoke the Mother's mercy? For a century and a half, records showed that whenever Florence suffered from drought, flood, famine, plague, or war, fetching this potent image within their walls had secured deliverance. Wealth had been lavished upon her shrine, and lands granted until disputes arose over the honor of serving her. The Florentines were deeply convinced of her grace; the sight of her tabernacle within their walls was akin to clouds parting, leading to the proverb that they possessed a Madonna who would grant whatever they wished. Never had they needed her intercession more urgently.

The previous evening, the tabernacle containing the miraculous hidden image had been escorted from L'Impruneta, six miles beyond the Gate of San Piero facing Rome, and placed in the church of San Gaggio outside the walls. From there, it was to be retrieved in a solemn procession by all the fraternities, guilds, and authorities of Florence. But the Pitying Mother had not yet entered the city. Morning broke over unchanged misery and despair. Pestilence trailed the footsteps of famine. Hospitals were overflowing, and private courtyards had been converted into infirmaries, yet unsheltered want persisted. Early that morning, as was custom, members of the various burial fraternities carried away corpses that had perished by the roadside. Among the crowds moved sweet, well-born women in simple garments, diligently tending the sick and feeding the hungry.

One such figure was unmistakably Romola de' Bardi. Dressed in the simplest black serge, with a plain black veil hiding her hair except for the bands of gold rippling across her brow, she advanced from the Ponte Vecchio toward the Porta Santa Maria. Her path was blocked by a bier paused by members of the Company of San Jacopo del Popolo, searching for the unburied dead. The brothers at the front bent to inspect something, while a cluster of idle workers, their faces gaunt and sharpened by hunger, gathered around, speaking in a chaotic murmur.

"He's dead, I tell you! Messer Domeneddio loved him enough to take him!"
"And wouldn't it be wonderful if we could stretch out our legs and walk ahead with our heads two or three *bracci* further? Standing upright with hunger is no support."
"Well, he's an old fellow. Death got a bad bargain; life won out."
"No Florentine, ten to one! A beggar turned out of Siena. May San Giovanni protect us! They don't need soldiers to fight us; they send us an army of starving men."
"No, no! This man is a prisoner released from the Stinche. I know by the grey patch where the prison badge was."
"Hush! Help them! Don't you see the brothers are lifting him onto the bier?"
"It's possible he's alive if he could just look it. Maybe the soul is still inside if he had a drop of *vernaccia* to warm him."
"In truth, I think he isn't dead," said one of the brothers after lifting him. "He has probably just collapsed from lack of food."
"Let me try giving him some wine," Romola said, stepping forward.

She loosened the small flask at her belt, leaned toward the prostrate body, and with a skilled hand inserted a small ivory implement between his teeth, pouring a few drops of wine. The stimulus worked; the liquid was swallowed. She poured more until the old man's head shifted slightly toward her, and his eyes opened fully upon her with a vague look of returning consciousness. Only then did a sense of complete recognition strike Romola. Those wild, dark eyes opening in the sallow, deep-lined face, framed by a long white beard, were like an unmistakable signature to a remembered handwriting. The light of two summers had not faded that image in her memory: the picture of the escaped prisoner she had seen in the Duomo the day Tito first donned armor, the very man whose grasp had made Tito turn pale in the strange sketch found in Piero's studio.

A trembling seized her, a palpitation of fear. Now, perhaps, she was about to learn a secret more bitter than anything past. An impulse drove her to flee as from a horror, yet a stronger, imperious need compelled her to stay beside this old man whom her intuition told her her husband had harmed. In that moment of conflict, she remained leaning over him, her right hand ready to offer more wine, her left supporting his neck. Her hands trembled, yet their habitual kindness guided them without conscious direction.

Baldassarre looked at *her* for the first time. The isolation of Romola's recent troubles had prevented him from seeing the wife who lived in the Via de' Bardi, and the descriptions he had heard of the fair, golden-haired woman had vanished like yesterday's waves.

"Shall we not carry him to the steps of San Stefano?" Romola asked. "Then we shall not block the street, and you may proceed with your bier."

They moved only thirty yards before reaching the church steps. By then, Baldassarre was strong enough to sit up, propping himself against the doorway. The charitable brothers continued on, but the crowd of idlers, having nothing to do and much to say, had swelled. The sentiment toward the old man had shifted; knowing he was alive made the atmosphere less friendly, though Romola's presence tempered the comments into a quieter tone.
