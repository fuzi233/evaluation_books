It’s strange, at fifty-five—just past my last birthday—that I should find myself picking up a pen to write down a story. I wonder what kind of tale it will become when I’m done—if I ever finish it at all. I’ve lived a long life, though not one that most would call ordinary. I began working young, before many boys had even left school, earning my living as a trader in the old Colony. Since then, I’ve traded, hunted, fought, and mined—all across the wild corners of Africa. And yet, just eight months ago, I finally made my fortune. It’s a sizable pile now—though I still don’t know exactly how big—and I wouldn’t go through the last fifteen or sixteen months again for all the gold in the world. Not even if I knew I’d come out unscathed.

I suppose it’s because I’m naturally cautious. I hate violence. And lately, I’ve grown weary of adventure. So why am I writing this? It’s not something I’d normally do. I’m no writer. But I have my reasons—four of them, to be precise.

First: Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked me to. Second: I’m laid up here in Durban with pain in my left leg. Ever since a lion clamped his jaws around it, I’ve been plagued by flare-ups—especially around the same time each year. There must be some poison in those teeth, or else why would a healed wound reopen like that? It’s hard enough to take when you’ve shot sixty-five lions in your lifetime, only for the sixty-sixth to chew your leg like a piece of tobacco. It disrupts everything. And I’m an orderly man—I dislike disorder.

Third: I want my son Harry, who’s studying medicine at a hospital in London, to have something to read. Hospital work can grow dull—cutting up dead bodies, day after day. This story won’t be dull. It’ll give him a little excitement while he’s reading about our journey.

And fourth: Because I’m going to tell the strangest tale I’ve ever lived through. A tale without women—well, almost. There’s Foulata, and perhaps Gagaoola, though she was more monster than woman. She was over a hundred, so marriageable she wasn’t. No petticoats in this book, I promise.

Now, let’s get started. It’s a heavy task, and I feel as though I’m stuck in mud up to the axle. But “sutjes, sutjes,” as the Boers say—slow and steady wins the race. A strong team will pull through, if they’re not too weak. You can’t do much with poor oxen.

So here goes: I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, begin this account—not with a legal oath, as I did when giving testimony about the deaths of Khiva and Ventvögel—but simply as a man recalling events from memory. I’m not sure I’m a gentleman. What is a gentleman? I don’t know. But I’ve known men—white men with money—who weren’t, and others—natives—who were. I was born into a decent family, though I’ve spent most of my life as a hunter and trader. Whether I’ve stayed one, well, that’s for you to judge. I’ve killed men—many of them—but never without cause. Never innocent blood. I believe God gave us life to defend, and I’ve always acted on that. Heaven knows, I’ve tried.

Still, the world is cruel and wicked. And though I’m timid by nature, I’ve found myself in plenty of fights. I can’t justify every action, but I’ve never stolen—except once. I cheated a Kafir out of a herd of cattle. He’d wronged me first, and I’ve regretted it ever since.

But let’s return to the beginning. It was eighteen months ago when I first met Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good.

I’d been elephant hunting beyond Bamangwato, and things had gone badly. Everything went wrong. Then I caught a severe fever. When I recovered, I sold my ivory, my wagon, and my oxen, discharged my hunters, and took the post-cart to the Cape.

After a week in Cape Town—where I found the hotel overpriced and saw the botanical gardens (a fine thing for the country) and the new Parliament buildings (which I doubt will amount to much)—I decided to return to Natal by sea. The *Dunkeld* was waiting at dock, bound for England via the *Edinburgh Castle*. I booked a berth and boarded the ship.

That afternoon, passengers from the *Edinburgh Castle* transferred over, and we set sail. Among them were two men who caught my attention.

The first was a tall, broad-shouldered man of about thirty—perhaps the largest-chested and longest-armed fellow I’d ever seen. He had thick yellow hair and beard, sharp features, and deep-set grey eyes. He looked like an ancient Dane—or at least like someone from a painting I once saw of Norsemen drinking from horns, their long hair flowing down their backs. If he’d let his hair grow, slipped on a chain shirt, and gripped a battle-axe, he might have stepped straight out of that picture. Later, I learned his name was Sir Henry Curtis—and yes, he had Danish blood. He reminded me of someone else, too, though I couldn’t place who at the time.

The second man was shorter, dark, and stocky. I sensed immediately he was a naval officer—something about the way he stood, the set of his jaw. I’ve traveled with navy men before, and they’ve always been brave, honest, and good company—though some could swear like sailors.

I soon confirmed my guess. His name was Captain John Good—a lieutenant turned commander after seventeen years of service, but forced out of the Royal Navy with no prospects. That’s the fate of many who serve the Queen: trained, experienced, and then cast aside when they’re at their peak. I pity them. I’d rather earn my bread as a hunter. The pay may be less, but you don’t get kicked as often.

Captain Good was neat, clean-shaven, and wore an eye-glass in his right eye—no string, just fixed there like a part of him. He never removed it except to wipe it. At first, I thought he slept in it, but later I discovered he tucked it in his pocket along with two sets of false teeth—so perfect, in fact, that I’ve often envied them.

The weather turned foul shortly after we sailed. A cold wind blew in from shore, bringing a thick, driving mist. The *Dunkeld*, being flat-bottomed and light, rolled heavily—so much so it seemed she might capsize. I stood near the engine room, where it was warm, watching the pendulum swing back and forth with each roll.

“That pendulum’s off,” said a voice beside me. “It’s not properly weighted.”

I turned. It was Captain Good.

“Indeed?” I replied. “How so?”

“I don’t think—it’s obvious. Look—when the ship rights herself, if it truly showed the angle, she’d never come back up. But these merchant skippers are always careless.”

Just then, dinner was called. I was glad. Nothing worse than listening to a naval officer complain about merchant captains—unless it’s hearing a captain complain about the navy.

We went down together. Sir Henry Curtis was already seated. He and the captain sat side by side; I took the seat opposite. We fell into conversation—about hunting, animals, the land. Then Captain Good brought up elephants.

“Ah,” said a voice nearby, “you’ve found the right man. Hunter Quatermain knows elephants better than anyone.”

Sir Henry, who had been quiet, suddenly leaned forward.

“Excuse me, sir,” he said, his voice low and deep—fitting for such a large man—“but is your name Allan Quatermain?”

“It is,” I answered.

He said nothing more, but muttered “fortunate” under his breath.

After dinner, Sir Henry invited me to his cabin. I accepted. It was a spacious deck cabin—originally two, but knocked together when Sir Garnet Wolseley passed through. There was a sofa, a table, and a lamp. He sent for whisky, and we lit our pipes.

“Mr. Quatermain,” he said, once the lamp was lit, “the year before last, around this time, you were at Bamangwato, north of the Transvaal?”

“I was,” I replied, surprised he knew.

“You were trading there?”

“Yes. I brought a wagonload of goods, set up camp outside the settlement, and waited until I sold them.”

Sir Henry leaned forward, his grey eyes fixed on mine. There was something urgent in them.

“Did you meet a man named Neville?”

“Oh, yes. He stopped beside me for two weeks to rest his oxen before heading deeper into the interior. I received a letter from a lawyer asking if I knew what became of him. I answered as best I could.”

“Your letter reached me,” Sir Henry said. “You mentioned that Neville left Bamangwato in May with a driver, a voorlooper, and a Kafir hunter named Jim. He planned to reach Inyati—the farthest trading post among the Matabele—sell his wagon, and continue on foot.”

“Yes,” I said.

A silence followed.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “do you know anything more about the purpose of his journey? Or where he intended to go?”

“I heard something,” I said, hesitating.

They exchanged glances. Captain Good nodded.

“Then I shall tell you a story,” Sir Henry continued. “And ask for your advice—and perhaps your help. The agent who forwarded your letter told me I could trust you implicitly. You’re well known in Natal—respected, discreet.”

I bowed. “I do my best.”

“Mr. Neville,” Sir Henry said, “was my brother.”

I started. Now I understood why he reminded me of someone. His brother was smaller, with a dark beard, but the eyes—same shade of grey, same sharpness. The features were similar.

“He was my only younger brother,” Sir Henry went on. “We were inseparable until five years ago, when a quarrel tore us apart. I behaved unjustly in my anger.”

Captain Good nodded slowly.

“At that time, our father died intestate. He’d delayed making a will. As a result, the estate passed to me—his eldest son. My brother, who had no profession, was left with nothing. I should have provided for him. But the quarrel was bitter, and I waited for him to reach out. He didn’t. To my shame, I did nothing.”

He sighed. “I’ve come to understand that blood is thicker than water.”

“I’ve learned that too,” I said, thinking of Harry.

“And I would have given half my fortune,” Sir Henry said, “to know my brother George was safe. To see him again.”

“But you never did,” Captain Good interrupted, looking at him sharply.

“No,” Sir Henry said quietly. “Time passed. I grew anxious. I began searching. Your letter was one result. It proved George was alive—at least recently. But it didn’t tell me where he was. So I decided to come to Africa myself. Captain Good offered to join me.”

“Nothing else to do,” the captain said. “Turned out by my Lords of the Admiralty. Half-pay and no prospects. So I came.”

He turned to me. “Now, Mr. Quatermain—what do you know of this man Neville?”
