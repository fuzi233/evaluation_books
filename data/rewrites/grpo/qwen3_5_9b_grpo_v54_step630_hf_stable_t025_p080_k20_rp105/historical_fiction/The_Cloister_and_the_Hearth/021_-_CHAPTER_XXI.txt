Speech is often the vessel for human thought, yet there exist emotions so primal and overwhelming that they spill forth not in articulate words, but in raw, eloquent cries. In such moments, man sheds his civilized veneer, revealing himself as nothing more than a creature of instinct; for when beasts are driven to the brink, they too utter sounds, though they lack the gift of speech.

What followed was a spectacle of pure, terrifying animality. The roar of triumph erupted from the thicket as Dierich Brower and his men burst forth upon their quarry, while a sharp, piercing shriek of terror tore from the lips of the fugitives. The pursuers' hands grasped at empty air mere feet behind them as the trio—Gerard, Margaret, and the mule—darted away for their lives. For a heartbeat, the hunters stood stunned, like lions who have missed their spring, allowing Gerard and the beast to gain a ten-yard advantage. Then, weapons raised, they surged forward once more. They were certain of capture; this was not the first time these groups had tested their mettle against one another. On the open ground earlier that morning, the pursuers had visibly closed the gap, and now, in this renewed chase, victory would be decided solely by speed. A hundred yards vanished in the blink of an eye, yet that ten-yard buffer remained stubbornly intact.

This sudden surge in the fugitives' velocity baffled Dierich. The explanation lay in the dynamics of their flight. When three flee together, their pace is dictated by the slowest member. From Peter's house to the forest's edge, Gerard had been tethered to Margaret's slower stride. But now, freed from her burden, he ran at his own youthful best; moreover, the mule was swift enough to leave them all in the dust. Furthermore, the benefits of youth and a life of chastity began to manifest. Light seemed to grow perceptibly between the hunted and the hunters. Desperate to close the distance, Dierich made a final, frantic effort, gaining two yards, only for Gerard to steal them back within seconds. The pursuers cursed under their breath.

Martin heard the commotion, and a glimmer of hope ignited in his face. "Courage, Gerard! Courage, brave lad! They are straggling!"

It was true. Dierich now led only one man, while another had fallen entirely to the rear.

They ascended a long, gentle rise. Here, the advantages of youth, grit, and sober living proved decisive. Before Gerard reached the summit, the weight of Dierich's forty years pressed upon him like forty leaden bullets. "Our prey is dough," he gasped, abandoning the run to follow at a walk. Jorian Ketel trailed off next, followed by others one by one. Gerard ran them all to a standstill, save for one who pressed on with the tenacity of a bloodhound, though steadily losing ground. His name, if my memory serves, was Eric Wouverman. Following him, they approached a shorter but far steeper rise within the wood.

"Hand on the mane!" Martin cried.

Gerard obeyed instantly, and the mule hauled him up the incline faster than he had ever run before.

Witnessing this maneuver, Dierich's remaining man lost his nerve. Now nearly eighty yards behind Gerard and significantly ahead of his nearest comrade, he halted abruptly. Obedient to Dierich's silent command, he lowered his crossbow, leveled it with deliberate precision, and, just as the trio sank out of sight over the crest of the hill, released the bolt. It whistled through the air.

A cry of dismay rang out. Then, as if struck by a thunderbolt, they all collapsed onto the earth, mule and rider alike, brought low by the sudden violence.
