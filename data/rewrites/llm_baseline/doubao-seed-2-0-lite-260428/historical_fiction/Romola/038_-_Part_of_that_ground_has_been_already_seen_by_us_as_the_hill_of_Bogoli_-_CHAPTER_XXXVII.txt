Romola was roused at dawn by a soft tap at her bedchamber door. Thin, frigid winter light seeped through the window slats, and there stood old Maso, come to collect the travelling wallet he had stowed for her the night before. The old servant started so hard when she pulled the door open he nearly dropped his staff: where he had always known the soft, graceful line of her figure framed by the bright spill of her golden hair, there was only the thick heavy folds of a rough grey pilgrim’s mantle, her pale face half-shrouded in the deep shadow of a dark cowl.
“All is well, Maso,” Romola said, forcing her voice to stay calm and steady, to ease the old man’s surprise. “Here is the wallet, all packed. Go on slowly, and I shall not be far behind you. Once you pass the city gates you may linger at a gentler pace; I will likely catch up to you long before you reach Trespiano.”
She shut the door after him, and her fingers closed around the small brass key she had slipped from her father’s old oak casket the very last thing before she slept. It was the original key to the little painted tabernacle Tito had forgotten to hurl into the Arno years before — it had lodged, as tiny, easily missed objects often do, in the stitched corner of the embroidered scarlet pouch he wore slung at his belt with his formal purple tunic. Romola had found it there months after their wedding, tucked it away without a word, never using it, but clinging to the quiet satisfaction that it was hers to call on whenever she needed it.
The small cabinet that held the tabernacle had been moved to the room’s edge, set close enough to the window that the pale dawn light spilled soft across its painted panels, clear enough for Romola, who knew every inch of the art by heart, to make out every detail: the triumphant Bacchus, heavy with grape clusters, his thyrsus wound thick with vines, clasping the crowned Ariadne to his chest; winged cupids showering rose petals, gilded wreathed ewers, dolphins with sly, knowing eyes leaping through ripples of painted blue sea, all framed in a border of wild summer blooms that looked like the walls of a small enclosed paradise bower.
On this cold, bleak morning, when she woke determined to walk away from every part of her old life, the once-beloved images felt far more cruel and hollow than they ever had. They were no gentle memento of lost joy, but a lying, glittering screen that had hidden years of betrayal from her. Foolish Ariadne, gazing up at her divine lover with all her faith, as if that bright face framed in hyacinth curls that twisted like vine tendrils held no secret that would leave her abandoned and alone on a rocky shore. “That girl would not recognise herself now,” Romola thought to herself, “wrapped in this coarse grey, tramping the cold roads in the dark of winter.”
She picked up her small hand mirror, glanced at her face for one last check, and flinched, setting it down fast. The sight of herself framed by the cowl, pale and sharp, sent a shiver through her. That old, once-disdained dread pricked at her skin: the fear that she might finally end up one of those raving fanatics, those weeping cloistered nuns she had scorned from girlhood, surrendering every part of her own sharp, curious mind to a blind, mindless superstition.
She jammed the key into the tabernacle’s lock, twisting it fast, yanking the tiny door open. She grabbed the carved crucifix inside without daring to look at it, fumbled a length of thin cord through the small iron ring at its top, hung it around her neck, and tucked it deep into the folds of her mantle, pressed to her breast. For Dino’s sake, she whispered to herself, honoring her brother’s final dying wish.
Only two brief letters remained to write, to send back with Maso when he turned for Bologna. The first, addressed to Tito, was spare, no softening words:
> “Tito, my love for you is dead, and so the part of me that belonged to you as your wife is dead too. Do not waste your effort invoking Florentine law to drag me back: it will bring you no joy, no comfort. The Romola you married can never return. I need explain nothing to you after the hard truths I spoke the last long night we argued. If you mistook those words for transient, angry spite, you will know now they were the sign of an irreversible end.
> “I ask that you send my bridal chest, gifted to me by my godfather, to him at his home. It holds my wedding clothes, my parents’ portraits, and every small memento I kept of them.”
She slipped her wedding ring inside the folded letter, sealed it, and scrawled Tito’s name across the front.
The second letter was for Bernardo del Nero, her godfather:
> “Dearest second father, — If I could have stayed and brought any comfort to your life, I would never have wandered so far from Florence. But I cannot stay here any longer. Do not ask anyone to hunt for me, and if you ever loved my father Baldassarre, do all you can to stop others from seeking me out. My life in Florence had grown unendurable, and I cannot bear to speak of the reasons to a single soul. Help me hide what has become of me in quiet, no prying eyes, no gossip. I have asked Tito to send my bridal chest to you: when you open it, all that I cannot say will be clear. Give every one of my mother’s old belongings to my cousin Brigida, and tell her I beg her forgiveness for not stopping to say my goodbyes.
> Farewell. The greatest gift I carry with me out of this city is the memory of your lifelong kindness, and my gratitude will never fade.
> Romola.”
She tucked both letters next to the crucifix, pressed to her heart, and realized every last loose end was tied. She was ready to leave.
No one else in the household had stirred yet. She drifted down the stairs as quiet as a grey phantom, stepping out onto the flagstone street that still lay empty of passersby. Her heart thudded so hard she could hear it in her ears, but the firm, steady weight of her boots on the broad paving stones brought her a sharp, bright sense of release: the chains she had carried for years, forged out of blind faith and misplaced loyalty, were unshackled at last. All her dread of being caught, of someone stopping her, pushed grief far from her mind, and when she crossed Ponte Rubaconte, she barely even registered the distant spires of Santa Croce rising ahead. The thin yellow streak of dawn splitting the grey sky was growing broader by the minute, and if she did not hurry, the city would wake, and curious eyes would start turning toward the tall cowled stranger hurrying alone down the road.
Her intended route led her straight along Borgo Pinti, then parallel to the outer city walls to the Porta San Gallo, the gate she needed to exit Florence. That path carried her right through the Piazza di Santa Croce, but she kept her gaze fixed to the paving at her feet, walking as fast and steady as she could, never daring to glance toward the great stone church. The thought of strangers recognizing her, staring, gossiping over her private sorrows, digging into the shame Tito had left her to bear, made her shrink as if from torture. No one peered out of their windows to notice her, though: tall, cowled, her head held with a quiet proud tilt, her grey mantle swishing around her ankles, she looked like any other devout pilgrim heading out to the hill shrines at dawn. The streets were still clear of early traffic, and when she reached the Porta San Gallo, the guards were so busy bickering with a crowd of peasant farmers over the toll for panniers of eggs and root vegetables that she slipped through the gate completely unchallenged.
She was out. Past the last huddled houses of the Borgo, the fringe of the city fell away, and the wide open sky stretched over her head. She had stepped into her new life: a life of loneliness, of hardship, of quiet endurance, but unshakably free. She would never again feel the pressure of Tito’s soft, hated lips against her cheek, never again breathe the stifling air of his lies, his cold calculated selfishness that had been slowly smothering her own spirit for years. The harsh chill of the wintry air, the bare leafless trees lining the road, the dark sombre hills rolling out before her, were welcome for their honesty, no trace left of the glittering false gods of joy and beauty she had worshipped for so long, and now renounced forever.
Then the sun burst over the eastern horizon all at once, flooding the road with golden light, throwing long sharp shadows across the path. There is no moment that makes the dawn light feel more like a tangible divine presence, stirring all the quiet, wordless feelings we carry deepest in our bones, than that exact second when it wakes every sleeping shadow. A soft, reverent awe settled over Romola as she stared at her own long, shrouded shadow stretching out ahead of her, a spectre she could never outrun. Until that point she had met no one on the road but the occasional peasant leading a mule, and the many gentle curves of the flat lane had hidden Maso from her sight. But when she had passed the small village of Pietra and set foot on rising ground, she lifted the brim of her cowl to peek ahead — and dropped it fast. Two black-robed monks were barely a few yards away, walking downhill straight toward her.
The edge of her cowl had formed a little awning over her brow that blocked everything above her line of sight, and for minutes she had been staring at nothing but the gilded path and her own long shadow, never seeing them coming. The false disguise she had hidden under now filled her with sharp unease: they might ask her for a pious password, a prayer or pilgrimage ritual she knew nothing of, and unmask her. She walked onward, her face arranged in the blank, faraway look of a pilgrim lost in devout thought, until the hems of their black robes passed her by, and they vanished down the hill behind her.
The encounter had left her heart thudding unpleasantly. The shame of her false piety, the discomfort of this carefully constructed lie, burned bright under her skin. But the monks were well on their way now, and she spotted a broad flat stone set beside a tall cypress tree growing out of a grassy bank, and sank down to rest, yielding to the quiet urge to pause for breath after the shock. She turned her back to Florence, refusing to glance toward the city until the monks were completely out of sight, lifted the brim of her cowl again, and spotted Maso and the mules bobbing slowly along far ahead, moving at a leisurely pace while the old man waited for her to catch up.
For the first time in years, she was free. She was alone. No obligations, no lies, no one to answer to. The open road stretched out before her, and she was entirely her own.
