There is a certain class of human beings, often satirized in fiction, whose existence I confess I have rarely encountered in real life: the genuine religious hypocrite. These are individuals who preach incessantly without believing a single word of their own sermons—men who expound scripture, offer blessings, and deliver warnings with a solemnity that masks a complete lack of faith in either heaven or hell. Consider the pious crowds that flock to the churches on Sunday evenings: the maid-servants trailing ribbons, following the young apprentices; the schools of boys in their best coats; the neat maids and staid matrons marching with glistening prayer-books as the chapel bell rings. Are they truly devout? Likely not. The maid servant probably thinks of her sweetheart; the grocer calculates how to dispose of his sugar stock and whether the County Bank will accept his notes; the schoolboy memorizes Latin verses for the coming exercise; the young rake remembers the father's exposition at home but anticipates pie for supper; the clerk calling out the psalm has a troubled daughter and drones through his responses with a mind elsewhere; and the moment the parson hides his face on his cushion, he is likely fretting over a bill due on Monday. These people are not heavenly-minded. They remain firmly rooted in the world, treating religion merely as a convenient mental lockbox—a remedy to be dispensed when physical ailments arise. "My dear madam, you suffer from spasms? Try these drops!" "Have you been drinking too freely, sir? This pill will safeguard you against excess!" Who are more enthusiastic dispensers of spiritual and bodily physic than women? Indeed, a century past, every lady in the countryside maintained a still-room stocked with pills, powders, and potions for the entire village.

Lady Warrington assumed the role of physician to the consciences and digestions of her husband's tenants and household. She held the faith and health of the servants' hall in her keeping. Whether she possessed the divine wisdom to heal them effectively remains a matter for Heaven alone to judge; however, whether offering a pill or a doctrine, she administered them with equal conviction in her authority, and her disciples swallowed them obediently. She regarded herself as one of the most virtuous, self-denying, wise, and learned women in creation, and by constantly repeating this sentiment, she successfully persuaded many around her to adopt her views.

At Sir Miles's dinner, the sideboard displayed such an array of plate, and the livery-clad staff numbered so greatly, that it required a sharp mind to discern that the beer poured into the splendid tankards was of the poorest quality and that the grand silver dish held but a solitary joint of mutton. Yet, when Sir Miles toasted the King's health and smacked his jolly lips over his wine, he viewed the liquid as ambrosia. He turned to Harry Warrington and inquired if they drank such port in Virginia. Asserting that the wine Harry tasted in Norfolk was inferior, Sir Miles extolled the drink until Harry nearly believed it was exquisite, winking into his own glass as if trying to perceive the virtues his uncle so clearly admired in the ruby nectar.

Like many well-regulated families of this era, the Warringtons boasted two paragons among their grown daughters. One, the eldest, was the greatest beauty, recently engaged to Tom Claypool, a fact Lady Warrington shared with Harry in confidence. The second daughter, however, was the genius and angel of any young lady alive, according to her mother. Yet, despite her celestial reputation, she seemed determined to improve Harry's wits and morals. She sang to him at the harpsichord—a performance Harry found slightly out of tune for an angel—and offered endless advice, instruction, and conversation, perhaps with too much of the latter. Harry, preferring the company of a younger cousin who reminded him of Fanny Mountain at home, found himself somewhat weary of the constant tutelage. Nevertheless, after dinner, while Beauty departed on her own pursuits, the mother attended to her correspondence or the needs of the poor, and the father dozed in his armchair, the "Genius" remained to keep company with her young cousin.

A strange calm pervaded the house, a quiet sanctuary from the riot and dissipation Harry usually inhabited. The welcome he received was unqualified. Doors stood open to him at all hours. If Flora was absent, Dora was always ready to receive him. Within days of their acquaintance, Harry and his little cousin Miles had ridden together in the Park. Generous to every soul who crossed his path, Harry had even contemplated purchasing a superior horse for Miles, far better than the steed the boy currently rode, though circumstances later forced a sudden breakdown of Harry's own carriage and horses.

Although Sir Miles Warrington seemed to imagine Virginia to be an island, the ladies were far better grounded in geography and eagerly listened to Harry's accounts of his homeland. He, in turn, relished the opportunity to describe the vast extent of his estate, the rivers that bordered it, and its abundant produce. Drawing upon a childhood practice of surveying with a friend, Harry produced a map of his county, marking towns that were, in reality, little more than log-huts (though he wished, for the honor of his country, that they appeared more stately). There stood the Potomac and the James River; there were the wharves where his mother's ships and cargoes of tobacco bound for the sea. In truth, his estate was as large as an English county. He spoke of it without excessive boasting, yet the sight of the handsome young fellow, dressed in velvet and silver lace, drawing maps and pointing out hills and forests, conjured the image of a traveling prince describing realms to a royal mother. For fleeting moments, he almost fancied himself one. With miles of land where English gentlemen possessed mere acres, he captivated everyone. Not only did Dora listen intently, but the beautiful Flora also bowed her fair head in attention. Compared to young Tom Claypool, the brother baronet's son in Norfolk, with his massive boots, booming voice, and inheritance of merely five thousand acres, what chance did the "poor" heir have against this young American prince and charming stranger? So enchanted was Dora by Harry's sophistication that she began to view Flora as a mere coquette. Claypool, in his red waistcoat, sat silent before the splendid Harry in his ruffles and laces, listening as Harry discoursed on March, Chesterfield, Selwyn, and Bolingbroke—the entire galaxy of macaronis. Consequently, the mother grew to love Harry more and more, viewing him almost as a son. Her thoughts drifted anxiously to the spiritual welfare of the poor Indians and the enslaved in Virginia. What aid could she provide to dear Madam Esmond, a woman of precious virtue? She noted the serious demeanor of her butler and housekeeper, delighted as they were by the spiritual behavior and sweet musical gifts of Gumbo.

"Oh! Harry, Harry! You have been a sad wild boy!" the mother exclaimed. "Why did you not come to us sooner, sir, and avoid wasting your time among spendthrifts and the vain world? But 'tis not yet too late. We must reclaim thee, dear Harry! Mustn't we, Sir Miles? Mustn't we, Dora? Mustn't we, Flora?"

The three ladies gazed upward toward the ceiling. They resolved to reclaim the prodigal son. It became a contest of sorts: which sister would succeed most? Dora sat by, observing Flora. As for the mother, whenever the girls were away, she addressed Harry with increasing seriousness and tenderness. Determined to fill the void left by his absent parents, she vowed to be a mother to him. She presented him with a hymn-book, kissed his forehead, and acted from the purest love, tenderness, and religious regard toward her dear, wayward, wild, yet amiable nephew.

While these sentimentalities unfolded, Mr. Warrington kept his counsel regarding his disastrous affairs, which had reached a point of extreme ruin. After weeks favored by fortune, he had been abruptly abandoned by her. A few days sufficed to dismantle the heap of winnings he had amassed. Did Lord Castlewood, his own kinsman, deal unfairly with the young Virginian, robbing him through clandestine sessions? We need not insinuate disrespect for his lordship's character; nevertheless, Castlewood had won every shilling properly belonging to Harry and would have gambled for his reversions were it not for the young man's refusal to continue the battle once hopelessly defeated. Remembering a small reserve set aside for the poor clergyman, Sampson, Harry ventured it at the gaming table. That last resource sank with the rest of his possessions, and Fortune fled the storm, leaving the luckless adventurer almost naked on the shore.

A young man who is generous and hearty is seldom deeply afflicted by the loss of money. Harry would sell his horses and carriages, curtail his train, and live simply. If immediate funds were needed, surely his Aunt Bernstein would act as his banker, or his kinsman who had won so much from him, or his kind Uncle Warrington and Lady Warrington, who constantly preached virtue and benevolence, declaring their love for him as a son? He could call upon them at his leisure. Meanwhile, Sampson's tale of his landlord's distress moved the young gentleman, prompting him to raise a hasty sum for the clergyman by carrying all his trinkets to a pawnbroker's shop in St. Martin's Lane.
