The air hummed with unspoken certainty as Trent climbed the library stairs—the quiet thrill of a man on the brink of revelation. Fragments of theories, half-formed deductions, and nagging inconsistencies swirled in his mind, yet beneath the chaos, an instinct whispered: *The truth is here.*

The upper floor sprawled before him—a hushed, carpeted passage lined with doors, sunlight pooling at the far end from a tall window. Manderson’s room stood first on the right. Trent tested the latch, the lock, then stepped inside.

The room was stark, almost frugal in its simplicity. The bed lay unmade, sheets tangled as if abandoned mid-thought. Sunlight glinted off a glass bowl on the bedside table, where a set of dental plates rested in water—an odd detail that made Trent’s frown deepen. Clothing draped carelessly over a chair; a candlestick, unlit; a dressing table cluttered with the detritus of a hurried man. The emptiness felt charged, as though the walls still held the echo of Manderson’s last, agitated moments.

Trent’s imagination conjured the scene: a hollow-eyed man dressing in the pale dawn, stealing glances at the door behind which his wife slept, his movements tight with dread. He shook off the image and turned to the wardrobes, flinging them open. Rows of fine suits, a connoisseur’s collection of shoes—all meticulously kept.

Then his gaze snagged on a pair of patent-leather shoes, polished to a high sheen. The ones Manderson had worn the night before. Trent lifted them, examining the soles. His breath hitched. Faint traces of red gravel clung to the heels.

A low whistle escaped him—a habit when the pieces clicked. He measured the shoes with a tape, then paced to the window, staring blankly at the lawn below as his mind raced.

---

Mrs. Manderson’s adjoining room was a study in contrasts—elegant yet stripped of personal touches, like a stage after the actress had exited. Trent noted the French windows, the balcony overlooking the lawn, the precise angles of the doors. He sketched a rough floor plan, then rang for the maid.

Célestine arrived with the poised energy of a woman itching to unburden herself. Her account of the morning ritual—the tea tray, the open door she’d shut without a glance into Manderson’s room—was vivid, punctuated by expressive hands. Then, abruptly, her composure cracked.

*“Je ne le regrette pas du tout!”* she spat, eyes blazing. *“Un homme comme lui—maussade, insupportable!”*

Trent cut her off with a sharp command in French, though her venom lingered in the air like gunpowder.

---

In Marlowe’s room, disorder reigned. Fishing rods, books, a jumble of pipes and trinkets. Trent studied the photographs—Marlowe and Manderson on horseback, alpine peaks, a trio of Renaissance-clad youths. Then his fingers brushed a flat leather case.

Inside lay a revolver, initials *J.M.* etched into the stock.

Inspector Murch appeared in the doorway, eyes narrowing at the weapon. Without a word, he produced a battered bullet from his pocket and laid it beside a cartridge from the revolver’s case. The rifling marks matched.

Trent exhaled. “So. We’re to believe Marlowe sneaked back, lured Manderson outside, shot him, cleaned the gun, hid all day, and then strolled in at nine?”

Murch’s mouth twitched. “Except he was in Southampton at dawn. Witnesses, telegrams, the works.”

Trent turned the revolver over in his hands. “Then why,” he murmured, “does this feel like the wrong answer?”

The room held its breath. Outside, the trees rustled, indifferent to the weight of the puzzle left unsolved.
