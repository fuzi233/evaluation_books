In the aftermath of that fateful evening in Grosvenor Place, Mr. Harry Foker's demeanor had undergone a profound transformation. His usual air of detachment, honed through years of indulging in the finer things in life, seemed to have abandoned him. It was as if the very fabric of his being had been rent asunder by the tender touch of Miss Amory's presence. The memories of their encounter lingered, refusing to be vanquished by the allure of his usual haunts – the Back Kitchen's raucous gatherings, the theatre's vibrant ladies, or even the rare, yet tolerable, company of his own kind.

As he settled into his chambers, the din of laughter and jests from the Back Kitchen faded into the background, replaced by the insistent whisper of his own heart. The gin-twist and devilled turkey, once a staple of his convivial nights, now seemed pedestrian, lacking the je ne sais quoi that had captured his imagination. Even the musical talents of Mr. Hodgen, whose 'The Body Snatcher' had once been the talk of the town, failed to stir his passions, and his half-hearted applause felt like a hollow gesture.

In the silence of his bedchamber, Foker's thoughts swirled, taunted by the ghostly apparition of Miss Amory. Her words, 'Here I am, I am your princess and beauty,' echoed within his mind, conjuring visions of a world where she held court, and all else paled in comparison. His former attachments, once the epitome of sophistication and refinement, now struck him as dull and uninspired. The notion of 'modest women,' a concept he had previously regarded with a patronizing air, began to take on a new, intriguing hue.

As he reflected on his past, Foker realized that his acquaintance with women of his own social standing had been limited, at best. His mother, though well-meaning, had not been able to engage his interests; his cousins, the daughters of his maternal uncle, the Earl of Rosherville, had been a trying bunch, each with their own peculiarities – the geologist, the horsewoman, and the zealous devotee of the High Church.
