We set out at first pale dawn, strung across the wide plain like a living tapestry slung between unbroken blue sky and sun-dappled emerald grass. We planned to march all morning, rest through the heat of the afternoon, then advance under cover of dark, pause to sleep through the next full day, and push onward again in the short dim twilight of the Bulikan lowlands. The final leg of our journey we would time perfectly, so we slipped through the city gates at the very first break of dawn, before any guard had rubbed the sleep from their eyes.

It felt as if the whole forest had upped and migrated with us. Flocks of iridescent songbirds wheeled ahead, no doubt fully convinced they were the official vanguard of our procession, while clouds of swallowtails, darting dragonflies, and fuzzy bumbling wasps bobbed an arm’s length above our riders’ heads. A throng of four-footed forest folk – foxes, otters, frisky fawns – trotted at our heels, most peeling off once the dark deepened, but every last winged creature stuck with us all the way to the shadow of Bulika’s walls.

We halted at midday, and slept deep and dreamless through the afternoon. It was our first real long march, and not a single one of us was tired. When night fell, the sharp cool air pricked our cheeks and we moved faster still. Many of the Little Ones nodded off slouched against the shaggy fur of their riding beasts, or atop the thick piles of hay stacked on the elephants’ backs, and woke at sunrise bright and fresh, no one tumbling off their mount to hit the dust. Some rode shambling, broad-pawed bears that kept pace easily with the great elephants; others were mounted on fleet deer, and would have raced each other the whole length of the plains if I had not forbidden it. The children perched on the elephant hay stacks, unable to see their mounts far below, chattered to the beasts nonstop whenever they were awake. Once, when we halted to let the elephants feed, I heard a tiny boy tugging at a tuft of hay, leaning in earnest against his elephant’s trunk to murmur: “Nosy dear, I’m digging you out of the mountain, see? I’m almost all the way down! Be patient, yeah? Soon you’ll push your nose up to look for me, and then we’ll kiss just like good elephants do, we will!”

That same night, a raucous tumult burst out across the levels: elephant trumpets, horse whinnies, and the Little Ones mimicking every animal’s call so the noise rang for miles across the silent plains. Terrified we would give away our position before we were close enough to the gates, I quickly stilled the uproar.

Then one morning, the sun and Bulika rose together, as if the first light had birthed the whole jagged mass of city walls out of the bare earth. To the children, the fortifications looked like nothing but a great barren heap of rock, and when I told them the inside was crammed full of hollow stone nests, carved chambers stacked high to hold people, a cold dread settled over every small heart. It was the first real fear most of them had ever known, even the ones who’d lived long little lives out in the woods. How were they supposed to find soft, loving mothers in a cold, stony place like that? But they did not falter, for they trusted Lona – and me too, fool that I was, for little did I deserve that faith.

We rode through the echoing archway, the drumming of hooves, padding of paws, and thud of bare feet booming off the ancient vaulted pavement. The horses spooked at the sound of their own echoes, some skidding to a halt, others rearing and wheeling wildly, but we calmed them fast and pushed onward. The Little Ones shivered, going dead still as death, the three older girls clutching the swaddled infants they carried tight to their chests. Only the bears and the flitting butterflies showed no trace of fear. The woman we had rescued back in the woods wore a face tight with dark, heavy anxiety, and even I was not untouched by the general dread: the whole army of innocents was on my hands, my responsibility, my fault if harm came to them. I had dreamed of the coming kingdom of the Little Ones, where the cruel bad giants would serve, and the animals would be their loving, obedient friends – but I had been stupid enough never to learn to obey the quiet, gentle law that governed that good world. My own stubborn, untrusting willfulness had put me at the head of this procession of pure hearts, and I was no better than any selfish king in the world I had left behind, who only ever did what pleased himself. But Lona rode at my side, a child in body and therefore a truly free woman: calm, silent, watchful, not a flicker of fear crossing her face.

We were nearly to the very heart of the city before any of its inhabitants noticed we were there. Then window shutters began to creak open, and sleepy heads poked out. At first every face wore a dull, uncomprehending stare of astonishment, which curdled into unmasked terror the second they spotted the bears, elephants, and deer moving through the streets. Even through their fear, when they saw that their invaders were almost all small children, the women poured out of the houses into the streets, men trailing after them, all huddling tight against the walls, too afraid of the wild animals to step into the open middle of the road.

At length a five-year-old boy, half out of his mind searching for a mother, spotted a woman in the crowd whose face felt soft and safe, and launched himself off his antelope straight into her arms, clinging tight around her neck. She hugged him back, kissing his cheeks like he was her own long-lost child. Then a brute of a man loomed over her shoulder, and seized the boy by the scruff of his neck. Instantly an older girl drove her sharp wooden spear through his arm. He let out a savage howl, took two more stabs from other Little Ones, and fled screaming down the lane. “They are just bad giants,” Lona said, her eyes flashing like embers as she kicked her horse forward straight at an unusually tall brute who had worked up the nerve to bar her path with a heavy club. He dared not meet the full force of her horse’s charge, and scuttled aside, only to be knocked flat a second later by a hail of small stones. Another giant dodged my charger, stepped suddenly between me and the small boy riding behind me, snarling a curse whose rudeness alone I could parse, and swung a heavy hammer at the little boy’s pony, caving its skull in. Before he could strike again, the elephant behind me slammed its trunk down, knocking him flat, and trampled him so thoroughly he did not dare try to stand until hundreds of feet had walked over him and the whole army had moved past.

But the second Lona looked at the crowd of city women, a great shadow of dismay clouded her face. Hardly a single one of them was pleasant to look upon. Were her darlings supposed to find good mothers among these hard, cruel faces? We barely halted in the central square when two girls rode up in breathless haste, with tidings that two small boys had been snatched and hurried away by a group of the city’s women. We wheeled around at once, and that was when we discovered that the woman we had befriended back in the woods had vanished, taking her baby with her.

At that exact moment, a great white leopardess came bounding down the narrow lane that led from the square straight to the palace. The Little Ones had not forgotten the brutal fight between the two leopardesses in the forest, a few flinched, their ranks wavering – but they remembered the order I had given them, and stood fast. We paused to see what would happen next, when little Odu – the swiftest, bravest of the Little Ones, who had heard me speak once of the white leopardess’s gentle nature – leaped off the back of his shambling bear, who lumbered loyally after him, and ran straight to meet her. The leopardess, to avoid knocking him flying, skidded to a halt so fast she rolled head over paws across the cobblestones, and when she righted herself, the grinning small boy was perched right on her back. No Bulikan who saw that, who lived in daily terror of that leopard, could doubt our victory.

Confident the whole city’s will to resist was broken, we rode onward. When we pulled up outside the house our guides pointed to, a sharp scream cut the air. I sprang down from my horse and thundered my fists against the locked door. My great war horse nudged me out of the way with his nose, turned, and began battering the door with his hooves, until Odu trotted up on the leopardess. The horse froze, trembling, but the leopardess had heard the scream too, and forgetting the child on her back, hurled herself full force at the door. Odu was dashed against the wood and fell senseless. Before I could reach him, Lona had him swept up in her arms, and as soon as he blinked awake, set him back on his loyal bear, who had never left his side.

The leopardess hit the door a third time, and it splintered open. She darted inside before any of us could follow. We rushed in after her, but she had already vanished. We tore up the stairs, searched every room in the house, and found no one. Darting back down, we spotted a low hidden door under the staircase, and ducked through into a twisting labyrinth of excavated tunnels running under the foundations. We had not gone far when the white leopardess came trotting back, the missing snatched boy draped safe across her back. He told us the woman he had thought was his mother had thrown him down the dark hole, saying she would feed him to the leopardess – but the good leopardess had climbed right down after him and pulled him out.

We went next door easily, searching for the other missing boy, but we were too late. One of the savage giants had already killed the small captive. It consoled Lona, though, to hear which boy it was: she had long suspected he was fated to grow up into a bad giant, the worst possible fate that could befall any Little One, and death had saved him from that dark end. The leopardess sprang on his murderer, seized him by the throat, and dragged him out into the street, following Lona like a great cat carrying an overgrown rat in her jaws.

“Let us leave this horrible place,” Lona said. “There are no mothers here. This people is not worth delivering.” The leopardess dropped her burden, and charged into the panicking crowd, tearing back and forth wherever the Bulikans were packed thickest. The city’s enslaved residents screamed and ran, tripping over each other, piling up in heaps on the cobblestones.

When we got back to our army, we found it exactly as we had left it, standing in perfect order, ready for anything. But I was far from easy. The princess of Bulika had not shown her face once, and we had no idea what dark plot she might be weaving. We would have to keep watch and ward all through the night. The Little Ones were such hardy, resilient creatures they could rest anywhere: we told them to lie down right there in the square, curled up against their animals, and sleep until we called them. In one moment they were all settled, and in the next the whole square hummed with that soft, unique sound of sleeping Little Ones, like water trickling over smooth grass, or a warm summer wind sifting through oak leaves. Their animals slept more lightly, ears twitching, always half awake, ready to rouse at the first sign of trouble. The older boys and girls patrolled soft and silent through the dreaming multitude. All was still. The whole wicked city seemed at rest, hiding all its worst secrets in the dark.
