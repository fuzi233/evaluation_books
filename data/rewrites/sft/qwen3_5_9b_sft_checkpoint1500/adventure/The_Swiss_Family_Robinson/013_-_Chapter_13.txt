As twilight descended, the campfire crackled, sending up a savory perfume from the stewing bear paws simmering for supper. We gathered around the flames, our hearts beating a rhythm of anxious hope as we listened for the distant sounds that would signal the return of our young explorers. Finally, the rhythmic tramp and the clatter of hooves broke the silence. The little troop appeared, crossing the open ground before us at a sharp trot, their arrival heralded by a shrill, ringing cheer as we rose to meet them.

They sprang from their saddles, releasing the animals to refresh themselves, while the riders eagerly displayed their acquisitions and recounted their adventures. What funny figures they cut! Franz and Jack each bore a young kid slung across their backs, its four legs tied together and sticking out comically beneath their chins. Fritz's game-bag looked remarkably odd—bulging with round lumps and sharp points, occasionally twitching to suggest living creatures trapped within.

"Hurrah for the chase, Father!" cried Jack. "There is nothing like real hunting after all. Just to see how Storm and Grumble handle a grassy plain! It is simply splendid! We soon tired out the little antelopes and caught them."

"Yes, Father," said Franz, "and Fritz has two Angora rabbits in his bag. Besides, we wanted to bring you some honey. Only think! Such a clever bird—a cuckoo—showed us exactly where it was hidden!"

"My brothers forget the chief thing," corrected Fritz. "We have driven a little herd of antelopes right through the Gap into our territory. There they are, all ready for us to hunt whenever we please—or to catch and tame!"

"Well done!" I exclaimed. "Here is indeed a list of achievements. But to your mother and me, the chief thing above all is God's goodness in bringing you safe back to us. Now, let us hear the whole story so we may have a definite idea of your performances."

"We had a splendid ride," said Fritz. "Down Glen Verdant and through the defile of our Rocky Barrier, the morning air was so cool and fresh that our steeds galloped nearly the whole way at full speed. Once through the Gap, we slowed to a trot, keeping a sharp lookout for game. We spotted two herds grazing beside the stream below. We were about to charge down for a shot when I realized it would be wiser to drive the entire herd through the Gap into our own domain. Effectively, it would trap them in a park, free yet within easy reach."

"Down the hill we rode as hard as we could, forming a semicircle behind the larger herd of magnificent antelopes. Aided by the dogs and loud shouts, we drove them along the stream toward the Gap. Near the opening, they hesitated, turning like sheep about to be led to the butcher's yard. It took every ounce of effort to prevent them from bolting past us. But eventually, one made a rush for the opening, and the rest followed. Soon, they were all on the other side of the frontier, inhabitants of New Switzerland."

"Capital!" I said. "But I don't see what will keep them from returning through the Gap whenever they feel inclined."

"Stop, Father," he replied. "You interrupted me too soon. We anticipated that possibility. We stretched a long line right across the defile, stringing it with feathers, rags, and all sorts of fluttering debris. In the wind, they dance and look so strange that I am certain the herd will never attempt to pass. In fact, Levaillant mentions in *Voyage au Cap de Bonne Espérance* that the Hottentots use this exact method to pen in antelopes they have caught."

"Well done," I said, pleased to see him recall what he had read. "The antelopes are welcome to New Switzerland. But, my boy, I cannot say the same for the rabbits you have there. They increase so rapidly that if you establish a colony of those little wretches, your next difficulty will be to get rid of them."

"True," he admitted, "but my idea was to place them on Whale Island, where they would find abundant food and trouble us in no way. May I not establish a warren there? It would be so useful. Do you know, Father, my eagle caught these pretty little fellows for me? I saw a number running about, so I unhooded him. In a few minutes, he brought me three—one dead, whose body I used to reward him, and these two here, unhurt."

"Now, Father," interrupted Jack, "do listen to me and hear my story, or else Fritz will begin his rigmarole descriptions and tire you out."

"Certainly, Jack," I said, ready to listen. "First and foremost, how did you bring down those beautiful little animals?"

"Oh, we galloped them down," Jack explained. "The dogs sniffed about in the grass while Fritz was away after the rabbits. Suddenly, these little fawns popped out and bounded away at thirty miles an hour, with Storm, Grumble, and the dogs at their heels. In about a quarter of an hour, we left the dogs behind and were close upon our prey. Down went the little creatures into the grass, overwhelmed with terror and fatigue, falling at our mercy. We shouted to Fritz, and—"

"My dear boy," I interjected, "according to your statement, Fritz must have been seven and a half miles away."

"Oh, well, Father, perhaps we didn't ride for quite a quarter of an hour, and I can't say exactly how fast we were going. Besides, the fawns didn't run in a straight line. At any rate, Fritz heard us, and he, Franz, and I leashed the legs of the pretty creatures. Then we mounted again and saw a wretch of a cuckoo leading us far out of our course by cuckooing and making faces. Franz declared it must be an enchanted princess, so I thought I'd rid it of its spell. But Fritz stopped me from shooting it, saying it was a 'Honey Indicator' leading us to a bees' byke. So we spared its life, and sure enough, it stopped close by a bees' nest in a hollow tree."

"This was capital!" I thought. "But as we were in a great hurry to taste the honey, I threw in a lot of lighted lucifer matches. Somehow, it didn't kill the bees; instead, it made them awfully angry. They flew out in a body and stung me all over. I rushed to Storm and sprang on his back. Though I galloped away for bare life, it was an age before I got rid of the little wretches. Now my face is in a perfect fever. I think I'll get Mother to bathe it for me," he concluded, rushing off, leaving Fritz and me to tend to the fawns and examine the rabbits.

With the latter, I decided to follow Fritz's proposal and colonize Whale Island with them. I was all the more willing because I had been considering establishing a fortress on that island, a refuge in case of extreme danger, where a constant supply of animal food would be invaluable.

Having attended to the needs of the antelopes, I tried to interest the boys in my discovery of a block of talc, but just then their mother summoned us to dinner. The principal dish consisted of the bear's paws—so savoury-smelling delicacies that their close resemblance to human hands, and even Jack's roguish "Fee-fo-fum," failed to prevent every member of the family from enjoying them heartily.

After supper, we lit our watch-fire, retired to our tent, and slept soundly. Our work had been diligent: the bear's meat was smoked, the fat melted and stored, and a large supply of bamboos collected. Yet, wishing to make another excursion, I aroused the boys at early dawn. Fritz mounted his mule, I rode Lightfoot, and Jack and Franz took their usual steeds. With the two dogs, we galloped off—first to visit the euphorbia to collect gum, then to check if the ostrich had deserted her eggs in the sand.

Ernest watched us depart without a sigh of regret, returning to the tent to assist his mother and study his books. Our steeds carried us down the Green Valley at a rapid rate. We soon reached Turtle Marsh, filled our water-flasks, and arrived at the rising ground where Fritz had previously discovered the mounted Arabs. Since Jack and Franz wanted a gallop, I allowed them to press forward while Fritz and I visited the euphorbia trees. A quantity of red gum had exuded from the incisions I had made, coagulating in the sun. I rolled it into little balls and stored it in a bamboo jar.

"Father," Fritz remarked as we rode after the boys, "did you not tell me that the juice of that tree was poisonous? Why have you collected such a quantity?"

"I did indeed say so," I replied. "It is a most deadly poison. The inhabitants of the Cape of Good Hope use it to poison springs where wild animals assemble to drink, slaughtering immense numbers for their hides. I intend, however, to use it to destroy the apes should they again commit depredations, and also in preparing animal skins to protect them from insects."
