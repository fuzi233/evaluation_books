No one, not even the most punctual soul with a watch-chain taut across his waistcoat, ever truly begins at the beginning. We choose a point—any point—like a cook laying down the first board upon the trestles and calling it the start of the feast, though the fire has been banked since dawn and the broth has been murmuring long before the ladle ever touched it. So it was that Daniel Deronda’s first fixed moment with her came not in an English drawing-room with tea and buttered toast, but in a room where the air itself seemed over-reduced—breath upon breath cooked down to a visible haze, thick with gas and perfumes that had lost their innocence under heat.

It was near four o’clock on a September day, when sunlight outside is still clean and the world ought to smell of stone warmed by noon; but here there was only the casino’s enclosed atmosphere, brewed and stale, gilding and velvet acting like a lidded pot that kept every exhalation in. The place had a hush that was not peace, rather the strained quiet one hears when a household has stopped speaking at table: broken only by the small hard acoustics of chance—the light rattle of ivory, the chink of coin, the soft sweep of metal across cloth, and, floating over all, an occasional French phrase from the croupier, level and mechanical, like a clock that has forgotten what time means and still insists on striking.

Two long tables drew their crowds as hearths draw hands in winter. Faces were bent down as if toward a dish that must not be missed in its crucial moment; eyes fixed with the same narrow appetite. All but one: a melancholy little boy, bare-kneed and bare-calfed, the skin of him his only honest garment, while the rest was fancy-dress finery. He stood close behind a lady at the roulette-table, staring toward the doorway with the blank patience of a child made into an ornament—like a sweetmeat figure on a cake that no one is allowed to eat.

Around the roulette-table there were ranks—players pressed close, spectators behind them deposited and removed like extra chairs at a noisy family supper. Now and then a new-comer, often a woman, would lay down a five-franc piece with a little simper, as if tasting a forbidden sauce and wanting to be seen tasting it. Those who had swallowed the thing whole were every variety of European type—Livonian and Spanish, Graeco-Italian and mixed German; English aristocratic near English plebeian—shoulders brushing, hands almost touching. Equality here was bought with the price of appetite.

Deronda’s eye noted the odd intimacy: the white, jeweled fingers of an English countess near the bony yellow hand of a creature that looked as if it had been made for clutching—wrist bare, knuckles sharp, the whole hand crab-like and hungry, and easy to match with the gaunt square face, deep-set eyes, grizzled brows, and scanty ill-combed hair that suggested a vulture in a bad disguise. Near them, too, a dry-lipped woman prematurely withered, as though she had bloomed briefly like forced flowers and then dried, holding a shabby velvet reticule; she now and then put into her mouth the point with which she pricked her card—an action as sharp and joyless as biting a pin.

There was a respectable London tradesman—soft-handed, fair, his sleek hair parted with scrupulous care both behind and before—who carried on him the invisible paper of circulars addressed to “the nobility and gentry,” as if that printed flattery might follow him even here. He did not have the gambler’s fever that kills appetite; his was the well-fed leisure of a man who, between making money and spending it showily, found no better entertainment than to win a little and spend it more showily still. In his mind Providence disapproved of nothing that left him warm and undisturbed. To lose was the vice; to win was, apparently, a blessing.

Standing close behind that tradesman’s chair was a handsome Italian with a calm, carved composure, reaching across to place a first pile of napoleons from a new bag just brought by an envoy with a scrolled mustache. The pile slid away almost at once to an old bewigged woman with eye-glasses pinching her nose; she showed only the faintest gleam, a mumbling smile. The Italian remained impassive, as if his system were a recipe that never failed, and began another pile. Elsewhere an emaciated beau—or a worn-out libertine—looked at life through a single eye-glass, held out his hand trembling for change, driven not by method but by some private superstition as thin and fierce as hunger.

And yet, for all their differences, a sameness lay on them, a mask of uniform negation—as if each had chewed some bitter root that for an hour narrowed the mind to one repetitive motion. Deronda, feeling the room’s dull, gas-poisoned absorption, had the sudden thought that the gambling of Spanish shepherd-boys in open air—tossing coppers on a ruined wall, the wind clean, the smell of earth and sheep and sun-baked stone—had seemed more enviable than this gilded stew of breath and coin. Here even the soundscape was wrong: not the varied music of living, but the petty percussion of money and the monotonous voice that kept time for it, a kind of industrial rhythm disguised as amusement.

Then, as often happens when a quiet dish suddenly turns in the pot and releases a stronger scent, the moment sharpened. His attention was arrested by a young lady standing at an angle not far from him, the last person his eyes would have traveled to if they had been merely wandering. She had been bending to speak English to a middle-aged lady seated beside her at play; then she returned to the table and showed the full height of a graceful figure, and a face that might possibly be looked at without admiration, but could hardly be passed without a second look.

Was she beautiful? Deronda’s mind asked it as one might ask what gives a certain fruit its bite—why one peach is mere sweetness and another has that keen edge that compels you to taste again. What was it in the form or the expression that made her glance dynamic, not soothing? Did the good genius or the evil dominate in those beams? Probably the evil—if evil can be named as that which disturbs rather than charms. The wish to look again felt coercive, not consenting; like hearing a note that is not quite in tune, which one cannot ignore, which sets the teeth on edge and fixes the ear.

He watched her movements—her arms and hands as she bent forward to place a stake with an air of firm choice. The hands were taper, delicately gloved in pale-gray, touching the coins with the precision of someone accustomed to being obeyed by objects; as her winnings were pushed toward her and she adjusted them to send them back again to the winning point, she looked round with a survey too cold and neutral not to be an art concealing exultation. But in that survey her eyes met Deronda’s.

She would have wished, it seemed, to avert them. Instead she was unpleasantly conscious that they were arrested. How long? Long enough for a thought to sting her into feeling: that he was measuring her; that he looked down on her as inferior; that he was of a different quality than the human dross around her; that he held himself outside and above and examined her as a specimen of a lower order.

Resentment pricked her quick and sharp. It did not redden her cheeks; it sent the blood away from her lips, leaving them paler, as if the heat of her had withdrawn into herself to keep her pride from spilling out. She controlled her face with an inward defiance and turned back to the game.

But Deronda’s gaze—so she felt it, without looking—worked on her like an evil eye. Her stake was swept away. No matter. She had been winning since she first put down a few napoleons, and she had reserve enough. Luck had begun to feel like something that belonged to her, like a well-stocked larder to a confident housekeeper; others had begun to believe in it too. She had even let herself imagine the ridiculous triumphs that pride will cook up from nothing: a little court of watchers, a cortège, worshiping her as a goddess of luck, waiting for her hand to dictate their auguries. Men had been treated so; why should a woman not command it?

Her friend and chaperon—the middle-aged lady—had not wished her to play at first. Now she began to approve, only offering prudent counsel: stop in time and carry money back to England. Gwendolen had answered that she cared for the excitement, not the winnings. If that were true, this should have been the flood-tide of delight.

Yet when the next stake was swept away, Gwendolen felt the orbits of her eyes grow hot, as if the air itself had turned into pepper. And the certainty she had—without looking—that the man was still watching her pressed like a finger on a bruise, beginning to torture. All the more reason, she told herself, not to flinch. She would go on as if loss and gain were nothing. She would not give him the satisfaction of seeing her turn from the table like a scolded child.

Her friend touched her elbow, proposing they should quit. In reply Gwendolen laid ten louis on the same spot. She was in that defiant mood where the mind forgets ends and lives only for the satisfaction of resistance; and with the puerile stupidity of a dominant impulse, she included luck itself among the things to be defied. If she could not win strikingly, she would lose strikingly.

She kept her muscles obedient; no tremor showed in mouth or hands. Each time her stake was swept off, she doubled it. The watchers increased; their attention was a new layer of heat in the room. But she was conscious of one observation only—Deronda’s. She did not look at him; she did not need to. She was sure he had not moved away.

The drama did not take long; catastrophe can be counted by the moment-hand, swift as a sauce turning when you have stepped away. “Faites votre jeu, mesdames et messieurs,” said the automaton voice, destiny speaking from between the mustache and imperial of the croupier. Gwendolen’s arm stretched out with her last poor heap of napoleons.

“Le jeu ne va plus,” said destiny.

In five seconds she turned from the table—turned resolutely with her face toward Deronda and looked at him.

There was a smile of irony in his eyes as their glances met. Yet that was better, in her swift calculation, than to be disregarded as part of an insect swarm with no individual physiognomy. Besides, in spite of what she read as superciliousness, it was hard to believe he did not admire her—her spirit as well as her person. He was young, handsome, distinguished-looking; not one of those dowdy, ridiculous Philistines who passed a gaming-table with a sour air of protest, as if virtue were a duty they performed with their faces.

Her mind, trained in a certain easy vanity, had lived on the assumption that she knew what was admirable and that she herself was admired. That foundation had been jarred. It reeled but did not fall. Vanity, when met with a cold reception, rarely yields; it often insists that a little more performance will win the dissident.

In the evening the same room was hotter, more stifling, brilliant with gas and with ladies’ costumes—satins and feathers and ornaments that glittered like sugar-work under lamps. The air held more perfume, as if the night’s finery were meant to drown the day’s breath, though it only made a richer suffocation. The soundscape was fuller too: not silence now, but a hum of voices, a rustle of trains, the clink still present like a persistent utensil dropped on stone.

Gwendolen Harleth had dressed as a Nereid in sea-green robes with silver ornaments; a pale sea-green feather fastened in silver fell backward over her green hat and light-brown hair. She moved under the wing—or rather, she soared by the shoulder—of the same lady who had sat by her at roulette, Madame von Langen. With them walked a gentleman with a white mustache and clipped hair, stiff and German, his brow solid as a lintel.

As they passed seated groups, Gwendolen was much observed.

“A striking girl—that Miss Harleth—unlike others.”

“Yes, she has got herself up as a sort of serpent now—all green and silver, and winds her neck about a little more than usual.”

“Oh, she must always be doing something extraordinary. She is that kind of girl, I fancy. Do you think her pretty, Mr. Vandernoodt?”

“Very. A man might risk hanging for her—I mean a fool might.”

“You like a nez retroussé, then, and long narrow eyes?”

“When they go with such an ensemble.”

“The ensemble du serpent?”

“If you will. Woman was tempted by a serpent; why not man?”

“She is certainly very graceful; but she wants a tinge of color in her cheeks. It is a sort of Lamia beauty she has.”

“On the contrary, I think her complexion one of her chief charms. It is a warm paleness; it looks thoroughly healthy. And that delicate nose with its gradual little upward curve is distracting. And then her mouth—there never was a prettier mouth, the lips curled backward so finely, eh, Mackworth?”

“Think so? I cannot endure that sort of mouth. It looks so self-complacent, as if it knew its own beauty—the curves are too immovable. I like a mouth that trembles more.”

“For my part, I think her odious,” said a dowager. “It is wonderful what unpleasant girls get into vogue. Who are these Langens? Does anybody know them?”

“They are quite comme il faut. I have dined with them several times at the Russie. The baroness is English. Miss Harleth calls her cousin. The girl herself is thoroughly well-bred, and as clever as possible.”

“Dear me! and the baron?”

“A very good furniture picture.”

“Your baroness is always at the roulette-table,” said Mackworth. “I fancy she has taught the girl to gamble.”

“Oh, the old woman plays a very sober game; drops a ten-franc piece here and there. The girl is more headlong. But it is only a freak.”

“I hear she has lost all her winnings to-day. Are they rich? Who knows?”

“Ah, who knows? Who knows that about anybody?” said Mr. Vandernoodt, moving off to join the Langens.

The remark about Gwendolen winding her neck more than usual was true, but not from zeal for the serpent-idea. She watched for Deronda. The measuring gaze she had felt in the afternoon still smarted in her like a taste that would not leave the mouth. It was not merely that she wanted to conquer his opinion; it was that she could not bear an unspoken judgment fixed on her from a man whose silence seemed to claim a higher language than the room’s bright chatter. There are dominions, she felt dimly, in which some people are native and others are foreign; and she hated the notion that she might be a foreigner without knowing why.

At last she had her chance. Mr. Vandernoodt drifted near, that easy man-about-hotel who seemed to have acquaintances as other men have loose change.

“Mr. Vandernoodt, you know everybody,” Gwendolen said—not too eagerly, but with the languid utterance she could summon at will, like a clear soprano made indolent for effect. “Who is that near the door?”

“There are half a dozen near the door. Do you mean that old Adonis in the George the Fourth wig?”

“No, no; the dark-haired young man on the right with the dreadful expression.”

“Dreadful, do you call it? I think he is an uncommonly fine fellow.”

“But who is he?”

“He is lately come to our hotel with Sir Hugo Mallinger.”

“Sir Hugo Mallinger?”

“Yes. Do you know him?”

“No.” Gwendolen colored slightly, and the warmth rose into that warm paleness the critics had praised, as if her blood resented being mentioned aloud. “He has a place near us, but he never comes to it. What did you say was the name of that gentleman near the door?”

“Deronda—Mr. Deronda.”

“What a delightful name! Is he an Englishman?”

“Yes. He is reported to be rather closely related to the baronet. You are interested in him?”

“Yes. I think he is not like young men in general.”

“And you don’t admire young men in general?”

“Not in the least. I always know what they will say. I can’t at all guess what this Mr. Deronda would say. What does he say?”

“Nothing, chiefly. I sat with his party for a good hour last night on the terrace, and he never spoke—and was not smoking either. He looked bored.”

“Another reason why I should like to know him. I am always bored.”

“I should think he would be charmed to have an introduction. Shall I bring it about? Will you allow it, baroness?”

“Why not?—since he is related to Sir Hugo Mallinger. It is a new rôle of yours, Gwendolen, to be always bored,” Madame von Langen continued, when Mr. Vandernoodt had moved away. “Until now you have always seemed eager about something from morning till night.”

“That is just because I am bored to death. If I am to leave off play I must break my arm or my collar-bone. I must make something happen; unless you will go into Switzerland and take me up the Matterhorn.”

“Perhaps this Mr. Deronda’s acquaintance will do instead of the Matterhorn.”

“Perhaps.”

But that evening, for all her watching and for all the cloying heat and the talk that rose and fell like the boil of a crowded kitchen, she did not make Mr. Deronda’s acquaintance. Mr. Vandernoodt did not succeed in bringing him up to her; introductions failed like a sauce that separates at the last moment.

And when Gwendolen re-entered her own room, the air at last cooler and quieter, the sudden hush of privacy ringing in her ears like a relief from machinery, she found a letter waiting—its paper and ink carrying her back to England more sharply than any lecture could have done, recalling her home as inevitably as the smell of a familiar loaf can summon an entire childhood.
