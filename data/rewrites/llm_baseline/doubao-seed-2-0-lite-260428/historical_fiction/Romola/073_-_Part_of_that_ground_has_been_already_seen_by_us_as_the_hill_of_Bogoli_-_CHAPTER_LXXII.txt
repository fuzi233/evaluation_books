Romola had carried Savonarola’s parting words to his San Marco brethren as if they were a raw, unbidden cry ringing in her ears: “Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy.” The line shook her loose of every easy certainty she had ever held about his unshakable resolve, leaving new, gnawing doubts of how he looked back on his years of preaching, his grand promises of reform, in the quietest, most unguarded moments of self-awareness.

Worse, far more piteous tales soon reached her. The nineteenth of May had dawned bright over Florence, and its sun lit the way for two Papal Commissaries riding into the city, tasked with wrapping up the trial of the Frate. The crowds lined the streets cheering them, roaring for his death. Now the public cry was unanimous: all their misfortunes—famine, failed military campaigns, rising prices, foreign threats—were the Frate’s fault, the fruit of his deception. Burn him, and every ill would be mended, every wrong put right.

The next day, certified accounts spread across the city of fresh, repeated torture wracking the already shattered, sensitive frame of the friar. At the very first sight of the gleaming, brutal implements, Savonarola convulsed, fell to his knees, and in a rush of passionate, trembling words retracted his earlier forced confession. He cried that he had lied when he denied his prophetic gift, that if he died, he died for the truth: “The things that I have spoken, I had them from God.” But the torturers did not cease. When he was still stretched on the rack, they pressed him to explain why he had spoken those retractions. For all that men were not unthinking demons in those days, no mercy, no release from pain, was granted to a man who did not confess to the guilt his captors demanded. At last he broke again. “I said it that I might seem good; tear me no more, I will tell you the truth.”

Those conflicting words of twofold, contradictory retraction spread fast among Florentines, filling Romola with a cold, dismayed uncertainty. But a spark of hope flared in her chest. *There will come a moment*, she thought, when no fear of the rack hangs over him, no hand on his arm to silence him. When he is face to face with death, lifted above the crowd on the scaffold, they cannot stop him from speaking one last decisive, honest word. I will be there.

Three days later, on the 23rd of May 1498, a long, narrow wooden platform stretched across the great Piazza della Signoria, running straight from the steps of the Palazzo Vecchio toward the Tetta de’ Pisani. No wall of stacked fuel stood waiting at its far end as it had before: instead, a single great heap of dry kindling sat on the circular paved platform at the platform’s terminus, and above the fuel rose a gibbet strung with three halters. Its two arms had been crudely shortened on one side to deliberately avoid the shape of a cross, but even so it bore a faint, uncanny resemblance that made many onlookers shift uncomfortably where they stood.

Three tribunals had been set up on the marble terrace of the Palazzo: the first, near the main door, for the Bishop, who would perform the rite of degradation to strip Fra Girolamo and his two condemned followers of all their sacred clerical status. The second belonged to the Papal Commissaries, who would formally declare the three men heretics and schismatics and hand them over to the secular arm for punishment. The third, placed close to the ancient marble statue of Marzocco at the corner of the terrace where the scaffold platform began, was for the Gonfaloniere and the Eight, who would pronounce their official sentence of death.

The piazza seethed with a throng of expectant faces, all gathered to watch the great fire they had waited for. Most of the crowd pressing around the gibbet burned with ferocious hatred, or else bore the cold, hard curiosity of people come to gawk at a barbarous public spectacle. But many others, huddled on the pavement, crowded onto roof tiles and pressed behind upper-floor window grates, were Piagnoni followers, enduring jibes and insults even as they clung to a last, faint, desperate hope that God would send some miraculous sign at this eleventh hour to prove their beloved prophet was truly His servant. More than a few, like Romola, trembled with tense, waiting eagerness for that final moment, when Savonarola would cry out to the crowd: “O people, I was innocent of all deceit.”

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the piazza, far from the marble terrace where the tribunals sat. Beside her, watching the scene unfold with the same painful, tangled doubt for the man he had once revered above all others, stood twenty-two year old Jacopo Nardi, who would go down in history as one of the very few Florentines who wrote of Fra Girolamo with nothing but a simple, unyielding commitment to the truth. He spoke to her gently, respectfully, when he saw her shuddering with revulsion at the sight below, torn between the urge to look away and the desperate need not to miss the last word. “Madonna, there is no need for you to force your eyes on these cruel things. I will tell you the moment he steps out of the Palazzo. Trust to me; I know exactly what you hope not to miss.”

Romola pressed her hands over her face, but she could not shut out the roar of hoots that made the hideous scene feel as clear as if she were staring right at it. At last, a soft touch on her arm made her lift her head. “He comes,” Nardi said.

She looked toward the Palazzo steps, and saw Savonarola led out in his Dominican habit, standing before the Bishop as his black mantle, white scapulary, and long white tunic were stripped away one by one, until he stood clad in nothing but a plain, close-fitting woollen under-tunic, no mark of sacred office or rank left on him. He was degraded, cut off utterly from the Church Militant.

The baser sort of crowd delights in public degradation even when they hold no personal grudge: it is the kind of crude, obvious mockery they understand best. A fresh roar of triumphant hoots rose up as the three stripped, degraded friars were led on to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries, there to be formally named schismatics and heretics. Did not the prophet look exactly like a heretic now? It was far too easy to believe a man was damned when he stood stripped, humiliated, and powerless in front of thousands.

Next they passed the third tribunal, where the Florentine officials waited to pronounce their death sentence. Even at her distance, Romola could make out the loathsome figure of Dolfo Spini, dressed in the formal grave black lucco of a senior city magistrate, sitting as one of the Eight.

The three figures, in their plain thin white raiment, trod the long length of the scaffold platform amid yells, coarse jeers, and grating tones of unrelenting insult. “Cover your eyes, Madonna,” Nardi said. “Fra Girolamo will be the last to mount.”

It was only a few minutes before he bade her look again. Savonarola was there, near the top of the gibbet steps, so close she could see the lines of his worn, weary face as he looked out over the multitude.

In that exact instant, Romola’s last spark of expectation died. She saw exactly what he saw: hands waving torches to light the kindling that would burn his corpse, faces lit with a glare far crueller than the fire below. She heard exactly what he heard: gross jests, taunts, curses. The moment she had waited for never came. He spoke no vindication, no final word of truth. Romola covered her face again, and knew only that Savonarola’s voice had fallen into eternal silence.

On the evening of the 22nd of May 1509, eleven years after that execution, five people whose lives we have followed sat in a bright upper room that opened onto a wide loggia. At its right-hand corner, the loggia looked straight down the length of Borgo Pinti, over the city gate, and out toward the solemn, rolling heights of Fiesole beyond.

An archway at one end of the room led into a narrow inner recess, where light fell from a high ceiling onto a small altar draped in fine white linen. Above the altar hung a small full-length portrait of a Dominican brother, partially shaded by overhanging branches and woven flower wreaths, the unlit tapers below it glinting soft gold in the dim light. The decoration of the little shrine was not yet finished: part of the floor was strewn with loose petals, green boughs, and scattered blooms, and among them sat a delicate, blue-eyed thirteen-year-old girl named Ninna, tossing her long light brown hair out of her eyes as she sorted through flowers to weave into wreaths, pausing now and then to give her mother small, earnest instructions on how to get the knots right.

Her mother, Tessa, had never been very clever at weaving flowers, or much else of that kind of fine work. Her fingers had not grown more deft with the passing of the years—they had only grown rounder, plumper, softer. She worked slowly, twisting her head from side to side, and constantly asked Ninna’s opinion with gentle deference, for Tessa still never ceased to be astonished by how very wise her children were. She still wore her simple contadina gown, only cut broader now to fit her rounded middle; the same silver pin was stuck in her rough curly brown hair, and around her neck hung the old familiar necklace, strung over a red cord that vanished mysteriously beneath the neck of her dress. Her round, warm face wore an even deeper air of childlike, uncomplicated content than it had in her youth: Tessa thought everyone in the whole world was good. Even Monna Brigida never found fault with her now, and spent most of her days asleep, a habit Tessa considered the most amiable possible, and one she enjoyed very much herself.

Monna Brigida was asleep that very moment, propped in a straight-backed armchair a few paces away. Her hair, parted back from her face under her black hood, had that soft, silvery whiteness that is not like snow or anything else in the world, but simply the lovely, distinct whiteness of well-cared-for aged hair. Her chin had sunk to her bosom, her hands resting light on the chair’s arm. She had not been weaving flowers or doing any other work at all—she had only been sitting watching, as was her custom, and drifted off to sleep as she always did.

The other two sat farther away, right at the wide doorway that opened onto the loggia. Lillo, Tessa’s twelve-year-old son, sat on the floor with his back propped against the doorpost, his long legs stretched out before him, a large finely printed copy of Petrarch’s works open on his knee. Every few moments he swatted playfully at an inquisitive fly buzzing near his face, far more interested in the chase than the poem he was supposed to be memorizing, the *Spirto gentil* marked open and forgotten on the page.

Romola sat nearly opposite Lillo, but she was not watching him. Her hands were folded in her lap, her eyes fixed absently on the distant mountain peaks far beyond the loggia, and she seemed utterly unaware of anything happening around her. A life of eager struggle and sharp sorrow had left its marks on her: her finely moulded cheek had sunk a little, her golden hair was streaked with faint silver and far less thick than it had been in her youth, but her face held a placidity she had never once known in her younger days. We only ever bear our worst, sharpest sorrows once, and Romola had borne hers while life was still new and untested.

Lost in her thoughts, she did not notice at first that Lillo had abandoned his book and was watching her, a little impatient, clearly wanting to talk but unsure if she would welcome the interruption. But persistent, quiet gazes always make themselves felt at last. Romola turned her eyes from the distant hills, met Lillo’s dark, earnest gaze, and her face broke into a bright, soft smile. He shuffled across the floor toward her, the book still balanced on his knee, until he could rest his chin comfortably on her knee.

“What is it, Lillo?” Romola said, brushing his dark hair back from his forehead. Lillo was a handsome lad, his features growing broader and sturdier than his father’s, the warm blood of Tuscan peasant farmers running strong in his veins.

“Mamma Romola,” he said, very pleased that there was a good chance they could talk long enough to make memorizing that Petrarch poem unnecessary. “What am I going to be, when I grow up?”

“What would you like to be, Lillo? You could be a scholar, you know. My father was a scholar, and he taught me everything I know, so I could pass it all on to you.”

Lillo hesitated, fidgeting a little. “But the picture of him I’ve seen, the one where he’s old and blind—did he get a lot of glory for it?”

“Not much, Lillo,” Romola said softly. “The world was not kind to him, more often than not. He saw smaller, meaner men than him rise to far higher positions, just because they could flatter the powerful and lie whenever it suited them. Then his only son, the child he loved more than anything in the world, left him to join the Church. After that, blind and lonely, he did not have the strength to finish all the great work that would have made his learning useful to everyone, the work that would have kept his name alive for centuries after he died.”

“I don’t want that kind of life,” Lillo said, frowning. “I want to be a great man, and be happy, too—something that doesn’t stop me from having lots of pleasure and fun.”

“That is not easy, my Lillo. Any happiness that comes only from chasing your own small, narrow pleasures is thin, and fragile. The highest, truest happiness, the kind that comes with being truly great, only comes when you have wide, generous thoughts, and care for the rest of the world as much as you care for yourself. That kind of happiness almost always brings great pain along with it, so much pain that you can only tell it apart from ordinary suffering by the fact that you would choose it over anything else, because your soul knows it is good. The world is full of wrong and hardship, and no man can be truly great—he can barely even avoid being wicked—unless he stops thinking so much about his own pleasure, his own rewards, and finds the strength to endure what is hard and painful. My father had the greatness that comes from unbreakable integrity: he chose poverty and obscurity over telling lies to advance himself. And then there was Fra Girolamo—you know why we hold tomorrow sacred—he had the greatness that comes from a whole life spent fighting powerful, widespread wrong, and trying to lift ordinary people up to the best things they are capable of being. So if you want to act nobly, and seek out the best things God has put within reach of man, you have to fix your mind on that end, not on what reward or comfort you will get out of it. And remember, even if you choose something smaller, if you make it your whole life’s rule to chase your own pleasure and run from every hard, unpleasant thing, calamity will find you anyway. And then it will fall on a base, small mind, and that is the only kind of sorrow that has no balm at all, the kind that makes a man wish he had never been born. I will tell you something, Lillo—”

Romola paused, cupping the boy’s cheeks between her hands, her eyes meeting his wide, awed gaze.

“There was a man who was very close to me, whose whole life I got to see. Everyone loved him, because he was young, clever, beautiful, and always gentle and kind to everyone he met. When I first knew him, I do not think he ever had a cruel or base thought in his head. But because he spent every day running from anything unpleasant, caring more than anything else about his own safety and comfort, he ended up committing the basest acts, the kinds of deeds that make a man infamous for all time. He denied his own father, left him to die in misery, betrayed every trust anyone ever placed in him just to keep himself safe, get rich, get ahead. And calamity still found him, in the end.”

Her voice grew unsteady, and she stopped, smiling gently to brush the tears away before Lillo could see them. “Some other time, my Lillo. I will tell you the rest another day. Look—old Piero di Cosimo and Nello the barber are coming up Borgo Pinti, carrying armfuls of flowers for us. Let us go wave to them, so they know we see them.”

“How strange old Piero is!” Lillo laughed, as they leaned on the loggia rail watching the two old men trudge toward the house, their arms heaped with wild roses and jasmine. “He is always grumbling at you for decorating the altar and honouring Fra Girolamo, but he still brings you the best flowers he can find.”

Romola smiled, the soft wind tangling the silver threads in her hair. “Never mind him. There are many good people who never loved Fra Girolamo. And perhaps I would never have learned to love him, if he had not found me when I was lost, and given me the strength to keep going when I was at my very lowest.”
