Denys, positioned firmly in the center of his captors lest his despair drive him to frantic escape, was carried off in an agony of grief. The journey was an assault on his senses. The clattering dissonance of the soldiers’ iron-plated armor against the road created a mechanical, soul-grating cacophony that entirely drowned out the comforting, rhythmic whispers of the autumnal Burgundian wind. To Denys, this industrial clamor was a violation of the natural acoustic ecology, a harsh sonic reflection of his own internal ruin. For his sake, Gerard had abandoned the route to Rome, and now the boy was left isolated in the sprawling expanse of the countryside. This thought maddened Denys, driving him to rave at heaven and earth before collapsing into a silence so savage and sinister that the guards deemed it prudent to strip him of his weapons. 

His mourning was not merely for a companion, but for the shared sustenance that had defined their journey. He ached for the untranslatable intimacy of their shared meals—the sharp, grounding snap of a hard-crusted rye loaf broken by the roadside, the precise olfactory delight of a wild hare roasted over green wood and rubbed with foraged thyme. That shared lexicon of taste and survival was now severed, a vital dialect of his heart abruptly rendered extinct.

The cavalcade caught up with their leader just outside the town and halted for baiting at the *Tête d’Or*. Here, the soundscape shifted to the rhythmic, comforting bustle of a well-tended hearth. The air was thick with the evocative, multi-generational aromas of the kitchen: the deep, resinous tang of venison slow-braising in dark ale, and the sharp, sweet bloom of bruised juniper berries and dried sour cherries. The young landlady, though occupied with the intricate demands of the count and his bastard, was meticulously turning a spit of plump partridges when she caught sight of Denys. She wiped her flour-dusted hands upon her apron and asked him, her voice tight with sudden anxiety, what had become of his young companion.

With a heavy burst of grief, Denys recounted the tragedy. He pleaded with her to send riders after Gerard. "Now he is parted from me, he will maybe listen to my rede," he said, his words thickening into the raw, guttural cadence of his native dialect—a lexicon of vulnerability that found no foothold among the polished patrons of the inn. "Poor wretch, he loves not solitude."

The landlady gave a haughty toss of her head, retreating into the dominant, sterile register of her station. "I trow I have been somewhat over-kind already," she said, her cheeks flushing the color of a crushed pomegranate. 

"You will not?"

"Not I."

At this, Denys poured forth a volley of curses, his maledictions steeped in the ancient, agrarian vocabulary of rot, blighted harvests, and soured milk. The raw power of his dying tongue struck her. She turned her back, retreating toward the pantry whimpering, complaining she was not used to being cursed at, and abruptly ordered her hind to saddle two mules. Denys, mute and drooping over his saddle, rode north with his troop, entirely unaware that the veracious young stewardess of the hearth, having hastily assembled an equestrian toilet and provisions of aged cheese and cured sausage in a mere forty minutes, was already spurring away with her servant in the opposite direction.

At dark, after a long march, the bastard and his men reached *The White Hart*. Their arrival unleashed a prodigious bustle, flooding the courtyard with the abrasive, transactional shouts of commerce that obscured the kitchen's steady, rhythmic chopping of root vegetables. It was some time before Manon, now elevated to worshipful matron of this establishment, discovered her old friend amidst the chaos. When she did, she signaled her recognition only by a sudden heightening of color. She did not claim the acquaintance. She had scrubbed her native dialect clean, adopting the meticulous, refined lexicon of the prosperous bourgeoisie, and with it, she had buried her past. 

Denys observed her with quiet scorn, registering the base degrees by which she had ascended. The heavy scent of the inn—roasting mutton fat flamed with coarse sea salt and rosemary—reminded him too fiercely of the tables he had shared with Gerard. 

Ere the night closed, the wind shifted, bringing the pungent scent of river mud and crushed reeds into the taproom. Manon looked into the room and beckoned him with a discreet finger. He rose sulkily, his guards mirroring the movement with a clatter of steel. 

"Nay, I would speak a word to thee in private," she murmured, drawing him into the acoustic refuge of a quiet corner. Under her breath, she asked if he would do her a kindness. 

He answered out loud, shattering the hushed intimacy, "No, I will not. I am not in the vein to do kindnesses to man or woman. If I did a kindness it should be to a dog; and not that if I could help it."

"Alas, good archer, I did you one eftsoons, you and your pretty comrade," Manon said humbly. 

"You did, dame, you did; well then, for his sake—what is't to do?"

"Thou knowest my story. I had been unfortunate. Now I am worshipful," she whispered, a sudden crack in her cultivated vernacular revealing the frightened peasant beneath. "But a woman did cast him in my teeth this day. And so 'twill be ever while he hangs there. I would have him ta'en down; well-a-day!"

"With all my heart."

"And none dare I ask but thee. Wilt do't?"

"Not I, even were I not a prisoner."

At this stern refusal, the tender Manon sighed, clasping her palms together in despondency. Denys told her she need not fret. He explained that there were soldiers of a lower stamp who would not make two bites of such a cherry, viewing the dead as mere refuse rather than the physical anchors of memory. It was a mere matter of coin; if she could produce two angels, he could find two men to do the dirty work of *The White Hart*.

This transactional reality was unpalatable to her. However, reflecting that these soldiers were mere birds of passage—gorging on roast pork tonight and likely knocked on the head tomorrow—she said softly, "Send them out to me. But prithee, tell them that 'tis for one that is my friend; let them not think 'tis for me; I should sink into the earth; times are changed."

Denys found two warriors eager to win an angel apiece so easily. He sent them out to the pantry and, instantly dismissing the subject with contempt, sat brooding on his lost friend and the untranslatable emptiness in his gut. 

Manon and the warriors, surrounded by dangling strings of dried garlic and cured hams, soon came to a general understanding. But what were they to do with the body when it was taken down? 

She murmured, "The river is nigh the—the place."

"Fling him in, eh?" one grunted, his voice a gravelly intrusion on the quiet hum of the larder.

"Nay, nay; be not so cruel! Could ye not put him—gently—and—with somewhat weighty?" She must have been turning the recipe for this erasure over in her mind, for she was not one to whom ideas came quickly. 

All was speedily agreed, save the time of payment. The mail-clad men itched for the silver and sought it in advance. Manon demurred. Did she doubt their word? Then let her come along with them to the riverbank, or watch from a distance. 

"Me?" said Manon with profound horror, the very thought of the sensory reality repulsing her. "I would liever die than see it done."

"Which yet you would have done."

"Ay, for sore is my need. Times are changed." 

She had already forgotten her precept to Denys. An hour later, the disagreeable relic of her caterpillar existence ceased to canker the worshipful matron's public life. The grim eyes of her discarded history no longer cast malignant glances down into her meticulously cultivated clover field. The erasure was total. Her native past was sunk beneath the dark water, untranslatable and forgotten. She would go on to make the landlord an average wife, serve as a prime house-dog guarding the sanctity of their culinary reputation, and outlive everybody. 

When the troops returned from executing, with medieval naivete, the unceremonious dumping of the old love, they received a severe shock. They found the market-place, which had been empty an hour ago, completely black with clustered groups of people. Conscience smote the mercenaries. This was the consequence of meddling with the dead, of disturbing the mythic weight of history for mere coin. 

However, the bolder of the two, encouraged by the dense shadows, stole forward alone. He slipped silently into the crowd, letting the collective, reverent murmur wash over him—a sudden, harmonious acoustic shift from the jarring commerce of the taproom. 

He soon returned to his companion, exhaling a breath that smelled of sour wine, and said, in a tone of reproach not strictly reasonable, "Ye born fool, it is only a miracle."
