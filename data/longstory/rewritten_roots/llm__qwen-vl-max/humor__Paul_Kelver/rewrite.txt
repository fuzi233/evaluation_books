At the far end of a street so straight and brick-built it might have been designed by a bored architect who’d lost his coffee, in the East End of London—where the buildings stand like tired soldiers in formation, their windows aligned with the precision of a geometry exam gone wrong—there stands a house that insists on dragging me back to it. Not because I want to go, mind you. No, it’s more like an old relative who keeps calling, “Come visit! I’ve got your favorite cheese!” except the cheese is mildew, and the visit is mandatory.

I’ll be strolling along some bustling thoroughfare, dodging flaring naphtha lamps and people whose faces look like they’ve been dipped in lead and left out in the rain, when suddenly—*bam!*—my feet take over. They start marching without my permission, past narrow alleys where children fight over gutters like they’re treasure maps, past doorways that seem to vomit chaos like a bad takeaway, until finally I arrive at this particular house, which has somehow managed to stand out among its drab peers like a slightly-too-bright sock in a drawer full of grey ones.

It’s bigger than the others—built when the street was still just a muddy track near the marshes—and now it sits there, looking both proud and profoundly embarrassed. It’s surrounded by what used to be a garden but is now a patch of stones, dust, and clothes hanging from lines as if someone once thought, “Maybe I’ll wash these one day.” The porch is half-collapsed, the stucco peeling off like sunburnt skin, revealing the house’s secret: it’s not even trying anymore.

I sometimes wander by during daylight hours, when women gossip near area gates like they’re auditioning for a reality show called *Neighbours: East End Edition*, and the air is pierced by the mournful cry of “Coals—any coals—three and sixpence a sack—co-o-o-als!” sung in a tone so despondent it sounds like the voice of a man who’s already given up on life but hasn’t quite figured out how to stop singing.

But during the day, the house ignores me. It turns its face away, ashamed of its shabbiness under the unforgiving sun. It’s only when night falls—when darkness, the only true friend of the ugly, wraps itself around the place—that the house finally acknowledges me. Then, like a long-lost aunt who’s forgotten her manners but remembers her grudges, it opens its arms wide and begins to talk.

And oh, does it talk.

We sit there—me and the house—in silence at first, then it starts reminiscing about people I never knew, faces I can barely remember, lives that ended before I was born. Footsteps echo down the empty street, ghosts pass through the gate, and we discuss them like old friends catching up over tea (though the tea would probably be cold and made with water from the Thames).

“Look,” says the house, pointing at a shadowy figure approaching with brisk steps that pause every fifty yards or so, leaning heavily on a Malacca cane. “That one had a handsome face, didn’t he?”

“Aye,” I reply, though I’m not sure if I agree. “Handsome enough.”

“Handsome enough?” the house scoffs. “He was beautiful once. Before you and I met him. Before worry carved wrinkles into his forehead like a sculptor with a grudge.”

I ask, “Who did he resemble?”

The house sighs. “I never could tell. His parents were both good-looking, though Lord help us—what a pair of babies they were!”

“Babies?” I say, surprised. My father must have been well past thirty-five when this house first saw him. And my mother… well, she’s close enough to me in the dark that I can see the grey hairs mixing with her brown ones like salt in pepper.

“Children,” the house repeats, annoyed. “The most helpless pair I ever laid eyes on. Who else but a child would decide at thirty-eight to become a solicitor? And why Poplar of all places? What kind of genius thinks ‘Ah yes, let me open my law practice near the docks where everyone smells like fish and regret’?”

“It was supposed to be a rising area,” I protest, feeling defensive. Because no son likes hearing his parents mocked—even if deep down he agrees.

“Rising?” the house snorts. “The smell alone would drive any sensible man away. And”—its brick face contorts into something resembling a goblin grin—“he, of all people, the ‘cute solicitor,’ giving advice to shady clients eager to escape trouble the quick way? Can you imagine it? The man who once lived on five shillings a week, starving himself so no one would lose a penny from trusting him. Even your mother didn’t know about it until years later. Do you really think dock hustlers and freight wreckers would come to him for help? He’d be worse than useless.”

I don’t answer. The house doesn’t wait anyway.

“And your mother!” it continues. “Would any adult woman have taken that soft-tongued flirt into her heart without seeing right through her? Would anyone but a complete idiot have tried to live a life based on such nonsense? Children! Poor little fools, both of them.”

“Was there truth in the story?” I ask, because at moments like this, I begin to wonder if the house isn’t just bricks and mortar—but something older, wiser, maybe even a bit haunted.

“Truth?” the house snaps. “Just enough to plant a lie. You know how weeds grow. Not much soil needed. I saw what I saw. Your mother had a good man. Your father had a true wife. But men and women walk different paths. That’s the old tale. Doubt always follows.”

“But they came together in the end,” I say, remembering.

“Aye,” says the house. “That’s when you learn. When you reach the end.”

Then, from behind the railings, a small angel statue—clearly not recently washed—peeks shyly at me before darting back into hiding.

“What happened to her?” I ask.

“She’s fine,” the house replies. “Lives nearby. You passed her shop. She weighs fourteen stone, has nine kids. Would love to see you.”

“Thanks,” I say, laughing—though it’s not entirely laughter. “I think I’ll skip it.”

But I still hear the tiny tap-tap of her feet fading down the street.

Faces crowd around now. A large red-faced man smiles kindly, bringing back that odd mix of affection and disapproval I felt long ago. I turn away, and there’s another: clean-shaven, with the ugliest mouth and the sweetest eyes I’ve ever seen in a man.

“Was he as bad as they said?” I ask.

“Shouldn’t wonder,” the house replies. “Never knew a worse—or better.”

The wind blows, and the next face is a little old woman hobbling along with a stick. Three corkscrew curls bounce beside each ear. As she passes, she whispers, almost to herself, “I’d like to skin ’em. I’d like to skin ’em all. I’d like to skin ’em all alive!”

It sounds terrible. I laugh anyway. The old lady gives one final grotesque grimace and disappears.

Then comes a fair, laughing face—beautiful, but marred by time and sorrow. I saw it recently, though not really—it was just a faded version, painted over by Evil, who hates beauty. I stare, wishing it were dead so it wouldn’t change. And then, drifting from the shadows, comes the other face—the one with the wicked mouth and tender eyes. And suddenly I’m caught between two people I loved deeply: him, who taught me how to be a man; her, who showed me the mystery of women.

And again, the question rises: “Whose fault was it—hers or his?”

And again, I hear his mocking laugh: “Whose fault? God made us.”

Thinking of her, of the pure love I bore her—like a pilgrim loving a saint—I feel anger surge toward him. But when I look into his eyes and see the pain living there, my pity swells louder than my rage. So I whisper, “God made us.”

Merry faces, sad ones, pretty ones, ugly ones—they float on the breeze. But the center remains the same: a small boy with golden curls that look more feminine than masculine, shy, awkward, silent, with a face too serious for his age.

I turn to the house. “Do you think he’d recognize me if he saw me now?”

“How should he?” the house answers. “You’re nothing like what he’d expect. Would you recognize your own ghost?”

“It’s sad to think he wouldn’t know me,” I say.

“It might be sadder if he did,” grumbles the house.

We sit in silence. Then, as expected, the house speaks again.

“You—you writer of stories—why don’t you write a book about him? There’s something you know.”

Ah, yes. The house’s favorite idea. Every time I visit, it brings it up.

“But he did nothing,” I argue.

“He lived,” says the house. “Isn’t that enough?”

“Aye,” I retort, “but only in London, in these boring modern times. How do you make a story out of ordinary folk?”

The house gets impatient.

“The people!” it snaps. “What are you all but children waiting to be called to bed? One climbs on a stool and tells a tale. Who knows what will please them?”

Walking home through quiet streets, I ponder this. Is the house just being sentimental, like a mother convinced the whole world wants to hear about her kid? Or is there wisdom here?

Then I remember: there’s a certain section of the Public—usually female, often middle-aged—who demands stories from me every evening. I tell them tales of giants, princes, magic swords, ogres guarding castles, maidens rescued by wood-cutters’ sons, and princesses so beautiful they glow.

And still, they lean in closer, eyes gleaming, and say: “Now tell me a real story.” Then, helpfully: “You know—the kind about a little girl who lived in a big house with her parents, and sometimes got into trouble. You know.”

So perhaps—just perhaps—among all those readers, there might be someone who’d pause from the heroic battles and grand quests to listen to the tale of an ordinary boy growing up in a normal London street, becoming an ordinary man who loves a little, grieves a little, helps a few, hurts a few, hopes, fails, and struggles.

And if such a person exists—please come to me.

But don’t come if you expect fireworks or dragons or villains with dramatic monologues. Don’t come if you demand perfection. Because I warn you: I’m telling only what I’ve seen.

My villains aren’t evil—they’re just flawed. My heroes aren’t noble—they’re just trying. My dragons sometimes eat the princes. The good fairy often loses. The magic thread leads astray. And the hero? Sometimes he’s not brave. Sometimes he’s not true.

So come only if you’re willing to hear your own life told by someone else. If you’ll nod and say, “Yes, that’s true. That’s exactly how it was.” If you’ll smile and whisper, “Ah yes. So dreamt I.”

PAUL, ARRIVED IN A STRANGE LAND, LEARNS MANY THINGS, AND GOES TO MEET THE MAN IN GREY.

Fate had clearly marked me for greatness—well, at least for a life of dramatic irony. According to the calendar, I should have been born in June, which, as every sensible person knows, is the most auspicious month for launching a human being into existence. It’s like winning the lottery before you even buy a ticket. But no—some cosmic mix-up landed me in May, the month that apparently specializes in bad luck, heartbreak, and unfortunate coincidences. Whether this was due to my own premature eagerness (as Mrs. Fursey, my first and most memorable human contact, insisted) or some grand design by the universe remains a mystery. She also predicted, with the solemnity of a prophetess who’d seen too many baby teeth fall out, that my impatience would land me in deeper trouble later. Spoiler alert: she was right.

She told me the full story of my arrival, too. Apparently, I showed up two hours after the news reached the house that my father’s mining venture had gone under due to flooding. Because, obviously, misfortunes come in batches, like cheap sandwiches at a wedding buffet. To top it off, my mother—already reeling from the financial disaster—tripped while trying to ring the bell and shattered the cheval-glass in the hall. This, naturally, confirmed her Welsh superstition that any act of clumsiness involving glass spelled seven years of bad luck for the child involved. And so, with one broken mirror and one unexpected infant, the stage was set for what would become a very eventful life.

“Honestly,” sighed Mrs. Fursey, a woman whose knobby fingers seemed permanently attached to her thimble, “it does look like there might be something to that old saying.”

“Then ain’t I a lucky little boy?” I asked, because until then, she’d always painted my life as a divine gift wrapped in cotton wool and tied with a bow of destiny.

She blinked. “Oh, *you*? You’re lucky enough. I was just thinking about your poor mother.”

“Isn’t mamma lucky?”

“Well, not since you came.”

“Wasn’t it lucky her having me?”

“I can’t say it was, at that particular time.”

“Didn’t she want me?”

Mrs. Fursey, a woman of firm beliefs and questionable tact, paused. Then, with the weight of generations behind her, she said: “Well, I daresay she could’ve done without you.”

I sat there on my low chair, knee tucked under my chin like a philosopher contemplating the void, while her needle scraped against her thimble with the rhythm of a metronome trained in despair. The question formed itself in my mind like smoke rising from a campfire: *If she didn’t want me, why did she take me in?*

The scraping stopped.

“Took you in? What’s the child talking about? Who’s took you in?”

“Why, mamma. If she didn’t want me, why did she take me in?”

Even as I asked it—heart full of dignified indignation—I already knew the answer: *Because she did.* I couldn’t help but imagine the stork arriving at the bedroom window, flapping his wings like an overworked delivery drone, only to be shut out by a furious mother. I pictured him standing there, bewildered, scratching his head with one of those long, compass-like legs, muttering, “Geez, another one? No return address.” I wondered how he’d carried me. Had I squirmed in his beak like a worm in a blackbird’s mouth? Would I have ended up at Mrs. Fursey’s cottage? Probably not—she was stern, and I sensed she’d reject me with a glare and a muttered “No room for strays.” Mr. Chumdley, the cobbler, lived next door, but he was lame, sat in a hole all day hammering boots with filthy hands, and looked like a cross between a troll and a disgruntled accountant. I didn’t want to be his son. Maybe no one would have taken me. The thought made me feel small, adrift in a world where nobody wanted me.

And then, like a sudden lightning strike in a dark sky, I asked:
“Nurse, where did I come from?”

“You know,” she said, “the stork brought you.”

“Yes, but *where* did the stork get me from?”

She hesitated. Long enough that I started to wonder if she was afraid I’d start believing I was royalty. Eventually, she said: “Oh, from Heaven.”

“But I thought Heaven was where you went *to*, not where you came *from*.”

“Comed,” I added proudly, because at that age, I’d invented my own verbs and was quite proud of them.

Mrs. Fursey ignored the grammar. My mother had specifically instructed her not to correct my speech, claiming that confusion bred creativity. Or something like that.

“You came from Heaven,” she repeated, “and you’ll go to Heaven—if you’re good.”

“Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?”

“So they say.” Her tone suggested she wasn’t entirely convinced.

“And did you come from Heaven, Mrs. Fursey?”

She snapped back: “Of course I did. Where do you think I came from?”

That moment, Heaven lost its magic. Suddenly, it felt like a place where people were sent, not born. Like a post office for souls. And if Mrs. Fursey came from there… well, maybe it wasn’t such a great place after all.

But then came the real kicker:
“Didn’t they want me in Heaven either? Weren’t they fond of me up there?”

She softened. “Oh, they liked you well enough, I suppose. I like you, but sometimes I’m glad when you’re asleep.”

That didn’t comfort me. So God didn’t want me. My mother didn’t want me. Nobody wanted me. Why was I here?

And then—like a flickering candle in a storm—I felt it: Something. Not a thing, not a person, but a *presence*. Something that needed me. Something I belonged to. Something that was as much a part of me as my own breath. For a second, I *knew* I existed. I wasn’t just a body; I was *me*. Then the light went out, and I was back to being a confused child asking questions about life.

Then a new thought struck me:
“Nurse, why haven’t we got a husband?”

She dropped her needle.

“What’s gotten into the child now?”

“Mamma.”

“Don’t talk nonsense, Master Paul. Your mamma has a husband.”

“No, she ain’t.”

“And don’t contradict. Your papa is in London.”

“What’s the good of *him*?”

She gasped. “You wicked child! Your father works hard to earn money so you can live in idleness, and you ask ‘What’s the good of him?’ I’d be ashamed to be such an ungrateful brat!”

I hadn’t meant to be ungrateful. I’d overheard my mother and aunt arguing earlier:

“Auntie,” my mother had said, “I hate not having a husband.”

“Help us and save us!” Aunt replied. “How many more does a girl need? She’s got one.”

“But what’s the good of him all the way off in London? I want him here where I can *get at* him.”

Ah. That explained it. My father was a ghost—a man of spiritual importance but zero physical presence. He didn’t carry me piggyback. He didn’t take me sailing. He was just a name on a letter, a distant figure in a foggy city.

So I said: “I mean a husband you can *get at*.”

Mrs. Fursey sighed. “Well, you’ll get at him one of these days. When he’s ready, he’ll send for you. Then you’ll go to London.”

“How do you get to London? Do you have to die first?”

She stared. “I do think,” she said, voice thick with resignation, “that you are the silliest little boy I’ve ever known.”

“I’m sorry, nurse,” I said. “I thought—”

“Then you shouldn’t think. London is a big town. You go there by train. Soon, your father will write, and you’ll leave this place. And I’ll be rid of you.”

“Shan’t we come back?”

“Never again.”

“Never play in the garden? Never go to the pebble-ridge? Never see Anna or Tom Pinfold or Pincher or you?”

“Never again.” She said it like a blessing.

I clung to her. “Then I’ll never see anyone I love again?”

“Never again. You’ll begin a new life. And I hope, Master Paul,” she added, piously, “it may be better.”

But I wasn’t listening. I was staring into the fire, wondering how life worked. One minute you were here, comfortable, familiar. The next, you were gone. Friends vanished. Places disappeared. Life was a strange, fleeting thing. And if you wanted to understand it, you had to sit quietly, stare into the flames, and stop talking.

That night, when my mother kissed me goodnight, I turned my face to the wall. But when I felt her curls brush my cheek, I gave in. I grabbed her neck, pulled her down, and whispered:
“I suppose you couldn’t send me back now? You’ve had me so long.”

“Send you back?”

“Yes. I’d be too big for the stork to carry now, wouldn’t I?”

She knelt beside me, our faces level. The fear melted away.

“Who’s been talking foolishly to a foolish little boy?” she asked, still holding me tight.

“Oh, nurse and I were discussing things,” I said. “She said you could have done without me.”

She laughed. Then grew serious. “I didn’t know you thought about such things. We must be together more, you and I. You tell me everything. Nurse doesn’t understand you.”

“It’s true,” I said. “I came at a bad time.”

She smiled. “It was hard. But I couldn’t have done without you. You were sent to comfort me.”

“Then it was lucky?”

She laughed again. Then, seriously: “Will you remember what I’m going to say?”

“I’ll try. But I ain’t got a very good memory.”

“Not very,” she smiled. “But if you think about it, it won’t leave you. When you’re a good boy, and later a good man, I’ll be the luckiest mother in the world. And every time you fail… that means bad luck for me. Remember that after I’m gone, Paul?”

I promised. And though I laugh now at the image of two earnest, childish faces, I believe it shaped me. Even if I’ve failed often, I’d have failed worse had I forgotten.

From then on, my mother grew brighter in my memory. Mrs. Fursey, with her thimble and her wisdom, faded. We had sunny mornings in the garden, twilight whispers in the window seat, stories of saints and demons, kings and fairies. I saw Aphrodite walk toward King Canute, who refused to let her wet his feet. Robin Hood shot King Rufus, but Queen Eleanor saved him. Oliver Cromwell married Charles’s widow and was stabbed by Hamlet. Ulysses discovered America. Romulus and Remus rescued Little Red Riding Hood. Good King Arthur was murdered for burning cakes. Prometheus was saved by St. George. Paris gave the apple to William Tell.

Who cared? The facts were there. They just needed rearranging.

Sometimes we climbed the winding path through the woods—past haunted cliffs, fairy-danced meadows, and caves where monsters lurked—until we reached Jacob’s Tower, the Devil’s Tower, or Jacob’s Folly. Legend said Jacob and the Devil met there to lure ships to their doom. I never learned who Jacob was, nor why he built it. But on stormy nights, fishermen swore lights flashed from its ivy-covered windows.

By day, it was magical. From the top platform, you saw mountains like ghosts, ships drifting into silence, farms and rivers below. The world felt vast. Now, behind those hills lie smoky towns. Now, the ocean is six days to New York. Then, it stretched beyond the moon, beyond the clouds, to lands of red shores and golden suns. I never dreamed the world could be so small.

On the tower’s ledge, hand in hand, my mother would fill the air with myths—perhaps too many—but they were beautiful. She taught me pity, love, wonder. And sometimes, forgetting the time, we’d race home through the dark, gripping each other’s hands.

Spring became summer, summer autumn. Then one morning, my aunt and I waited at breakfast as my mother skipped down the garden path, waving a letter.

“Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, then comes Wednesday morning!” she sang.

She danced with me around the room.

My aunt, chewing bread and butter like a robot, muttered: “Just like 'em all. Goes mad with joy. What for? Moving to a poky hole in the East End, keeping one servant.”

“Not a poky hole,” my mother said. “An old-fashioned house near the river.”

“Plaistow marshes!” my aunt spat. “Calls it the river!”

“So it is the river,” my mother said. “The river is on the other side of the marshes.”

“Let’s hope it stays there,” my aunt said.

“And it’s got a garden,” my mother continued. “Unusual in London. And it’s not the East End—it’s a rising suburb. And you won’t make me miserable because I’m happy.”

“Drat the woman!” my aunt said. “Why can’t she sit down and give us tea before it’s cold?”

“You’re a disagreeable thing!” my mother said.

“Not half milk,” my aunt replied. She never cared what others thought.

For three days, my mother packed and sang, unpacked and laughed, finding treasures at the bottom of boxes. Anna, waiting for a certain undergarment, suggested: “If I were you, ma'am, I’d check the last box first.”

My mother, ignoring her, found it in the first box—because she’d reserved the first one for last. She scolded Anna for wasting time.

By Tuesday, everything was ready. We left early Wednesday.

That evening, I found my mother sitting under the lime tree, tears in her eyes.

“But I thought you were glad,” I said.

“I am,” she said, drying her eyes. “But I’m sad to leave.”

Adults were confusing. Still are. But that night, I understood something important: love isn’t just joy. It’s sorrow, too. And that’s what makes it real.

Let me just say right off: my parents’ marriage was not your average run-of-the-mill domestic disaster. No, it was something far more complicated—like a comedy written by a poet who’s had one too many and then tried to make sense of it all the next morning. They’d been separated for years, though not legally divorced, and while they weren’t exactly ancient (my father was nearly forty, my mother barely thirty), they looked like relics from the Stone Age as far as I was concerned. And yet, in their hearts? Well, let’s just say they were still very much boys and girls playing at being grown-ups. It took me some time to realize this, but if I’d ever been asked by a curious spinster or a lonely bachelor what marriage was really like, I might have given them the wrong idea entirely.

I’d have described a husband as someone who couldn’t stop calling out “Maggie, are you doing anything important? I need to talk business!” twenty times a day, even when he was standing five feet away. Or worse: “Maggie, are you alone? Oh, never mind, I’ll come down.” A wife, I would have said, was a woman whose eyes sparkled with love every time she saw her man—glad when he was near, anxious when he wasn’t. But honestly? That description might have been wildly inaccurate.

And I’d have also warned against certain marital hazards. Like leaving your feet under the table—because if they’re mistaken for someone else’s, you might end up getting trodden on. Or entering a room without first making noise outside—what we called “noise without” in Stageland. Nothing ruins an entrance like opening a door only to be greeted by the sound of people leaping into the air, followed by frantic scurrying, and then discovering two stiff figures sitting in opposite corners pretending to read books or sew. It’s enough to make a person question whether they’ve entered a house or a scene from *The Hound of the Baskervilles*.

But again—some households didn’t need such precautions. Ours did. Not because my parents were particularly dramatic, but because Aunt Fan was.

Now, Aunt Fan was a woman of extreme sensitivity. She moved through the house like a ghost with a coughing fit. Every step she took was accompanied by a deep, resonant cough that could have powered a small steam engine. My father once joked—ungratefully, I must say—that her daily output of phlegm could rival the final gasps of an entire ward full of consumptives. To a healthy woman, this must have been exhausting. Yet she never spared their feelings. If she was going into a room where my parents might be, she’d enter backwards, slamming the door shut before turning around. In dark hallways, she’d sing. Not a tune, mind you—a kind of mournful, off-key hum that sounded like a bad omen. No woman could have been more considerate. Still… human nature being what it is, neither my father nor my mother appreciated her efforts.

In fact, the more she tried to help, the more they resented her.

“I believe, Fanny,” my mother said one day, catching her dropping teaspoons at five-second intervals outside the drawing-room door, “you hate seeing Luke and me happy together.”

“Hate it?” Aunt Fan replied, unfazed. “Why should I? Why shouldn’t a pair of turtle doves bill and coo when their combined age is only seventy-one? The pretty dears!”

It was her calmness that always surprised me. I expected fireworks. Instead, I got tea and sympathy.

My father, however, was another story. He could get genuinely furious. I remember the moment clearly—we were walking along the canal bank, the sun setting behind us like a burnt orange.

“Your aunt,” he began, his voice low but heavy. I couldn’t tell if he meant it, but whenever he mentioned Aunt Fan, he made it sound like I’d personally summoned her from the depths of hell. This used to weigh on me. “Your aunt is the most cantankerous, the most—” He broke off, shaking his fist at the sky like a man cursing fate itself. “I wish to God,” he said, “your aunt had a comfortable little income and a freehold cottage in the country. By God, I do!”

Then, suddenly ashamed, he added, “Not that she isn’t sometimes nice. You know that. Don’t tell your mother what I just said.”

So there you go. One minute he’s ready to send her packing, the next he’s defending her.

Another observer of our household drama was Susan, our maid-of-all-work. The first in a long line of domestics (culminating in Amy, whom heaven bless), Susan was stout, elderly, and prone to sudden sleep attacks—likely due to “trouble,” as we were told. But her heart, she insisted, was always in the right place. Which was good, because every time she saw my parents sitting close together, she’d flop down and weep. Connubial bliss reminded her, she explained, of the glorious days of her own marriage.

I never quite understood what happened to her husband. Her stories were like a train wreck—confusing, dramatic, and hard to follow.

On Monday, I watched her cry over the Brussels sprouts, lamenting how she hadn’t been able to water “his” grave lately—Manor Park Cemetery, she said. Then, on Tuesday, she told me in chilling detail what she’d do if she ever got her fingers near the scruff of his neck again.

“But, Susan,” I said, “I thought he was dead.”

“So did I, Master Paul,” she replied. “That was his artfulness.”

“Then he isn’t buried?”

“Not yet. But he’ll wish he was, the half-baked monkey, when I get hold of him.”

“Wasn’t he a good man?”

“Who?”

“My husband.”

“Who says he ain’t? If anyone says he ain’t, I’ll gouge their eye out!”

Her shifts in tone were baffling. One second she was grieving, the next threatening murder. I’d usually retreat to German grammar or mixed fractions at this point.

Susan caused us no end of trouble. We felt obliged to overlook her flaws—except Aunt Fan, who had no patience for sentimentality.

“She’s a lazy hussy,” Aunt Fan declared one morning, rinsing her hands. “A gulping, snorting, lazy hussy, that’s what she is.”

She had a point. It was eleven o’clock, and Susan was still asleep, recovering from what she called “new-ralgy.”

“She’s seen a lot of trouble,” my mother said gently, wiping the sink.

“And if she was my cook and housemaid,” Aunt Fan snapped, “she’d see more, the slut!”

“She’s not perfect,” my mother admitted, “but I think she’s good-hearted.”

“Oh, drat her heart,” Aunt Fan shot back. “The right place for that heart is on the doorstep. And that’s where I’d put it—if I had my way.”

Susan left shortly after. It happened one Saturday night. My mother came upstairs pale as a sheet.

“Luke,” she said, “run for the doctor.”

“What’s wrong?” my father asked.

“Susan,” she gasped. “She’s lying on the kitchen floor, breathing strangely, can’t speak.”

“I’ll go for Washburn,” my father said. “If I’m quick, I’ll catch him at the dispensary.”

Five minutes later, he returned, panting, followed by a man so large and black-bearded he seemed to have stepped out of a pirate novel. Dr. Washburn. He descended the stairs two at a time, shaking the whole house. He brushed my mother aside and bent over Susan, who lay on her back with her mouth wide open. Then he stood up, looked at my parents, and opened his mouth—only to unleash a roar of laughter so loud it made the walls tremble.

Next thing we knew, he grabbed a pail of water and threw it over Susan. She sat up, sputtering.

“Feeling better?” he asked, still holding the pail. “Want another dose?”

Susan started to protest, but he pushed us all out and slammed the door.

From the stairs, we heard her thick voice raging, punctuated by the doctor’s booming laughter. When she paused for breath, he’d yell encouragement: “Bravo! Go it, my beauty! Bark, bark! I love to hear you!” He clapped, stamped, roared—like a man conducting a circus of rage.

“What a beast,” my mother muttered.

“He’s fascinating,” my father said. “You wouldn’t think it, but he’s worshipped around here.”

“I don’t ever mean to know him,” my mother said stiffly.

But of course, we already did.

Eventually, the noise stopped. A quiet voice spoke, then we heard the doctor’s footsteps returning.

My mother held out her purse. “How much do we owe you, Doctor?” she asked, voice trembling.

He closed the purse and handed it back. “A glass of beer and a chop, Mrs. Kelver,” he said. “I’m coming back in an hour to cook them myself. And since you’ll be without a servant, I’ll cook some for you too. I’m an expert at grilling chops.”

“But, Doctor—” she began.

He laid his huge hand on her shoulder. “My dear lady,” he said, “she’s a person you never ought to have had in your house. She promised to leave in half an hour. I’m coming back to make sure she does. Give her a month’s wages and keep a fire ready.”

Before she could respond, he slammed the front door.

“What a strange man,” my mother said.

“He’s a character,” my father said. “You’d never guess, but he’s beloved in these parts.”

“I don’t know what to make of him,” my mother said. “I suppose I should go out and get some chops.”

She did.

Susan left as sober as a judge on Friday—her main concern being to escape before the doctor returned. He arrived on time, and I lay awake—no one ever slept near Dr. Washburn—and listened to the gales of laughter sweeping through the house. Even Aunt Fan laughed. When the doctor laughed, it felt like the bed itself was shaking.

I couldn’t take it anymore. I wrapped my blanket around my legs and tiptoed downstairs, face set in the expression of a child desperately seeking parental affection. My mother pretended to be angry, but I knew it was just her act. Besides, I really did feel a peculiar discomfort—like I’d swallowed the dome of St. Paul’s. The doctor said it was common in children, caused by late hours and too much study. He took me on his knee and wrote a diet plan on the spot: one tablespoon of golden syrup four times a day, one ounce of sherbet ten minutes before each meal, taken neat.

That evening remains vivid in my memory. My mother was brighter than ever—rosy cheeks, sparkling eyes. Sitting across from her, shielding her face from the fire with a painted screen, I suddenly felt the birth of beauty within me. Without thinking, I slipped from his knee and squatted by the fender, stealing glances at her face whenever I thought no one was watching.

My father seemed taller, more dignified, speaking with new energy. Aunt Fan was witty—well, as witty as she ever got. Even I asked a few questions, which somehow made everyone laugh. I resolved to ask them again later.

That was the magic of Dr. Washburn. With his raw vitality, he drew the best from everyone. Clever people became geniuses; dull ones discovered hidden brilliance. Podsnap might have been sharp, Dogberry incisive. But best of all was listening to him talk. I couldn’t tell you what he said—just like the children of Hamelin couldn’t recall the Pied Piper’s tune. But his voice, strong and tangled, made the walls fade. Beyond them, I saw a world of joyous battle, where some won, some lost—but it didn’t matter. There was a grand game for all, a world where merry gentlemen feared neither life nor death, and Fate was merely the Master of the Revels.

That was my first meeting with Dr. Washburn—or Dr. Fighting Hal, as he was known in every alley and slum of the neighborhood. And that evening was a miniature portrait of the man. Sometimes he wrinkled his nose like a dog about to bite—brutish in instinct, appetite, pleasure, fun. Other times, his deep blue eyes softened like a mother’s, and he seemed an angel in a soft felt hat and a coat so loose it hinted at wings folded beneath.

I’ve often wondered whether knowing him helped me or hurt me. But it’s like asking a tree whether the wind shaped it or warped it. Impossible to say.

Susan’s position remained empty for months. My mother would explain to visiting friends that her “housemaid” had been dismissed and we were waiting for a better specimen. But weeks turned into months. My father, on rare client visits, would open the door himself and shout down indignantly: “Why can’t Jane or Mary do their work?”

My mother, fearing gossip, would hide behind a veil and fetch everything herself from distant streets.

We were weak, yes—human. Ashamed of our poverty, we lived in constant pretense. The larder was often bare, but the port and sherry with sweet biscuits always stood on the sideboard. The fire was kept low so my father’s hat could shine on Sundays, my mother’s silk rustle.

But I wouldn’t sneer. Some writers—men whose world ends at Bond Street—see poverty’s attempts to hide its flaws as mere comedy. But I can’t laugh. I know the cost of the cream and ice jelly ordered weeks in advance. I know the hope tied to a hired waiter. It’s pathetic, not ridiculous. Heroism comes in many forms. Dr. Washburn would have shared his bread and cheese with the Prince of Wales. My parents served weak tea so guests might have wine. I remember the one egg at breakfast—my mother arguing my father should have it for work, my father insisting my mother needed it for shopping. They split it, each fighting for the white. And though the meal looked meager, I always left satisfied.

To me, this life was golden. Eating treacle instead of butter? Brilliant. Sausages for dinner? Perfect. A stealthy carpet-bag supper of fried fish and potatoes? Better than any formal meal.

There was fun in everything—the cleaning of the doorstep at night, when a gentleman with a sack round his legs might pass for a tall charwoman. I’d watch at the gate, whistle softly, help my father find the hearthstone in the dark—he always lost it.

Better than studying French verbs or calculating when X would overtake Y, especially when no one told you how thirsty either of them were.

Even my parents took it lightly—full of laughter, no groaning. Because above it all hovered hope, like sunlight beyond a dungeon wall.

“I had no idea,” my mother said one day, “there’s so much to do in a house. From now on, servants should have regular hours and time to rest. Don’t you agree, Luke?”

“Quite right,” my father said. “And I’ll tell you another thing—we’ll insist the landlord puts a marble doorstep in the next house we rent. You wipe marble, and it’s always clean.”

“Or tesselated,” my mother suggested.

“Or tesselated,” he agreed. “But marble is more uncommon.”

Only once did clouds gather. One Sunday, mid-dinner, my mother said, “We might save the stew, Luke. There’s an omelette coming.”

My father dropped his spoon. “An omelette?”

“Yes,” she said. “I thought I’d try again.”

He marched to the back kitchen and returned with the wood chopper.

“What are you going to do with that?” she asked.

“Divide the omelette,” he said.

She began to cry.

“Why, Maggie—!”

“I know the last one was leathery,” she said, “but it was the oven’s fault, Luke.”

“My dear,” he said, “I was only joking.”

“It’s not nice,” she said. “I don’t like jokes like that.”

I suspect my mother didn’t like any joke that targeted her. Neither did I, come to think of it.

Still, my father and I grew close. He was the youngest thing I’d ever met. Sometimes my mother seemed young too, and I’d meet peers closer to my age—but they changed. My father stayed the same. His temples were grey, lines creased his face, especially around the mouth. But his eyes were boyish, his laugh boyish, his heart boyish. So we were inseparable.

In our tiny garden, we played cricket with rules made for survival—no hitting to leg (danger to windows), no hitting to off (ball lost to urchins). We’d rise early and walk across the marshes to bathe in a creek—muddy, yes, but fun. On rare days, we’d take the train to Hackney, walk to the bridge, and row up the Lea River—maybe as far as Ponder’s End.

And that, dear reader, is how I came to know the man with the ugly mouth.

“Louisa!” bellowed my father down the kitchen stairs, “are you all asleep? Here I am, forced to answer the front door myself!” Then he stomped into his office and slammed the door behind him. My father could be *very* angry when nobody was around to witness it—like a lion roaring at an empty cage.

Fifteen minutes later, his bell rang with a sharp, authoritative jingle. My mother, who was peeling potatoes in wash-leather gloves (a task that looked more like torture than cooking), glanced at my aunt, who was shelling peas with the precision of a nervous squirrel. The bell rang again—louder this time.

“Once for Louisa, twice for James, isn’t it?” asked my aunt, as if she were solving a cryptic crossword.

“You go, Paul,” said my mother. “Say that Louisa—” But before she could finish, her face flushed crimson, like a tomato that’d just been told it was on a diet. She yanked off her gloves and stepped past me. “No, don’t stop your lessons—I’ll go myself,” she declared, and dashed out.

A few minutes later, the kitchen door creaked open softly, and my mother’s hand appeared through the crack, beckoning me like a conspirator in a spy novel.

“Walk on your toes,” she whispered, leading the way up the stairs—her own feet barely touching the floor. And oh, how the stairs protested! They groaned and squealed louder than ever before, as though they knew we were doing something *very* wrong. Still, we crept upward, silent as shadows, alert as cats, until we reached my parents’ bedroom.

There, tucked away in the old-fashioned wardrobe—a relic from better days—lay my best suit of clothes. Or rather, my *better* suit. Never before had I worn it on a weekday morning. But my mother ignored all conversation not directly related to getting me into it *quickly*. Once fully dressed—even down to the new Bluchers (we called them that back then)—she took my hand, and together we tiptoed back down the stairs, just as stealthily as we’d come.

She led me to the street door and opened it.

“Shan’t I want my cap?” I whispered.

But my mother only shook her head and shut the door with a bang. That was when the mystery of the pantomime unfolded. With such dramatic flair—comic? Tragic?—I was becoming quite familiar. And so, with her hand firmly on my shoulder, we entered my father’s office.

Now, whether it was because our drawing-room was reserved as a showroom in case of unexpected visitors—Cowper’s poems left open, face-down, on the wobbly loo table; half-finished crochet work draped carelessly over the armchair, suggesting elegant leisure—and all the books and papers shoved aside as if they were yesterday’s trash; or because my father would often burst out of the office to play cricket in the back garden on dry days, or ninepins in the passage on wet ones, charging back in whenever a knock sounded at the front door—I can’t say. But as a child, I never once thought of my father’s profession as serious business. To me, he was merely playing a game, surrounded by big books and bundles of documents labeled with grand titles but filled with nothing but blank paper. Japanned tin boxes, labeled with imposing names, mostly held air. “Sutton Hampden, Esq.” was practically my mother’s sewing box. “Drayton Estates” produced apples—my father’s favorite fruit. And “Mortgages”? Well, I didn’t realize until much later that it had nothing to do with poetry manuscripts, some still being corrected, others already complete.

As the door opened, my father rose and came toward us. His hair stood up in tufts—he had a habit of ruffling it while talking—and combined with his effort to look grave and businesslike, he resembled a schoolboy trying too hard to act like a man.

“This is the youngster,” said my father, handing me over. “Tall for his age, isn’t he?”

With a twist of his thick lips, he rolled the stinking cigar from one corner of his mouth to the other and extended a fat, slightly greasy hand. When it closed around mine, I immediately pictured the walrus from my natural history book. With his free hand, he patted me kindly on the head.

“Like ‘is mother, wonderfully like ‘is mother, ain’t ‘e?” he observed, still holding my hand. “And that,” he added, winking one small eye toward my father, “is about the highest compliment I can pay ‘im, eh?”

His eyes were tiny, but startlingly bright and piercing. When he turned them on me, I felt compelled to think of something nice about him—because I was absolutely certain he could see right into my soul.

“And where are you thinking of sending ‘im?” he continued. “Eton or ‘Arrow?”

“We haven’t quite decided yet,” replied my father. “At present, we’re educating him at home.”

“You take my tip,” said the fat man, “and learn all you can. Look at me! If I’d had the chance to be a scholar, I wouldn’t be here offering your father an extravagant price for doing my work—I’d be able to do it myself.”

“You seem to have done pretty well without it,” laughed my father. And honestly, the man’s air of prosperity might have justified even greater pride. Rings sparkled on his blunt fingers, and the massive gold cable chain on his waistcoat swayed with every breath he took.

“I’d have done better with it,” he grunted.

“But you look very clever,” I said. Though I sensed—childishly—that I was supposed to impress him, I genuinely meant it. The words just slipped out.

He roared with laughter, his whole body shaking like a jellyfish caught in a storm.

“Well, old Noel Hasluck’s not exactly a fool,” he admitted, “but I’d like myself better if I could talk about something besides business and didn’t drop my h’s. And so would my little gell.”

“You have a daughter?” asked my mother, whose sympathy for strangers was always sparked by children—just as most women find solace in incompetent maids and disrespectful cooks.

“I won’t tell you about ‘er,” he said. “But I’ll bring ‘er to see you now and then, ma'am, if you don’t mind. She don’t often meet gentle-folks, and it’ll do ‘er good.”

My mother glanced at my father, but the man intercepted her unspoken question.

“You needn’t worry, ma'am,” he assured her cheerfully. “She’s nothing like me. Nobody believes she’s my daughter—except me and the old woman. She’s a little lady, she is. A freak o’ nature, I call it.”

“We shall be delighted,” said my mother.

“Well, you will be when you see ‘er,” he replied, satisfied.

Then he pushed a half-crown into my hand, brushing aside my parents’ protests with the easy confidence of someone used to getting his way.

“No squanderin’ it on the ‘eathen,” he warned as I left the room. “You spend that on a Christian tradesman.”

That was the first money I ever remember having to spend—old Hasluck’s half-crown. Until then, dreams of Sunday gloves or a Latin grammar had danced in my head whenever coins landed in my palm. But this time, I was free to decide.

The anxiety! The sleepless nights tossing in bed! The endless tramping through bewildering streets! Even advice was denied.

“You must learn to think for yourself,” said my father, speaking with the eloquence of a man convinced early judgment and “commercial aptitude” were life’s greatest virtues.

“No, dear,” said my mother. “Mr. Hasluck wanted you to spend it as you liked. If I told you what to do, that would be spending it as *I* liked. Your father and I want to see what you’ll do with it.”

The good little boys in books bought presents or gave to the poor. For this, I hated them with the intensity of a lower animal scornful of higher ideals. I consulted my aunt Fan.

“If somebody gave you half-a-crown,” I asked, “what would you buy?”

“Side-combs,” she replied. She was always losing or breaking hers.

“But I mean if you were me,” I explained.

“Drat the child!” she snapped. “How do I know what he wants if he doesn’t know himself? Idiot!”

I stared into shop windows, nose pressed flat against the glass. I asked prices. I decided on things, then changed my mind. Even my patient mother began to show signs of irritation. It was rapidly becoming a family curse—old Hasluck’s half-crown.

Then one day, I made up my mind. And so ended the trouble.

In the window of a small plumber’s shop near our house, among brass taps, rolls of lead piping, and cistern parts, sat squares of colored glass—used, I believe, for lavatory doors and staircase windows. Some had stars in the center; others, more elaborate, bore severe but inoffensive designs. I bought a dozen. The plumber, a cheerful man who seemed glad to see me, threw in two extra “out of sheer generosity.”

Why I bought them, I couldn’t say then—and I still don’t know now. My mother cried when she saw them. My father could only manage: “But what are you going to do with them?” To which I had no answer. Only my aunt offered comfort.

“If a person fancies colored glass,” she said, “then he’s a fool not to buy colored glass when he gets the chance. We haven’t all the same tastes.”

Eventually, I cut myself badly with them and agreed to throw them in the dustbin. But looking back, I’ve come to see myself less as a victim of folly and more of fate. I’ve met many since—recipients of Hasluck’s half-crowns—men who slap their pockets and bless the day they met that “Napoleon of Finance,” as he later became known among friends. But it always ends the same: colored glass and bleeding fingers. Is it fairy gold they fling around? It seems so.

Next time old Hasluck knocked, a maid in cap and apron opened the door. That was just the beginning. New oilcloth gleamed in the hall. Lace curtains—such as only plutocrats wore—adorned the windows, announcing our rising fortunes to the entire street. And the greatest marvel of all—at least to my awed eyes—my father’s Sunday clothes now came out on weekdays, replaced by fresh ones in the great wardrobe that had once been our last stronghold of gentility. The place where we went when the outer walls of our lives looked weak. “Seeing that everything was all right,” my mother would explain. She’d lay the lilac silk on the bed, smoothing its rustling folds, lingering over the deep frosted flounces of rich Honiton lace. Maybe she’d entered tired and downcast, but soon she’d begin humming—softly, like a tiny winged creature warmed by sunlight. By the time the Indian shawl (which could pass through a wedding ring, but never would when needed) was refolded and pinned with the great cameo brooch, and the poke bonnet—like a fractious child—was coaxed into submission, she’d be singing softly, nodding her head to the tune: “Somebody’s too old, somebody else too bold, another too cold—so he wouldn’t do for me.” And she’d step lightly, as though the weight of years had lifted.

One evening—before Hasluck arrived—I remember climbing out of bed. Trouble was within me. Heavy, indescribable creatures sat upon my chest. Then I fell downstairs—slowly at first, then faster and faster—until I found myself in the street wearing only my nightshirt. Personally, I was horrified. But no one else seemed to care. I hailed a two-penny bus and climbed aboard. But when I tried to pay, I realized I had no pockets. So I jumped out and ran. The conductor chased after me. My feet were like lead. With each step, he gained ground. Finally, I screamed and woke up.

Feeling the need of comfort, I wrapped some clothes around me and crept downstairs. The office was dark, but to my surprise, a light shone under the drawing-room door. I opened it.

Candles burned in silver candlesticks. My father and mother sat regally—one in each easy chair—in their finest clothes. My father wore buckled shoes and a frilled shirt I’d never seen before. My mother wore the Indian shawl and the ceremonial cap that lived three hundred and sixty days a year in its round wicker nest lined with silk. They jumped guiltily as I entered, but I wisely said nothing. I had sense—or instinct.

Earlier that evening, I’d seen them cleaning the ground-floor windows—my father outside, my mother inside. Now, their appearance, manner, even their voices, had transformed. My father brought over sherry and sweet biscuits from the sideboard, poured a glass for my mother, and they drank to each other. I ate the biscuits between them. The conversation drifted to Byron’s poems and the great glass palace in Hyde Park.

Do I betray them by writing this? Perhaps to others, it shows only foolishness. But that’s not my intent. I dwell on such trifles because they’re tender to me. We admire our loved ones’ virtues—but that’s expected. Their failings we wish to forget. But their follies? Those we linger over, smiling.

To me personally, old Hasluck’s arrival changed everything. My father became busy all day—either in his office or off in the grim city of giants, as I still thought of London. My mother took on more social and domestic duties. For a time, I was left to my own devices.

Rambling—“bummelling,” as the Germans call it—was my passion. My mother would have stopped me, but my father said:

“Don’t molly-coddle him. Let him learn to be smart.”

“I don’t think smart people are always the nicest,” she demurred. “I don’t call you ‘smart,’ Luke.”

My father looked surprised, then thoughtful.

“I suppose I’m smart—in a sense,” he admitted.

“Perhaps you’re right, dear,” she replied. “And of course boys are different from girls.”

Sometimes I wandered toward Victoria Park, then surrounded by leafy cottages. Occasionally, I reached Clapton, where red-brick Georgian houses stood behind high palings, and tall elms cast peaceful shadows on sunny afternoons. But mostly, I was drawn to the narrow, squalid streets—not the main thoroughfares, thrumming with the dull pulse of a tireless iron machine, where human chains streamed endlessly, crossing and recrossing like gears in a clockwork beast—but the dim, weary, lifeless lanes—the dark, twisted roots, as I imagined them, of that grim forest of brick. Mystery lurked in their gloom. Fear whispered from their silence. Dumb figures flitted swiftly, never pausing, never glancing. Footsteps echoed, rising and fading. I dreaded them, yet was drawn to them, creeping along like a ghost through a city of the dead, imagining eyes watching from every window, jumping at every muffled sound behind the long, dreary walls.

One day, a cry came from behind a curtained window. I froze. Then I ran. But before I could escape, I heard it again—a long, piercing cry, growing fiercer before it stopped. I ran harder, not knowing where I was going, until I found myself in a raw, unfinished street ending in black waste land beside the river.

I stopped, gasping, wondering how to get back. To regain my breath, I sat on the doorstep of an empty house. Then, dancing down the road with a strange, half-running, half-hopping step—like a water wagtail—came a boy, about my age. After staring at me oddly, he sat down beside me.

We watched each other for a moment. His mouth kept opening and closing, though he said nothing. Then, edging closer, he whispered in a thick voice, as if his mouth were full of wool.

“Wot ‘appens to yer when yer dead?”

“If you’re good, you go to Heaven. If you’re bad, you go to Hell.”

“Long way off, both of ‘em, ain’t they?”

“Yes. Millions of miles.”

“They can’t come after yer? Can’t fetch yer back again?”

“No, never.”

Our doorstep was the last. A yard beyond, the black mud began. From the far end of the street, now growing dark, he never took his eyes off me.

“Ever seen a stiff ‘un—a dead ‘un?”

“No.”

“I ‘ave—stuck a pin into ‘im. ‘E never felt it. Don’t feel anything when yer dead, do yer?”

All the while, he swayed, twisted, made faces. He reminded me of those gingerbread men sold in bakeries—curved limbs, grinning features. He made me hungry.

“Of course not,” I said. “When you’re dead, you’re not there. Our bodies are but senseless clay.” I was glad I remembered that line. I tried to recall the next one—about food for worms—but it slipped away.

“I like you,” he said. Then, making a fist, he punched me in the chest. It was the sign of friendship in that neighborhood. I returned it solemnly, meaning it. Friendship with children is instant or nothing. He was my first chum.

He wormed himself up.

“Yer won’t tell?” he said.

I had no idea what I wasn’t to tell, but our pact demanded agreement.

“Say ‘I swear.’”

“I swear.”

The heroes of my favorite books bound themselves with such oaths. Here was a comrade after my own heart.

“Good-bye, cockey.”

But he turned again, pulled an old knife from his pocket, and thrust it into my hand. Then, with that peculiar hopping gait, he ran across the mud.

I watched him, wondering where he was going. He stumbled as the mud softened. But he struggled up and hopped on toward the river.

I shouted, but he didn’t look back. Every few yards, he sank almost to his knees in the black ooze, wrenching himself free and floundering forward. Then, still far from the river, he fell face-first and didn’t rise. I saw his arms beating feebler and feebler until the oily slime closed over him. Nothing remained but a faint heaving beneath the mud. Then even that stopped.

It was late when I got home. Luckily, my parents were still out. I told no one what I’d seen. I’d sworn not to. Over time, the memory faded—until it became something I could control. But it cured me, for a while, of my fascination with those silent, lifeless streets. Behind their walls, I’d hear that cry. Down their alleys, I’d see that writhing figure—hopping, springing, falling—like a gingerbread man come to life.

Yet in the crowded streets, another trouble awaited—more real, more painful.

Have you ever seen a flock of sparrows fighting over crumbs? One moment, peace. The next, chaos—tiny wings flapping, heads bobbing, shrill chirps erupting. Comical.

“Spiteful little imps,” you chuckle, amused.

So I’ve heard decent folk laughing.

“There go those young devils chivvying that poor little beggar again. Should be ashamed.”

But oh, the agony of the poor little beggar! Can anyone who hasn’t felt it imagine it? In truth, what was it? Words. Pinches. Slaps. At worst, a few hard knocks. But the dread beforehand! Terror lived in every street, hiding behind every corner. The half-dozen boys arguing over marbles—had they seen me? The boy whistling outside the print shop—would I pass unnoticed, or would he turn, raise that shrill cry, and summon the whole street?

The shame when caught—cornered, grinning faces watching, passers-by giggling as if it were a bird fight. Worst of all: rare pity. The humiliation afterward—darting away, pursued by shouts and laughter, every eye following me, shrinking by. My whole body trembling with dry sobs of rage and bitterness!

If only I could have turned and faced them! As far as enduring pain went, I knew I was brave. Physical pain from fights? Trivial compared to the mental torment. That I, the comrade of a hundred heroes—I, who nightly rode with Richard Coeur de Lion, who couched a lance against Sir Lancelot himself, who rescued damsels in distress—should run from such scum!

My friend, Robin Hood! How would he have reacted? Ivanhoe? Jack Harkaway? Would they not have laughed and scattered the rabble?

But alas! The joyous shout died on my lips. What scattered to the wind was *me*, the first chance I saw.

Sometimes, blood boiling, I’d turn, determined to stand my ground. But before I’d taken ten steps back, I’d see it all again in my mind—the laughing crowd, the mocking faces, the halting pedestrians. And instead, I’d creep home, climb silently into my room, and cry my heart out in the dark.

PAUL, FALLING IN WITH A GOODLY COMPANY OF PILGRIMS, LEARNS OF THEM THE ROAD THAT HE MUST TRAVEL. AND MEETS THE PRINCESS OF THE GOLDEN LOCKS.

The East India Dock Road today is a bustling artery of urban life—trams jingle, buses rumble, and pedestrians stampede like ants on a hot plate. But back in my day, it was quieter than a library after closing time. On one side stretched the long, echoing wall of the docks, and on the other, scattered houses stood like lost souls amid market gardens, drying grounds, and heaps of rubbish that looked suspiciously like someone’s weekend project gone wrong. Only one thing remains from those days—the little brick toll-house at the start of the bridge. I remember it well. That’s where I officially lost my childhood. And if you’ve ever felt your inner child quietly slip away, you’ll understand why I still look back at that spot with a pang.

It wasn’t dramatic. No thunder, no tears—just a slow fade-out. That afternoon, I’d been to Plaistow to see the family dentist. Not exactly a five-star location for oral care, but he had perks. For one, he was kind. For another, he was weirdly generous with his half-crown.

“Keep it in your hand,” my mother would say, tucking her purse deep into her knickerbocker pocket like it was a secret treasure. “But if he won’t take it, bring it back.”

I don’t know if he was actually related—maybe a cousin twice removed or something—but he was friendly enough. He’d sit me in his velvet throne and open his case of instruments like a magician unveiling a magic trick. “Now then,” he’d say, “which shall we use? The pliers? The needle? Or perhaps the *scissor-thing*?” He’d describe each tool with such affectionate praise that I nearly believed they were all named after famous explorers.

Then I’d open my mouth—*snap!*—and out would come a pair of tiny metal hands from under his sleeve. Before I could even say “Ow!” the whole ordeal was over. Then came tea.

He lived in a big garden house—Plaistow was still a village then—and his housekeeper made jams so good they probably caused minor riots at the dinner table. We’d sip tea while he told stories about my mother as a child—prankish, wilful, full of mischief. She’d laugh when I repeated them, saying she didn’t remember any of it. “Honestly, Paul,” she’d say, “you and him need to find better things to talk about.” But then she’d immediately ask, “And what else did he say about me?”

After tea, he’d pull out his microscope. To me, it was a portal to another world—tiny dragons, monstrous amoebas, creatures that looked like they’d escaped from a bad dream. I thought he was a wizard. In fact, now that I think about it, maybe he *was*. He was small in stature, yes, but enormous in heart. The man had more soul in his pinky finger than most people have in their entire lives.

When I left, I’d hand him the half-crown “with mamma’s compliments.” He’d accept it with solemn dignity. But when I reached the gate and checked my pocket, the coin was still there. First time, I returned it. He shrugged. “Must be another half-crown,” he said. “They’re slippery things.”

That evening, standing on the bridge, I watched the barges float lazily between the piers. It was one of those quiet summer nights when even London whispers secrets. As I passed through the white toll-gate, I suddenly felt… gone. Like I’d left myself behind on the bridge. I turned around, half-expecting to see a little boy leaning over the railing, staring into the water. But no one was there.

It sounds silly, but I still feel it sometimes. My childhood didn’t just grow up—it vanished. Like a ghost slipping through a keyhole. I tried to play the same games, dream the same dreams, but it was all hollow. I was just a shadow pretending to be a person. The real Paul—*that* Paul—had walked off into the sunset.

For a while, I cried. Not because I was sad—I was terrified. Growing up felt like walking into a dark forest with no map. I didn’t want to go. Could I stop it? Maybe stay forever young, playing knight, riding broomsticks, dreaming?

But slowly, over months and years, a new Paul emerged—one with different thoughts, different feelings, different fears. The old Paul faded. And so, reluctantly, I must let him go from this book too. But before I do, let me tell you what else I remember.

One memory stands out: Aunt Fan, crouched by the kitchen fire, skirt and crinoline rolled up like she was preparing for war. Her knees—oh, her knees!—were her sacred ground. She’d rub them rhythmically, swaying like a metronome, while I stood opposite, sword in hand (a paper knife), horse in tow (a broomstick). Sometimes I was a knight, she a wicked ogre. She’d growl, swear, and then—*poof!*—she’d become the princess I carried off on my flying broomstick.

She never moved from her chair. So the rescue was mostly symbolic. But it worked. We were both actors in a play that only we understood.

Other times, I was an Indian brave, wielding the meat chopper like a tomahawk. She was Laughing Water or Singing Sunshine. We’d go scalping together—though I suspect she enjoyed the idea more than the actual scalping. Or I’d be the Fairy Prince, she the Sleeping Beauty. But she wasn’t great at being passive. Much better when I was Captain Cook and she was the Cannibal Chief.

“I shall skin him and hang him in the larder till Sunday week,” she’d declare, smacking her lips. “Then he’ll be just right—not too tough, not too high.”

She had a gift for detail. If she’d written a cookery book for giants, it would’ve been a bestseller. Most recipes are boring: “Boil him,” “Roast her,” “Put ‘em in a pie with gravy.” But Aunt Fan added flair. “Stuffed with chestnuts, served on toast. Don’t forget the giblets—they make excellent sauce.”

And for imprisoned maidens? “Not too much fish,” she’d warn. “It spoils the flesh for roasting.”

She had a talent for transformation. One minute a noble prince, the next a Glyptodon. I’d look up the word later—“Glyptodon” sounded like a typo, but it was real. And once you saw it, you couldn’t love the prince again. Warriors became plaice. Queens became Brussels sprouts. Kings turned into prawns. She was a master of metamorphosis.

Her death scenes were also elaborate. She’d groan, writhe, gurgle—sometimes so convincingly I’d jump. Then, with the final gurgle, she’d drop her skirt—*curtain down*—and get started on tea.

Another vivid memory: me, wearing a glazed-peaked cap, walking through grimy streets, explaining the meaning of life to a smaller figure—curly-haired, wide-eyed, utterly captivated by my brilliance. Every few steps, she’d run ahead, then turn and stare at me with adoration. That day she first saw me, she was hooked. I was the greatest thing since sliced bread.

I liked her company. Not just because she was cute—though she was—but because she gave me courage. With her beside me, I wouldn’t run from danger. Together, we faced the world like two tiny adventurers. To me, a walk was just a walk. To her? An expedition. “Is that man asleep on the doorstep going to bite my head off?” “Will this cross-eyed loafer attack us?” Impossible to tell.

But her presence made me braver. In lonely places, I’d let her hold my hand. And when blind people tried to walk through us instead of around us, she’d speak up.

“Now then, where yer coming to, old glass-eye? We ain’t sperrits. Can’t yer see us?”

If they answered, her voice would rise: “Garn! They must’ve run out of heads when they made you. That’s only a turnip stuck on top!”

No one dared argue. It was like trying to scold a terrier with an hippopotamus.

She was always submissive to me—meek, almost annoyingly so. She’d wait hours outside my house, face pressed against the railings, until I either came out or waved from my window. Then she’d run off, and I’d hear her feet fade into silence—leaving me feeling strangely sad.

I cared for her, I think. But she never appeared in my dreams. She existed only in the real world, not in my imagination. And that’s why she never stayed.

She introduced me to her father’s shop—a fishmonger’s. From six in the evening onward, fried fish. I searched history. Fried fishmongers? None. Not in the Bible, not in mythology. This was beyond the pale.

So our meetings dwindled. I knew she waited every afternoon in Stainsby Road, where the Poplar aristocracy lived in semi-detached villas. I saw her crying once—tears tracing paths through dust on her cheeks. Then Barbara arrived—bright, dazzling, the light of my life—and she stopped coming altogether.

My first romance was over. One day, when Pigott Street hums with tenderness and memory sighs, I’ll walk into that little grocer’s shop and ask to see her. I’ve tracked her. I’ve seen her through the door. She’s fat now, I’m told. Probably sharper-tongued than ever. But beneath the layers, I know I’ll see the same little girl—slim, bright-eyed, admiring me.

How precious those eyes were. I didn’t realize it until they were gone. How hard it is to find people who truly admire you. If we wrote honest confession books, wouldn’t we admit that what we admire most in others is admiration of ourselves? Come on, isn’t that true? Indifference makes an indifferent man. Contempt makes a contemptible one. But praise? Praise makes a man grow. Let me wax under your compliments, friend. Because without them, I might just wither.

Among the other figures in my childhood were the servants—our “generals.” They came and went like migrating birds. Only a few stayed long enough to leave an impression. Mostly, they were women who’d fled domestic life elsewhere—disappointed, heartbroken, or just looking for a change. Some took the job like nuns took the veil. Our basement, quiet and secluded, suited their moods.

I made friends with them. No other kids around—so I bonded with the grown-ups. They weren’t great at pretend play, but they’d stop work for ten minutes to romp with me. That was healthy. What wasn’t so healthy was how they treated my talking. I had a knack for sounding wise. They listened with wide eyes, thinking I was some kind of prodigy.

Sometimes they’d invite me home for tea. I’d “hold forth” to grinning faces, loving the sound of my own voice.

“As good as a book, ain’t he?” they’d say.

“One said, ‘As good as a play!’”

I already felt immortal.

There was Janet—dear, wholesome Janet. Daughter of the barge captain “Nancy Jane.” She stayed with us for months. Then, one day, she got kicked out.

Why? Because she swore. Loudly. Constantly.

“Janet!” my mother would cry, covering her ears. “How can you use such words?”

“What words, mum?”

“The ones you called the gas man.”

“Him! Well, did you see what he did? Walked straight into my clean kitchen, boots uncleaned, the—” And before my mother could stop her, Janet had let loose the full fury of her vocabulary.

We were close. So I decided to reform her. I told her plainly: “If you keep swearing, you’ll go to hell.”

“Then where’s my father going?” she asked.

“Does he swear?”

“He doesn’t mean anything by it,” she said. “It’s the only way he can talk.”

I sighed. “People who swear go to hell, Janet.”

“But God wouldn’t send my dear, kind father to hell just because he talks like a sailor!” she insisted. “Don’t you believe it of Him, Master Paul. He’s got more sense.”

I still remember those words. When I’m sinking into despair, I whisper them to myself: “I don’t believe it of Him. He’s got more sense.”

Around that time, I needed all the comfort I could get. Children see the end of the road clearly. We stand at the valley’s edge, wondering what lies beyond. I never asked my parents directly. We all avoid talking about the important things. They gave vague answers, hiding their own doubts.

But others weren’t so careful. The books—especially the illustrated ones—were brutal. If you sinned, you burned in a fiery furnace forever. And the pictures showed it: screaming souls, red devils stoking the flames. I’d been burned once—by live coals falling on me. I imagined that pain everywhere, never ending. Forever. Ten thousand years, a million, and still—*forever*. I couldn’t sleep.

“Then be good,” the voices said. “Don’t sin.”

But sinning was easy. There were so many ways to do it. And doing it felt natural.

“Then repent,” they said.

But what was repentance? Did I hate my sin—or just hate the idea of going to hell? The latter wasn’t real repentance. But how could I know the difference?

Above all, I feared the Unforgivable Sin. What was it? I never found out. I didn’t dare ask. What if I’d already committed it?

“Believe,” they said. “Only then will you be saved.”

But how? How do you know you believe? I’d kneel in the dark, whispering:

“I believe, I believe. Oh, I do believe!”

Then rise, knuckles white, wondering if I really did.

And so, Paul—little Paul—faded. Not with a bang, but with a quiet sigh. And now, as I write this, I let him go. But I carry him with me—like a ghost who once wore my face.

Let me tell you about Doctor Florret. Not that I’m trying to make fun of him—well, maybe a little—but he was the kind of man who made “correct” sound like a religion. He wasn’t just correct; he *was* correctness. Picture a man built like a sturdy oak tree, but not so big it would be rude to sit beside him at dinner. He had the perfect size for a headmaster—stout enough to seem comfortable, but not so fat you’d worry about him tipping over during a school assembly. His hands? White, well-shaped, and one sported a diamond ring that looked more like a polite suggestion than a flashy display. It didn’t sparkle—it just… existed. Like his voice. When he talked about the weather, you felt like you were hearing a royal proclamation about atmospheric conditions.

His face—clean-shaven, calm, and radiating the kind of benignity that only someone who’s never been late for anything could achieve. And then there was his wife: his mirror image in woman form. You’d look at them together and think, “Ah yes, they must have been created from the same mold. Probably even the same rib.” Their house was full of furniture that looked like it had survived three world wars and still hadn’t lost its dignity. The pictures on the walls? Subtle. Quiet. They didn’t shout, “Look at me!” They whispered, “I’m here. Don’t worry about me.”

And the man himself? Always saying the right thing at the right time in the right tone. If you ever wondered what to do in a social situation, just wait until Dr. Florret did it—and then copy him. Because obviously, whatever he did was *the* way. To disagree with him wasn’t just wrong—it was treason. Revolutionary, even.

In practice, though, he taught exactly what every English school had been teaching since Edward VI probably invented the alphabet: Latin, Greek, grammar, and the art of memorizing things that would never matter. I spent four years trying to learn all this, and I’ll admit—I worked hard. I really wanted to learn. Ambition? Oh, I had it. The kind that makes you see yourself as a statesman, a general, a literary genius—all before breakfast.

I’d walk past a street speaker shouting about Utopia, hatless and sweating, and suddenly I’d be Demosthenes, moving crowds with my oratory. Or I’d hear a distant drumbeat, and boom—there I was, leading troops into battle, heroically advancing, promoted to Commander-in-Chief by sheer merit (because in my dream army, promotion was based on talent, not nepotism). I’d ride through London on a snow-white horse—wait, no, maybe coal-black? That was a debate I took very seriously. I didn’t want any flaws in my victory parade. Then I’d walk into a lamp-post or step on an old gentleman’s toes and wake up, drenched in sweat, thinking, “That was real.”

The next moment, a drunken sailor would stumble out of a pub, yelling at the top of his lungs, and the factory chimney would become a ship’s mast. The costers near me would shout, “Ay, ay, sir! Ready, ay, ready!” and suddenly I was Columbus, Drake, Nelson—all rolled into one. I discovered continents that didn’t exist. I defeated the French (those useful French!). I died in triumph. A nation mourned. I was buried in Westminster Abbey. Then I lived again and became a Duke. Both endings had charm. Personally, I didn’t care which.

Meanwhile, my classmates—boys whose mothers wrote letters explaining why they couldn’t come to school because of toothaches—would tell me about the Lord Mayor’s Show on November 10th, after Dr. Florret read their letters with a snort. I’d hear them chattering, fainter and fainter, like echoes fading into the distance. The bells of Bow would ring in my ears. I’d see myself as a merchant prince, young but powerful. Nobles crowded my counting house. I lent them millions and married their daughters. I’d overhear discussions about new books and instantly become a famous author—everyone praising me. Poetry, fiction, history—I wrote them all. Everyone read them. Everyone admired me. Except for one tiny problem: my handwriting was a disaster. Spelling? A nightmare. I’d write with sorrow, blotting and scratching so much it looked like a dog had chewed the page. Almost I gave up on being an author.

But no matter which path I imagined—statesman, general, writer—education was the key. So, with a heart heavy from ambition and a head throbbing from too many Latin verbs, I sat at my shaky, one-legged desk in my tiny bedroom, elbows planted like anchors, hair frizzling from the candle flame. On cold nights, I wore my overcoat pulled up around my neck, a blanket wrapped around my legs, and often had to stuff my fingers into my ears just to block out the sounds of life below: “A song, Of a song, To a song…” “I love, Thou lovest, He she or it loves. I should or would love.” Over and over. Until my own voice sounded like a buzzing insect, and my head seemed to shrink until I panicked, wondering if it was still attached.

Was I just stupid? Or is a boy’s brain physically incapable of handling the education system’s demands?

“Latin and Greek,” I heard Dr. Florret say, voice smooth as buttered toast, “are useful as mental gymnastics.” My dear Doctor Florret and Co., can’t you find something better than forcing ten-year-olds to parse Ovid while holding dumbbells made of ancient philosophy? Take your Browning, your Shakespeare—books you actually understand. Try construing them. Parsing them. Analyzing them. Would you honestly say that’s good for Tommy, tear-stained, ink-splattered, barely awake? It’s like asking a toddler to lift a piano.

We were sons of clerks, shopkeepers, minor officials—men who lived in the real world. Yet half our lives were spent studying things we’d never use. Homer? I wished the fishermen had asked him that riddle earlier. Horace? Why couldn’t that shipwreck have succeeded? It would’ve worked for anyone but a classic.

Then, one glorious day, salvation arrived.

Hear me, ye burdened little souls! Forget tops, marbles, and tuck (do you still call it “tuck”?). Save your pennies. In Paternoster Row, there lives a wizard who, for a small fee, will sell you a “Key”—a magical device that unlocks the gates of Hades and lets you understand Latin without crying.

With this Key, Aristophanes’ Frogs became my best friends. I wandered through Wonderland with Ulysses. Dr. Florret was delighted—my progress was real. Finally, I was learning by understanding first, explaining later. Let youth, trapped by the folly of age, arm itself with Keys!

But let me not take credit I don’t deserve. Dan—the strong-armed, soft-smiling Dan, the wise hater of useless work, sharp-witted and easygoing Dan—was the one who discovered this miracle.

Dan came into the Lower Fourth a term after me. Within a week, he was handling Latin like it was second nature—like he’d been secretly trained by the Devil. One afternoon, in a quiet corner of Regent’s Park, he revealed his secret.

“Don’t tell the others,” he said. “If it gets out, nobody wins.”

“But is it right?” I asked.

“Look,” Dan replied, “what are you here for? What’s your father paying for? To play a silly game, or to learn something?”

“If it’s a game,” he continued, “then let’s play fair. But if it’s to learn—well, I’ve only been here a week, and I know more Ovid than you do already.” Which was true.

So I thanked him, shared the Key, and everything I remember about Latin, for whatever good it does me now, I owe to Dan.

And I owe him even more—for his practical wisdom corrected my dreamy nonsense. From the beginning, he was my friend. To be chosen by Dan, sharp judge of character, was no small honor. He “took to” me because I was “jolly green” and “such a rummy little mug.” That was all he’d ever say. Just a smile and a tousle of my hair. But I understood. I loved him because he was big, strong, handsome, and kind. No one knows how brutal or how kind a big boy can be—except another little boy.

I was still a bit effeminate, nervous, shy, with a pink-and-white face and hair that refused to stay straight no matter how wet I made it. I was growing too fast, which drained my strength. Plus, every day I walked miles—up at six, out before seven, catching the 7:15 from Poplar, walking from Chalk Farm another two miles. But I didn’t mind. Because Dan was always waiting for me at Chalk Farm station. We walked back together. When I was tired, my back aching as if someone had cut a piece out of it, he’d put his arm around me. And oh, how strong and restful it was—like walking in an easy chair.

Thinking back, walking beside him, looking up at his face, feeling glad he liked me, I can almost understand how a woman feels when she loves. He was solid. With his arm around me, it was good to feel weak.

At first, we were in the same class—the Lower Third. Dan didn’t belong there. He was head and shoulders taller than everyone, years older. It was a disgrace he wasn’t in the Upper Fourth. Dr. Florret reminded him of this twenty times a week. Old Waterhouse (I call him “Old Waterhouse” because “Mister Waterhouse, M.A.” means nothing to me) liked him but was genuinely sad. We, his friends, were proud of him but embarrassed by his placement. Only Dan himself was content. It was his way. Others had opinions about what was good for him. Dan had his own—and that was the only opinion that mattered.

He stayed in the Lower Third, and always sat at the back. Near the door. First out, last in. A place of retreat. Over time, he became almost invisible. To Dan, the practical advantages outweighed any sentimental objections.

Only once did he lose that seat. Usually, questions about participles or meridians never reached him—they were snapped up by eager boys. The few that did, he ignored. But one day, caught off guard, he answered correctly. Instantly, he was moved to the front bench.

I’d never seen Dan angry. But that moment, if anyone had whispered congratulations, they’d have gotten punched. I’m sure of it.

Old Waterhouse saw hope. “Come, Brian,” he exclaimed, rubbing his hands, “you’re not such a fool as you pretend!”

“Never said I was,” muttered Dan, glancing back with regret at his lost solitude. By the end of the day, he’d returned to the back.

As we left, Old Waterhouse passed us on the stairs. “Haven’t you any sense of shame, my boy?” he asked, laying a hand on Dan’s shoulder.

“Yes, sir,” Dan replied, smiling. “Plenty. It isn’t yours, that’s all.”

He was an excellent fighter. Out of two hundred boys, only a handful—Upper Sixth boys who came in top hats and umbrellas—could have challenged him. But he fought rarely, and always with bored indifference, as if he were doing it just to please the other guy.

One winter afternoon, near Hanover Gate, a boy with an empty basket blocked our way.

“Can’t come in here,” he said.

“Why not?” Dan asked.

“'Cos if you do, I’ll kick you.”

Without a word, Dan turned away. The boy followed. “Now, I’m going to give you your coward’s blow,” he said. “Will you take it quietly?”

“I’ll tell you afterwards,” Dan said, stopping.

The boy slapped him lightly on the cheek. It didn’t hurt. But the insult did. No boy of honor could accept it.

“Is that all?” Dan asked.

“That’s all—for now.”

“Good-bye,” Dan said, and walked on.

“Glad he didn out fight,” Dan remarked cheerfully. “I’ve got a party tonight.”

But once, in a street off Lisson Grove, he insisted on fighting a rough kid half his size who knocked his hat into the mud.

“I wouldn’t have minded him knocking it off,” Dan explained later, brushing the hat gently with his sleeve. “But he kicked it.”

Another time, on our way to Hadley Woods, Dan pulled out a huge, juicy pear.

“Where’d you get that?” Dudley asked.

“From the greengrocer opposite Barnet Church,” Dan said. “Want a piece?”

“You told me you had no money,” Dudley said, reproachfully.

“No more I had,” Dan replied, offering a slice.

“You must’ve had some,” Dudley argued, taking it.

“Didn’t buy it.”

“Do you mean you stole it?”

“Yes.”

“You’re a thief!”

“I know it. So are you.”

“No, I’m not.”

“What’s the good of talking nonsense? You robbed an orchard at Mill Hill last Wednesday and gave yourself stomach-ache.”

“That’s not stealing.”

“What is it?”

“It’s not the same.”

“What’s the difference?”

And Dan couldn’t understand. “Stealing is stealing,” he said. “Whether you take it from a tree or a basket. You’re a thief, Dudley. So am I. Anybody else say a piece?”

The thermometer was high. We all ate. But we all scolded Dan. He didn’t care.

To Dan, I could speak my deepest thoughts. He understood. Sometimes he helped. Sometimes he confused. The yearly exam was coming. My parents said nothing, but I knew they waited anxiously. My father, to see what I’d achieved. My mother, to see how hard I’d tried.

I’d worked hard. But I doubted. Prizes aren’t won by knowledge—they’re won by making people believe you know.

“Are you going in for anything, Dan?” I asked, crossing Primrose Hill one June morning.

I knew the question was absurd. I asked it because I wanted him to ask it of me.

“They’re not giving away anything I particularly want,” Dan drawled.

“You’re sweating yourself, young ‘un?” he asked, as I expected.

“I mean to try for History,” I admitted. “Wish I was better at dates.”

“It’s always two-thirds dates,” Dan said. “Old Florret thinks you can’t eat a potato until you know when Raleigh was born.”

“I’ve prayed so hard I’ll win the prize,” I confessed.

“You shouldn’t have done that,” Dan said. “It’s unfair to the others. That won’t be winning—it’ll be favoritism.”

“But they can pray, too.”

“If you all pray, it goes to the one who prayed hardest—not the one who knows most. That’s not old Florret’s idea.”

“But we’re told to pray for what we want.”

“Beastly mean way of getting it,” Dan retorted. And nothing I said ever changed his mind.

He judged everything by himself. He thought Achilles was a coward.

“He should’ve told the Trojans he could only be hurt in the heel,” Dan argued. “Let them aim there. King Arthur and his magic swords? Not playing the game. There’s no pluck in fighting if you know you’ll win. Beastly cads, I call them all.”

I won no prize that year. Dan did—for arithmetic. The only subject in the Lower Fourth he cared about. He liked seeing things come out right.

My father examined me privately.

“It’s curious, Paul,” he said. “You seem to know a lot.”

“They asked me all the things I didn’t know. On purpose,” I blurted, resting my head on my arm.

He sat beside me.

“Spud,” he said—his first use of that nickname in years—“maybe you’re going to be with me, one of the unlucky ones.”

“Are you unlucky?” I asked.

“Invariably,” he said, rumpling his hair. “I do the right thing, but it turns out wrong. Always.”

“But Mr. Hasluck brought us good fortune,” I said.

“I’ve thought so before. And it always ends in collapse.”

I hugged him. He was like another boy—older, bigger, but not too far ahead.

“When I married your mother,” he said, “I was rich. She had everything.”

“But you’ll get it back,” I said.

“I try to think so. But sometimes—things come. You wouldn’t understand.”

“But she’s happy. We’re all happy.”

He shook his head.

“I watch her,” he said. “Women suffer more. They live in the present. I see my hopes. She sees only me—and I’ve always failed. She’s lost faith in me.”

I said nothing. I didn’t understand fully.

“That’s why I want you to be educated, Paul,” he continued. “Education helps a man bear adversity. A well-stored mind makes life interesting—even on bread and tea. If it weren’t for you and your mother, I wouldn’t bother.”

And yet, at that time, our fortunes were brightest. When they darkened again, he’d rise anew—Antaeus-like—renewed by contact with his mother.

His despondency wasn’t constant. Most of the time, he was full of confidence. He’d already picked our new house in Guilford Street—a good residential area, central enough for office work, close to Lincoln’s Inn and Grey’s Inn.

“Poplar disappointed me,” he said. “It seemed promising—a rising district, lacking solicitors. Should’ve worked. But it hasn’t.”

“A few came,” my mother reminded him.

“Of a sort,” he admitted. “A criminal lawyer might do well here. But for general work, you need to be central. In Guilford Street, people will come to me.”

“It would be a nicer place to live,” my mother agreed.

“Later,” he said, “if I need the whole house for offices, we could live in Regent’s Park. It’s near the Park.”

“Have you consulted Mr. Hasluck?” she asked.

“For Hasluck, it’s more convenient. He grumbles about the distance.”

“I’ve never understood,” she said, “why Mr. Hasluck came so far out. There must be plenty of solicitors in the City.”

And so the dream went on.

OF THE SHADOW THAT CAME BETWEEN THE MAN IN GREY AND THE LADY OF THE LOVE-LIT EYES.

“There’s nothing missing,” said my mother, “so far as I can tell. It must be that she’s gotten spooked and legged it.”

“But what could have spooked her?” asked my father, pausing mid-pour with a decanter in one hand and the bottle in the other—like a man caught between two sins.

“It was the *idea* of it,” replied my mother. “She’s never waited on table before. She actually sobbed about it last night. ‘I can’t do this,’ she said. ‘It’s too much.’ And then she burst into tears. Like a child.”

“But what are we supposed to do now?” asked my father. “They’ll be here in under an hour.”

“There won’t be any dinner,” said my mother, “unless I put on an apron and carry it up myself like some kind of Victorian servant girl.”

“Where does she live?” asked my father.

“At Ilford,” said my mother.

“We should make a joke of it,” suggested my father.

My mother sat down and began to cry. Honestly, it had been a rough week. A real dinner party—a full-on, anchovy-to-ice-confectionery feast—was a seismic shift from our usual domestic routine. No prior event had ever come close. First came the house: scrubbed from attic to basement, rearranged like a puzzle no one could solve. Then there were the endless small purchases—things you never need until Ostentation sneaks in through the back door with her smug little nose and demands them all. The kitchen range, naturally, took offense. It went on strike. Not just refused to bake—it demanded better conditions. Permanently improved ones. Which meant days of plumbers, arguments, and me sitting at the sink eating cold toast while they tried to reanimate a stove that clearly wanted to retire.

Cookbooks, long forgotten on their shelf, were dragged out, argued with, insulted. Experiments failed. Successes were noted with cautious joy. Costs were calculated like a miser counting pennies. Hope bloomed, only to be shadowed by fear.

And now, victory practically in hand, the reward was snatched away at the last moment! Downstairs, dinner sat cooling, waiting for guests. Upstairs, guests would sit around a glittering table, waiting for dinner. Between them? A chasm. The bridge—the poor soul who was supposed to connect them—had vanished without a word. Probably already halfway to Ilford, waving goodbye from a train window.

So yes, my mother’s tears were justified.

“Can’t we get someone else?” asked my father.

“Impossible,” said my mother. “I trained her all week. We rehearsed every course like a military parade. She knew exactly where to stand when the soup arrived.”

“Have it in the kitchen,” suggested my aunt, folding napkins into shapes resembling ships—though they looked more like crumpled paper boats. “Call it a picnic. That’s the only sensible option.”

A light knock came at the front door.

“It can’t be them yet!” exclaimed my father, already reaching for his coat like a man expecting a duel.

“It’s Barbara,” said my mother. “She promised to come help me dress. But now, of course, I won’t need her.” My mother had a talent for pessimism.

But just as she said it, Barbara burst into the room. I’d let her in—I knew shadows slipped out the window when Barbara came in. Back then, anyway.

She kissed them all three in one swift motion—so fast it seemed like a single gesture—and instantly, everyone saw the humor of it.

“There’s going to be no dinner,” laughed my father. “We’ll pretend it was yesterday. It’ll be fun to see their faces when they realize we’re all playing dumb.”

“There *will* be a very nice dinner,” smiled my mother, “but it will be in the kitchen, and there’s no way to get it upstairs.” She explained the situation.

Barbara stood still for a second, her face the most serious in the room. Then—*pop!*—a spark lit up her eyes.

“I’ll get you someone,” she declared.

“My dear,” began my mother, “you don’t even know the neighborhood.”

But Barbara had already snatched the latchkey from its nail and was gone.

With her departure, darkness fell again. “If only there were a hotel in this godforsaken neighborhood,” muttered my father.

“You’ll have to entertain them yourself, Luke,” said my mother. “And I’ll wait. That’s all.”

“Don’t be absurd, Maggie,” snapped my father. “Can’t the cook bring it up?”

“No one can cook *and* serve,” replied my mother, exasperated. “Besides, she’s not presentable.”

“What about Fan?” whispered my father.

My mother merely looked. That look said everything.

“Paul?” suggested my father.

“Thank you,” retorted my mother. “I don’t intend to turn my son into a footman, even if you do.”

“Well, hadn’t you better go and dress?” he asked.

“It won’t take long to put on an apron,” she replied.

“I was looking forward to seeing you in that new frock,” said my father. In another man, this might’ve been tact. In my father, it was pure accident.

My mother confessed—speaking with gentle indulgence, as one does about past foolishness—that she, too, had looked forward to wearing it. Tension melted into mutual sympathy.

“I so wanted everything to be perfect,” she said, “for your sake, Luke. I know you hoped it would help with the business.”

“I was only thinking of you, Maggie, dear,” answered my father. “You *are* my business.”

“I know, dear,” she said. “It is hard.”

The key turned in the lock. We all froze.

“She’s come back alone,” said my mother. “I knew it was hopeless.”

The door opened.

“Please, ma'am,” said the new parlour-maid, “will I do?”

She stood there, framed by the doorframe, in the daintiest apron, the daintiest cap perched on golden hair. Every objection dissolved in the wind of her merry wilfulness. No one ever got their own way with her—nor wanted to.

“You shall be footman,” she ordered, turning to me. This time, my mother only laughed. “Wait here till I come down again.” Then to my mother: “Now, ma'am, are you ready?”

It was the first time I’d seen my mother—or any woman, really—in evening dress. To be honest, I was slightly stunned. More than slightly. I showed it, I suppose; because my mother flushed and pulled her shawl over her gleaming shoulders, claiming coldness.

Barbara objected. “It’s a sin to hide such beauty!”

My father, quick as a thief, filched a shawl and dropped to one knee—as though the world were a picture book—and raised my mother’s hand to his lips with solemn reverence. Barbara, standing behind her chair, insisted I follow suit. “The Queen is receiving,” she announced.

So I knelt too, glancing up shyly toward the graceful face of a lady I’d never seen before. That was when I caught my first glimpse of the philosophy of clothes.

My memory lingers on that scene, contrasted with the sad, changed days that followed—when my mother’s eyes flashed angry glints at my father, her voice sharp and cruel; but the moment he left, her lips trembled, her eyes softened, filled with tears. When my father sat with his face turned away, lips pressed tight in sullen silence—or worse, burst into a flood of bitter words and slammed out of the room. Hours later, I’d find him alone in the dark, head bowed between his hands.

Wretched, I’d lie awake, hearing their voices through thin walls—passionate, rising high, then forced into cold whispers. Their words sounded even crueler in the silence between.

In their growing distance, both clung tighter to me—though they told me nothing. I wouldn’t have understood anyway. When my mother sobbed, clutching me tightly, I hated my father—he’d caused her pain. But when my father drew me onto his knee, and I saw the kindness in his eyes, full of sorrow, I felt angry with my mother—for her sharp tongue.

It felt as though some cruel, invisible thing had crept into the house, always standing between them. So that they could never look into each other’s loving eyes—only into the eyes of this shadow. The idea grew stronger. Sometimes I thought I could see its outline, feel a chill as it passed. It moved silently through the cramped rooms, alert, grinning, always ready to step between them.

One moment it would whisper beside my mother; the next, cross to my father and answer in his voice—but strangely altered. I used to think I could hear it laughing to itself as it retreated into the shadows.

To this day, I seem to see it—following men and women with noiseless feet, waiting patiently for its chance to thrust its face between them. So I can’t read a love story without glancing over my shoulder and seeing its mocking eyes, reading too, with a silent laugh. I can’t pass a couple whispering in twilight without seeing it lurking in the half-light, creeping after them, stealthy, as they walk hand in hand.

Will any of us escape? Or is the road for all of us through this dark valley of dead love? Is it Love’s trial—testing the weak-hearted from the strong in faith, who will find each other again once the darkness passes?

As for the dinner—until dessert, I can’t give a clear account. As footman, under the command of this enthusiastic parlour-maid, my duties were no joke. Few chances to peek through the door crack. All I remember clearly is that the chief guest was Mr. Teidelmann—or Tiedelmann, I can’t recall which—a snuffy, mumbling old frump whose name I knew from seeing it in huge letters (with a Co. added) on bleak blank walls along the Limehouse reach.

He sat at my mother’s right. I wondered how she could possibly be so interested in him—shouting often, smiling, attending to his every whim. He was deaf, so he’d constantly cup his ear, yelling, “Eh? What? Say it again!” Even old Hasluck, across from him—pleasant enough in his careless way, but not overly polite—roared himself purple praising Teidelmann’s disinfectant, which, apparently, he owned.

“My wife swears by it!” bellowed Hasluck, leaning across the table.

“Our drains!” chimed in Mrs. Hasluck, a homely soul. “You’d hardly know there were any since I started using it.”

“What are they talking about?” asked Teidelmann, appealing to my mother.

“Your disinfectant,” she explained. “Mrs. Hasluck swears by it.”

“Who?”

“Mrs. Hasluck.”

“Does she? Delighted to hear it,” grunted the old gentleman, clearly bored.

“Nothing like it for a sick-room,” persisted Hasluck. “Might almost call it a scent.”

“Makes one quite anxious to be ill,” remarked my aunt, addressing no one in particular.

“Reminds me of cocoanuts,” continued Hasluck.

Teidelmann didn’t seem to hear. But Hasluck wasn’t giving up.

“I say it reminds me of cocoanuts.” He screamed it.

“Oh, does it?” replied Teidelmann.

“Doesn’t it you?”

“Can’t say it does,” answered Teidelmann. “As a matter of fact, I don’t know much about it myself. Never use it.”

Old Teidelmann resumed eating. But Hasluck was relentless.

“Take my advice,” he shouted, “and buy a bottle.”

“Buy a what?”

“A bottle,” roared Hasluck, straining beyond his strength.

“What’s he say? What’s he talking about now?” asked Teidelmann, again turning to my mother.

“He says you ought to buy a bottle,” she repeated.

“What of?”

“Of your own disinfectant.”

“Silly fool!”

Whether he meant it to be heard or simply misjudged the volume, I don’t know. But outside in the hall, I heard it clearly. So it reached the table.

A pause. Then Hasluck chuckled.

“He’s quite right,” he said. “That’s what I am. Because I can’t talk about anything but shop, I assume everyone else is the same sort of fool.”

But he was being unfair. On my next trip down the hall, he was shouting again—this time, Teidelmann was listening.

“Well, if you could spare the time, I’d be more obliged than I can tell you,” Hasluck was saying. “I know absolutely nothing about pictures myself. Pearsall says you’re one of the best judges in Europe.”

“He ought to know,” chuckled Teidelmann. “He’s tried often enough to palm off rubbish on me.”

“That last purchase of yours must have been a good thing for young—” Hasluck mentioned a painter now famous worldwide. “Been the making of him, I should say.”

“I gave him two thousand for the six,” replied Teidelmann. “They’ll sell for twenty thousand.”

“But you’ll never sell them?” asked my father.

“No,” grunted Teidelmann. “But my widow will.” A soft laugh came from a corner I couldn’t see.

“It’s Anderson’s great disappointment,” said a languid, musical voice (the laugh translated into prose), “that he’s never been able to educate me to a proper appreciation of art. He’ll pay thousands for a child in rags or a badly dressed Madonna. Such a waste of money, it appears to me.”

“But you’d pay thousands for a diamond to hang on your neck,” argued my father.

“It would enhance the beauty of my neck,” replied the voice.

“An even more absolute waste of money,” my father murmured. And I heard the soft, musical laugh again.

“Who is she?” I asked Barbara.

“The second Mrs. Teidelmann,” she whispered. “She’s quite a swell. Married him for his money—I don’t like her, but she’s very beautiful.”

“As beautiful as you?” I asked, incredulous. We were sitting on the stairs, sharing a jelly.

“Oh, me?” she said. “I’m just a kid. Nobody notices me—except other kids, like you.” For some reason, she seemed out of sorts—unusual for her.

“But everybody thinks you’re beautiful,” I insisted.

“Who?” she asked quickly.

“Dr. Hal,” I answered.

We were facing away from the light. I couldn’t see her face.

“What did he say?” she asked, her voice warmer.

I couldn’t remember the exact words, but I knew the meaning.

“Ask him what he thinks of me,” Barbara instructed, “as if you really wanted to know. And don’t forget what he says this time. I’m curious.” Though it seemed silly—what could he say that I couldn’t already tell her—I obeyed.

Yet if jealousy of Mrs. Teidelmann dimmed Barbara’s sunny nature for a moment, it made no sense—because no one attracted more attention than the parlour-maid.

“Where did you get her?” asked Mrs. Florret, as Barbara descended from the kitchen.

“A neat-handed Phillis,” commented Dr. Florret with approval.

“I’ll take good care she never waits at my table,” laughed Mrs. Cottle, wife of our minister, a broad-built, breezy-voiced woman with eleven children—eight boys.

“To be honest,” said my mother, “she’s only here temporarily.”

“In fact,” said my father, “we owe her to Mrs. Hasluck.”

“Don’t leave me out,” laughed Hasluck. “Can’t let the old girl take all the credit.”

Later, my father absent-mindedly called her “My dear.” Mrs. Cottle shot a swift glance at my mother. Before anyone could react, Hasluck, unnoticed, pinched her elbow. It wouldn’t have mattered—except the surprise made her gasp “Augh!” With the dinner nearing its end, my mother decided to share the whole story. Naturally, Barbara gained even greater admiration. When dessert arrived, Barbara—now apron-free but still wearing her dainty cap—sat among the guests, drawing even more attention.

“It was very nice of you,” said Mrs. Cottle, completing the round of compliments. “And, as I always tell my girls, that’s better than being beautiful.”

“Kind hearts,” added Dr. Florret, “are more than coronets.” He always had the right quote—though from him, it never grated. It felt like a necessary Amen.

Only my aunt would have more to say.

“When I was a girl,” she said suddenly, “I’d look in the mirror and say: ‘Fanny, you’ve got to be amiable.’ And I *was* amiable,” she added, challenging contradiction. “Nobody can say I wasn’t, for years.”

“It didn’t pay?” suggested Hasluck.

“It attracted,” replied my aunt, “no attention whatsoever.”

Hasluck had switched places with my mother and, having learned the correct pitch for talking to Teidelmann, carried on conversation with him as much as possible. Hasluck never wasted time on anything but business. It was in his opera box on the first night of *Aida*—in future years—that he arranged his famous guano deal. His religion, I’ve heard, shifted with the enterprise. Once, during a cocoa scheme, he became a Quaker.

For most of us, interest lay in a debate between Washburn and Florret about the advantages of living in the East End.

As a rule, incorrect opinions couldn’t survive near Dr. Florret. Like birds silenced by an owl, originality died under his cold stare. But Dr. “Fighting Hal” wasn’t a gentle warbler. Vehement, direct, indifferent, he swept through polite argument like a gale through a forest, carrying allies farther than intended, leaving opponents clinging upside-down to their perches, feathers ruffled, furious.

“Life!” flung out Washburn—Dr. Florret having just laid down rules for all mankind—“What do you respectable folk know of life? You’re not men and women—you’re marionettes. You don’t move to your emotions. You dance to etiquette books. You live, love, laugh, weep, sin—by rule. Only one moment do you face life: when you die, leaving others to wear black and pretend to cry.”

It was his favorite rant—the artificiality of us all.

“Little doll,” he’d once called me, and I resented it.

“That’s all you are, little Paul,” he’d insisted. “A good little hard-working doll. You do what you’re made to do. Think what you’re made to think. Your father’s a gallant-hearted, soft-headed doll. Your mother, the sweetest, primmest doll. And I’m a silly, dissatisfied doll who wants to be a man but lacks the courage. We’re all dolls, little Paul.”

“He’s a trifle—well, a trifle whimsical on some subjects,” my father explained, when I repeated it.

“There are certain men,” said my mother, “who talk just to talk. They don’t know what they mean. And neither does anyone else.”

“But what would you have?” argued Dr. Florret. “That every man do what’s right in his own eyes?”

“Far better than doing what every other fool thinks right,” retorted Washburn. “The other day, I visited a patient. His wife was washing clothes. ‘How’s your husband?’ I asked. ‘I think he’s dead,’ she replied. Then, without stopping, ‘Jim, are you there?’ No answer. ‘He’s a goner,’ she said, wringing out a stocking.”

“But surely,” said Dr. Florret, “you don’t admire a woman for being indifferent to her husband’s death?”

“I don’t admire her for that,” said Washburn. “Nor do I blame her. I didn’t make the world. But I admire her for not pretending grief she didn’t feel. In Berkeley Square, she’d meet me at the door with a tear-streaked face and a handkerchief.”

“Assume a virtue, if you have it not,” murmured Dr. Florret.

“Go on,” said Washburn. “How does it run? ‘That monster, custom, who all sense doth eat, of devil’s habit, is angel yet in this, that to the use of actions fair and good he gives a frock that aptly is put on.’ So was the lion’s skin by the ass—but it showed him only the more an ass. Here, asses go about as asses. But there are lions too. I had a woman under my care recently. I could’ve cured her easily. Why she worsened, I couldn’t understand. Then I learned: instead of helping me, she was trying to kill herself. ‘I must, Doctor,’ she cried. ‘I must. I’ve promised. If I get well, he’ll leave me. If I die now, he’s sworn to be good to the children.’ Here, I tell you—they live, think, act, kill, die. Savages, if you like. But *savage* men and women—not bloodless dolls.”

“I prefer the dolls,” concluded Dr. Florret.

“I admit they’re pretty,” said Washburn.

“I remember,” said my father, “my first masked ball in Paris. It struck me just as you say, Hal—everyone looked exactly alike. I was glad to get out into the street and see real faces.”

“But I thought they always unmasked at midnight,” said the second Mrs. Teidelmann, in her soft, languid voice.

“I didn’t wait,” said my father.

“That was a pity,” she replied. “I should have liked to see what they looked like underneath.”

“I might have been disappointed,” answered my father. “I agree with Dr. Florret—sometimes the mask is an improvement.”

Better is little, than treasure and trouble therewith. Better a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith. Only a truly great man—or perhaps a very tired one—would have the gall to say such an obvious thing out loud. But then, life has a way of making us all philosophers when we’re too broke to care about sounding profound.

Now, Sundays weren’t the only days we got a hot joint. Every day had one. And pudding? Pudding was practically a religion. We had it every single day until you started seeing the same flan in your dreams and waking up with a craving for something *different*—like despair. So much for joy-giving luxuries. What began as a treat became just another item on the list of things that didn’t matter anymore. No more worrying about tomorrow’s hash. No more measuring bread like it was gold dust so we could save some for Saturday’s pudding. We could eat now. Drink now. Live like kings. Except… we didn’t.

Because somewhere between the roast and the apple pie sat a silent guest—no one invited, no one noticed, but always there. A ghost made of silence, whose presence turned our laughter into awkward pauses and our teasing into polite questions about the weather. The old days, when we’d joke over half a pound of sausages or two fragrant herrings, were gone. Now we talked about nothing—except what we couldn’t talk about.

Moving to Guilford Street had been discussed—briefly, hopelessly. My parents would mention it in those flat, lifeless voices they’d adopted since everything stopped being fun. But sooner or later, someone would slip—say something careless—and the conversation would collapse like a poorly built house. Then the months would drag on, heavier than before, and the cloud above us grew thicker.

Then came the news: Old Teidelmann was dead. Not from old age. Not from illness. From suddenness. In his counting house. Like a bad check bounced without warning.

“You’re going to her?” my mother asked.

“I’ve been sent for,” said my father. “It might mean business.”

My mother laughed—a short, sharp sound that made me want to hide under the table. My father stormed out of the house like he’d just been told the world was ending. I stayed behind, listening. Later, I heard her crying in her room. I didn’t understand why she’d be upset over *him*. He was a grumpy old man who smelled of ink and regret.

But after that, Mrs. Teidelmann came around more often. She wore black, yes—but it suited her. Made her look even more mysterious, like a character in a book I hadn’t read yet. Her beauty wasn’t flashy. It was quiet, deep, like a well you don’t know how far down it goes. And she was kind—to everyone, especially my mother, whom she treated like a lost sister. To me? She was magic. Always showing up with books I actually wanted to read (not the ones my mother picked, which were full of moral lessons and zero adventure), or plans for treats I’d never dreamed of.

She knew what I liked better than I did. Maybe because she understood what I *didn’t* say.

One morning, she looked at me with a mischievous twist of her mouth. “Has he ever seen a pantomime?” she asked my father.

My heart nearly jumped out of my chest.

“What would your mother say?” my father replied, raising an eyebrow.

“She thinks theatres are wicked places,” I answered. And suddenly, for the first time in my life, I wondered if maybe… just maybe… she was wrong.

Mrs. Teidelmann smiled. “Dear me,” she said. “I suppose that makes me quite wicked. I’ve always thought pantomimes were perfectly moral. All the villains get their comeuppance—right away. And we can promise to leave before the Clown steals the sausages, can’t we, Paul?”

My mother came in then. She looked small beside Mrs. Teidelmann—pale, plain, stiff in her dark dress. Like a shadow next to sunlight.

“You’ll let him go, Mrs. Kelver,” Mrs. Teidelmann pleaded, soft as silk. “It’s Dick Whittington. Such a fine moral tale.”

My mother stood silently, twisting her hands. I’d seen her do this before—when she was scared, or sad, or trying not to cry. Her lips trembled.

“I’m very sorry,” she said. “It’s very kind of you. But I’d rather he didn’t go.”

“Just this once,” Mrs. Teidelmann insisted. “It’s holiday time.”

A beam of sunlight hit her face, turning her into gold. My mother seemed to shrink into the corner.

“I’d rather he didn’t go,” she repeated. Her voice cracked. “When he’s older, others may judge for him. But for now, I must guide him—alone.”

“I really don’t think there’s any harm,” my father said, trying to lighten the mood. “Things have changed since we were young.”

“That may be,” my mother said, cold as ice. “It’s been a long time.”

“I didn’t mean it that way,” my father said, laughing shortly.

“I know,” she said. “I just feel so old. So out of place. I’ve tried to change. But I can’t.”

“We won’t say anything more about it,” Mrs. Teidelmann said, sweetly. “I just thought it might bring him joy. And he’s worked hard this term, hasn’t he?”

She touched my shoulder—gently, like a secret. And left it there.

“It was very kind of you,” my mother said. “I’d do anything to give him pleasure. Anything. He knows that. He understands.”

Her hand reached for mine. But I was angry. I pulled away. Without another word, she left the room.

She never mentioned it again. But the next afternoon, she took me to a hall nearby. There was a magic lantern show—then a conjurer. She wore a nicer dress than usual, and she was brighter, happier. Laughing. Talking. But I was still mad. I didn’t care about the sleeping man who swallowed rats or the rabbit found inside a gentleman’s hat (which, honestly, was the most ridiculous thing I’d ever seen). All I could think about was the theatre—the real one—the one with the lights, the music, the *magic* I’d heard other boys talk about.

We walked home through muddy streets, saying little. That night, I woke—half-awake—and saw a figure in white crouching at the foot of my bed. I must have fallen back asleep. But later, I opened my eyes again—and it was still there.

I screamed.

My mother rose from her knees.

She laughed—a strange, broken laugh. “It was just a dream,” she said. “I must have been thinking about the conjurer. I dreamed a wicked magician stole you from me. I couldn’t find you. I was all alone.”

She hugged me so tightly I could barely breathe. And we stayed like that until I fell asleep again.

Later, during the holidays, my mother and I visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her house in Clapton. She’d written a note saying she was suffering terrible headaches and couldn’t come out. She was leaving England soon. Would my mother come say goodbye?

My father read the letter. “Of course you’ll go,” he said. “Poor girl. I wonder what’s wrong. She used to be so healthy.”

“Do you think it’s wise for me to go?” my mother asked. “What could she possibly want to say?”

“Oh, just to say farewell,” my father said. “It would seem odd not to.”

The house outside was dull and gloomy. But inside? It was like stepping into a museum that forgot to charge admission. Old Teidelmann had collected everything—pictures stacked like firewood, statues glowing in corners, carvings, embroideries, china, gems, illuminated manuscripts, books bound in leather and gold. The man himself must have been the only thing in the place that wasn’t interesting. And now he lay shriveled in a coffin, beneath a monument that made an otherwise forgotten cemetery famous.

We were shown upstairs to Mrs. Teidelmann’s boudoir. She was asleep on a sofa near the fire, dressed in a lace robe that slipped open slightly, revealing her thin arms and dark hair falling loose. In sleep, she looked less beautiful—harder, almost coarse. My mother said she’d wait. Maybe she’d wake.

The servant closed the door quietly. Left us alone.

An old French clock ticked softly—Cupid holding a heart, ticking like a lullaby. My mother sat by the window, staring at the sleeping woman. I watched them both. And then—I swear I saw it—her lips moved. A faint smile. For just a second. Then nothing.

Neither of us spoke. Just the ticking.

Then she began to murmur in her sleep.

At first, it was nonsense. Then—clear as glass—she whispered my father’s name.

“Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling.”

My mother stood up quickly. But her voice was calm.

“Go, Paul,” she said. “Wait downstairs.” She pushed me gently out, shut the door behind me.

Half an hour passed. Then she came down. We left. On the walk home, she said nothing. Walked like a ghost. At the gate, she finally snapped back to life.

“You must say nothing, Paul,” she said. “Understand? When people are delirious, they say things that mean nothing. You must never speak a word. Never.”

I promised.

And from that day, everything changed.

My mother never raised her voice again. No anger. No flashes in her eyes. She wrote letters to Mrs. Teidelmann—long ones—always in the twilight, by the window. My father didn’t know. He’d sometimes catch her writing and ask why she chose such dim light.

“You’ll strain your eyes,” he’d say.

“I think better this way,” she’d reply.

When Mrs. Teidelmann returned, my mother was the first to visit her—even before my father knew she was back. They became friends. Almost like sisters. My mother praised her to everyone. Peace returned. My father was tender again. My mother was gentle, smiling, soft-spoken—just like before. Almost.

But every day, she grew weaker. Little things—habits, quirks—faded from her. Like she was slowly dissolving.

Summer came and went. Hot, stifling days. Nights heavy with city smells. The air crawled through the windows like a dirty breath. At night, you could hear the whole city breathing—deep, uneven, like a beast too tired to rest.

We took a holiday. Went to the coast. Climbed the steep path to old Jacob’s tower—the one he built in a fit of madness. Sat on the top platform, sheltered from the wind. Saw the distant mountains, ghostly in the haze. Ships appearing and vanishing. Farm fields below. The river, calm and sweet.

We stayed in a small village—smaller than I remembered. Sunnier. Calmer. Mrs. Fursey was still there—cheerful now, eager to show off me to the neighbors. “The material was unpromising,” she’d say. “But with patience—and a natural gift—you see what can be done!”

Anna was married now—buxom, affectionate, forever lunging to kiss me at the worst moments. I had to watch my back. Old Chumbley still cobbled shoes in his tiny cave. His bench was full of boots talking:

“Be quick, be quick—we want to toddle!” piped the little ones.

“Oh, please make haste—we’re waiting to dance!” sighed the dapper ones.

“As soon as you can,” said the stout ones. “We’ve work to do.”

“And just a patch,” muttered the old, flat-footed ones. “We haven’t much further to go.”

Old Joe still peddled his pack, telling the same jokes. Tom Pinfold still scratched his head, holding up the rejected fish. One might have thought it was the same fish.

Grown-ups hadn’t changed much. But children—oh, the children! The ones I’d known as babies now swaggered in knickerbockers. The ones I’d teased were now mincing like adults. It annoyed me. Why grow up so fast?

One afternoon—near the end of our stay—my mother and I climbed the tower again. She leaned on the parapet, staring at the mountains. A smile touched her lips.

“What are you thinking of?” I asked.

“Oh, just things that happened over there,” she said, nodding toward the hills. “When I was young.”

“You lived there, didn’t you?” I asked. (Boys don’t always think about tone.)

“You’re very rude,” she said. “It was only a little while ago.”

Then she lifted her head. Listened.

For twenty seconds, she stood like that—lips parted. Then, from the woods, came a long-drawn “Coo-ee!”

We ran to the edge. And there he came—my father—emerging from the pines, waving his stick, shouting.

His eyes sparkled. His step was springy. We knew instantly—he’d faced trouble. And he’d met it with courage. That old Norse blood, I suppose. Like a pirate rising when the storm hits.

He climbed the stairs. Breathed hard. Laughed.

He stood beside my mother. Neither spoke. Both stared at the distant hills—where, my mother had said, things had happened.

“Do you remember,” my father said, “the first time we came here? We walked up together. Talked about our future. How we’d fix things.”

“And the future,” my mother replied, “has a way of fixing its own plans.”

“It would seem so,” he said, chuckling. “I’m an unlucky beggar, Maggie. I dropped all your money—along with mine—down that cursed mine.”

“It was fate,” she said. “You couldn’t help it, Luke.”

“If only that damn pump hadn’t jammed,” he muttered.

“Do you remember Mrs. Tharand?” she asked.

“Yes. What of her?”

“A worldly woman. Called on me the morning we left. Said she’d been through more worries than you’d think. ‘And of all the troubles in this world,’ she said, ‘money troubles are the easiest to bear.’”

“I wish I could believe that,” my father said.

“She irritated me at the time,” my mother continued. “I thought it was just one of those comforting lies. But now I know she was right.”

They were silent.

Then my father grinned. “I’ve broken with Hasluck.”

“I knew it would happen sooner or later,” she said.

“Hasluck,” he said, suddenly fierce, “is no better than a thief. I told him so.”

“What did he say?”

“Laughed. Said that was better than some people.”

My father laughed too.

I don’t mean to dishonor Noel Hasluck. He was kind to me—then and later. And for Barbara’s sake, I’d prefer to write only good things about him. But if his thick-skinned, good-natured ghost were reading this now, I think it would nod. Because even though he was a friend, he was also—let’s be honest—one of the most unprincipled men in the City.

To him, business meant doing to others what you’d expect them to do to you—if given half a chance. And usually, he won.

“I play a game,” he’d say, “where cheating is the rule. Nine out of ten players are sharpers like me. The tenth is a fool who shouldn’t be there. We prey on each other. The cleverest wins.”

“But the widows and orphans?” I once asked.

“My dear lad,” he said, patting my shoulder, “I once got a letter from a widow. She’d invested in my Silver Company. Her savings were gone. Her son was her only support. You know the sort of thing. So I offered to buy her out at par. A week later, she wrote back—thanking me, but saying it didn’t matter. The shares had risen. She sold them—at a profit. To her brother-in-law. I found out later. You can find widows and orphans at Monte Carlo card tables, if you look. They’re no more deserving than anyone else. Besides,” he added, laughing, “I’m an orphan myself.”

And he laughed again—that deep, honest, hearty laugh that sounded like truth itself.

No one ever had a laugh like that.

So there we were—on the hill, under the sky, with the past and the future whispering between us. And though the shadow had passed, it left behind a silence that would never truly go away.

The eighteen months that followed—though the end came sooner than anyone expected—were, I believe, the most blissfully uneventful days my parents had ever known. Or at least, if “blissful” isn’t quite right, let’s say they were the most beautifully ordinary. It was as though God, in a rare moment of sentimentality, had tapped gently on the door and said: “Not yet. You’ve got just a little more time together. In a little while.” And so they did. They stopped pretending. The pretense of respectability, the fear of what the neighbors might whisper, the petty worries about things that didn’t matter—like whether the teapot matched the sugar bowl—fell away like autumn leaves. What remained was love, pure and simple. Not romantic love, not grand passion, but the kind that grows quiet and deep, like moss on a stone. They loved each other. They comforted each other. They learned to understand each other. And when you’re doing all that, who needs anything else?

We lived poorly again, but now it felt good. There was no shame in it. No need to hide the fact that our boots were patched or that we’d eaten cold mutton for three days straight. Life, stripped bare, turned out to be surprisingly pleasant. My father seemed younger. Lighter. As though the weight of being responsible had finally lifted, and he could breathe again. He even started humming tunes from his childhood—ones I’d never heard before, and which sounded suspiciously like hymns, though I wouldn’t have dared point that out.

And then there was Aunt Fan.

Now, Aunt Fan was a woman of strong opinions, most of which she expressed with the force of a gale. She had decided, for the second time in her life, to give amiability a try. This announcement came one afternoon shortly after we returned from Devonshire, where we’d spent a summer that felt like a dream—sunshine, sea air, and the occasional argument over whether clams should be boiled or steamed (they’re both wrong, by the way).

“I’m a beast of an old woman,” she declared suddenly, without warning.

“Don’t say that, Fan,” my mother urged.

“What’s the use of saying ‘Don’t say it’ when I’ve just said it?” snapped Aunt Fan.

“It’s your manner,” my mother explained patiently. “People think you’re disagreeable.”

“They’d be daft if they didn’t,” Aunt Fan shot back. “Of course you don’t really mean it,” she continued, addressing my mother.

“Stuff and nonsense,” she snorted. “Does she think I’m a fool? I like being disagreeable. I like to see ’em squirming.”

My mother laughed. “I can be agreeable,” Aunt Fan announced, “if I choose. Nobody more so.”

“Then why not choose?” my mother suggested.

“I tried it once,” said Aunt Fan, “and it fell flat. Nothing could have fallen flatter.”

“It may not have attracted much attention,” replied my mother with a smile, “but one shouldn’t be agreeable merely to attract attention.”

“It wasn’t only that,” Aunt Fan insisted. “It gave no satisfaction to anybody. It didn’t suit me. A disagreeable person is at their best when they are disagreeable.”

“I can hardly agree with you there,” said my mother.

“I could do it again,” Aunt Fan muttered to herself, with a hint of menace in her voice. “It’s easy enough. Look at the sort of fools that are agreeable.”

“I’m sure you could be if you tried,” my mother said encouragingly.

“Let ’em have it,” Aunt Fan whispered, still to herself. “That’s the way to teach ’em sense. Let ’em have it.”

And here’s the irony: she was right, and my mother was entirely wrong. My father was the first to notice the change.

“Nothing the matter with poor old Fan, is there?” he asked one evening, a few days after Aunt Fan had launched her new campaign.

“No,” my mother replied. “Nothing I know of.”

“She’s acting so strangely,” my father said. “So… weird.”

My mother smiled. “Don’t say anything to her. She’s trying to be agreeable.”

My father chuckled. “I almost wish she wouldn’t. We were used to it. She was rather amusing.”

But Aunt Fan, being a woman of willpower (and perhaps a little too much pride), stuck to her guns. And around the same time, something happened that confirmed her in her new path: the arrival of James Wellington Gadley.

Properly speaking, he should have been Wellington James. That was the order in which he’d been christened in 1815. But by the time he reached school, he’d realized that Wellington was a burden for a man of modest talents to carry. So wisely, he reversed the arrangement. He became James Gadley, a slightly pompous but thoroughly innocent little gentleman, proud of his position as head clerk to Mr. Stillwood, the solicitor to whom my father was now assistant.

Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal was a firm with roots stretching back to the Georges—a name once synonymous with aristocratic intrigue, shady deals, and the occasional nobleman’s secret divorce. These days, however, it was fading. Old Mr. Stillwood refused new partners, claiming the business was “dying” and that reviving it would be like putting new wine into old skins. Yet, despite its shrinking clientele, it still handled respectable cases, and my father considered himself lucky to work there.

James Gadley had joined the firm as office boy during its glory days and had never noticed that the world had moved on. He delighted in recounting the firm’s legendary cases—always with professional discretion, referring to people as Mr. X and Lady Y, and places as “the capital of a foreign country” or “a certain town not a thousand miles from where we are now.”

Most people found him tedious. But Aunt Fan? She adored him. Especially when the case involved murder, mystery, or moral decay.

During their first conversation, he exclaimed: “Now why—why, after keeping away from his wife for nearly eighteen years, never even letting her know whether he was alive or dead, why this sudden resolve to return to her? That’s what I want explained!”

He paused, expecting sympathy.

Aunt Fan didn’t yawn. She didn’t sigh. Instead, she leaned forward and said, with perfect clarity:

“To murder her—by slow poison.”

“To murder her! But why?”

“In order to marry the other woman.”

“What other woman?”

“The woman he had just met and fallen in love with. Before that, it didn’t matter what became of his wife. This woman had said to him: ‘Come back to me a free man—or never see my face again.’”

“Dear me! Now that’s very curious.”

“Nothing of the sort,” Aunt Fan said. “Plain common sense.”

“And indeed,” she added, “it’s curious because, as a matter of fact, his wife did die a little later, and he did marry again.”

“Told you so,” she remarked.

From that moment on, every case in the Stillwood annals was reviewed through Aunt Fan’s lens. Into every seemingly innocent act, she read dark criminal intent. An elderly maiden lady in “a western town once famous as the resort of fashion, but which we will not name”—in reality, Bath—was, according to Aunt Fan, guilty of arson and forgery, having burned down a house containing a will and forged another so her future children (should she ever marry) could inherit seven hundred pounds.

Mr. Gadley was fascinated. “I have to thank you, ma'am,” he would say, rising from his chair, “for a most delightful conversation. I may not agree with your conclusions, but they afford food for reflection.”

To which Aunt Fan would reply: “I hate talking to anyone who agrees with me. It’s like taking a walk to see one’s own looking-glass. I’d rather talk to somebody who doesn’t, even if he were a fool.” Which, for her, was a compliment.

Mr. Gadley was a stout little man whose stomach protruded about a foot in front of him. He was unaware of this, and it didn’t matter—except when he talked. For he had a habit of leaning forward, closer and closer, until his belly, acting like a gentle battering ram, pushed his listener backward across the room. On his first visit, he started from the corner of his chair. As he grew more animated, he drew nearer. Aunt Fan, ever graceful, retreated inch by inch. Finally, she ended up sitting on the edge of her seat. His next move sent her tumbling onto the floor. She said nothing—this surprised me—but on his next visit, she was busy darning stockings, an unusual occupation for her.

He approached again. She sat firm. This time, he was the one who leapt back, exclaiming something entirely unrelated to the topic.

After that, Aunt Fan always met him with stockings in hand, and they conversed with a respectful space between their chairs.

Nothing serious came of it—though the fact that he was a widower added a touch of possibility, which no woman is ever too old to enjoy. But it was clear he admired her intellectually. Once, he even said: “Miss Davies, you should have been a solicitor’s wife!”—which, in his mind, was the pinnacle of feminine achievement.

She replied: “Chances are I would have been, if one had ever asked me.”

And so, warmed by appreciation, Aunt Fan’s amiability took root—and flourished. Though, like all late-blooming growths, it developed in strange directions.

She decided that flattery was the quickest way to be agreeable. So she began flattering everyone—constantly, relentlessly, and with such intensity that it came off as thinly veiled sarcasm.

When my father told us a funny story—something trivial he’d seen that day—she would gasp, hands raised in mock horror:
“What a funny man he is! And to think it comes to him naturally, without effort! What a gift!”

When my mother appeared in a new bonnet—retrimmed, naturally—Aunt Fan would step back, eyes wide:
“Don’t tell me it’s a married woman with a boy getting on for fourteen. It’s a girl. A saucy, tripping girl. That’s what it is.”

I suspect some people’s vanity, unchecked, becomes a disease. But I think a dose of Aunt Fan would cure even the worst case.

She also adopted a sprightly demeanor—despite her age and rheumatism—which must have cost her dearly. From this, I formed the perhaps immoral opinion that virtue, like everything else, is best when unassuming.

Occasionally, the old Adam—or Eve—would surface. Then, still thinking of others, she’d descend into the kitchen and be disagreeable to Amy, our new servant.

Amy, bless her, never minded. She was a philosopher. She believed there were only twenty-four hours in a day. Sounds grim, but she managed to extract perpetual cheerfulness from it. My mother would apologize to her for Aunt Fan’s behavior.

“Oh, Lord bless you, mum,” Amy would say, “it don’t matter. If I wasn’t listening to her, something worse might be happening. Everything’s all the same when it’s over.”

Amy had come to us as a stopgap, explaining she was about to marry and needed temporary work. “It’s rather unsatisfactory,” my mother said. “I dislike changes.”

“I can quite understand it, mum,” Amy replied. “I dislike ’em myself. Only I heard you were in a hurry, and I thought maybe—while you were looking for someone permanent—”

So she came. A month later, my mother asked when the wedding would be.

“Oh, some time in the spring, I expect,” Amy said.

“Oh!” said my mother. “I thought it was coming off soon.”

Amy looked shocked. “I must know a little bit more about him before I go as far as that.”

“But you told me it was in a few weeks.”

“Oh, that one!” Her tone implied she was struggling to recall. “I didn’t feel I wanted him as much as I thought I did when it came to the point.”

“You’d met someone else?” my mother asked, smiling.

“Well, we can’t help our feelings, can we, mum?” Amy admitted. “And what I always say is—better change your mind before it’s too late afterwards.”

Amiable, sweet-faced, broad-hearted Amy! Most faithful of friends, but oh! Most faithless of lovers. Age has not withered nor custom staled her liking for infinite variety. Butchers, bakers, soldiers, sailors, Jacks of all trades! Does the sighing procession never pass before you, Amy, pointing ghostly fingers of reproach? Still, Amy is engaged. To whom, I cannot say. I suspect an early one who’s lately become a widower. But I don’t care to ask. To confess ignorance would hurt her. To pry would entangle me. To call him “Tom” when Tom’s long gone, or “Dick” when Dick hasn’t existed in years—well, that’s just asking for trouble.

In my mother’s day, we always called him “Dearest.” It avoided confusion.

“Well, and how’s Dearest?” my mother would ask, opening the door to Amy on Sunday evenings.

“Oh, very well indeed, mum, thank you. And he sends you his respects.”

“When you’re married, you’ll be able to take good care of him.”

“That’s really what he wants—somebody to take care of him. It’s what they all want, the poor dears.”

“And when is it coming off?”

“In the spring, mum.” Always the spring, when possible.

Amy was nice to all men. And to Amy, all men were nice. Had she married a dozen, she might have settled down, with only occasional regrets. But to ask her to pick just one? That was asking for a waste of affection.

We meant to keep our grim secret to ourselves. But hiding troubles from Amy was like hiding cold hands from the fire. Soon, she knew everything—drawn from my mother like sap from a tree.

Then she sat my mother down and stood over her.

“Now you leave the house and everything connected with it to me, mum,” she commanded. “You’ve got something else to do.”

And from that day, we were in Amy’s hands. All we had to do was praise the Lord for His goodness.

Barbara also found out—probably from Washburn—but said nothing. She came often. Old Hasluck would have come himself, had he thought he’d be welcome. As it was, he sent fruit and flowers by Barbara, and always welcomed me warmly whenever she allowed me to see her home.

She brought sunshine with her, making all trouble seem distant and shadowy. My mother tended the fire of love; Barbara lit the cheerful lamp of laughter.

And as the days grew fewer, my father seemed to grow younger. Life lay lighter on him.

One summer night, he and I walked with Barbara to Poplar station. Sometimes, when he wasn’t looking tired, she’d order him to fetch his hat and stick, caressing him and saying: “I like them tall and slight and full grown. The young ones, they don’t know how to flirt! We’ll take the boy with us as gooseberry.” And he, pretending to worry that my mother might see, would kiss her hand and slip out quietly with her arm linked under his. He entered into the spirit admirably.

The last cloud faded from before the moon as we turned the corner. Even the East India Dock Road lay restful in front of us.

“I’ve always regarded myself,” said my father, “as a failure in life. It’s troubled me.” I felt him pull slightly away from me, as though Barbara, holding his other arm, had drawn him toward her with a swift pressure. “But do you know the idea that’s come to me in the last few months? That on the whole, I’ve been successful. I’m like a man who, in a deep wood, has been frightened, thinking he’s lost his way, and suddenly, at the end of it, finds that by some lucky chance, he’s been guided to the right point after all. I can’t tell you what a comfort it is.”

“What is the right point?” Barbara asked.

“Ah, that I can’t tell you,” my father answered, laughing. “I only know that for me, it’s here. All the time I thought I was wandering away from it, I was drawing nearer. It’s wonderful. I’m just where I ought to be. If I’d only known, I never would have worried.”

Whether it would have troubled him or my mother much, even if it hadn’t been so, I can’t say. Life, so small beside death, seemed to have lost all terror for them. But I like to remember that Fortune, at the last, was kind to my father—prospering his adventures not as much as he’d dreamed, but enough. Enough so that no fear for our future marred the peaceful passing of his tender spirit.

Or should I credit not Fate, but sweet Barbara? And behind her, do I not detect the shameless old Hasluck, grinning good-naturedly in the background?

“Now, Uncle Luke,” Barbara said one day, “I want your advice. Dad’s given me this cheque as a birthday present. I don’t want to spend it. How shall I invest it?”

“My dear,” said my father, “why not consult your father?”

“Now, Uncle Luke,” she replied, “dad’s a dear, especially after dinner, but you and I know him. Giving me a present is one thing. Doing business for me is another. He’d unload on me. He’d never resist the temptation.”

My father would suggest something. Barbara would thank him. But a minute later, she’d murmur: “You don’t know anything about Argentinos.”

My father didn’t. But Barbara did—to a remarkable extent for a young girl.

“That child has insisted on leaving this cheque with me,” my father would observe after she’d left. “I’ve advised her to buy Argentinos. I’m going to put a few hundreds in myself. I hope they turn out all right, if only for her sake. I have a presentiment somehow that they will.”

A month later, Barbara would greet him: “Isn’t it lucky we bought Argentinos!”

“Yes,” he’d say. “They haven’t turned out badly, have they? I had a feeling, you know, for Argentinos.”

“You’re a genius, Uncle Luke. And now we’ll sell out and buy Calcuttas, won’t we?”

“Sell out? But why?”

“You said so. You said, ‘We will sell out in about a month and be quite safe.’”

“My dear, I’ve no recollection of it.”

But Barbara did. And before she was done with him, so did he. And the next day, Argentinos would be sold—not too soon—and Calcuttas bought.

Could money so gained bring a blessing? The question plagued my father.

“It’s very much like gambling,” he’d mutter uneasily after each success. “Uncommonly like gambling.”

“It’s for your mother,” he’d impress upon me. “When she’s gone, Paul, put it aside. Keep it for doing good. That may make it clean. Start your own life without any help from it.”

He needn’t have worried. It went the road that all luck derived from old Hasluck ever took. Yet it served good purpose on its way.

But the most marvelous feat, to my thinking, ever accomplished by Barbara was the bearing off of my father and mother to witness *A Voice from the Grave, or the Power of Love, New and Original Drama in Five Acts and Thirteen Tableaux.*

They had been bred in a narrow creed. Both my father and mother carried Puritan blood in their veins—the kind that has drowned much harmless joyousness across our land. But those who know it only from hearsay do foolishly to speak ill of it. If earnest times return—if we must show not what we have, but what we are—we may regret that such people are fewer. They trained themselves to despise pleasure, because in pleasure they saw the subtlest foe to principle and duty. No graceful growth, this Puritanism, for its roots are in hard facts. But it is strong. From it has sprung all that is worth preserving in the Anglo-Saxon character. Its men feared God. Its women loved Him. Their words were harsh, but their hearts were tender. They shut out the sunshine so their eyes might see better the glory beyond. And if earth’s threescore years and ten are but preparation for eternity, who shall call them foolish for turning away from its allurements?

“Still,” my father argued, “I think I should like to have a look at one, just to see what it is like. One cannot judge of a thing one knows nothing about.”

I imagine it was his first argument that convinced my mother.

“That is true,” she answered. “I remember how shocked my poor father was when he found me one night at the bedroom window reading Sir Walter Scott by the light of the moon.”

“What about the boy?” said my father, for I had been included in the invitation.

“We will all be wicked together,” said my mother.

So a few evenings later, the four of us stood at the corner of Pigott Street, waiting for the bus.

“It is a close evening,” said my father. “Let’s go the whole hog and ride outside.”

And so we did.

Ah, the golden days of youth—where have you gone? Tubby with his sun-kissed curls; Langley, whose nose bore the permanent dent of a childhood skirmish; Shamus, stout-hearted but physically underwhelming, who could be knocked down in three seconds flat but never once said “I give you best”; Neal, thin as a reed; Dicky, “Dicky Dick,” fat and forever on the verge of a nap; Ballett, whose one eye perpetually wept from sheer emotional overload; Beau Bunnie, lord of a hundred ties and always fighting in black kid gloves like some dandyish avenger of fashion—where are you all now? Have you been swallowed by time, or merely relocated to that sad limbo where memories go when they’re no longer useful?

I swear I saw Tubby just last week. He was trudging down Throgmorton Street, chained to a tiny black bag like a convict of the soul. His hair, once thick and golden, was now grey and sparse, like a badger’s after a long winter. And those side whiskers—good heavens, they looked like he’d grown them specifically to make himself look more like a man who might be able to afford a decent breakfast. I didn’t stop him—I had a train to catch at Liverpool Street and a shave to get done en route. But I swear he looked at me. Hard. Like he recognized something—maybe not my face, but the ghost of the boy who once believed he could discover the North Pole by staring hard enough at the Regent's Park canal.

And poor Shamus! They say he’s now an Income Tax assessor. A man so skeptical he can doubt the existence of rain on a rainy day. No faith, no charity—just cold numbers and colder judgment. May God keep him far from my tax return.

Time, Nature’s most efficient janitor, bustles through life’s rooms, tidying up messes, covering over volcanoes with grass, turning ancient rubble into fertile soil, healing bark, smoothing out scars. It’s a relentless cleaner-up, really. And it’s not kind to the lonely.

In the beginning, I wasn’t exactly the class favorite. In fact, I was the walking definition of “unpopular.” Which is why trouble first came knocking—not with a bang, but with a quiet, persistent *tap-tap-tap* on the door of my soul.

Later, adults often convince themselves they were beloved, even if only slightly. We tell ourselves: “Well, I may not have been *the* favorite, but I *was* liked.” Without that illusion, few of us would survive. But children? Children don’t get the luxury of self-deception. We see the truth—cold, sharp, and unkind—and we suffer.

I suffered. I brooded. I stared into my own reflection and wondered what was wrong with me.

One day, a towering figure—head of the Lower Fourth, nose like a hawk’s beak, manner like a future prime minister—asked me: “Can you run?”

“Yes,” I said, heart thumping like a drum.

“Run round the playground twice,” he commanded. “Let me see.”

So I ran. Not because I wanted to win, but because someone had finally noticed me. I ran like a man possessed, lungs burning, legs screaming. When I stopped, I was gasping, but he nodded. Satisfied.

“Why don’t the others like you?” he asked bluntly.

Oh, how I wished I could speak freely! How I longed to confess:
“O Duke of the Lower Fourth! You, who command adoration like a king commands tribute! What is your secret? Is it Tommy’s swagger? Because I’ll try it too—though they mock me for it, calling me ‘cock-a-doodle-do’ and imitating pouter pigeons. Is it Dicky’s playfulness? Because I’ve tried that too—only to end up with a bloody nose and a wider circle of disdain. Is it Billy’s friendliness? I’ve tried hugging them—only to be shoved away like a wet towel. Is indifference better? I’ve walked alone, trying to look casual. No one followed. No arm slipped through mine. Should I offer help? I’d do their math, write their essays, take their punishments—anything for a little love, a little admiration!”

But instead, I muttered: “They do like me. Some of them.”

“Don’t lie,” he snapped. “You know they don’t. None of them.”

I hung my head.

“But here’s what I’ll do,” he continued, grandly. “We’re starting hare and hounds next Saturday. You can be a hare. Just show up. No need to tell anyone. Mind, you’ll have to run like the devil.”

He walked off, leaving me to meet Joy, who rushed toward me with open arms. That moment—the moment of sudden recognition, of being chosen—felt like destiny itself cracking open its door. The politician getting praised by the Party whip. The playwright receiving a call from the manager. The subaltern summoned to his general’s stirrup. The world, suddenly, lay before me.

I didn’t tell a soul at school. But I burst into the house like a meteor: “I’m going to be a hare! Because I run fast! Only two hares, and everyone wants me! Can I have a jersey? And shoes? We start at Hampstead Heath!”

My mother and I raced to the shops. Fear gripped me—what if the last blue-and-white striped jersey in London had already sold out? What if running shoes were extinct? We bought both. That night, I dressed in full regalia and stared into the mirror, admiring myself like a future champion. For the rest of the week, I practiced leaping over chairs (nearly breaking a leg), and descended stairs like a drunkard escaping justice. My father supported me: “The honor of the Lower Fourth is at stake!” My mother added another prayer.

Saturday arrived. We met at the turnpike gate outside the Spaniards. I ate lunch in Regent’s Park, then took the bus to the Heath. One by one, the boys arrived. None spoke to me. I wore my ordinary clothes over my jersey. They thought I was just there to watch. I smiled inwardly—soon they’d see the surprise.

Then the Duke arrived. I sidled up behind him.

“But we only have one hare!” someone cried. “We need two, in case one gets blown.”

“We’ve got two,” said the Duke. “Young Kelver’s the other.”

Silence.

“Oh, come on—we don’t want him,” someone said. “He’s a muff.”

“He can run,” the Duke insisted.

“Let him run home,” another voice suggested. Laughter followed.

“You’ll run home in a minute,” the Duke threatened. “Who’s captain here—me or you? Young ‘un, are you ready?”

I started unbuttoning my jacket. Then stopped.

“I don’t want to come,” I said, “if they don’t want me.”

“He’ll get his feet wet,” said the skeptic. “Don’t spoil him—he’s his mother’s pet.”

“Are you coming or not?” the Duke shouted.

Tears pricked my eyes. I turned away.

“All right, stay,” the Duke said, impatient. “Keefe, you take the bag. Off you go. It’ll be dark before we start.”

Keefe grabbed the bag and ran. The hares vanished. I walked slowly away, face hidden.

“Cry-baby!” shouted a sharp-eyed lad.

“Leave him alone,” growled the Duke.

I wandered to the cedars.

Minutes later, I heard them start—with a whoop.

How could I go home? My father would ask questions. My mother would have hot water and blankets ready. What excuse could I give? “I was rejected”? No. So I did the only thing left: I became the hare.

I stripped off my shirt and jacket, rolled them under my arm, and began running—alone, hare and hounds combined in one miserable, mud-splattered boy.

I see it still: that absurd little figure, jogging across damp fields, sometimes falling into muddy ditches, scrambling through dripping hedges, climbing over tarred fences, dodging farmhouses, darting across roads when no one was looking. Mile after mile, tears smearing my cheeks, mud coating my face. Up Crouch End Lane—now lit by electric lights and lined with prim villas—then into Seven Sisters Road station, where I tore off my soaked jersey and headed home.

And when I arrived, I told my parents: “It was jolly! I ran miles! They never caught me! I was brilliant!”

My mother blinked. “Be careful, dear. Don’t overstrain yourself.”

She was proud. I knew it.

And soon, imagination helped. I began hearing footsteps behind me. Through gaps in the trees, I’d glimpse a pack of pursuers. I’d redouble my speed. I wasn’t running from anything. I was running toward a myth.

But for Dan—my salvation. Dan was my anchor. He never cared about popularity. He hated crowds. He stood for the underdog, the unpopular cause, the man nobody else would defend. He loved giving, but receiving bored him. He was like a fountain: it gave, but couldn’t receive.

“You’re such an un-understandable chap,” a fellow club member once complained.

“I hate a crowd,” Dan replied.

That was it.

And then—miraculously—popularity found me. Not because I earned it. Not because I changed. But because I was funny.

At first, I didn’t know why. Then Norval—our nickname for George Grampian—explained it.

“Because you’re funny,” he said. “You say such funny things.”

I was slapped in the face by the truth.

I’d dreamed of heroism. Of nobility. Of being admired for courage, integrity, brilliance. Not for making people laugh. But here I was—chosen not for virtue, but for humor.

I was to be the Fool. The court jester of the lower school. And yet... I couldn’t refuse.

My vanity won. I chose to cut capers rather than walk in dignity.

Soon, I was sought after. Groups fought over me. “Come with us!” one would shout. “He promised!” another would retort. “No, he didn’t!” “Yes, he did!” “Well, he ain’t!” “Punch his head, Dick!” “I’ll punch yours!” And so on—until I was carried off in triumph by the victors.

I asked Norval why.

“Because you’re funny,” he repeated.

“Why am I funny?”

“Why? Because you say funny things.”

I’d never considered that. In books, heroes were noble, brave, clever. Never funny. Never ridiculous. Never *me*.

But I accepted it. I began crafting jokes. Preparing impromptus. Twisting ideas into absurd shapes. I became a comedian by accident.

And I was rewarded. Everyone wanted me. But I wasn’t satisfied. I’d rather have been captain of the football team. Or even Jerry, the stutterer, who once scored a century and won us the match by three runs. That single round of applause—worth more than all my laughter.

Still, I clung to hope. I’d rise early to practice throwing at broomsticks. I’d try stilts—only to be humiliated when fat Tubby outran me. My dreams of laurels never died.

Then came Wakeham—a Sixth Form boy, dull as dishwater, desperate to be witty. He watched me like a scientist studying a rare bird.

One afternoon, he pulled me aside.

“I say, Kelver,” he whispered, “you really are funny!”

It didn’t please me. If he’d said he admired my bowling, I might have believed him. But this? This was different.

“So are you,” I snapped. “Only you don’t know it.”

“No, I’m not,” he said. “Wish I was. Do you think you could teach me to be funny?”

I nearly said no. Then I remembered: Wakeham could whistle. With two fingers in his mouth, he could produce a sound that made dogs flee and people jump six inches into the air. A talent I envied, though I couldn’t understand it. It was vulgar. It annoyed people. Yet I’d spent weeks trying to learn it.

So we made a deal: I’d teach him to be funny. He’d teach me to whistle.

We worked hard. He tried everything I told him. I followed his instructions. The result? Weak jokes. A feeble whistle.

“Do you think anyone would laugh at that?” he’d ask, defeated.

Honestly? I didn’t think so.

“How far off could someone hear that?” I’d wheeze.

“Well,” he’d say kindly, “it depends whether they knew it was coming.”

After two weeks, we quit.

“I suppose it’s something you have to have inside you,” I said.

“I don’t think your roof of the mouth is shaped right for it,” he replied.

And so we both admitted defeat.

Popularity, I realized, isn’t earned. It’s given. And sometimes, it comes disguised as ridicule. But in the end, I learned this:
You can’t chase it.
You can’t force it.
But if you stop caring—
and start laughing—
it might just follow you anyway.

My father died, of all things, on the morning of his birthday. We hadn’t expected it—nobody ever does when they’re still breathing and eating breakfast—and at first we didn’t even realize he was gone. It was just another quiet Sunday morning in Poplar, where silence is a rare commodity and even the wind sounds like gossip.

“I’ve left him sleeping,” said my mother, slipping out of the house in her dressing gown like a ghost with a nightcap. “Washburn gave him a draught last night. We won’t disturb him.”

So we sat around the breakfast table, whispering like conspirators in a bad novel. The house was small, flimsy, and built for people who believed that sound travels faster than light. Every cough, every spoon clink, every sigh from the next room echoed like a judgment.

Later, my mother crept upstairs, me trailing behind like a nervous shadow. We tiptoed to the door and peeked through a crack. The blinds were down, the room dark. My mother stood there, ear pressed to the door, as if listening for the faintest breath of life. Then came the costermonger girl, shrieking into the dawn: “Watercreases, fine fresh watercreases with your breakfast—a’penny a bundle!” And farther off, a hoarse youth wailed: “Mee-ilk-mee-ilk-oi.” A symphony of urban decay.

Inch by inch, my mother opened the door. We slipped inside. He lay there, eyes closed, lips slightly parted. I’d never seen death before. All I could see was that he looked younger—like someone who’d just taken a nap instead of checking out of existence. It took days, maybe weeks, for the truth to sink in. I’d hear his footsteps behind me on the street, catch his voice calling my name, spot his face in a crowd—only to turn and find nothing but air. But at first? No pain. Just… absence. Like forgetting a tune you once knew.

To my mother, though, it was merely a temporary separation. She had faith—not the kind that asks questions, but the kind that answers them with a nod and a smile. He was waiting for her. In God’s good time, they’d meet again. Why mourn? Without him, the house felt quieter, yes, but not empty. It was just… less cheerful. That was all.

She’d talk about him constantly—his kindnesses, his quirks, the way he used to sneeze three times in a row without warning. Not with tears, but with laughter. He wasn’t gone—he was simply busy in the future, beckoning her forward. It was oddly comforting.

We stayed in the old house, even though we kept talking about moving. The great brick monster—the city—had been creeping closer year after year, swallowing fields, gardens, trees, cottages, and any trace of greenery like a greedy toddler with a crumb cake. One by one, the last vestiges of charm vanished under its ugly teeth. This east end of London, this vast ghetto of the poor, grew larger and darker each season. Hopelessness stretched its long, shrivelled arms across endless rows of reeking cells where London herded its slaves.

Sometimes, on misty afternoons when we knew life was blooming elsewhere—sunshine, birds, real grass—we’d go house-hunting in the suburbs. Highgate, then just a tiny town linked by a coach to leafy Holloway; Hampstead, with its red-brick houses and views of domes beyond the woods; Wood Green, where smock-coated labourers debated politics beneath wide elms; Hornsey, then just a village with an ivy-covered church and one grass-bordered road.

We’d find “the very thing for us”—a cottage with a garden, a fireplace, a dog—but always pause.

“We must think it over,” my mother would say. “There’s no hurry. For some reason, I’ll be sorry to leave Poplar.”

“For what reason, mother?”

“Oh, well, no particular reason, Paul. Only we’ve lived here so long. It will be a wrench leaving the old house.”

Ah, the human instinct to cling. We wrap our tendrils around the nearest wall—whether castle or pigsty—and let rain and sunshine bind us tighter. Dying Sir Walter Scott, remember? Rushing home from Italy, terrified he might miss the damp mists of his barren hills. An old woman I knew was carried from her slum attic to breathe sea air, only to pine for her “home”—her alley’s stench, her noisy neighbors, her familiar rot. Wife, mother, widow—she’d lived there so long that the grime smelled like perfume. Who can understand us except the One who made us?

So the old house held us. Not until both my aunt and my mother had passed—and I’d said goodbye to Amy, crying amid the litter of emptiness—did I finally leave.

My aunt died exactly as she lived: grumbling.

“You’ll be glad to get rid of me, all of you!” she announced, dropping straight into the retort. “And I can’t say I’ll be very sorry to go myself. It hasn’t been my idea of life.”

Poor old soul. That was two weeks before the end. I don’t suppose she suspected how certain it was—or perhaps she wouldn’t have been so blunt if she had.

“Don’t be foolish,” said my mother. “You’re not going to die!”

“What’s the use of talking like an idiot?” snapped my aunt. “I’ve got to do it sometime. Why not now? Everything’s ready. It’s not like I’m enjoying myself.”

“I’m sure we do all we can for you,” said my mother.

“I know you do,” replied my aunt. “I’m a burden to you. I always have been.”

“Not a burden,” corrected my mother.

“What does the woman call it then?” snapped my aunt. “Does she reckon I’ve been a sunbeam in the house? I’ve been a trial to everybody. That’s what I was born for—it’s my metier.”

My mother hugged her. “We should miss you very much.”

“I’m sure I hope they all will!” answered my aunt. “It’s the only thing I’ve got to leave ‘em worth having.”

My mother laughed.

“Maybe it’s been a good thing for you, Maggie,” grumbled my aunt. “If it wasn’t for cantankerous, disagreeable people like me, gentle, patient people like you wouldn’t get any practice. Perhaps, after all, I’ve been a blessing to you in disguise.”

Honestly? I don’t think we ever missed her—though we did miss her jokes, her sharp tongue, her cornery ways. It takes all sorts to make a world. If only perfect folk existed, life might become painfully dull.

As for Amy, she actually regretted her.

“One never knows what’s good for one till one’s lost it,” she sighed.

“I’m glad to think you liked her,” said my mother.

“You see, mum,” Amy explained, “I was one of a large family. A bit of a row now and again cheers one up, I always think. I’ll be losing the power of my tongue if something doesn’t come along soon.”

“Well, you’re getting married in a few weeks,” reminded my mother.

But Amy remained glum. “They’re poor things, the men, at a few words, the best of them. As likely as not, just when you’re getting interested, you turn round to find they’ve put on their hat and gone out.”

After my aunt’s death, my mother and I were mostly alone. Barbara had gone abroad to finish her education—“to learn the tricks of the Nobs’ trade,” as old Hasluck put it—and I’d left school to work for Mr. Stillwood, without salary, the idea being that I’d study law.

“You’re in luck’s way, my boy, in luck’s way,” old Mr. Gadley assured me. “To start your career in the office of Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal is a passport anywhere. It will stamp you, my boy.”

Mr. Stillwood himself was ancient and frail—so frail it seemed odd he hadn’t retired years ago.

“I’m always meaning to,” he told me early on. “When your poor father came to me, he told me frankly he had only a few more years. ‘Mr. Kelver,’ I said, ‘don’t let that trouble you. There are a few matters I’d like cleared up. You can help me. When they’re done, I’ll retire.’ Yet here I am—jogging on. Like the old hackney coach horse, Mr. Weller—or is it Mr. Jingle?—says: if the shafts were pulled away, I’d probably collapse. So I jog on. I jog on.”

He’d married late—a common woman much younger than himself, who brought with her a horde of needy relatives. No doubt, escaping her noisy household, the peace of Lombard Street was a relief. We saw her occasionally. She was one of those blustering, “managing” women who believe making a fuss is somehow “putting things right.” Ashamed of her past, she tried to pass herself off as a duchess, but really resembled a Sunday barmaid who’d had too many sherry cobbler.

Only old Gadley wasn’t afraid of her. In fact, he kept her in constant fear. He’d known her in her glory days—before the coal shed—and resented her entry into the aristocratic firm of Stillwood & Co. as a personal insult.

Her story was peculiar. Mr. Stillwood, a bored man-about-town in his forties, had first seen her—a dirty, fair-haired child—playing in the gutter. To his lasting shame, it was young Gadley himself, accompanying his boss home from Westminster, who drew attention to her by boxing her ears for slapping his face with a sprat. Stillwood, acting on impulse, grabbed her hand and dragged her—unwilling—to her father’s coal shed. The arrangement amounted to buying the child. She was sent abroad to school, the shed shut down. Ten years later, a big, handsome young woman, he married her. And learned, slowly, the truth of the saying: “What’s bred in the bone will come out in the flesh,” no matter how hard you scrub, paint, or hide it.

Her constant complaint? That he was only a solicitor—a profession she considered vulgar. Nothing “riled” old Gadley more than hearing her talk about it.

“It’s not fair to the gals,” I once heard her say to him. I was working in the next room, door slightly ajar, and she talked at the top of her voice. “What real gentleman is going to marry the daughter of a City attorney? As I told him years ago, he ought to have retired and gone into the House.”

“The very thing your poor father used to talk of doing whenever things were going a bit queer in the retail coal and potato business,” grunted old Gadley.

Mrs. Stillwood called him a “low beast” in her most aristocratic tones and swept out.

Still, Mr. Stillwood himself never claimed to love the law.

“I’m not sure, Kelver,” he said once, “that you’ve chosen wisely. The law makes you regard humanity morally the way medicine regards it physically: universally unsound. You suspect everyone of being a rogue. When people behave themselves, we lawyers hear nothing. All we hear is roguery, trickery, hypocrisy. It deteriorates the character, Kelles. We live in a perpetual atmosphere of transgression. Sometimes I wonder if it’s infectious.”

“It doesn’t seem to have infected you, sir,” I replied—since the firm was known for its old-fashioned rectitude.

“I hope not, Kelver, I hope not,” he said. “Yet sometimes I suspect myself—wonder if I might be a scamp without realizing it. A rogue, you know, Kelver, can always explain himself into an honest man. A scamp is never a scamp to himself.”

For a moment, I was alarmed—especially since I’d followed Gadley’s advice and put my mother’s savings into Mr. Stillwood’s hands, which had actually increased our income. But looking into his smiling eyes, my fear vanished.

Laughing, he laid a hand on my shoulder. “One person you should always be suspicious of, Kelver—yourself. Nobody can do you more harm than you can.”

We saw more of Washburn—Hal, we called him now. With his gentle, masterful hands, he’d gently peeled away my mother’s shyness, becoming almost part of the family. She treated him like an absurdly bearded boy entrusted to her care without knowing it. I looked up to him like a wise elder brother.

“You rest me, Mrs. Kelver,” he’d say, lighting his pipe and sinking into the deep leather chair reserved for him. “Your even voice, your soft eyes, your quiet hands—they soothe me.”

“It’s good for a man,” he’d say, looking through the smoke at us, “to test his wisdom by two things: the face of a good woman, and the ear of a child—I beg your pardon, Paul—of a young man. A good woman’s face is white sunlight. Under gaslights, who can tell diamond from paste? Bring it into the sun—does it stand the test? Then it’s good. And children—they’re the waiting earth where we plant our seeds. Is it chaff or living seed? Wait and watch. I shower my thoughts over our Paul. He swallows them, forgets them—rubbish. Then I say something that stays, grows. Ah, that was alive. That was a seed. The waiting earth uses only what is true.”

“You should marry, Hal,” my mother would say. It was her cure-all.

“I would, Mrs. Kelver,” he replied, “if I could marry half a dozen women. I’d make an ideal husband for six wives. One I’d neglect for five days, be a burden on the sixth.”

From anyone else, such a remark would have offended my mother. But Hal’s smile made anything palatable.

“I’m not one man, Mrs. Kelver. I’m half a dozen. If I married one wife, she’d be married to six husbands. Too many for any woman to manage.”

“Have you never fallen in love?” asked my mother.

“Three of me have. But each time, the other five outvoted him.”

“You’re sure six would be enough?” she smiled.

“Just the right number. One of me must worship, adore a woman madly—grovel before her like a Troubadour, eat her slipper, drink her washwater, scourge himself before her window, die for a kiss of her glove flung down with a laugh. She must be scornful, cruel. Another would cherish a tender, yielding creature—one whose face lights at my coming, clouds at my going. A third, a child of Pan—an ugly little beast, Mrs. Kelver—horns on head, hoofs on feet, leering through the wood, seeking its fit mate. A fourth would wed a wholesome, homely wench—deep of bosom, broad of hip—fit mother of a sturdy brood. A fifth would want only a true friend, a comrade, sharer of joys and thoughts. And a last—Mrs. Kelver—yearns for a woman pure and sweet, clothed in love and crowned with holiness. Wouldn’t we be a handful for any one woman in an eight-roomed house?”

But my mother wouldn’t give up. “You’ll find the woman one day, Hal, who will be all of them to you—all worth having. And your eight-roomed house will be a kingdom!”

“A man is many, and a woman but one,” answered Hal.

“That’s what men say who can’t see more than one side of a woman,” retorted my mother, a little sharply. Her pride in her sex was fierce. And indeed, I’ve learned: the flag of Womanhood is always upheld by true women, flouted only by false ones. For a judge in petticoats is just a witness in a wig.

Hal put down his pipe. “Now tell us, Mrs. Kelver, for our guidance—what must the lover of a young girl be?”

Always serious on love, my mother answered gravely: “She asks for the whole of a man, Hal—not a sixth, nor any part. She’s a child asking for a lover to whom she can look up, who will teach her, guide her, protect her. She’s a queen demanding homage—and yet, he is her king whom it is her joy to serve. She wants to be his partner, his playmate, his fellow-worker—and still love to think of him as her child, her big baby she must take care of. Whatever he gives, she responds with equal measure. You need not marry six wives, Hal—you’ll find your six in one.

‘As the water to the vessel, woman shapes herself to man,’ said an old heathen three thousand years ago. That’s what you mean.”

“I don’t like that way of putting it,” said my mother. “I mean that as you say of man, so in every true woman is contained all women. But to know her completely, you must love her with all love.”

Sometimes we talked religion. My mother’s faith was alive, not a relic wrapped in cotton wool.

One evening, Hal cried: “Who are we that we should live? The spider is less cruel; the pig less greedy; the tiger less tigerish; our cousin ape less monkeyish. What are we but savages, clothed and ashamed?”

“But Sodom and Gomorrah,” reminded my mother, “would have been spared for ten just men.”

“Much more sensible to have hurried the ten out, leaving the rest to be buried under their own ashes,” growled Hal.

“And we shall be purified,” continued my mother. “The evil in us washed away.”

“Why make us ill just to mend us? If the Almighty wanted us, why not make us decent from the start?”

He’d just come from a Poor Law Guardians meeting and was furious with humanity.

“It is His way,” said my mother. “The precious stone lies hidden in clay. He has His purpose.”

“Is the stone so very precious?”

“Would He have taken so much pains to fashion it if it weren’t? You see it everywhere, Hal—in heroism, self-sacrifice, love stronger than death. Can you think He’ll waste it? He uses even the dead leaf again.”

“Shall the new leaf remember the new flower?”

“Yes, if it ever knew it. Shall memory be the only thing to die?”

Often, I’d accompany Hal on his rounds. Among the savage tribe he served and ruled, he was both medicine man and priest—tyrant and slave, working tirelessly, bullying mercilessly, enforcing rules with tongue or fist. Counsellor, helper, ruler of thousands. His income barely covered his rent, but few men enjoyed more power. And that, I think, was why he stayed.

“Nature laid me by and forgot me for two thousand years,” he said. “Born in my proper time, I’d have climbed to chieftainship on uplifted shields. I might have been Attila, Alaric. Among the civilized, one can only climb by crawling—and I’m too impatient to crawl. Here, I’m king at once, by force of brain and muscle.”

So he stayed in Poplar—poor in fees, rich in honor.

Justice was a passion with him. The oppressors of the poor feared him. Once proven guilty, vengeance followed swiftly. If the law wouldn’t act, he used lawlessness—always available in ample supply. Bumble might have the Board behind him, Shylock legal backing for his pound of flesh—but sooner or later, darkness brought punishment: a ducking in the dock basin, a “Brutal Assault Upon a Respected Resident” (according to local papers), the miscreants always escaping. He was an excellent organizer.

One night, a child needed immediate hospital care.

“It ain’t no use my taking her now,” said the mother. “I’ll only get bullyragged for disturbing ‘em. My old man was carried there three months ago when he broke his leg, but they wouldn’t take him in till the morning.”

“Oho! oho! oho!” sang Hal, lifting the child and putting on his hat. “You follow me—we’ll have some sport. Tally ho! tally ho!” And off we went, Hal leading the procession, singing a rollicking song, the baby staring at him openmouthed.

“Now ring,” he said at the Workhouse gate. “Ring modestly, as becomes the poor ringing at the gate of Charity.” The bell tinkled faintly.

“Ring again!” he shouted, stepping back into the shadows. Finally, the wicket opened.

“Oh, if you please, sir, my baby—”

“Blast your baby!” answered a husky voice. “What d’ye mean by coming here this time of night?”

“Please, sir, I’m afraid it’s dying, and the Doctor—”

The man was no sentimentalist. He cursed them all and started to close the gate—but Hal’s stick stopped it.

“Open the gate!” roared Hal. You couldn’t pretend not to hear him within half a mile. “Open it quick, you blackguard! You gross vat-load of potato spirit, you—”

The Romans believed rulers should speak the language of the ruled. Hal agreed. His command of slang, idioms, curses—understood by his people—was one of his rods of power. Before the trembling porter could loosen the bolts, Hal had painted a word picture of him that must have crushed his ego.

“I didn’t know it was you, Doctor,” the man muttered.

“No, you thought you had only to deal with some helpless creature you could bully. Stir your fat carcass, you ugly cur! I’m in a hurry.”

The surgeon was away, but a few attendants lounged about—unfortunately for them. Hal, being there, took it upon himself to inspect the ward and fix everything crooked. They had a busy, alarming time.

Not until hours later did he emerge, thoroughly satisfied.

And so, we returned—back to the house, back to the quiet, back to the slow drift of grief and memory. Life moved on, as it always does, in the shadow of loss and the echo of laughter.

“Room to let for a single gentleman.” Sometimes, when boredom strikes like a rogue with a wet towel, I’ll knock on that door. It opens after an eternity—or maybe just a minute—by the “slavey,” who’s either slatternly in the morning (arms hidden under her apron like a secret weapon) or smart in the afternoon (dirty cap and apron, but somehow dignified). How well I know her! She hasn’t aged a day—same round eyes, same open mouth, same hair as if she wrestled a hedgehog, same red hands scored by life’s rough edges. I resist the urge to mutter, “Sorry, forgot my key,” and barrel past her up the stairs two at a time. Instead, I say, “Oh, what rooms have you to let?” And off she scuttles to the top of the kitchen stairs, yelling over the banister: “A gentleman to see the rooms!”

Up comes the landlady—a harried, elderly woman in genteel black, panting like she’s run a marathon in heels. She squeezes past the slavey, eyes me like I might be a thief or a poet (both equally suspicious), and says:

“I have a very nice room on the first floor—and one behind on the third.”

I agree to see them, explaining I’m looking for a young friend. We squeeze past the hat-and-umbrella stand—just barely, but only if you stay glued to the wall like a nervous shadow.

The first-floor room is impressive. A marble-topped sideboard measuring three feet by two, whose doors only close if you wedge paper between them like a tiny hostage negotiation. A green tablecloth (matching the curtains) covers the loo-table. The lamp? Perfectly safe—if it stays exactly in the center. Move it? Disaster. A paper fire-stove ornament adds an air of moral purity, like the room has taken a vow of chastity. Above the mantelpiece hangs a fly-blown mirror, its frame so gilded once upon a time that invitation cards can still be slipped between glass and wood. One or two remain, proving even bed-sitting-room tenants aren’t immune to social ambition.

On the opposite wall: an oleograph. You know the kind—sold by Cheap Jacks on Saturday nights in Pimlico Road, guaranteed “hand-made.” Usually a Swiss landscape. Why Swiss? Because they’re full of drama: a dilapidated mill, a foaming torrent, a mountain, a maiden, and a cow. Always. You can count on it.

Then there’s the easy chair. Let’s not call it comfortable. It’s stuffed with steel wire coils, each with a business end ready to puncture your backside. Covered in highly glazed horsehair. Only one way to sit: edge of seat, feet planted firmly. Lean back? You’ll slide out like a poorly secured soufflé. When you do, it whispers: “Excuse me, you’re heavy. You’d be far more comfortable on the floor. Thank you.”

The bed is behind the door. Washstand behind the bed. If you face the window, you forget the bed exists. But if more than one friend drops by? You’re glad it’s there. In fact, experienced visitors go straight for it, refusing to trade it for the easy chair. They know the truth: comfort isn’t always about softness.

“And this room?” I ask.

“Eight shillings a week, sir—with attendance, of course.”

“Any extras?”

“The lamp’s eighteenpence a week. Kitchen fire—two shillings if you dine at home.”

“And coal?”

“Sixpence a scuttle, sir. Standard size.”

“It’s rather small.”

She bristles. “Standard, I think, sir.” One assumes there’s a special scuttle made just for lodging-house keepers—like a miniaturized version of the universe.

I decide to see the other room—the third floor back. She opens the door but stays on the landing. She’s stout, and doesn’t want to dwarf the space. Inside, the bed dominates. It refuses to hide. Its only rival is the washstand—straw-colored, with a glaring white basin and jug, staring defiantly from the corner. “I may be small,” it seems to say, “but I’m useful. Don’t ignore me.”

Other furniture: two chairs—no pretense. Not easy. Never were. A small chest of drawers painted light, with white china handles and a tiny mirror on top. And a four-legged table—thin, stretched, likely homemade—occupying every inch of floor space after allowing three square feet for the tenant. The only decoration? A funeral card framed in beer corks, hanging above the fireplace. Like the ancient Egyptians used to place corpses at their feasts, perhaps it’s a reminder: life ends. Or maybe it’s comforting: hey, could be worse.

Rent: three-and-sixpence a week, including attendance. Lamp: eighteenpence. Kitchen fire: one shilling.

“But why is the first floor’s kitchen fire two shillings and this one only one?”

“Well, sir,” she says, “the first floor needs more cooking.”

Ah yes. The gentleman in the third floor back. His breakfast? Plain. Lunch? He takes it out. You might spot him walking the quiet square, up and down the narrow street that leads nowhere in particular. Carrying a paper bag. At intervals, when no one’s watching, his mouth disappears into it. You can guess what’s inside.

Saveloys are plentiful—twopence each. Pie shops offer meat pies for twopence, fruit for a penny. The lady behind the counter uses a flat knife to lift the little dainty clean from its dish—miraculous, given how thin the pastry is. Roley-poley pudding—sweet, satisfying, especially cold—is a penny a slice. Peas pudding? Awkward to eat from a bag, but warming on cold days.

Tea? Two eggs or a haddock (fourpenny size). Occasionally, a chop or steak—fry it for him, madam, though he insists it should be grilled. But frying in your one frying pan? Flavor gets confused. Maybe that’s better. Shut your eyes, trust smell and taste—you can pretend you’re eating herrings, then liver and bacon, then a joint, then toasted cheese. Monotony? Avoided.

Dinner? He goes out again. Maybe not hungry. Late meals are a mistake. Or maybe he stops under a lamp-post, counts pennies in his pocket, and appetite arrives. Then there are places buzzing with sizzling fat—fried plaice for three halfpence, sliced potatoes for a penny. Stewed eels for fourpence, vinegar ad lib. Or sevenpence—red-letter evenings—for half a sheep’s head. A supper fit for any king… who happened to be hungry.

I tell the landlady I’ll discuss it with my friend when he arrives. She nods: “Certainly, sir.” She’s seen her share of “wandering Christians.” I slip a shilling into the slavey’s astonished hand—her lukewarm fingers clutch it like a miracle—and step back into the long, dreary street. Maybe now echoing with the mournful cry: “Muffins!”

Or sometimes, in the evening, lamp lit, meat tea cleared away, firelight flickering in the dingy rooms, I go visiting. No need to ring. Through the thin walls, I see you, old friends—fashions changed, but spirits intact. We wore bell-bottom trousers—eight-and-six to measure, seven-and-six if from stock. Neckties fastened with horseshoe pins. Our waistcoats? Gayer, braver. Cuffs and collars? Paper—sixpence-halfpenny the dozen, three-halfpence the pair. White on Sunday. Less aggressive Monday. Dappled Tuesday morning. By Tuesday evening, natty cane in hand, we strolled Oxford Street—fashionable hour nine to ten p.m.—and cocked our chins with pride.

Your india-rubber linen has advantages—storm-proof, heat-resistant. A sponge, and your “Dicky” is reborn. We had to use bread crumbs—sacrificing the glaze. But for the first few hours? Our paper was dazzling.

No change in you, old friends. I wave from the misty street. God rest you, gallant gentlemen—lonely, friendless, despised—making the best of joyless lives, keeping yourselves genteel on twelve, fifteen, or eighteen shillings a week. Saving some to send to the old mother in the country, proud of her “gentleman” son with book learning and a job in the City.

May nothing dismay you. Bullied from nine to six? From six to bedtime, you’re rorty young dogs. That half-guinea topper—“as worn by the Prince of Wales”—warmed before the fire, brushed, polished, shines resplendent. Second pair of trousers pulled from beneath the bed—gaslight makes them look new. A pleasant evening to you!

May your cheap necktie make all the impression your soul desires! May your penny cigar pass for Havana! May the barmaid call you “Baby!” May a sweet shop-girl glance kindly, invite you to walk. May she snigger at your jokes. May other dogs envy you. May no harm come of it.

You dreamers of dreams—while others play and sleep, you toil upward in the night. You’ve read Smiles’ *Self-Help*, Longfellow’s *Psalm of Life*. You attack *French Without a Master*, *Bookkeeping in Six Lessons* with confidence. With a sigh, you turn from bright streets, from bewitching eyes, into the stuffy air of Birkbeck Institutions, Polytechnic Schools.

May success compensate you for your youth devoid of pleasure! May the partner’s chair you see in visions be yours! May you live one day in Clapham in a twelve-roomed house!

And after all—we have our moments, don’t we?

Saturday night at the play. Waiting? Short. We converse with kindred souls about the British Drama—past and future. We dream of Florence This, Kate That. Her photo on our mantelpiece—turning the dismal room into a shrine. The poem we’ve started? When finished, we’ll send it. At least she’ll acknowledge it. We can kiss the paper her hand touched.

The great doors groan, then quiver. Ah, the wild thrill! Now push, wriggle, squirm! An epitome of life. Through—collarless, panting, pummeled. But what of that? Upward, still upward. Then leap from bench to bench in the gloom. Front row! Would we trade sensations with the stallite, strolling languidly to his seat?

The extravagant dinner once a week! Banquet *à la française* in Soho—for one-and-six, including wine. Does Tortoni ever give his customers a meal they enjoy more? I trow not.

My first lodging? An attic across from Blackfriars Bridge. Rent: three shillings a week with cooking, half-a-crown without. I bought a methylated spirit stove with kettle and frying-pan—and took it without.

Old Hasluck would have helped. Others too. But boy’s pride held me back. I’d make my way alone. Win my place in the world myself. To Hal, who’d understand, I confessed the truth.

“Had your mother lived,” he said, “I’d have something to say. But as it is—well, you needn’t fear. I won’t offer help. Indeed, I’d refuse it if you asked. Put your Carlyle in your pocket. He’s not all voices, but he’s the best maker of men I know. The great thing is not to be afraid of life.”

“Look me up now and then,” he added. “We’ll talk stars, Socialism, the Woman Question—anything except yourself and your twopenny-halfpenny affairs.”

From another, it’d sound brutal. But I understood. We shook hands and parted—longer than either expected. The Franco-German War broke out weeks later. Hal, drawn by adventure, volunteered. Accepted. Years passed before we met again.

On a doorpost in Farringdon Street stood a brass plate: “Ludgate News Rooms. Third and fourth floors. Admission: one penny.” Every morning, a seedy crowd gathered—clerks, shopmen, artisans, travellers, warehousemen—all out of work. Most were young. A few elder men—silent, sad, like ghosts of jobs past.

We split into two: newcomers, cheery, confident. They buzzed from newspaper to newspaper, selecting ads like choosing desserts. Their replies? Full of assurance:

“Dear Sir—In reply to your advertisement in today’s _Standard_, I shall be pleased to accept the post. I am of good appearance and address. Excellent in French, German, bookkeeping, arithmetic, geometry. Passionate lover of work. Moral character worthy of a Free Kirk Elder. I could call tomorrow or Friday between eleven and one, or Saturday until two. Salary required: two guineas a week. Early answer will oblige. Yours truly.”

The old stagers? Silent. Hour after hour, writing steadily, methodically, hope fading:

“Sir—Your advt. in today’s _D. T._ I am—” age, qualifications shorter, more modest. “If engaged, I will endeavour to give satisfaction. Any time convenient, I will call. I would not ask a high salary to start. Yours obediently.”

Dozens of the first. Hundreds of the second. I wrote with care—pen chosen, margin dotted. Three or four replies came: “Call at the office.” But shyness—once buried during school days—returned. A shy child? Understood. A shy young man? Regarded as a fool. I looked awkward, stupid, sulky. The harder I tried to act natural, the more rude I became.

“Well, I must see others. We’ll write and let you know.” That was the end. For me.

My pounds dwindled. Looking back, it’s easy to laugh. But then? No way of knowing if my story would be comedy or tragedy. Moments when I feared it was the latter. Occasionally, when feeling well, it’s not unpleasant to imagine one’s own deathbed—friends finally understanding, regretting. But me? No one to regret. I felt small, helpless. The world was big. I feared it might walk over me, trample me down, never seeing me. I couldn’t attract attention.

One morning, a letter waited: “Will Mr. P. Kelver call at the above address tomorrow between ten-thirty and eleven.” From “Lott and Co., Indian Commission Agents, Aldersgate Street.” Without hope, I returned to lodgings, changed clothes, donned silk hat, gloves, wrapped umbrella in its case. Armed for battle, I marched to Aldersgate.

Too early. I walked up and down, staring at second-floor windows. Could not recall the ad. Firm seemed unthriving. Wondered what salary they’d offer. Dreamt of a Cheeryble Brother offering thirty shillings. Then remembered my usual fate—considered whether climbing stairs was worth it.

Rain began. Didn’t want to open umbrella. Nervous for my hat. Five minutes to half-hour. I crossed the road, mounted bare stairs. Two doors. One marked “Private.” I tapped lightly. No response. Tapped again. A sound—unintelligible. Knocked louder.

“Oh, do come in,” came a shrill, plaintive voice.

Tone of desperate pleading. I turned handle, entered.

Small room. Dimly lit. Window bottom half covered in tissue paper. Looked less like an office, more like a village shop. Jam, pickles, wine, biscuit tins, drapery, candles, soap, boots, stationery, cigars, tinned food, guns, cartridges—enough to equip a desert island. Everything littered the corners.

At a desk sat a youth—tiny body, enormous head. Proportion so odd, I half expected him to raise hands and remove it.

Half in, half out, I paused.

“Is this Lott & Co.?” I asked.

“No,” he replied. “It’s a room.” One eye fixed on me—dull, glassy, unblinking. The other continued writing.

“I mean—are these offices of Lott & Co.?”

“It’s one of them,” he said. “The back one. If you really want a job, shut the door.”

I did. Then announced: “Mr. Kelver—Paul Kelver.”

“Minikin,” he said. “Sylvanus Minikin. You happen to know why you’re here?”

Looking at his body, I wanted to pull his ears. His face—grave, massive—might’ve been the head clerk.

“I have an appointment with Mr. Lott,” I said, dignity intact. I produced the letter, leaned across a sewing machine, handed it over.

He read it, then suddenly removed the eye he’d been using. Polished it on his handkerchief. Turned his other eye toward me. Handed back the letter.

“Want my advice?” he asked.

I said yes.

“Hook it,” he said.

“Why? Isn’t he a good employer?”

He replaced the glass eye, turned back to work. “If employment is what you want, you’ll get it. Best employer in London. He’ll work you twenty-four hours a day, then offer overtime at half salary.”

“I must get something to do,” I admitted.

“Sit down,” he suggested. “Rest while you can.”

I took the only chair. Except the one he was on.

“Apart from being a bit of a driver,” I asked, “what sort of man is he? Pleasant?”

“Never saw him put out but once,” Minikin said.

“That sounds good. When was that?”

“All the time I’ve known him.”

My spirits sank. Had I stayed longer, I might’ve followed his advice—hooked it—before Mr. Lott arrived.

But then—door opened. Someone entered the private office. Bell rang. Minikin vanished, leaving the door ajar.

I heard:

“Why isn’t Mr. Skeat here?”

“Because he hasn’t come.”

“Where are the letters?”

“Under your nose.”

“How dare you answer me like that?”

“Well, it’s the truth. They are under your nose.”

“Did you give Thorneycroft’s man my message?”

“Yes.”

“What did he answer?”

“Said you were a liar.”

“Oh, he did, did he! What did you reply?”

“Asked him to tell me something I didn’t know.”

“Thought that clever, didn’t you?”

“Not bad.”

Whatever faults Mr. Lott had, he had self-control. I concluded.

“Anybody been here?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Mr. Kelver—Paul Kelver.”

“Kelver, Kelver. Who’s Kelver?”

“Know what he is—a fool.”

“What do you mean?”

And I left.

PAUL, ESCAPING FROM HIS SOLITUDE, FALLS INTO STRANGE COMPANY. AND BECOMES CAPTIVE TO ONE OF HAUGHTY MIEN.

All things pass—except perhaps the awkwardness of being twenty-three and convinced you’re destined for literary immortality. Winter came, and with it, a strange new resolve: I would work. Not because I loved writing (I didn’t), but because working was better than sitting in silence, staring at the wall like a condemned man awaiting execution. With my tiny fire crackling, two candles flickering like nervous witnesses, and paper stacked higher than my self-esteem, I became the reluctant medium between my own thoughts and the world—hopefully not too much of a disappointment to either.

London lay before me, vast, chaotic, and full of stories begging to be stolen. It had millions of characters, ten thousand romances, mysteries that could keep a detective busy for decades, and humour so sharp it could cut through fog. And yet, when I tried to write about real life—real people, real places—I found myself instead drafting tales set in windmills, moated granges, or mountains so dramatic they probably didn’t exist outside of postcards. My heroines? Cottage maidens with eyes that danced, hair that curled like a question mark, and hearts that beat just once before giving up the ghost. My heroes? They usually died too—though not from love, no, from something far more poetic: *the atmosphere*. You know, that deadly, invisible force that creeps into your soul and whispers, “You were never meant for this world.”

And what did these poor souls die among? Snowdrops and primroses, naturally—planted by me in a book called *Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated*, which I’d read twice and still couldn’t tell the difference between a daisy and a dandelion. If near the coast, their final resting place was often chosen by sea breezes, who apparently liked to play football with gravestones. Years later, some old man with white hair and a voice like a rusty hinge would come by with children, telling them the story of how their ancestor died tragically young—and the kids would probably ask, “Why do we have to hear this every Sunday?”

Occasionally, the gentleman would meet his end too. He’d sigh dramatically in his last soliloquy: “It was better so.” Then he’d vanish into the African veldt or a desert where no one ever went—unless they were looking for a lost camel or a particularly stubborn idea. For such scenes, I’d visit the British Museum reading room, where I’d study maps and pretend I knew what “arid” meant.

These little tragedies—dismal, overwrought, and utterly unmarketable—kept coming back to me. Like pigeons with bad aim, they’d fly off to editors, only to return, scorched, crumpled, and slightly singed. I sent them with businesslike notes (“Take it or leave it”), then with tearful pleas (“Please, please, give it a chance!”), sometimes offering five guineas (crossed cheque, thank you), other times sending them free of charge as a “sample packet”—because clearly, if I could get them published once, I’d be rich beyond dreams.

Once, I even enclosed a rejection note written in bold, black ink: “Rejected. Too many commas.” I thought it might impress someone. It didn’t.

I’d curse them, fling them into the fire, watch them burn… then snatch them out before they turned to ash, smooth them flat, and reread them like a lover’s letter. The editor must’ve seen them, right? But no—some jealous underling had done this. Or I’d sent them to the wrong magazine. Or they’d arrived on a Tuesday when the editor was having a bad day. Their time would come. Rewriting the first and last pages, I’d send them forth again—always with fresh hope, always with the same result.

Meanwhile, I decided it was time to become a proper man of the world. Because let’s face it: if you want to be taken seriously as an artist, you need to smoke, drink, and generally look like you’ve seen things. Smoking, I discovered, was hard. At school, I’d tried a cigarette once. It tasted like burnt regret and made me cough like a dying badger. I told myself smoking was bad for growing boys—which was true—but also a convenient excuse for being physically weak. I pretended to disapprove of it while secretly envying those who could inhale without choking. Hypocrite? Yes. But I was young, and youth is the season of pretending.

Drinking was worse. Wine? Fine. Beer? Tolerable. Spirits? I nearly died trying. Once, I confused whiskey with hair oil—yes, really—and drank it. It was less nauseous than I expected. So maybe I was onto something. Twice a week, I forced myself to down a glass of beer, standing over myself like a drill sergeant. Afterward, I’d reward myself with chocolates—because nothing says “self-improvement” like sugar.

But progress wasn’t all about vice. I was slowly overcoming shyness. The trick? Stop fighting it. Accept it. Laugh at it. And eventually, others would laugh with you. The cold world hardened me. Life’s little rubbings rounded off my edges. I became less of a wallflower and more of a… mildly interesting person.

Then Fate stepped in—or rather, pointed me toward Nelson Square. What a house! A leaky roof, a cast of characters, and enough drama to fill a soap opera. Our landlady, Mrs. Peedles, had once been an actress in Charles Keane’s company at the old Princess’s Theatre. She played Juliet, Amy Robsart, Mrs. Haller in *The Stranger*—and possibly a few roles I didn’t recognize because she’d forgotten her lines. Her proof? Handbills a yard long, screaming “ON TUESDAY EVENING! BY SPECIAL DESIRE!!!” and “UNDER THE PATRONAGE OF COL. SIR WILLIAM AND THE OFFICERS OF THE 74TH!!!!” She’d recite Lady Macbeth’s sleep-walking scene on demand—though I’m not sure she remembered whether she was asleep or awake.

Now, she was a faded, watery-eyed woman prone to sudden sobs about her misfortunes. “But there,” she’d say, wiping her eyes, “fretting’s no good. We must grin and bear things.” Then she’d laugh—loudly, abruptly—as if to prove she meant it.

She also had trouble breathing. “You don’t know ‘ow to breathe,” our second-floor front once told her kindly. “You don’t swallow it—you gargle with it. Take a good draught and shut your mouth. Don’t let it out till it’s done something.” He pressed his handkerchief against her mouth to help. It didn’t work.

“There don’t seem any room for it inside me,” she said.

Bells were her lifeblood. She lived for the sound of them—her whole existence structured around waiting for interruptions. Conversation? Just filler until the next chime.

That Christmas, I received a bottle of whiskey—a gift from a commercial traveller. I didn’t like whiskey, so I kept it for Mrs. Peedles. When she sat down, breathless, near my door, I’d offer her a sip. Her face would light up. “Ah, well,” she’d say, “I don’t mind if I do. It’s a poor heart that never rejoices.”

Then, loosened by liquor, she’d tell me stories of past lodgers—men like me, bright-eyed and hopeful, who ended up in prison or lunatic asylums. One had written poetry on my very table. Now he was doing five years for forgery. Mrs. Peedles seemed to think poetry and crime were two sides of the same coin. Another tenant, studious and ambitious, was now at Hanwell. “Always the most deserving,” she’d say, “are the ones who get hit hardest.”

I was glad when the bottle ran out. A second might have driven me to suicide.

There was no Mr. Peedles—not for her, anyway. He’d been “general utility” at the theatre—a fine figure of a man, though easily led astray by minxes. Mrs. Peedles spoke of him bitterly. “General utilities,” she’d say, “they’re no use at all.”

On weekdays, she wore black clothes and a plain cap. But Sundays brought transformation. She’d emerge in her old stage wardrobe—paste-decked shoes, tinsel jewelry, classic garb that made her look like a rejected puppet from a Greek tragedy. Her thin grey hair and worn hands were the only reminders of reality. But beneath the costume, Miss Lucretia Barry stirred—aged, yes, but still arch.

Next door lived a law-writer and his wife—old, poor, and resigned. They’d been a schoolmaster and pupil teacher, married young, smiled upon by fate—until illness struck. Bad drains, typhoid fever—no moral, just bad luck. They’d rebuilt, cleared debts, only to fall again helping a friend. Now they lived on the edge of starvation, content with beer or spirits when they could afford it. I’d wake in the night to hear the scratch of his pen through the thin partition—our silent duet of ambition and despair.

We worked side by side: youth and age, dream and reality. From him, I fashioned a vision of my future. Past his door, I’d tiptoe like a thief. Once, I heard him mutter: “The world’s mine oyster.” Hadn’t he once been king? Was I just another fool mistaking desire for power?

Then there was Jarman—our second-floor front, Young London incarnate. Blatant, kind-hearted, aggressively cheerful. His motto: “Never say die.” And he meant it. He woke with a start, bathed like a porpoise, sang while dressing, roared for boots, whistled at breakfast. His entrance and exit were accompanied by banging doors, shouted orders, and brooms flying like projectiles. He’d leave behind a sense of chaos—like the house had been evacuated.

Sometimes, he’d march into my room with a supper tray for two. At first, I’d protest. “I’m busy,” I’d say.

“You fire away, Shakespeare Redivivus,” he’d reply. “Don’t delay the tragedy. Why should London wait? I’ll keep quiet.”

His idea of quiet was retreating into a corner and acting out a melodrama in hoarse whispers:
“Ah, ah! I 'ave killed 'er good old father; I 'ave falsely accused 'er young man of all the crimes that I 'ave myself committed; I 'ave robbed 'er of 'er ancestral estates. Yet she loves me not! It is streeange!”

Then, switching to falsetto:
“It is a cold and dismal night: the snow falls fast. I will leave me 'at and umbrella be'ind the door and go out for a walk with the chee-ild. Aha! who is this? 'E also 'as forgotten 'is umbrella. Ah, now I know 'im in the pitch dark by 'is cigarette! Villain, murderer, silly josser! it is you!”

Then, lightning-fast:
“Mary, I love yer!”
“Sir Jasper Murgatroyd, let me avail myself of this opportunity to tell you what I think of you--”
“No, no; the 'ouses close in 'alf an hour; there is not tee-ime. Fly with me instead!”
“Never! Un'and me!”
“'Ear me! Ah, what 'ave I done? I 'ave slipped upon a piece of orange peel and broke me 'ead! If you will kindly ask them to turn off the snow and give me a little moonlight, I will confess all.”

I gave up trying to write. Instead, I listened—because Jarman’s talk was gold. He talked about himself—everything, all the time. Love affairs? Dozens. No secrets. What he “told her straight,” what she “up and said to him,” it was all fair game. His search for the ideal woman? Still ongoing.

“Girls,” he’d say, “they’re all alike till you know ‘em. So long as they’re trying to sell themselves, they’ll tell you there isn’t another like ‘em. But once they’ve got your order—ah, then you see what they’re really made of. Most of ‘em are put together cheap. Bah! It sickens me to read how you paper-stainers talk about ‘em—angels, goddesses, fairies! They’ve just been getting at you. You’re giving ‘em exactly what they’re asking for.”

“But mind yer,” he’d add, “there are good ones. Real good ‘uns. All I say is, taking ‘em at their own valuation ain’t the way to do business.”

What he wanted was a truly first-class article—one that wouldn’t lose its paint before you got it home. “They’re to be found,” he’d cheerfully insist, “but you’ve got to look for ‘em. They’re not the sort that advertises.”

And so, amid the chaos, the sorrow, and the absurdity, I found myself not alone anymore. I was part of a menagerie—of ghosts, dreamers, drunks, and fools—all sharing a roof, a life, and a faint hope that somewhere, somehow, one of us might actually make it.

GOOD FRIENDS SHOW PAUL THE ROAD TO FREEDOM. BUT BEFORE SETTING OUT, HE WILL GO A-VISITING.

The sun was doing its best to shine through my window when I woke up—though “woke” might be a stretch. More like *reawakened*, after being dragged back from the brink of oblivion by the sound of traffic outside. The city had already begun its daily performance, and here I was, still in bed, as if I’d been caught napping during the opening act.

I reached for my watch—only to find it missing. That’s when I remembered: last night, I’d left it somewhere between the floor and the ceiling, possibly both at once. My clothes were scattered like they’d been thrown out by an angry ghost. One boot sat proudly on the chair by the fireplace, as though it were guarding the hearth; the other? Vanished. Perhaps it had run away in shame.

My head felt like it had expanded overnight—either that or I’d accidentally swapped hats with Minikin. This one was top-heavy, lopsided, and throbbing with the kind of pain usually reserved for people who’ve just been told their favorite sandwich is no longer available.

Then it hit me—the previous night came rushing back like a drunkard chasing his own hat. From the steeple across the way, chimes rang out. Eleven o’clock. I leapt out of bed—and immediately collapsed onto the floor.

I recalled how the room had spun around me like a carousel gone mad. It wasn’t spinning anymore, but it was still swaying slightly, like a drunkard trying to stand straight. I crept to the washbasin and, after two near-misses where I nearly dunked my face into the water, managed to get my head inside. Then, pulling on trousers that felt like they were made of wet rope, I sank into the easy chair and tried to reconstruct the events of the evening—starting from now and working backward.

I was feeling terrible. Very terrible. Something had clearly gone wrong.

“That strong cigar,” I whispered, “I should never have touched it. And then that tiny room, packed with people. Plus, I’ve been overworking. I really must take more exercise.”

A small part of me felt proud. At least I wasn’t easily fooled. Not completely.

“Nonsense,” I said aloud, speaking to myself like a judge sentencing a criminal. “Don’t lie to me. You were drunk.”

“No,” I protested weakly. “Not drunk. Just… affected. Some people can’t handle sweet champagne. It must have upset my liver.”

“Drunk,” I insisted, “hopelessly, vulgarly drunk—drunk as any 'Arry after a Bank Holiday.”

“It’s the first time,” I murmured.

“It was your first chance,” I replied.

“Never again,” I promised.

“The standard line,” I muttered.

“How old are you?”

“Nineteen.”

“So no excuse for youth. How do you know this won’t become a habit? That you won’t spiral downward, sink deeper and deeper, until you end up a full-blown sot?”

My head dropped into my hands. Shame washed over me. I remembered all those temperance stories I’d read on Sunday afternoons—how men started with one glass and ended in graves lined with empty bottles. I imagined myself walking down that path: heroic resistance, then weakness, then craving, then surrender. In the foggy air, I saw my father’s pale face, my mother’s sorrowful eyes. Barbara’s scorn, sharp enough to cut glass. Hal’s contempt, heavy enough to crush a man’s spirit. Guilt and dread twisted together in my gut.

“It shall never happen again!” I shouted. “By God, it shall not!”

(At nineteen, you’re allowed to be dramatic.)

“I’ll leave this house right now,” I declared. “I’ll escape this foul atmosphere. I’ll erase it from my mind. I’ll start fresh. I’ll—”

Then, something flickered at the edge of consciousness: Miss Rosina Sellars. What was she doing in my thoughts? Why did she stand between me and redemption?

“The right of your fiancée,” my inner voice sneered, grinning like a villain in a melodrama.

“Did I actually go that far?”

“We won’t discuss details,” I answered. “Let’s just say… it happened.”

“I wasn’t myself,” I pleaded. “I didn’t know what I was doing.”

“Usually we don’t when we do foolish things. But consequences don’t care. Unfortunately, witnesses were present. And she’s not the sort you can casually dump. You’ll marry her. You’ll live in two cramped rooms. Her family will be yours. You’ll meet them soon. They call her ‘the lady.’ You can guess what that means. A grand start to your life, isn’t it, ambitious young man? A lovely mess you’ve made.”

“What am I supposed to do?” I asked.

“I honestly don’t know,” I replied.

The rest of the day passed in misery. Too ashamed to face Mrs. Peedles—or even the slavey—I locked myself in my room. By dusk, I felt marginally less ill—though not much. I slipped out and ate a meal so simple it could have been served in a prison: tea without sugar and a kippered herring, at a nearby coffee-house. Another gentleman ordered hot buttered toast. I fled.

At eight o’clock, Minikin arrived from the office, curious about my condition. With shame as my only ally, I confessed everything.

“Thought so,” he said. “Looks like you had a proper knock-out.”

“I’m glad it’s over,” I said. “It’ll be a lesson I’ll never forget.”

“Nothing like a good, honest drunk,” Minikin said, “to make you feel better than any sermon.”

In my distress, I needed advice. Minikin, though younger, was far more worldly than I. So I confided in him.

“That’s not the worst,” I said. “What do you think I’ve done?”

“Killed a policeman?” he suggested.

“Got engaged.”

“Ah,” Minikin said, raising an eyebrow. “Quiet types like you tend to go full throttle when you finally break loose. Nice girl?”

“I don’t know. All I know is I don’t want her. How do I get out of it?”

Minikin removed his left eye and began polishing it with his handkerchief—a trick he used when thinking. He seemed to find inspiration in the reflection.

“Take-her-own-part type of girl?”

I nodded. He’d nailed it.

“Know how much you earn?”

“She knows I live in this attic and cook my own meals,” I said.

Minikin looked around. “Must be fond of you.”

“She thinks I’m clever,” I explained. “That I’ll make something of myself.”

“And she’s willing to wait?”

I nodded.

“Then let her wait,” Minikin said, replacing his eye. “There’s plenty of time.”

“But she’s a barmaid,” I argued. “She’ll expect walks, Sundays out, visits to her friends. I can’t do that. Besides, she’s right—I *do* want to get ahead. Then she’ll cling to me. It’s awful!”

“How did it happen?” Minikin asked.

“I don’t know. I didn’t realize I’d done it until it was over.”

“Any witnesses?”

“Half a dozen,” I groaned.

The door opened. Jarman walked in—no knocking, as usual. Instead of his usual loud greeting, he crossed quietly and shook my hand solemnly.

“Friend of yours?” he asked, nodding at Minikin.

I introduced them.

“Proud to meet you,” Jarman said.

“Glad to hear it,” Minikin replied. “You don’t look like you’ve got much else to be proud of.”

“Don’t mind him,” I said. “He was born like that.”

“Wonderful gift,” Jarman said. “D’ye know what I’d do if I had it? Hire myself out to break up evening parties. Ever thought of it?”

Minikin said he’d consider it.

“You could make your fortune touring the suburbs,” Jarman assured him. “Pity you weren’t here last night. Might’ve saved our young friend a world of trouble. Has he told you the news?”

I said I’d already filled Minikin in.

“Now you’ve got a steady eye,” Jarman said, staring intently at Minikin’s glass orb. “How d’you think he’s looking?”

“As well as can be expected,” Minikin replied.

“Does he know the circumstances? Seen the girl?”

“No,” I said.

“Then he doesn’t know the worst,” Jarman said. “One hundred and sixty pounds of her, and still growing! Bit of a load for him, ain’t it?”

“Some folks do have luck,” Minikin remarked.

Jarman leaned forward, studying Minikin. “Nice head you’ve got there. All your own? No offence,” he added quickly. “Just thinking there’s room for a lot of sense in it.”

“So what would you advise?” Jarman asked. “Poison? Or Waterloo Bridge and a brick?”

“I suppose we’re actually engaged?” I interrupted.

“Not a shadow of doubt,” Jarman said cheerfully. “From her side, anyway.”

“I’ll tell her I was drunk,” I said.

“And how will you prove it?” Jarman asked. “You think saying you loved her proves it? It would to anyone else—but not to her. She’ll just see it as proof you meant it. Besides, if every girl gave up her catch because the fellow wasn’t all there at the time—what then?”

He turned to Minikin.

“To which,” Minikin said, “girls might as well shut up shop.”

Jarman, now in better spirits, pulled his chair closer and launched into a private discussion—leaving me out entirely.

“You see,” he said, “this isn’t ordinary. This boy’s going to be Poet Laureate. When the Prince of Wales invites him to dinner at Marlborough House, he won’t want to show up attached to a woman who carries her soup spoon in a bag and doesn’t know which end to use.”

“Makes a difference,” Minikin agreed.

“What we need,” Jarman said, “is to get him out of it. And I’ll be blessed if I see how.”

“She likes him?” Minikin asked.

“She fancies she was meant to be a lady,” Jarman said. “And you can’t argue with that. She hasn’t got the brains to see the mistake.”

“No point talking straight to her,” Minikin suggested. “Telling her it can’t be?”

“That’s the problem,” Jarman said. “It *can* be. This chap—well, he doesn’t know enough to come in out of the rain. He’s exactly the type this happens to.”

“But he doesn’t want her,” Minikin urged.

“Yes, to us,” Jarman said. “But let her come along, cry a bit, say he’s breaking her heart, appeal to his honor—and what do you think will happen?”

Minikin sighed. “Difficult.”

“If it were you or me,” Jarman said, “we’d just tell her to vanish and wait till we send for her. But with him—it’s different.”

“He’s a bit soft,” Minikin admitted.

“It’s not his fault,” Jarman said. “It’s how he was raised. He thinks women are like the ones in plays—saying ‘Unhand me!’ ‘Let me pass!’ Maybe some are. But not this one.”

“How did it happen?” Minikin asked.

“How does it happen nine times out of ten?” Jarman replied. “He’s a bit fuzzy. She’s wide awake. He gets sentimental. And—well, you know.”

“Artful things, girls,” Minikin commented.

“Can’t blame ’em,” Jarman said generously. “It’s their job. They’ve got to dispose of themselves somehow. Oughtn’t to be binding without a written order dated the next morning. That’d fix it.”

“Couldn’t prove a prior engagement?” Minikin suggested.

“She’d want to see the girl first,” Jarman said. “Only natural.”

“Couldn’t get another girl?” Minikin pressed.

“Who could you trust?” Jarman asked. “Besides, no time. She’s letting him rest today. Tomorrow evening, she’ll be on him.”

“Only thing left,” Minikin said, “is for him to run.”

“Not a bad idea,” Jarman mused. “But where would he run?”

“Not far,” Minikin said.

“She’d follow,” Jarman said. “She can take care of herself. Don’t underestimate her.”

“He could change his name,” Minikin suggested.

“How would he get a place to stay?” Jarman asked. “No references. No character.”

“I’ve got it!” Jarman exclaimed, jumping up. “The stage!”

“Can he act?” Minikin asked.

“Can do anything,” Jarman said, “that doesn’t require too much sense. That’s his sanctuary—the stage. No questions. No background. Lord, why didn’t I think of it sooner?”

“Needs a bit of work,” Minikin said.

“Depends where you want to go,” Jarman replied. He turned to me. “Can you sing?”

I said I could a little—never in public.

“Sing something,” Jarman demanded. “Now. Wait!”

He rushed out, knocked on Rosina’s door, returned.

“All clear,” he said. “She’s not in yet. Sing for your life.”

I sang “Sally in Our Alley”—not with much passion. Every time I mentioned Sally, I saw Rosina’s enormous form and features, which dulled the emotion. But Jarman wasn’t impressed—just satisfied.

“It’s not a grand opera voice,” he said, “but it’ll do for a chorus where economy matters. Now, go tomorrow to O’Kelly. Tell him everything. He’s decent. He’ll help you. Lucky timing—there are shows coming up. If Fate isn’t against you, you’ll lose your amorita—whatever that is—and never find her again.”

I wasn’t hopeful that evening. But I thanked them both and promised to think it over.

They left. Jarman paused at the door.

“Consols are down this week,” he whispered. “If you need cash, don’t sell out. I can lend you a few pounds. Suck a couple of lemons—you’ll be fine by morning. Goodbye.”

I followed his advice. The lemons worked. I went to the office. Lott & Co., pleased I’d agree to a two-shilling pay cut, overlooked the incident.

I’d planned to visit O’Kelly that night. I’d sent a note. But I received a reply: he’d be out. Could I come Friday instead?

Disappointed, I returned home, feeling worse than ever. Jarman’s plan, which had seemed promising earlier, now looked impossible. Passing the empty parlor, I felt a chill. It reminded me of a friend I’d grown attached to—despite disapproving of him.

Unable to work, loneliness crept back. I sat in darkness, listening to my neighbor’s pen scratch. The sound always made me feel depressed—but tonight, it felt heavier than usual.

Wasn’t the sympathy of Lady Hortensia—however self-serving—better than nothing? At least someone cared whether I lived or died.

That’s when I heard a tap at the door.

In the dim hall stood the slavey, holding a note.

I took it, lit a candle. The envelope was pink, scented. Addressed to “Paul Kelver, Esquire.” I opened it:

“Dr mr. Paul—
I herd as how you was took hill hafter the party. I feer you are not strong. You must not work so hard or you will be hill and then I shall be very cros with you. I hop you are well now. If so I am going for a wark and you may come with me if you are good. With much love. From your affechonat ROSIE.”

Despite the spelling, a strange warmth spread through me. My first love letter. Tears blurred my vision. Through the mist, I saw Rosina—pasty, yes, but glowing. Her arms stretched toward me, inviting.

Driven by sudden impulse, I dressed quickly—hands trembling. I descended the stairs, knocked nervously at the second-floor back door.

“Who’s that?” came Rosina’s sharp voice.

“It’s I—Paul.”

“Oh, wait a minute, dear.” Her tone softened.

Footsteps. Rustling. A drawer slamming. Silence. Then:

“You can come in now, dear.”

I entered. The room was small, messy, smoky. But all I saw was Rosina, pinning her hat atop her straw-colored hair.

And suddenly, my feelings reversed. While waiting, I’d wanted to burst in. Now, I’d have run away.

She wasn’t ugly. But the aura around her—coarse, common—repelled me. The same finicky instinct that made me recoil from a waitress with dirty nails now made me focus on her rough hands, her warts.

“You’re a very naughty boy,” she said, finishing her hat. “Why didn’t you come see me at dinner-time? I’ve a great mind not to kiss you.”

Her face was powdered hastily. Her arms were hidden under ill-fitting sleeves. I wished she’d been stronger in her threat. Instead, she offered her flowery cheek. I kissed it—gingerly. It tasted like the thin dough-cakes sold by the school porter’s wife—four a penny, popular for their cheap satisfaction.

At the kitchen stairs, she called down.

“Oh, me and Mr. Kelver are going out for a walk, Mrs. Peedles. I shan’t want supper. Good night.”

“Oh, good night, my dear,” Mrs. Peedles replied. “Hope you enjoy yourselves. Is Mr. Kelver there?”

“He’s round the corner,” Rosina whispered. A snigger followed.

“He’s a bit shy, ain’t he?” Mrs. Peedles whispered.

“I’ve had enough of the other sort,” Rosina replied.

“Ah, well,” Mrs. Peedles said. “It’s the shy ones that come out strongest. That’s been my experience.”

“He’ll do all right. So long.”

Rosina buttoned a torn glove and rejoined me.

“Never had a sweetheart before?” she asked as we stepped onto Blackfriars Road.

I admitted it was my first.

“I can’t bear flirty men,” she said. “That’s why I liked you. You were quiet.”

I wished I’d been louder.

“Anyone could see you were a gentleman,” she continued. “Hundreds of offers I’ve had. But I always told them, ‘I like you as a friend, but I’ll only marry a gentleman.’ Don’t you think I was right?”

I murmured it was only what I’d expect.

“You may take my harm, if you like,” she suggested as we crossed St. George’s Circus.

We linked arms and walked along Kennington Park Road.

Luckily, I didn’t have to talk much. Rosina supplied all the conversation—and all of it was about herself.

She’d always dreamed of gentility. Her mother’s side was connected to “the highest in the land.” But her “Mar” had married a waiter—what did I think? I wouldn’t guess.

She shuddered at the memory, nearly getting hit by a tramcar.

Mr. and Mrs. Sellars hadn’t gotten along. Could you blame them? Mrs. Sellars had an uncle on the Stock Exchange. Mr. Sellars had one on Peckham Rye. His main occupation: “three shies a penny.” Mrs. Sellars was now dead—thank goodness. And if nothing else came of it, her death had taught Rosina to avoid marrying beneath her.

With my help, she’d restore the family to its rightful place.

“It used to be a joke,” she said. “Even when I was a child. I couldn’t stand anything low. Why, one day when I was seven—what do you think happened?”

I confessed I had no idea.

“Well,” she said, “it’ll just show you. Uncle Joseph—that was father’s uncle—”

And so she went on, telling tales of her noble lineage, her lofty aspirations, her determination to rise above the riff-raff.

And I walked beside her, silent, thinking: *This is the woman I’m supposed to marry?*

But I didn’t say it. Because, deep down, I knew I’d already made my choice.

“Don’t be nervous,” said the O’Kelly, “and don’t try to do too much. You’ve got a decent voice—just not loud enough. Stay calm and open your mouth.”

It was eleven o’clock in the morning. We stood at the entrance of a narrow courtyard leading to the stage door. For two weeks, the O’Kelly had been drilling me in vocal gymnastics, which, frankly, felt more like torture than artistry. It wasn’t easy on either of us—but especially not on him. Mrs. O’Kelly, a wiry, sharp-faced woman whom I’d glimpsed once or twice while loitering around Belsize Square waiting for her husband’s signal, was a serious-minded soul with a deep-seated disdain for anything musical that didn’t come from a hymnbook. In a noble attempt to save her husband from one of his many sins, she’d banished the piano and replaced it with an American organ so mournful in tone it could bring tears to a tombstone. With this instrument—a musical equivalent of a haunted house—we were expected to rehearse *Sally in Our Alley*. The O’Kelly, bless him, was a musical genius trapped in a machine designed to produce funeral dirges. He managed to coax something resembling melody out of it, but the result was less cheerful ballad and more existential crisis in song form. My rendition, naturally, came out sounding like a man who’d just lost his fiancée, his dog, and his last hope of happiness all in one afternoon. It became, as they say now, a “problem ballad.” One couldn’t help wondering if the young man’s optimism about marriage was misplaced—wasn’t there a certain melancholy beneath his surface cheer? And Sally, charming though she might be, seemed awfully shallow—surely no match for such a soulful fellow?

“Lighter! Lighter!” the O’Kelly would snap. “Not so soulful!”

And then, the solemn notes would grind their way out of the groaning organ, each one heavier than the last.

Once, we nearly got caught. Mrs. O’Kelly returned from a district visitors’ committee meeting earlier than expected. I was hastily shoved into a small conservatory off the first-floor landing, where I crouched behind potted ferns like a criminal in a Victorian melodrama, trembling as I listened to her interrogation.

“William,” she said, “do not prevaricate. It was not a hymn.”

“Me dear,” he replied, “so much depends upon the time. Let me give ye an example.”

“William, pray do not play tricks with sacred melodies. If you have no respect for religion, please remember that I have. Besides, why should you be playing hymns at ten o’clock in the morning? It is not like you, William, and I do not credit your explanation. And you were singing. I distinctly heard the word 'Sally' as I opened the door.”

“Salvation, me dear,” corrected the O’Kelly.

“Your enunciation, William, is not usually so much at fault.”

“A little hoarseness, me dear,” he offered.

“Your voice did not sound hoarse. Perhaps it will be better if we do not pursue the subject further.”

The O’Kelly agreed.

“A lady a little difficult to get on with when ye're feeling well and strong,” he later explained. “But if ye happen to be ill, one of the kindest, most devoted women in the world. When I was down with typhoid three years ago, a tenderer nurse no man could have had. I shall never forget it. And she would be again tomorrow, if there was anything serious wrong with me.”

I murmured the famous line: “A woman of a thousand parts…”

“Mrs. O’Kelly to a T,” he nodded. “I sometimes wonder if Lady Scott may not have been the same sort of woman.”

“The unfortunate part,” he continued, “is that I’m such a healthy beggar. It doesn’t give her a chance. If I were only a chronic invalid, now—there’s nothing that woman wouldn’t do to make me happy. As it is—” He struck a chord. We resumed our studies.

Back to the stage door.

“Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one o’clock,” said the O’Kelly, shaking my hand. “If ye don’t get on here, we’ll try something else; but I’ve spoken to Hodgson, and I think ye will. Good luck to ye!”

He walked off, and I followed my own path. Just behind the door, a glass box held a tiny, round-eyed man with a curved nose, looking like an angry bird in captivity. He peered at me.

“What’s your business?” he demanded.

I showed him my letter of appointment.

“Up the passage, across the stage, along the corridor, first floor, second door on the right,” he instructed in one breath, then snapped the window shut like a trapdoor.

I obeyed. To my surprise, I wasn’t nervous at all. This was supposed to be my first time behind the scenes—the promised land of glamour and mystery—and yet I felt… oddly indifferent. Like someone who’d just discovered the magic trick.

I remembered my father asking a young soldier returning from the Crimea what it felt like to charge into battle.

“Well,” the lad said, “I was worrying all the time about leaving the beer tap running in the canteen. Couldn’t stop thinking about it.”

That summed up my feelings perfectly.

I reached the stage without incident. Pausing, I looked around. The illusion shattered instantly. No grandeur, no mystique—just tawdry curtains, cheap props, and a stage cluttered with the kind of nonsense you’d expect in a child’s toy theatre. The whole thing looked like a grown-up’s dollhouse, complete with painted walls and cardboard scenery. The Theatre, I realized, was a place of shreds and patches—of fantasy built on flimsy foundations. Drama could inspire, elevate, teach—yes, I believed that. But so could dressing dolls, opening their houses, and putting them to bed in rooms that swallowed them whole. Toys! What wise child despises them? Art, fiction, music—they’re all training wheels for adulthood. We’re all just kids learning how to act grown-up.

Beyond the stage, I got lost in a maze of corridors, but voices led me to a large room furnished with worn settees and too many chairs. Twenty to thirty people sat scattered about—men and women of every age and size, all sharing the same unmistakable air of theatricality. The men mostly had hair on their heads but none on their faces. The women—every single one—had complexions like fresh cream and eyes that sparkled with artificial life. Conversations hummed in hushed, penetrating tones, mostly about “him” and “her.” Everyone called each other “My dear,” “Old chappie,” “My love”—Christian names only when absolutely necessary. I hesitated at the door, thinking I’d wandered into a family reunion. But since no one blinked, I took a seat next to a tiny, boyish-looking gentleman and asked if this was where applicants for the chorus waited.

He stared at me with enormous, fishy eyes. So long did he stare that a large gentleman nearby, having overheard, stepped in.

“Yes,” he said, “this is the place. But from what I know of Butterworth, we’ll all be waiting here longer than any gentleman should keep ladies and gentlemen waiting for no reason at all.”

“I think it exceedingly bad form,” said the fishy-eyed man in a deep contralto, “for any gentleman to reply to a remark addressed to another gentleman.”

“I beg your pardon,” said the big man. “I thought you were asleep.”

“I think it very ill manners,” the small man continued, “to tell another gentleman, who happens to be wide awake, that you thought he was asleep.”

“Sir,” the big man replied, adopting a Johnsonian pose with the aid of a large umbrella, “I decline to alter my manners to suit your taste.”

“If you are satisfied with them,” the small man countered, “I cannot help it. But I think you are making a mistake.”

“Does anybody know what the opera is about?” asked a bright little woman at the far end.

“Does anybody ever know what a comic opera is about?” asked another, older woman.

“I once asked the author,” said a weary man in the corner. “His reply: 'Well, if you'd asked me at the beginning of rehearsals, I might’ve known. But damned if I do now!'”

“It wouldn’t surprise me,” said a handsome man in a velvet coat, “if there’s a drinking chorus for male voices somewhere.”

“Possibly,” added a thin woman with golden hair, “the heroine will confide her love troubles to us—something to interest and excite us.”

Then the door opened, and a name was called. An elderly lady rose and left.

“Poor old Gertie!” sighed the golden-haired woman. “I’m told she really had a voice once.”

“When poor young Bond first came to London,” said the big man beside me, “he applied to Lord Barrymore’s ‘tiger,’ Alexander Lee—I mean, of course, who was running the Strand Theatre—asking for a spot in the chorus. Lee heard him sing two lines, jumped up, and said, 'Thanks, that’ll do. Good morning.' Bond knew he had a good voice, so he asked what was wrong. 'What's wrong?' Lee shouted. 'Do you think I hire a chorus to show up my principals?'”

“Having regard to the company present,” remarked the fishy-eyed man, “I consider that anecdote distinctly lacking in tact.”

The others murmured agreement.

For the next half hour, the door kept opening and closing, swallowing one applicant after another—chorus men and women alike. Conversation grew quieter, tension rising. Then:

“Mr. Horace Moncrieff!”

I glanced around, wondering who this Mr. Moncrieff could be. The door opened wider. A pale-faced young man with a drooping moustache stuck his head in.

“Moncrieff?” he repeated impatiently.

And then it hit me: *I* was Mr. Horace Moncrieff.

“So glad you’ve found yourself,” he said. “Please don’t lose yourself again. We’re rather pressed for time.”

I followed him through a deserted refreshment bar—about as lively as a graveyard—and into a dim room. A gloomy-looking man sat at the piano. Beside him stood a tall, handsome man reading letters. A burly man tried hard to look amused while a sharp-eyed, restless man talked nonstop—never looking at anyone, always at something else.

“Moncrieff?” asked the tall man—Mr. Hodgson, the manager—without lifting his eyes.

The pale-faced man answered for me.

“Fire away,” said Hodgson.

“What is it?” asked the pianist wearily.

“'Sally in Our Alley,'” I replied.

“What are you?” Hodgson interrupted. He hadn’t looked at me once.

“A tenor,” I said. “Not a full tenor,” I added, remembering the O’Kelly’s advice.

“Utterly impossible to fill a tenor,” said the restless-eyed man, looking at me and speaking to the pianist. “Ever tried?”

Everyone laughed—except the pianist, whose laugh was short, sharp, mechanical, devoid of joy. The restless-eyed man, I learned, was the lead low comedian.

“Go on,” said the pianist, starting the accompaniment.

“Tell me when he’s going to begin,” Hodgson muttered after the first verse.

“He has a fair voice,” said the pianist. “He’s evidently nervous.”

“There’s a prejudice among theatrical audiences,” Hodgson observed, “in favor of a voice they can hear. That’s all I’m trying to impress upon him.”

I sang the second verse—what I imagined was a trumpet blast. The burly man (the French libretto translator, or “author of the English version,” as he preferred) admitted he’d heard something. The comedian suggested a stage announcement requesting silence during my performance.

Fear crept over me. My voice, disappointed by its own effect, had retreated into my boots and refused to return.

“Your voice is fine—very good,” whispered the conductor. “They just want to hear the best you can do.”

At that, my voice raced up my legs and burst out of my mouth.

“Thirty shillings a week, half salary for rehearsals. If that’s acceptable, Mr. Catchpole will give you your agreement. If not, very much obliged. Good morning,” said Hodgson, still absorbed in his letters.

I went with the pale-faced man to a desk, signed the papers in seconds, and left. I tried to catch the eye of the melancholy pianist, but he seemed sunk in despair. The comedian, looking at the “author of the English version” and calling me “Boanerges,” wished me good morning. Everyone laughed. I was directed out by the pale-faced Mr. Catchpole and left.

The first call was Monday at two. The theatre buzzed with activity. The principals had just finished rehearsing and stood in a group. The chorus filled the center of the stage. I spotted Gertie, the golden-haired woman, the massive man, and the fishy-eyed young man—still together, as if magnetically drawn to each other, yet clearly disliking one another.

The fishy-eyed man was explaining the proper age for retired chorus singers to retire—for the sake of themselves and the public. The massive man countered with the age at which boys should be taught manners over their mother’s knee.

Hodgson stood near the footlights, still reading letters. The musical director, violin in hand, led the orchestra. The adapter and the stage manager—a Frenchman I pretended was English—sat deep in conversation under a gas jet. After a quarter-hour, the stage manager suddenly rang a bell furiously.

“Clear, please; all clear!” shouted a small boy with the air of a fox terrier. We retreated to the wings.

The comedian and leading lady joined Hodgson. We were sorted into couples by height. The stage manager, admiring the fishy-eyed man (barely five feet two), declared, “Ah! I have the very girl for you—a beauty!” He dashed into the group of women and returned with the largest specimen—a woman of magnificent proportions—who he handed over with the air of a virtuous uncle in a melodrama.

The massive man got a sharp-faced little woman who looked girlish from afar. I was paired with the golden-haired woman.

We formed a semi-circle. The orchestra began. I’d practiced my part with the O’Kelly—confident there. But the rest? Ill at ease.

“I’m afraid,” said the golden-haired woman, “I must ask you to put your arm around my waist. It’s very shocking, I know, but our salary depends on it. Do you think you could manage?”

I looked at her. A whimsical smile met mine, and my shyness vanished. I did as she asked.

The stage manager darted among us, adjusting positions—pushing back, pulling forward, herding us like sheep. He was a master shepherd.

“Very good, very good indeed,” Hodgson said at the end. “Let’s go over it again—in tune.”

“And we’ll make love,” added the stage manager. “Not like marionettes, but like real ladies and gentlemen—all alive.” He grabbed the nearest woman and demonstrated how a peasant behaves under passion: hands clasped over heart, head tilted at forty-five degrees, face radiant with adoration.

“If he expects,” the massive man muttered to an experienced young woman, “a performance of Romeo thrown in, I, for one, shall want an extra ten shillings a week.”

The stage manager dropped the woman and seized a man to show the ladies how a village maiden should receive affection: one shoulder higher, body waggling gently, roguish glint in the left eye.

“Oh, he’s new to it,” the young woman replied. “He’ll get over it.”

We started again. I focused on the fishy-eyed man, who followed every instruction with deadly seriousness. Soon, everyone was watching him—ignoring their own roles. Hodgson looked up. The orchestra played out of time. The author and leading lady exchanged glances. Only three weren’t enjoying it: the comedian, the stage manager, and the fishy-eyed man himself, who worked methodically.

A whispered conference between the comedian, Hodgson, and the stage manager ended with the music stopping. The fishy-eyed man was asked to explain.

“Only making love,” he said.

“You were playing the fool, sir,” the comedian retorted.

“That’s a very unkind remark,” the fishy-eyed man replied, hurt. “To make to a gentleman doing his best.”

Hodgson, still behind his letters, chuckled. “Poor fellow,” he murmured. “I suppose he can’t help it. Go on.”

“We’re not producing a pantomime,” the comedian urged.

“I want to give him a chance,” Hodgson whispered. “Only support of a widowed mother.”

The comedian relented. Hodgson’s correspondence became absorbing.

In Act Two, the chorus became soldiers and vivandières—each warrior with a personal bar. The stage manager demonstrated patriotism (slaps on the chest) vs. cheap cognac (slaps on the forehead). Later, we were conspirators—shown how to conspire with a tablecloth. Then a mob—led by the sentimental baritone. The stage manager ruffled his hair and explained mob behavior. The act ended with a fight. The stage manager, coatless, showed how to fight and die—rolling, dusty, painful.

Throughout, the fishy-eyed man remained the focus. Whether slapping his chest or patting his head, he was the soldier we wanted to see. Who led the mob mattered less than seeing *him* lead. How others fought mattered nothing—only seeing *him* fall.

Was his unconsciousness real or assumed? I couldn’t tell. Either way, it was perfect.

An earnest young man, perhaps overly eager to serve his employers, was the only criticism possible.

The comedian frowned. The stage manager despaired. But Hodgson and the author were sympathetic. Hodgson had invented five younger brothers and sisters for the man—utterly destitute without his earnings. To deprive such a son and brother of his livelihood was unthinkable.

The comedian dissociated himself from uncharitable thoughts—offering to contribute from his own pocket. His only concern: the success of the opera.

The author insisted the young man wouldn’t accept charity—he was sensitive.

Rehearsal continued. In the final act, I had to kiss the golden-haired woman.

“I’m very sorry,” she said, “but duty is duty. It has to be done.”

I followed instructions. She congratulated me afterward.

And so, I survived my first day in the theatre—not with glory, but with a quiet sense of accomplishment. The glamour was gone. But the absurdity? That, I realized, was the real show.

HOW ON A SWEET GREY MORNING THE FUTURE CAME TO PAUL—AND ALSO, BY ACCIDENT, TO HIS FRIEND DAN.

Over our supper Dan and I swapped life stories like two old conspirators comparing notes on how many times they’d been caught sneaking into the kitchen. There were startling similarities. Dan had left school earlier than me—by about a decade—and headed straight for Cambridge. But then, two years in, his father died of an old wound from the Indian Mutiny that never healed properly. So Dan’s college career ended abruptly, like a poorly timed curtain call.

“You wouldn’t have thought it would’ve hurt,” he said with a grin, “but honestly, it did. At Cambridge, I discovered I was born to be a scholar. Back at school? Learning was made as dull as wet bread. We were treated like tourists in a museum—rushed through the rooms, told to look at things we couldn’t see, and expected to say ‘I’ve seen it’ without actually understanding anything. But at college? I picked my own subjects, studied them my way. I *fed* on knowledge. Not stuffed with it like a Strasbourg goose who’s just been told he’s going to die.”

After returning to London, Dan took a job at a bank—his dad’s old friend was chairman. The pay was decent enough, and he had time to read. But the work was so boring it threatened to turn him into a statue. Then his mother passed away, and he decided to shake off the City dust like a dog after a swim. He joined a tiny “fit-up” theatre company—no name, no fame, just a lot of ambition and very little talent.

On stage, he played everything: Romeo, Paul Pry (a role that required more awkwardness than acting), painted scenery, and posted bills. The latter, he claimed, was one of the hardest jobs in the world. “You think you’re putting up a poster,” he said, “but the paste has a mind of its own. It’ll slap you right in the face and stick there like a bad decision.”

Eventually, tired of being on stage, he joined a circus. Became “Signor Ricardo, the Daring Bare-Back Rider”—a title he earned mostly by not falling off. Also one half of “The Brothers Roscius” in their legendary trapeze act—though the only thing truly legendary about it was the number of times they nearly fell. After that, he tried respectability again—waiter in Ostend, footman in a house where the butler once refused to serve tea because the kettle wasn’t boiling fast enough.

“Never know,” he mused. “Maybe I’ll become a society novelist. If so, I’ll have the inside scoop on aristocrats—more than most writers will ever dream of.”

He’d dabbled in other careers too: fiddling through Ireland, lecturing on Palestine in Scotland (with dissolving views that mostly dissolved into smoke), billiard-marker, schoolmaster. For the past three months, he’d been a journalist—dramatic and musical critic for a Sunday paper. I’d often dreamed of such a position myself.

“How’d you land it?” I asked.

“Pure luck,” he said. “One afternoon, walking down the Strand, wondering what to do next, I found myself broke and hungry. So I walked into the first newspaper office I saw, went upstairs, opened the first door without knocking, and stepped into a room full of books and chaos. It was getting dark. A young man—looked like he’d just escaped from a grammar school—was running around cursing because he’d spilled ink, lost matches, and broken the bell-pull. In the gloom, I assumed he was the office boy. So I lit the gas, found him another inkpot, and waited.”

Then the editor—this boyish-looking man in his mid-twenties—asked if I knew him. Before I could answer, he started ranting.

“People come in here like it’s a shelter from the rain,” he said. “Strangers! Men with articles on safety valves! People who just want to complain about something! Half my work is done on the stairs!”

I suggested he make strangers write their business on a slip of paper. He liked the idea.

“For the last three-quarters of an hour,” he said, “I’ve been trying to finish this column—and four times I’ve been interrupted.”

At that moment, someone knocked.

“I won’t see him!” he yelled. “No matter who it is. Send him away! Everyone away!”

I went to the door. An elderly gentleman tried to push past. I blocked him, closed the editorial door behind me, and handed him a piece of paper. “Please write your business here,” I said.

I stayed in the anteroom for half an hour, turning away about ten or twelve people. None seemed important—otherwise, I’d have heard about it later. The last was a tired-looking man smoking a cigarette.

“What’s your name?” I asked.

He stared. “Idiot.”

I stood firm. “Sorry, sir. No exceptions.”

“It’s awkward,” he said. “Can’t you make an exception for the sub-editor on press night?”

“Instructions are instructions,” I replied.

“Oh, all right,” he sighed. “Just tell me who’s going to the Royalty tonight. It’s seven o’clock.”

That gave me an idea. If the sub-editor didn’t know who was going to the theatre, maybe the dramatic critic post was open.

“Oh, that’s easy,” I said. “I’ll be there in time.”

He looked unimpressed. “Have you arranged with the Guv’nor?”

“I’m waiting to see him again,” I said. “It’ll be fine. Got the ticket?”

“Haven’t seen it,” he replied.

“About a column?” I suggested.

“Three-quarters,” he said, and left.

As soon as he was gone, I slipped downstairs and met a printer’s boy.

“What’s the sub-editor’s name?” I asked. “Tall guy, black moustache, looks exhausted.”

“Oh, you mean Penton,” the boy said.

“That’s him,” I said. “Couldn’t remember.”

I marched straight into the editor. He was still furious.

“What is it now?” he snapped.

“I need the ticket for the Royalty Theatre,” I said. “Penton says you’ve got it.”

“I don’t know where it is,” he growled.

I found it on his desk. “Who’s going?” he asked.

“I am,” I said. And I went.

They’ve never figured out I appointed myself. Penton thinks I’m a relative of the owner. Mrs. Wallace, the proprietor, thinks I’m Penton’s discovery—she trusts him implicitly. I get along with her perfectly. The paper isn’t doing great, and the salary’s low—but enough. Journalism suits me. I’ll probably stay.

“You’ve been a rolling stone,” I said.

He laughed. “From the stone’s point of view, why should it want moss? I’d rather stay free, able to move, see the world. But enough about me. What are *your* plans?”

My opera—thanks to the gods, or perhaps just the audience’s poor eyesight—had been dubbed “Goggles.” It was running through the winter. “Thirty shillings a week,” I said. “Full pay for matinees.”

“Two pounds,” Dan said. “With my three, we could set up house together. I’ve got an idea. Original. Want to hear it?”

I was already sold.

“There are four lovely rooms in Queen’s Square,” he said. “Charming: front sitting room, two bedrooms, kitchen. Last tenant was a Polish revolutionary. Went back to Russia three months ago—poor fellow—and now lives rent-free. The landlord’s Deleglise, an engraver. Odd old chap. Lives in the rest of the house. He told me I could have the rooms for whatever I wanted—thirty shillings a week. They’re worth three or four pounds under normal circumstances. But he insists we ‘do for’ ourselves. He hates petticoats—especially old ones. Has one servant, an old Frenchwoman who used to be his mother’s housekeeper. They do the chores together, mostly arguing about it. No other women allowed—not even a charwoman. But the house is beautiful—Georgian, Adams mantelpieces, stone staircase, oak paneling. Our part? Entire second floor. No pianos. No landlady. He’s a widower with a daughter—fourteen or so. What do you say? I’m a fair cook. Will you be house-and-parlour-maid?”

I needed no urging. A week later, we moved in. For nearly two years, we lived there—without female assistance, supervision, or even a single cry of “Why can’t men just do anything right?” Truth be told, Dan wasn’t bluffing. His cooking? Better plain food I’ve never tasted. Coffee, omelettes, devilled kidneys—excellent. But when he got ambitious—inspired by old Deleglise, who’d spent twenty years making cooking his hobby—he ventured into danger.

Pastry was his downfall. His hand was heavy, his temperament too serious. Lemon sponge cake, according to the book, was supposed to be light, airy, white as snow. Dan’s version? More like a miniature earthquake. Spongy? Yes. You had to use spoon and fork to pull it apart—it resisted gently, like a wounded animal. Manna-cake? Let’s just say if the Children of Israel had eaten this, their complaints about Egyptian fleshpots would’ve seemed reasonable. One slice—crusty outside, marshy inside—satisfied anyone completely. For parties, he tried soufflés—guests called them pudding, despite my kicks. Pancakes in winter? Early attempts were disasters. Tossing them was risky. I’d suggest using a knife and fork—less showy, safer. You avoid catching the pancake on your head, dropping it in the fire, or among the cinders. But Dan believed in “Thorough.” And hey, if you find coal or hair in your food, you can always remove it.

For twenty-three hours a day, Dan was the calmest man alive. But for the remaining hour? Volcano. I’d try to help—suggest why the custard was pimply, the mayonnaise smelled like hair oil. What did I get? Sneers, insults, abuse. If I didn’t leave quickly, I’d get hit with cold coffee grounds or spoiled tomatoes. His greatest enemy? The oven. Always the oven. It turned his snowy buns into red sandstone, macaroni cheese into Cambrian clay. And his language—oh, his language! If he’d kept it civil, we might’ve felt sorry for him. As it was, his curses made you almost side with the oven.

Our housemaid? I loved her. Not because she cleaned—because she *didn’t*. She was the kind who knew when to stop. Most maids overdo it—stirring dust like it’s a science experiment. But dust left alone is harmless. It settles. It becomes part of the furniture. Like a delicate bloom. Why keep waking it up? Crumbs, string, eggshells, match stubs—what better place than under the nearest mat? Sweep them up? Waste of time. They cling, you curse, you feel childish. Under the mat? They go willingly. Compromise is true statesmanship. One day, you’ll be glad you didn’t clean the floor. Then you can lift the mats and pretend you’ve been working hard.

Women fail at distinguishing essential from non-essential. Why wash a cup that’ll be dirty again in half an hour? If the cat cleans a plate perfectly, why take that pleasure from her? If a bed looks made and feels made—why disturb it? Needlework? I never mastered it. Dan could handle a thimble. Me? My second finger refused to cooperate. Had to be coaxed, isolated, pushed forward—then pricked badly by the needle, terrified, and useless afterward. I resorted to pushing the needle through with the table edge.

The opera ran far into the next year. When it ended, another began—one where “Goggles” was a lead. Even more successful. My salary? Thirty-five shillings, sometimes forty. I felt rich. I imagined becoming a great opera singer. Six hundred pounds a week? I could live on that. Then O’Kelly burst my bubble.

“Ye’d be wasting yer time,” he said. “I wouldn’t tell ye so if I weren’t sure.”

“I know it’s not powerful,” I admitted.

“Thin, almost,” O’Kelly added.

“Maybe comic opera?” I suggested. “People succeed there without much voice.”

“Sure, ye’re right,” he sighed. “And if ye were strikingly handsome…”

“One can do a lot with make-up,” I said.

O’Kelly shook his head. “It’s never quite the same. It depends on your acting.”

I dreamed of being a second Kean, replacing Macready. Why not combine it with literature? Fill Drury Lane at night, write novels in the morning, pen my own plays. Every day, I studied at the British Museum. Maybe I’d eventually enter Parliament—a Prime Minister with a deep knowledge of history. Why not? With Ollendorf, I learned French and German. Maybe diplomacy? An ambassadorship? A pleasant end to a brilliant career.

There was reason for optimism. Everything was going well. A story of mine had been accepted. I can’t recall the journal—now dead. Most of my early publications are gone. My contributions were their swan songs. Proofs came—I corrected them, returned them. But proofs aren’t facts. This had happened before. Once, I’d been lifted to the heavens—only to fall harder when the paper collapsed before my story appeared. (Ah, why delay? It might’ve saved them!) This time, I remembered the proverb. I kept quiet, slipping out each morning on publication day to buy the paper, scanning its columns with trembling hands.

For weeks, hope deferred. Then, one bright January day, walking down Harrow Road, I stopped suddenly. Stunned. Before a news vendor’s shop, a bill proclaimed: *“The Witch of Moel Sarbod: a legend of Mona, by Paul Kelver.”*

My legs trembled. I entered the shop. A grubby man in shirt-sleeves, surprised I wanted a copy, found one under the counter. I bought it. Walked back like a ghost. Sat on a bench in Paddington Green and read.

The hundred best books! I’ve read them all. Never one charmed me like this short story in that forgotten journal. It was sad. Sentimental. About a mighty king—names unpronounceable—who loved a princess named Purity. While hunting, he lost his way, slept near the Cave of the Waters, where a wicked sorceress cast a spell. He woke forgetting his duty, loving only the Witch of Moel Sarbod.

But Purity, wise and brave, sought a great magician. The only way to save the king? She must climb barefoot to the witch’s cave, kiss her on the mouth. Her purity would enter the witch, transforming her into a woman. But the witch’s spirit would enter Purity, turning her into a monster. Purity climbed, bleeding, kissed the witch. The witch became human, ruled wisely. Purity became a hideous witch, doomed to dwell on Moel Sarbod forever. Today, those who climb the mountain hear her sobbing—above the waterfall. Or maybe it’s a joyous song of love triumphant.

No writer, I’ve been told, is ever satisfied with his work. So I try to believe it. But I’m clearly not worth my salt. Friends once told me this first story was a weak, ill-shaped child—its English a mix of *The Pilgrim’s Progress* and *Old Moore’s Almanac*, its scenery a bizarre blend of Eden and Hell, its pathos cheap and overwrought. Maybe they were right. I’ve written worse since. But this—my first-born—my harbinger of dreams—I cannot judge. Touching the yellowed pages, I feel again that thrill. I’m young again. Life stretches before me—limitless, glorious. This child of my brain shall spread my name across the world. It shall live beyond me. Generations unborn shall read it and remember me. My thoughts, words, spirit—through it, I shall live again.

OF THE GLORY AND GOODNESS AND THE EVIL THAT GO TO THE MAKING OF LOVE.

During my time of struggle, I’d avoided old Hasluck like the plague. Not because he was a bad man—far from it—but because he was the kind of fellow who’d cheerfully offer you a job, a meal, and a lecture on how to “pull yourself together,” all in one breath. He was a jolly sort, full of hearty laughter and advice that always came with a side of unsolicited wisdom. I could have taken his help, sure—I might’ve even lived better than I did with Lott and Co., half-starving on cold beans and dreams. But something in me rebelled at the idea of accepting kindness from someone I knew. It wasn’t about money or dignity, though I’ll admit I didn’t care much for honesty either—Lott and Co.’s business dealings were about as ethical as a fox in a chicken coop. No, it was deeper than that. It had nothing to do with Barbara’s father, or even Barbara herself, really. To me, she wasn’t part of any family tree, not even a real one. She was more like a myth, a legend whispered in the dark corners of my mind. If I’d been forced to name her parents, I’d have said they were some ancient gods who’d abandoned their temple long ago, leaving only the echo of their love behind.

I’ve always preferred maps to asking directions. Give me a complicated route, a foreign railway timetable, anything that lets me figure things out myself—even if I end up lost in a swamp. There’s a certain pride in getting lost by design, rather than being led straight into a trap by someone who claims to know the way. Maybe I’m descended from a grumpy cave-dweller who refused to dance in tribal rituals, preferring to make his own tools and mutter curses under his breath while everyone else sang songs of unity. That’s probably why I never felt right around people who were too nice. They made me nervous.

But now? Now I had a foothold. I wasn’t begging anymore. So I wrote to old Hasluck—not a letter of appeal, but of simple courtesy. And lo and behold, almost before the ink dried, he sent back a note so warm and cheerful it made me feel like I’d just won a lottery. He told me Barbara had returned from abroad—“just yesterday!”—and added, with the confidence of a man who knows exactly what’s going on in other people’s lives, that she’d be “delighted” to see me. Naturally, he invited me over. Of course he did. The door was wide open, metaphorically speaking, and probably literally too.

No one ever knew about my boyhood crush on Barbara—not even my mother, who adored her, though she’d talk about her faults like she was reviewing a new play. “She’s got a temper,” she’d say, “and she doesn’t wear her hair properly.” And I’d cringe inside, like someone watching a priest dissect a holy relic. I used to walk around Stamford Hill at night, circling that enormous red-brick house like a ghost haunting its own past. Each lap brought me closer to peace, yet farther from reality. I’d return to the noisy streets feeling purified, as if I’d just left a cathedral and stepped back into the world.

During those four years she was away, my feelings didn’t fade—they grew. She became less a person and more an ideal. A vision crafted entirely from memory and longing. I didn’t want to see her again; I wanted to *remember* her. To keep her perfect, untouched by time. In truth, I feared seeing her as a woman—someone who might sneeze, or complain about a sore toe, or leave her curlers in her hair after a bath. That would ruin everything. I wouldn’t have minded worshipping her from afar. In fact, I preferred it. To live with her, eat beside her, watch her yawn during dinner—that would be torture. I didn’t want to drag her down into the messy business of daily life. I was glad I couldn’t.

And now she was older. A woman. I still wasn’t quite a man. She’d marry. I didn’t mind. My feelings weren’t romantic—they were spiritual. She wasn’t a person to me; she was a symbol. A representation of love itself, stripped bare and placed on a pedestal. Like how we build statues of saints and then get angry when they don’t behave like saints. We forget: we made them. We gave them form. We’re the ones who expect miracles.

I waited a week after receiving Hasluck’s letter before visiting. It was late afternoon in early summer. Hasluck was still in the City. Mrs. Hasluck was visiting someone. Miss Hasluck—Barbara—was in the garden. I told the footman not to bother—he could go fetch tea or whatever. I’d find her myself. I knew exactly where she’d be. Her favorite spot: a sunny corner surrounded by yew hedges shaped like birds and animals, Dutch-style. She was there, reading, bent over her book. And when I saw her, the same shock hit me—the same moment I’d felt as a boy when she first appeared in my father’s office, silhouetted against the dusty books: *All the fairy tales had come true.* Only this time, she wasn’t a princess. She was a queen.

Taller. More dignified. More beautiful. I stood in the shadow of the hedges, watching her profile, the curve of her neck, the elegance of her hand resting on the lilac fabric of her dress. I didn’t speak. I didn’t move. I could’ve stayed there forever.

Then she turned. Saw me. Smiled faintly. Held out her hand.

I knelt. Took it. Kissed it. It wasn’t planned. It just happened. Like breathing.

Her smile widened slightly, but there was a hint of mockery beneath it. Yet she seemed pleased. Not offended. Not even surprised.

“So you’re still in love with me?” she asked. “I wondered.”

“Did you know?”

“I should’ve been blind not to.”

“But I was only a boy.”

“You weren’t the usual type of boy. You won’t be the usual type of man.”

“You don’t mind me loving you?”

“I can’t help it. Neither can you.”

She sat on a stone bench facing a sundial, hands behind her head, laughing softly.

“I’ll always love you,” I said. “But it’s a strange kind of love. I don’t understand it.”

“Tell me,” she said, still smiling. “Describe it.”

I leaned against the sundial column. The sun was setting, casting long shadows across the grass, lighting her face in golden warmth.

“I wish you were a queen,” I said. “An ancient queen. And I could serve you. Be near you. Do your bidding. Your love would ruin everything. I wouldn’t ask for it. I wouldn’t want it. Just to look at you. To touch your hand once in a while. To kiss the glove you dropped, the shoe you kicked off. To know you knew I loved you. That you owned me. That I lived or died by your whim.”

She laughed lightly. “I’d prefer to be the queen. The priestess role isn’t for me. Temples are too cold.”

A shiver passed through her. She gestured toward her feet. “That’s how you shall love me, Paul. Adoring me. Worshipping me—blindly. I’ll be your queen. And I’ll treat you—however I please. Everything I think, everything I do, you’ll tell me is right. The queen can do no wrong.”

She took my face in her hands, looked deeply into my eyes. “You understand, Paul? The queen can do no wrong—never, never.”

“I understand,” I whispered. “My queen can do no wrong.”

She laughed and let go.

“Now you may sit beside me. This honor will have to last you a long time. Tell me what you’ve done. Maybe it’ll amuse me. Then I’ll tell you what I’ve done—and you’ll say it was right.”

I told her everything—briefly, honestly. Even the Lady *Ortensia*, which I was ashamed of. She frowned slightly.

“You must never do anything again,” she said sharply, “that makes me feel ashamed of you. Or I’ll banish you forever. I must be proud of you. Dishonoring yourself dishonors me. I’m angry with you, Paul. Don’t make me angry again.”

And so it was settled. Though my love for her—exactly as she wanted it—didn’t always save me from the whispers of my darker self, it did give me strength. The desire to be worthy of her, to honor her, shaped my life. Love, in its purest form, does that.

The morning light fades. The magic veil tears. We see clearly. My love was a woman. She is dead. They dressed her corpse in rags, but love sees through lies. God knows I loved her purely. Only false love loves falsehood. Beneath the dirt, she sleeps fair.

When I finished, she said, “Now I’ll tell you mine.”

“I’m getting married soon,” she said. “I’ll be a Countess. Countess Huescar—I’ll teach you how to say it. I’ll have a real castle in Spain.”

“You’re not scared?” I asked.

“No,” she laughed. “We won’t live there. It’s a ruined mess, high in the mountains. He hates it even more than I do. Paris and London will be my courts. You’ll see me often. You’ll meet the great world, Paul. The world I mean to conquer.”

“Is he rich?”

“As poor as a Spanish nobleman,” she said. “The money’s mine—or Dad’s. He gives me title, position. I don’t love him. He’s handsome. Don’t look so solemn. Queens don’t make love matches. They make alliances. I’ve done well. Congratulate me. Say I’ve acted rightly.”

“Does he love you?”

“He says he does,” she laughed. “How uncourtier-like of you! Do you think any man could see me and not fall in love?”

She stood up abruptly. “I don’t want his love. It would bore me. Women hate love they can’t return. Not like yours, little Paul. That’s sweet incense. I want that. Give it to me. But the love of a husband? Horrible!”

For a moment, I saw her not as a goddess, but as a child needing protection.

“But have you thought—”

“Yes, yes,” she cut in. “I’ve thought until I can’t think anymore. There must be sacrifice. As little as possible. He doesn’t love me. He’s marrying me for my money. I know that. And I’m glad. You don’t know me, Paul. I need rank. Position. What am I? The daughter of a butcher who became rich? As Princess Huescar, society forgets. As Mrs. Jones or Brown, it remembers. I’m vain. Ambitious. I have my father’s blood. He spent his life building wealth. I must build something else.”

“But you don’t have to be Mrs. Nobody,” I said. “Why not wait? Someone might come along who can give you both status and love.”

“He won’t come,” she said. “Because he already has. Hush, Paul. The queen can do no wrong.”

“Who is he?”

“You’ll know,” she said. “I want you to know. Then you’ll say I’ve done right. That you still respect me. That you honor me.”

He couldn’t help me. As his wife, I’d be less than I am now—a rich nobody giving dinners to other rich nobodies, surrounded by retired tradesmen and opera singers. Is that the court you want your queen to reign in?

“Is he so ordinary?” I asked.

“He’s not ordinary,” she said. “It’s I who am ordinary. The things I want are beneath him. He’ll never care to get them.”

“Not even for love of you?”

“I wouldn’t have him do it, even if he would. He’s great. Great in a way I can’t understand. He’s not suited to these times. In old days, I’d have married him. I’d have known he’d rise by strength alone. But now men don’t climb. They crawl. He couldn’t crawl. I’ve done right, Paul.”

“What does he say?”

“Shall I tell you?” She laughed bitterly. “‘You’re half a woman and half a fool. You’ll follow your folly. But make sure your woman doesn’t make a fool of herself.’”

I recognized the voice instantly.

“Hal,” I said.

“So you never guessed,” she said. “I thought it would be sweet to cry it out. That it wouldn’t matter. That everyone who knew me could read it in my eyes.”

“But he never paid you much attention,” I said.

She laughed. “What did I ever do for you that was more than snubbing? We boys and girls—we’re not so different. We love our masters. But don’t think so poorly of me. I was just a child to him then. But we were locked together in Paris during the siege. Have you heard? He did take notice of me, Paul. I assure you.”

Years later, on a winter night in the Julier Pass, I received a pencilled note:

“Come up and see me. I’m a prisoner here till the weather breaks. Hal.”

I barely recognized him. A shadow of the boy I once knew. His body, once magnificent, was broken by war and excess. He sat propped up on pillows in a dim, vaulted room that used to be a chapel, lit by a cheap lamp, heated by a German oven that smelled like hell.

“I saw you from the window,” he said. “Twice a day, the sledges come in. That’s my only excitement. I broke down coming across the pass. Stuck in a drift for eighteen hours. Nearly killed my last lung. And no books. By God, lad, I was glad to see your frozen face.”

He gripped my hand. “Sit down. Let me hear my own language again. You were always a good listener. Eight years, I haven’t spoken English. Can you stand this room? The windows should be open, but what does it matter? Might as well get used to the heat before I die.”

I sat close. Between coughing fits, he talked—boisterously, wildly, laughing at my protests, choking on his own words.

Then: “What is she doing? Do you ever see her?”

“She’s playing in—” I named a comic opera. “No, I haven’t seen her lately.”

He laid his hand on mine. “What a pity you and I couldn’t have rolled into one. You, the saint. Me, the satyr. Together, we’d have made her perfect lover.”

I remembered nights listening to my parents argue through the wall. I was always caught between them, helpless.

“Tell me,” I said. “I loved her, knowing her. I wasn’t blind. Whose fault was it? Yours or hers?”

He laughed. “Whose fault, Paul? God made us.”

Thinking of her face, I hated his laugh. Then I saw the pain in his eyes. Pity replaced anger.

“You’ve been on the stage,” he said. “You’ve heard the saying: ‘Ah, well, the curtain must come down, however badly things are going.’ It’s only a play, Paul. We don’t choose our parts. I didn’t even know I was the villain until I heard the booing. I thought I was the hero—noble, sacrificing myself for her happiness. She would’ve married me if I’d bothered enough.”

I opened my mouth, but he went on.

“Ah, yes. It might have been better. Easy to say, not knowing. It might have been worse. Probably the same. All roads lead to the end. I was always a fatalist, Paul. We tried both ways. She loved me. But she loved the world too. I thought she loved it more. So I kissed her forehead, mumbled a prayer, and walked away. She sobbed. First act ended. Wasn’t I the hero? I thought so. Slapped myself on the back. Then—well, you know what happened. She was finer clay than she thought. Love is woman’s kingdom, not the world. Even then, I cared more for her than myself. I could’ve borne my share—had I not seen her suffering. Shamed. Degraded. So we dared to be ourselves. Lost the world for love. Wasn’t it brave? Were we not hero and heroine? They printed the playbill wrong. I was the hero. But the printer made a mistake. So instead of applause, you booed. How could you know?”

“But that wasn’t the end,” I said. “If the curtain had fallen then, I could’ve forgiven you.”

He grinned. “That fatal last act. Even yours don’t always come right, the critics say.”

The grin faded. “We may never see each other again, Paul. Don’t think too badly of me. I realized I’d made a second mistake. Or thought I had. She wasn’t happier with me than with him. Our longings aren’t one. Life’s hard. What can we do but toss a coin? And if it lands heads, we wish it was tails. If tails, we mourn the heads we lost. Love isn’t all a woman’s life. Nor a man’s. He didn’t file for divorce. Smart of him. We were shunned. Ignored. Some women wouldn’t care. But she was used to being courted. She pretended to be happy. Tried to laugh. I watched her grow listless. Angry with her. With myself. Our bond was passion. Real enough. ‘Grand,’ I suppose. Good for a summer in a cave. But not for marriage. We fought. Screamed. Became ugly. Most of us look better from a distance. The sordid side of life became important. I wasn’t rich. Compared to her past, I was poor. The sight of our twelve-pound-a-year cook’s food made her lose her appetite. Love doesn’t change taste. You can’t learn to like cheap claret after champagne.

“She fell ill. I felt I’d dragged her from fertile ground only to watch her die. Then he came. At the perfect moment. I admire him. Most men take revenge clumsily. He was neat. Patient. I’m not ashamed of falling into his trap. It was well baited. Maybe I despised him for seeming to accept the blow quietly. But what did he care for me? He cared only for her. He knew her better than I. Knew she’d tire—not of love, but of the cottage. Long for what she’d lost. Fool! Cuckold! What did it matter that the world would laugh? Love like his made fools of men. Would I not give her back to him?”

And so it ended. Not with glory, not with tragedy, but with quiet resignation. The queen had chosen her throne. The prince had chosen his exile. And I? I remained the loyal subject, forever kneeling in the shadow of the yews.

Let’s be honest—there are few things more charming than a Sunday supper hosted by old Deleglise. Picture this: the man himself, dressed from head to toe in linen so crisp it could cut glass, bustling about his kitchen like a conductor leading an orchestra of pots and pans. His face, flushed with culinary passion, glows as he stands before a stove that shines like a jewel under the afternoon sun. Behind him, guests sit hushed and expectant around a massive oak table adorned with silverware that sparkles like diamonds and cut-glass decanters that seem to hold not just wine but the very essence of refinement. And there’s Madeline, his ancient Bonne, white cap perched like a tiny cloud on her head, flitting about with hands that move faster than thought, placing napkins, adjusting candles, whispering secrets into the ears of dishes.

These were no ordinary dinners. The guests? Artists, actors, musicians, writers, scholars, and the occasional rogue who’d wandered in from the gutter only to become the toast of the ton. Names you’ve likely read in memoirs or biographies—people whose jokes once made London laugh until it wept, whose wit still echoes in the halls of academia and the back rooms of pubs. If their epigrams now sound less sharp, their gags less hilarious, well—times change. Humor is fickle. One generation finds brilliance in a pun; another thinks it merely “trying too hard.” But I digress.

I’m not here to retell history. You can find all that elsewhere. My purpose is simpler: to tell you what it was like to glimpse the edge of that glittering world—not from the outside, but from within the dusty, slightly chaotic apartment where Dan and I held our own little version of high society.

Our version, of course, was far less refined. We called them “Afternoon At Homes,” and they took place on the second floor of our Georgian house in Queen Square—a quiet, pleasant spot where even pigeons seemed to know better than to disturb the peace. But on these days, chaos reigned. Cook and housemaid would spend the morning in frantic activity, and Dan and I had agreed on a system: we’d critique each other’s work. I’d judge Dan’s cooking; he’d judge my housekeeping.

“Too much soda,” I’d declare, taking a bite of cake.

“You silly Juggins!” Dan would snap. “It’s meant to taste of soda—it’s a *soda* cake.”

“I know it’s a soda cake,” I’d say, exasperated. “But it shouldn’t taste like nothing but soda. This thing has the flavor of a Seidlitz powder that’s been left out in the rain. You can’t serve people solidified laxatives for tea!”

Dan would sputter, but I’d stand firm. The cake would be tossed aside, and something else attempted. Dan’s cooking was sacred to him. He never admitted fault—even when he devoured his own failures later with mock enthusiasm. I remember one sponge cake so dry it required a teaspoon to eat. Dan ate it all, declared it “delicious,” and then sat in silence, drinking brandy from a wineglass like a man who’d just lost a war.

Then it would be my turn. “Call these knives clean?” Dan would demand.

“Yes,” I’d reply.

He’d draw his finger across the blade, creating a dramatic chiaroscuro effect.

“Not if you’re going to keep touching them. Why don’t you leave them alone and get on with your work?”

“I just wiped them,” I’d protest.

“Well, there isn’t any knife-powder.”

“There is.”

“Over-cleaning ruins knives. It takes the edge off. We’ll need them sharp for those lemon buns.”

“Over-cleaning? You don’t take any pride in the place.”

“Good Lord! Don’t I work from morning till night?”

“You lazy young devil!”

“Your cooking makes me lazy. How can a man work when he’s suffering all day from indigestion?”

And so it went—back and forth—until Dan was satisfied. Perhaps it was good that once a week, everything got sorted. After lunch, the staff vanished, leaving two impeccably dressed gentlemen to entertain their guests.

Our visitors came mostly from Dan’s journalistic friends and my theatre companions. Occasionally, Minikin and Jarman joined us. Mrs. Peedles—yes, *Mrs. Peedles*, the woman who ran a lodging house in Blackfriars—showed up breathless on our landing once or twice. I wouldn’t have invited her myself—she didn’t quite fit—but Dan, having met her once, insisted.

“My dear Lord Chamberlain,” Dan would say, “an ounce of originality is worth a ton of convention. Little tin ladies and gentlemen, all made to pattern! You can find them everywhere. Your friends would be an acquisition to any society.”

“But are they quite good form?” I’d ask, nervously.

“We’ll forget she runs a boarding house,” Dan replied. “We’ll call her ‘Lady P.’—that dear, quaint old creature. Her wardrobe suggests a provincial actress. Your society novelist would make a fortune out of her. The characters in her stories? Let’s say they’re Earl Blank and the Baroness de Dash. I’ll sell her anecdotes to society journals at a shilling a line. Jarman? He’ll be the son of a South American millionaire. Vulgar? Nonsense—you mean *racy*. Minikin? He looks forty—he’ll be an eminent scientist. His glass eye? Result of a chemical experiment. His rudeness? A lovable trait. He’ll wear a yard of red ribbon across his shirt-front and be addressed as Herr Professor. That explains his grammar and accent. They shall be our lions. You leave it to me. We’ll invite middle-class folk to meet them.”

And so we did. To my horror. Jarman embraced the joke with gusto. Our guests believed every word. The more he swaggered, the more he bragged, the more they adored him. Dan’s journalist friends tried to tempt him with visions of new magazines—“just a couple thousand pounds, and we’ll have half a million readers”—and I could laugh. But watching my actress friends try to charm him—cornering him, batting their eyes, performing little dances just for him, quarreling over who had his attention—I felt guilty. What if the joke went too far?

Luckily, Jarman was a man of sense. He enjoyed the attention, but he wasn’t fooled. He eventually married the eldest daughter of a small blacking factory. She was plain, but pleasant—and later, she inherited the business. Now, when I see him—stout, rosy-cheeked—he looks like a man who’s achieved his dreams.

Minikin was harder to manage. People turned up with scientific knowledge. We had to explain: “The Professor doesn’t talk shop.” Others spoke German. We said: “He’s learning English. He’s vowed not to speak his native tongue.” Still, he occasionally slipped—telling ladies he didn’t like English women “if they’re silly,” or telling Dan’s friend, “I got it for fourpence” when asked about the ribbon. We explained: “He’s soured by love.” The ladies forgave him. The men said it was lucky for the girl.

Lady Peedles? Our guests decided she must be the widow of some knighted City man. They mocked her behind her back, but lavished praise upon her face. “My dear Lady Peedles” became the most common phrase in our rooms. At the theatre, “my friend Lady Peedles” was whispered loudly. My social standing improved—rumors spread that I was her presumptive heir. I was suddenly courted as a gentleman of expectations.

Then there was the fishy-eyed young man—our regular guest. Dan won his heart by never laughing at him.

“I like talking to you,” he said one afternoon. “You don’t laugh when I say it’s been a fine day. Most people do. On stage, I don’t mind. I know I’m a funny little devil. “I know,” Dan said sympathetically. “The world never grasps that man is a collection, not a single exhibit. I remember being at a house where the guest was a great Hebrew scholar. “If I walked into a room and said my mother had been run over by an omnibus,” the young man said, “they’d think it the funniest story they’d heard in years.”

He was now playing a lead in the opera—drawing the crowds. But he wasn’t happy.

“I’m not a comic actor,” he said. “I could play Romeo. I have poetry in me. But this face—what can I do?”

“But aren’t you sinning your mercies?” Dan asked. “There’s Paul Kelver here. At school, he could make us laugh better than anyone. His ambition? To kick a ball like everyone else. “Can’t you understand?” the little man said. “The poorest tragedian never wishes to be a low comedian. The court fool had a good salary—maybe two-thirds of the brain in the palace. “Ah, I’m not a philosopher,” the little man sighed.

“And I’m not a comic actor on my way to a hundred a week,” Dan replied. “We all want the other boy’s cake.”

The O’Kelly was another frequent visitor. His attic in Belsize Square had been closed. He’d tried incense, pastilles, eau-de-Cologne—nothing worked. He’d talked of rats, hinted at drains.

“She believes the home should be pure—a temple. She thinks it will influence me. A beautiful idea.”

Meanwhile, in six other rooms, the O’Kelly kept his pipe and tobacco. One stood always on our mantelpiece.

In spring, temptation raged around the O’Kelly’s conscience. The Signora had returned to England, performing at Ashley’s Theatre. The O’Kelly would fall silent, puffing vigorously. Or sing praises to Mrs. O’Kelly.

“If anything could make a model man of me,” he said, “it would be her example.”

“I don’t believe she’s ever done anything wrong,” he added.

“Maybe she has,” I suggested. “You just don’t know.”

“I wish I could think so,” he said. “Not really wrong—just a little wrong. I feel I’d like her better if she had.”

“Not that I don’t like her as she is,” he corrected quickly. “I respect her—more than I can say. There was one morning, about a month ago. She gets up at six every day. Prayers at half-past. “I shouldn’t dwell on that,” I said.

“I can’t help it,” he replied. “I feel I’m not.”

“I don’t say you’re not,” I said. “But it’s not good for you.”

“I never will be,” he said gloomily. “Never.”

“Most people like it dry,” he admitted. “I prefer it with a hint of fruitiness.”

“There was a champagne,” I said, “you used to like.”

“I think I know the one,” he said. “Not bad for the price.”

We found the place—half wine-shop, half office.

“Just the same,” he said, pushing open the door. “Not changed a bit.”

Twelve months had passed, maybe. Not surprising. But clearly, the O’Kelly measured time by sensation. I ordered a bottle. I asked for claret. The O’Kelly finished the bottle. We spoke but once of Barbara. A photograph of her in her wedding dress stood on my desk. Occasionally, I’d prepare the room—sweep away impurities, dress carefully—and place the photo among flowers. One day, Hal picked it up. He studied it long and hard.

AND HOW I CAME BACK AGAIN—WITH A FEW EXTRA LUGGAGE AND A LOT LESS PRINCIPLE.

I left London with the drums of ambition beating in my chest and flags of self-importance fluttering wildly in my brain. About a year later, on a foggy December evening so wet it felt like the sky had developed a leak, I crept back—defeated, disgraced, and slightly damp. Not that the disgrace was permanent; you can always redeem yourself if you’re willing to forget about dignity, honesty, and your own soul. But at the time, I was returning not as a triumphant hero, but as a ghost trying to haunt a city that didn’t know it was being haunted.

I came back to hide. To vanish into the crowd like a dropped handkerchief in a busy market. To waste away unnoticed until I could summon the courage to end my miserable existence—preferably by drowning myself in a pub or jumping off a bridge while muttering something poetic about “the futility of dreams.” I’d once been ambitious. Now, I write about it in the past tense because, well, I’m not ambitious anymore. I’m just… tired.

I wanted to be great. Not just famous—great. A man whose name would echo through drawing rooms and theater lobbies alike. The kind of man people would whisper about: “Ah, yes, he’s the one who never lied, never cheated, and once gave a beggar his last shilling.” I wanted to be Galahad—no, not even Launcelot, because he *had* a weakness. Urban Vane turned out to be a swindler. Vane sat there, calmly smoking, sipping wine, and telling me the truth like it was a weather report. “You know,” he said, “you’ve been helping me steal a play. And you’ve been getting paid for it.”

I was furious. He smiled. “Your language,” he said, “might wound me—if I cared what you thought. But you preach morality while doing exactly what I do. You took credit for lines that weren’t yours. I stammered. “But you asked me to put my name on it! You said you didn’t want yours attached—for family reasons!”

“But you said it was a translation from the French!” I argued.

I left London with the drums of destiny pounding in my ears and the flag of virtue waving proudly above my head. A little over a year later, on a night so foggy it looked like God had sneezed all over the city, I slipped back in—defeated, dishonored, and wearing a coat that smelled faintly of regret.

I returned not as a conqueror, but as a fugitive. Not from justice—though I deserved it—but from myself. I needed a place to hide, somewhere crowded enough that no one would notice me, quiet enough that I could pretend I didn’t exist. London was perfect. It was full of people who didn’t care about me, and I didn’t care about them either—at least, not yet.

I’d once been ambitious. I wanted to make a name for myself. Not just any name—*a good name*. I wanted to be admired, respected, feared (a little), and remembered. I wanted to be the knight without fear, without flaw—Galahad, not Launcelot. I wanted to be a man whose conscience didn’t waver when the lights went out. But now? Now I was a coward. A thief. A fraud. And worse—I was *happy* about it.

It started with Urban Vane. A charming man, clever, cultured, and utterly devoid of scruples. He claimed to be a playwright, but the play we were touring—*Drama, in Five Acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff*—wasn’t his. It was stolen. From a sick writer named Moncrieff, whom Vane had befriended during a visit to Deleglise’s kitchen. Moncrieff fell ill, went to Italy, and nearly died. If he had, the theft might have remained undiscovered. But he didn’t die. He came back. And so did the trouble.

One evening, after a performance, Vane lit a cigar, poured a glass of wine, and told me the truth. “That play,” he said, “isn’t yours. It’s not mine. It’s Moncrieff’s. I stole it. And you helped.”

I was stunned. “But you said it was a French translation! You said I was adapting it!”

He smiled. “And you believed me. How naive. You were eager to be a playwright. I offered you a shortcut. You took it. You put your name on it. You managed the tour. You even visited Moncrieff. You’re the one who’d get caught.”

I protested. “But you asked me to sign it!”

“Because I didn’t want my name on it. For private reasons. But you didn’t hesitate. You jumped at the chance. Your career was beginning. My problem became your opportunity.”

I was angry. Then afraid. Then confused. Because deep down, I knew he was right. I had taken the easy path. I had let pride and ambition blind me.

“You’re not going to pin this all on me?” I begged.

He patted my shoulder. “Don’t worry. Moncrieff will just think the manuscript was lost. He’ll forget whether he wrote it or just imagined it. No one will talk. We’ll rewrite it. Keep the third act—it’s the only good part. In two weeks, it’ll be unrecognizable. If he sees it, he’ll think we stole *from him*.”

So I stayed. I worked. I rewrote scenes. I added lines. I even started enjoying it. Every word I wrote felt like a betrayal. But I kept writing. Vane praised me. Called me brilliant. Said I had genius. And I began to believe him.

There was one line I wrote that became a hit:
> “Yes, I admit it—I do love her. But there is something I love more—myself.”

Every night, the audience roared. The actor slapped his chest like it was a treasure chest full of virtue. I sat in the wings, sweating, wishing the floor would open and swallow me whole.

Then there was the role Vane wanted me to play—a vicious old man. I refused at first. I preferred the noble countryman. But Vane insisted. I gave in. And soon, I found myself enjoying the role. The leers. The smirks. The crude jokes. I was becoming someone I didn’t recognize.

Vane called me “brilliant.” He said I understood human nature. I began to like him. Not because he was honest—because he wasn’t—but because he was *real*. He didn’t pretend. He didn’t care about rules. He lived by his own code: *Don’t get caught.*

He also tried to set me up with our leading lady—a woman who looked like she’d been carved from marble and then dipped in honey. She was married to a novelist who had abandoned his career for her. Now he played small parts badly, and she barely noticed. She was beautiful, loud, and utterly vulgar.

Vane told me she was in love with me. I wasn’t. But I wanted to be. I wanted to be the man who could charm such a woman. So I practiced. I read books about passion. I studied how artists behaved—how they suffered, how they loved, how they destroyed themselves. I wanted to be tragic. I wanted to be *interesting*.

But nothing happened. Her glances annoyed me. Her hand felt heavy. I couldn’t feel anything. I was disappointed. Was I not a real man?

Then fate intervened.

Moncrieff produced his play. Our version was exposed. Rumors spread. People began asking questions. Vane, ever the opportunist, vanished. He left a note:

I found the note. The money was gone. The cast was furious. They blamed me. I told them the truth. They didn’t believe me. They saw me as the manager—the one responsible. I stood there, dry-mouthed, surrounded by angry faces, feeling like a ghost in my own life.

Then the leading lady showed up. I expected her to demand payment. Instead, she laughed. “So you’re the rich guy Vane told me about?” she said. “Or just his accomplice?”

“I’m both,” I said. “But not rich.”

She laughed again. “I knew he was a fraud. I should’ve known better than to sign. But I did. And I’m glad I did. It’s a joke. I came to say ‘yes’ to what you asked me last night.”

I stared. “What?”

“You asked me to run away with you. To unite our souls. I said I’d think about it. Lucky I did. Good-night.”

She left. I stood there, gripping a chair, wanting to scream, to cry, to throw something. But I did nothing. I just walked thirty miles to Belfast.

In London, I rented a room in Deptford—far from everyone I knew. I taught singing for sixpence a half-hour. I gave French lessons to shopboys who dreamed of being poets. I balanced ledgers. I sold jewelry I’d kept. I lived like a shadow.

For four months, I existed. I didn’t think. I didn’t feel. I ate, slept, walked, stared. I saw tragedies, farces, dramas—all around me—but I didn’t see them. I was numb.

Yet somehow, I remember them. The details are clear. The faces. The voices. The laughter. The tears. I don’t know how. I don’t know why. But I remember.

And now, looking back, I realize: I wasn’t dead. I was just waiting. Waiting for the next chapter. Waiting for the moment when I’d finally decide—whether to stay buried, or rise again.

But that’s another story.

It took me three years to win that handshake. For the first six months, I stayed put in Deptford—no grand plans, just a rickety table, a leaky roof, and a head full of ideas. There was plenty to write about: comedy, tragedy, pathos—all of it bubbling up from the streets like cheap gin at a Sunday school fete. But here’s the catch: I owed £150—£200 if you counted the interest and the time I spent wondering why I hadn’t been born with a trust fund. So I shut my mouth on the sad stuff and focused on laughter. Tears were someone else’s job. My patch? Humour. And I farmed it like a man who’d finally learned how to grow turnips.

At first, I sent every manuscript to Norah. Still, it was slow. Humour isn’t industrious. It doesn’t answer to deadlines. It’ll sit in its dressing gown, sip tea, and refuse to work unless the mood strikes. To make ends meet, I turned to journalism—the kind that pays three halfpence a line. I joined a band of shabby outsiders: young men trying to climb, older ones who had slid down. We attended inquests, police courts, chased fire engines, rejoiced in street accidents, and dreamed of murders. We lived off other people’s misfortunes like vultures on a butcher’s cart.

Our leader would return from a scene and whisper, “Gentlemen, I think the corpse’s brother-in-law wants this kept quiet.”

We’d nod solemnly. Notes would be taken with the precision of a coroner’s report. Then, five minutes later, our spokesman would wipe his mouth and say, “Not very interesting, really. Shall we say five shillings apiece?”

Sometimes, dignity kicked in, and we’d hold out for ten.

“‘I can get round,’ he says.”

Before I knew it, *smack!* I rushed to the window. Saw him sitting up on the pavement.

“Hullo, Jim,” I says. “You hurt yourself?”

“Think I’m all right,” he says, “as far as I can tell. But I wish you’d come down. This bloke I’ve fallen on looks a bit sick.”

The others wrote “Sad Accident.” I titled mine: “Plumber in a Hurry—Fatal Result.” I barely mentioned the sailor. Instead, I addressed plumbers everywhere, warning them of the dangers of haste, quoting the coroner’s words with solemn gravity. It made waves. One evening paper’s sub-editor came out in shirt sleeves and shook my hand.

“That’s the sort of thing we want,” he said. “A light touch. A bit of humour.”

I snatched fun from fires (I hope the insurance premiums weren’t overdue), picked oddities from street brawls, and found merriment in disasters so painful they should’ve been illegal. And I prospered.

Yet I avoided Poplar. Not because I was ashamed—though I was—but because I was two men. One was Paul Kelver, practical, pushing, determined. The other was Paul Kelver, the dreamer with sad eyes who still believed in magic and moonlight. Sometimes, in the cracked mirror, I’d see him—wistful, pleading. “Come,” he’d whisper. “It’s so near. Let’s go back. Just once.”

But I’d shake my head. “Later,” I’d say. “When I’m stronger. When I’ve built something real. Then I’ll go with you. But not now.”

So he’d fade. And in his place, a sharp-eyed, wide-awake fellow would nod wisely.

But one sentimental impulse I couldn’t resist. I’d made it. I wasn’t hiding anymore. I decided to move west. Passing through Guildford Street, I saw the creeper-covered house my father had once wanted. A card in the fanlight. I knocked. A bed-sitting-room on the third floor was vacant. The landlady opened the door. I knew it instantly.

“This shall be your room, Paul,” my father’s voice said behind me. I turned—just for a second, forgetting it was memory. “You’ll be quiet here. We can screen off the bed and washstand.”

So my father got his way. The room was sunny, overlooking hospital gardens. I screened off the bed and washstand, just as he’d suggested. And sometimes, at night, I’d hear him turn the handle.

“How are you getting on?”

“Famously.”

His words came back too: “You must be the practical man, Paul. Get on. I’ve always been a dreamer. I meant to do great things. But I aimed too high. I wasn’t… practical.”

“But oughtn’t one to aim high?” I’d asked.

He’d shifted in his chair. “It’s hard to say. It’s all so ununderstandable. You aim high and miss—seems like you don’t hit anything. Maybe it’s better to aim low and hit. But it’s a pity—your ideals, the best part of you—I don’t know why.”

For months, I’d written under the pen name “Jack Homer.” Then one day, an editor forwarded a letter addressed to me. It was from Mrs. Sellars—my maternal aunt—inviting me to her daughter’s wedding to Mr. Reginald Clapper. I’d nearly forgotten the Lady ‘Ortensia incident, but it was nice to learn it ended happily. Also, the invitation suggested she didn’t mind my leaving her. That was a relief.

So I bought the most expensive present I could afford—a silver butter dish, no less—and arrived at Kennington Church in a frock coat and light grey trousers, looking like a man who’d just inherited a bakery.

The ceremony was already underway. I crept up the aisle, ready to slip into an empty pew—when a hand grabbed my sleeve.

“We’re all here,” whispered O’Kelly. “Just room for ye.”

I squeezed his hand and sat between the Signora and Mrs. Peedles. Both were crying. The Signora, silently, one tear at a time, clinging to her cheek like a reluctant guest. Mrs. Peedles, copiously, gurgling like a kettle.

“It’s such a beautiful service,” murmured the Signora, pressing my hand. “I should so love to be married.”

“Me darling,” O’Kelly whispered, kissing her hand behind his open Prayer Book, “perhaps you will be—one day.”

She smiled through tears, then sighed and shook her head.

Mrs. Peedles, dressed in what looked like Queen Elizabeth’s third-best frock (or maybe fourth), explained that weddings reminded her how fleeting love was.

“The poor dears!” she sobbed. “But there, there’s no telling. Perhaps they’ll be happy. I hope they may be. He looks harmless.”

Jarman, from the other side, urged me to cheer up. “Don’t wear your heart on your sleeve,” he advised. “Try and smile.”

In the vestry, I met old friends. Mrs. Sellars, stouter than ever, sat in a chair—well, I assumed so; the chair itself was invisible. She greeted me with more grace than I expected, asking after my health with such tenderness I nearly thought she’d grown fond of me.

Mr. Reginald Clapper was small but energetic, clearly thrilled with his luck. He shook my hand vigorously, hoping we’d be friends forever.

“Won’t be my fault if we’re not,” he added. “Come and see us whenever you like.” He repeated it three times. I sensed he was overcompensating.

Mrs. Reginald Clapper, now officially Mrs., received my salute to a soft murmur of applause. She looked prettier than before—or maybe my standards had dropped. She trusted I’d always be a friend. I said I hoped to deserve it. Then she kissed me—without prompting—and hugged her mother, shedding tears.

“Everybody’s so good to me,” she said.

Brother George, less lank, wearing enormous white kid gloves, supervised my signing of the register. “Better luck next time, old cock,” he whispered sympathetically.

The fat young lady (or maybe the lean one, grown plumper) told me I was worth all the others combined.

“And so I told her,” she added. “A dozen times if I told her once. But there!”

I murmured thanks.

Cousin Joseph, identifiable by his watery eyes, seemed less cheerful.

“You take my tip,” he said, pulling me aside, “keep out of it.”

“You speak from experience?” I asked.

“I’m as fond of a joke as any man,” he replied. “But when it comes to buckets of water—”

Mrs. Sellars interrupted: “Breakfast at eleven! Don’t dilly-dally!”

Joseph muttered, “See you again, perhaps,” and pushed past.

What Mrs. Sellars would call a “cold col-la-shon” was arranged at a nearby restaurant. Uncle and Aunt Gutton walked with me—not because I wanted their company, but because Uncle Gutton seized my arm and left me no choice.

“Now then, young man,” he boomed, once we were in the street, “if you want to pitch into me, go ahead. I won’t mind. Might do you good.”

I said nothing of the sort.

“Oh, all right,” he said, disappointed. “If you forgive and forget, so do I. I never liked you—admitted it to Rosie. ‘He may be cleverer than he looks,’ I said, ‘or a bigger fool than I think. You take my advice and get a full-grown article.’”

I said his advice had been sound.

“I’m glad you think so,” he replied, puzzled. “Though if you wanted to call me names, I wouldn’t blame you. Anyhow, you’ve taken it like a sensible chap. You’ve moved on, as I always told her you would. Young men out of storybooks don’t die of broken hearts—even if they feel like standing on their heads in the water-butt.”

“I was in love three times before I married the old woman,” he added.

Aunt Gutton sighed. “Gentlemen don’t feel these things as much as they ought.”

“They’ve got their living to earn,” Uncle retorted.

I agreed. Life couldn’t be wasted on regrets.

“As for the rest,” he admitted, “I was wrong. You’ve turned out better than I expected.”

I thanked him. We shook hands as we entered the restaurant.

Minikin was waiting. He’d only gotten limited leave from work, so he chose the restaurant over the church. Others were there too—fellow clerks, admirers of the Lady ‘Ortensia’. Altogether, about twenty.

Breakfast was laid out in a large room. Wedding presents stood on a side-table. My gift—silver, with my card—hadn’t been seen by Mrs. Clapper until now. She and her mother lingered, examining it.

“Real silver!” Mrs. Sellars whispered. “Must’ve cost a tenner.”

“I hope you find it useful,” I said.

Mrs. Sellars drifted away.

“I suppose you think I set my cap at you just because you were a gentleman,” said the Lady ‘Ortensia.

“Let’s not talk about it,” I said. “We were both foolish.”

“I don’t want you to think it was just that,” she continued. “I did like you. And I wouldn’t have disgraced you—at least, I’d have tried not to. Women learn fast. You never gave me time.”

“Believe me,” I said, “things are better this way.”

“I suppose so,” she said. “I was a fool.” She glanced around. “I told lies,” she confessed. “Couldn’t help it. I was sore all over. But I’ve always been ashamed. I’ll tell the truth, if you like.”

I saw a way to ease her mind. “My dear girl,” I said, “you’ve taken the blame. You’ve let me off scot-free. That’s generous.”

“You mean that?” she asked.

“The truth,” I said, “would shift all the shame onto me. It was I who broke my word. I acted shabbily from start to finish.”

She hadn’t seen it that way. “Very well,” she said. “I’ll keep quiet.”

At breakfast, I sat beside Mrs. Sellars, the Signora next to me, O’Kelly opposite. Uncle Gutton faced the couple. Joseph was hidden by flowers, so his voice, raised now and then, carried the mysterious weight of an oracle.

For the first fifteen minutes, silence reigned. Mrs. Sellars worked sums on the tablecloth with a blunt pencil. The Signora, unusually quiet, murmured, “It was kind of them to invite me. But I don’t belong here.”

“Why not?” I asked.

“I’m not fit to be here,” she whispered. “What right have I at wedding breakfasts? Of course, for dear Willie, it’s different. He’s been married.”

O’Kelly, usually eager to include her, fell into abstraction. Jarman tried jokes—no one noticed. The Lady ‘Ortensia’s admirers giggled among themselves. The only sounds were cutlery clattering, waiters shuffling, and Uncle Gutton’s curious hissing as he drank champagne.

Then came the cake. Bride and groom struggled to slice it—more like smashing it into pieces. The mood lifted. The room warmed. Mrs. Sellars smiled broadly.

“But it’s a sad thing,” she sighed, shaking her head, “when your daughter marries and leaves you.”

“Damned sight sadder,” Uncle Gutton muttered, “when she stays home year after year and expects you to feed her.”

He meant it as an aside. But his voice wasn’t subtle. One bridesmaid—plain, elderly—flushed. I guessed it was Miss Gutton.

Aunt Gutton added, “Gentlemen aren’t marrying as keenly as they used to.”

“Got to know a bit about it, I expect,” piped Joseph.

“A great evil,” declared a hatchet-faced man, “is the expense of divorce. At present, it’s the rich man’s luxury.”

Jarman stepped in. “Girls have gone up in value. Once, they were given away with a chair or a piece of linen. Now? Only chaps like Clapper can afford a first-class article.”

Clapper roared with laughter—genuine, infectious delight.

“Give and take,” said Mrs. Sellars. “That’s marriage.”

“Give a deal more than you bargained for and take what you don’t want—that’s the sum of it,” Joseph muttered.

“Oh, do be quiet, Joe,” said the stout young lady.

“You look less foolish when you keep it shut,” she returned.

“We’ll show them how to get on,” the Lady ‘Ortensia said to her husband, smiling.

Clapper gurgled.

“When me and the old girl fixed things up,” Uncle Gutton began, “we didn’t talk nonsense. ‘I’m not a duke,’ I says—”

“Had she been mistaking you for one?” Minikin asked.

Clapper chuckled. I feared Uncle Gutton’s eyes might pop out.

And so the feast continued—laughter, whispers, absurdity, and the quiet triumph of a man who’d survived his past and was finally, awkwardly, alive.

I climbed—slowly, steadily, like a man who’s decided he’s not going to fall off the ladder this time. No dreams. No distractions. Just work. The other me—the one with the sad eyes and the soft voice that said things like “What if we just stopped trying?”—kept popping up, whispering about poetry, politics, editorships, and all those noble careers that end in mild depression and a bookshelf full of unread books. But I had a mission: buy back my honesty. And I had a hunger—growing daily, until it felt like a physical ache—to feel Norah’s hand again, strong and white and real, gripping mine as though the world couldn’t possibly be so terrible after all.

Heaven knows what the dream-Paul would’ve done to me. Probably turned me into a minor poet with a beard and a dog named “Crisis.” Or maybe a politician who only ever made speeches during thunderstorms. Either way, I’d have been useless. So I kept climbing.

Now and then, when I had a week to spare—my humor on holiday, nothing to do but wait for inspiration to return—I’d write stories just for fun. They didn’t make waves. But success in serious work? That takes time. Had I stuck with it? Maybe. But there was money to earn. By the time the debts were paid, I had a name. A reputation. Not bad for a man who used to think his greatest achievement was avoiding being late for dinner.

“Madness!” said practical friends. “You’re throwing away a sure fortune for a dubious future. You know you can write comedy. This new path? It’s like starting over.”

“You’ll never get a foot in the door,” warned others, speaking with the wisdom of experience. “Once a humourist, always a humourist. Like a comic actor trying to play Hamlet. Even if he’s brilliant, the audience will just laugh—or stay home.”

So I found solace in the company of “Goggles,” a fellow sufferer in the great struggle between talent and temperament. We’d retreat to a quiet corner of the Club and pour our souls out. He’d expound at length on his vision of Romeo—passionate, tragic, deeply misunderstood. I listened, nodding earnestly. Because that’s what friends do. In return, he’d listen to me talk about monumental literature, how it should be built like cathedrals, helping mankind rise above itself. I could see him nodding, but I knew he wasn’t really listening—he was already planning his next joke.

One evening, as the clock struck seven, Goggles rose with a grin. “Perhaps in a future life,” he said, “I’ll be a great tragedian, and you a famous poet. Meanwhile, as Brian says, we’re both sinning our mercies. After all, to live is the most important thing.”

We reached the cloakroom. I helped him into his coat. He tapped me on the chest, fixed those absurd fishy eyes on me, and said, “Take my advice: marry a dear little woman. One never understands life properly till the babies come to explain it.”

I returned to my chair by the fire. Wife. Children. Home. Wasn’t that the real purpose of life? Not dreams, not ambitions, but a real, warm, slightly chaotic existence? I saw them around me—laughing, arguing, demanding toast at three in the morning. The fair, sweet woman, helper, comrade, comforter. The little faces, made in our image, asking questions that had no answers—except the ones we invented. All other hopes? Phantoms. Mist fading in sunlight.

Then Hodgson came to me. “I want you to write a comic opera,” he said, holding an open letter. “The public’s tired of French translations. I want something English. Something new and original.”

“The English part’s easy,” I replied. “But I wouldn’t clamour for ‘new and original’ if I were you.”

“Why not?”

“You might get it. Then you’d be disappointed.”

He laughed. “Well, you know what I mean—something we can label ‘new and original’ on the programme. What do you say? It’s a big chance. I’m willing to risk it. People are beginning to talk about you.”

I’d written farces, comediettas. They’d worked. But a full-length comic opera? That was different. It could make or break a man. Especially one who’d spent years pretending he wasn’t a clown.

I explained my fears. I didn’t mention the four-act drama in my desk—thumping with humanity, with soul, with the kind of truth that makes audiences cry. I’d hoped to storm the theatre world with it. Now, the hope was fainter than a candle in a hurricane.

“It’s simple,” Hodgson urged. “Give Atherton plenty of comic business. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time. Let Duncan change her frock every quarter hour. Done.”

“I’ll tell you what,” he continued. “I’ll take the whole crew down to Richmond on Sunday. Coach leaves at half-past ten. You can meet them, see what they can do. Atherton has ideas. Next week, we’ll draw up a contract.”

It was too good an opportunity to pass up. Even if it meant being permanently branded as a jester. But comic opera pays. Well, sometimes.

A small crowd gathered in the Strand to see us off.

“Nothing wrong?” asked the leading lady, alighting late from her cab, “Is it a fire?”

“Just here to see you,” said Hodgson, without looking up.

“Oh, good gracious!” she cried. “Let’s go quickly!”

“Box seat, my dear,” Hodgson replied.

She accepted help from myself and three other gentlemen, mounting the ladder with charming hesitation. Our low comedian, however, delayed things twice—pretending to lose his footing, sliding back into the arms of the stolid doorkeeper. The crowd—mostly small boys—laughed and applauded. Encouraged, he tried again—on hands and knees—and ended up sitting on the tenor, who smiled mechanically.

For the first dozen buses, our low comedian greeted each by rising and bowing profoundly, then collapsing onto either the tenor or me. Only the tenor and I seemed unimpressed. Charing Cross passed. Nobody cared. He finally settled down.

“People ask me,” he said, brushing dust from his knees, “why I do this sort of thing offstage. It amuses me.”

“I was coming up from Birmingham,” he went on. “At Willesden, the ticket collector opened the door. I jumped out and ran down the platform. He chased me, shouting. I dodged for a minute. You wouldn’t believe the excitement. Fifty people left their seats. I told them I’d been travelling second class with a first-class ticket. True story. People think I do it for attention. I do it for fun.”

“It must be a troublesome way to amuse oneself,” I said.

“Exactly what my wife says,” he replied. “She can’t understand why we all feel the urge to play the fool now and then. When she’s with me, I don’t do it.”

“She’s not here today?” I asked.

“She suffers from headaches,” he said. “Hardly ever goes anywhere.”

“I’m sorry.” I meant it.

During the drive to Richmond, he amused himself again. In Kensington, he balanced the coach horn on his nose. At Kew, he stopped the coach to ask a boarding school for change. At the foot of Richmond Hill, he tried to convince a deaf old gentleman in a Bath-chair to let his man race us up the hill for a shilling.

Our party roared with laughter—except Hodgson, busy with letters, and an elegant young lady recently emerged from the Divorce Court with a weekly income worth a hundred pounds.

Arriving at the hotel early, we strolled into the garden. Our low comedian spotted an elderly gentleman sipping Vermouth. He froze. Then bolted across the lawn, seized the man’s hand, and shook it violently.

We exchanged glances. “Charlie’s in fine form,” we whispered.

The gentleman looked puzzled. “And how’s Aunt Martha?” asked our low comedian.

“Dear old Aunt Martha! Well, I’m glad! You look bonny! How is she?”

“I’m afraid—” began the gentleman.

Our low comedian stepped back. “Don’t tell me anything’s happened! Not dead? Don’t say it!”

He grabbed the man’s shoulders, face twisted in terror.

“I really have no idea what you’re talking about,” said the gentleman, annoyed. “I don8t know you.”

“Not know me? Forgotten—? Isn’t your name Steggles?”

“No,” said the stranger shortly.

“My mistake,” said our low comedian. He drank the rest of the Vermouth in one gulp and walked away.

The gentleman, still baffled, was eventually informed it was Atherton—*the* Atherton.

“Oh, is it?” he growled. “Tell him from me that when I want his damned tomfoolery, I’ll come to the theatre and pay for it.”

“What a disagreeable man,” we said, following Charlie into the hotel.

Lunch was a disaster. He kissed the bald back of the waiter’s head, pretended it was a face. Called for hot mustard and water. Pretended to steal the silver. When the finger-bowls arrived, he took off his coat and asked the ladies to look the other way.

After lunch, he became serious. He slipped his arm through mine and led me down a quiet path.

“About the opera,” he said. “No stale stuff. Something new.”

I suggested it might be hard.

“Not at all,” he said. “I’m a young fellow in love with a girl.”

I promised to note it.

“Her father—apoplectic old idiot. Make him comic: ‘Damme, sir! By gad!’ all that.”

I nodded sagely.

“He won’t have me. Thinks I’m an ass. I’m simple on the outside. But not such a fool.”

“You don’t think we’re getting too far out of the groove?” I asked.

“More so, the better.”

“In act two, I disguise myself. Organ-grinder. Sing a song in broken English. Then a policeman. Or a young swell. Give me chances. Plenty of opportunities to be funny. No old tricks.”

I agreed.

“Put in some pathos,” he added. “A scene where I show I’ve got more than just humour. Make them feel. Send them out saying: ‘Charlie’s made me laugh—but I never knew he could make me cry!’ See?”

I said I did.

The leading lady met us later, took my hand, and pulled me to a secluded bench.

“I want to talk about my part,” she said. “Can’t you give me something original? Do.”

Her pleading was irresistible. I pledged compliance.

“I’m so tired of being the village maiden,” she said. “I want a coquettish part. I can flirt,” she assured me, archly.

I believed her.

“You might,” she said, “make the plot depend on me. Women are always underused in comic opera. A comic opera built around a woman? It could be a smash hit. Don’t you agree, Mr. Kelver?” She pouted, laid her hand on mine. “We’re much more interesting than men, aren’t we?”

I said yes. Personally.

The tenor, sipping tea on the balcony, beckoned me aside.

“About the opera,” he said. “Isn’t it time we gave the story more weight? Less clowning? More human interest?”

I agreed.

“If you wrote it around a love story,” he said, “you’d win. Humour’s fine—as support. But when it’s the whole structure, it gets tiresome.”

I said I saw his point.

“Of course, personally,” he went on, “it doesn’t matter. I get paid the same whether I’m on stage five minutes or an hour. But when you have someone of my standing and give him nothing to do… the public gets disappointed.”

“Naturally,” I said.

“The lover,” he whispered, seeing Charlie approaching, “that’s the character everyone thinks about. Men and women both. Make him interesting. That’s the secret.”

As we waited for the horses, I found myself standing with a tall, thin man—Marmaduke Trevor, Duke of Bayswater in act two.

“You’ll forgive me, Mr. Kelver,” he said. “Trevor. Marmaduke Trevor.”

I didn’t recall him. But I looked into his sensitive face and lied: “A capital performance. Always amused me.”

He flushed. “I made a hit in America once—with a soda-water syphon. If you could, Mr. Kelver, drop in a small part involving a soda-water syphon… it might help.”

I wrote it in. Insisted on it. Critics called it cliché. But Marmaduke was right—it helped.

The return journey was worse. Charlie produced noises from the horn. Argued with bus drivers (he lost). At Hammersmith, he stood on the back seat, selling our hats at cut-rate prices.

“Got any ideas?” Hodgson asked, as we said goodnight.

“I’m thinking,” I said, “of going down to the country to write it quietly.”

“Good idea,” he said. “Too many cooks. Be sure it’s ready for autumn.”

I wrote it in the Yorkshire Wolds. Read it aloud to the cast before the season ended. Silence. Then the leading lady checked the clock. “If it’s accurate, I can catch my train.”

Hodgson consulted his watch. “It might be fast.”

She left. The tenor recalled a comic opera that was rubbish until five minutes before curtain. He had a train. Marmaduke gave me a discreet handshake. Charlie told Hodgson he might salvage parts—if given free rein.

Hodgson and I were alone.

“It’s no good,” he said. “From a box-office point of view. Very clever.”

“How do you know?” I asked.

“I never make mistakes.”

“You’ve had failures.”

“And shall again,” he laughed. “The right thing isn’t easy to get.”

“Cheer up,” he added. “This is only your first try. We’ll knock it into shape.”

They knocked it to pieces.

“We’ll cut that scene,” said Charlie, drawing a pencil through four pages. “Half-a-dozen words will do. ‘Hallo, what are you doing here?’ ‘Oh, escaped from prison.’ ‘Good business. How’s Miriam?’ ‘Happy—going to be my wife!’ What more?”

“But it’s essential,” I argued.

“Not at all.”

The leading lady: “I’ve spoken to Mr. Hodgson. He agrees—I should fall in love with John instead of Peter.”

“But John loves Arabella.”

“Oh, we’ve cut Arabella. I can sing her songs.”

The tenor: “I want a scene with Miss Duncan at the start of act one. She comes out onto a balcony, bathed in moonlight.”

“But act one is morning.”

“We’ll change it to evening.”

“But the opera opens with a hunting scene. People don’t hunt by moonlight.”

“It’ll be a novelty. That’s what comic opera needs.”

I endured it for a week. “They’re experienced,” I told myself. “They must know better.” By the end, I realized they didn’t. I lost my temper. Advice to aspiring dramatists: lose it early. I took both manuscripts to Hodgson’s office.

One was clean, neat, coherent—mine. The other was a mess—cut, rewritten, reversed, interlined, a hopeless tangle. I pushed it across.

“That’s your opera,” I said. “If you can understand it, if you can produce it, it’s yours. This is mine.” I placed the clean manuscript beside it. “It may be good. It may be bad. But if it’s played, it’s played as written. Cancel the contract. Decide.”

He argued for forty minutes—appealing to self-interest, to morality, to my future. Then he called me an obstinate young fool, threw the ruined manuscript into the wastebasket, and stormed into the room.

“Play it as written,” he snapped. “And be damned to it!”

The company shrugged. For a month, they kept shrugging. Hodgson stayed away, then returned with growing melancholy.

I’d won. But keeping the win was harder. Charlie recited lines with flat monotony, pausing to ask: “Is this supposed to be funny or serious?”

The leading lady: “Do you think any real girl would behave like this?”

The tenor: “Maybe the audience will understand. I don’t.”

I held firm. Aggressively disagreeable. Hodgson supported me—against his own judgment.

“It’s bound to fail,” he said. “I’m spending twelve to fifteen hundred pounds to teach you a lesson. When you learn it, we’ll settle up with a profitable opera.”

“And if it succeeds?” I asked.

“My dear boy,” he said, “I never make mistakes.”
