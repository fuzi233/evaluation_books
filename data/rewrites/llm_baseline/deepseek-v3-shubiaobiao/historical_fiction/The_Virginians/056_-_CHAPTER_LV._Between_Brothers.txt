The reunion of the Warrington brothers was, as might be expected, a torrent of confidences, each pouring into the other’s ear the events of their separation. Harry, ever eager and affectionate, demanded every detail of home—how the hounds fared, whether little Dempster still chased squirrels, if old Nathan kept his place in the kitchen, and if Fanny Mountain had bloomed into the beauty their mother predicted.

“So Polly Broadbent is to marry Tom Barker and exile herself to Georgia?” Harry laughed. “Lord, I near lost my wits—and my purse—over that girl once. Drank the parson’s port till my head spun, just to linger near her. Not that Mother would have stood for it. A Broadbent in the family? She’d sooner have welcomed a highwayman.”

George, with dry amusement, recounted the state visit of Colonel Washington and his bride to Castlewood. “Picture it, Harry—Mother stiff as an oak, doling out courtesies like a queen granting pardons. And Mountain, of course, whispering that we’d nearly acquired a stepfather in Washington himself.”

“Mountain!” Harry groaned. “She’d marry off the cook to the boot-boy if left unchecked.”

But George’s humor faded as he spoke of his own reception at home. The hero’s welcome he had expected—the sole survivor of Braddock’s massacre—had curdled into suspicion. The ladies deemed him cold; the officers, dull. And then came the final blow: the wretched Biche, the Indian woman who had nursed him in the wilderness, arrived bearing Museau’s ransom demand.

“The whispers started before she’d left the courtyard,” George said bitterly. “By sundown, I was the villain of every fireside tale—a seducer, a libertine. The pious Mrs. Stack preached sermons on my corruption. The boys pelted the poor creature with stones if she dared show her face. And Mother—God help me—she thanked Biche with a look that could freeze hell itself.”

Harry, pacing like a caged bear, swore vengeance. “Name me one of those lying dogs, and I’ll thrash him senseless! Clubber got his already—”

“Ah, Clubber.” George’s mouth twisted. “A pity the magistrate intervened. I’d have preferred to finish the job in Maryland.”

Yet it was Harry’s own troubles that now loomed largest. Of Castlewood’s clan, he spared no one—not the Earl, whose false coin of friendship had bought Harry’s ruin at the card table; not Will, whose cheating at horseflesh had earned him a broken nose. As for Lady Fanny and her mother, his words were guarded but damning. “Gossip paints them as sharp as adders,” he muttered. “But then, who’s to say what’s true in such a nest?”

But Maria—here his voice warmed. “She gave me her jewels, George. Every trinket she owned, flung into my hands to pay my debts. I’ll not cast her off, not if the King himself ordered it.”

George, tasked by their mother to sever the match, could only sigh. “I’ll not ask you to break your word. But weigh it well, Harry. A lifetime is longer than a promise.”

Yet in the days that followed, George’s doubts festered. Aunt Bernstein’s warnings gnawed at him. Was it love that drove Maria—or ambition? Harry’s fortune was no secret; his heart, alas, was all too trusting. Resolved to test her, George began to weave a plan, one that might reveal the truth—even at the cost of Harry’s fleeting happiness.

*The world is quick to judge, and slow to pardon. And he who sets himself as judge of another’s heart may find his own weighed in the balance.*
