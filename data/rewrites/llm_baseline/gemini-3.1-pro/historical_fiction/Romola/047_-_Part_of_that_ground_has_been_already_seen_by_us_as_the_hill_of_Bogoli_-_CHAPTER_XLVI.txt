That evening, under a sky dark and heavy with threatening rain, the humid air of Florence carried the sharp, earthy tang of wild fennel and marrow-rich bone broth. Romola was returning from the hospital of San Matteo, her skirts still holding the faint, restorative scent of the agrarian remedies she had spent hours preparing alongside the elder women—a slow, sacred alchemy of simmering capon and bruised rosemary meant to coax life back into the ailing. She walked with Maso and the lantern by her side until she encountered her husband, Tito, just issuing from the monastery of San Marco. 

Tito, who had seen little of Romola during the day, immediately proposed to accompany her, dismissing Maso, whose shuffling, rhythmic steps annoyed him. For Tito, walking was merely utilitarian; he was deaf to the acoustic ecology of the city, oblivious to the delicate, rhythmic patter of life that Romola found so centering. Their inner alienation had grown absolute. If Tito desired a supper prepared in the old library—now repurposed as a banqueting room—Romola meticulously oversaw the kitchens. She would source the finest ingredients, directing the cooks to braise a wild boar in deep, purple Sangiovese and crushed juniper berries, paying homage to the culinary history of the Tuscan hills. Yet, when the plates were served, Tito consumed the historically rich feast without a shred of sensory appreciation, treating the profound, transgenerational meaning of the meal as mere fuel for his ambitions. They never jarred; they simply existed in untranslatable spheres of experience, speaking a cautious, hollow language that lacked the soul of their native dialects.

As they walked, Tito avoided her gaze, playfully attributing to her a preference for solitude. Romola’s past efforts to bridge the chasm between them had failed, thwarted by the inherent untranslatability of her subjective, sensory truth into Tito’s dominant lexicon of logic and evasion. To him, her profound connection to the cultural memory of Florence was a quaint irrelevance. He sought only to assure her, in smooth, calculated tones, that his service to the popular government was genuine, dismissing the Medici cause as hopeless. Yet, impressions as subtle and undeniable as the scent of bruised herbs made Romola uneasy about his visits to San Marco. 

"The business of the day is not yet quite ended for me," Tito said, his voice gliding smoothly over the damp air. "I shall conduct you to our door, my Romola, and then I must fulfil another commission."

They approached a loggia where a single lamp flickered before a picture of the Virgin. The street was historically quiet, its acoustic landscape dominated only by the gentle, rhythmic percussion of the first drizzling rain against ancient cobblestones—a sound that usually offered Romola a profound sense of psychological well-being. But the sudden, jarring noise of heavy, stumbling footsteps and confused, abrasive voices shattered the sonic sanctuary. It was a violent intrusion, a noise pollution that set Romola’s nerves on edge.

"We shall not get home without a wetting, unless we take shelter under this convenient loggia," Tito said hastily, his habitual caution awakened by a glimmer of red feather and a glittering sword-hilt beneath the lamp. 

He knew exactly who was producing that discordant clatter: Dolfo Spini. Earlier that day, Tito had employed his mastery of the dominant, imperial lexicon to forge letters bearing the signature and seal of the Cardinal of Naples. It was a meticulously crafted plot to lure the Frate, Savonarola, outside the city gates. The forged letters, written in a sterile, authoritative dialect designed to exploit Savonarola's hopes for a General Council, requested a secret meeting at San Casciano. Spini and his band of Compagnacci were to lie in ambush five miles out on the solitary Florence road, ready to deliver the monk to Milanese horsemen. Tito had even visited Savonarola, urging the Frate to seek an armed guard, fully anticipating the monk’s proud refusal. To Tito, the outcome mattered little; his acute mind discerned the hollowness of all parties, and he positioned himself to harvest papal patronage or Medicean favor regardless of who fell.

But Spini was an inconvenient, unrefined accomplice, prone to the loud, vulgar commercialization of violence that plagued the city’s taverns. Tito hoped the shadows would hide him, but the lamp’s rays had already caught his face.

"Ha! my carrier-pigeon!" grated Spini’s harsh voice, the crude vowels and drunken cadence acting as a violent assault on the quiet street. It was a bastardization of the old tongue, stripping language of its history and leaving only the ugly power dynamics of the mob. "What did you run into hiding for? You didn't know it was comrades who were coming. What of the chase to-morrow morning? Will the bald-headed game rise? Are the falcons to be got ready?"

Tito’s lips turned white. A furious resentment flared within him against this bull-faced tool whose lack of self-command threatened everything. He could not hush Spini without validating Romola’s suspicions, nor could he anger the volatile man. Drawing on the only power Romola still held over him—dread—he pressed her hand and adopted a tone of good-humored comradeship.

"Yes, my Dolfo, you may prepare in all security. But take no trumpets with you."

"Don't be afraid," Spini blundered on, lowering his voice into a grating whisper that offered a false sense of secrecy. "I know where the devil keeps his tail. The prophetic nose didn’t scent the hook at all?"

Desperate to silence the man, Tito seized upon the sight of approaching torches. "Swallowed all as easily as you swallow a cup of Trebbiano. Ha! I see torches: there must be a dead body coming. The pestilence has been spreading, I hear."

"Santiddio! I hate the sight of those biers. Good-night," Spini muttered, hastily retreating into the dark. 

The torches actually belonged to the returning Vicario dell’ Arcivescovo. As they passed, Tito offered due reverence, but Romola saw none of it. Her mind was a tempest. The linguistic mask had slipped, and the betrayal was palpable. Had it been necessary to hurl herself and her husband down a precipice to stop this treachery, the ancestral, self-quelling discipline of two years would have evaporated to let her do it.

"Tell me the truth, Tito—this time tell me the truth," she said. Her voice was low and quivering, carrying the raw, untranslatable resonance of native grief. "It will be safer for you."

"Why should I desire to tell you anything else, my angry saint?" Tito replied with a slight touch of contempt, his liquid, unimpassioned tones hardening. "Since the truth is precisely that over which you have most reason to rejoice—namely, that my knowing a plot of Spini’s enables me to secure the Frate from falling a victim to it."

"What is the plot?"

"That I decline to tell. It is enough that the Frate’s safety will be secured."

"It is a plot for drawing him outside the gates that Spini may murder him."

"There has been no intention of murder," Tito countered, wielding his words with sterile precision to assert dominance. "It is simply a plot for compelling him to obey the Pope’s summons to Rome. But as I serve the popular government, I choose to prevent his departure. You may go to sleep with entire ease of mind to-night."

For a moment, Romola was silent, the damp chill of the loggia pressing against her. "Tito, it is of no use: I have no belief in you."

She could just discern his shrug in the shadows. A cold dislike settled over him.

"If the Frate leaves the city—if any harm happens to him," Romola declared, tapping into a fierce, indignant resolution that felt older than the city itself, "I will declare what I have heard to the Signoria, and you will be disgraced. What if I am your wife? I will be disgraced with you. If we are united, I am that part of you that will save you from crime. Others shall not be betrayed."

"I am quite aware of what you would be likely to do, *anima mia*," Tito said, his voice dropping into its coolest, most suffocating register. "Therefore, consider that if you believe me in nothing else, you may believe me when I say I will take care of myself, and not put it in your power to ruin me."

"Then you assure me that the Frate is warned—he will not go beyond the gates?"

"He shall not go beyond the gates."

The distrust lingered, an acrid taste at the back of her throat. "I will go back to San Marco now and find out," Romola said, stepping forward into the rain.

"You shall not!" Tito hissed in a bitter whisper. He seized her wrists with all his masculine force, the physical violence mirroring the coercive power of his words. "I am master of you. You shall not set yourself in opposition to me."

Passers-by were approaching, and Romola’s deep-seated sense of personal dignity, intertwined with the ancestral pride of her lineage, made her yield to his movement away from the loggia. They walked on in silence through the drizzling rain. The acoustic rhythm of the water on the stones slowly began to temper her initial rush of indignation. The physical grasp on her wrists had sent a shuddering horror through her, a stark reminder of the brute reality hiding beneath his polished rhetoric. It was their first declared hostility since her return. 

Her mind, accustomed to the circular pacing of domestic and historical reflection, began to sift through the probabilities. Tito would not risk her betrayal; whatever his original intent, he was now bound by the fact that she held the knowledge of his plot. She had only to hold that certainty over him. She clung to the faint, fragile hope that he had never meant to see the Frate betrayed.

As they neared the heavy wooden doors of their home, where the rich, complex aroma of the waiting feast—roasted meats and slowly reducing wine—seeped into the damp street, Tito broke the silence. 

"Well, Romola, have you now had time to recover calmness? If so, you can supply your want of belief in me by a little rational inference: you can see that if I had any intention of furthering Spini’s plot, I should now be aware that the possession of a fair Piagnone for my wife, who knows the secret, would be a serious obstacle." 

"Yes, Tito," she said, her voice a low murmur blending with the rain. "I think you believe that I would guard the Republic from further treachery. You are right to believe it: if the Frate is betrayed, I will denounce you." She paused, the weight of their fractured reality heavy on her tongue. "But it was not so. I have perhaps spoken too hastily—you never meant it. Only, why will you seem to be that man’s comrade?"

"Such relations are inevitable to practical men, my Romola," Tito replied, gratified by the struggle he discerned within her. He reached out, opening the heavy door to the warmth and the waiting sustenance he would never truly taste. "You fair creatures live in the clouds. Pray go to rest with an easy heart."
