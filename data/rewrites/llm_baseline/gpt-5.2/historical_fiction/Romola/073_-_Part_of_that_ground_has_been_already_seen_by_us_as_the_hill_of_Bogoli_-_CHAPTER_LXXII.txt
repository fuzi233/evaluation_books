Romola could not drive from her mind the sentence that had gone from mouth to mouth through Florence, repeating itself like the refrain of a street-song no one dared to sing aloud in full: “Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy.”

It was not the words alone that shook her, but the sound of them as they were carried—broken by corners, thinned by distance, thickened by crowds—through the city’s lanes and under its arches. Florence had a way, in those days, of making every judgment a kind of chorus. Even when a man whispered, the stones seemed to take it up and give it back, altered. Those syllables, once spoken within the walls of San Marco, had become something else on the Piazza: half pity, half triumph, and always, always noise.

And with the noise came new reports, piteous enough to make certainty feel like cruelty. On the nineteenth of May, under a sunshine that made the Arno look harmless and the city’s washed walls seem newly forgiven, the two Papal Commissaries rode in to finish the trial. They entered to acclamations—acclamations not for justice, nor even for peace, but for a scapegoat.

Now the cry ran, “It is the Frate’s deception that has brought on all our misfortunes; let him be burned, and all things will be right, and our evils will cease.” Romola heard it as one hears an ugly tune hammered out by boys on a door: the rhythm of it was simple enough to catch anyone, and all the more dangerous for that.

The next day there was fresh torture—fresh, as if pain were a loaf that could be cut again and again from the same scant dough. Savonarola’s shattered, sensitive frame was brought to meet the implements; and at the first sight of them he fell in convulsed agitation to his knees. In brief, passionate words he retracted his confession—declared he had spoken falsely when he denied his prophetic gift, and that if he suffered, he would suffer for the truth.

“The things that I have spoken, I had them from God.”

It did not save him. The torture was laid on still; and under it he was asked why he had uttered those retracting words. Men were not demons in those days—Romola clung to that thought, the way one clings to the memory of a familiar domestic sound when the city outside is howling—but the law of the chamber held that only concessions of guilt counted as reasons for release.

And the answer came, dreadful in its human doubleness: “I said it that I might seem good; tear me no more, I will tell you the truth.”

There were Florentine assessors there; words did not stay inside the chamber. These two retractions—one from confession, one from retraction—ran out into the streets like spilled oil, impossible to gather back. They entered Romola’s mind and made everything slide. Doubt, which she had tried to hold at the edge of her life, came and sat down at the hearth.

But another thought—sharp as a blade and just as cold—flashed through her. There would be a moment when he could speak without hope of escape; when dread of pain would no longer bargain with dread of falsehood; when they had brought him into the presence of death. When he was lifted above the people and looked on them for the last time, they could not hinder him from a final, decisive word.

I will be there.

Three days later, on the twenty-third of May, 1498, the Piazza was prepared again. A long narrow platform stretched across it from the Palazzo Vecchio towards the Tetta de’ Pisani. But there was no grove of fuel as before—no cunningly arranged thicket to pretend at some old rite. Instead, there was one great heap of fuel set on the circular area at the platform’s end: wood stacked as if it were winter provision for a poor quarter, except that this was provision for a different kind of hunger.

Above it rose a gibbet with three halters. It had two arms, and in spite of care to avoid resemblance—one arm sawn short—it still looked enough like a cross to make some beholders uneasy, as if the city had tried to disguise a sacrilege and failed.

On the marble terrace of the Palazzo stood three tribunals: one by the door for the Bishop, who would perform the degradation of Fra Girolamo and the two brethren chosen to suffer as his followers and accomplices; another for the Papal Commissaries, to pronounce them heretics and schismatics and deliver them to the secular arm; and a third near Marzocco, close to where the platform began, for the Gonfaloniere and the Eight, who were to pronounce sentence of death.

Again the Piazza was thronged with expectant faces; again there was to be a great fire kindled. In the mass pressed around the gibbet the expectation had the hard glitter of hatred, or the stony stare of curiosity that finds its entertainment in barbarity. Yet there were still those—Piagnoni, insulted, grieving, enduring—who kept a last thin thread of hope, that God would interpose with some sign, even now, to declare His servant.

And there were more, trembling as Romola trembled, who looked forward to that final moment not for a miracle of flame or storm, but for a sentence: “O people, I was innocent of deceit.”

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the Piazza, far from the marble terrace and its formal cruelty. Near her, also looking on with painful uncertainty, was a young Florentine of two-and-twenty, Jacopo Nardi—one of those rare men whose love of a great figure is not stronger than their desire to tell the truth about him.

He watched Romola’s struggle between shuddering horror and the yearning to witness what might happen at the last, and said to her with respectful gentleness, “Madonna, there is no need for you to look at these cruel things. I will tell you when he comes out of the Palazzo. Trust to me; I know what you would see.”

Romola covered her face. But sound is a tyrant even when eyes are shut. The hootings rose and fell like a dirty tide; the crowd’s voices rubbed against one another—coarse laughter, splintered curses, the sharp, nasal mockery that turns human suffering into a jest. In the midst of it she heard other things too: the hollow tremor of the platform under many feet; a bell in the distance that kept its own slow truth; the faint clatter of metal that might have been a soldier’s harness, or might have been the implements waiting.

At last Jacopo touched her arm. “He comes.”

She looked.

Savonarola was led out in his Dominican garb. Even at that distance she could see how the robe hung on him, as if cloth itself had become heavy with the months of prison and strain. He was brought before the Bishop, and the ceremony began: stripped of the black mantle, the white scapulary, the long white tunic—piece by piece, like a household taking away a table’s linen after a feast has been declared sinful. There is something in degradation, apart from hatred, that the baser part of any multitude understands and enjoys: it is the satire of it, the sudden transformation of a man into “only a man,” as if that were humiliation enough.

When he stood in the close woollen under-tunic, telling of no sacred office and no rank, there rose another hoot of triumph. He had been degraded, cut off from the Church Militant; and to eyes eager for simple pictures, that was proof.

The three degraded brethren passed on to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries. “Schismatic,” “heretic”—the words rang out with a kind of official neatness, as if naming could finish the work and leave the speakers clean. Then they were led past the third tribunal—that of Florentine officials to pronounce the sentence; and even from her window, Romola could discern among them Dolfo Spini, odious in his grave black lucco, one of the Eight. His very stillness seemed like a sneer.

Then the three figures, in their close white raiment, began to tread along the platform amidst yells and grating tones of insult.

“Cover your eyes, Madonna,” Jacopo murmured. “Fra Girolamo will be the last.”

Romola did not keep them covered long. The compulsion to see was like thirst: it returned as soon as one tried to deny it.

Savonarola had mounted the steps. He was not far off now. She could see him look round upon the multitude.

For one instant the air seemed to pull itself taut, as when a cook pauses with the knife above the herb-bundle, listening for a child’s footfall—waiting for the one sound that will change what comes next. It was the instant of expectation, when even hatred wants its victim to play his part well.

But in that same moment, expectation died. Romola saw what he was seeing: torches waving to kindle the fuel beneath his dead body, their flames restless and hungry; faces glaring with a yet worse light than the torches gave. She heard what he was hearing: gross jests flung up at him like refuse, taunts and curses that tasted of sour wine and stale garlic in the breath that carried them.

The moment was past. Whatever word she had dreamed of—whatever final clear note that might cut through all this noise—was swallowed. She covered her face again; and though the Piazza still roared, and the city still strained to be heard, she knew only this: Savonarola’s voice had passed into eternal silence.

On the evening of the twenty-second of May, 1509, five persons whose lives had crossed and recrossed in ways none of them could have foreseen were seated in a handsome upper room opening onto a loggia. From its right-hand corner one could look along the Borgo Pinti and over the city gate towards Fiesole and the solemn heights beyond—those hills that kept their own old language of wind and bird-call when cities forgot.

At one end of the room an archway opened into a narrow inner recess where light fell from above on a small altar covered with fair white linen. Over it hung a picture, discernible at the distance of the seated party only as the small full-length portrait of a Dominican Brother. Branches and wreaths of flowers shaded it from the overhead light; the fresh tapers below it were unlit, waiting. Yet the decoration was not complete. Part of the floor was strewn with green boughs and a confusion of flowers—some still cool with evening, some already breathing out sweetness as they warmed.

Among them sat a delicate blue-eyed girl of thirteen, tossing her long light-brown hair out of her eyes as she chose blossoms for the wreaths she was weaving, or looked up at her mother’s work and told her how to do it with an air of gentle authority—like one correcting the pinch of salt in a sauce, not out of pride but out of instinctive rightness.

For Tessa was not clever at weaving flowers, nor had her fingers grown more adroit with time—only much fatter. She went slowly, turning her head this way and that, asking Ninna’s opinion with sincere deference. Tessa never ceased to be astonished at the wisdom of her children. She still wore her contadina gown—only broader now; and the silver pin was in her rough curly brown hair. Around her neck lay the memorable necklace: a red cord under it, ending mysteriously in her bosom. Her rounded face had an even more perfect look of childish content than it had borne in youth: everybody was so good, she thought. Even Monna Brigida never found fault now; she did little else than sleep—which, to Tessa’s mind, was an amiable practice and one she herself enjoyed.

Monna Brigida was asleep at that moment in a straight-backed arm-chair a couple of yards away. Her hair, parting backward under her black hood, had that soft whiteness that is like nothing else—simply the lovely whiteness of aged hair. Her chin had sunk on her bosom; her hands rested on the chair’s elbow. She had not been weaving or helping; she had been looking on, as usual, and as usual had fallen asleep.

The other two figures were seated farther off, at the wide doorway opening onto the loggia. Lillo sat on the ground with his back against the angle of the doorpost, long legs stretched out, a large book open on his knee. Now and then he made a dash at an inquisitive fly with a livelier interest than he gave to the finely printed copy of Petrarch held open at one place, as if he were learning something by heart.

Romola sat nearly opposite him, but her eyes were fixed absently on the distant mountains. Life’s eager strain had left its marks: the finely moulded cheek had sunk a little; the golden crown of hair was less massive. Yet there was a placidity in her face that had never belonged to it in youth, as if the worst sorrows—known once, while life was new—had burned away a certain fever of expectation and left only a steadier warmth.

At first she was not aware that Lillo had stopped looking at his book and was watching her with a slightly impatient air—wanting to talk, but unsure whether she would accept the interruption. But persevering looks make themselves felt. Romola turned from the mountains and met his dark gaze; her smile grew brighter as she looked at him, as if he had called her back by sound alone.

He shuffled closer, still keeping the book on his lap, until he could lodge his chin on her knee.

“What is it, Lillo?” she said, pulling his hair back from his brow.

He was a handsome lad, but his features were turning more massive and less regular than his father’s: the Tuscan peasant blood showed itself, like the darker grain in a loaf made from mixed flours.

“Mamma—Romola, what am I to be?” he asked, well content that there was now a prospect of talk until it would be too late to con “Spirto gentil” any longer.

“What should you like to be, Lillo? You might be a scholar. My father was a scholar, you know, and taught me a great deal. That is why I can teach you.”

“Yes,” Lillo said, hesitating. “But he is old and blind in the picture. Did he get a great deal of glory?”

“Not much, Lillo. The world was not always kind to him. He saw meaner men put into higher places because they could flatter and say what was false. And then his dear son thought it right to leave him and become a monk; after that my father, blind and lonely, felt unable to do the things that would have made his learning of greater use to men—so that he might still have lived in his works after he was in his grave.”

“I should not like that sort of life,” Lillo said promptly. “I should like to be something that would make me a great man, and very happy besides—something that would not hinder me from having a good deal of pleasure.”

“That is not easy, my Lillo,” Romola said. “It is only a poor sort of happiness that comes from caring very much about our own narrow pleasures. The highest happiness—the kind that can go with being a great man—comes from wide thoughts and much feeling for the rest of the world as well as ourselves. And often it brings so much pain with it that we can only tell it from pain by this: that we would choose it before everything else, because our souls see it is good.”

Her voice had that quiet rhythm which, in a house, can steady even restless children: not the rhythm of a sermon shouted at a crowd, but of counsel given while hands are busy and flowers are being sorted. Outside, through the open doorway, Florence had its usual evening sounds—distant footsteps, a cart rumbling where stones were uneven, a calling voice softened by distance. In the loggia the air carried the scent of bruised greenery and blossom; every movement among the strewn boughs released another small cloud of sweetness.

“There are so many things wrong and difficult in the world,” Romola went on, “that no man can be great—he can hardly keep himself from wickedness—unless he gives up thinking much about pleasure or rewards, and gets strength to endure what is hard and painful. My father had the greatness that belongs to integrity; he chose poverty and obscurity rather than falsehood. And there was Fra Girolamo—you know why I keep to-morrow sacred: he had the greatness that belongs to a life spent in struggling against powerful wrong, and trying to raise men to the highest deeds they are capable of.”

Lillo’s eyes were fixed on her now, no longer wandering to the fly.

“So, my Lillo,” she said, “if you mean to act nobly and seek to know the best things God has put within reach of men, you must learn to fix your mind on that end, and not on what will happen to you because of it. Remember, if you were to choose something lower and make it the rule of your life to seek your own pleasure and escape what is disagreeable—calamity might come just the same. And it would be calamity falling on a base mind. That is the one form of sorrow that has no balm in it, and that may well make a man say, ‘It would have been better for me if I had never been born.’”

She paused, as if the words had brought a taste into her mouth that she did not wish to deepen. Her hands took Lillo’s cheeks between them; his young eyes met hers, intent and a little troubled.

“I will tell you something, Lillo,” she said.

He waited, still as a child listening to an old tale—half craving its horror, half afraid of it.

“There was a man,” Romola said, and her voice grew unsteady, “to whom I was very near, so that I could see a great deal of his life. He made almost every one fond of him: he was young, clever, beautiful, and his manners were gentle and kind. I believe, when I first knew him, he never thought of anything cruel or base. But because he tried to slip away from everything unpleasant, and cared for nothing so much as his own safety—he came at last to commit some of the basest deeds. He denied his father and left him to misery; he betrayed every trust that was reposed in him, that he might keep himself safe and get rich and prosperous. Yet calamity overtook him.”

Again she paused. The room was full of small domestic sounds—the dry whisper of petals as Ninna chose them, the faint scrape of Tessa’s clumsy fingers among stems, the soft breathing of Monna Brigida asleep. These were sounds that kept memory from becoming only a story: they insisted on the present.

Lillo looked up at Romola with awed wonder, as if he had been shown, in a single sentence, how a familiar dialect can be turned against itself—how words like “safety,” “prosperity,” “pleasure” can hide a cruelty that does not name itself.

“Another time, my Lillo,” Romola said, drawing back from what she might have confessed further. “I will tell you another time. See—there are our old Piero di Cosimo and Nello coming up the Borgo Pinti, bringing us their flowers. Let us go and wave our hands to them, that they may know we see them.”

They rose and stood at the corner of the loggia, looking down the street. The evening air brought with it the city’s layered murmur, softened now: a vendor’s last call, a shutter closing, the distant splash of water poured into a basin. Under it all, like a remembered refrain, Florence carried the older sounds of human life at rest—so different from the roar Romola had heard on the day of fire.

“How queer old Piero is!” Lillo said, as the two figures approached, burdened with their flowers, the greenery bobbing with each step. “He abuses you for dressing the altar and thinking so much of Fra Girolamo, and yet he brings you the flowers.”

“Never mind,” Romola said quietly. “There are many good people who did not love Fra Girolamo. Perhaps I should never have learned to love him if he had not helped me when I was in great need.”

And as she waved, the gesture seemed to bind together what Florence so often tore apart: the public cry and the private grief, the remembered voice and the last silence, the wreaths being woven by a child’s sure hands and the unlit tapers waiting beneath a painted Dominican’s calm gaze.
