The following morning, as Elinor and Marianne strolled together through the garden, Marianne broke the silence with a revelation that left her sister momentarily stunned. Despite Elinor's prior knowledge of Marianne's impulsiveness and lack of foresight, the sheer extravagance of this new development caught her completely off guard. With radiant enthusiasm, Marianne announced that Willoughby had gifted her a horse—a magnificent animal he had personally bred on his Somersetshire estate, perfectly suited to carry a woman.

Marianne had accepted the gift without a second thought, failing to consider the logistical nightmare it presented. Her mother had explicitly ruled against keeping horses; altering that decision would require purchasing a mount for the servant, hiring an additional staff member to tend to it, and constructing a stable to house them. Yet, Marianne spoke of the arrangement in raptures. "He intends to send his groom to Somersetshire immediately for it," she exclaimed. "Once it arrives, we shall ride every single day. You must share its use with me, Elinor! Just imagine the joy of galloping across those downs!"

Elinor found herself desperately reluctant to shatter this vision of happiness with harsh realities. She reasoned that the cost of an extra servant would be negligible; surely her mother would never object. Any ordinary horse would suffice for the man-servant, who could likely find one at the park, and even a simple shed would serve as a stable. However, Elinor dared to question the propriety of accepting such a substantial gift from a man she barely knew.

"You are mistaken, Elinor," Marianne retorted, warming to her defense. "Do not suppose I know Willoughby poorly. While I have not known him long, my acquaintance with him surpasses that of anyone else in the world save yourself and Mother. Intimacy is not determined by time or opportunity, but solely by disposition. Seven years might fail to bring two people close, while seven days can be more than enough for others. I would feel far greater impropriety in accepting a horse from my brother John, whom I have known for years yet understand little of, than from Willoughby, whose character I have thoroughly judged."

Seeing that pressing this point further would only inflame Marianne's sensitive nature, Elinor wisely chose to drop the argument. Instead, she appealed to Marianne's deep love for her mother, painting a vivid picture of the inconvenience and burden such an expansion of the household would impose upon their kind-hearted parent. Slowly, Marianne yielded. She promised not to tempt her mother into imprudence by mentioning the offer and vowed to decline Willoughby's gift the next time she saw him. True to her word, when Willoughby visited the cottage later that day, Elinor heard Marianne express her regret in a hushed tone regarding her inability to accept the present. She explained the reasons clearly, rendering any further persuasion on Willoughby's part futile.

His distress was palpable. After voicing his sorrow earnestly, he leaned closer and whispered, "Yet, Marianne, the horse remains yours, even if you cannot use it now. I shall keep it merely until you are ready to claim it. When you depart Barton to establish your own permanent home, 'Queen Mab' awaits you."

Miss Dashwood, listening intently, seized upon every nuance of his speech—the intimate use of her sister's first name, the directness of his tone, and the unmistakable familiarity in his manner. In that instant, she perceived a bond so profound and explicit that it signaled a mutual engagement. There was no shock in realizing they were betrothed; rather, she marveled that two individuals of such open natures could allow their union to remain hidden until discovered by chance.

Later, Margaret added weight to Elinor's suspicions. Having spent the previous evening alone in the drawing-room with Willoughby and Marianne, Margaret had witnessed events that confirmed her elder sister's fears. With a grave expression, she approached Elinor. "Oh, Elinor!" she cried. "I have a secret concerning Marianne. I am certain she will marry Mr. Willoughby very soon."

"You have suggested this daily since they first met on High-church Down," Elinor replied dryly. "We hadn't known each other a week before you were convinced she wore his portrait around her neck, only to discover it was merely a miniature of our great-uncle."

"But this is entirely different!" Margaret insisted. "They will wed soon because he possesses a lock of her hair."

"Be careful, Margaret. It might simply be the hair of some great-uncle of *his*."

"No, truly, Elinor, it is Marianne's. I am nearly certain, for I saw him cut it off. Last night, after tea, when you and Mother left the room, they were whispering furiously. He seemed to beg something of her, then snatched her scissors, severed a long lock from her tumbling hair, kissed it reverently, folded it in white paper, and tucked it safely into his pocket-book."

Given the gravity of Margaret's account and its alignment with what Elinor had observed firsthand, she could not deny the truth of it. However, Margaret's insight often lacked tact. One evening at the Park, when Mrs. Jennings persistently demanded the name of Elinor's favorite young man—a query that had long amused the group—Margaret responded by glancing at her sister and asking, "May I not tell, Elinor?"

The comment drew laughter from everyone, including Elinor, though the effort to smile was painful. She suspected Margaret had selected a suitor whose identity she could not comfortably discuss as a public jest. Marianne, feeling genuine sympathy for her friend, inadvertently exacerbated the situation by flushing angrily and telling Margaret, "Remember that regardless of your conjectures, you have no right to repeat them."

"I never made any conjectures," Margaret countered cheerfully. "It was you who told me yourself."

This only fueled the merriment, prompting Mrs. Jennings to press further. "Pray, Miss Margaret, reveal everything. What is the gentleman's name? Where does he reside?"

"I cannot tell, ma'am. But I know precisely who he is and where he lives."

"Yes, yes, we can guess. At his own home in Norland, undoubtedly. He is the parish curate, I presume."

"No, *he* is not," Marianne interjected sharply. "He holds no profession whatsoever."

"Well, then, he must be recently deceased, Marianne, for I am sure such a man existed once, and his name begins with an F."

At this critical juncture, Elinor breathed a sigh of relief when Lady Middleton remarked, "It is raining very heavily." Though Elinor suspected the interruption stemmed more from Lady Middleton's distaste for the frivolous banter enjoyed by her husband and mother than from any consideration for Elinor's discomfort, the change of subject proved timely. Colonel Brandon, ever attentive to the feelings of others, eagerly joined in discussing the weather, successfully steering the conversation away from the awkward topic.

Willoughby then rose, opening the pianoforte and inviting Marianne to play, allowing the tension to dissipate amidst music. Yet, Elinor remained unsettled by the implications of the afternoon. That evening, plans were made for a grand excursion the following day to view a magnificent estate located twelve miles from Barton. The property belonged to a brother-in-law of Colonel Brandon; access was strictly controlled by the absent owner, whose instructions required the intervention of Colonel Brandon to arrange a viewing.

Sir John, a frequent visitor who had organized trips there twice annually for the past decade, enthusiastically praised the grounds as exceptionally beautiful. The itinerary included sailing on a large lake, picnicking with cold provisions, and traveling exclusively in open carriages, adhering to the traditional style of a leisure outing. However, the timing raised concerns among some guests, given the relentless rainfall over the previous fortnight. Consequently, Mrs. Dashwood, already suffering from a cold, was gently persuaded by Elinor to remain behind at home.
