“Through cross to crown!—And though thy spirit’s life
Trials untold assail with giant strength,
Good cheer! good cheer! Soon ends the bitter strife,
And thou shalt reign in peace with Christ at length.”

“Ay sooth, we feel too strong in weal, to need Thee on that road;
But woe being come, the soul is dumb, that crieth not on ‘God.’”

That afternoon, Margaret walked swiftly toward the Higgins household. Mary stood near the door, her face pale and uncertain. Margaret smiled gently, seeking to calm her friend’s anxiety. They passed through the quiet house, up the narrow stairs, and into the stillness of the room where Bessie lay. There, Margaret found a strange peace. The face, once worn by pain and restless thoughts, now bore a soft, quiet smile—the mark of rest eternal. Tears gathered in Margaret’s eyes, but within her heart, a deep serenity settled. This was death—not as she had feared, but as something gentle, more peaceful than life itself. Verses from Scripture rose in her mind: “They rest from their labours.” “The weary are at rest.” “He giveth his beloved sleep.”

Slowly, she turned away from the bed. Mary knelt in silence behind, her sobs barely heard. Together, they descended the stairs without a word.

Nicholas Higgins stood in the centre of the room, one hand resting on the kitchen table. His eyes were wide, stunned by the news he had just received—his daughter dead. He had been told while walking through the court, his mind overwhelmed by voices and chaos. Now, he stared blankly, dry-eyed, trying to grasp the truth. She had been ill for so long that he had convinced himself she would recover. That she would “pull through.” But now, she was gone.

Margaret felt out of place—too close to this moment of grief, too familiar with death, when he, her father, had only just begun to feel it. For an instant, she hesitated on the stair, then tried to move past him, quietly, respectfully, leaving him to his sorrow.

Mary sank onto the nearest chair, pulled her apron over her head, and wept.

The sound seemed to awaken Nicholas. Suddenly, he seized Margaret’s arm, gripping it tightly as if afraid she might vanish. His voice came thick and strained, choked by emotion.

“Were you with her? Did you see her die?”

“No,” Margaret replied, standing still, patient and composed, now that she was noticed.

A long silence followed. He held her arm, lost in thought.

“All men must die,” he said at last, his voice heavy with a strange solemnity. It struck Margaret that he might have drunk—just enough to blur his thoughts, not enough to make him drunk. “But she was younger than me.”

He kept staring ahead, not looking at her, yet holding her fast. Then, abruptly, he looked up, his gaze wild, searching.

“Are you sure she’s dead? Not in a faint? Not dreaming? She’s been like that before—often.”

“She is dead,” Margaret answered firmly. She did not fear him, even as his grip tightened and confusion flickered across his face.

“She is dead,” she repeated.

His eyes lingered on hers, then slowly dimmed. At last, he released her. With a sudden, violent cry, he threw himself half across the table, shaking the furniture, sobbing uncontrollably. Mary trembled, stepping forward.

“Get away! Get away!” he shouted, striking wildly at her. “What do I care for you?”

Margaret took Mary’s hand, holding it softly. He tore at his hair, beat his head against the wooden wall, until he collapsed, spent and hollow. Still, neither girl moved. Mary shook with silent terror.

After what felt like hours, Nicholas lifted himself. His eyes were swollen, bloodshot. He ignored them at first, then scowled when he saw them watching. He gave them one harsh glance, said nothing, and moved toward the door.

“Oh, father, father!” Mary cried, throwing herself at his arm. “Not tonight! Any night but tonight. Help me—he’s going out to drink again! Father, I won’t leave you. You may strike me, but I won’t go. She told me—she said, ‘Keep your father from drink!’”

Margaret stood in the doorway, silent but firm. He turned to her, defiant.

“It’s my own house. Stand out of the way, girl, or I’ll throw you aside!”

He had already pushed Mary away violently. He looked ready to strike Margaret—but she did not flinch. Her eyes remained steady, calm, unshaken. He glared back, furious, but something in her presence held him.

“What are you staring at me like that for?” he asked finally, shaken by her quiet strength. “You think you can stop me from going where I please—because she loved you? In my own house, where I never asked you in—you’re wrong. It’s hard on a man to be denied the only comfort left.”

Margaret sensed he acknowledged her power. What could she do now?

He sat down near the door, half-defeated, half-resisting. He meant to leave as soon as she stepped aside—but he no longer dared use violence. Margaret placed her hand gently on his arm.

“Come with me,” she said. “Come and see her.”

Her voice was low, grave—but without fear or doubt. He rose slowly, reluctantly. He paused, stubborn, testing her patience. She waited—calm, unwavering. He enjoyed making her wait. But eventually, he moved toward the stairs.

They stood together beside the body.

“Her last words to Mary were, ‘Keep my father from drink.’”

“It can’t hurt her now,” he muttered. “Nothing can hurt her now.” Then, raising his voice in a broken cry, “We may quarrel, fall out—we may make peace—we may fight till we’re skin and bone—and none of it will touch her. She’s had her share. Hard work first, sickness at last—she lived like a dog. And to die without ever knowing joy! No, girl, whatever she said, she knows nothing now. And I must have a drink—to steady myself.”

“No,” Margaret said, her tone softening. “You shall not. If her life was as you say, at least she didn’t fear death as others do. You should have heard her speak of the life to come—the life hidden with God. That is where she has gone.”

He shook his head, glanced sideways at her. His face was pale, gaunt—pained.

“You’ve been through much. Where have you been all day? Not at work?”

“Not at work,” he said, giving a short, grim laugh. “Not at what you call work. I was at the Committee, sickened by fools who wouldn’t listen. I was called to Boucher’s wife early this morning—she’s bedridden, raving about her husband, as if I were supposed to control him—as if he were fit to be ruled by me. That fool—ruined our plans. I’ve walked miles to find men who wouldn’t see me. The law is against us now. And I was heartbroken—worse than tired. And if I’d met a friend offering a drink—I didn’t know she was dying here. Bess, lass—you’d believe me, wouldn’t you?” He turned to the corpse, his voice pleading.

“I’m sure,” Margaret said, “you didn’t know. It was sudden. But now you do. You see her. You hear her last words. You won’t go?”

No answer. Where could he turn for comfort?

“Come home with me,” she said at last, trembling slightly, yet bold. “At least you’ll have food. I’m certain you need it.”

“Your father—he’s a parson?” he asked suddenly.

“He was,” she replied shortly.

“I’ll go take tea with him, since you ask. I’ve always wanted to talk to a parson. Doesn’t matter if he’s preaching or not.”

Margaret hesitated. Her father, unprepared—her mother ill—it seemed impossible. But if she refused now, he would surely go to the gin-shop. If she could bring him to their house, that would be a victory. The rest, she decided, would depend on chance.

“Goodbye, dear girl. We’ve parted at last. But you’ve been a blessing to your father since you were born. Bless your white lips—there’s a smile on them now. And I’m glad to see it, even if I’m alone forever.”

He bent down and kissed Mary tenderly, covered her face, then followed Margaret. She hurried downstairs to tell Mary of the plan—to keep him from the gin-shop. She urged Mary to come too, feeling guilty at leaving her alone. But Mary said neighbours would stay with her. “It’s all right. But father—”

He was already there. He had composed himself, ashamed of his earlier breakdown, even adopting a bitter mockery, like thorns crackling under a pot.

“I’m going to take tea with her father,” he declared.

Yet he pulled his cap low over his brow, avoiding eye contact as they walked. He feared the pitying looks of neighbours.

As they neared her street, he looked down at his clothes, hands, shoes.

“I should’ve cleaned up first.”

Margaret assured him he could wash at the pump, and he wouldn’t be allowed to slip away.

While he followed the servant through the passage, careful not to mark the oilcloth with dirt, Margaret ran upstairs. She met Dixon on the landing.

“How is mamma? Where is papa?”

Mrs. Hale was tired, resting in her room. She had wanted to sleep, but Dixon had persuaded her to lie on the sofa instead, with tea brought to her. Better than lying restless in bed.

So far, so good. But where was Mr. Hale? In the drawing-room. Margaret rushed in, breathless.

“Papa—Mr. Higgins is coming. He’s been drinking—very little, but enough. I brought him home to keep him from the gin-shop. He’s waiting in the study.”

Mr. Hale was startled. A drunken weaver—coming to his quiet home, while his wife was ill? He looked dismayed. Yet Margaret pleaded earnestly.

“Oh, papa—he’s not really drunk. Just upset. And he’s not like that usually. He’s a decent man—once you get past the shock.”

Mr. Hale took her face in his hands, kissed her forehead.

“It’s all right, dear. I’ll go see him. You attend to your mother. But if you can come in later, I’d be glad.”

“Oh, yes—thank you.” As he turned to leave, she caught him.

“Papa—don’t judge him by what he says. He doesn’t believe in our ways. He’s… well, he’s not religious.”

“Oh dear,” Mr. Hale murmured to himself. “A drunken infidel weaver.” But aloud, he only said, “If your mother sleeps, come directly.”

Margaret went to her mother’s room. Mrs. Hale stirred.

“When did you write to Frederick, Margaret? Yesterday or the day before?”

“Yesterday, mamma.”

“And the letter went?”

“Yes. I carried it myself.”

“Oh, Margaret, I’m so afraid he’ll come! If he’s recognized! If he’s taken! If he’s executed after all these years of hiding! I keep dreaming he’s caught, tried…”

“Oh, mamma, don’t worry. There’s some risk—but we’ll minimize it. And here, no one knows us. Dixon will guard the door—won’t you, Dixon?”

“They’ll be clever if they get past me!” Dixon grinned fiercely.

“And he needn’t go out—only at dusk.”

“Poor fellow,” echoed Mrs. Hale. “But I almost wish you hadn’t written. Could we stop him now?”

“I’m afraid not, mamma,” Margaret said, remembering how urgently she had begged him to come.

“I hate things done in such haste,” Mrs. Hale sighed.

Margaret was silent.

“Now, ma'am,” Dixon said firmly, “seeing Master Frederick is exactly what you’ve been longing for. And I’m glad Miss Margaret wrote straightaway. I’ve thought of doing it myself. We’ll keep him safe. Only Martha might be a problem—she’d help him if needed. But I’ll send her to visit her mother—she’s been wanting to go. Her mother had a stroke. So trust me. Take your tea and rest.”

Mrs. Hale trusted Dixon more than Margaret. Her fears eased. Margaret poured tea in silence, struggling to think of something pleasant. But her mind clung to the danger—Frederick’s peril. Her mother chatted with Dixon, forgetting the threat. She was one who scattered fears like sparks—until they caught fire. Margaret was relieved when she could go downstairs.

In the study, she found her father and Higgins engaged in quiet conversation. Mr. Hale, courteous and kind, had drawn out the best in the rough man. He treated Nicholas with respect—calling him “Mr. Higgins,” placing a chair, standing until he sat. Nicholas, now clean, tidy, and composed, seemed transformed. He had washed his face, smoothed his hair, polished his clogs with a candle stub. He spoke earnestly, with a lowered voice, despite his thick accent.

Margaret watched, surprised and pleased. Her father listened intently. He smiled as she entered, offered her his chair, then sat again quickly, bowing slightly in apology.

Higgins nodded at her. She quietly arranged her knitting, preparing to listen.

“As I was saying, sir,” Nicholas began, “if you’d grown up here, you’d likely not believe much. I beg your pardon for my words—but I mean belief as thinking on promises made by people you never saw, about lives you’ve never known. You say they’re true. I say—where’s the proof? I see wise men, learned men around me—men who’ve had time to ponder. Their lives are open to me. They don’t believe in the Bible—not truly. They may say they do, but their first thought each morning isn’t, ‘How do I hold on to eternal life?’ It’s, ‘How do I fill my purse today? Where shall I go? What bargains can I make?’ The purse, the gold—those are real. Eternal life is talk. Fit for—well, forgive me, sir—you’re a parson out of work, I believe. I won’t speak disrespectfully. But let me ask—suppose salvation, life to come, were truly real—wouldn’t they preach it as they preach political economy? They’re eager to convert us to that. But this? That would be a greater conversion—if it were true.”

“But the masters aren’t concerned with your religion. They only deal with you in trade—so they think. And all they want to fix is trade.”

“I’m glad,” Nicholas said, with a curious wink, “you said ‘so they think.’ Otherwise, I’d call you a hypocrite. Though you’re a parson, or rather because you are. If you said religion wasn’t urgent—didn’t matter above all else—then I’d think you a knave. But I’d rather think you a fool than a knave. No offence, sir.”

“None at all. You think me mistaken. I think you more dangerously mistaken. I won’t convince you in a day. But let us know each other. Speak freely. Truth will prevail. I believe in God because I believe in truth. Mr. Higgins—I trust, whatever else you’ve given up—you believe”—his voice dropped reverently—“you believe in Him.”

Nicholas stiffened, stood rigid. Margaret jumped to her feet—thinking he might collapse. Mr. Hale looked alarmed.

“Man!” Nicholas burst out. “I could knock you down for tempting me. What right have you to question me now? Think of her—lying there, after her life. And then deny me the one comfort—that there is a God, and He gave her peace. I don’t believe she’ll live again,” he said, sinking down, speaking to the fire. “I don’t believe in any other life. Only this one—where she suffered so much. I can’t bear to think it was all chance—could have changed with a breath. Many times I’ve thought I didn’t believe in God—but I never said it plainly. I’ve laughed at those who did—but I’ve looked around afterward, wondering if He heard me. But today, when I’m left alone—I won’t listen to your doubts. There’s only one thing steady in this world—and I’ll cling to it, reason or no reason. It’s easy for happy folk—”

Margaret touched his arm gently. She had not spoken before.

“Nicholas—we don’t argue. You misunderstand my father. We don’t argue—we believe. And so do you. That’s the only comfort in times like this.”

He turned, grasped her hand.

“Ay! It is. It is.” He wiped his eyes. “But you know—she lies dead at home. I’m dazed with sorrow. I don’t know what I’m saying. It’s like the smart things I’ve heard—clever speeches—rising up now that my heart’s broken. The strike failed, you know—did you hear? I was coming home to ask her for comfort—like a beggar. And I was told she was dead—just dead. That was all. But it was enough.”
