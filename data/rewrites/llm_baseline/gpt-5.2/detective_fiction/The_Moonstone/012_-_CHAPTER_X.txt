One after another the Ablewhites arrived and were folded into place until the dining-room held them all—four-and-twenty souls, family and followers together—like a harvest sheaf bound tight with manners. It was, to do it justice, a handsome muster when they had settled round the long table, glass catching candle, linen shining like new-milled flour; and the Rector of Frizinghall rose with his beautiful elocution and laid grace over us as neatly as sifted sugar.

I will not trouble you with a catalogue of the guests. The greater part of them vanish from my sight after this night, and (in my share of these matters at least) never come back again—except for two persons placed, by chance or fate, on either side of Miss Rachel.

She sat there as queen of the day, pale and proud in her white dress; and the birthday gift—more than any cake, more than any toast, more than any compliment—drew eyes to her bosom as naturally as scent draws bees. The Diamond had come to her without setting; but Mr. Franklin, being that universal genius who will make a tool out of anything and an instrument out of nothing, had contrived with neat fingers and a twist of silver wire to fix the Moonstone as a brooch. It lay against the white stuff like a bit of captured moonlight pinned to snow.

Everybody, of course, admired the prodigious size and the pure fire of it. But only the two who sat nearest said anything that went beyond the ordinary gasp of envy. On Miss Rachel’s left was Mr. Candy, our doctor from Frizinghall; on her right sat the celebrated Indian traveller, Mr. Murthwaite.

Mr. Candy was a pleasant little man to have by you—companionable, quick to laugh, quick to make you laugh too, though (if you ask me) overfond of his joke in season and out, and forever plunging into talk with strangers without testing the depth first. In company, that habit of his set people by the ears without his intending it; in practice, oddly enough, he was a safer hand—picking up discretion by instinct, so his enemies said, and proving right where grave doctors, full of carefulness, proved wrong.

His remark on the Diamond came out in his usual way—half profession, half mystification. He leaned toward Miss Rachel with a grave face that could never quite hide the twinkle at the edge of it, and entreated her, in the interests of science, to let him take the jewel home and burn it.

“We will first heat it, Miss Rachel,” says the doctor, “to such and such a degree; then we will expose it to a current of air; and, little by little—puff!—we evaporate the Diamond, and spare you a world of anxiety about the safe keeping of a valuable precious stone!”

My lady, who had been eating little and watching much, listened with a careworn look, as if she could have wished the doctor’s chemistry were no jest and her daughter’s courage zealous enough to sacrifice the gift for peace of mind.

Mr. Murthwaite, on the other side, made a stranger contrast—long, lean, wiry, brown as if sun-dried by other climates, silent as a man whose ears have been taught by deserts and jungles to hoard sound. His eye, steady and attentive, seemed always to be listening as much as looking; and I have often thought there is a kind of hearing in the gaze of such men—an attention that takes the measure of a room as a farmer takes the measure of weather by the trees.

It was rumoured he was sick to death of our humdrum English talk and longing to be off again where the world speaks in unfamiliar tongues. I doubt if he uttered six words to any one all dinner, or drank even a single glass of wine. The Moonstone was the one thing that roused him. The fame of it, it seemed, had travelled even to those perilous Indian places where his own footsteps had been laid.

He looked at the Diamond so long in silence that Miss Rachel began to colour under his regard; then he spoke to her in his cool, immovable way, as if he were stating a fact about weather.

“If you ever go to India, Miss Verinder, don’t take your uncle’s birthday gift with you. A Hindoo diamond is sometimes part of a Hindoo religion. I know a certain city, and a certain temple in that city, where, dressed as you are now, your life would not be worth five minutes’ purchase.”

Miss Rachel, safe in England, was delighted—delighted!—to hear of her danger in India. The two Miss Ablewhites (whom I call the Bouncers, as I have called them before, for their way of bursting out) were more delighted still; down went their knives and forks with a crash, and out came together, like a chorus in a cheap song, “O! how interesting!” My lady fidgeted, and changed the subject as if she had been stung.

As the dinner went on, I began to feel, little by little, that our festival did not prosper as other festivals have prospered. Looking back now—by the light of all that followed—I am half inclined to believe the cursed Diamond cast a blight on the company, the way a bad smell will spoil a whole kitchen, though the roast be sound and the sauce made to perfection.

And mind you, our table was no poor one. There were dishes sent up that had cost good money and good thought; there were wines that might have coaxed a parson into a second glass and a miser into a smile. I plied them well, I can tell you. Being privileged as old Betteredge is privileged, I followed the unpopular dishes round with a confidential whisper—“Please to change your mind and try it; for I know it will do you good.” Nine times out of ten they changed their minds, out of regard for me they were pleased to say. It did no good. The talk ran into gaps of silence that made me feel personally responsible, as if I had under-seasoned the very air. When tongues did start again, they started innocently and fell into the worst possible mischief—like a cook who means well and drops vinegar where sugar should go.

Mr. Candy, for one, was more unlucky than I ever knew him.

Among the ladies sat worthy Mrs. Threadgall, widow of the late Professor. She talked of her husband perpetually, and never mentioned to strangers that he was dead. She seemed to think every able-bodied adult in England ought to know it of course. In one of the silences, somebody touched on the dry and rather nasty subject of human anatomy; and Mrs. Threadgall at once brought in the Professor, as usual, without saying he was deceased. Anatomy, she declared, had been his favourite recreation in his leisure hours.

Unluckily Mr. Candy, opposite, knew nothing of the Professor’s ten years in the grave. Being politeness itself, he seized the chance of being useful on the spot.

“They have got some remarkably fine skeletons lately at the College of Surgeons,” says Mr. Candy, across the table in a loud cheerful voice. “I strongly recommend the Professor, ma’am, when he next has an hour to spare, to pay them a visit.”

You might have heard a pin fall into the gravy. Out of respect to the Professor’s memory the company sat speechless. I was behind Mrs. Threadgall at the time, offering her, in my private way, a glass of hock. She dropped her head and said, very low:

“My beloved husband is no more.”

Mr. Candy heard nothing, and suspected less. On he went, louder and politer than ever.

“The Professor may not be aware,” says he, “that the card of a member of the College will admit him, on any day but Sunday, between the hours of ten and four.”

Mrs. Threadgall dropped her head right into her tucker and, lower still, repeated:

“My beloved husband is no more.”

I winked hard at Mr. Candy. Miss Rachel touched his arm. My lady looked unutterable things. All useless. The doctor’s good breeding drove him on like a horse with blinkers.

“I shall be delighted,” says he, “to send the Professor my card, if you will oblige me by mentioning his present address.”

“His present address, sir, is the grave,” cries Mrs. Threadgall, temper bursting out at last, emphasis and fury making the glasses ring again. “The Professor has been dead these ten years.”

“Oh, good Heavens!” says Mr. Candy.

Excepting the Bouncers, who burst into laughter as if a funeral were a fair, such a blank fell then on the company that you might have thought we were all going the same way as the Professor, and hailing him from the direction of the earth.

So much for Mr. Candy; and the rest were nearly as provoking, each in their own manner. When they ought to have spoken, they did not; when they did speak, they managed to speak at cross purposes, like a pair of fiddlers playing different tunes in the same room. Mr. Godfrey, who can be eloquent enough in public to make a collection-box tremble, declined to exert himself in private. Whether he was sulky or bashful after his discomfiture in the rose-garden, I cannot say. He kept his talk for the private ear of the lady of our family next to him—one of his committee-women; spiritually minded, with a fine show of collar-bone and a pretty taste in champagne. Dry, you understand, and plenty of it.

Being stationed close behind them at the sideboard, drawing corks and carving mutton, I picked up scraps of what the company lost. What they said about Charities I did not catch. When I had time to listen, they were beyond women to be confined and women to be rescued, and disputing on serious subjects, with the clink of glass and the soft, greedy sound of wine into crystal marking their pauses.

Religion, I understand Mr. Godfrey to say, meant love; and love meant religion; and earth was heaven a little the worse for wear; and heaven was earth done up again to look like new. Earth had some very objectionable people in it; but, to make amends, all the women in heaven would be members of a prodigious committee that never quarrelled, with all the men in attendance as ministering angels. Beautiful! beautiful! But why the mischief did he keep it all to his lady and himself, and let the rest of us sit chewing our words as well as our food?

You will tell me Mr. Franklin at least must have stirred them to better spirits.

Nothing of the sort.

He had quite recovered himself and was in wonderful force and animation—Penelope having, I suspect, informed him of Mr. Godfrey’s reception in the rose-garden. But talk as he might, nine times out of ten he pitched on the wrong subject or addressed the wrong person; so that he offended some and puzzled all. That foreign training of his—the French and German and Italian sides I have spoken of—came out at my lady’s hospitable board in a manner bewildering enough to curdle the best cream.

Imagine him discussing the lengths to which a married woman might let admiration go for a man who was not her husband—and putting it, too, in his clear-headed, witty French way—to the maiden aunt of the Vicar of Frizinghall. Imagine him shifting to the German side and telling the lord of the manor, while that great authority on cattle quoted his experience in breeding bulls, that experience, properly understood, counted for nothing; that the proper way to breed bulls was to look deep into your own mind, evolve the idea of a perfect bull, and produce him. Imagine our county member, heated at cheese and salad time about the spread of democracy, bursting out, “If we once lose our ancient safeguards, Mr. Blake, I beg to ask you, what have we got left?”—and Mr. Franklin answering from the Italian point of view, “We have got three things left, sir—Love, Music, and Salad.”

He terrified them with outbreaks like these; and when the English side of him turned up in its turn, he lost his foreign smoothness and spoke so downright on the subject of doctors that he actually put good-humoured little Mr. Candy into a rage.

The dispute began in Mr. Franklin being led—how, I forget—to admit he had slept badly of late. Mr. Candy told him his nerves were out of order and he ought to go through a course of medicine immediately. Mr. Franklin replied that a course of medicine and a course of groping in the dark were, in his estimation, one and the same thing. Mr. Candy hit back smartly that Mr. Franklin himself, constitutionally speaking, was groping in the dark after sleep and nothing but medicine could help him to find it. Mr. Franklin kept the ball up: he had often heard of the blind leading the blind, and now for the first time he knew what it meant.

So they went on—cut and thrust—till they were both hot, and the doctor, especially, lost self-control in defence of his profession. My lady was obliged to interfere and forbid the dispute. That act of authority put the last extinguisher on the company. The talk spurted up here and there for a minute or two, like damp wood trying to catch; then sank again into miserable lifelessness. The Devil—or the Diamond—possessed that dinner-party; and it was a relief to everybody when my mistress rose and gave the ladies the signal to withdraw, leaving the gentlemen over their wine.

I had just ranged the decanters in a row before old Mr. Ablewhite (who represented the master of the house), when a sound from the terrace startled me clean out of my company manners. Mr. Franklin and I looked at each other.

It was the Indian drum.

As I live by bread, here were the jugglers returning—returning with the return of the Moonstone to the house!

As they rounded the terrace corner into sight, I hobbled out to warn them off. But ill-luck had its own feet that night. The two Bouncers were beforehand with me—whizzing out like a pair of rockets, wild to see the Indians exhibit their tricks. Other ladies followed; the gentlemen came out on their side. Before you could say “Lord bless us,” the rogues were making their salaams and the Bouncers were kissing the pretty little boy.

Mr. Franklin got on one side of Miss Rachel and I took my place behind her. If our suspicions were right, there she stood, innocent as milk, showing the Indians the Diamond pinned in her dress—putting, as it were, the sacred thing under their very noses.

I cannot tell you what tricks they performed or how. Between vexation at the dinner and provocation at the rogues coming back at the very nick of time, I lost my head. The first thing I clearly remember is the sudden appearance of Mr. Murthwaite.

Skirting the half-circle of gentlefolks, he came quietly behind the jugglers and spoke on a sudden in their own language.

If he had pricked them with a bayonet, I doubt they could have started and turned on him with a more tigerish quickness than they did at the first words. The next moment they were bowing and salaaming in their politest, snakiest way. A few sentences passed in that unknown tongue; then Mr. Murthwaite withdrew as quietly as he had approached.

The chief Indian—acting interpreter—wheeled again toward the company. I noticed his coffee-coloured face had turned grey since Mr. Murthwaite spoke to him. He bowed to my lady and said the exhibition was over. The Bouncers, indescribably disappointed, burst out with a loud “O!” aimed at Mr. Murthwaite for stopping the performance. The chief Indian laid his hand humbly on his breast and repeated that the juggling was over. The little boy went round with the hat. The ladies withdrew to the drawing-room; the gentlemen (excepting Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite) returned to their wine. I and the footman saw the Indians safe off the premises.

Going back by way of the shrubbery, I smelt tobacco and found Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite walking slowly among the trees—the traveller smoking a cheroot. There is a particular sound among shrubs at night—the leaves rubbing like paper, the distant clink from the dining-room, a footfall softened by grass—that makes a man feel he is eavesdropping on the house itself. It did not comfort me.

Mr. Franklin beckoned me.

“This,” says he, presenting me, “is Gabriel Betteredge, the old servant and friend of our family of whom I spoke to you just now. Tell him, if you please, what you have just told me.”

Mr. Murthwaite took the cheroot from his mouth and leaned, weary as ever, against a tree trunk.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he said, “those three Indians are no more jugglers than you and I are.”

It struck me dumb. I asked if he had met them before.

“Never,” says he. “But I know what Indian juggling really is. All you have seen tonight is a very bad and clumsy imitation of it. Unless, after long experience, I am utterly mistaken, those men are high-caste Brahmins. I charged them with being disguised, and you saw how it told on them—clever as the Hindoo people are in concealing their feelings. There is a mystery about their conduct that I can’t explain. They have doubly sacrificed their caste—first, in crossing the sea; secondly, in disguising themselves as jugglers. In the land they live in, that is a tremendous sacrifice. There must be some very serious motive at the bottom of it, and some justification of no ordinary kind to plead for them, in recovery of their caste, when they return.”

He went on smoking, as if caste and sacrifice were no more than weather signs. Mr. Franklin, who seemed to me to veer inwardly between the different sides of himself before he spoke, broke the silence.

“I hesitate, Mr. Murthwaite, to trouble you with family matters in which you can have no interest, and which I am not very willing to speak of out of our own circle. But, after what you have said, I feel bound, in the interests of Lady Verinder and her daughter, to tell you something that may possibly put the clue into your hands. I speak in confidence. You will oblige me by not forgetting that.”

With that he told him all he had told me at the Shivering Sand. Even Mr. Murthwaite, immovable as he was, grew so interested that he let his cheroot go out.

When Mr. Franklin had done, he asked, “What does your experience say?”

“My experience,” answered the traveller, “says that you have had more narrow escapes in your life, Mr. Franklin Blake, than I have had in mine—and that is saying a great deal.”

Mr. Franklin stared. “Is it really as serious as that?”

“In my opinion it is,” said Mr. Murthwaite. “I can’t doubt, after what you have told me, that the restoration of the Moonstone to its place on the forehead of the Indian idol is the motive and the justification of that sacrifice of caste I spoke of. Those men will wait their opportunity with the patience of cats, and use it with the ferocity of tigers. How you have escaped them I can’t imagine.” He lit his cheroot again and stared hard at Mr. Franklin. “You have been carrying the Diamond backwards and forwards in London and here, and you are still alive. Let us account for it. It was daylight, both times, when you took the jewel out of the bank in London?”

“Broad daylight,” says Mr. Franklin.

“And plenty of people in the streets?”

“Plenty.”

“You arranged to arrive here at a certain time? It’s lonely country between this and the station. Did you keep your appointment?”

“No. I arrived four hours earlier.”

“I beg to congratulate you on that proceeding! When did you take the Diamond to the bank at the town here?”

“An hour after I brought it to this house—and three hours before anybody was prepared for seeing me.”

“I beg to congratulate you again! Did you bring it back here alone?”

“No. I happened to ride back with my cousins and the groom.”

“I beg to congratulate you for the third time! If you ever feel inclined to travel beyond the civilised limits, Mr. Blake, let me know, and I will go with you. You are a lucky man.”

I could not let such talk pass without speaking, for it did not square with my English ideas.

“You don’t really mean to say, sir,” I asked, “that they would have taken Mr. Franklin’s life, to get their Diamond, if he had given them the chance?”

“Do you smoke, Mr. Betteredge?” says the traveller.

“Yes, sir.”

“Do you care much for the ashes left in your pipe when you empty it?”

“No, sir.”

“In the country those men came from, they care just as much about killing a man as you care about emptying the ashes out of your pipe. If a thousand lives stood between them and the getting back of their Diamond—and if they thought they could destroy those lives without discovery—they would take them all. The sacrifice of caste is serious in India, if you like. The sacrifice of life is nothing.”

I gave it as my opinion they were a set of murdering thieves. Mr. Murthwaite gave it as his opinion they were a wonderful people. Mr. Franklin, offering no opinion at all, brought us back to what mattered.

“They have seen the Moonstone on Miss Verinder’s dress,” he said. “What is to be done?”

“What your uncle threatened to do,” answered Mr. Murthwaite. “Colonel Herncastle understood the people he had to deal with. Send the Diamond tomorrow—under guard of more than one man—to be cut up at Amsterdam. Make half a dozen diamonds of it instead of one. There is an end of its sacred identity as the Moonstone—and there is an end of the conspiracy.”

Mr. Franklin turned to me. “There is no help for it. We must speak to Lady Verinder tomorrow.”

“What about tonight, sir?” I asked. “Suppose the Indians come back?”

“The Indians won’t risk coming back tonight,” said Mr. Murthwaite. “The direct way is hardly ever the way they take to anything—least of all to a matter like this, where the slightest mistake might be fatal to their reaching their end.”

“But suppose the rogues are bolder than you think?” I persisted.

“In that case,” says Mr. Murthwaite, “let the dogs loose. Have you got any big dogs in the yard?”

“Two, sir. A mastiff and a bloodhound.”

“They will do. In the present emergency, Mr. Betteredge, the mastiff and the bloodhound have one great merit—they are not likely to be troubled with your scruples about the sanctity of human life.”

As he fired that shot at me, the strumming of the piano reached us from the drawing-room—thin and tinkling through the closed windows, as if the house were trying to play at calm while danger walked under the trees. Mr. Murthwaite threw away his cheroot and took Mr. Franklin’s arm to go back to the ladies. I followed, and noticed the sky clouding over fast, the air thickening with the smell of wet earth to come.

Mr. Murthwaite noticed it too. He looked round at me in his dry, droning way and said:

“The Indians will want their umbrellas, Mr. Betteredge, tonight!”

It was all very well for him to joke. He was an eminent traveller; his life had led him into playing ducks and drakes with his own skin among outlandish risks. Mine had not. I went into my own little room and sat down in my chair in a perspiration, wondering helplessly what was to be done next.

Other men, in such a state, might have worked themselves up into a fever. I ended differently. I lit my pipe, and took a turn at Robinson Crusoe.

Before five minutes were over, I came upon this amazing bit—page one hundred and sixty-one:

“Fear of Danger is ten thousand times more terrifying than Danger itself, when apparent to the Eyes; and we find the Burthen of Anxiety greater, by much, than the Evil which we are anxious about.”
