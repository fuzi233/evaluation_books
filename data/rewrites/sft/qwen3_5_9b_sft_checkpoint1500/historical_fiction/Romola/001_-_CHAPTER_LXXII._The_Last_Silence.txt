It was the middle of spring in the year 1492, more than three hundred and fifty years ago, when the angel of dawn, spreading his broad, slow wings, journeyed from the Levant to the Pillars of Hercules, crossed the snow-capped Alps, and descended upon the bare western islands. In that timeless flight, he beheld the same vast outlines of firm earth and restless sea, the identical mountain shadows stretching over the same valleys, and the olive groves and pine forests standing where they stand today. He saw the great domes and spires of cities rising beside the rivers, intertwined with the curved masts along the coast, in those exact spots they occupy now. As the pale light of his passage pierced the homes of men, it illuminated the same scenes it illuminates today: the rosy warmth of sleeping infants, the weary awakening of the sick and sorrowful, the hurried rise of the laborer, and the final slumber of the student who had spent the night questioning the stars, the sages, or his own soul in search of a hidden truth to break the barriers of mortal life and reveal a path through the darkness, an arc within an immeasurable circle of glory. The great rivers that shape human destiny have scarcely altered their courses, nor have the life-streams flowing within the human heart ceased to pulse with the same profound needs, loves, and terrors. As our thought trails slowly in the wake of that dawn, we are struck by the broad uniformity of the human condition, unchanged in its essential history: hunger and toil, planting and harvest, love and death.

If, instead of following the dim twilight, our imagination halts at a specific point in history to await the full morning, we may behold a world-renowned city that has retained its outline almost untouched since the days of Columbus. It stands as an almost unblemished symbol amidst the torrent of changing things, reminding us that we resemble the men of the past far more than we differ from them, for the great mechanical principles upon which those domes and towers were erected ensure a similarity in human construction that transcends all possible alteration. Doubtless, if the spirit of a Florentine citizen, whose eyes were closed forever while Columbus waited and argued over his three humble vessels at the port of Palos, could return from the shades and pause where our thoughts now pause, he would find fellowship and understanding among the inheritors of his birthplace.

Let us imagine that such a shade has been granted permission to revisit glimpses of that golden morning and stands once more upon the famous hill of San Miniato, overlooking Florence from the south.

The spirit wears the habit of his living days: the folds of his well-lined black silk garment, or *lucco*, hang in solemn, unbroken lines from neck to ankle. Upon his head rests a plain cloth cap, adorned with a *becchetto*, a long hanging strip of drapery serving as a scarf in times of need, covering a penetrating face. It is a countenance, perhaps not strikingly handsome, yet distinguished by a firm, well-cut mouth, preserved in distinct humanity by a closely shaved lip and chin. It is a face heavy with the memories of a keen and varied life passed upon the banks of the gleaming river below. As he gazes upon the scene before him, the sense of familiarity overwhelms his perception of change, leading him to think it possible to descend once more into the streets and resume that bustling life where he left it. For it is not merely the mountains and the westward-curving river that he recognizes, nor the dark flanks of Mount Morello opposite him, nor the long valley of the Arno stretching its grey, low-tufted luxuriance toward the distant ridges of Carrara, nor the steep height of Fiesole crowned with monastic walls and cypresses. He sees all the green and grey slopes sprinkled with villas, naming them as his gaze sweeps over them. He perceives other familiar objects intimately tied to his daily walks. Though he notes the absence of the seventy or more towers that once surmounted the city walls like a regal diadem, his eyes refuse to dwell on that void; they are irresistibly drawn to the single tower springing, like a tall flower-stem reaching for the sun, from the square, turreted mass of the Old Palace in the very heart of the city—the tower that looks no worse after four centuries have passed since he used to stroll beneath it. The great dome, too, the greatest in the world, which in his early boyhood had been nothing more than a daring dream in the mind of a small, quick-eyed man—there it rises, casting its large curves over the hills. And the well-known bell-towers—Giotto's, with its distant hint of rich color, the gracefully spired Badia, and the others—he viewed them all from the shoulder of his nurse.

"Surely," he muses, "Florence can still ring her bells with the solemn hammer-sound that once beat upon the hearts of her citizens and kindled the fires within them. And here, on the right, stands the long, dark mass of Santa Croce, where we buried our famous dead, laying laurels upon their cold brows and fanning them with the breath of praise and banners. Yet Santa Croce had no spire then; we Florentines were so consumed with great building projects that we could not execute them all in stone and marble. We had our frescoes and shrines to fund, not to mention rapacious condottieri, bribed royals, and purchased territories, so our façades and spires were forced to wait. But what architect could the Frati Minori, the Franciscans, have employed to construct that spire for them? If it had been built in my day, Filippo Brunelleschi or Michelozzo would have devised something of another fashion—something worthy to crown the church of Arnolfo."

At these words, the spirit lets out a sigh, and his gaze travels onward to the city walls, where now he dwells with wonder at these modern times. Why have five of the eleven convenient gates been closed? And why, above all, should the towers that were once a glory and defense have been leveled? Has the world become so peaceful, and do Florentines dwell in such harmony, that there are no longer conspiracies to bring ambitious exiles home with armed bands at their backs? These are difficult questions; it is far easier and more pleasant to recognize the old than to account for the new. And there flows the Arno, with its bridges precisely where they stood—the Ponte Vecchio, least like any other bridge in the world, laden with the same quaint shops where our spirit remembers lingering, perhaps to observe the progress of that great palace which Messer Luca Pitti had begun constructing with huge stones gathered from the Hill of Bogoli (now Boboli) close behind, or perhaps to conduct some business with the cloth-dressers in Oltrarno. The exorbitant line of the Pitti roof remains hidden from San Miniato, but the yearning of the old Florentine is not to see Messer Luca's overly ambitious palace, built for his own aggrandizement; it is to be down among those narrow streets and busy, humming piazzas where he inherited the eager life of his fathers. Is not the anxious voting with black and white beans still taking place down there? Who are the Priori in these months, dining with sober regulation in the Palazzo Vecchio, feasting on tripe and boiled partridges seasoned by practical jokes against the unfortunate butt among those potent lords? Are not the significant banners still hung from the windows, still distributed with decent pomp under Orcagna's Loggia every two months?

Life possessed a zest for the old Florentine when he, too, trod the marble steps and shared in those dignities. His politics spanned an area as wide as his trade, stretching from Syria to Britain, yet they retained the passionate intensity and detailed practical interest belonging only to a narrow stage of corporate action; only to members of a community shut in close by hills and walls of six miles' circuit, where men knew one another as they passed in the street, set their eyes daily upon the memorials of their commonwealth, and were conscious not merely of the right to vote, but of the chance of being voted for. He loved his honors and his gains, the business of his counting-house, his guild, and the public council chamber; he loved his enmities, too, and fingered the white bean destined to keep a hated name out of the *borsa* with more complacency than if it had been a golden florin. He loved to strengthen his family through a good alliance, returning home with a triumphant light in his eyes after concluding a satisfactory marriage for his son or daughter under his favorite loggia in the evening cool. He loved his games of chess played under that same loggia, his biting jests, and even his coarse jokes, seeing nothing beneath the dignity of a man eligible for the highest magistracy. Through these experiences, he had gained insight into all manner of affairs at home and abroad: he had served in the "Ten" managing the war department, in the "Eight" attending to home discipline, as a Prior or Signore heading the executive government, and even risen to the supreme office of Gonfaloniere. He had participated in embassies to the Pope and the Venetians, and served as commissary to the Republic's hired army, directing inglorious, bloodless battles in which no man died of brave breast wounds—*virtuosi colpi*—but only of casual falls and trampling.
