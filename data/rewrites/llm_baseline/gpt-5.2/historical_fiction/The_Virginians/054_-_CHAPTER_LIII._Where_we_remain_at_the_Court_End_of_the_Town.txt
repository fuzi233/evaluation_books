George Warrington had told the same history the night before to Madame de Bernstein—so much of it as could be kept above water whilst an old lady, like a ship in a calm, drifted perpetually into sleep and as perpetually woke with a start when the voice ceased. Each time George paused, she blinked, gathered the lace at her throat with three little mittened fingers, and said, with the air of granting him an audience out of pure beneficence, that it was “most interesting,” and that he was to proceed.

He did proceed, but ill at ease; hemming, stammering, reddening as though he were confessing theft, and not recounting marches, hunger, fort-prisons, and the dumb, stubborn endurance of men who have no choice but to go on. He spoke with half the spirit he had found in Hill Street, where the very room listened to him: Hetty’s eyes, wide as bowls; Theo’s quick sympathy, like a hand set on the shoulder; mamma’s ready tears; and the General’s comic gravity which, by mere good-nature, could make a timid young man eloquent. The General had said, laughing, that Mrs. Lambert was as serviceable as Mr. Addison’s trunk-maker; you had only to seat her in the front box, engaged to cry, and any tragedy might take its fortune. That was why they had chosen my Lord Wrotham’s drawing-room for George’s first performance: a theatre where a youth might be understood, not cross-examined by a shrewd, wakeful cynic who slept through the tender passages and opened her eyes at the hard ones.

“Very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir,” Madame de Bernstein said now, and lifted her fingers to hide a twitch at the corner of her mouth, half laughter and half malice. “And your mother must have been delighted to see you.”

George’s shoulders moved the merest fraction, like a man shrugging off a cloak; he bowed low, and met, for an instant, his aunt’s keen gaze.

“Have been delighted to see you,” she went on, dry as a chip in the grate, “and killed the fatted calf, and—and that kind of thing. Though why I say calf, I don’t know, nephew George, for you never were the prodigal. I may say calf to thee, my poor Harry! Thou hast been amongst the swine sure enough. And evil companions have robbed the money out of thy pocket and the coat off thy back.”

“He came to his family in England, madam,” George said, with heat rising into his throat, “and his friends were your ladyship’s.”

“He could not have come to worse advisers, nephew Warrington—and so I should have told my sister earlier, had she condescended to write to me by him, as she has done by you,” the old lady answered, tossing up her head as if to shake off both sister and reproach.

And later, when she had dismissed them to make her toilette for the night’s rout, she chuckled in her chair as her maid laced, pinned, and smoothed her into splendour.

“Hey! hey!” she said. “This young gentleman’s mother is half sorry that he has come to life again—I could see that in his face. She is half sorry, and I am perfectly furious. Why didn’t he lie still where he dropped there under the tree, and why did that young Florac carry him to the fort? I knew those Floracs when I was at Paris, in the time of Monsieur le Régent. Of Ivry. No great house before Henri Quatre. His ancestor was the King’s favourite—his ancestor—he! he!—his ancestress! Brett! entendez-vous? Give me my card-purse. I don’t like the grand airs of this Monsieur George; and yet he resembles, very much, his grandfather—the same look and sometimes the same tones. You have heard of Colonel Esmond when I was young? This boy has his eyes. I suppose I liked the Colonel’s because he loved me.”

Cards were her true sacrament; she never missed them—weekday or Sabbath—so long as she could sit upright and hold trumps. Therefore, soon after George ended his unwilling narration, she dismissed her nephews briskly. To the elder she gave two fingers and a curtsey stiff as a state paper; to Harry, she extended both hands and patted his cheek with an odd, almost maternal kindness.

“My poor child, now thou art disinherited, thou wilt see how differently the world will use thee!” she said. “There is only, in all London, a wicked, heartless old woman who will treat thee as before. Here is a pocket-book for you, child! Do not lose it at Ranelagh to-night. That suit of yours does not become your brother half so well as it sat upon you! You will present your brother to everybody, and walk up and down the room for two hours at least, child. Were I you, I would then go to the Chocolate-House, and play as if nothing had happened. Whilst you are there, your brother may come back to me and eat a bit of chicken with me. My Lady Flint gives wretched suppers, and I want to talk his mother’s letter over with him. Au revoir, gentlemen!”

Her chairmen, her flambeaux, the whole little machinery of rank and habit were already at the door; and in a moment she was borne away into the night, where coaches rolled like distant thunder and London’s evening began its usual racket—iron wheels, calling footmen, fiddles tuning, laughter spilling from open doors.

The gentlemen went to Ranelagh.

If you would know a place by its sound as much as by its sight, you must remember that famous rotunda not merely as a lantern of light, but as a shell filled with music. The crowd made a continuous surf: the rustle of stiff silks, the soft click of fans, the scrape of buckles on the boards, the murmuring exchange of gossip—names dropped like coins—and over all a full orchestra, not the thin piping of a tavern, but a body of instruments breathing together. George, who loved music far more than Harry ever did, stood as though he had been led into church. It was his first time hearing such a band, and a piece of Mr. Handel done with satisfaction: the violins bright as poured wine, the bass like a steady pulse underfoot, the hautboys carrying a keen scent through the air, as if sound itself had an aroma.

Harry, master of ceremonies, brought George round the circle, pointing out the curiosities with the practiced ease of a man who knew how to move, how long to pause, whom to greet, whom to avoid. They met Mr. Tom Claypool with some country friends; they listened; they took tea in a box where steam rose faintly from the cups and mingled with perfume and candle-smoke. There was lemon-peel in the air, and the sharp, comforting tannin of the leaves, and beneath it the hot, animal tang of crowded humanity. For all its gaiety, Ranelagh had its fatigue: noise that pressed upon the ear until the mind grew tired of itself. Yet for George, new-come from a prison-fort’s silence and the harsher music of wilderness—wind in pine, musket-crack, the wooden thump of a sentry’s step—this ordered harmony seemed almost miraculous; a civilised soundscape, made by men agreeing, for once, not to shout.

Harry’s humility showed itself in a small thing. In those days, fiddling was voted effeminate; the patriotic prints called daily upon Britons to sneer at the foreign accomplishments of Squallinis and Monsieurs. Yet Harry, who could laugh at much, did not laugh at George’s attention. He let his brother listen, and even looked pleased that George could take delight in what the town despised. It is a strange point in our national character, that we once prided ourselves upon ignorance as if it were courage. There is less of that conceit now; art has, in some fashion, taken out her letters of naturalisation among us, and lives here almost as an equal. If a great heiress chose to marry a music-master and kept a good cook, there are many who would go dine with her nevertheless, and treat the musician with civility—nay, perhaps forget to call his art frivolous if the capon were well roasted and the sauce properly reduced.

After Ranelagh, pursuant to Madame de Bernstein’s counsel, George returned to her ladyship’s house; whilst Harry showed himself at the club where gentlemen assembled nightly to sup and then to gamble away, with solemn faces, what their cooks and tailors had earned for them.

At the club no one alluded to Mr. Warrington’s little temporary absence. Mr. Ruff, his ex-landlord, waited on him with the utmost gravity, as though there had never been any scene between them; as though debt, like yesterday’s dish, might be scraped away and forgotten. Harry’s trunks and habiliments had been removed from Bond Street that morning; he and George were lodged elsewhere now, with the freedom of men who pay in ready money—or can borrow it.

Supper was served; and if London’s great world had any religion beyond cards, it was supper: the bright clink of glass, the low argument over wine, the knife’s short stroke through meat, the fat hissing as it met heat, the thick scent of pepper, vinegar, and smoke. There were bones cracked for marrow; there were sauces too rich to be honest; there was bread torn and buttered with the unconscious tenderness men reserve for what will not contradict them. The very room had its own acoustic: talk rising and falling like flame, laughter snapping, the scrape of chairs as men leaned in, the occasional thud of a bottle set down with decision. A man might almost persuade himself such noises were health, and not merely the din of appetite.

When the cloth was removed and, as usual, the gentlemen prepared to seek the macao-table upstairs, Harry said quietly that he was not going to play any more. He had burned his fingers already, and could afford no more extravagance.

“Why,” Mr. Morris said, rather flippantly, “you must have won more than you have lost, Mr. Warrington, after all is said and done.”

“And of course I don’t know my own business as well as you do, Mr. Morris,” Harry replied sternly—he had not forgotten the other’s manner when the arrest was spoken of; “but I have another reason. A few months or days ago, I was heir to a great estate, and could afford to lose a little money. Now, thank God, I am heir to nothing.”

He looked round, blushing not a little, to the knot of his gaming companions lounging by the tables and gathered round the fire.

“How do you mean, Mr. Warrington?” cried my Lord March. “Have you lost Virginia too? Who has won it? I always had a fancy to play you myself for that stake.”

“And grow an improved breed of slaves in the colony,” said another, with that light cruelty which passes, in such places, for wit.

“The right owner has won it,” Harry answered. “You have heard me tell of my twin elder brother.”

“Who was killed in that affair of Braddock’s two years ago! Yes. Gracious goodness, my dear sir—I hope in Heaven he has not come to life again?”

“He arrived in London two days since. He has been a prisoner in a French fort for eighteen months; he only escaped a few months ago, and left our house in Virginia very soon after his release.”

“You haven’t had time to order mourning, I suppose, Mr. Warrington?” Mr. Selwyn asked very good-naturedly; and simple Harry did not catch the joke until later, when his brother explained it to him.

Harry took his leave.

As the door closed, the room’s sound altered—the way a kitchen changes when the cook steps out and the scullions begin to whisper. My Lord March, who had a gift for making every sentiment look like a vice, broke out laughing.

“Hang me, if I don’t believe the fellow is absolutely glad of the reappearance of his confounded brother!”

“These savages practise the simple virtues of affection—they are barely civilised in America yet,” Selwyn yawned.

“They love their kindred, and they scalp their enemies,” simpered Mr. Walpole. “It’s not Christian, but natural. Shouldn’t you like to be present at a scalping-match, George, and see a fellow skinned alive?”

“A man’s elder brother is his natural enemy,” said Mr. Selwyn placidly, ranging his money and counters before him.

“Torture is like broiled bones and pepper. You wouldn’t relish simple hanging afterwards, George!” continued Horry.

“I’m hanged if there’s any man in England who would like to see his elder brother alive,” said my lord.

“No, nor his father either, my lord!” cried Jack Morris.

“First time I ever knew you had one, Jack. Give me counters for five hundred.”

Jack, once set going, loved the sound of his own suspicion.

“I say, ’tis all mighty fine about dead brothers coming to life again,” he continued. “Who is to know that it wasn’t a scheme arranged between these two fellows? Here comes a young fellow who calls himself the Fortunate Youth, who says he is a Virginian Prince and the deuce knows what, and who gets into our society—”

A great laugh went round at Jack’s “our society,” as though the phrase itself were a jest too plump to swallow.

“—Who is to know that it wasn’t a cross?” Jack persisted. “The young one is to come first. He is to marry an heiress, and, when he has got her, up is to rise the elder brother! When did this elder brother show? Why, when the younger’s scheme was blown, and all was up with him! Who shall tell me that the fellow hasn’t been living in Seven Dials, or in a cellar dining off tripe and cow-heel until my younger gentleman was disposed of? Dammy, as gentlemen, I think we ought to take notice of it: and that this Mr. Warrington has been taking a most outrageous liberty with the whole club.”

“Who put him up? It was March, I think, put him up?” asked a bystander.

“Yes. But my lord thought he was putting up a very different person. Didn’t you, March?”

“Hold your confounded tongue, and mind your game!” said the nobleman; but Jack’s opinion, like a bad smell, found its way into more than one corner. In the world outside that room, too, there were many willing to agree that it was indecorous of Mr. Harry Warrington ever to have believed his brother dead; that there was something suspicious in his first appearance and subsequent actions; and that, regarding foreigners, adventurers, and the like, one ought to be especially cautious—especially when such caution cost nothing but another man’s reputation.

Yet Harry, for his part, though he was out of prison and difficulty; though he had Madame Bernstein’s liberal donation of money in his pocket; though his dearest brother was restored to him—whose return to life Harry never once thought of deploring, as the gentlemen at White’s supposed he must—though Maria had shown herself in so favourable a light by her behaviour during his misfortune: yet, when he was alone, he was not cheerful.

He sat and smoked his pipe of Virginia, and the tobacco’s sweet, resinous heaviness filled his mouth and nose, and seemed to lay a warm hand on his thoughts without soothing them. The room about him was quiet—no dice rattling, no fiddles, no coach-wheels—only the faint tick of London life beyond the panes, and the occasional settling of the fire. After the club’s rattle, that quieter soundscape ought to have been peace; but it made space for anxiety to speak.

It was not that he was deposed from his principality: the loss of it never once vexed him. He knew George would share with him, as he would have done with George. But after all those struggles and doubts in his own mind, to find himself poor—and yet irrevocably bound to his elderly cousin!

Yes, she was elderly; there was no doubt. When she came to that horrible den in Cursitor Street and the tears washed her rouge away, she looked as old as his mother. Her face, without its paint, had seemed yellow and wrinkled; and thinking of it now gave him the same sick qualm he had felt when she was taken ill in the coach on the Tunbridge road. What would his mother say when he brought her home? What battles there would be between them! He would go and live on one of the plantations—the farther from home the better—and have a few negroes, and farm as best he might, and hunt a good deal. But at Castlewood, or in any home he could make for her, what a life for poor Maria, used to court, to cards, to balls and assemblies every night! The very silence of a country evening would ring in her ears like punishment.

If he could be but the overseer of the estates—oh, he would be an honest factor, and try to make up, by labour and care, for his useless life and extravagance in Europe. Five thousand pounds—his patrimony and the accumulations of his long minority—squandered in six months! He a beggar, except for dear George’s kindness, with nothing left him but an old wife: a pretty beggar, dressed out in velvet and silver lace forsooth. He had been in his best clothes; he had made, to the eye, so fine a figure; and here was the end of the show.

With all his fine friends at White’s and Newmarket, with all his extravagance, had he been happy a single day since he had been in Europe? Yes—three days, four days: yesterday evening, when he had been with dear, dear Mrs. Lambert, and those affectionate kind girls, and that brave good Colonel. The Colonel had been right to rebuke him; and he had been a brute to be angry. God bless them all for their generous exertions in his behalf.

Such were the thoughts Harry put into his pipe, and smoked there, in the hush between one London noise and the next, whilst he waited his brother’s return from Madame Bernstein.
