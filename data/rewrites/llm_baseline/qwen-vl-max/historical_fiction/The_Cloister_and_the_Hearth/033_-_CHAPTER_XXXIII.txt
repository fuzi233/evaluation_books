They met the innkeeper in the hall.

“Welcome, gentlemen,” he said, doffing his cap with a deep bow.

“We are not in Germany,” Gerard remarked lightly.

In the common room, they found the hostess—a sturdy woman of forty—curtsying and smiling warmly. “Please, sit down, sir,” she said to Gerard, dusting two chairs with her apron, though they were already clean.

“Thank you, good woman,” Gerard replied. “Well,” he mused inwardly, “this is a courteous people indeed: ‘the trouble of sitting down’? I shall endure it with patience—and then come the labor of eating, the effort of digestion, and finally, by Hercules, the struggle of falling asleep.”

“Why, Denys, what are you doing? Ordering supper for only two?”

“Why not?”

“Can we dine without waiting for forty more? Burgundy forever!”

“Aha! Courage, camarade. Le dia—”

“C’est convenu.”

The Salic Law seemed to have no hold here. In this French inn, women ruled—wimple and kirtle held sway, while doublets and hose were few and faint in their presence. The landlord wandered aimlessly, perpetually removing his hat out of habit; the women passed him by, brushing him aside as one might a twig in a thicket.

A maid brought in supper, followed by the mistress, hands empty.

“Dig in, my lords,” she said cheerfully. “You’ve but one enemy here—and he lies beneath your knife.” (I suspect this was a stock phrase.)

They began to eat. The mistress pulled her chair closer to the table, offering not just food but companionship. She chatted easily, like an old friend. But once her duty was done, she left, sending in her daughter—a lovely young woman of about twenty—who took the vacant seat. She was less broad than her mother, but gentle and bright. Learning how far Gerard had traveled, she showed quiet kindness toward him. She stayed nearly half an hour, and when she left, Gerard murmured, “Is this an inn? It feels like home.”

“Qui fit François, il fit courtois,” said Denys, swelling with pride.

“Courteous? No, Christian—welcoming us as if we were family, us wanderers, here today and gone tomorrow. Yet who deserves pity more than the weary traveler far from kin? Hola! Here comes another.”

The newcomer was the chambermaid—a woman of twenty-five, with a sharp nose, wide laughing mouth, sparkling black eyes, and a bare arm strong but not graceful.

As soon as she entered, one of the travelers made a rather bold jest at her expense. Instantly, the whole company erupted into laughter—the joke had been delivered with such speed and skill that it struck its mark before anyone could react. Like a novice fencer facing a master, the man was caught off guard. One after another tried to spar with her, but Marion planted her hands on her hips and held the room spellbound. This country girl possessed a raw, unpolished wit—coarse on paper, yet electrifying when spoken. Nature had given her much, and daily life in the inn had honed the rest.

Yet I will not try to capture her fully—only hint at her. For it was four hundred years ago, and the banter was rough. She returned every insult in kind, and though virtuous, said things without shame that no decent man today would utter even among men.

Gerard sat stunned. To him, this was almost a new species of humanity. He whispered to Denys, “Now I understand why you French say, ‘A woman’s tongue is her sword.’” At that moment, she turned on another challenger. The gallant Denys, ever the protector of the “weaker vessel,” stepped in: “Courage, ma mie, le—” etc.

She turned on him instantly. “How can he be dead so long as there’s still an archer alive?” (General laughter at his expense.)

“It is ‘washing day,’ masters,” she announced suddenly, with solemnity.

“After that? We travelers can’t strip and go bare while you wash our clothes,” grumbled an old fellow by the fire, who had kept silent during the jests but now ventured into safe territory.

“I didn’t mean you, ancient man,” Marion replied with hauteur. “But since you ask—” (she surveyed him slowly from head to toe)—“I’d wager you could take a turn in the tub, clothes and all, and come out none the worse.” (Laughter.) “But what I meant was, I thought this young lord might like his beard starched.”

Poor Gerard was next. His beard was thin and soft.

The loudest laugh came from Denys himself—his own beard was thick, stiff, and bristling. Shakespeare, though he never saw him, had nailed it perfectly:

“Full of strange oaths, and bearded like the pard.” —*As You Like It.*

Gerard bore the teasing calmly. He had little vanity. “Nay, *chambrière*,” he said with a smile, “mine is unworthy of your care. Take this fine growth instead!” He pointed to Den up.

“Oh, wait until I starch the brooms.”

While they roared over the clear jab, the mistress returned. And in the blink of an eye, the fierce Amazon transformed into a meek Madonna.

Mistresses are marvels—subjugators beyond compare. I think none like them breathe upon the earth. Housemaids, judge for yourselves. Yet it was wasted talent—she had heard everything and did not truly disapprove of the laughter.

“Ah, Marion, lass,” she said good-naturedly, “if you laid me an egg every time you cackled, *L’es Trois Poissons* would never lack an omelet.”

“Now, dame,” said Gerard, “what do we owe you?”

“For what?”

“Our supper.”

“Why hurry? Can’t you wait till you leave? Lose the guest, find the money—that’s the rule at *The Three Fish*.”

“But, dame, outside *The Three Fish*, it says—‘Ici-on ne loge—’”

“Bah! Let that flea stick on the wall. Look here,” she said, pointing to the smoky ceiling covered in scratches. These were accounts—scores—written in smoke, visible only to her and her daughter, who could climb a stool and etch lines through the grime with a knife. She explained that these marks were meant to scare off penniless guests—or used when someone refused to pay but insisted on being served. “We can’t flat-out refuse them. The law forbids it.”

“And how do you know mine isn’t such a face?”

“Out fie! Yours is the best face that’s entered *The Three Fish* this autumn.”

“And mine, dame?” asked Denys. “Do you see no deceit here?”

She studied him quietly. “Not as good as the boy’s, nor ever will be. But it’s the face of a true man. Still,” she added dryly, “if I were ten years younger, I’d as lief avoid meeting that face on a dark night far from home.”

Gerard stared. Denys laughed. “Why, dame, I’d only sip the night dew from the flower. And you needn’t subtract ten years or ten days to be worth risking a scratched face for.”

“There, our mistress,” Marion said, having just returned, “did I not tell you yesterday you could still fool them, if you chose?”

“I dare say you did—it sounds like some foolish wench’s talk.”

“Dame,” said Gerard, “this is astonishing.”

“What? Oh, no, no—it’s nothing remarkable. I’ve lived here all my life. Reading faces is the first thing a girl learns in an inn.”

Marion: “And frying eggs is second—no, lying is second. Frying eggs is third.”

Mistress: “And holding your tongue is last. Modesty? That’s never seen the light of day.”

Marion: “Alack! Speak of my tongue. But I say no more. She under whose wing I live delivers the blow. I’m undone—‘tis but a chambermaid gone. Catch what’s left!” And she staggered backward, landing squarely on the most handsome man in the room—Gerard.

“Tic! tic!” he exclaimed, annoyed. “Don’t be absurd! That’s too heavy a jest for me. Can’t you see I’m speaking to the mistress?”

Marion regained her balance with a grimace, bounded twice across the floor, and spun into a pirouette. “There, mistress,” she declared, “I surrender. You reign supreme with the men—at least with the young ones.”

“Young man,” said the mistress, “this girl is not so silly as she seems. In reading faces and frying omelets, we are skilled. It would be hard if we failed at these arts—they are nearly all we know.”

“You don’t quite grasp me, dame,” said Gerard. “That honesty should shine from a face under your trained eye—that makes sense. But how, looking at Denys here, could you detect his one weakness—his madness, his miserable mulierosity?” Poor Gerard grew angrier the more he pondered it.

“His mule—his what?” (Crossing herself in superstitious dread at the word).

“Nay, it’s only a word I invented for him.”

“Invented? Can a child like you make words not born in Burgundy? Beware what you do! We’re overrun with them already—especially bad ones. Lord, these are troubled times. Next I expect to hear of a new thistle being invented.”

“Well then, dame, *mulierose*—meaning wrapped up, body and soul, in women. So tell me—how did you uncover the fool’s mulierosity?”

“Alas, good youth, you make a mountain of a molehill. Women are keen observers. We see more with the tail of our eye than most men can with a telescope. As I move about, my glance remains on my guests. I noticed this soldier’s eyes never left the women—my daughter, Marion, even me. All was gold to him. And there he sat, glaring—oh, you foolish, foolish man! You, however, turned to the speaker, whoever they were. That is common sense.”

Denys burst into hoarse laughter. “You’re never more wrong. This smooth-faced companion is a Turk—except for his beard. He’s sharper than any archer in the Duke’s guard. He’s more wrapped up in one Dutch girl named Margaret than I am in all of you, fair and brown.”

“Man alive, that’s just the opposite,” said the hostess. “Yours is the curse, his the cure. Cling to Margaret, my dear. I hope she’s honest.”

“She is an angel.”

“Ay, ay—they all are until better known. I’d as lief she were no more than honest. Then she’ll keep you from worse company. As for you, soldier, trouble awaits. Your eyes were never made for your soul’s good.”

“Nor your purse,” Marion interjected. “And your lips—well, they’ll sip the dew, as you call it, from many a bramble bush.”

“Enough chatter, Marion!”

“Ods bodikins, mistress—did you hire me to be one of your three fishes?” And Marion sulked for thirty seconds.

“Is that how you speak to our mistress?” the landlord asked, slipping in.

“Hold your tongue,” his wife snapped. “It’s not your place to reprimand the girl. She’s a good servant.”

“What, is the cock never to crow, while the hens run wild all day?”

“You may crow as loud as you like, my man. But the hen means to rule the roost.”

“I know a saying to match that,” Gerard offered.

“Do you? Then speak it.”

“Femme veut en toute saison, Estre dame en sa mason.”

“I’ve never heard it—but it’s as true as gospel. Ay, those who coined these sayings had eyes and tongues, and tongues and eyes. Before all the world, give me an old proverb.”

“And me a young husband,” Marion chimed in. “Now there was a chance for you all—and no one spoke. Oh! It’s too late now—I’ve changed my mind.”

“All the better for some poor fellow,” Den suggested.

Then the arrival of the young mistress—called the *petite maîtresse*—was the signal for everyone to gather round the fire, like a single happy family: travelers, host, hostess, servants forming the outer ring—all telling stories until bedtime. Gerard, in turn, told a chilling tale from his collection of saints’ lives, making them shudder delightfully. But soon he began to nod, worn out by the effort. The young mistress noticed and gave Marion a look. Marion lit a rushlight, laid a hand on Gerard’s shoulder, and led him away.

She showed him a room with two crisp white beds and invited him to choose.

“Either is paradise,” he said. “I’ll take this one. Do you know, I haven’t lain in a naked bed since I left Holland.”

“Alack! Poor soul!” she said. “Then the sooner my flax and your down—(he! he!)—meet, the better. So—*allons!*” And she presented her cheek as casually as if it were a hand for payment.

“*Allons*? What does that mean?”

“It means ‘goodnight.’ Ahem! Don’t they kiss the chambermaid in your land?”

“Not all at once.”

“What, do they make a business of it?”

“Nay, perverter of words—I mean we don’t flirt so freely with strangers.”

“They must be strangers if they don’t think you fools. Here’s a tangle. Why, all the old greasy greybeards at our inn kiss us chambermaids—faugh! And what have we poor creatures to set against it but now and then a nice young—? Alack! Time flies. Chambermaids can’t stay long in the nursery. So how is it to be?”

“If you please, arrange with my comrade for both. He is mulierose; I am not.”

“Nay, he needs a curb, not a spur. Well then—you shall sleep without paying the usual toll. And oh, it’s sweet to meet a young man who resists these ancient customs and refuses brazen hussies. You shall have your reward.”

“Thank you! But what are you doing with my bed?”

“Me? Only removing these sheets and replacing them with the pair the drunken miller slept in last night.”

“Oh, no! No! You cruel, black-hearted creature! There! There!”

“A la bonne heure! What won’t perseverance achieve? But note the stubbornness of a mad girl! I didn’t care a button. I’m sick of that game after five years. But you denied me—so I *had* to have it. I’d have walked through fire and water for it. Alas, young sir, we women are tricky beasts—poor, perverse toads. Excuse us. Keep us at arm’s length—and goodnight!”

At the door, she turned, voice and manner completely changed: “The Virgin guard thy head, and the holy Evangelists watch the bed where lies a poor young wanderer far from home! Amen!”

And the next moment, Gerard heard her dash down the stairs—then a peal of laughter from the great hall revealed her whereabouts.

“That is a character,” Gerard said profoundly, yawning at the discovery.

Within minutes, he was wrapped in cool, clean linen—refreshing after so long without. Then came warmth—and then, Sevenbergen.

The next morning, Gerard awoke, refreshed and eager to rise, but found himself trapped. His linen had vanished. This was paralysis—for the nightshirt was a modern invention. In Gerard’s time, men did not discard clean sheets lightly. They slipped into them clothed in innocence, like Adam. Out of bed, they emerged as Cain.

He complained to Denys, but at that moment the door opened and Marion swept in, carrying their freshly washed and ironed linen on her arms. She placed it on the table.

“Oh, you good girl!” Gerard exclaimed.

“Alack, have you discovered me at last?”

“Yes, indeed. Is this another custom?”

“No—unless you take them unasked. But at night, we always ask travelers: would you like washed linen? I came in, but you were both fast asleep. So I said to the little mistress, ‘La! What sense is there in waking tired men to ask if Charles the Great is dead, or whether they’d prefer dirty linen or clean—especially this one with skin like cream?’ And she said, ‘Indeed he has!’”

“That was me,” Denys remarked, sounding like a commentator.

“Guess again, and you’ll hit the mark.”

“Don’t notice her, Marion—he’s impudent. And I’m sure we can’t thank you enough for your kindness. I regret I ever refused you—anything you fancied.”

“Oh, are ye there?” said the mischievous one. “I take that to mean you’d like to brush the morning dew off, as your bashful friend calls it. Well then, excuse me—it’s customary, but not wise. I decline. Quits with you, lad.”

“Wait! Wait!” cried Denys as she fled victoriously. “I’m curious—how many of you were here last night, feasting your eyes on us?”

“It was such a satisfying feast—we weren’t half a minute over it. Who? The big mistress, the little mistress, Janet, and me—and the whole posse comitatus, tiptoeing. We usually make our rounds last thing, so we don’t get burned down. And we come in great numbers. Somehow, that makes us bolder—especially when archers are scattered about.”

“Why didn’t you tell me? I’d have stayed awake.”

“Beau sire, the saying goes: the good and the ill are all one while their lids are closed. So we said, ‘Here is one who serves God best asleep. Break not his rest.’”

“She’s amusing,” Gerard declared.

“I must be either that—or knavish.”

“How so?”

“Because *The Three Fish* pays me to be amusing. You’ll eat before you leave? Good! Then I’ll go see the meat is fit for such noble teeth.”

“Denys!”

“What is it?”

“I wish that was a great boy, going with us to keep us cheerful.”

“So do I. But I wish it was going with us as it is.”

“Heaven forbid! A fine fool you’d make of yourself.”

They broke their fast, settled their bill, and bid farewell. Then they discovered Marion had not exaggerated the “custom of the country.” The three main women kissed them warmly—and they kissed them back. The landlord kissed them—and they kissed him. The cry rang out: “Come back—soon as possible!”

“Never pass *The Three Fish*. If your purses are empty, bring yourselves. *Le sieur crédit* is not dead for you.”

And they set off again.

They reached a small town, and Denys went to buy shoes. The shopkeeper stood in the doorway, alert and attentive. He bowed deeply to Denys. The customer was quickly fitted, escorted to the street, and dismissed with graceful gestures.

The friends agreed it was Elysium to deal with such a shoemaker. “Not that my German shoes haven’t lasted well enough,” said Gerard fairly.

Outside the town was a pebbled path.

“This keeps the burghers’ feet dry when walking on Sundays with their wives and daughters,” Denys explained.

Those simple words painted “home” in Gerard’s heart. “Oh, how sweet!” he said.

“Mercy! What is this? A gibbet! And ugh—two skeletons upon it! Oh, Denys, what a dreadful sight to greet us!”

“Nay,” said Denys, “a comforting sight. For every rogue hanging there, one fewer walks free.”

A little farther on, they came to two pillars, between which stood a massive wheel studded with iron spikes. Bones and tattered cloth were tangled in the prongs.

Gerard covered his face. “To think—those scraps and bones are all that remains of a man! Of one who was what we are now.”

“Excusez! A thing that walked on two legs and stole—are we no more than that?”

“How can you know he stole? Have honest men never suffered death and torture too?”

“None of my kin ever ended up on the gibbet, I know.”

“The better their luck. Tell me—how did the saints die?”

“Hard. But not in Burgundy.”

“You massacred them wholesale at Lyons, right on Burgundy’s border. To you, the gibbet proves guilt—because you don’t read history. Alas, had you stood on Calvary that bloody day we mourn to this hour, I tremble to think you might have shouted joyfully at the gibbet built there. For the cross was but the Roman gallows, Father Martin says.”

“The blaspheming old hound!”

“Oh, fie! fie! A holy and learned man. Yes, Denys, you’d have judged those crucified by the bare light of the gibbet. ‘Drive in the nails!’ you’d have cried. ‘Drive in the spear!’ Here are three malefactors. Three *roues*. Yet of those three, one was the first Christian saint—and another was the Savior of the world, whom they gibbeted.”

Denys swore on his honor that things were handled better in Burgundy. After deep thought, he added that the horrors Gerard described had once made him curse and swear in rage when told by the village priest at Easter—“but they happened in a foreign land, a thousand years past. Mort de ma vie, let us hope it’s not true—or at least greatly exaggerated. See how tales grow as they travel!”

Then he paused, flushed with sudden anger. “Don’t you blush to play with your book-learning on your unlearned friend, throwing dust in his eyes, equating saints with these vermin?”

Then, abruptly, he smiled. “Since your heart beats for vermin, feel for the carrion crows! They’re as good vermin as these. Would you send them to bed supperless, poor pretty poppets? These are their larder. Hunger would kill them without cold cut-purses hung here and there.”

Gerard, who had remained silent, declared the subject closed—forever. “There are things,” he said, “where our hearts seem as wide apart as the poles—and our minds too. But I love you dearly all the same,” he added, with grace and tenderness.

Late afternoon brought a faint wailing sound ahead. It grew louder as they approached. Being swift walkers, they soon reached its source: a dozen pikemen, accompanied by constables, marched forward, driving a herd of animals. Over a hundred in number, mostly young, only a few elderly. The males were subdued and silent. The females screamed—loudly, continuously.

In other words, the creatures driven along at the point of law were men and women.

“Good Heaven!” cried Gerard. “So many! But surely not all these children are thieves—some are armed! What on earth is this, Denys?”

Denys advised him to ask the “bourgeois” with the badge. “This is Burgundy: here, a civil question draws a civil answer.”

Gerard approached the officer, removed his cap—a courtesy immediately returned—and said, “For Our Lady’s sake, sir, what are you doing with these poor people?”

“What concern is it of yours, my lad?” the official replied suspiciously.

“Master, I’m a stranger, and thirsty for knowledge.”

The officer looked at him, then nodded. “They are condemned criminals, sentenced to exile. Some for theft, others for violence. They will be sent beyond the borders—never to return.”

Gerard stared. “And these children?”

“Some are guilty. Others are innocent—taken with their parents. The law sees no mercy.”

Gerard fell silent. Denys placed a hand on his shoulder. “We must go on,” he said softly. “The road is long, and justice is harsh.”

And so they continued, the echoes of suffering fading behind them, the weight of human cruelty pressing upon their souls.
