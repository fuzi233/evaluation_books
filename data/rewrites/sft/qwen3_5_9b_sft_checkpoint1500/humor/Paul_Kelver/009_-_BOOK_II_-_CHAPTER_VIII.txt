The eighteen months that followed—though the finale arrived quicker than our wildest calculations anticipated—are, I am convinced, the golden era of my parents' existence. If "happy" feels too cliché for a description of perfection, let us call it "sublime." To them, it seemed as though Divine Providence had knocked gently at the door of mortality, whispering, "Not yet. Hold onto each other a trifle longer. Just a little while." In those final, luminous days, they discarded every artificial burden. The world's noise, the neighbors' prying eyes, the petty anxieties of maintaining a facade—all vanished. They stripped themselves bare, caring for nothing but the warmth of their mutual embrace, learning to understand one another with a tenderness that bordered on the miraculous. Though their circumstances remained humble, the poverty they endured was spiritual rather than material; they wore their rags with dignity because they felt no shame, no terror of judgment. A profound charity bloomed within them, wider than their needs, overflowing toward the universe. Looking back, I suspect we err when we try to banish the thought of Death entirely. Perhaps He is not a grim reaper but a friend who whispers, "Is it worth it? To worry so much about yourselves? Be kind."

From them radiated a grace that transformed everyone around. Even Aunt Fan, in a reversal of her lifelong policy, decided to attempt amiability. One afternoon, shortly after returning from Devonshire, she announced this new regime with dramatic flair.

"I'm a beast of an old woman!" she declared.

"Don't say that, Fan!" my mother pleaded.

"What's the point of saying 'Don't' when you've already said it?" Aunt Fan retorted sharply.

"It's your manner," my mother explained gently. "People think you're disagreeable."

"They'd be fools if they didn't!" Aunt Fan interrupted. "Do you really mean it? Of course not."

"Nonsense," my mother laughed. "You like seeing people squirm."

"I *am* disagreeable," Aunt Fan insisted, though there was a glint of mischief in her eye. "I can be agreeable if I choose, and I'm the queen of agreeableness."

"So why not choose?" my mother asked.

"Tried it once," Aunt Fan mused, falling into a reverie. "Fell flat. Nothing could fall flatter."

"It didn't attract attention," my mother smiled. "But one shouldn't be agreeable just to stand out."

"It wasn't about standing out," Aunt Fan reasoned. "It gave no satisfaction. A disagreeable person shines brightest when they are disagreeable."

"My dear sister, I struggle to agree with you there."

"And yet," Aunt Fan murmured to herself, "I could do it again. Look at those silly, agreeable fools."

"She could do it if she tried," my mother encouraged.

"Let 'em have it!" Aunt Fan whispered fiercely. "That's how you teach 'em sense."

Strangely, my aunt was right, and my mother wrong. My father, the first to notice, asked one evening if "poor old Fan" was unwell.

"Her manner is... weird," he observed wistfully. "I almost wish she wouldn't try. We were used to it, and she was amusing."

But Aunt Fan, a woman of iron will, persisted. Soon, a new figure entered our circle: James Wellington Gadley. Properly, Wellington James Gadley, christened in 1815, but having realized early on that "Wellington" was too grandiose for a mortal man, he had swapped the names. He was a slightly pompous, simple-minded little gentleman, head clerk to Mr. Stillwood, the solicitor to whom my father was apprenticed. Stillwood, Waterhead & Royal, founded in the reign of George III, was a firm steeped in the shady, glorious history of aristocracy, though now it was a dying trade. Old Mr. Stillwood refused new partners, citing the wisdom of not pouring new wine into old skins, yet the remaining clients were of high caliber.

James Gadley, a former office boy, remained blissfully ignorant of the firm's decline. He loved to recount the bizarre cases of Stillwood's past, turning serious matters into theatrical tales where characters became "Mr. Y" and locations "a certain town near here." When he posed a riddle about a husband who disappeared for eighteen years only to return suddenly, Aunt Fan, unlike others who yawned, offered a prompt, intelligent deduction:

"To murder her—by slow poison."

"To murder her! Why?"

"In order to marry the other woman."

"The woman he met recently? Who told him to come back as a free man?"

"Yes. And plain common sense says he intended to kill the first to secure the second."

"Aha!" Aunt Fan nodded. "And he did. Told you so."

Thus, every case in the firm's annals was dissected by Aunt Fan's razor-sharp insight. She read dark criminal intent into every innocent glance. An elderly spinster, Miss Z., likely burned a house containing a will to forge a new one for her hypothetical children. "We're all miserable sinners," she would cheerfully assure Gadley, "but we don't all get the same chances." Gadley, a stout little gentleman with a stomach that protruded a foot, adored her intellect. He would approach her with such intensity, his belly acting as a battering ram, that he would physically push her backward across the room until she was pinned against a wall or, eventually, forced onto the floor. On subsequent visits, armed with knitting needles, Aunt Fan maintained a safe distance, and Gadley, realizing his tactics failed, retreated with a startled exclamation.

Yet, despite the physical comedy, there was a genuine intellectual spark. Gadley once proclaimed, "Miss Davies, you should have been a solicitor's wife!" To which she replied, "Chances are I should have been if anyone had ever asked." Warmed by this appreciation, her amiability took root, albeit in a fantastical shape. Convinced that flattery was the quickest path to agreement, she set about flattering everyone with a sincerity that often sounded like thinly veiled sarcasm. When my father told a trifling joke, she would erupt in exaggerated delight. When my mother displayed a new bonnet, Aunt Fan would simulate shock, declaring it the attire of a saucy fourteen-year-old girl.

For a brief, chaotic interlude, she also targeted Amy, our new servant, descending into the kitchen to be deliberately disagreeable. Amy, however, was a philosopher who accepted the world by calculating that there were only twenty-four hours in a day. She reconciled herself to Aunt Fan's rudeness with perpetual cheerfulness. "If I wasn't listening to her, something worse might be happening," Amy would tell my mother. "Everything's all the same when it's over."

Amy herself was a whirlwind of romantic potential. A stop-gap arrangement, she had arrived claiming to marry in a few weeks. A month later, she revealed she was reconsidering. "Better change your mind before it's too late," she advised my mother, hinting at a new suitor. Indeed, Amy was a creature of infinite variety, engaging a dozen "poor dears"—butchers, bakers, soldiers—and treating them all with equal devotion. To ask her to choose was to insult her capacity for love. Eventually, she revealed her plans to manage my household entirely, commanding my mother to sit down and relax. "You've got something else to do," she decreed. And so, we found ourselves in the capable, chaotic hands of Amy, left to praise the Lord for His goodness.

Meanwhile, Barbara appeared frequently, bringing sunshine that made troubles seem distant. Old Hasluck, though unable to visit himself, sent fruit and flowers through her. My father, growing lighter in spirit as the end approached, seemed to age backward. One summer night, walking to Poplar Station with Barbara, he confessed, "I have always regarded myself as a failure... But do you know? Within the last few months, the idea has come to me that I have been successful." He described finding himself at the end of a dark wood, realizing he had been guided to the right point all along. "All the time I thought I was wandering away, I was drawing nearer."

Fortune, it seemed, was kind. With Barbara's astute guidance regarding stock investments—"Argentinos," "Calcuttas"—my father managed to secure a modest sum. "It's for your mother," he told me, urging me to save it for charity once she was gone, ensuring the money remained "clean." Whether this blessing was truly divine or merely the result of old Hasluck's grinning influence in the background, I could not say.

The climax of these final days was a trip to the theater to see *"A Voice from the Grave, or the Power of Love."* Bred in a narrow, puritanical creed, my parents had never experienced such joy. "One cannot judge a thing one knows nothing about," my father argued, convincing my mother to attend. We waited at Pigott Street, then climbed the "knife-board" ladder of the omnibus, my mother clutching her skirts nervously. Inside, the air was thick with anticipation.
