To understand the architecture of Caroline's solitude, one must first grasp the absolute sterility of her universe during those long, bright hours of June. She existed within a microscopic empire of one, subjugated by a silence so profound it felt like a physical weight pressing against her ribs. Outside, the English garden bloomed—a riot of color and life—but inside the alcove, the world had contracted into a shadowless, trackless waste, desolate as the Sahara dunes despite the verdant surroundings. Time, usually measured by the ticking clock or the rustle of leaves, seemed to stretch into an eternity of static waiting. Her fingers moved mechanically over the needle, stitching braid onto brown holland, but her brain worked restlessly, trapped behind a veil of anxious anticipation.

Then, the barrier broke. Fanny appeared at the threshold, her gaze sweeping the lawn and borders until it landed on the figure hunched over her work. With a call that cut through the stillness—"Miss Caroline!"—the spell fractured.

A low voice answered, issuing from the darkness of the alcove. "Fanny!"

The servant hastened forward, clutching a note that seemed to vibrate with urgent energy. She delivered it to fingers that trembled slightly, barely possessing the nerve to maintain their grip. Miss Helstone did not inquire of its origin; she did not examine the handwriting. Instead, she allowed the paper to slip, falling unnoticed among the intricate folds of her embroidery.

"Joe Scott's son, Harry, brought it," Fanny announced, her tone betraying a mixture of awe and trepidation.

Though Fanny was no enchantress and wielded no arcane powers, her words triggered a metamorphosis in her young mistress. Caroline lifted her head with the sharp, electric motion of a creature waking from a coma. Her eyes, previously dull with fatigue, snapped open, shooting a question that burned with renewed vitality.

"Harry Scott! Who sent him?"

"He came from the Hollow."

The dropped note was snatched up with desperate eagerness. The seal shattered, and the message was consumed in two seconds. It was an affectionate billet-doux from Hortense, informing her cousin that she had returned from Wormwood Wells, now solitary save for Robert's absence at the market. Hortense wrote that nothing would please her more than Caroline's company for tea, adding a maternal assurance that such a change would be profoundly beneficial to a girl who, since the rupture between Robert and Mr. Helstone, had been severed from her *meilleure amie*, Hortense Gérard Moore. The postscript was a command: don the bonnet and descend immediately.

Caroline required no further injunction. Relieved to abandon the brown holland child's slip she had been trimming, she ascended the stairs with alacrity. She covered her curls with a straw bonnet and draped the black silk scarf around her shoulders, the fabric's simple drapery accentuating the purity of her dress and the fairness of her complexion. She fled the solitude, the sadness, the suffocating nightmare of her confinement. She ran down the green lane sloping toward the Hollow, inhaling the fragrance of hedge flowers that smelled sweeter than any moss-rose or lily.

It was true that Robert was not currently in the cottage, yet the act of going where he had recently been was a delight in itself. To walk where he had walked, to enter the room where he had sat that morning, felt akin to a resurrection. As she stepped across the threshold, illusion once again donned its peri-mask. The soft agitation of invisible wings caressed her cheek, and the air, breathing from the azure summer sky, carried a whisper: *Robert may come home while you are in his house. Then, at least, you may look in his face—at least you may give him your hand; perhaps, for a minute, you may sit beside him.*

"Silence!" was her austere retort, yet she cherished the comforter and the consolation.

From the window, Miss Moore likely caught the gleam and flutter of Caroline's white attire filtering through the branchy garden shrubs. Advancing from the cottage porch, she met her with a demeanor straight, unbending, and phlegmatically dignified. No haste or ecstasy was permitted to disorder the precision of her movements, yet she smiled, well pleased to witness the delight of her pupil, to feel the kiss and the gentle, genial strain of her embrace. She led her tenderly inward, half-deceived and wholly flattered. Had she known clearly to whose account this childlike joy was owed, Hortense would most likely have felt both shocked and incensed. Sisters rarely approve when young ladies fall in love with their brothers; it appears, if not presumptuous, simply silly, weak, a delusion, an absurd mistake. They do not love these gentlemen—whatever sisterly affection they may cherish—and the notion that others should, repels them with a sense of crude romance. The initial reaction to such a discovery, as with many parents finding their children in love, is a mixture of impatience and contempt. Reason, if present, corrects the false feeling eventually; but if absent, the daughter or sister-in-law remains disliked to the end.

"You would expect to find me alone, from what I said in my note," observed Miss Moore, conducting Caroline toward the parlour. "But it was written this morning: since dinner, company has come in."

Opening the door, she revealed an ample spread of crimson skirts overflowing the elbow-chair at the fireside, and above them, presiding with regal authority, a cap more awful than a crown. This cap had never entered the cottage under a bonnet; it had been smuggled in a vast bag, or rather a middle-sized balloon of black silk, held wide with whalebone. The screed, or frill of the cap, stood a quarter of a yard broad around the wearer's face. The ribbon, flourishing in puffs and bows about the head, was of the sort called love-ribbon. There was a good deal of it, indeed, a very great deal. Mrs. Yorke wore the cap—it became her; she wore the gown also—it suited her no less.

That great lady had arrived in a friendly manner to take tea with Miss Moore. It was almost as great and as rare a favor as if the Queen were to go uninvited to share pot-luck with one of her subjects. A higher mark of distinction she could not show—she who generally scorned visiting and tea-drinking, holding cheap and stigmatizing as "gossips" every maid and matron of the vicinage.

There was no mistake, however; Miss Moore was a favorite with her. She had evinced this fact more than once—by stopping to speak to her in the churchyard on Sundays; by inviting her, almost hospitably, to come to Briarmains; evidenced today by the grand condescension of a personal visit. Her reasons for the preference, as assigned by herself, were that Miss Moore was a woman of steady deportment, without the least levity of conversation or carriage; also that, being a foreigner, she must feel the want of a friend to countenance her. She might have added that her plain aspect, homely, precise dress, and phlegmatic, unattractive manner were to her so many additional recommendations. It is certain, at least, that ladies remarkable for the opposite qualities of beauty, lively bearing, and elegant taste in attire were not often favored with her approbation. Whatever gentlemen are apt to admire in women, Mrs. Yorke condemned; and what they overlook or despise, she patronized.

Caroline advanced to the mighty matron with some sense of diffidence. Knowing little of Mrs. Yorke, and as a parson's niece, she was doubtful what sort of reception she might receive. She got a very cool one, and was glad to hide her discomfiture by turning away to remove her bonnet. Nor, upon sitting down, was she displeased to be immediately accosted by a little personage in a blue frock and sash, who started up like some fairy from the side of the great dame's chair, where she had been sitting on a footstool, screened from view by the folds of the wide red gown. Running to Miss Helstone, unceremoniously, she threw her arms round her neck and demanded a kiss.

"My mother is not civil to you," said the petitioner, as she received and repaid a smiling salute, "and Rose there takes no notice of you; it is their way. If, instead of you, a white angel, with a crown of stars, had come into the room, mother would nod stiffly, and Rose never lift her head at all; but I will be your friend—I have always liked you."

"Jessie, curb that tongue of yours, and repress your forwardness!" commanded Mrs. Yorke.

"But, mother, you are so frozen!" expostulated Jessie. "Miss Helstone has never done you any harm; why can't you be kind to her? You sit so stiff, and look so cold, and speak so dry—what for? That's just the fashion in which you treat Miss Shirley Keeldar and every other young lady who comes to our house. And Rose there is such an aut—aut—I have forgotten the word, but it means a machine in the shape of a human being. However, between you, you will drive every soul away from Briarmains; Martin often says so."

"I am an automaton? Good! Let me alone, then," said Rose, speaking from a corner where she was sitting on the carpet at the foot of a bookcase, with a volume spread open on her knee.
