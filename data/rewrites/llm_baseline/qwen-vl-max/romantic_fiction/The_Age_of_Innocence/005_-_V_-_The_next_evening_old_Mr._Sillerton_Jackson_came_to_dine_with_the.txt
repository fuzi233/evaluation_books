V. The next evening, Mr. Sillerton Jackson came to dine with the Archers. Mrs. Archer was a quiet woman, reserved by nature and somewhat withdrawn from the bustling world of society, yet she had a deep curiosity about its doings. Her old friend, Mr. Jackson, approached the affairs of others with the patience of a scholar and the precision of a naturalist, while his sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, who lived with him and was highly sought after for her company, brought home fragments of gossip that filled in the gaps of his social map. So whenever something stirred interest in Mrs. Archer’s mind—especially if it concerned the upper echelons of New York life—she would invite Mr. Jackson to dinner. Since her invitations were rare and her household charming, he usually accepted personally rather than sending his sister.

If he could have chosen, he might have preferred evenings when Newland was away—not because he disliked the young man (they were quite congenial at their club), but because Newland occasionally displayed an analytical reserve that made conversation less spontaneous. Still, Mr. Jackson, ever the diplomat, would often say to his sister, “I’ve been a little gouty since my last dinner at the Lovell Mingotts’—a visit to Adeline’s will do me good.” And indeed, it did. While the Mingotts dazzled with canvas-back and vintage wines, the Archers offered refined conversation and intellectual companionship, with the added comfort of Madeira that had journeyed around the Cape.

Mrs. Archer, a widow long settled in West Twenty-eighth Street, lived with her son Newland and daughter Janey. An entire floor was set aside for Newland, while the two women shared modest quarters below. Together, they cultivated ferns in glass cases, stitched intricate lace and wool embroidery, collected Revolutionary-era pottery, and read novels by Ouida—particularly those set among Italian peasants, which they found both romantic and morally satisfying. They avoided Dickens, whom they felt failed to portray gentlemen properly, and though they admired Thackeray, they believed Bulwer-Lytton better captured high society—even if he was now considered outdated.

Both mother and daughter loved scenery above all else. On their travels abroad, they sought out landscapes rather than art or architecture, which they regarded as men’s pursuits, especially those of learned men who read Ruskin. Mrs. Archer had been born a Newland, and both she and Janey bore the unmistakable traits of the family: tall, pale, slightly stooped shoulders, long noses, gentle smiles, and a certain melancholy elegance reminiscent of faded Reynolds portraits. Their resemblance was nearly complete—except that age had softened Mrs. Archer’s figure, while Janey’s slender frame remained untouched by time.

Though outwardly alike, their inner lives differed. Years of close companionship had given them identical speech patterns, habits of saying “Mother thinks” or “Janey thinks,” depending on who wished to voice an opinion. But beneath this surface harmony, Janey harbored dreams and impulses that flickered like hidden flames—romantic notions suppressed by decorum, yet never fully extinguished. Mother and daughter adored each other deeply and revered Newland, their only child and brother. In turn, Newland loved them tenderly, though his affection was tinged with guilt over their idealized view of him—and secretly pleased by it. After all, wasn’t it comforting to be respected at home, even if one sometimes doubted the weight of one’s own authority?

That evening, Newland knew Mr. Jackson would have preferred him absent. But he had decided to stay. He understood what was coming: talk of Ellen Olenska. And he knew his mother and sister would want to hear every detail. All three would be subtly uneasy with his presence—now that his engagement to May Welland had become public knowledge. Yet he waited, amused and curious, to see how they would navigate the tension.

They began cautiously, with a mention of Mrs. Lemuel Struthers. “It’s a pity the Beauforts invited her,” Mrs. Archer said gently. “But then Regina always does what he tells her. And _Beaufort_—”
“Certain _nuances_ escape Beaufort,” Mr. Jackson replied, carefully examining the broiled shad, wondering once more why Mrs. Archer’s cook always burned the roe to a crisp. (Newland, who had long shared the same observation, saw the familiar look of quiet dismay cross the older man’s face.)

“Oh, necessarily,” Mrs. Archer agreed. “Beaufort is vulgar. My grandfather Newland used to warn my mother: ‘Whatever you do, don’t let that fellow Beaufort near the girls.’ But at least he’s had the advantage of mixing with gentlemen—over in England, they say. It’s all very mysterious—” She glanced at Janey and paused. Though they both knew the full story, Mrs. Archer still maintained propriety in public.

“But Mrs. Struthers,” she continued, “what did you say _she_ was, Sillerton?”

“Out of a mine—or rather, from the saloon at the top of the pit,” Mr. Jackson replied. “Then with Living Wax-Works, touring New England. After the police shut that down, they say she lived—” He looked toward Janey, whose eyes widened slightly. There were still blanks in Mrs. Struthers’s past that intrigued her.

“Then,” Mr. Jackson went on, “Lemuel Struthers came along. His advertiser used her head for shoe-polish posters—her hair’s intensely black, you know, Egyptian style. Eventually—” He lingered on the word, giving it careful emphasis. “Eventually, he married her.”

There was a wealth of implication in that single syllable.

“Oh well,” Mrs. Archer said lightly. “At the pass we’ve come to nowadays, it doesn’t matter.”

The truth was, they weren’t really interested in Mrs. Struthers. Ellen Olenska was too fresh, too compelling. Mrs. Archer had mentioned her only so she could soon ask: “And Newland’s new cousin—the Countess Olenska? Was _she_ at the ball too?”

There was a hint of irony in her tone, directed at her son, and Newland recognized it. He’d expected it. Even Mrs. Archer, usually composed and unimpressed by worldly events, had been genuinely pleased by his engagement. (“Especially after that silly business with Mrs. Rushworth,” she’d whispered to Janey, referring to a past heartbreak Newland had once thought unbearable.) No match in New York was finer than May Welland—regardless of how one viewed it. Of course, such a marriage was what Newland deserved. But young men were unpredictable, and some women dangerously persuasive. To see her only son safely beyond temptation and into respectable domesticity was nothing short of miraculous.

Yet Mrs. Archer had been unsettled by the timing of the announcement—by the fact that it coincided with Ellen Olenska’s return. And so, feeling a sense of duty and tenderness, Newland had stayed home that night.

“It’s not that I disapprove of the Mingotts’ pride,” Mrs. Archer murmured to Janey, her only confidante in moments of mild rebellion. “But why must Newland’s engagement be tied to that Olenska woman’s comings and goings?”

She had behaved impeccably during the visit to Mrs. Welland—but Newland knew, and May likely guessed, that both women had been on edge, watching for any sign of Madame Olenska’s appearance. When they left, Mrs. Archer had whispered, “I’m thankful Augusta received us alone.”

These small betrayals of anxiety touched Newland deeply, for he too sensed the Mingotts had crossed a line. But neither mother nor son could speak openly of what troubled them. Instead, Newland simply said, “Oh, well—there’s always a phase of family gatherings when one gets engaged. The sooner it’s over, the better.”

His mother merely pursed her lips beneath her lace veil, trimmed with frosted grapes. Her revenge, he knew, would be to draw Mr. Jackson into a full discussion of the Countess Olenska. And since he had already fulfilled his role as a future Mingott, Newland had no objection to hearing about her—except that the subject was beginning to weary him.

Mr. Jackson had taken a slice of the lukewarm filet mignon, handed to him by the solemn butler with a look of silent judgment. He sniffed at the mushroom sauce and rejected it. He looked hungry and baffled, and Newland suspected he would finish his meal with stories of Ellen Olenska.

Leaning back, Mr. Jackson studied the candlelit portraits lining the dark walls—Archers, Newlands, van der Luydens. “Ah, how your grandfather Archer loved a good dinner, my dear Newland!” he said, gazing at a portrait of a robust young man in a blue coat, standing before a stately country house. “Well… well… well… I wonder what he would have said to all these foreign marriages!”

Mrs. Archer ignored the reference to cuisine. Mr. Jackson continued slowly: “No, she was _not_ at the ball.”

“Ah—” Mrs. Archer murmured, relieved. “She had that decency.”

“Perhaps the Beauforts don’t know her,” Janey suggested, her eyes twinkling with innocent malice.

Mr. Jackson took a sip as if sipping invisible Madeira. “Mrs. Beaufort may not—but Beaufort certainly does. She was seen walking up Fifth Avenue this afternoon with him by the whole of New York.”

“Mercy—” Mrs. Archer breathed, realizing the futility of expecting foreigners to follow local codes of conduct.

“I wonder if she wears a round hat or a bonnet in the afternoon,” Janey mused. “At the Opera, I saw her in dark blue velvet—perfectly plain and flat—like a nightgown.”

“Janey!” her mother scolded, and the young woman blushed, trying to appear bold.

“At least,” Mrs. Archer said, “it was in better taste not to attend the ball.”

A spark of defiance lit in Newland’s eyes. “I don’t think it was a matter of taste,” he replied. “May said she meant to go, but changed her mind because the dress wasn’t smart enough.”

Mrs. Archer smiled, satisfied. “Poor Ellen,” she said softly. “We must remember what an eccentric upbringing Medora Manson gave her. What can you expect of a girl who wore black satin at her debut ball?”

“Oh, I remember her in it!” Mr. Jackson exclaimed, then added, “Poor girl!”—a phrase that carried both fondness and foreboding.

“It’s odd,” Janey remarked, “that she kept such an ugly name as Ellen. I’d have changed it to Elaine.”

She glanced around the table, awaiting reactions. Newland laughed.

“Why Elaine?”

“I don’t know—it sounds more… Polish,” she said, blushing.

“It sounds more conspicuous,” Mrs. Archer said coolly. “And that can hardly be what she wants.”

“Why not?” Newland shot back, suddenly animated. “Why shouldn’t she stand out if she chooses? Why should she hide as if she were the one who disgraced herself? She’s poor Ellen, yes—because she married badly. But that doesn’t make her an outcast.”

“That,” Mr. Jackson mused, “is probably the line the Mingotts intend to take.”

Newland flushed. “I didn’t need their cue, if that’s what you mean. Madame Olenska has suffered. That doesn’t justify condemning her.”

“There are rumors,” Mr. Jackson began, glancing at Janey.

“Oh, I know—the secretary,” Newland interrupted. “Nonsense, mother—Janey’s grown-up. They say the secretary helped her escape from her brute of a husband, who held her prisoner? Well, what if he did? I hope there isn’t a man here who wouldn’t have done the same.”

Mr. Jackson turned to the butler. “Perhaps… that sauce… just a little, after all—” He took a portion and said, “I’m told she’s looking for a house. She means to live here.”

“I hear she intends to get a divorce,” Janey said boldly.

“I hope she will!” Newland declared.

The words fell like a thunderclap in the quiet room. Mrs. Archer raised her eyebrows—the subtle signal: “The butler—” And Newland, remembering the impropriety of discussing such matters aloud, quickly shifted to recounting his visit to old Mrs. Mingott.

After dinner, as tradition dictated, Mrs. Archer and Janey ascended to the drawing-room, where they sat beside a Carcel lamp, facing each other across a rosewood work-table, stitching a tapestry band of field-flowers meant for the chair of the future Mrs. Newland Archer.

Below, Newland settled Mr. Jackson into an armchair near the fire in the Gothic library and handed him a cigar. Mr. Jackson sank into the chair with contentment, lit his cigar with confidence (it was Newland who bought them), stretched his thin ankles toward the coals, and said: “You say the secretary merely helped her escape? Well, he was still helping her a year later—for someone met them living together in Lausanne.”

Newland reddened. “Living together? Why not? Who had the right to reshape her life if not she? I’m tired of the hypocrisy that would bury a woman alive just because her husband prefers harlots.”

He stopped, turned away to light his own cigar, and declared, “Women ought to be free—as free as we are.”

He spoke without thinking, consumed by anger and conviction. But he hadn’t realized how profound those words truly were.

Mr. Jackson drew his ankles closer to the fire and emitted a low, sardonic whistle. “Well,” he said after a pause, “apparently Count Olenski takes your view. I’ve never heard of him lifting a finger to bring her back.”
