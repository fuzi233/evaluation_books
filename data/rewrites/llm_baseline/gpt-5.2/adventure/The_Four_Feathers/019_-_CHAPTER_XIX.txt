Ethne had meant to slip from the house as a cook steals out to taste a sauce in secret—quietly, without witness, without the clatter of notice. But Mrs. Adair sat on the terrace, in the lee of the warm stone wall where the day’s heat still breathed, and from that shadowed angle she commanded the open drawing-room window as neatly as an archivist commands a shelf.

She saw Ethne cross the flags with the lightness of a girl who has forgotten her own dignity, and then—too quickly—take the steps into the garden. Not the unhurried walk of someone seeking air, but the sudden flight of someone escaping a sound that has turned sharp.

The room behind the window had changed a moment before: Ethne had turned down the lamp, and the bright rectangle of domesticity—chairs, music-stand, the familiar instruments of polite evening—had fallen into darkness, leaving only moonlight. To Mrs. Adair that swift snuffing was like a lid clapped over a pot to keep in fragrance: secrecy, closeness, the private heat of words spoken low. She had not run from the sting. She stayed, listening.

Music floated out—its sweetness and yearning rolling over the terrace and into the hedges the way the smell of baked bread drifts into a lane and makes you suddenly hungry for a home you do not possess. When the notes ceased, there followed that other intimacy: the murmured talk that comes after a shared song, as if the air itself has been seasoned and any word will take the flavor.

Mrs. Adair’s jealousy flared—clean and scorching, like spirits set alight in a pan.

Then Ethne ran.

Mrs. Adair could not believe in a quarrel. Ethne was not made for loud scenes; she did not slam doors. And yet something more singular happened: Colonel Durrance continued speaking in the darkened room, his voice steady, confident—addressing an audience that was no longer there. Mrs. Adair rose and moved to the open window on the soft soles of her shoes, careful as anyone crossing a kitchen at night so as not to wake the sleepers. She drew near enough that the sill between her and the room was no wider than a knife’s back.

Durrance sat in shadow. She could not see him clearly at first, but she could hear him laugh—light-hearted, the laugh of a man recounting a difficulty already mastered, like one describing how he once spoiled a dish and now knows exactly where his hand went wrong.

“I began to find out,” he was saying, “even on that first afternoon at Hill Street two months ago, that there was only friendship on your side. My blindness helped me. With your face and your eyes in view I should have believed without question just what you wished me to believe. But you had no longer those defences. I on my side had grown quicker. I began in a word to see. For the first time in my life I began to see.”

Mrs. Adair did not move. It was dreadful, this confession poured into darkness, as if he were decanting his heart into an empty glass.

“I should have definitely broken off our engagement, I suppose, at once,” he went on. “For I still believed, and as firmly as ever, that there must be more than friendship on both sides. But I had grown selfish. I warned you, Ethne—selfishness was the blind man’s particular fault. I waited and deferred the time of marriage. I made excuses. I led you to believe that there was a chance of recovery when I knew there was none. For I hoped, as a man will, that with time your friendship might grow into more than friendship. So long as there was a chance of that, I—Ethne, I could not let you go.”

His voice softened on her name, as if he were tasting it, searching for an after-note.

“So I listened for some new softness in your voice, some new buoyancy in your laughter, some new deep thrill of the heart in the music which you played—longing for it—how much! Well, to-night I have burnt my boats. I have admitted to you that I knew friendship limited your thoughts of me. I have owned to you that there is no hope my sight will be restored. I have even dared to-night to tell you what I have kept secret for so long—my meeting with Harry Feversham and the peril he has run. And why? Because for the first time I have heard to-night just those signs for which I waited. The new softness, the new pride, in your voice, the buoyancy in your laughter—they have been audible to me all this evening. The restraint and the tension were gone from your manner. And when you played, it was as though some one with just your skill and knowledge played, but some one who let her heart speak resonantly through the music as until to-night you have never done. Ethne, Ethne!”

But Ethne was not there to receive his ardour. She was already in the small enclosed garden by the bank—the place she had used that morning like a larder for secrets, where one could shut a gate and make the world manageable. Her collie had come to her, padding without fuss, its paws muffling the ground—one creature in the house who understood that comfort is sometimes only the absence of noise. She had reached what she needed most: solitude and the thin, honest soundscape of the night.

A few more sentences from Durrance and her prudence would have cracked. The careful wall she had built these months—brick by brick of attention, mortar of practiced tenderness—so that he would never look over into the country of her true thoughts, would have been knocked flat. The bitter part was that he had already begun to see over it; he was peering now with that quickened inner sight. Ethne did not know it. She fled to stop him from knowing it.

The creek lay silvered, as if the moon had poured skimmed milk across its surface. Trees stood black and tall, their leaves barely stirring. The tide lapped the bank with the soft persistence of a spoon against a bowl; it was no louder than a river’s continuous whisper. Ethne sat on the bench, trying to draw the quiet into herself the way one draws warmth from a hearth, trying to learn from the growing things around her their patience, their obstinate perseverance through seasons.

But the day had stretched her beyond endurance.

This morning, in this very garden, good news had come and she had regained Harry Feversham—so she thought of it, as if he were a lost heirloom returned to the household: not held in hand, but recovered in possibility. This evening bad news had come and she had lost him again, perhaps for the whole remainder of mortal life. Harry meant to pay for his fault to the uttermost scruple, and Ethne—who had once demanded that scruple as the only language of honour—cried out against his thoroughness now that it turned its blade upon him.

Surely he might have been content. Surely, in redeeming himself to one of the three, he had redeemed himself to all. But he had gone south to join Colonel Trench in Omdurman.

Ethne knew nothing of Omdurman’s squalor and bare glare, nothing of what a prison-house meant there; yet Willoughby’s hints had seasoned her imagination with dread until every thought tasted of iron. He had offered to tell her more, and she had refused—like someone who cannot bear to hear how an animal is slaughtered, yet cannot stop picturing it.

What cruelties might not be practiced? Even now, perhaps, at this very hour—on a summer night that ought to have smelled of honeysuckle and cut grass—someone might be—

She checked herself. She could not go that way.

The tide’s small sound kept up its steady work, and in it she heard another water: a Donegal river that had sung in her ears five years ago while she watched a whole night drain into dawn. She hungered for her own country with a fierceness that was bodily—the imagined peat-reek, the damp sweetness of heather, the homely speech that fit the mouth the way a familiar spoon fits the hand. Donegal had always seemed to her a place where one’s very words were cleansed, where the old names held the right weight. Here, among people who spoke smoothly and listened for advantage, she had learned concealments and maneuvers like recipes she despised but must keep making.

And now, longing towards those brown hills and streams, she felt a sudden loathing for the shifts she practiced—and still must practice. Weariness fell on her like a heavy cloak. Yet her resolve did not change. Two lives were not to be spoiled because she lived in the world. Tomorrow she would gather her strength and begin again. Durrance must never know that another man stood before him in her heart.

Inside the drawing-room Durrance’s confession ran to its close, his voice returning to plain statement.

“So you see,” he said, “I could not speak of Harry Feversham until to-night. For I was afraid that what I had to tell you would hurt you very much. I was afraid that you still remembered him, in spite of those five years. I knew, of course, that you were my friend. But I doubted whether in your heart you were not more than that to him. To-night, however, I could tell you without fear.”

Now he waited. Mrs. Adair heard him shift in the gloom.

“Ethne!” he called, surprised at the silence.

No answer.

He rose and walked toward the chair where Ethne had been sitting. Mrs. Adair could see him now as he moved into the moon’s wash. His hands went out and found the chair-back. He bent over it, as though he imagined Ethne leaning forward, hands on knees, stricken by his news.

“Ethne,” he said again, and the second time his voice carried trouble and doubt, the dread that he might find her weeping without sound.

He leaned further, his fingers exploring—and suddenly a violin string twanged loud in the room. Ethne had left the instrument on the chair, and his hand had brushed it. The sound cut the air with a nakedness that made Mrs. Adair’s skin prickle. In that one note was exposure: a thing handled without seeing.

Durrance straightened and stood perfectly still. Then he passed his hand over his forehead once or twice, as if to smooth away the shock, and without calling again he moved to the open window.

Mrs. Adair did not retreat. She held her breath. The sill’s narrow width separated them like a line between two territories.

Moonlight struck Durrance’s face full, and she saw comprehension dawn slowly, as if a picture were coming up in a tray of developing fluid. He sensed her presence.

“Ethne,” he said a third time—appealing now—and stretched out a hand timidly. His fingers touched the fabric of Mrs. Adair’s dress.

“It is not Ethne,” he said, starting back.

“No, it is not Ethne,” Mrs. Adair answered quickly.

He withdrew a step. For a little while he said nothing.

“Where has she gone?” he asked at last.

“Into the garden. She ran across the terrace and down the steps very quickly and silently. I saw her from my chair. Then I heard you speaking alone.”

“Can you see her now?”

“No. She went across the lawn towards the trees and their great shadows. There is only the moonlight in the garden now.”

Durrance stepped over the sill and stood beside Mrs. Adair on the terrace. The final slip Ethne had made betrayed her to the man who had grown quick. There could be only one reason for such sudden, secret flight. He had spoken of Feversham wandering south from Wadi Halfa into savage country; he had spoken his fears without reserve, thinking her indifferent—almost blaming her in his heart for what seemed callousness. But that callousness had been a mask, and she had run because she could not keep it in place.

His first suspicions had been right. Feversham still stood between Ethne and himself, holding them apart.

“She ran as though she was in great trouble and hardly knew what she was doing,” Mrs. Adair added. “Did you cause that trouble?”

“Yes.”

“I thought so, from what I heard you say.”

She wanted to hurt him, and despite his composed face she felt she had done it—a thin compensation for weeks of mortification. There were extenuations, if not defenses, for Mrs. Adair. She too had been overtaxed. Her calm, pale face hid passions that ran hot and sudden, and tonight they had shaken her to the edge. There had been something grotesque—almost horrible—in Durrance’s confession poured out to an empty chair. She had stood outside with a bitter hunger that he should speak like that to her and the bitter certainty that he never would. The irony stung like smoke in the eyes. And at the bottom of it all lay that memory of the lamp extinguished, the voices in the dark—an intimacy she could not buy.

So she struck where she could.

“There’s one thing I don’t understand,” Durrance said. “The change we both noticed in Ethne to-night. I mistook the cause of it, that’s evident. I was a fool. But there must have been a cause. The gift of laughter had been restored to her. Her gravity, her air of calculation, had vanished. She became just what she was five years ago.”

“Exactly,” Mrs. Adair said. “Just what she was before Mr. Feversham disappeared from Ramelton. You are so quick, Colonel Durrance. Ethne had good news of Mr. Feversham this morning.”

Durrance turned sharply toward her. At last—movement, a crack in the polished surface. Mrs. Adair took a small pleasure in it, as one might take pleasure in hearing a silent room finally creak.

“Are you quite sure?” he asked.

“As sure as that you gave her the worst of news to-night.”

He did not need the answer. Another small thing Ethne had said earlier came back to him now—unremarked at the time, now suddenly weighted: her mention that Feversham still drew an allowance from his father. “I heard it only to-day,” she had said.

“Yes,” Durrance murmured. “Ethne heard news of Feversham to-day.” His voice slowed, as if he were turning a hard seed between his teeth. “Did she make a mistake five years ago? There was some wrong thing Harry Feversham was supposed to have done. But was there really more misunderstanding than wrong? Did she misjudge him? Has she to-day learnt that she misjudged him?”

“I will tell you what I know,” Mrs. Adair said. “It is not very much. But I think it is fair that you should know it.”

“Wait a moment, please, Mrs. Adair,” Durrance said sharply.

He had been speaking as much to himself as to her, and suddenly the idea of taking Ethne’s secret from another mouth struck him as treacherous. He walked away to the balustrade and leaned, face toward the garden, listening—to what? Not to voices, but to the night’s fine textures: the tide’s small repetition, the distant leaf-rustle, the absence of wheels and machinery that made every soft sound significant. In such quiet a man’s thoughts spoke loud. He knew why Ethne concealed the truth: she wanted him never to suspect that she loved Harry Feversham still. Yet he could not abandon his conviction that marriage between a crippled man and a vigorous woman could not be right unless both brought more than friendship.

He turned back.

“I am no casuist,” he said. “But here disloyalty seems the truest loyalty of all. Tell me what you know, Mrs. Adair. Something might be done perhaps for Feversham. From Assouan or Suakin something might be done. This news—this good news came, I suppose, this afternoon when I was at home.”

“No,” Mrs. Adair said. “This morning when you were here. It was brought by a Captain Willoughby, who was once an officer in Mr. Feversham’s regiment.”

“He is now Deputy-Governor of Suakin,” Durrance said. “I know the man. For three years we were together in that town. Well?”

“He sailed down from Kingsbridge. You and Ethne were walking across the lawn when he landed from the creek. Ethne left you and went forward to meet him. I saw them meet, because I happened to be looking out of this window at the moment.”

“Yes,” Durrance said. “Ethne went forward. There was a stranger whom she did not know. I remember.”

“They spoke for a few moments, and then Ethne led him towards the trees at once, without looking back—as though she had forgotten,” Mrs. Adair said, and could not resist the sting.

“As though she had forgotten me, you mean,” Durrance said quietly. “No doubt she had.”

She went on. “They went together into the little enclosed garden on the bank.”

Durrance started at that, the smallest jerk betraying him.

“Yes,” Mrs. Adair continued, watching him closely. “You followed them.”

“They were there then,” he said slowly, “on that seat, in the enclosure, all the while.”

She waited, curious, for him to explain how he had missed them—how blindness had been cheated by the place’s shape and hedge and gate. But he gave her nothing.

“Well?” he asked.

“They stayed there a long while. You had gone home across the fields before they came outside into the open. I was in the garden, and indeed happened to be actually upon the bank.”

“So you saw Captain Willoughby. Perhaps you spoke to him?”

“Yes. Ethne introduced him, but she would not let him stay. She hurried him into his boat and back to Kingsbridge at once.”

“Then how do you know Captain Willoughby brought good news of Harry Feversham?”

“Ethne told me they had been talking of him,” Mrs. Adair replied. “Her manner and her laugh showed me no less clearly that the news was good.”

“Yes,” Durrance said, and nodded. Willoughby’s tidings had fermented into that buoyancy and pride he had heard in Ethne’s voice tonight. He had taken the warmth for himself, as a man mistakes the smell of bread for welcome when in truth the oven was lit for another. His very penetration had led him astray.

Mrs. Adair searched his face in the moonlight for anger, for resentment of Ethne’s secrecy, for any bruise she could press. She found none.

“And that is all?” he asked.

“Not quite. Captain Willoughby brought a token from Mr. Feversham. Ethne carried it back to the house in her hand. Her eyes were upon it all the way, her lips smiled at it. I do not think there is anything half so precious to her in all the world.”

“A token?”

“A little white feather,” Mrs. Adair said, “all soiled and speckled with dust. Can you read the riddle of that feather?”

“Not yet,” Durrance answered.

He paced once or twice along the terrace and back, lost in thought, the way a man walks off a shock as if movement could grind it smaller. Then he went into the house and returned with his cap from the hall. Coming back to Mrs. Adair, he paused.

“It was kind of you to tell me this,” he said. “I want you to add to your kindness. When I was in the drawing-room alone and you came to the window—how much did you hear? What were the first words?”

Mrs. Adair’s reply relieved him. Ethne had heard nothing of that confession, nothing of his admission that he had used the pretence of hope to delay their marriage.

“Yes,” he said. “She moved to the window to read a letter by the moonlight. She must have escaped the moment she had read it. Consequently she did not hear that I had no longer any hope of recovering my sight, and that I merely used the pretence of a hope in order to delay our marriage. I am glad of that—very glad.”

He held out his hand and shook hers. “Good-night.”

Then, as if his mind had already stepped away from the terrace into the harsher world of deserts and garrisons, he added absently, “You see—if I hear that Harry Feversham is in Omdurman, something might perhaps be done—from Suakin or Assouan, something might be done. Which way did Ethne go?”

“Over to the water.”

“She had her dog with her, I hope.”

“The dog followed her,” Mrs. Adair said.

“I am glad,” Durrance replied.

He knew, with that quickened hearing of his, what comfort the animal’s silent companionship would be—the soft pad of paws, the warm press at the knee, the steady breathing that steadies one’s own. Perhaps, in the depth of his own distress, he envied the dog.

Mrs. Adair watched him cross the garden to the stile in the hedge. He walked straight and steady along the path like a man who sees. Nothing in his gait or bearing confessed that the one thing he had clung to—his belief in Ethne’s heart—had been taken from him that evening as cleanly as a plate removed from a table. The night’s small natural sounds continued behind him, uncommercial, unadorned, as if the world itself refused to turn tragedy into spectacle.
