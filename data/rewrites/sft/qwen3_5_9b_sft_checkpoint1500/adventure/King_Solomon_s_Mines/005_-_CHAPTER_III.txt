The voyage from the Cape to Durban usually consumes four or five days, depending on the vessel's pace and the whims of the weather. Occasionally, if the landing at East London proves treacherous—despite the endless talk of a "wonderful harbour" that swallows fortunes in construction costs—a ship may be held up for a day until the tide lifts the cargo boats. On this occasion, however, fortune smiled upon us. There were no breakers on the Bar, and the tugs surged forward immediately, towing their long strings of ugly, flat-bottomed skiffs. Packages were bundled into them with a violent crash, indifferent to their contents. Whether they held fragile porcelain or sturdy wool, they received identical treatment. I watched a crate containing four dozen bottles of champagne shatter instantly; the liquid boiled and fizzed in the bottom of the dirty boat, a wicked waste. Yet, the Kaffirs aboard, spotting two unbroken bottles, knocked off the corks and drained them. They failed to account for the carbonation expanding within their bellies, causing them to roll and moan, shouting that the "good liquor" was *tagati*—bewitched. I shouted from the deck that it was merely the white man's strongest medicine and that they stood as good as dead men. Terrified, they fled to shore, and I doubt they will ever touch champagne again.

While we steamed northward toward Natal, I pondered Sir Henry Curtis's proposal. We avoided the subject for two days, filling the silence with tales of the chase, stories born of necessity rather than invention. Finally, on a beautiful evening in January—the height of our heat—we passed the coast of Natal, aiming to drop anchor off Durban Point by sunset. The shoreline was a masterpiece of nature: red sandhills sweeping into vivid green plains, dotted with Kaffir kraals, and laced with a ribbon of white surf crashing against the rocks. Approaching Durban, the landscape grew richer, etched with sheer kloofs carved by centuries of rain, sparkling rivers, and the deep, untamed green of the bush. White houses smiled upon the placid sea, adding a touch of civilization to the wild scene. To me, beauty finds its completion only with human presence; perhaps this stems from a lifetime spent in the wilderness, where I learned to value order, even if it drives the game away.

Our calculations were slightly off, and the sun sank before we anchored. A gunshot announced the arrival of the English Mail to the town, signaling that it was too late to clear the Bar. We dined comfortably while the mailbags were hoisted onto a lifeboat. By nightfall, the moon hung bright, outshining the lighthouse's frantic flashes. Sweet, spicy scents drifted from the shore, mingling with the hum of sailors raising anchors on a nearby brig. It was a perfect night, draped in silver and peace. Even the great bulldog of a sporting passenger, usually eager to tear apart the baboon in the forecastle, surrendered to the gentle influence, snoring contentedly at his cabin door.

Later, the three of us—Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, and I—took our station by the wheel, falling into a quiet contemplation.

"Well, Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said eventually, breaking the silence. "Have you given thought to my proposals?"

"Ay," echoed Captain Good. "Do you intend to join us for Solomon's Mines, or wherever the gentleman you knew as Neville may have ended up?"

I rose and tapped out my pipe. I needed a fraction longer to decide. Just as the glowing ember fell into the sea, I made up my mind. That brief pause was decisive.

"Yes, gentlemen," I replied, seating myself again. "I will go. Let me explain why, and on what conditions.

"First, regarding terms: You shall cover all expenses. Any ivory or valuables we acquire shall be split between Captain Good and myself.
"Second, you must pay me £500 upfront for my services, guaranteeing my loyalty until you abandon the venture, succeed, or meet disaster.
"Third, prior to our departure, you must sign a deed promising that, in the event of my death or disability, you will provide an annuity of £200 per year for five years to my boy, Harry, who studies medicine at Guy's Hospital in London. By then, he should be self-sufficient.

"That is all, I believe."

"No," Sir Henry answered warmly. "I accept them gladly. I am resolved on this project and would pay more for your aid, given your unique knowledge."

"Pity I didn't ask for more, but I shall not renege. Now, for my reasons. I have observed you both closely; I like you, and I believe we will work well together. That counts for much on a long journey.

"As for the journey itself, I tell you plainly, Sir Henry and Captain Good, that survival is improbable if we attempt to cross the Soliman Mountains. Recall the fate of Dom da Silvestra three centuries ago, his descendant twenty years later, and your brother. Their ends suggest ours will follow suit."

I paused to gauge their reaction. Captain Good looked uneasy, but Sir Henry's expression remained stone. "We must take our chance," he said.

"You may wonder," I continued, "why a man like me, who considers himself timid, undertakes this. It comes down to two factors. First, I am a fatalist. I believe my hour is appointed by a power greater than my will. If destiny calls me to die in the Soliman Mountains, I shall go and I shall fall. Second, I am a poor man. Forty years of hunting and trading have barely sustained me. The average elephant hunter survives only four or five years. I have lived seven times that long, so my time is likely short. Were I to perish in ordinary business, my debts would leave Harry destitute. Here, however, he secures five years of income. That is the essence of it."

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said, his gaze serious, "your willingness to embark on an enterprise doomed to disaster speaks volumes of your character. Time alone will reveal if you are right. But rest assured, I proceed to the bitter or the sweet. If we must fall, I pray we have a good shot first, eh, Good?"

"Yes, yes," the captain agreed. "We are accustomed to holding life in our hands. Turning back now would be folly. Let us descend to the saloon for a toast to luck."

And we did, clinking glasses beneath the moonlight.

The following morning, we disembarked. I lodged Sir Henry and Captain Good in the small shanty I maintain on the Berea, which I consider home. It comprises three rooms and a kitchen, built of green brick with a galvanized iron roof. A generous garden surrounds it, featuring fine locust trees and promising mango saplings gifted by the botanical curator. An old hunter named Jack tends the grounds; a Griqua by birth, his leg shattered by a buffalo cow in Sikukuni, he is unfit for the field but perfect for gardening—an art foreign to the restless Zulu spirit. My guests slept in a tent pitched within my grove of orange trees, where the scent of bloom and the sight of ripening fruit created a serene atmosphere, largely free of mosquitoes unless the rains were exceptionally heavy.

With my decision made, preparations commenced. I secured the deed from Sir Henry, a process complicated by his status as a stranger and the overseas location of the property, but eventually finalized with a lawyer's fee of £20, a sum I deemed exorbitant. I cashed my £500 cheque, honoring my "bump of caution."

On Sir Henry's behalf, I procured a twenty-two-foot wagon with iron axles, crafted from seasoned stinkwood. Having visited the Diamond Fields, it bore the marks of hard travel, ensuring the timber was properly dried. Designed as a "half-tented" wagon, it offered a sheltered rear section for sleeping and gear, leaving the front open for necessities. At £125, I considered it a bargain.

I then acquired a team of twenty Zulu oxen, smaller and lighter than the standard Africander breeds but capable of enduring terrain where larger oxen would starve. Capable of covering five miles a day with a moderate load, they were ideal. Crucially, they were "salted"—hardened against red water through exposure across South Africa—and inoculated against "lung sickness," a virulent pneumonia common in the region. This inoculation involved slitting the tail and inserting diseased lung tissue; the ox survived a mild case, losing its tail but gaining immunity. It seemed a cruelty to deny the beast its tail in a fly-ridden land, yet sacrificing a stump to save the ox was a wise trade. Trekking behind twenty stumps rather than tails appeared as though Nature had confused prize bulldogs with working cattle.

Provisioning required meticulous planning to avoid burdening the wagon while carrying essentials. Fortunately, Captain Good, a former student of medicine, possessed a splendid traveling chest and a comprehensive set of instruments, his practical knowledge surpassing many formally educated doctors. During our stay in Durban, he performed a remarkable procedure, removing a Kaffir's big toe with ease, only to be baffled when the patient requested a replacement, noting that a "white one" would suffice in an emergency.
