Newland Archer, in the space of a few minutes, found himself flushed with a kind of unwilling shame that crept up like heat from an over-crowded kitchen. It was irritating enough that the box on which the whole masculine attention of New York had converged should be the very one in which his betrothed sat—May Welland between her mother and her aunt—calm as a white dish set out for admiration; but for an instant he could not even make out the identity of the woman in the Empire dress, nor guess why her mere presence had quickened the house to that peculiar rustle—like the first crackle of kindling before a flame takes.

Then recognition came, and with it a sharp spurt of indignation, as sudden as a cut finger touched by salt.

No—surely the Mingotts had not meant to do this. No one would have thought the Mingotts would have tried it on. Yet they had, unmistakably; the low, clipped comments behind him—little hisses of scandal carefully kept under the cover of Mephistopheles’ phrases—left no room to doubt that the young woman was May Welland’s cousin, the cousin always spoken of at home with that air of dutiful pity New York liked to wear: “poor Ellen Olenska.”

He knew she had arrived from Europe only a day or two earlier; he had even heard from May—almost tenderly, and certainly without censure—that she had gone to see her cousin, who was staying with old Mrs. Mingott. Archer believed in family solidarity as in a creed, and one of the things he had long respected in the Mingotts was their obstinate championship of whatever black sheep their blameless flock produced. There was no smallness in him; he was glad May should be generous enough to be kind—privately—to an unhappy woman. But to receive Countess Olenska inside the family walls was one thing, like keeping a troubled relative fed and sheltered in the back rooms; to display her publicly at the Opera—at the Opera of all places, with May beside her, and May’s engagement to him to be announced within weeks—was another. That was bringing the whole household’s stewpot into the drawing-room and asking the guests to discuss the ingredients.

No: he found himself siding, for once, with old Sillerton Jackson’s offended certainty. He did not think the Mingotts would have tried it on.

Of course, he reflected, whatever man dared (within Fifth Avenue’s limits) old Mrs. Manson Mingott would dare. He had always admired the great, high-handed old lady—Catherine Spicer of Staten Island once, with a father mysteriously discredited, and neither money nor position enough to make people forget it—who had married into the wealthy Mingott line, married two of her daughters to “foreigners” (an Italian marquis and an English banker), and then capped her audacities by building a vast house of pale cream-coloured stone in a wilderness near Central Park, when brownstone was as obligatory as a frock-coat at four o’clock.

Her foreign daughters had become legend: they never returned to visit; and she—like many minds that rule by sheer will—had grown sedentary and enormous, and had stayed at home, reigning where she sat. But the cream-coloured house, said to be modelled on Parisian private hotels, stood like a public challenge. She throned there among pre-Revolutionary furniture and souvenirs of the Tuileries of Louis Napoleon—where she had shone in her middle age—as placidly as if it were the most natural thing in the world to live above Thirty-fourth Street, or to have French windows that opened like doors instead of sashes that pushed up.

Everyone agreed (including Mr. Jackson) that old Catherine had never been beautiful—beauty being, in New York’s eyes, the one sauce that could make any dish go down, and excuse a certain number of dubious flavours. Unkind people said that, like her Imperial namesake, she had won by sheer will, hardness, and a haughty effrontery that was somehow justified—perhaps even sterilized—by the extreme decency and dignity of her private life. Her husband had died when she was twenty-eight, after tying up the money with an extra caution born of the city’s distrust of the Spicers; but his young widow had gone on fearlessly: mixing with foreign society, marrying her daughters heaven knew where, hobnobbing with Dukes and Ambassadors, associating familiarly with Papists, entertaining Opera singers, and counting Mme. Taglioni among her intimates; and all the while (as Sillerton Jackson loved to insist) there had never been a breath on her reputation—the only respect, he always added, in which she differed from the earlier Catherine.

Mrs. Mingott had long since succeeded in untying her husband’s fortune and had lived in affluence for half a century; yet early privations had made her thrifty to the point of perversity. When she bought a dress or a piece of furniture she insisted on the best—sound fabric, honest workmanship, the sort of thing that lasted and could be handed down like a recipe written in a sure hand. But she could not bring herself to spend much on the transient pleasures of the table. So, for reasons wholly different, her food was as poor as Mrs. Archer’s, and her wines did nothing to save it. The clan felt that the penury of her dinners disgraced the Mingott name, so long associated with good living; yet people continued to crowd her drawing-room in spite of the made dishes and the flat champagne. And when her son Lovell—determined to restore the family credit like a chef hired to rescue a sinking house—imported the best cook in New York, she only laughed and said: “What’s the use of two good cooks in one family, now that I’ve married the girls and can’t eat sauces?”

Thinking of these things, Archer let his gaze drift back to the Mingott box. Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law were facing their semicircle of critics with that Mingott _aplomb_ old Catherine had baked into all her tribe—so firm and smooth a crust that no knife of comment could break it. Only May betrayed, by a heightened colour, a sense of the gravity of the moment—perhaps because she knew he was watching her.

And the cause of the commotion—Ellen Olenska—sat in her corner with a composed grace, her eyes fixed on the stage. When she leaned forward she showed a little more shoulder and bosom than New York was accustomed to seeing—at least in a lady who had reasons for wishing to pass unnoticed. There was something in the naked line of it that disturbed him, as if an ingredient had been left out of a familiar dish and the absence threw the whole balance off. Few things seemed to Archer more awful than an offence against Taste—that far-off divinity of whom Form was the mere visible representative. Madame Olenska’s pale, serious face appealed to his fancy as suited to her unhappy situation; but the way her dress—without a tucker—sloped away from her thin shoulders both shocked and troubled him. He hated to imagine May exposed to the influence of a young woman so careless of the dictates that kept their world from turning rancid.

Behind him, as everyone chatted through the Mephistopheles-and-Martha scenes—those stretches of music New York treated like the clatter between courses—he heard one of the younger men begin: “After all, just _what_ happened?”

“Well—she left him; nobody attempts to deny that.”

“He’s an awful brute, isn’t he?” the youth went on, in the candid tone of one preparing to play the chivalric champion.

“The very worst; I knew him at Nice,” Lawrence Lefferts said, with the authority of a man who prided himself on knowing exactly how much to say and how little to risk. “A half-paralysed white sneering fellow—rather handsome head, but eyes with a lot of lashes. You know the kind: when he wasn’t with women he was collecting china. Paying any price for both, I understand.”

There was a general laugh—dry, well-bred, and somehow loud against the music, like a spoon tapping a glass too insistently.

“Well, then—?” the young champion pressed, and the question hung for a moment, anxious as a palate waiting for a verdict.

“Well, then; she bolted with his secretary.”

“Oh. I see.”

The youth’s face fell, as if a romantic sauce had curdled. Lefferts went on, more lightly: “It didn’t last long. A few months later I heard she was living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott went out to get her. He said she was desperately unhappy. That’s all right—but this parading her at the Opera’s another thing.”

“Perhaps,” the young man ventured—Thorley, Archer remembered; always earnest, always in danger of sincerity—“she’s too unhappy to be left at home.”

The remark was met with an irreverent laugh; the youth coloured, and tried to look as if he had meant one of those knowing innuendoes New York liked to brandish while pretending not to understand them.

“Well—it’s queer to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow,” someone murmured, with a sideways glance that found Archer without seeming to.

“Oh, that’s part of the campaign: Granny’s orders, no doubt,” Lefferts laughed. “When the old lady does a thing she does it thoroughly.”

The act was ending; the house shifted and stirred. Fans flickered like white wings; silk whispered; in the pit, voices rose and fell in that controlled murmur Archer always thought of as the city’s own dialect—an idiom of half-said things, of meanings carried in tone rather than words.

And suddenly he felt himself pushed—by pride, by protectiveness, by the odd desire to seize the sound of the room and change it—to decisive action. He wanted to be the first man to enter Mrs. Mingott’s box, to proclaim to the waiting world his engagement to May Welland, and to stand by her, publicly, through whatever difficulties her cousin’s anomalous situation might draw down. The impulse swept aside his scruples and sent him quickly through the red corridors to the farther side of the house, past doorways where the hum of talk thickened and thinned like the roar of a distant sea.

As he entered the box, May’s eyes met his. He saw at once that she understood his motive—understood without any need of explanation; for in their world the most important things were said in implication, and the closest alliances were made in silence. It gave him a sensation—almost like the sudden comfort of a familiar smell, bread or broth—that they were nearer in this wordless exchange than they could have been through any confession.

Her eyes said: “You see why Mamma brought me.”

His answered: “I would not for the world have had you stay away.”

“You know my niece Countess Olenska?” Mrs. Welland inquired, taking his hand with her smooth, sheltered smile.

Archer bowed, as was customary on being introduced to a lady, without offering his hand. Ellen Olenska bent her head slightly; her pale-gloved hands remained clasped on a huge fan of eagle feathers, its plumes spreading like some archaic trophy—something taken from another landscape, another set of sounds.

After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott—a large blonde lady in creaking satin—he sat down beside May and leaned toward her.

“I hope you’ve told Madame Olenska that we’re engaged?” he asked in a low tone. “I want everybody to know—I want you to let me announce it this evening at the ball.”

May’s face went rosy as dawn rising behind a white curtain; she looked at him with eyes that shone like a hearth newly kindled.

“If you can persuade Mamma,” she said; “but why should we change what is already settled?”

He answered only with his look; and, smiling more confidently, she added: “Tell my cousin yourself: I give you leave. She says she used to play with you when you were children.”

She pushed back her chair to make room, promptly and a little ostentatiously, with the innocent desire that the whole house should see what he was doing. Archer, feeling the weight of the watching—hearing it, too, in the faint change of the surrounding murmur—went and seated himself at Countess Olenska’s side.

“We _did_ use to play together, didn’t we?” she asked, turning her grave eyes on him.

“You were a horrid boy, and kissed me once behind a door; but it was your cousin Vandie Newland, who never looked at me, that I was in love with.” Her glance travelled over the horseshoe curve of boxes. “Ah—how this brings it all back to me—I see everybody here in knickerbockers and pantalettes,” she went on, with a slightly foreign trailing accent, and then looked back at him.

Her eyes were agreeable—too agreeable, Archer thought, in what they reflected. He was shocked that she could let them summon so unseemly a picture of the august tribunal before which, at that very moment, her case was being tried. Nothing could be in worse taste than misplaced flippancy. He stiffened his tone.

“Yes. You have been away a very long time.”

“Oh, centuries and centuries—so long,” she said, “that I’m sure I’m dead and buried, and this dear old place is heaven.”

For reasons he could not at once define, the words struck him as even more disrespectful than the first—an airy twist of language that made the whole of New York society sound like a joke told in another tongue. And all around them the house kept breathing—silk, voices, orchestral sound—like a great creature whose hearing was keenest when it pretended not to listen.
