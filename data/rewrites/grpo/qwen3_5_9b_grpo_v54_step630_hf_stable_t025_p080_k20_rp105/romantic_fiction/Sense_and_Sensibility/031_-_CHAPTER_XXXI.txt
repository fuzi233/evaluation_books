Marianne awoke the next morning to a consciousness of misery that was as heavy and unyielding as the night from which she had fled. Sleep had offered no respite, only a deeper immersion in her despair. Before breakfast was served, Elinor had exhausted every word of comfort, yet the cycle remained unbroken. With Elinor's steady, affectionate counsel on one side and Marianne's impetuous, fluctuating emotions on the other, they revisited the tragedy of Willoughby again and again. One moment Marianne could cling to the delusion of his innocence, viewing him as a victim of fate just as she was; the next, she rejected all consolation, convinced of his treachery. Her mood swung violently between a desperate indifference to the world's judgment and a frantic desire to hide away forever, only to rally with sudden energy against the very isolation she sought. Yet, in one matter, her resolve was absolute: she avoided Mrs. Jennings whenever possible, and when forced to endure her presence, she met it with a cold, determined silence. Her heart had hardened against the belief that Mrs. Jennings possessed any true compassion for her sorrow.

"No, no, no, it cannot be!" Marianne cried, her voice trembling with irritation. "She cannot feel. Her kindness is merely politeness; her good-nature is not tenderness. All she desires is gossip, and she cherishes me only because I provide it."

Elinor, observing her sister, recognized the injustice of this conclusion. She understood that Marianne, brilliant and well-intentioned though she was, lacked the candor to judge others fairly. Driven by her own intense sensibility and a polished exterior, Marianne expected everyone else to mirror her own depth of feeling, interpreting motives solely by their immediate impact on herself. This flaw became painfully evident shortly after breakfast. Mrs. Jennings entered their room with a letter held out eagerly, her face beaming with the joy of bringing comfort.

"Now, my dear, I bring you something that I am sure will do you good," she announced.

To Marianne, these words were a death knell. In an instant, her imagination conjured a letter from Willoughby, overflowing with tenderness and repentance, explaining everything, satisfying every doubt. She pictured him rushing into the room, kneeling at her feet, his eyes eloquently pleading his case. The fantasy lasted but a heartbeat before shattering into dust. Instead of Willoughby, she held the handwriting of her mother. The crushing weight of this disappointment, born of such a fleeting ecstasy, felt sharper than her initial grief. She could not bear to look at Mrs. Jennings. The kindness of the older woman seemed crueler than any malice; Marianne could only reproach her with violent tears, a silent accusation that Mrs. Jennings would never understand.

Mrs. Jennings, oblivious to the depth of the wound she had inflicted, offered only pity and urged Marianne to read the letter of comfort. But when Marianne finally calmed enough to open it, she found no solace. Every page was filled with Willoughby's name. Her mother, still convinced of their engagement and trusting in his constancy, had written only to plead for Marianne's honesty and openness. The letter was a torrent of maternal affection, of belief in their future happiness, so overwhelming that Marianne wept with agony as she read it.

The longing to return home seized her with renewed force. Her mother was dearer to her than ever, precisely because her confidence in Willoughby was so tragically misplaced. Marianne became wildly urgent to leave. Elinor, torn between the desire to keep her sister in London or send her to Barton, offered no advice of her own, suggesting only patience until her mother's wishes were clear. Eventually, Marianne agreed to wait.

Mrs. Jennings departed earlier than usual, unable to rest until the Middletons and Palmers could share in her grief. Refusing Elinor's offer of company, she left alone. Elinor, her heart heavy with the pain she was about to deliver, sat down to write to her mother, recounting the day's events and seeking guidance. Marianne, entering the drawing-room as Mrs. Jennings left, stood fixed beside her sister at the table. She watched the movement of Elinor's pen, grieving for the burden of the task, and grieving even more deeply for the effect it would have on their mother.

They sat in this quiet communion for about fifteen minutes when a sharp rap at the door shattered the stillness. Marianne's nerves, already frayed, could not withstand the sudden noise.

"Who can this be?" Elinor cried, startled. "So early! I thought we were safe."

Marianne moved to the window. "It is Colonel Brandon!" she said, her voice tinged with vexation. "We are never safe from him."

"He will not come in, as Mrs. Jennings is away," Elinor reasoned.

"I will not trust to that," Marianne retorted, retreating to her own room. "A man who has nothing to do with his own time possesses no conscience regarding his intrusion on others."

Her conjecture proved correct, though founded on error. Colonel Brandon did enter. Elinor, seeing the genuine solicitude in his disturbed, melancholy gaze and his anxious inquiry for Marianne, could not forgive her sister's harsh judgment.

"I met Mrs. Jennings in Bond Street," Brandon began after their initial greetings. "She encouraged me to come. I was eager to find you alone, for my sole wish is to offer comfort—not fleeting comfort, but lasting conviction to your sister's mind. My regard for her, for yourself, and for your mother... will you allow me to prove it by relating some circumstances? Nothing but sincere regard and an earnest desire to be useful could justify this, though after so many hours spent convincing myself of my rightness, I fear I may be wrong." He paused, looking uncertain.

"I understand," Elinor said gently. "You have something to tell me of Mr. Willoughby that will reveal his true character. Your testimony would be the greatest act of friendship for Marianne. My gratitude is immediate; hers will follow in time. Please, let me hear it."

"You shall," Brandon replied. "Briefly, when I left Barton last October... but I must go further back. I am an awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood; I hardly know where to begin. A short account of myself seems necessary. On such a subject, I have little temptation to be diffuse." He sighed heavily.

"You have probably forgotten a conversation," he continued, recovering his composure. "Between us, one evening at Barton Park, during a dance. I alluded to a lady I once knew, noting she resembled your sister, Marianne, in some measure."

"I have not forgotten it," Elinor answered.

Brandon looked pleased. "If I am not deceived by the partiality of memory, the resemblance is striking, both in mind and person. The same warmth of heart, the same eagerness of fancy. This lady was my nearest relation, an orphan from infancy under my father's guardianship. Our ages were nearly the same, and from our earliest years we were playfellows and friends. I cannot recall a time when I did not love Eliza. My affection for her, as we grew, was profound—perhaps so deep that, judging by my present forlorn gravity, you might think me incapable of such feeling. Hers, for me, was as fervent as Marianne's attachment to Mr. Willoughby, and though caused differently, it was equally unfortunate. At seventeen, she was lost to me forever. She was married against her will to my brother. Her fortune was large, but our family estate was encumbered. I fear this is all that can be said for the conduct of one who was both her uncle and guardian. My brother did not deserve her; he did not even love her. I had hoped her regard for me would sustain her, and for a time it did. But eventually, the misery of her situation, marked by great unkindness, overcame her resolution. Though she had promised me nothing, I have never explained how this came to pass. We were within hours of eloping to Scotland. The treachery—or folly—of my cousin's maid betrayed us. I was banished to a distant relative's house, and she was confined without liberty or society until my father's anger subsided. I had relied too heavily on her fortitude; the blow was severe. Yet, had her marriage been happy, so young as I was, a few months might have reconciled me. But it was not. My brother held no regard for her; his pleasures were base, and from the start he treated her unkindly. The consequence, upon a mind so young, lively, and inexperienced, was inevitable. She resigned herself to her misery initially, but it would have been better had she not lived to overcome the regrets my memory caused. With such a husband provoking inconstancy, and without a friend to advise or restrain her—my father died shortly after their marriage, and I was stationed in the East Indies—how could she avoid falling? Had I remained in England, perhaps... but I intended to promote their happiness by removing her from harm's way, hence my exchange to the colonies. The shock of her marriage was trifling compared to what I felt two years later when I heard of her divorce. It was that which cast this gloom upon me, even now recalling the suffering I endured."

He stopped, unable to continue, and paced the room. Elinor, deeply affected by his tale and his distress, remained silent. He approached her, taking her hand and pressing it with grateful respect. After a few moments of silent recovery, he proceeded.

"It was nearly three years after this unhappy period before I returned to England. My first care was to seek her, but the search was as melancholy as it was fruitless. I could not trace her beyond her first seducer, and feared she had only moved to sink deeper into sin. Her legal allowance was insufficient, and I learned from my brother that the power to receive it had been transferred months prior. He imagined, calmly, that her extravagance and distress had forced her to sell it for immediate relief. Finally, after six months in England, I found her. A former servant, now fallen into misfortune, directed me to a spunging-house where he was confined for debt. There, under similar confinement, lay my unfortunate sister. So altered, so faded, worn down by acute suffering of every kind! Hardly could I believe the sickly figure before me to be the remains of the lovely, blooming girl I once adored. What I endured in beholding her... I have no right to wound your feelings by describing it. That she appeared to be in the final stages of consumption was, in such a situation, my greatest comfort. Life could do nothing but grant time for a better preparation for death, and that was granted. I saw her placed in comfortable lodgings with proper attendants; I visited her daily during the rest of her short life. I was with her in her last moments."

He stopped again, and Elinor expressed her tender concern for his friend's fate.

"Your sister, I hope, cannot be offended," Brandon said, regaining his composure. "By the resemblance I fancied between her and my poor disgraced relation. Their fates and fortunes differ; had the natural sweetness of the one been guarded by a firmer mind or a happier marriage, she might have been all that you will live to see the other be. But to what does this lead? I seem to have distressed you for nothing. Ah, Miss Dashwood—a subject untouched for fourteen years is dangerous to handle! I will be more collected. She left to my care her only child, a little girl, the offspring of her first guilty connection, then about three years old. She loved the child and kept it with her. It was a precious trust. Gladly would I have educated her myself, had our situations allowed, but I had no family, no home. Thus, little Eliza was placed at school. I visited her whenever I could, and after my brother's death five years ago, she visited me at Delaford. I called her a distant relation, though I am aware I have often been suspected of a closer connection. It was three years ago, when she reached her fourteenth year, that I removed her from school to place her under the care of a respectable woman in Dorsetshire, who supervised several girls of her age. For two years, her situation gave me pleasure. But last February, almost a year ago, she suddenly disappeared. I had imprudently, at her earnest desire, allowed her to go to Bath with a young friend accompanying her father for his health. I knew the father to be a good man and thought well of his daughter—better than she deserved, for with obstinate secrecy she revealed nothing, gave no clue, though she certainly knew all. The father, well-meaning but not quick-sighted, could give no information; he had been confined to the house while the girls ranged the town, and he tried to convince me, as he convinced himself, that his daughter was entirely unconcerned. In short, I learned only that she was gone. For eight long months, the rest was left to conjecture. What I thought, what I feared, may be imagined; and what I suffered."

"Good heavens!" cried Elinor. "Could it be—could it be Willoughby?"

"The first news of her reached me in a letter from herself, last October," Brandon continued. "It was forwarded from Delaford, and I received it on the very morning of our intended party to Whitwell. This explains my sudden departure from Barton, which must have seemed strange to everyone and likely gave offense. Little did Mr. Willoughby imagine, when his looks censured me for incivility in breaking up the party, that I was called away to relieve one whom he had made poor and miserable. Had he known, what would it have availed? Would he have been less gay or less happy in the smiles of your sister? No, he had already done what no man who can feel for another would do. He left the girl whose youth and innocence he had seduced in a situation of utmost distress, with no creditable home, no help, no friends, ignorant of his address! He left her, promising to return; he neither returned, nor wrote, nor relieved her."
