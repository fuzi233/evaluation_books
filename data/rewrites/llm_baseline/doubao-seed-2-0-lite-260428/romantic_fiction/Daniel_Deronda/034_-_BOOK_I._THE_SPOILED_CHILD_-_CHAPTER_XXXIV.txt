*“His name is Israel. A witch’s spell once turned him into a dog… But every Friday at dusk, the magic breaks, and the dog is human once more.”*
—Heine, *Princess Sabbath*

When Daniel Deronda arrived at five o’clock sharp, the pawnshop’s front shutter was bolted tight, and the gentile servant girl opened the door just a crack to usher him in. She led him through to the back parlor, and he stopped short, surprised by how soft and warm the scene was, nothing like the harsh, clattering daylight of the shop floor he’d visited days before.

The old house stretched deep behind the storefront, and the large back room would no doubt have felt dim and gloomy at midday—but now a fine seven-branched brass oil lamp hung low over a table spread with snow-white linen, gilding every corner of the space. The smoky, dark-stained ceiling and walls threw the family gathered there into sharp, glowing relief, their dark eyes and warm complexions lit to a rich Venetian amber hue. The grandmother wore a gown of soft yellow-brown, a heavy thick gold chain strung around her throat in place of a necklace, and her lined face, framed by neat coiled silver hair, looked as stately as any old matriarch’s portrait under the lamplight. Young Mrs. Cohen was dressed in bold red and black, strands of oversized faux pearls looped again and again around her neck, the baby fast asleep in its cradle swaddled under a bright scarlet counterpane. Little Adelaide Rebekah was tricked out in braided amber, and Jacob Alexander darted around in black velveteen and bright scarlet stockings. All four pairs of dark eyes turned to him with unfeigned, glittering welcome, and Deronda felt almost ashamed of the supercilious, distant dislike he’d carried for this loud, prosperous family when he’d last called in the glare of day. Their hospitality was so unforced, so cordial, that both the younger and elder Mrs. Cohen seemed to carry a quiet, unshakable dignity on their own hearth, as they fussed to make him comfortable.

He glanced around at the mismatched old furniture as he talked: the heavy oaken bureau, the tall scuffed side table, the great blue-and-yellow glazed platter perched on it flanked by two tarnished old silver goblets, and the big dark vellum tome with its deep ribbed spine propped in front of them. A door at the far end of the room stood open to a lit inner chamber, and Deronda stored those little details away in the back of his mind while he pulled the new knife he’d bought for Jacob out of his pocket.

“Is this the sort you were asking for?” he said, offering it to the little boy.

Jacob subjected it to a rigorous, critical scrutiny, popping open every blade and the curved hook, then fished out his own old knife with the corkscrew attachment to hold them side by side for comparison.
“Why do you like the hook better than the corkscrew?” Deronda asked him, half smiling.
“’Caush I can get hold of things with a hook. Corkscrew won’t go into nothing but corks. But it’s better for you, you can draw corks,” Jacob lisped, perfectly serious.
“Then we’ve got a swap?” Deronda said, glancing over to see the grandmother watching them, delighted.
“What else have you got in your pockets?” Jacob asked, squinting up at him deliberatively.
“Hush, little love,” the grandmother chided softly, but Deronda, not wanting to spoil the game, shook his head gently.
“I think that’s our secret, isn’t it? Our deal was only about the knives.”
Jacob stared up into his face for a long, careful moment, as if weighing whether Deronda could be trusted, then solemnly held out his old corkscrew knife. Deronda pocketed it with equal gravity, and the small dark-haired boy darted off into the next room chattering a mile a minute, before skittering back just as his father stepped through the door. He snatched a tiny velveteen cap off a chair and pulled it onto his head to approach Cohen, who kept his own hat firmly on and gave his new visitor no immediate attention. He stood still as the two children ran to clasp his knees, laid a hand on each of their heads in turn, and murmured a soft Hebrew benediction, before Mrs. Cohen lifted the sleeping baby out of its cradle and held it up under his outstretched palms, so it might be blessed even in its rest. For a long quiet second, Deronda forgot this was a pawnbroker whose shop was lined with other people’s forfeited trinkets—there was nothing prosaic about that small, sacred scene.

“Hope you see what a welcome you’ve got in my house, sir,” Cohen said at last, setting his own hat down and slipping back into his familiar brisk, jovial self. “And you’re punctual. Nothing like a little pressure to sharpen the wits, eh?” He tapped his side pocket lightly. “It’s good for all of us in turns. I’ve had my share of scrambling to make payments, back when I first started mending every last kind of box you could name. Bracing for the mind. Now then, let’s have a look at that ring you mentioned.”

Deronda slipped the diamond ring off his finger. “I believe it cost me a hundred pounds. It’s surely enough collateral for fifty, and I’ll almost certainly redeem it in a month or so.”

Cohen’s glistening dark eyes drew a little closer together as he took the ring and turned it over in his fingers, clearly amused that this naive young gentleman actually thought pawnbrokers were eager to see people come back for their pledges. He handed it back, his face perfectly neutral. “Good, good. We’ll talk numbers after we’ve eaten. You’ll join us for our Sabbath meal, if you’ve no objection? My wife, my mother, we’d all be honored.”

The invitation was warmly echoed by the whole family, and Deronda accepted gladly. Everyone moved to gather around the table, where the only visible dish was one covered with a starched linen napkin. Mrs. Cohen set a china bowl of water near her husband so he might wash his hands—then Cohen pulled his hat back on, and called out in a loud, clear voice: “Mordecai!”

Deronda assumed it was some part of the Sabbath ritual, until a soft “Yes” drifted from the inner room, and he turned to look at the open door. He froze. Standing in the doorway was the exact enigmatic Jewish man he’d met that very morning in the old bookshop, the one whose face had lit up with such desperate hunger when he’d spotted the rare volume of Judaica Deronda had bought. Their eyes locked, both equally shocked, neither giving the smallest sign that they’d met before. When Mordecai took his seat at the far end of the table, he only bent his head to Deronda in a cold, distant nod, as if the sharp disappointment of that morning still hung sour between them.

Cohen washed his hands, reciting Hebrew blessings under his breath, then pulled the napkin off the dish to reveal two long, flat seed-dusted loaves: the Sabbath challah, the legacy of the manna that had fed the Israelites wandering in the desert. He broke off small pieces for everyone gathered around the table, even little Adelaide Rebekah, who stood balanced on her chair, her amber dress spread out, her small face solemn as she tried to look suitably reverent. Cohen spoke another blessing, the men removed their hats, and everyone sat down to eat. Deronda barely noticed what he was eating, too preoccupied by the quiet exchange of half-furtive, fascinated glances passing between him and Mordecai. The man no longer wore the threadbare rusty black coat he’d had on at the bookshop, but a faded light drab overcoat that had once been a fine loose paletot, now shrunk thin from years of washing—and the contrast made his sharp, eager dark face look even more striking, like the prophet Ezekiel pulled straight out of an old, forgotten scroll. The family passed him the tenderest fried fish tails, the best bits of the meal, as if tending to a fragile, cherished poor relation.

Cohen kept the conversation bright and lively, gushing about the Queen and the royal family, the Emperor and Empress of France—topics the whole family threw themselves into with gusto. Young Mrs. Cohen could recite every distinguished royal birthday off by heart, and her mother-in-law chimed in with stories of when the Emperor and Empress had visited London ten years prior.
“Our baby’s named Eugenie Esther,” she told Deronda brightly.
“Would you believe the Emperor looks exactly like a cousin of mine?” the grandmother put in. “I saw him through the crowd and it hit me like lightning. Couldn’t believe my eyes.”
“Me and my mother went to see them at the Crystal Palace,” Cohen laughed, stroking his mother’s shoulder affectionately. “I had a job keeping her from getting squeezed flat. Told myself even if I had a hundred mothers, I’d never drag one to a royal public viewing again—especially not when you’ve only got the one.”

Deronda seized his chance to speak. “Your mother’s been a widow a long while, I imagine? That must have made caring for her all the more important when you were young.”
“Ay, a good many yahrzeits have passed since I had to manage for the both of us,” Cohen said, nodding quickly. “I started young. Makes you a sharp knife, as they say.”
“Makes what a sharp knife, father?” Jacob lisped, his cheek stuffed full of sweet cake.
Cohen winked at Deronda. “Having your nose put to the grindstone, lad.”
Jacob slipped off his chair, cake still in hand, and trotted over to Mordecai, who had not spoken a single word all meal. “What does that mean? Putting my nose to the grindstone?”
“It means you bear being hurt without making a noise,” Mordecai said, his soft voice gentle as he turned his kind dark eyes to the small boy. Jacob held out his cake for Mordecai to take a bite, and the thin man smiled and obliged, making the little boy glow with pride. Deronda took the chance to turn to him directly.
“I suspect you’re the one with all the book learning in this house, aren’t you?” he said. “You have studied a great deal, I think.”
“I have studied,” Mordecai said quietly, looking back at him, the old spark of interest from the bookshop waking in his eyes, as if his earlier disappointment was melting away. “And you? You read German, from the book you were buying this morning.”
“Yes, I studied in Germany. Do you work in Mr. Ram’s bookshop full time?” Deronda asked.
“No, I only look after it for him while he goes to eat his midday meal,” Mordecai said, leaning forward a little. “Do you know Hebrew?”
“I’m sorry to say I don’t, not at all.”
Mordecai’s face fell. He dropped his gaze to his folded hands in his lap, and said no more. Deronda noticed for the first time the faint, laborious catch in his breath, the quiet sign that consumption was slowly wasting him away.

“I never had time for that sort of book learning,” Cohen put in, tapping Deronda’s ring lightly. “I had to learn about things that make a living, like precious stones. I’m not afraid to take that ring of yours at my own valuation. So—what sum were you looking for?”
“Fifty or sixty pounds,” Deronda said, a little carelessly.
Cohen pushed his hands into his pockets, stared at him with those glistening bright eyes, and shook his head. “Can’t do that. Happy to oblige, but forty pounds is the highest I can go. Forty, take it or leave it.”
Deronda glanced up to see Mordecai looking at him again, his curious gaze fixed on his face. “Very well,” he said. “I’ll redeem it in a month or so.”
“Good. I’ll write the ticket out later,” Cohen said casually, holding up a finger to signal silence. All the men pulled their hats back on, and Cohen opened the Sabbath grace, the rest of the family chiming in with responses, until Mordecai lifted his chin, his thin hands clasped loosely before him, and chanted the final, long solemn portion of the thanksgiving in a low, ringing tone. There could be no starker contrast between the loud practical pawnbroker at the head of the table and this otherworldly, emaciated man singing the old prayers as if he spoke directly to heaven. The moment the last note faded, Mordecai gave Deronda a small, slight nod, rose quietly, walked back into his inner room, and shut the door behind him.

“He’s a very remarkable man, that Mordecai,” Deronda said, turning to Cohen. Cohen immediately tapped the side of his temple, sticking his tongue out just a little to make it clear he thought Mordecai was a little touched, not quite right in the head.
“Is he part of your family?” Deronda asked.
The whole family smiled, as if the idea was absurd. “No, no, we took him in out of charity,” Cohen said. “He used to work for me, got weaker and weaker, so we gave him a roof. He’s a bit of a burden, but he brings blessings down on the house, and he teaches the boy his letters, plus he mends all the watches and fine jewelry that comes through the shop.”

Deronda could not press for more details. Cohen immediately steered the conversation back to their business, writing out the pawn ticket, counting out forty pounds in notes and gold, and handing both over to Deronda in exchange for the diamond ring. It would have felt rude to linger any longer than necessary, so Deronda took his leave, the pawn ticket tucked safely in his breast pocket. He knew then that he would come back, the very second he returned to town after Christmas. There was some strange, unnameable thread of fate tying him to that quiet, burning-eyed man in the back room—some secret history he had to uncover, some purpose waiting to be revealed between them.
