Luckily for us, Infadoos and his fellow chiefs knew every unmarked side path snaking through the great city of Loo like the backs of their own hands, so we slipped past unspotted in the pitch of the post-eclipse dark, making steady, fast progress for well over an hour. Then the shadow began to lift: the curved edge of the sun peeped back out from behind the moon, spilling a thin silver streak of light across the black sky, followed by a soft, vivid ruddy glow that hung over the horizon like a lantern strung between stars. Five minutes later the stars faded, and we could finally make out our surroundings.

We stood clear of Loo’s walls, approaching a massive flat-topped hill almost two miles around, a formation common across the South African highveld. It barely rose 200 feet high, but it curved in the shape of a horseshoe, its sides steep, boulder-strewn, and near-precipitous. The wide grass tableland on its flat summit made for a perfect natural camping ground, long used as a military cantonment strong enough to hold thousands. We toiled up its slopes in the returning moonlight, and quickly spotted far more than the usual 3,000-man garrison: regiments upon regiments were camped across its top, their fires dotted like pinpricks through the dark.

When we reached the summit, we found thousands of men roused from their sleep, huddled in tight, shivering clusters, half-convinced the sun had died forever. We passed through their bewildered ranks without a word, until we reached a small hut at the very center of the plateau – and stared, astonished, to find two attendants waiting with the small pile of personal goods we’d been forced to abandon mid-flight from Loo.
“I sent messengers back for them,” Infadoos explained, holding up a familiar bundle of fabric: Good’s long-lost trousers.
Good let out a yell of unfiltered delight, lunged for them, and immediately began yanking them on over his legs.
“Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs away?” Infadoos said, sounding genuinely regretful.
But Good, a very modest man, was not to be swayed. The Kukuana people only got one more glimpse of those famous pale calves after that; from then on, they had to sate their curiosity with his single half-grown whisker, his famous transparent glass eye, and the detachable set of false teeth he loved to pop out at stunned onlookers for jokes.
Still gazing fondly at Good’s newly fastened trousers, Infadoos next told us he’d ordered every regiment on the hill to muster at dawn, to lay out their full plan of rebellion to the men, and formally introduce Ignosi as their rightful, exiled king.

When the sun cleared the horizon, 20,000 men – the absolute flower of the entire Kukuana army – formed up on the open plain at the plateau’s center, drawn up three sides of a massive dense square, their spears glinting so bright they looked like a field of shards of light. We took our place on the open fourth side, ringed by all the kingdom’s highest chiefs and officers, and after a roar for silence died down, Infadoos began to speak.
A natural orator like all high-born Kukuanas, he wove the story of Ignosi’s father, the old rightful king, who had been basely murdered by his own brother Twala, the usurper, before Twala’s men chased his pregnant wife and newborn child out into the cold veld to starve. He laid out the cruelty of Twala’s reign, pointing to the previous night’s witch-hunt, where the king’s men dragged dozens of the noblest families in the land out of their huts to slaughter for no crime at all. Then he told the crowd the white lords from the stars had seen their suffering, crossed the snow-capped mountains to find the lost exiled king Ignosi, and used their great magic to snuff out the full moon to save Foulata, and slay Twala’s vicious son Scragga. The crowd hummed with approval, their voices rising like distant thunder.

Ignosi stepped forward, his massive frame towering over the chiefs around him, and repeated his uncle’s story, his deep voice booming across the square. “Ye have heard my words! Now choose between me, and the man who sits on my father’s throne – the uncle who slit his brother’s throat, who chased his brother’s baby out to die in the dark cold. The chiefs here can prove who I am, for they have seen the royal snake mark coiled around my waist. Would these white magic lords stand at my side if I were not your true king? Look up! The dark they wove to confound Twala and cover our flight, dark in the hour of the full moon, is still fresh in your memory!”
“It is!” the 20,000 men roared back as one.
“I am your king!” Ignosi shouted, lifting his broad-bladed battleaxe high over his head so the sun flashed off its edge. “If any man here says that is a lie, let him step forward now. I will fight him single-handed, and his blood will be the proof I speak truth. Step forward!”
Not a single man moved.
“I am your king,” he continued, “and if you stand at my side through this coming battle, you will share in all my victory. You will get cattle, new wives, the highest honor among all the kingdom’s regiments. If you fall, I will fall fighting beside you. And I make this sacred promise to you: when I sit on my father’s throne, bloodshed will end. No more witch-finders dragging you off to die for no crime. No more raiding parties eating your kraals bare. Every man will sleep safe in his own hut, no fear. Justice will be blind, and equal for all. Have you chosen, chiefs, captains, soldiers, people?”
“We have chosen, O King!” the shout rolled over the hill.
“It is well. Look east, west, north and south – see Twala’s messengers racing out of Loo right now, summoning every loyal soldier he can find to come and kill me, kill you, and kill my white friends. By tomorrow, or the day after at latest, he will march on us with an overwhelming army. I will see who among you is truly my man, who does not fear to die for what is just – and no man who fights bravely will be forgotten when we divide the spoils. Now go to your huts, and make yourselves ready for war.”
A single chief lifted his hand, and 20,000 voices roared the ancient royal salute: “*Koom*!” The rebellion had the full army’s backing. The battalions marched off to their positions at once.

Half an hour later, we held a war council with all the regiment commanders. From our vantage point on the hill we could see Twala’s forces mustering in Loo, runners streaming out in every direction. We had 20,000 men, seven of the kingdom’s finest regiments. By all Infadoos and the chiefs’ calculations, Twala already had 30 to 35 thousand loyal men assembled in Loo, and would gather at least another 5 thousand by midday tomorrow. Deserters might trickle over to our side, but we could not count on that. It was clear Twala planned to crush us fast. Already, armed patrols circled the base of our hill, and all signs pointed to a full attack coming the next day – the usurper would spend the rest of the day undoing the “magic” eclipse propaganda that had shaken his troops’ morale.

We threw every man we could onto fortification work. The paths up the hill were the only weak points, so we blocked every single one with massive stacked boulders, piled more loose rocks at the cliff edges to roll down on advancing attackers, mapped out every regiment’s exact battle station, and turned the old sanatorium hill (once used to let troops recover from malaria in the lowlands) into a near-impenetrable fortress.

Just before sundown, we spotted a small party of men climbing towards us from Loo, one holding a palm leaf of truce high above his head. Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and the three of us went down to the foot of the hill to meet him. The herald was a tall, arrogant man wrapped in a fine leopard-skin cloak.
“Greeting from the king!” he shouted. “The lion greets the jackals that snarl at his heels, who make unholy war against their rightful ruler!”
“Speak,” I said.
“These are Twala’s words. Surrender to his mercy before worse befalls you. He has already torn the shoulder from the black war bull, and drives it bleeding around his camp. His terms are merciful, fit for a great king: one in ten of you rebels will die, all the rest will be spared. But the white man Incubu who slew his son Scragga, the false king Ignosi, and traitor Infadoos, will die by slow torture as an offering to the Silent Three Giants in the mountains. That is the king’s mercy.”
I leaned forward, and shouted my reply loud enough that all the nearby soldiers could hear. “Go back to Twala, you dog. Tell him we – Ignosi rightful king of the Kukuanas, Incubu, Bougwan, Macumazahn the star-men who snuffed the moon, Infadoos of the royal blood, and all the thousands of armed men here – will never surrender. Before two suns set, Twala’s corpse will stiffen at his own city gate, and Ignosi, the son of the king he murdered, will sit on his throne. Run back now, before we whip you off our hill. Be careful you do not lift a hand to strike men who can command the sky to go dark.”
The herald laughed loud and mocking. “Big words will not frighten us, star-men. Show half that bravery on the battlefield tomorrow. Fight all you like, before the crows pick your bones cleaner and whiter than your pale skins. Farewell. Don’t run back to your stars – wait for me, I’ll find you in the fight.” He turned and vanished back down the slope, and the sun sank below the horizon.

It was a long, busy night. Even exhausted, we kept working on final battle preparations by moonlight, messengers coming and going nonstop from our council hut. An hour after midnight, everything that could be done was done. The camp fell quiet, save for the occasional sharp challenge from a sentry. Sir Henry, Ignosi, a chief, and I went down to patrol the outposts. Spears glinted out of the dark from every bush and rock the second we came near, vanishing again the moment we gave the password. No man was sleeping at his post. We picked our way back through thousands of sleeping warriors, many of whom would never wake up to see another sunrise. The moonlight streaked over their spears, gilding their still faces, making them look gaunt, ghostly, almost unhuman. Their tall war plumes tossed in the cold night wind.
“How many of these do you think will be alive this same time tomorrow?” Sir Henry asked me, quiet.
I shook my head. My tired mind could almost see the shadow of death already hovering over half the camp. I thought of how tonight they slept healthy and whole, tomorrow they would lie cold and still, their wives widows, their children fatherless, their names forgotten by all but the grass that grew over their graves. The moon would keep shining, the wind would keep blowing over the plains, long after all of us were gone.
“I am properly frightened, Curtis,” I admitted. “I doubt any of us will be alive by tomorrow night. They outnumber us too greatly. We’re lucky if we even hold this hill for a few hours.”
Sir Henry stroked his thick yellow beard and laughed. “I’ve heard you say that before, Quatermain. But we’ll take a good number of them down with us. This whole mess is not a fight we ever should have gotten tangled up in, but we’re in it now, so we might as well make the most of it. Personally, I’d far rather die fighting than any other way. And since it looks more and more like we’ll never find my poor lost brother, it makes that idea easier to bear. Fortune favors the brave. We might still pull this off. Either way, this battle will be hell. We’ve got reputations to keep up – we’ll be right in the thick of it.” There was a glint in his eye that told me he actually, secretly, loved the thrill of a good fight.

We slept for a couple of hours, woken at dawn by Infadoos, who came to tell us thousands of troops were pouring out of Loo, and Twala’s skirmishers were already pushing our outposts back to the foot of the hill. We got dressed for the fight, pulling on our chain mail shirts – we were more grateful for those heavy steel garments than any treasure we’d ever found. Sir Henry went full native war regalia: a fine leopard commander’s cloak slung around his neck, the rare black ostrich feather plume only worn by top generals bound around his forehead, a magnificent white ox-tail loincloth, heavy rhino-handle battleaxe, round white ox-hide shield, throwing knives at his waist, and his revolver tucked in his belt. He looked magnificent, the perfect picture of a giant warrior king. Ignosi matched him, equally resplendent.
Good and I looked far less imposing. Good refused to take off his newly recovered corduroy trousers, tucking the chain mail carefully down over them, monocle perched on one half-shaved face, and he looked more like a befuddled traveling salesman dragged into a war than a great wizard. My chain mail was far too big, so I pulled it over all my clothes, making me look strangely bulky, then took off my trousers entirely, keeping only my soft veldschoen shoes, my legs bare so I could run fast if we had to retreat fast. I pinned a huge bloodthirsty-looking plume to the top of my shooting hat, grabbed a spear and a shield I had no idea how to use, two throwing knives, my revolver, and we gave our rifles over to bearers to carry behind us – ammo was too scarce to waste it on the front lines, and they’d be useless in a mass spear charge.
We climbed up to the small brown stone koppie lookout on the edge of the plateau, our command post, surrounded by Infadoos’s finest regiment, the Greys, 3,500 of the best fighters in the entire Kukuana army, held in reserve. We looked out over the plains below, and saw Twala’s army pouring out of Loo in endless ant-like columns. Three separate forces, each 11 or 12 thousand men strong. One veered right, one veered left, the third marched slow and steady straight for our hill.
“Ah,” Infadoos said, grim. “They are coming to attack us on three sides at once.”
Our plateau was a mile and a half around, far too wide for our 20,000 defenders to spread thin across every point. There was nothing for it but to send our orders out immediately: every regiment would hold their assigned stretch of slope, no matter the cost. The battle was about to begin.
