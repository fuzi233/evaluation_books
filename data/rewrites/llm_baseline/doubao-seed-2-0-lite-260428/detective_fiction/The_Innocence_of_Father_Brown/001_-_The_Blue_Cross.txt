Where the silver ribbon of dawn bleeds into the glittering green ribbon of the North Sea, the Harwich ferry bumped the quay and spilled a chattering swarm of passengers onto English soil. One man in the crowd drew no second glance, and had no wish to: his pale grey holiday jacket, crisp white waistcoat and silver straw hat strung with a grey-blue ribbon clashed only faintly with the sharp, official gravity of his lean, dark face, crowned by a neat, short black beard that looked distinctly Spanish, as if it belonged tucked under an Elizabethan ruff. He smoked a cigarette with the exaggerated stillness of a man pretending to laze. No one would have guessed that the grey wool hid a loaded revolver, the white waistcoat a top-tier Paris police identification card, and the straw hat one of the sharpest investigative minds on the continent. That man was Aristide Valentin, head of the Paris Sûreté, the most famous detective alive, travelling from Brussels to London to pull off the arrest of the century.

Flambeau was on English ground. Three nations’ police had tracked the colossal criminal across borders from Ghent to Brussels, then on to the Hook of Holland, and their best guess was he would use the chaos and crowds of London’s ongoing Eucharistic Congress to vanish into the throng. He could pose as a low-ranking clerk, a visiting secretary, any unassuming hanger-on. No one could be certain what Flambeau would do. No one ever was.

It has been decades now since that titan of crime abruptly vanished from public view, leaving the world as quiet as it was said to be after Roland fell. At the peak of his career (or the nadir, to be exact) Flambeau was a larger-than-life international figure as famous as the Kaiser. Every morning’s papers ran headlines of him evading the law for one spectacular crime by pulling off an even more outrageous second. He was a giant, reckless Gascon, and wild tales spread of his absurd, athletic sense of humour: how he once flipped a investigating magistrate upside down to “clear his overcluttered mind”, how he ran down the full length of the Rue de Rivoli carrying one uniformed policeman under each arm. For all his superhuman strength he rarely resorted to violence; his real speciality was ingenious, large-scale robbery, each heist wilder and more creative than the last. He founded the fake Tyrolean Dairy Company in London with no cows, no carts, no premises, serving a thousand paying customers simply by picking up the milk bottles left on his unsuspecting neighbours’ doorsteps and delivering them to his own subscribers. He once tricked a young woman into sending him every detail of her fortune hunting scheme by photographing his instructions infinitesimally small onto the glass slides of a microscope she kept at her desk. His tricks were often so simple they bordered on genius: legend says he snuck out at midnight to repaint every house number on an entire street just to divert a wealthy traveller into a trap. He definitely invented a portable fake post box, which he planted on quiet suburban corners to catch unwitting strangers dropping postal orders into it. And for all his six foot four frame, he was a world class acrobat, able to leap like a grasshopper and vanish into treetops like a monkey. Valentin knew perfectly well that catching Flambeau would be only the start of the adventure.

But he still had no idea how to find him.

There was one flaw Flambeau could never disguise no matter how brilliant his costumes: his absurd height. If Valentin spotted a 6 foot 4 apple seller, a giant grenadier, or even an exceptionally tall duchess, he would have arrested them on the spot. But there was no one on the ferry, no one on the train north from Harwich, who could possibly be Flambeau, any more than a house cat could be a disguised giraffe. The full list of fellow passengers came to six: a short railway official bound for the terminus, three stocky market gardeners picked up two stations out, a tiny widowed lady heading up from a small Essex town, and a very short Roman Catholic priest from an even smaller Essex village. Valentin almost laughed out loud when he laid eyes on the priest. He was the very essence of the flat, quiet East Anglian countryside: a round, dull face like a Norfolk dumpling, eyes as blank and unremarkable as the North Sea, and a pile of crumpled brown paper parcels he could barely keep hold of. The Eucharistic Congress had clearly dragged dozens of such mild, unworldly men out of their isolated parishes, blinking and helpless as moles dug up from the earth. Valentin was a strict, sceptical French atheist with no fondness for priests, but he felt nothing but pity for this one. The man toted a large shabby umbrella that kept slipping out of his grip and clattering to the floor. He could not seem to work out which end of his return ticket was up, and cheerfully explained to every passenger in the carriage that he had to be extra careful, because one of his brown paper parcels held a piece of solid silver “stuck with blue stones” that he was bringing to the congress. His soft, bumbling innocence amused Valentin all the way to Tottenham, where the priest somehow managed to get all his parcels off the train, then turn around to climb back on for his lost umbrella. Valentin even took pity to warn him, in good nature, that he should not go round telling strangers he was carrying valuable silver.

By the time the train pulled into Liverpool Street, Valentin was certain he had not missed his quarry. He headed straight for Scotland Yard to register his status and arrange backup if he needed it, lit a fresh cigarette, and set off wandering the London streets. Pacing past quiet residential squares beyond Victoria Station, he stopped dead. The square was a perfect, quiet little slice of London: tall, flat prosperous houses that looked almost entirely uninhabited, a patch of shrubbery in the middle as empty and isolated as a green Pacific islet. One side of the square jutted up higher than the rest, and built into its terrace was a tiny, out-of-place restaurant that looked as if it had wandered over from bohemian Soho. Potted dwarf plants lined its windows, striped lemon-yellow and white blinds were drawn half way down, and a steep flight of steps ran up from the pavement to its front door, at a sharp angle like a fire escape leading up to a first floor window. Valentin stood smoking, staring at that odd little restaurant for a long minute.

The most unbelievable part of miracles is that they really do happen. Clouds do sometimes drift together to form the distinct shape of a human eye. A tree will stand alone in the middle of a long, confusing journey shaped exactly like a question mark. Valentin, a fiercely logical Frenchman who trusted only hard, proven facts, knew better than most that wisdom has to prepare for the unforeseen. He had no leads. Flambeau could be anywhere: a tall tramp camping on Wimbledon Common, a head waiter at the Hotel Metropole, anyone. When he had no clues to follow, Valentin followed the irrational. He did not head for banks, or known criminal hideouts, or police safe houses. He wandered down empty terraces, blind alleys, lanes blocked with rubbish, crescent streets that looped pointlessly back on themselves. His logic was simple: if you have no clues, the odds are that the same odd little quirk that catches your eye now will have caught the eye of the criminal you are chasing. Something about that steep, strange flight of steps up to the restaurant, something about the quiet off-kilter charm of the place, pricked his intuition. He climbed the steps, sat by the window, and ordered a cup of black coffee.

It was mid-morning, and he had not eaten yet. Remnants of other guests’ breakfasts littered the table, reminding him he was hungry, so he added a poached egg to his order, and absentmindedly reached for the sugar basin to tip some white granules into his coffee. He lifted the cup to his lips, then set it down so fast the coffee sloshed over the rim. He had just taken a big sip of pure salt.

He stared at the vessel he had poured from. It was unmistakably a sugar basin, made for holding sugar as surely as a champagne bottle is made for holding champagne. He checked the rest of the table. The two salt cellars sitting next to it were full of white granules too. He tasted them. They were sugar. He looked around the room, newly alert for other traces of the same weird joke, and spotted only one oddity: a dark splotch of spilled liquid on one of the papered white walls. He rang the bell for the waiter.

The bleary, fuzzy-haired waiter hurried over, and Valentin asked him to taste the sugar, pretending to tease him about the restaurant’s supposed famous bad quality. The man took a sip, yawned, and instantly jolted fully awake.

“Have you and the staff got some joke you play every morning on guests, swapping the salt and sugar round?” Valentin asked dryly. “Does it never get boring?”

The stammering waiter swore no one at the restaurant meant any such thing, that it must be a terrible mistake. He picked up the sugar basin, then the salt cellars, looking more and more confused, before he made an excuse to run and fetch the proprietor. The proprietor inspected the vessels too, and looked just as bewildered.

Then the waiter suddenly blurted out, “I think – I think it was those two clergymen.”

“Two clergymen?”

“The two priests that came in first thing this morning, before we even took the shutters down,” the waiter said, pointing excitedly at the dark splotch on the wall. “They threw soup at the wall!”

Valentin stared. “Threw soup at the wall?”

“Yes!” the owner cut in, stepping forward to explain. “They were quiet, perfectly respectable men. One of them paid the bill and left almost at once. The other, who was the slower, more confused one, took ages to gather all his things together. Right before he stepped out the door, he picked up his half-full soup bowl and hurled the whole thing straight at that wall. We were both in the back kitchen, and by the time we ran out they were gone down the street. We only spotted them turn the next corner into Carstairs Street.”

Valentin was on his feet instantly, hat pulled on, walking stick in hand. In the total absence of clues, this was the first strange, obvious breadcrumb he had found. He paid his bill, pushed through the glass doors, and turned sharp into Carstairs Street.

His sharp detective eye caught a flash of something odd on a shop front half way down the road, and he doubled back. It was a greengrocer’s stall, piles of fruit and vegetables laid out on the pavement with handwritten price cards. The largest two piles were oranges and shelled nuts. The card sitting on top of the nuts read in bold blue chalk: *Best Tangerine Oranges, Two a Penny*. The card sitting on the oranges read *Finest Brazil Nuts, 4d a Pound*. Valentin smiled. He recognised this very specific brand of chaotic humour, very recently seen. He pointed it out to the red-faced, sullen shopkeeper loitering by the door.

The shopman did not smile back. He just swapped the cards to their right places.

“Forgive the odd question,” Valentin said, leaning lazily on his cane, “but what connects a couple of wrong labels at a greengrocer’s shop with two clergymen, one very tall and one very short, who passed by here earlier?”

The shopkeeper’s eyes bulged. He looked seconds away from lunging over his stall to grab Valentin by the collar. “If you’re a friend of theirs,” he snarled, “you can tell them from me that next time they knock my whole apple stack all over the road I’ll crack their stupid clerical heads, priest collars or no priest collars.”

“Ah,” Valentin said, sympathetic. “They spilled your apples?”

“One of the big ones did,” the shopman said. “Rolled every last one of them all over the pavement. I couldn’t chase after him, I had to stop and pick them all up.”

“Which way did they go?” Valentin asked.

“First left up that road, then straight across the next square.”

Valentin thanked him and vanished. On the other side of the square he stopped a passing policeman. “Urgent. Have you seen two clergymen in shovel hats, walking that way?”

The policeman chuckled. “Aye sir. One of them was so lost he stood right in the middle of the road like he didn’t know which way was up –”

“Which direction?” Valentin snapped.

“They hopped that yellow bus over there, the one that runs all the way to Hampstead.”

Valentin flashed his official police card. “Call two constables to come with me at once.” Before the slow-moving officer could protest, he was across the road, and a minute later he was joined on the pavement by an inspector and a plainclothes officer. They piled onto the top deck of the lumbering yellow bus.

“Sir, we could go three times faster in a taxi,” the inspector protested.

“True,” Valentin said calmly, lighting a fresh cigarette. “If only we knew exactly where we were going. When you are hunting a man whose path makes no logical sense, you do not get ahead of him. You follow him. Stop when he stops, wander when he wanders, slow to his pace. That way you see exactly what he saw, and spot exactly what he thought was strange.”

The bus crawled up the long roads of north London for what felt like hours, rolling past scruffy suburban taverns, bare scrubby patches of waste ground, the endless stretch of roads that seemed to unspool forever. Twilight was beginning to bleed into the sky when Valentin jolted upright, slapped both his officers on the shoulder, and yelled at the driver to stop.

They stumbled off the bus, confused, and followed his pointing finger to a large restaurant window built into the front of a grand gilt-trimmed public house. The whole window was made of frosted patterned glass – except right in the centre, where there was a huge, jagged, star-shaped black crack, as if someone had smashed it with a heavy blunt object.

“There,” Valentin yelled, waving his stick. “That is our next clue.”

“Proof of what?” the inspector said, bewildered. “Why on earth would this broken window have anything to do with the two priests?”

Valentin almost snapped his bamboo walking stick in half with frustration. “Proof? Good man, there is no proof. The odds are twenty to one this has nothing to do with them. But what else do we have? We can either follow this one wild chance or give up and go home to bed.”

They tramped into the restaurant, sat down for a late lunch, and Valentin called the waiter over, slipping him a huge tip when he paid. The waiter perked up instantly.

“Ah yes, sir, that smashed window,” he said, grinning. “Oddest thing you ever heard. Two gentlemen in black, two foreign priests, came in earlier for a quiet cheap lunch. One of them paid up and left straight away. The other was just heading out the door when I looked at the change he gave me and realised he’d paid more than three times the bill. I ran after him, held up the slip of paper to show him – and that’s when I realised I’d written 14 shillings on the bill by accident, not 4. He just smiled at me and said, ‘No worries, that extra will cover the window.’ ‘What window?’ I said. And before I could move, he whacked our front window straight through with his umbrella.”

The inspector cursed under his breath, wondering if they were chasing two escaped lunatics.

“I was so stunned I couldn’t even stop him,” the waiter said. “He walked straight out, joined his friend round the corner, and they ran so fast up Bullock Street I couldn’t catch them even when I gave chase.”

“Bullock Street.” Valentin was already running for the door, charging up the narrow thoroughfare after the two strange priests, his two bewildered backup officers trailing fast behind him.
