The air inside Castlewood House carried the weight of a thousand small grievances, settled into the woodwork and the silence between the ticking clocks. Madam Warrington moved through this domestic architecture with the brisk efficiency of a woman who believed that order was the only antidote to chaos. To her, the sprawling estate was not a collection of rooms but a machine of duties, and she was its sole operator. A pie was not baked until her little finger had approved the dough; a spinster maid was not left alone until her loom was humming; the overseers in the tobacco fields were watched with eyes that missed nothing. Even sickness was managed with a clinical resolve, her medicine chest stocked with distillations and elixirs that terrified the neighbors into health. She was a queen of the mundane, ruling a kingdom where the currency was labor and the law was routine.

Outside, the world of Virginia was still a patchwork of scattered settlements, little villages clinging to the land like barnacles. Around Castlewood, other gentry ruled their own fiefdoms, often with a desperate grandeur. They were men of noble blood but ragged pockets, hosting endless feasts for strangers while their liveries frayed at the cuffs. Among them, Madam Warrington stood apart—not by wealth, for she was generous, but by a rigid economy of spirit. Rumors circulated that the Duke of Cumberland might be appointed viceroy, a joke that made her gossips titter. With grave dignity, she dismissed the notion, reminding them that royalty itself had once sought alliance with the Esmonds. Her authority was absolute, rooted in a belief that her station demanded a certain hauteur, however misplaced.

Her household included Mrs. Mountain, a widow who had arrived from England aboard the *Young Rachel*, carrying with her the ghost of a marriage that had cost her everything. Once a companion to Madam Esmond at the Kensington Academy, Mrs. Mountain had defended the American girl against the teasing of English peers, forging a bond that survived the deaths of husbands and fortunes. She was a woman of cheerful resilience, playing cards, singing, and managing the linen press with a jolly competence that charmed the unmarried gentlemen of the colony. Yet, she refused to remarry, choosing the solitude of Castlewood over the uncertainty of new vows. The two women existed in a state of perpetual friction, a dance of quarrels and reconciliations that defined the rhythm of the house. When illness struck, they fought side by side, their rivalry dissolving into shared maternal vigilance. Mrs. Mountain's loyalty was absolute; she stayed year after year, declining Captain Franks' invitations to leave, content to serve the memory of the Colonel, whom Madam Warrington revered above all else.

Suitors inevitably arrived, drawn by the reputation of the Esmond heiress. Madam Warrington received them with practiced grace, accepting their flattery as tribute due to her rank. She played the role of the imperious princess, growing bored and cruel or kind depending on her whims, aware that such behavior was expected of her. She tolerated the verses stolen from pocketbooks by Rattling Jack Firebrace and the bets placed by Tom Humbold regarding knighthoods, treating them as the inevitable taxes of her position. But beneath this performance lay a deep-seated insecurity. The younger son, Harry, reveled in the chaos, hunting and fighting alongside the jesters, while the elder, George, viewed their mother's vanity with a burning, private rage. He despised the sycophants, mocking their presence with juvenile satire that drove his mother to tears. "The Esmonds were always of a jealous disposition," she would lament, unaware that her own pride was the very poison her son identified.

Seeking salvation from this domestic discord, Madam Warrington took the boys to Williamsburg, hoping the formal education of the capital would civilize them. There, they encountered the thunderous voice of Mr. Whitfield, a preacher whose eloquence shattered the dull complacency of the local clergy. The boys, initially swept up in the fervor, sang hymns with genuine zeal, imagining lives of piety instead of military service. But Whitfield's mission was too vast for a single congregation; he departed to preach to the benighted masses, promising to send a successor who could teach them not just letters, but the "precious" sciences of the soul.

That successor arrived in the person of Mr. Ward, a handsome young man with a voice as resonant as his predecessor's. He preached night and day, drawing crowds of servants and slaves alike to the porch, his sermons striking a chord particularly deep within the hearts of the enslaved population. Madam Warrington was captivated, her credulity feeding on his confident delivery. She insisted that her neighbors attend, ordering conversions with the same authority she wielded over her household staff. Her favorite, the young George Washington, was urged to abandon his travels and settle at Castlewood, but he remained elusive, always riding out just as the sermon began.

The dynamic shifted when the boys turned their attention to their new instructor. They found him lacking—a dull scholar, a man of coarse manners who stumbled over Latin and Greek. George, already sharp-tongued, exposed his master's errors with surgical precision, reducing the divine to choking confusion. Harry, bolder still, mimicked Ward's table manners and speech, forcing laughter from Mrs. Mountain and Madam Esmond alike. The mother, furious at the defiance of her authority, doubled down, insisting that spiritual worth outweighed worldly refinement. "Heaven does not choose its elect from among the great and wealthy," she declared, commanding her sons to kneel and pray away their pride. Ward seized upon this moment, interpreting the boys' resistance as the biblical pride of Naaman the Syrian, convinced that only submission to his guardianship could save them from damnation.

The tension reached a breaking point as the boys, now fourteen, rebelled openly. George, inheriting a cynical streak from his grandfather, weaponized sarcasm, making the entire household uncomfortable with his wit. He mocked Ward's pomposity until the young divine choked on his food, and he provoked his mother into anger by sending Harry into fits of uncontrollable laughter. Her command was defied; her officers were scorned; her youngest child was corrupted. Desperate to restore order, Madam Warrington consulted Mr. Ward, seeking a solution to the insurrection. The athletic pedagogue, having secured his comfortable berth at Castlewood, readily provided the theological justification for force. In those days, the cane was a common instrument of discipline, wielded by sergeants, masters, and overseers alike. Having grown accustomed to it under their former tutor, Harry had long since desensitized himself to pain, but the atmosphere had changed. Since the Colonel's death, the rod had been laid aside, allowing the youths to roam unchecked. Now, with their natural authority spurned, the mother and her lieutenant resolved to reclaim control through coercion, preparing to bring the rebellion to heel with the familiar crack of the switch.
