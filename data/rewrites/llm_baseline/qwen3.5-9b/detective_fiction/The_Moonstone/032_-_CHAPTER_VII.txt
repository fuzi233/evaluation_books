The letters preceding this account suffice to explain why I must now briefly record Lady Verinder's passing, concluding the narrative of my fifth chapter. Resolving to restrict my future narration strictly to my own direct observations, I proceed to relate that a full month elapsed between my aunt's demise and the day Rachel Verinder and I encountered one another again. This reunion became the catalyst for my spending several days under the same roof as her. During this brief visit, a matter concerning her betrothal to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite arose—one significant enough to warrant specific mention herein. Once this final, painful episode of our family history is revealed, my task shall be complete; for I shall have recounted everything I witnessed as an unwilling observer of these tragic events.

Lady Verinder's remains were transported from London and interred in the small cemetery adjacent to the church within her own estate grounds. I received an invitation to the funeral alongside the rest of the family. However, given my religious convictions, it was impossible for me to summon the strength to overcome the profound shock of her death within such a short timeframe. Furthermore, I was informed that the rector of Frizinghall was to deliver the eulogy. Recalling that in years past I had observed this particular ecclesiastical castaway playing whist with Lady Verinder, I doubted—even had I been physically capable of traveling—that I would feel morally justified in attending the ceremony.

Following Lady Verinder's death, her daughter fell under the guardianship of her brother-in-law, Mr. Ablewhite the elder. Appointed as guardian by the will until his niece either married or reached majority, Mr. Godfrey presumably apprised his father of this new familial dynamic. Consequently, within ten days of my aunt's passing, the previously concealed nature of the engagement became public knowledge within the family circle. The pressing dilemma for Mr. Ablewhite Senior—another confirmed castaway—became how to render himself and his authority as palatable as possible to the wealthy young lady destined to wed his son.

Initially, Rachel presented difficulties regarding her place of residence. The house in Montagu Square was tainted by the memory of her mother's death, while the Yorkshire estate was forever linked to the scandalous disappearance of the Moonstone. Her guardian's home at Frizinghall offered no such objections, yet Rachel's presence there following her recent bereavement acted as a dampener on the gaiety of her cousins, the Misses Ablewhite. Moreover, she herself requested that her visit be postponed to a more favorable season.

Ultimately, Old Mr. Ablewhite proposed renting a furnished house in Brighton. His wife, an invalid daughter, and Rachel were to inhabit it together, with the expectation that he would join them later in the season. They were to avoid society save for a handful of old acquaintances, accompanied by his son, Godfrey, who would travel back and forth from London by train, always available to them. I describe this aimless wandering from one dwelling to another, this insatiable physical restlessness coupled with a terrifying stagnation of the soul, solely to arrive at a conclusion.

The event that, under Providence, facilitated the reunion of Rachel Verinder and myself was none other than the securing of the Brighton residence. My Aunt Ablewhite is a large, silent woman of fair complexion, possessing one defining characteristic: since birth, she has never been known to accomplish anything independently. Throughout her life, she has accepted assistance from everyone and adopted the opinions of all. In a spiritual sense, she is a hopeless case; there is absolutely no resistant material upon which to work. Aunt Ablewhite would listen to the Grand Lama of Tibet with the same deference she shows me, reflecting his views just as readily as mine.

She secured the Brighton house by staying at a London hotel, settling onto a sofa, and summoning her son. She procured the necessary staff by dining in bed one morning (still at the hotel) and granting her maid a holiday on the condition that the girl "begin enjoying herself by fetching Miss Clack." I found her placidly fanning herself in her dressing gown at eleven o'clock.

"Drusilla, dear, I require some servants. You are so clever—please arrange them for me."

I glanced around the untidy room. The church bells were tolling for a weekday service, suggesting a need for affectionate admonition on my part.

"Oh, Aunt!" I said sorrowfully. "Is *this* becoming of a Christian Englishwoman? Must the transition from time to eternity occur in *this* manner?"

My aunt replied, "I'll put on my gown, Drusilla, if you will kindly assist me."

What could one say after that? I have accomplished wonders with murderers, yet I have never advanced an inch with Aunt Ablewhite.

"Where is the list," I asked, "of the servants you require?"

My aunt shook her head; she lacked even the energy to maintain a list.

"Rachel has it, dear," she said, "in the next room."

Entering the adjoining chamber, I saw Rachel again for the first time since our separation in Montagu Square. She appeared pitifully small and thin in her deep mourning. If I placed any weight on such fleeting attributes as personal appearance, I might note that hers was one of those unfortunate complexions that invariably suffer without a border of white near the skin. Yet, what are our complexions and looks? Mere hindrances and pitfalls besetting women on their path to higher things!

To my great surprise, Rachel rose as I entered and approached me with an outstretched hand.

"I am glad to see you," she said. "Drusilla, I have been in the habit of speaking very foolishly and rudely to you on previous occasions. I beg your pardon. I hope you will forgive me."

My countenance, I presume, betrayed the astonishment I felt. She flushed momentarily before explaining further.

"In my poor mother's lifetime," she continued, "her friends were not always my friends. Now that I have lost her, my heart seeks comfort among the people she loved. She liked you. Try to be friends with me, Drusilla, if you can."

To any properly constituted mind, this motive was simply shocking. Here in Christian England was a grieving young woman, with so little concept of where to seek true solace, that she expected to find it among her mother's associates! Here was a relative of mine, awakening to her shortcomings toward others, not through conviction or duty, but through sentiment and impulse! Most deplorable indeed—but, still, suggestive of hope for someone experienced in the work of reform.

I resolved to ascertain the extent of the transformation the loss of her mother had wrought in Rachel's character. Deciding to use her marriage engagement as a test, I first met her advances with maximum cordiality. Sitting beside her on the sofa at her request, we discussed family affairs and future plans—excluding, naturally, that one future plan destined to conclude in matrimony. Despite my efforts to steer the conversation, she resolutely declined the hint. Any overt reference to the topic on my part would have been premature at this early stage of our reconciliation. Besides, I had gleaned all I needed to know. She was no longer the reckless, defiant creature I had witnessed during my ordeal in Montagu Square. This change alone encouraged me to take charge of her potential conversion, beginning with earnest warnings against hastily forming marital ties, and progressing to loftier subjects.

Observing her now with fresh interest, recalling the headlong speed with which she had embraced Mr. Godfrey's matrimonial proposals, I felt a solemn duty to intervene with fervor, confident of achieving extraordinary results. Speed was essential in this case. Returning immediately to the matter of the required servants:

"Where is the list, dear?"

Rachel produced it. "Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, and footman," I read aloud.

"My dear Rachel, these servants are needed only temporarily—during the period your guardian occupies the house. We shall face immense difficulty finding individuals of character and capacity to accept a temporary engagement of that sort if we search in London. Has the house in Brighton been located yet?"

"Yes. Godfrey has taken it; and the people desired by the house insisted he hire them as servants. He deemed them unsuitable for us and returned without settling anything."

"And you possess no experience in these matters yourself, Rachel?"

"None whatsoever."

"And Aunt Ablewhite will not exert herself?"

"No, poor dear. Do not blame her, Drusilla. I believe she is the only truly happy woman I have ever met."

"There are degrees of happiness, darling. We must discuss this sometime. Meanwhile, I will address the servant issue. Your aunt will write a letter to the household staff—"

"She will sign a letter if I draft it for her, which amounts to the same thing."

"Exactly the same. I shall obtain the letter and depart for Brighton tomorrow."

"How exceedingly kind of you! We shall join you as soon as you are ready. And you will stay, I hope, as *my* guest. Brighton is so lively; you are sure to enjoy it."

In those words, the invitation was extended, and the glorious prospect of intervention opened before me. It was mid-week at the time. By Saturday afternoon, the house was prepared for occupancy. Within that brief interval, I had scrutinized not only the characters but also the religious views of all unemployed servants applying to me, successfully selecting those whose conscience approved. I also contacted two serious friends residing in the town, individuals to whom I could confide the pious purpose of my journey to Brighton.
