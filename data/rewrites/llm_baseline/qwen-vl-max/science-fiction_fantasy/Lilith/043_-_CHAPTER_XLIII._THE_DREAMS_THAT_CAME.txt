I awoke—not to light, but to an endless cold, so profound it seemed not absence of warmth, but the very essence of being. I was blessed beyond measure, more so than memory could now grasp. Warmth existed only as a forgotten idea, no longer tied to pleasure. I had known it once—once—but its meaning slipped like smoke through my fingers. This cold, though absolute, cradled me. It dissolved sorrow, erased pain, and hushed every restless thought. *Comforted?* No. Sorrow was not merely eased—it was swallowed whole by something greater: a life drawing near, ready to restore all that had ever been good or beautiful, multiplied a hundredfold.

I lay still, breath slow, body stretched upon the damp earth, breathing in the rich, moist scents of the world’s deep bosom. I felt the souls of primroses, daisies, snowdrops—all asleep beneath the soil, waiting for spring. How can one describe the joy of such a frozen wakefulness? I had nothing left to do but be. No effort, no striving—only presence. The cold grew deeper, yet I welcomed it, embracing it as a lover might embrace a long-lost friend. My awareness of self faded, replaced by a growing sense of bliss—unimaginable, yet undeniable. I had not earned it. I had not prayed for it. It simply was. Because I existed, I was graced. And because I existed, I was held within a Will that dwelled inside me.

Then the dreams began.

I was naked atop a mountain of snow, windless, silent. Below, white mist swelled like a sea of clouds. Above, the moon hung close, pale and cold. Beyond her, the sky—colder still—held us both in its infinite silence. I was Adam, waiting for God to breathe into my nostrils the breath of life. But then I was not Adam—I was a child nestled in the arms of a mother whose whiteness glowed with inner radiance. Then again, I was a youth on a white horse, leaping from cloud to cloud across a blue heaven, moving calmly toward some radiant destination.

Centuries passed—or were they millennia? Or just one eternal night? Time meant nothing. I was beyond dimensions, beyond senses. I think I was where I am—deep within the heart of God.

I dreamed within the core of a melting glacier. The spectral moon drew nearer, its light thinning the ice. The wind howled, and a torrent roared in my ears. I listened—no fear, only peace. The wind, the water, the moon—they sang together, a hymn of quiet anticipation, a song of redemption nearing.

But suddenly—without breaking my serenity—the sum of all my wrongs rushed in. Every lie, every betrayal, every hurt I had ever caused, from before memory to this moment, flooded my soul. I stood in full confession. Each act lived again, and with it, I wept. I lamented the dead. I made atonement to every soul I had wounded. To each person I had offended, I bowed low, begging forgiveness, promising service.

I would build a house for one—a structure never seen on any world. For another, I would train horses of impossible grace and speed. For a third, I would create a garden alive with running streams and pools so still they mirrored the stars. I would write songs that stirred hearts, tales that set minds aflame. I would redirect the forces of nature into wonders that would make even gods laugh in awe.

Love consumed me. Love was my life. Love was everything—just as it must be for the One who made me.

Then darkness—absolute, solid blackness. No trace of light, not even the faintest glow in the eyes. Yet my heart remained unafraid, full of hope. I imagined what light would bring when it came, what new creation would emerge—when suddenly, without thought, I sat up.

The moon peered through the lowest windows of a crypt-like chamber. Her long silver beam stretched across the floor—over fallen sheaves, I thought, ripening still under the care of the great husbandman. But no. There were no sheaves. Not one. The harvest was gone. Gathered in, or swept away by storm—none remained. My dead were gone.

I was alone.

Desolation pierced me deeper than I had ever known. Had there never been any dead? Had I only dreamed them, their beauty, their peace? Why then these walls? These empty beds?

No. They were awake. They were out in the new eternal day—and had forgotten me. They had left me behind.

The tomb, now empty, became ten times more terrible. The quiet ones had brought peace to my mind; now, with them gone, I had no friend, no love near. A moment I sat, staring in horror.

I had been alone with the moon on a peak in the sky. Now I was alone with her in a vast cenotaph. She too stared about, searching, her gaze hollow, lost.

I rose, staggering from the place. The cottage was empty. I ran into the night. No moon. As I stepped out, a thick cloud had risen, covering her. But from far over the heath came a shimmering light, mingled with a ghostly music—like rain falling from the moon, plashing softly on the ground.

I stumbled across the moor, found a lake—still, clear, surrounded by reeds. The moon, hidden behind the cloud, gazed down upon it. But the music continued, drawing me forward. I walked around the edge, climbed the hills.

And then—what I saw!

Where once I had crossed dry riverbeds, cracked and sun-baked, now flowed rivers, torrents, still pools—“a river deep and wide.” The moon flashed upon the water, and the water flashed back—white bursts breaking everywhere, dancing against the rocks. From its depths arose a great song, jubilant, wild—music of freedom born.

I stood, transfixed. My heart swelled. My life was not a failure. I had helped set this river free. My dead were not lost. I had only to follow—follow until I found them. Our meeting might be thousands of years off, but at last—*at last*—I would hold them.

Why else did the floods clap their hands?

I hurried down the hill. My pilgrimage had begun. I knew not which way to turn—but I must go. I must go until I found my living dead.

A swift torrent lay at the foot of the ridge. I rushed in—it did not hold me. I waded through. The next, I leapt across. The third, I swam. The fourth, I waded again.

I paused, enchanted by the ceaseless flow, the broken symphony of rushing water. At times, a melody seemed to rise—almost clear—then dissolve back into the roar. The waters did not overwhelm me with force, but with silence—great, echoing silence that wandered into sound.

As I stood, lost in wonder, a hand touched my shoulder.

I turned.

A man stood before me—strong, radiant, young as if untouched by time. He wore a white robe, and the moon shone in his hair.

“Father,” I said, “where is she? Where are the dead? Has the resurrection come and gone? I cannot bear this loneliness! I fled the chamber—where shall I go to find them?”

“You mistake, my son,” he answered, his voice like calm after storm. “You are still in the chamber of death. Still upon your bed. Asleep. Dreaming. The dead are around you.”

“But when I dream—how can I know?” I pleaded. “The best dream feels truer than waking!”

“When you are truly dead,” he said, “you will dream no false dream. A true soul cannot generate falsehood. The false cannot enter it.”

“But how,” I faltered, “can I tell truth from dream when both feel real?”

“You cannot,” he smiled, “not while you are not yet fully dead. Nor will you when you are—because then the false will not appear. You are on your bed. In the house of death.”

“I believe you,” I whispered, “though I cannot see it.”

“Do not blame yourself. Even in a dream, you believe me. That is why I help you. Extend your left hand—open it—close it gently. It will clasp the hand of your Lona.”

I reached out. My fingers closed—soft, firm, warm—on hers.

“But father,” I cried, “she is warm!”

“Your hand is warm to hers. Cold does not exist in our land. Neither of you is yet in the fields of home. But to each other, you are alive, warm, whole.”

My heart lifted. But doubt pricked—sharp, sudden.

“Father,” I said, “how can I know this is not part of the dream? That even this touch is not illusion?”

“You doubt,” he said, “because you love the truth. Others would gladly believe life a phantom, if only it gave them endless pleasant dreams. But you are not such. Be content for now not to know. The hour will come—soon—when you will see the truth face to face. Then doubt will die. You will remember only the shadow of this doubt. You have not yet looked Truth in the eye. You have seen him through a veil. That which cannot be known except by its own light—you must doubt until you see it directly. But to him who has glimpsed even a shadow of truth, and tries to obey it—even in absence—he will come. And abide forever.”

“I think I understand,” I said.

“Remember,” he replied. “Trials await you—heavy, unknown. Remember what you have seen. You do not know them—yet you know what they meant. Remember also what you will see. Truth is all. And the truth lies hidden and revealed in appearance.”

“How can that be?” I asked, lifting my eyes.

He was gone. Only the sound of water remained—swift, singing, eternal.

I stretched my hands—nothing. Alone. In the land of dreams.

To myself, I seemed awake. But I believed I dreamed—because he had told me so.

Yet even in dreams, one must act. So I resumed my journey.

I crossed many channels, reached a wide rock plateau. Tired, I lay down—longing to wake.

Just as I rose, I noticed a pit beside me—deep, dark, bottomless. Like a grave.

In childhood dreams, I had learned: fall, and you wake. So, with one glance at the sky, one at the rushing waters, I rolled myself over the edge.

For a moment—darkness.

Then—light.

I stood in the garret of my own house. In the wooden chamber. The cowl. The mirror.

Despair crashed over me. Between me and Lona—now—lay an abyss. Space. Time. Being itself. Walls of adamant, impenetrable. I could not reach her.

True, I might return—through the door of light—back to the chamber of the dead. But even then, only a heath and a starry night separated me from the mirror. And only the sun, far across the world, could open it.

But here—here the chasm was wider. She slept. I was awake. I was no longer worthy to share her sleep. I could not hope to awaken with her again.

I had failed. I had fled the dream. The dream was not mine to make—nor was my life. I should have seen it to the end. By fleeing, I had abandoned the holy sleep itself.

I would return to Adam. Confess. Submit.

I crept to my room, threw myself on the bed, and slept—dreamless.

I rose. Listlessly, I went to the library. No one met me. The house felt dead.

I sat with a book, waiting for noon. But no words made sense.

The mutilated manuscript appeared—offered from the masked door. Its sight sickened me. What did the princess matter? Her devilry—mere dust.

I rose, looked out the window.

Brilliant morning. The fountain shot high, fell roaring. The sun sat in its feathery crown. No bird sang. No creature moved. Raven nor librarian came near.

The world was dead around me.

I took another book. Sat. Waited.

Noon approached.

I climbed the stairs to the roof. Closed the door behind me. Turned to open the door out of this dreary world.

I left the chamber with a heart of stone.

I pulled the chains. Adjusted the hood. Rearranged the mirrors. Nothing happened.

I waited. And waited. The mirror remained blank—only my haggard face, and the opposite mirror, staring back.

No vision came.

I returned to the library. The books repelled me—once loved, now hateful.

That night, I lay awake. The next day, I tried again—last time.

Still nothing.

Hours passed. No sound. No one. No movement.

Not weary. Only desolate.

Another sleepless night.

Morning came. I went to the chamber—last time.

No door opened.

My heart died.

I had lost Lona.

Was she anywhere? Had she ever been—except in the decaying cells of my brain?

“I must die one day,” I thought. “And then—straight from my death-bed—I will set out to find her. If she is not there, I will go to the Father and say: ‘Even thou canst not help me. Let me cease. I pray thee.’”
