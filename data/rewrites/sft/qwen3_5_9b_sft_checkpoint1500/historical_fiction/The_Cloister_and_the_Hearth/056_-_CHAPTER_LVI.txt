Gerard secured a modest chamber on the west bank of the Tiber, embarking each morning on a pilgrimage for employment. He presented his samples to every workshop known to execute such commissions, only to be met with icy indifference. "Our letters must be somewhat thinner than this," remarked one merchant, critiquing the density of his ink. Another sneered, "How dark!" Yet the prevailing cry cut deeper: "What avail does this serve? Latin writings grow scarce here. Cannot you render Greek?"

"Aye, but not nearly so well as Latin," Gerard admitted humbly.
"Then you shall never earn your bread in Rome."

Undeterred, Gerard borrowed a magnificent Greek manuscript at a steep price, returning home with a hollow purse but an unbroken spirit. Within two weeks, he had mastered the Greek characters with remarkable speed. Eager to capitalize on this skill, he dedicated his mornings to study and his afternoons to hunting clients. When he finally circulated a superior Greek specimen, the traders delivered a crushing verdict: both Greek and Latin were unsaleable. The city was flooded with works from across Europe; he should have arrived a year prior.

Desperate, Gerard purchased a psaltery. His landlady, charmed by his demeanor, often offered words of kindness. One evening, moved by his evident despondency, she invited him to dine and gently probed the cause of his gloom. He confessed his struggles, and she offered her interpretation. "Those sly traders," she declared, "employ scribes in their pay, paying nobly for their labor while offering you a pittance. No wonder they blow cold on you. Methinks you write too well. Do you not lock your door, like the churl Pietro? Women are naturally curious. Yes, you write too beautifully for them."

Gerard sought clarification. "Pray, why?"
"Because," she replied, "your fine work might blind those who sell inferior goods."
Gerard sighed. "Alas, Dame, you read people on the wrong side, yet you are so kind and frank."
"My dear little heart," she countered, "Romans are a subtle race. I am a Siennese, blessed by the Virgin. Your error was leaving Augsburg."
"Augsburg?" she scoffed haughtily. "A place to compete with Rome? I have never heard of it."

Despite the book sellers' lies, she assured him of his future fortune. "Seeing thee a stranger, they deceive thee without sense or discretion. All the world knows our great folk are bitten by the writing spider, pouring out money like water to convert lands and houses into scribbles kept in chests. God help them from this madness! Since the advent of scribbling, there has been less cutting and stabbing among rival factions, less vendetta. Two men prove this: Eminence Cardinal Bassarion and the Pope himself. They could keep a score like thee working night and day. I spoke to Teresa; she confirms five more signori are similarly afflicted."

The following day, armed with Teresa's gossip, Gerard gathered the names of five additional patrons. He purchased parchment and prepared specimens, leaving a card with his name and address at each doorstep. Hope faded quickly. Day after day passed, leaving him heartsick.

It was a time when Margaret, far away in Rotterdam, fought valiant odds to feed her dependents, recently arrested for practicing medicine without a license rather than killing with it. Back in Rome, ruin seemed to stare Gerard in the face. To stave off starvation, he spent his afternoons transcribing canzonets and laying in playing cards to color, earning a meal a day. This ingenuity, however, bred further trouble. In the *camere locande*, the landlady dressed all meals, though lodgers supplied the provisions. Her sharp eyes soon detected Gerard's culinary contributions. When she confronted him, asking if he lacked shame, her brusque opening made him blush and tremble. Yet, her tone softened instantly, appealing to his common sense regarding adversity and hunger.

"Patience, my lad! Times will mend; meanwhile, I will feed you for the love of heaven."
"Nay, Hostess," Gerard protested. "My purse is not yet void, and it would add to my tribulation if true folk lost their due by me."
"Why, you are as mad as your neighbor Pietro, with his single bad picture."
"How do you know 'tis a bad picture?"
"Because no one buys it. There is one who lacks gift; he must don casque and glaive, carrying his panel as a shield."

Gerard listened intently. The landlady recounted Pietro's tale: a Florentine who arrived wealthy with an unfinished canvas, furnished a neat room opposite Gerard's, and displayed his completed work. Though offers were reportedly generous, Pietro had refused them disdainfully, making enemies. Since then, he had dragged the painting out repeatedly, only to return it defeated. Recently, furniture vanished from his room until he wore a single suit and slept atop a large chest. The landlady peered through the keyhole, noting how vigilantly he locked his door. "Is he afraid thieves will steal his chest or picture, that no soul in Rome is weak enough to buy?"
"No, sweet Hostess," Gerard mused. "See you not 'tis his poverty he screens from view?"
"And the more fool he! Are all our hearts as ill as his? A trial first, anyway."
"How you speak of him. Why, his case is mine; and your countryman to boot."
"Oh, Siennese love strangers. His case yours? Nay, 'tis the contrary. You are the comeliest youth ever lodged here; hair like gold. He is a dark, sour-visaged loon. Besides, you know how to charm a woman on her better side; not he. Nevertheless, I cannot bear to see a starveling die in my house and tarnish my reputation. One starving guest suffices. You are far from home, and it falls to me, the mistress, to count your meals—for me and the Dutch wife, your mother, afar off. We two women shall settle that. Mind your business, being a man, and leave cooking to us, who exist merely to roast fowl and suckle grown men, sweeping up their cobwebs."

"Dear kind Dame," Gerard said, "you truly remind me of my mother."
"All the better," she beamed. "I shall put you more in mind of her before I finish."

Gerard, neither an egotist nor blinded by female partiality, saw his own grief mirrored in poor, proud Pietro. Resolving to share his scant means, he attempted to intercept the artist, but failed. One afternoon, a groan echoed from the garret. Gerard knocked, receiving no answer, then knocked again. A gruff voice commanded him to enter. Timidly, Gerard stepped inside to find a garret furnished sparsely with a chair, a picture facing the wall, an iron basin, an easel, and a long chest. Coiled upon it sat a haggard young man with a brilliantly bright eye, resembling a cobra ready to strike.

"Good Signor Pietro," Gerard began, "forgive me that, weary of solitude, I intrude on yours. I am your nearest neighbor and, methinks, your brother in fortune. I am an artist too."
"You are a painter? Welcome, signer. Sit down on my bed."
With a magnificent gesture, Pietro leaped off and waved him onto the vacant "throne." Gerard bowed and smiled, yet hesitated. "I may not call myself a painter. I am a writer, a calligrapher. I copy Greek and Latin manuscripts when commissioned."
"And you call that an artist?"
"Without offense to your superior merit, Signor Pietro..."
"No offense, stranger. Only, I deem an artist one who thinks and paints his thought. Now a calligrapher but draws thoughts of others in black and white."
"'Tis well distinguished, signor. But a writer can chronicle the thoughts of ancient giants and matters of pure reason, inaccessible to paint—nay, the thoughts of God, which angels could not depict. Let that pass. I am a painter as well; but a sorry one."
"The better thy luck. 'They will buy thy work in Rome.'"
"But seeking favor from one of thy eminence, I deemed it wise to present myself as a capable writer rather than a scurvy painter."

Just then, footsteps sounded on the stairs. "Ah! 'tis the good dame!" cried Gerard. "Hostess, I am conversing with Signor Pietro. I trust he permits me to take my humble dinner here."
The Italian bowed gravely.
The landlady entered with Gerard's dinner, steaming and savory. She set the dish upon the bed with an expressionless face and departed.
Gerard ate, but midway, he paused. "I am an ill-mannered churl, Signor Pietro. I never eat to my satisfaction when alone. For our Lady's sake, lend me a spoon for this ragout; 'tis not unsavory, I promise."
Pietro fixed his glittering eye upon him.
"What, good youth, thou a stranger, and offerest me thy dinner?"
"Why, see, there is more than one who can eat."
"Well, I accept," said Pietro, taking the dish with feigned calm, then flinging the entire contents out the window.
Turning, trembling with mortification and wrath, he declared, "Let that teach thee to offer alms to an artist thou knowest not, master writer."
