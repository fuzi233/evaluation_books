From that moment, my days held no lack of change. I ventured out often, with Madame Beck’s full approval—her regard for me had grown steadily since the beginning, and now, seeing me welcomed at noble houses and grand estates, her respect deepened into something like admiration. Yet she never overstepped herself in praise; she was too sharp, too balanced to let emotion cloud her judgment. Her pride was carefully measured, her interests pursued with cool precision. She did not flatter or fawn, but she made it clear—without words—that she valued the company I kept, not because they were wealthy or powerful, but because they were decent, thoughtful people whose presence elevated rather than diminished her establishment.

Still, she never spoke kindly of me or my friends. Only once, when she sat beneath the sun in the garden, a cup of coffee beside her and the Gazette in hand, looking calm and satisfied, did she offer a remark as I asked leave to go out for the evening.

“Oui, oui, ma bonne amie,” she said, smiling slightly. “I give you permission freely and gladly. Your work here has always been admirable—full of care and discretion. You have earned the right to enjoy yourself. Go whenever you wish. And your choice of acquaintances—I approve. It is wise, proper, worthy.”

She closed her lips and returned to her paper.

It was then I noticed something strange: the sealed packet of five letters, hidden in my drawer, had vanished. At first, panic seized me. But within seconds, I steadied myself.

“Patience,” I whispered. “Say nothing. Wait quietly. They’ll return.”

And they did. The next day, neatly folded and tied again, they lay exactly where they’d been. They had simply been borrowed—examined, perhaps—and then returned. I knew who had taken them.

Madame Beck. I could only suppose so.

What must she have thought of those letters? What did she make of Dr. John Bretton’s writing—the clarity, the warmth, the quiet strength woven through each line? Did she recognize the wit beneath the modest tone, the intelligence behind the simple phrasing? How did she view the gentle kindness scattered through the pages—not lavished, but placed like rare stones in a quiet stream? Oh, Madame Beck—what did these words mean to you?

I believe she liked them. Once, after she had taken the letters from me (and what a word to use—*borrowed*—in speaking of such a woman!), I caught her watching me with a steady, thoughtful gaze. Not harsh, not cruel—but curious, as if she were trying to understand something beyond her reach.

It was during the brief break between classes, when the girls ran out into the courtyard. I remained in the first classe with her. When our eyes met, she spoke without hesitation.

“There is,” she said, “something very remarkable in the English character.”

“How?” I asked.

She laughed softly, repeating the word in English: “How?”

“I cannot say *how*. But the English—they think differently. In friendship, in love, in everything. And yet,” she added, rising and moving briskly away like the compact little horse she resembled, “they do not need to be watched.”

Then I murmured to myself, “Then may you, Madame, leave my letters undisturbed from now on.”

But a sudden wave of sorrow crashed over me. I remembered—never again would such letters come to me. The last one had been read. That river I had drunk from, whose waters had sustained me, was turning its course elsewhere. It left my small shelter dry and barren, flowing far off into lands I could not follow. The change was just, natural—even necessary. But still, I grieved. I had loved that stream. I had almost revered it. And now it was gone, like a vision fading in the desert heat.

Though I tried to remain composed, I could not help weeping. Tears fell fast onto my hands, my desk—silent, heavy, and brief.

Yet soon I reminded myself: “The hope I mourned brought pain, and caused me pain. It lived long enough. Its death should be welcome.”

And so I tried to accept it. Long suffering had taught me patience. In time, I closed the eyes of my grief, covered its face, and laid it to rest with quiet dignity.

But the letters—those letters—must be hidden. People who have lost something precious always guard their mementos closely. To see them every day would be unbearable—a constant wound reopened.

On a quiet holiday afternoon, I went to retrieve them, intending to decide their final fate. But this time, I felt a pang of anger. The packet was there, yes—but the ribbon was undone and retied. Other signs told me someone had opened my drawer.

This was too much. Madame Beck was discreet, intelligent, and cautious. If she had read them, I could bear it—though it pained me. But the idea that she might have shared them with others… that she might have shown them to someone else… it filled me with horror.

And I suspected whom. Her cousin, M. Paul Emanuel, had visited her the night before. She often consulted him, discussing matters she kept from everyone else. That morning, in class, he had looked at me with an odd glance—one I hadn’t understood then, but now recognized clearly: cold, angry, unreadable. He had seen something in those letters—something he disliked.

He would not judge me fairly. I had always found him suspicious, severe. The thought that he might have read them—letters meant only for friendship—shook me to the core.

Where could I hide them? Where in this house could anything truly be safe? Could a lock protect them? A padlock seal them from prying eyes?

Not in the attic. Too damp. Rats gnawed through wood, mice built nests among old things. My letters—still dear, even now—might be eaten by vermin, their ink blurred by moisture. No. The attic would not do.

I sat in the dormitory window seat, thinking. It was a frosty afternoon, the winter sun low and pale. Beyond the garden, the “allée défendue” stretched quiet and still. The old pear-tree—the nun’s tree—rose bare and gaunt against the sky.

A sudden idea struck me—one of those strange thoughts that come unbidden to lonely minds. I put on my bonnet, cloak, and furs, and walked into the city.

I wandered through the ancient quarter, drawn instinctively toward shadowed streets and forgotten corners. Finally, I came upon an old shop—like a treasure chest of forgotten things. I needed a metal box that could be sealed, or a thick glass jar that could be stopped tightly. Amongst dusty heaps, I found a large glass bottle. I bought it.

Back in my room, I rolled up the letters, wrapped them in oiled silk, bound them with twine, and placed them inside the jar. Then I took it to the old Jewish broker, who sealed it tightly, stopping the neck with wax and making it air-tight. As he worked, he glanced at me with wary eyes—white lashes like frost. I think he believed I was planning something dark.

There was no joy in this act—only a strange, solemn satisfaction. The mood was like that which led me to the confessional. It was a burial.

I returned to the pensionnat just as dusk fell. By seven, the moon rose. At half-past seven, when all was quiet—pupils studying, teachers busy, Madame Beck with her family, Rosine gone from the hall—I wrapped myself in a shawl, took the sealed jar, and slipped out through the first-classe door.

I passed through the berceau, then entered the “allée défenduhe.” The old pear-tree stood at the far end, near my favorite seat. Methusaleh, they called it—ancient, sturdy, with a deep hollow near its roots. I knew of it, hidden beneath ivy and vines.

I cleared the growth, found the hole—large enough for the jar—and pushed it deep inside. From a shed at the garden’s edge, I fetched a slate and some mortar. I covered the hollow with the stone, secured it with cement, filled the space with black earth, and replaced the ivy.

Then I leaned against the tree, resting, as any mourner might linger beside a fresh grave.

The night air was still, veiled in a soft mist that turned the moonlight into a hazy glow. Something in that mist—perhaps electricity—touched me strangely. I felt as I had once in England, under the northern lights, when the sky shimmered with banners and swift messengers. I did not feel happy—but strong. Fortified.

If life is war, it seemed my lot to fight alone. Now I considered how to leave this place—where supplies ran thin, where hope had dried up. Perhaps another battle must be fought. If so, I was ready. I had nothing to lose. God might grant me gain.

But what path lay open? What plan could I follow?

I was still pondering when the moon, dim until now, grew brighter. A beam of light cut through the haze, and a shadow became distinct. I looked closer—whiter and darker it grew. Shape formed suddenly. I stood three yards from a tall figure: a woman in black robes, her face covered by a white veil.

Five minutes passed. I did not run. I did not scream. She stood silent. I spoke.

“Who are you? Why do you come to me?”

She did not answer. Her face was hidden—no features visible—but her eyes stared at me.

I felt not brave, but desperate. And desperation can sometimes stand in for courage. I stepped forward. I reached out to touch her. She moved back. I followed. She retreated swiftly. A thicket of evergreens blocked the way. I passed through—then saw nothing.

I waited. “If you have a message,” I said, “return and deliver it.”

Nothing came. Nothing returned.

No Dr. John to turn to now. No one to whisper, “I have seen the nun again.”

Paulina Mary began to visit me often in the Rue Crécy. In the old days, though she had never declared affection for me, my presence had become a kind of habit to her—unnoticed, yet necessary. I used to notice that if I withdrew to my room, she would soon come running after me, open the door, peep in, and say with her firm little voice: “Come down. Why do you sit alone? You must come into the parlour.”

Now she urged me again: “Leave the Rue Fossette. Come live with us. Papa would pay you far more than Madame Beck.”

Mr. Home himself offered me three times my current salary to serve as companion to his daughter. I refused. I think I would have refused even if I were poorer, more uncertain. I was not made for that role. I could teach. I could instruct. But being a governess or a companion felt unnatural—like wearing clothes that did not fit.

Rather than be a governess in a great house, I would willingly take a maid’s job—buy gloves, clean stoves, sweep stairs—in peace and independence. Rather than be a companion, I would sew shirts and starve.

I was not a bright shadow, nor a reflection of beauty. Not like Miss de Bassompierre. I was often quiet, subdued—by nature, not by force. But that quietness had to be chosen, not imposed. It suited me at my desk, among my pupils, in the garden seat that was mine. My qualities were fixed, not flexible. I could not be a foil, a decoration, a part of someone else’s grand design.

Madame Beck and I, though different, understood each other. I was not her companion, nor her children’s governess. She gave me freedom. She tied me to nothing—not even to her own interests. Once, when she returned from a fortnight’s absence due to a relative’s illness, anxious about the school, she found everything well-ordered. She rewarded each teacher—except me. At midnight, she came to my bedside.

“I have no gift for you,” she said. “But I will make fidelity profitable for the St. Pierre. If I try to make it profitable for you, misunderstanding may follow—separation. But one thing I *can* do: leave you free. That is what I will do.”

She kept her word. Every restraint she had ever placed on me, she removed quietly, one by one. So I found pleasure in obeying her rules—not out of fear, but out of respect. I gave double effort to the pupils entrusted to me, not because I was forced, but because I chose to.

As for Paulina Mary, I visited her gladly, though I would not live with her. Soon, I realized her need for me would fade. M. de Bassompierre seemed blind to this truth—unaware of the changes taking place in his daughter. He still saw her as a child, though she was growing into a woman.

I often wondered whether he would approve of her future. He was deeply engaged in science—intense, focused, sometimes stubborn in his pursuits—but trusting in everyday matters. He spoke of what “Polly” would do when she was grown, as if she were still a girl. And she, standing beside him, would sometimes smile, take his head in her hands, and kiss his grey hair. At other times, she would pout, toss her curls. But she never said, “Papa, I *am* grown up.”

She had different ways with different people. With her father, she was childlike—affectionate, playful. With me, serious and thoughtful. With Mrs. Bretton, respectful but reserved. With Graham, shy—very shy. Sometimes she tried to be cold, avoided him. His footsteps startled her. His entrance silenced her. When he spoke, her answers faltered. When he left, she remained flustered.

Even her father noticed.

“My little Polly,” he said once, “you live too quietly. If you grow up this way, you won’t fit into society. You’ve made Dr. Bretton a stranger. Why?”

“_Rather_, papa,” she replied gently, “I liked him as a child.”

“And now?”

“Nothing. Yes, I like him a little—but we are grown strange to each other.”

“Then rub it off, Polly. Talk when he is here. Don’t fear him.”

“He does not talk much. Is he afraid of me, papa?”

“Oh, certainly! What man wouldn’t be afraid of such a quiet little lady?”

“Then tell him not to mind my silence. Say it’s my way, and I mean no harm.”

“Your way? You little chatterbox! It’s not your way—it’s your whim!”

“Well, I’ll improve, papa.”

And the next day, she tried. I saw her attempt to speak easily with Dr. John. His face softened, warmed. He answered her gently, as if afraid to disturb a fragile thing. There was something delicate, almost magical, in her effort—timid yet sincere.

When he left, she approached her father.

“Did I keep my word, papa? Did I behave better?”

“My Polly behaved like a queen. If this continues, I shall be proud. Soon we’ll see her receiving guests with grace. Miss Lucy and I must polish ourselves—lest we be outshone. Still, there’s a little flutter, a tendency to stutter—like when you were six.”

“No, papa!” she protested. “That’s not true.”

“I appeal to Miss Lucy. Didn’t she say ‘yeth’ when asked about the palace of the Prince of Bois l’Etang?”

“Papa, you’re teasing! I can pronounce every letter perfectly. But tell me—why are you so eager for me to be civil to Dr. Bretton? Do you like him?”

“To be sure. For old sake, yes. He’s a good son to his mother, kind-hearted, clever in his work. Yes, the callant is well enough.”

“_Callant_! Ah, Scotchman! Papa, is it Edinburgh or Aberdeen accent?”

“Both, my pet. And likely Glasgow too. That’s why I speak French so well—a good Scots tongue makes for good French.”

“_The_ French! Again, Scotch! You’re incorrigible.”

“Well, Polly, you must persuade Miss Snowe to teach both of us—to make you steady and womanly, and me refined and classical.”

The way M. de Bassompierre spoke of me—of “Miss Snowe”—often gave me pause. How different people saw me! Madame Beck thought me learned and cool. Miss Fanshawe, sharp and cynical. Mr. Home, a model of propriety—perhaps too strict, too cautious—but perfect in her role. Professor Paul Emanuel, however, saw me as fiery, reckless, bold. I smiled at all of them. If anyone truly knew me, it was Paulina Mary.

Since I would not be her paid companion, though our talks were warm and pleasant, she suggested we study together—German, which we both found difficult. We agreed to take lessons from the same teacher in the Rue Crécy. This gave us regular hours to meet. M. de Bassompierre approved—delighted that Madame Minerva Gravity would spend her leisure with his beloved daughter.

And so, in the quiet of the garden, beneath the watchful eye of the old pear-tree, I buried my letters—my hopes, my past—and stepped forward into the unknown.
