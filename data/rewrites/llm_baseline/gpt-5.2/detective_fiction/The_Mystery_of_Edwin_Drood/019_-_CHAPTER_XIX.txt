Once more Miss Twinkleton had poured out her valedictory periods as smoothly as her white-wine, and once more had pressed pound-cake upon lips that smiled because it was expected of them to smile. Once more the young ladies had fluttered away to their several homes, like ribbons loosened from a bonnet-string. Helena Landless had gone too, following her brother’s fortunes with that firm, bright fidelity of hers; and Rosa Bud was left at the Nuns’ House with no companion but her own thoughts, which were worse than solitude.

Cloisterham, in these midsummer days, wore a look so clear, so steeped in light, that the Cathedral and the monastery-ruin seemed less like stone than like some pale honeycombed substance through which the sun had learned to shine. The heat lay on the corn-fields beyond, and the distant roads smoked with a fine white dust that drifted at every passing cart-wheel. In the gardens the fruit was turning—plums blushing into their bloom, pears swelling with a weight that promised juice, currants hanging like bright beads, and the sharp green scent of crushed leaf and warmed earth rising whenever a foot brushed the borders.

The town’s old story seemed to move about in the day’s sounds. There had been a time when travel-stained pilgrims rode through in clattering parties, and the ring of hoofs and harness had been a kind of music. Now there were other wayfarers: reapers and haymakers living between two seasons like wandering tribes, so thick with road-dust that they appeared made of it. They lounged on cool doorsteps, bending over boots with broken soles, worrying at knots and stitches as if to mend what could not be mended, then flinging the hopeless things aside and rummaging for others in the straw-bound bundles where their yet unused sickles lay swathed. At the public pumps there was much bubbling, gurgling, and splashing; bare feet were held under the spouts; hands were cupped for drinking, and the water struck the basin with a bright, quick chatter. The Cloisterham police, set in their beats like ornaments of authority, watched askance and wished the intruders gone, as if the very human noise of thirst and travel offended the town’s measured hush.

On such an afternoon, when the last Cathedral service was done and the shaded side of the High Street lay gratefully cool, with only the Nuns’ House garden open to the west where sunlight sifted between boughs, a servant came to Rosa with news that turned her blood cold.

“Mr. Jasper desires to see you, miss.”

The words fell into the quiet like a dropped knife. It was the worst time he could have chosen to find her unguarded; and she had a frantic sense that he had chosen it for that very reason. Helena was gone. Mrs. Tisher had leave. Miss Twinkleton, in her amateur state of being, had contributed herself and a veal pie to a picnic. Even the house seemed emptied of its little protections.

“O why, why, why, did you say I was at home!” Rosa cried, clasping her hands.

The maid, surprised, answered that Mr. Jasper had never asked. He said he knew she was at home, and begged she might be told that he asked to see her.

What shall I do? what shall I do? The question beat in Rosa’s head as fast as the pump’s gurgle in the street. Desperation, and that instinct which seeks air when danger presses, made her speak.

“I will come to Mr. Jasper in the garden.”

The house, with its corridors and shutting doors, was a trap. Out there, at least, there were windows—eyes. Out there she could be seen as well as heard; out there she could shriek in free air and run. It was a wild plan, but it fluttered through her like a bird seeking escape.

She had not seen him since the fatal night—except when she had been questioned before the Mayor, and Jasper had been present, in gloomy watchfulness, representing his lost nephew and burning, as he said, to avenge him. Edwin Drood, the lost, had long been given up and mourned for as dead; and deep mourning now clothed both the man and the girl, black upon black, as if grief had made a uniform of them.

Rosa took her garden-hat upon her arm and went out. The moment she saw him from the porch—leaning upon the sun-dial—an old, horrible sensation closed round her: the sense of being compelled. It was as if his will drew her feet across the gravel. She felt she would even then turn back; yet she could not. She went to the seat beside the dial and sat with her head bent, seeing nothing but the grass, unable to lift her eyes to him for abhorrence.

He would have begun by touching her hand. She felt it before it happened, as one feels thunder in the air; she drew her hand away. She knew his eyes were fixed on her. She kept her own eyes on the blades at her feet.

“I have been waiting,” he began, “for some time, to be summoned back to my duty near you.”

She formed answers and unformed them. At last, in a voice that seemed not her own: “Duty, sir?”

“The duty of teaching you, serving you as your faithful music-master.”

“I have left off that study.”

“Not left off, I think. Discontinued. I was told by your guardian that you discontinued it under the shock that we have all felt so acutely. When will you resume?”

“Never, sir.”

“Never?” His tone sharpened, and a cruel little edge of reproach slid into it. “You could have done no more if you had loved my dear boy.”

“I did love him!” she cried, and anger, like a sudden flame in a lantern, lit her for an instant.

“Yes; but not quite—not quite in the right way, shall I say? Not in the intended and expected way. Much as my dear boy was, unhappily, too self-conscious and self-satisfied”—he paused, as if tasting the words—“(I’ll draw no parallel between him and you in that respect) to love as he should have loved, or as any one in his place would have loved—must have loved!”

She did not change her attitude, but she shrank a little more, as if the air itself pressed on her.

“Then, to be told that you discontinued your study with me,” he pursued, “was to be politely told that you abandoned it altogether?”

“Yes,” said Rosa, with a flash of spirit. “The politeness was my guardian’s, not mine. I told him that I was resolved to leave off, and that I was determined to stand by my resolution.”

“And you still are?”

“I still am, sir. And I beg not to be questioned any more about it. At all events, I will not answer any more; I have that in my power.”

She felt, with a sickening certainty, that he looked at her with a gloating admiration of the colour in her cheek and the quickness in her breath; that her anger was a spectacle he enjoyed, as some coarse palate enjoys too hot a sauce. Even while her spirit rose, it fell again into shame, affront, and fear, as it had fallen that night at the piano when his presence had made music feel like a snare.

“I will not question you any more, since you object to it so much; I will confess—”

“I do not wish to hear you, sir,” she cried, starting up.

This time he did touch her, with an outstretched hand. The contact was not made, for she shrank; but in shrinking she shrank into her seat again, as if the very act of escape folded her back upon herself.

“We must sometimes act in opposition to our wishes,” he said in a low voice. “You must do so now, or do more harm to others than you can ever set right.”

“What harm?”

“Presently, presently. You question me, you see, and surely that’s not fair when you forbid me to question you. Nevertheless, I will answer the question presently. Dearest Rosa! Charming Rosa!”

She sprang up again. He did not touch her now. But his face—wicked, menacing—made the sunny garden seem suddenly without sun. Leaning against the sun-dial-setting, he looked like a black mark struck upon the very face of day. Horror arrested her flight.

“I do not forget how many windows command a view of us,” he said, glancing towards them. “I will not touch you again; I will come no nearer to you than I am. Sit down, and there will be no mighty wonder in your music-master’s leaning idly against a pedestal and speaking with you, remembering all that has happened, and our shares in it. Sit down, my beloved.”

She would have gone—was all but gone—and then his face, darkly threatening what would follow if she went, stopped her as surely as a hand. With the expression of that instant frozen upon her features, she sat down again.

“Rosa,” he said, and though his posture remained composed, there was something in his voice like a knife drawn along a stone, “even when my dear boy was affianced to you, I loved you madly; even when I thought his happiness in having you for his wife was certain, I loved you madly; even when I strove to make him more ardently devoted to you, I loved you madly; even when he gave me the picture of your lovely face so carelessly traduced by him, which I feigned to hang always in my sight for his sake, but worshipped in torment for years, I loved you madly; in the distasteful work of the day, in the wakeful misery of the night, girded by sordid realities, or wandering through Paradises and Hells of visions into which I rushed, carrying your image in my arms, I loved you madly.”

His words were hideous enough; but worse was the contrast—his violence of look and delivery held under an assumed ease, as if he wished the watching windows to read their scene as light talk. Faces did indeed come and go behind panes: the domestic life of the house flowed on, innocent and ignorant, like broth simmering in a kitchen while some poison is mixed in a corner.

“I endured it all in silence,” he went on. “So long as you were his, or so long as I supposed you to be his, I hid my secret loyally. Did I not?”

The lie in his mouth—gross and brazen—was more than Rosa could bear. Indignation broke through her fear like a cry forced out of a throat held down.

“You were as false throughout, sir, as you are now. You were false to him, daily and hourly. You know that you made my life unhappy by your pursuit of me. You know that you made me afraid to open his generous eyes, and that you forced me, for his own trusting, good, good sake, to keep the truth from him, that you were a bad, bad man!”

He kept his easy attitude still; but his features worked and his hands convulsed upon the dial as if the stone burnt him. The restraint made him seem less human than if he had raged openly.

“How beautiful you are!” he returned, with a fierce extreme of admiration. “You are more beautiful in anger than in repose. I don’t ask you for your love; give me yourself and your hatred; give me yourself and that pretty rage; give me yourself and that enchanting scorn; it will be enough for me.”

Tears—impatient, angry tears—rose to her eyes. Her face flamed; her breath came quick and shallow. She rose again to go, to seek protection within the house; and he stretched out his hand towards the porch, not to touch her but as though he invited her to enter it—like a host offering the door with dreadful courtesy.

“I told you, you rare charmer, you sweet witch, that you must stay and hear me, or do more harm than can ever be undone. You asked me what harm. Stay, and I will tell you. Go, and I will do it!”

She did not understand the meaning, but she understood the menace. It struck her with the certainty of thunder in the oppressive air. She quailed before his face and remained. Her breathing fluttered as if it would choke her; she pressed her hand upon her bosom, trying to still it.

“I have made my confession that my love is mad,” he said. “It is so mad, that had the ties between me and my dear lost boy been one silken thread less strong, I might have swept even him from your side, when you favoured him.”

A film swam before her eyes, as if his words had turned her faint.

“Even him,” he repeated. “Yes, even him! Rosa, you see me and you hear me. Judge for yourself whether any other admirer shall love you and live, whose life is in my hand.”

“What do you mean, sir?” she whispered.

“I mean to show you how mad my love is. It was hawked through the late inquiries by Mr. Crisparkle, that young Landless had confessed to him that he was a rival of my lost boy. That is an inexpiable offence in my eyes. The same Mr. Crisparkle knows under my hand that I have devoted myself to the murderer’s discovery and destruction, be he whom he might, and that I determined to discuss the mystery with no one until I should hold the clue in which to entangle the murderer as in a net. I have since worked patiently to wind and wind it round him; and it is slowly winding as I speak.”

There was something sickening in the calm with which he spoke of patience, of winding, of nets—like a cook describing the slow binding of a joint with twine, the careful trussing that makes escape impossible. Outside the garden, Cloisterham’s familiar hush held—only the faint noises of summer: a distant cart, a pump’s last drip, a bird’s sharp call. Those ordinary sounds made his threat more monstrous, as if brutality could hide itself in the town’s measured quiet.

“Your belief, if you believe in the criminality of Mr. Landless,” Rosa retorted, finding her voice in her fear, “is not Mr. Crisparkle’s belief, and he is a good man.”

“My belief is my own,” Jasper answered, “and I reserve it, worshipped of my soul! Circumstances may accumulate so strongly even against an innocent man, that directed, sharpened, and pointed, they may slay him. One wanting link discovered by perseverance against a guilty man proves his guilt, however slight its evidence before, and he dies. Young Landless stands in deadly peril either way.”

“If you really suppose,” Rosa pleaded, turning paler, “that I favour Mr. Landless, or that Mr. Landless has ever in any way addressed himself to me, you are wrong.”

He put it from him with a slighting action and a curled lip.

“I was going to show you how madly I love you,” he said. “More madly now than ever, for I am willing to renounce the second object that has arisen in my life to divide it with you; and henceforth to have no object in existence but you only. Miss Landless has become your bosom friend. You care for her peace of mind?”

“I love her dearly.”

“You care for her good name?”

“I have said, sir, I love her dearly.”

“I am unconsciously,” he observed, and folded his hands upon the sun-dial, leaning his chin on them so that, from the windows, he might appear the very pattern of idle friendliness—“I am unconsciously giving offence by questioning again. I will simply make statements, therefore, and not put questions. You do care for your bosom friend’s good name, and you do care for her peace of mind. Then remove the shadow of the gallows from her, dear one!”

“You dare propose to me to—”

“Darling, I dare propose to you. Stop there. If it be bad to idolise you, I am the worst of men; if it be good, I am the best. My love for you is above all other love, and my truth to you is above all other truth. Let me have hope and favour, and I am a forsworn man for your sake.”

Rosa put her hands to her temples and pushed back her hair, staring at him as if trying to piece together his purpose from the fragments he flung at her. The words he used—hope, favour, truth—were the same bright coins respectable men pass from hand to hand; in his mouth they were clipped and false, a dominant lexicon used to command and purchase. She could not translate what she felt into any phrase that would make it less vile; her very terror seemed to have no clean language.

“Reckon up nothing at this moment, angel,” he continued, “but the sacrifices that I lay at those dear feet, which I could fall down among the vilest ashes and kiss, and put upon my head as a poor savage might. There is my fidelity to my dear boy after death. Tread upon it!” He flung down the invisible offering with his hands. “There is the inexpiable offence against my adoration of you. Spurn it!” Another cast-down. “There are my labours in the cause of a just vengeance for six toiling months. Crush them!” Again. “There is my past and my present wasted life. There is the desolation of my heart and my soul. There is my peace; there is my despair. Stamp them into the dust; so that you take me, were it even mortally hating me!”

The vehemence that had been held back now surged up to its full height, and it terrified her so much that the spell which had pinned her broke at last. She moved swiftly towards the porch.

In an instant he was at her side, and his voice, close to her ear, changed—smoothed—self-repressed again, as if he had put a lid on boiling.

“Rosa, I am self-repressed again. I am walking calmly beside you to the house. I shall wait for some encouragement and hope. I shall not strike too soon. Give me a sign that you attend to me.”

Her hand moved—slightly, constrainedly—the smallest motion that could pass for obedience.

“Not a word of this to any one,” he breathed, “or it will bring down the blow, as certainly as night follows day. Another sign that you attend to me.”

Her hand moved again, as if it were not her hand.

“I love you, love you, love you! If you were to cast me off now—but you will not—you would never be rid of me. No one should come between us. I would pursue you to the death.”

The handmaid came out to open the gate. Jasper, as if nothing had occurred but ordinary civil speech in a garden, quietly pulled off his hat in parting salute, and went away with no more show of agitation than could be seen in the stony effigy of Mr. Sapsea’s father opposite.

Rosa, going up-stairs, fainted. They carried her carefully to her room and laid her on her bed. A thunderstorm was coming on, the maids said; the air was hot and stifling; no wonder the pretty dear was overset. They had felt their own knees all of a tremble all day long.

Outside, the summer hush thickened, and the sky gathered its heavy soundless weight—one more shadow moving toward the sun-dial of Cloisterham.
