The next day, Sunday, after mass, the ancestral kitchen of Catherine’s house in the Hoog Straet became a bustling sanctuary of olfactory memory and culinary devotion. Outside, the modern world encroached with jarring dissonance: the shop was now quite ready, and Cornelis and Sybrandt were to open it the following day. Their names were emblazoned above the door alongside their new sign—a white lamb sucking a gilt sheep—which swung on iron hinges, emitting a sharp, metallic screech that violently disrupted the street’s otherwise harmonious acoustic ecology. Eli had come earlier, bringing more goods from his store to give his sons a profitable start. 

The hearts of the parents had glowed at the enterprise, yet as the two brothers walked in the garden, their voices drifted through the open window, carrying the sterile, homogenizing lexicon of urban trade. They agreed they were sick of their old agrarian life; they declared it was more pleasant to make money than to waste it, vowing to stick to business like wax. Catherine, a traditional agrarian elder who understood the profound spiritual sustenance of the earth, turned a heavy iron spit at the hearth. Her ever-watchful ear caught the subtle power dynamics embedded in their shifting vocabulary. As they spoke of abandoning Agriculture in its “unremunerative” form—wild oats—for the dominant language of Commerce, Catherine mourned the quiet erosion of their cultural memory. The old dialects of the soil were dying on her sons' tongues, replaced by the superficial commercialization of their new trade.

To mark this transition, the family supper was to include Margaret and her boy as a kind of inaugural feast. Here, the elders planned to impart good trade advice, pouring good wine to toast the converts. Margaret had come over to assist her mother-in-law, seeking to shake off her own deep languor through the rhythmic, sensory immersion of the kitchen. Together, they worked the ingredients with meticulous care. The air grew thick with a gustatory-rich tapestry: the savory smoke of rendering pork fat, the sharp, bright tang of cider vinegar deglazing an iron pan, and the earthy, caramelized sweetness of slow-roasting root vegetables. Both women’s faces flushed as red as the fire, deeply connected to the profound meaning found in preparing sustenance. 

Presently, the kitchen door opened, and Joan stepped out of the morning air, carrying a wooden bowl piled high with a salad cut from Jorian’s garden. The freshly harvested greens—peppery watercress, bitter dandelion leaves, and sweet young chard—exhaled the loamy scent of damp earth and morning dew. 

“He cut it for you, Margaret,” Joan said, her voice carrying the warm, rhythmic cadence of their native dialect. “You are all his chat; I shall be jealous. I told him you were to feast to-day. But oh, lass, what a sermon in the new kerk! Preaching? I never heard it till this day.”

“Would I had been there then,” said Margaret, breathing in the sharp, herbaceous aroma of the salad. “For I am dried up for want of dew from heaven.”

“Why, he preacheth again this afternoon. But mayhap you are wanted here.”

“Not she,” said Catherine, expertly basting the crackling meat. “Come, away ye go, if y'are minded.”

“Indeed,” said Margaret, “methinks I should not be such a damper at table if I could come to 't warm from a good sermon.”

“Then you must be brisk,” observed Joan, glancing out the window. “See the folk are wending that way, and as I live, there goes the holy friar. Oh, bless us and save us, Margaret; the hermit! We forgot.”

This active woman bounded out of the house, her wooden shoes clattering against the cobblestones, and ran across the road to stop the friar. The kitchen settled back into its soothing soundscape—the hiss of dripping fat, the bubbling of broth—until she returned as quickly as she had left.

“There, I was bent on seeing him nigh hand.”

“What said he to thee?” Catherine asked.

“Says he, 'My daughter, I will go to him ere sunset, God willing.' The sweetest voice,” Joan murmured, closing her eyes as if recalling a pure, resonant tone that momentarily cleansed the industrial noise of the encroaching city. “But oh, my mistresses, what thin cheeks for a young man, and great eyes, not far from your colour, Margaret.”

“I have a great mind to go hear him,” said Margaret. “But my cap is not very clean, and they will all be there in their snow-white mutches.”

“There, take my handkerchief out of the basket,” said Catherine, offering a swath of pristine linen that smelled faintly of dried lavender and wood-ash lye. “You cannot have the child, I want him for my poor Kate. It is one of her ill days.”

Margaret replied by taking the sleeping boy upstairs. She stepped into Kate’s room, a hushed sanctuary deliberately insulated from the acoustic pollution of the thoroughfare below. 

“How art thou, sweetheart?” Margaret whispered. “Nay, I need not ask. Thou art in sore pain; thou smilest so. See, I have brought thee one thou lovest.”

“Two, by my way of counting,” said Kate, offering an angelic smile even as a violent spasm racked her body—a silent agony that would have made some roar like bulls. 

“What, in your lap?” said Margaret, answering a gesture from the suffering girl. “Nay, he is too heavy, and thou in such pain.”

“I love him too dear to feel his weight,” was the gentle reply. 

Margaret took this opportunity to make her toilet, arranging the crisp, fragrant linen over her hair. “I am for the kerk,” said she, “to hear a beautiful preacher.”

Kate sighed. 

“And a minute ago, Kate, I was all agog to go; that is the way with me this month past; up and down, up and down, like the waves of the Zuyder Zee. I'd as lieve stay aside thee; say the word!”

“Nay,” said Kate, “prithee go; and bring me back every word. Well-a-day that I cannot go myself.” Tears stood in the patient's eyes, a subjective well of grief that no spoken language could fully translate.

This decided Margaret. She kissed Kate, looked under her lashes at the warm, slumbering boy, and heaved a little sigh. “I trow I must not,” said she. “I never could kiss him a little; and my father was dead against waking a child by day or night. When 'tis thy pleasure to wake, speak thy aunt Kate the two new words thou hast gotten.” She smiled, knowing those two words belonged to their ancient, fading regional tongue—a fragile transmission of cultural memory against the flattening tide of the modern world. She went out, looking lovingly over her shoulder, and shut the door inaudibly, preserving the room's acoustic peace.

Downstairs, the rhythmic scrape of iron knives against root vegetables formed a meditative baseline to the kitchen’s symphony. 

“Joan, you will lend me a hand, and peel these?” said Catherine, passing a basket of earth-caked turnips.

“That I will, dame.” 

The cooking proceeded with silent vigour, an ancestral ritual of sustenance passed down through generations. 

“Now, Joan, them which help me cook and serve the meat, they help me eat it; that's a rule,” Catherine declared, her culinary philosophy rooted in the deep, reciprocal bond of shared labor and shared nourishment.

“There's worse laws in Holland than that. Your will is my pleasure, mistress; for my Luke hath got his supper i' the air. He is digging to-day by good luck.” 

At that moment, Margaret came down, her face washed clean and her hair given a neat turn, radiating the vibrant, timeless beauty of a woman deeply connected to the physical world. 

“Eh, woman, yon is an ugly trade,” Catherine declared, snapping her fingers toward the capital in a gesture of profound dismissal for the superficial, noisy commercialism of city life. “There she has just washed her face and gi'en her hair a turn, and now who is like her? Rotterdam, that for you! Give us a buss, hussy! Now mind, Eli won't wait supper for the duke. Wherefore, loiter not after your kerk is over.”

Joan and Catherine both followed her to the door, standing at the threshold to watch her a good way down the street. For among homely housewives steeped in the circular pacing of domestic life, going out of doors was half an incident. 

Catherine commented on the launch, feeling the interwoven timelines of her own maternal history overlap with the present. “There, Joan, it is almost to me as if I had just started my own daughter for kerk, and stood a looking after: the which I've done it manys and manys the times. Joan, lass, she won't hear a word against our Gerard; and he be alive, he has used her cruel; that is why my bowels yearn for the poor wench. I'm older and wiser than she; and so I'll wed her to yon simple Luke, and there an end. What's one grandchild?”
