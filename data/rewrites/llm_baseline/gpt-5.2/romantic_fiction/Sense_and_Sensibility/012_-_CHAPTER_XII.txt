Elinor and Marianne were abroad early the next morning, the air rinsed clean by night rain and full of those small, particular sounds that make a place feel inhabited—the drip of eaves into puddles, the soft complaint of damp leaves, the distant answering of rooks from the higher trees. Their footsteps kept time upon the gravel as they walked; and yet it was Marianne’s voice that filled the lane, bright and hurried, as if she had been holding her happiness in her mouth all night and could no longer keep it.

“I must tell you,” she said, clasping Elinor’s arm with a warmth that was half triumph, half tenderness. “Willoughby has given me a horse.”

Elinor stopped short. The news had that extravagant air which always startled her most—not because she had not long understood Marianne’s capacity for rapture, but because such raptures, once accepted, could not be unaccepted without consequence. “A horse?”

“A horse he bred himself in Somersetshire,” Marianne went on, as if naming the county were like naming an herb in a recipe—proof of quality, of origin, of care. “Exactly calculated to carry a woman. He says it moves like music—light, obedient. He intends to send his groom immediately for it. Think, Elinor—think of a gallop on these downs, every day. You shall ride it too; I insist upon it.”

The downs lay pale and open beyond them, under a sky that had not decided whether it would break or weep again. Marianne’s eyes, however, were already elsewhere: in wind, in speed, in the imagined quickening of blood. To Elinor, the vision came with other attachments—like a rich dish whose cost one tastes at once beneath the first delicious mouthful.

“My dear Marianne,” she said as gently as she could, “have you considered what it implies? Mama has no intention of keeping a horse. If she alters her plans for this—if you accept it—we must buy another for the servant, keep a man to ride it, and we have not even a stable. A gift is not only the thing itself; it draws an establishment after it.”

Marianne’s smile wavered, then returned with the impatience of one who will not have felicity measured out by the spoonful. “An additional servant would be a trifle. Mama will never object. Any horse would do for him; he might always borrow one at the Park. And as for a stable—what is a stable? The merest shed would answer.”

Elinor heard in her sister’s voice that dangerous ease with which people dismiss what they do not wish to reckon. It was like watching a cook fling handfuls of spice into the pot with a lavish wrist, enchanted by fragrance, forgetting that someone must pay for saffron.

She walked on a little, letting the sound of their steps and the thin, bright calls of birds between hedges temper her words before she said them. “There is another consideration.”

Marianne looked at her quickly. Elinor could have wished to keep her eyes upon the rain-beaded hawthorn instead of meeting that ardour, but she did not flinch. “Is it proper,” she asked, “to receive such a present—from a man so little, or at least so lately known to you?”

That touched what could not be touched without pain. Marianne’s colour rose as if a warm oven-door had opened in her breast. “You are mistaken, Elinor,” she said, and the sweetness of her earlier tone sharpened. “You suppose I know very little of Willoughby. I have not known him long indeed; but I am much better acquainted with him than I am with any other creature in the world, except yourself and mama. It is not time or opportunity that determines intimacy—it is disposition. Seven years would be insufficient to make some people acquainted with each other, and seven days are more than enough for others. I should hold myself guilty of greater impropriety in accepting a horse from my brother than from Willoughby. Of John I know very little, though we have lived together for years; but of Willoughby my judgment has long been formed.”

Elinor felt the ground give slightly beneath her, not from Marianne’s reasoning—which was all fire and no structure—but from her certainty. Opposition now would only feed that certainty, as wind feeds flame. She changed her tack, and, with that careful economy of feeling that was her own kind of devotion, appealed where Marianne’s tenderness was most steady.

“Then think of mama,” she said. “Not of whether she would object—but of what she would do, out of kindness. You know she would stretch herself, deny herself, take on trouble gladly, rather than refuse you. It is not right to tempt her to such imprudent generosity. We are settled here to live simply. We must not let any one draw her into expense and inconvenience.”

The mention of their mother—of her easy, affectionate readiness to be persuaded—worked like a cool hand laid on a fevered brow. Marianne’s expression softened; her gaze fell to the wet grass at the path’s edge. She struggled, for a moment, between her desire and her conscience; and Elinor, listening, could almost hear that struggle in the quiet of the morning, as plainly as the ticking drip of water from a leaf.

At last Marianne said, subdued, “You are right. I will not mention it to mama. I will tell him—when I see him—that it must be declined.”

Elinor, relieved, pressed her sister’s hand. Marianne’s promise had cost her something, and Elinor honoured the cost.

She kept her word. When Willoughby called at the cottage that day, Elinor—passing through the parlour with some household errand—heard Marianne’s voice lowered, as if they were trying to keep their words from the very walls. Marianne spoke of her disappointment; she named, plainly and without exaggeration, the reasons that made acceptance impossible. They were reasons no man of any delicacy could combat without becoming indelicate.

Willoughby’s concern was unmistakable. There was a quick sincerity in it, like a sudden hush falling over a room that has been too loud. Elinor could not see his face from where she stood; but she heard the altered weight of his voice as he answered, also low.

“But, Marianne—the horse is still yours, though you cannot use it now. I shall keep it only till you can claim it. When you leave Barton to form your own establishment, in a more lasting home, Queen Mab shall receive you.”

Queen Mab. The name, spoken with that intimate ease; and Marianne’s own name too, given without ceremony—Christian name only, as if there were no need for any other—told Elinor what argument and observation had been leading her toward, and told it with a directness that made her almost giddy. The promise contained a whole future: a home not borrowed but made; an establishment “more lasting;” Marianne leaving Barton—not as a guest of circumstance, but as a bride.

From that moment Elinor doubted no longer. They were engaged. The only surprise remaining was that, with tempers so frank, they could have left even their nearest friends to discover it by accident, as one stumbles upon a hidden hearth by the smell of bread.

The next day, Margaret brought her confirmation with the gravity of a child who believes herself entrusted with history.

“Oh, Elinor!” she cried, drawing her sister aside with a face so important it made Elinor smile in spite of herself. “I have such a secret to tell you about Marianne. I am sure she will be married to Mr. Willoughby very soon.”

“You have said so,” Elinor returned, “almost every day since they first met on High-church Down; and before they had known each other a week you were certain Marianne wore his picture round her neck—when it proved to be only our great-uncle.”

“But indeed this is quite another thing!” Margaret’s eyes widened. “I am sure they will be married very soon, for he has got a lock of her hair.”

Elinor could not prevent a short laugh. “Take care, Margaret. It may be only the hair of some great-uncle of his.”

“It is Marianne’s,” Margaret insisted, affronted by such injustice to her powers of observation. “I am almost sure it is, for I saw him cut it off. Last night, after tea—when you and mama went out of the room—they were whispering and talking as fast as could be, and he seemed to be begging something of her; and presently he took up her scissors and cut off a long lock of her hair, for it was all tumbled down her back; and he kissed it, and folded it up in a piece of white paper, and put it into his pocket-book.”

The detail was too exact to be merely invented. Elinor believed it at once; and she was not disposed to resist believing it, for it fell into place with what she had overheard and with all the unguarded language of Willoughby’s promise. In Marianne’s loosened hair, in the private snip of scissors and the kiss and the careful folding—there was the same kind of intimacy as in the naming of Queen Mab: a domestic confidence, like seasoning tasted from the spoon and approved without comment.

Margaret’s sagacity, however, did not always serve her sisters so well.

One evening at the Park, when the noise of company made a sort of weather of its own—voices rising and falling like gusts, chairs scraping, fireside tools clinking—Mrs. Jennings, in the midst of her comfortable raillery, turned suddenly upon Margaret with that hungry curiosity of hers, which delighted in guessing other people’s feelings aloud.

“Come, Miss Margaret,” she said, “you must tell me the name of the young man who is Elinor’s particular favourite. It has been a prodigious mystery this long while.”

Margaret, pleased to be teased and to tease in turn, looked straight at Elinor and said, with a cautious air that would have been comic if it had not been cruel, “I must not tell, may I, Elinor?”

Everybody laughed; the sound struck Elinor like a spoon against a cracked cup. She tried to laugh too, because to refuse would be to confess; but the effort was painful, and she felt the heat of it on her face as if she were too near a blaze. In an instant she was certain Margaret had fixed—by chance or childish inference—upon a name Elinor could not bear to have made a standing joke in Mrs. Jennings’s mouth.

Marianne felt for her, and yet, by the very intensity of her sympathy, made it worse. She flushed deeply and spoke sharply to Margaret. “Remember that whatever your conjectures may be, you have no right to repeat them.”

“I never had any conjectures,” Margaret retorted. “It was you who told me of it yourself.”

That only increased the company’s mirth; the room, already too loud, grew louder. Mrs. Jennings clapped her hands, delighted.

“Oh! Pray, Miss Margaret, let us know all about it. What is the gentleman’s name?”

“I must not tell, ma’am,” Margaret said, enjoying her own importance. “But I know very well what it is; and I know where he is too.”

“Yes, yes, we can guess where he is,” cried Mrs. Jennings. “At his own house at Norland to be sure. He is the curate of the parish, I dare say.”

“No, that he is not,” Margaret returned triumphantly. “He is of no profession at all.”

“Margaret,” Marianne said, warm with indignation, “you know that all this is an invention of your own, and that there is no such person in existence.”

“Well then,” Margaret cried, refusing to be crushed, “he is lately dead, Marianne; for I am sure there was such a man once, and his name begins with an F.”

Elinor’s heart seemed to stop and then go on again with a different rhythm. The air of the room grew thick; even the familiar sounds—the laugh, the rustle of silk, the crackle of the fire—felt suddenly like noise, not life, as if something fine in her had been jostled by careless hands.

At that moment Lady Middleton, with a composure that always appeared like disapproval, observed, “It rains very hard.”

Elinor could have loved her for the sentence, though she suspected the interruption owed less to any concern for Elinor than to Lady Middleton’s dislike of such coarse sport. Still, the topic—so harmless, so general—fell like a cloth thrown over a dish one is glad to hide.

Colonel Brandon, who seemed always to notice what others suffered and to step quietly between pain and the public eye, took it up at once. Much was said of the rain by him and by Lady Middleton; the company, eager for any change, let the raillery drop as easily as an emptied cup.

Willoughby opened the piano-forte and asked Marianne to sit down. The lid rose with a soft wooden sigh; the first notes, when she played, gathered the room into a more tolerable harmony. Conversation broke into smaller threads. Sound became music again instead of laughter sharpened into mockery.

But Elinor did not recover so quickly. The alarm remained in her—less the fear of discovery than the knowledge of how lightly other people could throw a name about, how a private feeling might be dragged into the common language of jest, changed by being spoken. She sat with her hands folded, listening as much to what was not said as to what was: the way Colonel Brandon’s voice kept low; the way Marianne’s playing covered awkwardness; the way Mrs. Jennings, balked, soon sought new amusement.

Before the evening ended a party was arranged for the following day: they were to go, by Colonel Brandon’s interest, to see a place some twelve miles from Barton, belonging to his brother-in-law. The owner was abroad and had left strict orders that it was not to be shown without permission; but Brandon could procure admittance. Sir John, warm in praise and loud in certainty, declared the grounds to be as fine as any in the county, and might be allowed a judge’s confidence, having made parties to visit them at least twice every summer for the last ten years.

There was, he said, a noble piece of water—a sail upon it to be the great entertainment of the morning. Cold provisions were to be taken; open carriages only employed; everything conducted in the full, bustling style of a pleasure-party.

To some of the company it appeared bold—almost wilful—considering the season and that it had rained every day for the last fortnight. The very mention of cold provisions, of damp air and open carriages, made Elinor think at once of her mother’s cough, like a faint thread of sound in the night that tells you the house is not as warm as it should be.

Mrs. Dashwood, who had already caught cold, was persuaded by Elinor to stay at home. Elinor urged it not as command but as care, imagining their mother by the fire, with tea properly made, the kettle singing softly instead of the wind; a small domestic refuge against the wet world’s ceaseless, needling noise.
