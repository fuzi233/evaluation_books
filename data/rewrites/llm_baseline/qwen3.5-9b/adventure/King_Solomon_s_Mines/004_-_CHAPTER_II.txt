"What news did you gather regarding my brother's expedition at Bamangwato?" Sir Henry inquired as I paused to pack my pipe, awaiting Captain Good's response.

"I heard this," I replied, "and kept it strictly confidential until today. I was informed that he had departed for Solomon's Mines."

"Solomon's Mines!" both my listeners exclaimed simultaneously. "Where exactly are they located?"

"I do not know their precise location," I admitted. "I only know where they are *said* to be. Years ago, I caught a glimpse of the mountain peaks bordering them, but there stretches a barren desert of one hundred and thirty miles between those heights and me. To my knowledge, no white man has ever successfully crossed that wasteland, save for one solitary individual. However, perhaps the wisest course is for me to recount the legend of Solomon's Mines as I understand it, provided you pledge not to disclose a single word of what follows without my explicit permission. Are you willing to swear to this secrecy? I have specific reasons for requesting it."

Sir Henry nodded solemnly, and Captain Good affirmed, "Certainly, certainly."

"Well," I began, "as you might surmise, elephant hunters are typically a rugged breed of men, concerned primarily with the necessities of survival and the customs of the local tribes. Yet, occasionally, one encounters a man dedicated to collecting oral traditions from the indigenous peoples, striving to reconstruct fragments of this continent's obscure history. Such a man was the source of the legend I am about to share, nearly thirty years ago. This occurred during my first elephant hunt in the Matabele lands. His name was Evans, a fine fellow tragically killed the following year by a wounded buffalo; he now rests near the Zambesi Falls.

One evening, I recounted to Evans some remarkable excavations I had discovered while tracking koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. I noted that recent prospectors had rediscovered these sites, though I had known of them long prior. A vast roadway, hewn directly from solid rock, led to the entrance of a massive gallery. Within, stacks of gold-bearing quartz were piled high, ready for smelting, indicating that the workers had fled in haste. Furthermore, approximately twenty paces inside, the tunnel was abruptly sealed by exquisite masonry."

"'Ay,' Evans remarked, 'but I shall weave you a far stranger tale.' And so he did. He described discovering a ruined city deep within the interior, which he believed to be the biblical Ophir—a theory shared by scholars long before his time. Young and captivated by tales of ancient civilizations and the treasures plundered by Jewish or Phoenician adventurers from a land now shrouded in barbarism, I listened intently. Suddenly, he posed a question: 'Lad, have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains to the northwest of the Mushakulumbwe region?'

'I had never heard of them,' I confessed.

'Ah, well,' he continued, 'that is precisely where Solomon established his true mines—his diamond mines.'

'How can you possibly know that?' I asked.

'Know it!' he retorted. 'Why, what is "Suliman" but a corruption of Solomon? Moreover, an elderly Isanusi, a witch-doctoress from the Manica region, revealed everything to me. She spoke of the people dwelling beyond those mountains as a distinct branch of the Zulus, speaking a refined dialect, composed of taller, stronger men. Among them, she claimed, resided powerful wizards who had mastered their arts from white men in a time when the world was still dark, possessing the secret to a miraculous mine of "bright stones."'

At the time, I dismissed the story with laughter, though it intrigued me, especially as the Diamond Fields remained undiscovered. Poor Evans perished shortly thereafter, and for twenty years, the matter faded from my mind. However, just twenty years later—a considerable span for an elephant hunter, whose life is rarely measured in decades—I received more concrete information regarding Suliman's Mountains and the territory beyond.

I was stationed at Sitanda's Kraal, a desolate outpost in the Manica region where sustenance was scarce and game elusive. Stricken by fever and in a wretched state, I was visited one day by a Portuguese traveler accompanied by a single half-caste companion. While I am familiar with the predatory nature of low-class Delagoa Bay Portuguese, who thrive on human suffering and slavery, this visitor was entirely different. He possessed the bearing of a gentleman; tall, slender, with piercing dark eyes and curling grey mustaches. He spoke broken English, and I understood enough Portuguese to converse briefly. His name was José Silvestre, and he maintained a property near Delagoa Bay.

On the second day, as he prepared to depart with his companion toward the west, he tipped his hat in the traditional manner and said, "'Good-bye, señor. Should our paths cross again, I shall be the wealthiest man in the world, and I shall remember you.'"

I chuckled weakly, watching him stride confidently into the vast western desert, wondering if he were mad or harboring delusions of grandeur.

A week elapsed, and my fever subsided. One evening, seated before my small tent, gnawing on the final leg of a bird purchased for a mere scrap of cloth, I stared at the setting sun bleeding crimson over the desert sands. Suddenly, a figure appeared on the ridge opposite, roughly three hundred yards away. Clad in a coat, it appeared to be a European. The figure crawled on hands and knees, then attempted to rise, staggering a few steps before collapsing and crawling again. Recognizing signs of extreme distress, I dispatched one of my hunters.

When he returned, I gasped. It was José Silvestre—or rather, his skeletal remains wrapped in tattered skin. His face was jaundiced yellow, his eyes bulging from sockets devoid of flesh, leaving only parchment-thin skin stretched over white hair and gaunt bones.

'"Water! For Christ's sake, water!" he moaned. His lips were cracked, and his tongue, protruding grotesquely, was swollen and black.

I offered him water mixed with milk. He consumed nearly two quarts in desperate gulps, refusing further intake. The fever seized him anew, and he collapsed, babbling incoherently of Suliman's Mountains, diamonds, and the desert. I dragged him into the tent, doing what little aid I could, knowing his end was inevitable. By eleven o'clock, he quieted. Exhausted, I slept.

At dawn, I awoke to see Silvestre sitting upright, a ghostly figure gazing fixedly toward the horizon. As the first rays of sunlight swept across the plain, illuminating the distant crest of a towering Suliman Mountain over a hundred miles away, the dying man cried out in Portuguese, '"There it is! But I shall never reach it... Never! No one will ever reach it!"

He paused, steeling himself. "Friend, are you there? My vision fades."

"Yes," I replied softly. "Lie down and rest."

"Ay," he whispered. "Rest soon I shall. All eternity remains. Listen, I am dying. You have been kind. I leave you this writing. Perhaps you possess the strength to traverse the desert that claimed my servant and me."

Groping in his shirt, he produced what resembled a Boer tobacco pouch crafted from the skin of the sable antelope (*Swart-vet-pens*), secured with a leather thong (*rimpi*). Unable to loosen it, he handed it to me. "Untie it," he commanded.

I complied, extracting a fragment of torn yellow linen inscribed with rust-colored script. Tucked inside was a folded paper.

His voice grew faint. "The paper contains all that is written on the linen. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor, a political exile from Lisbon and among the first Portuguese to set foot on these shores, penned this while dying in those mountains untouched by white feet since antiquity. His name was José da Silvestra, living three centuries ago. His slave, waiting on this side, found him dead and brought the message home to Delagoa. It rested in our family, ignored until I finally read it. I have paid the ultimate price, but another may succeed and become the richest man in the world—the richest man alive. Give it to no one, señor. Go yourself!"

Before wandering into delirium again, he expired peacefully. I buried him deeply under heavy boulders, ensuring the jackals could not disturb his grave. Then, I departed."

"But the document?" Sir Henry interjected, his voice thick with anticipation. "What did it contain?"

Captain Good echoed, "And the contents of the writing?"

"Well, gentlemen, if you desire to know, I shall tell you. I have never displayed it to anyone save a drunken Portuguese trader who translated it for me, only to forget it by morning. The original rag resides at my home in Durban, alongside Dom José's translation. However, I carry the English rendering in my pocket-book, along with a facsimile of the map—which, if it can be called that. Behold."

*I, José da Silvestra, currently dying of starvation in a small cave where no snow falls on the northern slope of the southernmost peak of the twin mountains I have named "Sheba's Breasts," write this in the year 1590 using a splintered bone against a scrap of my garment, my own blood serving as ink.*
