Lona’s disgust had risen all morning like milk left too long by a warm hearth. The streets, the faces, worst of all the women with their hard eyes and sharper elbows—she would have shaken the place from her sandals and been gone at once. I understood her; the very air tasted of soot and old malice. Yet I could not yield.

To turn back now would be to teach the Little Ones that fear is a wisdom, and the lesson would poison them more surely than any city-brewed well. If we retreated, the princess would hunt us, certain as flies find a cut. If we went forward, we carried with us the one bright, dangerous hope bound up in the prophecy: mother and daughter must meet. I held to that line as a cook holds to a thread of scent in a market—follow it, and you find what you need, even if the crowd jostles and curses. Lona’s loveliness might, by some miracle of flesh and memory, strike through the princess’s crusted heart. And if violence came, I would stand between them. If I had no other power left, I was ready, for Lona’s sake, to strike the princess’s closed hand without pity. I felt—though I did not say it—that doom had already set its fire beneath her, and perhaps it was decreed that we should be the ones to bring the boil.

I told Lona the matter as it lay with me, but still I gave her no hint of the bond between her and the woman on the dais. She listened, jaw tight, like an elder tasting a broth for bitterness and finding too much of it. Then, suddenly, she nodded once, as if accepting a difficult duty in the household.

“Then we go,” she said.

From the top of one of the palace towers, the princess had seen us at dawn—had seen the Little Ones’ strange array moving like a living tide toward her sleeping city. It woke in her a terror that mastered her more than any army could: she had failed to destroy them, and now they came. The prophecy had found legs. When she came back to herself, she went down into the black hall and set herself in the north focus of its ellipse, directly beneath the opening in the roof.

For she must think.

But what she named thinking was only the careful tending of an image: consciousness not of what she was, but of what she chose to believe she was. To feed that false hunger, she had suspended, a little above and before her—hidden in the darkness—a mirror positioned to catch the sun reflected from her own body, so that, for a brief exact time, she could see herself as she wished: drenched in light, clothed in her beauty like a garment freshly shaken out.

Shadows moved about her in the dark—many, restless as mice behind a larder wall. When her inward eye glimpsed one, she refused it attention. Close under the mirror stood the Shadow that attended her walks, but she did not see him; self-occupation blinded her more thoroughly than night.

The city fell. Its people cowered. The Little Ones and their odd cavalry made camp in the square. And at noon the sunlight struck the princess, and for a few minutes she saw herself glorious. When the vision thinned and passed, she still sat there. Night came; darkness filled even the glass; still she did not move. The palace swarmed with gloom; servants shivered, but dared not flee for fear of the Little Ones’ beasts. All night she sat, motionless, waiting to taste again that sweet, thin draught of her own presence. But courage and will had grown tired of her and went away, like faithful dogs finally turned from a door.

In the morning we chose twelve of the tallest and bravest boys to go with us to the palace—lads with steady hands and clear ears, the sort that can hear trouble coming through stone. We rode our great horses; the boys had their small horses and their elephants. In the square the soundscape had changed: the usual city clatter, that ceaseless metallic fret of market and quarrel, was muted, as if the stones themselves were holding their breath.

We climbed. The palace rose ahead, and above it the dome of the great hall. The sun climbed with us, and as we neared, its light crept toward the roof-eye. The princess was waiting, poised like a woman waiting for the first smell of bread from an oven she believes she owns. Not a ray could enter the black hall until the sun stood at the meridian. Then, suddenly, he looked in through the opening—and with a quick radiance the princess flashed upon her own sight in the hidden mirror.

But the cry that tore from her was not triumph. It was despair.

Her whiteness—her prized blank perfection—was broken. A spot covered half her side, black as the marble around her, as if a giant hand had seared her through robe and flesh. She clutched at the fabric and fell back into her chair, breath catching like a person choking on smoke. The Shadow glided out, and this time she saw him go.

We found the gate open as it always was, as if the palace had long ago given up on guarding itself against what belonged to it. We passed through the paved grove toward the door, hooves tapping a dry, hollow rhythm that the stones threw back at us. In the vestibule the spotted leopardess lay in her cage, either asleep or dead. The Little Ones paused, staring. Then she sprang up, all teeth and brindled muscle, rearing against the bars. The horses reared and plunged; the elephants took a startled step back, their feet thudding like slow drums in a cavern.

The next instant the leopardess fell supine, writhed in quivering spasms, and went still again, like a fire suddenly starved.

We rode into the great hall. The darkness swallowed hoofbeats, chewed them, then let them out again as dull echoes under the dome. The princess still leaned back in her chair, caught in a shaft of sunlight that made her look, to us, like some splendid fruit gleaming in a cellar.

She heard us from the court stones first—the alien thunder of horses in a place used only to soft slippers and the padding of shadows. She started and listened, and the sound made her shake: never had such noise rung in her palace. She pressed her hand to her side and gasped. The trampling came nearer, entered the hall itself, and moving figures—not shadows—approached through the black.

Lona saw her and was off her horse before I could speak, springing forward with the same certainty as a girl running to the kitchen where her mother stands over the pot. I leapt down after her, heart pounding with a terrible hope.

“Mother! mother!” she cried.

Her voice went up into the dome and came back, pure and clear, a sound the hall did not deserve. For an instant the princess shivered. Her face darkened as if hatred were smoke rising under skin; her brows drew together until they nearly met. She stood.

“Mother! mother!” Lona cried again, already mounting the dais, arms flung wide. She reached her and clasped her as if she could warm her, as if she could fold her into the old domestic circle where hands pass bread and names are spoken without fear.

One step more and I would have been there.

In that single step, the princess lifted Lona high—high as a butcher lifts a lamb by the hind legs—and dashed her upon the marble floor.

The sound of her fall was the sound of a whole household dropped and broken. Lona fell at my feet and lay still.

The princess sat down, and the smile she wore belonged to something that had forgotten what tenderness tastes like.

I dropped beside Lona, gathered her up from the stones, and pressed her to my breast. My glance went to the princess with a hate so sharp it seemed to slice my sight; she answered it with her sweetest smile, like a sugared crust hiding rot. I would have sprung at her, seized her throat, and ended her there, but Lona’s weight in my arms anchored me: love of the child was stronger than hate of the mother.

Lona’s arms hung helpless. Her blood trickled over my hands and fell to the floor in soft, slow plashes, as if some careful cook were dripping sauce a drop at a time—only this was life, and it was leaving her. The scent of it spread quickly, raw and iron, and the horses caught it. Mine first: he reared, shivering, wild-eyed, then wheeled and thundered blindly down the dark hall, the little horses following in frantic, clattering imitation. Lona’s horse stood gazing down at his mistress, trembling all over.

The boys flung themselves from their horses’ backs to save them, but in the darkness they did not see the black wall. The terrified beasts dashed, with mine, straight into it and broke themselves. The shock sounded like crockery thrown against stone—too many lives shattered at once.

The elephants came on to the foot of the dais and stopped, trumpeting wildly, their voices filling the hall with an awful, living protest—as if the very ecology of sound in that place rejected what had been done. The Little Ones sprang up onto the dais and stood, horrified and rigid. The princess lay back in her seat. Her face had gone corpse-white, her eyes alone alive, wickedly flaming. She was withered and wasted again, as she had been when first I found her in the wood, and the brand on her side looked deeper, as if the darkness itself had pressed its seal upon her.

But Lona saw nothing. And I saw only Lona.

“Mother… mother…” she sighed—less a word now than a breath of habit, the last syllables of a native tongue leaving the mouth that learned it at the first breast. Then her breathing ceased.

I carried her into the court. The sun shone upon her white face, and a pitiful shadow lay on her lips like the memory of a smile. Her head hung back. She was dead as earth.

In that moment my mind tore itself free from sense, as if the world had become an intolerable noise. I forgot the Little Ones. I forgot the murderous princess. I forgot even the body in my arms. I wandered, looking for my Lona—as if the true Lona might be somewhere ahead, barefoot in some quiet garden, calling me in the language that belonged only to us.

Doors and windows were crowded with brute-faces, jeering at me, but they did not dare speak. They had seen the white leopardess behind me, head hanging close at my heel. I spurned her with my foot; she drew back a moment, then followed again, as if bound by a leash made of old guilt.

I reached the square. The little army was gone.

The emptiness struck me like hunger. Where were the Little Ones—her Little Ones? I had lost her children. I stared helplessly, then staggered to the pillar and sank upon its base with Lona still in my arms. The square’s silence was not peace; it was the kind of quiet that comes when a factory shuts down for lack of fuel and leaves only the ringing in one’s ears.

As I sat there gazing on her still countenance, it seemed—only for a living moment—to smile, not as a corpse might be arranged to smile, but as she herself smiled when tasting something sweet and familiar. I never doubted it was illusion, yet I believed what it said: I should see her alive again. It was not she who was lost. It was I. And she would find me.

I rose to go after the Little Ones and sought instinctively the gate by which we had entered. I looked about; the leopardess was gone.

The street was rapidly filling with a fierce crowd. They saw me encumbered with my dead, and for a time did not assail me—perhaps the sight of blood and stillness shook even their brutish courage. But as I neared the gate, their confidence swelled. Women began to hustle me with shoulders and hips, the way they might jostle a stranger away from a bread stall. I held on, heedless. A man pushed against my sacred burden; with a kick I sent him away howling.

The crowd pressed harder. Fearing their hands on the dead—hands too curious, too cruel—I clasped my treasure closer and freed my right arm.

That instant a commotion rose behind me, a change in the street’s sound like a wind shifting through trees. The crowd broke apart. Through it came the Little Ones I had left in the palace.

Ten of them rode upon four elephants. On two other elephants lay the princess—bound hand and foot, quite still save for the rolling of her eyes in their ghastly sockets. Two Little Ones rode behind her on Lona’s horse. Every now and then the wise creatures that bore her curled their trunks back to feel her cords, checking them with patient care, the way an old farmer checks the ties on a load before taking the road.

I walked on in front, and out of the city.

So ended the hopes with which I had entered that evil place. We had captured the bad princess—and lost our all-beloved queen. My life was bare; my heart was empty. The sounds of the city fell behind us, harsh and mechanical again as the crowd closed in upon itself, but ahead there was only the trudging rhythm of elephants, the soft creak of leather, and the silence where Lona’s voice should have been.
