Our kin at Castlewood extended their hospitality until the new year’s turn, and after a fortnight’s absence—an eternity to a lovestruck young fool—I returned to my sweetheart’s side. Madame de Bernstein seemed almost relieved to depart from her ancestral home. The farther we traveled, the more freely she spoke, though she never divulged what passed in that fiery confrontation with her niece. The old woman no longer spoke of molding *cette petite*, and when she mentioned the young Countess at all, it was with a nervous laugh, devoid of malice.

“My nephew Eugene,” she mused, “is fated to be henpecked all his days—that much is clear. A little order in that household will do wonders. That sharp little American fellow will make a decent steward. As for the girl’s mother—well, they say she was a convict shipped from England. No need to shout it from the rooftops. And really, does birth matter so much for such people? She’s clever enough, and she’ll learn English soon enough. The name she’s claimed will open every door.”

She dismissed old Van den Bosch’s dubious past with a wave. “A smuggler, a slave-trader—what does it matter? The Countess of Castlewood can afford to be anyone’s daughter. Now that my nephew has presented her, we must stand by her.”

I had hoped her affection might extend to my own choice—dear Hetty Lambert—but here the Baroness proved immovable. When I spoke of Hagan and his wife, her face darkened. “Do not speak that name,” she snapped, before proceeding to talk of nothing else for hours. The tales she spun of Lady Maria’s escapades shall not stain these pages, for they are unfit for young eyes. Yet let it be said: if she sinned, she was not alone, and if she repented, others might take heed.

Hagan, after leaving the stage, led a blameless life, his eloquence finding a nobler calling in the pulpit. His wife embraced fervent piety, though I suspected her spiritual fervor owed more to strong drink than divine revelation. Still, I remembered her kindness in darker days, when colder hearts turned away.

My aunt’s favor was as fickle as the wind. After my play’s success, I basked briefly in society’s glow, admitted to the wits’ circle, deemed passably genteel by the macaronis. I might have risen among them, had my purse been deeper—or had I not preferred a pair of quiet, loving eyes to all the painted beauties of Ranelagh.

“You might have married anyone,” lamented my pious Lady Warrington, her gaze ever flitting between heaven and the ledger. My uncle, ever the pragmatist, sighed. “A hundred thousand pounds, thrown away for a penniless country girl!”

“But I gave my word,” I protested.

“Words are wind, boy! Duty to family—to the Fifth Commandment!—demands prudence.” He spoke of his own ambitions: his son Miley, paired with Nabob Barwell’s daughter at dancing school. “I told the Nabob myself—’twould be a fine match!”

Yet I held fast to my choice, and the old man turned away in disgust. The Baroness, too, scoffed at my rustic attachments. My star waned; Harry’s rose. And why not? I bore no jealousy.

From the war’s front, Harry’s letters came—brief, plain-spoken. At first, the Baroness paid them little heed. But then came news of Minden, where Wolfe’s old regiment had fought gloriously. And then—Quebec.

Ah, Quebec! That bold, mad gamble! Wolfe, fever-wracked, scaling the heights under cover of night, drawing Montcalm into open battle against all odds. Had the ascent been detected an hour sooner, had the French held their lines—what then? Yet Fortune smiled. The audacity of a gambler, the genius of a general—who can say which won the day?

When word reached England, despair turned to delirium. The nation exulted. Friends embraced in the streets; coffeehouses buzzed. Chatham, the architect of victory, was hailed as a demigod. And Harry—my dear Harry, who had shared in that immortal moment—was a hero by association. Even Uncle Warrington wrote in jubilation. Aunt Beatrix held court, basking in reflected glory. I, by mere resemblance, was fêted. Parson Sampson preached a sermon so rousing his flock nearly cheered in the pews.

“Grief?” scoffed General Lambert when his wife mourned Wolfe’s burial. “If our boys could die so, would you deny them? Would you not climb those cliffs yourself to see it done?”

Little Charley, wide-eyed, agreed. “I’d not mind it—and we got a holiday from school besides.”

As for Harry, his promotion was assured. And the Baroness, ever practical, announced she would buy him a company.

Thus ends the tale—not with sorrow, but with a nation’s pride, a brother’s triumph, and the inexorable turn of fate’s wheel.
