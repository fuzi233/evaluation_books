Marianne rose the following morning as per her habit, responding to inquiries with the assurance that she was convalescing, attempting to validate this claim by resuming her customary duties. Yet, a day consumed by shivering beside the fire with a book unread, or lying listlessly upon a sofa, offered little evidence of genuine improvement. By the time she retired early, her condition had deteriorated further. Colonel Brandon marveled at her sister's composure; despite attending to Marianne throughout the day against her wishes and administering necessary remedies at night, Elinor, like Marianne, placed absolute faith in the healing power of sleep and harbored no true alarm.

However, a restless and fever-laden night dashed their expectations. When Marianne, having stubbornly insisted on rising, finally confessed her inability to sit upright and returned to her bed of her own volition, Elinor readily adopted Mrs. Jennings's counsel to summon the Palmers' physician. Dr. Harris arrived, examined his patient, and while encouraging Miss Dashwood to anticipate a swift recovery within a few days, his diagnosis carried a putrid tendency and the ominous word "infection." This pronouncement sent instant panic through Mrs. Palmer regarding her infant. Mrs. Jennings, who had suspected the severity of Marianne's ailment from the outset, grew grave upon hearing Mr. Harris's report, validating Charlotte's fears and urging immediate removal for the baby. Though Mr. Palmer dismissed their anxieties as idle, he found his wife's anxiety and importunity insurmountable. Consequently, her departure was settled; within an hour of Dr. Harris's arrival, she departed with her son and nurse for the residence of a relative living several miles beyond Bath, promising to follow shortly thereafter. She was nearly as eager for her mother to accompany her. Yet, Mrs. Jennings, with a kindness that earned Elinor's deep affection, resolved to remain at Cleveland until Marianne recovered, offering her own attentive care to replace the absent mother. Elinor found her a most willing and active partner, eager to share the burdens and often proving invaluable through superior nursing experience.

Poor Marianne, drained and weak from the nature of her malady, sensing universal illness, could no longer hope for recovery by tomorrow. The prospect of what that day would have wrought without this illness—the start of their journey home, a surprise visit to their mother the following morning—made every symptom acutely severe. Her few words were lamentations of this inevitable delay, though Elinor strove to lift her spirits, genuinely believing as she spoke that the postponement would be fleeting.

The subsequent day brought little alteration; Marianne was neither better nor seemingly worse. Their circle diminished further as Mr. Palmer, though reluctant due to human kindness and a distaste for fleeing in fright, was eventually persuaded by Colonel Brandon to fulfill his promise. As he prepared to depart, Colonel Brandon himself began to speak of going as well. Here, Mrs. Jennings intervened with characteristic grace; to send the Colonel away while his heart bled for his sister would deny them both solace. She firmly asserted that his presence was essential, suggesting evenings of piquet while Miss Dashwood tended her sister, and argued so persuasively that Colonel Brandon, gratified by the chance to comply with his deepest desire, could not long feign hesitation. Mr. Palmer warmly seconded her plea, finding relief in knowing someone so capable to assist Miss Dashwood in any emergency.

Marianne remained ignorant of these arrangements, unaware that she had become the cause of the Cleveland household's imminent departure just seven days after their arrival. She saw nothing of Mrs. Palmer, causing her no surprise, and mentioned her name with no sign of concern. Two days elapsed since Mr. Palmer's exit, and Marianne's state remained stagnant. Dr. Harris, visiting daily, still spoke boldly of a speedy cure, and Miss Dashwood shared his sanguinity, whereas the others held far less cheerful outlooks. Mrs. Jennings had long ago decided Marianne would not survive, and Colonel Brandon, chiefly useful as a listener to her forebodings, was too distressed to resist their influence. He struggled to reason away fears rendered plausible by the apothecary's differing judgment, yet the solitary hours afforded him ample opportunity for melancholy thoughts to take root, convincing him he would never see Marianne again.

Then, on the morning of the third day, the gloom lifted slightly. When Dr. Harris arrived, he declared his patient materially improved. Her pulse was stronger, symptoms more favorable. Elinor, buoyed by these pleas, radiated cheerfulness, rejoicing that her letters to her mother, which minimized the indisposition, had aligned with her own judgment rather than her friend's cautious warnings. She began to fix a date for Marianne's travel. However, the day ended less auspiciously. Toward evening, Marianne fell ill again, becoming heavier, restless, and uncomfortable. Elinor, still optimistic, attributed the change merely to the fatigue of sitting up for her bed to be made. Administering cordials, she watched with satisfaction as Marianne sank into a slumber, expecting beneficial results. Though not perfectly quiet, the sleep lasted considerable time. Anxious to witness the outcome, Elinor resolved to stay with her throughout. Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the shift, retired unusually early; the chief nurse sought recreation elsewhere, leaving Elinor alone with Marianne.

Marianne's repose grew increasingly disturbed. Her sister, watching her constant shifting of posture and hearing frequent, inarticulate murmurs of distress, almost wished to rouse her from such painful dreams. Suddenly, awoken by a casual noise in the house, Marianne sprang up with feverish wildness, crying, "Is Mama coming?"

"Not yet," replied Elinor, concealing her terror and helping Marianne lie down again. "But she will be here, I hope, soon. It is a great distance to Barton."

"But she must not go round by London!" cried Marianne, urgently. "I shall never see her if she does!"

Elinor, alarmed by Marianne's lack of lucidity, felt her pulse eagerly. It was lower and quicker than ever. As Marianne continued to babble wildly of her mother, Elinor's alarm surged, compelling her to summon Dr. Harris instantly and dispatch a messenger to Barton for their mother. A consultation with Colonel Brandon regarding the best method to secure her mother's arrival followed immediately. Having rung for the maid to relieve her, Elinor hastened downstairs to the drawing-room, where she knew he was usually found later in the day.

There was no time for hesitation. Her fears, he possessed no courage or confidence to dispel; he listened to them in silent despondence. Yet, her difficulties were instantly resolved. With a readiness that seemed pre-arranged by destiny, he offered himself as the messenger to fetch Mrs. Dashwood. Elinor made no resistance that proved difficult to overcome. Thanking him briefly but fervently, while he hurried off his servant with orders for post-horses and a message to Dr. Harris, she penned a few lines to her mother.

How gratefully she felt the comfort of such a friend at that critical juncture—a companion whose judgment would guide, whose attendance would relieve, whose friendship would soothe! To the extent that the shock of the summons could be mitigated, his presence, manners, and assistance would surely lessen it. Meanwhile, whatever emotions he harbored, Colonel Brandon acted with the firmness of a collected mind, arranging necessities with utmost despatch and calculating the timeline for his return with exact precision. No moment was wasted. The horses arrived sooner than anticipated. Colonel Brandon pressed her hand with a look of solemn gravity, uttering a few words too low to be heard, before hurrying into the carriage. It was nearly twelve. Returning to Marianne's chamber, she awaited the apothecary and watched over her sister through the remainder of the night. It was a night of almost equal torment for both: hours passed in sleepless agony and delirium for Marianne, and crushing anxiety for Elinor, before Dr. Harris's arrival. Once her fears were raised, they paid for all her prior security with excessive intensity. The servant who sat with her, refusing to wake Mrs. Jennings, only tormented her further with hints of what her mistress had always feared.

Marianne's thoughts remained incoherently fixed upon her mother. Every mention of her name caused a fresh pang to Elinor's heart, who reproached herself for trifling with so many days of illness and dreaded that relief might prove futile, fearing everything had been delayed too long. She pictured her suffering mother arriving too late to see this beloved child or to find her sane.

Just as Elinor considered summoning Dr. Harris again, or seeking further advice if he could not come, the physician appeared—but not until past five. His opinion, however, offered some consolation for his tardiness. While acknowledging a very unexpected and unpleasant alteration in his patient, he refused to deem the danger material, speaking confidently of the relief a fresh mode of treatment would bring, communicating a measure of this confidence to Elinor. He promised to return in three or four hours, leaving both the patient and her anxious attendant somewhat more composed than he had found them.

Mrs. Jennings, with profound concern and many self-reproaches for not being summoned sooner, learned in the morning of the events. Her former apprehensions, now restored with greater reason, left her no doubt of the impending tragedy. Although she attempted to speak comfort to Elinor, her conviction of Marianne's peril prevented her from offering false hope. Her heart truly grieved.
