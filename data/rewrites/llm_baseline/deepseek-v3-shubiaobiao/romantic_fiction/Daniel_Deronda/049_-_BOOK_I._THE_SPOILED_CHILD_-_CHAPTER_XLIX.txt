A noble heart knows no bitterness where gratitude should dwell. It is the mark of a soul that walks in wider light, capable of deeper joys—not those petty minds that delight in magnifying faults until all goodness crumbles to dust. Such creatures are the devil’s own monks, sworn to poverty in love and trust, yet forever begging scraps of kindness from a world that still holds some humanity.

When Deronda left Gwendolen that day, he had carefully avoided saying, *I will not see you again for some time—I am leaving.* He would not give Grandcourt the satisfaction of thinking his departure mattered to her. Yet as he walked to fulfill his promise of a final visit, his heart was heavy with the weight of what lay ahead. Sir Hugo’s summons had come like a thunderclap—*Come at once. Something has happened.* The words had filled him with dread, but when he stood at last in the baronet’s study, he found not distress, but a quiet, solemn affection in the man’s gaze.

“Nothing that will grieve you, sir?” Deronda asked, more a hope than a question, as he clasped the offered hand.

Sir Hugo’s voice was uncommonly soft, his expression grave. “No, Dan. Sit down. I have something to tell you.”

Deronda obeyed, a silent foreboding tightening his chest. It was rare for Sir Hugo to wear his heart so plainly.

“Not grief, my boy—not for me. Though I don’t know how it will be for you.” The baronet hesitated, weighing his words. “I never prepared you for this. There were reasons… reasons I never spoke of your parentage. But I hoped, in keeping silent, I did not wound you.”

Deronda could not answer. Even if he had trusted his voice to say, *I was never wounded*, it would not have been the whole truth. Too much of his life—too many unanswered yearnings—hung upon this moment.

Sir Hugo studied the young man’s face, the pallor of it, the pressed lips holding back some old sorrow. His own voice grew more tender. “I acted on your mother’s wishes. The secrecy was hers. But now… now she wishes to end it. She wants to see you.” He held out a letter, foreign paper, unfamiliar handwriting. “This will tell you what she asks of you—where to find her.”

Deronda took it, slipping it into his pocket with a fleeting relief that he need not read it yet. His hands were steady, but his soul trembled.

Sir Hugo, watching him, felt his own composure waver. The air between them hummed with unspoken thoughts. And then, at last, Deronda lifted his eyes—not with accusation, but with a reverence that feared its own question.

“Does my father live?”

The answer came swift and firm. *“No.”*

For a moment, neither joy nor sorrow could be untangled in Deronda’s heart. Some old shadow shifted; some new light fell across the past. Sir Hugo, too, seemed to see something newly revealed.

After a silence thick with unspoken reckoning, the baronet spoke again, low and contrite. “Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to take this upon myself. Perhaps I kept you too close—liked it too well, having you as my own. If I’ve caused you pain… I ask your forgiveness.”

Deronda shook his head. “There is nothing to forgive. The love I bear you has been the greater part of all my life. Any sorrow was never for myself—only for the one I never knew… until now.”

The words hung between them. Then, as if moved by the same impulse, the two men reached out, clasping hands in a silent understanding deeper than speech.
