Speech is the familiar vent of human thought; yet there are moments—simple, brutal, and too swift for any tongue—when the heart breaks cover not in words, but in sound. The voice becomes not speech but a cry, raw as a struck bell in a lonely field. In that instant a man is no scholar, no lover, no tradesman with a name to keep; he is a creature in flight, listening with his whole skin for the next footfall behind him, the next branch snapped, the next breath that is not his own.

So it was when the pursuers burst from the thicket upon the three fugitives. Their shout of triumph rolled out like a hunting-horn blown too close to the ear, a rough, tearing noise that made the birds lift in a black scatter and the leaves tremble. And the hunted answered it with a single sharp cry—not shaped by language, not Dutch nor Latin nor any mother-dialect a hearth might cherish, but pure terror made audible. Then there was nothing in the world but the drumming of feet on turf, the whip of grasses against shins, the harsh rasp of breath, and, under all, the mule’s quick, steady snort, warm and animal, as if the beast itself were a bellows keeping a small life-fire going.

Hands clawed at empty air—scarce two feet behind—so near that Gerard could almost feel the heat of their fingers at his back as they sprang. For a heartbeat the men were checked, baffled like lions that have struck at a shadow; and in that breath’s-space Gerard and Martin and the mule stole ten yards as cleanly as if some invisible hand had pushed them forward.

Then the hunters flew after them again with uplifted weapons. They had measured speed before; they were sure of their prey. In the open ground that morning they had gained on the three; and now, at last, it was a fair race, to be settled by legs and lungs alone. A hundred yards vanished beneath them as if the earth were being rolled backwards. Yet those ten yards held—then held more.

It puzzled Dierich Brower, whose mind, for all its violence, could reckon simple things. Why did the distance not lessen? Why did it, by such faint degrees he could not at first believe, begin to widen?

The reason lay in the plain arithmetic of bodies. When three run together, the pace is the pace of the slowest. From Peter’s house to the edge of the forest Gerard had matched himself to Margaret’s gait, as one tempers a fire so it does not scorch the bread; but now he ran his own true pace. The mule was fleet, a creature made for roads and burdens, and it could have left them all behind as a cart-horse leaves children at play. And besides—youth, clean blood, sober living, all the small daily choices that seem as homely as kneading dough and yet make a man’s sinews honest—began to tell. It was as if the very air in his chest tasted clearer now, resinous with pine, damp with leaf-mould, and he drank it greedily. Each breath came with a scent: crushed fern, bruised nettle, the cold iron tang of stream-water somewhere under the roots. The world was not an abstraction; it was a mouthful of forest, and he swallowed it and ran.

Daylight—imperceptible at first—grew between the hunted and the hunters.

Dierich made a desperate effort, a long lunge that brought him two yards nearer, the sound of his boots suddenly loud as a mallet on a board. But in a few seconds Gerard stole those yards back again without seeming to alter anything, as quietly as a careful cook slides a pan away from flame before it spits.

The pursuers began to curse. Their oaths cracked through the wood and fell wrong upon it, like bad music in a holy place. Martin heard, and his face, drawn tight with fear a moment before, lit as if a candle had been shielded and then uncovered. “Courage, Gerard! courage, brave lad! they are straggling.”

It was true. The pack began to break. One of the men edged ahead of Dierich; another dropped behind altogether, his breath now a loud, ragged sawing that made him seem less a hunter than a broken bellows. Even the forest seemed to listen and judge them by their sound: the young ran silently, the old announced their coming with every gasp and stumble.

They came to rising ground—not sharp, but long—and there grit and youth and temperate living told more than ever. Before he reached the top, Dierich’s forty years weighed on him like forty bullets. His chest burned; his mouth tasted of sour sweat, as if he had chewed bitter herbs. He tried to call out a command and could only force it out in a strangled groan.

“Our cake is dough,” he gasped at last—an image dragged up from some remembered kitchen where batter had failed and hope had sunk with it. “Take him dead, if you can’t alive.” And he left running, sinking to a foot’s pace, stubbornly walking as if he could trample down the hill by mere will.

Jorian Ketel tailed off next; then another; and so, one by one, Gerard ran them all to a standstill—save one who kept on staunch as a bloodhound, though losing ground every minute. His name, if I am not mistaken, was Eric Wouverman. He ran with a grim economy, jaw clenched, the sound of his breath thin and measured, like a file at work; and though the distance grew, he did not break.

Followed by him alone, the three fugitives came to another rise in the wood—shorter but much steeper than the last. The trees stood close; the air was cooler; and the ground, where roots broke through the earth like knuckles, demanded nimbleness. Martin, who had eyes for the small aids of the world, cried out over the panting, “Hand on mane!”

Gerard obeyed. His fingers sank into the mule’s coarse hair—warm, living, salted with sweat—and he let the beast lend him its climbing strength. The mule heaved up the slope with a sure-footed urgency; its hooves struck the earth in quick, hard notes, and Gerard, half-running, half-drawn, found himself moving faster than before. It was a simple trick, like using a spoon to lift a delicate wafer from a hot stone: small knowledge that saves much loss.

At the sight of this manoeuvre Eric Wouverman’s heart faltered. He was now full eighty yards behind Gerard—and, worse for him, rather more than that ahead of his nearest comrades. Alone between prey and help, he felt suddenly the silence behind him where the pack ought to be. The wood seemed to widen; the space filled with nothing but his own breath and the pounding in his ears. He pulled up short.

Obeying Dierich’s brutal order, he took down his crossbow. The action had its own calm, like a cook pausing in the midst of panic to sharpen a knife—deliberate, practised, almost domestic in its routine. He set his feet, raised the weapon, levelled it carefully. The string creaked; the bolt lay ready, a small straight thing of wood and iron with death in its point. As the trio were sinking out of sight over the crest of the hill, he loosed.

The bolt went whizzing among them with a sound like a sudden wasp in the ear—thin, vicious, impossible to reason with.

There was a cry of dismay; and next moment, as if a thunderbolt had fallen on them, they were all lying on the ground—mule and all.
