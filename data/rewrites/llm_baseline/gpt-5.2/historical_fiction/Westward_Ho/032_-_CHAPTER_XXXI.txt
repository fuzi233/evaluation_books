And now began that long sea-fight, twelve days of iron weather and salt smoke, in which men’s souls, like their victual, were tried by fire; and it was to decide, not only who should command the Narrow Seas, but what tongue should be spoken, and what prayers should be said, from half Europe to the unmeasured coasts beyond the Atlantic.

The first gun was a proclamation. Lord Howard sent ahead the little pinnace called the Defiance, and she, like a sharp-tongued herald, denounced war with the crack and cough of her ordnance. Then the Ark Royal—some called her the Triumph—came in, closing to musket-shot, her broadside answering the Spaniards’ in a steady thunder, and fell upon what was taken for the Spanish admiral, though it proved to be Alfonso de Leon’s ship. Drake and Hawkins and Frobisher played on the hindmost squadron under Recalde; and soon the Spaniards learned what nimbleness meant when it was built of English oak and handled by men who had grown up with tide-rips in their ears.

Medina Sidonia drew his ships into a half-moon, striving to go forward in stately order, as if sheer bulk and sanctity could thrust aside the smaller craft that ringed him. The Armada moved like a herd with consecrated banners—solemn, stubborn, and blind to the quick teeth at its flank. But those “wolves” were hunters, and they had wind for a horse and powder for a spur. In the first short hours, pride leaked away from many a Spanish deck like wine from a split cask.

One of the great galliasses was already riddled; shot had splintered the trim “pulpits” and chapels set upon her, and the friars who had come to bless and boast were made suddenly mortal in a spray of oak and cordage. The fleet must close round her, or Drake and Hawkins would sink her outright. In the confusion the “principal galleon of Seville,” with Pedro de Valdez and a crowd of blue-blooded Dons, ran foul of her neighbor, carried away her foremast, and—Spanish chivalry notwithstanding—was left behind, wallowing helpless, the sea already beginning to take possession of her.

It did not look like victory. Yet the Spaniards still had Our Lady, and saints in plenty, and the dreadful paper-bull that thundered doom; and it was held blasphemy to doubt that Heaven was a Spaniard. Meanwhile, if this was their showing against a third of the English fleet, what would they do when the belated ships whitening the blue between the Mewstone and the fight came up to add their mouths of fire?

So ended the first day. Hardly an English ship, hardly a man, was hurt. Something broke in English minds that day—the old spell of Spain’s invincibleness—and it did not mend again. Lord Howard’s running fight, urged by Raleigh and Drake, was justified to the last splinter; for had they “clapped ships together without consideration,” the lesser and quicker must have been crushed under sheer weight of men.

Amyas Leigh, with the Bideford ships, had been busy for two hours knocking holes in a huge galleon which bore, high on her poop, the painted figure of a maiden with a wheel, and on her sprit a coat of arms that was shot away early. She was called the Sta. Catharina. Whether those arms had been De Soto’s or no Amyas could not tell; but he did not need heraldry to fix his hate. When at last the admiral’s signal called him off, he went to his hammock and slept as soundly as a boy, his body well spent with honest labour, his mind full of a darker appetite.

But before he had been an hour below, Cary came down and shook him up.

“We were to follow Drake’s lantern, Amyas; but where it is, I can’t see—unless he’s been taken up aloft among the stars for a new Drakium Sidus.”

Amyas turned out grumbling. There was no lantern to be seen—only, far off, a sudden explosion and a great fire on board some Spaniard, that flared like a kitchen chimney when fat catches, and then was gradually got under. All that night they lay-to with the fleet, the sea full of creaking yards and the low complaining of cordage, and, underneath, the eternal hush of the deep listening.

Next morning found them off Torbay. A pinnace came alongside, and brought a letter from Drake. It ran, with spelling as free as the man’s fortune, somewhat thus:

“DEAR LAD,—I have been wool-gathering all night after five great hulks, which the Pixies transfigured overnight into galleons, and this morning again into German merchantmen. I let them go with my blessing; and coming back, fell in (God be thanked!) with Valdez’ great galleon; and in it good booty, which the Dons his fellows had left behind, like faithful and valiant comrades, and the Lord Howard had let slip past him, thinking her deserted by her crew. I have sent to Dartmouth a sight of noblemen and gentlemen, maybe a half-hundred; and Valdez himself, who when I sent my pinnace aboard must needs stand on his punctilios, and propound conditions. I answered him, I had no time to tell with him; if he would needs die, then I was the very man for him; if he would live, then, buena querra. He sends again, boasting that he was Don Pedro Valdez, and that it stood not with his honor, and that of the Dons in his company. I replied, that for my part, I was Francis Drake, and my matches burning. Whereon he finds in my name salve for the wounds of his own, and comes aboard, kissing my fist, with Spanish lies of holding himself fortunate that he had fallen into the hands of fortunate Drake, and much more, which he might have kept to cool his porridge. But I have much news from him (for he is a leaky tub); and among others, this, that your Don Guzman is aboard of the Sta. Catharina, commandant of her soldiery, and has his arms flying at her sprit, beside Sta. Catharina at the poop, which is a maiden with a wheel, and is a lofty built ship of 3 tier of ordnance, from which God preserve you, and send you like luck with.

“Your deare Friend and Admirall,

“F. Drake.

“She sails in this squadron of Recalde. The Armada was minded to smoke us out of Plymouth; and God’s grace it was they tried not: but their orders from home are too strait, and so the slaves fight like a bull in a tether, no farther than their rope, finding thus the devil a hard master, as do most in the end. They cannot compass our quick handling and tacking, and take us for very witches. So far so good, and better to come. You and I know the length of their foot of old. Time and light will kill any hare, and they will find it a long way from Start to Dunkirk.”

“The admiral is in a gracious humour, Leigh, to have vouchsafed you so long a letter,” Cary said, eyeing Amyas, who read the lines about Don Guzman twice, as a man tastes a bitter herb to be certain of its sting.

“St. Catherine! Why, that was the galleon we hammered all yesterday!” Amyas stamped, as if the deck itself had cheated him.

“Of course it was. We shall find her again,” Cary answered. “That cunning old Drake—how he has contrived to line his own pockets, even while keeping the whole fleet waiting.”

“He has given the lord high admiral the dor,” Amyas growled.

“Lord Howard is too high-hearted to stop and plunder, Papist though he is.”

Amyas answered with a sound that was not assent. He worshipped Drake; and for Papists he had little justice.

They did not find Lord Howard till nightfall. He and Lord Sheffield had held on the whole night after the Spanish lanterns with two ships only; and whatever men had muttered before, there could be no doubting now the loyalty of English Roman Catholics, for the Howards fought as if to wash suspicion away in blood. A great Spaniard drifted by, deserted and partly burnt, smelling of scorched pitch and wet ash. Some of Amyas’s men looked at her as at a larder thrown open by Providence; but Amyas refused.

“I have come out to fight, and not to plunder. Let the nearest ship take her luck without my grudging.”

They passed on. The men made long faces when they saw the next neighbour snap her up and tow her toward Weymouth; and she proved to be Miguel d’Oquenda’s, the vice-admiral’s, which they had seen the night before all but blown up by a desperate Netherland gunner, who, being “misused,” had chosen to pay old scores in a flash and roar.

So ended the second day, and Portland rose clearer each hour.

The next morning they were off the island. Would the Spaniards try Portsmouth, though they had spared Plymouth? The wind shifted to the north and blew clear and cool off Weymouth Bay, smelling of chalk and short grass, and—between the gun-smokes—carrying plainly the cries of gulls and the thin piping of shore-birds. The Spaniards turned and faced the English, meaning to stand off and on till the wind changed and then to try for the Needles. At least, they should have work to do before rounding Purbeck.

The English went westward—only to come back on the opposite tack; and then began the chess-play for the weather-gage. It did not last long. Before noon the English slipped close-hauled between the Armada and the land, and came down upon them right before the wind.

Now began a fight most fierce and fell. It went confusedly, fortunes changing with every shift of sail and heave of sea. English ships rescued the ships of London when Spaniards hemmed them in; Spaniards delivered Recalde when he was hard pressed. The Spaniards’ shot flew for the most part over the English without harm, while English gunnery, leveled and deliberate, tore into towering sides as a cook’s knife lays open a great fish, straight to the bone. One Cock, an Englishman—Devon claims him—died with honour in the midst of enemies in his small ship, swallowed by the smoke as by a sudden night.

Lord Howard fought amid the enemy’s fleet; and seeing Fenner far off, cried out, “O George, what doest thou? Wilt thou now frustrate my hope and opinion conceived of thee? Wilt thou forsake me now?” The words stung like vinegar in a wound. Fenner came in and did the part of a most valiant captain; as did all, for shame and love of England are sharp seasonings, and make poor fare rich.

Night fell on that floating volcano. Morning found them far past Purbeck, the white peak of Freshwater ahead. Now, from all havens, flocked the chivalry of England in vessels fitted at their own cost, as to a set field where immortal fame might be won—Oxford, Northumberland, Cumberland, Pallavicini, Brooke, Carew, Raleigh, Blunt, and many another name that had been spoken by hearths and in halls. Spain too had staked her noble houses: not a great family of Arragon or Castile but had lent a brother or a son—and would have cause to mourn.

Lord Howard sent light craft into Portsmouth for ammunition; but the wind fell dead, and all the evening both fleets drifted helpless with the tide and shouted idle defiance, trumpet answering trumpet, fife and drum calling across the water. Those sounds mattered. A sea without wind becomes a great ear; every beat of a drum goes farther than it should, and the nerves of men tighten to it. The Spaniards’ music, bright with bells and measured chants, tried to make a market of devotion; the English answered with their own rougher cries and the hard clink of tackle, as if the very ships refused to be lulled.

The sun went down on a glassy sea and rose on a glassy sea again. It was the twenty-fifth—St. James’s-day, sacred to Spain’s patron. Would nothing be attempted in his honour? The galleys should attack; and in their van three of the great galliasses thrashed the sea to foam with three hundred oars apiece. If any saint rode before them, he rode in the mind, not on the water.

Out were towed Lord Howard’s Triumph, his brother’s Lion, Southwell’s Elizabeth Jonas, Lord Sheffield’s Bear, Barker’s Victory, and Fenner’s Leicester, to meet them; and they greeted the galliasses with chain-shot that smashed oars and cut rigging till those proud engines of Rome’s confidence became crippled creatures, wallowing and helpless. Had not the wind sprung up again toward noon and the Spanish fleet come up to rescue them, they had shared the fate of Valdez and the Biscayan.

Then the fight became general. Frobisher beat down the Spanish admiral’s mainmast. Mexia and Recalde fell on him, and Lord Howard rescued him; and Howard, endangered in turn, was rescued in turn, while the afternoon thundered under Freshwater’s virgin cliffs. Sea-fowl rose screaming from every ledge, dark wings fretting the chalk; a lone shepherd hurried down the slopes, forgetting the wheatear fluttering in his snare, to peer over the dizzy edge. What he heard first was not the sight but the sound: a long rolling concussion that turned the air into a drum, and then the smaller, crueler noises—splintering wood, shouts snatched away by wind, the hiss of shot. Smoke lay in a league-broad veil, sulphurous and greasy; through it, masts and flags pierced and vanished like thoughts men cannot hold.

So fared St. James’s-day, as Baal’s did on Carmel. If the saint answered, it was only with English cannon. The Armada gathered itself into a roundel and would fight no more, but made the best of its way to Calais, where perhaps French help might be ready, and then to Dunkirk to join Parma and the Netherlands flotilla.

A fair Etesian gale followed clear out of the south-southwest. Past Brighton cliffs and Beachy Head, Hastings and Dungeness, the two great fleets glided. Was it battle or triumph? For Lord Howard, by sea, seemed almost to reward rather than to fight; and after Lord Thomas Howard, Lord Sheffield, Townsend, and Frobisher had received knighthood, old Admiral Hawkins knelt and rose Sir John, and shook his shoulders after the accolade, saying the “old woman will hardly know herself again, when folks call her My Lady.”

Along the shore the cliffs were lined with pike-men and musketeers, and with every countryman and groom who could bear arms, led by squires and sheriffs, marching eastward toward Dover. Nor were they alone. From miles inland came women and children and aged folk in wagons, to join their feeble shouts—and their prayers, which were not feeble—to that great cry of faith and fear that rose from Britain’s spectators.

Let them pray on. The danger was not over. Howard had news from Newhaven that the Guises would not stir; and Seymour and Winter had left their watch on the Flemish shore to swell the fleet to a hundred and forty sail—slightly more than the Spaniards in number, but not half the tonnage nor a third the men. The Spaniards were battered and dispirited, but not broken. When they slid to anchor in Calais Roads on the Saturday evening of that memorable week, prudent men knew England’s hour had come, and that the bells which would call all Christendom to church upon the morrow would sound either death-knell or triumph.

Solemn was that Sabbath, in town and country. Many a light-hearted coward who had scoffed at the Armada because he dared not face the thought gave himself now to abject fear, seeing and hearing what he would not believe. Many a brave man, kneeling beside wife and daughters, felt his heart sink to the pavement at the thought of what those beloved might endure from a profligate and fanatical soldiery, or from the cold, patient cruelty of the Inquisition. St. Bartholomew’s, Smithfield’s fires, the immolation of the Moors, the extermination of West Indians—these were not dim histories then, but recent wounds, still raw in memory. Men knew what language might be forced into their mouths, what prayers might be forbidden them, what names might be erased, as if a nation could be silenced like a parish whose old words are outlawed by a conqueror’s lexicon.

And none knew it better than she who guided that devoted land, the especial mark of the invaders’ fury; and who, by what men then not unwisely held for a Divine inspiration, devised the daring stroke that would anticipate the coming blow.

But where was Amyas Leigh, in all this great hazard and glory?

Day after day he sought the Sta. Catharina in the thickest press, and could not come at her, could not even hear of her. One moment he dreaded she had sunk by night and balked him of his prey; the next, that she had repaired her damages and would escape him after all. He grew moody, discontented, restless—peevish even with his men, for the first time in his life. He could speak of nothing but Don Guzman. At every spare moment he took his sword from the sheath and handled it—fondling it, talking to it, bidding it not fail him in the day of vengeance, as if steel could understand the dialect of a man’s wrath when the man himself scarcely could.

At last he sent to Squire the armorer for a whetstone, and, half-ashamed of his own folly, whetted and polished the blade in bye-corners. The stone rasped with a dry, hungry sound; the rhythm of it, back and forth, became his private litany, harsher than any chant from the Spanish fleet. He muttered to himself, words that were scarcely words—more like the broken syllables of hate, the one tongue he would not lose. That fixed thought of selfish vengeance possessed his whole mind. He forgot England’s need, her triumph, his own safety—everything but his brother’s blood.

And yet this was the day for which he had been longing since he brought home the magic horn as a boy of fifteen: the day when he should stand face to face with an invader, and that invader, as he believed, Antichrist himself. He had held for years with Drake and Hawkins and Grenville and Raleigh that he was sent into the world to fight the Spaniard; and he was fighting now, for such a stake as he would never see again—and yet he was not content.

While throughout the fleet whole crews received the Communion side by side and rose with cheerful faces to shake hands, rejoicing that they were sharers in Britain’s Salamis, Amyas turned away from the holy elements.

“I cannot communicate, Sir John. Charity with all men? I hate, if ever man hated on earth.”

“You hate the Lord’s foes only, Captain Leigh.”

“No, Jack. I hate my own as well.”

“But no one in the fleet, sir?”

“Don’t try to put me off with the same Jesuit’s quibble which that false knave Parson Fletcher invented for one of Doughty’s men, to drug his conscience when he was plotting against his own admiral. No, Jack. I hate one of whom you know; and somehow that hatred keeps me from loving any human being. I am in love and charity with no man, Sir John Brimblecombe—not even with you. Go your ways in God’s name, sir, and leave me and the devil alone together, or you’ll find my words are true.”

Jack departed with a sigh. While the crew received the Communion on deck—bread broken, wine passed, the sea-wind carrying the low responses—Amyas sat below in the cabin sharpening his sword. The smell down there was of oil and steel and the closed warmth of timber, as different from the open, briny air above as a pantry is from a field. When he had done, he called for a boat and went aboard Drake’s ship to ask news of the Sta. Catharina. Across the water came the Spanish chants and the tinkling bells, neat and insistent, as if holiness could be made by sound alone and sold by the measure. Amyas listened scowling, his jaw set as tight as a clenched fist.

At last Drake was summoned by the lord admiral and returned with a secret commission that should bear fruit that night. Amyas, who had gone with him, helped him till nightfall; and then returned to his own ship as Sir Amyas Leigh, Knight, to the joy and glory of every soul aboard—save his moody self, who felt the new title sit upon him like a fresh chain.

So all that livelong summer Sabbath, before the little high-walled town and the long range of yellow sandhills, lay those two mighty armaments, scowling at each other, hardly out of gunshot. Messenger after messenger hurried toward Bruges to the Duke of Parma, begging for light craft that might follow these nimble English better than Spain’s floating castles, and, above all, entreating him to put to sea at once with all his force.

But the duke was not at Dunkirk. He was inland, on the future field of Waterloo, paying devotions to St. Mary of Halle in Hainault, to make all sure in his Pantheon; and already he saw, in visions of the night, Cardinal Allen placing England’s crown upon his head. He returned for answer, first, that his victual was not ready; next, that his Dutch sailors—kept many weeks at their posts by sword-point—had run away like water; and thirdly, that besides all, he could not come, so “strangely provided” were those five-and-thirty Dutch ships with great ordnance and musketeers, watching the entrance of Nieuwport and Dunkirk like terriers at a rat-hole.

Having ensured, as he thought, the private patronage of St. Mary of Halle, he would return on the morrow to make experience of its effects. But he heard, across the flats of Dixmude, the far thunder of fleets; and at Dunkirk he met the open curses of his officers. For while he had been praying and nothing more, the English had been praying—and something more; and all that was left for the Prince of Parma was to hang a few purveyors as peace-offerings to his sulking army, and then to chafe, as Drake said of him, “like a bear robbed of her whelps.”
