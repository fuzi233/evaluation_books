As the rain pattered against the windows of their modest London lodgings, Newland Archer gazed at his wife, May, with a gentle smile. "Of course we must dine with Mrs. Carfry, dearest," he said, and May's eyes clouded with a mixture of anxiety and trepidation.

Their lives in London were a quiet, measured affair, a refuge from the vibrant chaos of the city outside. In a foreign land, they had adhered to a strict code of reserve, eschewing any unnecessary connections with strangers. It was a principle they had learned from their upbringing in New York, where it was considered undignified to force oneself on the acquaintance of foreigners. May and Newland had taken this notion to heart, cultivating an aura of detachment that bordered on haughtiness.

Their rare interactions with fellow travelers were limited to perfunctory exchanges in hotel lobbies and railway stations. Among their compatriots, they were even more reticent, treating anyone beyond their inner circle with a certain disdain. Their months abroad were often spent in companionable silence, punctuated only by the occasional encounter with familiar faces – the Chivers, the Dagonets, or the Mingotts.

However, life had a way of defying careful planning. During their stay in Botzen, one evening, a knock on their door had interrupted their quiet routine. A neighbor, Mrs. Carfry, had been stricken with bronchitis, and her sister, Miss Harle, had sought out May's expertise in medicine. May, ever prepared, had obliged with a bottle of liniment, and the two women had been forever changed by the kindness.

Though May and Newland had long since parted ways with Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle, they had remained in each other's thoughts. The Englishwomen, oblivious to the strictures of propriety, had seen their gestures as a testament to an enduring bond. They had tracked the Archers' travels, always managing to coincide with their paths, and in doing so, had forged a lasting connection.

Now, as they sat at their breakfast table, May's brow furrowed in concern. "It's all very well for you, Newland; you know them. But I shall feel so shy among a lot of people I've never met.
