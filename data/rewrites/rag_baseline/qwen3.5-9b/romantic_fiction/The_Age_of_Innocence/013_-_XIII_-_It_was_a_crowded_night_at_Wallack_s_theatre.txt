The air inside Wallack's Theatre was thick with the humidity of a hundred bodies and the scent of cheap perfume, a dense fog that swallowed the distinction between the gallery's fervor and the box's polite amusement. The play, *The Shaughraun*, ran on a predictable rhythm, a mechanical repetition of hackneyed sentiments and slapstick situations that the audience consumed with varying degrees of hunger. In the front rows, smiles were reserved for the obvious traps; in the rear, the applause was unreserved. Yet, amidst this manufactured chaos, there existed a single, silent axis around which the entire performance revolved.

Harry Montague parted from Miss Dyas. The scene was stripped of ornamentation: a monosyllabic exchange, a turning away, a retreat. Miss Dyas stood by the mantelpiece, her gray cashmere dress molding to a tall, severe frame, devoid of fashionable loops or trimmings. A narrow black velvet ribbon hung from her neck, its ends trailing down her spine. When Montague turned to leave, she did not follow. Instead, she leaned her arms against the stone shelf, burying her face in her hands. Montague paused on the threshold, stealing a glance back. He reached out, touched the velvet ribbon, pressed a kiss to the fabric, and vanished into the darkness without a sound. The curtain fell on this mute tragedy.

Newland Archer watched the exit, his chest tight with a retrospective ache. He had come specifically for this moment, finding the reticent sorrow of Montague and Dyas superior to the histrionic outpourings of Croisette and Bressant in Paris or Madge Robertson and Kendal in London. Tonight, however, the scene carried a weight beyond the script. It mirrored, with uncanny precision, his own departure from Madame Olenska a week prior.

There was no resemblance in the actors. Archer possessed none of the young Englishman's romantic features; Miss Dyas was a monument of red hair and pale, pleasant ugliness, utterly unlike the vivid, startling countenance of Ellen Olenska. Nor was there similarity in the roles. Archer and Olenska were not lovers parting in heartbreak; they were a lawyer and a client, separated after a conversation that had cast the darkest shadow over her legal prospects.

So where lay the connection that made his heart hammer against his ribs? It resided solely in Olenska's capacity to suggest tragedy where none existed. She had rarely spoken of it, yet she radiated a quality of inherent drama, a passion that seemed to belong to her very bones. Archer believed that fate was not a matter of chance, but of innate tendency—the pull of circumstances toward certain souls. Olenska was that soul. She was the type of person to whom disaster was inevitable, regardless of her efforts to shrink from it. Her survival in an atmosphere so saturated with violence suggested she had been plucked from a maelstrom, her calm demeanor serving as proof of the storm she had endured.

He remembered the accusation of Count Olenski, the "secretary" who had facilitated her escape. In the eyes of the law and the world, her gratitude to her rescuer equated her to her abominable husband. Archer had forced her to understand this cruel equivalence, to see that New York's superficial charity offered her no refuge. Witnessing her resigned acceptance of this judgment had been a torture of pity and jealousy, a strange mixture of emotions that placed her at his mercy. He had taken it upon himself to reassure her family that she had abandoned her pursuit of divorce, citing the futility of the endeavor. The relief that followed was palpable; Mrs. Welland praised his management, and Old Mrs. Mingott, summoning him for a private conference, had chided the girl for her delusions, calling her a silly goose for imagining she could pass as an unmarried spinster.

These memories flooded back as the curtain dropped. Archer's eyes stung with tears. He rose to leave, turning his head toward the wings and spotting the source of his thoughts seated in a box with the Beauforts, Lawrence Lefferts, and others. He had avoided her since their last meeting, trying to maintain a distance that felt increasingly impossible. Now, their eyes met. Mrs. Beaufort recognized him instantly, offering a languid, inviting gesture. There was no choice but to proceed.

Lefferts and Beaufort stepped aside. After exchanging a few perfunctory words with Mrs. Beaufort, who preferred the artifice of beauty to the labor of speech, Archer settled behind Madame Olenska. The only other occupant was Mr. Sillerton Jackson, whispering confidentially to Mrs. Beaufort about the dancing at Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's recent Sunday reception. Behind the veil of this trivial gossip, Olenska turned.

"Do you think," she asked softly, her gaze drifting toward the darkened stage, "he will send her a bunch of yellow roses tomorrow morning?"

Archer flushed, a sudden heat rising in his cheeks. He had visited her twice since their separation, sending a box of yellow roses each time, accompanied by no card. She had never acknowledged the sender, assuming perhaps she had never considered him the giver. Now, linking the flowers to the silent farewell on stage, she revealed a sudden, intimate awareness that startled him. A surge of agitated pleasure washed through him.

"I was thinking of that too," he admitted, his voice betraying him. "I was planning to leave the theatre to take the picture away with me."

To his surprise, her color deepened, a reluctant, dusky flush spreading across her face. She looked down at the mother-of-pearl opera glass in her gloved hands. "What do you do while May is away?"

"I stick to my work," he replied, faintly irritated by the intrusion of the question.

The Wellands had departed for St. Augustine the previous week, adhering to a rigid family tradition born of Mr. Welland's bronchial susceptibility. The mild, opinionless patriarch required the constant presence of his wife and daughter to maintain his sanity; without them, he would lose track of his hairbrushes and fail to procure stamps. The family adored him, and his sons, bound by their own professional calendars, joined the pilgrimage for Easter. For Archer, discussing the necessity of May's attendance was impossible. The Mingott physician's reputation relied on the miracle of Mr. Welland's survival, rendering the doctor's insistence on the southern trip inflexible. Archer, despite his desire to spend weeks of sunshine with his betrothed, was shackled by convention. To request a holiday in mid-winter would be branded frivolity by the entire Mingott clan. Thus, he accepted May's departure with the stoic resignation he knew was required of a man preparing for marriage.

He was acutely aware that Olenska watched him, her eyelids lowered.

"I have done what you wished," she said abruptly. "What you advised."

"Ah—I am glad," he returned, embarrassed by the timing of her confession.

"I understand—that you were right," she continued, her breath catching slightly. "But sometimes life is difficult... perplexing..."

"I know."

"And I wanted to tell you that I *do* feel you were right; and that I'm grateful to you," she concluded, raising the opera glass to her eyes just as the box door swung open. Beaufort's resonant voice cut through the silence.

Archer stood, bowing slightly, and exited the box, stepping out into the cool night air of the theatre lobby. Only the day before, he had received a letter from May Welland, written with her characteristic candor. She had asked him to "be kind to Ellen" in their absence.

"She likes you and admires you so much—and you know, though she doesn't show it, she's still very lonely and unhappy," May had written. "I don't think Granny understands her, or Uncle Lovell Mingott either; they really think she's much worldlier and fonder of society than she is. And I can quite see that New York must seem dull to her, though the family won't admit it. I think she's been used to lots of things we haven't got; wonderful music, and picture shows, and celebrities—artists and authors and all the clever people you admire. Granny can't understand her wanting anything but lots of dinners and clothes—but I can see that you're almost the only person in New York who can talk to her about what she really cares for."

His wise May. How he had cherished that letter. Yet, he had not acted on it. He was too occupied, and as an engaged man, he hesitated to play the conspicuous champion of Madame Olenska. He harbored a belief that she knew far better how to navigate her own isolation than the ingenuous May imagined. She had Beaufort at her feet, Mr. van der Luyden hovering like a protective deity, and a host of suitors, including Lawrence Lefferts, waiting in the shadows.

Still, whenever he saw her, or exchanged a word with her, the realization persisted, undeniable and sharp: May's innocence was, in its own way, a form of prophecy. Ellen Olenska was lonely. She was unhappy.
