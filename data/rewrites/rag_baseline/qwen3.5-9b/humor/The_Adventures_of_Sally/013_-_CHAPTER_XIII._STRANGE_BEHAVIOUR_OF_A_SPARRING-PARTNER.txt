The air in Sally's apartment tasted of dust and unresolved tension. Her emotions sat heavy in her chest, akin to the trembling anticipation of a swimmer standing on the edge of a freezing pool on a raw morning. She ached, yes, but beneath the pain lay a sharp, stimulating clarity. She had finally ceased wavering on the brink. For months she had shirked the fight, but today she stepped fully into the stream, ready for whatever currents awaited. It hurt, as she had anticipated, yet it was a vital ache, not the dull smothering of melancholy. She felt alive, defiant.

With packing complete and the room tidied, the next imperative was clear: she must see Ginger. A sudden, urgent need had taken root in her to find him. His stolid friendliness offered the only support she craved. Regret washed over her instantly; she should have cabled him to meet her at the docks. The journey there had been a descent into isolation. The echoing customs sheds had drained her valor, leaving her forlorn and exposed.

She checked her watch, startled by the early hour. She could intercept him at the office and force a lunch. Putting on her hat, she stepped out into the city.

New York, that restless engine of change, had not spared the outer office of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. during her absence. Upon arrival, she found a stranger where she had once found a familiar face. The replacement was a stripling, generously pimpled like his predecessor, yet utterly distinct in demeanor. Where the former had been cordial, this new guard was grim, his manner stern and suspicious. He peered at Sally with narrowed eyes, as if catching her in the act of stealing the office blotting-paper, before demanding her business with acerbity.

"I want Mr. Kemp," Sally said.

The boy scratched his cheek dourly with a ruler. No observer would have suspected, amidst his austere facade, that he had been attempting to balance the instrument on his chin, juggling simultaneously with a pair of paper-weights. Impervious to human frailty, he harbored a secret ambition to enter vaudeville.

"What name?" he asked, coldly.

"Nicholas," Sally replied. "I am Mr. Nicholas' sister."

Previously, this declaration had triggered disaster. Today, however, it struck the boy like a bullet. He convulsed, dropping the ruler. While he stooped to retrieve it, regaining his composure, a shift occurred. He had not cared for Sally's name; he had sought the name of the person she sought. Realizing he had stumbled onto a stroke of luck—a break in his tedious routine of balancing weights—he transformed instantly. The frown vanished, replaced by a smile so winning it seemed the sun breaking through a London fog.

"Will you take a seat, lady?" he asked, offering polished courtesy that extended to dusting her sleeve with his coat. "Fine morning, isn't it?"

"Thank you," Sally said. "Tell him I'm here."

"Mr. Nicholas is out, miss," the boy replied with feigned regret. "Back in New York, but gone out."

"I don't want Mr. Nicholas. I want Mr. Kemp."

"Mr. Kemp?"

"Yes, Mr. Kemp."

Sorrow, genuine and deep, etched lines across the boy's face.

"Don't know of anyone of that name around here," he apologized.

"But surely..." Sally hesitated. A grim foreboding seized her. "How long have you been here?"

"All day, ma'am," he answered with the detached manner of a Casablanca.

"I mean, how long have you been employed here?"

"Just over a month, miss."

"Hasn't Mr. Kemp been in the office all that time?"

"Name's new to me, lady. Does he look like anything? I mean, what's he look like?"

"He has very red hair."

"Never seen him in here," the boy stated. The truth landed coldly between them. Sally blamed herself for leaving. She should have known. Left to his own devices, Ginger had once again made a mess of things. This particular mess, however, seemed unprecedented; surely Fillmore would not dismiss someone under her special protection so lightly.

"Where is Mr. Nicholas?" she asked, realizing Fillmore was the only source of truth. "Did you say he was out?"

"Really out, miss," the boy confirmed with candid engagement. "Went off to White Plains in his automobile half-an-hour ago."

"White Plains? What for?"

The stripling surrendered completely to social chatter. Usually, he preferred solitude, resenting the intrusion of the outside world, for one who chose jugglery as a path must practice constantly. Yet, charmed by Sally's presence, he delighted in conversation.

"I guess what's happened is, he's gone up to take a look at Bugs Butler," he explained.

"Whose butler?" Sally asked, mystified.

The boy smiled tolerantly, aware that women rarely grasped the truly significant currents of life. He accepted this limitation without judgment.

"Bugs Butler is training up at White Plains, miss."

"Who is Bugs Butler?"

Something of his earlier bleakness returned. Sally's question touched a nerve.

"Ah!" he replied, shedding his deference. "Who is he! That's what the wise guys are all saying. Who has Bugs Butler ever licked?"

"I don't know," Sally admitted, sensing the weight of his gaze.

"Nor nobody else," the stripling declared vehemently. "A lot of stiffs out on the coast, that's all. Ginks nobody has ever heard of, except Cyclone Mullins, and it took that false alarm fifteen rounds to get a referee's decision over him. The boss would go and give him a chance against the champ, but I could have told him the legitimate contender was K-leg Binns. K-leg put Cyclone Mullins out in the fifth. Well," he continued, his voice rising in chafing indignation at human folly, "if anybody thinks Bugs Butler can last six rounds with Lew Lucas, I've two bucks right here in my vest pocket that says it ain't so."

Sally began to see daylight.

"Oh, Bugs—Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in this fight that my brother is interested in?"

"That's right. He's going up against the lightweight champ. Lew Lucas is the lightweight champ. He's a bird!"

"Yes?" Sally asked. The youth had a habit of cocking his head, expecting her to supply the missing link.

"Yes, sir!" the stripling emphasized. "Lew Lucas is a hot sketch. He used to live on the next street to me," he added as clinching proof of his hero's prowess. "I've seen his old mother as close as I am to you. Say, I seen her a hundred times. Is any stiff of a Bugs Butler going to lick a fellow like that?"

"It doesn't seem likely."

"You spoke it!" the lad cried crisply, striking unsuccessfully at a fly resting on the blotting-paper.

A pause stretched between them. Sally began to rise.

"And there's another thing," the boy interrupted, reluctant to end the discourse. "Can Bugs Butler make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?"

"It sounds awfully difficult."

"They say he's clever." The expert laughed, a dry, satirical sound. "Well, what's that going to get him? The poor fish can't punch a hole in a nut-sundae."

"You don't seem to like Mr. Butler."

"Oh, I've nothing against him," the boy said magnanimously. "I'm only saying he's no licence to be mixing it with Lew Lucas."

Sally stood. More pressing matters demanded her attention.

"How shall I find my brother when I get to White Plains?" she asked.

"Oh, anybody'll show you the way to the training-camp. If you hurry, there's a train you can make now."

"Thank you very much."

"You're welcome."

He held the door open with an old-world politeness, slightly rusted by disuse. Then, as if returning to business after a frivolous interlude, he picked up the paper-weights once more, placing the ruler with careful precision on his upturned chin.

Fillmore heaved a sigh of relief and began to sidle toward the exit. The room was vast, a hybrid of barn and gymnasium, with athletic apparatus hanging from the rafters. In the center, a wide, roped-off space surrounded by an expectant crowd served as the stage. In this commercial age, the privacy of a champion's training was a relic; if the public possessed fifty cents, they were entitled to gaze their fill. That afternoon, roughly forty plutocrats had assembled, though many, to the regret of manager Lester Burrowes, had failed to part with solid coin. Among them were newspaper representatives on the free list, eager to polish Bugs Butler's crude prognostications into quotes about his "superb condition," and artists poised to depict him in semi-nudity with feet comically oversized.

Fillmore's relief stemmed from Burrowes, a relentless conversationalist who had buttonholed him fifteen minutes prior, finally diverting his attention to a matter requiring personal handling. This left Fillmore free to slip away to the hotel for the meal he desperately needed.
