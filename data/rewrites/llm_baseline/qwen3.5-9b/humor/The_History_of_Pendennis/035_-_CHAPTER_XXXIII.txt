Captain Shandon, our incarcerated hero, delivered a prospectus with the crisp authority of a general addressing his troops. He proclaimed that the hour had finally arrived for the gentlemen of England to unite in defense of their "glorious order," which faced threats from every quarter: foreign revolutions, domestic radicalism, the cunning slander of mill-owners, and the foolish antagonism of the masses they had duped.

"The ancient monarchy was insulted by a rabble of ferocious republicans!" the Captain declaimed. "The Church was abandoned by jealous dissenters and eroded by stealthy infidels. Our noble institutions, the bedrock of our glory, and the title 'English Gentleman'—the proudest in the world—were left undefended, exposed to assault from men who worshipped nothing holy, knew no venerable history, and obeyed no law stronger than their leader's command to plunder."

Mr. Shandon paused for effect, then turned to Mr. Bungay, the publisher. "Consider the French kings," he noted wisely. "Saint Louis fell because his monarch distrusted his gentlemen. England survived its greatest foe precisely because the people trusted theirs. It was our gentlemen who drove the eagles back from the Don to the Garonne; it was a gentleman who shattered the lines at Trafalgar and swept the plains of Waterloo."

At the mention of Waterloo, Bungay nodded sagely, winking knowingly. Warrington, however, burst into laughter.

"Observe how our venerable friend Bungay is affected," Shandon whispered to Warrington, glancing up from his papers with a sly smile. "That is the true test. I've invoked the Duke of Wellington a hundred times, and the Duke never fails."

The Captain then confessed, with admirable candor, that until now, English gentlemen, confident in their rights and indifferent to critics, had delegated their political interests just as they managed their estates or legal affairs—to specialists. They had allowed their representation in the press to fall into the hands of professional proctors. "But that era is over," Shandon declared. "Gentlemen must become their own champions. Their enemies are brave, strong, numerous, and uncompromising. They must meet them in the field, not be defamed by hired scribblers in Grub Street publishing gazettes from Whitehall."

"That's a dig at Bacon's people, Mr. Bungay," Shandon added, turning to the publisher.

Bungay slammed his stick onto the floor. "Hang him! Pitch into him, Captain!" he shouted triumphantly. Turning to Warrington, he wagged his dull head vigorously. "For a slashing article, sir, there is no one like the Captain—nobody like him."

Shandon continued, revealing that certain gentlemen, whose names were withheld for "obvious reasons," intended to launch a new journal. "These men are proud of their order and eager to uphold it!" cried the Captain, waving his paper with a grin. "They are loyal to their Sovereign by faith and ancestry; they love their Church, where they wish their children to worship; they love their country and intend to preserve it as the gentlemen of England have made it: the greatest and freest in the world. As the names of some of them appear on the deed securing our liberties at Runnymede—"

"What's that?" asked Mr. Bungay, leaning forward.

"An ancestor of mine sealed it with his sword-hilt," Pen interjected gravely.

"It's the Habeas Corpus, Mr. Bungay," Warrington corrected. The publisher merely yawned, muttered "All right, I dare say," and urged, "Go on, Captain."

"At Runnymede," Shandon resumed, "they were ready to defend that freedom today with sword and pen, rallying around the old laws and liberties of England."

"Bravo!" cheered Warrington. Little Mary stood watching in wonder; her mother worked silently nearby, casting looks of fond admiration. "Come here, little Mary," Warrington said, patting her fair curls with his large hand. She shrank back from his rough affection, preferring to seek refuge at Pen's knee to play with his fine watch-chain. Pen was delighted; he was a soft-hearted soul, though he hid his gentleness beneath a shy, pompous exterior. Thus, she climbed onto his lap while her father continued reading the program.

"You were laughing," the Captain observed to Warrington, "about the 'obvious reasons.' Well, I'll show you what they are, ye unbelieving heathen. 'We have stated,' he continued, "'that we cannot reveal the names of the parties involved... We possess influential friends in both Houses of Parliament and allies in every diplomatic circle in Europe. Our sources of intelligence are so exclusive that no other London or European journal could possibly acquire them. Yet, we can assure you that the earliest news of English and Continental politics will be found exclusively in the columns of *The Pall Mall Gazette*."

He listed the intended subscribers: *The Statesman*, *The Capitalist*, *The Country Gentleman*, and *The Divine*. "Why should the Gentlemen of England be unrepresented in the Press?" he asked rhetorically. "The field-preacher has his journal, the radical free-thinker has his; why not the Gentlemen?"

With modesty bordering on arrogance, Shandon detailed the literary and fashionable departments of the new paper, promising they would be run by men of acknowledged reputation—men famous at the universities (which caused Pen to blush and chuckle involuntarily), known in the clubs, and belonging to the very society they described. He politely informed potential advertisers that *The Pall Mall Gazette* was not suitable for selling goods, then eloquenty called upon the nobility, the baronetage, the clergy, the bar, the matrons, and the daughters of England to rally to the good old cause. By the end, Bungay had woken from a second nap, nodded approvingly, and declared everything was perfect.

Once the reading concluded, the group discussed the paper's management. Bungay sat listening, nodding as if understanding complex matters, though his approval was based on simplicity. He believed the Captain could write the finest "smashing article" in England. To him, anything that destroyed the opposition house of Bacon qualified as smashing. Whether the Captain wrote a letter in the style of Junius or copied the Church Catechism, Bungay would be satisfied. Pocketing the manuscripts with immense satisfaction, he paid for them, called little Mary over, and gave her a penny before departing.

Afterward, Shandon, sporting a jaunty, fashionable air, entertained his guests with anecdotes of the "beau monde." Though he knew little of the great world, he had seen enough to mimic it convincingly. He spoke of current characters and great figures with easy familiarity, recounting private lives and social gatherings as if he lived among them daily. Pen found it amusing to hear the shabby prisoner in a tattered dressing gown discuss the elite so fluently. Mrs. Shandon listened with delight, believing every word. She didn't aspire to mix with high society herself, nor was she clever enough, but she cherished the idea that her Charles shone there. In fact, she still kept an invitation card from the Earl of X in her workbox.

Soon, Mr. Bungay grew tired of the chatter and rose to leave. Warrington and Pen followed, though Bungay lingered, hoping to revisit this interesting family. Shandon grinned ruefully, assuring him he was always at home and delighted to see Mr. Pennington.

"I'll see you to my park gate, gentlemen," Captain Shandon announced, grabbing his hat despite Mrs. Shandon's faint cry of "Charles." Leading the way through the dismal prison passages, he shuffled out in his shabby slippers, his hand already fiddling with his waistcoat pocket where Bungay's five-pound note rested. At the wicket, he bid farewell to the three gentlemen, including Pen, who breathed a sigh of relief to finally step onto the flags of Farringdon Street.

Left alone, Mrs. Shandon sadly resumed her work by the window overlooking the courtyard. She watched Shandon sprint toward the prison tavern with two men trailing behind. She had hoped to share a dinner of meat and salad with her husband and daughter that evening, but now there was no hope. He would spend the night drinking or playing cards elsewhere, returning late with glazed eyes and a staggered walk requiring her nursing. *Oh, what varieties of pain we inflict upon our women!* she thought.

She prepared tea instead. How many tears have flowed over that humble pot? How many sickbeds have warmed beside it? From lovers whispering secrets to widows finding solace, the tea-pot has witnessed it all. Finally, Mrs. Shandon and little Mary sat down to drink their tea together while the Captain went forth to seek his pleasures.

Just then, Mr. Jack Finucane, a townsman and frequent benefactor of the Shandon household, arrived. He carried a sweetmeat for little Mary and viewed Shandon as the greatest genius alive, a view supported by the occasional guinea or helpful word from the generous prodigal. Jack was ready to run errands, handle finances, and manage the myriad debts of an embarrassed Irish gentleman—a role filled by a whole hierarchy of indebted dependents marching behind their chieftain.
