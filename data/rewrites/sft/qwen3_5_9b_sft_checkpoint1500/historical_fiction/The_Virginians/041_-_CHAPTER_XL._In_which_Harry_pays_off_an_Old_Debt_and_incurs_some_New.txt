Our friends at Tunbridge were growing wearied of the waters, their spirits restless with the desire to depart. Autumn was approaching, and Bath lay ahead as Madame de Bernstein's ultimate destination—a realm of greater cards, livelier company, and endless life. Before reaching that glittering capital, however, she intended to pay respects to her country acquaintances. Harry, with a grace that was distinctly lacking, had promised to escort Lady Maria and the chaplain to Castlewood.

Their carriage wheels rattled past Oakhurst village once more, gliding by the hospitable home where Harry had once been so warmly welcomed. Lady Maria, buoyed by a mischievous spirit, peppered the air with sharp remarks concerning the young ladies of Oakhurst, suggesting they were setting their caps at Harry, and noting her mother's obvious eagerness to secure him for one of them. Irritated, Mr. Warrington declared in a petulant voice that he would bypass his friends' doorstep, citing his ladyship's apparent disdain and abuse of them. That evening, at the inn where they halted a few miles further along the road, the atmosphere grew thick with ill humor.

At supper, Lady Maria's smiles failed to coax any warmth from Harry's countenance. Her calculated tears—so easily summoned—elicited no sympathy from Mr. Warrington. To her querulous complaints, he offered only a surly grunt. Eventually, the lady was compelled to retire to her chamber without securing even a brief tête-à-tête with her cousin; the obstinate chaplain, seemingly by some unseen decree, refused to leave the room. "Had Harry given Sampson orders to remain?" she wondered as she departed, drawing a deep sigh. With an expression of stubborn politeness, he bowed her to the door, consigning her to the care of the landlady and her maid.

Ten minutes after Lady Maria had vanished into her chamber, a horse galloped frantically from the inn-yard. An hour later, when Mrs. Betty returned to fetch her mistress's smelling-salts, she discovered Parson Sampson smoking a solitary pipe. Mr. Warrington was supposedly gone to bed—or perhaps taking a moonlit stroll—but how could anyone know where Mr. Harry truly was? When pressed by the maid, Sampson revealed the truth: Mr. Warrington was ready to depart the following morning and had taken his seat beside Lady Maria's carriage. Yet his brow remained black, shadowed by a dark spirit. He scarcely spoke to her throughout the journey. Within herself, Lady Maria whispered, "Great heavens! she must have told him that she stole it!"

In reality, as they ascended a steep hill roughly three miles from Oakhurst on the Westerham road, Lady Maria Esmond, leaning heavily on the arm of her beloved youth, had poured out the most sentimental vows, protests, and declarations of affection. She was deeply in love with him. As her affections deepened, his demeanor grew colder. Looking up into his face, she saw the sun illuminate features that, though fresh and well-preserved, betrayed the lines and wrinkles of two-score years. Poor Harry, feeling the weight of her arm pressing upon him, found it intolerable. The prospect of carrying such a burden for all eternity was a dreary thought, causing him to curse the moonlit walk, the stifling heat, and the hot wine that had prompted him to make a foolish pledge, binding him fatally.

Maria's praises and raptures annoyed Harry beyond measure. The poor girl spilled fragments of the few plays she knew that pertained to her situation, striving with every ounce of her power to charm her young companion. She called him, repeatedly, her champion, her Henrico, her preserver, vowing that her Molinda would remain faithful forever. She clung to him tightly. "Ah, child! Have I not thy precious image, thy precious hair, thy precious writing here?" she exclaimed, gazing into his face. "Shall it not accompany me to the grave? It would, sir, were I to meet with unkindness from my Henrico!" she sighed.

A strange tale unfolded before them. Madame Bernstein had given Harry the small silken case, burning the hair and note it contained; yet Maria still held the memory of it close to her heart. It was then, as Harry started suddenly and moved as if to drop her arm, that Lady Maria felt her first pang of remorse—not merely for telling a fib, but for being exposed in doing so, a far more compelling reason for repentance. *Heaven help us,* she thought, *if everyone were to perform penance for lying, would they ever emerge from sackcloth and ashes?*

Upon arriving at Castlewood, Harry's good humor had not improved. The Lord was absent, and the ladies were away. The sole family member present was Mr. Will, who had just returned from partridge shooting. His complexion turned ghastly upon seeing his cousin, who bestowed a sulky scowl of recognition upon him.

Yet, Will attempted to maintain a pleasant facade. They sat down to supper, their conversation initially civil if not particularly lively.
"Have you been to some races?" asked Mr. Warrington.
"To several," replied Will.
"Were you successful in your bets?"
"I hope so," said Mr. Warrington dryly. "Pretty well, then?"
"And you have brought back my horse sound?" asked Mr. Warrington.
"Your horse! What horse?" Will asked, bewildered.
"What horse? My horse!" Harry said curtly.
"Protest, I don't understand you," said Will.
"The brown horse for which I played you, and which I won of you the night before you rode away upon it," Mr. Warrington stated sternly. "You remember the horse, Mr. Esmond."
"Mr. Warrington, I perfectly recall playing you for a horse, which my servant handed over to you on the day of your departure."
"The chaplain was present at our play. Mr. Sampson, will you act as umpire between us?" Mr. Warrington requested gently.
"I am bound to decide that Mr. Warrington played for the brown horse," said Mr. Sampson.
"Well, he got the other one," said sulky Mr. Will, with a grimace.
"And sold it for thirty shillings!" Mr. Warrington remarked, maintaining his composure.
Will, ever waggish, chuckled. "Thirty shillings? And a devilish good price, too, for the broken-kneed old rip. Ha, ha!"
"Not a word more. 'Tis only a question about a bet, my dear Lady Maria. Shall I serve you some more chicken?"

Nothing could be more studiously courteous and gay than Mr. Warrington while the lady remained in the room. When she rose to leave, Harry followed her to the door, offering the most courtly bow of farewell. Standing before the closed door for a moment, he then commanded the servants to retire. Once the menials had departed, Mr. Warrington locked the heavy door, slipping the key safely into his pocket.

As the lock clicked, Mr. Will, who had been sitting over his punch and occasionally glancing askance at his cousin, asked, with one of the oaths common to his speech, what on earth Mr. Warrington meant by that maneuver.
"I guess there's going to be a quarrel," said Mr. Warrington, blandly, "and there is no use in having these fellows look on at rows between their betters."
"Who is going to quarrel here, I should like to know?" asked Will, looking very pale and clutching a knife.
"Mr. Sampson, you were present when I played Mr. Will fifty guineas against his brown horse?"
"Against his horse!" Will shouted.
"I am not such a fool as you take me for," said Mr. Warrington, "although I do come from Virginia!" He repeated his question. "Mr. Sampson, you were here when I played the Honourable William Esmond, Esquire, fifty guineas against his brown horse?"
"I must own it, sir," said the chaplain, casting a deprecatory look toward his lord's brother.
"I don't own no such a thing," said Mr. Will, forcing a laugh.
"No, sir: because it costs you no more pains to lie than to cheat," said Mr. Warrington, stepping toward his cousin. "Hands off, Mr. Chaplain, and see fair play! Because you are no better than a—"

Before he could finish, his cousin flung a wine bottle at Mr. Warrington's head. The Virginian bobbed just in time, sending the missile flying across the room where it shattered against the wainscot, destroying the portrait of an Esmond ancestor before splattering a pint of port wine over the chaplain's face and powdered wig. "Great heavens, gentlemen, I pray you to be quiet!" cried the parson, dripping with liquid gore.

However, gentlemen were not inclined to heed the Church's commands in moments of rage. Having failed to strike its mark, Mr. Esmond seized a large silver-handled knife and lunged at his cousin. But Harry caught up the attacker's right hand with his left, mimicking the boxers he had observed at Marybone, and delivered a rapid blow to Mr. Esmond's nose.
