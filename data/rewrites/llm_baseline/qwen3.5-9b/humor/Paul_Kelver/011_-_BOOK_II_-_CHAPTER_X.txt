My father died, strangely enough, on the morning of his birthday. We hadn't expected the end anytime soon, and initially, we didn't realize it had happened.

"I left him asleep," my mother whispered, slipping out of the room in her dressing gown. "Washburn gave him a dose last night. Let's not wake him."

So we sat around the breakfast table, whispering. The house was small and flimsy; every creak echoed through the thin walls. Later, my mother crept upstairs, and I followed, prying the door open just a crack.

The blinds were down, the room dark. My mother stood there for a long time, her ear pressed against the wood. Outside, a girl's voice shrieked, *"Watercreases! Fine fresh watercreases with your breakfast—a'penny a bundle!"* Further off, a hoarse youth wailed, *"Mee-ilk—mee-ilk—oi."*

Inch by inch, my mother widened the gap. We stole inside. He lay there, eyes shut, lips slightly parted. I'd never seen death before; I couldn't grasp it. All I saw was that he looked even younger than I remembered. Slowly, agonizingly, the realization hit me: he was gone. For days, for weeks, I heard his footsteps behind me in the street, his voice calling my name, saw his face in the crowd, rushing to meet him only to find he had vanished into thin air. But at first, I felt no pain.

For my mother, it was a brief farewell. Fear and doubt had never touched her placid faith. He was waiting for her. In God's good time, they would reunite. What need for sorrow? Without him, the days dragged on; the house grew dim when the good man was absent. But that was all. She spoke of him constantly—his kindness, his quirks, his follies we cherished—not through tears, but smiles. She viewed him not as someone from the past, but as someone beckoning from the future.

We stayed in the old house, though we planned to move constantly. The great brick monster of the East End was creeping closer, year by year, devouring everything beautiful. Fields, gardens, trees, cottages, and time-worn stories—all crushed between its ugly teeth. This was a world apart, a growing ghetto of poverty where dreariness piled upon dreariness. Hopelessness stretched its shriveled arms endlessly across rows of reeking cells where London herded its slaves. On misty afternoons, when we knew life stirred in the sunshine elsewhere, we would venture out to hunt for homes in pleasant suburbs. Now, even those places were swallowed by the catacomb of narrow streets. We looked at Highgate, then a tiny town linked by coach to leafy Holloway; Hampstead, with its ancient red-brick houses overlooking wind-blown heath and distant London domes; Wood Green, where laborers debated politics beneath wide-spreading elms; and Hornsey, a village of an ivy-choked church and a single grass-lined path. We found "the very thing for us" repeatedly, discussing every possibility, finding them promising. Yet, we delayed.

"We must think it over," my mother would say. "There's no hurry. For some reasons, I'll be sorry to leave Poplar."

"For what reasons, Mother?"

"Oh, well, no particular reason, Paul. Just that we've lived here so long. Leaving the old house will be a wrench."

Everything molds the man, even the instinct of the clinging vine. We wrap our tendrils around the nearest wall, whether castle keep or pigsty, rain and sun binding us tighter. Dying Sir Walter Scott rushed home from Italy, terrified he might not reach the damp mists of the barren hills before his final breath. An old lady I knew was carried from her attic slum so she could feel the sea breeze, yet she pined for what she called "home." Wife, mother, widow, she had lived there until the alley's stench smelled sweet to her nose, until its chaos was the voice of her people. Who understands us except He who made us?

Thus, the old house held us to its dismal embrace. We didn't leave until, within its homely but unlovely arms, first my aunt, then my mother, had died. And finally, I had bid goodbye to Amy, weeping amidst the cluttered emptiness.

My aunt died as she lived: grumbling.

"You'll be glad to get rid of me, all of you!" she declared, dropping into a direct retort for the first and last time I could recall. "And I can't say I'll be sorry to go. It hasn't been my idea of life."

Poor old lady! That was only two weeks before the end. I doubt she realized how certain it was, or perhaps she would have been more sentimental.

"Don't be foolish," my mother said. "You're not going to die!"

"What's the use of talking like an idiot," my aunt snapped. "I've got to do it sometime. Why not now, when everything's ready? It isn't as if I'm enjoying myself."

"I'm sure we do all we can for you," my mother insisted.

"I know you do," my aunt replied. "I'm a burden to you. Always have been."

"Not a burden," my mother corrected gently.

"What does the woman call it then?" my aunt retorted. "Does she think I've been a sunbeam? I've been a trial to everyone. That's what I was born for; it's my métier."

My mother embraced the poor old soul and kissed her. "We shall miss you terribly," she said.

"I'm sure I hope they all will!" my aunt answered. "It's the only thing I've got to leave them that's worth having."

My mother laughed.

"Maybe it's been a good thing for you, Maggie," my aunt grumbled. "If it weren't for cantankerous people like me, gentle folks like you wouldn't get any practice. Perhaps, after all, I've been a blessing in disguise."

Honestly, we never wished her back, though we missed her dearly—many a joke at her oddities, many a laugh at her corner-cutting ways. It takes all sorts to make a world. If only perfect people remained, life might become uncomfortably monotonous.

As for Amy, I believe she truly regretted losing her.

"One never knows what's good till it's lost," Amy sighed.

"I'm glad you liked her," my mother said.

"You see, Mum," Amy explained, "I was one of a large family. A bit of a row now and again cheers one up. I'll lose the power of my tongue if something doesn't happen soon."

"Well, you're getting married in a few weeks," my mother reminded her.

Amy remained despondent. "They're poor things, the men, at a few words. The best of them. Likely as not, just when you're getting interested, you turn around to find they've put on their hats and gone out."

After my aunt's death, my mother and I were very much alone. Barbara had gone abroad to finish her education—to learn the tricks of the upper class, as old Hasluck phrased it—and I had left school to work with Mr. Stillwood, unpaid, intending to study law.

"You're in luck's way, my boy," old Mr. Gadley had assured me. "Starting your career at Stillwood, Waterhead, and Royal will be a passport anywhere. It will stamp you, my boy."

Mr. Stillwood himself was an extremely old and feeble gentleman—so frail it seemed strange that a wealthy man hadn't retired long ago.

"I've always meant to," he explained one day shortly after I arrived. "When your poor father came to me, he told me frankly he had only a few years left. 'Mr. Kelver,' I answered, 'don't let that trouble you. There are a few matters in the office I want cleared up, and you can help me. Once they're done, I'll retire!' Yet, you see, I linger on. I'm like the old hackney coach horse Mr. Weller—or maybe Mr. Jingle—describes; if the shafts were pulled away, I'd probably collapse. So I jog on, I jog on."

He had married late in life a common woman much younger than himself, who brought a horde of needy, greedy relatives. No doubt, the daily peace of Lombard Street was a refuge from her noisy neighborhood. We saw her occasionally. She was one of those blustering "managing" women who believed making a disturbance was "putting things to rights." Ridiculously ashamed of her origins, she tried to hide them under what her friends claimed was the air of a duchess, but which actually resembled the Sunday manners of an elderly barmaid. Mr. Gadley alone wasn't afraid of her; instead, he kept her in fear, often speaking to her with refreshing candor. He had known her in the days she wanted her past buried, and he always resented her entry into the established aristocratic firm of Stillwood & Co. as a personal insult.
