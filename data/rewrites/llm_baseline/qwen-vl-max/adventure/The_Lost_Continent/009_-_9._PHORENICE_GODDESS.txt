The passage I entered was no ordinary tunnel—its entrance had been concealed by human hands, but the shaft itself was ancient, a long-forgotten artery of fire once pulsing with molten earth. Though the lava had long since cooled, the air still carried a faint warmth and the sharp tang of sulfur. It was no place to linger. I lit a lamp from a niche carved into the wall and pressed forward, coughing as the thick air irritated my throat. I wished for the simples I used to carry—herbs that soothed catarrh—but those were lost in the sea battle with Dason’s fleet. Since arriving in Atlantis, survival had taken precedence over comfort, and medicine had been forgotten.

The tunnels branched endlessly, like roots beneath the world. If any other of the Seven Priests had stumbled upon this labyrinth, they would have perished—either lost in its twists or falling into one of the deep pits that led down into the boiling depths below. But I knew the way. The route was etched in my mind from my initiation twenty years prior, when I was raised to the highest rank in our Clan short of the Supreme Priesthood. I had been given the secrets, the rites, and the maps of hidden paths—and now, they were my only guide.

The journey was long. The floor was uneven, treacherous underfoot. The air grew heavier with each step. I knew it would be late afternoon before I passed beneath the city walls and emerged within the sacred precincts. Yet the vow of the Seven bound me: no one could leave this passage until mortal eyes had seen what lay beyond.

I tested one branch after another, but each led to an exit where figures loitered. I had hoped to emerge near the royal pyramid, but the area was swarming—like a hive stirred into frenzy. The same was true of the next five exits. The city was alive with restless energy, trembling with anticipation.

Then I found it—a temple dedicated to a lesser god, abandoned and silent. Dust covered every surface, thick enough to bury footprints. I waited behind the statue, observing. No one came. The silence was absolute. I moved quickly, clearing my path with a broom left on one of the pews, then sealed the entry behind me.

The temple door was shut. Even though I was a priest, I did not know the mechanism to open it. Only the attendants possessed that knowledge. If they were all gone—drawn away by whatever spectacle gripped the city—I might be trapped here.

No sound came from within the temple grounds. Decay was everywhere—cracked pillars, fallen tiles, dust on the altars. Yet according to ancient law, there should always be at least one priest watching. I followed the passages to the living quarters, located in the roof above the main hall.

I expected emptiness. I was not surprised when the door opened slowly. A man stood in the dim light, his face gaunt, his beard poorly curled. His clothes hung loosely, unbrushed, unwashed. He was Ro.

“I remember you,” I said. “We studied together in the Sacred College.”

He bowed deeply. “My lord. I am your servant.”

“You’re still here?” I asked.

“The duty remains,” he muttered. “Even if the rest have fled.”

He was known as the dullard—the one who grasped little of theology, less of statecraft, and nothing of war. He had risen to second priest only because others had abandoned their posts. And yet, he stayed.

“The temple door is locked,” he said. “Only I can open it. You come by the secret way, as one of the Seven. I serve you.”

“Tell me,” I said, “what stirs the city?”

“Phorenice has declared herself a goddess,” he replied. “She says she will light the sacrifice with divine fire. She will do it. She does everything. But I pray the flames consume her when she calls them down. This woman is the ruin of our Clan. The people ignore us. Offerings are gone. And since the rebels began hammering at the gates, I’ve lived off my own stores. Oh, Deucalion, the old faith is nearly dead.”

I frowned. “And yet the priests have failed to hold the people?”

“We did what we were taught,” Ro said bitterly. “But if no one comes to hear, what power do we have? The gods test us, Deucalion. Come—look at my bed.”

There, wrapped in bloodstained bandages, lay the body of a man—once his superior, the priest who had performed the weekly sacrifice to the Sun God.

“He was mangled,” Ro whispered. “It happened yesterday. The wood was laid. The offering placed. The procession formed. The drums and horns sounded. Then—above the noise—came wings. A great bird, featherless, monstrous, larger than any seen before.”

“A spear through the eye,” I said, “or a long shaft—those are the best weapons.”

“Yes, we learned that as children,” Ro said. “But the priests are unarmed during sacrifice. They trust in divine protection. The bird swooped down, landed on the pyre, and attacked the offering. My superior leapt onto the altar—armed only with his teeth and bare hands. He called on the Sun God for strength. But the bird struck him with its wings, tore at him with its talons. It threw him aside like a fly. The sacrifice was devoured in plain sight. Then the bird flew away.”

“And no one helped?”

“They screamed it was a sign,” Ro said. “That the Sun God no longer protected His own. Some said there was no god left. Others claimed a new god had come—this creature, this bird. But then a few shouted, ‘Phorenice is the true goddess!’ And the cry spread. The stones of the great circle rang with it. Some believed. Many joined because it was safe. Others feared to oppose her.”

“She is Empress,” I said. “Her name commands respect.”

“I am a priest,” Ro replied. “I speak what I was taught. But I wonder—does faith remain? We’ve had no offerings here in five months. If not for my corn jars, I’d have starved. And this sacrifice? It won’t bring back the people.”

“It happened twenty hours ago,” I said. “And today, she conducts it herself?”

“Yes. That’s why the city is aflame. She’s preparing a pageant. You must see it.”

“I must,” I said. “Open the door.”

“I cannot advise a member of the Seven,” he said. “But… they say she is angry at your absence.”

“I do not fear her,” I said. “The future belongs to the High Gods. Open the door.”

He grumbled something about “force of habit” as he activated the levers. The massive stone slid aside.

“I might have needed you,” I said, stepping out. “But you owe me nothing.”

“Nor do I have anything,” he muttered. “One must live.”

I walked into the street. The crowd surged past the temple, all heading in one direction. With Ro’s words in mind, I knew where they went—the great stone circle of the Sun God. My heart sank. For centuries, that temple had stood untouched, a monument to reverence. Now, it would witness sacrilege.

Could the Sun God Himself be drawing Phorenice toward destruction? Would He strike down her blasphemy with fire from heaven?

But the crowd cared little. They were excited, hungry, desperate. From one side of the city, the sounds of battle echoed—rams battering walls, fire-darts streaking through the air. Yet no one noticed. The people had grown numb. Hunger and chaos had stripped them of civilization. They fought over scraps, robbed each other, trusted no one. Their minds had regressed to savagery.

Yet rising from this decay were palaces and pyramids of unmatched splendor—monuments to a greatness now crumbling beneath a tyrant’s rule.

The crowd swallowed me whole, pushing forward through streets once clean and proud, now choked with mud and bones. Men and women had died here, left to rot. We walked on their remains.

At last, we reached the central square—the only space Phorenice had not touched with her buildings. Here stood the oldest, grandest temple in the world: the circle of the Sun God.

Forty-nine stones, undressed, massive, arranged in perfect symmetry. Across the top, equally huge slabs formed a roof. In the center, the altar—carved with the snake and the outstretched hand, symbols of life and eternity.

The crowd stopped at the edge. To cross was death. I remained among them, unseen, waiting.

The sun blazed overhead. The siege raged beyond the city, but no one listened. All attention was fixed on the coming event.

I later learned that Phorenice had first gone to the walls, rallying defenders with her brilliance. But now, midday burned hot, and exhaustion took its toll. Many fainted. Some died. But no one cared. Life was cheap.

Then came the music—drums, horns, a procession glinting in the sun. Lords and courtiers paraded in gold and silk. The poor watched, starving, envious.

And then—she arrived.

Riding atop a shaggy mammoth, in a golden chariot, Phorenice swept into the square. Her presence transformed the crowd. Sullen faces brightened. A wave of joy rose—not forced, not commanded—but spontaneous. She smiled, her cheeks flushed, her eyes alight with triumph.

She stepped down, and those nearby fell to their knees. Only Ylga, her fan-girl, remained standing—pale, troubled. A daughter of Zaemon, the chief priest of the Sun God, she could not forget her roots. She looked torn between loyalty and horror.

Phorenice strode into the circle, bold, proud, claiming the role of high priestess. Behind her came men dressed as priests—some mere followers, others traitors born in the Sacred College. Shameful.

The wood was already stacked. The victim—a cloven-hoofed horse—was bound and placed upon the pyre. It neighed, struggling. Then it was left alive.

An innovation. A mockery.

The false priests stepped back. Phorenice stood alone before the altar.

She raised her voice—clear, sweet, carrying over the din. She spoke in the common tongue, so all could understand. She told of her divine birth, of the gods’ favor, of miracles wrought by her hand.

Then, lifting both arms to the sky, she cried:

“O my high Father, the Sun, I call upon You! Acknowledge me as Your daughter! Give this people a sign! I offer sacrifice unlit, as mortal priests have done for ages. Send down Your heavenly fire! Let it burn this beast! Prove that though You reign above, You have given me Atlantis, and the people of the Earth, to rule!”

She strained upward, her face contorted, her limbs trembling.

“Hear me, mighty Father! Hear me!”

A flash—blinding—spat from the sky. The horse shrieked once, convulsed, and fell dead.

Flames burst from the pyre. Smoke rose in a thin blue spiral.

Above, the dark cloud vanished.

Phorenice stood, radiant, triumphant.

The priests knelt. The crowd fell to their knees.

“Phorenice, Goddess!” they roared. “Phorenerce, Goddess of Atlantis!”

But I did not kneel.

I stood firm.

For I knew—this was not the fire of the gods.

This was the fire of madness.

And I would not bow.
