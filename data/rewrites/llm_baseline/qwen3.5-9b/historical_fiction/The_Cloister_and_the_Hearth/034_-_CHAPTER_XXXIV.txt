"Here be we!" roared a chorus of voices, followed immediately by a blessing. "God bless you all! God bless you!"

In response, there was a frantic rush toward the staircase. Half a dozen hands, calloused yet warm with genuine friendship, shot out to grasp the rescuers.

"You've saved our lives, lads!" cried Denys, his voice thick with relief. "You've saved our lives this very night!"

The scene that greeted the eyes of the rescued pair was one of chaotic brilliance. Torches flared wildly, casting dancing shadows against the walls. The room was filled with the glittering breastplates of the archers, their faces bronzed by firelight and exertion. Bound tightly were the pale, terrified faces of the thieves, and lying amidst the gore was the bleeding giant, his life extinguished by the hard men who now stood guard over him.

Gerard moved among the archers, shaking each hand with glistening eyes. In turn, they kissed him—a gesture of profound gratitude—and this time, he returned their kisses. Turning to a handsome archer of his own age, Gerard spoke softly. "Prithee, good soldier, watch over me. A strange drowsiness overtakes me. Let no one cut my throat while I sleep—for pity's sake."

The archer laughed, assuming Gerard was merely jesting, and promised faithfully. Almost immediately, Gerard sank into a deep slumber. Denys watched, surprised but unmoving; the unconscious state suited his immediate needs perfectly.

Elsewhere, two archers were examining the Abbot's body, kicking it gently to turn it over as they asked, "Which of the two cast this enormous rogue from an upper storey like that? We would fain learn the trick of his arm."

Denys initially scoffed, unwilling to boast. *That young vagabond,* he thought, *will claim it was the finger of Heaven or some divine intervention, making me look like a fool.* But seeing Gerard lost in dreams, Denys decided to provide the necessary account.

"Well, then, comrades," Denys declared loudly. "I ran my sword through this one to the hilt. Others swarmed around me, so I needed my blade or I must die. Thus, I planted my foot against his stomach, gave a tug with my hand, sprang with my leg, and sent him flying straight to kingdom come! He died in mid-air, and his carcass rolled down among you without ceremony—making quite a jump, I warrant. But pikes and pillage! What avail does prattle serve once the deed is done? *Buvons, camarades, buvons!* (Drink, friends, drink!)"

The archers noted that saying "drink" was easy when no liquor was present, though doing so was harder.

"Nay, I shall soon find you liquor," Denys insisted, his nose twitching with alacrity. "My nose is naturally gifted at scenting wine. Follow me, and I will lead the way with this torch!"

They descended a short flight of stone steps into a large, low, and damp cellar. The air was close and dank; the walls were encrusted here and there with what looked like cobwebs but proved to be saltpetre oozing from the moist stones and crystallizing.

"Oh! The fine mouldy smell," Denys murmured. "In such places, good wine often still lurks. Advance your torch."

Suddenly, a cry broke the silence. "Diable! What is that in the corner?"

Gathering around the torchlight, they discovered a figure crouching on a heap in the gloom, pale as ash and shivering violently.

"Why, it is the landlord," Denys identified. "Get up, thou craven heart!" shouted one of the archers. "The thieves are bound, and we are thirsty. Rise and show us your wine; for no bottles are visible here."

"What? Are the rascals bound?" the pale landlord stammered, his voice trembling. "Good news... wine? Yes, yes, I will, honest sirs."

He rose with unsteady joints, offering to guide them to the wine cellar. But Denys interjected sharply. "You are all in the dark, comrades. He is in league with the thieves."

"Alack, good soldier! Me in league with the accursed robbers? Is that reasonable?" the landlord protested.

"The girl said so anyway," an archer retorted.

"The girl! What girl? Ah! Curse her, traitress!" the landlord spat.

"Well," another archer added, "the girl is not here; she has gone to the bailiff. Let the burghers decide if this coward is guilty or not. We caught him not in the act, so let him simply draw us our wine."

"One moment," Denys said shrewdly. "Why did he curse the girl? If he were a true man, he should bless her as we do."

"Alas, sir!" the landlord pleaded. "I have but my good name to live by. I cursed her to you because you accused her of bellying me."

"Humph! I trow thou art a thief, and where is the thief who cannot lie with a smooth face?" Denys countered. "Therefore, hold him, comrades. A prisoner can draw wine even if his hands are not bound."

Offering no objection, the landlord agreed to show them his stock, hoping they would pay, noting sadly that his rent was overdue by two months. The archers smiled grimly at his simplicity. One laid a firm hand on his shoulder while the other led the way with the torch.

As they reached the threshold, Denys cried, "Halt!"

"What is it?"

"Here be bottles in this corner. Advance your light."

The torch-bearer stepped forward, removing his scabbard to probe the heap where the landlord had just crouched.

"Nay, nay!" cried the landlord. "The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing here."

"Nothing is mighty hard, then," Denys replied, reaching into the heap and pulling out an object. It was a bone. He tossed it onto the floor, where it rattled.

"There is naught there but the bones of the house," the landlord insisted. "Just now it was empty. Now that we have found something, it is nothing but bones. Here is another."

Denys handed the bone to an archer named Philippe, who held it up to the light, turning it over and over.

"Well?" Denys prompted.

"Well, if this were a battlefield," Philippe mused, "I should say it was the shankbone of a man. No more, no less. But this isn't a battlefield, nor a churchyard; it is an inn."

"True, mate; but that knave's ashen face is as good a lantern as any battlefield. Read the bone by it. Bring that face nearer, I say. When the chine is missing and the house dog won't look at you without his tail tucked between his legs, who was the thief? Good brothers, my mind misgives me. The deeper I thrust, the more there seems to be. Perhaps if these bones could speak, they would make true men's flesh creep."

"Alas, young man, what hideous fancies are these!" the landlord shrieked. "These are bones of beef, sheep, and kids, not of men and women! Holy saints preserve us!"

"Hold thy peace! Thy words are air. Thou hast not got burghers by the ear who cannot distinguish a veal knuckle from their grandsire's ribs; but soldiers-men who sought their comrades and found their bones picked clean by crows, perhaps as thoroughly as these by thee and thy mates." Denys thrust a small object suddenly into the landlord's face. "Men and women, didst thou say? Prithee, when did I speak of women's bones? Thou wouldst make a child suspect thee. Field of battle, comrade! Was not this house a field of battle half an hour ago?"

Dragging the landlord closer, Denys demanded, "Read his face now. What is this, thou knave?"

The landlord stared, paralyzed. "Alas! I know not."

"I would not swear either," Denys admitted, drawing his sword. "But it looks too much like the thumb bone of a man's hand. Mates, my flesh creeps. Churchyard! How do I know this is not one?"

Raking the heap of earth, broken crockery, and bones onto the floor, Denys searched relentlessly. The landlord assured him he was wasting time.

"We poor innkeepers are sinners," the wretch sobbed. "We give short measure and baptize the wine; we are fain to do these things because the laws are so unjust to us. But we are not assassins. How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven's lightning strike me dead if there be any bones here but those used for meat. 'Tis the kitchen wench who flings them here. I swear by God's holy mother, by Saint Paul, by Saint Dominic, and by Saint Denys, my patron saint..."
