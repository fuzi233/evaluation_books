On the first of May, all twenty boarders and four teachers of Rue Fossette woke to a notice taped to every dormitory door: rise at five, dress and be ready by six, to place ourselves under the full command of M. le Professeur Emanuel. The day he had promised us weeks before, the country breakfast excursion, had finally arrived.

I, for my part, had never thought I’d be invited. When he first floated the plan, I’d been pointedly excluded, and when I teased him gently about that, wondering how it was I’d ended up on the guest list after all, he gave my ear a playful, sharp little tug, and threatened the other with the same if I dared raise a single further complaint. “I’d advise you not to make people beg for the pleasure of your company,” he said, imperious as a little Napoleon, and one of his characteristic half-gruff, half-gentle gestures was enough to make me resolve not to risk being left behind.

The morning broke as soft and warm as high summer, blackbirds singing loud in the garden, a thin dew mist curling low over the flowerbeds that promised a glowing, hot afternoon. We all folded away our thick winter wool gowns with giddy relief, pulling on the crisp, new print dresses and neat straw bonnets the Parisian seamstress had stitched for us that spring—modest, unshowy, and perfectly flattering, no faded cast-off silks or shabby hand-me-downs in sight.

At six, the bell pealed merrily, and we all poured down the staircase, through the central common room, down the corridor and out into the vestibule. There stood M. Paul, not wrapped in his usual severe dark overcoat and flat Greek cap, but wearing a light belted linen blouse and a jaunty straw hat, looking ten years younger than he did in the classroom. He greeted every last one of us with a warm good-morning, and most of us smiled back, bright and eager, as he marshalled us into line to march out of Villette.

The streets were still quiet, the boulevards smelling of fresh cut grass, as peaceful as open fields. None of us could stop smiling. Our professor had a rare gift for stoking joy in the people around him, the very same gift that could send a shiver of fearful attention down the row of students in his lecture hall when he was cross. He didn’t lead the procession, or follow it—he walked alongside the line, stopping to say a word to every single girl, teasing his favourites, even sparing a kind glance for the students he found dull or tiresome.

I had no wish to draw his eye, though. I was paired with Ginevra Fanshawe, whose arm was heavy and warm on mine, chattering nonstop about how the walk would ruin her new boots. I used her as a deliberate human shield, shifting my position every time I heard M. Paul’s step draw near to my right or my left. The reason for my silly little manoeuvre? My new print dress was pale pink, and I felt exactly as I had once when I’d had to cross a meadow full of grazing bulls wearing a shawl with a bright red border. For a little while, hiding behind Ginevra and fanning out the wide fringe of my black silk scarf worked, but M. Paul soon noticed that no matter which side of the line he walked, Ginevra was right there, prattling away in her sharp, affected English accent. The two of them had never gotten along: he thought her shallow and vain, she thought him rude, meddlesome and unbearably gruff.

On the sixth time he switched sides, he leaned forward, fixed his dark eyes on mine, and demanded, half laughing half annoyed: “What is this? Are you playing tricks on me?”

He caught on before I could even stammer a reply. I shook my scarf out wider, but he laughed, and said, “Ah! It’s the pink dress!” The sound was just as startling as a bull’s low, but it held no anger.

“It’s only cotton,” I hurried to explain, flustered. “It’s cheap, and washes far better than any darker colour.”

“And Mademoiselle Lucy is more vain than ten Parisiennes,” he teased. “Who ever saw such a thing from an English girl? Look at her bonnet, her gloves, her little boots!” Those things were no fancier than any of my classmates’—if anything, mine were plainer—but he’d found his excuse to tease me, and I’d braced for a lecture. It never came. All I got was one quick, bright, bantering smile, like a flash of summer lightning, and he softened: “Courage. I’m not cross at all. In fact, I’m very pleased you made yourself look so nice for my little party.”

“But my dress isn’t nice at all, monsieur,” I protested. “It’s just clean.”

“I love cleanliness,” he said, and his good mood held, bright and warm as the rising sun, chasing away every last wisp of the tiny, silly cloud of tension.

Soon we were out in the country, winding down the soft green lanes locals called les bois et les petits sentiers, so fresh and lush in their May greenness, before the summer dust would settle over them and turn them dry and dull. We stopped at an old stone well ringed with neat lime trees, and M. Paul ordered us all to sit on the soft grass around it, settling himself right in our midst. The little girls who loved him more than they feared crowded close; the ones who feared him more than they loved hung back a little; the few of us who felt both affection and a soft, giddy thrill of fear of him kept the farthest distance of all.

Then he told us a story. He narrated it masterfully, simple in its strength, vivid and full of tender little touches, the kind of twilight scene he painted so clearly in words that I can still see it in my mind’s eye, more real than any painting I have ever seen. He was not a man to sit still and write books, but he scattered such sharp, bright, unconsciously generous gems of thought that day that I thought, if someone who loved him got to hoard every one of those gold-dust fragments, they would have a treasure far greater than any printed volume. His mind was my favourite library, and every time he opened it to me, I felt like I was stepping into bliss.

When the story ended, he walked over to the little knoll where Ginevra and I sat apart. He never had the patience to wait for someone to volunteer an opinion, so he asked directly: “Were you interested?”

I answered, quiet as I always was: “Yes.”

“Was it good?”

“Very good.”

“I could never write it down,” he said. “I hate the drudgery of sitting still to write. But I could dictate it, happily, to an amanuensis who suited me. Would Mademoiselle Lucy write for me, if I asked her?”

“Monsieur would speak too fast,” I told him. “You’d get impatient and angry if my pen couldn’t keep up with your lips.”

“Try it some day,” he laughed. “We’ll see how much of a monster I can be. But no talk of dictation now. I need you for another job. Do you see that farm-house over there, half hidden in trees?”

“Yes.”

“That’s where we’ll have breakfast. While the farmer’s wife makes café au lait on the hearth, you and five others I pick will spread butter over fifty fresh rolls.”

He marched us all down to the farm, which surrendered at once to our little cheerful army. Half a dozen of us worked under his direction to butter the huge basket of rolls the baker had dropped off that morning, while coffee and chocolate steamed on the fire, and bowls of cream and plates of new-laid eggs waited on the table. M. Paul, ever generous, was about to order whole legs of ham and jars of jam extra, when a group of us pretended to scold him for wasting food, calling him a spendthrift. He complained that we were a pack of miserly housewives, but we got our way, trimming the feast just enough to avoid waste. He stood by the kitchen hearth, grinning, happier than I’d ever seen him, just to watch all of us happy and laughing around him. We dragged the big heavy oak farmer’s chair to the head of the long table and sat him down there. For all his quick, fiery temper, all his storms of irritation, he was as soft and gentle as a lamb when someone met him halfway, understood him, and was kind to him. Even the youngest children felt perfectly safe with him.

Before we ate, he made the smallest little girl say a short grace, crossing himself as devoutly and simply as a child, a quiet act of piety I had never seen from him before. His eyes caught my soft, pleased smile, and he reached out his hand to me. “Give me your hand. I see we worship the same God, in the same spirit, even if we follow different rites.” Most of the other professors at the school were free-thinkers, cynics, men whose lives would not stand close scrutiny, but M. Paul was like an old knight of legend: passionate, impulsive, but honourable to his bones, with a pure, uncomplicated piety that tamed every wild, sharp part of him.

Breakfast was merry, no empty silly chatter, but the warm, easy kind of joy that only comes when everyone around the table is relaxed and at peace. When the meal ended, the girls ran off to play in the pastures. A few of us stayed behind to help the farmer’s wife wash up the earthenware, until M. Paul called me out to sit with him under a broad spreading tree, where he could watch the rest of the troop gambol across the fields. He sat on a rough wooden bench, and I sat on the root of the tree beside him, reading him from a tattered pocket copy of Corneille while he smoked his cigar. He listened so quiet, so soft, the look of calm on his face so rare for his usually impetuous, energetic nature, that my heart felt light and full, brimming with the perfect happiness of the warm May day, his company, his unexpected kindness.

After a while, he asked if I would not rather run off and play with my friends. I told him no, I was perfectly content right where I was, at his side. He asked me if, if I were his sister, I would always be happy to stay near a brother like him. I said I thought I would, and I meant it. Then his tone softened, turned a little mournful. He asked if I would be sorry if he left Villette, to go far away across the sea. I dropped the Corneille book, and could not speak.

“Little sister,” he said, “how long would you remember me, if we were separated?”

“Monsieur,” I whispered, “I cannot tell you that, because I do not know how long it will be before I stop remembering anything at all on this earth.”

“If I went overseas for two, three, five years, would you welcome me home when I came back?”

“Monsieur,” I said, my throat tight, “how would I live through the time in between?”

“I have been very hard on you, you know,” he said. “Very demanding.”

I hid my face behind the Corneille volume, tears spilling over the pages. I asked him why he was talking so sadly, and he promised he would not say another word, cheering me up with soft little jokes and gentle words for the rest of the afternoon. But his unusual tenderness made my heart ache. It felt too soft, too mournful. I almost wished he would go back to being his usual sharp, teasing, unpredictable self.

By noon the day had turned as hot and bright as mid-June, and he herded his “flock” of girls back towards home. The farm was a full league away from Rue Fossette, and the little girls were tired from running and playing, dragging their feet at the thought of walking over the hot, dusty, flinty roads in the midday sun. But he had thought ahead. Just past the farm gate, two large hired school carriages were waiting for us, and we all piled in, reaching the Rue Fossette safe and sound in under an hour. It had been a nearly perfect day—only that faint, wispy note of melancholy had dimmed its sunshine for a moment.

That same evening, just after sunset, that faint sadness bloomed again. I looked out the window of the first classe to see M. Paul walking slowly down the central garden alley, arm in arm with Madame Beck. They talked earnestly for almost an hour: he grave, restless, frowning, she looking surprised, expostulating, trying to talk him out of something. When Madame Beck finally went back inside as the dark fell, leaving M. Paul leaning against a tree in the half light, arms crossed, brow bowed, my heart ached. He had called me little sister that morning. If he really were my brother, I would run out to him right now, and ask what was weighing on his mind. Madame Beck would never comfort him. She would only remonstrate. He needed someone to listen.

Then he started walking, fast and straight, towards the house. I thought he was going to water the potted orange trees, the little chore he sometimes did of an evening—but he turned abruptly, heading straight for the berceau trellis and the glass door of the first classe, where I was standing, watching him. My cowardly, panicky heart took over. I did not wait for him. I ran, as if a bullet was flying at my head, slipping down the corridor to hide in the empty oratory. I leaned against the door, breath coming fast, and I heard him storm through every classroom, clashing the doors open impatiently, bursting into the refectory where the evening pious lecture was in session. I heard him call out, loud and clear: “Where is Mademoiselle Lucie?”

Just as I had summoned my courage to walk out, to go meet him, the sharp, false voice of Mademoiselle St. Pierre answered glibly: “She’s in bed.”

He stamped his foot in frustration, and turned away. Madame Beck met him in the corridor, scolded him, and marched him straight to the front street door, shutting it firmly behind him.

The second that door clicked shut, I was hit by a blow of utter, stunned misery. I knew he had come to find me. I knew he had something to tell me, something soft and important he had been leading up to all day. I wanted to hear it more than I wanted anything else in the world—and I had run away from it like a coward. I could have had that confidence, that solace, two minutes of standing my ground, and I threw it all away. There was nothing now but dark, empty suspense, no answers, no chance to know what he had been about to say.

I carried that bitter, foolish weight to my bed, and lay awake all night, counting every second of the silly, self-inflicted loss.
