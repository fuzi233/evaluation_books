Mr. Thornton had found it easier to set iron in motion than to persuade his mother to be gracious.

Mrs. Thornton did not squander herself in society. When she paid calls, she did it as she did everything else—solidly, with a sense of obligation and a certain ceremonial weight, as if civility were a fabric to be cut straight, pinned firmly, and worn without frill. Her son had given her a carriage; she would not keep horses for it. Horses were hired like extra hands at harvest-time—only for needful occasions, when custom demanded she should go forth and be seen.

Not a fortnight before, she had hired them for three days and had, as she said with a dry satisfaction, “killed off” her acquaintances—visited all who could claim her attention, so that now they might take their turn and spend their own money and time in returning it. Yet Crampton lay too far for her to compass on foot; and more than once she had looked up from her stitching to ask, in the tone of one reckoning flour by the ounce, whether his wish that she should call upon the Hales was truly strong enough to bear the cost of a cab.

She would have been quietly relieved if it had not been. “I see no use,” she said, “in making up friendships and intimacies with all the teachers and masters in Milton. Why, you’ll be wanting me to call on Fanny’s dancing-master’s wife next.”

“And so I would, mother,” he answered, quick as a knife laid down on a board, “if Mr. Mason and his wife were friendless in a strange place—like the Hales.”

“Oh! you need not speak so hastily. I’m going to-morrow. I only wanted you exactly to understand about it.”

“If you are going to-morrow, I shall order horses.”

“Nonsense, John. One would think you were made of money.”

“Not quite, yet. But about the horses I’m determined. The last time you were out in a cab, you came home with a headache from the jolting.”

“I never complained of it, I’m sure.”

“No. My mother is not given to complaints,” he said, and there was pride in it, as if endurance were a family possession. “But so much the more I have to watch over you. Now, as for Fanny—there—a little hardship would do her good.”

“She is not made of the same stuff as you are, John. She could not bear it.”

At that Mrs. Thornton grew still. The words touched a sore place she kept covered, even from herself. She had an instinctive contempt for weakness—an unreasoning, almost bodily dislike of it—yet her daughter was weak exactly where she and her son were hard and strong. Mrs. Thornton did not argue herself into opinions; she struck them out, as a flint gives sparks. She knew—without ever admitting it aloud—that nothing could steel Fanny into patience, nor teach her to meet trouble squarely. The knowledge mortified her; and the mortification turned oddly to a pitying tenderness, like that with which some mothers handle a sickly child: careful, shielding, indulgent.

A stranger might have thought she loved Fanny best, because to her she said “dear” and “love,” while to her son she said only “John.” But her gratitude went after him like an unspoken prayer; and for his sake she walked proud among women.

“Fanny dear,” she said presently, as if arranging a household matter, “I shall have horses to the carriage to-day, to go and call on these Hales. Should not you go and see nurse? It’s in the same direction, and she’s always so glad to see you. You could go on there while I am at Mrs. Hale’s.”

“Oh, mamma, it’s such a long way—and I am so tired.”

“With what?” Mrs. Thornton asked; and her brow drew together just a little, like linen pulled tight.

“I don’t know—the weather, I think. It is so relaxing. Couldn’t you bring nurse here, mamma? The carriage could fetch her, and she could spend the rest of the day here, which I know she would like.”

Mrs. Thornton laid her work down upon the table. The needle stopped; the thread slackened. She considered, weighing comfort against propriety, expense against feeling, as women do who have been poor and remember it in the muscles of their hands.

“It will be a long way for her to walk back at night,” she said at last.

“Oh, but I will send her home in a cab. I never thought of her walking.”

At that moment Mr. Thornton came in, hat in hand, on his way to the mill, bringing with him—almost as a draught brings in smoke—a sense of the outer world: of wheels, of engines, of those unceasing Milton sounds that seemed to live in the very boards and brick. He looked from one to the other with the impatience of a man who cannot bear delays in things he thinks plain.

“Mother! I need hardly say, that if there is any little thing that could serve Mrs. Hale as an invalid, you will offer it, I’m sure.”

“If I can find it out, I will,” she replied. “But I have never been ill myself, so I am not much up to invalids’ fancies.”

“Well! here is Fanny then, who is seldom without an ailment. She will be able to suggest something, perhaps—won’t you, Fan?”

“I have not always an ailment,” Fanny said sharply; the peevishness rose in her like heat in milk when it is watched too late. “And I am not going with mamma. I have a headache to-day, and I shan’t go out.”

Mr. Thornton’s face darkened; annoyance came over it as over a clear broth when something bitter is dropped in. His mother bent over her work again and began to stitch with a brisk, business-like speed.

“Fanny! I wish you to go,” he said, with the authority he used in his counting-house. “It will do you good, instead of harm. You will oblige me by going, without my saying anything more about it.”

He went out abruptly, as if any further speech would only loosen his temper. Had he stayed another minute Fanny would have cried—his tone commanded tears from her as surely as some people’s tenderness commanded them. As it was, she grumbled the moment the door had shut.

“John always speaks as if I fancied I was ill, and I am sure I never do fancy any such thing. Who are these Hales that he makes such a fuss about?”

“Fanny, don’t speak so of your brother,” Mrs. Thornton answered, though the question struck in her own mind like a refrain. “He has good reasons of some kind or other, or he would not wish us to go. Make haste and put your things on.”

Yet the little clash between son and daughter did not soften her towards “these Hales.” Jealousy—quiet, respectable jealousy, that hid itself behind duty—kept asking, “Who are they, that he is so anxious we should pay them all this attention?” It went on repeating itself long after Fanny had forgotten everything in the pleasant flutter of trying her new bonnet before the glass, turning her head this way and that, as if a different angle might make her brother’s command sit easier.

Mrs. Thornton was shy; her sternness covered it. It was only in late years she had had leisure to go among people, and she did not relish it. She could take satisfaction in dinners—in giving them, in judging them, in the quiet triumph of serving something well and seeing it appreciated or, better still, envied. That was a kind of mastery she understood: the ordering of plates and joints, the proper hotness of a dish when it came to table, the exact moment a pudding should be cut so it stood and did not sink. But to go and make acquaintance with strangers, without the defence of her own board between them, left her ill at ease. She looked more than usually forbidding as she entered the Hales’ little drawing-room, as if she feared she might be cheated of her dignity if she let it drop for an instant.

Margaret Hale sat with her embroidery—cambric stretched fine beneath her fingers—for some small article of dress for Edith’s expected baby. Mrs. Thornton’s glance fell on it at once. Flimsy work, she thought; useless, too—pretty enough, perhaps, but no more nourishing to life than froth. Her eye travelled next to Mrs. Hale’s double knitting, and there she felt a grudging approval. That was sensible in its way; it made warmth; it had a purpose.

The room itself seemed full of knick-knacks—little ornaments and trifles that would take time to dust. And time, to people of limited means, was money. These reflections came and went through her mind while her mouth delivered the correct phrases of a morning call—those easy commonplaces people can utter as mechanically as stirring tea. Mrs. Hale answered with a little more effort than her health might have spared; yet a certain interest had been kindled in her by the lace Mrs. Thornton wore—real old lace, not new-fangled mimicry.

Afterwards, Mrs. Hale told Dixon, with the air of one tasting something rare and recognising it at once, that it was old English point, such as had not been made for seventy years and could not be bought. It must be an heirloom, she said; it showed the woman had ancestors. So the ancestral lace, like a spice that gives value to an otherwise plain dish, made its wearer worth more attention than mere politeness would have required.

Meanwhile Margaret, racking her wits to talk to Miss Thornton, heard the older women sink—as if by a natural gravitation—into the subject of servants, that inexhaustible domestic history-book in which ladies read each other’s fortunes and failings.

“I suppose you are not musical,” Fanny said, looking round with a faint disdain, “as I see no piano.”

“I am fond of hearing good music,” Margaret replied; “I cannot play well myself; and papa and mamma don’t care much about it; so we sold our old piano when we came here.”

“I wonder how you can exist without one. It almost seems to me a necessary of life.”

Margaret felt the words as she might feel a draught of cold air across warm skin. Fifteen shillings a week, and three saved out of them! The thought came sharp and vivid—like the exact measure of flour in a poor household where every loaf must be counted. She looked at Fanny’s face and tried to be just: the girl must have been very young; perhaps she had forgotten. Yet surely she must have known, by some memory, that people did without such “necessaries.”

When Margaret spoke again there was a new chill in her courtesy, a clear edge to it. “You have good concerts here, I believe.”

“Oh, yes! Delicious! Too crowded—that is the worst. The directors admit so indiscriminately. But one is sure to hear the newest music there. I always have a large order to give to Johnson’s the day after a concert.”

“Do you like new music simply for its newness, then?”

“Oh! one knows it is the fashion in London, or else the singers would not bring it down here. You have been in London, of course.”

“Yes,” said Margaret. “I have lived there for several years.”

“Oh! London and the Alhambra are the two places I long to see!”

“London and the Alhambra!”

“Yes! ever since I read the Tales of the Alhambra. Don’t you know them?”

“I don’t think I do. But surely it is a very easy journey to London.”

“Yes; but somehow,” Fanny said, lowering her voice and leaning a little nearer, as if the secret were a sweetmeat to be shared, “mamma has never been to London herself, and can’t understand my longing. She is very proud of Milton—dirty, smoky place as I feel it to be. I believe she admires it the more for those very qualities.”

“If it has been Mrs. Thornton’s home for some years, I can well understand her loving it,” Margaret answered; and her voice, clear as a bell, carried further than Fanny intended.

Mrs. Thornton turned her head. “What are you saying about me, Miss Hale? May I inquire?”

Margaret, taken by surprise, found her mind blank for an instant—as if a word she knew in one dialect would not come when demanded in another. Fanny stepped in quickly.

“Oh, mamma! we are only trying to account for your being so fond of Milton.”

“Thank you,” said Mrs. Thornton, and the words fell like a shut lid. “I do not feel that my very natural liking for the place where I was born and brought up—and which has since been my residence for some years—requires any accounting for.”

Margaret coloured, vexed with herself and with the other. Fanny had indeed sounded impertinent; and yet Mrs. Thornton’s offence, so stiffly shown, seemed itself a kind of dominion—an insistence that even talk about her must be measured in her terms.

After a moment Mrs. Thornton went on, her manner controlled but sharpened. “Do you know anything of Milton, Miss Hale? Have you seen any of our factories? our magnificent warehouses?”

“No,” Margaret said. “I have not seen anything of that description as yet.” And then, unwilling to let even politeness make her speak falsely, she added, “I dare say papa would have taken me before now if I had cared. But I really do not find much pleasure in going over manufactories.”

“They are very curious places,” Mrs. Hale put in gently, as if trying to soften the angle between them; “but there is so much noise and dirt always. I remember once going in a lilac silk to see candles made, and my gown was utterly ruined.”

“Very probably,” Mrs. Thornton returned shortly, displeased—whether at the lilac silk or at the tone of complaint, it was hard to say. “I merely thought, that as strangers newly come to reside in a town which has risen to eminence in the country from the character and progress of its peculiar business, you might have cared to visit some of the places where it is carried on—places unique in the kingdom, I am informed. If Miss Hale changes her mind and condescends to be curious as to the manufactures of Milton, I can only say I shall be glad to procure her admission to print-works, or reed-making, or the more simple operations of spinning carried on in my son’s mill. Every improvement of machinery is, I believe, to be seen there, in its highest perfection.”

Margaret bowed slightly. The offer was made like a favour, yet it carried with it an assertion of power; the very words “condescends” and “procure her admission” seemed to set the terms of who granted and who received. Outside, beyond the curtained windows, the town’s sound lay like an invisible weather: the far throb and hammer of industry—so constant that Milton folk ceased to hear it, as they ceased to taste the hardness of their water—yet to strangers it was an oppression that could not be translated into ordinary talk.

“I am so glad you don’t like mills and manufactories, and all those kind of things,” Fanny whispered, as she rose when her mother began to take leave with rustling dignity.

“I think I should like to know all about them, if I were you,” Margaret replied quietly; and if there was a rebuke in it, it was delivered without heat.

Mrs. Thornton’s farewell to Mrs. Hale was correct, neither warm nor cold; and then the two Thorntons withdrew, leaving behind them the faint scent of well-kept clothes and old lace, and the impression—like the lingering taste of something strong—that their visit had been less an interchange than a trial of strength.

As the carriage rolled away, Mrs. Thornton spoke at once, as she often did when the door had closed upon other people and she could set her judgments down plain.

“Fanny! we will be civil to these Hales; but don’t form one of your hasty friendships with the daughter. She will do you no good, I see. The mother looks very ill, and seems a nice, quiet kind of person.”

“I don’t want to form any friendship with Miss Hale, mamma,” Fanny said, pouting. “I thought I was doing my duty by talking to her, and trying to amuse her.”

“Well! at any rate John must be satisfied now,” Mrs. Thornton answered; and as the wheels carried them back through Milton’s ceaseless din, she let the matter drop—as if shutting away a spice she did not care to use, but had agreed to keep in the house for her son’s sake.
