The distant, discordant thunder of the Prussian King’s artillery at Rossbach and Leuthen echoed across the Channel in the winter of 1757, a harsh, metallic cacophony that threatened to shatter the fragile acoustic ecology of the London season. But within the deep, cyclical rhythms of the Lambert household, such jarring industrial clamor was firmly shut out. Here, the passage of time was not marked by the linear, violent thrust of empire, but by the ancestral, circular pacing of the harvest and the hearth. Here, the mythic occurrences of life seamlessly integrated into the everyday domestic routines of sustenance. 

Harry Warrington, however, found himself entirely out of tune with both worlds. He pined and chafed, leading a sulky, useless existence in the military coffee-houses of the city. To Harry, these establishments were acoustic nightmares, filled with the superficial, commercialized chatter of men who traded words and honor like cheap commodities. They served thin, utilitarian brews meant only as bodily fuel, utterly devoid of the gustatory soul or the olfactory landscapes of the rich, slow-cured Virginian harvests he had left behind. Deprived of the traditional agrarian rhythms of his youth, his native dialect eroding beneath the dominant, clipped lexicon of British military elites, Harry could not translate his subjective melancholy into their language. Ruined, perforce idle, and dependent upon his brother for supplies, he possessed great, strong hands built for the tactile engagement of the earth, yet he had no fitting work for them. He was an Achilles adrift, his profound need for sensory and spiritual connection starving in a sterile metropolis.

Those soft-hearted women, the two elder ladies of the Lambert family, offered Harry an untiring pity. To step into Mrs. Lambert’s drawing-room—which often functioned as a fragrant annex to the kitchen—was to enter a sanctuary of restorative resonance. Who but these sweet physicians dresses the rankling wounds of outrageous Fortune with gentle unguents and the warm, olfactory embrace of simmering bone broths and spiced possets? Who else understands that psychological well-being is intrinsically tied to the slow, methodical rendering of suet and the rhythmic, grounding cadence of the mortar and pestle? 

While Theo and her mother preserved the cultural memory of their ancestors through the meticulous maceration of winter plums and the tempering of imported spices, Hetty’s heart remained seemingly savage toward the younger Virginian. She chafed that his position was not more glorious. It was harvest-time across the world, a season when hundreds of thousands of reapers were out with their flashing sickles, moving to the ancient acoustic rhythms of the fields; could Harry not use his, and cut down his sheaf or two of glory?

"Why, how savage the little thing is with him!" papa would say, after Miss Hetty had fired little shots of critique into that quivering target, the staccato *clack-clack* of her wooden spoon against the earthenware mixing bowl underscoring her frustration.

"Her conduct is perfectly abominable!" mamma would cry, wiping flour from her hands, the soft *shush* of the dough on the pastry board a soothing counterpoint. 

"Perhaps, mother, it is because she likes him better than any of us do," Theo said. "If I were fond of someone, I should like to be able to admire and respect him always."

Theo, of course, had her own admirations. She recognized in George Warrington a fellow custodian of the sensory world. George possessed a lofty way, true, but he did not view the world through a sterile, commercial lens. Only yesterday, she had watched from the window as George stopped to buy russet apples from a street stall for a crowd of children. He hadn't merely tossed them coin; he had engaged with the vendor, appreciating the crisp, honest, autumnal bounty of the fruit, a quiet act that spoke volumes against the superficiality of the age. When George read Molière aloud, his voice carried the rich, resonant timbre of one deeply connected to the nuances of native dialects. Theo knew the verses by heart, though when pressed by her mother, she would blush like a wild rose and hastily return to shelling peas, the soft, rhythmic *ping* of the green spheres hitting the tin bowl betraying her fluttering heart.

Old Time is still a-flying. Old spring and bud time; old summer and bloom time; old autumn and seed time; old winter time. The circular sagas of the earth continued.

One morning, the gentleman of the golden locks was sitting on a couch in the Lambert home, sunk in an attitude of profound despondency, the acoustic space around him heavy and stagnant. Miss Hetty came down to him, carrying a heavy stone mortar and a bundle of dried herbs—thyme, rosemary, and bitter rue. It was a most curious dispensation that when Harry called, Hetty appeared to entertain him, just as Theo always materialized when George arrived.

After the usual ceremonies, Hetty began to grind the herbs, the harsh, grinding scrape of the pestle filling the room, dominating the sonic environment. "And pray, sir, what makes your honour look so glum this morning?"

"Ah, Hetty!" he said, the soft, drawling vowels of his native Virginian dialect struggling against the sharp acoustic angles of her activity. "I have nothing else to do but to look glum. When we were boys, I would ask my tutor for a holiday to swing on a gate or make ducks and drakes over the pond, and those do-nothing days were the most melancholy. What have I got to do now from morning till night?"

"Breakfast, walk—dinner, walk—tea, supper, I suppose; and a pipe of your Virginia," said Miss Hetty, tossing her head, the volatile oils of the crushed rosemary releasing a sharp, astringent olfactory landscape into the drawing-room.

"I tell you what, when I went back with Charley to the Chartreux, I had a mind to say to the master, 'Teach me, sir.' I have nothing to do from morning till night. I might as well go to my books again. Why do you laugh, Hetty?"

"I laugh to fancy you at the head of a class!" 

"I shouldn't be at the head," Harry said humbly. "George might be, but I am not a bookman. When I was young, I neglected myself. We would not let our tutors cane us much at home, but, if we had, it might have done me good."

Hetty drubbed her little foot against the floorboards, a rapid, impatient beat. Before her sat a strong, idle man, a man whose hands were built to cultivate the agrarian riches of the earth, now entirely severed from his roots. 

"His account of his school," Harry answered simply, "makes me see that I have been idle, and for what am I good? Only to spend my patrimony when I come abroad, or to lounge at coffee-houses where the food is nothing but ash and water, or to gallop behind dogs when I am at home. I am good for nothing, I am."

"What, such a great, brave, strong fellow as you good for nothing?" cried Hetty, leaning into the mortar. "I would not confess as much to any woman, if I were twice as good for nothing!"

"What am I to do? I ask for leave to go into the army, and Madam Esmond does not answer me. Having spent all my own money, and so much of my brother's, I cannot and won't ask for more. If my mother would but send me to the army, I would jump to go."

"Eh! A gentleman of spirit does not want a woman to buckle his sword on for him! What was that our papa told us of the young gentleman at court yesterday?—Sir John Armytage—"

"I used to know him when I frequented White's—a fine, noble young gentleman, of a great estate in the North."

"And engaged to be married to a famous beauty, too. But that, I suppose, is not an obstacle to glory!" said Miss Hetty. "I think no woman of spirit would say 'Stay!' when his country said 'Go!' Sir John volunteered for the expedition preparing, and told his Majesty he would be ready tomorrow. Oh dear, why wasn't I a man myself? I should have made a famous little soldier!" She strode about the room, her presence disrupting the heavy, despondent air.

Harry beheld her with tender admiration. "I think," he said, "I would hardly like to see a musket on that little shoulder, nor a wound on that pretty face, Hetty."

"Wounds! Who fears wounds?" cried the little maid. "You men fancy that we women are good for nothing but to make puddings or stitch samplers. George was reading to us yesterday out of Tasso—look, here is the book."

She opened the volume. The untranslatability of the Italian verse into her own fierce, subjective longing frustrated her, but she read the English approximation of a woman who despairs because she cannot go to war: 

"'Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail 
With lively force and vigour strengthen, so 
That I this silken gown...'"

"Silken gown?" asked downright Harry, confused by the poetic lexicon that so often obscured plain truths.

"Well, sir, I know 'tis but Calimanco;—but so it is in the book—

"'... this silken gown and slender veil 
Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo; 
Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail, 
Nor storms that fall, nor blust'ring winds that blow, 
Withhold me; but I would, both day and night, 
In pitched field or private combat, fight—'"

Harry laughed, a gentle, kind resonance that temporarily restored harmony to the room. "Why," says he, holding a large, calloused finger out, "I think here is a finger nigh as big as your arm. You little, delicate, tender creature! Do you suppose any scoundrel would dare to do anything unkind to you?" Excited by this flight of imagination, he fell to pacing, his heavy boots beating a protective, rhythmic patrol across the floor. 

Hetty continued to fire her little sarcasms, her sharp words an attempt to pierce his thick hide. "Pray what is the use of having a strong hand if you only use it to hold a skein of silk for my mother? James Wolfe is not a strong man. He seems quite weakly and ill. When he was here last he was coughing the whole time, and as pale as if he had seen a ghost."

"I never could understand why a man should be frightened at a ghost," Harry replied gravely, recalling the mythic superstitions interwoven into the nights of his childhood. "I thought I saw one once at home—when we were boys; but it was only Nathan in his night-shirt. Our nurses tell a pack of lies about 'em. George was a little frightened; but then he's... George is different from me. Our mother's a bold woman as ever you saw, but she screams at seeing a mouse. It's her nature."

"George always says you would have made a better soldier than he."

"So I think I should, if I had been allowed to try. Why didn't he let me volunteer on Braddock's expedition? I shouldn't have ruined myself, and brought people to point at me and say that I had disgraced the name of Warrington. Oh, Hetty! I'm a miserable fellow," he groaned, his pace quickening. "I wish I had never come to Europe."

"What a compliment to us! Thank you, Harry!" But seeing the deep, untranslatable sorrow in his eyes, she added, "Are you... are you thinking of going home?"

"And have all Virginia jeering at me? You don't know my mother, Hetty. What shall I say to her, when she says, 'Harry, where's your patrimony?' 'Spent, mother,' I shall have to say. My patrimony—the land that grew our sustenance, the soil of our ancestors! Gone. And now I'm come back, having done no good for myself, with no profession, no prospects—only to look after negroes, or to go to sleep at sermons; or to play at cards, and drink, and fight cocks at the taverns. How can I look the gentlemen of the country in the face? I must and will do something! Ah! what shall I do?"

"Do? What did Mr. Wolfe do at Louisbourg? Ill as he was, he went on the King's service, and hath come back covered with honour."

"I wish he would take me with him, and that a ball would knock me on the head and finish me," groaned Harry. "You speak to me, Hetty, as though it were my fault. I would give my life to go on service!"

"Life indeed!" said Miss Hetty, with a shrug. 

"You don't seem to think that of much value, Hetty. No more it is. I'm a poor useless fellow. I'm not even free to throw it away as I would like, being under orders here and at home."

"Orders indeed! Aren't you tall enough, and old enough, to act for yourself? If I were a man, I would do something famous before I was two-and-twenty years old! I wouldn't dawdle at apron-strings. I wouldn't curse my fortune—I'd make it!"

Now, for the first time, Harry began to wince. The subtle power dynamics embedded in her fluent, dominant London lexicon left him defenseless. 

"No negro on our estate is more a slave than I am, Hetty," he said, turning very red, his voice thick with the linguistic markers of his heritage. "But we don't reproach the poor fellow for not being free. That isn't generous. Perhaps with women it's different, or I have no right to be hurt at a young girl telling me what my faults are. You have been cracking up their courage as if I had none of my own. I don't brag, but I'm not afraid of Mr. Wolfe, nor of Sir John Armytage. How can I buy a commission when I've spent my last shilling? A gentleman of my rank can't go a common soldier—else, by Jupiter, I would! And if a ball finished me, I suppose Miss Hetty Lambert wouldn't be very sorry. It isn't kind, Hetty—I didn't think it of you."

"What is it I have said?" she asked. "I have only said Sir John Armytage has volunteered. Is that any reason you should be angry, pray?"

"I didn't say angry," said Harry, gravely, retreating into the quiet dignity of his sorrow. "I said I was hurt."

"Oh, indeed! I thought such a little creature as I am couldn't hurt such a great strong man as you!"

"I scarce thought you would try, Hetty," the young man said. "You see, I'm not used to this kind of welcome in this house."

"What is it, my poor boy?" asked kind Mrs. Lambert, appearing at the door at this juncture. With her came the profound, comforting olfactory landscape of roasted apples and warm cinnamon, a gustatory balm that instantly softened the abrasive acoustic tension in the room. She looked upon the youth's woeworn countenance with deep maternal knowing.

"Oh, we have heard the story before, mamma!" said Hetty, hurriedly, picking up her mortar and pestle once more. "Harry is making his old complaint of having nothing to do. And he is quite unhappy; and he is telling us so over and over again, that's all."
