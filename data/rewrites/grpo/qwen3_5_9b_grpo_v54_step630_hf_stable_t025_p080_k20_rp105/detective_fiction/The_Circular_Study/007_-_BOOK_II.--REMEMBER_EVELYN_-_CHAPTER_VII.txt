Miss Butterworth was a creature of rigid discipline, raised in a school where stillness was virtue and movement was calculated. When she sat, she sat like a statue; when she rose, she moved with the firm, swift economy of a machine. Fidgeting was a sin she had spent her life condemning in others. Yet, trapped in the silence following her interview, the dam broke. The sensible woman within her struggled to suppress the outward tremors of anxiety she usually repressed, her hands betraying the unease her mind tried to hide.

The relief came only when Mr. Gryce returned, his face breaking into a smile that was almost gay.

"Well, that is seen to!" he declared. "All that remains is to wait for the result. Madam, do you harbor any further thoughts? If so, I shall be delighted to hear them."

Her composure snapped back into place instantly.

"You would?" she echoed, her gaze searching his face with a newfound skepticism. "I would prefer to verify the worth of the single idea I have offered before risking another. Let us hold a conference, compare notes. For instance, explain to me why neither of us considers Bartow the guilty party."

"I believed we had drained that well dry," Gryce replied smoothly. "Your suspicions stemmed from the young couple fleeing the house. My own... well, Madam, I must confess to you that the mute's gestures suggest he is rehearsing a scene he witnessed, not one he committed. I see no reason to doubt this."

"I felt the same pull," she admitted. "Even without my other suspicions, his manner spoke of observation, not action. Besides, it is more natural for a man to be driven mad by another's cruelty than by his own."

"Only if he loved the victim," Gryce countered.

"But did not Bartow love Mr. Adams?"

"He does not mourn him."

"Yet he is no longer master of his emotions."

"True. But if we use his actions as a clue, we must accept them wholly. We believe his pantomime is a literal copy of what he saw. So, why does his face light up with infernal joy once the performance ends? It suggests he rejoices in the deed itself."

"Perhaps it is still a copy; the murderer may have smiled. But no," Miss Butterworth insisted, her voice tightening. "There was no joy in the face of the youth I saw rushing away. Quite the opposite. Mr. Gryce, we are in deep water. I feel nearly submerged."

"Keep your head above, Madam. Every flood recedes. If you drown, what becomes of me?"

"You are disposed to humor, Mr. Gryce. A good sign. You never jest when perplexed. Somewhere, you must see the light."

"Let us proceed. Illumination often comes from the most unexpected quarter."

"Very well. Let us file the old man's joy among the mysteries to be solved later. Have you considered him an accomplice?"

"Certainly. But that hypothesis shares the fatal flaw that made him the motive for the murder. One is not driven insane by an expected horror; it requires a shock. He was not expecting his master's death."

"Agreed. That is settled. Bartow was an innocent witness, having nothing to fear, and thus his mimicry should be exact."

"Continue, Madam. Nothing but profit can come from an argument led by Miss Butterworth."

The detective's tone was grave, his manner impeccable. Miss Butterworth, ever vigilant for sarcasm, bristled slightly, though she gave no other sign of displeasure.

"Let us recall his precise movements," she began. "He must have surprised the attacker from the study doorway, viewing the assault over his master's shoulder."

"In other words, directly in front of him. What was his first move?"

"As recorded, he raises his right arm, stretching it behind him while leaning forward to grasp an imaginary dagger. What does that signify?"

"I find it difficult to say. But I did not see that. When I arrived, he was thrusting with his *left* hand across his own body—a vicious motion. That is a critical detail, Mr. Gryce."

"Yes, particularly since the doctors confirm Mr. Adams was struck down by a left-handed blow."

"You don't say! Don't you see the contradiction?"

"The contradiction, Madam?"

"Bartow stood face-to-face with the assailant. In his unreasoning state, he would naturally mimic the opposite arm. If the killer used his right hand, Bartow, mirroring him, would lift his left. Try it yourself. Lift your right hand; instinctively, you raise the one opposite. It is like looking in a mirror. You turn your head right; your reflection turns left."

Mr. Gryce's laugh rang out, involuntary and bright. He was rarely caught off guard, but this woman possessed a peculiar fascination over him, and he found it amusing rather than offensive to be bested.

"Excellent! You have proven your point satisfactorily. But what conclusions follow? That the killer was not left-handed, or that he was not standing where you placed him?"

"Shall we defy the doctors? They insist the blow was left-handed. Mr. Gryce, I would give anything for an hour spent in Mr. Adams's study with Bartow free to move. I believe we would learn more by watching him for a few moments than by talking for an hour."

The suggestion was tentative, tinged with the timidity she claimed she didn't possess. "I don't wish to be disagreeable," she smiled.

She was anything but. Mr. Gryce was taken aback, and for once, he acted impulsively.

"I believe it can be arranged, Madam; perhaps after the funeral. There are too many officials cluttering the house just now, and..."

"Of course, of course," she acquiesced. "I should not dream of intruding at present. But the case is so fascinating, and my connection so peculiar, that I sometimes forget myself. Do you think"—her voice faltered, betraying a rare loss of nerve—"that I have opened myself to a summons from the coroner?"

Gryce grew thoughtful, his eyes softening with a mock compassion as he looked at her folded hands.

"The facts are emerging so slowly that I doubt the inquest will occur for several days. Meanwhile, we may locate those two young people ourselves. If we do, the coroner may overlook your role in bringing them to our attention."

There was a sly emphasis on the word *overlook*, a subtle humor that revealed the detective at his sharpest. Miss Butterworth did not resent it; she was too preoccupied with a fresh confession.

"Ah," she said, "if they had been the only ones I encountered. But they were not. Another person entered the house before I departed, and I must speak of him."

"Of him? Truly, Madam, you are a mine of intelligence."

"Yes, sir," she replied meekly—a tone that carried weight given her listener. "I ought to have mentioned him sooner, but I dislike mixing matters. This old gentleman..."

"Old gentleman!"

"Yes, sir. Very old, very much of a gentleman, seemingly unconnected to the crime save for knowing the deceased."

"Ah, but that is a significant connection, Ma'am. To find someone who knew Mr. Adams... patience has its limits, and I must urge you to speak."

"Oh, I will speak! The time has come. Besides, I am eager to discuss this new thread; it is remarkably interesting."

"Then let us begin with a detailed account of your adventures in this house of death," Gryce suggested dryly. "Your full adventures, Madam, with nothing omitted."

"I appreciate the sarcasm, but nothing has been left out except what I am about to reveal. It happened just as I was leaving."

"What did? I hate to ask for more explicitness, but for the sake of justice..."

"You are quite right. As I exited, I met an elderly gentleman entering. His hand had just touched the bell handle. You will admit it was a perplexing moment. His face, well-preserved for his age, wore an air of expectation, almost gay. He glanced at me in astonishment; whatever my usual serenity, my face must have shown deep emotion. We said nothing. Finally, he asked politely if he might enter, mentioning an appointment with someone in the study. I stepped aside briskly—this could mean anything. What did you say? Did I close the door? I certainly did. Should I have let the entire street of ---- enter the horrors of this house when a poor old man—no, I didn't go out myself. Why should I? Was I to leave a man nearing eighty—excuse me, not every man of eighty is as hale as yourself—to wander into this scene alone? Besides, I hadn't warned him of the condition of the only other occupant."

"Discreet, very. Exactly what one expects of you, Miss Butterworth. More than that. You followed him, no doubt, with careful supervision, down the hall."

"Most certainly! What would you have thought if I hadn't? He was in a strange house, had no servant to guide him, needed directions to the study, and I politely showed him the way."

"Kind of you, Madam—very. It must have been an interesting moment."

"Too interesting! I own I am not made entirely of steel, sir, and the shock he received at finding a dead man awaiting him instead of a live one was communicated to me. Yet, I stood my ground."

"Admirable! I could have done no better. And so, this man, who had an appointment with Mr. Adams, was shocked, truly shocked, to find him lying there under a cross, dead?"

"Yes, there was no doubt. Shocked, surprised, terrified, and something more. It is that 'something more' that baffles me. Was it the fascination of a horrible sight on a morbid mind, or a sudden realization of danger escaped or yet to come? Hard to say, Mr. Gryce, hard to say; but you may take my word there was more to this meeting than stumbling upon a corpse. Yet, he made no sound after that first cry, hardly any movement. He simply stared at the figure on the floor, then at the face, devouring it first with curiosity, then hate, then terror, and finally—with how can I express it?—a sort of hellish humor that might have broken into laughter had the bird, which I had failed to notice until then, not awakened in its high cage. Thrusting its beak between the bars, it shrilled in an alarming tone: 'Remember Evelyn!' That startled the old man more than the sight on the floor. He turned, and I saw his fist rise as if against a menacing intruder, only to fall again as his eyes met the picture hanging before him. With a cringe painful to see in one of his years, he sidled back to the doorway. Here he paused to cast one last look at the man stretched at his feet, and I heard him mutter:

"'It is Amos's son, not Amos! Is it fatality, or did he plan this meeting, thinking—'"

"But here he caught sight of my figure in the antechamber. Resuming his former debonair manner instantly, he bowed very low and opened his lips as if to ask a question. But he evidently changed his mind, strode past me, and made his way to the front door without a word. As an intruder myself, I hesitated to stop him. But I regret the consideration I showed him; for just before he stepped out, his emotion—whose specific character I admit I cannot fathom—culminated in a burst of raucous laughter that added the final horror to this amazing adventure. Then he went out, and in my last glimpse before the door closed, he wore the same look of easy self-satisfaction with which he had entered this house of death some fifteen minutes prior."

"Remarkable! Some secret history there! That man must be found. He can shed light on Mr. Adams's past. 'Amos's son,' he called him? Who is Amos? Mr. Adams's name was Felix. Felix, the son of Amos. Perhaps this connection of names leads somewhere. It is not common, and if given to the papers, it may yield a clue to a mystery that seems impenetrable. Your stay in Mr. Adams's house was quite productive, Ma'am. Did you prolong it after the old man's departure?"

"No, sir. I had had my fill of the mysterious and left immediately after him. Ashamed of the investigative spirit that led me into the house, I enlisted a street boy to deliver my message to the police and would have been glad to escape all responsibility. But the irony of fate follows me as it does others. A clue remained of my presence, involving me in this affair whether I will or no. Was it the hand of Providence? Perhaps. The future will tell. And now, Mr. Gryce, since my budget is empty and the hour late, I must take my leave. If you hear from that scrap of paper..."

"If I hear from it in the way you suggest, I shall let you know. It will be the least I can do for a lady who has done so much for me."

"Now you flatter me—proof positive that I have stayed a minute longer than was judicious. Good evening, Mr. Gryce. What? I have not stayed too long? You have something else to ask."

"Yes, and this time it concerns a matter personal to yourself. May I inquire if you wore the same bonnet yesterday as you do today?"

"No, sir. I know you have a good reason, so I shall not express my surprise. Yesterday I was in reception costume, and my bonnet was of jet..."

"With long strings tied under the chin?"

"No, sir, short strings; long strings are no longer the fashion."

"But you wore something that fell from your neck?"

"Yes, a boa—a feather boa. How did you know, sir? Did I leave my image in one of the mirrors?"
