The Weight of Words

"If you insist," Trent conceded, his voice carrying a heavy, reluctant tone, "then your way shall be taken. But I confess, I would far rather compose this missive in solitude. Should I be compelled to proceed, fetch me a sheet of paper so pristine it rivals the snow of a winter's peak, or perhaps the parchment of a celestial choir—something devoid of your address stamped upon it. Do not mistake this for mere formality; I am offering a sacrifice I have never before felt the need to make. I have never felt less inclined to engage in correspondence."

She smiled, a gesture that seemed to soften the tension in the room.

"What shall I write?" he asked, his quill trembling slightly above the blank page. "Shall I liken him to a summer's day? Or is there something else entirely?"

"Write what you feel," she offered, her tone encouraging.

He shook his head vigorously. "What I feel, and what I have been desperate to articulate to every soul I encounter since dawn, is that 'Mabel and I are betrothed, and all is gas and gaiters.' But such a declaration makes a woefully poor opening for a letter of strictly formal, indeed, ominous intent. I have managed only as far as 'Dear Mr. Marlowe.' What follows?"

"I am enclosing a manuscript," she prompted gently, "which I believe you might find of interest."

"Do you realize," Trent countered, his eyes narrowing, "that in that sentence, there are merely two words exceeding a single syllable? This letter is designed to command respect, not to invite comfort. We require gravitas; we require the weight of long, complex terms."

"I fail to see the necessity," she replied. "While it is customary, I question the logic. I have received countless missives from attorneys and merchants, all beginning with phrases like 'with reference to our aforementioned correspondence,' continuing in that vein until the final line. Yet, when I meet these individuals face-to-face, they speak plainly. It strikes me as absurd."

"It is no absurdity to them," Trent declared, setting down his pen with a sigh of relief and rising to his full height. "Allow me to elucidate. A nation like ours, possessing a profound aversion to mental exertion, advances through life with a vocabulary that is both small and rudimentary. Consequently, long words become anomalies. Like any anomaly, they are either comical or terrifyingly solemn. Consider the phrase 'intelligent anticipation.' Were such a construct uttered in any other European nation, it would pass unnoticed. Here, however, it has become a proverb; we grin upon hearing it in a speech or reading it in a headline, regarding it as a masterpiece of wit. Why? Simply because it comprises two lengthy words. The concept expressed is as mundane as boiled mutton. Then there is 'terminological inexactitude.' We still roar with laughter at that phrase! The entire jest rests solely on the length of the words. It is identical when we seek to appear profoundly serious; we signal our gravity by turning to verbose obscurity. When a solicitor opens a sentence with 'pursuant to the instructions previously communicated to our representative,' he feels he has earned his six-and-eightpence. Do not laugh! It is the absolute truth. Now, the Continental mind operates differently. They are perpetually preoccupied with ideas themselves, resulting in a lexicon where every shopkeeper or peasant possesses a vocabulary that remains utterly alien to the vast majority of Britons. I recall dining recently with a friend, a Parisian cab driver. We ate at a grimy establishment opposite the central post office, surrounded by porters and drivers. The conversation was universal, yet I felt a London cabman would have felt profoundly out of his element. Terms like 'functionary,' 'unforgettable,' 'exterminate,' and 'independence' flew across the table with alarming frequency. And these were merely ordinary, jovial, red-faced men. Mind you," he hastened to add as she moved to retrieve his pen, "I mention this solely to illustrate my point. I am not suggesting cabmen should aspire to intellectualism. I agree with Keats: happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen, enough their simple loveliness for me. But when one considers the collective industrial intellect of the nation..."

"Oh, no, no, no!" Mrs. Manderson interrupted sharply. "I know nothing at this moment save that your discourse must cease if we are to progress with this letter to Mr. Marlowe. You shall not evade this. Come!" She placed the pen firmly into his palm.

Trent regarded the instrument with distaste. "I warn you not to dampen my spirits," he said, looking dejected. "Believe me, men who remain silent are far more difficult to endure than those who chatter. Beware of mute natures. I admit, I am avoiding this task. It borders on indecency. To compose such a letter while seated in the same room as you creates a discordant mixture of moods."

She guided him back to his chair before the desk and gently pressed him down. "Very well, but please attempt it. I wish to witness your handiwork, and I desire it sent forthwith. You see, I could be content to let matters rest; yet you insist on uncovering the truth. If you must, I want it done immediately. Do it now—you possess the ability if you choose—and I shall dispatch it the moment it is finished. Have you never felt that urge? The desperate longing to thrust the troubling letter into the post, to cast it off your hands so that it cannot be recalled, rendering further fretting pointless?"

"I shall comply with your wishes," he said, turning to the paper, which he dated from his hotel. Mrs. Manderson watched him, her dark eyes holding a gentle light, as she prepared to smooth his unruly hair. But she restrained herself. Moving silently to the piano, she began to play with soft, tentative fingers. Ten minutes passed in silence before Trent spoke.

"If he chooses to reply that he will say nothing?"

Mrs. Manderson glanced over her shoulder. "Of course he dares not take that path. He will speak to prevent you from denouncing him."

"But I have no intention of doing so," Trent insisted. "You would not permit it—you said so; moreover, I shall not if you forbid it. The evidence is too tenuous now."

"But," she laughed softly, "poor Mr. Marlowe does not know you refuse, does he?"

Trent sighed deeply. "What extraordinary things codes of honor are!" he murmured, lost in abstraction. "I know there are actions I should perform without hesitation, yet which would disgrace you if I did—such as striking a man who grossly insults me, or swearing violently when I injure my shin in the dark. And now, you calmly advise me to bluff Marlowe with a tacit threat I do not intend to carry out—a maneuver that even hell's most abandoned fiend would never employ in the drunkenness of guilt. Well, I shall not do it." He resumed his writing, and the lady, wearing an indulgent smile, returned to her quiet music.

A few moments later, Trent announced, "At last, 'Yours faithfully.' Would you care to review it?" She crossed the dim room, switching on a reading lamp beside the desk. Leaning against his shoulder, she read the following:

DEAR MR. MARLOWE,—_You will perhaps recall that we met, under unfortunate circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone._
_On that occasion, it was my duty, as a representative of the press, to conduct an independent inquiry into the circumstances surrounding the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I did so, and I arrived at specific conclusions. You may learn from the enclosed manuscript, originally drafted as a dispatch for my newspaper, what those conclusions were. For reasons requiring no further elaboration, I decided at the last moment not to make them public, nor to communicate them to you; they are known to only two persons besides myself._

At this juncture, Mrs. Manderson raised her eyes quickly from the letter. Her dark brows knit together in concern. "Two persons?" she asked, her voice tinged with inquiry.

"Your uncle is the other," Trent explained. "I sought him out last night and revealed the entire narrative. Do you harbor any objection? I always felt uneasy withholding this from him, having led him to expect a full disclosure; my silence appeared as mystery-making. Now, it is to be resolved finally, and there is no question of shielding you. I wished him to know everything. He is a shrewd adviser in his own right, and I should prefer his presence when I confront Marlowe. I have a strong feeling that two minds will serve us better than one in this interview."

She sighed. "Yes, naturally, your uncle ought to know the truth. I hope there is no one else involved." She pressed his hand. "I so desperately wish all that horror buried—buried deep. I am very happy now, dear, but I shall be happier still once you have sated that curious mind of yours, uncovered everything, and stamped the earth down upon it all." She continued reading.

_Quite recently, however, facts have come to my knowledge which have altered my decision. I do not mean that I shall publish what I discovered, but that I have determined to approach you and request a private statement. If you possess anything to say that would cast the matter in a different light, I can imagine no reason why you should withhold it._
_I therefore expect to hear from you regarding when and where I may call upon you; unless you prefer the interview to take place at my hotel. In either case, I desire that Mr. Cupples, whom you will remember, and who has reviewed the enclosed document, should also be present.—Faithfully yours,_

_Philip Trent._

"How very stiff!" she remarked. "Now I am certain you could not have made it any stiffer within your own chambers."

Trent slid the letter and its enclosure into a long envelope. "Indeed," he said, "I believe it will cause him to sit up abruptly. Now, this matter must not risk failure. It would be best to send a special messenger with orders to deliver it directly into his hands. If he is away, it must not be left unattended."

She nodded. "I can arrange that. Wait here for a moment."

Upon her return, he was rummaging through the music cabinet. She sank onto the carpet beside him, her dark brown skirts pooling around her. "Tell me something, Philip," she said.

"If it is among the few things I know."

"When you saw your uncle last night, did you tell him about—about us?"

"I did not," he answered. "I remembered you had said nothing of telling anyone. It is for you—is it not?—to decide whether we reveal our confidence to the world immediately or later."

"Then will you tell him?" She looked down at her clasped hands. "I wish you to tell him. Perhaps if you think you can guess why.... There! That is settled." She lifted her eyes again to his, and for a time, silence hung between them.

He leaned back in the deep chair. "What a world!" he exclaimed. "Mabel, will you play something on the piano that expresses mere joy, the genuine article, nothing feverish or like thorns under a pot, but joy that has decided in favor of the universe? It is a mood that cannot last forever, so we had better extract all we can from it."

She moved to the instrument and struck a few chords while she pondered. Then, with all her soul, she began to work the theme from the final movement of the Ninth Symphony, a melody akin to the sound of the opening of the gates of Paradise.
