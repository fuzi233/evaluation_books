Our old pal Major Arthur Pendennis trundled up to Fairoaks right on schedule, fresh off the worst 24 hours of his gout-prone life, courtesy of the Victorian mail coach. Some great oaf of a passenger, swaddled in so many overcoats he looked like a walking down duvet, had wedged him into the corner so tight his ankle ached for hours, and snored so loud it could have roused a whole parish of dead churchgoers. Across from him, a widow lady had sealed every window shut so tight no fresh air could sneak in, and was steadily guzzling Jamaica rum punch out of a secret bottle stashed in her reticule, until the whole carriage reeked of spiced booze. Any time the Major even started to doze, he’d get jolted awake by the turnpike horn blaring right outside the window, his giant neighbor shoving him even further into the woodwork, or the widow digging her pointy boot heels into his already sore toes. Ah, the good old days of 19th century travel, huh? Eight whole miles an hour for 20+ hours, hard seats, grumbling coachmen mad you didn’t tip them enough, passengers who brought their own private rum supplies—who among us hasn’t suffered through that grand old adventure? And somehow people still managed to show up smiling back then, go figure.

Up on the roof next to the Major’s valet sat two schoolboys, bound home for midsummer holidays. The Major nearly choked on his sad watery tea at the Bagshot inn stop when he watched those two tuck into a mountain of ham, eggs, pickled pie, boiled beef, tea, and coffee that could have fed a whole cricket team, while he moped thinking Lord Steyne was tucking into a seven-course fancy dinner back in London right that very minute. The lads’ unhinged vacation appetite was charming enough, though, and the Major perked right up when he learned one of the boys riding inside with him was a lord’s son—someone he’d naturally rubbed shoulders with in fancy London society circles. That little lord slept through the entire night’s squeezing, horn blaring, and rum fumes, and hopped off at the end of the journey looking fresh as a daisy, while the Major stumbled down at Fairoaks lodge gate yellow-faced, beard bristly, his wig totally out of curl, and rheumatism shooting through every limb like tiny little angry firemen. The lodge keeper and gardener’s wife bowed so deep their bonnets nearly fell off, and his valet Morgan greeted him with the reverence due a man who’d survived 24 hours of mail coach hell.

Helen Pendennis had been watching for him from the window, but she didn’t rush out to greet him. She knew the Major would never let a soul see him looking that rough before he’d done his full beauty routine—years ago, a 10-year-old Pen had gotten in so much trouble for nicking a little morocco box off the Major’s dressing table, which turned out to hold his set of false back teeth, something the Major obviously refused to wear while jolting around in a mail coach, and wouldn’t be seen in public without. Morgan treated his master’s wigs like top-secret state documents, curling them behind closed doors and smuggling them into his room like he was passing off a crown jewel. So the Major trundled straight to his bedroom, groaned, wheezed, cursed Morgan through every step of the toilet routine until his teeth were in, his wig was properly curled, his cravat was tied just right, and he descended the staircase to the drawing room looking every inch the dignified, distinguished man of fashion he was determined to be.

Pen wasn’t in the room, just Helen and little Laura, who was sewing at her feet. The Major, who never had much use for Laura anyway (he had his secret reasons, we’ll get to those later at some point), didn’t even bother to shake her hand—just stuck out a single stiff forefinger for her to touch. Laura took the wobbly offered finger, dropped it immediately, and bolted out of the room as fast as her little legs could carry her.

She found Pen out in the orchard, pacing up and down a path deep in a very heated conversation with his tutor Mr. Smirke. Pen was so wrapped up in talking he didn’t hear Laura yelling his name until Smirke tugged on his coat sleeve and pointed at her running over. She grabbed his hand and said, “C’mon Pen! Uncle Arthur’s here!”

“Is he, now?” Pen said, and Laura felt him squeeze her hand tight. He glared at Smirke like he was ready to take on his uncle, or any man who dared get between him and what he wanted. Smirke, as per usual, rolled his eyes heavenward and sighed softly. “Lead on, Laura,” Pen said, half fierce, half trying not to look nervous. “I’ll go pay my respects to my uncle.” He was laughing to cover up how anxious he was, though—he knew this was the big showdown, the moment he had to defend his grand great love.

See, Pen had spilled every single detail about Miss Emily Fotheringay, the actress, to Smirke over the last 48 hours, nonstop. Smirke, who was a little bit in love himself with the idea of grand romance and had never even been to a play before, ate every word up, and was half convinced that Pen’s plan to marry the 30-something actress at 18 was totally sensible. They could stick her drunk old dad Captain Costigan in the spare yellow bedroom forever, right? How hard could that be? Even better, Pen had nearly talked his own soft mom Helen into going along with it too! He’d parried every one of her objections with that ridiculous indignant “I know better than you” logic only a teen boy can pull off, and Helen was halfway to believing that if the stars were aligned for the match, why fight it? All she cared about was that her boy was happy, and she was dreading the Major turning up and blowing the whole thing up, for all the usual reasons two people look at marriage from totally different points of view: the London fancy set thinks you need a Belgravia house, a tiny dinner party spread, a brougham to drive in Hyde Park, and money stashed away for all the future kids before you even think about marrying, while soft country Helen thought two people in love could make it work on nothing but hope and hard work.

Helen, the soft romantic, had spent years daydreaming about Pen bringing a wife home, and giving up the best bedroom and the head of the table so he could be happy—she thought any woman alive would be lucky enough to marry her perfect boy, even if she was a nobody. But an actress? A woman who’d spent years performing on stage under the gaze of hundreds of strangers, who probably hung around with all kinds of loud, ill-bred theatre folk? That was a hard pill to swallow, but Pen had talked her around so much that she was ready to do almost anything to keep him from being sad. He’d spent one whole evening at Chatteris bragging to Emily about how much influence he had over his mom, and the other night scribbling the most dramatic, over-the-top poetry ever written for her, vowing like the great Montrose that he’d make her famous with his sword and his pen, that no man had ever loved a woman half as much as he loved her.

That midnight, Helen had crept past his bedroom door, seen a light under the crack, and heard him tossing around muttering verses. She snuck in to check on him, like she’d done a hundred times when he was a sick little kid, and found him surrounded by scattered paper, chewing on a pencil, full of Byron, Moore, and every over-the-top romantic poem he’d ever read, fully in the throes of teen dramatic frenzy. He nearly jumped out of his skin when she spoke, hid all his poems under his pillow, and then burst into tears hugging her neck, yelling that he loved Emily more than anything in the world. Of course she coddled him, what else could a soft mom do? Emily didn’t even understand the half of his fancy poetry, but her dad Costigan winked and told her to tuck all the letters away, said Pen’s “pomes” were better than anything ever written by that old fool Poldoody.

So when the Major finally made his grand appearance in the drawing room, Helen grabbed his sleeve immediately, begging him to be gentle with Pen. “He’s got a very high spirit, he won’t stand unkind words!” she said. “Dr. Portman yelled at him the other night, and Pen got so mad it frightened me! He thinks he’s a grown man now, please be careful.”

The Major gallantly kissed her hand, but inside he was rolling his eyes so hard he almost saw his own brain. Good grief, the boy had managed to wrap his own mother right around his little finger! She’d let him have a pet elephant if he cried loud enough for it. “Don’t you worry, my dear,” he said out loud, “I’ll be perfectly gentle. But that boy is not marrying a strolling stage actress. No Pendennis head of the house is going to run off and marry someone from a travelling fair booth, ma’am.”

Helen panicked, said if he broke the news too suddenly Pen would be heartbroken, maybe do something stupid. The Major waved her off, launched into his own tragic life story about how when he was a young penniless lieutenant he’d been in love with a girl with 15 thousand pounds, whose parents made them break up, he’d gone off to India, and they’d both gotten over it just fine. She was now a baronet’s wife with 13 grown kids! He rambled on and on about all the high society horror stories he’d seen: Lord Brouncker got cut by everyone for marrying a druggist’s daughter, Lady Snapperton refused to invite her to a fancy ball, Lord Clodworthy’s actress wife looked old enough to be his mother, Lord Willowbank’s wife kept trying to hang herself out of jealousy of a dancer. Why on earth would you suffer that lifelong embarrassment when you could just break a silly little teen romance off quick?

Helen sighed, agreeing that young unequal marriages were the worst thing in the world, and kept alluding again and again to that sad old family tragedy of Laura’s dad that she couldn’t stop thinking about. Since we’ve brought Laura up so many times, and you’re all probably dying to know who she actually is, we’ll clear that all up nice and easy in the next chapter.
