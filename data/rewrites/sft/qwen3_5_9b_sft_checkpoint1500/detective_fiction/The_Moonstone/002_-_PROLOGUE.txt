These lines, penned far from home amidst the heat and dust of India, are addressed to my kin across the sea. My sole purpose is to lay bare the motive behind my refusal to extend the right hand of friendship to my cousin, John Herncastle. For months, a heavy silence has fallen between us, a reserve that has been mistaken for malice by those who hold my regard in high esteem. I implore you, dear relations, to withhold judgment until you have read the following account. Let me swear, upon my honor, that what follows is not mere speculation, but the strict and literal truth.

The rift between my cousin and me did not spring from petty grievances, but from a great public calamity in which we were both bound: the storming of Seringapatam, under General Baird, on the fourth of May, 1799.

To make sense of what transpired, I must briefly step back in time, to the eve of the assault, and recount the wild tales circulating within our encampment regarding the treasures hidden within the Palace of Seringapatam.

Among these fables, none was more extravagant than the story of the Yellow Diamond, a legendary gem revered in the ancient chronicles of India. Tradition holds that this stone was once embedded in the brow of the four-armed Hindu deity who personifies the Moon. Its hue, combined with a superstitious belief that it somehow shared the life cycle of its divine wearer—growing brighter with the waxing moon and dimming with its waning—earned it the name it bears to this day: *The Moonstone*. A similar, though lesser, mythos once gripped ancient Greece and Rome, where a semi-transparent stone was thought to tremble under lunar influence, earning the same moniker among collectors.

The saga of the Yellow Diamond begins in the eleventh century. At that time, the Muslim conqueror Mahmud of Ghazni swept through India, seizing the holy city of Somnath and stripping its magnificent temple of its riches. Yet, amidst the looting, the moon-god alone remained untouched. Saved by three Brahmin priests, the deity, bearing the Yellow Diamond upon his brow, was secretly spirited away from the desecrated shrine and carried to Benares, the second holiest city.

There, in a newly consecrated hall inlaid with precious stones and roofed with pillars of gold, the moon-god was reinstated. But on the very night the shrine was finished, Vishnu, the Preserver, visited the three guardians in a dream. He breathed his divine essence upon the Diamond, causing the priests to kneel and veil their faces in terror. Their lord commanded that the Moonstone be watched eternally, day and night, by three priests in rotation, passing down through endless generations. He prophesied that any mortal bold enough to seize the sacred gem would invite ruin upon himself, his lineage, and his name. This warning was etched in golden letters above the shrine's gates.

Centuries bled into one. Generation succeeded generation, keeping vigil over their priceless charge. Until the dawn of the eighteenth century, when Aurangzeb, Emperor of the Moghuls, unleashed chaos upon the temples of Brahma. The shrine was profaned, idols shattered, and the Moonstone snatched by a high-ranking officer of the imperial army.

Though powerless to reclaim it by force, the three guardian priests trailed the stolen jewel in disguise. Generations passed; the original thief perished in misery, and the cursed stone drifted from one outlaw's hand to another, always carrying the weight of Vishnu's wrath. Still, the descendants of the guardians kept their watch, waiting for the day destiny would return their gem.

Time marched on through the late eighteenth century until the Diamond fell into the grasp of Tipu Sultan, ruler of Seringapatam. He ordered it affixed to the hilt of a dagger, placing it among his most prized armaments. Yet, even within the Sultan's own palace, the three guardians, disguised as trusted officers of his household, continued their silent vigil. They were strangers to the court, having won favor by outwardly adopting the Muslim faith, but beneath their uniforms lay the old watch.

Thus ran the tale told around our campfires. To most of us, it sounded like nonsense, a fairy tale unworthy of serious thought. My cousin, however, possessed a mind that hungered for the marvelous. On the night before the assault, he grew absurdly incensed at me and others for dismissing the story as fiction. A foolish squabble ensued, fueled by Herncastle's volatile temper. In a boastful fit, he declared that if the English forces conquered Seringapatam, he would wear the Diamond upon his finger. The remark drew a roar of laughter, and so, we believed, the matter ended.

But let us move forward to the day of the storm.

From the start, my path diverged from my cousin's. I did not witness him when we forded the river, nor when we raised the Union Jack atop the first breach, nor as we fought inch by inch into the town. We met again only at dusk, when the city was ours and General Baird himself had discovered the corpse of Tipu Sultan beneath a pile of the slain.

Herncastle and I were assigned to separate parties tasked with preventing the plunder and chaos that inevitably follow a conquest. The camp followers had already committed horrific excesses, but worse still, soldiers had slipped through an unguarded gate into the palace treasury, loading themselves with gold and jewels. It was in the courtyard outside this chaotic vault that we encountered one another, charged with enforcing discipline upon our own men.

I saw plainly that Herncastle's fiery spirit had been pushed past the point of sanity by the sheer horror of the slaughter we had witnessed. He was, in my view, utterly unfit for the solemn duty entrusted to him.

Inside the treasury, there was indeed riot and confusion, yet no overt violence that I observed. The men shamed themselves with a grim, good-humored debauchery, trading rough jests and catchphrases. Then, unexpectedly, the story of the Diamond resurfaced, twisted into a malicious game. "Who hath the Moonstone?" became the rallying cry, driving the looters from one heap of loot to another whenever they paused. As I struggled vainly to impose order, a dreadful yowl echoed from the opposite side of the yard. Terrified of fresh outbreaks of pillaging, I ran toward the noise.

I reached an open doorway and beheld the bodies of two men, dressed as palace officers, sprawled dead across the threshold. A cry from within drove me into a chamber that served as an armoury. There, a third Indian, mortally wounded, slumped at the feet of a man whose back was turned to me. The stranger spun around just as I entered. It was John Herncastle, clutching a torch in one hand and a dagger dripping with blood in the other. As he turned, the stone set like a pommel upon the dagger's hilt caught the torchlight, flashing like a sudden burst of fire.

The dying Indian collapsed to his knees, pointing a trembling finger at the weapon in Herncastle's grip. Speaking in his native tongue, he gasped, "The Moonstone shall have its vengeance yet upon you and yours!" With that, he fell dead upon the floor.

Before I could intervene, the men who had followed me across the courtyard surged into the room. My cousin leaped toward them like a madman. "Clear the room!" he roared at me, "And set a guard on the door!" The soldiers retreated as Herncastle threw himself upon them, brandishing his torch and bloody blade. I quickly stationed two reliable sentinels from my own company to block the exit. For the remainder of that night, I saw not a glimpse of my cousin.

Early the next morning, as the looting continued, General Baird announced by the beating of drums that any thief caught red-handed, whoever he might be, would be hung. The provost-marshal was present to show the General's seriousness. In the thick of the crowd following this proclamation, Herncastle and I met once more.

As was his custom, he extended his hand. "Good morning," he said.

I hesitated before returning the gesture.

"First, tell me," I demanded, "how the Indian in the armoury met his end, and what those final words meant when he pointed to the dagger in your hand."

"The Indian died, I suppose, of a mortal wound," Herncastle replied casually. "As for his last words, I know no more meaning in them than you do."

I scrutinized him closely. The frenzied rage of the previous day had settled, leaving a cold calm in its wake. I resolved to grant him another chance.

"That is all you have to say?" I asked.

His answer was curt. "That is all."

I turned my back upon him. And ever since, we have not spoken a word.

It must be clear that what I write concerning my cousin (unless some urgent necessity demands publication) is intended solely for the information of the family. Herncastle has offered no justification that would permit me to accuse him to our commanding officer. He has been teased repeatedly about the Diamond, recalling his angry outburst prior to the assault, but—as one might imagine—the memory of being caught in the act within the armoury has kept him silent. Rumors swirl that he intends to transfer to another regiment, ostensibly to distance himself from me. Whether this is true remains unknown.
