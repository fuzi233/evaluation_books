She held the spindle as she sat,
Errina with the thick-coiled mat
Of raven hair and deepest agate eyes,
Gazing with a sad surprise
At surging visions of her destiny—
To spin the byssus drearily
In insect-labor, while the throng
Of gods and men wrought deeds that poets wrought in song.

When Daniel Deronda knocked upon the door of his mother's apartment in the *Italia*, a familiar chill of boyhood agitation seized him, stirring memories of early, unearned anxieties. Two servants in the antechamber regarded him with marked surprise; the striking young gentleman who appeared to be the doctor his mistress sought was a startling deviation from expectation, his evening attire lending an accidental grandeur to the severe lines of his countenance. Yet Deronda paid them no heed until the second door swung open, revealing a figure standing at the far end of the vast room, awaiting his approach.

She was draped in black lace, falling loosely from the summit of her whitening hair to a long train that trailed behind her tall form. Her arms, bare to the elbow save for rich bracelets, were folded before her, and the fine poise of her head rendered her visage somehow more handsome than reality warranted. But before Deronda could truly observe her, he stood before her, taking the hand she offered and pressing it to his lips. She retained his grasp, studying him intently, while his chief awareness was struck by the piercing quality of her eyes and the mobile nature of her face, which seemed poised to transform into a stranger in the next instant. Even as she examined him, a play across her brow and nostrils formed a silent, tacit language. Deronda dared not move, unable to fathom what emotional manifestation she required; he felt his cheeks flushing like a girl's, yet marveled at his own lack of profound emotion. After so many idealized imaginings of his mother, this encounter felt curiously unreal. He could not conjecture the language she would use. Suddenly, she released his hand and laid both palms upon his shoulders, her face flashing with an admiration so radiant that every trace of weariness vanished, restoring a youthful bloom.

"You are a beautiful creature!" she said, her voice low and melodious, the syllables possessing a foreign, agreeable outline. "I knew you would be." She kissed him on each cheek, and he returned them, yet the exchange felt akin to a greeting between sovereigns.

She paused as the lines returned to her face, then spoke in a colder tone, "I am your mother. But you can have no love for me."

"I have thought of you more than of any other being in the world," Deronda replied, his voice trembling with nervous intensity.

"I am not like what you thought I was," she stated decisively, withdrawing her hands to fold her arms again, inviting him to scrutinize her. He had often pictured her face as bearing a likeness to his own; he saw traces of it now, but amidst striking differences. She was a remarkable being. What caused his painful sense of aloofness? Her worn beauty possessed a strangeness, as if she were not quite a human mother, but a Melusine bound to some realm independent of theirs.

"I used to think that you might be suffering," Deronda said, driven above all by a fear of wounding her. "I used to wish that I could be a comfort to you."

"I *am* suffering. But with a suffering that you can't comfort," the Princess said, her voice hardening as she moved to a sofa where cushions were carefully arranged. "Sit down." She indicated a seat near her, and sensing distress in his expression, softened, "I am not suffering at this moment. I am at ease now. I am able to talk."

Deronda seated himself, waiting for her to continue. It seemed he stood before a Mysterious Fate rather than the longed-for mother. He watched her with wonder, drawn to the spiritual distance she had erected between them.

"No," she began. "I did not send for you to comfort me. I could not know beforehand—I don't know now—what you will feel toward me. I have not the foolish notion that you can love me merely because I am your mother, when you have never seen or heard of me in all your life. But I thought I chose something better for you than being with me. I did not think I deprived you of anything worth having."

"You cannot wish me to believe that your affection would not have been worth having," Deronda said, noting her pause as if she awaited an answer.

"I don't mean to speak ill of myself," the Princess declared with proud impetuosity, "but I had not much affection to give you. I did not want affection. I had been stifled with it. I wanted to live out the life that was in me, and not to be hampered with other lives. You wonder what I was. I was no princess then." Rising with a sudden movement, she stood as she had before; Deronda rose instantly, feeling breathless.

"No princess in this tame life that I live in now. I was a great singer, and I acted as well as I sang. All the rest were poor beside me. Men followed me from one country to another. I was living a myriad lives in one. I did not want a child."

A passionate self-defense vibrated in her tone. She had discarded precedent entirely, seeking justification only in the most intense words available for her experience. She seemed to fling her final words against any possible reproach in her son's mind, who stood clutching his coat-collar as if clinging to a lifeline, his blood churning with a commotion akin to witnessing a sacred rite of criminality. What else could she tell him? She continued with the same intensity, a pale illumination in her face.

"I did not want to marry. I was forced into marrying your father—forced, I mean, by my father's wishes and commands; and besides, it was my best way of getting some freedom. I could rule my husband, but not my father. I had a right to be free. I had a right to seek my freedom from a bondage that I hated."

Seating herself again, a subtle movement played through her eyes and closed lips, suggesting a suppressed continuation of speech. Deronda remained standing. After a moment, she looked up with a less defiant plea.

"And the bondage I hated for myself I wanted to keep you from. What better could the most loving mother have done? I relieved you from the bondage of having been born a Jew."

"Then I *am* a Jew?" Deronda burst out with deep-voiced energy that made his mother shrink slightly backward against her cushions. "My father was a Jew, and you are a Jewess?"

"Yes, your father was my cousin," she said, watching him with a change in her look, as if she sensed a shadow looming.

"I am glad of it," Deronda exclaimed impetuously, in a voice veiled with passion. He could not have anticipated how he would arrive at such a statement, nor dreamed it would oppose his mother so sharply. He was shaken by a mixed anger, unchecked by reflection, against this mother who seemed to have borne him unwillingly, willingly made herself a stranger, and—perhaps—was now revealing herself unwillingly. This last suspicion flashed an explanation over her speech.

But the mother was equally shaken by an anger differently mixed, her frame unequal to any repression. The shaking visible in her was physical, her eyes appearing larger due to her pallid excitement as she cried violently,

"Why do you say you are glad? You are an English gentleman. I secured you that."

"You did not know what you secured me. How could you choose my birthright for me?" Deronda asked, throwing himself sideways into his chair, leaning his arm over the back, his gaze averted.

An intolerant fire seemed foreign to him, yet he struggled desperately to master himself and remain silent. A horror swept over him, lest he utter something too harsh in this epoch-making moment. A pause ensued before his mother spoke again, her voice becoming more firmly resistant in its finely varied tones:

"I chose for you what I would have chosen for myself. How could I know that you would have the spirit of my father in you? How could I know that you would love what I hated?—if you really love to be a Jew." The last words held such bitterness that any listener might have suspected a hatred arising between mother and son.

Yet Deronda had recovered his fuller self. He recalled his sensibilities to what life had been and was for her, whose best years were gone and whose frame bore signs of suffering as she exerted herself to recount a past that belonged not only to him but to her as well. His habitual shame at accepting events as solely his burden aided him here. As he gazed at his mother silently after her words, his face regained a penetrative calm, yet it exerted a strangely agitating influence over her: her eyes were fixed upon him with a sort of fascination, devoid of maternal delight.

"Forget me, if I speak hastily," he said with diffident gravity. "Why have you resolved now on disclosing to me what you took care to have me brought up in ignorance of? Why—since you seem angry that I should be glad?"

"Oh—the reasons of our actions!" the Princess said, with a ring of sarcastic scorn. "When you are as old as I am, it will not seem so simple a question—'Why did you do this?'
