Significant shifts in Daniel Deronda's fortunes began that same evening following his visit to Chelsea regarding Mirah's public identity. However, for the Meyrick household, the true turning point arrived two days later. At four o'clock, the familiar clatter of wheels halted before the door, followed by a knock and ring that resonated with the weight of social ceremony in a neighborhood usually animated only by the muffin man. Inside, the girls were assembled; the two rooms had been merged to accommodate Kate's latest drawing and a sprawling piece of embroidery that had displaced the satin cushions, serving as a centerpiece for needlework sessions. Mrs. Meyrick leaned over one corner of this tapestry, Mab occupied the center, and Amy worked at the far end. Mirah, whose stitching skills resembled those of a tailor-bird building a nest, acted as the reader. Seated on a camp-stool, she simultaneously served as Kate's model for a title-page vignette depicting a fair public engrossed in the family's literary offerings. As she read Charles Lamb's "Essay on Chimney-Sweepers" with charming distinctness, the room filled with smiles over the "innocent blackness" until the imposing knock shattered the atmosphere.

"My goodness!" exclaimed Mrs. Meyrick. "Is it Lady Mallinger? Does a grand carriage await?"

"No, merely a hansom cab," Kate replied dryly. "Hans insists that the greatest man in London can fit into one."

"Oh, perhaps it is Lord Russell!" cried Mab.

Five pairs of bright eyes turned toward the parlor door, where the old maid-servant emerged carrying a card, leaving it slightly ajar. There stepped a figure markedly unlike the dignified Prime Minister—tall, physically imposing, dressed in kid gloves and kerseymere, with a massive face, flamboyant hair, and gold spectacles. As Mrs. Meyrick glanced at the card, the visitor was revealed: Julius Klesmer.

Even embarrassment could scarcely have rendered the "little mother" so flustered; perceptive as she was, she instantly grasped the situation. She felt a surge of satisfaction that this towering figure had sought her out rather than summoning Mirah to him, interpreting it as a sign of profound, active interest. Yet, the moment Klesmer entered, the cozy rooms seemed to contract into closets. To Mab, the cottage piano became a ridiculous toy, and the entire domestic existence felt as trivial and private as a mouse colony in the Tuileries. Klesmer's persona, particularly his sweeping glance around the room, evoked vast horizons and a multitude of unseen audiences. Perhaps this was indeed the landscape of his consciousness, for everyone carries their thoughts within a habitual locus populated by the presence of others, and those who perceive on a broader scale often appear vain and affected. Klesmer was certainly vain, yet no more so than many heavy-set contemporaries whose vanity springs forth startlingly, like a spear from a cane. His carriage and gestures were as natural to him as the length of his fingers; the most affectation he could display would likely be feigned diffidence. While his grandiose manner made Mab feel like a doll beside the miniature piano, he observed the surroundings with a keen, thoroughly kind sensitivity. Having known a home no larger than this on the outskirts of Bohemia, and having experienced the romance and variety inherent in small incomes, he approached Mrs. Meyrick with utmost deference.

"I hope I have not intruded too freely. Being in the neighborhood, I ventured to save time by calling. My friend, Mr. Deronda, mentioned that I should have the honor of meeting a young lady here—Miss Lapidoth."

Klesmer had indeed spotted Mirah instantly, yet with subtle politeness, he circled the room, bowing at the three sisters as if unsure which one was the intended guest.

"These are my daughters," Mrs. Meyrick indicated, waving her hand toward Mirah. "And this is Miss Lapidoth."

"Ah," Klesmer murmured, his voice brimming with gratified anticipation. He turned a radiant smile and a deep bow toward Mirah. Unlike the others, she was not taken aback; a calm pleasure softened her features. She liked the look in his eyes, sensing that he would treat her with the stern kindness of a great musician.

"You will not mind if we begin our acquaintance with a song?" he asked, aware that preliminaries could be dispensed with quickly.

"I shall be delighted. It is generous of you to listen," Mirah replied, moving toward the piano. "Shall I play my own accompaniment?"

"Certainly," Klesmer agreed, settling himself where Mrs. Meyrick had invited him, ensuring a clear view of the singer. The astute mother did not acknowledge her own vulnerability in wishing to be seen, yet she thought to herself, *"He will appreciate her singing far more if he watches her perform."*

While all the feminine hearts save Mirah's beat rapidly with anxiety, viewing Klesmer as a terrifying judge, Mirah felt strangely at ease. She chose a fine setting of Leopardi's *Ode to Italy*. First came the recitative, *"O patria mia, vedo le mura..."*, followed by the mournful melody *"Ma la gloria... non vedo"*, leading to a climax of devout triumph. From the subdued adoration of the Andante—"Beatissimi voi, che offriste il petto alle nemiche lance"—she moved to the joyful outburst of an exultant Allegro: *"Oh viva, oh viva!"*

When she concluded, Klesmer waited a moment before remarking, "That is Old Leo's music."

"Yes, he was my last master at Vienna. So fierce and so good," Mirah answered with a melancholy smile. "He prophesied that my voice would never suit the stage. And he was right."

"*Con*tinue, if you please," Klesmer urged, puckering his lips and shaking his long fingers, though his articulation remained unintelligible to the listeners.

The three girls detested him unanimously for withholding praise. Mrs. Meyrick grew alarmed. Determined to fulfill Klesmer's request, Mirah imagined he wished to hear German, and she proceeded through Prince Radзивill's arrangements of Gretchen's songs from *Faust*, one after another, without waiting for interrogation. Upon finishing, Klesmer rose and paced to the far end of the room before returning to the piano. Mirah stood near it, hands clasped, awaiting judgment. Suddenly, his brow unknitted, his eyes beamed, and he extended his hand. "Let us shake hands," he declared abruptly. "You are a musician."

Mab felt tears rising, and the girls held Klesmer in awe. Mrs. Meyrick drew a long breath. But instantly, the frown returned. The long hand, palm upturned, reached out again, this time with a fingertip gently touching the back of Mirah's hand, accompanied by a protruded lip. "Not for great tasks. No high roofs. We are no skylarks. We must be modest." He paused. And Mab's adoration vanished; it was as if Mirah had displayed the slightest hint of conceit!

Mirah fell silent, sensing a verdict awaited. Klesmer continued, "I would not advise—and I repeat, I would not encourage you to sing in any space larger than a private drawing-room. But you will excel there. Here in London, that is one of the finest careers available. Lessons will follow. Will you join me for a private concert at my house on Wednesday?"

"Oh, I shall be so grateful," Mirah said, pressing her hands together devoutly. "I would prefer earning my living in that way to anything more public. I will strive to improve. What should I focus on most?"

Klesmer offered a preliminary response in sounds that seemed like words bitten short and swallowed, shaking his fingers rhythmically, before speaking clearly: "I shall introduce you to Astorga. He is the foster-father of good singing and will guide you." Turning to Mrs. Meyrick, he added, "Mrs. Klesmer will call before Wednesday, with your permission."

"We shall consider that a great kindness," Mrs. Meyrick responded.

"You will sing to her," Klesmer instructed, turning back to Mirah. "She is a thorough musician, possessing a soul with more ears than you will often encounter in a professional. Your singing will satisfy her." Quoting Goethe again, he intoned, "'Vor den Wissenden sichstellen.' Do you know the rest?"

"'Sicher ist's in allen Fällen,'" Mirah recited promptly. Klesmer nodded approvingly, "Schön!" and extended his hand once more for farewell.

He had certainly chosen the most delicate method of praise, and the Meyrick sisters now bestowed their highest esteem upon him. Yet, imagine Mab's shock when Klesmer suddenly fixed his gaze upon her and decided, "That young lady is musical, I see!" A flush of burning shame swept over her.

"Yes," Mirah spoke for her. "And she has a touch."
