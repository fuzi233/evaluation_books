The coach wheels bit into the mud of the Strand, a rhythmic, grinding complaint that echoed the weariness of the journey itself. From Piccadilly to Fleet Street, the distance stretched unnaturally long, measured not in miles but in the slush of winter quagmires and the uneven, treacherous pavement. It was a landscape of shadows and lantern-light, where the only illumination came from the flickering torches of link-boys guiding the weary traveler through the dark. For George Warrington, this drive felt as interminable as the march from Marlborough to London earlier that day, a circular progression of fatigue and anticipation.

His mind replayed the correspondence that had precipitated this moment. He had written to Harry, announcing his safe arrival in Bristol, and prior to that, the great news of his own survival and return from captivity. Yet, the letter carrying the intelligence of George's restoration had vanished, swallowed by the chaos of war. Between England and France, privateers swept the seas, seizing merchant vessels within sight of port. That specific missive, bearing the weight of a brother's life restored, had likely fallen into the hands of a French ship among the American fleet. The letter confirming George's actual arrival in England lay unread in Harry's apartments, delivered on the third morning after Harry's imprisonment, only to remain sealed because the angry Mr. Ruff refused to surrender any further items of his lodger's property.

Now, George stood before the door of those very apartments, seeking his brother. The maid-servant who answered recoiled, screaming and retreating to her mistress, terrified by the uncanny resemblance between the two brothers. The mistress, unwilling to admit the truth—that Harry was a prisoner held by her husband—devised a lie. Mr. Warrington had left; she knew nothing of his whereabouts.

George knew the geography of London better than the map suggested. Clarges Street bordered Bond Street. His aunt, Mrs. Bernstein, lived nearby. Perhaps Harry was there, under her care. He recalled the kindness Harry had described in his letters to Virginia, the way even Madam Esmond had been softened by tales of her, particularly by a letter from the Baroness that had once spurred George to rush to Europe. He hoped, with a fervent prayer, that Madam Beatrix had finally found reason to repent of her past transgressions. At her age, surely there was ample cause for contrition. Yet, society remained inexorable; female virtue, especially, forgave no sins committed decades prior, regardless of the order for remission or the handwriting on the floor of the Temple.

Perhaps the Virginian agent in Bristol had fed George fearful stories of Harry's conduct. Gumbo, the negro servant, had emerged from the shock of recognizing his master, recovering quickly enough to stammer out fragments of information regarding Harry's location and pitiable state. This explanation accounted for George's stern demeanor as he presented himself to the old lady. It seemed natural to him that a brother in distress should be rescued by kin. But George, how little he understood the labyrinthine ways of London! A philanthropist attempting to rescue every impoverished soul he met would require the combined wealth of all American provinces.

Yet, as the coach jolted through the dark streets toward the house of his brother's captivity, George reconsidered. He thought of his aunt's behavior. *She feels my poor Harry's misfortune,* he reasoned, chastising his own haste. *I judged too quickly.* Throughout his life, George had frequently rebuked himself for this exact error—the crime of premature judgment. How many shared this flaw? And once the damage was wrought, no repentance could fully mend it.

"Quick, coachman!" George urged, though the vehicle moved with the sluggish inertia of the season. "We are almost as slow as you are getting from Clarges Street to the Temple."

Gumbo knew the path to the bailiff's house intimately. The bell rang again. The first door yielded to George and his attendant, then locked warily behind them, sealing them in a narrow passage guarded by a Jewish janitor. A second door swung open, admitting them into the house proper. The janitor stared, his face illuminated by a flaring tallow-torch, as he beheld a second Mr. Warrington.

"To see that gentleman?" the janitor asked.

"Yes," George replied. "But wait. This is Mr. Warrington's brother from America."

Gumbo retreated to prepare his master, instructing the porter that another gentleman awaited upstairs concerning Mr. W.'s affairs. The room George entered was a small apartment known as Mr. Amos's office. By the light of a guttering candle, a stout gentleman in a cloak and laced hat sat talking to the bailiff. The young porter preceded George, casting sufficient light into the cramped space.

"We are not angry anymore, Harry!" the stout gentleman declared, rising with a cheery voice and extending a hand toward the newcomer. "Thank God, my boy! Mr. Amos assures me there will be no difficulty about James and me acting as your bail. We shall settle your business by breakfast tomorrow. Why... angels and ministers of grace! Who are you?"

He recoiled as the other seized his hand. But the stranger gripped it tighter.

"God bless you, sir!" George said. "I know who you are. You must be Colonel Lambert, whose kindness to my poor Harry my brother wrote of. I am the brother you have heard of, sir—the one left for dead in Mr. Braddock's action, revived after eighteen months among the French, living now to thank God and you for your kindness to Harry." His voice faltered.

"James! James! News!" cried Mr. Lambert to a gentleman in red who entered the room. "The dead are alive! Here comes Harry Scapegrace's brother, and with his scalp intact!" George had removed his hat, standing by the light. "This is my brother-bail, Mr. Warrington! This is Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, at your service. There has been a slight difference between Harry and me, Mr. George. He is pacified, is he, James?"

"He is full of gratitude," Mr. Wolfe responded, bowing to Mr. Warrington.

"Harry wrote home about Mr. Wolfe, too, sir," George added. "I hope my brother's friends will extend the same kindness to me."

"I wish he had none other but us, Mr. Warrington. Poor Harry's fine folks have been too fine for him, ending by landing him here."

"Nay, your honors, I have done my best to make the young gentleman comfortable. Knowing your honor before, when you bailed Captain Watkins, and that your security is impeccable—if your honor wishes, the young gentleman can leave tonight. I shall rectify matters with the lawyer in the morning," said Harry's landlord, recognizing the rank of the two officers offering bail.

"The debt is five hundred and odd pounds, I believe?" George asked.

"With a hundred thanks to these gentlemen, I can pay the amount instantly into the officers' hands, taking the usual acknowledgment and caution. But I can never forget, gentlemen, that you aided my brother in his need. For this, I say thank you, and God bless you, in my mother's name and mine."

Meanwhile, Gumbo ascended to Harry's apartment. Harry, seated over his pipe of Virginia tobacco in a somber mood, would likely have scolded the negro for returning so late. Maria's recent goodness and affection had offered little solace; instead, they had stirred a dread of the consequences to which such fidelity bound him. Then, Mr. Wolfe's homely features and eager, outstretched hand broke the gloom. Hearing of Lambert's presence below and the purpose of their visit, Harry's resistance crumbled. Despite his ill-temper and needless suspicion, the good gentleman was resolved to help, even against Wolfe's counsel.

"For you were wrong, Mr. Warrington," the Colonel said bluntly. "And you would not be set right. You, a young man, used hard words and unkind behavior to your senior, one of the best gentlemen who walks God's earth. Consider his answer to your wayward temper. Will you bear with a friend who speaks frankly? Martin Lambert acts thus always—as the best Christian, the best friend, the most kind and generous of men. If you seek another proof of his goodness, here it is: He has converted me. Though I do not care to disguise that I was angry with you for your treatment of him, he has absolutely brought me down here to be your bail. Let us both cry *Peccavimus!* Harry, shake hands with our friend! He sits in the room below. He would not come until he knew how you would receive him."

"I think he is a good man!" Harry groaned. "I was very angry and wild when we met last, Colonel Wolfe. Perhaps he was right in sending back those trinkets; I was hurt by it. Go down to him, will you be so kind, sir? Tell him I am sorry, ask his pardon—and, God bless him for his generosity."

Turning his head away, Harry rubbed his hand across his eyes.

"Tell him all this yourself, Harry!" the Colonel insisted, taking the young man's hand. "No deputy will say it half so well. Come with me now."
