As the scorching summer sun beat down upon the town, the once-quaint streets now teemed with the hum of discontent. The air was heavy with the whispers of desperation, and the rhythmic chant of the Corn Law Rhymes echoed through the streets:

“But work grew scarce, while bread grew dear,
       And wages lessened, too;
     For Irish hordes were bidders here,
       Our half-paid work to do.”

Inside the Thornton household, Margaret sat in the drawing-room, surrounded by the remnants of the morning's disarray. The Venetian blinds cast a peculiar gloom over the room, as if the very shadows themselves had grown restless. The green-tinged light filtering through the glass created an eerie atmosphere, making Margaret's reflection in the mirror appear wan and spectral. She waited, her eyes fixed on the door, as the distant murmur of a gathering crowd grew louder, only to dissipate into an oppressive silence.

Fanny entered, her face pale and drawn, and whispered to Margaret that her mother would join her shortly. The explanation came in a hesitant tone: her brother, Mr. Thornton, had brought in Irish laborers, a decision that had stirred up the local population, who saw it as an affront to their rights. The workers at the mill refused to work alongside the newcomers, and the tension boiled over into threats and intimidation. The poor Irish immigrants, cowed and frightened, huddled together in the top room of the mill, waiting for someone to intervene.

Mrs. Thornton appeared, her face set in a stern expression, as if she had just received news of a grave misfortune. Her eyes narrowed as Margaret asked about her mother's condition and the doctor's recommendation for a water-bed. The conversation was cut short as Mrs. Thornton's gaze snapped towards the window, where the sound of a gathering crowd grew louder, their voices rising to a deafening roar.

"The gates!" she exclaimed, her voice trembling with anxiety. "Call John, Fanny! Call him in from the mill! They'll break them down!"

As she spoke, the sound of pounding and shouting intensified outside, the wooden gates shuddering beneath the weight of the mob. The women gathered around the windows, transfixed by the chaos unfolding before them. Fanny, overcome with fear, fled upstairs, leaving behind a trail of hysteria. Mrs. Thornton watched anxiously for her son, who emerged from the mill, his face set in a determined smile as he locked the factory door behind him
