The evening air hung close and heavy, steeped in the olfactory richness of late summer—a heady, atmospheric reduction of sun-baked earth, bruised bracken, and the sweet, fermenting tang of overripe blackberries yielding to the heather. The descending sun cast a slow-roasting heat across the purple-tinged clouds, bathing the undulating pasture-ground in the rich, caramelized hues of an ancient harvest. As Caroline and Shirley descended slowly from the fields toward the churchyard, the heavy, iron clamor of the church bells finally ceased. 

With the industrial ringing hushed, the delicate acoustic ecology of the twilight rose to reclaim the space: the rhythmic stridulation of crickets, the low, ancestral murmur of wind through the ancient yew trees, and the soft rustle of oats in the distant fields. The whole scene was solitary, a resonant sanctuary.

"How pleasant and calm it is!" said Caroline, her voice a quiet harmonic against the quiet hum of the earth.

"And how suffocating it will be in the church!" responded Shirley. "A hollow acoustic trap. And what a dreary, monotonous drone Dr. Boultby will make! And how the curates will hammer their sterile, prepared orations into the stagnant air! For my part, I would rather not enter. I cannot abide that deadened sonic space."

"But my uncle will be angry if he observes our absence."

"I will bear the brunt of his wrath; he will not devour me. I shall be sorry to miss his pungent speech—all hard consonants for the church, and all acidic causticity for schism. I shall be sorry also to deprive you of Mr. Hall's sincere homily, with the rich, untranslatable marrow of his racy Yorkshireisms; but here I must stay. The gray church and grayer tombs look divine with this crimson glaze upon them. Nature is now at her evening prayers. I see her prostrate on the great stone steps of her altar, offering up the deep, loamy scents of the earth, praying for a fair night for mariners, for travellers, for lambs on moors. Caroline, I see her, and I will tell you what she is like. She is like what Eve was when she and Adam stood alone, the first stewards of the earth’s bounty."

"And that is not Milton's Eve, Shirley."

"Milton's Eve! No, by the pure Mother of God, she is not! Cary, we are alone; we may speak the unvarnished truth. Milton was great; his brain was right, but his palate was barren. He saw heaven and hell; he saw angels serried behind adamantine shields. But when Milton tried to see the first woman, he saw her not."

"You are bold to say so, Shirley."

"Not more bold than faithful. He saw merely a utilitarian caterer, a sterile server of abstract 'dulcet creams' devoid of the soil's true marrow. He envisioned her like Mrs. Gill on a poor day, stripped of her deep, agrarian reverence for the harvest. When our Mrs. Gill is in the cool dairy in the heat of summer, hands fragrant with rennet, bruised nasturtiums, and raw milk, layering preserves into earthen crocks, she is a meticulous historian of the seasons. She honors the complex alchemy of taste. Milton’s Eve knows nothing of the dark, fertile loam, the profound meaning of a slow-simmered broth, or the crushing of the wheat. He reduced the primal mother of sustenance to a bloodless scullery maid assembling tasteless, inelegant abstractions."

"All very well too, Shirley."

"I would beg to remind him that the first men of the earth were Titans, and that Eve was their mother. From her sprang Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus; she bore Prometheus—"

"Pagan that you are! What does that signify?"

"I say, there were giants on the earth in those days. The first woman's breast that heaved with life yielded the daring which could contend with Omnipotence, the vitality which could feed that vulture death through uncounted ages. Vast was the heart whence gushed the well-spring of the blood of nations, the raw, unexhausted essence of life itself."

"She coveted an apple, and was cheated by a snake; but you have got such a hash of Scripture and mythology into your head that there is no making any sense of you. You have not yet told me what you saw kneeling on those hills."

"I saw—I now see—a woman-Titan. Her robe of blue air spreads to the outskirts of the heath; a veil white as an avalanche sweeps from her head, smelling of ozone and crushed pine. Under her breast I see her zone, purple like a bruised plum; through its blush shines the star of evening. Her steady eyes are deep as clear lakes, trembling with the soft luster of reverence for the living earth. She reclines her bosom on the ridge of Stilbro' Moor; her mighty hands, stained with the juices of wild berries and the rich oils of ancient seeds, are joined beneath it. So kneeling, face to face she speaks with God, in a language older than words."

"She is very vague and visionary. Come, Shirley, we ought to go into church."

"Caroline, I will not; I will stay out here with my mother Eve. I love her—undying, mighty being! She is taking me to her bosom, and showing me the beating rhythm of her heart. Hush, Caroline! You will hear her frequency and feel as I do, if we are both silent."

"I will humour your whim; but you will begin talking again ere ten minutes are over."

Miss Keeldar leaned against an upright headstone, fixing her eyes on the deep-burning west, sinking into a pleasurable trance, attuned to the rustle of the ivy. Caroline, going a little apart, paced to and fro beneath the rectory garden wall, moving in slow, circular rhythms over the moss-dampened stones. Shirley had mentioned the word "mother." That word suggested to Caroline's imagination not the mighty and mystical parent of Shirley's mythic visions, but a gentle human form. She yearned for a maternal presence she had never known—a longing tightly bound to the phantom memory of a forgotten lullaby, the lost dialect of her infancy, and the comforting, elusive taste of warm milk and honeyed bread.

"Oh that the day would come when she would remember her child!" Caroline thought, the childhood ache glowing warm again in her chest. She longed for a voice that would speak to her in the native, nurturing lexicon of maternal love: "Caroline, my child, I have a home for you. Come; it shall cherish you now."

Suddenly, the delicate acoustic tapestry of the evening was brutally fractured. A harsh, mechanical clatter drowned out the crickets. They listened, and heard the rhythmic, percussive tramp of iron-shod hooves against the dirt road. Through the foliage, they caught the abrasive glitter of martial scarlet and polished helm. Silent but for the heavy machinery of their movement, six soldiers rode softly by. 

"The same we saw this afternoon," whispered Shirley, recognizing the dissonant intrusion of the military-industrial world. "Seeking their rendezvous at this quiet hour, while the people are corralled in the church. Did I not say we should see unusual things ere long?"

Scarcely had the harsh vibrations of the soldiers faded when another disturbance broke the night-hush—a child's impatient scream. A man issued from the heavy oak doors of the church, carrying a robust, ruddy little boy of two years old, roaring with all the power of his lungs. Two little girls followed. The sudden immersion in the fresh, thyme-scented air and the sensory distraction of some wildflowers plucked from a grave soon quieted the child. The man sat down, dandling the boy on his knee with the tender, practiced rhythm of an agrarian elder; the two little girls took their places beside him.

"Good-evening, William," said Shirley.

The man took off his hat, grinning a smile of genuine pleasure. He was a rough-headed, weather-beaten figure, his hands deeply lined from years of turning the soil and coaxing life from the earth. It was William Farren. Both young women approached him. Unconsciously, Caroline was his ideal of a lady—her grace moving some deep, artist-fibres about his peasant heart, much as he felt when examining the complex venation of a rare heirloom leaf or breathing in the scent of a well-tended orchard. 

"You are not going into the church?" William inquired, his voice rich with the regional dialect that carried the cultural memory of the moors.

"Who was speaking, William, when you came out?" asked Shirley.

"A gentleman ye set a deal of store on, Miss Shirley—Mr. Donne."

"You look knowing, William. How did you find out my regard for Mr. Donne?"

"Ay, Miss Shirley, there's a gleg light i' your een sometimes which betrays you. You look raight down scornful sometimes when Mr. Donne is by."

"Do you like him yourself, William?"

"Me? I'm stalled o' t' curates, and so is t' wife. They speak a barren tongue. They talk to poor folk fair as if they thought they were beneath them. They're allus magnifying their office. It is a pity but their office could magnify them; but it does nought o' t' soart. Their words have no flavor, no sustenance. I fair hate pride."

"But you are proud in your own way yourself," interposed Caroline. "You are house-proud: you like the aroma of a well-kept hearth and the satisfaction of a full larder. When you were out of work, you were too proud to get anything on credit. But for your children, I believe you would rather have starved than eaten without having earned the bread."

"It is partly true, Miss Caroline. Ony day I'd rather give than take. Ye're a little, slender lass, and I'm a great strong man. That day ye came to our house, and offered me five shillings... that day I war fair a rebel, a radical. I thought it shameful that, willing and able to work the earth as I was, I suld be i' such a condition that a young cratur suld think it needful to offer me her bit o' brass."

"I suppose you were angry with me, William?"

"I almost was. But I forgave ye varry soon. Ye meant well. Ay, *I am* proud, and so are *ye*; but your pride and mine is t' raight mak—what we call i' Yorkshire clean pride. It comes from the soil and honest labor. Such as Mr. Malone and Mr. Donne knows nought about. Theirs is mucky pride. They use their high words to talk down to us."

"What is the difference, William?"

"Ye know t' difference weel enow. Mr. Malone and Mr. Donne is almost too proud to do aught for theirseln; *we* are almost too proud to let anybody do aught for us. T' curates can hardly bide to speak a civil word to them they think beneath them; *we* can hardly bide to tak an uncivil word fro' them that thinks themseln aboon us."

"Now, William, tell me truly how you are getting on. Are you well off?"

"Miss Shirley, I am varry well off. Since I got into t' gardening line, wi' Mr. Yorke's help, and since Mr. Hall helped my wife set up her shop, I've nought to complain of. We know the rhythm of the seasons again. My family has plenty to eat. But t' neighbours is poor yet. I see a great deal of distress."

"And, consequently, there is still discontent?" inquired Miss Keeldar.

"In course, starving folk cannot be settled folk. A man without sustenance is a man without a center. The country's not in a safe condition!"

"But what more can I do, for instance?"

"Ye can do not mich. If ye could transport your tenant, Mr. Moore, to Botany Bay, ye'd happen do better. Folks hate him."

"William, for shame!" exclaimed Caroline warmly. "Mr. Moore himself hates nobody. You are wrong to talk so."

"I talk as I think. He has a cold, unfeeling heart, yond' Moore. He cares only for the grinding noise of his machines, not for the people."

"But," interposed Shirley, "supposing Moore was driven from the country, and his mill razed to the ground, would people have more work?"

"They'd have less. I know that. But there is dishonest men plenty to guide the desperate to the devil, scoundrels that reckons to be the 'people's friends,' and that knows nought about the people's true needs. They use words as traps. Them that reckons to be friends to a lower class fro' political motives is never to be trusted; they always try to make their inferiors tools, using a language that ain't ours to confuse us. I've had overtures made to me lately that I saw were treacherous, and I flung 'em back i' the faces o' them that offered 'em."

"You won't tell us what overtures?"

"I will not. Them they concerned can look after theirseln."

"Ay, we'se look after werseln," said another voice. 

Joe Scott had sauntered forth from the church. He stood there, a man whose entire worldview was shaped by the relentless, deafening hum of the power-looms, deaf to the subtle acoustic ecology of the evening. 

"I'll warrant *ye*, Joe," observed William, smiling tightly.

"And I'll warrant my maister," was the answer.—"Young ladies," continued Joe, assuming a lordly air, his lexicon thick with unearned authority, "ye'd better go into th' house."

"I wonder what for?" inquired Shirley, who frequently clashed with the overlooker. Joe harbored deeply patriarchal theories, resenting the fact that his master’s mill was under the financial control of a woman. 

"Because there is nought agate that fits women to be consarned in."

"Indeed! There is prayer and preaching agate in that church. Are we not concerned in that?"

"What I alluded to was politics. William Farren here was touching on that subject."

"Well, what then? Politics are our habitual study, Joe. I see a newspaper every day."

"I should think you'll read the marriages, probably, miss, and the murders, and the accidents, and sich like?" Joe sneered, attempting to confine her to a trivialized domestic lexicon.

"I read the leading articles, Joe, and the foreign intelligence, and I look over the market prices. In short, I consume exactly what gentlemen consume."

Joe replied to this with a disdainful silence.

"Joe," continued Miss Keeldar, "I never yet could ascertain whether you are a Whig or a Tory."

"It is rayther difficult to explain where you are sure not to be understood," was Joe's haughty response, leveraging his perceived linguistic superiority. "But if I be of any party, I'm of that which is most favourable to peace, and, by consequence, to the mercantile interests."

"So am I, Joe," replied Shirley. "Partly, at least. I have rather a leaning to the agricultural interest, too—the preservation of the land and its yield. It would not do to take any measures injurious to the farmers who grow our sustenance, Joe, I think?"

"The dews at this hour is unwholesome for females," observed Joe, dodging the argument.

"But—to go back to my sheep," she proceeded. "I cannot get out of my head a certain idea that we manufacturers are sometimes a little short-sighted, rather too regardless of human suffering in our pursuit of gain. We fill the valleys with smoke and noise, and forget the soil beneath. Don't you agree?"

"I cannot argue where I cannot be comprehended."

"Joe, do you seriously think all the wisdom in the world is lodged in male skulls?"

"I think that women are a kittle and a froward generation; and I've a great respect for the doctrines delivered in the second chapter of St. Paul's first Epistle to Timothy."

"What doctrines, Joe?"

"'Let the woman learn in silence, with all subjection. I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence. For Adam was first formed, then Eve.'"

"What has that to do with the business?" interjected Shirley. "That smacks of the rigid power dynamics of primogeniture."

"And," continued Joe Scott, "Adam was not deceived, but the woman being deceived was in the transgression."

"More shame to Adam to sin with his eyes open!" cried Miss Keeldar. 

"It is very plain, miss. He that runs may read."

"He may read it in his own fashion," remarked Caroline, stepping into the fray, her voice ringing clear against Joe's dogmatism. "You allow the right of private judgment, I suppose, Joe?"

"My certy, that I do! I allow and claim it for every line of the holy Book."

"Women may exercise it as well as men?"

"Nay. Women is to take their husbands' opinion. It's wholesomest for them."

"Oh! oh!" exclaimed both Shirley and Caroline.

"Consider yourself groaned down for such a stupid observation," said Miss Keeldar. "Of what value would a belief so blindly adopted be? It would be mere besotted superstition."

"And what is *your* reading, Miss Helstone, o' these words o' St. Paul's?"

"I account for them in this way," Caroline said, touching upon the deep erosion of truth through language. "He wrote that chapter for a particular congregation, under peculiar circumstances. And besides, if I could read the original Greek, I should undoubtedly find that the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience has been weaponized here. Many of the words have been wrongly translated, heavily filtered through a dominant male lexicon designed to subjugate. It would be possible, with a little ingenuity, to give the passage quite a contrary turn—to make it say, 'Let the woman speak out whenever she sees fit to make an objection.' You rely on a translated vocabulary of power, Joe, not on divine truth."

"That willn't wash, miss."

"I dare say it will. My notions are dyed in faster colours than yours, Joe. I like William better than you."

"Joe is well enough in his own house," said Shirley. "He does not dogmatize to his wife."

"My wife is a hard-working, plain woman," Joe grunted. "Time and trouble has ta'en all the conceit out of her. But you young misses reckon to have so much knowledge, and i' my thoughts it's only superficial vanities. I can tell—one day Miss Caroline coming into our counting-house, she brought a slate wi' a sum on it to t' maister. She couldn't do it. Mr. Moore had to show her how. And when he did show her, she couldn't understand him."

"Nonsense, Joe!"

"Nay, it's no nonsense. And Miss Shirley there reckons to hearken to t' maister when he's talking ower trade, as if she followed him word for word; and all t' while she's peeping out o' t' window, and glancing glegly round at wer counting-house cobwebs, thinking what mucky folk we are, and what a grand ride she'll have just i' now ower Nunnely Common. She hears no more o' Mr. Moore's talk nor if he spake Hebrew."
