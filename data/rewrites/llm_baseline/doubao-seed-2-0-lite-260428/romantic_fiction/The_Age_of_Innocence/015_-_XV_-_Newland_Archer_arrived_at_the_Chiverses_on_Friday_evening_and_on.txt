Newland Archer arrived at the Chiverses’ door at Highbank on that crisp Friday evening, and for the whole of Saturday he moved through the expected week-end rituals as if wading through warm honey, half his attention already drifting far from the crowd around him. He spun across frozen lake ice in the hostess’s sail boat with the other hardy, red-faced guests, the wind stinging his cheeks until his eyes watered, and he scarcely registered the thrill of the speed. He tramped the farm paths with Reggie Chivers in the afternoon, nodding politely through long, meandering lectures on horse conformation in the elaborately carved stalls of the stables, not hearing a single word of the technical jargon. After tea, he sat in a shadowed corner of the fire-lit hall listening to a young society girl gush over her own upcoming marriage, the same girl who’d cried publicly that his engagement to May would break her heart only a month prior. By midnight, he was drawn into the usual silly pranks: tucking a goldfish bowl into a visiting aunt’s bed, dressing a friend up as a bumbling burglar to jump out and scare that same nervous old aunt in the bathroom, and tumbling through a raucous pillow fight that spilled from the top-floor nurseries all the way down to the basement.

By Sunday, the second the last fork was set down after luncheon, he borrowed a sharp little one-horse cutter and urged the mare into a fast trot toward Skuytercliff.

The old Van der Luyden estate had always been passed off as a proper Italian villa to New York society, a story believed just as strongly by many travelers who’d actually visited Italy as those who’d never left the Hudson valley. It was a great square wooden house, tongued-and-groove walls painted soft pale green and white, topped with a grand Corinthian portico, fluted pilasters standing stiff between every window. From the high rise of land it stood on, a series of balustraded terraces dotted with marble urns sloped down in the stiff, formal style of a steel engraving to a small, irregular lake edged in asphalt, overhung with rare, drooping evergreen trees. On either side, famously weedless lawns dotted with “specimen” trees each of a distinct, exotic variety rolled away toward stretches of long grass crowned with ornate cast-iron ornaments, and far below in a huddle at the bottom of the hill sat the tiny four-room stone cottage the very first Patroon had built on the land granted to him in 1612.

Against the unbroken sheet of winter snow and the soft grey overcast sky, the so-called Italian villa loomed almost grim, severe even in high summer when no bright coleus bed ever dared to plant itself closer than thirty feet to its intimidating front door. When Archer rang the bell, the high, clear tinkle echoed so far down empty halls it felt as if he’d rung at the door of a mausoleum. The butler who finally pulled the heavy oak open looked as shocked as if he’d been woken from his own grave.

Luckily, Archer was considered family, so no questions were raised at his unannounced arrival, and the butler informed him Countess Olenska was out, having driven to afternoon church service with Mrs. Van der Luyden exactly three quarters of an hour prior. “Mr. Van der Luyden is in, sir,” the butler added, “but I believe he is just finishing his nap, or else catching up on yesterday’s Evening Post. He said after morning service he intended to read the paper all through luncheon. If you’d like, sir, I can tiptoe to the library door and listen to see if he’s awake—”

Archer thanked him and waved the offer away, saying he would simply walk out to meet the ladies on their way back. The butler looked so obviously relieved not to have to disturb his employer that he closed the door behind Archer with a stately, grand finality.

A groom led the cutter away to the stables, and Archer cut straight through the snow-dusted park toward the main high road. Skuytercliff village was only a mile and a half away, but he knew Mrs. Van der Luyden never walked a step if she could ride, so he kept to the roadway to intercept their carriage. Before long, however, he spotted a small, slight figure in a bright red cloak coming down a footpath that crossed the highway, a great shaggy dog bounding ahead of her through drifts. He hurried forward, and Madame Olenska stopped short, a bright, surprised smile spreading across her face.

“Ah, you’ve come!” she said, pulling her hand out of her fur muff to meet his.

The red cloak made her look gay and vivid, the wild, untamed Ellen Mingott he’d remembered as a bright, mischievous little girl. He laughed as he wrapped his gloved fingers around hers. “I came to see what you were running away from.”

Her face clouded over for a heartbeat, but she answered, “Ah, well—you will see, presently.”

Puzzled, he leaned a little closer. “Wait—do you mean whoever it is has already caught up to you?”

She shrugged her shoulders with a small, familiar movement he’d once seen her Russian maid make back at her New York home, then laughed to lighten the tone. “Shall we walk on? My feet are half frozen after that long sermon. And what does it matter, now you’re here to protect me?”

Warm blood rushed up to his temples, and he caught a loose fold of her cloak. “Ellen—what is it? You have to tell me.”

“Oh, not yet—let’s run a race first, my toes are freezing solid to the ground,” she cried, gathering up the hem of her cloak and darting off across the snow, the dog leaping around her, barking playfully as he chased her heels. Archer stood still for a moment, watching that flash of red burn bright against the endless white, delight warming his chest, before he broke into a run after her. They collided, panting and laughing, at a small wicket gate leading back into the park’s grounds.

She looked up at him, cheeks pink from the cold, and smiled. “I knew you’d come!”

“That only proves you wanted me to,” he returned, absurdly, disproportionately giddy over their silly, meaningless banter. The bare boughs of the snow-laden trees glittered around them, spilling a soft, mysterious brightness through the air, and the packed snow under their boots seemed to hum softly as they walked.

“Where did you come from?” Madame Olenska asked him.

He told her about the Chiverses’ week-end, and added, “I came because I got your note.”

There was a faint, perceptible chill in her voice when she spoke again. “May asked you to take care of me.”

“I didn’t need any asking.”

“You mean—I’m so obviously helpless and defenseless? What a sad, pitiable thing you must all think me! But none of the women here ever seem to need protecting, the way you’d need it if the world was chasing you. They’re as safe and unneeding as the blessed souls in heaven.”

He lowered his voice, leaning in a little. “What kind of need are you talking about, Ellen?”

“Ah, don’t ask me! I don’t speak your language,” she retorted, a little petulant.

The words hit him like a sharp blow, and he froze in the path, staring down at her. “Then what am I doing here, if I don’t speak yours?”

“Oh, my friend—” She laid her hand lightly on his arm, and he pleaded earnestly, “Ellen—why won’t you tell me what’s happened, what’s bothering you?”

She shrugged again. “Does anything ever happen in heaven, anyway?”

They walked on in silence for a few dozen yards, until she spoke. “I will tell you—but where? Where on earth can we talk? That great big house up there is like a seminary, all the doors flung wide open, every minute a servant popping in with tea, or a new log for the fire, or a newspaper. Is there nowhere in an American house where a person can be alone for five minutes? You’re all so shy about showing emotion, and yet every single part of your lives is on public display. I keep feeling like I’m back in the convent again— or on a theater stage, standing in front of a dreadfully polite audience that never claps.”

“Ah, you don’t like us!” Archer exclaimed, stung.

They were just passing the tiny old Patroon cottage now, its squat stone walls and small square windows clustered tight around a central chimney. The shutters were propped wide open, and through one of the newly washed panes, Archer caught the warm glow of a kitchen fire.

“Why—this little house is open!” he said.

Madame Olenska stopped short. “Only for today, at least. I wanted to see it, so Mr. Van der Luyden had them light a fire and air it out this morning, so we could stop in on our way back from church.” She ran up the low steps and jiggled the door handle. It turned freely. “It’s still unlocked—how lucky! Come in, we can talk in peace. Mrs. Van der Luyden drove over to visit her elderly aunts in Rhinebeck, no one will notice we’re gone back at the big house for at least an hour.”

He followed her through the narrow little front passage, and his earlier melancholy lifted in an irrational leap of joy. The cozy little cottage felt like it had been conjured by magic just for them, its dark wood panels and polished brass fixtures glinting in the firelight. A great bed of embers still glowed in the kitchen hearth under an iron pot hanging from an ancient iron crane. Two rush-bottomed armchairs faced each other across the tiled hearth, rows of hand-painted Delft plates lined shelves nailed to the walls. Archer leaned down to toss a new log on the embers, sending sparks dancing up the chimney.

Madame Olenska dropped her red cloak on a chair and sank into one of the armchairs by the fire. Archer leaned against the mantel, looking down at her. “You’re laughing now, but when you wrote me that note, you sounded so unhappy,” he said.

“Yes.” She paused, her eyes soft in the firelight. “But I can’t feel unhappy when you’re here.”

“I won’t be here long,” he returned, his lips going stiff with the effort to hold himself back, to not say the words he’d been turning over in his head for weeks.

“No; I know. But I’m a foolish, improvident person— I only live for the moment when I’m happy.”

The words stole through him like a temptation he had no defense against, and he stepped away from the hearth to stare hard out the front window at the dark tree trunks standing out against the white snow. Even with his back half turned, he could still see her, leaning forward over the fire with that soft, lazy smile of hers, between him and the glass. His heart was beating so hard it felt like it would burst right through his ribs. What if she had been running away not from a man, but from him? What if she had waited to tell him that, right here in this secret little room, just the two of them?

“Ellen, if I’m really a help to you—if you truly wanted me to come—tell me what’s wrong, tell me what it is you’re running away from,” he insisted, not moving from his spot by the window, not even turning to look at her. If that unspoken, impossible thing between them was going to happen, it would happen this way, with the whole width of the small room between them, his eyes still fixed on the snow outside.

For a long moment she was silent, and in that quiet, Archer could almost imagine her, could almost feel her, stealing up behind him to wrap her light, soft arms around his neck. He waited, every nerve thrumming with the miracle he thought was about to happen, when his eyes caught sight of a heavily coated man with his fur collar turned all the way up to his ears, tramping up the snowy path toward the cottage.

It was Julius Beaufort.

Archer burst out in a sharp, bitter laugh.

Madame Olenska jumped to her feet and darted to his side, slipping her hand tight into his, but one glance through the window made her face go deathly pale, and she shrank back a step.

“So that was what you were running from?” Archer said, his voice sharp and derisive.

“I didn’t know he was here,” Madame Olenska murmured, her hand still clinging to his. But Archer pulled gently away from her, walked out into the passage, and threw the cottage front door wide open.

“Hallo, Beaufort—over here! Madame Olenska was just expecting you,” he called.

On his train ride back to New York the next morning, Archer re-lived every last minute of that hour at Skuytercliff, the memory wearing on him so sharply he felt exhausted. Beaufort, clearly annoyed to find Archer alone with Madame Olenska, handled the situation with his usual brash, high-handed ease. His habit of simply ignoring people who inconvenienced him made Archer feel almost invisible, like a ghost walking beside them as the three of them tramped back through the snow toward the big house. Humiliating as that invisibility was to his pride, it let him watch and observe without being noticed. Beaufort was smiling and casual as ever, but the deep vertical line between his eyebrows refused to smooth out. It was painfully clear Madame Olenska had not known he was coming, even if she’d hinted to Archer that someone might turn up; she’d obviously not told him where she was going when she left New York, and her unexplained disappearance had left him seething with frustration. His stated reason for showing up was that he’d stumbled on a “perfect little house” the night before, not even listed for sale, that was exactly the right size for her, that someone else would snap it up instantly if she didn’t put an offer in that day. He complained loudly, mock-reproaching her for leading him on such a wild goose chase running off to the country right when he’d found the perfect place for her.

“If only this newfangled telephone they keep talking about was a little bit more reliable, I could have told you all this from town, and been toasting my feet in front of my club fire right now, instead of tramping through all this snow after you,” he grumbled, hiding his very real irritation under a veneer of playful complaining. At that, Madame Olenska smoothly steered the conversation to the absurd, fantastic idea that one day people might be able to talk to each other across the streets, or even — impossible as it sounded — across entire states. The three of them traded references to Edgar Allan Poe and Jules Verne, making small, safe platitudes about the wild new invention, and the topic of the telephone carried them all the way back to the big villa without any more awkward moments.

Mrs. Van der Luyden had not yet returned when they got back. Archer said his goodbyes and walked off to fetch his cutter, while Beaufort followed Madame Olenska indoors. Archer knew perfectly well the Van der Luydens barely tolerated unannounced visitors, but Beaufort would no doubt wheedle an invitation to stay for dinner, then catch the nine o’clock train back to the city. There was no chance he’d be asked to spend the night; the Van der Luydens would never imagine a gentleman would turn up to their estate with no luggage and expect a bed, and they would never offer such hospitality to someone on such limited, formal terms as Beaufort. Beaufort knew all that, too, so the fact that he’d made the long, cold journey just for that tiny sliver of time alone with Ellen showed just how impatient and determined he was. There was no question he was openly chasing the Countess Olenska. His dull, childless home had bored him for years, and on top of his other long-term affairs, he was always hunting for a new amorous adventure among the families of New York high society. That was the man Ellen was running from. The only question left hanging in Archer’s mind was whether she’d fled because his advances disgusted her, or because she didn’t trust herself to resist them — or if all her talk of running away had just been a trick, and she’d left New York intentionally to meet him.

Archer didn’t truly believe the last part. Even though he’d only known Ellen for a few weeks, he thought he could read the shifts in her face, the nuances of her voice, and both had shown real, unfeigned dismay when Beaufort appeared out of the snow. But then again, he thought bitterly, wasn’t that worse? If she’d run away just to meet him, she’d be no more interesting than any other silly woman sneaking around for an affair. But if she knew Beaufort was vulgar, uneducated, and arrogant with his money, and still found herself drawn to him anyway, because he knew the wider world she’d come from, because he didn’t care about all the stupid, petty prejudices that bound New York society tight — how could any woman who’d lived across two continents not feel that pull? Ellen had told him, angry, that they didn’t speak the same language, and Archer knew that was true. But Beaufort spoke her language perfectly, his whole worldview just a coarser, cruder version of the same life she’d lived with her husband in Europe. Archer knew she thought she’d turned her back on all that old, messy European life — but the parts that had charmed her there would still charm her, even against her will.

He walked home through the next days turning the facts over in his head, with painful, impartial clarity, making the case for why Ellen might be drawn to Beaufort anyway. The urge to pull her aside, to warn her, to enlighten her about what Beaufort really was, burned hot in his chest, and more than once he imagined all she wanted was for him to be the one to pull her away from that temptation.

That night, he unpacked the crate of new books he’d ordered from London. There was a new volume of Herbert Spencer’s essays, a new collection of Alphonse Daudet’s sharp, sparkling short stories, and a novel called *Middlemarch* that all the literary reviews had been raving about lately. He’d turned down three separate dinner invitations just to spend the evening curled up with his new books, but though he turned the pages with the soft, loving delight of a lifelong book lover, he couldn’t absorb a single paragraph. Book after book slipped from his fingers onto the couch. Then, under the stack, he found a small thin volume of poetry he’d ordered just because the name on the spine had caught his eye: *The House of Life*.
