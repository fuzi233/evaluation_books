Lieutenant Sutch was the first guest to arrive at Broad Place, reaching the estate around five o'clock on a sun-drenched afternoon in mid-June. The old red-brick mansion, perched on a southern slope of the Surrey hills, glowed warmly against the dark, pine-clad depths of the surrounding forest, resembling a rare jewel warmed by the sun.

Sutch limped across the hall, where the portraits of the Feversham lineage ascended one above another toward the ceiling, and stepped out onto the stone-flagged terrace at the rear. There he found his host sitting upright as a boy, gazing southward toward the Sussex Downs.

"How's the leg?" General Feversham asked, rising briskly from his chair. He was a small, wiry man; despite his white hair, he possessed an alertness that was purely physical. His face was bony, framed by a high, narrow forehead and steel-blue, inexpressive eyes that suggested a barrenness of mind.

"It gave me trouble during the winter," Sutch replied. "But that was to be expected."

Feversham nodded, and for a moment, both men fell silent. From the terrace, the land sloped steeply down to a vast plain of brown earth and emerald fields, dotted with dark clusters of trees. Through the sunlight, voices drifted up—small but distinct. In the distance, near Horsham, a coil of white smoke from a passing train snaked rapidly among the trees, while on the horizon, the Downs rose, patched with white chalk.

"I thought I should find you here," Sutch said.

"It was my wife's favorite corner," Feversham answered in a voice devoid of emotion. "She would sit here by the hour. She had a peculiar fondness for wide, empty spaces."

"Yes," Sutch agreed. "She had imagination. Her thoughts could populate them."

Feversham glanced at his companion as if he barely comprehended the remark, yet he offered no questions. Whatever he failed to understand, he habitually dismissed as unworthy of comprehension. Instead, he shifted topics immediately.

"There will be a vacancy at our table tonight."

"Yes. Collins, Barberton, and Vaughan left this winter. Well, we are all permanently relegated to the world's half-pay list anyway. The obituary column is merely the final formality that gazettes us out of the service entirely," Sutch said, stretching to ease his crippled leg—a limb crushed and twisted fourteen years prior during the fall of a scaling ladder.

"I am glad you arrived before the others," Feversham continued. "I wish to take your opinion. Today holds more weight for me than the anniversary of our assault on the Redan. At the exact moment we stood under arms in the dark—"

"To the west of the quarries; I recall," Sutch interrupted, drawing a deep breath. "How could one forget?"

"At that precise moment, Harry was born in this house. Therefore, I thought that if you did not object, he might join us tonight. He is currently at home. Naturally, he intends to enter the service, and perhaps he will learn something useful along the way—one never knows."

"By all means," Sutch replied with alacrity. Since his visits to General Feversham were restricted to these anniversary dinners, he had never yet met Harry Feversham. For many years, Sutch had been puzzled by the qualities in the General that had attracted Muriel Graham—a woman renowned equally for the refinement of her intellect and the beauty of her person—and he could never decipher the mystery. He had to settle for knowing that for some inexplicable reason, she had married a man so much older than herself and so diametrically opposed to her in character. Personal courage and an indomitable self-confidence were the only traits that surfaced in General Feversham.

Sutch retreated into memory, sitting in his garden chair, recalling a season in London following his return from the China station, before he had participated as an officer of the Naval Brigade in that disastrous charge on the Redan. He was genuinely curious to observe Harry Feversham. He did not admit that this curiosity stemmed from more than the natural fascination of a man disabled in his youth who had made the study of human nature his hobby. He simply wanted to know if the boy resembled his mother or his father—that was all.

That evening, Harry Feversham took his place at the dinner table, listening to the tales recounted by his elders, while Lieutenant Sutch observed him intently. The stories were all drawn from that grim winter in the Crimea, and a new tale was always spun before the previous one concluded. They were narratives of death, hazardous feats, the bite of famine, and the chill of snow. Yet, they were delivered in clipped words and a matter-of-fact tone, as though the storytellers viewed these events as distant occurrences. Rarely was there a comment sharper than a mere "That's curious," or an exclamation more significant than a casual laugh.

However, Harry Feversham sat listening as though the incidents being casually narrated were occurring right then and there within the walls of the room. His dark eyes—the eyes of his mother—followed each speaker, wide-open and fixed, waiting until the very last word was spoken. He listened, fascinated and entranced. The expressions shifting across his face were so vivid that it seemed to Sutch the boy must actually hear the drone of bullets overhead, actually withstand the stunning impact of a charge, actually ride down into the thick of a squadron where cannons screamed tongues of flame through the fog.

Once, a Major of Artillery described the agonizing suspense of the hours between parading the troops before a battle and the first command to advance. Harry's shoulders tensed under the intolerable strain of those dragging minutes. But he did more than tense his shoulders. He cast a single, furtive, wavering glance backward.

Lieutenant Sutch was startled, indeed more than startled—he was pained. After all, this was Muriel Graham's boy. The look was too familiar to Sutch to misunderstand. He had witnessed it on the faces of recruits during their first exposure to battle too frequently to ignore it. A specific image rose before his mind: an advancing square at Inkermann, a tall, large soldier rushing forward from the line in the eagerness of his attack, then stopping abruptly as if suddenly realizing he was alone, forced to face the charge of a mounted Cossack by himself. Sutch remembered clearly the fatal, wavering glance that soldier had thrown backward toward his comrades—a glance accompanied by a strange, sickly smile. He recalled, with equal vividness, the consequence. Though the soldier carried a loaded musket with a bayonet fixed to the muzzle, he had succumbed without a struggle, receiving the Cossack's lance-thrust in the throat.

Sutch glanced hurriedly around the table, fearing that General Feversham or one of his guests had noticed the same look and smile on Harry's face. But no one paid the boy any attention; every visitor was too eager to seize an opportunity to tell their own story.

Sutch drew a breath of relief and turned to Harry. But the boy was sitting with his elbows on the tablecloth and his head propped between his hands, lost in the glare of the room and its glittering silverware. He was reconstructing, out of the rapid succession of anecdotes, a world of screams, wounds, maddened riderless chargers, and men writhing in a fog of cannon smoke. Even the most cursory, least graphic description of the biting days and nights in the trenches set the boy shivering. His face grew pinched, as though the iron frost of that winter was literally gnawing into his bones.

Sutch touched him lightly on the elbow. "You renew those days for me," he said. "Though the heat drips down the windows, I feel the chill of the Crimea."

Harry roused himself from his absorption. "The stories renew them," he replied. "No. It is you listening to the stories."

Before Harry could respond, General Feversham's voice cut sharply from the head of the table: "Harry, look at the clock!"

Immediately, all eyes turned to the boy. The clock hands formed the sharpest angle possible. It was close to midnight. From eight o'clock, without uttering a single word or asking a question, he had remained seated at the dinner table. Yet, even now, he rose with evident reluctance.

"Must I go, father?" he asked.

General Feversham's guests interjected in a chorus. "The conversation is beneficial for the lad; it is his first taste of powder that might serve him well later," the Major of Artillery added. "Besides, it is the boy's birthday. He wishes to stay; that is plain. You wouldn't find a fourteen-year-old sitting these hours without kicking the table leg unless the conversation entertained him. Let him stay, Feversham!"

For once, General Feversham relaxed the iron discipline under which the boy lived. "Very well," he said. "Harry shall have an hour's furlough from his bed. A single hour makes little difference."

Harry's eyes turned to his father, resting upon his face with a curious, steady gaze. It seemed to Sutch that they posed a question, and, rightly or wrongly, he translated the query into words: "Are you blind?"
