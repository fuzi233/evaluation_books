No sooner had Mrs. Dashwood sealed and dispatched her reply accepting Sir John Middleton’s offer of Barton Cottage, than she could not hold back her bright, relieved news any longer. She announced to her stepson John Dashwood and his wife, alongside quiet Edward Ferrars who was staying with them, that she had secured a home at last, and would not be a burden on their household a single day longer than it took to pack their belongings and move.
The pair stared back in unmasked surprise. Mrs. John Dashwood (Fanny, as everyone who knew her well named her) said not a single word, her lips pressing thin, but her husband made a show of civil courtesy, saying he hoped they would not settle too far from Norland Park for easy visits. Mrs. Dashwood smiled, brimming with quiet satisfaction, and answered that they were bound for Devonshire.
Edward turned toward her in that instant so fast his half-full teacup nearly sloshed over its rim. His face was soft with unmistakeable, startled concern, no explanation needed for Elinor, who watched him from across the room, to understand he was thinking of the miles that would stretch between them. “Devonshire!” he repeated, his voice quiet and warm with dismay. “Are you truly going there? So very far from here? What part of the shire do you mean?”
She explained the location: the small cottage stood four miles north of Exeter, tucked into the rolling green Devonshire hills. “It is only a little place,” she went on, “but there is more than enough room for those I love. We can add an extra two rooms easily enough, and any friend who is willing to travel so far to see us will never want for a warm welcome, or a bed, or a place at our table.”
She finished with a kind invitation for Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to come stay at Barton as soon as they were settled, and then turned to Edward with an even softer, more affectionate invitation that left no room for protest. Though her recent sharp conversation with Fanny had convinced her to leave Norland as quickly as possible, it had not changed her resolve a single bit: she would never do anything to separate Elinor and Edward, and this open, pointed invitation to Fanny’s own brother was her quiet way of showing Fanny that her petty disapproval of the match meant nothing at all.
John Dashwood reiterated over and over how terribly sorry he was that they had chosen a home so far away, that he would not be able to lend a hand moving all their furniture. He truly did feel quietly, conveniently vexed: the small, token act of help he had limited himself to promising his father on his deathbed was now perfectly, comfortably impossible to fulfill.
All their belongings were loaded onto a barge to be sent west by water: stacks of soft household linen they had used for years, the family silver, well-loved chipped china, shelves of treasured books, and the handsome new pianoforte Marianne had saved nearly a year of her pin money to buy. Fanny watched every crate carted off with a dramatic little sigh, deeply resentful that Mrs. Dashwood, with her tiny, modest income compared to their own, should be allowed to keep such fine, expensive things for herself and her girls.
Mrs. Dashwood had taken the cottage on a full twelve-month lease, it came fully furnished, and they could move in the very day they arrived. No disagreements over the terms delayed them, and with Mrs. Dashwood’s characteristic speed when it came to things she cared deeply about, every last arrangement was sorted in no time. The horses left to her by her late husband had been sold not long after his death, and when a fair offer came in for their old family carriage, Elinor gently persuaded her mother to sell that too, even though Mrs. Dashwood would have kept it for the comfort of her girls if she had followed her own heart. Elinor’s good sense also trimmed their household staff down to just three servants: two maids and one man, all long-time trusted members of the old Norland household they had known for years.
The man and one of the maids were sent ahead straight to Devonshire to air the cottage linens, light the fires, and get the place ready for their arrival. Mrs. Dashwood did not know Lady Middleton at all, and much preferred to walk straight into her own home as its mistress than impose as a guest at grand Barton Park first. She trusted Sir John’s glowing descriptions of the cottage completely, and felt no need to see it before she stepped over its threshold as its owner.
Her eagerness to leave Norland never faded, not even a little, especially when she saw how openly delighted Fanny was at the prospect of their departure — a delight Fanny only bothered to hide behind a cold, half-hearted suggestion that they could stay a few more weeks if they wished.
This, of course, would have been the perfect time for John Dashwood to make good on the promise he had made to his dying father, to give his stepmother and half-sisters a little sum of money to make their new life comfortable. But Mrs. Dashwood soon gave up all hope of that kindness, and realized from every word he said that he never intended to do more than let them live in his house for free for six months. He talked constantly of how expensive running a large estate was, how many endless demands there were on his purse from every neighbor and acquaintance who expected parties and gifts and favors, until he sounded more like a man on the edge of ruin than a wealthy heir to a grand property, who never intended to give away a single penny of his own money.
Only a few short weeks after Sir John Middleton’s first letter arrived at Norland, every last arrangement was finalised, and Mrs. Dashwood and her three daughters were ready to begin their long journey west.
They shed so many tears over their final goodbyes to the place they had loved so deeply for so many years. On the very last evening they would ever spend at Norland, Marianne wandered alone through the grounds, trailing her fingers over the bark of every old familiar oak tree, her cheeks streaked with quiet sobs. “Dear, dear Norland!” she whispered to the quiet house behind her. “When will I ever stop grieving to leave you? When will I ever learn to call any other place home? Oh, happy house, if only you knew how my heart aches to look at you from this spot, the very last spot I may ever stand to see you from! And all of you, old well-loved trees — you will stay just the same. No leaf will fall early because we are gone, no branch will stop swaying in the wind even when we cannot see you anymore. You will go on, never noticing the joy or the sorrow you gave us, never caring that the people who walked and sang and read beneath your shade have gone away. But who will be left to love you now?”
