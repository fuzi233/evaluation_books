In the old guard of New York, dinner was a solemn affair concluded precisely at seven, followed by the ritual of post-prandial calls—a custom that, though mocked within the circles of Newland Archer, remained the iron law of the city. As the young lawyer strolled up Fifth Avenue from Waverley Place, the thoroughfare lay largely empty save for a cluster of carriages parked before the residence of the Reginald Chiverses, where a dinner for a visiting Duke was underway. Here and there, the silhouette of an elderly gentleman, bundled in a heavy overcoat and muffler, ascended a brownstone stoop and vanished into the warmth of a gas-lit hallway.

Crossing Washington Square, Archer noted that the venerable Mr. du Lac was paying a courtesy call upon his cousins, the Dagonets. Turning the corner onto West Tenth Street, he spotted Mr. Skipworth, a colleague from his firm, clearly en route to visit the Miss Lannings. A short distance further up Fifth Avenue, Mr. Beaufort emerged from his doorway, silhouetted against a burst of interior light, stepped down to his private brougham, and rolled away toward a destination shrouded in mystery—and likely secrecy. For it was not an Opera night, nor was anyone hosting a party; thus, Beaufort’s outing was undeniably illicit. Archer’s mind immediately supplied the missing piece: a modest house beyond Lexington Avenue, recently adorned with ribboned window curtains and flower boxes, before whose freshly painted door the canary-yellow carriage of Miss Fanny Ring waited with uncanny frequency.

Beyond the small, slippery pyramid comprising Mrs. Archer’s entire world lay an almost unmapped quadrant inhabited by artists, musicians, and “those who write.” These scattered fragments of humanity had never shown a desire to integrate with the rigid social structure. Despite rumors of eccentric habits, they were generally considered respectable, yet they preferred solitude. Medora Manson, in her prosperous era, had attempted to inaugurate a “literary salon,” but it had quickly withered due to the literary class's refusal to attend. Others had tried similarly, including a household of the Blenkers—an intense, voluble mother and three dowdy daughters who mimicked her—where one might encounter Edwin Booth, Patti, William Winter, the rising Shakespearean actor George Rignold, and various magazine editors and critics.

Mrs. Archer and her circle harbored a distinct timidity regarding these individuals. They were unpredictable, opaque, possessing backgrounds and inner lives that defied categorization. While literature and art were held in high esteem by the Archer set, Mrs. Archer constantly reminded her children of a bygone era when society included luminaries like Washington Irving, Fitz-Greene Halleck, and the author of *The Culprit Fay*. The celebrated writers of that generation were “gentlemen”; perhaps their successors shared similar sentiments, yet their origins, their appearances, their hair, and their intimate ties to the stage and the Opera rendered any traditional New York standard inapplicable to them.

“When I was a girl,” Mrs. Archer would muse, “we knew everyone between the Battery and Canal Street; and only the people one knew possessed carriages. It was perfectly simple to identify anyone then. Now, one cannot tell, and I prefer not to try.”

Only the formidable Mrs. Catherine Mingott, with her lack of moral prejudice and a parvenu indifference to subtle distinctions, might have bridged the chasm. Yet she had never opened a book or examined a painting, caring for music solely as a reminder of gala nights at the Italiens during her triumphant days at the Tuileries. Perhaps Beaufort, who matched her in audacity, could have engineered a fusion; but his grand mansion and silk-stockinged footmen stood as barriers to informal sociability. Furthermore, he was as illiterate as old Mrs. Mingott, viewing “fellows who write” merely as paid purveyors of wealthy men's pleasures, a sentiment reinforced by no influential critic.

Newland Archer had known these divisions since childhood, accepting them as the bedrock of his universe. He understood that elsewhere, in cities like Paris, painters, poets, novelists, scientists, and actors were courted with the same fervor as Dukes. He had often imagined the intoxicating intimacy of drawing rooms where conversation revolved around Mérimée, Thackeray, Browning, or William Morris. Such fantasies, however, were inconceivable in New York and deeply unsettling to entertain. Archer knew many of these “writers,” musicians, and painters, meeting them at the Century Club or the nascent musical and theatrical societies. He found them enjoyable there, though bored at the Blenkers’, where they were treated like exotic curiosities amidst fervid and dowdy women. Even after his most stimulating conversations with Ned Winsett, he departed with the conviction that both worlds were too small, merging only when manners evolved to a point of natural assimilation.

These thoughts surfaced as he visualized the society in which the Countess Olenska had lived, suffered, and perhaps tasted mysterious joys. He recalled her amused recounting of how her grandmother Mingott and the Wellands objected to her residing in a “Bohemian” quarter dedicated to “people who write.” It was not the peril, but the poverty, that her family detested; however, they likely viewed literature as a compromise.

Olenska herself held no fear of it. The books scattered throughout her drawing-room—a space where books were typically deemed “out of place”—though mostly fiction, had sharpened Archer's curiosity with names like Paul Bourget, Huysmans, and the Goncourt brothers. As he approached her door, reflecting on these matters, he became acutely aware of the strange inversion of values she represented, and the necessity of mentally transporting himself into conditions utterly alien to his own experience to assist her.

Nastasia opened the door with a mysterious smile. Upon the bench in the hall lay a sable-lined overcoat, a folded opera hat of dull silk bearing a gold “J.B.” on its lining, and a white silk muffler; there could be no mistaking that these expensive items belonged to Julius Beaufort.

Anger surged within Archer, hot and immediate, bringing him to the brink of scribbling a note and departing. Then he remembered: in his letters to Madame Olenska, excessive discretion had prevented him from stating his wish to see her privately. Thus, he blamed no one but himself for her opening her doors to others. With a dogged resolve, he entered the drawing-room determined to make Beaufort feel displaced and to outlast him.

The banker leaned against the mantelpiece, draped with an antique embroidery secured by brass candelabras holding yellowish church candles. His chest was thrust forward, supporting his shoulders against the mantle, his weight resting on one large patent-leather shoe. As Archer entered, Beaufort smiled down at his hostess, who sat on a sofa angled to the fireplace. A table laden with flowers screened her from view, and behind the orchids and azaleas—which Archer recognized as tributes from Beaufort’s hot-houses—sat Madame Olenska, half-reclined, her head propped on her hand, her wide sleeve revealing her arm bare to the elbow.

Ladies receiving guests in the evening typically donned “simple dinner dresses”: a close-fitting armor of whalebone silk, slightly open at the neck with lace ruffles filling the gap, and tight sleeves ending in a flounce that uncovered just enough wrist to display an Etruscan gold bracelet or a velvet band. But Madame Olenska, heedless of tradition, wore a long robe of red velvet bordered with glossy black fur around her chin and down the front. Archer recalled seeing a portrait by the new sensation, Carolus Duran, in the Salon, depicting a lady in a similar bold, sheath-like robe with her chin nestled in fur. There was something perverse and provocative in wearing fur indoors on a warm evening, combining a muffled throat with bare arms; yet the effect was undeniably striking.

“Lord love us—three whole days at Skuytercliff!” Beaufort boomed in his loud, sneering voice as Archer entered. “You’d better pack all your furs and a hot-water bottle.”

“Why? Is the house so cold?” she asked, extending her left hand to Archer in a gesture that mysteriously suggested she expected him to kiss it.

“No; but the missus is,” Beaufort replied, nodding casually at the young man.

“But I thought her so kind. She came herself to invite me. Granny says I must certainly go.”

“Granny would, of course. And I say it's a shame you're going to miss the little oyster supper I planned for you at Delmonico's next Sunday, with Campanini and Scalchi and a lot of jolly people.”

She looked dubiously from the banker to Archer.

“Ah—that does tempt me! Except the other evening at Mrs. Struthers's, I haven't met a single artist since I've been here.”

“Artists? I know one or two painters, very good fellows, that I could bring to see you if you'd allow me,” Archer offered boldly.

“Painters? Are there painters in New York?” Beaufort asked, implying there could be none since he did not purchase their work. Madame Olenska turned to Archer with a grave smile. “That would be charming. But I was really thinking of dramatic artists, singers, actors, musicians. My husband's house was always full of them.”
