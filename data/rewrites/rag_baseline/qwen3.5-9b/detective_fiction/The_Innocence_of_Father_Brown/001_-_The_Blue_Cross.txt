"Harwich. Departure confirmed."

The steamship cut through the Channel, shedding its passengers onto the quayside of Harwich with the mechanical indifference of a factory releasing waste. Among the throng—tourists in silk, workers in grease-stained canvas, families clutching picnic baskets—there was no one who commanded attention. Yet, to the trained eye, he was the only variable worth tracking.

He wore a pale grey jacket that strained slightly against a lean frame, a pristine white waistcoat, and a silver straw hat adorned with a grey-blue ribbon. His face was a study in contrasts: dark-skinned, sharp-featured, framed by a short, black beard that evoked the stiff collars of Elizabethan portraiture. He smoked a cigarette with the deliberate lethargy of a man who owns the clock. To the casual observer, he was merely a wealthy traveler enjoying the transit. In reality, the grey jacket concealed a loaded revolver, the white waistcoat hid a police identification card, and the straw hat shielded the mind of Aristide Valentin, the head of the Paris Police and arguably the finest investigator in Europe.

His destination was London. His objective: the capture of Flambeau.

Flambeau was currently operating within British soil. Interpol, alongside Belgian and Dutch counterparts, had finally triangulated the fugitive's location, tracing him from Ghent to Brussels, and finally to the Hook of Holland. Intelligence suggested he intended to exploit the chaos of the upcoming Eucharistic Congress in London, likely disguising himself as a minor clerical functionary. However, Valentin knew the truth: with Flambeau, certainty was a luxury reserved for the dead.

Decades ago, the world held its breath when Flambeau vanished following the death of his rival, Roland. Since then, a strange, unnatural quietude had descended upon the globe. But during his prime, Flambeau was a force of nature, a colossus of crime as imposing and terrifying as the Kaiser himself. Daily newspapers chronicled his escapades: escaping justice only to commit a fresh atrocity moments later. He was a Gascon giant, possessing a physique that defied logic and a sense of humor that bordered on the macabre. Tales circulated of him flipping judges upside down to "clear their minds," or sprinting down the Rue de Rivoli with a policeman draped over each shoulder like a sack of grain. While his physical feats were often theatrical distractions, his true genius lay in logistical impossibilities. He had operated a dairy company in London with zero inventory, serving thousands of subscribers by simply swapping milk cans door-to-door. He maintained a secret correspondence with a young woman by micro-filming her letters onto microscope slides. He had rerouted travelers by repainting street numbers in the dead of night and invented portable post boxes to intercept mail orders. Despite his massive build, he possessed the agility of a gymnast, leaping like a grasshopper and vanishing into foliage like a monkey.

Valentin knew that finding Flambeau was only the beginning.

The problem remained: *How* to find him?

Valentin's mental map was incomplete. There was one biological constant Flambeau could not alter: his height. If Valentin's keen vision had spotted a towering fruit vendor, a grenadier, or even a tall duchess, he would have moved instantly. But scanning the train carriages and the dockside crowds yielded nothing. No one matched the profile. The group accompanying him on the boat consisted of a railway official, three market gardeners, a diminutive widow from Essex, and a Roman Catholic priest.

The priest was the anomaly. He was the embodiment of rural stagnation, a creature pulled from the mud of the East Anglian fens. His face was round and dull, resembling a boiled potato; his eyes were vacant pools reflecting the grey North Sea. He carried several brown paper parcels he clearly couldn't manage, a large shabby umbrella that perpetually slipped from his grip, and a return ticket he couldn't decipher. With a simplicity bordering on infantile, he explained to every passenger that he needed to protect a parcel containing "real silver with blue stones." This bizarre blend of provincial ignorance and saintly naivety amused Valentin until the priest miraculously reached Tottenham, delivered his parcels, and returned to retrieve his lost umbrella. Even then, Valentin, displaying a rare moment of condescension, warned him not to advertise his silver. Yet, amidst the absurdity, Valentin's gaze remained fixed on the horizon, searching for a silhouette exceeding six feet. Flambeau stood four inches taller.

Arriving at Liverpool Street, Valentin felt a surge of confidence. He had not missed the criminal. He proceeded to Scotland Yard to formalize his request for assistance, then extinguished his cigarette and wandered into the London afternoon.

Beyond Victoria Station, the city unfolded in a grid of grey stone and gaslight. He stopped abruptly in a peculiar square, a pocket of accidental stillness. The surrounding buildings were flat and imposing, projecting an aura of prosperity that felt entirely hollow. In the center lay a patch of shrubbery, desolate as a forgotten island in the Pacific. One side of the square rose significantly higher than the others, forming a natural dais. Breaking the monotony of this elevation was a restaurant, an architectural anomaly that seemed transplanted from Soho. It featured dwarf potted plants and long, striped blinds of lemon-yellow and white. Unusually, a flight of steps ascended directly from the street to the entrance, functioning less as an entryway and more as a fire escape.

Valentin stood before the yellow-and-white blinds, lighting another cigarette. He watched the steps.

"The impossible happens," he muttered, the thought echoing the philosophy of Edgar Allan Poe. Miracles occur when probability collapses under the weight of necessity. Just as Nelson died at the moment of victory, or a man named Williams accidentally murdered a man named Williamson, life is governed by an element of elfin coincidence that the pragmatic miss. Wisdom, Valentin reasoned, required accounting for the unforeseen.

Aristide Valentin was uniquely French. He was not a "thinking machine"—a term he despised, associated with the cold fatalism of modern materialism. Machines fail because they cannot adapt; they operate on rigid algorithms. Valentin was a thinker, a practitioner of pure, unadulterated logic. The French electrified the world not by inventing paradoxes, but by executing truisms with relentless precision. They took a simple principle and drove it to its absolute limit, much like the French Revolution. Because Valentin understood reason, he understood its boundaries. Without strong first principles, logic becomes a tool for exploration rather than deduction.

Currently, he lacked those principles. Flambeau was missing from Harwich. If the criminal was in London, he could be anywhere—from a tramp on Wimbledon Common to a toastmaster at the Metropole. In this vacuum of information, Valentin adopted a counter-intuitive strategy. When the rational path was obscured, he followed the trail of the unreasonable. Instead of checking banks or police stations, he sought out the illogical. He knocked on empty houses, ventured down dead-end alleys choked with refuse, and navigated crescents that led nowhere.

"If you have a clue, this is suicide," he argued internally. "If you have none, it is the only viable option. Any oddity that catches the pursuer's eye may be the same anomaly that caught the fugitive's."

Something about the flight of steps leading to the restaurant, combined with the eerie quietude of the square, triggered a spark of romantic intuition in the detective. He resolved to act on impulse. He climbed the stairs and sat at a table by the window, ordering a cup of black coffee.

It was mid-morning, and he had not eaten. The remnants of previous breakfasts cluttered the table, a testament to his hunger. Adding a poached egg to his order, he began to stir sugar into his coffee, his mind drifting back to Flambeau. He recalled the criminal's methods: escaping via nail scissors, fleeing a burning building, paying for an unstamped letter, and distracting guards by pointing out a comet. Valentin believed his deductive faculties were equal to Flambeau's creativity, yet he acknowledged the inherent disadvantage. "The criminal is the creator," he murmured with a bitter smile, lifting the cup. "The detective is merely the critic."

He paused. He had added salt to the coffee.

He stared at the vessel holding the granules. It was undoubtedly a sugar bowl, designed with the same purposefulness as a champagne flute. Why was salt stored there? He scanned the table for conventional containers. There were two salt cellars, brimming with white crystals. Perhaps the condiment in the cellars was unique? He tasted it. Sugar.

Confusion rippled through him. He looked around the restaurant, searching for other signs of this singular artistic sensibility—the inversion of sugar and salt. Aside from a dark, viscous stain on one of the white-papered walls, the establishment appeared immaculate, cheerful, and utterly ordinary.

He rang the bell.

A waiter, fuzzy-haired and blinking sleepily, hurried over. Valentin, appreciative of simple humor, asked him to taste the contents of the sugar bowl and verify its quality. The result was instantaneous: the waiter yawned violently, then snapped awake.

"Do you perform this delicate prank on your patrons every morning?" Valentin inquired, his tone laced with irony. "Does the reversal of salt and sugar never grow stale as a jest?"
