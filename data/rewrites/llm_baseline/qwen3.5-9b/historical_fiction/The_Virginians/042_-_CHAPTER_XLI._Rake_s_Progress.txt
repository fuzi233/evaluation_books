In the era of Harry Warrington, the intellectual world was deeply engrossed in the debate concerning the relative merits of ancient versus modern literature—a controversy to which our young hero paid little attention. The scholars and the general public alike tended to side with the ancients. Yet, looking backward from our vantage point today, we realize that "the moderns" of that age are simply "the ancients" of ours. Just as we judge the writers of the mid-18th century, so too will our descendants, a century hence, pass judgment upon us.

Regarding book learning, one might argue that the respectable ancestors of that time—despite possessing the formidable works of Gibbon—are ultimately defeated. One need only look to the professors of Cambridge and Glasgow today to find individuals who command a knowledge of Greek surpassing what was available across all European universities, including the famed academy of Athens itself. In matters of science, they were scarcely more advanced than the very heathens to whom they admitted inferiority in literature. But what of public and private morality? Is the year 1858 truly superior to the century preceding it?

Gentlemen of Mr. Disraeli's House of Commons, has every one of you a price, as was common in the times of Walpole or Newcastle—or, more delicately put, have you all succumbed to corruption? Ladies, I do not claim you are a society of Vestals; however, the chronicles of a hundred years ago bristle with scandal, making you fortunate not to have lived in such perilous times. On my conscience, I believe men and women are both better now. Not only are the Susannas more numerous, but the Elders are far less wicked. Have you ever read *Clarissa*, *Tom Jones*, or *Roderick Random*? Have you seen the paintings of contemporary artists depicting the men, women, and society of that day? Imagine describing the exploits of a Mr. Lovelace or Lady Bellaston, or that remarkable "Lady of Quality" who lent her memoirs to the author of *Peregrine Pickle*. How the pure, outraged Nineteenth Century would blush, scream, flee the room, summon the young ladies, and order Mr. Mudie to cease sending such odious volumes!

Perhaps you, madam, at fifty-eight, are too squeamish, crying out before you are harmed, when no offense was intended. Perhaps the novelist's art suffers from the restraints placed upon him, much like an honest statue at St. Peter's or the Vatican, marred by the tin draperies in which pious old women have swaddled the fair limbs of marble. Yet, in your prudery lies reason, just as there is in state censorship of the press. If a page contains matter dangerous to good morals, out with the scissors, censor, and clip the prurient paragraph! We have no choice but to submit. Society, the despot, has issued his imperial decree. We may lament that the statue looked better without the drapery, or plead that the moral lesson would be clearer if the whole fable were told. But away with him—not a word!

I have never seen pianofortes in the United States wearing frilled muslin trousers on their legs; but depend on it, that muslin covered some of the notes as well as the mahogany, muffling the music and stopping the player. To what does this prelude lead? It introduces us to Harry Warrington, Esquire, in his Bond Street lodgings in London, and the life he and many other fashionable young bucks led at that time. I cannot take my fair young reader into such scenes any more than Lady Squeams could take her daughter to Cremorne Gardens on an ordinary evening.

My dear Miss Diana (pshaw! I know you are eighty-three, despite your shyness and your desire to convince us you have just left schoolroom dinners and a pinafore): consider your grandfather when he was a young man about town, a member of a club at White's, dining at Pontac's off the feasts provided by Braund and Lebeck, riding to Newmarket with March and Rockingham, and toasting the best in England alongside Gilly Williams and George Selwyn—and failing to understand George's jokes, the flavor of which has largely evaporated since the bottling process. The old gentleman led a life your noble aunt, author of *Legends of the Squeams*; or, *Fair Fruits of a Family Tree*, has not hinted at. This was before your grandmother adopted those serious views that characterized her later residence at Bath, and after Colonel Tibbalt married Miss Lye, the wealthy heiress of a soap boiler. During those days, her ladyship's wild oats were sown.

When young, she was as giddy as the rest of the genteel world. At her Hill Street mansion, she hosted ten card tables on Wednesday and Sunday evenings, save for the brief period when Ranelagh opened on Sundays. Every night of her life she gambled for eight, nine, or ten hours. Everyone else in society did the same. She lost; she won; she cheated; she pawned her jewels—who knows what else she was unwilling to pledge to fund her fury for play? What of that infamous after-supper duel at the Shakespeare's Head in Covent Garden, between your grandfather and Colonel Tibbalt? They drew swords and engaged solely in the presence of Sir John Screwby, who sat drunk beneath the table. Their fight was interrupted by Mr. John Fielding's constables, and your grandfather was carried home to Hill Street wounded in a sedan chair.

Let me tell you, those gentlemen in powder and ruffles, who turned the toes of their buckled pumps with such delicate precision, were terrible fellows. Swords were perpetually drawn; bottle after bottle was consumed; oaths roared incessantly in conversation; tavern drawers and watchmen were stabbed and maimed; chairmen were beaten; and citizens were insulted by reeling pleasure-seekers. Have you visited Cremorne with proper vouchers? Do you recall our great theaters thirty years ago? You were deemed too refined for the stage. Yet, you have no idea what the playhouses were, or what the green boxes represented, when Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard performed before them! And I, for my children's sake, thank that good actor in his retirement who was the first to banish that shame from the theater.

No, madam, you are mistaken. I do not boast of superior virtue. I do not claim you are naturally better than your ancestress in her wild, rouged, gambling, tear-stained days, nor even than poor Polly Fogle, recently arrested for shoplifting, who would have been hanged a century ago. Simply, I am heartily grateful that my temptations are fewer, having quite enough to contend with those of the present century. Thus, if Harry Warrington rides down to Newmarket for the October meeting and loses or wins his fortune there; if he joins a party at the Shakespeare or Bedford Head; if he dines at White's Ordinary and sits down to macco and lansquenet afterward; if he boxes the watch and appears at the Roundhouse; if he briefly becomes a wild, dissipated, harum-scarum young Harry Warrington—I, knowing the weakness of human nature, shall not be surprised. Quite aware of my own shortcomings, I do not intend to be overly savage at my neighbor's expense.

Mr. Sampson was different. In his chapel in Long Acre, he whipped Vice tremendously; he gave Sin no quarter; he cursed Blasphemy with superior anathemas; he knocked Drunkenness down and trampled the prostrate brute wallowing in the gutter; he dragged out conjugal infidelity and pounded her with endless rhetorical stones. Yet, after service, he would come to dinner at the Star and Garter, make a bowl of punch for Harry and his friends at the Bedford Head, join a hand at whist at Mr. Warrington's lodgings or Lord March's, or attend anywhere there was a supper and good company.

I often reflect, regarding Mr. Warrington's conduct during this period of his arrival in London, that I may have held my usual degrading and uncharitable view of him—for, seeing as I have uttered not a single word of virtuous indignation against his behavior, and if it was not reprehensible, I have certainly judged him most cruelly. Oh, Truthfulness, Oh Beauty, Oh Modesty, Oh Benevolence, Oh Pudor, Oh Morals, Oh Blushing Shame, Oh Namby-Pamby—each with your respective capital letters honoring your names! Oh Niminy, Oh Piminy! How shall I dare to say that a young man ever was a young man?

No doubt, dear young lady, I am calumniating Mr. Warrington according to my heartless custom. As proof, here is a letter from the Warrington collection, written by Harry to his mother, containing not a single word suggesting he was leading a wild life. Such a letter from an only son to a fond and exemplary parent must be true:

"BOND STREET, LONDON, October 25, 1756.
